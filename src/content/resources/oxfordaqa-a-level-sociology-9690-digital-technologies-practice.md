Revise first with the [digital technologies study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-digital-technologies/) and the [digital technologies revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-digital-technologies-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by sousveillance. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two different types of hacking. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by healthism. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two differences between the panopticon and the surveillant assemblage as models of surveillance. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two reasons why official figures may understate the extent of cybercrime. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for adults in the fictional country of Maranth.

| Age group | Adults with home internet access (%) | Of those with access: able to complete an online government form unaided (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 18 to 34 | 95 | 90 |
| 65 and over | 60 | 45 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of all adults aged 65 and over who both have home internet access and can complete an online form unaided. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain the difference between the first-level and second-level digital divide. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> *Starfall Realms* is a fictional online game with 2 million players. Players join "houses" that meet online every evening and sometimes in person at fan events. The game is free to download, but players can buy "mystery chests" containing random rare items. A survey by a fictional players' group found that many female players hide their gender in voice chat to avoid abuse. The game company says it bans abusive accounts, but players say bans are slow.

**(a)** Identify two ways in which *Starfall Realms* creates social relationships. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse how online games can be sites of community, commercialisation and inequality. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which digital surveillance may lead to social sorting. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three ways in which on-demand and streaming services have changed audiences' relationship with media. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how self-tracking technologies for health and fitness may reinforce inequalities. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that cyber security can effectively control cybercrime. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent is digital surveillance a form of social control? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** Watching or recording those in power from below [1], for example citizens filming police officers on their phones (Steve Mann's term) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: criminal hacking (unauthorised intrusion for gain) [1]; ethical hacking (paid testing of security) [1]; hacktivism (hacking for political causes). **[2]**

**3.** The view that health is mainly an individual responsibility [1] achieved through lifestyle choices such as diet and exercise (Crawford) [1], which can lead to people being blamed for ill health while social causes such as poverty are ignored [1]. **[3]**

**4.** The panopticon is a single central point of observation [1], while the surveillant assemblage combines many scattered data flows from cards, phones and cameras [1]. The panopticon works by making people discipline themselves because they might be watched [1], while the assemblage builds "data doubles" of people, often without their awareness [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Many victims do not report cybercrime [1], because they are embarrassed, do not know they have been targeted, or think police cannot help [1]. Offences often cross national borders [1], so they may be recorded nowhere or in a different country from the victim's [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** 60% x 45% = 0.60 x 0.45 [1] = 27% of all adults aged 65 and over [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The first-level divide is unequal access: 95% of 18 to 34 year olds have home internet but only 60% of those aged 65 and over [1], showing an age gap in material access [1]. The second-level divide is unequal skills among those with access (Hargittai): 90% of younger users can complete a form unaided compared with 45% of older users [1], so closing the access gap alone would leave older people unable to use online services fully [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: players join houses [1]; houses meet online every evening [1]; fan events bring players together in person. **[2]**
**(b)** Houses that meet nightly and at fan events show games creating lasting communities [1], stretching Huizinga's magic circle into everyday social life [1]. "Mystery chests" with random items show commercialisation through loot-box style purchases [1], which may resemble gambling and target young players [1]. Female players hiding their gender to avoid abuse shows inequality and hostility in gaming spaces, as in Gamergate [1], and slow bans suggest the company profits from the game while doing little to protect those targeted [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Data from cameras, cards and online activity are used to place people into risk categories [1], so groups labelled "high risk", such as residents of poorer areas, face more stops, checks or refusals (Lyon) [1]. Companies use data profiles to sort customers by profitability [1], offering better prices and services to some and worse terms or exclusion to others [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Audiences choose when to watch rather than following broadcast schedules [1], weakening shared national viewing moments [1]. Streaming offers huge numbers of niche titles (the long tail) [1], so audiences fragment into smaller interest groups [1]. Recommendation systems suggest what to watch next [1], so platforms rather than broadcasters shape viewing choices [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Self-tracking turns bodies into data (Lupton) [1].
- Devices and apps cost money, so wealthier people have more access [1].
- Healthism (Crawford) frames health as personal responsibility [1].
- Those who cannot meet targets, such as disabled or ill people, may be blamed [1].
- Social causes of ill health, such as poverty and shift work, are ignored [1].
- Insurers or employers may use data to sort people [1].
- Counter: tracking can help people manage long-term conditions [1].
- Analysis: benefits flow to the already healthy and affluent, widening gaps [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Cyber security includes passwords, encryption, two-factor authentication and laws [1].
- Strong security reduces the chances of successful attacks [1].
- Yar's routine activity theory: capable guardianship reduces crime [1].
- Estonia invested heavily in cyber defence after the 2007 attacks [1].
- International cooperation helps trace offenders [1].
- Application: own-society example of a bank or government security measure [1].
- Counter: WannaCry (2017) hit organisations with out-of-date systems, showing gaps [1].
- Offenders are anonymous and cross borders [1].
- Many victims do not report, so the scale is unknown [1].
- Security costs money, so smaller firms and poorer users are less protected [1].
- Social engineering such as phishing targets people, not systems [1].
- Judgement: security reduces some risks but cannot control cybercrime alone [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Foucault: the panopticon produces self-discipline [1].
- People behave differently when they may be watched online or on camera [1].
- Lyon: social sorting shapes life chances [1].
- Haggerty and Ericson: data doubles built without awareness [1].
- Snowden (2013): mass state collection of communications data [1].
- Own-society application: CCTV, facial recognition or ID systems [1].
- Workplace monitoring controls workers' time [1].
- Parents tracking children extends control into families [1].
- Counter: surveillance can protect people and solve crimes [1].
- Mathiesen's synopticon: the many watch the few, holding power to account [1].
- Sousveillance lets citizens watch the powerful [1].
- Many people accept surveillance for convenience [1].
- Own-society application: citizens filming officials or reporting abuses [1].
- Surveillance falls unequally on minorities and poorer areas [1].
- Analysis: control depends on who collects data and for what purpose [1].
- Judgement: largely a form of social control, though it can also be turned against the powerful [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Use more than one model of surveillance; the panopticon alone cannot carry a top-band answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating access as the whole of the digital divide.
- Describing surveillance only through the panopticon.
- Using "hacking" for every cybercrime.
- Presenting game-related moral panics as proof of harm.
- Leaving data unused in an "explain using the table" question.
