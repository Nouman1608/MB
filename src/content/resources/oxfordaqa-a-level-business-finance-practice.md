## Questions

**1.** State **two** internal sources of finance. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of crowd funding for a business that is just starting up. **[4]**

*Questions 3 to 5 use this information.* Fernhollow Pottery sells hand-made mugs at £24 each. Variable cost is £9 per mug and fixed costs are £6,300 a month.

**3.** Calculate Fernhollow's break-even output per month. **[2]**

**4.** In one month Fernhollow sells 560 mugs. Calculate the margin of safety and the profit for that month. **[4]**

**5.** The price of clay rises, so variable cost increases to £11 per mug. Price and fixed costs do not change.

**(a)** Calculate the new break-even output. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how this change would appear on Fernhollow's break-even chart. **[2]**

**6.** Thornbury Optics Ltd reports: revenue £900,000; cost of sales £495,000; operating expenses £288,000; interest £18,000; taxes £22,500. Calculate Thornbury's gross, operating and net profit margins. **[6]**

**7.** Complete the budget statement for Pellmore Garden Supplies. Calculate the missing figures and label each variance favourable (F) or adverse (A). **[4]**

| Item | Budget (£) | Actual (£) | Variance (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 72,000 | 68,400 | ? |
| Materials | 21,600 | 20,520 | ? |
| Labour | 18,000 | 18,900 | ? |
| Overheads | 15,000 | 15,000 | 0 |
| Profit | 17,400 | ? | ? |

**8.** Pellmore has an opening cash balance of £3,200. Complete its cash flow forecast. **[4]**

| £ | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 14,500 | 15,200 | 19,000 |
| Total outflows | 16,900 | 17,600 | 15,300 |
| Net cash flow | ? | ? | ? |
| Opening balance | 3,200 | ? | ? |
| Closing balance | ? | ? | ? |

**9.** Hollinsby Electrical has revenue of £547,500, cost of sales of £328,500, trade receivables of £63,000 and trade payables of £16,200. Calculate its receivables days and payables days. **[4]**

**10.**

**(a)** Explain the difference between profit and cash. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the difference between profit and profitability. **[2]**

**11.** Larkspur Textiles makes curtain fabric and sells it to retailers on credit. Its revenue is £876,000 and its trade receivables are £144,000. Larkspur is short of cash and is choosing between debt factoring and offering retailers a discount for paying early.

**(a)** Calculate Larkspur's receivables days. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse whether debt factoring or an early payment incentive would better improve Larkspur's cash flow. **[6]**

**12.** Mina is starting Kilnworth Coffee Roasters. She needs £75,000 for a roasting machine and to fit out a unit, and has £15,000 of her own savings. A business angel offers £60,000 in return for 30% of the shares. Alternatively, Mina could apply for a five-year bank loan of £60,000. Evaluate which source of finance Mina should choose. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: retained profits [1]; sale of assets, sale and leaseback, or working capital [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "State" needs only the names, but trade credit and overdrafts are external sources, so listing them here earns nothing.

**2.** Advantage: the business can raise money from many small backers without a trading record [1], and a successful campaign shows there is demand before the business commits to production [1]. Disadvantage: if the campaign misses its target the business may receive nothing [1], and putting the idea online lets competitors see and copy it before launch [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each "explain" point needs a reason or consequence after it; a one-word advantage such as "cheap" gives the examiner little to credit.

**3.** Contribution per unit = 24 − 9 = £15 [1]. Break-even = 6,300 ÷ 15 = **420 mugs** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the contribution line separately; if the final figure is wrong, visible working is the only evidence of correct method.

**4.** Margin of safety = 560 − 420 [1] = **140 mugs** [1]. Profit = 140 × £15 [1] = **£2,100** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Margin of safety here is in units, so write "mugs", not "£"; the long route (revenue £13,440 − total costs £11,340) gives the same profit and is a useful check.

**5. (a)** New contribution = 24 − 11 = £13 [1]. Break-even = 6,300 ÷ 13 = 484.6, so **485 mugs** [1].
**(b)** The total cost line becomes steeper but still starts at £6,300 on the vertical axis [1]. It now crosses the revenue line further to the right, at a higher output, so break-even rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Round break-even up, since 484 mugs would still leave a small loss; and in part (b) say which line moves, because "the chart changes" describes nothing.

**6.** Gross profit = 900,000 − 495,000 = £405,000 [1]; gross margin = 405,000 ÷ 900,000 × 100 = **45%** [1]. Operating profit = 405,000 − 288,000 = £117,000 [1]; operating margin = **13%** [1]. Net profit = 117,000 − 18,000 − 22,500 = £76,500 [1]; net margin = **8.5%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each profit figure before its margin and include the % sign; a margin worked out over cost of sales instead of revenue is a different (wrong) ratio.

**7.** Revenue 72,000 − 68,400: **£3,600 A** [1]. Materials **£1,080 F** and labour **£900 A** [1]. Actual profit = 68,400 − 20,520 − 18,900 − 15,000 = **£13,980** [1]. Profit variance = 17,400 − 13,980 = **£3,420 A** [1], which checks: 1,080 − 3,600 − 900 = −3,420. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A variance needs both the amount and F or A; give "3,600" with no label and the answer is incomplete.

**8.** Net cash flows: **(2,400)**, **(2,400)**, **3,700** [1]. Month 1 closing = 3,200 − 2,400 = **£800** [1]. Month 2 opening £800, closing 800 − 2,400 = **(£1,600)** [1]. Month 3 opening (£1,600), closing −1,600 + 3,700 = **£2,100** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Carry each closing balance across as the next opening balance and bracket negatives clearly; a missing minus sign changes every later figure.

**9.** Receivables days = 63,000 ÷ 547,500 × 365 [1] = **42 days** [1]. Payables days = 16,200 ÷ 328,500 × 365 [1] = **18 days** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Receivables go over revenue but payables go over cost of sales; swapping the denominators is the usual slip.

**10. (a)** Profit is revenue minus costs for a period, counted when sales are made [1]; cash is money actually in the bank, so a credit sale adds to profit now but to cash only when the customer pays [1].
**(b)** Profit is an absolute amount [1]; profitability measures profit relative to revenue, such as a profit margin, so a larger firm can make more profit yet be less profitable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use a short example in each part; it shows understanding, not a memorised definition.

**11. (a)** Receivables days = 144,000 ÷ 876,000 × 365 [1] = **60 days** [1].
**(b)** Debt factoring turns most of Larkspur's £144,000 of invoices into cash almost at once [1], so cash no longer waits about 60 days for retailers to pay [1]. But the factor keeps a fee, reducing Larkspur's profit on every invoice factored [1]. An early payment incentive could bring cash in within days for retailers who take it [1], but the discount reduces revenue and retailers who are short of cash themselves may ignore it, so the benefit is uncertain [1]. Judgement: if Larkspur needs cash urgently, factoring is more reliable; if the gap is small, the incentive may be cheaper -- it depends on how many retailers respond [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Analyse" asks for chains of reasoning tied to Larkspur's 60-day figure; generic lists of advantages earn little without that link.

**12.** For the business angel: no repayments are due while sales are still building, which protects a start-up's cash flow [1]; the angel may bring experience and contacts in the coffee trade [1]. Against: Mina gives up 30% of the shares, so 30% of future dividends and some control [1]. For the loan: Mina keeps full ownership and all future profit [1]; fixed repayments make cash flow forecasting easier [1]. Against: repayments and interest start straight away, before the business is established [1]; with no trading record the bank may demand security such as a personal guarantee, or refuse [1]. Judgement: the angel is probably more appropriate because a new roastery's early cash flow is uncertain [1]; but if Mina expects strong sales quickly and values control, the loan keeps the business hers -- it depends on how fast sales grow [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An "Evaluate" answer needs a clear recommendation and a "depends on" that uses Kilnworth's facts (the 30% stake, the start-up stage, the £60,000 gap); balanced points with no decision leave the judgement credit unearned.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by price rather than by contribution per unit.
- Rounding break-even output down, or leaving a decimal.
- Giving variance amounts without F or A.
- Treating a cost above budget as favourable because the number is bigger.
- Using the wrong opening balance in month 2 or 3 of a cash flow forecast.
- Putting payables over revenue instead of cost of sales.
- Subtracting only interest, or only taxes, to reach net profit.
- Listing finance sources without linking them to the amount, time period, control and stage of the business.
- Ending an "Evaluate" answer without a recommendation.
