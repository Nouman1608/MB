## What the specification lists

- Explain why the results of one decision making tool may conflict with another.
- Evaluate a business's decisions based on quantitative and qualitative information.
- Evaluate the likely success of a business proposal using decision making tools.
- Evaluate which course of action a business should take.
- Evaluate the usefulness, strengths and limitations of decision making tools.
- Evaluate reasons for success or failure of a business.

## Core recall

### What each tool measures and ignores

| Tool | Measures | Ignores |
|---|---|---|
| Decision tree | Average outcome under risk | Timing; qualitative factors |
| Payback | Speed of recovering the outlay | Cash after payback; time value |
| ARR | Average yearly return on outlay | Timing |
| NPV | Today's value of cash flows less cost | Sensitive to discount rate |
| Break-even | Output needed to cover costs | Demand; assumes constant prices and costs |
| Ansoff's matrix | Distance from current knowledge (risk) | All figures |
| SWOT, qualitative views | Fit with strengths, culture, mission | Measurement |

### Five reasons tools conflict

1. They measure different things (speed, average return, value, risk).
2. They treat time differently (discounted or not).
3. They rely on estimates; a small change can reverse a ranking.
4. They use different information (numbers or judgement).
5. They take different time horizons.

### Standard formulas

- Payback: years until cumulative inflows equal the outlay; part year = amount still needed ÷ that year's inflow.
- ARR = (total inflows − outlay) ÷ years ÷ outlay × 100.
- NPV = sum of (inflow × discount factor) − outlay.
- Decision tree net gain = EV − cost.

### Resolving a conflict: five steps

1. Decide which tools matter most for the objectives (cash-short: payback; long-term value: NPV).
2. Find the weakest assumption.
3. Test it: how far can it move before the decision changes?
4. Add qualitative factors and stakeholders.
5. Judge, with a condition.

### Judging likely success of a proposal

- Realistic inputs (compare with past record).
- Sensitivity (which input reverses the result?).
- Resources (cash, skills, time).
- Fit with objectives and mission.
- Competitor and stakeholder reactions.

### Usefulness of tools

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Each covers another's blind spot | Same forecasts feed every tool, so agreement can mislead |
| Agreement builds confidence | Time and skill needed |
| Disagreement shows where uncertainty lies | Managers may choose the tool that suits them |

### Reasons for success or failure

- Sort causes into internal (forecasts, finance, staff, management, review) and external (economy, interest rates, competitors, tastes, regulation).
- Link them: external pressure often exposes an internal weakness.
- Choose the most important cause and say why.

## Quick worked reminder

Kestle Leisure (invented), £500,000 trampoline park: tree net gain £280,000 (yes); payback 3.56 years against a 3-year target (no); ARR 11.2% against 15% (no); NPV +£73,840 at 10%, +£480 at 15% (marginal); Ansoff related diversification (caution). The tree ignores timing; NPV shows the value depends on the cost of finance.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Decision making tool | A method that turns information into a recommendation |
| Time value of money | £1 now is worth more than £1 in the future |
| Discount factor | The multiplier converting a future cash flow to its present value |
| Sensitivity | How much a result changes when an input changes |
| Quantitative information | Numerical data such as costs, forecasts and ratios |
| Qualitative information | Non-numerical evidence such as opinions, ethics and brand |
| Internal cause | A reason for success or failure within the business's control |
| External cause | A reason for success or failure outside the business's control |

## Exam traps

- Explain why tools differ; do not just report results.
- NPV is not automatically right: it depends on the discount rate and forecasts.
- Tie the choice of tool to the business's objectives.
- Prioritise reasons for failure; do not list them.
- Agreement between tools is only as good as the shared forecasts.

## Self-test

1. Give two reasons why tools may conflict.
2. Why can a decision tree favour a project that payback rejects?
3. Outlay £200,000; inflows £50,000, £70,000, £90,000. Find payback.
4. For question 3, find the ARR over three years.
5. Why does NPV fall as the discount rate rises?
6. Which tool should matter most to a business short of cash?
7. What is the weakest assumption in most investment decisions?
8. Give two questions to ask when judging the likely success of a proposal.
9. Why can agreement between quantitative tools be false comfort?
10. Give one internal and one external reason for business failure.
11. Why should you name the most important reason for failure?
12. Give one qualitative factor that could override a positive NPV.

### Answers

1. Any two: they measure different things; treat time differently; rely on estimates; use different information; take different time horizons.
2. The tree adds up undiscounted cash over the whole life, so large late inflows count fully; payback looks only at how fast the outlay is recovered.
3. Cumulative 50,000, 120,000, 210,000; 2 + 80,000 ÷ 90,000 = **2.89 years**.
4. Total profit 210,000 − 200,000 = 10,000; 10,000 ÷ 3 = 3,333; 3,333 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **1.7%**.
5. A higher rate reduces the present value of each future cash flow, especially later ones.
6. Payback, because it shows how quickly cash is recovered.
7. The forecast of future sales or cash inflows.
8. Any two: are the inputs realistic? Which input would reverse the result? Does the business have the resources? Does it fit the objectives? How will rivals react?
9. Because every tool uses the same forecasts, so if the forecasts are wrong, all the tools are wrong together.
10. Internal: over-optimistic forecasts or poor cash management. External: rising interest rates or a new competitor.
11. Evaluation requires a judgement; prioritising shows which cause, if fixed, would most likely have prevented failure.
12. For example, a conflict with the mission or CSR policy, a risk to reputation, or staff opposition.
