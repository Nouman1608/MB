## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between quantitative data and qualitative data. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** sources of secondary market research. **[2]**

**3.** Ravenscroft College has 4,800 students: 2,400 full-time students aged 16 to 18, 1,440 adult part-time students and 960 apprentices. It wants a stratified sample of 120 students. Calculate how many students should be chosen from each group. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **one** advantage of using a focus group for a business developing a new board game. **[3]**

**5.** A sports drinks company finds a correlation value of −0.72 between the price of its drink in different shops and weekly sales in those shops.

**(a)** Describe this correlation. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** way the company could use this finding in its pricing decisions. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **one** disadvantage for a small bakery of relying on government publications for its market research. **[3]**

**7.** A survey of target customers found that 31% would subscribe to a meal-kit service, with a 95% confidence interval of 28% to 34%. The service will be profitable if more than 25% of target customers subscribe. Explain what the confidence interval tells the business. **[3]**

**8.** Explain **one** reason why businesses research a sample rather than the whole population. **[2]**

## Section B

**9.** Hollis Hotels has found that fewer guests are returning for a second stay. Its managers want to find out why. Analyse whether Hollis should use questionnaires or interviews for this research. **[6]**

**10.** Fernvale Dairy tested two new yoghurt flavours on a sample of shoppers.

| Flavour | Would buy | 95% confidence interval |
|---|---|---|
| Honey fig | 38% | 33% to 43% |
| Rhubarb | 34% | 29% to 39% |

**(a)** Explain whether Fernvale can conclude that honey fig is more popular than rhubarb among all shoppers. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** way Fernvale could make its findings more reliable. **[2]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways in which correlation data could mislead a marketing manager. **[4]**

**12.** Wrenfield Cosmetics plans to launch a range of skincare for men. It has a research budget of $20,000 and must decide within two months whether to launch. It is considering three options:

- **Option 1:** a commissioned survey of 1,200 men, chosen by quota sampling, costing $18,000 and taking six weeks.
- **Option 2:** buying a published market report on men's grooming for $2,500, available immediately.
- **Option 3:** running four focus groups with men who already buy its products, costing $4,000 and taking three weeks.

Evaluate which approach, or combination of approaches, Wrenfield should use. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Quantitative data is numerical, so it can be measured and compared, such as 62% of respondents [1]; qualitative data is descriptive, recording opinions, feelings or reasons, such as why customers prefer a brand [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two of: websites or social media [1]; academic journals [1]; government publications; media articles. **[2]**

**3.** Full-time: 2,400 ÷ 4,800 × 120 = **60** [1]; adult part-time: 1,440 ÷ 4,800 × 120 = **36** [1]; apprentices: 960 ÷ 4,800 × 120 = **24** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** A focus group lets a small group play or discuss the prototype together [1], so the business learns why players like or dislike features, such as rules that are too long [1]. This qualitative feedback can be used to change the game before costly production starts [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** It is a negative correlation [1] that is fairly strong [1].

**(b)** Higher prices are associated with lower sales [1], so the company could avoid raising prices in competitive shops, or test small price cuts where it wants higher volume, remembering that other factors may also affect sales [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Government publications describe whole industries or the whole country [1], so they will not show demand in the bakery's own neighbourhood or for its particular products [1]. The bakery could make decisions on figures that do not reflect its local customers, and the data may also be out of date [1]. **[3]**

**7.** The business can be 95% confident that the true proportion of target customers who would subscribe lies between 28% and 34% [1]. Even the lowest value in the interval, 28%, is above the 25% needed [1], so the findings support launching, although the result depends on the sample being representative [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Researching every member of the population would be too expensive and slow [1], whereas a well-chosen sample gives results close to those of the whole population at much lower cost and in less time [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Hollis needs to know **why** guests are not returning, which calls for reasons and feelings [1]. Interviews allow follow-up questions, so a guest who mentions "poor value" can be asked which parts of the stay disappointed them [1]; this depth helps managers fix the right problem [1]. However, interviews are slow and costly per guest, so only a few guests can be interviewed and they may not be typical [1]. A questionnaire emailed to past guests is cheaper and reaches many people, giving numbers on how common each complaint is [1]. On balance, a short questionnaire to find the most common reasons, followed by a few interviews to explore them, would give Hollis both breadth and depth [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** No [1]. The intervals overlap between 33% and 39% [1], so the true figure for rhubarb could be higher than the true figure for honey fig; the difference in the sample may be due to chance [1].

**(b)** Use a larger sample [1], which would narrow the confidence intervals and might show whether the difference is real [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Correlation shows association, not cause [1]: sales and advertising may both rise in December because of the season, so a manager might wrongly increase advertising in other months [1]. A correlation found over a narrow range of data may not hold outside it [1]: doubling the advertising budget beyond anything tried may not double sales [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Credit balanced reasoning using the case, for example:

- Option 1 gives quantitative data from a large sample, so Wrenfield could estimate the share of men who would buy [1].
- But it uses $18,000 of the $20,000 budget [1] and takes six weeks of the two months, leaving little time to act on the results [1].
- Quota sampling lets interviewers choose whom to ask, so the 1,200 may not be representative [1].
- Option 2 is cheap and immediate, so it can show market size and trends in men's grooming quickly [1].
- But it was written for other purposes and will not test Wrenfield's own brand or products [1], and rivals can buy the same report [1].
- Option 3 gives qualitative insight into why men would or would not buy, such as packaging or scent [1].
- But the groups use existing customers, who already like the brand, so their views may be more positive than the wider market's [1].
- A combination of Options 2 and 3 costs $6,500 and fits the deadline [1], giving market context and reasons, but no reliable estimate of demand [1].
- Judgement: Wrenfield should buy the report at once and run the focus groups, then use a cheaper online survey of non-customers if time allows, because Option 1 leaves too little budget and time for the decision it is meant to support [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers test each option against all three influences in the specification (cost, time, reliability), using the case figures, before reaching a judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stratified samples whose group sizes do not add up to the sample size.
- Describing a correlation by sign only, without its strength.
- Treating overlapping confidence intervals as proof that one option is more popular.
- Recommending research without checking the budget and the deadline.
- Explaining focus groups or interviews without saying what the business would do with the findings.
