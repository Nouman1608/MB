## What the specification asks

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.9.2 a | Simple calorimetry (heating water in a glass or metal container) compares energy released by fuels; use Q = mcΔT |
| 3.9.2 b | Energy in J or kJ, per gram or per mole |
| 3.9.2 c | Temperature change in an insulated container for reactions in solution: solids with water, or neutralisation |
| 3.9.2 d | Energy level diagrams: relative energies, activation energy and overall energy change |
| 3.9.2 e | Energy is supplied to break bonds and released when bonds form |
| 3.9.2 f and g | Exothermic: more energy released forming bonds than needed to break them; endothermic: the reverse; calculate using bond dissociation energies supplied |
| 3.9.2 h | Catalysts provide a different pathway with lower activation energy; show this on a diagram |

## Q = mcΔT: the one equation

- **Q** = energy transferred to the water or solution (J)
- **m** = mass of water or solution heated (g); for dilute solutions, 1 cm³ has a mass of 1 g
- **c** = specific heat capacity of water, 4.2 J/g °C
- **ΔT** = temperature change (°C)

Then convert: kJ = J ÷ 1000; per gram = kJ ÷ grams of fuel burned; per mole = kJ ÷ moles that reacted.

## Calorimetry with a fuel (3.9.2 a and b)

### Worked example 1: ethanol in a spirit burner

A spirit burner of ethanol, C₂H₅OH (Mᵣ = 46), heats 250 g of water in a copper can. The burner loses 0.92 g. The water warms from 18.5 °C to 36.5 °C.

1. ΔT = 36.5 − 18.5 = 18.0 °C.
2. Q = 250 × 4.2 × 18.0 = 18 900 J = **18.9 kJ**.
3. Per gram: 18.9 ÷ 0.92 = **20.5 kJ/g**.
4. Moles of ethanol = 0.92 ÷ 46 = 0.0200 mol; per mole: 18.9 ÷ 0.0200 = **945 kJ/mol**.
5. Energy was released, so ΔH = **−945 kJ/mol**.

A data book value is much more negative, because some energy heats the can and the air instead of the water, and some ethanol burns incompletely. Results are still useful for **comparing** fuels if every fuel is tested the same way.

## Reactions in solution (3.9.2 c)

Use an **insulated** container, usually a polystyrene cup with a lid, so less energy is exchanged with the surroundings. m is the **total** mass of solution.

### Worked example 2: neutralisation with sulfuric acid

25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³ sulfuric acid is added to 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide. The temperature rises by 9.0 °C.

H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

1. Mass of solution = 25.0 + 50.0 = 75.0 g.
2. Q = 75.0 × 4.2 × 9.0 = 2835 J = 2.835 kJ.
3. Moles of NaOH = 1.00 × 0.0500 = 0.0500 mol, which forms 0.0500 mol of water.
4. Energy per mole of water = 2.835 ÷ 0.0500 = 56.7 kJ/mol.
5. Temperature rose: **ΔH = −56.7 kJ/mol** of water formed.

### Worked example 3: a solid reacting with water

0.56 g of calcium oxide (Mᵣ = 56) is added to 50.0 g of water. The temperature rises by 3.1 °C.

CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq)

1. Q = 50.0 × 4.2 × 3.1 = 651 J = 0.651 kJ (use the mass of water).
2. Moles CaO = 0.56 ÷ 56 = 0.0100 mol.
3. Energy per mole = 0.651 ÷ 0.0100 = 65.1 kJ/mol.
4. **ΔH = −65.1 kJ/mol**. This reaction is the kind used in self-heating cans (3.9.1).

## Energy level diagrams (3.9.2 d and h)

An energy level diagram plots energy (vertical) against progress of the reaction (horizontal). It shows the reactant and product levels, joined by a curve that rises over a peak.

- **Activation energy** = peak − reactant level.
- **Overall energy change, ΔH** = product level − reactant level.
- Exothermic: products **below** reactants. Endothermic: products **above** reactants.
- A **catalyst** gives a lower peak between the same two levels; ΔH is unchanged.

### Worked example 4: an endothermic profile

Reactants are at 50 kJ, the peak is at 230 kJ and the products at 120 kJ. With a catalyst the peak is at 160 kJ.

- Activation energy (no catalyst) = 230 − 50 = **180 kJ**.
- ΔH = 120 − 50 = **+70 kJ**: endothermic, products higher than reactants.
- Activation energy with catalyst = 160 − 50 = **110 kJ**; ΔH is still +70 kJ.

When you sketch one: label both axes, label the reactant and product levels with formulae, draw ΔH as a vertical arrow from reactants to products (pointing down for exothermic, up for endothermic), and draw activation energy as a vertical arrow from the reactant level to the peak.

## Bond energies (3.9.2 e to g)

**energy change = energy needed to break bonds in reactants − energy released forming bonds in products**

Negative → exothermic; positive → endothermic. Bond energies are always supplied in the question.

Bond energies used below, in kJ/mol: C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464, C=C 612, C–C 347, H–H 436, H–Br 366, Br–Br 193, Cl–Cl 243.

### Worked example 5: burning methane

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

- Broken: 4 C–H + 2 O=O = 4 × 413 + 2 × 498 = 1652 + 996 = 2648 kJ
- Formed: 2 C=O + 4 O–H = 2 × 805 + 4 × 464 = 1610 + 1856 = 3466 kJ
- Energy change = 2648 − 3466 = **−818 kJ/mol**: exothermic.

### Worked example 6: adding hydrogen to ethene

C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆

A shortcut: the four C–H bonds in ethene appear again in ethane, so they cancel. Only the bonds that change count.

- Broken: C=C + H–H = 612 + 436 = 1048 kJ
- Formed: C–C + 2 extra C–H = 347 + 826 = 1173 kJ
- Energy change = 1048 − 1173 = **−125 kJ/mol**.

### Worked example 7: an endothermic decomposition

2HBr → H₂ + Br₂

- Broken: 2 H–Br = 732 kJ. Formed: H–H + Br–Br = 436 + 193 = 629 kJ.
- Energy change = 732 − 629 = **+103 kJ** for the equation as written: endothermic, because more energy is needed to break bonds than is released forming them (3.9.2 g).

### Worked example 8: finding an unknown bond energy

H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, energy change = −185 kJ/mol. Find the H–Cl bond energy.

- Broken: 436 + 243 = 679 kJ. Formed: 2 × (H–Cl).
- 679 − 2(H–Cl) = −185, so 2(H–Cl) = 864 and **H–Cl = 432 kJ/mol**.

## Common mistakes

- Using the mass of fuel, not water, in Q = mcΔT.
- Using only one solution's volume when two are mixed.
- Forgetting to convert J to kJ before dividing by moles.
- Leaving out the negative sign for an exothermic ΔH.
- Calculating formed − broken.
- Forgetting coefficients when counting bonds (2O₂ means two O=O bonds).
- Drawing a catalysed curve that ends at a different product level.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Calculate:** show Q, the unit conversion, the division, then the sign.
- **Explain** why a result is lower than the data book value: name a specific energy loss.
- **Draw** or **sketch** an energy level diagram: labels and arrows earn the marks.
- **Explain in terms of bonds:** compare energy needed to break bonds with energy released making bonds, saying which is larger.
