## 1.3.1 The definition, word by word

**Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.** Each part of that sentence does a job.

- **Next best:** the decision maker ranks the options. Only the option ranked second counts. If you reject four alternatives, the opportunity cost is the single best of those four, not all four added together.
- **Alternative:** it must be a real, available option. A job you were never offered cannot be the opportunity cost of the job you took.
- **Forgone:** it is given up. The chosen option is the benefit; the forgone option is the cost.

Opportunity cost exists because of scarcity. Resources are limited and wants are unlimited, so using a resource one way means it cannot be used another way. This is why a **free good**, such as air in most places, has no opportunity cost: using more of it takes nothing away from any other use. An **economic good** is scarce, so producing or consuming it always has an opportunity cost.

### Opportunity cost is not the price

The money price measures what you hand over. The opportunity cost is what that money, time or resource could otherwise have achieved. Three situations show the difference.

1. **A free ticket.** A student is given a free ticket to a three-hour football match. The money price is zero, but the opportunity cost is the next best use of those three hours, perhaps paid work or revision.
2. **"Free" public services.** A state school charges no fees, yet the teachers, buildings and books could have been used elsewhere. Society bears an opportunity cost even when the user pays nothing.
3. **Using your own resources.** A shop owner who uses her own building pays no rent, but she gives up the rent another business would have paid her.

### Contexts the examiner may use

| Context | Choice | Opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| Money | Spending savings on a phone | The next best purchase, or the interest the savings would have earned |
| Time | Two hours on social media | The next best use of the two hours, such as paid work or study |
| Land | A field used for a car park | The crops or housing that land could have supported |
| Labour | A nurse working in a private clinic | The care she would have given in the public hospital |
| Capital | A machine making shirts | The trousers it could have made in the same hours |
| Public budget | Building a stadium | The best alternative project, such as a hospital |

### Measuring opportunity cost in units of the alternative

When a resource can make two products, express the cost of one unit of a product in units of the other.

**Worked example: one machine, two products.** In a week, a workshop's machine can make either 600 chairs or 200 tables.

1. Giving up 600 chairs gains 200 tables.
2. Opportunity cost of 1 table = 600 ÷ 200 = **3 chairs**.
3. Opportunity cost of 1 chair = 200 ÷ 600 = **one third of a table**.

The two answers are reciprocals. Always state the unit ("3 chairs per table"), or the number is meaningless.

**Worked example: savings and interest forgone.** The Osei family has 900 in a savings account paying 4% a year. They spend it on a fridge.

1. Interest forgone in one year = 900 × 0.04 = **36**.
2. If their next best alternative was keeping the money saved, the opportunity cost of the fridge is the saving itself: 900 kept in the account, growing by 36 of interest in a year.
3. If instead their next best alternative was a holiday costing 900, the opportunity cost is the holiday, not the interest. Only one option can be second best.

## 1.3.2 How opportunity cost influences decisions

A rational decision maker compares the benefit of an option with the benefit of the next best option. If the next best option is worth more, the choice should change. The four decision makers named in the syllabus face different versions of this.

### Consumers

Consumers have limited incomes. The cost of buying one product is the other products, or the saving, given up. When the price of an alternative falls, its attraction rises, so the opportunity cost of the original choice rises and some consumers switch. Consumers also choose between spending now and saving for later, so the interest forgone is a cost of spending.

### Workers

Workers choose between jobs, between work and leisure, and between work and education.

**Worked example: overtime.** Nadia normally earns 12 an hour. Her employer offers 4 hours of overtime on Saturday at 18 an hour.

1. Overtime earnings = 4 × 18 = **72**.
2. If she spends Saturday with friends instead, her opportunity cost is the 72 she could have earned.
3. If the overtime rate rose to 24 an hour, the cost of leisure would rise to 96, making it more likely she works.

This is why higher wages tend to encourage people to work longer hours: leisure has become more expensive in terms of earnings forgone.

### Producers

Firms have limited land, labour, capital and enterprise. Every use of a factor has an alternative use.

**Worked example: oven time.** A bakery's oven is free for 8 hours a day. In that time it can bake either 400 loaves, earning 0.50 profit each, or 160 cakes, earning 1.50 profit each.

1. Profit from loaves = 400 × 0.50 = **200**.
2. Profit from cakes = 160 × 1.50 = **240**.
3. Baking loaves has an opportunity cost of 240; baking cakes has an opportunity cost of 200.
4. On profit alone the bakery should bake cakes, because the benefit (240) is greater than the opportunity cost (200).

The bakery might still bake some loaves if regular customers expect them or if cake sales are less certain. Opportunity cost guides the decision; it does not settle every factor.

Producers face the same question with profit: money kept in the business to buy equipment cannot also be paid to the owners.

### Governments

Governments have limited tax revenue and borrowing. Spending on one priority means less for another.

**Worked example: a budget choice.** A government has 60 million to spend. A new hospital costs 36 million. A secondary school costs 12 million.

1. The hospital uses 36 ÷ 12 = **3 schools'** worth of funds.
2. If schools are the next best use, the opportunity cost of the hospital is 3 schools.
3. The government should go ahead only if it judges the hospital's benefits to the community greater than those of 3 schools.

Governments also face opportunity costs in what they do not spend on: a tax cut gives up the public services the revenue would have paid for.

## Limits of opportunity cost as a decision tool

- **Hard to value.** Benefits such as cleaner air, safety or leisure have no market price, so comparing them is a judgement.
- **Uncertainty.** The value of the forgone option is an estimate. A farmer who plants maize instead of beans does not know next season's bean price.
- **Different rankings.** Two people facing the same choice may name different next best alternatives, so the opportunity cost differs from person to person.
- **Sunk costs.** Money already spent and not recoverable is not an opportunity cost of a future decision. A firm that has paid 5,000 for a market study should not let that payment decide whether to launch a product.

## Common mistakes

- Adding up every rejected option instead of naming only the next best.
- Giving the money price as the opportunity cost.
- Saying a free service has no opportunity cost. The user may pay nothing, but society uses resources.
- Leaving out the unit when calculating the cost of one product in terms of another.
- Naming a decision maker in a "Discuss" answer but never applying the cost to that decision maker.

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Define** (give precise meaning): "the next best alternative forgone". The words "next best" are essential; "what you give up" alone is incomplete.
- **Identify** or **State**: name the forgone option from the information given, such as "the 72 of overtime earnings".
- **Calculate**: show the division and write the unit, such as "3 chairs per table".
- **Explain**: link the choice, the alternative and the consequence. "The government builds the hospital, so it cannot build three schools, so fewer pupils gain places."
- **Discuss**: give the case for the choice, the opportunity cost against it, and a conclusion that names what the decision depends on.
- Paper 1 multiple-choice items often list several rejected options; pick the one ranked second, not the most expensive.
