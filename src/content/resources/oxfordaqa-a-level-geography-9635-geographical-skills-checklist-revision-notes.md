Condensed recall for **3.5.1 Geographical skills checklist** (OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography 9635, Unit 5, and examinable in Units 1 to 4). For explanations and worked examples, read the [skills checklist study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-geographical-skills-checklist/); then try the [skills checklist practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-geographical-skills-checklist-practice/).

## The checklist in five lines

- **3.5.1.1 Core:** use and annotate base maps, sketch maps, diagrams, graphs, field sketches, photographs and geospatial, geo-located and digital imagery; literacy; numeracy; questionnaire and interview techniques.
- **3.5.1.2 Cartographic:** atlas maps; located proportional symbols; flow, desire and trip lines; choropleth, isoline and dot maps.
- **3.5.1.3 Graphical:** line and bar graphs (simple, comparative, compound, divergent); scatter graphs with best fit line; pie charts and proportional divided circles; triangular graphs; logarithmic scales; dispersion diagrams.
- **3.5.1.4 Statistical:** mean, mode, median; range, inter-quartile range, standard deviation; Spearman's rank, chi-square and significance tests.
- **3.5.1.5 ICT:** remotely sensed data; electronic databases; crowd sourcing and 'big data'.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Annotation | A label that explains a feature, usually linking it to a process or cause |
| Interpolation | Estimating a value between two measured points, as when drawing isolines |
| Located proportional symbol | A symbol at a point whose area is proportional to the value there |
| Desire line | A straight line from origin to destination, showing where trips go, not the route |
| Residual | The distance of a point from the best fit line; large residuals are anomalies |
| Compound graph | Values stacked so the top line or bar shows the total |
| Divergent graph | Values plotted above and below a central value, such as zero or a mean |
| Logarithmic scale | Each cycle is ten times the one below; equal slopes show equal percentage change |
| Null hypothesis | The statement that there is no relationship or difference, which a test tries to reject |
| Critical value | The value a test statistic must reach to be significant at a chosen level |
| Significance level | The probability that a result arose by chance; 0.05 (5%) and 0.01 (1%) are usual |
| Degrees of freedom | For chi-square, categories minus 1, or (rows - 1) x (columns - 1) for a table |
| Ground truthing | Checking remotely sensed data against observations on the ground |
| Big data | Very large, often real-time data sets, such as phone location or social media records |

## Formulas to know

```
Percentage change      = (new - old) / old x 100
Mean rate of change    = total change / time period
Lower quartile (LQ)    = value at position (n + 1) / 4
Upper quartile (UQ)    = value at position 3(n + 1) / 4
Inter-quartile range   = UQ - LQ
Standard deviation     = square root of [sum of (x - mean)^2 / n]
Spearman's rank        rs = 1 - (6 x sum of d^2) / (n^3 - n)
Chi-square             = sum of (O - E)^2 / E
Expected value (table) = row total x column total / grand total
Proportional circle    radius proportional to square root of value
Pie chart segment      = percentage x 3.6 degrees
```

## Choosing techniques: quick rules

- **Point data** -> located proportional symbols or bars. **Area data** -> choropleth of rates. **Continuous surface** -> isolines. **Movement** -> flow lines for volume, desire lines for pattern.
- **Two continuous variables** -> scatter graph, then Spearman's rank (needs at least about 10 pairs for a reliable result).
- **Counts in categories** -> bar graph, then chi-square (raw counts; each expected value at least 5).
- **Three percentages summing to 100** -> triangular graph.
- **Wide range of values** -> logarithmic scale.
- **Skewed data or outliers** -> median and inter-quartile range rather than mean and standard deviation.

## Interpreting a test result: the five steps

1. State the null hypothesis.
2. Calculate the statistic, showing working.
3. Compare it with the critical value at a named significance level. For Spearman's rank, compare the size of rs, ignoring its sign.
4. Accept or reject the null hypothesis.
5. Explain the result geographically, and say what the test cannot show (cause, or why the difference exists).

## Reading graphs and maps accurately

- On a **triangular graph**, read each axis along its own gridlines; the three readings must sum to 100%.
- On a **log scale**, count cycles first, then estimate position within the cycle; gridlines inside a cycle are not evenly spaced.
- On a **choropleth**, describe the overall pattern, the highest and lowest areas with values, and any anomalies.
- On a **flow line map**, compare widths using the scale; describe the main routes and the direction of flow.
- On a **dispersion diagram**, describe clustering, gaps and outliers, and compare medians and inter-quartile ranges.

## ICT sources: strengths and limits

| Source | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Remotely sensed data | Large or inaccessible areas, repeated over time | Resolution, cloud cover, needs ground truthing |
| Electronic databases | Large, consistent, often free | Collected for other purposes; boundaries may not match the study area |
| Crowd sourcing and big data | Huge, near real-time | Unrepresentative contributors; privacy concerns; accuracy varies |

## Exam traps

- Labelling instead of annotating.
- Drawing a choropleth of totals rather than rates or densities.
- Making circle radius, not area, proportional to the value.
- Quoting a percentage change without stating the base year or showing working.
- Comparing a negative rs with the critical value as if its sign mattered.
- Using percentages instead of raw counts in chi-square.
- Treating a significant correlation as proof of cause.
- Choosing a technique with no justification linked to the data.

## Self-test

1. What is the difference between labelling and annotation?
2. Why should a choropleth show rates rather than totals?
3. Point X records 30 and point Y records 70, 40 mm apart. How far from X does the 45 isoline lie?
4. A circle of radius 5 mm shows 900 tonnes. What radius shows 3,600 tonnes?
5. Distinguish a flow line from a desire line.
6. What does a straight rising line on a logarithmic graph show?
7. When is the median a better average than the mean?
8. Calculate the percentage change from 2,400 to 3,000.
9. What does an rs value of -0.85 tell you before any significance test?
10. How are degrees of freedom found for a chi-square table with 3 rows and 2 columns?
11. Why must expected values in chi-square be calculated from raw counts?
12. Give one limitation of crowd-sourced data.

**Answers:** 1. A label names a feature; an annotation explains it, often linking to a process. 2. Larger areas tend to have larger totals simply because of their size, so rates allow fair comparison. 3. (45 - 30) / (70 - 30) = 15 / 40 = 0.375 of 40 mm = 15 mm from X. 4. Four times the value needs twice the radius: 10 mm. 5. A flow line's width shows the volume along a route; a desire line is a straight line from origin to destination showing the pattern of trips. 6. A constant percentage rate of change. 7. When data are skewed or contain extreme values. 8. 600 / 2,400 x 100 = 25%. 9. A strong negative relationship, not yet shown to be significant. 10. (3 - 1) x (2 - 1) = 2. 11. Percentages hide sample size, so the test result would be invalid. 12. Contributors are not representative of the whole population (or accuracy varies, or privacy concerns).
