These questions cover topic 1.1 Population dynamics in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. All countries in the data questions are fictional and the data are invented, except the world population figures in Question 7, which are rounded approximations.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "natural increase". **[2]**

**2.** State the equation that shows how a country's population changes over a year, including migration. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows birth and death rates for four fictional countries.

| Country | Birth rate (per 1,000) | Death rate (per 1,000) |
|---|---|---|
| Arvel | 38 | 8 |
| Bresk | 10 | 12 |
| Calden | 18 | 7 |
| Dorrin | 9 | 8 |

**(a)** Calculate the natural increase rate of Arvel as a percentage. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the country that has a natural decrease. **[1]**
**(c)** Bresk has better healthcare than Calden. Suggest why Bresk still has the higher death rate. **[2]**

**4.** Give two reasons why death rates fell in many countries during the twentieth century. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how HIV/AIDS can affect the birth rate and death rate of a country. **[3]**

**6.** State two signs that a country may be under-populated. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows approximate world population.

| Year | World population (billions) |
|---|---|
| 1800 | 1.0 |
| 1900 | 1.6 |
| 1950 | 2.5 |
| 2000 | 6.1 |
| 2022 | 8.0 |

**(a)** Describe the changes in world population shown in the table. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the increase in world population between 1950 and 2000. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why world population grew so rapidly in the second half of the twentieth century. **[4]**

**8.** In one year the fictional Country M, population 5 million, recorded 60,000 births, 35,000 deaths, 4,000 immigrants and 19,000 emigrants.

**(a)** Calculate the total change in Country M's population during the year. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate Country M's birth rate per 1,000 people. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why emigration from Country M may be high. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why birth rates are often higher in rural areas of low-income countries than in their cities. **[4]**

**10.** For a named country with a low rate of population growth or a declining population, describe the causes and explain the consequences. **[7]**

**11.** Describe and evaluate a population policy you have studied. **[7]**

**12.** "Over-population is caused by having too many people in a small area." To what extent do you agree with this statement? Use examples. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The increase in a population when the birth rate is higher than the death rate [1], not counting migration [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "More births than deaths" gains the first mark; the second mark is for showing that migration is excluded.

**2.** Population change = (births − deaths) [1] + (immigrants − emigrants) [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** (38 − 8) ÷ 10 [1] = 3.0% per year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Bresk [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Bresk probably has an ageing population, with a large proportion of elderly people [1], and older people are more likely to die each year whatever the quality of healthcare, so the crude death rate is higher [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The crude death rate is affected by age structure; "worse healthcare" contradicts the information given and earns nothing.

**4.** Vaccination and medicines such as antibiotics reduced deaths from infectious diseases [1]. Clean water and sanitation reduced water-borne diseases such as cholera [1]. Also credit more reliable food supplies and better diets. **[2]**

**5.** Deaths of young adults from AIDS-related illnesses raise the death rate [1]. Fewer women survive through their child-bearing years, so fewer babies are born and the birth rate can fall [1]. Children can be infected at birth, raising infant mortality [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Labour shortages, with jobs unfilled [1]. Resources such as minerals or farmland not being fully used [1]. Also credit the government encouraging immigration. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Population increased throughout, from 1.0 billion in 1800 to 8.0 billion in 2022 [1]. Growth was slow at first: only 0.6 billion was added between 1800 and 1900 [1]. Growth then accelerated, with 3.6 billion added between 1950 and 2000, more than in the previous 150 years [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 6.1 − 2.5 = 3.6 billion [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Death rates fell rapidly in lower-income countries because of vaccination and medicines [1] and better water supply and sanitation [1]. Birth rates stayed high for longer because of cultural traditions, the value of children's labour and limited family planning [1]. So the gap between births and deaths (natural increase) was very large in the countries with the most people [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer about high birth rates alone misses the key point: growth speeded up because deaths fell while births stayed high.

**8. (a)** Natural change = 60,000 − 35,000 = +25,000; net migration = 4,000 − 19,000 = −15,000 [1]. Total change = +10,000 people [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 60,000 ÷ 5,000,000 × 1,000 = 12 per 1,000 [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Higher wages and more jobs in nearby countries [1]. Young adults leaving to study or join family abroad, or to escape conflict or persecution [1]. **[2]**

**9.** In rural areas children can work on family farms, so they add to household income [1]. Children are expected to care for parents in old age where there are no pensions [1]. Rural women often have less access to education and family planning clinics, so they marry younger and have more children [1]. Infant mortality is often higher in rural areas, so families may have more children to make sure some survive [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. Indicative creditworthy content, for example for South Korea:

Named country with accurate detail: fertility below one child per woman since 2018 [1]. Cause: very high costs of housing and private education make raising children expensive [1]. Cause: long working hours and the difficulty for women of combining careers and children lead to later marriage and fewer births [1]. Consequence: a rapidly ageing population, so spending on pensions and healthcare rises [1]. Consequence: a shrinking workforce and fewer taxpayers, so labour shortages in some industries [1]. Consequence: schools close in rural areas as the number of children falls [1]. Explanation linking cause to consequence, such as fewer births today meaning fewer workers in twenty years' time [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Describe the causes and explain the consequences" needs two different skills; a list of consequences without saying why they follow cannot reach the top level.

**11.** Indicative content, for example for Singapore:

The aim and type: an anti-natalist policy in the 1960s and 1970s, "Stop at Two" [1]. The methods: incentives for small families and disincentives for large ones [1]. The change of direction: pro-natalist from 1987 ("Have Three or More, if you can afford it") [1] and a Baby Bonus of cash payments from 2001 [1]. Evaluation: the anti-natalist phase succeeded, as fertility fell quickly [1]. Evaluation: the pro-natalist phase has had little effect, as fertility fell below one child per woman in 2023 [1]. Judgement: incentives cannot easily outweigh the high costs of housing and education, so the policy has been only partly successful [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a judgement with evidence; describing the policy in detail without saying how well it worked caps the answer.

**12.** Indicative content:

Disagree in part: over-population compares people with resources, not with area [1]. A small, crowded country can be wealthy if it trades and uses technology, as Singapore shows [1]. A large country can still be over-populated if its farmland, water or jobs cannot support its people, for example where drought limits food production [1]. Agree in part: very high densities on limited farmland, as in Bangladesh, can lead to small plots, flooding risk and out-migration [1]. Over-population is also caused by rapid natural increase outrunning the growth of resources [1]. And by a lack of capital, technology or trade to develop resources [1]. Consequences used as evidence, such as unemployment, food shortages or overcrowded housing [1]. Judgement: the statement is too simple because density alone does not decide whether a place is over-populated [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" needs both sides and a final judgement; examples must be named countries, not "some countries in Africa".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating the natural increase rate without dividing by 10, giving "30%" instead of 3.0%.
- Forgetting migration when a question asks for total population change.
- Saying a high death rate means poor healthcare, without checking the age structure.
- Defining over-population as high density.
- Case study answers with no named country or no facts specific to it.
- Policy answers that describe but never evaluate.
