These notes condense the **interpersonal relationships** strand of the human relationships context (SL and HL, first assessment 2027). The guide says to study one or more of social relationships or interpersonal conflict and aggression. Read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-interpersonal-relationships/) for the detail, then try the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-interpersonal-relationships-practice/).

## The four areas at a glance

| Area | Learning objective (summarised) | Key ideas | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chemical messengers | Role of one or more in relationships | Oxytocin and trust; dopamine and reward in love; testosterone and aggression; pheromones | Kosfeld et al. (2005); Aron et al. (2005); Dabbs et al. (1987); Wedekind et al. (1995) |
| Cognitive explanations | One or more explanations | Similarity, social exchange, investment, attributions, hostile attribution bias | Rusbult; Fincham and Bradbury (1993); Dodge |
| Communication and language | Role of communication | Self-disclosure, conflict patterns, accommodation, digital communication | Altman and Taylor; Aron et al. (1997); Gottman |
| Strategies for improving relationships | One or more strategies | Communication training, attribution retraining, structured disclosure, shared goals | Linked to the explanations above |

## Key definitions

- **Chemical messenger:** a substance that carries a signal in the body (neurotransmitter, hormone, pheromone).
- **Oxytocin:** a hormone and neuromodulator linked to bonding and trust.
- **Pheromone:** a chemical released to affect others of the same species; human pheromones are not conclusively shown.
- **Biological reductionism:** explaining complex behaviour only by biological parts.
- **Propinquity:** physical closeness; people form relationships with those nearby.
- **Social exchange theory:** relationships continue while perceived rewards exceed costs.
- **Comparison level:** what a person expects from a relationship.
- **Investment model:** commitment depends on satisfaction, investment and the quality of alternatives.
- **Attribution:** an explanation for why someone behaved as they did.
- **Hostile attribution bias:** reading ambiguous actions as deliberately hostile.
- **Self-disclosure:** sharing personal information.
- **Accommodation:** adjusting speech to match another person.

## Chemical messengers

- **Oxytocin and trust (Kosfeld et al., 2005):** nasal oxytocin versus placebo; the oxytocin group invested more with unknown partners. Strength: controlled, random allocation, so cause is plausible. Limitation: all-male student sample, trust game is artificial.
- **Dopamine in love (Aron et al., 2005):** fMRI of people intensely in love; activity in reward areas. Limitation: correlational, small sample.
- **Testosterone and aggression (Dabbs et al., 1987):** higher levels in violent male prisoners. Limitation: direction unclear.
- **Pheromones (Wedekind et al., 1995):** preference for the smell of genetically different men; debated and mixed replication.
- **Overall evaluation:** reductionist; most evidence is correlational outside the oxytocin experiments; effects depend on context.
- **Concepts:** causality, measurement, perspective.

## Cognitive explanations

| Explanation | Core claim | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Similarity-attraction | We like people with similar attitudes | Supported in many studies; lab samples; similarity may follow liking |
| Social exchange | We weigh rewards and costs | Simple and testable; assumes rational calculation |
| Investment model | Satisfaction + investment - alternatives = commitment | Predicts staying in unhappy relationships; mostly self-report |
| Attributions | Happy couples excuse negatives, credit positives | Longitudinal evidence; bidirectional ambiguity |
| Hostile attribution bias | Seeing hostility in ambiguous acts leads to aggression | Practical for therapy; evidence mostly from children and self-report |

- **Concepts:** causality (bidirectional ambiguity), bias, measurement, perspective.

## Communication and language

- **Self-disclosure:** gradual, reciprocal sharing deepens a relationship (social penetration). In the fast-friends study (Aron et al., 1997), escalating personal questions produced greater closeness than small talk.
- **Gottman's four damaging patterns:** criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling.
- **Accommodation:** convergence signals liking.
- **Digital communication:** less tone and body language, more chance of misreading, but easier to keep in touch.
- **Evaluate:** observation has high ecological validity but participant reactivity and subjective coding; culture changes what counts as polite or direct.

## Strategies for improving relationships

| Strategy | Explanation it relies on | One strength | One limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communication training | Gottman-style observation | Concrete behaviours to practise | Needs both partners to engage |
| Attribution retraining | Cognitive | Low cost, targets thinking | Hard to measure lasting change |
| Structured self-disclosure | Social penetration | Closeness gains in lab tests | Unclear long-term effect |
| Shared goals | Social identity and contact | Evidence from schools | Depends on equal status |
| Anger management | Cognitive and social learning | Targets triggers and interpretations | Transfer to daily life varies |

## The class practical: a survey or questionnaire

- **Method:** written questions answered by a sample; closed items give quantitative data, open items give qualitative data.
- **Hazan and Shaver (1987):** newspaper love quiz grouped respondents into secure, avoidant and anxious. Limitations: self-selected sample, retrospective self-report, forced choice.
- **Buss (1989):** survey of mate preferences across many countries. Limitations: self-report; samples not representative of whole populations.
- **Paper 2 Section A prompts:** know sampling methods, question wording, pilot testing, anonymity, social desirability bias, response bias and the ethical steps (consent, right to withdraw, debrief).

## Concept links to prepare

- **Causality:** oxytocin experiments versus correlational scans and attribution studies.
- **Measurement:** operationalising closeness, trust or conflict.
- **Perspective:** biological, cognitive and communication accounts, plus culture.
- **Bias:** positive illusions, hostile attribution bias, sampling bias in surveys.
- **Change:** do the strategies work, and for how long?
- **Responsibility:** privacy, consent and advice that assumes one model of relationship.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "oxytocin causes love". It is one factor in an interaction with context.
- Describing a study with no link to the area of study.
- Listing strategies without saying which explanation supports them.
- Forgetting to evaluate (sampling, culture, direction of cause).
- Ignoring the survey method for Paper 2.

## Self-test

1. What is the difference between a hormone and a pheromone?
2. Why can Aron et al. (2005) not show that dopamine causes love?
3. State the three variables in Rusbult's investment model.
4. What is hostile attribution bias?
5. Name two of Gottman's four damaging patterns.
6. Why might a structured self-disclosure exercise build closeness?
7. State one limitation of the Hazan and Shaver (1987) love quiz.

**Answers:** 1. A hormone is a chemical carried in the blood within the body; a pheromone is released into the environment to affect another individual. 2. The scans are correlational: they show activity while participants viewed a photograph, not that dopamine caused the feeling. 3. Satisfaction, investment and quality of alternatives. 4. Interpreting ambiguous actions as deliberately hostile. 5. Any two of criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling. 6. Gradual and reciprocal sharing signals trust and builds a sense of knowing each other. 7. Self-selected newspaper readers are not representative; retrospective self-report may be inaccurate.
