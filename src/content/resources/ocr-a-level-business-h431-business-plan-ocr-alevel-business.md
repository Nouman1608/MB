## Purposes at different stages

A business plan is a written document setting out what a business intends to do, how, and the results it expects. Its main purpose shifts with the stage of the business.

- **Start-up:** to test whether the idea is viable and to persuade a bank or investor to provide finance. The owner often learns most from writing it.
- **Growth:** to justify an expansion, such as a new site or product, and to raise the extra finance it needs.
- **Established business:** to set targets for the coming period, coordinate departments and provide a benchmark for later review.

In an exam answer, say which purpose matters most for the business in the case. A business seeking a loan needs the plan to persuade; a business funding itself from savings needs it mainly to think clearly and to measure progress.

## Contents through a reader's eyes

The specification asks why each content is included. The best answers link a content to the stakeholder who uses it.

| Reader | What they look for | Contents they read most closely |
|---|---|---|
| Bank lender | Can the loan and interest be repaid on time? Is there security? | Cash-flow forecast, profit forecast, owner's own investment, risks |
| Equity investor | How fast can the business grow, and what return will the investor's shares earn? | Market research, growth forecasts, management team, competitive advantage |
| Owner | Is the idea viable, and what targets should be set? | All of it; especially break-even and cash flow |
| Employees and new managers | Is the business heading somewhere secure, and what is expected of them? | Objectives, staffing plan, strategy |
| Suppliers | Will the business pay on credit terms? | Financial forecasts, sources of finance |

This shows the **impact and importance to stakeholders**. For a lender the plan may decide whether the business exists at all. For employees it matters less directly, but a credible plan supports job security.

## Worked example 1: testing a plan's cash-flow forecast

**Case.** Juniper & Moss is a new florist. The owner puts in £10,000 and borrows £5,000, so the business opens with **£15,000** cash. The plan forecasts:

| £ | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 | Month 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Receipts from sales | 6,000 | 9,000 | 11,000 | 14,000 |
| Shop fit-out | 10,000 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Stock | 3,000 | 4,000 | 4,500 | 5,500 |
| Rent and wages | 4,500 | 4,500 | 4,500 | 4,500 |
| Loan repayment | 0 | 500 | 500 | 500 |
| **Total payments** | 17,500 | 9,000 | 9,500 | 10,500 |
| **Net cash flow** | −11,500 | 0 | 1,500 | 3,500 |
| Opening balance | 15,000 | 3,500 | 3,500 | 5,000 |
| **Closing balance** | 3,500 | 3,500 | 5,000 | 8,500 |

On these figures the business never runs out of cash. The lowest balance is £3,500, in months 1 and 2.

**Testing the assumption.** Suppose sales receipts are 20% lower each month, while payments stay the same because stock is ordered in advance and rent and wages are fixed.

| £ | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 | Month 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Receipts (80%) | 4,800 | 7,200 | 8,800 | 11,200 |
| Total payments | 17,500 | 9,000 | 9,500 | 10,500 |
| Net cash flow | −12,700 | −1,800 | −700 | 700 |
| Closing balance | 2,300 | 500 | −200 | 500 |

The balance turns negative in month 3. A sales shortfall of one fifth would leave the business unable to pay its bills without extra finance.

**Evaluation.** The plan looks sound at first glance, but its safety margin is thin: the lowest balance is only £3,500. A lender would ask whether the sales forecast is supported by research and would want an overdraft facility arranged before opening, for example £2,000 to £3,000. The owner could also reduce the risk by ordering less stock in the first months. This is the main **advantage** of a plan: it shows the danger before the shop opens. Its main **disadvantage** is also visible: the whole plan rests on a sales estimate that may be wrong.

## When a plan helps most

The advantages of a plan outweigh its costs most clearly when:

- the business needs outside finance, since lenders and investors usually expect one;
- the sums involved are large and hard to recover;
- the owner lacks experience of the market;
- several people must coordinate their work.

A plan is less valuable when the market changes so quickly that forecasts are out of date within weeks, or when the plan is written only to win a loan and then ignored. A plan written with over-optimistic forecasts to persuade a lender can be worse than none, because the owner may then believe it.

## Plan-Do-Review

The Plan-Do-Review cycle is a repeating loop. **Plan**: set objectives, targets and budgets. **Do**: carry out the plan. **Review**: compare results with the plan, find the causes of differences, and feed the lessons into the next plan.

### Worked example 2: reviewing several variances

After its first quarter, Juniper & Moss compares its plan with actual results.

| £ | Plan | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 26,000 | 24,500 | 1,500 adverse |
| Stock costs | 11,500 | 12,800 | 1,300 adverse |
| Rent and wages | 13,500 | 13,500 | 0 |
| Wedding orders (number) | 6 | 11 | 5 favourable |

The review asks why:

- Walk-in sales were below plan, but wedding orders were nearly double the target. Wedding work brings large, planned orders.
- Stock costs were higher because flowers for walk-in sales were wasted when they did not sell.

**Acting on it.** The next plan could shift marketing towards weddings and events, order less stock for walk-in sales and set a waste target. This is how the cycle **improves performance**: the plan sets a measurable target, the review finds the cause of the gap, and the next plan acts on it. Without the review the owner might simply have cut prices to raise walk-in sales, which would not have solved the waste problem.

The cycle fails if reviews are skipped, if data is poor, or if managers use the review to blame staff, who then hide problems.

## Strategic review

A **strategic review** steps back from targets and asks whether the strategy itself still fits. It typically examines:

- whether the objectives are still right;
- how the market and competition have changed;
- the business's strengths and weaknesses now;
- whether the resources match the strategy;
- the options for the future, including stopping some activities.

It is usually triggered by a major event: persistent under-performance, a new competitor, a change of owner or a change in technology.

**Impact and importance.** For the business, a review can prevent it from continuing with a failing strategy and can open new opportunities, such as Juniper & Moss becoming an events florist. For stakeholders, it may bring change: staff may need new skills or face redundancy, suppliers may lose orders, and owners may face new investment. Its importance is greatest when the environment has changed sharply; for a stable business in a stable market, frequent reviews cost management time for little gain.

## Common mistakes

- Listing plan contents without saying who uses each and why.
- Treating the plan's forecasts as facts rather than assumptions to be tested.
- Describing Plan-Do-Review as a one-off rather than a repeating loop.
- Confusing a strategic review with an ordinary budget review.

## Exam technique

"Explain why a content is included": name it, say who reads it, say what decision it informs. "Analyse how the cycle improves performance": link each stage to its effect on results. "Evaluate": weigh benefits against costs for this business and give a judgement.
