These questions practise 3.1.1 Textual variations and representations from AQA A-level English Language (7702).
All three texts below are **original**, written for this page.

**Text A** (contemporary): a post on a personal walking blog.

> *"Okay, confession time: I used to think hiking was for people with very expensive socks. Then I found a muddy
> path behind the industrial estate, and honestly? It saved my sanity. You don't need gear. You don't need an app
> with fifty filters. Just lace up whatever trainers you've got, step outside and breathe. Trust me, the hills
> aren't going anywhere. Your stress might."*

**Text B** (older): a passage written in the style of a 1930s rambling club handbook.

> *"The rambler who would enjoy the countryside to the full must first learn to respect it. Gates are to be closed
> behind him, livestock left undisturbed and wild flowers admired where they grow. Let him carry a good map and a
> sound pair of boots, for the moors are no place for the careless. The reward of such discipline is great: clean
> air, a quiet mind and the fellowship of those who walk the same paths."*

**Text C** (transcript): a local radio phone-in. Key: (.) micropause; a square bracket opening two lines marks
overlapping speech.

> PRESENTER: morning Dave you're on the air (.) go ahead  
> CALLER: yeah morning (.) erm I just wanted to say about the car park charges  
> PRESENTER: the new ones  
> CALLER: yeah [it's  
> PRESENTER: [two pounds an hour now isn't it  
> CALLER: two pounds an hour (.) for a beach (.) I mean it's ridiculous  
> PRESENTER: okay but the council would say they need it for the sea wall Dave

## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by **representation**, and why no text can represent its subject neutrally. **[2]**

**2.** Using an invented example, explain the difference in effect between an active clause and an agentless passive
when reporting a negative event. **[3]**

**3.** Identify the presupposition in the question *"Why do so many parents still let their children walk to school
alone?"* and explain how it positions the reader. **[3]**

**4.** Explain the term **synthetic personalisation**, identifying **two** language features that create it. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **two** ways in which the date of an older text should inform your analysis of it. **[4]**

**6.** A news report could refer to the same group as *"thugs"*, *"young people"* or *"local teenagers"*. Explain
how each choice creates a different representation. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Analyse how the writer of Text A constructs an identity for herself as a producer. Refer to **three** language
features. **[6]**

**8.** Analyse how Text B represents the countryside and the walker. **[8]**

**9.** Analyse how Text B positions its reader, with reference to its context. **[6]**

**10.** Compare how Texts A and B present the purpose and value of walking. **[8]**

**11.** Analyse how the relationship between the presenter and the caller is enacted in Text C. **[6]**

**12.** "Text A is more personal than Text B, but both tell the reader what to do." Evaluate this view, with
reference to language features in both texts. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Representation is the version of a person, group, place, event or idea that a text constructs through its
language choices [1]. It cannot be neutral because every text selects some details and omits others, and every
wording carries connotations and a viewpoint [1].

**2.** Example: active "The council closed the library" versus agentless passive "The library was closed" [1]. The
active clause names the council as agent, assigning responsibility [1]; the agentless passive deletes the agent, so
the event appears to have no cause and no one is blamed, which may protect the people responsible [1].

**3.** It presupposes that letting children walk to school alone is wrong or risky, and that the number doing so is
"so many" [1]. "Still" adds a presupposition that parents should already have stopped [1]. To process the question
the reader must accept these assumptions, so they are positioned as already sharing the writer's disapproval and
are invited only to ask *why*, not *whether* [1].

**4.** Synthetic personalisation, Norman Fairclough's term, is the way a text aimed at a mass audience addresses each
reader as if they were an individual known to the producer [1], to create an impression of friendliness that
supports the text's purpose [1]. Features (any two) [1] [1]: direct second-person address ("you"); imperatives that
sound like personal advice; a conversational register with contractions and colloquial lexis; questions to the
reader; first-person confiding ("I'll be honest").

**5.** Two developed points. The date explains conventions of formality, sentence length, spelling or address that
were normal for the period [1], so these should be read as evidence of the original audience's expectations rather
than as errors [1]. The date also points to the social attitudes of the time, for example towards gender, class or
childhood [1], which shape what the text represents and whose perspective it assumes [1]. (Also creditable: the
technology and mode available at the time, such as print rather than digital, and its effect on layout and
interaction.)

**6.** "Thugs" is a strongly negative, dysphemistic label that represents the group as violent and threatening [1].
"Young people" is a neutral, general noun phrase that represents them by age only, without judgement [1]. "Local
teenagers" adds the modifier "local", representing them as members of the community, which can create familiarity
or sympathy [1].

**7.** Three features, each worth 1 mark for identification and 1 for analysis. First-person confession ("Okay, confession time: I used
to think") [1] constructs a candid, relatable persona who admits past ignorance [1]. Humorous hyperbole and self-
deprecation ("very expensive socks") [1] present her as an ordinary person rather than an elitist expert [1].
Spoken-style features in writing, such as the discourse marker "Okay", the questioning "honestly?" and the fragment
"Your stress might." [1], create an informal, conversational identity suited to a personal blog [1]. (Personal
testimony such as "It saved my sanity", building authority from experience rather than expertise, is an equally
valid choice.)

**8.** Credit up to 8 points, 1 mark each, for example: the countryside is represented as something to "respect", an
object of moral duty rather than leisure [1]; the agentless passives ("Gates are to be closed", "livestock left
undisturbed") present rules as impersonal and absolute, not one writer's opinion [1]; the countryside is
represented as fragile, needing protection from walkers (gates, livestock, wild flowers) [1]; it is also dangerous,
"no place for the careless", a judgement that raises the stakes [1]. The walker is the generic "rambler", assumed
male ("behind him", "Let him"), reflecting assumptions of its period about who walks [1]; the walker is represented
as needing "discipline" and the right equipment ("good map", "sound pair of boots") [1]; the reward is represented
in a list of abstract benefits ("clean air, a quiet mind and the fellowship") that elevates walking to a moral and
social good [1]; the reference to "those who walk the same paths" represents walkers as a community with shared
values [1].

**9.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: the generic third-person "The rambler who would enjoy" addresses the reader
indirectly, positioning them as a would-be member who must earn the right [1]; modal "must" and the obligation
structure "are to be closed" position the reader as subject to rules [1]; the third-person imperative "Let him
carry" gives instruction with formal distance, reflecting a handbook genre and an era of more formal printed
address [1]; the conditional framing implies that only the disciplined reader truly enjoys the countryside [1];
"the careless" creates an out-group the reader is invited to avoid joining [1]; context: a club handbook speaks with
institutional authority to members, so deference and acceptance of collective rules are assumed [1].

**10.** Up to 8 points, 1 mark each, credited when both texts are connected: Text A presents walking as personal therapy
("saved my sanity", "your stress"), Text B as moral discipline and fellowship ("such discipline", "fellowship") [1]
[1]; A minimises requirements ("You don't need gear") while B insists on equipment ("good map", "sound pair of
boots"), reflecting a shift from expert rules to accessible leisure [1]; A addresses the reader directly with "you"
and imperatives ("lace up", "breathe"), while B addresses a generic "rambler" in the third person, a difference in
relationship from intimacy to distance [1]; A's countryside is close and ordinary ("behind the industrial estate"),
B's is remote and demanding ("the moors") [1]; similarity: both use imperatives or directives and both promise
rewards, so both are persuasive [1]; context: blog genre and synthetic personalisation versus institutional
handbook conventions [1]; a clear overall judgement on how the purpose of walking has been reframed [1].

**11.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: the presenter opens and allocates the turn ("go ahead"), controlling the structure of
the exchange [1]; first-name address ("Dave") creates informality while confirming the presenter's role as host [1];
the presenter completes and overlaps the caller's turn ("[two pounds an hour now isn't it"), which could be read as
supportive cooperation or as taking control of the topic [1]; the tag question "isn't it" invites agreement and
keeps the caller engaged [1]; the presenter introduces a counter-view attributed to a third party ("the council
would say"), which challenges without direct face threat, a typical broadcast politeness strategy [1]; the caller
uses hedges and fillers ("erm", "I just wanted") as he takes the floor, then gains confidence with repetition ("two
pounds an hour") and an ironic tag question ("wouldn't they"), resisting the presenter's challenge [1].

**12.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: agree that Text A is more personal, through first-person anecdote, confession and
contractions [1]; Text B is impersonal, using a generic third person and passives [1]; agree that both direct the
reader: A through second-person imperatives ("lace up", "step outside"), B through obligation ("must", "are to be")
and "Let him" [1]; but evaluate the nature of the direction: A's imperatives are framed as friendly reassurance that
lowers barriers, B's as rules that raise them [1]; Text A's "personal" quality is partly synthetic, a persona built
for a public audience, so it is also strategic [1]; a reasoned judgement linked to context (blog conventions and
contemporary informality versus an older institutional handbook) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing what the walkers do rather than how the language represents them.
- Missing the gendered generic pronoun in the older text, or mentioning it without linking it to context.
- Treating Text C's fillers as deliberate persuasive devices instead of features of spontaneous speech.
- In comparison, writing about Text A, then Text B, then adding one linking sentence.
- Evaluation questions answered with agreement only, without weighing the claim.
