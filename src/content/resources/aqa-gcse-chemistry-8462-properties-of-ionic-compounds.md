This guide covers **4.2.2.3 Properties of ionic compounds** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification,
for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It is part of topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the
properties of matter, examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. No statement in it is marked (HT only),
and there is no required practical.

For quick recall use the [properties of ionic compounds revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-ionic-compounds-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [properties of ionic compounds practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-ionic-compounds-practice/).
How ionic bonds form, and how to draw them, is covered in the
[ionic compounds study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-ionic-compounds/). This page is about what the
structure makes the compounds do.

## What the specification asks for

| Statement or skill | What to be ready for |
|---|---|
| Ionic compounds have regular structures (giant ionic lattices) | Describing the lattice |
| Strong electrostatic forces of attraction act in all directions between oppositely charged ions | Naming the force and its direction |
| High melting points and boiling points because of the large amounts of energy needed to break the many strong bonds | Explaining a melting point |
| When melted or dissolved in water, ionic compounds conduct electricity because the ions are free to move and so charge can flow | Explaining conduction, and when it does not happen |
| Knowledge of the structures of specific ionic compounds other than sodium chloride is not required | Using sodium chloride as the model |

## The giant ionic lattice

An ionic compound is made of **positive ions** and **negative ions**. They are arranged in a **regular,
repeating pattern** called a **giant ionic lattice**. In sodium chloride, sodium ions (Na⁺) and chloride ions
(Cl⁻) alternate in all three dimensions. Each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge.

Between oppositely charged ions there is a strong **electrostatic force of attraction**. Because the force
acts in **all directions** through the lattice, there are no separate molecules. The whole crystal is held
together by a huge number of these bonds.

Two points to remember:

- "Giant" means the lattice continues on and on. A single crystal of salt holds an enormous number of ions.
- The formula of an ionic compound shows the **ratio** of ions, not the number in a molecule. NaCl means one
  sodium ion for every chloride ion in the lattice.

You only need to know the structure of sodium chloride. If another ionic compound appears in a question, the
question will tell you what you need. Treat it as having the same kind of lattice.

## High melting points and boiling points

To melt an ionic compound, the ions must gain enough energy to move out of their fixed positions. That means
overcoming the electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions. Since there are **many strong bonds**
in the lattice, a **large amount of energy** is needed. So ionic compounds have **high melting points and
high boiling points**. At room temperature almost all ionic compounds are solids.

| Compound | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium chloride, NaCl | 801 | 1413 |
| Potassium chloride, KCl | 770 | 1420 |
| Magnesium oxide, MgO | 2852 | 3600 |

These values are for comparison only. You do not need to learn them. A useful rule is that an ionic compound
that melts at several hundred degrees Celsius or higher is typical.

## Electrical conductivity

An electric current is a flow of charge. A substance conducts if it has charged particles that are **free to
move**.

| State | Are the ions free to move? | Conducts electricity? |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | No. The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice and only vibrate. | **No** |
| Molten (liquid) | Yes. The lattice has broken down and the ions can move. | **Yes** |
| Dissolved in water (aqueous) | Yes. The ions separate and are surrounded by water. | **Yes** |

The ions carry the charge. In a metal, it is electrons that carry the charge. This difference is a standard
exam point. A well-written answer says "the **ions** are free to move, so **charge** can flow".

A common mistake is to say that ionic compounds "conduct because they contain ions". A solid ionic compound
contains ions and still does not conduct. What matters is that the ions can move.

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: explaining a high melting point

*Explain why magnesium oxide has a high melting point.*

```
1. Magnesium oxide has a giant ionic lattice.
2. There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions,
   in all directions.
3. There are many of these strong bonds, so a large amount of energy is needed to break them.
4. So the melting point is high.
```

A common error is to write "the bonds between molecules are strong". There are no molecules in an ionic
compound. Talk about **ions** and **ionic bonds** (electrostatic forces).

### Worked example 2: explaining when a compound conducts

*Lead bromide does not conduct when solid but conducts when molten. Explain why.*

```
Solid:  the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice, so they cannot move and charge cannot flow.
Molten: the lattice has broken down, so the ions are free to move and carry the charge.
```

### Worked example 3: identifying a substance from data

*A student tests four solids, W, X, Y and Z. The results are in the table.*

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts as a solid? | Conducts when molten? |
|---|---|---|---|
| W | 1085 | Yes | Yes |
| X | 801 | No | Yes |
| Y | -7 | No | No |
| Z | 1710 | No | No |

*Which substance is most likely to be ionic? Explain your answer.*

```
W: conducts as a solid and melts at 1085 -> metal (free electrons)
X: high melting point, does not conduct as a solid, conducts when molten -> ionic
Y: low melting point, no conduction -> small molecules
Z: very high melting point, no conduction in either state -> giant covalent

Answer: X. High melting point and conducts only when molten (the ions are free to move).
```

The pattern "does not conduct when solid, does conduct when molten" is the signature of an ionic compound.

### Worked example 4: dissolving

*Explain why a solution of sodium chloride in water conducts electricity but dry salt does not.*

In dry salt the ions are fixed in the lattice. When salt dissolves, the lattice breaks up. The Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions
are free to move through the solution, so they can carry charge. This idea is used again in electrolysis, where
a molten or dissolved ionic compound must be used so that the ions can move to the electrodes.

## Ionic compounds compared with other substances

It helps to see the full set of patterns together. You will use them to classify unknown substances in exam
questions.

| Substance type | Particles | Melting point | Conducts as a solid | Conducts when molten |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic compound | Ions | High | No | Yes |
| Small molecules | Molecules | Low | No | No |
| Giant covalent | Atoms | Very high | Usually no | Usually no |
| Metal | Atoms and delocalised electrons | Usually high | Yes | Yes |

The best single clue for an ionic compound is **not conducting as a solid but conducting when molten**.

## Linking properties to structure: a model answer

Questions often ask for a longer response. A six-mark answer needs several linked points:

1. The compound is a giant ionic lattice of oppositely charged ions.
2. There are strong electrostatic forces between the ions, in all directions.
3. A large amount of energy is needed to overcome many strong bonds, so the melting point is high.
4. In the solid the ions cannot move, so it does not conduct.
5. When molten or dissolved, the ions are free to move, so it conducts.
6. The charge is carried by the moving ions.

## Common mistakes

- Referring to molecules of an ionic compound. They are lattices of ions.
- Saying that "ionic bonds are broken" without saying they are electrostatic forces between ions.
- Writing "electrons carry the charge" for an ionic compound. The ions carry the charge.
- Saying a solid conducts "if it is wet". Be precise: it conducts when dissolved in water or melted.
- Writing "high melting point because the bonds are strong" with no mention of **many** bonds and the **large
  amount of energy** needed.
- Mixing up the melting point of a covalent compound (low, weak forces between molecules) with that of an ionic
  compound.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** link the structure, the forces and the energy, in that order.
- **Why does it conduct (or not)?** Always state whether the ions are free to move.
- **Identify from data:** use two pieces of evidence, usually melting point and conductivity.
- **Name the lattice:** use the phrase "giant ionic lattice" for sodium chloride and other ionic compounds.
