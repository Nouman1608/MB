All data below are illustrative unless a real place is named.

## Section A

**1.** Define infant mortality rate. **[2]**

**2.** State the difference between natural increase and population growth. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows data for three countries.

| Country | Birth rate (per 1,000) | Death rate (per 1,000) | Net migration (per 1,000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 38 | 11 | −1 |
| Q | 9 | 12 | +4 |
| R | 15 | 6 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the natural change for each country. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify which country's population is growing only because of migration. **[1]**

**4.** Country Q has a higher death rate than Country R. Suggest **one** reason why a high-income
country can have a higher death rate than a middle-income country. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** features of a pro-natalist policy. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain why the world's population grew rapidly after about 1800. **[5]**

**7.** Explain **three** reasons why birth rates fall as a country becomes more economically
developed. **[6]**

**8.** A country of 25 million people has a birth rate of 30 per 1,000, a death rate of 8 per
1,000 and net migration of −4 per 1,000.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage growth rate of the population per year. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many people the population will grow by in one year. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons why people may be emigrating from this country. **[2]**

**9.** "A country with a high population density must be over-populated." Using examples, explain
why this statement is not correct. **[4]**

**10.** For a named country with a population policy, describe the policy and evaluate how
successful it has been. **[7]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The number of deaths of children under one year of age [1] per 1,000 live births per year
[1].

**2.** Natural increase is the difference between the birth rate and the death rate only [1].
Population growth also includes the effect of migration, so it is natural change plus net
migration [1].

**3. (a)** P: 38 − 11 = 27 per 1,000 [1]. Q: 9 − 12 = −3 per 1,000, a natural decrease [1].
R: 15 − 6 = 9 per 1,000 [1].
**(b)** Q: it has a natural decrease of 3 per 1,000, but net migration of +4 gives total growth of
+1 per 1,000 [1].

**4.** It has an **ageing population** [1], so a larger proportion of its people are elderly and
more likely to die each year, even though healthcare is good [1].

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: child benefit or family allowances; tax relief for larger families;
paid maternity or parental leave; subsidised childcare or housing; campaigns encouraging couples to
have more children.

**6.** Up to 5 marks for developed points: death rates fell [1] because of better food supply and
storage, reducing famine [1]; clean water and sewage systems reduced waterborne disease [1];
vaccination and antibiotics reduced deaths from infectious disease [1]; birth rates stayed high
for some time because attitudes to family size change slowly [1]; so the gap between birth and death
rates widened, producing rapid natural increase [1]. Maximum 5.

**7.** Any three reasons, 2 marks each (1 for the reason, 1 for the explanation):
- More **women in education and paid work** [1], so they marry later and have fewer children [1].
- Wider access to **contraception and family planning** [1], so couples can choose family size [1].
- **Lower infant mortality** [1], so parents no longer need extra births to ensure some children
  survive [1].
- **Children become costly** as people move to cities and education lasts longer [1], rather than
  contributing labour on farms [1].
- **Pensions and state welfare** [1] reduce the need for children to support parents in old age [1].

**8. (a)** Total change = 30 − 8 − 4 = 18 per 1,000 [1]; 18 ÷ 10 = **1.8% per year** [1].
**(b)** 25,000,000 × 18 ÷ 1,000 = **450,000 people** [1].
**(c)** Any two, 1 mark each: lack of jobs because the workforce is growing faster than
employment; low wages; poor access to services; conflict or political instability; natural hazards
or land shortage. Credit any reasonable push factor linked to a fast-growing population.

**9.** Over-population is about the balance of **people and resources**, not people and area [1].
A dense country may be wealthy enough to import food and resources and use technology to raise
output [1], for example the **Netherlands** or **Singapore**, which are densely populated but have a
high standard of living [1]. A country can be sparsely populated and still over-populated if its
resources are very limited, as in dry parts of the Sahel [1].

**10.** Mark in levels.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** simple description of a policy, such as "China allowed one child per family",
  with little evaluation.
- **Level 2 (4–5):** developed description with some detail (dates, incentives and penalties) and an
  evaluation that gives successes **or** problems.
- **Level 3 (6–7):** detailed, named description and a balanced evaluation with a judgement.

Indicative content (China): introduced around 1979–80 to slow rapid growth; incentives such as
priority for housing and education for one-child families; penalties such as fines and loss of
benefits; exceptions in some rural areas and for ethnic minorities. **Successes:** growth slowed;
less pressure on food, housing and jobs; more investment per child. **Problems:** the birth rate was
already falling before the policy, so not all of the fall is due to it; an imbalanced sex ratio; a
rapidly ageing population and a shrinking workforce; the "4-2-1" family burden; coercion and human
rights concerns. **Judgement:** successful in slowing growth but with long-term costs, shown by the
move to a two-child policy from 2016 and a three-child policy in 2021.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculations that ignore migration or forget to divide by 10 for a percentage.
- Answers to question 7 that list reasons without the "so" that explains the effect.
- Policy answers that describe but never reach a judgement.
