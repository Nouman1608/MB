It is International AS content. The specification lists 3.1.6 among the sections assessed in Paper 1: Introduction to Financial Accounting. All figures below are in dollars and every business is invented.

## What section 3.1.6 covers

| Specification content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| The use of concepts in the preparation of financial statements | Record other payables and other receivables, depreciation (straight line and reducing balance), disposal of non-current assets, provision for doubtful debts, irrecoverable debts, recovery of irrecoverable debts, income due, income received in advance, goods taken for own use, goods on sale or return, and inventory |
| Preparing financial statements of sole traders from ledger accounts, including adjustments | Write up the ledger accounts for these items and transfer them to the income statement, for service businesses and trading businesses |
| Preparing income statements and statements of financial position from a trial balance, including adjustments | Start from a trial balance and year-end notes and produce an adjusted income statement and statement of financial position |

FIFO, AVCO and LIFO are outside this topic: the specification states that these methods of inventory valuation will not be examined.

## Why the adjustments exist

A trial balance shows what was recorded. The concepts from section 3.1.5 decide what should be reported.

| Adjustment | Main concept behind it |
|---|---|
| Other payables, other receivables, income due, income received in advance | Accruals: income and expenses belong to the period they relate to, not the period cash moves |
| Depreciation | Accruals (spreading cost over the years the asset is used) and consistency (same method each year) |
| Provision for doubtful debts, inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value | Prudence: do not overstate assets or profit |
| Goods on sale or return | Realisation: no sale until the customer accepts the goods |
| Goods taken for own use | Business entity: the owner and the business are separate |

## Other payables and other receivables

An expense owing at the year end is an **other payable** (a current liability). An expense paid in advance is an **other receivable** (a current asset).

**Worked example (ledger account).** Insurance at 1 January 2025 was owing, 385. During 2025 the business paid 4,860. At 31 December 2025, 1,140 of that was for 2026.

```
                      Insurance
2025              Dr           2025                 Cr
Bank           4,860           1 Jan Balance b/d   385
                               Income statement  3,335
                               31 Dec Balance c/d 1,140
               4,860                             4,860
2026
1 Jan Balance b/d 1,140
```

The charge is 4,860 - 385 - 1,140 = **3,335**. The 385 paid off last year's debt, so it is not a 2025 cost. The 1,140 debit balance carried down is an other receivable in the statement of financial position.

## Income due and income received in advance

Income earned but not received is **income due**, shown with other receivables. Income received before it is earned is **income received in advance**, shown with other payables.

**Worked example.** A business earns commission. At 1 January 2025 it had received 270 in advance for 2025 work. It received 7,350 during 2025, and 480 was due at 31 December 2025.

```
                 Commission receivable
2025              Dr           2025                 Cr
Income statement 8,100         1 Jan Balance b/d   270
                               Bank              7,350
                               31 Dec Balance c/d  480
                 8,100                           8,100
2026
1 Jan Balance b/d  480
```

Income = 7,350 + 270 + 480 = **8,100**. The 480 debit balance is income due, an other receivable.

## Depreciation

- **Straight line:** (cost - residual value) / useful life. The same charge each year.
- **Reducing balance:** rate x carrying amount at the start of the year. The charge falls each year.

Double entry: debit the income statement (depreciation), credit provision for depreciation. In the statement of financial position, show cost, accumulated depreciation and carrying amount.

**Worked example.** A machine costs 32,000, residual value 2,000, life 6 years: (32,000 - 2,000) / 6 = **5,000** a year.

A vehicle costs 30,400 and is depreciated at 25% reducing balance:

| Year | Carrying amount at start | Charge (25%) | Accumulated depreciation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 30,400 | 7,600 | 7,600 |
| 2 | 22,800 | 5,700 | 13,300 |
| 3 | 17,100 | 4,275 | 17,575 |

Carrying amount after three years: 30,400 - 17,575 = **12,825**.

## Disposal of non-current assets

Use a disposal account. Transfer in the cost, transfer out the accumulated depreciation and the proceeds. The balance is the profit or loss on disposal, which goes to the income statement.

**Worked example.** The machine above was bought on 1 January 2022 and sold on 1 October 2025 for 13,500. Policy: a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal. Charges for 2022, 2023 and 2024 total 3 x 5,000 = 15,000.

```
                     Disposal of machinery
Machinery (cost)  32,000    Provision for depreciation  15,000
                            Bank                        13,500
                            Income statement (loss)      3,500
                  32,000                                32,000
```

Carrying amount 32,000 - 15,000 = 17,000; proceeds 13,500; **loss 3,500**, shown as an expense.

## Irrecoverable debts, their recovery and the provision for doubtful debts

- **Irrecoverable debt written off:** debit irrecoverable debts (expense), credit the customer's account. Trade receivables fall.
- **Recovery of an irrecoverable debt:** reinstate the debt (debit the customer, credit irrecoverable debts recovered), then record the cash (debit bank, credit the customer). Irrecoverable debts recovered is income in the income statement.
- **Provision for doubtful debts:** only the change goes to the income statement. An increase is an expense; a decrease is income. The full provision is deducted from trade receivables in the statement of financial position.

**Worked example.** Trade receivables are 52,400 before writing off a debt of 1,400. The provision is to be 4% of trade receivables; it was 1,850 last year.

- Trade receivables after write-off: 52,400 - 1,400 = 51,000.
- New provision: 4% x 51,000 = 2,040. Increase: 2,040 - 1,850 = **190**, an expense.
- Statement of financial position: 51,000 - 2,040 = **48,960**.

Always write off irrecoverable debts before calculating the provision.

## Goods for own use, sale or return, and inventory

**Goods taken for own use.** Record at cost: debit drawings, credit purchases. Purchases fall, so cost of sales falls.

**Goods on sale or return.** If goods sent to a customer on sale or return have been recorded as a sale but the customer has not accepted them by the year end, reverse it. Remove the selling price from revenue and trade receivables, and include the goods in inventory at cost. Goods sent at a selling price of 2,700, priced at cost plus 50%, cost 2,700 / 1.5 = **1,800**.

**Inventory.** Value at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV). NRV is the expected selling price less costs to finish and sell. An item cost 950 and can be sold for 900 only after a repair costing 200: NRV 700, so it is valued at 700, a write-down of **250**.

## Common errors

- Adding last year's accrual to this year's charge instead of deducting it.
- Charging the whole new provision for doubtful debts, not just the change.
- Calculating reducing balance on cost rather than carrying amount.
- Recording goods for own use at selling price, or forgetting to add them to drawings.
- Leaving goods on sale or return in revenue, or adding them to inventory at selling price.
- Showing income received in advance as a current asset; it is an other payable.
