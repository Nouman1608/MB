## 4.1 Introduction to marketing

### Market orientation versus product orientation

A **market-oriented** business begins with the customer. It finds out what people want, then designs, prices and promotes something to match. A **product-oriented** business begins with the thing it is good at making. It perfects the item and then looks for people to buy it.

Neither is wrong in every case. Market orientation lowers the risk of making something nobody wants, but research costs money and customers cannot always describe a need they have never met. Product orientation can produce a breakthrough, but it leaves the business guessing about demand.

**Worked example: Aldersley Oat Drinks.** Aldersley's founder, a food scientist, spends two years perfecting a smooth, low-sugar oat drink. Only after production starts does she ask retailers whether they will stock it. Retailers say shoppers in their stores want larger, cheaper cartons.

```
Step 1  Where did the idea start?   With the founder's recipe, not with buyers.
Step 2  When was demand checked?    After production began.
Step 3  Conclusion                  Product orientation.
Step 4  Consequence                 Price, pack size and promotion must now be
                                    adjusted to fit demand that was not tested.
```

A stronger answer also offers a way forward: Aldersley could run a short taste panel and price test with shoppers, then change pack size before a full launch. That moves it toward market orientation.

### Market share

**Market share** is the proportion of total sales in a market that one business wins. The guide lists it without a formula, so this is the standard form:

```
market share (%) = (business's sales revenue / total sales revenue of the market) x 100
```

Use the same measure (revenue or units) and the same time period on the top and bottom.

### Market growth

**Market growth** is the percentage change in the total size of the market between two periods. Again, this is the standard form:

```
market growth (%) = (market sales this period - market sales last period) / market sales last period x 100
```

Market growth describes the market, not your business. A business can grow faster or slower than its market, and the gap between the two decides whether its share rises or falls.

**Worked example: Lunthorpe Tea Merchants.** Total sales in the loose-leaf tea market were USD 48.0 million in Year 1 and USD 51.84 million in Year 2. Lunthorpe's sales were USD 4.8 million and USD 5.7 million. A rival, Greywick Teas, sold USD 12.0 million and USD 12.5 million.

```
Market growth     = (51.84 - 48.0) / 48.0 x 100              = 8.0%
Lunthorpe share Y1 = 4.8 / 48.0 x 100                         = 10.0%
Lunthorpe share Y2 = 5.7 / 51.84 x 100                        = 11.0% (3 s.f.)
Lunthorpe sales growth = (5.7 - 4.8) / 4.8 x 100              = 18.75%
Greywick share Y1  = 12.0 / 48.0 x 100                        = 25.0%
Greywick share Y2  = 12.5 / 51.84 x 100                       = 24.1% (3 s.f.)
```

Interpretation: Lunthorpe's sales grew by 18.75%, more than double the market's 8.0%, so its share rose by about one percentage point. Greywick's sales also rose, but by only about 4.2%, which is below market growth, so its share slipped even though it sold more. Rising sales do not guarantee a rising share. Say "percentage points" when you compare two shares.

When a question says "comment", follow the calculation with a judgment: Lunthorpe is gaining ground, but it is still far behind the leader, so the number alone does not prove it is winning the market.

### The importance of market share and market leadership (HL only)

The market leader is the business with the largest share of its market. The guide asks HL candidates to judge why share and leadership matter. Points to weigh:

- **Buying power and costs.** A large share means large volumes, which can bring lower unit costs and better terms from suppliers.
- **Pricing and brand strength.** Leaders are often seen as the safe choice, which can support a premium price and make retailers keen to stock them.
- **Confidence of stakeholders.** Investors, lenders and talented recruits may favor an established leader.
- **Costs and risks.** Winning share can mean heavy spending on promotion or price cuts that shrink profit. Chasing share for its own sake can hide weak profitability. A big share of a market that is shrinking is a weaker position than a small share of a growing one, which is why share and growth are best read together.

To make this an evaluation, finish with a verdict that depends on the situation: share matters most where costs fall steeply with volume, and least where customers buy on individual taste.

## 4.2 Marketing planning

### The role of marketing planning

A marketing plan turns the business's overall objectives into specific marketing aims and actions. It usually states who the customers are, what the business wants to achieve (such as a target share or sales level), how it will compete, who is responsible, and when and with what budget. Planning gives marketing a direction, helps managers allocate scarce money, makes results easier to measure against targets, and keeps marketing consistent with other departments. The weakness is that plans rest on assumptions: a plan written before a rival launches a cheaper product may need rapid revision. A SWOT or STEEPLE analysis is a sensible input because it records the strengths, weaknesses and external changes the plan has to respond to.

### Segmentation, targeting and positioning

These three steps usually run in order.

1. **Segmentation** divides a market into groups of customers who share needs or characteristics. Common bases are age and income (demographic), place (geographic), way of life (psychographic) and buying habits (behavioral).
2. **Targeting** chooses which segment or segments the business will serve: the **target market**.
3. **Positioning** decides how customers should see the product compared with rivals. A **position map** plots brands on two axes, such as price and quality, so the business can see crowded areas and gaps.

**Worked example: Cadwallow Cycles.** Cadwallow studies three segments of the adult bicycle market in one country.

| Segment | Buyers per year | Average spend (USD) | Segment value (USD million) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commuters | 40,000 | 450 | 18.0 |
| Weekend riders | 22,000 | 900 | 19.8 |
| Long-distance tourers | 3,500 | 2,400 | 8.4 |

The total across the three segments is USD 46.2 million, so tourers are about 18.2% of it. Cadwallow chooses tourers and aims for a 20% share of that segment: 20% of 3,500 buyers is 700 bicycles, or USD 1.68 million of sales. It then maps four brands, scoring price and suitability for touring from 1 (lowest) to 10 (highest).

| Brand | Price (1-10) | Touring suitability (1-10) |
|---|---|---|
| Cadwallow | 8 | 9 |
| Rival A | 9 | 5 |
| Rival B | 3 | 3 |
| Rival C | 5 | 6 |

```
Touring suitability
high    |  empty gap       |                  |  CADWALLOW       |
medium  |                  |  Rival C         |  Rival A         |
low     |  Rival B         |                  |                  |
          low price          medium price       high price
```

Reading the map: Cadwallow is positioned as a premium touring specialist. The empty top-left cell (a low-priced tourer) is a gap, but the map cannot say whether enough customers would pay for it. Further research would be needed before moving into it.

### Niche market and mass market

A **niche market** is a small, specialized segment with distinct needs. A **mass market** is a large market of customers with broadly similar needs, served with a standard offer.

| | Niche market | Mass market |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | Few, with specific needs | Many, with common needs |
| Competition | Often less, but a big rival can enter | Usually intense |
| Price | Often higher, since customers value the fit | Often lower, driven by volume |
| Risk | Dependence on a small customer base | Low margins; hard to stand out |

In the Cadwallow case, the niche has the smallest value but the highest spend per buyer, which is why a specialist can profit there.

### The importance of a USP

A **unique selling point/proposition (USP)** is the feature or benefit that sets a product apart in a way customers care about. It matters because it gives customers a reason to choose this brand, can support a higher price, simplifies advertising to one clear message, and makes the product harder to copy if it rests on something rivals cannot match. A USP is weakened when it is easy to imitate or when buyers do not value it. Cadwallow's USP might be frames sized and tested for loaded touring.

### How organizations differentiate themselves

Differentiation means making an offer different from rivals' in ways customers notice. Routes include:

- **Product features and quality**, such as design, materials or a longer guarantee.
- **Branding and image**, including packaging and the values the brand stands for.
- **Service**, such as fitting, delivery or after-sales support.
- **Place and access**, such as unusual locations or convenient online ordering.
- **Price positioning**, either clearly cheaper or clearly premium.
- **Ethical or sustainable practices**, if the target market values them.

Differentiation has a cost, and it only pays if the extra price or extra sales exceed it. Link the method to the target market: a service advantage helps little with customers who buy purely on price.

## Common errors

- Defining market orientation as "selling to customers". Every business does that; the difference is where decisions start.
- Calculating share with the wrong total, for example dividing by last year's market size.
- Treating market growth and business growth as the same thing.
- Describing segmentation without saying which segment is chosen and why.
- Naming a USP that is only a general claim such as "good quality".
- In HL answers, praising market leadership without a limitation or a verdict.
