These questions cover section **4.6.1 Waves in air, fluids and solids** of the AQA GCSE Physics (8463)
specification, examined on Paper 2 at both tiers. Questions 12 to 14 test content marked (HT only) and are
labelled **Higher tier only**. A calculator is allowed throughout.

[waves in air, fluids and solids study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-waves-in-air-fluids-and-solids/)
and the
[waves in air, fluids and solids revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-waves-in-air-fluids-and-solids-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Name the regions of a longitudinal wave where the particles are close together, and where they are
spread apart. **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by the frequency of a wave. **[1]**

**3.** A displacement–distance graph shows exactly 3 complete waves in a distance of 0.75 m. The crests are
2.0 cm above the undisturbed position.

**(a)** State the amplitude. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the wavelength. **[1]**

**4.** A wave has a period of 0.0020 s. Calculate its frequency. **[2]**

**5.** A sound wave has a frequency of 500 Hz and a wavelength of 0.66 m. Calculate its speed. **[2]**

**6.** A sound wave passes from air into water. State which one of speed, frequency and wavelength stays the
same. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Students measure the speed of sound using two microphones 3.0 m apart, connected to an electronic
timer. Three readings of the time are 9.1 ms, 8.9 ms and 9.0 ms.

**(a)** Calculate the mean time. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the speed of sound. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why placing the microphones further apart would improve the accuracy of the result. **[2]**

**8.** In a ripple tank, a student times one ripple travelling 0.36 m in 2.0 s. The signal generator is set to
6.0 Hz.

**(a)** Calculate the speed of the ripples. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the wavelength of the ripples in cm. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why a lamp is placed above the ripple tank. **[1]**

**9.** A music room has bare, hard walls, and players complain of echoes. Thick curtains are hung on the
walls. Explain why the room had echoes and how the curtains reduce them. **[3]**

**10.** A ray of light strikes a plane mirror. The angle between the incident ray and the mirror's surface is
35°.

**(a)** State the angle of incidence. **[1]**
**(b)** State the angle of reflection. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe how to construct an accurate ray diagram of the reflection. **[2]**

**11.** A worker strikes one end of a long steel rail with a hammer. A listener 1200 m away hears the sound
twice: once through the rail and once through the air. Sound travels at 5000 m/s in steel and 330 m/s in air.

**(a)** Calculate the time for the sound to travel through the rail. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the time for the sound to travel through the air. **[1]**
**(c)** The sound through the steel has the same frequency as the sound through the air but a different
wavelength. Explain why. **[2]**

**12.** (Higher tier only)

**(a)** Describe one process that converts sound waves into vibrations of a solid, and one that converts
vibrations of a solid into sound waves. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why such processes only work over a limited frequency range, and how this affects human
hearing. **[2]**

**13.** (Higher tier only) An ultrasound scanner sends a pulse into a pregnant woman's abdomen. Echoes return
from the front and back of the baby's head after 70 μs and 190 μs. Ultrasound travels at 1500 m/s in the
tissue.

**(a)** Calculate the depth of the front of the baby's head below the probe. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the width of the baby's head. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a single pulse can give echoes from more than one boundary. **[1]**

**14.** (Higher tier only) An earthquake happens 2000 km from a seismometer. P-waves travel at 8.0 km/s and
S-waves at 4.5 km/s through the rock between them.

**(a)** Calculate the time between the arrival of the P-waves and the S-waves. **[3]**
**(b)** A seismometer on the opposite side of the Earth detects P-waves but no S-waves. Explain what this
shows about the Earth's core. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Close together: compressions [1]; spread apart: rarefactions [1]

**2.** The number of waves passing a point each second [1]

**3. (a)** **2.0 cm** [1]
**(b)** 0.75 / 3 = **0.25 m** [1]

**4.** f = 1 / T = 1 / 0.0020 [1]; = **500 Hz** [1]

**5.** v = f λ = 500 × 0.66 [1]; = **330 m/s** [1]

**6.** Frequency [1]

**7. (a)** (9.1 + 8.9 + 9.0) / 3 = **9.0 ms** [1]
**(b)** v = 3.0 / 0.0090 [1]; = 333 m/s, about **330 m/s** [1]
**(c)** The time to be measured would be longer [1]; so the same small timing uncertainty would be a smaller
fraction of the measured time [1]

**8. (a)** v = 0.36 / 2.0 [1]; = **0.18 m/s** [1]
**(b)** λ = v / f = 0.18 / 6.0 = 0.030 m [1]; = **3.0 cm** [1]
**(c)** It projects an image of the ripples onto paper or the floor below, so they can be seen and measured
[1]

**9.** Hard, smooth walls reflect most of the sound that reaches them [1]; the reflected sound reaches the
players a short time after the original sound, heard as an echo [1]; curtains absorb more of the sound
energy and reflect less of it, so the echoes are weaker [1]

**10. (a)** 90 − 35 = **55°** [1]
**(b)** **55°** [1]
**(c)** Draw the normal at 90° to the mirror where the ray hits it [1]; measure both angles from the normal
with a protractor, drawing the reflected ray at the same angle as the incident ray on the other side of the
normal [1]
*Examiner insight:* Angles are always measured from the normal, not from the mirror surface.

**11. (a)** 1200 / 5000 = **0.24 s** [1]
**(b)** 1200 / 330 = **3.6 s** [1]
**(c)** The frequency is set by the source (the hammer blow), so it does not change between media [1];
the speed is different in steel, so the wavelength (λ = v / f) is different, longer in steel [1]

**12. (a)** Sound waves make the ear drum vibrate (sound to solid) [1]; a vibrating loudspeaker cone produces
sound waves in the air (solid to sound) [1]
**(b)** The solid parts can only vibrate in response to frequencies within a limited range [1]; in the ear
this limits human hearing to about 20 Hz to 20 kHz [1]

**13. (a)** 1500 × 70 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.105 m there and back [1]; depth = **0.0525 m** (about 5.3 cm) [1]
**(b)** Extra time = 190 − 70 = 120 μs; extra distance = 1500 × 120 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.18 m there and back [1];
width = **0.090 m** (9.0 cm) [1]
**(c)** Ultrasound is only partially reflected at each boundary, so some continues to deeper boundaries and
reflects from them too [1]

**14. (a)** P-waves: 2000 / 8.0 = 250 s [1]; S-waves: 2000 / 4.5 = 444 s [1]; difference = **194 s** (about
190 s) [1]
**(b)** S-waves are transverse and cannot travel through a liquid [1]; so part of the core must be liquid,
while P-waves can pass through it [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Measuring angles from the mirror instead of the normal.
- Forgetting to halve the distance in ultrasound and echo questions.
- Saying frequency changes when sound changes medium.
- Leaving ms or μs unconverted.
- Explaining echoes without saying the sound is reflected.
- Swapping P-wave and S-wave properties.
