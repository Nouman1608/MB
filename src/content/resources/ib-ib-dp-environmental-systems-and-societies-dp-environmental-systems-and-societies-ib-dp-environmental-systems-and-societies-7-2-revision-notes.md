## Core recall

- Primary energy is found in nature; secondary energy (electricity, refined fuels, hydrogen) is a carrier made from it.
- The impact of electricity depends on how it was generated.
- Every conversion loses energy as heat; thermal power stations lose most of their input this way.
- Combined heat and power uses the waste heat, so more useful energy comes from the same fuel.
- Capacity factor = actual annual output ÷ output at full power all year; variable renewables have lower factors.
- Variable sources are balanced by storage, interconnectors, a diverse mix and demand management.
- Energy security depends on import dependency, number and stability of suppliers, storage and diversity.
- National mixes reflect resources (Iceland: hydro and geothermal), policy and security (France: nuclear) and public attitudes (Germany: nuclear closed in April 2023).
- Efficiency gives the same service for less energy; conservation reduces the service; the rebound effect can cancel part of efficiency savings.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Primary energy | Energy in its natural form: fossil fuels, uranium, sunlight, wind, water, geothermal heat, biomass |
| Secondary energy | A carrier produced from primary energy: electricity, petrol, hydrogen |
| Conversion efficiency | Useful energy output ÷ total input × 100 |
| Combined heat and power | Generation that also supplies its waste heat for heating |
| Rated capacity | Maximum power output of a plant (MW) |
| Capacity factor | Actual output over a period ÷ maximum possible output |
| Variable (intermittent) source | Output depends on weather or daylight: wind, solar, wave |
| Dispatchable source | Output can be controlled to meet demand: gas, coal, nuclear, reservoir hydro, biomass |
| Pumped hydro storage | Water pumped uphill with surplus power and released through turbines later |
| Interconnector | Cable linking grids in different regions or countries |
| Import dependency | Net energy imports ÷ total primary energy supply × 100 |
| Energy efficiency | The same service with less energy input |
| Energy conservation | Using less of an energy service |
| Rebound effect | Increased use of a service after efficiency makes it cheaper |
| Jevons paradox | Jevons (1865): more efficient steam engines increased total coal use |

## Formulas and units

```
Efficiency (%)        = useful output ÷ input × 100
Annual output (MWh)   = capacity (MW) × 8,760 h × capacity factor
Import dependency (%) = net imports ÷ primary energy supply × 100
Energy (Wh)           = power (W) × time (h);   1 kWh = 1,000 Wh
```

Power is measured in W, kW or MW; energy in Wh, kWh, MWh, GWh, MJ or PJ. Do not mix them.

## Evaluation checklist for any energy option

| Criterion | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Emissions | CO₂ and air pollutants over the life cycle? |
| Reliability | Variable or dispatchable? Storage needed? |
| Security | Domestic or imported fuel? How many suppliers? |
| Cost | Build cost, running cost, who pays? |
| Land and ecosystems | Area used, habitats changed, water use? |
| Waste and risk | Radioactive waste, spills, dam failure? |
| Social acceptance | Public opinion, displacement, jobs? |
| Timescale | How quickly can it be built and scaled? |

## Exam traps

- Calling electricity a source.
- Forgetting the capacity factor, so output is overstated.
- Writing MW when the answer is energy (MWh).
- Treating "renewable" as "no environmental impact": hydro floods valleys; biomass competes for land.
- Treating security as self-sufficiency only.
- Assuming efficiency always cuts total use.
- Explaining a national mix without naming a factor.

## Quick self-test

1. Classify as primary or secondary: (a) natural gas; (b) electricity; (c) petrol; (d) sunlight.
2. A station uses 500 MJ of fuel energy and delivers 175 MJ of electricity. Calculate its efficiency.
3. In the station in question 2, how much energy leaves as waste heat?
4. A 2 MW turbine produces 5,256 MWh in a year. Calculate its capacity factor.
5. A 50 MW solar farm has a capacity factor of 15%. Calculate its annual output in MWh.
6. Give two ways a grid can cope with variable output from wind and solar.
7. A country's primary energy supply is 800 PJ, of which 520 PJ is produced at home. Calculate its import dependency.
8. Why might a country with low import dependency still be energy-insecure?
9. Distinguish energy efficiency from energy conservation, with one example of each.
10. A 40 W bulb is replaced with a 6 W LED, used 2,000 hours a year. Calculate the energy saved in kWh.
11. Explain the rebound effect.
12. Name the factor that best explains Iceland's electricity mix.

### Answers

1. (a) Primary; (b) secondary; (c) secondary; (d) primary.
2. 175 ÷ 500 × 100 = **35%**.
3. 500 − 175 = **325 MJ**.
4. 5,256 ÷ (2 × 8,760) = 0.30, so **30%**.
5. 50 × 8,760 × 0.15 = **65,700 MWh**.
6. Any two: storage such as pumped hydro or batteries; interconnectors; a diverse mix; demand management.
7. Net imports 800 − 520 = 280 PJ; 280 ÷ 800 × 100 = **35%**.
8. It may rely on one supplier or one source, on routes through unstable regions, or have little storage.
9. Efficiency: the same service from less energy, such as insulating a house. Conservation: using less of the service, such as lowering the thermostat.
10. (40 − 6) × 2,000 = 68,000 Wh = **68 kWh**.
11. When efficiency makes a service cheaper, people use more of it, so part of the expected saving is lost.
12. Availability of local resources: its geology and rivers make geothermal and hydroelectric power plentiful.
