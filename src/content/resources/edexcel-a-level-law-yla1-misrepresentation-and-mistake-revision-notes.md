For the full explanation, use the [misrepresentation and mistake study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-law-yla1-misrepresentation-and-mistake/); the whole market section is in the [market revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-law-yla1-the-market-revision-notes/).

## Misrepresentation checklist

An **untrue statement of fact** → made by a party → **before or at** the contract → that **induced** the contract.

| Element | Rule | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Statement by conduct | Conduct can represent a fact | *Spice Girls v Aprilia* [2002] |
| Silence | Generally not a misrepresentation | *Fletcher v Krell* (1873) |
| Half-truth | Misleading partial statement | *Dimmock v Hallett* (1866) |
| Change of circumstances | Must correct a statement that becomes untrue | *With v O'Flanagan* [1936] |
| Opinion | Not fact, if honestly held without special knowledge | *Bisset v Wilkinson* [1927] |
| Expert opinion | Can be treated as fact | *Esso v Mardon* [1976] |
| Intention | A lie about present intention is a fact | *Edgington v Fitzmaurice* (1885) |
| No reliance | Relied on own investigation | *Attwood v Small* (1838) |
| Reliance despite chance to check | Still relied | *Redgrave v Hurd* (1881) |

## Types and remedies

| Type | Key point | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Fraudulent | Knowingly, without belief, or recklessly (*Derry v Peek* (1889)) | Rescission + deceit damages for all direct loss (*Doyle v Olby* [1969]) |
| s 2(1) MA 1967 | Defendant must prove reasonable grounds for belief (*Howard Marine v Ogden* [1978]) | Rescission + damages as if fraud (*Royscot v Rogerson* [1991]) |
| Common law negligent misstatement | Special relationship (*Hedley Byrne* [1964]) | Damages |
| Innocent | Reasonable belief | Rescission, or damages in lieu under s 2(2) |

- **s 3 MA 1967**: exclusion of liability for misrepresentation must be reasonable (UCTA test).
- **1945 Act**: damages can be reduced for the claimant's own fault in a s 2(1) claim (*Gran Gelato v Richcliff* [1992]); **never** in fraud (*Standard Chartered v PNSC* [2002]).

## Bars to rescission

1. **Affirmation**: *Long v Lloyd* [1958].
2. **Lapse of time**: *Leaf v International Galleries* [1950].
3. **Restitution impossible**: *Clarke v Dickson* (1858).
4. **Third-party rights** acquired in good faith for value.

## Mistake map

```
Common   (same mistake)  existence  -> void (Couturier v Hastie)
                         ownership  -> void (Cooper v Phibbs)
                         quality    -> valid (Bell v Lever Bros;
                                       Great Peace: only if performance
                                       essentially different)
Mutual   (cross-purposes)            -> void if no objective meaning
                                       (Raffles v Wichelhaus;
                                        Scriven v Hindley)
Unilateral (one mistaken, other knows)
          terms                      -> void (Hartog v Colin and Shields)
          identity, in writing       -> void (Cundy v Lindsay;
                                        Shogun Finance v Hudson)
          identity, face to face     -> voidable (Phillips v Brooks;
                                        Lewis v Averay; contrast
                                        Ingram v Little)
```

## Void versus voidable

| | Void | Voidable |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | No contract from the start | Valid until rescinded |
| Typical cause | Operative mistake | Misrepresentation |
| Third party who bought in good faith | Gets no title | Keeps the goods if they bought before rescission |

## Model evaluation paragraph

*Section 2(1) of the Misrepresentation Act 1967 strongly favours the claimant: the defendant must disprove negligence, as the barge owners failed to do in* Howard Marine*, and after* Royscot *damages are assessed as if the defendant had been fraudulent. This may be harsh on a merely careless defendant, but the 1945 Act allows damages to be reduced where the claimant shared the fault (*Gran Gelato*), so the law can still apportion responsibility. Refusing that reduction in fraud (*Standard Chartered*) is justified because a dishonest party should bear the full consequences.*

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Misrepresentation | An untrue statement of fact inducing a contract |
| Voidable | Valid until the innocent party sets it aside |
| Void | Of no legal effect from the start |
| Rescission | Setting the contract aside and restoring the parties |
| Affirmation | Treating the contract as continuing after learning the truth |
| Deceit | The tort of fraudulent misrepresentation |
| Operative mistake | A mistake serious enough to affect validity |
| *Res extincta* | The subject matter no longer exists |
| *Res sua* | The buyer already owns the subject matter |
| Contributory negligence | The claimant's own fault, reducing damages |

## Exam traps

- Opinion versus fact: check the maker's expertise.
- The s 2(1) burden lies on the **defendant**.
- No contributory negligence reduction for fraud.
- Mistake as to quality rarely makes a contract void.
- Face-to-face identity mistakes usually make the contract voidable, not void.

7. What did *Royscot Trust v Rogerson* decide?
8. Can damages for fraudulent misrepresentation be reduced for contributory negligence?
9. Name the four bars to rescission.
10. What test for common mistake did *Great Peace Shipping* lay down?
11. Why was there no contract in *Raffles v Wichelhaus*?
12. Why is a face-to-face identity mistake usually only voidable?

**Answers:** 1. An untrue statement of fact made by one party to the other before or at the time of the contract, which induces the other to enter it. 2. It was an honest opinion by a seller with no special knowledge of sheep farming. 3. "The state of a man's mind is as much a fact as the state of his digestion." 4. Any two: half-truths; a change of circumstances; contracts of utmost good faith. 5. A false statement made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly, careless whether it is true or false. 6. The defendant, who must prove reasonable grounds for believing the statement. 7. Damages under s 2(1) are measured as if the misrepresentation were fraudulent. 8. No (*Standard Chartered v PNSC*). 9. Affirmation; lapse of time; restitution impossible; third-party rights. 10. The mistake must make performance essentially different from what was agreed, or impossible. 11. Each party meant a different ship called *Peerless*, and there was no way to decide which. 12. The seller is presumed to intend to deal with the person physically present, whatever name they give.
