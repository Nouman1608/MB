## Questions

**1.** Define the infant mortality rate. **[2]**

**2.** Outline why the natural increase rate may not reflect a country's actual population growth. **[2]**

**3.** Table 1 shows one year's data for a fictional country, Country W.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Population at start of year | 18.0 million |
| Live births | 252,000 |
| Deaths | 162,000 |
| Immigrants | 45,000 |
| Emigrants | 9,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the crude birth rate and crude death rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the natural increase rate. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the overall growth rate, including migration. **[2]**
**(d)** Use your answer to (c) to estimate the doubling time. **[1]**

**4.** Table 2 shows the age structure of two fictional countries (millions of people).

| Age group | Country K | Country L |
|---|---|---|
| 0–14 | 9.6 | 6.3 |
| 15–64 | 14.4 | 27.0 |
| 65 and over | 1.2 | 8.1 |

**(a)** Calculate the total dependency ratio for each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the old-age dependency ratio for Country L. **[1]**
**(c)** Contrast the challenges the two age structures create. **[4]**

**5.** Explain how a fall in infant mortality can lead to a fall in fertility. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **two** factors that may keep fertility high in a country. **[4]**

**7.** Distinguish between pro-natalist and anti-natalist policies, giving **one** measure for each. **[2]**

**8.** A fictional country records 5,000 deaths of children under one year old among 125,000 live births.

**(a)** Calculate the infant mortality rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the infant mortality rate is a useful indicator of development. **[2]**

**9.** Table 3 shows fictional data.

| Country | Population (millions) | Footprint per person (gha) |
|---|---|---|
| M | 30 | 4.5 |
| N | 150 | 1.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the total ecological footprint of each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate Country N's total footprint if its footprint per person rises by 40% with no change in population. **[1]**
**(c)** Discuss what these figures show about the link between population size and environmental impact. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why a population can keep growing for decades after its total fertility rate falls to replacement level. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate China's one-child policy. **[6]**

**12.** To what extent is reducing population growth the most effective way to reduce humanity's environmental impact? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The number of deaths of infants under one year of age [1] per 1,000 live births in a year [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Per 1,000 people" loses the second mark; the base is live births.

**2.** The NIR includes only births and deaths [1], so it ignores immigration and emigration, which can add to or subtract from growth considerably [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both flows the NIR leaves out.

**3. (a)** CBR = 252,000 ÷ 18,000,000 × 1,000 = **14 per 1,000** [1]; CDR = 162,000 ÷ 18,000,000 × 1,000 = **9 per 1,000** [1]
**(b)** (14 − 9) ÷ 10 = **0.5%** [1]
**(c)** Change = (252,000 − 162,000) + (45,000 − 9,000) = 90,000 + 36,000 = 126,000 [1]; 126,000 ÷ 18,000,000 × 100 = **0.7%** [1]
**(d)** 70 ÷ 0.7 = **100 years** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), migration adds 0.2 percentage points, two-fifths of the natural increase; projections from the NIR alone would underestimate growth.

**4. (a)** K: (9.6 + 1.2) ÷ 14.4 × 100 = **75.0** [1]; L: (6.3 + 8.1) ÷ 27.0 × 100 = **53.3** [1]
**(b)** 8.1 ÷ 27.0 × 100 = **30.0** [1]
**(c)** Country K's dependants are mostly young (youth ratio 66.7), so it needs schools and childcare now and jobs and housing as they grow up [1], and it faces strong population momentum, so its population and resource use will keep rising [1]. Country L's dependants include many older people (old-age ratio 30.0 against a youth ratio of 23.3), so it faces rising pension and healthcare costs [1] and a shrinking workforce, which may lead to labour shortages or reliance on immigration [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Contrast" needs a statement for each country tied to its data; a general account of ageing with no figures caps at 2.

**5.** When fewer infants die, parents do not need to have as many children to be confident that some survive [1], so after a time lag families choose to have fewer births and the fertility rate falls [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The time lag explains why death rates fall before birth rates in the demographic transition.

**6.** Any two developed factors, 2 marks each. Children's labour is valuable on family farms [1], so larger families bring economic benefit [1]. Cultural or religious values place importance on large families [1], so social pressure discourages smaller families [1]. Limited access to contraception [1] means families cannot easily control the number of births [1]. Lack of pensions [1] means parents rely on children for support in old age [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs a mechanism; a list of four factors with no explanation earns 2.

**7.** Pro-natalist policies aim to raise birth rates, for example cash payments for each child or paid parental leave [1]; anti-natalist policies aim to lower birth rates, for example free contraception or limits on family size [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs the aim and a matching measure.

**8. (a)** 5,000 ÷ 125,000 × 1,000 = **40 per 1,000 live births** [1]
**(b)** Infant deaths are strongly affected by access to clean water, sanitation, nutrition and healthcare [1], so a high or falling IMR shows quickly how well these basic needs are met in a population [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "It shows how developed a country is" restates the question; say what the rate depends on.

**9. (a)** M: 30 × 4.5 = **135 million gha** [1]; N: 150 × 1.0 = **150 million gha** [1]
**(b)** 1.0 × 1.4 = 1.4 gha; 150 × 1.4 = **210 million gha** [1]
**(c)** Country N has five times the population of Country M but only a slightly larger total footprint, because its consumption per person is much lower [1]. Impact depends on consumption and technology as well as numbers [1]. As incomes rise, footprints per person can grow quickly: a 40% rise in N would add 60 million gha with no population growth, so consumption can matter as much as population [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the calculated figures in (c); an answer that ignores the table cannot reach full marks.

**10.** A population with a young age structure has many people about to enter their child-bearing years [1]. Even if each woman has only about two children, the large number of parents produces many births [1], while deaths stay relatively low because few people are old, so births exceed deaths for decades until the age structure evens out [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The term population momentum earns credit only with the explanation.

**11.** Indicative points, up to 6.
- Aim: slow population growth and ease pressure on resources [1].
- Evidence of effect: fertility fell sharply, reducing growth [1].
- Some argue fertility was already falling with development, so the policy's own effect is debated [1].
- Unintended consequences: rapid ageing and a smaller future workforce [1].
- Sex ratio at birth skewed towards boys [1].
- Ethical concerns about state control over family size; reversal to two-child (2016) and three-child (2021) limits shows the government came to see low fertility as a problem; judgement weighs these [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs strengths, limitations and a judgement; a description of the rules earns little.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9. Real essays are marked holistically, so treat these as a guide.
- More people need more food, water, energy and land, so population size is a driver of impact [1].
- Neo-Malthusian views, as in *The Limits to Growth* (1972), see population growth as a central threat [1].
- Impact also depends on consumption per person and technology (IPAT), so a small, high-consuming population can have a larger impact [1].
- Footprint data show high-income countries with modest populations having large total footprints [1].
- Most future population growth is in lower-income countries where footprints per person are low, so cutting consumption in high-income countries may cut impact more [1].
- Technology, such as renewable energy and efficient farming, can reduce impact per person [1].
- Population growth is slow to change because of momentum, so it is a long-term lever [1].
- Policies that cut growth through education and healthcare also bring social benefits, while coercive policies raise ethical issues [1].
- Judgement: reducing growth helps, but it is not the most effective lever on its own; reducing consumption and changing technology act faster and target where impact per person is highest [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong essays use data or a model (IPAT, footprints) and end with a clear, justified judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total population as the base for the infant mortality rate.
- Ignoring migration when calculating a country's growth.
- Calculating dependency ratios without interpreting what they mean.
- Explaining fertility factors with no mechanism.
- Policy answers with outcomes but no aims, or no unintended consequences.
- Population essays that treat numbers as the only driver of impact.
