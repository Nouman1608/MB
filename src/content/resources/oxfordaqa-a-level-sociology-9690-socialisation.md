Section **3.1.2.2 Socialisation** in OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690) is
examined in Unit 2 Section A. The specification lists "Primary and secondary socialisation;
re-socialisation; inadequate socialisation", the agencies ("families; schools; peers; media;
religions; workplace"), the "processes/methods used by these agencies", and "functionalist;
Marxist; feminist; social action/interactionist" perspectives. Mead is the named sociologist most
closely tied to it. The unit overview is in the
[Exploring Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-exploring-sociology/).

Socialisation is the lifelong process by which people learn the culture of their society (3.1.2.1)
and come to see themselves as particular kinds of person (3.1.2.3). The key exam skill is
connecting **an agency** to **a method** and then to **a perspective's interpretation**.

## Four types of socialisation

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Early learning of basic norms, language and values, mainly in the family | Learning to speak, eat with others and recognise family roles |
| **Secondary** | Later learning from agencies beyond the family | Learning school rules, workplace codes or peer norms |
| **Re-socialisation** | Learning new norms that replace earlier ones | Military training, imprisonment, entering a religious order, migrating to a very different society |
| **Inadequate socialisation** | Failure to learn the norms needed to take part in society | Children kept in extreme isolation, or, in some accounts, children whose families fail to pass on mainstream norms |

**Inadequate socialisation** is shown most dramatically by cases of extreme isolation. The case
known as Genie, a girl found in California in 1970 after years of severe isolation and neglect,
is often cited: despite later care she never fully acquired language. Such cases suggest that much
of what seems "human nature" is learned. Sociologists also use the idea more controversially: some
New Right writers, such as Charles Murray, linked crime and welfare dependency to an "underclass"
whose children were said to be inadequately socialised. Critics reply that this blames poor
families for structural problems.

## Agencies and their processes

| Agency | Processes and methods | Notes for analysis |
|---|---|---|
| **Families** | Imitation; role modelling; positive and negative sanctions; **canalisation** (channelling children towards gender-typed toys and activities) and **manipulation** (encouraging or discouraging behaviour by sex), as described by Ann Oakley (1972) | The most powerful agency in early childhood; content varies by class, culture and religion |
| **Schools** | Formal curriculum; **hidden curriculum** (lessons learned from how schools are organised, such as punctuality and obedience); rewards and punishments; streaming | Transmits both knowledge and values; can reinforce or challenge home values |
| **Peers** | Approval and acceptance; teasing and exclusion; shared styles and slang | Strong in adolescence; can support or oppose adult norms |
| **Media** | Representations and role models; advertising; algorithms that select content; online likes and comments | Influence debated: from direct effects to active audiences |
| **Religions** | Teachings and sacred texts; rituals and festivals; community expectations; religious schooling | Can give a strong collective identity and moral framework |
| **Workplace** | Induction and training; codes of conduct; organisational culture; **anticipatory socialisation** (preparing for a role before entering it) | Continues socialisation into adult life |

Two distinctions help in **explain** questions:

- **Formal vs informal methods**: a school's written rules are formal; a peer group's mockery is
  informal.
- **Deliberate vs unintended**: a parent teaching manners is deliberate; a child copying a parent's
  attitudes to money is unintended.

## Perspectives on socialisation

### Functionalist

Functionalists see socialisation as the transmission of **shared values**, creating order and
consensus. Émile Durkheim saw education as passing on the society's collective values and
described the school as a society in miniature. Talcott Parsons argued families are "factories"
producing personalities committed to society's values, and that schools move children from the
**particularistic** standards of family life (treated as a unique individual) to the
**universalistic** standards of wider society (judged by the same rules as everyone).

*Criticism*: assumes values are shared by all and that people absorb them passively; Dennis Wrong
(1961) called this an "oversocialised conception of man".

### Marxist

Marxists see socialisation as passing on **ruling-class ideology**, so that inequality appears
natural and workers accept exploitation. Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis (*Schooling in
Capitalist America*, 1976) argued for a **correspondence principle**: the hidden curriculum of
schools mirrors the workplace, teaching obedience, acceptance of hierarchy and motivation by
external rewards such as grades, as wages are later. Louis Althusser saw education as the key
ideological state apparatus. Media ownership by wealthy elites is used to argue that mass media
also spread dominant ideas.

*Criticism*: deterministic; Paul Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977) showed working-class "lads"
resisting school values, though their resistance led them into manual work anyway.

### Feminist

Feminists see socialisation as reproducing **gender roles and patriarchy**. Oakley's canalisation
and manipulation describe how families create gendered behaviour. Sue Sharpe's study of girls'
priorities (*Just Like a Girl*, first published 1976) found girls in the 1970s ranked love,
marriage and children highly, while those she interviewed in the 1990s gave careers more
importance, showing that gender socialisation changes. Feminists also study gendered media
representations and school subject choices.

*Criticism*: may underplay change and the agency of girls and women; liberal, radical and
intersectional feminists disagree about how deep the problem goes.

### Social action / interactionist

Interactionists see socialisation as **active**. George Herbert Mead (*Mind, Self and Society*,
1934) argued the self develops through interaction: children first imitate, then in **play** take
the role of particular others, then in **games** learn the expectations of many people at once,
the **generalised other**. Charles Horton Cooley's **looking-glass self** (1902) describes how we
see ourselves through how we imagine others see us. Socialisation is therefore negotiated, and
individuals can reinterpret or resist what agencies teach.

*Criticism*: can underplay structural constraints of class, gender and ethnicity on how far
individuals can reinterpret norms.

## Worked example: annotated 8-mark answer

*Explain two ways in which schools act as agencies of secondary socialisation.*

> **The hidden curriculum.** Schools teach norms through how they are organised, such as bells,
> uniforms and respect for teachers' authority (**method**). Bowles and Gintis argued this hidden
> curriculum prepares pupils to accept hierarchy at work, so schools socialise future workers
> (**perspective and consequence**).
>
> **Universalistic standards.** In school, pupils are judged by the same rules and exams as
> everyone else, unlike at home (**method**). Parsons argued this bridges family and wider society,
> teaching achievement and fairness, which functionalists see as essential for a meritocratic
> society (**perspective and consequence**).

## Common mistakes

- Confusing re-socialisation with secondary socialisation.
- Listing agencies without the **processes/methods** they use, which the specification names.
- Writing only about Western societies: religion and extended kin may be stronger agencies
  elsewhere.
- Presenting perspectives in sequence without comparing them.
- Treating media effects as simple and direct.

## Exam technique

For 4-mark questions, name a method and say what is learned. For 8-mark questions, add a named
sociologist or perspective and a consequence. In 20-mark questions such as "evaluate the view that
the family is the most important agency of socialisation", weigh agencies against each other
across the life course and apply to a named society.
