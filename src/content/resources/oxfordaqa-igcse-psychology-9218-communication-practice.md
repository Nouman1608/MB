## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following statements matches Piaget's view of language and thought?
A Language determines what we are able to think
B Thought develops first and language reflects it
C Language and thought develop completely separately
D Words for colours change how colours are seen **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between the strong and the weak versions of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. **[2]**

**3.** For **each** of the following, identify the function of animal communication it shows:
**(a)** a male bird singing loudly from the edge of a hedge to keep other males away
**(b)** a meerkat giving a sharp call when an eagle flies overhead **[2]**

**4.** Define verbal communication **and** non-verbal communication. **[2]**

**5.** Outline what is meant by postural echo. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **one** gender difference and **one** status difference in personal space. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Two-year-old Zara looks for her ball under a blanket after watching her father hide it, although she does not yet know the word "hidden". Explain how this supports Piaget's theory of the relationship between language and thought. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how research on colour recognition in a Native American culture supports the weak version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate Von Frisch's bee study as evidence about animal communication. **[4]**

**10.** Using an example, explain why the ability to discuss future events shows that human communication differs from animal communication. **[3]**

**11.** During a group discussion, Omar notices that Leah has stopped speaking and is now looking directly at him. Explain what Leah's eye contact is likely to signal and why. **[2]**

**12.** Describe Yuki's study of emoticons **and** explain what it suggests about non-verbal behaviour. **[4]**

**13.** Describe **and** evaluate Darwin's evolutionary theory of non-verbal communication. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1].

**2.** The strong version says language **determines** thought, so people cannot think about something they have no words for [1]. The weak version claims only that language **influences** thought: a language with a word for a distinction makes that distinction quicker to spot and easier to recall [1].

**3.** **(a)** Territory [1]. **(b)** Survival [1].

**4.** Verbal communication is communicating using **spoken or written words** [1]. Non-verbal communication is communicating **without words**, for example through eye contact, posture, touch or personal space [1].

**5.** Postural echo is **mirroring the posture** of the person you are interacting with [1]; it usually signals **rapport** or agreement between them [1].

**6.** Gender: pairs of **women typically stand closer** together than pairs of men [1]. Status: people tend to keep **more distance from someone of higher status** [1].

**7.** Piaget argued that **language depends on thought** — concepts develop first [1]. Zara shows **object permanence**: she understands the ball still exists when hidden [1]. She has this concept **before she has the word** for it, so her thinking has developed ahead of her language, as Piaget predicts [1].

**8.** The weak version says language **influences** memory and perception rather than determining them [1]. Speakers of the Zuni language, which uses **one term for yellow and orange** [1], made **more errors than English speakers** when asked to pick out yellow and orange shades they had seen earlier [1]. They could still see the colours, but having no separate words made the difference **harder to remember**, which fits influence rather than determination [1].

**9.** Two strengths and two limitations, 1 mark each: a strength is that Von Frisch used **controlled field experiments**, moving food sources to show how the dances changed, so the findings are well supported [1]; observing bees in a **natural setting** gives high realism [1]. A limitation is that it shows only how **one species** communicates, so it may not generalise to other animals [1]; and it shows complex signalling about **one immediate need** (food), not language with unlimited meanings [1].

**10.** Humans can talk about things that **have not happened yet** [1], for example two friends arranging where to meet next Saturday [1]. Animal signals are tied to the **here and now**, such as a call about a predator that is present at that moment, so they cannot be used to plan ahead [1].

**11.** Leah is likely signalling that **she has finished speaking and it is Omar's turn** [1], because eye contact **regulates the flow of conversation**: speakers tend to look back at a listener as they finish to hand over the turn [1].

**12.** Participants from **Japan and the USA** judged how happy or sad a series of emoticons and edited face images looked, where the **eye region and the mouth region** were altered independently [1]. Ratings by **Japanese** participants were influenced more by the **eyes** [1], whereas ratings by **American** participants were influenced more by the **mouth** [1]. This suggests that how people interpret facial expressions is **learned through culture**, rather than being entirely innate [1].

**13.** *Description (up to 3):* Darwin proposed that non-verbal communication **evolved** because it was **adaptive** [1]; expressions such as baring the teeth or showing fear helped our ancestors survive and reproduce, so they were **passed on genetically** [1]. If this is true, basic expressions should be **universal** and appear without learning [1].
*Evaluation (up to 3):* a strength is that **neonates** show basic expressions before they could have learned them, which supports an innate basis [1]; children **born blind** still smile, frown and cry in recognisable ways despite never having seen anyone else do so [1]. A limitation is that **Yuki's study** shows that the interpretation of expressions differs between cultures, so learning must also play a part [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reversing Piaget and the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis in a scenario question.
- Describing the Zuni research without saying which version of Sapir-Whorf it supports.
- Naming a function of animal communication without matching it to the example given.
- Describing Yuki's findings without saying what they show about learning.
- Giving only supporting evidence for Darwin when the question asks for evaluation.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much detail do I need on Von Frisch?**
Know what the round and waggle dances communicate, how direction and distance are shown, and at least one strength and one limitation of the research.

**Which studies show non-verbal behaviour is innate?**
The specification names neonates and the sensory deprived as evidence for innateness, and Yuki's emoticon study as evidence that non-verbal behaviour is learned.

**What do examiners mean by "explain what this shows"?**
State the finding, then link it to the theory or debate it supports, such as innate versus learned.
