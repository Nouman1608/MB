This study guide teaches **Language, context, culture and power** for IB Diploma Programme Language
A: Language and Literature, at SL and HL. It follows the current IB subject brief (first assessments
for SL and HL 2021). This site maps the unit to **Time and space**, one of the three syllabus components
in the brief, which recommends 50 teaching hours for that component at SL and 80 at HL. The course
has no numbered subtopics, so this guide works from the brief's aims and assessment objectives.

Use it with the [revision
notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-language-context-culture-and-power-revision-notes/)
and the [practice
questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-language-context-culture-and-power-practice/).
For the whole course, see the [Language and Literature course
hub](/boards/ib/ib-dp/language-a-language-and-literature/) and the [printable
checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/language-a-language-and-literature/). The [Time and space
guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-literature-time-and-space/) introduces production and
reception context. This guide goes further: it asks **whose** view a text carries, **who** gains or
loses from it, and **how** that changes when the text travels to new readers.

## What the brief says this is for

The brief does not list detailed content for each component, but its wording points here. It
describes language as "reflecting experience and shaping the world", and says you will explore how
positions "are shaped by cultural belief systems". Assessment objective 1 asks you to know "contexts
in which texts are written and/or received". So a text does not just report the world: it offers **a
version** of it, from a position, to an audience.

## 1. Two contexts, many questions

Every text has a **context of production** (who made it, when, where, for whom, through which
institution) and a **context of reception** (who reads it now, where, and what they already
believe). This unit adds two power questions to both: *who benefits from this version of events?*
and *who is missing from it?*

## 2. Representation: every text chooses

**Representation** is the way a text presents people, groups, places or events. It is built from
choices: **selection and omission** (what is left in or out), **naming** ("protesters", "residents",
"a mob"), **framing** (a threat, a crisis, a human-interest story), **foregrounding** (what comes
first) and **agency** (who acts, and who only has things done to them).

A **stereotype** is a fixed, simplified representation of a group, repeated until it seems natural.
**Othering** presents a group as different from, and less than, an assumed "normal" reader.

### Worked example: one event, two versions

*Original texts written for Marlbridge; the port, paper and people are invented.*

> **Text 1.** DOCK STRIKE CHAOS: SHELVES EMPTY AS MILITANTS SHUT PORT
> Militant union bosses brought Portwell harbour to a halt yesterday, leaving shoppers facing empty
> shelves. Business leaders warned the walkout could cost the city millions.

> **Text 2.** Port workers walk out after ten years without a real pay rise
> More than 600 staff at Portwell harbour stopped work yesterday. "We keep this city fed," said crane
> driver Lena Morris, who has worked at the port for 18 years.

**Step 1 -- naming.** Text 1 calls the strikers "MILITANTS" and "Militant union bosses". Both labels suggest
aggression and a small group in control. Text 2 says "port workers", "staff" and gives one worker a
full name and job.

**Step 2 -- agency and cause.** In Text 1 the strikers act on others: they shut the port and leave
shoppers "facing empty shelves". No reason for the strike is given. Text 2 puts the cause in the
headline: "ten years without a real pay rise".

**Step 3 -- whose voice.** Text 1 quotes "business leaders", unnamed. Text 2 quotes a worker, whose
words "We keep this city fed" turn the strikers into the people the city depends on.

**Step 4 -- the reader's position.** Text 1 casts the reader as a harmed shopper; Text 2 as an ally
of a named worker.

**Step 5 -- judge both.** Text 2 leaves out the cost to the public, just as Text 1 leaves out the
cause. Show that each text selects; do not call one "biased" and the other "true".

## 3. Bias, stance and perspective

These words are not the same, and using them precisely earns credit.

- **Perspective** -- the position a text is written from (a worker, an employer, a tourist, a child).
  Every text has one.
- **Stance** -- the writer's attitude to the subject, shown in the language: approving, hostile,
  sceptical.
- **Bias** -- a one-sided treatment that favours a view while presenting itself as fair. Calling a
  text "biased" is a claim you must prove with evidence.

Features that reveal stance: **loaded words** ("chaos", "militants"); **modality** ("must" versus
"may"); **hedging and boosting** ("reportedly" versus "clearly"); the **agentless passive**
("Mistakes were made" hides who made them); **nominalisation** ("the closure" hides who closed
what); and **inclusive and exclusive pronouns** ("we" versus "they").

## 4. Power: who speaks, who is silent

Power in language is control over meaning. Ask who has the right to speak, define and name; who
gives instructions (imperatives, "must") and who receives them; whose words are quoted and whose are
left out; whether jargon or formal register excludes some readers; and whether **euphemism** softens
something harsh ("letting staff go").

### Worked example: literacy as power

Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave, Written by
Himself* (1845), Chapter VI. Public domain. Douglass has just described how his enslaver, Mr Auld,
stopped his wife teaching the boy to read, saying that reading "would forever unfit him to be a
slave". (…) marks cuts.

> These words sank deep into my heart, stirred up sentiments within that lay slumbering, and called
> into existence an entirely new train of thought. (…) I now understood what had been to me a most
> perplexing difficulty—to wit, the white man's power to enslave the black man. It was a grand
> achievement, and I prized it highly. From that moment, I understood the pathway from slavery to
> freedom. (…) What he most dreaded, that I most desired. What he most loved, that I most hated.

**Who holds power at the start?** Auld. His words decide what the boy may learn: keeping people
from language is one way power is kept.

**How does Douglass take it back?** He turns Auld's words into his own lesson. "These words sank
deep into my heart" makes the enslaver's speech a source of instruction he never intended.

**The key phrase.** Douglass names power directly: "the white man's power to enslave the black man".
The formal, almost legal "to wit" shows the writer in full command of an educated register -- the
very skill Auld tried to deny him.

**Structure as reversal.** "What he most dreaded, that I most desired. What he most loved, that I
most hated." The parallel sentences set master and boy against each other. Each of Auld's fears
becomes one of Douglass's goals.

**Context of production.** The subtitle "Written by Himself" and prefaces by the white abolitionists
William Lloyd Garrison and Wendell Phillips answered readers who doubted a formerly enslaved man
could write such a book. Writing it was itself taking power.

**Context of reception.** The book was written to persuade 1840s readers to oppose slavery. A reader
now meets it as a historical and literary work, often in a classroom, where its link between
literacy and power still applies.

## 5. Gender and identity

Language can treat people unequally without saying anything openly hostile. Look for:

- **Generic masculine** -- "Every doctor should update his records" assumes the doctor is a man.
- **Marked terms** -- "female engineer", "male nurse": the extra word marks someone as unusual for
  the role.
- **Asymmetric description** -- women described by appearance, age or family, men by action or skill.
- **Naming and titles** -- first name for one person and surname for another; "girls" for adult women.

### Worked example: two match reports

*Original texts written for Marlbridge; the athletes are invented.*

> **Report A.** Daniel Osei powered to victory in the 400 metres last night, his tactical brilliance
> leaving the field behind.
>
> **Report B.** Blonde mother-of-two Hannah Price, 29, was all smiles after her 400 metres win last
> night, which she dedicated to her husband.

Report A gives Osei an action verb ("powered") and credits his mind ("tactical brilliance"). Report
B describes Price by hair, age and family before her result, and turns her win into an emotion ("all
smiles") and a gift to someone else. Skill for one athlete; feeling for the other.

**Identity** is also built through dialect, a home language, **code-switching** (moving between
languages or varieties) and chosen names. Ask whether a group **names itself** or **is named by
others**.

## 6. Reception across time and place

A text's meaning is not fixed at the moment it is made. The cultural theorist Stuart Hall described
three ways an audience can read a media text:

- **Dominant (preferred)** -- the reader accepts the intended meaning.
- **Negotiated** -- the reader accepts some of it and resists some.
- **Oppositional** -- the reader rejects the intended meaning.

### Worked example: the same text, two readers

*Original text written for Marlbridge; the island is invented.*

> Welcome to Tarsa, the island time forgot. Life here moves at the pace of the tides. Our smiling
> islanders still fish the way their grandfathers did, and every evening the village comes alive with
> simple songs. Tarsa is waiting to be discovered.

**Intended reader: a tourist.** The preferred reading is escape from modern life, and "waiting to be
discovered" flatters the visitor as an explorer.

**Another reader: a resident of Tarsa.** An oppositional reading is likely. "Time forgot" and "still"
present the islanders as behind the times. "Our smiling islanders" makes them part of the product,
owned by the brochure's "our". "Waiting to be discovered" erases the people already living there.
"Simple songs" turns their culture into background for visitors.

Same words; the meaning changes with the reader's place, culture and stake in the text.

## How this unit is assessed

The brief has no separate paper for this unit, so it feeds every component. **Paper 1** (SL 1.25
hours, HL 2.25 hours; 35% at both levels) is a guided analysis of unseen non-literary passages "from
different text types", where news, adverts and brochures often raise representation and power.
**Paper 2** (1.75 hours; 35% SL, 25% HL) is a comparative essay on two literary works. The
**individual oral** (30% SL, 20% HL) is on how one literary work and one non-literary body of work
"have approached a common global issue". The **HL essay** (HL only, 20%) is 1,200--1,500 words. Your
teacher will share the assessment criteria for each component.

## Common errors

- **Calling a text "biased" and stopping.** Show the words that create the bias and their effect.
- **Treating one text as neutral.** Every text selects. Compare what each includes and omits.
- **Listing context as background.** Link context to a specific word or choice in the text.
- **Missing agency.** Ask who does what to whom, and whether any actor is hidden.
- **Assuming one "real" meaning.** Say whose reading you describe: the intended audience's, a later
  reader's, or an outsider's.
