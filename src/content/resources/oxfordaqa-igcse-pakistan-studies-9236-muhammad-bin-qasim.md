and its religious impact". This guide covers that content point in depth. It belongs to the optional
topic Islam in the subcontinent (c700-c1831), examined in Paper 1 Section C. For the whole optional
topic, from Sufism to Syed Ahmad Barelvi, use the

## The Umayyad Caliphate around 700

By the early eighth century the **Umayyad Caliphate**, ruled from Damascus, stretched from Spain in
the west to Central Asia in the east. Under **Caliph al-Walid I** (705-715) the empire expanded on
several fronts at once. The eastern provinces were governed by **Hajjaj bin Yusuf**, the powerful
governor of Iraq, who organised campaigns into Central Asia and towards India.

Arabs already knew the region. Arab merchants traded along the coasts of Makran and Sindh, and
Makran, to the west of Sindh, had come under Muslim control earlier. Sindh itself was ruled by
**Raja Dahir**, whose capital was at Alor (Aror) on the Indus. Its population was largely Hindu and
Buddhist, and its port of **Debal**, near the mouth of the Indus, was an important trading harbour.

## Why the Umayyads invaded Sindh

Historians and the traditional accounts give several connected reasons.

1. **The pirates of Debal.** Ships carrying gifts and the families of Muslim merchants from Sri
   Lanka (Ceylon) were seized by pirates near Debal. Hajjaj demanded their release; Dahir replied
   that he had no control over the pirates. Hajjaj treated this as an insult and a cause for war.
2. **Earlier failures.** Hajjaj had sent smaller expeditions that were defeated, and their commanders
   killed. Umayyad prestige required a decisive response.
3. **Shelter for rebels.** Dahir was said to have given refuge to Arab rebels who had fled Umayyad
   rule.
4. **Trade and wealth.** Control of Sindh would secure the sea route to India and the riches of the
   lower Indus.
5. **Expansion of Islam and the empire.** Sindh fitted the wider pattern of Umayyad expansion in
   these years.

In an exam answer, the pirate incident is the immediate or trigger cause; the others are longer-term
causes. Showing the difference is a mark of good analysis.

## The campaign, 711-713

Hajjaj chose **Muhammad bin Qasim**, a young relative from the Thaqif tribe, traditionally said to
have been about seventeen, and gave him a well-equipped army. Soldiers and horses marched through
Makran while heavy siege equipment came by sea.

- **Debal (711-712).** After a siege, the army broke into the port. Catapults were used against the
  city, and the traditional account says the fall of a great flag on the temple broke the defenders'
  morale. A mosque was built and Arab settlers were placed in the town.
- **Moving up the Indus.** Some towns, such as Nerun, surrendered peacefully on terms; Sehwan was
  taken; the army then crossed the Indus.
- **The defeat of Dahir (712).** In a major battle near Raor, Dahir was killed. His army had
  elephants and many soldiers, but it could not resist the Arab cavalry.
- **Brahmanabad, Alor and Multan.** Brahmanabad and the capital Alor fell, and in 713 Multan in
  southern Punjab was captured, with great wealth taken from its temple treasury.

By 713 Sindh and southern Punjab were under Umayyad rule.

## How Muhammad bin Qasim governed

His administration explains why historians treat him as more than a conqueror.

- **Protected status (dhimmi).** Hindus and Buddhists were treated as protected people. They could
  keep their religion and places of worship in return for paying **jizya**, a tax graded by wealth,
  from which some groups were exempt.
- **Use of local officials.** Brahmins and existing officials were kept in charge of revenue
  collection and local administration, which made the change of rulers less disruptive.
- **Temples.** In the settlement at Brahmanabad, local people were allowed to repair their temples
  and continue their worship.
- **Arab garrisons and mosques.** Soldiers and settlers were placed in key towns, and mosques were
  built, introducing Islamic worship and law alongside existing communities.

This policy combined the authority of the new rulers with tolerance for local religion, a pattern
that later Muslim rulers in the subcontinent often followed.

## Recall and death

In 714 Hajjaj died, and in 715 Caliph al-Walid died. The new Caliph, **Sulaiman**, was hostile to
Hajjaj and his family, and he recalled Muhammad bin Qasim. The young commander was imprisoned in Iraq
and died there in 715. His campaigns ended with the conquests already made.

## Religious impact

The specification asks specifically about religious impact.

- **The first Muslim-ruled region of the subcontinent.** Sindh became known as *Bab-ul-Islam*, the
  gateway of Islam, because Islam was first established as a ruling faith there.
- **Gradual conversion.** Some local people converted, attracted by trade, office or the faith itself,
  but many remained Hindu or Buddhist for generations. The conquest did not bring mass conversion.
- **Links to the Muslim world.** Sindh was joined to the trade, scholarship and law of the caliphate.
  Arab scholars and merchants settled, and later Indian works on mathematics and astronomy reached
  Baghdad by these routes.
- **A model of tolerance.** The dhimmi settlement offered a framework for Muslim rule over a mainly
  non-Muslim population.

**Limits.** The conquest stopped at Sindh and Multan, and Arab rule there later broke into smaller
states. The wider spread of Islam in the subcontinent came later, through the Ghaznavids, the Delhi
Sultanate and, above all, the work of Sufi saints.

## Why the conquest stopped at Multan

Several reasons explain why Umayyad rule did not spread further into the subcontinent at this time.

- **Politics in the caliphate.** The deaths of Hajjaj and al-Walid, and the recall of Muhammad bin
  Qasim, removed the leaders who had driven the campaign.
- **Distance and supply.** Sindh lay at the far eastern edge of an empire ruled from Damascus, and
  armies depended on long routes through Makran or by sea.
- **Stronger kingdoms beyond.** To the east and north lay powerful Hindu kingdoms with large armies,
  which later Arab governors were unable to defeat.
- **Competing frontiers.** The caliphate was also fighting in Central Asia, North Africa and Spain,
  so resources were spread thinly.

This explains the "limits" side of any judgement about the conquest's importance: Islam gained a
secure base in the Indus valley, but not yet a path into the rest of the subcontinent.

## The source problem

Much of the detail comes from the *Chachnama*, a Persian history written in the early thirteenth
century from older Arabic material, five hundred years after the events. Details such as the flag at
Debal may be legend. Saying "according to traditional accounts" is accurate and shows awareness.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain why Muhammad bin Qasim was able to conquer Sindh. **[4]**

```
Point 1   strong, well-supplied army sent by Hajjaj
Explain   cavalry marched through Makran while siege engines came by
          sea, so strongly defended towns such as Debal could be taken

Point 2   tolerant treatment of those who surrendered
Explain   towns such as Nerun submitted on terms, and local officials
          kept their posts, so resistance weakened as he advanced
```

## Common mistakes

- **Saying he converted Sindh to Islam.** Conversion was gradual and partial.
- **Confusing Hajjaj (governor) with al-Walid (Caliph).**
- **Treating the pirate incident as the only cause.**
- **Forgetting the recall.** His career ended because of politics in the caliphate, not defeat.
