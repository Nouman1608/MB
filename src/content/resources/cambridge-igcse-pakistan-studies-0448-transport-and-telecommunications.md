**Transport and telecommunications is Section 7 of Paper 2, The environment of Pakistan, in
Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies 0448.** It has three parts: (a) internal transport, (b)
international transport and (c) telecommunications. Questions are built around maps, photographs and
data, so you must be able to describe a pattern on a map, explain what helps or hinders transport,
and evaluate new schemes. This guide goes further than the combined
[Trade, Transport and Telecommunications study guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-pakistan-studies-trade-transport-and-telecommunications/).
Condense it with the [Transport and telecommunications revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-pakistan-studies-0448-transport-and-telecommunications-revision-notes/)
and test yourself with the [Transport and telecommunications practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-pakistan-studies-0448-transport-and-telecommunications-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE PAKISTAN STUDIES (0448), 2026 — PAPER 2, SECTION 7: TRANSPORT AND
TELECOMMUNICATIONS**

- **7(a) Internal transport:** interpret maps to describe regional variations in the density and
  pattern of road, rail and air networks; explain the factors that help and hinder their location,
  maintenance and development; describe recent improvements and consider the feasibility of new
  developments; compare road, rail and air for goods and people; evaluate new transport schemes,
  including motorway and airport development.
- **7(b) International transport:** identify on a map the roads (with the passes they use) and
  railways that cross the international boundary; identify the ports of Keamari, Qasim and Gwadar
  and the cities with international airports; explain the factors affecting the location and
  development of cross-border routes, seaports, dry ports and airports; explain what a dry port is,
  name one and explain why they have been developed.
- **7(c) Telecommunications:** explain the importance of radio, television, phones, fax machines,
  emails and the internet in education, industry, services and trade; understand the problems of
  providing them in some parts of Pakistan; evaluate their role in development.

## Describing the pattern of the networks

The syllabus glossary says "describe the pattern" means referring to arrangements such as linear or
nucleated, and locating them in relation to provinces, rivers and other features.

- **Densest:** the Indus Plain of Punjab and northern Sindh, where roads and railways form a close
  network linking Lahore, Faisalabad, Multan, Gujranwala and Rawalpindi.
- **Linear:** the main routes run roughly north-east to south-west along the Indus valley, from
  Peshawar through Lahore and Multan to Hyderabad and Karachi, following the flat land and the
  rivers.
- **Sparse:** Balochistan, the deserts of Thar and Cholistan, and the northern mountains, where a few
  routes follow valleys and passes.
- **Air:** airports are scattered and serve the large cities plus remote northern towns such as
  Gilgit, Skardu and Chitral, where air travel saves long road journeys.

**Key routes to know:** the N-5 National Highway (Karachi to Peshawar and the Afghan border at
Torkham); the N-55 Indus Highway on the west bank of the Indus; the N-25 from Karachi through Quetta
to Chaman; the N-35 Karakoram Highway to China; motorways such as the M-2 (Lahore to Islamabad) and
the M-1 (Islamabad to Peshawar); the main railway line from Karachi to Peshawar via Lahore, and the
line to Quetta through the Bolan Pass.

## What helps and hinders transport?

| Factor | Helps | Hinders |
|---|---|---|
| Relief | Flat Indus Plain: cheap to build | Steep mountains: costly cuttings, tunnels, bridges |
| Rivers | Valleys give natural routes | Wide rivers need long bridges; floods wash out roads and track |
| Climate | Dry weather in much of the south | Monsoon floods; snow and avalanches in the north; heat damages roads |
| Ground | Firm alluvial soils | Landslides in the mountains; shifting sand in deserts |
| Population and economy | Many people, farms and industry create demand | Sparse population means low traffic and little revenue |
| Finance | Loans and foreign investment | High building and maintenance costs; debt |
| Security | Stable areas attract investment | Unrest raises costs and deters building |

Maintenance is part of the syllabus too: overloaded lorries break road surfaces, floods destroy
embankments, and old track and locomotives slow the railways.

## Recent improvements and feasibility

- **Motorways:** the M-2 Lahore–Islamabad motorway, opened in the 1990s, was followed by others
  such as the M-1, M-4 and M-9, and by new links built under the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor
  (CPEC).
- **Karakoram Highway:** upgraded and partly realigned; tunnels were built around the lake formed
  by the Attabad landslide of 2010.
- **Railways:** plans to upgrade the main Karachi–Peshawar line for faster, heavier trains.
- **Airports:** the new Islamabad International Airport opened in 2018.
- **Urban transport:** bus rapid transit in Lahore and Peshawar, and Lahore's Orange Line metro.

**Feasibility** means asking whether a scheme is practical and worth it: the cost and who pays;
whether there is enough traffic; the physical difficulty of the land; the effect on the environment
and on people who lose land; and whether it can be maintained.

## Road, rail or air? Choosing for a purpose

| Task | Best choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh mangoes from Multan to Lahore market | Road | Door-to-door, quick, flexible timing for perishable goods |
| Bulk coal or cement over a long distance | Rail | Cheaper per tonne for heavy, bulky loads |
| Business trip from Karachi to Islamabad | Air | Saves a long journey; time matters more than cost |
| Supplies to a mountain village | Road (jeep) | Only option on narrow tracks |
| Passengers to Skardu | Air, weather permitting | Avoids a long, difficult road journey |

## Evaluating motorway and airport development

**Benefits:** faster, safer journeys; lower transport costs for business; industry attracted to
places along the route; jobs in building and services; better links to ports.

**Costs and problems:** very high construction costs and debt; farmland taken; tolls too expensive
for some users; benefits concentrated in already-developed areas; air and noise pollution; money
not spent on rural roads or railways.

A strong answer reaches a judgement, for example: motorways have boosted trade between the large
cities, but improving the railways would move bulky goods more cheaply and with less pollution.

## International transport

**Cross-border roads and passes**
- **China:** the Karakoram Highway over the **Khunjerab Pass**, closed by snow for part of the
  winter.
- **Afghanistan:** through the **Khyber Pass** at Torkham, from Peshawar towards Jalalabad and Kabul;
  and through Chaman, reached from Quetta over the **Khojak Pass**, towards Kandahar.
- **Iran:** from Quetta to **Taftan**.
- **India:** at **Wagah**, near Lahore.

**Cross-border railways:** Quetta to Taftan and on into Iran; the line to India at Wagah, and the
Khokhrapar route in Sindh. Passenger services to India have been suspended since 2019, so check
the current position in your course materials.

**Seaports**
- **Keamari (Karachi Port):** the oldest and busiest, in a sheltered natural harbour, but congested
  and hemmed in by the city.
- **Port Qasim:** east of Karachi, with space for bulk cargo such as oil, coal and steel-making
  materials.
- **Gwadar:** a deep-water port in Balochistan, developed with Chinese investment under CPEC, close
  to the Gulf shipping routes but far from Pakistan's main industrial areas.

**Factors affecting the location of ports:** deep, sheltered water; flat land for docks and storage;
road and rail links to the hinterland; demand from industry; investment; security.

**Dry ports:** inland terminals where goods are cleared through customs and packed into sealed
containers, which then travel by rail or road to and from the seaport. Examples include Lahore,
Faisalabad, Sialkot, Multan and Peshawar, and Sust on the Karakoram Highway for trade with China.
They have been developed to reduce congestion and delays at Karachi, let exporters complete
paperwork near their factories, reduce theft and damage, and cut costs for inland industry.

**International airports** include Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad, Peshawar, Quetta, Multan, Faisalabad
and Sialkot. Airports need large areas of flat land, a site clear of high buildings and hills, good
weather, road links and enough demand.

## Telecommunications

| Field | How telecommunications help |
|---|---|
| Education | Radio and television lessons and online courses reach distant students, for example through the Allama Iqbal Open University and the Virtual University |
| Industry | Orders, designs and payments sent instantly; contact with buyers abroad |
| Services | Mobile banking, call centres, software and freelancing, online health advice |
| Trade | Email and the internet link exporters to buyers; farmers check market prices by phone |

**Problems of providing them:** mountains block signals and make masts and cables costly; scattered
villages mean few customers per mast; power cuts; low incomes and literacy in some rural areas;
damage from floods; insecurity in some regions.

**Evaluating their role in development:** they lower costs, create jobs in information technology
and spread education and information; but the gap between connected cities and poorly served rural
areas can widen inequality, and they cannot replace roads for moving goods.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain why there are few roads and railways in northern Pakistan. **[4]**

**Model answer:** The relief is mountainous, with steep slopes and deep gorges, so building needs
costly cuttings, tunnels and bridges [1]. Landslides, snow and avalanches block routes and make
maintenance expensive [1]. The population is small and scattered, so there is little traffic to pay
for the cost [1]. Most routes can only follow narrow river valleys, such as the Karakoram Highway
along the Indus and Hunza valleys [1].

## Common mistakes

- Describing a map pattern without naming provinces, rivers or cities.
- Calling the Bolan Pass an international crossing: it links Sibi and Quetta inside Pakistan.
- Defining a dry port as "a port with no water" without explaining customs clearance.
- One-sided evaluations of motorways.
- Listing types of telecommunications without saying how each helps a named field.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which passes must I know?**
Khunjerab (China), Khyber (Afghanistan at Torkham) and Khojak (on the route to Chaman); also know
that the Bolan Pass carries the road and railway to Quetta.

**Do I need the names of motorways?**
Know two or three, such as the M-2 Lahore–Islamabad, and what they connect.
