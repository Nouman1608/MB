## A2.2.1 Cells as the basic unit of life

Cell theory has three parts. All living organisms are made of one or more cells. The cell is the smallest unit that carries out the functions of life. New cells come only from existing cells.

The nature of science point is **deduction**: a theory generates a prediction about a specific case.

**Worked example.** A team finds an unfamiliar organism in a hot spring. Cell theory predicts that it is made of at least one cell, and A2.2.4 adds that it will have DNA, watery cytoplasm and a plasma membrane.

## A2.2.2 Microscopy skills

The guide expects practical experience of making temporary mounts, staining, focusing, measuring with a graticule, calculating size and magnification, drawing scale bars and photographing the view. Measuring with an instrument is quantitative observation.

**Focusing.** Start on the lowest power with the coarse focus, then sharpen with the fine focus. After changing to a higher objective, use the fine focus alone.

**Staining.** Most cells are almost transparent, so a stain such as iodine solution or methylene blue adds contrast.

### Calibrating an eyepiece graticule

An eyepiece graticule is a ruler inside the eyepiece with arbitrary divisions. Its value changes with each objective lens, so you calibrate it against a stage micrometer, which has divisions of known length.

**Worked example.** Under one objective, 40 graticule divisions line up with 25 stage micrometer divisions of 10 µm each.

- Length of 25 stage divisions = 25 × 10 = 250 µm
- One graticule division = 250 ÷ 40 = **6.25 µm**
- An onion epidermal cell that spans 14 divisions is 14 × 6.25 = **87.5 µm** long.

Under a higher-power objective the value of a division is smaller, so you recalibrate for every objective.

### Magnification, image size and actual size

Use the triangle:

magnification = image size ÷ actual size

Put image size and actual size in the same unit before dividing. 1 mm = 1000 µm and 1 µm = 1000 nm.

**Worked example (electron micrograph).** A printed micrograph carries a scale bar 25 mm long labelled 1 µm.

- Magnification = 25 mm ÷ 1 µm = 25 000 µm ÷ 1 µm = **×25 000**
- A mitochondrion measures 46 mm on the print. Actual length = 46 mm ÷ 25 000 = 0.00184 mm = **1.84 µm**.

**Worked example (light micrograph).** A photograph taken at ×150 shows a cell 48 mm long. Actual length = 48 ÷ 150 = 0.32 mm = **320 µm**.

### Making a scale bar

A scale bar is a line on the image that stands for a stated real length. If a magnification is known, bar length on the page = real length × magnification.

**Worked example.** You want a 2 µm bar on an image at ×12 000. Bar length = 2 µm × 12 000 = 24 000 µm = **24 mm**. Draw a 24 mm line labelled "2 µm". A scale bar stays correct if the image is resized.

## A2.2.3 Developments in microscopy

**Electron microscopy.** Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than visible light, so electron microscopes give far higher resolution (the ability to tell two close points apart). They show ultrastructure such as ribosomes and membranes. Transmission electron microscopes image thin slices. Scanning electron microscopes show surfaces in three dimensions. The costs: specimens are dead, in a vacuum and stained with heavy metals, so images are greyscale and may contain artefacts.

**Freeze fracture.** A frozen specimen is cracked. Membranes tend to split between their two lipid layers, exposing embedded proteins. A replica of the surface is examined by electron microscopy.

**Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM).** Samples are frozen so quickly that water forms a glass-like state, not ice crystals. No stain or chemical fixing is needed, so molecules keep their natural shape. Many images are combined to work out molecular structure.

**Fluorescent stains and immunofluorescence.** A fluorescent stain absorbs light of one colour and emits another, so the stained structure glows against a dark background. In immunofluorescence a fluorescently labelled antibody binds one specific protein, showing where it is in the cell.

## A2.2.4 Structures common to all cells

All cells have:

- **DNA as genetic material.** It stores the instructions for making proteins and is copied before division.
- **Cytoplasm that is mostly water.** Water is a solvent, so metabolic reactions take place in solution.
- **A plasma membrane made of lipids.** Phospholipids form a barrier that lets the cell keep an internal environment different from outside.

## A2.2.5 Prokaryote cell structure

The guide's model is a Gram-positive bacterium such as *Bacillus* or *Staphylococcus*.

| Component | Detail | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Thick wall outside the membrane | Supports the cell and resists bursting |
| Plasma membrane | Lipid-based | Controls exchange |
| Cytoplasm | Watery, contains ribosomes, no membrane-bound organelles | Site of metabolism |
| Naked DNA in a loop | Not wrapped around histones, not enclosed by a membrane; lies in the nucleoid region | Genetic material |
| 70S ribosomes | Smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes | Protein synthesis |

Prokaryote structure varies, and you do not need details of those variations.

## A2.2.6 Eukaryote cell structure

Eukaryote cytoplasm is **compartmentalized**: membranes create regions with different conditions and enzymes, so incompatible reactions run at once.

| Structure | Features | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Double membrane with pores; chromosomes of DNA bound to histones | Stores DNA, controls gene expression |
| 80S ribosomes | Free in cytoplasm or on rough ER | Protein synthesis |
| Mitochondrion | Double membrane, inner membrane folded | Aerobic respiration, making ATP |
| Rough ER | Sacs with ribosomes | Makes proteins for secretion |
| Smooth ER | Tubes, no ribosomes | Makes lipids |
| Golgi apparatus | Stack of flattened sacs | Modifies, sorts and packages proteins into vesicles |
| Vesicles and vacuoles | Single membrane | Transport, storage |
| Lysosome | Vesicle of digestive enzymes | Breaks down unwanted material |
| Microtubules and microfilaments | Protein fibres of the cytoskeleton | Shape, support, movement of organelles and the cell |

## A2.2.7 Processes of life in unicellular organisms
A single cell must do everything. The eight functions to link to structures are reproduction, growth, metabolism, nutrition, excretion, movement, homeostasis and response to stimuli.

| Function | *Paramecium* (heterotroph) | *Chlamydomonas* (photosynthetic) |
|---|---|---|
| Nutrition | Engulfs bacteria into food vacuoles | Photosynthesis in its chloroplast |
| Movement | Beating cilia | Two flagella |
| Homeostasis | Contractile vacuole removes excess water | Contractile vacuoles do the same |
| Response to stimuli | Swims away from harmful chemicals | Eyespot helps it swim towards light |
| Excretion | Waste leaves across the membrane | Waste leaves across the membrane |
| Reproduction | Fission (asexual) | Asexual, and can be sexual |

Metabolism and growth occur in both: chemical reactions run in the cytoplasm, and the cell grows before it divides.

## A2.2.8 Animal, fungal and plant cells

| Feature | Animal | Fungus | Plant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | None | Present, contains chitin | Present, contains cellulose |
| Vacuole | Small, temporary | Often present, varying in size | Large central sap vacuole for storage and turgor |
| Chloroplasts and other plastids | None | None | Chloroplasts; other plastids store starch or pigments |
| Centrioles | Present | Generally absent | Absent in flowering plants |
| Cilia and flagella | Often present | Rare | Absent in flowering plants |

## A2.2.9 Atypical cell structure

Number of nuclei is one way to show atypical structure.

- **Aseptate fungal hyphae** have no cross walls, so one continuous cytoplasm holds many nuclei.
- **Skeletal muscle fibres** form when many cells fuse, so one fibre has many nuclei. 
- **Mature mammalian red blood cells** have no nucleus, leaving more room for haemoglobin, but they cannot make new proteins.
- **Phloem sieve tube elements** have no nucleus when mature. This leaves an open channel for sap.

## A2.2.10 Identifying cells and structures in micrographs

**Prokaryote, plant or animal?**

- Very small (a few µm), no nucleus, pale nucleoid region: prokaryote.
- Cell wall, large sap vacuole or chloroplasts: plant.
- Nucleus but no wall or chloroplasts: animal.

**Structures in electron micrographs.**

| Structure | How it looks |
|---|---|
| Nucleus | Large, double membrane with gaps (pores), dark clumps of chromatin |
| Mitochondrion | Oval or rod shape, double membrane, folded inner membrane |
| Chloroplast | Large, double membrane, stacks of flattened sacs inside |
| Sap vacuole | Large, pale, single membrane, often pushes other organelles aside |
| Golgi apparatus | Stack of curved, flattened sacs with small vesicles budding off |
| Rough ER | Flattened sacs with dark dots (ribosomes) on the surface |
| Smooth ER | Network of tubes with no dots |
| Ribosomes | Tiny dark dots, free or attached |
| Chromosomes | Dark rods, visible in dividing cells |
| Cell wall | Thick layer outside the thin plasma membrane |
| Plasma membrane | Thin double line of two dark layers around the cytoplasm |
| Secretory vesicles | Small round membrane-bound sacs near the Golgi apparatus or the cell surface |
| Microvilli | Finger-like projections of the surface that increase surface area |
| Nucleoid region | Pale area without a membrane, containing the DNA |

## A2.2.11 Drawing and annotating electron micrographs

You may be asked to draw the nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, sap vacuole, Golgi apparatus, rough and smooth ER and chromosomes, and also the cell wall, plasma membrane, secretory vesicles and microvilli. Use a sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading and ruled label lines that do not cross. Include a scale bar or magnification. Annotation means adding the **function** to the label, not just the name.

**Example annotations for a mitochondrion.**

- Outer membrane: boundary of the organelle.
- Inner membrane folded into cristae: large surface area for the enzymes that make ATP.
- Matrix: fluid containing enzymes for the early stages of aerobic respiration.
- Secretory vesicle: carries proteins to the plasma membrane for release.
- Microvilli: increase surface area for absorption.

## HL only: A2.2.12 Origin of eukaryotic cells by endosymbiosis

The theory says that all eukaryotes descend from a common unicellular ancestor that already had a nucleus and reproduced sexually. Mitochondria then arose when this ancestor took in an aerobic prokaryote that was not digested but lived on inside it. In some lineages, chloroplasts arose later in a similar way from a photosynthetic prokaryote.

Evidence: mitochondria and chloroplasts have **70S ribosomes**, **naked circular DNA**, and can **replicate** inside the cell. The nature of science point is that a theory is strong when it explains many observations and supports predictions.

## HL only: A2.2.13 Cell differentiation

Cells in an organism share the same genes, yet a muscle cell and a nerve cell differ greatly. Differentiation, the process of becoming specialized, depends on **different patterns of gene expression**: some genes are active in a cell type and others are not. These patterns are often triggered by changes in the cell's environment, such as chemical signals from neighbouring cells.

## HL only: A2.2.14 Evolution of multicellularity

Multicellularity evolved repeatedly. Many fungi, eukaryotic algae and all plants and animals are multicellular. The advantages are **larger body size** and **cell specialization**: different cells can concentrate on different jobs rather than every cell doing everything.

## Common errors

- Writing that prokaryotes have "no DNA" or "no ribosomes". They have both, but the DNA is naked and the ribosomes are 70S.
- Saying plant cell walls contain chitin. That is fungi.
- Saying that differentiating cells lose genes. They switch genes on or off.
