- **The concept of food security.** Causes and consequences of food shortages.

## Food security: a concept with four dimensions

The definition agreed at the 1996 World Food Summit states that food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life. It is usually broken into four dimensions, and each suggests different indicators:

| Dimension | Meaning | Possible indicators |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Enough food is produced or imported | Crop yields, food production per head, import volumes |
| Access | People can obtain food physically and afford it | Food prices relative to income, poverty rates, distance to markets |
| Utilisation | Food is safe and the body can use it | Clean water, sanitation, diet diversity, rates of stunting |
| Stability | The first three hold over time | Variation in harvests and prices, frequency of shocks |

A country can be food secure at national level while households within it are not, because access depends on income and distribution. This is the core idea to carry into any discussion of food shortages.

## Causes and consequences of food shortages

**Causes** are best grouped by dimension:

- **Availability:** drought, floods, pests such as desert locusts, soil degradation, and loss of farmland to urban growth.
- **Access:** poverty, sudden price rises, loss of income, poor roads and markets, and unequal distribution within households and regions.
- **Stability:** conflict, economic crises and climatic hazards that disrupt production and trade.

**Consequences** operate at different scales:

- **Individuals:** undernutrition, stunting in children, weakened immunity and higher mortality, especially among infants.
- **Households:** sale of livestock and tools, debt, children withdrawn from school.
- **Nations:** lower labour productivity, migration from rural areas, dependence on food aid or imports, and social unrest when prices rise sharply.

## Technology and innovation in food production

Technology has repeatedly raised the amount of food produced from the same land:

- **The Green Revolution** from the 1960s spread high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, with irrigation, fertilisers and pesticides, greatly raising yields in parts of Asia and Latin America, including India and Mexico.
- **Irrigation** extends growing seasons and stabilises yields in dry areas.
- **Genetically modified crops** can resist pests or tolerate herbicides, drought or salinity.
- **Mechanisation** raises output per worker.
- **Innovations in storage, transport and refrigeration** reduce post-harvest losses, which can be large in LICs.

**Evaluating technology:** gains have been large but unevenly shared. Green Revolution packages favoured farmers who could afford seeds, fertiliser and water; heavy irrigation has lowered water tables and caused salinisation in places; fertiliser runoff pollutes rivers; and genetically modified crops raise questions of cost, corporate control of seed and public acceptance. Technology raises **availability** but does not by itself solve problems of **access**.

## Constraints on sustaining population

The syllabus names **war** and **climatic hazards** as examples of constraints.

- **War** destroys crops, infrastructure and markets, displaces farmers, and can be used to cut off food supplies to opponents. It turns local shortages into famine by blocking aid and trade, so it affects access and stability as well as availability.
- **Climatic hazards** such as drought, floods and tropical cyclones reduce harvests and kill livestock. Their impact is greatest where farming is rain-fed and households have no savings or insurance. Repeated droughts in the Sahel in the 1970s and 1980s show how a run of dry years can exceed people's ability to cope.

Other constraints, such as poor soils, lack of capital, trade barriers and weak governance, work in the same way: they limit the population an area can sustain at a given standard of living.

## Carrying capacity

**Carrying capacity** is the maximum population an area can support sustainably with its resources and technology, without degrading the environment. It is not fixed: technology, trade and changing diets raise or lower it.

## Worked example: estimating carrying capacity

*A fictional region has 400 000 hectares of cropland. Grain yields average 2.5 tonnes per hectare per year. Each person needs about 0.25 tonnes of grain a year.*

Total production = 400 000 x 2.5 = 1 000 000 tonnes.
Carrying capacity = 1 000 000 / 0.25 = **4 million people**.

*Irrigation and improved seed raise yields to 3.5 tonnes per hectare.*

New production = 400 000 x 3.5 = 1 400 000 tonnes.
New carrying capacity = 1 400 000 / 0.25 = **5.6 million people**, an increase of 40%.

*But soil degradation removes 50 000 hectares from use.*

Production = 350 000 x 3.5 = 1 225 000 tonnes, supporting **4.9 million**.

The example shows why carrying capacity is dynamic and why technology and degradation pull it in opposite directions. It also ignores trade, which allows a region to support more people than its own land can feed.

## Optimum, over- and underpopulation

- **Optimum population** is the number of people that, with the available resources and technology, gives the highest standard of living (often measured as output or income per head).
- **Overpopulation** exists when there are too many people for the resources and technology available, so living standards are below what a smaller population could achieve.
- **Underpopulation** exists when there are too few people to use the resources fully, so a larger population could raise living standards, for example by filling labour shortages.

If output per head is plotted against population size, it rises at first (more workers allow specialisation and full use of resources), reaches a peak at the optimum, and then falls as each extra person adds less to output than they consume.

## Critically evaluating optimum population

The syllabus asks for critical evaluation. Strong points to use:

- **It is hard to measure.** Which resources count, and which measure of living standards, such as income, health or happiness?
- **It is dynamic.** New technology or a new resource discovery moves the optimum upwards; resource depletion moves it down.
- **Trade breaks the link with local resources.** Densely populated, high-income countries import food and raw materials, so density alone does not indicate overpopulation.
- **Distribution matters.** An area may appear overpopulated because of inequality and poor access, not too many people in total.
- **Values matter.** An optimum for economic output may not be optimal for the environment or for future generations.
- **Usefulness:** despite this, the concept is useful for comparing places and for arguing about policy, for example whether a country should encourage immigration (underpopulation) or reduce fertility (overpopulation).

## Annotated model answer

*"Evaluate the concept of optimum population." [extract]*

> Optimum population is the size that gives the highest standard of living for the resources and technology available **(definition)**. In theory it helps judge whether countries are over- or underpopulated **(use)**. However, it cannot be measured precisely, because it depends on which resources and which measures of living standards are chosen **(limitation)**. It also changes over time: technological change can raise output per head, so a country considered overpopulated in one decade may not be in the next **(limitation)**. Trade means a densely populated country can import what it lacks, so local resources are a poor guide **(limitation)**. The concept is therefore best treated as a useful idea for comparison rather than a precise target **(judgement)**.

## Common mistakes

- Defining food security as "having enough food in the country", ignoring access and utilisation.
- Treating high population density as proof of overpopulation.
- Describing carrying capacity as a fixed number.
- Praising technology without any evaluation of who benefits or environmental costs.
- Mentioning war or climatic hazards without explaining how they affect food systems.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: set out production and requirements clearly, with units.
- **Explain** food shortages: classify causes and link each to a dimension of food security.
- **Evaluate**: weigh the usefulness of concepts and the effectiveness of technology, then reach a judgement.
