This guide covers sub-topic **3(a) Units** of Topic 3, Waves, in the
Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. Statement
**3.1** asks you to use the units degree (°), hertz (Hz), metre (m),
metre/second (m/s) and second (s). It has no "P", so it is assessed on
both papers. For the wave physics itself, see the
[Waves study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-waves/).

## The five units

| Unit | Symbol | Quantities in Topic 3 |
|---|---|---|
| degree | ° | angle of incidence, reflection, refraction; critical angle |
| hertz | Hz | frequency |
| metre | m | wavelength, amplitude, distance |
| metre/second | m/s | wave speed, speed of sound, speed of light |
| second | s | time period, time for an echo or a wave to travel |

Only two equations in this topic produce a numerical unit:

```
v = f × λ      (m/s) = (Hz) × (m)
f = 1 / T      (Hz)  = 1 / (s)
```

The refractive index equations, n = sin i ÷ sin r and sin c = 1 ÷ n, use
angles in degrees, but n itself has **no unit**: it is a ratio of two
sines.

## The hertz

Frequency is the number of waves passing a point (or the number of
oscillations of a source) each second. One hertz is one wave per second:

```
1 Hz = 1 /s     (also written s⁻¹)
```

Because f = 1 ÷ T, a wave with a time period of 0.5 s has a frequency of
2 Hz, and a period of 0.001 s gives 1000 Hz.

Check v = f × λ with units: Hz × m = (1/s) × m = m/s. This is why
frequency must be in Hz (not kHz or MHz) and wavelength in m (not cm or
nm) for the speed to come out in m/s.

## Prefixes in wave questions

Waves span a huge range of sizes, so prefixes and standard form are
everywhere in Topic 3.

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiplier | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| giga | G | × 10⁹ | microwave frequencies: 2.4 GHz = 2.4 × 10⁹ Hz |
| mega | M | × 10⁶ | radio frequencies: 98 MHz = 9.8 × 10⁷ Hz |
| kilo | k | × 10³ | sound frequencies: 2.5 kHz = 2500 Hz; distances: km |
| centi | c | × 10⁻² | water wavelengths in a ripple tank: 1.5 cm = 0.015 m |
| milli | m | × 10⁻³ | time periods: 4.0 ms = 0.0040 s |
| micro | μ | × 10⁻⁶ | infrared wavelengths: 10 μm = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ m |
| nano | n | × 10⁻⁹ | visible wavelengths: 600 nm = 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m |

Watch the capital and lower-case letters. **M** (mega, a million) and
**m** (milli, a thousandth) differ by a factor of 10⁹.

## Standard form for electromagnetic waves

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in free space; questions
that need the value normally give it, as 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. Calculations with
it are best done in standard form.

**Worked example 1: a radio wave.** A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz.
Calculate the wavelength.

```
f = 100 MHz = 1.00 × 10⁸ Hz
λ = v / f = (3.0 × 10⁸) / (1.00 × 10⁸) = 3.0 m
```

**Worked example 2: visible light.** Green light has a wavelength of
500 nm. Calculate its frequency.

```
λ = 500 nm = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m
f = v / λ = (3.0 × 10⁸) / (5.00 × 10⁻⁷) = 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz
```

*Commentary.* Enter standard form with the calculator's ×10ˣ (EXP) key, or
put each number in brackets: typing 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ without
brackets multiplies by 10⁻⁷ instead of dividing by it. Dividing by a negative power of ten gives a large
answer, which matches the idea that very short wavelengths have very high
frequencies. Check the answer's size: visible light has frequencies of
order 10¹⁴ Hz, so 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz is sensible.

## Time units and the period

Times in wave questions are often given in milliseconds, especially on an
oscilloscope trace or when one wave takes a fraction of a second.

**Worked example 3.** A sound wave has a time period of 2.5 ms. It travels
at 340 m/s. Calculate its frequency and wavelength.

```
T = 2.5 ms = 0.0025 s
f = 1 / T = 1 / 0.0025 = 400 Hz
λ = v / f = 340 / 400 = 0.85 m
```

Using T = 2.5 directly would give f = 0.4 Hz, a thousand times too small.

## The degree

Angles in Topic 3 are measured in degrees and always **from the normal**,
the line at 90° to the surface where the ray meets it. An angle of
incidence of 30° means 30° from the normal, which is 60° from the surface.

When using sin or sin⁻¹ on a calculator, the calculator must be in
**degree** mode (usually "D" or "DEG" shown on the screen). In radian mode,
sin 30 gives −0.988 instead of 0.500. The specification's mathematical
skills include using a calculator to handle sin x and sin⁻¹ x with x in
degrees.

**Worked example 4.** Light enters glass at an angle of incidence of 40°
and refracts at 25°.

```
n = sin i / sin r = sin 40° / sin 25° = 0.643 / 0.423 = 1.52
```

n has no unit. A quick sense check: n for glass is about 1.5, and it is
always greater than 1.

## Reading the axes of wave graphs

Two kinds of wave graph look alike but give different quantities, and the
unit on the horizontal axis tells you which is which.

- **Displacement against distance** (axis in m or cm): a snapshot of the
  wave at one instant. The repeat length along the axis is the
  **wavelength**, in metres.
- **Displacement against time** (axis in s or ms): how one point moves.
  The repeat length along the axis is the **time period**, in seconds,
  and f = 1 ÷ T.

On both, the amplitude is read from the vertical axis, from the rest line
to a crest, in metres. Always check the axis label before deciding whether
a length you have measured is a λ or a T.

## Common mistakes

- Using kHz, MHz or GHz in v = f × λ without converting to Hz.
- Using cm or nm for wavelength without converting to m.
- Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal.
- Leaving the calculator in radian mode.
- Writing a unit for refractive index.
- Dividing by a value typed as "× 10 ^" without brackets; use the EXP key
  or brackets.
- Writing "Hz/s" or "m/Hz". Frequency is in Hz; wavelength is in m.

## Exam technique with command words

- **Calculate**: give the equation, convert prefixes, substitute, and give
  the answer with a unit (except for n), in standard form if very large
  or small.
- **Show that**: give your value to one more significant figure than the
  printed one, with every conversion visible.
- **Determine** from an oscilloscope trace or a wave diagram: read the
  scale (for example ms per division, or cm per square) and convert to SI
  units before using f = 1 ÷ T or v = f × λ.
- **State** the unit: "hertz (Hz)", or for n, "no unit".
