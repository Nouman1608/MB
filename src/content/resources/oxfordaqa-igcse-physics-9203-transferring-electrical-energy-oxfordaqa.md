This guide covers one subtopic in depth: **3.6.5 Transferring electrical energy**, statements a to f of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered. It is the most calculation-heavy part of Topic 6: you must **calculate the current through an appliance from its power and supply pd and choose a fuse**, and **calculate the cost of mains electricity** from a price per kilowatt-hour and from **meter readings**. The specification also states that you will **not** be asked to convert between kilowatt-hours and joules, which shapes how you should set out every energy calculation.

The [mains electricity, safety, power and cost guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-mains-electricity-safety-power-and-the-cost-of/) introduces these ideas alongside household safety. This page gives more practice in choosing units and linking the equations together. Afterwards, use the [electrical energy revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-transferring-electrical-energy-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) and the [electrical energy practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-transferring-electrical-energy-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Electrical appliances are designed to transfer energy; give examples and identify the transfers |
| b | Power is the rate of energy transfer: P = E ÷ t |
| c | P = I × V; find the current from power and pd, then the fuse size |
| d | E = V × Q |
| e | Energy transferred depends on time switched on and power rating; kWh is often more convenient than J |
| f | E = P × t for mains energy, in kWh; cost from price per kWh and meter readings |

## Appliances transfer energy (statement a)

Every appliance takes energy from the mains electrically and transfers it to other stores, usefully or wastefully.

| Appliance | Useful transfer | Wasted transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Electric kettle | Thermal store of the water | Heating the kettle and the air |
| Hairdryer | Thermal store of the air; kinetic store of the moving air | Sound |
| Food mixer | Kinetic store of the blades | Heating of the motor; sound |
| Phone charger | Chemical store of the battery | Heating of the charger |
| Television | Light and sound | Heating of the electronics |

For a heater or kettle, almost all the energy ends up heating, and that heating is the useful output.

## The three power and energy equations

| Equation | Units | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| P = E ÷ t | W, J, s | Energy and time given, in joules and seconds |
| P = I × V | W, A, V | Current and pd given or wanted |
| E = V × Q | J, V, C | Charge flowing and pd given |

All three must use the same set of SI units. Remember 1 kW = 1000 W and 1 minute = 60 s.

## Worked example 1: current and fuse choice (statement c)

A 2.8 kW kettle runs on a 230 V supply. Fuses of 3 A, 5 A and 13 A are available.

```
I = P ÷ V = 2800 W ÷ 230 V = 12.2 A
Choose the 13 A fuse: the smallest rating just above the working current.
```

The fuse must be **above** the normal working current, or it will melt every time the kettle is used. It should be the **next rating up**, not a much larger one, so that a fault current melts it quickly. With the same fuses, a 1.1 kW microwave oven (I = 1100 ÷ 230 = 4.78 A) needs the **5 A** fuse, and a 60 W lamp (I = 0.26 A) needs the **3 A** fuse.

## Worked example 2: linking charge, current and energy (statement d)

A 2.0 kW heater runs from a 230 V supply for 10 minutes. Find the charge that flows and the energy transferred.

```
Current:   I = P ÷ V = 2000 ÷ 230 = 8.70 A
Time:      t = 10 × 60 = 600 s
Charge:    Q = I × t = 8.70 × 600 = 5220 C
Energy:    E = V × Q = 230 × 5220 = 1.2 × 10⁶ J
Check:     E = P × t = 2000 × 600 = 1.2 × 10⁶ J
```

The two routes agree, which is a useful check. E = V Q tells you that **each coulomb** of charge transfers 230 J as it passes through the heater: that is what a pd of 230 V means. (The link Q = I t comes from the electrical circuits subtopic.)

## Kilowatt-hours (statements e and f)

A joule is a very small amount of energy for a household. A 2 kW heater transfers 7.2 million joules in an hour. So mains energy is measured in **kilowatt-hours (kWh)**: the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance in 1 hour.

> E = P × t, with E in kWh, P in kW and t in hours

Because the specification does not require kWh–J conversion, **pick one unit system and stick to it**:

- watts and seconds → joules;
- kilowatts and hours → kilowatt-hours.

Mixing them, for example using watts with hours, is the commonest error in this subtopic.

**Worked example 3 (cost).** A 2.5 kW tumble dryer runs for 1.5 hours. Electricity costs 15 cents per kWh.

```
E = 2.5 kW × 1.5 h = 3.75 kWh
Cost = 3.75 × 15 = 56.25 cents ≈ 56 cents
```

Convert minutes to hours by dividing by 60: 20 minutes is 0.333 h, so a 3.0 kW heater used for 20 minutes transfers 1.0 kWh.

## Meter readings (statement f)

An electricity meter shows the **total** energy supplied since it was installed, in kWh. To find the energy used over a period, subtract the earlier reading from the later one.

**Worked example 4.** A meter reads 45 672.4 kWh at the start of a month and 45 931.9 kWh at the end. Electricity costs 24 cents per kWh.

```
Energy used = 45 931.9 − 45 672.4 = 259.5 kWh
Cost        = 259.5 × 24 = 6228 cents
```

Then convert to the main currency unit if the question asks: 6228 cents is 62.28 in dollars or another 100-cent currency.

**Comparing appliances.** The amount of energy depends on both power and time. A 10 W lamp used for 5 hours a day for 30 days transfers 0.010 × 5 × 30 = 1.5 kWh; a 60 W lamp used in the same way transfers 9.0 kWh. A low-power appliance left on for a long time can use more energy than a high-power appliance used briefly: a 150 W fridge running for 24 hours uses 3.6 kWh, more than the 2.5 kW tumble dryer above.

## Common mistakes

- Using power in watts with time in hours, or kilowatts with seconds.
- Forgetting to convert minutes to hours or seconds.
- Choosing a fuse below the working current, or a much larger one than needed.
- Adding meter readings instead of subtracting.
- Leaving the cost in cents when the question asks for the main currency unit.
- Confusing power (rate, in W) with energy (amount, in J or kWh).

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: write the equation, show unit conversions on separate lines, then substitute.
- **Determine** a fuse: calculate the current first, then state the chosen rating and why it is the next one up.
- **Explain** why kWh are used: joules are too small a unit for household quantities.
- **Compare** energy use: calculate both values in the same unit before deciding.
