These questions cover sub-topic **11.1 The immune system** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027 (AS Level, Paper 1 and Paper 2 style). The number in square brackets after the last part of each question is the total for the whole question. All data are invented for practice. Calculators are allowed.

Revise first with the [immune system study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-the-immune-system/) and the [immune system revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-the-immune-system-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Which cell secretes antibodies?

A: macrophage

B: plasma cell

C: T-helper cell

D: T-killer cell **[1]**

**2.** Which cell both engulfs pathogens and presents their antigens to lymphocytes?

A: B-lymphocyte

B: macrophage

C: neutrophil

D: red blood cell **[1]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by an antigen. **[2]**

**4.** State the difference between self antigens and non-self antigens. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** differences between neutrophils and macrophages. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe how a neutrophil destroys a bacterium. **[5]**

**7.** Describe the sequence of events in a primary immune response to a virus, including the roles of macrophages, T-helper cells, B-lymphocytes, plasma cells and T-killer cells. **[7]**

**8.** The table shows antibody concentration in the blood of a person exposed to the same antigen on day 0 and again on day 50.

| Day | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 50 | 53 | 56 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody / arbitrary units | 0 | 2 | 18 | 9 | 3 | 120 | 270 | 210 |

**(a)** Calculate how many times higher the highest concentration after the second exposure is than the highest after the first.

**(b)** Calculate the mean rate of increase in antibody concentration between day 50 and day 56.

**(c)** Explain the differences between the two responses. **[7]**

**9.** Plasma cells contain large amounts of rough endoplasmic reticulum and many Golgi bodies. Explain how these features relate to the function of plasma cells. **[3]**

**10.** A person receives a kidney from a donor who is not a close relative. Explain why the immune system may attack the transplanted kidney. **[3]**

**11.** HIV infects and destroys T-helper cells. Suggest and explain why a person with very few T-helper cells produces few antibodies when infected with a bacterium. **[3]**

**12.** Explain the role of memory cells in providing long-term immunity. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**2.** **B** [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**3.** A molecule (usually a protein or glycoprotein) recognised by the immune system as self or non-self [1]; that can stimulate an immune response (production of antibodies) [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**4.** Self antigens are on the body's own cells and do not normally trigger an immune response [1]; non-self antigens are foreign (e.g. on pathogens) and do trigger an immune response [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**5.** Any two from: neutrophils have a lobed nucleus, macrophages a large rounded or kidney-shaped nucleus; neutrophils arrive first and in larger numbers; neutrophils are short-lived, macrophages long-lived; macrophages present antigens, neutrophils do not; macrophages develop from monocytes and live in tissues [2]. Total: 2 marks.

**6.** Moves towards chemicals released by the bacterium or damaged cells (chemotaxis) [1]; receptors on its membrane bind to the bacterium [1]; engulfs it by endocytosis into a phagocytic vacuole [1]; lysosomes fuse with the vacuole [1]; hydrolytic enzymes digest the bacterium [1]. Total: 5 marks.

**7.** Macrophage engulfs virus (or infected cell) and presents its antigens [1]; T-helper cell with complementary receptor binds to the antigen [1]; T-helper cell divides by mitosis and releases cytokines [1]; cytokines stimulate matching B-lymphocytes to divide by mitosis [1]; B cells differentiate into plasma cells that secrete specific antibodies [1]; cytokines activate T-killer cells, which kill body cells infected with the virus [1]; some B and T cells become memory cells [1]. Total: 7 marks.

**8. (a)** 270 ÷ 18 [1] = **15 times** [1].

**(b)** (270 − 3) ÷ 6 [1] = **44.5 arbitrary units per day** [1].

**(c)** The secondary response is faster and produces more antibody [1]; because memory cells formed after the first exposure [1]; there are many more of them than the original matching lymphocytes and they quickly divide and form plasma cells [1]. Total: 7 marks.

**9.** Antibodies are proteins, made on ribosomes on the rough ER [1]; large amounts of rough ER allow large quantities of antibody to be made [1]; Golgi bodies modify and package the antibodies into vesicles for secretion by exocytosis [1]. Total: 3 marks.

**10.** The donor's cells carry antigens (glycoproteins) that differ from the recipient's [1]; the recipient's immune system recognises them as non-self [1]; lymphocytes respond: T-killer cells attack the kidney cells and antibodies are produced [1]. Total: 3 marks.

**11.** T-helper cells release cytokines that stimulate B cells to divide [1]; with few T-helper cells, few matching B cells are activated or divide [1]; so few plasma cells form and little antibody is secreted [1]. Total: 3 marks.

**12.** Memory cells are formed from B and T clones during the primary response [1]; they are long-lived and remain in the body [1]; there are many more of them than the original matching lymphocytes [1]; on re-infection they rapidly divide into plasma cells (and T cells), so the pathogen is destroyed before symptoms appear [1]. Total: 4 marks.

*Examiner insight:* "memory cells remember the antigen" is not credited; they recognise it because their receptors are complementary.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying B cells, rather than plasma cells, secrete antibodies.
- Saying T-killer cells destroy free bacteria.
- Leaving cytokines out of the primary response.
- Describing antigens as "foreign cells" instead of molecules.
- Saying memory cells "remember".
