## 3.1.8.1 The enthalpy changes in a Born–Haber cycle

A Born–Haber cycle is a Hess's law cycle for forming an ionic solid. Learn each definition with its
equation.

| Term | Definition | Example equation |
|---|---|---|
| Enthalpy of formation, ΔfH⦵ | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states | K(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → KCl(s) |
| Enthalpy of atomisation, ΔatH⦵ | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state | K(s) → K(g) |
| Bond enthalpy | Enthalpy change to break 1 mole of a covalent bond, with reactants and products gaseous | Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) |
| First ionisation energy | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of electrons is removed from 1 mole of gaseous atoms to form 1 mole of gaseous 1+ ions | K(g) → K⁺(g) + e⁻ |
| Second ionisation energy | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of electrons is removed from 1 mole of gaseous 1+ ions to form gaseous 2+ ions | Mg⁺(g) → Mg²⁺(g) + e⁻ |
| First electron affinity | Enthalpy change when each atom in 1 mole of gaseous atoms gains one electron to form 1 mole of gaseous 1– ions | Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g) |
| Second electron affinity | Enthalpy change when each ion in 1 mole of gaseous 1– ions gains one electron to form gaseous 2– ions | O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) |
| Lattice enthalpy of formation | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a solid ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions | K⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → KCl(s) |
| Lattice enthalpy of dissociation | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a solid ionic compound is separated into its gaseous ions | KCl(s) → K⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) |

Points that cost marks:

- **Lattice enthalpy has two definitions.** Formation is negative; dissociation is positive, with
  the same size. Always say which one you mean.
- **Atomisation of a diatomic gas is half the bond enthalpy.** Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) makes 2 moles of
  atoms, so ΔatH⦵(Cl) = ½ × bond enthalpy. This only works when the element's standard state is the
  gaseous molecule. Bromine is a liquid, so ΔatH⦵(Br) also includes vaporisation.
- **Ionisation energies are always endothermic.**
- **First electron affinities are exothermic** for non-metals such as Cl and O: the incoming electron
  is attracted by the nucleus. **The second electron affinity of oxygen is endothermic**: an electron
  is being added to a negative ion, which repels it.

## Constructing a Born–Haber cycle

Draw the cycle as an energy level diagram: each species on its own horizontal level, endothermic steps
as up arrows and exothermic steps as down arrows. For KCl, the indirect route is:

```
K(s) + ½Cl₂(g)                     start: elements in standard states
  → K(g) + ½Cl₂(g)       ΔatH(K)          up (endothermic)
  → K(g) + Cl(g)         ΔatH(Cl)         up
  → K⁺(g) + e⁻ + Cl(g)   first IE(K)      up
  → K⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g)       first EA(Cl)     down (exothermic)
  → KCl(s)               lattice enthalpy of formation, down
Direct route: K(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → KCl(s)     ΔfH, down
```

The route from the elements to the solid can go two ways: directly (ΔfH⦵), or through gaseous atoms
and gaseous ions. Hess's law makes the two routes equal:

```
ΔfH⦵ = ΔatH(metal) + ΔatH(non-metal) + IE(s) + EA(s) + lattice enthalpy of formation
```

### Worked example 1: lattice enthalpy of potassium chloride

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH⦵(KCl) –437; ΔatH⦵(K) +89; first IE(K) +419; Cl–Cl bond enthalpy +242;
first EA(Cl) –349.

```
ΔatH(Cl) = ½ × 242 = +121
Sum of steps to gaseous ions = 89 + 419 + 121 + (–349) = +280
Lattice enthalpy of formation = ΔfH – (sum of steps)
                              = –437 – 280 = –717 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The lattice enthalpy of dissociation of KCl is therefore +717 kJ mol⁻¹.

### Worked example 2: finding a missing term (electron affinity)

A cycle can be rearranged for any single unknown. For MgCl₂, every chlorine step happens twice.

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH⦵(MgCl₂) –641; ΔatH⦵(Mg) +148; first IE(Mg) +738; second IE(Mg) +1451;
Cl–Cl bond enthalpy +242; lattice enthalpy of formation of MgCl₂ –2524.

```
Two Cl atoms: 2 × ΔatH(Cl) = 242 (one whole Cl–Cl bond)
ΔfH = 148 + 738 + 1451 + 242 + 2EA + (–2524)
–641 = 2579 + 2EA – 2524
2EA = –641 – 2579 + 2524 = –696
EA(Cl) = –348 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Both ionisation energies appear because Mg must become Mg²⁺, and two EAs because two Cl⁻ ions form.

## Comparing with the perfect ionic model

A lattice enthalpy can also be **calculated** from a model. The **perfect ionic model** assumes:

- the ions are perfect spheres with their charge spread evenly;
- the only attraction is electrostatic between oppositely charged ions (no covalent character).

The Born–Haber value comes from experimental data, so it reflects the real bonding.
- **Values close together:** the compound is close to perfectly ionic.
- **Born–Haber value more exothermic than the model value:** there is extra attraction the model
  leaves out. This is evidence for **covalent character**.

Covalent character arises when a small or highly charged cation **polarises** a large anion, pulling
its electron cloud towards the cation.

| Compound (illustrative data) | Born–Haber (kJ mol⁻¹) | Perfect ionic model (kJ mol⁻¹) | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compound A | –760 | –755 | Nearly perfectly ionic |
| Compound B | –890 | –820 | Significant covalent character |

## Enthalpies of solution and hydration

**Enthalpy of hydration, ΔhydH⦵**: the enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water
to form 1 mole of aqueous ions. Example: K⁺(g) → K⁺(aq). It is always exothermic, because water
molecules are attracted to the ion: the δ– oxygen towards cations and the δ+ hydrogens towards anions.

**Enthalpy of solution, ΔsolH⦵**: the enthalpy change when 1 mole of an ionic solid dissolves in
enough water that the ions no longer interact. Example: KCl(s) → K⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).

The cycle goes from the solid to gaseous ions (lattice dissociation, endothermic) and then from
gaseous ions to aqueous ions (hydration, exothermic):

```
ΔsolH = lattice enthalpy of dissociation + Σ ΔhydH(ions)
```

### Worked example 3: enthalpy of solution of potassium chloride

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): lattice dissociation of KCl +717 (worked example 1); ΔhydH(K⁺) –340;
ΔhydH(Cl⁻) –360.

```
ΔsolH = +717 + (–340) + (–360) = +17 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The positive value means the solution cools as KCl dissolves. For MgCl₂, include ΔhydH(Cl⁻) twice.
If you are given lattice enthalpy of **formation**, reverse its sign first.

## 3.1.8.2 Entropy and feasibility

### Why ΔH is not enough

Some endothermic changes are feasible: ice melts at room temperature, and ammonium nitrate dissolves
in water as the solution gets colder. ΔH alone cannot explain this. The missing factor is entropy.

**Entropy, S**, is a measure of disorder: the number of ways particles and their energy can be
arranged. Units: **J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**. Every substance has a positive absolute entropy, including elements.

Entropy increases when:

- a solid melts or a liquid boils (solid < liquid < gas);
- a reaction produces more moles of gas than it uses.

### Calculating ΔS from absolute entropies

```
ΔS = ΣS(products) – ΣS(reactants)
```

Multiply each value by its coefficient in the equation. Do not set elements to zero; that rule is for
ΔfH⦵, not for S.

### The Gibbs free-energy equation

The balance between enthalpy and entropy is given by

```
ΔG = ΔH – TΔS        (derivation not required)
```

with T in kelvin. A reaction is **feasible when ΔG is zero or negative**. ΔH is usually in kJ mol⁻¹
and ΔS in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, so **divide ΔS by 1000** before substituting.

At the temperature where ΔG = 0, the reaction is just feasible:

```
T = ΔH / ΔS        (ΔS in kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
```

How ΔG varies with temperature depends on the signs:

| ΔH | ΔS | Effect of temperature | Feasible |
|---|---|---|---|
| – | + | ΔG negative at every T | Always |
| + | – | ΔG positive at every T | Never |
| – | – | ΔG becomes less negative as T rises | Below T = ΔH/ΔS |
| + | + | ΔG becomes more negative as T rises | Above T = ΔH/ΔS |

"Feasible" means thermodynamically possible. A feasible reaction can still be very slow if its
activation energy is high.

### Worked example 4: thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate

CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g), ΔH⦵ = +178 kJ mol⁻¹.
Absolute entropies (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹): CaCO₃(s) 93; CaO(s) 40; CO₂(g) 214.

```
ΔS = (40 + 214) – 93 = +161 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = +0.161 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
ΔG at 298 K = 178 – (298 × 0.161) = +130 kJ mol⁻¹   → not feasible
T at which feasible = 178 / 0.161 = 1106 K
```

ΔS is positive because a gas forms from a solid. The reaction is feasible at 1106 K and above.

### Worked example 5: ΔH and ΔS from a graph of ΔG against T

ΔG = ΔH – TΔS has the form y = c + mx, so a straight-line graph of ΔG against T has:

- **gradient = –ΔS**
- **intercept on the ΔG axis (at T = 0) = ΔH**

A line passes through (300 K, +40 kJ mol⁻¹) and (500 K, +10 kJ mol⁻¹).

```
gradient = (10 – 40) / (500 – 300) = –0.150 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
ΔS = +0.150 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = +150 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
ΔH = ΔG + TΔS = 40 + (300 × 0.150) = +85 kJ mol⁻¹
Feasible from T = 85 / 0.150 = 567 K, where the line crosses ΔG = 0
```

### Worked example 6: ΔS of vaporisation using a kettle

The specification suggests finding ΔS for vaporising water with a kettle. At the boiling point, liquid
and gas are in equilibrium, so ΔG = 0 and ΔS = ΔH / T.

A 2.00 kW kettle keeps water boiling at 373 K for 60 s, and the mass falls by 50.0 g.

```
Energy supplied = 2000 W × 60 s = 120 000 J = 120 kJ
n(H₂O) = 50.0 / 18.0 = 2.78 mol
ΔvapH = 120 / 2.78 = 43.2 kJ mol⁻¹
ΔS = 43 200 / 373 = +116 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
```

Some energy heats the surroundings rather than vaporising water, so this method overestimates ΔvapH
and ΔS.

## Common errors

- Forgetting the second ionisation energy for a 2+ ion, or the second electron affinity for O²⁻.
- Including only one hydration enthalpy for an ion that appears twice in the formula.
- Setting the absolute entropy of an element to zero.
- Mixing ΔS in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ with ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹, or using T in °C.
- Taking the graph gradient as +ΔS: the gradient is –ΔS.
