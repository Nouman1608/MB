These questions cover section 3.7.8 investment appraisal of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-strategic-options-investment-appraisal/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-strategic-options-investment-appraisal-revision-notes/).

Questions 6 to 9 use this data. Corvane Printing is choosing between two projects, each costing £150,000. Net cash inflows are:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Project A (£) | 40,000 | 55,000 | 60,000 | 45,000 |
| Project B (£) | 70,000 | 50,000 | 30,000 | 20,000 |

Discount factors at 10%: Year 1 0.909; Year 2 0.826; Year 3 0.751; Year 4 0.683.

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A project costs £80,000 and returns £20,000 a year. What is its payback period? **[1]**

A: 2 years

B: 3 years

C: 4 years

D: 5 years

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a weakness of the average rate of return? **[1]**

A: It ignores the size of the initial investment

B: It ignores the timing of the cash flows

C: It cannot be expressed as a percentage

D: It ignores all but the first year's profit

**3.** Define "net present value". **[2]**

**4.** A project costs £90,000. Cash inflows are £30,000 in Year 1, £40,000 in Year 2 and £50,000 in Year 3. Calculate the payback period in years and months. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why a firm uses discount factors when appraising an investment. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Calculate the payback period for Project A in years and months. **[3]**

**7.** Calculate the average rate of return for Project A and for Project B, to one decimal place. **[4]**

**8.** Calculate the net present value of each project at 10%. **[6]**

**9.** Corvane requires a payback of three years or less and a positive NPV. Evaluate which project, if either, the firm should choose. **[12]**

**10.** Explain **two** limitations of using payback as the only method of investment appraisal. **[4]**

**11.** A brewery is considering a £500,000 investment in a new bottling plant that would cut 12 jobs. The NPV is positive. Analyse **two** non-financial factors that the directors should consider. **[6]**

**12.** Assess how far uncertainty reduces the usefulness of investment appraisal. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. £80,000 ÷ £20,000 = 4 years. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. ARR averages the profit and ignores when the cash arrives. **[1]**

**3.** The sum of the present values of future cash inflows [1] minus the initial cost, where cash flows are discounted to reflect the time value of money [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Cumulative cash: £30,000, £70,000, £120,000 [1]. At the end of Year 2, £20,000 is still needed; 20,000 ÷ 50,000 = 0.4 of Year 3 [1]. 0.4 × 12 = 4.8, so **2 years 5 months** (to the nearest month) [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Money received in the future is worth less than money today [1], because it could be invested or earn interest in the meantime and because inflation and risk reduce its value [1]. Discount factors convert future cash flows to present values so that all amounts can be compared on the same basis [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Cumulative: Year 1 £40,000; Year 2 £95,000; Year 3 £155,000 [1]. Still needed at the start of Year 3: £55,000 [1]. 55,000 ÷ 60,000 = 0.917 years = 11 months, so payback is **2 years 11 months** [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Project A: total £200,000; profit £50,000; average £12,500 [1]; ARR = 12,500 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **8.3%** [1]. Project B: total £170,000; profit £20,000; average £5,000 [1]; ARR = 5,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **3.3%** [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Project A: 40,000 × 0.909 = 36,360; 55,000 × 0.826 = 45,430; 60,000 × 0.751 = 45,060; 45,000 × 0.683 = 30,735 [1]; total £157,585 [1]; NPV = **+£7,585** [1]. Project B: 70,000 × 0.909 = 63,630; 50,000 × 0.826 = 41,300; 30,000 × 0.751 = 22,530; 20,000 × 0.683 = 13,660 [1]; total £141,120 [1]; NPV = **−£8,880** [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Payback: Project A is 2 years 11 months, within the 3-year target [1]. Project B recovers £70,000, £120,000, then £150,000 at the end of Year 3, so payback is exactly 3 years, which just meets the target [1]. NPV: Project A is +£7,585, so it meets the NPV criterion [1]; Project B is −£8,880, so it fails [1]. ARR agrees: A 8.3% against B 3.3% [1]. On the figures, Project A is the only one that meets both criteria and should be chosen [1]. Limitations: the NPV for A is small relative to the £150,000 cost (about 5%) [1], so a modest fall in cash flows could make it negative [1]. Project B returns cash faster, which is an advantage if cash is tight, but the later years are weak [1]. Non-financial factors, such as the effect on customers, staff skills and strategy, are not in the figures [1]. Judgement: choose A, but check the forecasts with sensitivity analysis before committing [1], and consider whether other options exist that give a higher return [1]. **[12]**

**10.** Limitation 1: it ignores cash flows after the payback period [1], so a project with large later returns could be rejected in favour of one with a quick but small return [1]. Limitation 2: it ignores the time value of money [1], treating £1 received in Year 1 the same as £1 in Year 3 [1]. (Credit also: the target period is arbitrary; it does not measure profitability.) **[4]**

**11.** Factor 1: employees [1]. Losing 12 jobs may cause redundancy costs, fear among the remaining workforce and lower morale [1], which could reduce productivity; the brewery's reputation as a local employer could suffer [1]. Factor 2: fit with strategy and brand [1]. If the brewery's brand depends on traditional, craft production, a large automated plant may conflict with its image [1], or if it supports growth into larger markets it could be a good fit [1]. (Credit also: environmental impact, flexibility of the plant, competitor reaction.) **[6]**

**12.** Uncertainty reduces usefulness: appraisal relies on forecasts of sales, costs and interest rates over several years [1], and the further ahead, the less reliable they are [1]. Small errors can change the result, particularly for NPV, which is sensitive to the discount rate [1]. A false sense of accuracy from precise figures may mislead managers [1]. But appraisal still helps: it forces managers to state their assumptions [1]. Sensitivity analysis and scenarios can test how robust the decision is [1], and staged investment can limit the risk [1]. Appraisal allows comparison of projects on the same basis, even if the absolute figures are uncertain. Judgement: uncertainty reduces precision but not usefulness [1]; the method is most useful when combined with sensitivity analysis and non-financial judgement [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Show every step in calculations, then use the numbers to make and justify a decision rather than stopping at the figure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not subtracting the initial cost when calculating NPV.
- Leaving payback as a decimal when months are asked for.
- Dividing total inflows, rather than total profit, by the years in ARR.
- Using the wrong year's discount factor.
- Accepting or rejecting a project on one method only, without caution about the forecasts.
