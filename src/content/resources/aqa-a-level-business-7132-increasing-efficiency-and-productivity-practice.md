## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following best describes Just in Time production? **[1]**

A: Holding large buffer inventories in case of supply problems

B: Receiving materials only when they are needed for production

C: Producing goods in advance of orders

D: Buying materials in bulk to gain discounts

**2.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to be a capital-intensive process? **[1]**

A: A hairdressing salon

B: A bottling plant with automated filling lines

C: A craft pottery

D: A personal tutoring service

**3.** Define the term "lean production". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** way in which a business with spare capacity could make better use of it. **[3]**

**5.** Vantage Print holds average inventory of paper and ink worth £90,000. Holding costs are 12% of the value of inventory each year.

**(a)** Calculate the annual holding cost. **[2]**

**(b)** Vantage introduces Just in Time and cuts average inventory to £25,000. Calculate the annual saving in holding costs. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** risk to Vantage of using Just in Time. **[2]**

**6.** Dunmore Bakery can make bread by hand or on an oven line. Hand production has fixed costs of £6,000 a month and variable costs of £5 a loaf. The oven line has fixed costs of £18,000 a month and variable costs of £2 a loaf.

**(a)** Calculate the total monthly cost of each method at an output of 5,000 loaves. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the output at which the two methods cost the same. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain which method Dunmore should use if it expects to sell 2,500 loaves a month. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** difficulty a business might face when trying to increase labour productivity. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** benefits of lean production to a manufacturer of car parts. **[6]**

**9.** Dunmore Bakery's sales are growing. The owner is considering replacing some bakers with an automated oven line. Assess whether this is the right decision. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse how technology might improve the operational efficiency of a hotel. **[6]**

**11.** A holiday resort has very high demand in July and August and low demand in winter. Explain **two** ways in which it could use its capacity more efficiently across the year. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether Just in Time is a suitable system for a supermarket chain. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. JIT means materials arrive when needed, so little inventory is held. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A bottling plant uses mainly machinery; the others rely mainly on labour. **[1]**

**3.** A way of producing that aims to minimise waste of materials, time, inventory and effort [1], with continuous improvement and quality built in at each stage [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The business could take on subcontract work for other firms [1], using machines and staff that would otherwise be idle [1], which spreads fixed costs over more units and lowers the average cost of its own products [1]. Accept: hire out space; promotions in quiet periods; find new markets. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 12% of £90,000 [1] = **£10,800** [1].

**(b)** New cost = £25,000 × 0.12 = £3,000 [1]. Saving = £10,800 − £3,000 = **£7,800** [1].

**(c)** Vantage would depend on suppliers delivering on time [1], so a late delivery of paper could stop production and delay customers' orders [1]. **[6]**

**6. (a)** Hand: £6,000 + 5,000 × £5 = **£31,000** [1]. Oven line: £18,000 + 5,000 × £2 = **£28,000** [1].

**(b)** 6,000 + 5x = 18,000 + 2x [1], so 3x = 12,000 and x = **4,000 loaves** [1].

**(c)** At 2,500 loaves hand production costs £6,000 + £12,500 = £18,500, while the oven line costs £18,000 + £5,000 = £23,000 [1], so hand production is cheaper because the output is below 4,000 loaves and the oven line's high fixed costs are spread over too few loaves [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Workers may resist change, for example if they fear that higher productivity will cost jobs or increase pressure [1], which may lead to low morale, absence or industrial action, so output per worker may not rise [1]. Accept: cost and time lag of training or equipment; quality may fall. **[2]**

**8.** Benefit 1: lower costs [1]. Holding little inventory reduces storage, insurance and finance costs [1], and cutting waste in each process lowers the cost per part [1]. Benefit 2: higher quality [1]. With continuous improvement, staff spot and solve defects quickly [1], so there are fewer returns and the manufacturer builds a reputation that wins contracts from carmakers [1]. Credit also: faster response to customers; engaged workforce. **[6]**

**9.** For: automation gives lower variable cost per loaf (£2 against £5 in the data), so unit costs fall once output passes the break-even point of 4,000 loaves [1]. Consistent quality and faster output help meet growing demand [1]. The oven line would raise labour productivity [1]. Against: the fixed costs are three times higher, so if demand falls, the bakery is exposed to losses [1]. Finance is needed, which may mean borrowing [1]. Redundancies cost money and hurt morale; customers may value handmade bread, which is part of the brand [1]. Machines are less flexible for small special orders [1]. Judgement: it is right if growing sales are likely to stay above 4,000 loaves a month for several years [1]; otherwise, a smaller step, such as one machine alongside the bakers, could keep flexibility while cutting costs [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Point 1: online booking and check-in [1] reduce the staff time spent at reception and lower errors in reservations [1], which means fewer staff are needed per guest and waiting times fall [1]. Point 2: energy and maintenance systems [1]. Sensors can switch off heating and lights in empty rooms and warn of faults before breakdowns [1], reducing energy costs and avoiding rooms being out of use [1]. Credit also: stock-control software for kitchens. **[6]**

**11.** Way 1: off-peak pricing and promotions [1], such as winter breaks at lower prices, to move demand into quiet months and fill empty rooms [1]. Way 2: develop other uses for the resort [1], for example conferences, weddings or winter activity packages, so the facilities earn money outside the summer peak [1]. Accept: flexible or seasonal staff; renting out space. **[4]**

**12.** For: JIT would cut the cost of holding stock and free up cash, and stores would need less warehouse space [1]. It reduces waste of perishable items [1]. Modern supply chain software and frequent deliveries make it easier to keep shelves stocked [1]. Fresh products stay fresher [1]. Against: a supermarket has thousands of lines and customers expect products always to be on the shelf, so a delay causes lost sales and loss of loyalty [1]. Demand can change quickly with weather, events or promotions, so forecasts may be wrong [1]. Delivering more often adds transport costs and congestion [1]. Supply disruptions, such as strikes or bad weather, can leave shelves empty [1]. Loss of bulk discounts [1]. Factors: how reliable the suppliers and the transport network are, and whether the goods are perishable or non-perishable [1]. Judgement: a modified form is suitable, with JIT for fresh goods with short shelf lives and some buffer stock of non-perishable essentials [1], because customers' expectation of availability is high and the cost of empty shelves can outweigh the savings in storage [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say what JIT depends on (reliable suppliers, forecasting) and test it against the business in the case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing JIT as "no stock" without the conditions it needs.
- Claiming capital-intensive production is always cheaper.
- Giving benefits of technology without any cost or risk.
- Forgetting that raising productivity needs time and money.
- Calculating costs without including fixed costs.
- Ignoring the effect on staff of changes to working methods.
