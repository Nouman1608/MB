These questions cover section **3.1.4.1 Enthalpy change** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Enthalpy change study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-enthalpy-change/) and the [Enthalpy change revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-enthalpy-change-revision-notes/). Use Aᵣ values: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0.

## Questions

**1.** State the sign of ΔH and the effect on the temperature of the surroundings for:

**(a)** an exothermic reaction **[2]**

**(b)** an endothermic reaction **[2]**

**2.** Define standard enthalpy of formation, ΔfH⦵. **[3]**

**3.** Write an equation, including state symbols, that represents the standard enthalpy of formation of:

**(a)** methane, CH₄(g) **[1]**

**(b)** calcium carbonate, CaCO₃(s) **[2]**

**(c)** ethanol, C₂H₅OH(l) **[2]**

**4.** Define standard enthalpy of combustion, ΔcH⦵. **[3]**

**5.** Write an equation, including state symbols, that represents the standard enthalpy of combustion of:

**(a)** butan-1-ol, C₄H₉OH(l) **[2]**

**(b)** hydrogen **[1]**

**6.** Explain why the standard enthalpy of formation of bromine, Br₂(l), is zero but that of bromine gas, Br₂(g), is not. **[2]**

**7.** The standard enthalpy of combustion of propan-1-ol, C₃H₇OH(l), is −2021 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the energy released when 1.50 g of propan-1-ol is burned completely. **[3]**

**8.** The standard enthalpy of combustion of ethanol is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the mass of ethanol (Mᵣ = 46.0) that must be burned to release 500 kJ. **[3]**

**9.** Sketch an enthalpy profile diagram for an exothermic reaction. Label the axes, the reactants, the products, the activation energy and ΔH. **[3]**

**10.** The standard enthalpy of formation of ammonia, NH₃(g), is −46 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Give the enthalpy change for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). **[2]**

**(b)** Give the enthalpy change for 2NH₃(g) → N₂(g) + 3H₂(g). **[1]**

**11.** The standard enthalpy of formation of nitrogen monoxide, NO(g), is +90 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** State what the sign shows about the reaction ½N₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → NO(g). **[1]**

**(b)** Explain this in terms of bond breaking and bond making. **[2]**

**12.** A student writes this equation for the standard enthalpy of formation of water: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Identify two errors. **[2]**

**13.** The enthalpy change for H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l) is −286 kJ mol⁻¹ and for H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(g) is −242 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain the difference. **[2]**

**14.** Explain why the equation C(s, graphite) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) can represent two different standard enthalpy changes. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** ΔH is negative [1]; the temperature of the surroundings rises [1]

**(b)** ΔH is positive [1]; the temperature of the surroundings falls [1]. **[4]**

**2.** The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements [1]; with all reactants and products in their standard states [1]; under standard conditions (100 kPa and a stated temperature) [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** **C(s) + 2H₂(g) → CH₄(g)** [1]

**(b)** **Ca(s) + C(s) + 1½O₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)** [1 for species, 1 for state symbols, with graphite accepted for C(s)]

**(c)** **2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l)** [1 for species and balance, 1 for state symbols]. **[5]**

**4.** The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen [1]; with all reactants and products in their standard states [1]; under standard conditions [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** **C₄H₉OH(l) + 6O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l)** [1 for balanced species, 1 for state symbols]

**(b)** **H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l)** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Bromine liquid is the element in its standard state, and the enthalpy of formation of an element in its standard state is zero because no change takes place [1]; Br₂(g) is not the standard state, so forming it from Br₂(l) needs energy and the value is not zero [1]. **[2]**

**7.** n = 1.50 ÷ 60.0 = 0.0250 mol [1]; energy = 0.0250 × 2021 [1] = **50.5 kJ** released [1]. **[3]**

**8.** n = 500 ÷ 1367 = 0.3658 mol [1]; m = 0.3658 × 46.0 [1] = **16.8 g** [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Vertical axis labelled enthalpy, horizontal axis labelled progress of reaction, with reactants higher than products [1]; a curve with a hump above the reactants, and the activation energy shown from the reactants to the top of the hump [1]; a downward arrow from the reactant level to the product level labelled ΔH (negative) [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Two moles are formed, so ΔH = 2 × (−46) = **−92 kJ mol⁻¹** [1 + 1]

**(b)** The reverse reaction: **+92 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** The reaction is endothermic [1]

**(b)** More energy is needed to break the bonds in nitrogen and oxygen than is released when the bonds in nitrogen monoxide form [1]; so the reaction takes in heat from the surroundings [1]. **[3]**

**12.** The equation shows two moles of water formed; the definition requires one mole [1]; state symbols are missing [1]. **[2]**

**13.** Water vapour has higher enthalpy than liquid water [1]; the difference of 44 kJ mol⁻¹ is the energy needed to vaporise one mole of water, so less heat is released when steam forms [1]. **[2]**

**14.** It forms one mole of carbon dioxide from its elements in their standard states, so it is the standard enthalpy of formation of CO₂ [1]; it also burns one mole of carbon completely in oxygen, so it is the standard enthalpy of combustion of carbon [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing state symbols incorrectly or omitting them.
- Forming two moles of product in a formation equation.
- Omitting "standard states" or "completely" from definitions.
- A positive sign for an exothermic reaction.
- Forgetting to convert grams to moles before using ΔH.
- Quoting kJ instead of kJ mol⁻¹.
