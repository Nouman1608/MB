## Matching supply to demand

Demand is rarely steady. A business that cannot match supply to demand either has **unsold goods and idle staff** (supply above demand) or **lost sales and unhappy customers** (demand above supply). Matching the two reduces waste, protects customer service and keeps costs under control. The value is higher profit, better capacity use and a stronger reputation.

### Ways of matching supply to demand

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Outsourcing** | A task or production is given to an outside firm | Extra capacity for peaks without investment; access to specialist skills; costs become variable | Less control over quality and timing; supplier may fail; confidentiality risk; possible damage to staff morale |
| **Temporary and part-time employees** | Staff hired for peaks or flexible hours | Labour costs follow demand; can be hired quickly; fewer long-term costs | Less trained and committed; recruitment and training costs; lower loyalty; quality may vary |
| **Producing to order** | Goods are made only after a customer places an order | No unsold stock; low inventory costs; products can be customised | Customers wait; peaks of orders may overload capacity; less able to use economies of scale |
| **Holding inventory** (build up before peaks) | Produce in quiet times and store | Smooths production and staffing; goods ready at once | Storage, insurance and finance costs; risk of obsolescence |
| **Flexible capacity and pricing** | Overtime, extra shifts, off-peak discounts | Moves demand towards quiet periods | Overtime is costly; discounts reduce margins |

A seasonal retailer might combine several: temporary staff for the Christmas peak, an outsourced warehouse, and discounts in January to shift demand.

## Inventory management

**Inventory** (stock) includes raw materials, work in progress and finished goods. Holding inventory protects against supply problems and demand surges, but it costs money, so businesses try to hold the right amount.

### Influences on the amount of inventory held

- **Demand**: the faster stock sells, the more is needed, and unpredictable demand calls for a bigger buffer.
- **Lead time**: the time between ordering and receiving; longer lead times need more inventory to cover the wait.
- **Reliability of suppliers**: unreliable deliveries need more buffer.
- **Cost of holding stock**: storage space, insurance, security, finance and the risk of damage.
- **Nature of the product**: perishable or fashionable goods cannot be stored long; durable goods can.
- **Cost of running out**: if a stock-out stops production or loses key customers, the business holds more.
- **Discounts for bulk orders** encourage larger orders and higher stock.
- **Production method**: JIT keeps stocks low; JIC holds more.
- **Cash available**: stock ties up money.

### Inventory control terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lead time** | Time between placing an order and its delivery |
| **Buffer inventory** | The minimum stock held as a safety margin against unexpected demand or late delivery |
| **Re-order level** | The stock level at which a new order is placed |
| **Re-order quantity** | The amount ordered each time |
| **Maximum inventory** | The highest stock level, reached just after a delivery |

A basic relationship: **re-order level = (usage per day × lead time in days) + buffer inventory**.

### Inventory control charts

An **inventory control chart** plots stock level against time. It shows stock falling as materials are used, a re-order when the level reaches the re-order level, and stock jumping back up on delivery.

*Worked example.* A bakery uses 100 kg of flour a day. The lead time for deliveries is 4 days. The buffer is 200 kg. The re-order quantity is 800 kg.

- Re-order level = (100 × 4) + 200 = **600 kg**.
- Maximum inventory = buffer + re-order quantity = 200 + 800 = **1,000 kg**.

| Day | Stock at start of day (kg) | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1,000 | Delivery just arrived |
| 1 | 900 | |
| 2 | 800 | |
| 3 | 700 | |
| 4 | 600 | Reaches re-order level; order placed |
| 5 | 500 | |
| 6 | 400 | |
| 7 | 300 | |
| 8 | 200 | Order arrives; stock rises to 1,000 |

On a chart, this shows a saw-tooth line: a straight fall from 1,000 to 200 over 8 days, then a vertical jump back to 1,000. The horizontal line at 200 is the buffer, and the horizontal line at 600 is the re-order level. The horizontal distance between the point where the stock line crosses the re-order level (day 4) and the delivery (day 8) is the lead time.

**What if conditions change?**

- If demand rises to 130 kg a day, usage in the 4-day lead time is 130 × 4 = 520 kg. Stock at delivery would be 600 − 520 = **80 kg**, so part of the buffer has gone. The re-order level should rise: (130 × 4) + 200 = **720 kg**.
- If the lead time lengthens to 6 days at 100 kg a day, usage during the wait is 600 kg and stock at delivery would be **0 kg**. The buffer has been used up and any further delay means a stock-out.

**Re-order quantity and cost.** Annual use is 25,000 kg. Each order costs £60 to place and process, and holding one kg in average inventory costs £3 a year.

- Re-order quantity of 800 kg: orders = 25,000 ÷ 800 = 31.25; ordering cost = 31.25 × £60 = £1,875. Average inventory = 800 ÷ 2 + 200 = 600 kg; holding cost = 600 × £3 = £1,800. Total = **£3,675**.
- Re-order quantity of 1,600 kg: orders = 15.625; ordering cost = £937.50. Average inventory = 800 + 200 = 1,000 kg; holding cost = £3,000. Total = **£3,937.50**.

Larger orders mean fewer orders but higher holding costs, so the best quantity balances the two. Here 800 kg is cheaper than 1,600 kg.

**Limits of control charts:** they assume steady use and a fixed lead time; they say nothing about quality; and modern software that updates stock continuously and orders automatically (technology) is more accurate and responsive than manual charts.

## Choosing suppliers

Influences on the choice include:

- **Price** and payment terms (credit period, discounts).
- **Quality** and consistency of materials.
- **Reliability** of delivery and lead time, vital for JIT.
- **Flexibility**: the ability to cope with changes in volume or specification.
- **Location**: nearby suppliers cut transport time, cost and carbon emissions.
- **Reputation and financial stability**: will the supplier survive?
- **Ethical and environmental standards**: fair labour practices, sustainable materials. Customers and the media increasingly hold businesses responsible for their suppliers' behaviour.
- **Relationship and communication**, including data links.
- Using **one supplier** (strong relationship, bulk discounts, but dependent) or **several** (less risk, but less loyalty and smaller discounts).

## Managing the supply chain

The **supply chain** is the whole sequence of organisations and activities from raw materials to the final customer: suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, retailers. Managing it effectively means coordinating all of these to deliver the right goods, in the right quantity, at the right time and cost.

Ways to manage it well:

- **Share information**: sales data, forecasts and stock levels, often through linked IT systems.
- **Build long-term relationships** and agree clear standards, with a few trusted suppliers.
- **Monitor performance** (delivery times, defect rates) and audit suppliers.
- **Reduce lead times** and remove waste, as in lean systems.
- **Manage risk**: have backup suppliers, avoid single points of failure, and watch for ethical issues.
- **Use technology**: tracking, automated ordering and analytics.

**Value:** lower costs, fewer stock-outs, better quality, quicker response to customers, and protection of reputation. **Difficulties:** global supply chains are long and complex; one failure can halt production; relationships take time to build; ethical standards are hard to check beyond the first supplier; and tight, lean chains have little slack.

## The value of outsourcing

**Outsourcing** means paying another firm to carry out an activity, such as manufacturing, delivery, IT or payroll.

- **Benefits**: lower costs through the supplier's economies of scale; access to expertise and technology; flexibility to match demand; managers can concentrate on core strengths; no need for capital investment.
- **Drawbacks**: less control over quality and timing; dependence on the supplier; the supplier may serve competitors; confidentiality risks; possible hidden costs in managing the contract; effect on morale and local jobs; reputation risk if the supplier behaves unethically.

Outsourcing suits activities that are not core to what makes the business distinctive, or that need specialist skills the firm lacks.

## Technique for exam questions

- When asked to **interpret a chart**, state the re-order level, buffer, lead time and maximum stock, and say what would happen if demand or lead time changed.
- Use **calculations** to support arguments about cost.
- **Evaluate** by considering the product (perishable or durable), the reliability of suppliers, the cost of a stock-out against the cost of holding stock, and the importance of control over quality.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up buffer inventory (a minimum) with maximum inventory.
- Forgetting to include the buffer when calculating the re-order level.
- Treating outsourcing as simply cheaper, ignoring loss of control.
- Choosing a supplier on price only.
- Ignoring the ethical and environmental side of supply chains.
- Saying "hold less stock" is always better without discussing stock-outs.
