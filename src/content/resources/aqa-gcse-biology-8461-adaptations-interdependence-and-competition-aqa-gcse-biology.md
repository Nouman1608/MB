## What makes this section different

Most questions in 4.7.1 describe a habitat you have never studied and ask you to **suggest** or **explain**.
The specification says you should be able to do this "when provided with appropriate information" or "given
appropriate data or context". So the skill is applying a small set of ideas to new examples, and quoting the
information you are given.

## Levels of organisation

You must describe the levels from the individual organism to the whole ecosystem:

| Level | Meaning | Example (a pond) |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | A single organism | One frog |
| Population | All the organisms of one species in a habitat | All the frogs in the pond |
| Community | All the populations of different species in a habitat | Frogs, pondweed, snails, beetles, algae |
| Ecosystem | The interaction of a community with the non-living (abiotic) parts of its environment | The community plus the water, light, temperature and mud |

A **habitat** is the place where an organism lives. The key word in the ecosystem definition is
**interaction**: an ecosystem is not just a list of what is there.

## Competition

To survive and reproduce, organisms need materials from their surroundings and from other living organisms.
When a resource is in short supply, organisms compete for it.

| Organisms | Compete for |
|---|---|
| Plants | Light, space, water, mineral ions from the soil |
| Animals | Food, mates, territory |

Competition happens between individuals of the **same species** (for example two robins for one territory) and
between **different species** that need the same resource (for example two kinds of seedling growing in the same
patch of soil).

**How to "suggest the factors for which organisms are competing"**: choose resources that the information shows
are limited. In a dense forest, ground plants compete mainly for **light**. On a dry hillside, plants compete
mainly for **water**. Naming all four plant resources without linking them to the habitat often earns less
credit than naming the one or two that fit.

## Interdependence and stable communities

Within a community each species depends on other species for **food, shelter, pollination, seed dispersal** and
so on. If one species is removed it can affect the whole community. This is **interdependence**.

**Worked example 1: following a chain of effects.** In a meadow, bumblebees pollinate clover. Field mice eat bee
nests. Cats from a nearby village hunt field mice. Explain what might happen to the clover if the village cats
were removed.

```
cats removed → fewer mice are killed → mouse population rises
→ more bee nests are eaten → fewer bumblebees
→ less pollination of clover → fewer clover seeds → less clover
```

Each arrow is one step that an examiner can credit. Write the chain in full sentences in the exam, and say
**increases** or **decreases** at each step.

A **stable community** is one where all the species and environmental factors are in **balance**, so that
population sizes remain fairly constant. Populations still go up and down a little; they do not drift steadily.

## Abiotic factors

Abiotic means **non-living**. The specification lists seven, and you must explain how a change in one would
affect a community:

| Abiotic factor | How it affects organisms |
|---|---|
| Light intensity | Limits photosynthesis, so affects plant growth and the food available for animals |
| Temperature | Affects the rate of enzyme-controlled processes, such as photosynthesis and respiration |
| Moisture levels | Plants need water for photosynthesis and to stay upright; many animals need moist conditions |
| Soil pH and mineral content | Affects which plants can grow; mineral ions such as nitrates are needed for protein |
| Wind intensity and direction | Affects water loss from plants and heat loss from animals; strong wind can damage plants |
| Carbon dioxide levels for plants | A raw material for photosynthesis |
| Oxygen levels for aquatic animals | Needed for aerobic respiration; low levels kill fish and some invertebrates |

**Worked example 2: interpreting abiotic data** (invented figures)

A student counts a moss species in quadrats placed at different distances from a stream.

| Distance from stream (m) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Soil moisture (%) | 62 | 48 | 35 | 22 | 15 |
| Mean moss plants per quadrat | 40 | 31 | 18 | 6 | 1 |

A full answer: the number of moss plants decreases as distance from the stream increases. It falls from 40 to 1
per quadrat over 8 m. Soil moisture also falls with distance, so the moss may need moist soil. This shows a
**correlation**; another factor, such as light, might also change with distance, so the data do not prove that
moisture is the cause.

## Biotic factors

Biotic means **living**. The specification lists:

- **availability of food**;
- **new predators arriving**;
- **new pathogens**;
- **one species outcompeting another** so that the numbers are no longer sufficient to breed.

A well-known British example of the last point is the grey squirrel, introduced from North America, which has
replaced the native red squirrel across much of the country. Grey squirrels compete successfully with reds for food,
and red squirrels have disappeared from much of their former range.

## Adaptations

**Adaptations** are features that enable organisms to survive in the conditions in which they normally live.
The specification groups them into three kinds:

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | Features of the body | Thick fur and small ears in Arctic mammals; spines instead of leaves in cacti |
| Behavioural | What the organism does | Migrating to warmer areas in winter; desert animals staying in burrows during the hottest part of the day |
| Functional | Processes inside the body | Producing very concentrated urine to save water; lowering metabolic rate during hibernation |

**Annotated model answer**

*Question: The fennec fox lives in hot deserts. It has very large ears with many blood vessels near the surface,
pale fur, and spends the day in a burrow. Explain how these features help it survive. [4]*

> The large ears give a large surface area [1], so more heat is transferred from the blood to the surroundings,
> which cools the fox [1]. Pale fur reflects more of the Sun's radiation, reducing heating [1]. Staying in a burrow
> during the day is a behavioural adaptation that avoids the highest temperatures and reduces water loss [1].

Notice the pattern: **feature → what it does → why that helps survival in this habitat**. A list of features
with no explanation earns little.

### Extremophiles

Some organisms live in environments that are very extreme, such as at high **temperature**, **pressure** or
**salt concentration**. These organisms are called **extremophiles**. **Bacteria living in deep-sea vents** are
extremophiles: they survive high temperatures and high pressure. Their enzymes stay active at temperatures that
would denature the enzymes of most organisms.

## Common mistakes

- Defining an ecosystem as "all the organisms in an area". That is a community; an ecosystem includes the
  abiotic parts.
- Writing that plants compete for food. Plants make their own food; they compete for light, space, water and
  mineral ions.
- Giving a chain of effects with no direction words ("affects the bees") instead of "increases" or "decreases".
- Listing an adaptation without explaining how it helps in that habitat.
- Calling oxygen an abiotic factor for plants on land. The specification lists oxygen for aquatic animals and
  carbon dioxide for plants.
- Treating a correlation in data as proof of cause.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Suggest** what organisms compete for: pick the resources that are limited in the habitat described.
- **Explain** the effect of a change: write a chain with direction words, ending at the organism asked about.
- **Describe** a pattern in data: state the trend and quote figures from the start and end.
- **Explain** an adaptation: feature, what it does, why that matters in that environment.
