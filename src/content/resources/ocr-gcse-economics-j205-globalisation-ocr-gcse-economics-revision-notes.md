Condensed notes for section 4.4, Globalisation, of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0), topic 4, International trade and the global economy. Fuller explanations and worked examples are in the [globalisation study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-globalisation-ocr-gcse-economics/); practise with the [globalisation practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-globalisation-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). Figures are invented.

## What you must be able to do

- **Explain** globalisation and its driving factors.
- **Explain** how development is measured: GDP per capita, life expectancy, access to health care, technology and education.
- **Evaluate** the costs and benefits for producers, workers and consumers in **developed** countries, including economic, social and environmental sustainability.
- **Evaluate** the same for **less developed** countries.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Globalisation | The growing integration of the world's economies through flows of goods, services, capital, people and ideas |
| Multinational company | A firm that produces or operates in more than one country |
| Foreign direct investment | Money a firm invests in production in another country, such as building a factory |
| Offshoring | Moving part of production to another country to cut costs |
| Global supply chain | The stages of making a product spread across several countries |
| Trade liberalisation | Removing barriers to trade such as tariffs and quotas |
| Development | Improvement in living standards, including income, health, education and choice |
| GDP per capita | GDP ÷ population; average output per person |
| Sustainability | Meeting present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs |

## Driving factors in one line each

- **Transport**: container ships and air freight make distant production cheap.
- **ICT**: the internet lets firms run worldwide supply chains, sell online and offshore services.
- **Trade liberalisation**: trade agreements and the World Trade Organization (from 1995) lower tariffs and quotas.
- **Multinationals**: locate each stage where costs are lowest, spreading capital and technology.
- **Capital mobility**: fewer controls make investing abroad easier.
- **Emerging economies**: rising incomes create new markets and suppliers.

Exam habit: factor, then "so", then the effect on integration.

## Development indicators

| Indicator | Typical measure | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| GDP per capita | $ per person per year | An average; hides inequality, unpaid and informal work |
| Life expectancy | Years a newborn can expect to live | Affected strongly by infant deaths |
| Access to health care | Doctors or hospital beds per 1,000 people | Says nothing about quality or cost |
| Technology | Internet users per 100 people | Access is not productive use |
| Education | Literacy rate, years of schooling | Time in school is not the same as skills |

**Method: GDP per capita.** Country T has GDP of $54 billion and 18 million people: 54,000,000,000 ÷ 18,000,000 = **$3,000**. If GDP grows faster than population, GDP per capita rises; if population grows faster, it falls even when GDP rises.

Using several indicators gives a fuller picture; composite measures such as the United Nations' Human Development Index combine health, education and income.

## Costs and benefits: developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Bigger markets, cheaper inputs, economies of scale | More foreign competition; closures of high-cost firms |
| Workers | Jobs in high-skill and export sectors | Job losses where production moves abroad; low-skill wage pressure |
| Consumers | Lower prices, wider choice | Fewer local products; income loss if their own job goes |

## Costs and benefits: less developed countries

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | World markets, technology, management skills, infrastructure | Local firms undercut; dependence on a few exports or one multinational |
| Workers | Jobs often better paid than farming; training | Low pay, long hours, unsafe conditions, weak rights |
| Consumers | Cheaper goods, more choice | Local products displaced; prices set by world markets |

Extra points: profits sent back to the multinational's home country; tax revenue for the host government; skilled workers emigrating.

## Sustainability checks

- **Economic**: lasting growth and skills, or dependence that collapses if a firm leaves?
- **Social**: less poverty, or wider gaps and poor working conditions?
- **Environmental**: more emissions from transport, pollution from fast industrial growth; but possible spread of cleaner technology.

## Evaluation: "it depends on"

- Which group, and in which type of country.
- The time period: costs of job losses come first, gains from lower prices spread over years.
- Government policy: retraining, minimum standards, environmental rules.
- How gains are shared: widespread small gains versus concentrated large losses.
- The type of industry: low-skill assembly moves easily; high-skill services less so.

## Exam traps

- Defining globalisation as trade alone.
- Listing driving factors without saying how each increases integration.
- Comparing countries' development with total GDP rather than GDP per capita.
- Saying globalisation is "good" or "bad" for a whole country without naming a group.
- Forgetting the environmental strand when the question says sustainability.
- Using developed-country costs when the question asks about less developed countries.

## Self-test

1. Define globalisation.
2. Explain how lower transport costs drive globalisation.
3. What is foreign direct investment?
4. Country V has GDP of $90 billion and 25 million people. Calculate GDP per capita.
5. Country V's GDP rises by 10% and its population by 10%. What happens to GDP per capita?
6. Name the five development indicators listed in the specification.
7. Give one limitation of GDP per capita as a measure of development.
8. Give one benefit of globalisation for consumers in a developed country.
9. Give one cost of globalisation for workers in a developed country.
10. Give one benefit of a multinational's factory for workers in a less developed country.
11. Explain one way globalisation may harm environmental sustainability.
12. Why might a less developed country gain less income than expected from a multinational's factory?

### Answers

1. The growing integration of the world's economies through flows of goods, services, capital, people and ideas.
2. Cheaper shipping and air freight cut the cost of moving goods, so firms can produce far from customers and still sell profitably.
3. Investment by a firm in production in another country, such as building a factory there.
4. 90,000,000,000 ÷ 25,000,000 = **$3,600**.
5. It stays the same, because output and population grow at the same rate.
6. GDP per capita, life expectancy, access to health care, technology, education.
7. It is an average, so it hides inequality; it also misses unpaid and informal work.
8. Lower prices or a wider choice of goods.
9. Job losses when production moves to lower-cost countries, or pressure on low-skilled wages.
10. Jobs that often pay more than farming, or training and new skills.
11. Transport over longer distances adds emissions, or fast factory growth pollutes air and water.
12. Profits may be sent back to the multinational's home country, and wages may be low.
