## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by supply. **[2]**

**2.** Three egg farms sell dozens of eggs per week at the prices shown.

| Price per dozen | Farm A | Farm B | Farm C |
|---|---|---|---|
| £2 | 40 | 10 | 0 |
| £3 | 60 | 30 | 20 |
| £4 | 80 | 50 | 40 |

**(a)** Calculate market supply at each price. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the market supply curve slopes upward. **[2]**

**3.** In a town's bicycle market, say whether each event causes a movement along the supply curve or a shift, and give the direction.
**(i)** The price of bicycles rises. **(ii)** New technology cuts the cost of making each bicycle. **(iii)** A tax is placed on each bicycle made. **(iv)** Several makers leave the market. **[4]**

**4.** A potter raises the price of a mug from £20 to £25 and the quantity supplied rises from 80 to 84 a week. Calculate price elasticity of supply and say what the value shows. **[4]**

**5.** Explain two reasons why the supply of a seasonal fruit is price inelastic in the short run. **[4]**

**6.** Calculate PES and say whether supply is elastic, inelastic, unit elastic or perfectly inelastic. **[4]**

| Product | % change in price | % change in quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| P | +10 | +25 |
| Q | +10 | +10 |
| R | +20 | +4 |
| S | +5 | 0 |

**7.** The table shows the market for table lamps in a town (per month).

| Price (£) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 500 | 140 |
| 30 | 400 | 220 |
| 40 | 300 | 300 |
| 50 | 200 | 380 |
| 60 | 100 | 460 |

**(a)** Identify the price and quantity at which the market clears. **[2]**
**(b)** At £30, work out the size of the gap between demand and supply and name the situation. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the price changes from £30 and why. **[2]**

**8.** A fashion trend raises demand for lamps. At £40, £44 and £50 quantity demanded becomes 372, 332 and 272. Quantity supplied at those prices is 300, 332 and 380.
**(a)** Give the new market-clearing price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the percentage rise in price and in quantity compared with the first equilibrium. **[2]**
**(c)** Work out how total revenue changes. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain one effect on consumers and one on producers. **[2]**

**9.** A tax of £9 on each lamp made shifts the supply curve left. With the original demand, quantity demanded at £40, £44 and £50 is 300, 260 and 200. With the tax, quantity supplied is 228, 260 and 308.
**(a)** Give the market-clearing price and quantity after the tax. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how much more consumers pay per lamp and how much producers keep per lamp after paying the tax. **[3]**
**(c)** Analyse the consequences for consumers and producers. **[3]**

**10.** Explain two roles of price in allocating resources in a market economy. **[4]**

**11.** A rise in demand for a product is expected. Evaluate the importance of price elasticity of supply for producers and consumers. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Supply is the quantity of a good or service producers are willing [1] and able to sell at each price in a given period [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying only "what is sold" leaves the definition thin. Adding "willing and able" and "at each price" shows supply is a relationship, not one quantity.

**2. (a)** Add the farms at each price [1]: £2: 40 + 10 + 0 = **50**; £3: 60 + 30 + 20 = **110**; £4: 80 + 50 + 40 = **170** [1].
**(b)** A higher price raises revenue per dozen [1], so farms can cover rising costs and expand output, and more farms are attracted into supplying [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the sum at each price; a visible method lets an examiner follow your thinking even if one addition slips. Linking "higher price" to "more profitable" is what turns a description into an explanation.

**3.** (i) Movement along (extension) [1]; (ii) shift right [1]; (iii) shift left [1]; (iv) shift left [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both the type (movement or shift) and the direction, since stating only one leaves the answer incomplete.

**4.** % change in price = 5 ÷ 20 × 100 = 25% [1]; % change in quantity = 4 ÷ 80 × 100 = 5% [1]; PES = 5 ÷ 25 = **0.2** [1]. Supply is inelastic: output barely responds to the price rise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the formula and each percentage. Dividing by the new value instead of the original is a common slip that changes the final answer.

**5.** Reason 1: fruit takes a season to grow, so farmers cannot raise output when price rises [1]; planting more now would not produce fruit this season [1].
Reason 2: fruit is perishable, so it cannot be stored and released later [1], which limits the quantity available when price rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the factor, then follow it through to output. A bare mention of "time" or "storage" has not yet explained anything.

**6.** P: 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5, elastic [1]; Q: 10 ÷ 10 = 1, unit elastic [1]; R: 4 ÷ 20 = 0.2, inelastic [1]; S: 0 ÷ 5 = 0, perfectly inelastic [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a number and a label. Judge elastic or inelastic by comparing PES with 1, not by how big the percentages look.

**7. (a)** Quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at **£40 and 300 lamps** [1] [1].
**(b)** At £30 demand is 400 and supply is 220, so 400 − 220 = **180** excess demand (shortage) [1] [1].
**(c)** With a shortage, buyers compete for scarce lamps and producers can sell at higher prices [1]; price rises, which contracts demand and extends supply, until they are equal at £40 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State the amount and the type of imbalance, then explain the movement in price with a reason linked to buyers and sellers; "price goes up" alone is a description.

**8. (a)** Demand equals supply at £44 [1] and 332 lamps [1].
**(b)** Price: 4 ÷ 40 × 100 = 10% [1]; quantity: 32 ÷ 300 × 100 = 10.7% [1].
**(c)** Old revenue 40 × 300 = £12,000; new revenue 44 × 332 = £14,608 [1]; increase of £2,608 [1].
**(d)** Consumers pay a higher price (£44 instead of £40) [1]; producers sell more lamps at a higher price, so revenue rises [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out percentage changes from the original figures and show the revenue sums. Use a number from the table in (d) to keep the effects tied to the data.

**9. (a)** Demand equals supply at **£44 and 260 lamps** [1] [1].
**(b)** Consumers pay 44 − 40 = **£4** more [1]. Producers receive 44 − 9 = £35 after the tax [1], which is £5 less than the old £40 [1].
**(c)** Consumers pay more and buy fewer lamps (300 to 260, a fall of 13.3%) [1]. Producers receive less per lamp and sell fewer, so revenue after tax falls from £12,000 to 35 × 260 = £9,100 [1]. The government gains 9 × 260 = £2,340 in tax, so the burden is shared between consumers and producers [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the price consumers pay from the amount producers keep; mixing them is the usual way to lose marks. Back each consequence with a figure.

**10.** Role 1: price acts as a signal [1]. A rising price shows that consumers want more of the good, so producers move resources towards it [1].
Role 2: price rations scarce goods [1]. Those willing and able to pay get the goods, so resources go to the uses buyers value most [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name each role, then complete the chain to resources or output; the second mark in each pair depends on the link to allocation.

**11.** Definition: PES measures how much quantity supplied responds to a price change [1]. Producers with elastic supply can raise output quickly and sell more as demand rises [1]; those with inelastic supply cannot, so they gain from the higher price but miss extra sales [1]. Knowing PES helps firms plan stocks, capacity and hiring [1]. Consumers: with inelastic supply the demand rise mostly raises price, with shortages persisting [1]; with elastic supply more is available at a smaller price rise [1]. Determinants such as spare capacity, stocks and time decide which case applies [1]. Evaluation: PES is higher in the long run, so producers' advantage and consumers' loss from an inelastic market are temporary [1]. Judgement: PES matters most when demand moves suddenly and capacity is scarce; with idle capacity or stocks it matters less [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the analysis to both groups and finish with a reasoned judgement that mentions time or spare capacity; a list of advantages, however long, tends to stay at description.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not turning changes into percentages before dividing in PES.
- Using the new figure as the denominator instead of the starting figure.
- Treating a move caused by the product's own price as if the supply curve had shifted.
- Describing a new price but not the new quantity.
- Leaving out units, labels or the words "excess demand" and "excess supply".
- Explaining a cause on a diagram without saying which curve moves.
- Defining PES in the answer but not applying it to the product in the question.
- Evaluation that says "it depends" and never names the factor it hinges on.
