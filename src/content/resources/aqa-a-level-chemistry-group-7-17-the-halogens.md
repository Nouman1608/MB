## Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond. It
**decreases** down Group 7, so fluorine is the most electronegative element in the group.

Going down the group:

1. Atomic radius increases, because each element has one more shell of electrons.
2. There is more shielding from inner shells.
3. So the bonding pair is further from the nucleus and is attracted less strongly, even though the
   nuclear charge increases.

## Boiling points

| Element | Formula | State at room temperature | Electrons per molecule | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fluorine | F₂ | pale yellow gas | 18 | −188 |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | pale green gas | 34 | −34 |
| Bromine | Br₂ | red-brown liquid | 70 | 59 |
| Iodine | I₂ | grey-black solid | 106 | 184 |

Boiling point **increases** down the group.

### Worked example 1: explaining the boiling point trend

*Explain why iodine has a higher boiling point than chlorine.*

- Both are simple molecular substances made of diatomic molecules.
- I₂ has more electrons (106) than Cl₂ (34), so I₂ molecules have larger temporary dipoles.
- The van der Waals forces between I₂ molecules are therefore stronger.
- More energy is needed to overcome them, so iodine boils at a higher temperature.

The I–I covalent bond is **not** broken when iodine boils. Only the forces between molecules are overcome.

## Oxidising ability and displacement

A halogen acts as an oxidising agent by gaining electrons: X₂ + 2e⁻ → 2X⁻. Oxidising ability **decreases**
down the group. The atoms get bigger and shielding increases, so an incoming electron is attracted less
strongly.

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from a solution of its halide ions.

| Halogen added | KCl(aq) | KBr(aq) | KI(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂(aq) | no change | turns yellow/orange (Br₂) | turns brown (I₂) |
| Br₂(aq) | no change | no change | turns brown (I₂) |
| I₂(aq) | no change | no change | no change |

### Worked example 2: bromine water and potassium iodide

*Bromine water is added to potassium iodide solution. Give the observation, the ionic equation and the
half-equations.*

```
Observation:      colourless solution turns brown
Ionic equation:   Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂
Reduction:        Br₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻
Oxidation:        2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻
```

Bromine is the oxidising agent, iodide ions are the reducing agent and potassium ions are spectators.
Iodine cannot oxidise bromide ions, so I₂ added to KBr(aq) shows no reaction.

## Reducing ability of halide ions

A halide ion acts as a reducing agent by losing electrons: 2X⁻ → X₂ + 2e⁻. Reducing ability **increases**
down the group: F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻. A larger ion holds its outer electrons further from the nucleus with
more shielding, so it loses an electron more easily.

Concentrated sulfuric acid shows the trend. Every solid sodium halide first reacts in an acid–base
reaction to give the hydrogen halide:

```
NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl
```

| Halide | What happens | Observations | Sulfur product (oxidation state) |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaF, NaCl | acid–base only; HF or HCl formed | steamy white fumes | none, S stays +6 |
| NaBr | HBr formed, then 2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O | steamy fumes, orange-brown fumes of Br₂ | SO₂ (+4) |
| NaI | HI formed, then H₂SO₄ is reduced to SO₂, S and H₂S | steamy fumes, black solid or purple vapour of I₂, yellow solid (S), smell of bad eggs (H₂S) | SO₂ (+4), S (0), H₂S (−2) |

The further the oxidation state of sulfur falls, the stronger the reducing agent. Iodide ions reduce
sulfur all the way from +6 to −2.

### Worked example 3: iodide reducing sulfuric acid to hydrogen sulfide

*Write a half-equation for H₂SO₄ forming H₂S, and an overall ionic equation for its reaction with iodide
ions.*

```
Step 1  balance S:               H₂SO₄ → H₂S
Step 2  balance O with H₂O:      H₂SO₄ → H₂S + 4H₂O
Step 3  balance H with H⁺:       H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ → H₂S + 4H₂O
Step 4  balance charge with e⁻:  H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ → H₂S + 4H₂O
Step 5  iodide half-equation ×4: 8I⁻ → 4I₂ + 8e⁻
Step 6  add:                     H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ + 8I⁻ → H₂S + 4I₂ + 4H₂O
```

Check: sulfur goes from +6 to −2, a gain of 8 electrons, matching 8 iodide ions each losing one electron.

## Identifying halide ions with silver nitrate

Silver ions form precipitates with chloride, bromide and iodide ions, and the colours differ, which is
why silver nitrate solution is used:

```
Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s)
```

| Ion | Precipitate | Dilute NH₃(aq) | Concentrated NH₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| F⁻ | none (AgF is soluble) | not needed | not needed |
| Cl⁻ | white AgCl | dissolves | dissolves |
| Br⁻ | cream AgBr | does not dissolve | dissolves |
| I⁻ | yellow AgI | does not dissolve | does not dissolve |

Solubility of the silver halides in ammonia **decreases** from AgCl to AgI.

**Why acidify?** Add dilute nitric acid first to react with any carbonate or hydroxide ions. Otherwise
they would form other silver precipitates (such as silver carbonate) and give a false result. Do not use
hydrochloric acid, because it adds chloride ions.

**Why add ammonia?** White, cream and yellow are hard to tell apart, especially in small amounts.
Ammonia confirms the halide by solubility. The dissolving forms a complex ion, for example:

```
AgCl(s) + 2NH₃(aq) → [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
```

### Worked example 4: identifying a halide by mass

*0.300 g of a potassium halide, KX, is dissolved and treated with excess acidified silver nitrate. The
dried precipitate of AgX has a mass of 0.473 g. Identify X. (Ar: K = 39.1, Ag = 107.9)*

```
Moles KX = moles AgX
0.300 / (39.1 + x) = 0.473 / (107.9 + x)
0.300(107.9 + x) = 0.473(39.1 + x)
32.37 + 0.300x = 18.494 + 0.473x
13.876 = 0.173x
x = 80.2
```

Ar(X) ≈ 80, so X is **bromine**, and the precipitate would be cream.

## Chlorine and water

Chlorine reacts with water in a reversible reaction to form chloride and chlorate(I) ions:

```
Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO      or      Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ 2H⁺ + Cl⁻ + ClO⁻
```

This is **disproportionation**: chlorine is oxidised (0 to +1 in ClO⁻) and reduced (0 to −1 in Cl⁻) in the
same reaction.

In bright sunlight, chlorine water decomposes to chloride ions and oxygen:

```
2Cl₂ + 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4Cl⁻ + O₂
```

### Chlorine in water treatment

Chlorine is added to drinking water to kill bacteria. Chlorine is toxic, and it can react with organic
matter in water to form chlorinated organic compounds that may be harmful. The amounts used are small and
controlled. The specification asks you to appreciate that the health benefits of chlorination outweigh
its toxic effects, because it prevents water-borne diseases.

More generally, society weighs advantages and disadvantages before adding any chemical to a water supply.
Sodium fluoride is one example: it is added in some areas to reduce tooth decay, but people disagree over
adding a substance to everyone's water without individual consent.

## Chlorine and cold, dilute sodium hydroxide

```
Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O
Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O
```

This is also disproportionation. The solution contains sodium chlorate(I), NaClO, and is used as
**bleach**, for disinfecting and cleaning.

### Worked example 5: mass of sodium chlorate(I)

*14.2 g of chlorine reacts completely with cold, dilute sodium hydroxide. Calculate the maximum mass of
NaClO formed. (Mr: Cl₂ = 71.0, NaClO = 74.5)*

```
n(Cl₂)  = 14.2 / 71.0 = 0.200 mol
n(NaClO) = n(Cl₂) = 0.200 mol      (1 : 1 ratio)
mass    = 0.200 × 74.5 = 14.9 g
```

## Common errors

- Saying covalent bonds break when halogens boil.
- Comparing van der Waals forces by "size" without linking to the number of electrons.
- Writing that bromide reduces sulfuric acid to H₂S; only iodide reaches H₂S.
- Calling the NaCl reaction redox. No oxidation state changes.
- Acidifying with HCl before the silver nitrate test.
- Saying AgBr is insoluble in all ammonia. It dissolves in concentrated ammonia.
- Missing the word "disproportionation" when chlorine forms Cl⁻ and ClO⁻.
