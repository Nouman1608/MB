## Questions

**1.** State the particles involved in each type of strong bond.

**(a)** Ionic bonding **[1]**
**(b)** Covalent bonding **[1]**
**(c)** Metallic bonding **[1]**

**2.** Name the type of bonding in each substance: lithium fluoride, oxygen (O₂), zinc, carbon dioxide. **[4]**

**3.** Explain how a covalent bond holds the two atoms together in a fluorine molecule, F₂. **[3]**

**4.** Calcium oxide has ionic bonding. Calcium metal has metallic bonding. Give one similarity and two
differences between these two types of bonding. **[3]**

**5.** A student writes: "Solid potassium chloride is made of KCl molecules. Each K⁺ ion is bonded to one
Cl⁻ ion." Explain what is wrong with this statement. **[3]**

**6.** Four structure diagrams are described.

- **W:** separate groups of three atoms, each joined by two lines.
- **X:** a regular grid of two kinds of particle, labelled 2+ and 2−, alternating, cut off at the edges.
- **Y:** one kind of positive particle in rows, with e⁻ symbols between them.
- **Z:** one kind of atom, every atom joined to four others by lines.

**(a)** Which diagram shows an ionic compound? **[1]**
**(b)** Give two features of that diagram that show it is a giant ionic structure. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why diagram Y does not show an ionic compound, even though it contains charged particles. **[2]**

**7.** The diagram shows part of one layer of an ionic compound. Ca = calcium ion, Br = bromide ion.

```
Br   Br   Br   Br
   Ca        Ca
Br   Br   Br   Br
   Ca        Ca
```

Work out the empirical formula. Use the charges Ca²⁺ and Br⁻ to show your formula is neutral. **[3]**

**8.** A ball and stick model of part of an ionic lattice contains 6 aluminium ions (Al³⁺) and 9 oxide ions
(O²⁻).

**(a)** Work out the empirical formula of the compound. **[2]**
**(b)** Show that the charges in your formula balance. **[1]**
**(c)** Give one limitation of a ball and stick model for showing an ionic lattice. **[1]**

**9.** A model of magnesium oxide is built in the same arrangement as sodium chloride, as a 3 × 3 × 3 cube of 27
ions. It contains 14 oxide ions and 13 magnesium ions. A student writes the formula Mg₁₃O₁₄.

**(a)** Explain why the model gives an uneven count. **[2]**
**(b)** Magnesium is in Group 2 and oxygen is in Group 6. Use the ion charges to give the correct empirical
formula. **[2]**

**10.** A dot and cross diagram is often used for sodium chloride.

**(a)** State one thing a dot and cross diagram shows well. **[1]**
**(b)** Give two limitations of a dot and cross diagram as a representation of solid sodium chloride. **[2]**

**11.** Sodium chloride can be represented by a ball and stick model, a two-dimensional diagram and a
three-dimensional space-filling model. Evaluate these three representations of the giant ionic structure of
sodium chloride. **[7]**

**12.** Potassium (Group 1) reacts with sulfur (Group 6) to form potassium sulfide.

**(a)** Name the type of bonding in potassium sulfide and give a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the charge on a potassium ion and on a sulfide ion. **[2]**
**(c)** A model of part of the lattice contains 12 potassium ions and 6 sulfide ions. Work out the empirical
formula. **[2]**
**(d)** Describe the structure of solid potassium sulfide and the forces holding it together. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Oppositely charged **ions** [1]
**(b)** **Atoms** that share **pairs of electrons** [1]
**(c)** **Atoms** that share **delocalised electrons** [1]
*Examiner insight:* For (b), "atoms sharing electrons" without "pairs" may not score; in (c) the key word is "delocalised", and naming ions alone for metallic bonding is not the answer the specification gives.

**2.** Lithium fluoride: **ionic** [1]. Oxygen: **covalent** [1]. Zinc: **metallic** [1]. Carbon dioxide:
**covalent** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each substance is a separate mark, so one slip costs only one mark -- but oxygen is the usual trap: a non-metallic element is covalent, not "no bonding".

**3.** The two atoms **share a pair of electrons** (one from each atom) [1]. There is an **electrostatic
attraction** [1] between the shared pair (negative) and the **nuclei of both atoms** (positive) [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Share electrons" earns the first mark at most; the explanation marks need the attraction and what it acts between -- "both nuclei", not "both atoms".

**4.** Similarity: both are **strong electrostatic attractions between opposite charges** [1]. Differences: in
calcium oxide electrons are **transferred**, forming **positive and negative ions** [1]; in calcium the outer
electrons are **delocalised** and shared through the structure, with **no negative ions** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each difference needs both sides stated or clearly implied; "ionic has ions" with nothing said about the metal scores nothing for that point.

**5.** Potassium chloride is a **giant structure (lattice) of ions**, not molecules [1]. Each K⁺ ion is attracted
to **all the Cl⁻ ions around it**, not to one [1], because the electrostatic forces act **in all directions**
in the lattice [1].
*Examiner insight:* You must correct both errors in the statement -- the word "molecules" and the one-to-one bonding -- to reach full marks.

**6. (a)** **X** [1]
**(b)** Any two: **two types of particle with opposite charges** (ions) [1]; a **regular, repeating
(alternating) arrangement** that continues beyond the drawing, so it is giant / has no separate molecules [1].
**(c)** Y has only **positive** particles and **delocalised electrons** [1]; there are **no negative ions**, so
it shows **metallic** bonding [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (c) "it has charges" is not the issue; marks come from saying what is missing (negative ions) and naming the bonding it does show.

**7.** Counting: 8 bromide ions and 4 calcium ions, so Ca : Br = 4 : 8 = 1 : 2 [1]. Empirical formula
**CaBr₂** [1]. Charges: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 [1]
*Examiner insight:* A correct formula with no count shown can lose the method mark; write the metal first and use a subscript, not "CaBr2" written as "Ca1Br2" or "Br2Ca".

**8. (a)** Al : O = 6 : 9 [1] = 2 : 3, so **Al₂O₃** [1]
**(b)** 2 × (+3) = +6 and 3 × (−2) = −6, total **0** [1]
**(c)** Any one: the sticks suggest **bonds between particular pairs** of ions, but forces act **in all
directions**; ions are shown **far apart** when they are closely packed; ion **sizes** may not be to scale;
it shows only a **small part** of the lattice [1]
*Examiner insight:* "It isn't realistic" gets no credit in (c); name the feature (sticks, gaps, sizes or fragment) and say why it misleads.

**9. (a)** The model is a **small piece** of the lattice, so ions at the corners, edges and faces do not split
evenly between the two types [1]. A real crystal contains so many ions that the pattern repeats and the
numbers are **equal** [1].
**(b)** Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ [1], so a 1 : 1 ratio is neutral: **MgO** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the charges must be stated or used to earn the first mark; an answer of MgO with no reasoning scores only the final mark.

**10. (a)** Which electrons are **transferred** / the **charge** on each ion [1]
**(b)** Any two: it shows only **one pair of ions**, not the giant lattice [1]; it does not show the **3D
arrangement** or that forces act **in all directions** [1]; it does not show the **relative sizes** of the ions.
*Examiner insight:* Two limitations must be two different ideas; "doesn't show 3D" and "doesn't show the shape" are likely to be treated as the same point.

**11.** Credit any valid points, up to the maximum:
Ball and stick: shows the **3D arrangement** and the alternating pattern of ions [1]; but the **sticks suggest
bonds between pairs**, when the forces act in all directions, and ions are shown **too far apart** [1].
2D diagram: shows the **alternating pattern** and the **1 : 1 ratio** clearly [1]; but it shows **only one
layer**, so ions above and below (and forces in those directions) are missing [1].
Space-filling: shows the **relative sizes** of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and how **closely packed** they are [1]; but
**inner ions are hidden** and no forces are shown, so the arrangement is hard to see [1].
Conclusion: every model shows only a **tiny fragment** of a lattice with an enormous number of ions, so more than
one representation is needed to show the whole picture [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs strengths and limitations of each model plus a judgement; listing only limitations, or giving no conclusion, caps the mark well below 7.

**12. (a)** **Ionic** [1]: potassium is a **metal** and sulfur is a **non-metal** [1]
**(b)** Potassium ion **1+** (K⁺) [1]; sulfide ion **2−** (S²⁻) [1]
**(c)** K : S = 12 : 6 = 2 : 1 [1], so **K₂S** [1]
**(d)** A **giant lattice of K⁺ and S²⁻ ions** in a regular arrangement [1], held by **strong electrostatic forces
of attraction between oppositely charged ions acting in all directions** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) is marked from the count, so a wrong charge in (b) does not cost marks there; the ratio must still be simplified, and "K₁₂S₆" scores only the method mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "sharing electrons" for covalent bonding without "pair" or without saying what the pair is attracted
  to.
- Classifying a non-metallic element such as oxygen or bromine as having no bonding.
- Classifying an alloy as ionic because it contains two different elements.
- Using the word "molecule" for sodium chloride or any other ionic compound.
- Describing ionic forces as acting between one pair of ions rather than in all directions.
- Reading positive particles among electrons as ionic: an ionic diagram must have negative ions.
- Generic limitations ("not realistic", "not accurate") with no specific feature named.
- Leaving an empirical formula as a raw count (K₁₂S₆) or writing the non-metal first.
- On "evaluate" questions, giving only drawbacks and no judgement.
