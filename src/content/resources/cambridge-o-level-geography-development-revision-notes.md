## Indicators

- **Economic**: GNP (GNI) per capita, in US dollars. Weakness: an
  average; hides inequality and informal work.
- **Social**: literacy rate, life expectancy, infant mortality, access
  to safe water, doctors per 1,000 people.
- **Composite**: HDI = life expectancy + education + income, scored
  0 to 1. Better because one weak measure cannot distort it, but still
  a national average.
- Always say **per capita** when comparing living standards.

## Why inequality exists

| Scale | Reasons |
|---|---|
| Between countries | Physical (landlocked, hazards, harsh climate, few resources); economic (reliance on primary exports, debt, low investment); political/historical (colonial legacy, conflict, poor governance); social (poor health and education) |
| Within countries | Core–periphery: investment, infrastructure and jobs concentrate in the capital and ports; young people migrate to the core; rural areas lag (e.g. south-east v north-east Brazil; coastal v interior China) |

**Inequality within Pakistan** (a home example of core–periphery):
- Core: the Punjab plains and the big cities (Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad–Rawalpindi), with most
  industry, universities, specialist hospitals and investment.
- Periphery: much of Balochistan and remote mountain districts, where distance, rugged relief,
  low rainfall and weaker infrastructure limit jobs and services.
- Result: higher literacy and better access to health care in the core; young people migrate from
  the periphery to the cities, which can widen the gap.

## Worked example: using indicators

*Table 1 gives GNI per capita, life expectancy and literacy for Countries P and Q. Compare their
levels of development.* Method:

1. State the overall judgement: "P is more developed than Q on all three indicators."
2. Quote paired figures with units for each indicator.
3. Explain one link, for example: higher income pays for health care and schools, which raises
   life expectancy and literacy.
4. Add a limitation: these are national averages, so they hide differences between rich and poor,
   and between core and periphery.

## Sectors

| Sector | Activity | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting raw materials | Farming, fishing, mining, forestry |
| Secondary | Manufacturing, processing | Car assembly, food processing, construction |
| Tertiary | Services | Retail, health, education, tourism |
| Quaternary | Knowledge and research | Software, R&D, data services |

**Shift with development**: primary falls (mechanisation of farming)
→ secondary rises (industrialisation, factory wages, TNC investment)
→ tertiary and quaternary dominate (rising incomes increase demand for
services; manufacturing automated or relocated abroad).

## Globalisation

- Definition: growing interconnection of economies and cultures through
  trade, investment, migration and information.
- Drivers: container shipping and air freight; ICT and the internet;
  TNCs; trade agreements and government incentives.
- Positives: jobs, investment, skills, cheaper goods, exports.
- Negatives: low wages, profits leave the host, local firms lose out,
  factory closures when firms move, pollution.

## TNC case study checklist

- Headquarters (city and country).
- Two or more named overseas sites and what each does.
- Reasons for locating there (markets, tariffs, labour, incentives).
- One positive and one negative impact on a host country, with detail.

Example outline: Toyota, HQ Toyota City (Japan); car plant at
Burnaston near Derby and engine plant at Deeside (UK); plant at
Georgetown, Kentucky (USA).

**Toyota in Pakistan**: Toyota cars are assembled at Port Qasim, Karachi, by Indus Motor
Company, a joint venture between a Pakistani group and Toyota. Reasons: access to the domestic
market and import duties that make locally assembled cars cheaper than imports. Benefits: jobs,
skills and work for local parts suppliers. Drawbacks: many components and the brand are imported,
and some profit returns to Japan.

## Interdependence: how countries depend on each other

- **Trade**: lower-income countries often export primary goods and import manufactured goods.
- **Investment**: TNCs move capital and technology between countries.
- **Migration and remittances**: for example workers from Pakistan in the Gulf states send money
  home, linking the two economies.
- **Aid and loans**: help development but can create debt.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Development | Improvement in people's standard of living and quality of life |
| GNP per capita | Total value of a country's goods and services plus income from abroad, divided by population |
| HDI | Composite index of health, education and income |
| Core–periphery | Wealthy central region contrasted with poorer outer regions |
| TNC | Company operating in more than one country |
| Deindustrialisation | Decline of manufacturing employment in a country or region |
| Multiplier effect | New investment creates further jobs and spending in linked businesses |
| Literacy rate | Percentage of adults who can read and write |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths of children under one year old per 1,000 live births |
| Employment structure | Proportion of workers in each economic sector |
| Interdependence | Countries relying on one another through trade, investment, aid and migration |
| Globalisation | Growing interconnection of economies and cultures around the world |

## Exam traps

- Comparing countries with total GNP instead of GNP **per capita**.
- Describing an indicator without saying what it shows about quality of life.
- A TNC case study with only the headquarters named and no overseas sites.
- One-sided TNC impacts; Cambridge asks for benefits **and** disadvantages.
- Saying the primary sector "disappears" with development; it shrinks as a share of jobs.

## Self-test

1. Name one economic, one social and one composite indicator.
2. Why can GNP per capita be misleading?
3. Give one physical reason for inequality between countries.
4. Why does primary employment fall as a country develops?
5. Give two drivers of globalisation.
6. What is deindustrialisation?
7. Give one negative impact of a TNC on a host country.
8. Give one reason why Balochistan is part of Pakistan's periphery.
9. Why might a TNC assemble cars in Pakistan rather than export finished cars there?
10. Explain how remittances make countries interdependent.
11. Why is HDI usually a better measure of development than a single indicator?

**Answers:** 1. GNP per capita; life expectancy or literacy; HDI.
2. It is an average, so it hides inequality and leaves out the
informal economy and subsistence production. 3. Any one: landlocked
position; frequent natural hazards; harsh climate; few resources.
4. Farming is mechanised so fewer workers are needed, and better-paid
jobs attract people into factories and services. 5. Any two: cheaper
transport such as container ships; ICT and the internet; TNCs; trade
agreements. 6. The decline of manufacturing employment. 7. Any one:
profits return to the home country; low wages or poor conditions;
local firms out-competed; jobs lost if the factory closes. 8. Any one: remoteness; rugged relief
and low rainfall; weaker roads and services; less investment and fewer jobs. 9. To reach the
domestic market and avoid import duties that make imported cars more expensive. 10. Migrant
workers earn in the receiving country and send money to the source country, so both economies are
linked: one depends on the labour, the other on the income. 11. It combines health, education and
income, so one weak measure cannot give a misleading picture on its own.
