## What the specification asks for

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| "How UK businesses compete internationally, offering: better designs; higher quality products at lower prices" | "demonstrate knowledge and understanding of globalisation and the benefits and drawbacks that it offers UK businesses" |
| Exchange rates | "understand the impact of exchange rates on the profit and sales of those businesses that import and/or export" |

The specification is clear that students "will not be asked to calculate exchange rate conversions". You will need to reason about direction: does a change make the business's costs, prices, sales and profit rise or fall?

## Globalisation in a business context

Globalisation is the process by which the world's economies become more closely connected, so that goods, services, money and information move between countries more easily. For a UK business it shows up in practical ways:

- selling to customers abroad (exporting), often through a website;
- buying materials, components or finished goods from abroad (importing);
- facing foreign rivals in its home market;
- having parts of its product made overseas.

Faster shipping, cheaper communication and e-commerce have all made these easier, even for small firms.

## How UK businesses compete internationally

The specification names two routes: better designs, and higher quality products at lower prices.

### Better designs

Design covers how a product looks, how it works and how easy it is to use. A distinctive design makes a product harder to copy and gives customers a reason to choose it over a cheaper foreign alternative. Design-led competition suits businesses that cannot match the low labour costs of some other countries but can offer something different. It often supports a premium price.

### Higher quality products at lower prices

Customers abroad compare value: what they get for what they pay. A business wins sales if it offers better quality than rivals at the same price, or the same quality at a lower price. Ways to achieve this include:

- cutting waste and improving efficiency in production, so unit costs fall without lowering standards;
- buying components more cheaply, including from abroad;
- producing in larger quantities, so fixed costs are spread over more units;
- building quality into the process so that fewer faulty items are made.

### Choosing a route

| Business situation | Likely route |
|---|---|
| Small craft producer with skilled staff | Better design and quality, premium price |
| Large manufacturer with high output | Quality at a lower price through efficiency |
| Brand known for heritage or style | Design, using the brand's reputation abroad |
| Product easily copied | Price and efficiency, since design alone will not protect it |

Many businesses combine routes, but trying to be both the best-designed and the cheapest at once is hard, because design and quality usually add cost.

## Benefits and drawbacks for UK businesses

### Benefits

- **Bigger market:** exporting raises potential sales far beyond the UK.
- **Spreading risk:** if UK demand falls, sales abroad can keep revenue up.
- **Cheaper supplies:** importing materials or components can lower costs.
- **Lower unit costs:** higher output from a larger market spreads fixed costs.
- **New ideas:** contact with other markets brings new products, designs and methods.

### Drawbacks

- **More competition at home:** foreign businesses can sell into the UK, sometimes at lower prices.
- **Longer supply chains:** goods from abroad take longer to arrive and can be disrupted.
- **Costs of selling abroad:** shipping, translation, adapting products and packaging to different rules and tastes.
- **Exchange rate risk:** changes in currency values can cut profit or sales.
- **Reputation risk:** an overseas supplier with poor working conditions can damage the UK business's image.

A strong answer weighs benefits against drawbacks for the specific business. A small café with no imports or exports is barely affected; a manufacturer that imports parts and exports half its output is affected greatly.

## Exchange rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. If the pound **strengthens** (appreciates), one pound buys more foreign currency. If it **weakens** (depreciates), one pound buys less.

### The reasoning, step by step

**Pound strengthens: effect on a UK exporter**
1. Overseas customers need more of their own currency to buy the same UK product.
2. The product looks more expensive abroad.
3. Some customers buy from rivals instead, so sales may fall.
4. If the exporter cuts its pound price to keep its overseas price unchanged, it keeps sales but earns less per item.

**Pound strengthens: effect on a UK importer**
1. Each pound buys more foreign currency.
2. Imported materials or goods cost fewer pounds.
3. Costs fall, so profit per item rises, or the business can lower its prices to win sales.

A weaker pound reverses both: exporters become more competitive abroad, and importers face higher costs. A memory aid many students use is SPICED: Strong Pound, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer.

### Worked example: an exporter's choice

Figures invented. Rydal Audio makes speakers in the UK and sells many to customers in Europe. Each speaker costs £60 to make and Rydal receives £100 for it, a profit of £40. The pound strengthens, so to European buyers the speaker now seems dearer.

Rydal has two options:

| Option | Effect on sales | Effect on profit per speaker |
|---|---|---|
| Keep receiving £100 per speaker | The price abroad rises, so sales may fall | Stays at £40 |
| Accept £90 per speaker so the price abroad stays about the same | Sales are protected | Falls to £90 − £60 = £30 |

If Rydal normally sells 2,000 speakers abroad:

- Option 2 gives profit of 2,000 × £30 = £60,000, compared with 2,000 × £40 = £80,000 before.
- Option 1 keeps £40 per speaker, so Rydal does better than Option 2 as long as sales stay above £60,000 ÷ £40 = 1,500 speakers.

So Rydal keeps its £100 price if it expects to lose fewer than 500 sales, and accepts the lower price if its customers are likely to switch to rivals. If Rydal imports its speaker drivers, the stronger pound also lowers its costs, which cushions the effect. These calculations are all in pounds; no currency conversion is needed.

## Exam technique

- **Identify or state:** name a benefit, drawback or effect.
- **Explain:** use the chain, for example stronger pound → exports dearer abroad → fewer sales.
- **Analyse:** say whether the business imports, exports or both, and follow the effect to profit.
- **Evaluate:** weigh size: how much does the business trade abroad, how price-sensitive are its customers, and does it import inputs as well as export products? Then decide.

## Common mistakes

- Converting currencies. The specification says you will not be asked to.
- Getting the direction wrong: a stronger pound makes exports dearer, not cheaper.
- Saying globalisation only brings competition. It also brings markets and cheaper supplies.
- Forgetting that a business can import and export at the same time.
- Ignoring the business's competitive route: a design-led business is less sensitive to price changes.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do UK businesses compete internationally according to the specification?**
By offering better designs, and by offering higher quality products at lower prices.

**Do I have to calculate exchange rate conversions?**
No. The specification states that students will not be asked to calculate exchange rate conversions. You must understand the effect on the profit and sales of importers and exporters.

**Does a weaker pound help or hurt a UK business?**
It helps exporters, whose goods become cheaper abroad, and hurts importers, whose costs rise. A business that does both feels a smaller overall effect.
