> **These are original tasks written for Marlbridge**, for practising the skills
> the fieldwork internal assessment (IA) depends on. The IA is a written report
> on your own investigation, not an examination, so these tasks do **not**
> replicate its criteria or mark scheme. The tariffs are a Marlbridge practice
> convention. Always follow the current subject guide and your teacher's
> instructions, which take priority over anything here.

Related: [Fieldwork study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-fieldwork-study-guide/) and [Fieldwork revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-fieldwork-internal-assessment-revision-notes/). All data are invented.

## Section A: The question and hypotheses

**1.** A student proposes the fieldwork question "What is the impact of tourism on the town?" Explain
**two** reasons why this is a weak question for an individual investigation, and rewrite it as a
focused, testable question. **[3]**

**2.** For the question "How does pedestrian density change with distance from the centre of a named
town?", write a hypothesis and a null hypothesis. **[2]**

**3.** Outline how a geographical model gives a student an expected pattern to test in an
investigation of downstream change along a river. **[2]**

## Section B: Methods and sampling

**4.** A student wants to compare environmental quality across six land-use zones of a town, using a
bipolar survey sheet. She has one afternoon. Recommend a sampling strategy and justify it. **[4]**

**5.** To study residents' views of a new shopping centre, a student hands questionnaires to people
sitting in the café inside the centre. Identify the source of bias and suggest a better sampling
approach. **[3]**

**6.** A student plans to measure river velocity by timing a float over 10 m. State **two** problems a
pilot study might reveal, and how each could be fixed. **[2]**

**7.** An investigation combines a beach profile survey with a visitor questionnaire. Identify **two**
hazards and **two** ethical issues the student should plan for. **[4]**

## Section C: Data presentation and analysis

**8.** A student tests the hypothesis that environmental quality increases with distance from the
centre of a town. The table shows her invented data for ten sites.

| Site | Distance from centre (km) | Environmental quality score |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0.2 | -6 |
| B | 0.5 | -4 |
| C | 0.8 | -5 |
| D | 1.1 | -1 |
| E | 1.4 | 2 |
| F | 1.7 | -3 |
| G | 2.0 | 5 |
| H | 2.3 | 3 |
| I | 2.6 | 7 |
| J | 3.0 | 6 |

**(a)** Calculate Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, rs = 1 - 6(sum of d squared) / n(n squared -
1). Show your ranks. **[5]**

**(b)** The critical value for n = 10 at the 5 per cent significance level (two-tailed) is 0.648.
Interpret your result. **[2]**

**(c)** Site F lies beside a busy ring road. Explain how the student should treat this result in her
analysis. **[2]**

**9.** Suggest the most suitable way to present each data set, and justify each choice. **[3]**

**(a)** Mean pebble length at five sites downstream.\
**(b)** Land use of every building along a 1 km transect.\
**(c)** The origin of 80 visitors to a national park, by country.

**10.** A student counts convenience shops in four zones of a town, expecting them to be spread evenly.
Observed counts are 34, 22, 14 and 10. Carry out a chi-squared test, chi-squared = sum of (O - E)
squared / E, and interpret it. The critical value for 3 degrees of freedom at the 5 per cent level is
7.81. **[4]**

## Section D: Conclusion and evaluation

**11.** A student's evaluation ends: "I should have collected more data." Explain why this is weak, and
rewrite it as a strong evaluation point for the pedestrian-count study in Question 2. **[3]**

**12.** Using a questionnaire survey as the example, distinguish between accuracy, reliability and
validity. **[3]**

**13.** Read this method summary from a river investigation.

> Five sites were chosen at 200 m intervals downstream from the footbridge. At each site, width was
> measured once with a tape, depth was measured at three points, and velocity was found by timing one
> orange over 10 m. All data were collected on one morning, the day after heavy rain.

Evaluate the methods used. **[8]**

**14.** Plan an investigation for this aim: **"To investigate how air temperature varies across the
school site."** Include a question, a hypothesis, the data to collect, a sampling strategy, one
presentation method, one statistical test and one likely limitation. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two reasons [2]: "impact" is too broad to measure in one investigation; it names no
measurable variable; "the town" gives no spatial frame for sampling; it could not be answered with
data one student can collect. A rewrite that names a place and measurable variables [1], for
example: *"How do residents' perceptions of tourism vary with distance from the main attraction in a
named town?"*

**2.** Hypothesis: pedestrian density decreases with distance from the town centre [1]. Null
hypothesis: there is no relationship between pedestrian density and distance from the town centre
[1].

**3.** A model such as the Bradshaw model predicts how channel characteristics change downstream, for
example width, depth and velocity increasing while sediment size decreases [1]. This gives the
student specific expected trends to write as hypotheses and to compare the data against [1].

**4.** Stratified systematic sampling [1]: stratify by the six zones so that each is represented [1],
then take survey points at regular intervals within each zone, for example every 100 m along the
main road [1]. Justification: it guarantees coverage of every zone, is quick to carry out in one
afternoon and reduces the bias of choosing "interesting" spots [1]. Credit stratified random
sampling with a justified reason.

**5.** Bias: people in the café are already users of the centre, so they are more likely to view it
positively [1], and they are not representative of all residents, including those who never visit [1]. Better: stratified or systematic
sampling of residents at several locations, for example every fifth person at points across
different neighbourhoods, at different times of day [1].

**6.** Any two, each with a fix [1 each]: the float catches on vegetation or the bank, so choose a
clearer section or retime the run; wind moves the float, so use a float that sits low in the water,
such as an orange; the start and finish are hard to judge, so mark them with ranging poles and use
one observer at each end.

**7.** Hazards [2], for example: incoming tides or waves; slippery rocks or soft sand; sun exposure.
Ethical issues [2], for example: gaining informed consent from questionnaire respondents; keeping
answers anonymous; not surveying children without adult permission; avoiding damage to dunes or
disturbance to wildlife.

**8. (a)** Ranks (largest = 1) [2]:

| Site | Rank distance | Rank score | d | d squared |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 10 | 10 | 0 | 0 |
| B | 9 | 8 | 1 | 1 |
| C | 8 | 9 | -1 | 1 |
| D | 7 | 6 | 1 | 1 |
| E | 6 | 5 | 1 | 1 |
| F | 5 | 7 | -2 | 4 |
| G | 4 | 3 | 1 | 1 |
| H | 3 | 4 | -1 | 1 |
| I | 2 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| J | 1 | 2 | -1 | 1 |

Sum of d squared = 12 [1]. rs = 1 - (6 x 12) / (10 x 99) = 1 - 72 / 990 [1] = 1 - 0.073 = **0.93**
(0.927) [1].

**(b)** 0.93 is a very strong positive correlation: environmental quality rises with distance from the
centre [1]. It exceeds the critical value of 0.648, so the result is significant at the 5 per cent
level and the null hypothesis can be rejected; the hypothesis is supported, though correlation does
not prove cause [1].

**(c)** Keep the result rather than deleting it [1], and explain it as an anomaly: traffic noise,
fumes and litter beside the ring road lower the score, which shows that local factors as well as
distance affect environmental quality [1].

**9.** One mark each for a suitable method with a reason: **(a)** a line graph or bar chart against
distance, or proportional symbols on a map, to show change downstream; **(b)** a land-use transect
map or strip diagram, to show the sequence of uses along the line; **(c)** a flow-line map or
proportional arrows, or a bar chart by country, to show where visitors come from.

**10.** Expected value = 80 / 4 = 20 in each zone [1]. (34 - 20) squared / 20 = 9.8; (22 - 20) squared
/ 20 = 0.2; (14 - 20) squared / 20 = 1.8; (10 - 20) squared / 20 = 5.0; total = **16.8** [1].
Degrees of freedom = 4 - 1 = 3 [1]. 16.8 exceeds 7.81, so the null hypothesis that shops are evenly
spread is rejected at the 5 per cent level: the shops are concentrated in some zones [1].

**11.** It is generic: it does not say what data, why the shortage mattered or how more data would
change the conclusion [1]. Strong version [2], for example: *"Pedestrians were counted for only
10 minutes at each site on one weekday morning, so a single bus arriving could distort a count;
repeating counts at three times of day on a weekday and at the weekend would show whether the
pattern holds and make the conclusion more reliable."*

**12.** One mark each. **Accuracy:** how close answers are to the truth, for example whether
respondents report their travel time correctly. **Reliability:** whether repeating the survey would
give similar results, for example with a larger sample or on another day. **Validity:** whether the
questions actually measure what the investigation asks, for example a question about shopping
frequency does not measure satisfaction with the shops.

**13.** Indicative content:

- Systematic sampling at 200 m intervals is quick and avoids choosing sites by convenience, but five
  sites is a small sample for a statistical test and may miss local changes, such as a tributary
  junction.
- One width reading per site cannot be checked; repeats would improve reliability.
- Three depth points may miss the deepest part of the channel; more points across a cross-section
  would give a more accurate cross-sectional area.
- A single timed float is strongly affected by wind and eddies; three to five repeats, or a flow meter
  at a set depth, would improve accuracy and reliability.
- Collecting the day after heavy rain gives higher discharge than usual, so the data describe one
  flow condition only and may not be valid for the river's typical behaviour; comparing with a
  second visit in normal flow would test this.
- Starting at the footbridge suggests sites were chosen for access, which may bias the stretch
  studied.

Marlbridge practice bands: **7-8** a balanced evaluation of most methods, each weakness linked to its
effect on accuracy, reliability or validity, with specific, justified improvements; **4-6** several
relevant weaknesses with some effect or improvement explained; **1-3** a list of weaknesses or
generic improvements with little explanation.

**14.** One mark for each element that is appropriate and fits the aim [7], plus one for overall
coherence, where the method could genuinely answer the question [1]. For example:

- **Question:** How does air temperature vary between open, shaded and built-up parts of the school
  site?
- **Hypothesis:** Air temperature is higher next to buildings and hard surfaces than in shaded or
  grassed areas.
- **Data:** air temperature with a digital thermometer at 1 m height; surface type; shade (yes or no);
  time of reading.
- **Sampling:** stratified by surface type, with systematic points on a grid within each type, all
  read within a 30-minute window to limit change in the weather.
- **Presentation:** an isoline map of temperature over a site plan.
- **Test:** Mann-Whitney U to compare temperatures over hard and grassed surfaces, or Spearman's rank
  for temperature against distance from the main building.
- **Limitation:** readings taken on one day reflect that day's weather; repeat on a cloudy and a sunny
  day.

## Where marks are usually lost

- A question too broad to answer with data one person can collect.
- Methods described but not justified, or sampling chosen for convenience.
- Ranking errors in Spearman's rank, especially reversing the direction for one variable.
- Treating a significant result as proof of cause.
- Deleting anomalies instead of explaining them.
- Generic improvements, such as "more data", without saying how they would change the conclusion.
