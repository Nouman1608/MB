## What this component actually asks

Not "what happened in this text" but **how the context of production and reception shapes meaning** --
the historical period, culture and place a text was written in, and the historical period, culture and
place it is now being read in, are not the same thing, and both matter.

## The core skill: reading across time and place

A single text can be read very differently by its original audience and by a reader today. For a
non-literary text especially, ask: how might this same event or issue be represented differently by
different media, institutions, or audiences at a different moment? A 1960s newspaper report on a
social issue and a 2020s article on the same issue will differ not just in facts covered but in
assumed audience, acceptable tone, and what goes without saying.

## Worked example: applying Time and Space to a non-literary text

A 19th-century newspaper editorial on a public health issue, read alongside a modern public-health
campaign leaflet on a comparable topic.

```
Production context (editorial):  written for a literate, middle-class
                                  19th-century readership; assumes
                                  formal register and authority of the
                                  writer's own opinion is unquestioned
Production context (leaflet):    written for a broad, general modern
                                  audience; assumes visual literacy,
                                  short attention span, direct
                                  address ("you")
Reception today:                 a modern reader may find the
                                  editorial's tone paternalistic where
                                  its original readers would have
                                  found it authoritative -- this SHIFT
                                  in reception is itself the analytical
                                  point, not just a description of two
                                  texts' differing styles
```

Identifying the shift between original and current reception -- not just describing two texts side by
side -- is the skill this component specifically rewards.

## Applying this to a literary text too

Because this course studies both literary and non-literary texts under every component, the same
Time and Space questions apply to a literary work as much as a non-literary one. For a novel or poem,
ask not just what historical period it depicts, but how its ORIGINAL readers would have understood
references, values or assumptions that a modern reader might miss or misread entirely — a 19th-century
novel's assumptions about social class, for example, may read very differently to a contemporary
audience than they did to its first readers, and identifying that gap is exactly the analytical move
this component rewards.

## Connecting to the individual oral and HL essay

Because the individual oral connects a literary work and a non-literary body of work through a global
issue, Time and Space's questions about shifting context and reception are a natural source of
material: a strong oral commentary can explicitly discuss how the chosen global issue is understood
differently now than when either text was produced, using this component's vocabulary of production
and reception context directly.

## Common mistakes

Treating "context" as background information to mention once, rather than as something that actively
shapes how meaning is made and received. Discussing only the text's original context and ignoring how
a reader today receives it differently. Applying this component's ideas only to literary texts, when
the course's dual literary/non-literary structure expects the same analytical questions to apply to
both text types equally.

## Quick revision checklist

- For every text studied under this component, identify both its production context and how a
  reception context today might differ from its original one.
- Practise applying the same "how might this be represented differently at a different time or place"
  question to a non-literary text type you have not yet discussed in class.
- Keep separate short notes on production context and reception context for each text -- conflating
  the two is a common source of vague answers.

## A note on assessment weighting

Time and Space is not examined as a separate, named section on any paper — like the other two syllabus
components, its concepts are woven into Paper 1's guided textual analysis and Paper 2's comparative
essay rather than tested in isolation. This means revision should focus on being able to apply its
production/reception framework fluently to whatever text appears, rather than memorising Time and
Space as a standalone content block disconnected from actual exam tasks.

## Self-test

1. What two contexts does this component ask students to consider for every text?
2. Why might a 19th-century text and a same-topic modern text differ in more than just facts?
3. Give one non-literary text type this component's questions could apply to.
4. Why is identifying a SHIFT in reception more valuable than simply describing a text's original
   context?
5. Does Time and Space apply only to literary texts in this course?

Reviewing both the production and reception sides for every text studied, rather than only the one
that feels more obvious to discuss, is the single most reliable way to strengthen this component's
contribution to an exam answer.

**Answers:** 1. Production context (when/where/by whom a text was made) and reception context
(when/where/by whom it is now being read). 2. Because assumed audience, acceptable tone and what goes
without saying change over time, not just the specific facts being reported. 3. Any of: journalism,
advertising, a speech, a report, digital/social media. 4. Because it demonstrates active analysis of
how meaning changes across time, rather than static description of one moment. 5. No — because the
course studies both literary and non-literary texts, this component's questions apply equally to both.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between context of production and context of reception?**
Context of production is when, where and by whom a text was made. Context of reception is when, where and by whom it is now being read, and in IB DP Language A: Language and Literature both shape meaning.

**Is Time and space a separate exam section?**
No. Like the other two areas of exploration, its ideas are woven into Paper 1's guided textual analysis and Paper 2's comparative essay rather than tested as a named section.

**How can Time and space help with the individual oral?**
The oral connects a literary work and a non-literary body of work through a global issue, so you can discuss how that issue is understood differently now than when either text was produced.

**What is a common mistake with context?**
Treating context as background information mentioned once, rather than as something that shapes how meaning is made and received. Keep separate notes on production and reception context for each text.
