## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Name the rhetorical device in each sentence: (a) *"Do we want a town centre, or a ghost town?"* (b) *"Easy to promise, hard to deliver."* (c) *"The report, all four hundred pages of it, says nothing new."* **[3]**

**2.** Write an original sentence using **antithesis** on the topic of school homework. **[2]**

**3.** Rewrite these two sentences as one sentence using **parenthesis**, and explain the effect: *"The mayor opened the new bridge. She had voted against it."* **[3]**

**4.** Rewrite this sentence so that the key idea receives **end focus**: *"Danger is what the crossing outside the school represents for every child."* **[2]**

**5.** Explain why *"Mr Hughes has eaten lunch alone every day since his wife died"* has more emotional impact than *"Loneliness is a problem for older people."* **[3]**

**6.** A student uses a rhetorical question at the start of every paragraph of a speech. Explain **two** reasons this may weaken the speech. **[2]**

**7.** Improve this use of evidence so that it is specific and pertinent: *"Studies show that exercise is good, so we should have more PE."* **[3]**

## Section B — Longer and structured questions

**8.** Read this original draft paragraph from a speech to Year 11 students.

> *Phones are bad in lessons. They distract people. Teachers get annoyed. Also people don't learn as much. We should
> ban them.*

Rewrite the paragraph for greater impact. Use at least **two** rhetorical devices and one piece of specific evidence, and keep the register suitable for the audience. **[6]**

**9.** You are writing an article arguing that the town should plant more trees. Put these four points in a **climactic** order and justify your choice: *(a) trees make streets look attractive; (b) trees provide shade in hot summers; (c) trees can cut flooding by absorbing rainwater; (d) trees give children a reason to play outside.* **[4]**

**10.** Write the **final paragraph** of a letter to a newspaper arguing that a local swimming pool should not close. Aim for a memorable ending that creates emotional impact. **[6]**

**11.** For a Paper 1-style descriptive task, *"Describe a busy kitchen just before a big family meal"*, write **three** sentences that use language creatively, then explain the effect you intended for each. **[6]**

**12.** Read this original opening from a student's article.

> *Every single person in the entire world absolutely hates litter!!! It is the most disgusting, horrible, revolting,
> terrible thing ever and it is literally destroying everything everywhere!!!*

Explain **three** reasons this opening fails to create the impact the student intends, and rewrite it. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) rhetorical question [1]; (b) antithesis [1]; (c) parenthesis [1].

**2.** A sentence with genuinely contrasting ideas [1] in a balanced structure [1]. For example: "Homework should deepen what we learn in the day, not steal what is left of the evening."

**3.** "The mayor, who had voted against it, opened the new bridge" (or with dashes or brackets) [1], with the inserted clause correctly punctuated on both sides [1]. Effect: the aside adds irony, quietly pointing out the mayor's change of position without a separate sentence to dwell on it [1].

**4.** A rewrite that moves "danger" to the end [1], for example: "For every child, the crossing outside the school represents one thing: danger" [1].

**5.** The first sentence gives a named individual and a specific, concrete situation [1], which helps the reader picture a real person and feel sympathy [1]; the second is abstract and general, so it informs without moving the reader [1].

**6.** It becomes predictable, so the audience stops feeling challenged [1]; and it can sound as though the speaker is asking questions rather than making confident points, weakening the argument [1].

**7.** Award 3 marks: a specific piece of evidence, for example an expert view ("Doctors recommend that teenagers are active for part of every day") or a concrete example [1]; a clear explanation of how it supports more PE [1]; integration into a fluent sentence rather than a vague assertion [1].

**8.** Award 6 marks:
- a stronger opening that grabs the audience, for example direct address or a striking image [1];
- a first rhetorical device used purposefully, such as a rhetorical question or tricolon [1];
- a second, different device used purposefully, such as antithesis or anaphora [1];
- specific, pertinent evidence, for example a concrete example of a lesson disrupted by notifications [1];
- emphasis through position, such as a short final sentence carrying the main point [1];
- register suited to fellow students: direct and lively but not slangy [1].

For example: "How many times today has your phone buzzed in the middle of a lesson? Each buzz pulls your attention away just as the teacher explains the one thing you will need in the exam. Phones connect us to everyone except the person at the front of the room. Put them away, and we get our lessons back."

**9.** A climactic order from least to most powerful, for example (a), (d), (b), (c) [1]. Appearance comes first as the least serious, more aesthetic point [1]; children's play and summer shade bring in health and wellbeing [1]; flood protection ends the sequence because it addresses safety and damage to homes, the most serious consequence [1]. Other orders are credited if the justification is clearly climactic.

**10.** Award 6 marks:
- a clear return to the main argument, keeping the stance [1];
- a concrete, human detail that creates emotional impact, such as a child learning to swim or an older swimmer's daily routine [1];
- an echo of an earlier idea or image, or a call to action [1];
- at least one device used for effect, for example antithesis ("easy to close, impossible to replace") [1];
- a powerful final sentence with end focus [1];
- register appropriate to a newspaper letter, with controlled emotion [1].

**11.** Three creative sentences, one mark each for originality and precision of language [3], and one mark each for a clear explanation of the intended effect [3]. For example: "Steam crawled up the windows and wrote its own blurred weather on the glass" (personification, suggesting warmth sealing the room off from the outside world); "Pans muttered on every ring" (personification and sound, creating a sense of restless, building pressure); "In the middle of it all, my grandmother stood perfectly still, tasting" (a contrast between stillness and chaos, placing her at the calm centre of the scene).

**12.** Three reasons, one mark each: the claims are sweeping and untrue ("every single person"), which damages credibility [1]; the pile of near-synonyms ("disgusting, horrible, revolting, terrible") dilutes rather than strengthens the emotion [1]; the repeated exclamation marks and "literally" make the tone melodramatic and informal for an article [1]. A rewrite earns up to 3 more marks: a concrete, specific detail in place of generalisation [1], one precise emotive word rather than a list [1], and a controlled, credible register [1]. For example: "On Saturday morning, a volunteer on Riverside Walk pulled forty-three plastic bottles out of a single stretch of reed bed. Nobody had meant to leave them there. That is the problem."

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using rhetorical devices with no clear purpose.
- Vague evidence such as "studies show" with nothing specific.
- Emotional exaggeration that undermines credibility.
- Burying the key point in the middle of a sentence.
- Punctuation errors in parenthesis.
