## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define scarcity. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** factors of production used by a fishing business, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**3.** A school has funds for one project. In order of preference it lists a science laboratory, a sports hall and a library. It builds the laboratory. State the opportunity cost of this decision. **[1]**

**4.** A potter sells mugs for $12 each. The clay and glaze for one mug cost $3.50. She sells 400 mugs a year. Calculate the added value per mug and the total added value for the year. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** difference between a need and a want, using an example of each. **[2]**

**6.** Outline **two** benefits of specialisation to a business. **[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** Riverbend Juice makes fresh fruit juice. Each bottle sells for $2.40. The fruit for one bottle costs $0.70 and the bottle and label cost $0.35. Riverbend sells 6,000 bottles a month. Its other costs, such as wages and rent, are $6,300 a month.

**(a)** Calculate the added value per bottle.

**(b)** Calculate Riverbend's monthly profit.

**(c)** Explain **two** ways Riverbend could increase its added value. **[8]**

**8.** A garment factory splits the making of each shirt into separate tasks: cutting, sewing sleeves, sewing collars, fitting buttons and packing.

**(a)** Define division of labour.

**(b)** Explain **two** disadvantages of this method for the factory. **[6]**

**9.** A bakery owner can use her only free afternoon to train a new worker or to meet a supplier offering cheaper flour. She chooses the training.

**(a)** Identify the opportunity cost of her decision.

**(b)** Explain why understanding opportunity cost helps a manager make decisions. **[3]**

**10.** A jewellery maker wants to increase added value. She could use a cheaper metal, saving $6 per ring, or spend more time on design so that each ring can sell for $15 more. Consider these two options and recommend which she should choose. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**11.** Explain the purpose of business activity, using a bus company as an example. **[4]**

**12.** A bicycle repairer charges $25 for a service, using parts that cost $9. He does 60 services a month. He raises his charge to $30, and the number of services falls to 48 a month. Calculate the change in his total monthly added value. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Scarcity is the situation where there are limited resources [1] compared with unlimited wants [1].

**2.** Any two, with an example: land, such as the fish in the sea or the harbour site [1]; labour, such as the crew [1]; capital, such as boats, nets or a cold store; enterprise, such as the owner who takes the risk of starting the business.

**3.** The sports hall [1].

**4.** $12 − $3.50 = **$8.50** per mug [1] [1]. 400 × $8.50 = **$3,400** for the year [1].

**5.** A need is essential for survival, such as clean water [1]; a want is desired but not essential, such as a branded soft drink [1].

**6.** Any two, each identified [1] and outlined [1]: workers become faster and more skilled at one task, so output per worker rises; less time is wasted moving between tasks; specialist machinery can be used for each task; recruitment and training are simpler because each job is narrower.

**7.** **(a)** Bought-in cost = 0.70 + 0.35 = $1.05 [1]. Added value = 2.40 − 1.05 = **$1.35** [1].

**(b)** Total added value = 6,000 × 1.35 = $8,100 [1]. Profit = 8,100 − 6,300 = **$1,800** [1].

**(c)** Two ways, each identified [1] and explained [1]: use attractive packaging or a strong brand, such as stressing that the juice is fresh and local, so that customers will pay more than $2.40; buy fruit in bulk or directly from farmers to cut the $0.70 fruit cost, raising added value if quality is kept; offer a convenient service such as home delivery for which customers pay extra; reduce waste of fruit so the input cost per bottle falls.

**8.** **(a)** Splitting the production of a product into separate tasks [1], each done by a different worker or group of workers [1].

**(b)** Two disadvantages, each identified [1] and explained [1]: workers repeat one task all day, which can be boring, lowering motivation and raising absence or labour turnover; if one stage stops, for example the button-fitting workers are absent, the shirts cannot be finished and the whole line stops; workers with one narrow skill are less flexible to move between tasks when orders change.

**9.** **(a)** Meeting the supplier, and so the chance of cheaper flour [1].

**(b)** Resources such as time and money are scarce, so every choice gives something up [1]; comparing what is given up with what is gained helps the manager choose the option with the greater benefit [1].

**10.** Cheaper metal raises added value by $6 per ring because bought-in costs fall [1], but customers may notice lower quality, which could harm the reputation and reduce sales [1]. Better design could raise added value by $15 per ring if the price rise is accepted [1], but it uses more of her time, so she may make fewer rings [1]. Recommendation: better design, because the gain per ring is larger and quality supports her reputation [1], provided enough customers will pay the higher price; if her customers mainly buy on price, the cheaper metal may be safer [1].

**11.** A bus company combines scarce resources such as buses (capital), drivers (labour) and fuel [1] to provide a service, transport [1], that satisfies customers' need or want to travel to work, school or shops [1]. It provides jobs and incomes for its workers and, if privately owned, aims to make a profit as a reward for the owner's risk [1].

**12.** Before: (25 − 9) × 60 = 16 × 60 = $960 [1]. After: (30 − 9) × 48 = 21 × 48 = $1,008 [1] [1]. Change: an **increase of $48** a month [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving every rejected option, or the money spent, as the opportunity cost.
- Calculating added value from only part of the bought-in costs, as in question 7.
- Calling added value profit, or forgetting to multiply by quantity.
- Listing ways to add value without linking them to price or cost.
- Ending a "consider... justify" answer without a clear recommendation and the condition it depends on.
