Paper 2 – Writing carries exactly half of the marks in Cambridge O Level
English Language (1123): 50 marks, 2 hours, worth 50% of the final grade.
Where the [Reading and Writing overview guide](/resources/o-level-cambridge-english-language-reading-and-writing/)
maps the whole syllabus at a glance, this guide stays entirely inside
Paper 2 and works through what each of its two sections actually asks
you to do, which assessment objectives they test, and how the marks
break down -- detail that matters once you're past the introductory
stage and into focused exam preparation.

## What Paper 2 is testing

Paper 2 is weighted 80% Writing (AO2) and 20% Reading (AO1) -- the
mirror image of Paper 1, which is 80% Reading and 20% Writing. That
20% of Reading marks on a "Writing" paper surprises a lot of
candidates, but it comes directly from Section A, where you must use
information from a printed source text to write your answer. You
cannot access full marks in Section A by writing fluently in isolation
from the text; you have to demonstrate you have read, selected from,
and evaluated it.

The five Writing assessment objectives tested across both sections are:

- **W1** – articulate experience and express what is thought, felt and imagined
- **W2** – organise and structure ideas and opinions for deliberate effect
- **W3** – use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context
- **W4** – use register appropriate to context
- **W5** – make accurate use of spelling, punctuation and grammar

Section A tests all five (W1–W5, worth 15 of its 25 marks) plus two
Reading objectives (R3, R5, worth the remaining 10). Section B tests
four of the five (W1, W2, W3, W5 -- W4, register, is not separately
assessed in Section B) across all 25 of its marks.

## Section A: Directed Writing (25 marks)

You answer **one compulsory question**. You are given one or two
source texts totalling roughly 400–450 words, printed on the question
paper itself (not in a separate insert, unlike Paper 1). Your task is
to use, develop and evaluate the information in the source text(s) to
produce a piece of your own writing in one of these forms: a
discursive, argumentative or persuasive **speech, email, report,
letter or article**. The expected length is about 250–350 words.

Marks split as follows:

- **15 marks for Writing (W1–W5)** -- your own voice, organisation,
  vocabulary, register and technical accuracy in the new text you
  produce
- **10 marks for Reading (R3, R5)** -- how well you have analysed,
  evaluated and developed the facts, ideas and opinions in the source
  material, and selected information appropriate to your specific
  purpose, rather than simply copying or listing it

The word "directed" is the operative one: you are not free to write
whatever you like on the topic. You are directed to respond to the
specific angle, audience and purpose set out in the question, using
the source material as your evidence base. A common way marks are
lost here is treating the task as a general opinion essay rather than
grounding every point in the given text(s).

**Practical approach**: read both source texts fully before planning.
Identify the register the task demands (a speech to students, a
formal letter to a local council, an email to a colleague) and match
vocabulary and tone to it from your opening line. Draw at least one
specific point from each source text rather than leaning on only one,
since Section A explicitly rewards evaluating and developing material
across what you've been given.

## Section B: Composition (25 marks)

You choose **one question from a choice of four**: two descriptive
and two narrative. There is no source text for this section -- it
draws entirely on your own ideas, and the required length is about
350–450 words, the longest single piece of continuous writing in the
qualification.

- **Descriptive** writing describes a person, place or situation
  (including atmosphere) in enough sensory and structural detail that
  the reader can picture it clearly. There is no requirement for a
  plot or sequence of events -- the skill being tested is controlled,
  vivid description, not storytelling.
- **Narrative** writing tells a story: a sequence of connected events,
  which may be real or imaginary, with a clear shape (a beginning,
  development and some form of resolution or turning point).

All 25 marks in Section B go to Writing objectives W1, W2, W3 and W5.
Register (W4) is not separately assessed here, which makes sense:
there is no set audience or format being tested, only your control of
expression, structure, vocabulary, sentence variety and technical
accuracy.

**Practical approach**: because Section B is unprompted by any source,
the biggest risk is a vague or unfocused answer. Spend two or three
minutes selecting a specific angle -- one clear moment, one clearly
defined place, one single narrative thread -- rather than trying to
cover too much ground in 350–450 words. Vary sentence length and
structure deliberately (a mix of short, punchy sentences and longer,
more complex ones) since sentence variety is explicitly part of W3,
and proofread the final few minutes purely for W5 (spelling,
punctuation, grammar), which is tested throughout the composition and
is easy to lose marks on under time pressure.

## Command words that appear on this paper

The syllabus defines a small, fixed set of command words used across
its question papers. On Paper 2, the ones most likely to shape how you
respond are:

- **Consider** – review and respond to given information
- **Describe** – state the points of a topic / give characteristics and main features
- **Explain** – set out purposes or reasons, make relationships between things clear, and support with relevant evidence

Knowing these precisely matters because Directed Writing questions are
built around a specific instruction (for example, being asked to
*consider* the viewpoints in a source text before responding), and
misreading the command word is a common, avoidable source of lost
marks.

## Timing across the two hours

With 25 marks per section and 2 hours for the paper, a working split
is roughly 55–60 minutes on Section A (including 8–10 minutes reading
and annotating the source text(s)) and 55–60 minutes on Section B
(including 5 minutes of planning before you start writing), leaving a
short buffer for a final proofread across both answers. Because
Section A's word count (250–350) is shorter than Section B's
(350–450), resist the temptation to under-plan Section B simply
because it feels more "creative" -- it carries exactly the same 25
marks and rewards structure just as much as Section A does.

## How to approach it

Treat Sections A and B as testing genuinely different skills rather
than variations on the same essay. Section A rewards disciplined use
of someone else's material for a stated purpose; Section B rewards
your own controlled, structured expression with no material to lean
on. Practising both regularly, and specifically checking your answers
against the W1–W5 objectives above rather than just "does this sound
good," is the most direct way to see where marks are actually being
gained or lost. Past papers and specimen mark schemes, freely
available from Cambridge International, show real examples of how
examiners apply these objectives to full-length answers -- use them
alongside this guide rather than as a substitute for understanding the
assessment structure first.
