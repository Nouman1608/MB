## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Attachment | A close two-way emotional bond in which each person seeks closeness and security from the other |
| Imprinting | Following the first moving object seen in a critical period after birth or hatching |
| Critical period | A fixed time window in which a behaviour must develop if it is to develop normally |
| Sensitive period | A softer version: development is easier in the window but still possible later |
| Contact comfort | Comfort from soft physical contact, which Harlow showed matters more than food |
| Classical conditioning (attachment) | The caregiver becomes a conditioned stimulus through association with food |
| Secondary reinforcer | The caregiver, rewarding because associated with the primary reinforcer, food |
| Monotropy | One special attachment that is more important than all others |
| Social releasers | Innate infant behaviours, such as smiling and crying, that trigger care |
| Internal working model | Mental template of relationships based on the primary attachment |
| Secure base | Caregiver from whom the infant explores and to whom it returns |
| Stranger anxiety | Distress at the approach of an unfamiliar person |
| Separation anxiety | Distress when the caregiver leaves |
| Imposed etic | Applying a method or idea from one culture as if it were universal |
| Maternal deprivation | Loss of maternal emotional care during the critical period |
| Privation | Never having formed an attachment |
| Affectionless psychopathy | Lack of guilt and empathy, linked by Bowlby to early deprivation |
| Disinhibited attachment | Indiscriminate friendliness towards strangers |
| Institutionalisation | Effects of growing up in an institution such as an orphanage |

## Studies at a glance

| Study | Key finding | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Lorenz (1935) | Incubator goslings imprinted on Lorenz in a critical period | Imprinting; critical period |
| Guiton (1966) | Chickens imprinted on gloves later preferred chickens | Imprinting reversible |
| Harlow (1958) | Monkeys preferred the cloth mother even when the wire one fed them | Contact comfort; challenges learning theory |
| Dollard and Miller (1950) | Attachment explained by conditioning ("cupboard love") | Learning theory |
| Bowlby (1969) | Monotropy, social releasers, critical period, internal working model | Monotropic theory |
| Ainsworth (Strange Situation) | Secure about two-thirds, avoidant about a fifth, resistant about an eighth | Attachment types |
| Van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988) | 32 studies, 8 countries; secure most common; within-country variation about 1.5 times between-country | Cultural variation |
| Bowlby (1944), 44 thieves | 12 of 14 affectionless psychopaths had early separation; 5 of the other 30 | Maternal deprivation |
| English and Romanian Adoptees project | Late adoption (after about 6 months) linked to disinhibited attachment and lower ability | Institutionalisation |
| Hazan and Shaver (1987) | Secure adults reported longer-lasting, happier relationships | Internal working model; adult relationships |

## Strange Situation in one table

| Behaviour measured | Secure | Insecure-avoidant | Insecure-resistant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exploration | Uses caregiver as a secure base | Explores freely, ignoring caregiver | Explores little |
| Stranger anxiety | Moderate | Low | High |
| Separation anxiety | Moderate | Low | High |
| Reunion | Seeks comfort, soothed quickly | Little response or avoids | Seeks and resists contact |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Critical versus sensitive period**: fixed window versus easier window.
- **Monotropic theory versus maternal deprivation**: how attachment forms versus what happens if it is broken.
- **Deprivation versus privation**: losing an attachment versus never forming one.
- **Within- versus between-culture variation**: variation is greater within countries.
- **Controlled observation versus experiment**: the Strange Situation manipulates episodes but does not compare an independent variable between groups.

## Mini essay plan: discuss Bowlby's monotropic theory [16]

- AO1: innate and adaptive; social releasers; monotropy with the law of continuity and law of accumulated separation; critical period about two and a half years; internal working model.
- AO3: Lorenz and Harlow support innate attachment and critical periods.
- AO3: Hazan and Shaver support the internal working model's prediction of continuity.
- AO3: many infants form multiple attachments, challenging monotropy.
- AO3: late-adopted children in the Romanian adoptee research could still form attachments, so a sensitive period fits better.
- AO3: placing responsibility on mothers has social implications for working mothers.
- Judgement: the theory's core idea of an innate, adaptive bond is well supported, but monotropy and a fixed critical period are overstated.

## Exam traps

- Saying Harlow's monkeys attached to the food source.
- Mixing up monotropic theory and maternal deprivation.
- Calling the Strange Situation an experiment.
- Claiming insecure attachment was most common in Japan.
- Describing the internal working model as fixing adult relationships.
- Writing about Schaffer's stages or caregiver–infant interactions instead of the specification's named content.
- Giving no evaluation of generalising animal studies to humans.

## Self-test

1. What did Lorenz find about incubator-hatched goslings?
2. Why do Harlow's findings challenge learning theory?
3. Explain how operant conditioning accounts for attachment.
4. Define monotropy.
5. Give two examples of social releasers.
6. What is the internal working model?
7. Name the five behaviours observed in the Strange Situation.
8. Describe an insecure-avoidant infant at reunion.
9. What did van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg find about variation within and between countries?
10. What is the difference between deprivation and privation?
11. What is disinhibited attachment?
12. What did Hazan and Shaver (1987) find?

### Answers

1. They imprinted on Lorenz, the first moving object they saw, and followed him, even when mixed with goslings reared by their mother.
2. The monkeys preferred the cloth mother that gave no food, so comfort, not food, drove attachment.
3. Hunger is a drive; feeding reduces it and is a primary reinforcer. The caregiver, associated with food, becomes a secondary reinforcer, so the infant seeks the caregiver.
4. The idea that one attachment, usually to the mother, is more important than all others.
5. Any two: crying; smiling; cooing; gripping.
6. A mental template of relationships, formed from the primary attachment, that guides expectations of later relationships.
7. Proximity seeking; exploration and secure-base behaviour; stranger anxiety; separation anxiety; response to reunion.
8. Shows little response to the caregiver's return and may avoid contact.
9. Variation within countries was about one and a half times greater than variation between them.
10. Deprivation is losing an attachment that has formed; privation is never forming one.
11. Indiscriminate friendliness and attention-seeking towards strangers as well as familiar people.
12. Adults' self-reported attachment types appeared in roughly similar proportions to infant types, and secure adults reported longer-lasting, happier relationships.
