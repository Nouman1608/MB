## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between total utility and marginal utility. **[2]**

**2.** A consumer's total utility from 1 to 5 units of a good is 10, 18, 24, 28 and 30 utils. What is the marginal utility of the fourth unit?

A: 4 utils

B: 6 utils

C: 7 utils

D: 28 utils **[1]**

**3.** Complete the missing values. Total utility from 1 to 4 units is 12, ?, 30, ?. Marginal utility of units 1 to 4 is ?, 10, ?, 4. **[2]**

**4.** State the condition for a consumer to maximise total utility when buying two goods. **[2]**

**5.** The last unit of good X bought gives 40 utils and costs 8. The last unit of good Y gives 30 utils and costs 5. To raise total utility, the consumer should:

A: buy more X and less Y

B: buy more Y and less X

C: make no change

D: buy more of both goods with the same income **[1]**

**6.** Explain how total utility can rise while marginal utility is falling. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A consumer has an income of 18 to spend on good X (price 4) and good Y (price 2).

| Unit | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MU of X | 32 | 28 | 20 | 12 | 8 | | |
| MU of Y | 18 | 14 | 10 | 8 | 6 | 4 | 2 |

**(a)** Using the equi-marginal principle, find the utility-maximising combination of X and Y. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the total utility from this combination. **[1]**

**(c)** The price of X falls to 2. Find the new utility-maximising combination. **[2]**

**(d)** Use your answers to state two points on the consumer's demand curve for X. **[1]**

**8.** Explain, using marginal utility, why an individual's demand curve usually slopes downward. **[4]**

**9.** Explain two assumptions of rational behaviour made by marginal utility theory, and for each give one reason why it may not hold. **[4]**

**10.** A café sells a first coffee for 3 and a second coffee on the same visit for 1.50. Using diminishing marginal utility, explain why this pricing could increase the café's sales. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of marginal utility theory in explaining how consumers behave. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Total utility is the total satisfaction from all units of a good consumed in a period [1]; marginal utility is the change in total utility from consuming one more unit [1].

**2.** **A** [1]. MU of the fourth unit = 28 − 24 = 4 utils.

**3.** MU of unit 1 = 12 and TU of 2 units = 12 + 10 = 22 [1]; MU of unit 3 = 30 − 22 = 8 and TU of 4 units = 30 + 4 = 34 [1].

**4.** Marginal utility per unit of money must be equal for both goods, MU of X ÷ price of X = MU of Y ÷ price of Y [1], and the consumer's income must be fully spent [1].

**5.** **B** [1]. X gives 40 ÷ 8 = 5 utils per unit of money and Y gives 30 ÷ 5 = 6, so switching spending towards Y raises total utility.

**6.** Total utility rises whenever marginal utility is positive, because each extra unit still adds some satisfaction [1]; a falling marginal utility means only that each addition is smaller than the last, so total utility rises at a decreasing rate [1].

**7.** **(a)** MU per unit of money: X gives 8, 7, 5, 3, 2; Y gives 9, 7, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 [1]. Buying in order of the highest ratio: Y1, X1, X2, Y2, X3, Y3, which costs 3 × 4 + 3 × 2 = 18 [1]. The combination is **3 X and 3 Y**, where the last units give 5 utils per unit of money for each good [1].

**(b)** TU = (32 + 28 + 20) + (18 + 14 + 10) = 80 + 42 = **122 utils** [1].

**(c)** MU per unit of money for X is now 16, 14, 10, 6, 4 [1]. Buying in order gives **5 X and 4 Y**, costing 10 + 8 = 18, where both last units give 4 utils per unit of money [1].

**(d)** At a price of 4 the consumer demands 3 units of X; at a price of 2 the consumer demands 5 units [1].

**8.** Marginal utility diminishes as more units are consumed in a period [1]. A consumer buys a unit only if the satisfaction it gives is worth at least its price [1]. When the price falls, the MU per unit of money of the good rises above that of other goods [1], so the consumer buys extra units with lower marginal utility until the ratios are equal again, so quantity demanded rises as price falls [1].

**9.** Any two assumptions, each with a reason, for example:

The consumer has full information about prices and satisfaction [1]; but consumers often do not know how much they will enjoy a new product before buying it [1].

The consumer has consistent preferences and calculates carefully [1]; but many purchases are made out of habit or on impulse, or are influenced by advertising and peers [1].

Utility can be measured and compared across goods; but utils cannot be observed.

**10.** The second coffee on the same visit gives lower marginal utility than the first [1], so at the full price of 3 many customers would not think it worth buying [1]. At 1.50, the MU per unit of money of a second coffee is higher, so more customers buy one and the café's sales rise [1].

**11.** Useful: it explains why demand curves usually slope downward through diminishing marginal utility [1]; the equi-marginal principle explains how consumers spread a limited income across goods [1]; it explains pricing such as discounts on extra units [1].

Limitations: utility cannot be measured, so the theory cannot be tested directly [1]; consumers lack full information and may not calculate [1]; habit, impulse, addiction and social influences break the assumption of rational behaviour [1]; the marginal utility of money may not be constant when prices change a lot [1].

Judgement: the theory is unrealistic as a description of how consumers decide, but it is useful for explaining the direction of their responses to price changes; its usefulness depends on whether we need precise predictions or only the general pattern [1].
