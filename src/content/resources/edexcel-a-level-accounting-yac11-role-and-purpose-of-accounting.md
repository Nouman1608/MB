This guide goes deeper into outcomes **1.1.1** and **1.1.2** of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11) specification, Issue 2, September 2018: "the role of accounting as a need to record, analyse and communicate accounting information to users" and "the purpose of accounting information as an aid to the management of a business". They open Topic 1.1 of Unit 1 (The Accounting System and Costing), which is International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business named is invented.

## Bookkeeping and accounting

The three verbs in 1.1.1 describe a sequence.

| Stage | What happens | Who usually does it |
|---|---|---|
| Record | Each transaction is entered in the books of prime entry and the ledger using double entry | Bookkeeper |
| Analyse | Balances are summarised into a trial balance, adjusted and turned into totals, comparisons and ratios | Accountant |
| Communicate | Results are presented in financial statements and reports that suit the reader | Accountant |

**Bookkeeping** is the recording stage. **Accounting** includes it but goes further: it turns records into information. A business with perfect books but no analysis knows what happened but not what it means.

## Financial accounting and management accounting

Accounting serves two broad audiences, and the specification's two outcomes line up with them.

| | Financial accounting (1.1.1) | Management accounting (1.1.2) |
|---|---|---|
| Main users | Mainly external: owners, lenders, suppliers, tax authorities | Internal: managers |
| Content | Financial statements of the whole business | Costings, budgets, forecasts, product or department figures |
| Time focus | Past periods | Past results used to plan the future |
| Format | Standard formats; IAS terms | Whatever helps the decision |
| Frequency | Usually annual | As often as managers need |

In a small sole trader business the owner is both the main external user and the manager, so the same figures serve both purposes.

## Users and the figures they look at

Knowing a user's question is half the answer; the other half is knowing which figures answer it.

| User | Question | Figures they would look at |
|---|---|---|
| Owner | Is the business worth my time and capital? | Profit for the year, drawings, capital |
| Manager | Where can costs be cut or sales grown? | Cost of sales, expenses by type, departmental profits |
| Bank or other lender | Can the business repay with interest? | Profit, cash, existing loans, non-current assets that could act as security |
| Trade supplier | Will invoices be paid on time? | Cash, current assets against current liabilities |
| Employee | Is my job secure, and can the business afford a pay rise? | Profit trend |
| Tax authority | How much tax is due? | Profit for the year |

Users can be **internal** (owner, managers, employees) or **external** (lenders, suppliers, tax authorities, customers, potential investors). Their interests can conflict. An owner may want to show a high profit to a lender, but a low taxable profit to the tax authority; accounting rules and concepts limit how far figures can be shaped for either purpose.

## What makes accounting information useful

To communicate well, information should be:

- **relevant**: it helps the user's decision;
- **reliable**: free from material error and bias, and checkable to documents;
- **comparable**: prepared on the same basis over time and between businesses;
- **understandable**: presented clearly, using standard terms;
- **timely**: available while it can still influence the decision.

These qualities can pull against each other. Figures produced quickly (timely) may contain more estimates (less reliable). Historic cost figures are reliable because they come from invoices, but may be less relevant when prices have changed. Recognising such trade-offs is a source of evaluation marks.

## Limits of accounting information

Accounting information helps users, but it has gaps:

- **Money measurement**: staff skill, customer loyalty and reputation are not recorded.
- **Historic**: financial statements report the past; the future may differ.
- **Estimates**: depreciation, allowances for irrecoverable debts and inventory values involve judgement.
- **Historic cost**: asset values may be far from current values.
- **Inflation**: comparisons over many years are distorted.
- **Summarised**: totals can hide problems in one product or customer.

## 1.1.2 Accounting information as an aid to management

Managers use accounting information to **plan**, **control** and **decide**.

- **Planning**: past costs and sales form the basis of targets and forecasts.
- **Control**: actual results are compared with plans so that overspending or falling sales are found early, and records of inventory, receivables and cash protect assets against loss and fraud.
- **Decision making**: figures for costs and revenues are compared between options.

### Worked example 1: a decision supported by accounting information

Kestrel Crafts hires a delivery van for $7,200 a year. The owner is considering buying a van for $24,000, expected to last 4 years with a residual value of $4,000. Running costs would be the same either way.

```
Annual depreciation if bought = (24,000 − 4,000) ÷ 4 = $5,000
Annual hire charge                                  = $7,200
Effect on annual profit of buying                   = 7,200 − 5,000 = $2,200 higher
```

The accounting figures show that buying improves profit by $2,200 a year. They also show what they do not settle: buying uses $24,000 of cash at once, which might be needed for inventory; the business takes the risk of breakdowns and of the van being worth less than $4,000; hiring allows a larger van at short notice. A manager would combine the figures with these points before deciding. An answer that shows both the figures and their limits is what "evaluate" requires.

## Worked example 2: an annotated evaluation

*Question:* Evaluate the usefulness of a sole trader's financial statements to a bank deciding whether to lend the business $30,000.

*Model answer, with the skill each part shows:*

"The statement of profit or loss shows whether the business makes enough profit to cover interest and repayments; a profit of $45,000 would comfortably cover them, so it is relevant to the bank's decision." *(Knowledge, applied to the bank's question.)*

"The statement of financial position shows existing loans and the non-current assets that could act as security, which the bank needs before adding more debt." *(Application.)*

"However, the statements describe the past year. If the business's main customer is about to leave, next year's profit could be much lower, and this would not appear in the statements." *(Analysis of a limitation.)*

"Sole trader statements are often not audited, so the bank cannot be sure they are free from error or deliberate overstatement." *(Analysis of reliability.)*

"Overall the statements are useful as a starting point, but the bank should also ask for a cash flow forecast and evidence about future orders before lending." *(Evaluation: a supported judgement in context.)*

Each paragraph links a point to the bank's decision. Lists of users or features with no link to the scenario gain knowledge marks only.

## Common mistakes

- Treating bookkeeping and accounting as the same thing.
- Naming users without stating what each needs to know.
- Saying financial statements show what a business is worth; they show assets mainly at cost less depreciation.
- Ignoring the limits of the information in an "evaluate" answer.
- Giving a decision based only on figures, with no non-financial factors.
- Writing one-sided answers without a conclusion.
- Using older terms such as "balance sheet" or "debtors"; the specification says examiners use IAS terms and expects candidates to use them.

## Exam technique for the command words

The specification defines its command words. For this outcome:

- **State** or **Identify**: recall, such as naming two users.
- **Explain**: a point plus a linked reason or example, such as "a supplier needs to know whether invoices will be paid, so it looks at cash and current liabilities".
- **Comment**: a statement about the scenario and why it matters to the scenario.
- **Evaluate**: review the information, weigh strengths against weaknesses or alternatives, and reach a supported judgement in context.
- **Recommend**: choose an option from the scenario and justify it.
