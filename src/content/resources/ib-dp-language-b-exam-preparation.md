The [assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-assessment-revision-notes/) set out what
Paper 1, Paper 2 and the individual oral weigh, and how SL and HL differ. These notes turn that into
an exam-day plan -- how to match Paper 1's text type to its purpose, extract specific detail on
Paper 2, and move beyond description on the oral -- with a worked scenario and a checklist,
alongside the [full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-syllabus-guide/) already on the site.

## First: confirm you're preparing for Language B, not ab initio

Language B and Language ab initio have different assessment structures, required vocabulary range
and oral formats, so confirm explicitly with your teacher which course you're entered for before
using any revision material -- assuming the wrong course's structure wastes preparation time on the
wrong exam format entirely.

## Paper 1 (25%): match the text type to its purpose

Productive writing is assessed against text-type conventions -- register and structure appropriate
to, say, a formal letter versus an informal blog post -- as much as against grammatical accuracy.
**Exam-preparation priority**: for each text type the syllabus specifies, practise writing a short
example and explicitly checking it against that type's expected register, structure and typical
opening/closing conventions in the target language, since a grammatically correct response in the
wrong register or structure still loses marks.

## Paper 2 (50%, split 25/25): extract specific detail, not just gist

Listening and reading comprehension consistently test detailed understanding rather than general
gist, so skimming for overall meaning is not sufficient preparation on its own. **Exam-preparation
priority**: because this single paper is worth half the final grade, practise locating and extracting
specific detail from passages and audio under timed conditions -- answering "what exactly does the
speaker say will happen next," not just "what is the passage broadly about."

## The individual oral: develop the stimulus, don't just describe it

A common weakness at both SL and HL is spending too much prepared time describing the surface
content of the stimulus (SL: a photograph/short stimulus; HL: a literary extract), leaving too little
time to develop an argument or connect it to the broader theme or literary work.
**Exam-preparation priority**: rehearse moving from description into interpretation within the first
third of the response -- state what is shown or narrated briefly, then spend the majority of the
time connecting it to the relevant theme (SL) or extending analysis to the literary work as a whole
(HL), since examiners specifically reward interpretation and connection over description.

## Worked practice scenario: moving from description to interpretation

An SL individual oral stimulus is a photograph showing an elderly person using a smartphone,
connected to the theme of human ingenuity.

```
Description-only (weak):    "The photograph shows an old person holding a
                             phone. They look confused."

Description then
interpretation
(stronger):                  Brief description: an elderly person examines a
                             smartphone with an uncertain expression.
                             Interpretation: this connects to human ingenuity
                             by raising a tension the theme invites --
                             technology designed to connect people can
                             simultaneously exclude those less familiar with
                             it, suggesting ingenuity's benefits are not
                             evenly distributed across generations.
                             Development: this tension could be discussed
                             further by considering specific examples of
                             technology designed (or not designed) with
                             older users in mind.
```

The stronger version spends only a sentence or two on description before moving into interpretation
and a specific connection to the theme -- this description-then-interpretation-then-development
shape is the reliable structure for any stimulus, at either level.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: confirm you're preparing for Language B specifically, not ab initio; write
  and check a short example of each Paper 1 text type against its register and structure
  conventions; practise extracting specific detail (not just gist) from listening and reading
  passages under timed conditions; rehearse moving from description to interpretation within the
  first third of an oral response.
- **During Paper 1**: identify the required text type before writing, and check register and
  structure match it throughout.
- **During Paper 2**: read/listen for specific detail the question asks about, not just the general
  topic.
- **During the individual oral**: keep the opening description brief and move promptly into
  interpretation and connection to the theme or literary work.

## HL-specific preparation: the two literary works

HL students study two literary works in the target language, and the HL individual oral is built
around an extract from one of them -- so both works need to be genuinely familiar, not just the one
that happens to come up in the oral. Build short notes on key themes, characters and passages from
both works well before the oral, since the specific extract used is not known in advance, and being
unprepared for whichever one is drawn on wastes preparation time that would otherwise transfer
directly into a stronger response.

## Self-test

1. Why should you confirm whether you're studying Language B or Language ab initio before revising?
2. What does Paper 1 assess beyond grammatical accuracy?
3. Why is skimming for gist insufficient preparation for Paper 2?
4. In the worked scenario, what does the "stronger" version do that the "weak" version does not?

**Answers:** 1. Because the two courses have different assessment structures, required vocabulary
range and oral formats, so preparing with the wrong course's format wastes revision time. 2. Whether
the response's register and structure match the conventions expected of the specific text type
required (e.g. a formal letter versus an informal blog post). 3. Because Paper 2's listening and
reading comprehension exercises consistently test detailed understanding, not just general meaning,
so only skimming for gist leaves genuine detail-extraction questions unanswered. 4. It briefly
describes the stimulus, then moves into interpretation connecting it to the relevant theme, and
suggests a specific direction for further development -- rather than stopping at description alone.
