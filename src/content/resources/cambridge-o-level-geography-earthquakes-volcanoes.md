## What 2.1 asks you to do

The syllabus lists five learning outcomes:

1. Describe the main **types and features** of volcanoes and earthquakes:
   strato-volcanoes (composite cones) and shield volcanoes; crater, vent and
   magma chamber; epicentre, focus and magnitude.
2. Describe and explain the **distribution** of earthquakes and volcanoes,
   using the global pattern of plates and their structure, plate movements,
   subduction zones, and constructive (divergent), destructive (convergent)
   and conservative plate boundaries.
3. Describe the **causes** of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions and their
   **effects** on people and the environment.
4. Demonstrate an understanding that volcanoes present **hazards** and offer
   **opportunities** for people.
5. Explain what can be done to **reduce the impacts** of earthquakes and
   volcanoes.

Two case studies are required: **an earthquake** and **a volcano**.

## The Earth's structure and plates

The Earth has a solid **inner core**, a liquid **outer core**, a thick
**mantle** of hot rock that flows very slowly, and a thin rigid **crust**.
The crust and the top of the mantle are broken into **tectonic plates**.

| | Oceanic crust | Continental crust |
|---|---|---|
| Thickness | Thinner | Thicker |
| Density | Denser | Less dense |
| Can it be subducted? | Yes, it sinks beneath other plates | No, it is too buoyant |

Plates move a few centimetres a year, driven mainly by heat from the
interior, often explained as convection currents in the mantle. The
difference in density between oceanic and continental crust is the key to
explaining what happens at destructive boundaries.

## Plate boundaries and what happens at each

| Boundary | Movement | What happens | Hazards | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Constructive (divergent)** | Plates move apart | Magma rises to fill the gap, forming new crust and an ocean ridge | Frequent small, shallow earthquakes; effusive eruptions of runny lava | Mid-Atlantic Ridge through Iceland |
| **Destructive (convergent), oceanic–continental** | Plates move together | Denser oceanic plate is subducted, melting to form magma; ocean trench and fold mountains form | Powerful earthquakes, explosive volcanoes, tsunamis | Nazca Plate beneath the South American Plate (Andes) |
| **Collision (convergent), continental–continental** | Plates move together | Neither plate is subducted; crust crumples into fold mountains | Strong earthquakes, but no volcanoes | Indian and Eurasian Plates (Himalayas) |
| **Conservative** | Plates slide past each other | Friction locks the plates until stress is released | Earthquakes, but no volcanoes | San Andreas Fault (Pacific and North American Plates) |

Most earthquakes and volcanoes lie in narrow belts along plate boundaries,
especially around the **Pacific Ring of Fire**, along the **Mid-Atlantic
Ridge** and along the **Alpine–Himalayan belt**. The main exception is a
**hotspot** such as Hawaii, where a plume of magma rises through the middle of
a plate.

Northern Pakistan lies in the collision zone between the Indian and Eurasian
Plates. This explains why the region experiences powerful earthquakes, such as
the Kashmir earthquake of 8 October 2005, but has no active volcanoes.

## Types and features of volcanoes

| | Strato-volcano (composite cone) | Shield volcano |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Tall, steep-sided cone | Wide, gently sloping dome |
| Structure | Alternating layers of ash and lava | Many layers of runny lava |
| Lava | Thick, viscous, flows a short distance | Runny, low-viscosity, flows long distances |
| Eruptions | Explosive, with ash clouds and pyroclastic flows | Frequent, gentle, effusive |
| Typical boundary | Destructive | Constructive or hotspot |
| Example | Mount Fuji, Japan; Mount St Helens, USA | Mauna Loa, Hawaii |

Features you must be able to label: **magma chamber** (store of molten rock
below the volcano), **main vent** (the pipe through which magma rises),
**secondary vent**, **crater** (the depression at the top), plus lava flows,
ash cloud and layers of ash and lava.

## Features of earthquakes

- **Focus**: the point underground where the rock fractures and energy is
  released.
- **Epicentre**: the point on the surface directly above the focus, where
  shaking is usually strongest.
- **Seismic waves**: shock waves that radiate out from the focus.
- **Magnitude**: the amount of energy released, measured on the moment
  magnitude scale (the Richter scale is an older scale). The scale is
  **logarithmic**: each whole step represents about ten times greater ground
  movement and roughly 32 times more energy.

A **shallow-focus** earthquake usually causes more surface damage than a
deep-focus one of the same magnitude, because less energy is lost before the
waves reach the surface.

## Effects on people and the environment

Separate **primary effects** (caused directly by the shaking or eruption) from
**secondary effects** (which follow from them).

| | Earthquakes | Volcanoes |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Buildings and bridges collapse; deaths and injuries; roads and pipes broken | Lava flows; pyroclastic flows; ash fall; volcanic gases |
| Secondary | Fires from broken gas pipes; tsunamis; landslides; disease from damaged water supply; homelessness; economic loss | Lahars (mudflows); crop and livestock loss; air travel disruption; climate cooling from ash and gas |

The impact of the same hazard varies with **magnitude**, **depth of focus**,
**distance from the epicentre**, **population density**, **building
quality**, **time of day**, **level of development** and **preparedness**.

## Why people live near volcanoes: hazards and opportunities

Volcanoes present the hazards above but also offer opportunities:

- **Fertile soils** from weathered ash and lava support farming, for example
  vineyards and orchards on the slopes of Mount Etna in Sicily.
- **Geothermal energy**: Iceland uses volcanic heat to generate electricity
  and heat homes.
- **Tourism**: visitors come to see craters, lava fields, hot springs and
  geysers.
- **Minerals and building materials** such as sulphur and volcanic rock.

Many people also stay because their families and land are there, because
eruptions are infrequent, or because they trust monitoring and warnings.

## Reducing the impacts

| Approach | Earthquakes | Volcanoes |
|---|---|---|
| **Monitoring and prediction** | Earthquakes cannot be reliably predicted; seismometers detect activity and tsunami warning systems give some minutes or hours of notice | Eruptions can often be forecast from small earthquakes, ground swelling (tiltmeters, satellites) and changes in gas emissions |
| **Protection** | Earthquake-resistant buildings: deep foundations, rubber shock absorbers, cross-bracing, flexible pipes; sea walls against tsunamis | Barriers or channels to divert lava; on Heimaey, Iceland, in 1973 seawater was pumped to cool and slow lava threatening the harbour |
| **Preparation** | Drills, emergency kits, public education; Japan holds a national Disaster Prevention Day each 1 September | Evacuation plans, exclusion zones, hazard maps |
| **Land-use planning** | Avoid building on unstable or reclaimed land near faults | Restrict settlement in the highest-risk zones |

## Worked example 1: distribution

*Describe and explain the global distribution of volcanoes.* [5]

**Strong answer.** Volcanoes are found in long, narrow belts rather than
spread evenly [1], for example around the edge of the Pacific Ocean in the
Ring of Fire [1] and along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge [1]. Most lie on plate
boundaries: at destructive boundaries the denser oceanic plate is subducted
and melts, and the magma rises to form explosive volcanoes [1]; at
constructive boundaries plates move apart and magma rises to fill the gap
[1]. A few, such as those in Hawaii, form over hotspots away from plate
boundaries [1]. (Maximum 5.)

The answer *describes* the pattern first with named places, then *explains*
it with processes.

## Worked example 2: case study of a volcano

*For a named volcanic eruption, describe its causes and its effects on people
and the environment.* [7]

A plan for **Mount St Helens, USA, 18 May 1980**:

- **Cause:** the Juan de Fuca Plate is subducted beneath the North American
  Plate; magma built up and the north side of the volcano bulged outwards.
- **Eruption:** an earthquake triggered a huge landslide of the bulging
  north face, releasing a sideways (lateral) blast.
- **Effects on people:** 57 people were killed; homes, roads and bridges were
  destroyed; ash fell across a wide area.
- **Effects on the environment:** forests were flattened over a large area;
  lahars filled river valleys with mud and debris; wildlife was killed; the
  summit was lowered and left with a large crater.
- **Management:** monitoring had led to an exclusion zone being set up before
  the eruption, which reduced the death toll.

For an **earthquake** case study you could use the Tōhoku earthquake and
tsunami in Japan (11 March 2011, magnitude 9.0), where the tsunami caused most
deaths and damaged the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power station, or the Haiti
earthquake of 12 January 2010 (magnitude 7.0), where a shallow focus close to
Port-au-Prince and poorly built buildings led to very heavy loss of life and a
cholera outbreak later that year. Add figures from your textbook.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up **focus** and **epicentre**.
- Saying volcanoes form at conservative boundaries.
- Saying both plates are subducted at a collision boundary.
- Saying earthquakes can be predicted as precisely as many eruptions.
- Listing effects without separating people from the environment when the
  question asks for both.
- Describing only hazards when asked about **opportunities**.

## Exam technique for 2.1

- **Describe** a distribution: give the overall pattern, name places, and
  mention exceptions.
- **Explain**: link each feature to a plate process ("because the oceanic
  plate is denser, it is subducted…").
- **Suggest** why impacts differed between two places: compare building
  quality, preparation and wealth, using the resource.
- **Sketch** or label a diagram: label the magma chamber, vent and crater
  clearly; show layers on a composite cone.
