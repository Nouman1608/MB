## Definitions to learn word for word (11.1)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | How fast a reactant or product concentration changes (change ÷ time) |
| Rate equation | rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ, with m and n equal to 0, 1 or 2; determined experimentally |
| Order with respect to A | The power of [A] in the rate equation |
| Overall order | m + n |
| Rate constant, k | The k that links rate to concentrations; constant at a fixed temperature |
| Half-life | Time needed for a reactant's concentration to drop to 50% of its starting value |
| Rate-determining step | The slowest step of a mechanism |
| Activation energy | Minimum energy needed by colliding particles for reaction |
| Homogeneous catalyst | Same phase as the reactants |
| Heterogeneous catalyst | Different phase from the reactants |

## Units of k at a glance

| Example rate equation | Overall order | k in |
|---|---|---|
| rate = k[A]²[B] | 3 | mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹ |
| rate = k[A][B] | 2 | mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ |
| rate = k[A] | 1 | s⁻¹ |
| rate = k | 0 | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |

**Small worked reminder.** Rate = k[A][B]. With rate = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹, [A] = 0.20 mol dm⁻³ and [B] = 0.050 mol dm⁻³: k = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ (0.20 × 0.050) = 0.030 mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹.

## Graph shapes (11.2, 11.5)

| Order | Concentration-time | Rate-concentration | Half-life |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Straight line, negative gradient | Horizontal line | Falls as [A] falls |
| 1 | Curve, levels off | Straight line through origin | Constant |
| 2 | Steep curve with long tail | Upward curve through origin | Rises as [A] falls |

**Method in steps: order from initial rates (11.5 iii)**

1. Pick a pair of runs in which a single concentration is altered.
2. Work out the factor change in concentration and in rate.
3. Factor in rate = (factor in concentration)ⁿ. Rate unchanged: n = 0. Same factor: n = 1. Factor squared: n = 2.
4. Repeat for each reactant. Write the rate equation, then calculate k from any one experiment, with units.

**Method in steps: half-life from a graph (11.2)**

1. Choose a starting concentration; read the time.
2. Read the time at half that concentration. The difference is t½.
3. Repeat from a different starting point. Same t½ both times: first order.

## Collecting rate data (11.3, 11.4)

- **Titration**: remove samples, quench, titrate (iodine with thiosulfate).
- **Colorimetry**: a coloured species; absorbance tracks concentration with no sampling.
- **Mass change**: a heavy gas (CO₂) lost from an open flask on a balance.
- **Gas volume**: gas syringe; best for light gases such as hydrogen.
- **Other**: conductivity when the number of ions changes; pH meter when [H⁺] changes.
- **Initial-rate method**: separate runs, one concentration changed each time. A clock reaction gives rate ∝ 1/t.
- **Continuous monitoring**: one run, readings over time, to plot concentration-time or volume-time graphs.

## Core Practicals 9a and 9b (11.12)

**9a, iodine-propanone by titration.** Propanone and acid in large excess. Samples quenched in sodium hydrogencarbonate (removes the H⁺ catalyst). Titrate iodine with sodium thiosulfate and starch. A straight-line titre-time graph shows zero order in iodine.

Rate = k[CH₃COCH₃][H⁺]. The slow step involves propanone and H⁺; iodine reacts after it. H⁺ appears in the rate equation but is regenerated, so it is a catalyst (11.6).

**9b, Harcourt-Esson iodine clock.** H₂O₂ + 2I⁻ + 2H⁺ → I₂ + 2H₂O, with a little thiosulfate and starch. The blue-black colour appears when the thiosulfate is used up. Keep total volume constant by adding water, so concentration is proportional to the volume of each solution.

## Mechanisms (11.7, 11.8, 11.9)

**Must-know distinctions**

- **Stoichiometric equation** tells you what reacts overall. **Rate equation** tells you what is in (or before) the slow step.
- **Intermediate**: made in one step, used in a later one. **Catalyst**: used in one step, regenerated later.
- **SN1** (tertiary): rate = k[RX]; slow ionisation to a carbocation, then fast attack by OH⁻.
- **SN2** (primary): rate = k[RX][OH⁻]; one step, through a transition state with OH⁻ and the leaving halogen on opposite sides of carbon.

**Checks for a proposed mechanism**

1. The slow step contains the species in the rate equation, in the right numbers (counting any species used to make an intermediate in an earlier fast step).
2. Zero-order species enter only after the slow step.
3. All steps add up to the overall equation.

## Activation energy (11.10, 11.13)

| Item | What to remember |
|---|---|
| Arrhenius equation | k = Ae^(−Ea/RT); given if needed |
| Straight-line form | ln k = −(Ea/R)(1/T) + ln A |
| Graph | ln k (y) against 1/T (x), with T in kelvin |
| Gradient | −Ea/R, so Ea = −gradient × R |
| Only two runs | ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂) |
| Core Practical 10 | Time the same amount of reaction at several temperatures; 1/t ∝ k, so plot ln(1/t) against 1/T |

R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, so Ea comes out in J mol⁻¹. Divide by 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹.

**Method in steps: Ea from a graph**

1. Convert every temperature to kelvin and work out 1/T.
2. Work out ln k (or ln(1/t)) for each run.
3. Plot, draw the best straight line, and read two well-separated points on that line for the gradient.
4. Ea = −gradient × 8.31, then convert to kJ mol⁻¹.

**Further suggested practicals (11.13):** marble chips and hydrochloric acid (mass change or gas volume); magnesium and hydrochloric acid to find Ea; the iodine-propanone reaction followed by colorimetry; cobalt(II) catalysing potassium sodium tartrate with hydrogen peroxide; urease acting on urea and thiourea.

## Solid catalysts (11.11)

Reactant gases bond weakly (adsorb) at active sites on the solid. Their bonds weaken and they meet in a good orientation, so Ea falls. Products desorb and free the sites. Adsorption must be neither too weak (nothing happens) nor too strong (products stay stuck). A large surface area (powder, mesh or thin coating on a support) gives more sites.

## Quick self-test

1. What are the units of k when rate = k[T]²[V]?
2. Rate = k[M][N]². [M] is tripled and [N] is doubled. By what factor does the rate change?
3. [Z] falls from 0.48 to 0.24 mol dm⁻³ in 35 s and from 0.24 to 0.12 mol dm⁻³ in the next 35 s. State the order in Z and find [Z] after 105 s.
4. A rate-concentration graph for reactant G is a horizontal line. State the order in G.
5. A clock run takes 64 s. With [S] doubled and nothing else changed, it takes 32 s. What is the order in S?
6. A graph of ln k against 1/T has gradient −9.20 × 10³ K. Calculate Ea. (R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)
7. k doubles when the temperature rises from 300 K to 310 K. Calculate Ea.
8. Choose a technique to follow zinc reacting with hydrochloric acid, and justify it.
9. 2-chloro-2-methylpropane is hydrolysed with rate = k[(CH₃)₃CCl]. Name the mechanism.
10. Why are samples in Core Practical 9a run into sodium hydrogencarbonate?
11. Mechanism: 2W → W₂ (slow), then W₂ + Z → W₂Z (fast). Write the rate equation.

### Answers

1. mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹.
2. 3 × 2² = **12**.
3. Constant half-life of 35 s, so **first order**. 105 s is three half-lives: 0.48 → 0.24 → 0.12 → **0.060 mol dm⁻³**.
4. **Zero order**.
5. Time halves, so 1/t (rate) doubles: **first order**.
6. Ea = 9.20 × 10³ × 8.31 = 76 500 J mol⁻¹ = **76.5 kJ mol⁻¹**.
7. ln 2 = (Ea/8.31)(1/300 − 1/310), so Ea = 53 600 J mol⁻¹ ≈ **53.6 kJ mol⁻¹**.
8. **Gas volume** in a gas syringe: hydrogen forms, but it is too light for mass loss to measure accurately.
9. **SN1**.
10. It neutralises the acid catalyst, which stops the reaction so the iodine left at that time can be titrated.
11. Rate = k[W]².

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing orders from the balanced equation instead of from data.
- Giving k without units, or with units for the wrong overall order.
- Using two experiments where more than one concentration changes.
- Confusing "rate halves" with "time halves" in clock reactions: rate ∝ 1/t.
- Plotting ln k against T instead of 1/T, or using T in °C.
- Mixing J and kJ in the Ea calculation.
- Calling H⁺ an intermediate in the iodination of propanone; it is a catalyst.
- Saying iodine "is not involved" because it is zero order. It reacts after the slow step.
- Describing heterogeneous catalysis without the words adsorption and desorption.
