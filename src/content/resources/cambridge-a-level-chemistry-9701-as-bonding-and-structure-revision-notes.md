## The named examples by structure

| Structure | Named examples | Particles | Forces between particles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | NaCl, MgO | cations and anions | ionic bonding in all directions |
| Simple molecular | I₂, C₆₀, ice | molecules | intermolecular: id-id (I₂, C₆₀); hydrogen bonding (ice) |
| Giant molecular | SiO₂, graphite, diamond | atoms | covalent bonds throughout (plus id-id between graphite layers) |
| Giant metallic | Cu | positive ions and delocalised electrons | metallic bonding |

## Lattice descriptions to learn

- **NaCl / MgO**: each ion surrounded by **6** ions of opposite charge.
- **I₂**: I₂ molecules in a regular lattice; weak id-id forces; m.p. 114 °C; sublimes on warming.
- **C₆₀**: cage of **60** C atoms, each bonded to **3** others (12 pentagons, 20 hexagons); molecules held by id-id forces.
- **Ice**: each H₂O hydrogen-bonded to **4** others, tetrahedrally; open lattice (less dense than water).
- **SiO₂**: each Si bonded to **4** O; each O bonded to **2** Si; tetrahedral network.
- **Diamond**: each C bonded to **4** C, tetrahedral; no free electrons.
- **Graphite**: layers of hexagons; each C bonded to **3** C; **1 delocalised electron** per C; id-id forces between layers.
- **Copper**: lattice of positive ions in delocalised electrons.

## Properties summary

| Structure | m.p. / b.p. | Conducts solid? | Conducts liquid? | Solubility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | high | no | yes (and in solution) | often soluble in water; insoluble in non-polar solvents |
| Simple molecular | low | no | no | non-polar: soluble in non-polar solvents; can H-bond: soluble in water |
| Giant molecular | very high | no (graphite: yes, along layers) | — | insoluble |
| Giant metallic | generally high | yes | yes | insoluble (some react) |

## Why ionic compounds dissolve in water

- Water is **polar**: δ− O atoms attract **cations**, δ+ H atoms attract **anions**.
- These attractions help pull ions out of the lattice.
- Non-polar solvents cannot attract ions, so ionic compounds do not dissolve in them.
- Not all ionic compounds are soluble: MgO is only very slightly soluble, because its lattice attraction is very strong.

## Deduction flowchart (in words)

1. **Conducts as solid?** Yes → metallic (or graphite, if a non-metal element with a very high m.p.).
2. **Conducts only when molten or dissolved?** → giant ionic.
3. **Never conducts, very high m.p., insoluble?** → giant molecular.
4. **Never conducts, low m.p.?** → simple molecular. Then use solubility to judge polarity.

## Key comparisons

| Pair | Explanation |
|---|---|
| CO₂ (sublimes −78 °C) vs SiO₂ (m.p. about 1700 °C) | CO₂ simple molecular, weak id-id; SiO₂ giant molecular, many Si–O bonds to break |
| Graphite vs diamond conductivity | graphite has 1 delocalised electron per C; diamond uses all 4 in bonds |
| MgO vs NaCl m.p. | 2+/2− ions, smaller; stronger ionic attraction |
| Ice vs liquid water density | ice's hydrogen-bonded open lattice holds molecules further apart |
| I₂ in hexane vs water | non-polar I₂ forms id-id forces with hexane; cannot compete with water–water hydrogen bonds |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Lattice | Regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of particles |
| Giant structure | Lattice in which strong bonds extend throughout, with no separate molecules |
| Simple molecular | Separate small molecules held by intermolecular forces |
| Delocalised electron | Electron not tied to one atom or bond, free to move |
| Sublimation | Change from solid directly to gas |

## Exam traps

- Simple molecular substances: melting/boiling overcomes **intermolecular forces**, never covalent bonds.
- Graphite is **giant molecular**, not simple molecular or metallic.
- C₆₀ is **simple molecular**, unlike graphite and diamond.
- Ions conduct only when **free to move** (liquid or solution).
- Say **which** attraction must be overcome and **how strong** it is.
- Use **every** piece of data in a deduction, not just melting point.
- A shared formula pattern (CO₂, SiO₂) does not mean a shared structure.

## Self-test

1. Name the structure type of buckminsterfullerene.
2. How many oxygen atoms is each silicon atom bonded to in SiO₂?
3. Explain why iodine has a low melting point.
4. Explain why graphite conducts along its layers.
5. Explain why molten magnesium oxide conducts electricity.
6. Explain why sodium chloride dissolves in water.
7. Explain why diamond is insoluble in all solvents.
8. Substance X melts at −23 °C, does not conduct as a liquid and dissolves in hexane. Deduce its structure.
9. Explain why carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature but silicon(IV) oxide is a high-melting solid.
10. Describe the structure of ice.
11. Why does copper conduct electricity when solid?
12. Why is iodine more soluble in hexane than in water?

**Answers:**

1. Simple molecular.
2. Four.
3. Only weak id-id forces between I₂ molecules must be overcome; the I–I covalent bonds do not break.
4. Each carbon has one delocalised electron, and these electrons move within the layer.
5. The Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions are free to move in the liquid and carry charge.
6. Water is polar; its δ− O atoms attract Na⁺ and its δ+ H atoms attract Cl⁻, helping to separate the ions from the lattice.
7. Dissolving would require breaking strong covalent bonds throughout the giant structure; no solvent attraction can compensate.
8. Simple molecular, non-polar.
9. CO₂ is simple molecular with weak id-id forces between molecules; SiO₂ is giant molecular, and melting needs many strong Si–O covalent bonds to break.
10. Each H₂O molecule is hydrogen-bonded to four others in a tetrahedral arrangement, forming an open lattice.
11. It has delocalised electrons that are free to move.
12. Iodine is non-polar; it forms id-id forces with hexane, but in water it would have to disrupt strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules.
