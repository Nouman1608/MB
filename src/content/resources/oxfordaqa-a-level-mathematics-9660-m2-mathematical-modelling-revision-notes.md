These notes condense section M2.1 (Mathematical modelling) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Mathematics (9660) specification, Version 5.2, at the start of International A-level Unit M2. The section covers the use of assumptions in simplifying reality, the mathematical analysis of models, and the interpretation and validity of models. For full worked examples, read the [mathematical modelling study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-mathematics-9660-m2-mathematical-modelling/); to practise, use the [mathematical modelling practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-mathematics-9660-m2-mathematical-modelling-practice/).

## The modelling cycle in one line

Real problem → assumptions → equations → solution → interpretation → comparison with reality → refinement → (repeat).

## The words the specification names

| Word | Means | So in your equations |
|---|---|---|
| Particle | A point mass with no size | No rotation; forces act at one point; air resistance from shape is ignored |
| Light | Mass is negligible | Same tension throughout a string; no weight for the string or rod |
| Inextensible string | Length cannot change | Connected particles share the same speed and acceleration along the string |
| Smooth surface | No friction | Only a normal reaction, perpendicular to the surface |
| Motion under gravity | Weight is the only force | Constant acceleration g = 9.8 m s⁻² downwards |

Related words you will also meet in Unit M2: **smooth pulley or peg** (tension is unchanged round it), **rough** (friction up to µR), **uniform** (centre of mass at the centre), **rigid** (shape does not change, so moments can be taken), **lamina** (a flat body with negligible thickness).

## Point masses

Large moving bodies can be point masses when their size is small compared with the distances involved.

| Situation | Particle suitable? |
|---|---|
| A car on a 50 km journey | Yes |
| A car overtaking a lorry | No: their lengths affect the time needed |
| A train on a long journey between cities | Yes |
| A train passing through a short station | No: its length matters |
| A satellite in orbit | Yes, for its path |
| A ladder leaning on a wall | No: it is modelled as a rod, since moments matter |

## Direction of the error

| Assumption ignored | Effect on the model's prediction |
|---|---|
| Air resistance on a falling object | Predicted time too short, predicted speed too high |
| Air resistance on a projectile | Predicted range and height too large |
| Friction on a surface | Predicted acceleration and distance too large |
| Mass of a pulley or its friction | Predicted acceleration too large |
| Size of a long moving body | Predicted time to pass a point too short |
| Stretch in a string | Connected bodies predicted to move together when they do not |

## Validity checks

- **Compare with data**: does the prediction match measurements? If not, which assumption is most likely to blame?
- **Common sense**: is a speed, force or time realistic?
- **Range of use**: does the formula stop making sense after some time, for example a braking car "reversing"?
- **Sensitivity**: would a small change in a measured value change the conclusion?
- **Accuracy**: give answers to a sensible accuracy, usually 3 significant figures, because g = 9.8 is itself approximate.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Model | A simplified mathematical description of a real situation |
| Assumption | A simplification chosen to make the problem solvable |
| Validation | Checking a model's predictions against reality |
| Refinement | Changing an assumption to improve the model |
| Range of validity | The values (often times) for which a model gives sensible results |

## Exam traps

- **Repeating the question's own assumptions** instead of stating a new one.
- **Vague comments** such as "it is not accurate" without naming the assumption and the direction of the error.
- **Mixing up "light" and "inextensible"**: light gives equal tension; inextensible gives equal acceleration.
- **Saying a smooth surface has "no forces"**: it still gives a normal reaction.
- **Using a model outside its range**: a formula that predicts negative distance or a speed sign change in a braking problem.
- **Over-precise answers**, such as 23.5200 N from data given to 2 or 3 figures.

## Worked check

A stone is dropped from rest from a height of 20 m. As a particle under gravity, 20 = ½(9.8)t², so t = √(40/9.8) = 2.02 s (3 s.f.). The measured time is 2.2 s. The measured time is longer, consistent with air resistance slowing the stone, so a refined model would include a resistive force. Before refining, check that the timing itself is reliable by repeating the measurement.

## Self-test

1. What does "light" tell you about a string?
2. What does "inextensible" tell you about two particles joined by a string?
3. What does "smooth" tell you about a surface?
4. What does modelling a body as a particle ignore?
5. What does "motion under gravity" assume about the forces?
6. Give one situation where a car may be a particle and one where it may not.
7. A dropped ball is modelled with no air resistance. Will the real fall time be longer or shorter than predicted?
8. A box on a floor modelled as smooth travels less far than predicted. Which assumption should be refined?
9. A cyclist slows with velocity v = 12 − 3t. For which values of t is this model valid?
10. Why is a tension of 23.5200 N a poor final answer when g = 9.8 is used?
11. List the steps of the modelling cycle.
12. How does a rough pulley change a connected-particle model?

### Answers

1. Its mass is negligible, so the tension is the same throughout.
2. They have the same speed and the same size of acceleration while the string is taut.
3. There is no friction; the only contact force is the normal reaction.
4. Its size, shape and rotation; all forces act at a single point.
5. The only force is weight, giving a constant acceleration g downwards.
6. Particle: a long journey between towns. Not a particle: overtaking or parking, where its length matters.
7. Longer, because air resistance reduces the acceleration.
8. The smooth floor: include a frictional force.
9. 0 ≤ t ≤ 4, since v = 0 at t = 4 and the cyclist does not then reverse.
10. g = 9.8 is given to 2 significant figures, so 4 decimal places claim accuracy the model does not have; 23.5 N is suitable.
11. Real problem, assumptions, formulate, solve, interpret, validate, refine.
12. Friction at the pulley makes the tensions on the two sides different, and the acceleration is less than predicted.
