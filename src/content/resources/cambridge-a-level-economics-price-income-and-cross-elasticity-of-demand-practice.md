## Questions

**1.** Define price elasticity of demand. **[2]**

**2.** A ferry company raises the price of a crossing from 25 cu to 30 cu. Weekly passengers fall from 2,000 to 1,700.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether demand is elastic or inelastic. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the change in the company's weekly revenue. **[2]**

**3.** In Country B, average income rises from 30,000 cu to 33,000 cu. Demand for organic vegetables rises from 400 to 500 units a week. Demand for dried noodles falls from 900 to 846 units a week.

**(a)** Calculate the income elasticity of demand for each good. **[2]**
**(b)** Classify each good. **[2]**

**4.** The price of Brand A coffee pods rises from 2.00 cu to 2.50 cu. Demand for Brand B pods rises from 1,200 to 1,350. Separately, the price of a coffee machine rises from 80 cu to 88 cu and demand for pods falls from 2,500 to 2,400.

**(a)** Calculate the cross elasticity of demand of Brand B pods with respect to the price of Brand A pods. **[2]**
**(b)** State the relationship between the two brands. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the cross elasticity between machines and pods, and state the relationship. **[2]**

**5.** Demand for a good is given by Q = 90 − 3P, where Q is units and P is price in cu.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand when P = 20. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate total revenue at P = 20, P = 15 and P = 10, and state which price gives the highest revenue of the three. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why elasticity differs along the curve although its slope is constant. **[2]**

**6.** A firm sells 800 units a week at 5.00 cu. It plans to cut the price to 4.50 cu. Its estimate of PED is −0.6.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in price. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the predicted new weekly quantity. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate total revenue before and after the cut. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why revenue falls, and give one reason the firm might still cut the price. **[2]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why demand for rail season tickets is likely to be more price elastic over five years than over one month after a fare rise. **[4]**

**8.** Good M rises in price from 6 cu to 9 cu. Good N rises from 60 cu to 63 cu. For each good, quantity demanded falls from 80 to 70 units.

**(a)** Calculate PED for each good. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the two values differ although both prices rose by 3 cu and both quantities fell by 10 units. **[2]**

**9.** A retailer expects incomes in Country B to rise by 4%. Its premium coffee has YED of +1.8 and its own-brand tinned beans have YED of −0.7.

**(a)** Calculate the expected percentage change in demand for each product. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two ways the retailer could use this information. **[4]**

**10.** A bus company charges a fare of 2.00 cu and carries 10,000 passengers a week. It considers raising the fare by 20%. Its research suggests PED is −0.4 in the short run and −1.3 in the long run.

Evaluate whether the company should raise the fare to increase revenue. **[8]**

**11.** A firm sells printers and cartridges. It cuts the printer price from 150 cu to 135 cu. Printer sales rise from 2,000 to 2,300 a month. Cartridge sales rise from 6,000 to 6,480 a month. A cartridge costs the customer 12 cu.

**(a)** Calculate PED for printers. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate XED of cartridges with respect to the printer price. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the change in monthly revenue from printers and cartridges together. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain one reason the firm might be wrong to rely on these figures when setting future prices. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** PED shows how far the quantity demanded of a good changes [1] when the price of that same good changes [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both halves: what responds (quantity demanded) and what changes (the good's own price).

**2. (a)** %ΔP = (30 − 25) ÷ 25 = +20% and %ΔQd = (1,700 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = −15% [1]. PED = −15 ÷ 20 = **−0.75** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The size is below 1, so demand is **inelastic** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Revenue before = 25 × 2,000 = 50,000 cu. After = 30 × 1,700 = 51,000 cu [1]. Revenue **rises by 1,000 cu** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both percentage changes, so a marker can see the method behind the final figure. Link part (c) back to part (b).

**3. (a)** %ΔY = +10%. Organic vegetables: %ΔQd = +25%, so YED = **+2.5** [1]. Noodles: %ΔQd = (846 − 900) ÷ 900 = −6%, so YED = **−0.6** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Organic vegetables are a normal good with income elastic demand (YED above 1) [1]. Noodles are an inferior good (negative YED) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The sign carries the meaning for YED. A lost minus sign turns an inferior good into a normal one.

**4. (a)** %ΔP of A = +25% and %ΔQd of B = +12.5% [1]. XED = 12.5 ÷ 25 = **+0.5** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The brands are **substitutes** (positive XED). [1] **[1]**
**(c)** %ΔP of machines = +10% and %ΔQd of pods = −4%, so XED = **−0.4** [1]. The goods are **complements** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the price of the other good as the denominator. Brand B's own price would give a PED, not an XED.

**5. (a)** At P = 20, Q = 90 − 60 = 30 [1]. PED = (change in Q ÷ change in P) × (P ÷ Q) = −3 × (20 ÷ 30) = **−2.0** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At P = 20: 20 × 30 = 600. At P = 15: Q = 45, revenue = 675. At P = 10: Q = 60, revenue = 600 [1]. Revenue is highest at **P = 15** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Slope compares absolute changes, but elasticity compares percentage changes [1]. Moving down the curve, a given price fall becomes a bigger percentage of a falling price while a given quantity rise becomes a smaller percentage of a rising quantity, so elasticity falls [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* At P = 15 the PED is −1, which is why revenue peaks there. Linking the results is stronger than listing figures.

**6. (a)** (4.50 − 5.00) ÷ 5.00 = **−10%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** %ΔQd = −0.6 × −10 = +6%, so new quantity = 800 × 1.06 = **848** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Before = 5.00 × 800 = 4,000 cu. After = 4.50 × 848 = 3,816 cu [1]. Revenue **falls by 184 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Demand is inelastic, so the quantity rise (6%) is smaller than the price fall (10%) [1]. The firm might still cut to win market share, or to clear stock before it spoils [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two percentages. "Price cuts raise sales" alone does not explain why revenue fell.

**7.** Time to find substitutes: over five years commuters can move closer to work or buy a car [1], so more stop buying tickets and quantity falls by a larger percentage [1]. Time to change habits: in one month travellers are tied to their routine and home [1], but these ties loosen over five years, so demand responds more to the same rise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the factor, then say why it makes demand more elastic.

**8. (a)** Good M: %ΔP = +50%, %ΔQd = (70 − 80) ÷ 80 = −12.5%, PED = **−0.25** [1]. Good N: %ΔP = +5%, %ΔQd = −12.5%, PED = **−2.5** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The same absolute rise of 3 cu is 50% of M's price but only 5% of N's price [1]. Elasticity uses percentage changes, so the same quantity response is small relative to M's large price change and large relative to N's small one [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote both bases (6 and 60) and give the reason: a different starting price.

**9. (a)** Premium coffee: 1.8 × 4 = **+7.2%** [1]. Own-brand beans: −0.7 × 4 = **−2.8%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The retailer could stock more premium coffee in anticipation of higher demand [1], avoiding shortages as incomes rise [1]. It could reduce shelf space for the beans [1], since sales are expected to fall, freeing space for goods with higher YED [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each use to a number from part (a), and note that YED values are estimates.

**10.** Fare rises 20%, from 2.00 to 2.40 cu [1]. In the short run, quantity falls 0.4 × 20 = 8% to 9,200 passengers. Revenue rises from 20,000 to 2.40 × 9,200 = 22,080 cu [1]. In the long run, quantity falls 1.3 × 20 = 26% to 7,400. Revenue = 2.40 × 7,400 = 17,760 cu, below 20,000 [1] [1]. Demand is more elastic over time because passengers find alternatives, such as cars or moving closer to work [1], and habits change slowly [1]. Judgement: the rise helps revenue only in the short run, so it depends on the time horizon [1]. Fewer passengers also reduce costs, and revenue is not profit, so the company would need cost data, and consider the effect on riders and rival transport [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculate both time horizons, then use the contrast for the judgement. Say under which conditions the rise works.

**11. (a)** %ΔP = −10%, %ΔQd = (2,300 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = +15% [1]. PED = **−1.5** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** %ΔQd of cartridges = (6,480 − 6,000) ÷ 6,000 = +8% [1]. XED = 8 ÷ −10 = **−0.8** (complements) [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Printers: 150 × 2,000 = 300,000 and 135 × 2,300 = 310,500, a rise of 10,500 [1]. Cartridges: 12 × 6,000 = 72,000 and 12 × 6,480 = 77,760, a rise of 5,760 [1]. Total rise = **16,260 cu** a month [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** The values come from past data and one price change [1]. Competitors may react, tastes may change, and the elasticity may differ at a different price level [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Revenue is only part of the decision: note that the firm needs cost data to judge profit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dropping the sign of YED or XED, or ignoring the sign on PED.
- Using the new value as the base without saying so.
- Calling a size of 0.75 or 0.9 elastic.
- Putting the wrong price in XED: it is the price of the other good.
- Assuming a steeper demand curve has lower elasticity at every point.
- Naming a factor affecting elasticity without explaining the direction.
- Concluding an evaluation question with a summary and no judgement.
