This guide covers **section 6.5 Policies to correct imbalances in the current account of the balance of payments** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content. The syllabus lists two statements: the "government policy objective of stability of the current account" and the "effect of fiscal, monetary, supply-side and protectionist policies on the current account".

The broader [current account and policies study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-current-account-of-the-balance-of-payments-and/) works through a multiplier-based fiscal example and a tariff example. This page goes further: it shows each policy correcting a surplus as well as a deficit, separates the routes by which one policy can push the account in opposite directions, and gives a method for matching a policy to the cause. All countries and figures are invented.

## 6.5.1 Stability, not zero

The objective is **stability** of the current account: avoiding deficits or surpluses that are large relative to GDP and that last for many years. A balance of exactly zero every year is neither expected nor necessary. Small, short-lived imbalances are normal as economies move through booms and slowdowns.

Why governments care:

- **Large deficits** must be financed by borrowing from abroad or selling assets to foreigners. If lenders lose confidence, finance can dry up, the currency can fall sharply and the government may be forced into sudden cuts in spending.
- **Large surpluses** mean residents consume less than the country produces, and they create matching deficits in partner countries, which can lead to pressure for the surplus country to change its policies or to protection against its exports.
- **Stability supports other objectives.** Sudden swings in the account bring swings in AD, the exchange rate and import prices, which make steady growth and stable prices harder to achieve.

## 6.5.2 How each policy affects the current account

Before choosing a policy, identify which line of the account it acts on and through which route.

| Policy | To reduce a deficit | Main route | To reduce a surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fiscal | Raise taxes or cut government spending | Lower incomes → less spending on imports | Cut taxes or raise spending |
| Monetary | Raise interest rates or tighten credit | Less borrowing and spending → fewer imports | Cut rates or relax credit |
| Supply-side | Training, infrastructure, support for technology | Lower costs and better quality → more exports, fewer imports | Not normally used to reduce a surplus |
| Protectionist | Tariffs, quotas, export subsidies, embargoes, administrative barriers | Imports dearer or limited; exports cheaper abroad | Remove or reduce protection |

### Fiscal policy: the cost of cutting imports

Contractionary fiscal policy reduces disposable income and AD. Only part of the fall in spending lands on imports; the rest lands on home-produced goods.

**Worked example 1.** Country H raises income tax by 10 billion. Households cut consumption by 0.8 of the fall in disposable income, and 30% of the spending they cut was on imports.

- Fall in consumption = 0.8 × 10 = 8 billion.
- Fall in imports = 0.3 × 8 = 2.4 billion, so the current account improves by 2.4 billion.
- Fall in spending on home-produced goods = 8 − 2.4 = 5.6 billion, before any further rounds of lower spending.

For every unit by which the deficit narrows, spending on domestic output falls by more than two units. This is why fiscal tightening to correct a deficit usually costs growth and jobs, and why it is most suitable when the deficit comes from an overheating economy.

### Monetary policy: two routes that pull in opposite directions

A rise in interest rates reduces borrowing-financed spending, so imports fall. But it also tends to attract money from abroad, which raises the exchange rate, making exports dearer and imports cheaper.

**Worked example 2 (illustrative figures).** Country J raises interest rates.

| Route | Effect on the current account (billion) |
|---|---|
| Lower consumption and investment reduce imports | +3.0 |
| Appreciation reduces export sales | −1.2 |
| Appreciation makes imports cheaper, raising import volumes | −0.8 |
| Net effect | +1.0 |

The net improvement is much smaller than the first route suggests, and with a large appreciation it could disappear. When you analyse monetary policy and the current account, always state both routes and say that the overall effect depends on how far the currency moves.

### Supply-side policy: the slow but lasting route

Supply-side policies raise productivity and quality. Lower unit costs improve **price competitiveness**; better design, reliability and innovation improve **non-price competitiveness**. Both raise exports and make home goods more attractive than imports. Unlike fiscal and monetary contraction, the improvement comes with higher output rather than lower output, but it takes years, and the policies cost government money now.

### Protectionist policy: quick, but with side effects

The syllabus tools of protection are tariffs, import quotas, export subsidies, embargoes and excessive administrative burdens.

**Worked example 3: a quota and retaliation.** Country K imports 2 million units of a good at a world price of 15 each, spending 30 million. It imposes a quota of 1.5 million units.

- If the world price is unchanged, import spending falls to 1.5 × 15 = 22.5 million, an improvement of 7.5 million.
- Domestic buyers face a shortage at the old price, so the domestic price rises and consumers lose.
- If a trading partner retaliates by restricting 10 million of Country K's exports, the current account ends 2.5 million **worse** than before.

Protection can also raise costs for firms that use imported inputs, reducing their export competitiveness, and it may break trade agreements.

## Matching the policy to the cause

| Cause of the deficit | Most suitable policy | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic boom drawing in imports | Contractionary fiscal or monetary | It reduces the excess spending that caused the problem |
| Low productivity and high unit costs | Supply-side | It improves competitiveness, the root cause |
| Poor quality or design of exports | Supply-side (technology, training) | It improves non-price competitiveness |
| Sudden surge of imports harming one industry | Temporary protection | It gives short-term relief, though retaliation is a risk |
| Fall in the world price of a main export | No quick fix; supply-side to diversify exports | Demand-side tools do not raise the export price |

For a **surplus** caused by weak domestic demand, expansionary fiscal or monetary policy raises spending at home, including on imports, and supports growth too.

**Annotated model answer.** *Question:* Explain why contractionary fiscal policy may reduce a current account deficit but harm employment.

> Higher taxes reduce disposable income, so consumption falls. **[Mechanism.]** Part of the fall is spending on imports, so import spending falls and the deficit narrows. **[Link to the account.]** The rest of the fall is spending on home-produced goods, so AD falls and firms cut output and jobs. **[Side effect.]** The lower the share of spending that goes on imports, the more jobs are lost for each unit of improvement in the account. **[Condition.]**

## Common mistakes

- Treating a fall in imports as the whole effect of fiscal tightening and ignoring the fall in domestic output.
- Giving only the spending route for monetary policy and forgetting the exchange-rate route.
- Expecting supply-side policy to work quickly.
- Ignoring retaliation when discussing protection.
- Assuming the aim is a zero balance.
- Using exchange rate policy or the terms expenditure-switching and expenditure-reducing as the main content at AS; these belong to A Level section 11.1.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Explain:** policy, route, line of the account, effect on the balance.
- **Assess** and **Evaluate:** match policy to cause, then weigh time lags, side effects on growth, employment and inflation, and retaliation. End with a conditional judgement.
