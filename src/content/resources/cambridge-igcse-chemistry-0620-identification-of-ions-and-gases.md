This guide covers subtopic **12.5 Identification of ions and gases** of Topic 12, Experimental techniques and chemical analysis, in the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. All four outcomes are **Core**. The syllabus provides the "Notes for use in qualitative analysis" in both practical papers, but the theory papers expect you to recall the tests, so learn them. For quick recall use the [ions and gases revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-identification-of-ions-and-gases-revision-notes/); for exam-style practice use the [ions and gases practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-identification-of-ions-and-gases-practice/). The full tables of results are also set out in [Identification of Ions and Gases](/resources/identification-of-ions-and-gases/); this guide concentrates on why each test works, how to tell close results apart and how to deduce an unknown.

## What 12.5 asks you to do

1. Describe tests for the **anions** carbonate, chloride, bromide, iodide, nitrate, sulfate and sulfite.
2. Describe tests using **aqueous sodium hydroxide** and **aqueous ammonia** for the cations aluminium, ammonium, calcium, chromium(III), copper(II), iron(II), iron(III) and zinc.
3. Describe tests for the **gases** ammonia, carbon dioxide, chlorine, hydrogen, oxygen and sulfur dioxide.
4. Describe **flame tests** for lithium, sodium, potassium, calcium, barium and copper(II).

A full "describe" answer has three parts: **what you add**, **what you see**, and (where asked) **what it shows**.

## Anion tests and why the reagents are chosen

| Anion | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| carbonate, CO₃²⁻ | add dilute acid; test the gas | effervescence; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky |
| chloride, Cl⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, add aqueous silver nitrate | white precipitate |
| bromide, Br⁻ | as for chloride | cream precipitate |
| iodide, I⁻ | as for chloride | yellow precipitate |
| sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, add aqueous barium nitrate | white precipitate |
| sulfite, SO₃²⁻ | add acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) | purple → colourless |
| nitrate, NO₃⁻ | add aqueous sodium hydroxide and aluminium foil; warm carefully | ammonia produced: turns damp red litmus blue |

### Why acidify with nitric acid first?

Carbonate ions also form precipitates with silver ions and barium ions. Adding dilute nitric acid first **removes any carbonate** (as carbon dioxide), so a precipitate can only come from the ion being tested for.

### Why nitric acid and not another acid?

- Hydrochloric acid would **add chloride ions**, giving a white precipitate with silver nitrate whatever the sample contained.
- Sulfuric acid would **add sulfate ions**, giving a white precipitate with barium nitrate whatever the sample contained.

Nitric acid adds only nitrate ions, which give no precipitate with either reagent.

### Halide colours

White (chloride), cream (bromide), yellow (iodide) are close, so describe them exactly as the syllabus does. "Off-white" or "pale yellow" for bromide risks losing the mark.

### The nitrate test needs care

Aluminium in alkaline conditions **reduces** nitrate ions to ammonia. But an **ammonium** salt also gives ammonia when warmed with sodium hydroxide, even without aluminium. If a sample might contain ammonium ions, warm it with sodium hydroxide alone first: if ammonia is given off, ammonium is present; any further ammonia produced after adding aluminium foil then comes from nitrate.

### Sulfite and sulfur dioxide share a reagent

Acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) goes from **purple to colourless** with sulfite ions and with sulfur dioxide gas. The context tells you which test is meant: a solution is being tested for an anion, or a gas is being bubbled through.

## Cation tests with sodium hydroxide and ammonia

Add the reagent **a few drops at a time**, look for a precipitate, then add **excess** and see whether the precipitate dissolves.

| Cation | Aqueous sodium hydroxide | Aqueous ammonia |
|---|---|---|
| aluminium, Al³⁺ | white ppt., **soluble** in excess, colourless solution | white ppt., **insoluble** in excess |
| ammonium, NH₄⁺ | ammonia produced on warming | — |
| calcium, Ca²⁺ | white ppt., insoluble in excess | no ppt. or very slight white ppt. |
| chromium(III), Cr³⁺ | green ppt., **soluble** in excess | green ppt., insoluble in excess |
| copper(II), Cu²⁺ | light blue ppt., insoluble in excess | light blue ppt., **soluble** in excess, **dark blue** solution |
| iron(II), Fe²⁺ | green ppt., insoluble in excess; turns brown near surface on standing | same as with sodium hydroxide |
| iron(III), Fe³⁺ | red-brown ppt., insoluble in excess | red-brown ppt., insoluble in excess |
| zinc, Zn²⁺ | white ppt., **soluble** in excess, colourless solution | white ppt., **soluble** in excess, colourless solution |

### Telling similar results apart

- **Three white precipitates with sodium hydroxide** (Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺). Excess sodium hydroxide separates calcium (insoluble) from the other two. **Aqueous ammonia** then separates them: zinc's precipitate dissolves in excess; aluminium's does not.
- **Two green precipitates** (Cr³⁺, Fe²⁺). Excess sodium hydroxide dissolves chromium(III) hydroxide but not iron(II) hydroxide. Iron(II)'s precipitate also turns brown at the surface on standing, as it is oxidised by air.
- **Copper(II)** is the only cation giving a dark blue solution with excess ammonia.

### Ionic equations *(Extended)*

The precipitates are insoluble hydroxides, for example:

```
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s)
Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)
Ag⁺(aq)  + Cl⁻(aq)  → AgCl(s)
Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
```

## Gas tests

| Gas | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| ammonia, NH₃ | damp red litmus paper | turns blue |
| carbon dioxide, CO₂ | bubble through limewater | turns milky |
| chlorine, Cl₂ | damp litmus paper | bleached |
| hydrogen, H₂ | lighted splint | "pops" |
| oxygen, O₂ | glowing splint | relights |
| sulfur dioxide, SO₂ | acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) | purple → colourless |

Litmus paper must be **damp**: gases act on litmus once they dissolve in water. "Lighted" splint for hydrogen, "glowing" splint for oxygen – swapping these words loses the mark.

## Flame tests

Dip a clean flame-test wire into the sample and hold it in a hot Bunsen flame; note the colour.

| Ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| lithium, Li⁺ | red |
| sodium, Na⁺ | yellow |
| potassium, K⁺ | lilac |
| calcium, Ca²⁺ | orange-red |
| barium, Ba²⁺ | light green |
| copper(II), Cu²⁺ | blue-green |

A flame test is the syllabus method for **sodium, potassium, lithium and barium** ions, which are not among the cations tested with sodium hydroxide and ammonia. Calcium and copper(II) can be confirmed by both methods.

## Worked example 1: identifying an unknown salt

*Solution W gives these results. (1) Aqueous sodium hydroxide: white precipitate, dissolves in excess. (2) Aqueous ammonia: white precipitate, does not dissolve in excess. (3) Dilute nitric acid then aqueous silver nitrate: yellow precipitate. Identify W.*

- Test 1: white, soluble in excess → **Al³⁺ or Zn²⁺**.
- Test 2: insoluble in excess ammonia → **Al³⁺** (zinc's would dissolve).
- Test 3: yellow precipitate → **I⁻**.
- W is **aluminium iodide**.

The method: use each result to narrow the options, and say which result rules each option out.

## Worked example 2: a gas from an unknown

*A white solid fizzes with dilute hydrochloric acid. The gas turns limewater milky. The solid gives an orange-red flame. Identify it.*

Effervescence plus limewater → **carbonate**. Orange-red flame → **calcium**. The solid is **calcium carbonate**.

## Common mistakes

- **Using hydrochloric or sulfuric acid** to acidify in the halide or sulfate tests.
- **Leaving out "in excess"** observations: precipitate colour alone often does not identify the ion.
- **Describing the limewater result vaguely**, such as "it reacts": the syllabus result is that limewater **turns milky**.
- **Dry litmus paper.**
- **Swapping lighted and glowing splints.**
- **"Purple flame"** for potassium: the syllabus colour is **lilac**.
- **Forgetting to warm** in the ammonium and nitrate tests.

## Exam technique

- **Describe a test:** reagent(s), conditions such as "warm", and the positive result.
- **Identify / Deduce:** link each observation to the ion it shows, and use the "excess" results to choose between similar ions.
- **Explain:** give the reason for a step, such as nitric acid removing carbonate.
