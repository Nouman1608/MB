These questions cover subtopic **1.2 Classification of businesses** of Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 (2026 series). All businesses, countries and figures are invented. The number in square brackets after the last part of each question is the total for the whole question.

Related: the [Classification of businesses study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-classification-of-businesses/) and the [Classification of businesses revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-classification-of-businesses-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the tertiary sector. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the economic sector of each activity: a company felling trees; a factory making tables; a firm that moves furniture between houses. **[3]**

**3.** In a country, 9.6 million of its 24 million workers are employed in the tertiary sector. Calculate the percentage of workers in the tertiary sector. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** examples of public sector organisations. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a public limited company is classified as part of the private sector. **[2]**

**6.** Explain, using an example, why businesses in the three economic sectors depend on each other. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** The table shows employment in Country X, in millions of workers.

| Sector | Year A | Year B |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 6.0 | 3.5 |
| Secondary | 4.5 | 3.0 |
| Tertiary | 4.5 | 7.5 |
| Total | 15.0 | 14.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of workers in the tertiary sector in Year A and in Year B.

**(b)** Identify whether Country X shows a pattern of industrialisation or deindustrialisation, giving a reason from the data.

**(c)** Explain **two** possible reasons for the change in the tertiary sector. **[8]**

**8.** A foreign car manufacturer opens a large factory in a developing country.

**(a)** Explain **two** reasons why the secondary sector often grows in developing economies.

**(b)** Explain **one** possible effect of the new factory on farms near it. **[6]**

**9.** Highmoor Coffee grows coffee beans on its own farm, roasts and packs them in its own factory and sells coffee in its own cafés.

**(a)** Identify the economic sector of each of Highmoor's three activities.

**(b)** A drought sharply reduces the coffee harvest. Explain how this could affect each part of Highmoor's business. **[6]**

**10.** In Country Y, water supply is run by a public corporation. Some politicians want it to be run by private sector businesses instead. Consider the arguments for keeping water supply in the public sector. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**11.** Classify each organisation as private sector or public sector: a state hospital; an airline that is a public limited company; a partnership of lawyers; a government-owned electricity company; a charity that runs an orphanage. **[5]**

**12.** Explain **two** reasons why the primary sector usually employs a larger share of workers in developing economies than in developed economies. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The part of the economy made up of businesses that provide services [1] to consumers and to other businesses, such as banking, retail or transport [1].

**2.** Felling trees: **primary** [1]. Making tables: **secondary** [1]. Moving furniture: **tertiary** [1].

**3.** 9.6 ÷ 24 × 100 [1] = **40%** [1].

**4.** Any two [1] [1]: a state school; a state hospital; a public corporation such as a state-owned railway or postal service; the armed forces; a government department.

**5.** It is owned by private shareholders, not by the government [1]; "public" only means its shares can be bought by members of the public on a stock exchange [1].

**6.** Each sector needs the others for inputs or customers: for example, a bakery (secondary) needs flour from farmers (primary) [1] and shops and transport firms (tertiary) to get bread to customers, so a problem in one sector affects the others [1].

**7.** **(a)** Year A: 4.5 ÷ 15.0 × 100 = **30%** [1]. Year B: 7.5 ÷ 14.0 × 100 = **53.6%** [1].

**(b)** Deindustrialisation [1]: the number of secondary workers fell from 4.5 million to 3.0 million while tertiary employment grew [1].

**(c)** Two reasons, each identified [1] and explained [1]: rising incomes mean people spend more on services such as restaurants, travel and health care, so service businesses employ more workers; factories face competition from lower-cost imports, so some close and workers move into service jobs; automation means factories need fewer workers, so jobs grow mainly in services.

**8.** **(a)** Two reasons, each identified [1] and explained [1]: foreign businesses build factories to benefit from lower wage costs, creating manufacturing jobs; mechanisation of farming releases workers who move to towns for factory work; rising incomes increase demand for manufactured goods such as clothing and appliances; more raw materials are processed at home instead of being exported raw.

**(b)** One effect identified [1] and explained [1]: farms may lose workers who move to the better-paid factory jobs, raising farm wage costs or reducing output; or farms may gain new customers if the factory's workers spend their wages on local food.

**9.** **(a)** Growing: **primary** [1]; roasting and packing: **secondary** [1]; cafés: **tertiary** [1].

**(b)** The farm has fewer beans to harvest, so its output falls [1]; the factory has fewer beans to roast, or must buy beans from other growers at higher prices, raising costs [1]; the cafés may have to raise prices or accept lower profit margins, and could lose customers [1].

**10.** For the public sector: water is essential for health, so it should be available to everyone whatever their ability to pay [1]; a government-owned supplier can keep prices low for poorer households instead of setting prices for profit [1]; it can supply remote areas where a private business might find it unprofitable [1]. Against: private businesses may be more efficient because they aim to cut costs to make profit [1], and they may invest more in new pipes if they expect a return [1]. Judgement: keep it in the public sector because access to safe water matters more than profit, unless the public corporation is clearly inefficient and the government can regulate private suppliers' prices and quality [1].

**11.** State hospital: **public** [1]. Airline plc: **private** [1]. Partnership of lawyers: **private** [1]. Government-owned electricity company: **public** [1]. Charity orphanage: **private** [1].

**12.** Two reasons, each identified [1] and explained [1]: farming is often less mechanised, so many workers are needed to produce food; incomes are lower, so a larger share of spending goes on basic food, keeping farming important; the economy may depend on exporting raw materials such as crops or minerals; there are fewer manufacturing and service jobs available for workers to move into.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling construction or food processing tertiary.
- Calculating a share from the wrong total, such as using 15.0 instead of 14.0 for Year B in question 7.
- Describing the trend in the data without giving a reason for it.
- Placing a public limited company or a charity in the public sector.
- Giving only one side in a "consider... justify" answer, as in question 10.
