Revise first with the [6.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-changes-in-rural-settlements/) and the [6.1 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-changes-in-rural-settlements-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "threshold population". **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by rural depopulation. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why remote rural areas often lose services faster than accessible rural areas. **[3]**

**4.** The table shows changes in the fictional village of Hollin, 30 km from a large city in a high income country.

| | 2000 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 900 | 1 350 |
| Residents working in the city (%) | 25 | 60 |
| Homes that are second homes (%) | 2 | 9 |
| Mean house price (thousands of dollars) | 120 | 390 |
| Shops and post offices | 3 | 1 |
| Primary school pupils | 70 | 95 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in Hollin's population between 2000 and 2025. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Using evidence from the table, describe how Hollin has changed. **[4]**

**(c)** Suggest why the number of shops fell while the population grew. **[3]**

**5.** A clinic serving a group of villages needs 4 000 registered patients to remain open. In 2015 the villages had 9 000 people, of whom 50% were registered. By 2025 the population was 7 500 and 48% were registered.

**(a)** Calculate the number of registered patients in 2015 and in 2025. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest **two** responses that could keep health care available if the clinic closes in future. **[2]**

**6.** Explain **two** ways in which remittances can change rural settlements in low income countries. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how the growth of a city can affect rural settlements on its edge in low and middle income countries. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the issues that second homes can create for rural settlements. **[4]**

**9.** Compare the issues facing rural settlements in high income countries with those facing rural settlements in low income countries. **[6]**

**10.** With reference to a rural settlement or rural area you have studied, evaluate the responses to the issues of its growth or decline. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** The minimum number of people [1] needed to support a service or shop so that it stays open and profitable [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A fall in the population of a rural area over time [1], mainly because more people move out, especially young adults, than move in or are born [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Remote areas have smaller, more scattered populations, so services fall below their thresholds [1]. Young people leave, so schools lose pupils and close [1]. Accessible areas gain commuters and families, which helps services such as schools stay above their thresholds [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** (1 350 − 900) ÷ 900 × 100 [1] = 50% increase [1].

**(b)** Population grew by half, from 900 to 1 350 [1]. Hollin became a commuter village: residents working in the city rose from 25% to 60% [1]. House prices more than tripled, from 120 to 390 thousand dollars [1]. Second homes rose from 2% to 9%, shops fell from 3 to 1, but pupils rose from 70 to 95 [1].

**(c)** Most residents now work in the city, so they shop there or at large supermarkets on the way home [1]. Car ownership and online shopping reduce spending in village shops [1]. Second homes stand empty for much of the year, so they add little spending, and the shops fall below their thresholds [1]. **[9]**

**5. (a)** 2015: 9 000 × 0.50 = 4 500 [1]. 2025: 7 500 × 0.48 = 3 600 [1].

**(b)** A mobile clinic that visits each village on set days [1]; telemedicine, with consultations by phone or video, or a community transport scheme to a clinic in town [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* note that the 2025 figure (3 600) is below the threshold of 4 000, which is why part (b) matters.

**6.** Remittances pay for new concrete houses and home improvements [1], changing the appearance and building materials of the village [1]. They also pay school fees and health care [1], raising education levels, though households without migrants fall behind, widening inequality [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Farmland is bought or taken for housing, industry and roads [1]. Some farmers lose their livelihood, while others gain money from selling land [1]. Absorbed villages are built up densely, often as rented rooms for migrants [1]. Water, sanitation and drainage cannot keep pace, so living conditions worsen [1]. Former farmers may become landlords or traders, changing the village economy [1]. Land prices rise, so poorer households cannot buy land [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Demand from buyers of second homes raises house prices [1], so local young people cannot afford to stay [1]. Homes are empty for much of the year, so school rolls fall and shops become seasonal or close [1]. Community life weakens, as fewer permanent residents take part in local organisations [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Both face loss of young people from remote areas and difficulty providing services to scattered populations [1]. In HICs, accessible villages face rising house prices and commuter pressure [1], whereas in LICs the main issue is poverty and lack of basic services such as electricity and clean water [1]. Second homes are an issue mainly in HICs [1], whereas absorption of villages by fast-growing cities is a greater issue in LICs and MICs [1]. Remittances are more important to rural incomes in LICs than in HICs [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* a named, located settlement or area [1] and whether it is growing or declining [1].

*Issues:* change in population and age structure with data [1]; changes in services, housing or employment [1]; causes linked to migration or urban growth [1].

*Responses:* at least two named responses [1], with their aims [1] and the organisations involved, such as government, community or businesses [1].

*Evaluation:* evidence that a response worked, such as population or service change [1]; limits or unintended effects [1]; who gained and who lost [1]; whether the response is sustainable without continuing funding [1].

*Use of detail:* specific dates, figures or place names from the case study [1].

*Judgement:* an overall view on how far the responses have succeeded [1], supported by the evaluation [1]. **[15]**

*Examiner insight:* responses must be evaluated, not just described; a full account of the village with no judgement stays in the lower levels.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a table without quoting values for both dates.
- Assuming population growth must mean more shops.
- Forgetting the threshold when interpreting service data.
- Giving only HIC examples in comparison questions.
- Case study answers with no data or dates.
- Responses described but not evaluated.
