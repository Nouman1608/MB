## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by commodity dependence. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two orientations that modernisation theorists, following Parsons, associate with modern societies. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what Sen meant by development as the expansion of capabilities. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two reasons why inequalities in health persist between countries. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which wars and conflicts harm development after the fighting ends. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for the fictional country of Odera.

| Indicator (fictional) | 2000 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Children aged 6 to 11 enrolled in primary school (%) | 61 | 94 |
| Children aged 10 able to read a simple short text (%) | 48 | 52 |

**(a)** Calculate the change, in percentage points, in enrolment and in reading between 2000 and 2025. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain how the data support Pritchett's argument about education in less developed countries. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> In the fictional district of Haveli, a development agency installed irrigation pumps. Training in using and repairing the pumps was offered to the registered head of each household, almost always a man. Before the project, women grew most of the vegetables sold at the local market. After the project, men took control of the irrigated plots and of the income from selling vegetables. Women continued to collect water for the household, now walking further because the pumps were placed in the fields.

**(a)** Identify two ways in which the project changed women's position in Haveli. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse how feminist sociologists would explain the project's effects. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which globalisation contributes to environmental problems in less developed countries. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three criticisms of the modernisation view of obstacles to progress. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how debt may contribute to the persistence of inequalities in health and education. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate Marxist views of the obstacles to progress in less developed countries. **[12]**

**12.** "Sustainable development is the best route to progress for less developed countries." To what extent do you agree? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** Reliance on exporting a small number of raw materials or crops [1], whose prices swing sharply and whose processed value is captured elsewhere, leaving the economy vulnerable [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: achievement [1]; universalism [1]; self-orientation; affective neutrality; specificity. **[2]**

**3.** Sen saw development as expanding people's real freedoms [1] to live lives they value, such as being healthy, educated and safe [1], so rising income alone is not progress if these freedoms do not grow [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Good care is least available where most needed (the inverse care law) [1], because clinics and doctors cluster in richer, urban areas [1]. Doctors and nurses trained in poorer countries migrate to richer ones [1], leaving health systems short of staff [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Schools, clinics and roads are destroyed and take years to rebuild [1], so services and trade remain weak long after peace [1]. Investors avoid post-conflict areas and the risk of renewed war remains (Collier's conflict trap) [1], so growth stays low and further conflict becomes more likely [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Enrolment rose by 33 percentage points (94 - 61) [1]; reading rose by 4 percentage points (52 - 48) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Enrolment rose sharply, by 33 points, so far more children attend school [1], but only 4 points more can read a simple text [1]. This supports Pritchett's claim that "schooling ain't learning": access improved much faster than quality [1], so inequality in real skills persists even when enrolment statistics suggest progress [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: men took control of the irrigated plots [1]; women lost income from selling vegetables [1]; women walked further to collect water; women were excluded from training. **[2]**
**(b)** Training went to registered household heads, almost always men, which reflects Boserup's finding that projects gave technology and skills to men [1], ignoring women's existing role as farmers [1]. Men took over the plots and the income, so a "modern" project lowered women's economic independence [1], supporting the view that development can reinforce patriarchy [1]. Women walked further for water, adding to unpaid domestic labour [1], which Gender and Development writers would say shows the need to change gender power relations, not just add women to projects [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Polluting production, such as textiles or electronics, moves to countries with weaker regulation [1], so emissions and toxic waste are concentrated where consumers do not live [1]. Global demand for minerals, timber and cash crops drives mining and deforestation [1], degrading land and water used by local communities [1]. **[4]**

**9.** It is ethnocentric, treating Western history as the only route to progress [1], which ignores other successful models such as state-led East Asian growth [1]. It blames poorer societies for their poverty [1], ignoring colonialism and global inequalities stressed by dependency theorists [1]. It treats tradition as a barrier [1], yet family and community networks often finance enterprise and provide welfare [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Borrowing in the 1970s and rising interest rates led to the 1980s debt crisis [1].
- Repayments take public money that could fund schools and clinics [1].
- Loan conditions under structural adjustment required spending cuts [1].
- User fees deterred the poorest from using services [1].
- Low pay pushes health workers and teachers to migrate [1].
- So the poorest groups lose most, widening gaps within countries [1].
- Counter: national spending choices and corruption also matter [1].
- Debt relief initiatives show the link can be weakened, though gaps remain [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Marxists locate obstacles in world capitalism, not local culture [1].
- Frank: satellites underdeveloped through surplus extraction by the metropolis [1].
- Wallerstein: core, semi-periphery and periphery in one world-system [1].
- Commodity dependence and unequal exchange keep the periphery poor [1].
- Harvey: accumulation by dispossession through privatisation and land grabs [1].
- Chang: rich states deny poorer ones the protection they used [1].
- Application: own-society or known example of debt or raw-material exports [1].
- Strength: explains why gaps persist despite aid and growth [1].
- Criticism: East Asian economies moved from periphery towards the core [1].
- Neo-liberals argue those countries gained by joining global markets [1].
- Modernisation theorists say internal factors such as corruption are underplayed [1].
- Judgement: strong on structural constraints, weaker on why some countries escape them [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Brundtland (1987): meeting present needs without harming future generations [1].
- SDGs combine poverty reduction with environmental targets [1].
- Climate change hits poorer countries hard, so protection is in their interest [1].
- Renewable energy can bring power to rural areas without grid expansion [1].
- Own-society application: a solar, water or forestry project [1].
- Raworth's doughnut: a social foundation without exceeding the ecological ceiling [1].
- Counter: rich countries industrialised with fossil fuels, so limits seem unfair [1].
- "Common but differentiated responsibilities" recognises this inequality [1].
- Green technology may be costly and controlled by richer countries [1].
- Marxists: sustainability under capitalism may protect profits, not people [1].
- Degrowth (Hickel): the burden of cutting consumption should fall on rich countries [1].
- Feminists: sustainability projects can ignore women's work and land rights [1].
- Conflict and debt may matter more than environmental strategy [1].
- Own-society application: tension between growth and environmental rules [1].
- Analysis: "best route" depends on how progress is defined [1].
- Judgement: necessary, but only fair and workable with support from richer states [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Connect sustainability to at least one other obstacle, such as debt, inequality or conflict; the question asks about the "best" route, which needs comparison.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing perspectives without comparing them.
- Using data without quoting the figures.
- Giving obstacles without explaining their effect.
- Ignoring within-country inequalities.
- Treating sustainable development as uncontested.
