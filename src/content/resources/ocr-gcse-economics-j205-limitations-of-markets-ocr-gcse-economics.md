| Evaluate the use and impact of these policies | Judge how well each one works in a given case |
| Evaluate the costs, including opportunity cost, and benefits of these policies | Weigh gains against costs to taxpayers, firms and consumers |

## Externalities and third parties

Markets work well when buyers and sellers bear all the costs and receive all the benefits of what they trade. They are **limited** when some effects fall on **third parties**, people outside the transaction. These effects are **externalities**.

- A **negative externality** is a cost to a third party: noise, pollution, congestion.
- A **positive externality** is a benefit to a third party: lower disease risk when others are vaccinated, a better-trained workforce when one firm trains staff.

**Social cost** = private cost + external cost. **Social benefit** = private benefit + external benefit.

The market ignores the external part, so goods with negative externalities are **overproduced** and overconsumed, and goods with positive externalities are **underproduced** and underconsumed.

## Four kinds of externality

| | Negative | Positive |
|---|---|---|
| From **production** | A cement works' dust harms nearby residents | A firm's research is copied and used by other firms |
| From **consumption** | Loud music at a late party disturbs neighbours | A vaccinated person protects people around them |

Naming whether the externality comes from producing or consuming helps you choose the policy: a tax on output targets producers; information campaigns often target consumers.

## Worked example 1: social cost per unit

A cement market has a private cost of £60 per tonne, and the market price is £60. Dust and emissions impose an **external cost of £15 per tonne** on local people. Weekly output is 100,000 tonnes.

- Social cost per tonne = 60 + 15 = **£75**.
- Total external cost per week = 100,000 × £15 = **£1.5m**, paid by people who neither make nor buy cement.

The government sets a tax of £15 per tonne. The price rises to £70 (buyers pay £10 of the tax and producers £5), and output falls to 80,000 tonnes.

- Tax revenue = 80,000 × £15 = **£1.2m** a week.
- External cost remaining = 80,000 × £15 = £1.2m, so the harm falls by **£0.3m** a week.

The tax does not remove the pollution; it **reduces** output towards the level where price reflects the full social cost, and gives the government revenue that could fund health care or cleaner technology.

## Worked example 2: a positive externality and state provision

A flu vaccine gives each person a private benefit valued at £20 (less chance of illness) and an external benefit of £12 to others (less spread). If people consider only the £20, some will not pay for it, so too few are vaccinated.

The government could subsidise the vaccine by £12 a dose, or provide it free through **state provision**. Free provision to 2 million people at a cost of £15 a dose costs **£30m**. The opportunity cost is £30m not spent on other health services, which is justified if the reduced illness and lost work are worth more than that.

## Choosing the right policy

| Situation | Best-suited policy | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Harm is roughly proportional to output (emissions per tonne) | Tax | Makes producers pay per unit of harm |
| Harm is severe or dangerous (toxic waste) | Legislation and regulation | A ban or strict limit is certain |
| Benefit is large and access matters (vaccines, schooling) | State provision | Use does not depend on ability to pay |
| Firms underprovide something with spillover benefits (training) | Subsidy | Lowers the cost of providing it |
| People misjudge risks to themselves and others (smoking) | Information provision, often with a tax | Changes behaviour at low cost |

## Worked example 3: the cost of regulation

A new rule requires 500 factories to fit dust filters costing £40,000 each, and the government spends £2m a year on inspections.

- Cost to firms = 500 × £40,000 = **£20m**.
- Cost to taxpayers = **£2m** a year.

If the filters prevent harm to health worth more than this, the rule is worthwhile. But firms may pass the cost on in higher prices, and some smaller firms may close.

## Evaluating the policies

| Policy | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Tax | Raises revenue; firms choose how to cut harm; reduces quantity | Hard to measure the external cost accurately; if demand is inelastic, output falls little; may hit low-income consumers |
| Subsidy | Increases output of beneficial goods; makes them cheaper | Costs taxpayers (opportunity cost); some goes to people who would have bought anyway |
| State provision | Universal access; can target need | Very costly; may be inefficient or overused when free |
| Legislation and regulation | Certain, clear limits; good for serious harm | Monitoring and enforcement cost; raises firms' costs; may be evaded |
| Information provision | Cheap; keeps freedom of choice; changes attitudes | Slow, uncertain; people may ignore it |

**Questions to ask in an evaluation:**

1. **How large is the externality**, and can it be measured?
2. **How effective** is the policy at changing behaviour, given price elasticity and enforcement?
3. **Who pays**, and what is the **opportunity cost**?
4. Are there **side effects**, such as job losses, higher prices or illegal trade?
5. Would a **combination** work better, such as a tax plus an information campaign?

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the use of a tax on sugary drinks to correct a negative externality.*

"Sugary drinks can lead to health problems that raise costs for the health service, a cost to taxpayers who are third parties. **[externality identified]** A tax raises the price, so consumers buy fewer drinks and the external cost falls; the revenue can fund health care. **[how the policy works]** However, if demand is price inelastic, quantity falls only a little, and the tax takes a larger share of income from low-income households. **[limits]** Firms may instead reduce sugar content to avoid the tax, which reduces harm even if sales do not fall. **[unexpected impact]** A tax is likely to work best combined with information about health risks, because the tax changes the price while the campaign changes preferences." **[judgement with a combination]**

## Common mistakes

- Describing an externality without naming the third party.
- Saying a tax "stops" pollution; it reduces output, and harm, towards the social optimum.
- Forgetting that subsidies and state provision have an opportunity cost.
- Picking a policy without saying why it suits the case.
- Confusing a private cost with an external cost.

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: name the third party, the cost or benefit, and why the market misallocates.
- **Calculate**: per-unit social cost or benefit, tax revenue, cost of a subsidy, and change in external cost.
- **Evaluate**: judge effectiveness, cost and side effects for the case given, and consider a combination.
