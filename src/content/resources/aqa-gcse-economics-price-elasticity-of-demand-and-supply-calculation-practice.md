These questions cover **3.1.3.5 Price elasticity of demand** and **3.1.3.6 Price elasticity of supply** from the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification, first taught from September 2017. The specification has no tiers. Every firm, place and figure is invented. For explanations see the [study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-price-elasticity-of-demand-and-supply-calculation/) and [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-price-elasticity-of-demand-and-supply-calculation-revision-notes/). ## Questions

**1.** (a) Write down the formula for price elasticity of demand. **[1]**
**(b)** A product rises in price from £5 to £6 and quantity demanded falls from 2,000 to 1,700. Work out the price elasticity of demand. **[3]**

**2.** A hotel (illustrative) cuts its room price from £80 to £72. Rooms booked each week rise from 150 to 195.
**(a)** Work out the price elasticity of demand. **[3]**
**(b)** Say whether demand is elastic or inelastic and give a reason. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the hotel's weekly revenue before and after the price cut. **[2]**

**3.** Explain two factors that make demand for petrol price inelastic. **[4]**

**4.** A tool shop (illustrative) sells 900 cordless drills a month. The price elasticity of demand is -1.6. The shop raises price by 5%.
**(a)** Find the percentage change in sales. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the new monthly sales. **[2]**

**5.** An orange grower (illustrative) sees the price of a crate rise from £12 to £15. Supply rises from 2,000 to 2,300 crates.
**(a)** Work out the price elasticity of supply. **[3]**
**(b)** Say whether supply is elastic or inelastic. **[1]**
**(c)** Give one reason, with an explanation, why the grower cannot raise output by more. **[2]**

**6.** A furniture workshop (illustrative) sees the price of a chair fall from £60 to £54. It cuts output from 400 to 340 chairs a month. Work out the price elasticity of supply and decide whether supply is elastic or inelastic. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why the supply of fresh flowers is likely to be inelastic in the short run but more elastic in the long run. **[4]**

**8.** The table shows price elasticity of demand for four goods in a small country (illustrative).

| Good | PED |
|---|---|
| A | -0.3 |
| B | -1.8 |
| C | -0.7 |
| D | -3.2 |

**(a)** State which good has the most elastic demand. **[1]**
**(b)** State two goods for which a price rise would increase revenue. **[2]**
**(c)** Price rises by 10%. Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded for good B and the percentage change in revenue. **[3]**

**9.** A gym (illustrative) raises its monthly fee from £30 to £36. Membership falls from 500 to 450.
**(a)** Work out the price elasticity of demand. **[3]**
**(b)** Find the change in monthly revenue. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why demand for gym membership might be inelastic. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether the gym should raise its fee by another 20%. **[5]**

**10.** A government (illustrative) adds a tax that raises the price of both good X and good Y by 10%. Good X has a PED of -0.3 and good Y has a PED of -2.5. Each good sells 1,000 units a week before the tax.
**(a)** Find the percentage change in quantity demanded for each good. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new weekly sales of each good. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain which good is better for raising tax revenue and which is better for cutting consumption. **[3]**

**11.** In a crowded town (illustrative) the supply of new houses has a price elasticity of 0.2. Demand for housing rises.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage price rise needed for the number of houses supplied to rise by 4%. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the effect of inelastic supply on people looking to buy a home. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one way builders could make supply more elastic. **[2]**

**12.** A small bakery (illustrative) sells 1,000 muffins a week at £2.50. Each muffin costs £0.90 to make. The owner estimates PED at -0.4 and wants to raise the price by 20%.
**(a)** Calculate the new price, the new weekly sales and the new weekly revenue. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the owner should raise the price. **[5]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP, the percentage change in quantity demanded over the percentage change in price [1].
**(b)** Price change = 1 ÷ 5 × 100 = +20% [1]. Quantity change = -300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = -15% [1]. PED = -15 ÷ 20 = **-0.75** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the two percentage changes separately before dividing, so that your reasoning is easy to follow and you can spot slips in the division.

**2. (a)** Price change = -8 ÷ 80 × 100 = -10% [1]. Quantity change = +45 ÷ 150 × 100 = +30% [1]. PED = 30 ÷ -10 = **-3.0** [1].
**(b)** Elastic, because the size (3.0) is above 1 [1].
**(c)** Before: 80 × 150 = **£12,000** [1]. After: 72 × 195 = **£14,040** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Elastic" needs a reason tied to the number. Comparing the size of the answer with 1 is the quickest.

**3.** Few close substitutes [1]: drivers cannot switch easily to another fuel, so they keep buying when price rises [1]. Necessity or habit [1]: many drivers need the car to get to work, so they cannot cut their purchases much [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs an explanation of how it limits the fall in quantity. A list of factor names on its own gives little to credit.

**4. (a)** -1.6 × 5% [1] = **-8%** [1].
**(b)** 900 × 0.92 [1] = **828 drills** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* When the question gives the elasticity, rearrange the formula. Convert a percentage to a multiplier carefully: a fall of 8% is 0.92, not 0.08.

**5. (a)** Price change = 3 ÷ 12 × 100 = +25% [1]. Quantity change = 300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = +15% [1]. PES = 15 ÷ 25 = **0.6** [1].
**(b)** Inelastic, because 0.6 is below 1 [1].
**(c)** Trees and crops take a long time to grow, so output cannot rise quickly in response to price [1]. The grower may also be at full capacity, with all land already planted [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason in (c) should match the product: growing time or land suits an orange grower.

**6.** Price change = -6 ÷ 60 × 100 = -10% [1]. Quantity change = -60 ÷ 400 × 100 = -15% [1]. PES = -15 ÷ -10 = **1.5** [1]. Supply is **elastic** as 1.5 is above 1 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two negatives give a positive PES. Check that your answer is positive, because supply normally moves in the same direction as price.

**7.** Flowers take weeks to grow [1], and cut flowers soon spoil so they cannot be stored [1]. In the long run, growers can plant more fields or build greenhouses [1], so output can respond more fully to price and PES rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Contrasting short and long run means you need a point about each. Writing only about the short run covers half the question.

**8. (a)** Good D [1].
**(b)** A [1] and C [1].
**(c)** Quantity: -1.8 × 10% = **-18%** [1]. Revenue index: 110% of price × 82% of quantity = 90.2% [1], so revenue **falls by about 9.8%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Pick goods by the size of PED, not its sign. A figure like -0.3 is nearer zero than -3.2, so it is the less elastic.

**9. (a)** The fee rises by £6 from £30, which is +20% [1]. Quantity change = -50 ÷ 500 × 100 = -10% [1]. PED = -10 ÷ 20 = **-0.5** [1].
**(b)** Before: 30 × 500 = £15,000. After: 36 × 450 = £16,200 [1]. Revenue rises by **£1,200** [1].
**(c)** Many members have a habit or a contract and are unwilling to change [1], so a price rise leads to a smaller percentage fall in membership [1].
**(d)** For: demand is inelastic at -0.5, so another 20% rise would be expected to cut membership by about 10% and raise revenue [1]. Gym costs are mostly fixed, so higher revenue is likely to mean higher profit [1]. Against: the PED comes from one price change and may not hold again, as members are likely to find cheaper gyms over time [1]. A bigger rise might also push the fee above what members regard as fair [1]. Judgement: a small increase may be sensible, but the gym should monitor sales and recompute PED, because the answer depends on rivals and on how long members stay [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation reaches a conclusion that depends on stated conditions, such as how quickly rivals respond, rather than repeating the calculation.

**10. (a)** X: -0.3 × 10% = **-3%** [1]. Y: -2.5 × 10% = **-25%** [1].
**(b)** X: 1,000 × 0.97 = **970** [1]. Y: 1,000 × 0.75 = **750** [1].
**(c)** Good X is better for raising revenue because few buyers are lost, so most of the tax is still collected [1]. Good Y is better for cutting consumption because quantity falls sharply [1]. A government wanting both revenue and lower use faces a trade-off [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each answer back to the number, using 970 units against 750 units. A statement about "inelastic goods" with no figure is weaker.

**11. (a)** Price rise = 4 ÷ 0.2 [1] = **20%** [1].
**(b)** Supply cannot rise quickly [1], so extra demand mostly raises price instead of the number of homes [1]. Buyers pay more, and some may be priced out of the market or face a shortage [1].
**(c)** Builders can hold a stock of land or materials [1], or train more staff, so they can build faster when demand rises [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), link inelastic supply to the price consequence; a shortage described without that link leaves the reasoning incomplete.

**12. (a)** New price = 2.50 × 1.20 = **£3.00** [1]. Quantity change = -0.4 × 20% = -8% [1], so sales are 1,000 × 0.92 = **920** [1]. Revenue = 3.00 × 920 = **£2,760**, up from £2,500 [1].
**(b)** Revenue rises by £260 because demand is inelastic [1]. Making 80 fewer muffins saves £72, so weekly profit rises from £1,600 to £1,932 [1]. However, the PED is only an estimate and a 20% rise is large [1], so customers may switch to rivals or buy a different bakery product over time, and the true response could be bigger [1]. A balanced judgement is to raise price, perhaps in steps, and check sales each week, because the benefit depends on how rivals and customers react [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Support an evaluate answer with the figures from (a), then qualify them.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking a percentage change of the new price or quantity, not the starting one.
- Putting the price change on the top line of the formula.
- Not stating whether demand or supply is elastic or inelastic when the question asks.
- Treating a negative PED as inelastic because the sign is negative.
- Writing 1,000 × 0.08 for an 8% fall, instead of 1,000 × 0.92.
- Naming a factor without saying how it changes the response to a price rise.
- Treating revenue and profit as the same thing.
- Evaluating with only one side of the argument, or with no final judgement.
