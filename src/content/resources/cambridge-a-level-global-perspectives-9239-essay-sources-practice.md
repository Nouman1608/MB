These questions practise two AO1 points in Component 2, the Essay, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028: "select and refer to a range of sources" and "assess and justify the appropriateness of the sources selected". For the essay as a whole, see the [Component 2 Essay study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-essay/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The Essay is coursework, not a written paper, so these questions test the decisions behind it rather than imitating an exam. All sources, organisations and figures described below are fictional. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [essay sources study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-essay-sources/) and the [essay sources revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-essay-sources-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State the two AO1 assessment points that concern sources in the Essay. **[2]**

**2.** Give the syllabus meanings of "assess" and "justify". **[2]**

**3.** State whether references and citations count towards the essay's word limit. **[1]**

**4.** Apart from the number of sources, name **two** kinds of range a source list should show. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** A candidate's question is "Should wealthy countries limit the recruitment of doctors from lower-income countries?" Her five sources are: two articles from one national newspaper in a recruiting country, a recruiting country's health ministry press release, a blog by a doctor who emigrated, and a recruiting country's hospital association report.

**(a)** Identify **two** weaknesses in the range of these sources. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest **two** sources she could add, and justify each. **[4]**

**6.** Rewrite this sentence so that it includes an evaluative clause: "A report found that 40% of rural clinics had no permanent doctor (Health Watch, 2023)." **[3]**

**7.** Source X, a ministry press release, says vacancies in rural clinics fell last year. Source Y, a study by an independent university research unit that sets out its method, says they rose. Write two or three sentences that compare their credibility and decide which to rely on. **[4]**

**8.** A candidate writes: "This report was written by an oil company, so it is biased and should be ignored."

**(a)** Explain **two** weaknesses in this evaluation. **[2]**
**(b)** Rewrite it as an effective evaluation. **[3]**

**9.** Complete a source-audit entry for this fictional source: a 2024 report by an international development charity, based on interviews with 60 nurses in four countries, arguing that recruitment drains rural health services. **[5]**

**10.** Explain why a source with clear limitations can still be justified in the essay. Use an example. **[3]**

**11.** A candidate puts all source evaluation in a final section headed "Evaluation of my sources". Explain why this is less effective than evaluating sources where they are used. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate how well this paragraph assesses and justifies its sources. **[6]**

> "Many experts believe recruitment is ethical because doctors have the right to move (Global Health Forum). A survey of 2,000 migrant doctors by a medical association found that most moved for training opportunities, which suggests the decision is often about career development rather than pay. However, the association represents doctors, so it may present migration positively. A ministry in one source country says it loses a fifth of its graduates each year."

## Answers

**1.** "Select and refer to a range of sources" [1]; "assess and justify the appropriateness of the sources selected" [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Assess: "make an informed judgement" [1]. Justify: "support a case with evidence/argument" [1]. **[2]**

**3.** No; the word count excludes the title, references and citations [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two: geographical range (regions) [1]; range of type (academic, official, organisational, journalism) [1]; range of standpoint (each perspective represented in its own terms) [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Any two: all sources come from recruiting countries, so the source-country perspective is missing [1]; there is no academic or independent research, only news, official and interested-party material [1]; the single dissenting voice is one individual's blog, so the opposing perspective rests on an anecdote [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Any two, 1 mark for the source and 1 for the justification:
- A report from a health ministry or professional body in a source country [1], to build the perspective of the countries doctors leave in its own terms [1].
- A peer-reviewed study of doctor migration across several countries [1], to give independent evidence that can check the claims of interested parties [1].
- A report by an international organisation on health workforce shortages [1], because it compares many countries and is not tied to one side [1]. **[4]**

**6.** For example: "A national survey of 1,200 rural clinics by Health Watch, an independent monitoring body, found that 40% had no permanent doctor (Health Watch, 2023)." The clause identifies the type and scale of the evidence [1], states why the producer is credible [1], and keeps the full citation [1]. **[3]**

**7.** For example: "The ministry's figures are more recent and official, but the ministry has an interest in showing its rural policy is working [1] [1]. The university unit is independent and sets out its method [1], so where the two conflict on rural vacancies, the essay relies on the study and treats the ministry's figure as the government's position rather than as settled fact [1]." **[4]**

**8. (a)** It assumes the company's interest makes everything in the report false, without examining the evidence [1]; it does not say which claim is affected or how [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For example: "The report was produced by an oil company, which benefits from continued fossil-fuel use, so its claim that a carbon tax would cost jobs needs independent confirmation [1]. Its employment data, however, come from the national statistics office and can be checked [1], so the essay uses the data but not the company's forecast of job losses [1]." **[3]**

**9.** One mark per accurate entry, up to five:
- Perspective: recruitment harms health services in source countries [1].
- Provenance: an international development charity, 2024, writing for policymakers and supporters [1].
- Basis: interviews with 60 nurses in four countries [1].
- Strength: first-hand qualitative evidence from more than one country, from those directly affected [1].
- Limitation: small sample; a charity campaigning on the issue may select interviewees who support its view; interviews show experience, not national scale [1].
- Use: to state the source-country perspective clearly and illustrate its human impact, alongside workforce data for scale [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Justification explains why a source is used, not that it is flawless [1]. A one-sided source can be the clearest statement of a perspective the essay must evaluate [1]; for example, a campaign group's report may be used to present the case for a ban in its strongest form, with its limits noted where its figures are used [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The syllabus wants evaluation of sources and perspectives to be a substantial element of the essay, so it should shape the argument [1]. Evaluation placed at the end cannot change how much weight a source received when it was used [1], and it costs extra words repeating what each source said [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- The first citation is incomplete (no date) and "many experts" is not assessed at all [1].
- The survey is described with its scale and producer, which helps the reader judge it [1].
- The paragraph assesses the survey by noting the association's interest [1], but does not say how this affects the specific finding about training [1].
- The ministry figure has no citation and no assessment, although an official source in a losing country has its own interest in stressing the loss [1].
- Judgement: partial assessment of one source, little justification of why any source was chosen, and missing citations; it needs an evaluative clause for each key source and a statement of what each contributes [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming sources rather than kinds of source when asked about range.
- Writing evaluative clauses that praise a source without saying why it is credible.
- Comparing two sources and then not deciding which to rely on.
- Rejecting a whole source for bias instead of separating checkable evidence from interested claims.
- Source-audit entries that summarise the source but leave out its use in the essay.
