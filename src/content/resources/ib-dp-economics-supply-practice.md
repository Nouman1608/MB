## Questions

**1.** Define the law of supply. **[2]**

**2.** In the fictional country of Kestria, firms X and Y make cycle helmets. Their weekly supply is shown below (units per week).

| Price (tarns) | Firm X | Firm Y |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 40 | 10 |
| 30 | 60 | 30 |
| 40 | 80 | 60 |

**(a)** Calculate market supply at each price. **[3]**
**(b)** A third firm, Z, enters and supplies 15, 25 and 35 at the three prices. Calculate the new market supply at a price of 30. **[1]**

**3.** In the fictional country of Orvale, cocoa is a storable crop. For each event, say whether it is a movement along the supply curve of cocoa or a shift of it, and give the direction. **[5]**
**(a)** The price of cocoa rises.
**(b)** The price of fertiliser falls.
**(c)** The government introduces a per-unit tax on cocoa.
**(d)** More growers enter the market.
**(e)** Growers expect the price of cocoa to be higher next season.

**4.** The fictional government of Lumeria gives tea producers a subsidy of 5 tarns per kilogram. Draw a diagram, described in words if you cannot sketch it, and explain the effect on the supply of tea. **[4]**

**5.** The weekly supply of notebooks in a fictional market is 100 at a price of 3, 200 at 4, 300 at 5, 400 at 6 and 500 at 7. A per-unit tax of 2 tarns is introduced.
**(a)** Calculate the quantity supplied after the tax at prices of 5, 6 and 7. **[3]**
**(b)** Say which way the curve moves. **[1]**

**6.** Beef and leather are produced together. Explain, with reference to joint supply, what happens to the supply of leather when the price of beef rises. **[3]**

**7.** A fictional farmer can use the same field for maize or soya. The price of soya rises. Explain the effect on the supply of maize. **[3]**

**8.** Dried bean growers in a fictional country can store their crop for several months. Explain why current supply may fall if growers expect a higher price next month. **[3]**

**9.** *(HL only)* A pottery has one kiln and pays each worker 60 tarns per day. The extra mugs each worker adds are 15, 12, 10, 6 and 4 for workers 1 to 5.
**(a)** Calculate the marginal cost of a mug for each worker. **[3]**
**(b)** State the number of mugs supplied at a price of 6 tarns if the pottery produces while price at least covers marginal cost. **[2]**

**10.** The table shows the weekly supply of shrimp in the fictional market of Vessia (tonnes).

| Price (tarns per kg) | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity supplied | 200 | 300 | 400 | 500 |

**(a)** New shrimp farms enter and add 60 tonnes at each price. Write the new supply schedule. **[2]**
**(b)** The government then pays a subsidy of 2 tarns per kg. Using the schedule from (a), calculate the quantity supplied at prices of 6, 8 and 10. **[3]**
**(c)** For each change, explain whether supply moves along its curve or the curve itself shifts. **[3]**

**11.** Distinguish between individual supply and market supply, and between a movement along and a shift of the supply curve. **[4]**

**12.** *(HL only)* Explain how diminishing marginal returns and increasing marginal costs help to account for the upward slope of the supply curve. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Other things being equal (*ceteris paribus*), [1] a higher price leads to a greater quantity supplied (and a lower price to a smaller quantity). [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the price and quantity link and state the ceteris paribus condition as a separate point, so the definition is complete.

**2. (a)** At 20: 40 + 10 = 50. [1] At 30: 60 + 30 = 90. [1] At 40: 80 + 60 = 140. [1] **[3]**
**(b)** At 30: 60 + 30 + 25 = **115**. [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* For a calculate question, set out each addition; a bare answer risks losing the working mark if one total is slipped.

**3. (a)** Movement along the curve (expansion), because the good's own price changed. [1]
**(b)** Shift right (increase), since lower input costs reduce cost per unit. [1]
**(c)** Shift left (decrease; equivalently up), since a tax raises cost per unit. [1]
**(d)** Shift right (increase), since more firms supply at each price. [1]
**(e)** Shift left (decrease) for a storable good, since growers hold back stock now. [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming "shift" without a direction does not complete the answer; state both the type of change and the direction.

**4.** The diagram has price on the vertical axis and quantity of tea per period on the horizontal axis, with an upward-sloping curve labelled S1. [1] A second curve S2 is drawn parallel to S1 and below it, with the vertical gap equal to 5 tarns. [1] The subsidy lowers producers' cost per kilogram, so they will supply each quantity at a price 5 lower. [1] Supply increases (a shift right), and the quantity supplied at every price is higher. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A diagram earns credit only when the axes and curves are labelled and the shift is stated in words as an increase in supply.

**5. (a)** After the tax, quantity at price P equals the old quantity at P - 2, because 2 tarns goes to the government. [1] At 5: old quantity at 3 = 100. At 6: old quantity at 4 = 200. [1] At 7: old quantity at 5 = **300**. [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Upward (equivalently left): supply decreases. [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the rule you used for the tax; a correct list with no stated method is harder to credit if one value is wrong.

**6.** A higher price of beef gives producers an incentive to raise more cattle, which is a movement along the supply of beef. [1] Leather is a by-product of the same animals, so more leather is produced at any given price of leather. [1] The supply of leather increases (shifts right). [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the link between the two goods; saying only that "supply goes up" without the joint production reason is a common gap.

**7.** Maize and soya compete for the same land, so this is competitive supply. [1] A higher soya price makes soya more profitable, so the farmer switches land away from maize. [1] The supply of maize decreases (shifts left). [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Competitive supply needs the idea of switching resources; a bare statement that the two goods are "related" earns little.

**8.** Producers who can store the good may prefer to sell at the higher expected price next month. [1] They hold back some stock now, so less is offered at each current price. [1] Current supply decreases (shifts left). [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The explanation depends on the good being storable, so mention storage; without it the reasoning is unsupported.

**9. (a)** Marginal cost = wage ÷ extra mugs. [1] Worker 1: 60 ÷ 15 = 4; worker 2: 60 ÷ 12 = 5; worker 3: 60 ÷ 10 = 6. [1] Worker 4: 60 ÷ 6 = 10; worker 5: 60 ÷ 4 = **15** tarns. [1] **[3]**
**(b)** The first three workers have marginal cost of 4, 5 and 6, all at most 6. [1] Output = 15 + 12 + 10 = **37 mugs**. [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* For HL calculations, show the formula first, then say that marginal cost rises as marginal product falls; that link strengthens the answer.

**10. (a)** Add 60 to each quantity: **260, 360, 460, 560**. [2] **[2]**
**(b)** The subsidy of 2 means the quantity at price P equals the quantity at P + 2 on the schedule from (a). [1] At 6: 360. At 8: 460. [1] At 10: **560**. [1] **[3]**
**(c)** Entry of firms changes the number of firms, not the price, so it is a shift (right). [1] The subsidy lowers cost per unit, a non-price determinant, so it is a second shift (right). [1] Neither is a movement along the curve, because the good's own price did not cause the change. [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A multi-part sequence is marked part by part, so use your own schedule from (a) in (b); a correct method on a wrong earlier answer can still earn credit.

**11.** Individual supply is one producer's quantities at each price. [1] Market supply is the sum of all producers' quantities at each price. [1] A movement along occurs when only the good's own price changes. [1] A shift is caused by a non-price determinant such as costs, technology or number of firms. [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A distinguish response has to state how the two items differ, not just define each in turn.

**12.** With a fixed input such as a kiln, adding more of a variable input eventually gives a smaller extra output (diminishing marginal returns). [1] If each worker is paid the same wage, the cost of each extra unit of output therefore rises. [1] This is increasing marginal cost. [1] Producers will expand output only if the price covers the higher marginal cost of the last unit. [1] So a higher price is needed for a larger quantity supplied, giving an upward-sloping supply curve. [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Chain the three links in order (returns, then cost, then slope); listing the terms without connecting them is the usual shortfall on HL explain questions.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out *ceteris paribus* in a definition of the law of supply.
- Calling a price change a shift, or a determinant change a movement.
- Stating a shift without saying whether supply increased or decreased.
- Moving a tax or subsidy shift the wrong way.
- Not showing the addition in market supply calculations.
- Confusing joint supply with competitive supply.
- Forgetting that expectations only reduce current supply for goods that can be stored.
- On HL questions, naming diminishing returns without linking to marginal cost and the slope of the curve.
