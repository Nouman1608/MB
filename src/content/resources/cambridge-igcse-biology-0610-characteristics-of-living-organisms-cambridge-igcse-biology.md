This study guide teaches sub-topic 1.1, Characteristics of living organisms, of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. A living organism shows **all seven** characteristics at some point in its life. Many non-living things show one or two of them, which is why examiners like to ask whether something is alive.

## The seven definitions, word by word

The mnemonic MRS GREN gives the names. The marks, though, are for the **definitions**, so learn the exact wording and know why each key word is there.

### Movement

**Definition:** an action by an organism or part of an organism causing a change of position or place.

- "**or part of an organism**" is what lets plants qualify. A plant does not walk, but its leaves turn towards light, its flowers open and close, and its roots grow down into the soil.
- "**change of position or place**": the whole organism moving from place to place (an animal running, a single-celled organism swimming) and a part changing position (an arm bending, a leaf turning) both count.

### Respiration

**Definition:** the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism.

- "**chemical reactions**" means respiration is a chemical process, not a physical one. It is **not breathing**. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs; respiration happens inside every living cell, in plants as well as animals.
- "**in cells**" tells you where it happens.
- "**release energy**": energy is released, never "made" or "produced". Writing "respiration produces energy" is a common way to lose a mark.
- "**for metabolism**": metabolism is all the chemical reactions in an organism. The energy is used for processes such as muscle contraction, building proteins and keeping warm.

### Sensitivity

**Definition:** the ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment.

- Two steps: **detect** and **respond**. A smoke alarm detects smoke and responds with a sound, so sensitivity alone does not prove that something is alive.
- "**internal or external**": external changes include light, sound and touch, for example a plant shoot growing towards light, or your hand moving away from a hot object. Internal changes include a rise in body temperature or blood glucose concentration.

### Growth

**Definition:** a permanent increase in size and dry mass.

- "**permanent**" rules out temporary swelling. A sponge that soaks up water gets bigger, but it shrinks again when dried.
- "**dry mass**" is the mass with all water removed. Water content changes from hour to hour (a plant wilts and recovers, you drink a glass of water), so total mass is a poor measure. An increase in dry mass shows new cells and new material have been made.
- Example: a seed soaking in water swells and gains mass, but this is mostly water. In the first few days of germination its dry mass can even fall, because stored food is broken down in respiration before the seedling can photosynthesise.

### Reproduction

**Definition:** the processes that make more of the same kind of organism.

- "**the same kind**" means the same species. Reproduction can be **asexual**, from one parent, as when a bacterium divides into two, or **sexual**, from two parents.
- An individual organism does not have to reproduce to be alive (a sterile mule is alive), but the kind of organism must be able to.

### Excretion

**Definition:** the removal of the waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements.

- "**waste products of metabolism**" means substances made by the organism's own chemical reactions, such as carbon dioxide from respiration and, in mammals, urea in urine.
- "**substances in excess of requirements**" covers substances that are not waste but of which there is too much, such as extra water and salts removed in urine and sweat.
- Excretion is **not egestion**. Faeces are mainly undigested food that never entered the body's cells, so passing faeces is egestion, not excretion.
- Plants excrete too: oxygen produced in photosynthesis and carbon dioxide produced in respiration diffuse out through the leaves.

### Nutrition

**Definition:** the taking in of materials for energy, growth and development.

- "**materials**" is broad. Animals take in food: carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins, minerals and water. Plants take in carbon dioxide, water and mineral ions, and use light energy to make their own food by photosynthesis.
- "**for energy, growth and development**" links nutrition to respiration (energy) and growth (new cells need raw materials).

## Applying the characteristics

### Plants and animals compared

| Characteristic | A typical animal | A typical flowering plant |
|---|---|---|
| Movement | Whole body moves from place to place | Parts move: leaves turn, flowers open, roots grow down |
| Respiration | In all cells | In all cells, day and night |
| Sensitivity | Senses such as eyes and ears; nervous responses | Shoots respond to light, roots to gravity |
| Growth | Grows to adult size, then mainly replaces cells | Can keep growing throughout life |
| Reproduction | Usually sexual | Sexual by flowers and seeds; many also reproduce asexually |
| Excretion | Carbon dioxide from the lungs; urea, water and salts in urine | Oxygen and carbon dioxide through the leaves |
| Nutrition | Takes in food made by other organisms | Takes in carbon dioxide, water and ions to make its own food |

### Worked example 1: is it alive?

*A student says a car is alive because it moves, takes in fuel and gives out waste gases. Explain why a car is not a living organism.*

> A car does move and takes in fuel and gives out waste gases, so it appears to show movement, nutrition and excretion (**shows the student's reasoning is understood**). However, the car does not grow, because it never permanently increases in size and dry mass, and it cannot reproduce to make more cars (**names two missing characteristics, using the definitions**). It has no cells, so it cannot respire, because respiration is a set of chemical reactions in cells (**uses the key words of the respiration definition**). A living organism must show all seven characteristics, so the car is not alive (**conclusion**).

Fire is another favourite: it "grows", "moves", "uses oxygen" and "gives out carbon dioxide", but it is not made of cells, cannot detect and respond to changes, and its increase in size is not new material being made by the fire.

### Worked example 2: classifying a description

*A water flea swims towards a light. Which characteristic is shown?*

Both **movement** (the whole organism changes place) and **sensitivity** (it detects light and responds) are shown. If a question asks for one, choose the one the question stresses. Here "towards a light" points to sensitivity, because the movement is a response to a change in the external environment.

## Common errors

- Writing "respiration is breathing", or "respiration makes energy".
- Defining growth as "getting bigger": you need **permanent** and **dry mass**.
- Calling the passing of faeces excretion.
- Saying plants do not move or do not respire. Parts of plants move, and every living plant cell respires.
- Giving MRS GREN names when the question says **describe**: a description needs the definition.
- Defining sensitivity as only "responding": detecting the change is half the definition.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **State:** a short answer, such as the name of a characteristic.
- **Describe:** give the characteristic **and** its meaning, as in the syllabus definitions.
- **Explain:** give reasons, for example why dry mass is used rather than total mass.
- **Suggest:** apply the definitions to something unfamiliar, such as a robot or a virus-like particle, and justify your choice.
- **Identify:** pick out the characteristic shown in a description or image.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is growth measured by dry mass?** Water content changes all the time, so only an increase in dry mass shows that new material has been made.

**Is respiration the same as breathing?** No. Respiration is chemical reactions in cells that release energy; breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs.

**Do plants show all seven characteristics?** Yes. Parts of plants move, all their cells respire, and they excrete oxygen and carbon dioxide.
