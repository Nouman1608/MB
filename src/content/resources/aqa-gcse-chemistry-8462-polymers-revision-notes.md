These notes condense **4.2.2.5 Polymers** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification (for teaching from
September 2016, version 1.2). It is Paper 1 content for both tiers, with no Higher-only statements and no
required practical. For full explanations, see the [polymers study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-polymers/);
then practise with the [polymers practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-polymers-practice/).

## Core facts

- Polymers have **very large molecules**.
- The atoms in the polymer molecules are linked to other atoms by **strong covalent bonds**.
- The **intermolecular forces** between polymer molecules are **relatively strong**.
- So polymers are **solids at room temperature**.
- You must be able to **recognise polymers from diagrams** showing their bonding and structure.

## Drawing and spotting a repeat unit

```
   H   H
   |   |
 [ C - C ]n
   |   |
   H   H
```

- Square brackets round the repeat unit.
- A bond line passes through each bracket.
- *n* after the second bracket: a very large number of repeat units.
- Carbon atoms in the chain are joined by single bonds.

| Polymer | Side group on one carbon | Repeat unit formula |
|---|---|---|
| Poly(ethene) | H | C₂H₄ |
| Poly(propene) | CH₃ | C₃H₆ |
| Poly(chloroethene) | Cl | C₂H₃Cl |

## Why polymers are solids

| Step | Idea |
|---|---|
| 1 | The molecules are very long |
| 2 | A long chain touches many neighbouring chains |
| 3 | The intermolecular forces add up, so they are relatively strong |
| 4 | More energy is needed to separate the chains than to separate small molecules |
| 5 | So the polymer is a solid at room temperature |

Covalent bonds along the chain are not broken when a polymer softens or melts.

## Three kinds of covalent substance

| Type | Example | State at room temperature | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small molecules | Methane | Gas | Weak intermolecular forces |
| Polymer | Poly(ethene) | Solid | Very large molecules, relatively strong intermolecular forces |
| Giant covalent | Diamond | Solid, very high melting point | Network of strong covalent bonds |

A polymer is **separate long molecules**. A giant covalent structure is **one continuous network**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Polymer | A substance made of very large molecules built from repeating units |
| Repeat unit | The short section, drawn in brackets, that repeats along the chain |
| *n* | A large number of repeat units |
| Intermolecular force | A force between separate molecules |
| Covalent bond | A strong bond from a shared pair of electrons |
| Backbone | The chain of atoms that runs along the polymer molecule |

## Worked examples in brief

**Example 1.** A chain has 400 repeat units of C₂H₄. Carbon atoms: 400 × 2 = 800. Hydrogen atoms: 400 × 4 = 1600.

**Example 2.** "Explain why poly(ethene) is a solid but ethene is a gas." Poly(ethene) has very large molecules, so
the forces between the chains are relatively strong and a lot of energy is needed to separate them. Ethene is a
small molecule with weak forces between molecules, so little energy is needed.

**Example 3.** "Which diagram shows a polymer?" Choose the one with brackets, bonds running through them and *n*.

## How to tell the structure types apart in a diagram

| Feature in the diagram | Structure |
|---|---|
| A few atoms in a separate group | Small molecule |
| Brackets, bonds through the brackets and *n* | Polymer |
| A network continuing in all directions with every atom bonded | Giant covalent |
| Alternating positive and negative charges in a regular pattern | Ionic |
| Positive ions in a "sea" of electrons | Metallic |

## Context from other topics

Polymers are made by joining many small molecules, called monomers, into long chains. In the organic chemistry
topic, alkenes such as ethene and propene are the monomers for addition polymers. For this subsection you only
need the structure and properties of the finished polymer.

## Command words

| Command word | What to write |
|---|---|
| Recognise / identify | Choose the diagram and name the feature (brackets, bond lines, *n*) |
| Describe | State the structure: very large molecules, covalent bonds inside, relatively strong forces between |
| Explain | Link the size of the molecules to the strength of forces and the energy needed |
| Compare | Name both substances and use comparative words |
| Calculate | Multiply the atoms in the repeat unit by the number of repeat units |

## Exam traps

- **"A polymer is a giant covalent structure."** It is made of separate very large molecules.
- **"The forces between polymer molecules are weak."** The specification says relatively strong.
- **"The covalent bonds break when a polymer melts."** They do not.
- **Missing *n*.** A repeat unit without *n* and the bond lines through the brackets is incomplete.
- **Double bonds in the repeat unit.** The carbon atoms in the polymer chain are joined by single bonds.
- **Confusing "strong bonds" with "strong forces between molecules".** They are different things.

## Quick self-test

1. What does *n* mean in a repeat unit?
2. What type of bond joins the atoms inside a polymer molecule?
3. Are the intermolecular forces between polymer molecules weak or relatively strong?
4. Why are polymers solids at room temperature?
5. How many carbon atoms are in 300 repeat units of C₂H₄?
6. How many hydrogen atoms are in 300 repeat units of C₂H₄?
7. Name one feature that tells you a diagram shows a polymer.
8. Give one difference between a polymer and a giant covalent structure.
9. Are covalent bonds broken when a polymer softens?
10. Give the repeat unit formula of poly(chloroethene).

### Answers

1. A very large number of repeat units.
2. Strong covalent bonds.
3. Relatively strong.
4. The molecules are very large, so the intermolecular forces add up and a lot of energy is needed to separate them.
5. 600.
6. 1200.
7. Square brackets with bonds through them and *n*; or a long repeating chain.
8. A polymer is separate long molecules; a giant covalent structure is one continuous network of bonds.
9. No. The intermolecular forces are overcome.
10. C₂H₃Cl.
