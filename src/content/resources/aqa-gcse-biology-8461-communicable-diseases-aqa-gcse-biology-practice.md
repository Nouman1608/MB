These questions cover section **4.3.1 Communicable diseases** (4.3.1.1 to 4.3.1.9) of the AQA GCSE Biology
(8461) specification, for first teaching from 2016. The content is on Paper 1, and every question suits both
tiers. All data in the questions are invented for practice. A calculator is useful for questions 6 and 7.

Learn the content first in the [Communicable diseases study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-communicable-diseases-aqa-gcse-biology/)
and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-communicable-diseases-aqa-gcse-biology-revision-notes/).
For questions across the whole of Topic 3, use the [Infection and response practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-infection-and-response-practice/);
the questions below are different from those.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.**

**(a)** Give the **three** ways the specification says pathogens can be spread. **[3]**
**(b)** Name the vector that spreads malaria. **[1]**

**2.**

**(a)** Describe the difference between an antibody and an antitoxin. **[2]**
**(b)** A person has antibodies against the measles virus. Explain why these antibodies do not protect the
person against HIV. **[2]**

**3.** Give the original source of each drug.

**(a)** Digitalis **[1]**
**(b)** Aspirin **[1]**
**(c)** Penicillin **[1]**

**4.** Gonorrhoea is a sexually transmitted disease.

**(a)** Give **two** symptoms of gonorrhoea. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why penicillin is no longer a reliable treatment for every case of gonorrhoea. **[2]**

**5.** A student writes: "Stomach acid is part of the immune system." Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**6.** After a party, some guests became ill with fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea. An
investigator recorded what the guests ate.

| | Ate the chicken | Did not eat the chicken |
|---|---|---|
| Number of guests | 120 | 80 |
| Number who became ill | 54 | 4 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of guests who became ill in each group. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest the most likely source of the infection. Give a reason from the data. **[1]**
**(c)** The illness was Salmonella food poisoning. Explain how the bacteria cause the symptoms. **[2]**
**(d)** Give **one** measure the kitchen could take to stop this happening again. **[1]**

**7.** A health team gave mosquito nets to every family in a village. Cases of malaria per 1000 people were
recorded for a year before and a year after.

| | Year before nets | Year after nets |
|---|---|---|
| Cases per 1000 people | 240 | 90 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage decrease in malaria cases. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the nets reduced the number of cases. **[2]**
**(c)** The team also drained pools of standing water near the village. Explain why this would reduce malaria
further. **[2]**

**8.** Babies in a country cannot be given the measles vaccine until they are 12 months old.

**(a)** Explain why a baby is less likely to catch measles in a town where 95% of children are vaccinated than in
a town where 70% are vaccinated. **[3]**
**(b)** Name the type of cell in the blood that is stimulated by a vaccine. **[1]**

**9.** A pharmaceutical company develops a new drug.

**(a)** Put these stages in the correct order: clinical trials with patients; testing on cells and tissues;
clinical trials with healthy volunteers; testing on live animals. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the **three** properties a new drug is tested for. **[3]**
**(c)** Describe what is meant by a double blind trial. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the results are peer reviewed before they are published. **[1]**

**10.** A grower has tomato plants infected with tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) and rose bushes infected with black
spot.

**(a)** Explain why both diseases reduce the growth of the plants. **[2]**
**(b)** The grower collects and burns the fallen rose leaves. Explain why this reduces the spread of black
spot. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why spraying fungicide would not help the tomato plants. **[1]**

**11.** Explain how the body's non-specific defences and its immune system protect a person who swallows
Salmonella bacteria in food. **[6]**

**12.** A student says: "Painkillers and antibiotics both make you feel better, so they do the same job."
Evaluate this statement. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Direct contact [1]; water [1]; air [1]
**(b)** Mosquito [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Insects" is too vague for (b), and "the protist" names the pathogen, not the vector.

**2. (a)** Antibodies bind to (specific) antigens on pathogens [1]; antitoxins neutralise toxins made by bacteria [1]
**(b)** Antibodies are specific to one antigen [1]; HIV has different antigens from measles, so the measles antibodies cannot bind to it [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Antibodies kill bacteria" earns no credit in (a); antibodies bind to antigens, and other white blood cell actions then destroy the pathogen.

**3. (a)** Foxgloves [1]
**(b)** Willow [1]
**(c)** Penicillium mould [1]
*Examiner insight:* Allow "a mould" or "a fungus" for (c); "bacteria" is wrong.

**4. (a)** Any two: thick yellow or green discharge (from the vagina or penis) [1]; pain on urinating [1]
**(b)** Many strains of the bacterium have become resistant to penicillin [1]; so penicillin does not kill these bacteria and the infection is not cured [1]
*Examiner insight:* "The bacteria have become immune" is wrong; immunity belongs to the host, resistance to the bacteria.

**5.** Stomach acid is a non-specific defence [1]; it kills pathogens in swallowed food before they enter the body, whereas the immune system (white blood cells) acts on pathogens that have already entered [1]
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs a contrast with what the immune system does, not just "it kills bacteria".

**6. (a)** Ate chicken: 54 ÷ 120 × 100 = **45%** [1]; did not eat chicken: 4 ÷ 80 × 100 = **5%** [1]
**(b)** The chicken, because a much higher percentage of those who ate it became ill (45% compared with 5%) [1]
**(c)** The bacteria reproduce rapidly in the body [1]; they secrete toxins that damage tissues / cause the symptoms [1]
**(d)** Any one: cook poultry thoroughly; keep raw and cooked food apart; wash hands and surfaces (hygienic preparation); use poultry from vaccinated flocks [1]
*Examiner insight:* For (b), comparing raw numbers (54 and 4) is weaker because the groups are different sizes; percentages make the comparison fair.

**7. (a)** 240 − 90 = 150 [1]; 150 ÷ 240 × 100 = **62.5%** [1]
**(b)** Nets stop mosquitoes biting people (especially at night) [1]; so the malarial protist is not passed from infected people to uninfected people [1]
**(c)** Mosquitoes breed in standing water [1]; so fewer mosquitoes (vectors) are produced to carry the protist [1]
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by 90 instead of 240 in (a) gives 167%, which scores only the first mark.

**8. (a)** Most children are immune, so there are few people the measles virus can infect [1]; the virus is passed on to fewer people, so there are very few cases [1]; so the baby is less likely to inhale droplets from an infected person [1]
**(b)** White blood cell (lymphocyte) [1]
*Examiner insight:* An answer that only says "the baby is protected by herd immunity" names the idea without explaining it and scores one mark at most.

**9. (a)** Cells and tissues, then live animals [1]; then healthy volunteers, then patients [1]
**(b)** Toxicity [1]; efficacy [1]; dose [1]
**(c)** Neither the doctors nor the patients know [1]; who has received the drug and who has received the placebo until the trial is complete [1]
**(d)** Other scientists check the methods and results are valid before the results are shared [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a), the first mark is for preclinical before clinical stages; the second needs volunteers before patients.

**10. (a)** The leaves are discoloured or damaged (and black spot leaves drop early) [1]; so less photosynthesis takes place, so less glucose is made for growth [1]
**(b)** The fallen leaves carry the fungus (spores) [1]; burning them destroys the fungus so it cannot be spread by water or wind to other leaves [1]
**(c)** TMV is a virus, and fungicides only kill fungi [1]
*Examiner insight:* "The plant cannot make food" is too vague for (a); link the damage to reduced photosynthesis.

**11.** Level-style answer; award one mark for each point up to 6:
- the stomach produces (hydrochloric) acid [1]
- which kills most of the swallowed bacteria [1]
- any bacteria that survive and enter the body are attacked by white blood cells [1]
- white blood cells engulf and digest bacteria by phagocytosis [1]
- white blood cells produce antibodies specific to the Salmonella antigens [1]
- white blood cells produce antitoxins that neutralise the toxins the bacteria secrete [1]
*Examiner insight:* Answers that describe the skin, nose and trachea gain no credit here, because the bacteria are swallowed; select the defences that fit the route.

**12.** One mark for each point:
- painkillers treat the symptoms but do not kill the pathogen [1]
- antibiotics kill bacteria inside the body, so they can cure a bacterial disease [1]
- antibiotics cannot kill viruses, so neither medicine kills a virus [1]
- conclusion: the statement is wrong, because both may make the person feel better but only antibiotics remove the cause, and only when it is a bacterium [1]
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a judgement; a list of facts about each medicine without a conclusion cannot reach full marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the vector when the question asks for the pathogen, or the reverse.
- Comparing raw numbers from groups of different sizes instead of percentages.
- Saying bacteria become "immune" to antibiotics.
- Choosing defences that do not match the route of entry.
- Saying fungicides or antibiotics work against viruses.
- Ordering drug-trial stages with patients before healthy volunteers.
