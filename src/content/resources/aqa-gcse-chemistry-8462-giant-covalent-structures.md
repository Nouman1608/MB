This guide covers **4.2.2.6 Giant covalent structures** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for
teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It is part of topic 4.2, Bonding, structure, and the
properties of matter, examined on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. No statement in it is marked (HT only),
and there is no required practical. The detail of diamond, graphite and graphene follows in section 4.2.3;
here the focus is on what makes a structure "giant covalent" and on silicon dioxide.

## What "giant covalent" means

In a **giant covalent structure** there are no separate molecules. Every atom is joined to its neighbours by
**strong covalent bonds**, and the pattern continues in all directions. The whole piece of material is one
enormous structure. Atoms are joined by shared pairs of electrons, with a regular arrangement that repeats.

Because the bonds link atoms in all directions, the formula shows only the **ratio** of the atoms. Silicon
dioxide is written SiO₂ because there are two oxygen atoms for every silicon atom, not because it contains
molecules of SiO₂.

| Giant covalent structure | Elements present | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diamond | Carbon only | C | Each carbon bonded to four others |
| Graphite | Carbon only | C | Layers; covered in section 4.2.3 |
| Silicon dioxide (silica) | Silicon and oxygen | SiO₂ | Each Si bonded to four O |

## Properties and their explanation

### Very high melting points and boiling points

To melt a giant covalent structure, atoms must be separated from their neighbours. That means **breaking the
covalent bonds** themselves. These bonds are strong and there are a huge number of them throughout the
structure, so a **very large amount of energy** is needed. Giant covalent structures are therefore **solids with
very high melting points**.

Contrast this with small molecules. When a small-molecule substance melts, only the weak intermolecular forces
are overcome. In a giant covalent structure the **covalent bonds are overcome**. That is the main reason for the
difference in melting point.

| Substance | Structure | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | Small molecules | -182 |
| Sodium chloride | Giant ionic | 801 |
| Silicon dioxide | Giant covalent | 1710 |
| Diamond | Giant covalent | above 3500 |

You do not need to learn the numbers. You do need to say that giant covalent structures have very high melting
points, usually higher than ionic compounds.

### Hardness and electrical conduction

Most giant covalent structures are hard, because breaking or moving atoms means breaking strong bonds. Diamond
and silicon dioxide do not conduct electricity, since all the outer electrons are used in bonds and no ions or
free electrons are present. Graphite is an exception to the general pattern, and its special structure is
covered in the graphite guide.

## Silicon dioxide

Silicon dioxide, also called silica, is the main compound in sand and quartz. In silicon dioxide:

- each silicon atom is bonded to **four** oxygen atoms;
- each oxygen atom is bonded to **two** silicon atoms.

A small section can be drawn in two dimensions:

```
  –O–Si–O–Si–O–
     |    |
     O    O
     |    |
  –O–Si–O–Si–O–
```

The diagram cannot show all four bonds around each silicon atom, because the real structure is in three
dimensions. It is a partial view of a structure that continues in all directions.

### Worked example 1: why the formula is SiO₂

*Explain why the formula of silicon dioxide is SiO₂.*

```
Each Si is bonded to 4 O atoms.
Each O is shared between 2 Si atoms.
Number of O atoms per Si atom = 4 / 2 = 2
Ratio Si : O = 1 : 2, so the formula is SiO2.
```

### Worked example 2: why silicon dioxide has a very high melting point

*Silicon dioxide melts at 1710 °C. Explain why.*

```
1. It has a giant covalent structure.
2. All of its atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds in all directions.
3. To melt it, these covalent bonds must be broken.
4. There are many strong bonds, so a very large amount of energy is needed.
```

A frequent error is to mention "intermolecular forces" here. There are no separate molecules in silicon dioxide.

### Worked example 3: comparing silicon dioxide with carbon dioxide

*Carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature but silicon dioxide is a solid with a very high melting point.
Explain the difference.*

```
Carbon dioxide: small molecules (CO2), weak intermolecular forces between them
                little energy to overcome -> gas
Silicon dioxide: giant covalent structure, strong covalent bonds in all directions
                 covalent bonds must be broken -> solid, very high melting point
```

The two oxides have similar-looking formulae, but the structures are completely different. This is a favourite
exam comparison.

## Recognising giant covalent structures from diagrams

Look for these features:

1. A **network of atoms** joined by bond lines.
2. A **repeating pattern** that continues, often with bonds running off the edge of the diagram.
3. **Every atom** bonded to its neighbours, with no separate small groups.
4. Atoms of one or two non-metal elements.

### Worked example 4: sorting diagrams

*Four diagrams are described. A: a ring of six carbon atoms with bonds running off each atom to more rings, in
a large pattern. B: two oxygen atoms joined by a double bond. C: positive and negative ions alternating in a
regular pattern. D: a short section of chain in brackets with n.*

```
A: giant covalent (network continuing; all atoms bonded)       -> giant covalent
B: one small group of atoms                                      -> small molecule
C: ions, electrostatic attraction                                -> giant ionic
D: brackets and n                                                -> polymer
```

### Worked example 5: classifying with data

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts when solid? | Conducts when molten? |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 2072 | No | No |
| Q | 801 | No | Yes |
| R | -78 | No | No |

P: very high melting point and no conduction in either state, so giant covalent. Q: high melting point and
conducts when molten only, so ionic. R: low melting point and no conduction, so small molecules.

## Three giant structures compared

Giant structures come in three types. Learning how they differ helps you answer comparison questions.

| | Giant covalent | Giant ionic | Giant metallic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particles | Atoms | Positive and negative ions | Positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons |
| Held together by | Shared pairs of electrons | Electrostatic attraction between ions | Attraction between ions and electrons |
| Melting point | Very high | High | Usually high |
| Conducts when solid | Usually not | No | Yes |
| Conducts when molten | Usually not | Yes | Yes |

For the melting point of a giant covalent structure the bonds to break are covalent. For an ionic compound they
are electrostatic. Both are strong and numerous, which is why both types have high melting points.

### Worked example 6: writing a full comparison

*Compare the melting points of silicon dioxide (1710 °C) and methane (-182 °C), referring to structure and
bonding.*

```
Silicon dioxide: giant covalent; all atoms joined by strong covalent bonds in all directions;
                 the covalent bonds must be broken; very large amount of energy; very high melting point.
Methane:         small molecules; weak intermolecular forces between molecules;
                 covalent bonds within molecules not broken; little energy; very low melting point.
```

The comparison works best when each sentence has a matching pair: structure, force, energy, result.

## Common mistakes

- Mentioning "intermolecular forces" or "molecules" for a giant covalent structure.
- Saying only "strong bonds" with no mention that the covalent bonds themselves must be broken.
- Treating SiO₂ as a small molecule because of its short formula.
- Forgetting that the structure is three-dimensional and that a diagram shows only part of it.
- Saying all giant covalent structures conduct electricity (or all do not). Diamond and silicon dioxide do not.
  Graphite is a special case.

## Exam technique

- **Explain a very high melting point:** giant covalent, strong covalent bonds in all directions, many bonds to
  break, large amount of energy.
- **Compare with small molecules:** covalent bonds broken versus weak intermolecular forces overcome.
- **Identify from a diagram:** say it is a network that continues, with every atom bonded.
- **Formula:** give the ratio of atoms and show the working.
