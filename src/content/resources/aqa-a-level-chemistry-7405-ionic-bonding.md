## What ionic bonding is

An ionic bond is the **electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions**. It forms when atoms of a metal transfer electrons to atoms of a non-metal, so both reach a stable outer shell. The result is not a pair of ions but a giant **ionic lattice**: a regular three-dimensional arrangement in which every ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge. In sodium chloride each Na⁺ has six Cl⁻ neighbours and each Cl⁻ has six Na⁺ neighbours, so the bonding acts in all directions, not just between a pair of ions.

For this reason an ionic compound has a **formula unit**, the simplest ratio of ions, and not a molecule. The formula NaCl means a 1 : 1 ratio across the whole crystal.

## Predicting the charge on a simple ion

Atoms gain or lose electrons to reach the electron configuration of the nearest noble gas.

| Group | Outer electrons | Ion formed | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | +1 | Na⁺ |
| 2 | 2 | 2+ | Mg²⁺ |
| 13 (3) | 3 | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| 15 (5) | 5 | 3− | N³⁻ |
| 16 (6) | 6 | 2− | O²⁻ |
| 17 (7) | 7 | − | Cl⁻ |

The rule for the simple ions is: metals in Groups 1, 2 and 13 lose their outer electrons (charge = group number); non-metals in Groups 15 to 17 gain electrons (charge = −(18 − group number) using the 1 to 18 numbering, or −(8 − group number) using the old numbering). Hydrogen can form H⁺ or H⁻. Transition metals form ions with more than one charge, so the charge is shown by Roman numerals: iron(II) is Fe²⁺ and iron(III) is Fe³⁺.

An isoelectronic series is a set of ions with the same electron configuration but different nuclear charge. N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Al³⁺ all have the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. Check any predicted charge by writing the electron configuration of the ion and confirming that it matches a noble gas.

## The compound ions

Learn these as pairs of formula and charge. They are not derived.

| Name | Formula |
|---|---|
| Hydroxide | OH⁻ |
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |
| Sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺ |

A compound ion is a group of atoms covalently bonded together carrying an overall charge. It stays together in reactions, which is why brackets are needed.

## Constructing a formula

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. Cross-checking the charges is the safest method.

1. Write each ion with its charge.
2. Find the smallest numbers that make the charges cancel.
3. Put brackets round a compound ion only if more than one is needed.

### Worked example 1: simple ions

Magnesium nitride. Mg²⁺ and N³⁻. The lowest common multiple of 2 and 3 is 6, so three Mg²⁺ (+6) balance two N³⁻ (−6). Formula: **Mg₃N₂**.

### Worked example 2: compound ions

Aluminium sulfate. Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻. The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so two Al³⁺ (+6) balance three SO₄²⁻ (−6). Brackets are needed because more than one sulfate is present: **Al₂(SO₄)₃**.

### Worked example 3: ammonium and carbonate

Ammonium carbonate. NH₄⁺ and CO₃²⁻. Two NH₄⁺ (+2) balance one CO₃²⁻ (−2). Brackets round the ammonium: **(NH₄)₂CO₃**. The carbonate needs none because only one is present.

### Worked example 4: working backwards

A compound has the formula Fe₂(SO₄)₃. Find the charge on iron. Three sulfate ions carry 3 × (−2) = −6, so the two iron ions carry +6 in total, which is +3 each. The compound is iron(III) sulfate.

## Giant lattice structure

In an ionic lattice every ion is held by many strong attractions, in every direction. This explains the usual properties.

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| High melting and boiling points | Large amounts of energy are needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions throughout the lattice |
| Hard but brittle | Hard because the attractions are strong. Brittle because a blow shifts a layer so that ions of the same charge line up and repel, and the crystal splits |
| Do not conduct when solid | The ions are fixed in position and there are no mobile charged particles |
| Conduct when molten or dissolved | The ions are free to move and carry charge |
| Many dissolve in water | Water molecules are polar and attract the ions, pulling them out of the lattice |

Remember that a conductor needs **mobile charged particles**: say "ions are free to move", not "electrons move" (the electrons in an ionic compound do not leave their ions).

## What controls the strength of the attraction

The size of the attraction between two ions depends on two things.

- **Ionic charge**: higher charges give a stronger attraction.
- **Ionic radius**: smaller ions let the opposite charges approach more closely, which gives a stronger attraction.

This is why magnesium oxide, with Mg²⁺ and O²⁻, melts at about 2850 °C while sodium chloride, with Na⁺ and Cl⁻, melts at 801 °C. Mg²⁺ has a charge twice that of Na⁺ and is smaller; O²⁻ has a charge twice that of Cl⁻ and is also smaller. Each factor strengthens the lattice.

### Worked example 5: comparing lattices

Explain why calcium oxide has a higher melting point than potassium chloride.

Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ have charges of 2+ and 2−, whereas K⁺ and Cl⁻ have 1+ and 1−. The attraction between ions with double the charge is much stronger, so more energy is needed to break the lattice. Although Ca²⁺ and K⁺ have similar radii, the charge is the dominant difference here.

A good answer always names the particles (ions), the force (electrostatic attraction), the factor (charge or radius) and the consequence (more energy to separate).

## Electron transfer in dot-and-cross diagrams

A dot-and-cross diagram shows the electrons in the outer shell. For sodium oxide, two sodium atoms each lose one electron and one oxygen atom gains two electrons. Draw each ion in square brackets with its charge outside the bracket at the top right: [Na]⁺, [Na]⁺ and [O]²⁻. Each sodium ion shows the full outer shell of eight electrons that it has left (the former third shell is empty). The oxide ion shows eight outer electrons, the original six plus the two gained, drawn with a different symbol so the transfer can be followed.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a formula such as MgCl without balancing the charges (it should be MgCl₂).
- Putting brackets round every compound ion, as in Na(OH), or forgetting them when two are needed, as in Ca(OH)₂.
- Giving transition metal ions a fixed charge without checking the name.
- Saying that ionic compounds contain "molecules" or that sodium chloride has a molecular formula.
- Writing that "ionic bonds" are between a pair of atoms instead of throughout a lattice.
- Explaining solid non-conduction by "no electrons" rather than "ions cannot move".
- Forgetting the charge on the dot-and-cross brackets.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define** ionic bonding: give "electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions".
- **Explain** a property: use the sequence strong attractions in a giant lattice, then energy needed or mobility of ions.
- **Deduce** a formula: show the charges so the examiner can award the working marks.
- **Compare**: use the charge and radius of both compounds in your answer.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is an ionic bond a full transfer of electrons?**
It is the electrostatic attraction that results from transfer. Real ionic compounds show some covalent character, which is studied later in the Born–Haber cycle work.

**Why is MgCl₂ not MgCl?**
Mg²⁺ has two positive charges, so two Cl⁻ ions are needed to give a neutral compound.

**Do I need to learn the charges of all transition metal ions?**
No. The specification asks for simple ion charges from the Periodic Table, and the compound ions listed above. Other charges are given in the name or the question.
