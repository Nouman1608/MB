These questions cover content statement **B1** of AQA A-level Mathematics (7357), Section B: Algebra and functions: "Understand and use the laws of indices for all rational exponents." Answer without a calculator and show every step. Learn the methods in the [B1 study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-mathematics-7357-b1-laws-of-indices-aqa-alevel-maths/) and recall them with the [B1 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-mathematics-7357-b1-laws-of-indices-aqa-alevel-maths-revision-notes/). ## Section A

**1.** Evaluate, giving each answer as an integer or a fraction.

**(a)** (27/64)^(−2/3) **[2]**

**(b)** (0.25)^(−5/2) **[1]**

**2.** Write (4x² − 3)/(2x√x) in the form Axᵖ + Bx^q, where A, B, p and q are constants. **[3]**

**3.** Given that (27x)^(1/3) × x² ÷ (9√x) = kxⁿ, find the values of k and n. **[3]**

**4.** Solve 8ˣ = 4^(x+1)/√2. **[3]**

**5.** Solve 3ˣ × 4ˣ = 1/144. **[2]**

**6.** Factorise fully x^(5/2) − 9x^(1/2). **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Without using a calculator, show that √5 > ∛11. **[3]**

**8.** Solve x^(2/3) + 2x^(1/3) − 8 = 0. **[4]**

**9.**

**(a)** Solve x − 7x^(1/2) + 10 = 0. **[3]**

**(b)** Hence solve (y + 1) − 7(y + 1)^(1/2) + 10 = 0. **[2]**

**10.** It is given that x ≥ 0.

**(a)** Solve 2x^(7/4) = 32x^(3/4). **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why dividing both sides by x^(3/4) as a first step would be an error. **[1]**

**11.**

**(a)** Factorise fully 3x(2x − 1)^(1/2) + (2x − 1)^(3/2). **[2]**

**(b)** Hence solve 3x(2x − 1)^(1/2) + (2x − 1)^(3/2) = 0, giving a reason for rejecting any value. **[2]**

**12.** In an invented model of a small moon's orbit, the time T days for one orbit is related to the orbit radius r (in thousands of kilometres) by T = 0.2r^(3/2).

**(a)** Find r when T = 25. **[2]**

**(b)** Show that if r is multiplied by 4, T is multiplied by 8. **[2]**

**(c)** Make r the subject of the formula. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Negative index: (64/27)^(2/3) [1]. Root first: (4/3)² = **16/9** [1]

**(b)** (1/4)^(−5/2) = 4^(5/2) = (√4)⁵ = **32** [1]

*Examiner insight:* flip the fraction for the negative index before anything else. Applying the root to 27/64 and then forgetting to invert is the commonest error.

**2.** Denominator: 2x√x = 2x^(3/2) [1]. First term: 4x²/(2x^(3/2)) = 2x^(1/2) [1]. Second term: −3/(2x^(3/2)) = −(3/2)x^(−3/2) [1]. So the expression is **2x^(1/2) − (3/2)x^(−3/2)**.

*Examiner insight:* combine x × √x into one power before dividing. Treating the denominator as 2x and forgetting the √x changes both indices.

**3.** (27x)^(1/3) = 3x^(1/3) [1]. Then 3x^(1/3) × x² = 3x^(7/3), and dividing by 9x^(1/2) gives (3/9)x^(7/3 − 1/2) = (1/3)x^(11/6). So **k = 1/3** [1] and **n = 11/6** [1].

*Examiner insight:* 7/3 − 1/2 needs a common denominator: 14/6 − 3/6 = 11/6. State k and n separately at the end.

**4.** 8ˣ = 2^(3x) [1]. 4^(x+1)/√2 = 2^(2x+2) × 2^(−1/2) = 2^(2x + 3/2) [1]. Equate indices: 3x = 2x + 3/2, so **x = 3/2** [1].

*Examiner insight:* write √2 as 2^(1/2) and move it with a negative index. Mixing a surd with powers of 2 in the same line invites mistakes.

**5.** 3ˣ × 4ˣ = 12ˣ and 1/144 = 12⁻² [1]. So **x = −2** [1].

**6.** Take out the lowest power: x^(1/2)(x² − 9) [1] = **x^(1/2)(x − 3)(x + 3)** [1].

*Examiner insight:* "fully" means the difference of two squares must also be factorised.

**7.** Raise both numbers to the power 6, the lowest common multiple of 2 and 3 [1]. (5^(1/2))⁶ = 5³ = 125 and (11^(1/3))⁶ = 11² = 121 [1]. Both numbers are positive and 125 > 121, so raising to the sixth power keeps the order, and √5 > ∛11 [1].

*Examiner insight:* the final mark is for the reason. A comparison of 125 and 121 with no comment about positive numbers is not a complete argument.

**8.** Let u = x^(1/3), so u² + 2u − 8 = 0 [1]. (u + 4)(u − 2) = 0, so u = −4 or u = 2 [1]. u = 2 gives **x = 8** [1]. u = −4 gives **x = (−4)³ = −64**, which is valid because a cube root of a negative number is real [1].

*Examiner insight:* candidates often reject x = −64 by habit from square-root questions. Check it: (−64)^(2/3) = 16 and 2(−64)^(1/3) = −8, and 16 − 8 − 8 = 0.

**9. (a)** Let u = x^(1/2): u² − 7u + 10 = 0 [1]. (u − 2)(u − 5) = 0, so u = 2 or 5, both positive and so both acceptable [1]. **x = 4 or x = 25** [1].

**(b)** The equation is part (a) with x replaced by y + 1, so y + 1 = 4 or y + 1 = 25 [1]. **y = 3 or y = 24** [1].

*Examiner insight:* "Hence" means use part (a). Starting again from scratch may still reach the answer, but it wastes time and may not earn the method mark.

**10. (a)** 2x^(7/4) − 32x^(3/4) = 0, so 2x^(3/4)(x − 16) = 0 [1]. x − 16 = 0 gives **x = 16** [1]. x^(3/4) = 0 gives **x = 0** [1].

**(b)** Dividing assumes x^(3/4) ≠ 0, so the solution x = 0 would be lost [1].

**11. (a)** The lowest power of (2x − 1) is 1/2, so the expression is (2x − 1)^(1/2)[3x + (2x − 1)] [1] = **(2x − 1)^(1/2)(5x − 1)** [1].

**(b)** (2x − 1)^(1/2) = 0 gives **x = 1/2** [1]. 5x − 1 = 0 gives x = 1/5, but then 2x − 1 = −3/5, which is negative, so (2x − 1)^(1/2) is not real and x = 1/5 is rejected [1].

*Examiner insight:* always check a factorised solution against the original expression. A value that makes the bracket under a square root negative cannot be a solution.

**12. (a)** r^(3/2) = 25/0.2 = 125 [1]. r = 125^(2/3) = (∛125)² = **25** (thousand km) [1].

**(b)** New time = 0.2(4r)^(3/2) = 0.2 × 4^(3/2) × r^(3/2) [1]. 4^(3/2) = (√4)³ = 8, so the new time is 8 × 0.2r^(3/2) = 8T [1].

**(c)** r^(3/2) = T/0.2 = 5T [1]. Raise both sides to the power 2/3: **r = (5T)^(2/3)** [1].

*Examiner insight:* in (b), the index applies to the 4 as well as to r. Writing 4r^(3/2) is the error that makes the answer look like "multiplied by 4".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Inverting a fraction for a negative index after taking the root, or not at all.
- Treating x√x as x^(1/2) or as 2x instead of x^(3/2).
- Subtracting fractional indices without a common denominator.
- Dividing by a power of x and losing x = 0.
- Rejecting negative cube roots, or keeping negative square roots.
- Not checking that a value makes an expression under a square root non-negative.
- Missing the reason in a "show that" comparison.
