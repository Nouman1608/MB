## 1. Who the stakeholders are, and what they want

A stakeholder is anyone, or any group, affected by or concerned with a business's actions. This is the standard meaning; the specification lists the groups rather than defining the word. The specification names eight groups and asks for their different objectives. It does not fix a list of objectives, so the ones below are typical examples.

| Stakeholder group | Typical objectives |
|---|---|
| Shareholders (owners) | A return on the money they put in, such as a share of profit; growth in the value of their shares; a business that survives |
| Employees | Fair pay, job security, safe and pleasant working conditions, training and chances of promotion |
| Customers | Good quality, fair prices, safe products, reliable service and a choice of goods |
| Managers | A good salary, perhaps bonuses, a secure post, a business that grows and decisions they are free to make |
| Suppliers | Regular orders, payment on time, a long-term relationship and fair prices for what they provide |
| Local community | Local jobs, little noise, traffic or pollution, and a business that supports local life |
| Pressure groups | A change in how a business behaves, such as better treatment of workers or less damage to the environment |
| The government | Tax revenue, jobs, businesses that obey the law and a healthy economy |

Describing a pressure group in this way is standard knowledge, not specification wording. Pressure groups are not owners, staff or customers. They choose a cause and try to influence the business, often by drawing public attention to it.

## 2. How stakeholders are affected by business activity

The specification asks you to explain how stakeholders are affected by what a business does. Link a decision to a group and say what changes for them.

- A business that takes on extra staff gives employees more work and income, and gives the government more income tax.
- A business that cuts its prices helps customers, but may squeeze the profit that shareholders hope to share.
- A factory that runs lorries through a village at night affects the local community through noise and traffic.
- A business that pays suppliers late hurts their cash flow.
- A business that closes a site ends jobs, which affects employees and, often, the area around it.

## 3. How stakeholders impact business activity

Stakeholders are not passive. They can change what a business does.

- Shareholders can press for higher profit or replace the managers.
- Employees who are well treated work harder and stay; poorly treated employees leave or take industrial action.
- Customers decide whether to buy. Poor reviews cut sales; loyal customers raise them.
- Managers make the day-to-day decisions that set the direction of the business.
- Suppliers can raise prices, change delivery times or stop supplying, which changes the business's costs and output.
- The local community can object to plans or support the business with custom.
- Pressure groups can run campaigns that change how customers see a business.
- The government sets taxes and rules that the business must follow.

### Worked example: a move to a larger unit

Brindle & Moor Joinery Ltd makes made-to-measure windows. It proposes to move to a bigger unit on the edge of a village and add a late shift. The owners expect annual profit to rise from £84,000 to £96,600. Payroll is £210,000 and a 3% rise is planned.

1. Profit rises by £96,600 − £84,000 = £12,600. As a percentage: 12,600 ÷ 84,000 × 100 = 15%.
2. The pay rise costs £210,000 × 0.03 = £6,300, so payroll becomes £216,300.
3. Affected groups: shareholders (more profit), employees (more hours and pay but later shifts), the local community (noise and lorries), suppliers (larger orders) and the government (more tax).
4. Impact on the business: residents might object, which could delay the move or add conditions, while a supplier that cannot meet the larger orders could limit output.

## 4. Possible conflicts between stakeholder groups

A conflict arises when the objectives of two groups cannot both be met in full. One group's gain is often another's loss.

| Conflict | Why it arises |
|---|---|
| Shareholders and employees | Higher wages reduce the profit available to share; cutting wages to lift profit upsets staff |
| Shareholders and customers | Lower prices or better quality cost money; higher prices raise profit but may lose customers |
| Managers and shareholders | Managers may want growth or high pay, while owners want profit returned to them |
| Local community and owners | The business wants to expand or extend its hours; residents want quiet |
| Pressure groups and managers | A campaign for greener methods may raise costs that the business would rather avoid |
| Suppliers and the business | The supplier wants a high price; the business wants low costs |

For Brindle & Moor, the late shift raises profit for shareholders but may anger neighbours. Good answers say how a business might settle a conflict, such as a trial period, noise limits or a staff bonus that is paid for out of the extra profit.

## 5. Types of technology used by business (1.5.2)

The specification names four types. The descriptions are standard knowledge, not specification wording.

- **E-commerce.** Trading goods or services online, through a business's own website or a marketplace.
- **Social media.** Online platforms where a business posts content, talks with customers and can pay to advertise.
- **Digital communication.** Electronic ways of passing information inside the business and with others, such as email, video calls and messaging apps.
- **Payment systems.** Ways of taking or making payments electronically, such as card readers, contactless payments and online payments.

## 6. How technology influences business activity

The specification asks for the influence on three things: sales, costs and the marketing mix.

### Sales

- E-commerce lets a small business reach customers far beyond its town and take orders outside opening hours.
- Social media can raise awareness and bring customers who would not otherwise have heard of the business.
- Easy payment can remove a reason to walk away, though a site that fails at the checkout loses sales.

### Costs

- Digital communication can cut postage, travel and printing costs.
- E-commerce can reduce rent if a business needs a smaller shop, but adds website, delivery and packing costs.
- Payment systems often charge a fee on each sale or a monthly rental.
- Setting up and training staff to use new technology costs money in the short run.

### The marketing mix

The marketing mix is product, price, promotion and place.

| Element | Technology's influence |
|---|---|
| Product | Customer comments online help a business improve or change what it sells; some products can be delivered digitally |
| Price | Customers compare prices online, which pushes prices down; lower selling costs can allow lower prices |
| Promotion | Social media, email and online adverts can target groups cheaply |
| Place | E-commerce makes the website a place to buy, alongside or instead of a shop |

### Worked example: opening an online store

Pennine Plant Nursery sells 2,500 items a month in its shop at an average of £8. It opens an online store that takes 600 orders a month at an average of £18.

1. Shop sales revenue = 2,500 × £8 = £20,000.
2. Online sales revenue = 600 × £18 = £10,800. Total = £30,800.
3. Sales revenue rises by 10,800 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = 54%.
4. Extra costs each month: stock at 55% of online sales = £5,940; payment fees at 2.5% = £270; postage at £3.40 an order = £2,040; website hosting £60; social media adverts £250. Total = £8,560.
5. Extra profit = £10,800 − £8,560 = £2,240.

The result is positive but modest. The online store raises sales, while the added costs take most of the gain. A strong answer says so rather than praising technology without limit.

## Common errors

- Listing stakeholder groups without saying what each wants.
- Writing that "everyone benefits" when the question asks about a conflict.
- Treating pressure groups as customers or owners.
- Saying technology "saves money" without naming the cost that falls, and ignoring the costs it adds.
- Describing a technology without linking it to sales, costs or a part of the marketing mix.
- Using general points that would fit any business instead of the one in the question.
