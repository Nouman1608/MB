## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between material and non-material culture, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**2.** Define placelessness. **[1]**

**3.** Distinguish between assimilation and multiculturalism. **[2]**

**4.** Outline one way in which a diaspora can change its place of origin. **[2]**

**5.** State one group within a population that is likely to adopt global cultural influences quickly. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A survey of 150 people in a town asked whether they watch television mainly in the national language or in a foreign language.

| Age group | Respondents | Mainly foreign-language |
|---|---|---|
| Under 30 | 60 | 42 |
| 30 to 59 | 60 | 21 |
| 60 and over | 30 | 3 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage watching mainly foreign-language television in each age group. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage for the whole sample. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest one limitation of using this survey as evidence of cultural change. **[1]**

**7.** Explain why global interactions may change material culture faster than non-material culture. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how a migrant festival can show both diffusion and hybridisation, using an example. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why rapid cultural change can strengthen local or national identity. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** "Globalisation has made places around the world increasingly alike." To what extent do you agree? **[10]**

**11.** Examine how migration changes the cultures and identities of both origin and destination places. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Material culture is the physical objects a group makes and uses, such as clothing or food products [1]; non-material culture is ideas, beliefs and practices, such as language, religion or values [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The loss of distinctive local character as standardised landscapes, such as identical malls and chain hotels, make places look and feel the same [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Assimilation expects migrants to adopt the host society's language and customs so that distinctive traits fade [1]; multiculturalism recognises different groups keeping distinct identities side by side [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Migrants send remittances, ideas and new tastes home [1], so origin places adopt new styles of housing, consumer goods or attitudes, for example to education or women's work [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example, young people, urban residents or frequent internet users [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Under 30: 42 / 60 x 100 = **70%** [1]. 30 to 59: 21 / 60 x 100 = **35%** [1]. 60 and over: 3 / 30 x 100 = **10%** [1].

**(b)** (42 + 21 + 3) / 150 x 100 = 66 / 150 x 100 = **44%** [1].

**(c)** For example, the sample is small and from one town, or watching foreign-language television does not show whether values or identity have changed [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Material goods such as clothing, food and electronics are traded easily and sold through global retailers and online platforms [1]. Non-material culture such as language, religion and family values is learned through upbringing and reinforced by communities and institutions [1], so people can adopt global products quickly while keeping their beliefs and practices largely unchanged [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Migration carries a tradition to a new place, which is diffusion: Caribbean migrants brought carnival traditions to London [1]. In the new setting the tradition blends with local influences and attracts new participants [1], producing new musical forms and a festival, the Notting Hill Carnival, that is distinct from its origins, which is hybridisation [1]. **[3]**

**9.** When people feel that their language, customs or landscape are being replaced, they may value them more [1]. This can lead to revival movements, language schooling or heritage protection [1], and sometimes to political support for parties that promise to defend national identity, so globalisation can intensify identity rather than dilute it [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines homogenisation and identifies what "alike" might mean [1].
- For: global brands, chains and malls appear in cities worldwide [1].
- For: standardised landscapes produce placelessness [1].
- For: English spreads as a global language and many languages are endangered [1].
- Against: non-material culture changes more slowly than material culture [1].
- Against: glocalisation and hybridisation create new, distinctive forms [1].
- Against: diffusion is multidirectional, including from Asia and from former colonies [1].
- Against: revival and backlash strengthen local identities [1].
- Change is uneven between urban and rural areas and between age groups [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that places are more alike in visible consumer culture but not in deeper values or identity [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines migration, diaspora and identity [1].
- Destination: migrants introduce food, music, religion and language [1].
- Destination: hybrid forms emerge, such as festivals and fusion food [1].
- Destination: host societies respond through assimilation, integration or multiculturalism [1].
- Destination: tension and discrimination can harden group identities [1].
- Origin: remittances and returning migrants bring new goods and ideas [1].
- Origin: emigration of young adults changes community structure [1].
- Migrants and their children often hold multiple identities [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence for both origin and destination [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on how far migration changes each place [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Questions that mention two kinds of place need both. An answer about destinations alone cannot reach the top marks for a question about origin and destination.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating an overall percentage by averaging group percentages instead of using the totals.
- Treating global brands as proof of complete homogenisation.
- Describing migration's effects only on the destination.
- Confusing culture (practices and values) with identity (how people see themselves).
