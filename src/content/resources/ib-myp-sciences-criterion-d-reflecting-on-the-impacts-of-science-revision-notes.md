## What the guide states

- Students gain global understanding of science by evaluating the implications of scientific developments and their applications to a specific problem or issue.
- Varied scientific language is applied to show understanding.
- Students must reflect on the implications of using science, interacting with one of these factors: **moral, ethical, social, economic, political, cultural or environmental**. The chosen factor may be interrelated with others.
- Students are expected to become aware of the importance of documenting the work of others.
- Maximum 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8. In the on-screen examination, the Applying science task is worth 25 marks, and answers are extended responses.

## The four Year 5 strands

| Strand | Top band (7-8) wording | Plain words |
|---|---|---|
| i | explain the ways in which science is applied and used to address a specific problem or issue | how does the science work on the problem? |
| ii | discuss and evaluate the implications of using science, interacting with a factor | what follows, good and bad, and is it worth it? |
| iii | consistently apply scientific language clearly and precisely | correct terms, no vague words |
| iv | document sources completely | cite in the text and in a reference list |

## Band climb in one line

- Strand i: outline (1-2), summarize (3-4), describe (5-6), explain (7-8).
- Strand ii: outline (1-2), describe (3-4), discuss (5-6), discuss and evaluate (7-8).
- Strand iii: limited success (1-2), sometimes (3-4), usually (5-6), consistently (7-8).
- Strand iv: limited success (1-2), sometimes correctly (3-4), usually correctly (5-6), completely (7-8).

## The seven factors

| Factor | Meaning (guide glossary) | Typical implication |
|---|---|---|
| Moral | principles of right or wrong behaviour derived from a particular society | is it acceptable to society's standards? |
| Ethical | rational inquiry to decide issues as right or wrong, as applied to people and their actions | is the action justified by reasoning (fairness, harm, consent)? |
| Social | interactions between groups of people: welfare, safety, rights, justice, class | health, access, safety, equality |
| Economic | production, distribution and use of income, wealth and commodities | cost, jobs, prices, who pays |
| Political | relates to government or public affairs | laws, policy, international agreement |
| Cultural | patterns of knowledge, behaviour, beliefs, shared attitudes, values and practices | traditions, values, identity |
| Environmental | circumstances, objects or conditions by which one is surrounded | pollution, resources, habitats, climate |

## Method: applying and evaluating

1. **Issue**: state the problem in one sentence.
2. **Science**: name the scientific idea, then explain how it is applied. Use the correct terms.
3. **Factor**: choose the factor that best fits (and one link if useful).
4. **Implications**: write two or three developed points. For each: cause, effect, who or what is affected.
5. **Weigh up**: benefits against harms, short term against long term, alternatives.
6. **Judgment**: state a conclusion that follows from your weighing, with a condition if appropriate.
7. **Sources**: cite in the text and list all sources at the end.

### Sentence frames

- "The science used is ..., which works by ..."
- "This leads to ..., because ..., which affects ..."
- "On the other hand, ..."
- "Taking everything into account, ... is justified provided that ..."

## Command terms for Criterion D

| Term | Guide meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Outline | brief account or summary | key points only |
| Summarize | abstract a general theme or major point(s) | the big picture |
| Describe | detailed account | what happens, with detail |
| Explain | detailed account including reasons or causes | how and why |
| Discuss | considered, balanced review of a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses, with opinions supported by evidence | both sides, supported |
| Evaluate | appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations | weigh up and judge |
| Document | credit sources by referencing or citing, following one recognised referencing system, in the text and in a reference list or bibliography | cite and list |

## Scientific language

- Replace everyday phrases with precise terms (for example "partially permeable membrane" and "salinity").
- Define a key term the first time you use it.
- Keep the same term for the same idea.
- Use correct units and symbols where numbers appear.
- Check spelling of technical words.

## Referencing checklist

- One recognised system, used throughout.
- In-text citation every time you use a fact, figure, quotation or idea from someone else.
- Reference list with all sources, each with enough detail (author or organisation, year, title, publisher or website, date accessed).
- Paraphrase in your own words and still cite.
- Check each source: author, purpose, evidence, date. A named scientific or government body generally deserves more weight than an unsigned post.

## Worked mini-example

Issue: spraying a crop with a chemical to kill insects.

- Science: the chemical blocks a process in the nervous system of insects.
- Implication (environmental): other insects such as pollinators may also be harmed, and rain may wash the chemical into streams, where it can affect aquatic organisms.
- Implication (economic): higher yield and income for farmers, but a possible cost to other farms if pollination falls.
- Judgment: use is justified only if the chemical is targeted, applied at the right time and monitored, because the harm to pollinators can be large.

This is a short outline. In a full answer, each implication would be developed in two or three sentences.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the issue and not the science.
- Naming factors without developing any.
- One-sided arguments.
- Claims with no evidence.
- Imprecise words instead of scientific terms.
- Missing in-text citations.
- A conclusion that does not follow.

## Quick self-test

1. List the seven factors in the guide.
2. Say the difference between "describe" and "explain" for strand i.
3. Give one social and one economic implication of building a new wind farm.
4. Replace "the water goes through the filter" with a more precise sentence.
5. What does "document" require?

### Answers

1. Moral, ethical, social, economic, political, cultural, environmental.
2. Describe gives a detailed account of what happens. Explain also gives the reasons and causes, which here means saying how the science makes the application work.
3. Social, for example: noise or changes to the view could affect people living nearby, while access to clean electricity may improve welfare. Economic, for example: construction and maintenance create jobs, and the cost of electricity may change over time.
4. Water molecules pass through the pores of the filter (or the partially permeable membrane), while larger particles are retained.
5. Crediting sources by referencing or citing them with one recognised system, in the text and in a reference list or bibliography.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to use more than one factor?**
The guide asks you to reflect on implications interacting with one of the factors, and says it may be interrelated with others. Developing one factor well is better than listing several.

**How long should a Criterion D response be?**
Long enough to explain the science, develop the implications and reach a judgment. The on-screen examination expects extended responses.

**Can I use a source from the internet?**
Yes, if you judge its reliability and cite it using a recognised system.
