## Section A

**1.** State what conventional plastics are made from. **[1]**

**2.** Name the products formed when a biodegradable plastic is decomposed. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** ways in which microplastics come to be in the environment. **[2]**

**4.** Name **one** biotic factor that affects the rate at which a biodegradable plastic decomposes. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why non-biodegradable plastics remain in the ocean for a long time. **[2]**

**6.** A cup is made from plant starch and decomposes in soil within a few months. Give the **two** syllabus terms that describe this cup. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Students buried strips of the same biodegradable plastic, each with a starting mass of 10.0 g, in three places. They measured the mass remaining after eight weeks. The figures are invented for practice.

| Location | Mass remaining after 8 weeks (g) |
|---|---|
| Warm, moist garden soil | 3.0 |
| Cold, waterlogged soil | 7.5 |
| Sea water in a harbour | 9.0 |

(a) Calculate the percentage of mass lost in the warm, moist soil. **[2]**
(b) Explain the differences in mass remaining between the three locations. **[3]**

**8.** Researchers examined 50 dead seabirds found on a beach. 41 had plastic in their stomachs.
(a) Calculate the percentage of seabirds with plastic in their stomachs. **[1]**
(b) Explain how swallowing plastic can cause a seabird's death. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how microplastics in the sea can end up harming people who eat fish. **[3]**

**10.** A fishing net is lost at sea. Explain **two** ways the net could harm marine ecosystems over the following years. **[4]**

**11.** A busy coastal market sells food in plastic bags and polystyrene boxes. Rubbish is often blown into the harbour. Describe how **safe disposal** and **alternative packaging** could reduce the plastic reaching the sea. **[4]**

**12.** A small island country depends on tourism. Discuss the benefits and limitations of a law banning single-use plastics as a way to reduce plastic pollution on its beaches. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Fossil fuels **[1]**.

**2.** Water and biomass **[1]**; gases, carbon dioxide and methane **[1]**.

**3.** Larger plastics break down into small pieces **[1]**; they are used in commercial products, such as beads in some cosmetics, and are washed away **[1]**.

**4.** The number or type of bacteria or fungi present **[1]**.

**5.** They are not decomposed by bacteria and fungi **[1]**; they only break down slowly into smaller fragments, so the plastic remains as microplastics **[1]**.

**6.** Bioplastic, because it is made from a biological raw material **[1]**; biodegradable, because it is decomposed in soil **[1]**.

**7.** (a) Mass lost = 10.0 − 3.0 = 7.0 g **[1]**; 7.0 ÷ 10.0 × 100 = 70% **[1]**.
(b) Warm, moist soil lost most mass because bacteria and fungi are most numerous and active in warm, moist, aerated conditions **[1]**. Cold, waterlogged soil has a low temperature and little oxygen, so decomposition is slower **[1]**. Sea water is cold and contains fewer decomposers suited to breaking down the plastic, so only 1.0 g was lost **[1]**.

**8.** (a) 41 ÷ 50 × 100 = 82% **[1]**.
(b) Plastic fills the stomach, so the bird feels full, stops eating and starves **[1]**; sharp or large pieces can block or damage the gut **[1]**.

**9.** Small animals such as plankton and small fish mistake microplastics for food **[1]**. The plastic, and toxic chemicals carried on it, build up in their bodies (bioaccumulation) **[1]**. Larger fish eat many small fish, so the amount increases up the food chain (biomagnification), and people who eat the larger fish take in the highest amounts **[1]**.

**10.** The net entangles turtles, seals, seabirds and fish **[1]**, which may drown or be unable to feed, and it can keep trapping animals for years **[1]**. Over time the net breaks into smaller pieces and microplastics **[1]**, which animals mistake for food, leading to starvation or to bioaccumulation and biomagnification **[1]**.

**11.** Safe disposal: provide covered bins and regular collection at the market **[1]**, so rubbish is taken to managed landfill or recycling instead of being blown into the harbour **[1]**. Alternative packaging: replace plastic bags and boxes with paper, card, leaves or reusable containers **[1]**, which are less harmful if they escape, because they break down quickly or are kept and reused **[1]**.

**12.** One mark per point; at least two benefits, two limitations and a judgement for full marks.
- Benefit: banning single-use items such as bags, straws and cups stops them at source, so fewer reach beaches and the sea **[1]**.
- Benefit: cleaner beaches reduce visual pollution and protect the tourism income the island depends on **[1]**.
- Limitation: much beach plastic arrives on ocean currents from other countries, and lost fishing gear is not covered by the ban **[1]**.
- Limitation: the law needs enforcement, such as inspections and fines, and alternatives may cost businesses more **[1]**.
- Limitation: the ban does nothing about plastic already on beaches or microplastics in the sea **[1]**.
- Judgement: for example, the ban is worthwhile but must be combined with safe disposal, beach cleaning and international cooperation **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 2**: giving only "carbon dioxide" and missing water, biomass or methane.
- **Question 6**: writing only "biodegradable" and missing that the source makes it a bioplastic.
- **Question 7(b)**: describing the figures without naming temperature, oxygen or decomposers.
- **Question 9**: missing the step where the amount increases up the food chain.
- **Question 12**: assuming the ban solves the problem, without considering plastic from other countries.
