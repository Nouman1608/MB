## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by supply chain management. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** reason why a distribution centre might reduce a retailer's delivery costs. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between outsourcing and subcontracting. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why a business might bring production back to the UK from overseas (re-shoring). **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is the best example of offshoring?

A: A business pays a courier firm to deliver its parcels.

B: A business moves its call centre from Leeds to another country.

C: A business moves from a city centre to an out-of-town site.

D: A business opens a second warehouse.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Calder Bikes makes 30,000 bikes a year with 8 staff and is choosing between two sites.

Site X (city): rent £72,000, wage £29,000 per member of staff, transport costs £21,000.

Site Y (out of town): rent £45,000, wage £26,000 per member of staff, transport costs £48,000.

**(a)** Calculate the total annual cost and the cost per bike at each site. **[4]**

**(b)** Calder scores the sites from 1 to 5 against four factors weighted by importance: cost (weight 40), skilled labour (30), access to customers (20) and room to expand (10). Site X scores 2, 5, 4 and 3. Site Y scores 4, 2, 3 and 5. Calculate the weighted score (out of 5) for each site. **[3]**

**(c)** Recommend a site for Calder Bikes and justify your answer. **[8]**

**7.** Calder Bikes makes 12,000 deliveries a year to shops. Running its own vans costs £60,000 a year fixed plus £1.50 a delivery. A courier firm would charge £7.00 a delivery.

**(a)** Calculate the cost per delivery of using its own vans at 12,000 deliveries. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of deliveries at which the two options cost the same, to the nearest whole delivery. **[3]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Calder Bikes should use its own vans or the courier firm. **[8]**

**8.** A UK furniture maker is considering moving all production to a factory in another country, where labour costs are far lower. Evaluate the impact of offshoring on the business and its stakeholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Supply chain management is coordinating all the businesses and activities involved in turning raw materials into finished goods delivered to customers [1], aiming for a smooth flow of materials and goods at low cost, with no shortages [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A distribution centre receives goods in bulk [1], sorts them and dispatches them to many shops together, so fewer, fuller vehicles are needed and journey costs per item fall [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Outsourcing is paying another business to carry out a whole function or process, such as payroll or delivery [1]. Subcontracting is passing part of a specific job or order to another business [1], often to cope with a peak in demand or to use a specialist skill [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Overseas wages may have risen, so the cost advantage has shrunk [1], and shipping delays and long lead times may mean the business cannot respond quickly to customers [1]. Accept: automation, quality problems or customers preferring UK-made goods, if explained. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Site X: rent £72,000 + labour 8 × £29,000 = £232,000 + transport £21,000 = **£325,000** [1]; per bike £325,000 ÷ 30,000 = **£10.83** [1]. Site Y: £45,000 + 8 × £26,000 = £208,000 + £48,000 = **£301,000** [1]; per bike £301,000 ÷ 30,000 = **£10.03** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Site X: (40 × 2) + (30 × 5) + (20 × 4) + (10 × 3) = 80 + 150 + 80 + 30 = 340 [1]; ÷ 100 = **3.4** [1]. Site Y: (40 × 4) + (30 × 2) + (20 × 3) + (10 × 5) = 160 + 60 + 60 + 50 = 330 [1]; ÷ 100 = **3.3**. **[3]**

**(c)** Site Y is £24,000 a year cheaper, about £0.80 a bike [1], and has more room to expand [1]. Site X scores slightly higher overall (3.4 against 3.3), because of skilled labour and access to customers [1], but the difference of 0.1 is small and depends on subjective scores and weights [1].
Y's transport costs are more than double X's, so any rise in fuel costs would narrow the cost gap [1]. X's labour pool matters if bike assembly needs skilled staff who may be hard to recruit out of town [1].
Calder's customers are shops, not people visiting the site, so access to customers matters less than the weighting of 20 suggests [1].
Judgement: Calder should choose Site Y, because the cost saving is certain whereas the benefit of skilled labour at X is less clear, provided it can recruit and train enough staff locally [1]. If recruiting proves difficult, the case for X would grow. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Cost = £60,000 + (£1.50 × 12,000) = £78,000 [1]. Per delivery = £78,000 ÷ 12,000 = **£6.50** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Fixed costs £60,000 + £1.50x = £7.00x [1]. £60,000 = £5.50x [1], so x = 10,909.09, which is **10,910 deliveries** (rounded up) [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** At 12,000 deliveries the vans cost £78,000 against £84,000 for the courier, so the vans save £6,000 a year [1]. The saving is small and depends on volume: below 10,910 deliveries the courier is cheaper [1], and 12,000 is only 10% above this point [1].
Own vans give control over delivery times and a chance to show the brand to shops [1], while the courier provides flexibility, gives access to a wider network and removes the risk of breakdowns or driver absence [1]. In-house drivers also have to be managed, trained and covered for holidays, which adds administration [1].
A fall in demand would leave Calder with fixed costs of £60,000 for vans it cannot fully use [1].
Judgement: with demand close to the break-even point, the courier is lower risk, and Calder should use it unless it expects deliveries to grow well above 12,000 or values control over deliveries highly [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Benefits to the business: labour costs are much lower, which could cut unit costs and raise profit or allow lower prices [1]. The business may also gain access to new overseas markets.
Costs and risks: shipping, import duties, extra stock because of longer lead times, and set-up and redundancy costs reduce the saving [1]. Quality is harder to control at a distance, and delays can leave shops short of stock [1]. Exchange rates and political changes can raise costs [1].
Stakeholders: UK employees may lose their jobs and the local community loses spending and skills [1]; customers may gain lower prices but may lose the benefit of "made in Britain" [1]; overseas workers gain jobs, but working conditions may harm the firm's reputation [1]; owners gain from lower costs but face higher risk [1].
Judgement: offshoring suits a business with stable high-volume products and a reliable partner, but for furniture, where design changes and fast delivery matter, the hidden costs and loss of control may outweigh the savings, so the firm should compare the full costs before moving all production [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a long list of location factors without choosing the important ones.
- Treating a weighted score as proof rather than a guide.
- Confusing outsourcing with offshoring.
- Comparing in-house and external logistics at one volume only.
- Ignoring hidden costs of offshoring such as shipping and quality control.
