Bond energies (kJ mol⁻¹): H–H 436; Cl–Cl 242; Br–Br 193; I–I 151; H–Cl 431; H–Br 366; H–I 299.

## Section A

**1.** Write the half-equation for bromine acting as an oxidising agent. **[1]**

**2.** State the trend in oxidising power of the halogens from chlorine to iodine. **[1]**

**3.** Write an ionic equation for the reaction of chlorine with aqueous potassium iodide. **[1]**

**4.** State the conditions under which hydrogen reacts explosively with chlorine. **[1]**

**5.** State which hydrogen halide, HCl, HBr or HI, is the least thermally stable. **[1]**

**6.** In the reaction Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂, identify the oxidising agent and state the change in oxidation number of iodine. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain why the oxidising power of the halogens decreases down Group 17. **[3]**

**8.** A student adds bromine water to separate solutions of potassium chloride and potassium iodide, then shakes each with cyclohexane.

**(a)** Describe the colour of the cyclohexane layer in each tube. **[2]**

**(b)** Write an ionic equation for any reaction that occurs. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain what the results show about the relative oxidising power of bromine, chlorine and iodine. **[2]**

**9.** Describe the reactions of hydrogen with chlorine, bromine and iodine, giving conditions and an equation for each. **[4]**

**10.** Use the bond energies given above.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change for H₂(g) + Br₂(g) → 2HBr(g). **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the enthalpy change for H₂(g) + I₂(g) → 2HI(g). **[1]**

**(c)** Use your answers to help explain why iodine reacts much less readily with hydrogen than bromine does. **[2]**

**11.** A hot glass rod is plunged into separate gas jars of hydrogen chloride and hydrogen iodide.

**(a)** Describe what is seen in each jar. **[2]**

**(b)** Write an equation for the reaction that occurs. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain the difference in terms of bond strength. **[3]**

**12.** A student explains the thermal stability of the hydrogen halides as follows: "HCl is more stable than HI because the H–Cl bond is more polar."

**(a)** Explain why this explanation is not the one required. **[1]**

**(b)** Predict, with a reason, how the thermal stability of hydrogen fluoride compares with that of hydrogen chloride. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Br₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻ [1].

**2.** Decreases from chlorine to iodine (Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂) [1].

**3.** Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂ [1].

**4.** In sunlight or ultraviolet light [1].

**5.** HI [1].

**6.** Br₂ is the oxidising agent [1]. Iodine changes from −1 to 0 [1].

**7.** Down the group, atomic radius increases, so an incoming electron is further from the nucleus [1]. There are more inner shells shielding it [1]. So the attraction for an extra electron is weaker and the halogen gains electrons less readily [1].

**8. (a)** With KCl: orange (the colour of the added bromine; no reaction) [1]. With KI: purple (iodine formed) [1].

**(b)** Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂ [1].

**(c)** Bromine oxidises iodide ions, so bromine is a stronger oxidising agent than iodine [1]. Bromine does not oxidise chloride ions, so chlorine is a stronger oxidising agent than bromine [1].

**9.** H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl: explosive in sunlight or UV light [1]. H₂ + Br₂ → 2HBr: needs heating with a platinum catalyst, less vigorous [1]. H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI: needs heating with a platinum catalyst [1]; slow and reversible, so it does not go to completion [1].

**10. (a)** Bonds broken: 436 + 193 = 629; bonds formed: 2 × 366 = 732 [1]. ΔH = 629 − 732 = −103 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** (436 + 151) − 2(299) = 587 − 598 = −11 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**(c)** The H–I bond formed is much weaker than the H–Br bond, so much less energy is released and the reaction is only slightly exothermic [1]. Iodine is also a weaker oxidising agent than bromine, so the reaction is slower and reaches equilibrium rather than going to completion [1].

**11. (a)** Hydrogen chloride: no visible change [1]. Hydrogen iodide: purple vapour (iodine) forms [1].

**(b)** 2HI → H₂ + I₂ [1].

**(c)** The iodine atom is larger than the chlorine atom, so the H–I bond is longer [1]. The bonding pair is held less strongly, so the H–I bond is weaker (299 compared with 431 kJ mol⁻¹) [1]. Less energy is needed to break the H–I bond, so HI decomposes at the temperature of the hot rod while HCl does not [1].

**12. (a)** Thermal decomposition involves breaking the H–X bond, so the explanation must be based on bond strength (bond energy), not polarity [1].

**(b)** HF is more thermally stable than HCl [1], because the H–F bond is shorter and stronger (higher bond energy) than H–Cl, so more energy is needed to break it [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing halogens as single atoms in equations.
- Describing the bromine-with-chloride result as colourless because no reaction occurs.
- Leaving out the reversible arrow for hydrogen with iodine.
- Counting only one H–X bond formed in bond energy calculations.
- Using polarity or electronegativity to explain thermal stability.
