## The Quaternary period

The **Quaternary** is the most recent period of geological time. It began about 2.6 million years ago and continues today. It is marked by repeated swings between **glacial periods**, when ice sheets spread across large parts of the northern continents, and warmer **interglacial periods**. We are living in an interglacial, which began about 11,700 years ago, after the end of the last glacial period.

## Evidence for climate change

Different kinds of evidence cover different lengths of time. A strong answer names the evidence, explains how it works, and says what period it covers.

| Evidence | How it works | Timescale |
|---|---|---|
| Ice cores | Layers of ice from Antarctica and Greenland trap tiny bubbles of air. Scientists measure the carbon dioxide in the bubbles and the chemistry of the ice, which reflects the temperature when the snow fell. | Antarctic cores reach back about 800,000 years |
| Ocean sediment cores | Shells of tiny sea creatures build up on the sea floor. Their chemistry records ocean temperature and the volume of ice on land when they formed. | The whole Quaternary and beyond |
| Pollen analysis | Pollen is preserved in peat bogs and lake beds. Each plant species has distinctive pollen, so the mix shows which plants grew, and therefore what the climate was like. | Thousands of years, especially since the last glacial period |
| Tree rings | Trees add one ring a year. Wide rings show good growing conditions; narrow rings show cold or dry years. | Hundreds to several thousand years |
| Historical records | Diaries, paintings, harvest dates and ships' logs describe past weather. For example, "frost fairs" were held on the frozen River Thames in London during a cold phase between about the 14th and 19th centuries known as the Little Ice Age. | Hundreds of years |
| Instrumental records | Thermometer readings across the world since about 1850; carbon dioxide measured continuously at Mauna Loa in Hawaii since 1958. | About 170 years |
| Satellite and modern observations | Satellites measure sea ice, ice sheets and sea level; photographs show glaciers retreating. | Since the 1970s |

**What the recent evidence shows.** According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), global surface temperature in 2011-2020 was about 1.1 °C higher than in 1850-1900. Carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has risen from about 280 parts per million before the industrial era to more than 420 parts per million today. Arctic sea ice in late summer has shrunk markedly since satellite records began, and global sea level is rising.

**Evaluating the evidence.** Older evidence is indirect: ice and pollen are "proxies" that stand in for temperature, so they need careful interpretation. Historical records are often local and descriptive. Instrumental records are precise but short. Confidence comes from the fact that different types of evidence point the same way.

## Natural causes

**Orbital changes.** The Earth's orbit and tilt change in regular cycles, often called Milankovitch cycles. The shape of the orbit changes from more circular to more oval over about 100,000 years; the tilt of the axis varies over about 41,000 years; and the axis wobbles over about 26,000 years. These cycles change how much solar energy reaches different parts of the Earth in different seasons, and they are linked to the timing of glacial and interglacial periods. They work over thousands of years, so they cannot explain change over decades.

**Volcanic activity.** Large eruptions throw ash and sulphur dioxide high into the atmosphere. Sulphur dioxide forms tiny droplets that reflect sunlight, cooling the Earth. The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines cooled global temperatures by about 0.5 °C for a year or two. Volcanic cooling is short-lived because the droplets fall out of the atmosphere.

**Solar output.** The energy given out by the Sun varies slightly, partly in a cycle of about 11 years linked to sunspots. A period with very few sunspots between about 1645 and 1715, the Maunder Minimum, coincided with part of the Little Ice Age.

## Human causes

Human activities add greenhouse gases, which trap heat that would otherwise escape to space. This strengthens the natural greenhouse effect, often called the **enhanced greenhouse effect**.

- **Use of fossil fuels.** Burning coal, oil and gas for electricity, transport and industry releases carbon dioxide, the largest single human contribution.
- **Agriculture.** Cattle and other livestock release methane as they digest food; flooded rice fields release methane; nitrogen fertilisers release nitrous oxide. Methane and nitrous oxide are both powerful greenhouse gases.
- **Deforestation.** Trees store carbon. Clearing forests, especially by burning, releases that carbon as carbon dioxide and removes a carbon store that would otherwise keep absorbing it.

**Why recent warming is linked to human causes.** Orbital changes are far too slow to explain warming over a century, and volcanic and solar changes are too small and short-term. The rise in temperature since about 1850 matches the rise in greenhouse gases from human activity.

## Effects on people and the environment

The specification asks for an overview. Organise effects into two groups and give examples.

**Effects on the environment**
- Sea-level rise, as warmer water expands and land ice melts.
- Loss of Arctic sea ice and retreat of glaciers.
- Coral bleaching as oceans warm.
- Species moving to higher latitudes or altitudes, and some unable to move fast enough.
- More frequent or more intense heatwaves, droughts and heavy rainfall in many regions.

**Effects on people**
- Coastal flooding and erosion threaten homes and farmland in low-lying areas and small island states.
- Changing rainfall and heat affect crop yields; some areas may gain longer growing seasons while others lose.
- Water supply problems where rivers depend on shrinking glaciers or where droughts become more common.
- Health effects such as heat stress and the spread of diseases carried by insects into new areas.

Effects are uneven: lower-income countries often have fewer resources to cope, though they have contributed least to the causes.

## Managing climate change: mitigation

Mitigation reduces the causes, mainly by cutting greenhouse gas emissions or removing them from the atmosphere.

- **Alternative energy production.** Generating electricity from wind, solar, hydroelectric and other low-carbon sources instead of fossil fuels. Limitations: some sources depend on weather, and new infrastructure is expensive.
- **Carbon capture.** Capturing carbon dioxide from power stations or factories and storing it, for example deep underground in rock layers. Limitation: costly and not yet used at large scale.
- **Planting trees.** Growing new forests absorbs carbon dioxide as the trees grow. Limitations: it takes decades, needs land that may be wanted for farming, and the carbon returns if the forest burns.
- **International agreements.** Countries agree to cut emissions together, because no single country can solve the problem. The Kyoto Protocol (1997) set targets for richer countries; the Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C, with each country setting its own plan. Limitation: targets depend on countries keeping their promises.

## Managing climate change: adaptation

Adaptation helps people live with the changes that are already happening.

- **Change in agricultural systems.** Planting drought-resistant or heat-tolerant crops, changing planting dates, using irrigation more efficiently, or switching to crops suited to the new climate.
- **Managing water supply.** Storing water in reservoirs, transferring water between regions, desalination in dry coastal areas, collecting rainwater, and reducing waste through water meters and repairs to leaking pipes.
- **Reducing risk from rising sea levels.** Building sea walls and flood barriers, raising buildings on stilts, restoring mangroves that absorb wave energy, and moving people and buildings away from the most at-risk coasts. The Netherlands, much of which lies below sea level, protects its coast with large engineering schemes such as the Delta Works.

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

**Question:** Explain how human activities have contributed to climate change. [6 marks]

**Model answer:** "Burning fossil fuels such as coal in power stations releases carbon dioxide, which is a greenhouse gas. More greenhouse gas in the atmosphere traps more of the heat radiated from the Earth's surface, so global temperatures rise. Agriculture adds methane, a more powerful greenhouse gas, from cattle and from flooded rice fields, so expanding livestock and rice farming to feed growing populations adds to warming. Deforestation contributes in two ways: burning trees releases the carbon they store, and the forest can no longer absorb carbon dioxide, so concentrations rise faster."

For each cause the answer links the activity to a gas and the gas to warming; that chain turns a list into an explanation.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing mitigation and adaptation.** Mitigation reduces the causes; adaptation responds to the effects.
- **Confusing the ozone hole with climate change.** They are separate problems.
- **Listing evidence without explaining it.** Say how each type of evidence shows past climate.
- **Overview of effects with no examples.** Name at least one place or group for each effect you give.
