## Questions

**1.** A student times how long an amylase–starch mixture takes to stop giving a blue-black colour with iodine. The times are 40 s, 64 s and 125 s. Calculate the rate as 1000/t for each, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**2.** At a fixed, limited quantity of substrate, a graph of initial rate against enzyme concentration rises as a straight line and then levels off. Explain the shape of the graph. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows the time taken for amylase to clear a starch solution at different temperatures, all at pH 7.

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time / s | 250 | 125 | 80 | 100 | 500 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate as 1000/t at each temperature. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage decrease in rate between 40 °C and 60 °C. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the change in rate from 20 °C to 40 °C and from 40 °C to 60 °C. **[4]**

**4.** A protease has an optimum pH of 8. Explain why it shows very little activity at pH 2. **[3]**

**5.** The initial rate of enzyme X was measured at different substrate concentrations. The curve levels off at a rate of 50 arbitrary units.

| Substrate concentration / mmol dm⁻³ | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Initial rate / arbitrary units | 10.0 | 16.7 | 25.0 | 33.3 | 40.0 | 44.4 | 47.1 |

**(a)** Use the data to find Km for enzyme X. Show how you used Vmax. **[2]**
**(b)** Enzyme Y acts on the same substrate, has the same Vmax and has a Km of 12 mmol dm⁻³. At 4 mmol dm⁻³ substrate, enzyme Y has a rate of 12.5 units. Compare the affinities of X and Y for the substrate, using the data. **[3]**

**6.** The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction was measured with increasing concentrations of a reversible inhibitor.

| Inhibitor concentration / mmol dm⁻³ | 0 | 0.5 | 1.0 | 2.0 | 4.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate / arbitrary units | 48 | 36 | 30 | 20 | 12 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage inhibition at 2.0 mmol dm⁻³ inhibitor. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the trend in the data. **[2]**

**7.** The rate of an enzyme reaction was measured with no inhibitor, with inhibitor A and with inhibitor B, at a low and a high substrate concentration. Each inhibitor was used at the same fixed concentration.

| Substrate concentration / mmol dm⁻³ | No inhibitor | Inhibitor A | Inhibitor B |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20.0 | 8.6 | 10.0 |
| 20 | 54.5 | 46.2 | 27.3 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate with each inhibitor as a percentage of the uninhibited rate at both substrate concentrations. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the type of inhibitor A and inhibitor B. Explain your answers. **[4]**

**8.** Describe and explain the effect of a competitive inhibitor and of a non-competitive inhibitor on Vmax and on Km. **[4]**

**9.** Equal quantities of an enzyme were used free in solution and immobilised in alginate beads. Initial rates were measured before and after both were kept at 60 °C for 10 minutes.

| | Free enzyme | Immobilised enzyme |
|---|---|---|
| Rate before heating / arbitrary units | 2.40 | 1.60 |
| Rate after heating / arbitrary units | 0.36 | 1.12 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of its original rate that each preparation kept after heating. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why the immobilised enzyme had a lower rate before heating. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why the immobilised enzyme kept more of its activity. **[1]**
**(d)** State two other advantages of using immobilised enzymes. **[2]**

**10.** Plan an investigation into the effect of pH on the activity of a bacterial protease, using a cloudy suspension of milk protein that becomes clear as the protein is digested. **[8]**

**11.** Catalase was added to hydrogen peroxide and the volume of oxygen collected was plotted against time. A tangent drawn at t = 0 passes through (0 s, 0 cm³) and (20 s, 13.0 cm³). A tangent drawn at t = 90 s passes through (60 s, 31.0 cm³) and (120 s, 37.0 cm³). By 30 s, 17.5 cm³ had been collected.

**(a)** Calculate the initial rate of reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the rate at 90 s. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the rate at 90 s is lower than the initial rate. **[2]**
**(d)** The mean rate over the first 30 s is 0.58 cm³ s⁻¹. Explain why the initial rate, not this mean rate, should be used to compare different catalase concentrations. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Uses 1000 ÷ time for each [1]; **25.0, 15.6 and 8.0** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Rates in one column must share the same decimal places; mixing 25 and 15.625 loses the accuracy mark.

**2.** Initially, as enzyme concentration increases, more active sites are available, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per unit time [1]; rate is proportional to enzyme concentration while substrate is in excess [1]; the line levels off because substrate becomes the limiting factor and some active sites are unoccupied [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Enzyme is used up" or "enzyme is saturated" scores nothing here; the mark needs substrate named as the limiting factor.

**3. (a)** Method 1000 ÷ t [1]; **4.0, 8.0, 12.5, 10.0, 2.0** [1]
**(b)** (12.5 − 2.0) ÷ 12.5 × 100 [1] = **84%** [1]
**(c)** 20–40 °C: more kinetic energy, so more frequent collisions between enzyme and substrate [1]; more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second [1]. 40–60 °C: hydrogen and ionic bonds in the tertiary structure break, so the active site changes shape [1]; substrate is no longer complementary, fewer enzyme–substrate complexes, enzyme denatured [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each half of (c) carries its own marks; error carried forward is allowed in (b) from wrong rates in (a).

**4.** The high H⁺ concentration changes the charges on R groups in the enzyme [1]; ionic and hydrogen bonds holding the tertiary structure are disrupted, so the active site changes shape [1]; the substrate no longer fits or is no longer attracted to the active site, so few enzyme–substrate complexes form [1]
*Examiner insight:* Mark schemes expect the link from H⁺ ions to R-group charges; "the pH is wrong for the enzyme" earns no credit.

**5. (a)** ½Vmax = 50 ÷ 2 = 25 units [1]; reading across to the data, **Km = 4 mmol dm⁻³** [1]
**(b)** X has a lower Km (4 vs 12 mmol dm⁻³), so X has the higher affinity [1]; X reaches half its maximum rate at a lower substrate concentration, so forms enzyme–substrate complexes more readily [1]; at 4 mmol dm⁻³, X works at 25 units, twice the rate of Y at 12.5 units [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Use the data" means a quoted or manipulated figure is needed for full marks.

**6. (a)** (48 − 20) ÷ 48 × 100 [1] = **58%** [1]
**(b)** As inhibitor concentration increases, rate decreases [1]; more enzyme molecules have inhibitor bound at any moment, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form [1]
*Examiner insight:* Describing the trend alone gets one mark; the second needs the reason in terms of enzyme–substrate complexes.

**7. (a)** A: **43% and 85%**; B: **50% and 50%** — one mark for each inhibitor's pair of values [1] [1]
**(b)** A is competitive [1]: its inhibition is much smaller at high substrate concentration, because substrate molecules out-compete A for the active site [1]. B is non-competitive [1]: it reduces the rate by the same proportion at both concentrations, because it binds away from the active site and more substrate cannot overcome it [1]
*Examiner insight:* Naming each type is one mark and the evidence from the figures is a separate mark.

**8.** Competitive: Vmax unchanged, because at very high substrate concentration substrate out-competes the inhibitor [1]; Km increased, because more substrate is needed to reach half Vmax [1]. Non-competitive: Vmax lowered, because inhibited enzyme molecules cannot form products whatever the substrate concentration [1]; Km unchanged, because uninhibited enzyme molecules bind substrate normally [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs the effect and its explanation; a bare list of effects scores little.

**9. (a)** Free: 0.36 ÷ 2.40 × 100 = **15%** [1]; immobilised: 1.12 ÷ 1.60 × 100 = **70%** [1]
**(b)** Substrate has to diffuse into the bead and product out [1]; some active sites inside the gel are less accessible to substrate [1]
**(c)** The alginate gel holds the enzyme molecules and limits unfolding, so fewer are denatured [1]
**(d)** Any two: product not contaminated with enzyme [1]; enzyme recovered and reused [1]; allows a continuous process in a column
*Examiner insight:* "Other" means stability will not be credited again in (d), and "cheaper" alone needs the reason (reuse) to score.

**10.** Independent variable: pH, using buffer solutions at five or more values, for example pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 [1]; same volume of buffer added to each tube [1]. Dependent variable: time for the milk suspension to clear, or change in absorbance measured with a colorimeter [1]. Standardised: temperature, using a thermostatically controlled water bath [1]; same concentration and volume of protease and of milk suspension [1]. Equilibrate enzyme and substrate separately at the test temperature before mixing [1]. Three or more replicates at each pH, calculating a mean and identifying anomalies [1]. Control with boiled protease to show clearing is due to enzyme activity; wear eye protection and gloves as enzymes can be irritants [1]
*Examiner insight:* Planning marks need specific detail; "keep the temperature the same" without a water bath, or "different pHs" without buffers and values, scores nothing.

**11. (a)** Gradient = 13.0 ÷ 20 [1] = **0.65 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** (37.0 − 31.0) ÷ (120 − 60) [1] = **0.10 cm³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** Hydrogen peroxide is being broken down, so its concentration falls [1]; fewer collisions between substrate and active sites, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes per second [1]
**(d)** At t = 0 substrate concentration is the same in every tube, so only enzyme concentration differs; the mean rate is affected by substrate being used up [1]
*Examiner insight:* Units are required; a correct value without cm³ s⁻¹ loses the accuracy mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Recording rates in a table to inconsistent decimal places.
- Saying the substrate or enzyme curve levels off "because the enzyme is used up".
- Explaining a fall above the optimum without linking broken bonds to active-site shape.
- Explaining pH effects without mentioning H⁺ ions and R-group charges.
- Reading Km at half the highest measured rate instead of half of Vmax.
- Naming an inhibitor type without quoting evidence from the data.
- Leaving units off rates calculated from a tangent.
- Using a mean rate over a long time interval instead of the initial rate.
