These questions cover sub-topic **1.5 Production possibility curves** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028 (AS Level, Paper 1 and Paper 2 style). All countries and data are invented. Revise first with the [production possibility curves study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-production-possibility-curves/) and the [production possibility curves revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-production-possibility-curves-revision-notes/).

Questions 7 and 8 use the production possibilities of Country Lorne, which produces fish (thousand tonnes) and furniture (thousand items).

| Point | Fish | Furniture |
|---|---|---|
| J | 0 | 200 |
| K | 20 | 190 |
| L | 40 | 165 |
| M | 60 | 120 |
| N | 80 | 0 |

## Section A: Short questions

**1.** A point inside a production possibility curve shows that: **[1]**

A: The economy is producing at maximum efficiency

B: Some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently

C: The economy's productive capacity has fallen

D: The combination of goods cannot be produced

**2.** A production possibility curve is a straight line. What does this show? **[1]**

A: Opportunity cost rises as more of either good is produced

B: Opportunity cost is zero

C: Opportunity cost is constant

D: Resources are not suited to producing either good

**3.** Which **one** of the following would shift a country's PPC inwards? **[1]**

A: A rise in unemployment during a recession

B: A flood that destroys factories and farmland

C: A switch of resources from producing cars to producing food

D: A fall in the price of imported machinery

**4.** Explain why a production possibility curve is usually drawn concave to the origin. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between a movement from inside a PPC to a point on it and an outward shift of the PPC. **[4]**

**6.** A workshop can produce either 40 bicycles or 160 scooters a week, with constant opportunity cost. State the opportunity cost of one bicycle and of one scooter. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Use the Country Lorne table.

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one thousand tonnes of fish when moving from K to L, and from M to N. **[2]**

**(b)** Using your answers, explain the shape of Lorne's PPC. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one thousand items of furniture when moving from M to L. **[2]**

**8.** Lorne currently produces 40 thousand tonnes of fish and 140 thousand items of furniture.

**(a)** State where this point lies relative to the PPC, and calculate how much more furniture could be produced without reducing fish output. **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** possible reasons for Lorne producing at this point. **[2]**

**9.** A new fishing technology doubles the amount of fish Lorne can catch with any given resources, but furniture production is unaffected.

**(a)** Describe how Lorne's PPC changes. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why Lorne can now produce more furniture even if it wants the same amount of fish as before. **[2]**

**10.** With the aid of a PPC diagram showing capital goods and consumer goods, explain the consequences for future growth of a country choosing to produce more capital goods now. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** causes of an outward shift in a country's production possibility curve. **[4]**

**12.** Assess whether a country should always aim to produce at a point on its production possibility curve. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. More of one or both goods could be produced with existing resources. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Each extra unit of one good costs the same amount of the other. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. A flood destroys resources, reducing capacity. A is a movement inside the curve; C is a movement along it. **[1]**

**4.** Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods [1]. As more of one good is produced, resources less suited to it must be transferred from producing the other good [1]. Each extra unit therefore requires giving up increasing amounts of the other good, so opportunity cost rises and the curve bows outwards [1]. **[3]**

**5.** A movement from inside to the curve means previously unemployed or inefficiently used resources are now being used fully [1]; productive capacity is unchanged and the extra output has no opportunity cost [1]. An outward shift means productive capacity has increased [1], because of more or better resources or improved technology, so combinations previously unattainable become possible [1]. **[4]**

**6.** One bicycle costs 160 ÷ 40 = **4 scooters** [1]. One scooter costs 40 ÷ 160 = **0.25 bicycles** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** K to L: (190 − 165) ÷ 20 = **1.25 thousand items** of furniture [1]. M to N: (120 − 0) ÷ 20 = **6 thousand items** [1].

**(b)** The opportunity cost of fish rises as more fish is produced [1], so the PPC is concave to the origin, because resources such as furniture workers and workshops are poorly suited to fishing [1].

**(c)** M to L gains 45 thousand items of furniture and gives up 20 thousand tonnes of fish [1], so one thousand items cost 20 ÷ 45 = **0.44 thousand tonnes** of fish [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Inside the curve, since point L shows 165 thousand items with 40 thousand tonnes of fish [1]. A further 165 − 140 = **25 thousand items** could be produced [1].

**(b)** Any two of: unemployment of workers, for example in a recession [1]; inefficient methods or outdated equipment; idle factories or boats; poor organisation of production [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** The PPC pivots outwards from the furniture axis [1]: the maximum fish catch doubles from 80 to 160 thousand tonnes, while the maximum furniture output stays at 200 thousand items [1].

**(b)** Fewer resources are now needed to catch the same amount of fish [1], so resources can be released from fishing to furniture production, raising furniture output without any fall in fish [1]. **[4]**

**10.** A diagram with capital goods on one axis and consumer goods on the other shows the economy choosing a point with more capital goods [1]. This means fewer consumer goods now, an opportunity cost in present living standards [1]. The extra capital raises productive capacity, so the PPC shifts further outwards in future [1] than if the country had chosen mainly consumer goods, allowing more of both goods later [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Cause 1: investment in education and training raises the quality of labour [1], so each worker produces more and the economy can produce more of both goods [1]. Cause 2: improved technology [1] allows more output from the same resources, shifting the curve outwards [1]. Other valid causes: population growth or immigration; discovery of natural resources; investment in physical capital. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 8 marks across knowledge, analysis and evaluation.
- A point on the curve means resources are fully and efficiently employed [1].
- A point inside means unemployment or inefficiency, so output is lost with no gain [1].
- Moving to the curve has no opportunity cost, so it raises living standards [1].
- But being on the curve does not say which point is best; the mix must match what people want [1].
- Producing at full capacity may cause pressure on resources, for example rising costs and prices, so a little spare capacity may be acceptable [1].
- Some unemployment is temporary, for example while workers move between jobs, so a point slightly inside may be unavoidable [1].
- The choice between capital and consumer goods on the curve matters for future growth [1].
- Judgement: being on the curve is generally desirable, but which point, and how quickly it can be reached, depend on the country's priorities and circumstances, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating total output instead of the amount given up per extra unit.
- Forgetting the reciprocal when the question reverses the direction of movement.
- Showing a fall in unemployment as an outward shift.
- Drawing a parallel shift when only one good is affected.
- Assuming any point on the curve is the best point.
