## Ansoff's matrix

### Nature and purpose

Ansoff's matrix is a model of growth strategy. It sorts the options open to a business by asking two questions: is the product new or existing, and is the market new or existing? The purpose is to help managers see the range of growth options and compare how risky each one is. It supports strategic decisions, which are long-term and costly. It is a framework for thinking, not a calculation.

### The four quadrants

| | Existing products | New products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration | Product development |
| **New markets** | Market development | Diversification |

- **Market penetration:** sell more of the existing products to the existing market. This can mean winning customers from rivals, raising how often current customers buy, or attracting people who have not yet bought.
- **Market development:** sell existing products in a new market. The new market might be a new geographical area, a new customer segment or a new use for the product.
- **Product development:** sell new or improved products to the existing market, using what the business already knows about its customers.
- **Diversification:** sell new products to new markets. The business has no direct experience of either side.

To tell two quadrants apart, change one thing at a time. If only the product is new, it is product development. If only the market is new, it is market development. If both are new, it is diversification. If neither is new, it is penetration. A bakery selling its loaves in another city is market development; a bakery opening a hardware shop is diversification.

### Example strategies

| Quadrant | Examples of strategies |
|---|---|
| Market penetration | Loyalty scheme, promotional pricing, more advertising, wider distribution, buying a rival to gain its customers |
| Market development | Opening in another region, selling online to new areas, targeting a different age group, exporting |
| Product development | New flavours or models, improved quality, a new service for current customers, extending a range |
| Diversification | Moving into an unrelated industry; related diversification (a new product with some link to current skills) is less risky than unrelated |

### Risk for the business and for stakeholders

Risk rises as the business moves away from what it knows. Penetration is usually the lowest risk, because product and market are both familiar. Market development and product development carry medium risk, though the risk differs: market development depends on understanding unfamiliar customers, while product development depends on design and production skills and on whether existing customers will buy. Diversification is usually the highest risk, because the business faces both uncertainties together and has least experience to draw on. Higher risk can bring higher potential reward, which links to the [risk and uncertainty](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-risk-and-uncertainty-and-opportunity-cost/) content.

Stakeholders feel the risk differently.

- **Owners and shareholders** may see profits and dividends fall if a risky strategy fails, or share value rise if it succeeds.
- **Employees** may gain jobs and promotion with growth, but face redundancy if a costly diversification fails.
- **Lenders** may be asked to fund the project, so they weigh the chance of repayment.
- **Suppliers** gain bigger orders if growth works, and face unpaid bills if the business collapses.
- **Customers** may gain new products, but quality could slip if the business is stretched.
- **Local community** may gain jobs or suffer extra traffic.

### Recommending and justifying a strategy

A recommendation is not enough on its own. Tie the choice to the business's objectives, its resources, the state of the market, the time available and its attitude to risk. Then weigh the other options and say why they are less suitable.

Worked example. Daskell Bakehouse has 14 shops and annual sales of £6.2m. Sales have been flat for two years, and the owner wants growth with limited borrowing. She sees four options.

| Option | Quadrant | Cost | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loyalty card and online ordering for current customers | Market penetration | £90,000 | Uses current shops; low cost |
| Three shops in a neighbouring city | Market development | £1.2m | Unfamiliar customers; borrowing needed |
| Ready-made meals for current customers | Product development | £350,000 | New skills in the kitchen, same customers |
| Retail of kitchen equipment | Diversification | £900,000 | New product and new customers |

The three new shops cost £1.2m in total, which is £400,000 each. A recommendation: Daskell should start with penetration and add product development later. Penetration costs the least and uses the knowledge it already has, so a mistake is cheap, and it fits an owner who wants limited borrowing. Market development needs the most money and an unfamiliar customer base. Diversification adds both kinds of uncertainty, with £900,000 at risk and no experience in kitchen equipment. The judgement depends on one assumption: that there is still room to win more sales from current customers. If shops already serve nearly everyone nearby, penetration may bring little extra, and product development becomes the better first step. A small trial would test this.

### Usefulness of the matrix

Strengths: it is simple; it shows all growth options in one place; it links risk to how far a business moves from its current position.

Limitations: it ignores the size and speed of reward; it gives no financial figures, so cannot say which option is profitable; it does not show whether the business has the resources to carry out an option; it says nothing of competitors' reactions or of changes in the market; it has only four boxes, so mixed strategies and the line between "new" and "existing" can be blurred; and the risk ranking is only a guide, since a well-researched diversification can be safer than a rushed market development. It is most useful when combined with other tools, which leads to the second topic.

## Conflicts in business decision making

### Why the results of tools can conflict

A decision making tool turns information into a recommendation. The tools you meet in the specification include investment appraisal (payback period, average rate of return and net present value), decision trees, forecasts, Ansoff's matrix, [Porter's models](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-models-of-strategic-choice/) and [financial and non-financial measures of performance](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-measures-of-performance-financial-and-non-financial/). They can disagree for several reasons:

- **They measure different things.** Payback measures speed of recovering the cost, ARR measures the average yearly profit as a percentage of the cost, and NPV measures the value of cash flows in today's money.
- **They treat time differently.** Payback and ARR ignore when cash arrives within the project's life; NPV discounts later cash.
- **They rely on estimates.** Forecast cash flows, probabilities and discount rates may be wrong or biased, and a small change can reverse a ranking.
- **They use different information.** Quantitative tools use numbers; qualitative factors such as brand, morale, ethics and strategic fit are not captured in the figures.
- **They look at different time horizons**, short-term return against long-term position.

Standard forms: payback period = time taken for cumulative net cash inflows to equal the initial cost. ARR = average annual profit divided by initial investment, as a percentage. NPV = the sum of discounted cash flows minus the initial cost.

### Worked example: a conflict between tools

Truvane Cycles can spend £200,000 on one of two projects. Project A is a range of accessories for current customers. Project B is a new electric bike for a new export market. Forecast annual net cash inflows (£000):

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 100 | 100 | 40 | 20 | 20 |
| B | 40 | 60 | 90 | 110 | 130 |

| Measure | Project A | Project B |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | 2.0 years | 3.1 years |
| ARR | 8.0% | 23.0% |
| NPV at 10% | +£29,700 | +£109,400 |

Working for B's payback: after three years £190,000 has come back, so £10,000 remains out of year 4's £110,000, which is 0.09 of a year, giving 3.09 years. For ARR on B, total inflows are £430,000, profit is £230,000, the average per year is £46,000, and £46,000 divided by £200,000 is 23.0%.

Payback favours A. ARR and NPV favour B. The conflict arises because A returns cash early and then fades, while B builds slowly and earns more overall. With a 15% discount rate B's NPV is still higher at about £66,900, against about £10,300 for A, so the ranking is not sensitive to the rate. The recommendation depends on context. If Truvane is short of cash, early payback matters. If it can wait, B offers much more value. In Ansoff terms, A is product development and B is diversification (a new bike and a new market), which the matrix rates as riskier. Truvane should test B's forecasts, for example the export demand, before committing. Qualitative factors then decide: does Truvane have export knowledge, will staff cope, and does the project fit its aims?

### Evaluating decisions using quantitative and qualitative information

Quantitative information (costs, forecasts, ratios, NPV, expected values) is objective in form and allows comparison, but depends on estimates. Qualitative information (reputation, ethics, staff views, fit with the mission) is harder to compare but often explains why a "good" project fails. A strong evaluation follows five steps:

1. State each tool's result.
2. Explain why they differ.
3. Test how reliable the inputs are.
4. Bring in qualitative factors and stakeholders.
5. Make a justified judgement on the course of action, saying what would change it.

### Likely success, strengths and limitations of the tools

To judge the likely success of a proposal, ask whether the numbers are realistic, whether the business has the people, money and time, how it compares with other options, and how rivals and the market may react. Each tool has strengths and limits:

- **Payback:** simple and stresses liquidity; ignores cash after payback and the time value of money.
- **ARR:** uses all the profits and is easy to compare with a target; ignores timing.
- **NPV:** accounts for time value; depends on the chosen discount rate and is harder to explain.
- **Decision trees:** weigh risk using probabilities; the probabilities are often guesses and the expected value is an average that may never occur.
- **Ansoff's matrix:** broad and simple but with no figures.

### Reasons for success or failure of a business

When you judge why a business succeeded or failed, link the tools to causes. Success often comes from decisions that fit the market, sound cash flow management, an effective strategy and the ability to adapt. Failure often follows over-optimistic forecasts, ignoring qualitative warnings, poor risk management, weak cash flow or entering unfamiliar markets without preparation. Say which reason mattered most.

## Common errors

- Describing the four quadrants without saying why one is riskier than another.
- Treating the matrix as a prediction rather than a guide.
- Giving payback, ARR and NPV results but not explaining a conflict.
