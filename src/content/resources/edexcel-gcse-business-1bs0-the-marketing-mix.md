This guide covers content item 1.4.3, The marketing mix, in Topic 1.4 (Making the business effective) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. In Theme 1 the marketing mix is studied from the point of view of a small start-up; Theme 2 (Topic 2.2) returns to each element in more depth for a growing business. The broader [guide to the marketing mix and the business plan](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-the-marketing-mix-and-the-business-plan/) introduces the four elements. This page concentrates on the decisions a start-up owner makes when fitting them together. All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why each element matters to a start-up

A new business has little money, no reputation and no regular customers. Each element of the mix helps solve one of those problems.

| Element | The start-up's problem it helps solve | What goes wrong if it is neglected |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Giving customers a reason to choose an unknown business | A product that does not meet a real need gets no repeat custom |
| Price | Covering costs while attracting first customers | Too high and nobody tries it; too low and every sale loses money or the product looks poor |
| Promotion | Making people aware the business exists | A good product nobody has heard of earns no revenue |
| Place | Making it easy to buy | Customers who cannot find or reach the product buy elsewhere |

The word "importance" in the specification invites you to judge. For some businesses one element dominates. A food stall at a busy station depends heavily on place. A new craft gin relies on product and promotion to stand out among many rivals. Saying which element matters most for the business in the source, and why, is a sign of strong application.

## Balancing the mix: one decision affects the others

"Balancing" means making the four elements consistent with each other and with what customers expect. A change in one element usually needs a change in another.

Example: Orchid Tea Rooms decides to raise its afternoon-tea price from £16 to £22. To keep the mix balanced it must also improve the product (better china, more choice), adjust promotion (stress the experience, not value) and perhaps place (accept bookings online for special occasions). A higher price with an unchanged product and "cheap and cheerful" promotion sends mixed messages.

## Balancing the mix based on the competitive environment

The competitive environment is the number and strength of rivals and what they offer. A start-up looks at the gaps rivals leave.

| Competitive situation | Typical way to balance the mix |
|---|---|
| Many similar rivals | Make the product different in some way customers value; promotion stresses that difference; price kept close to rivals' unless the difference justifies more |
| A dominant rival with low prices | Avoid a price war the start-up cannot afford; compete on product, service or convenience (place) |
| Few or no local rivals | More freedom on price, but promotion must build awareness from nothing |
| Rivals all sell in shops | Place can be the difference: delivery, online ordering or opening at different times |

### Worked example: Northway Smoothies

Northway Smoothies is a start-up stall in a shopping centre. A large café chain nearby sells smoothies at £3.50.

- If Northway also charged £3.50 for a similar drink, customers would have little reason to switch from a name they know.
- Instead it uses whole fruit, blended to order (product), charges £4.20 (price), sells from a stall by the main entrance (place), and offers a loyalty card with a free tenth drink (promotion).

Check the effect of the loyalty card on revenue. Out of every 10 drinks a loyal customer takes, 9 are paid for: 9 × £4.20 = £37.80. The average revenue per drink is £37.80 ÷ 10 = £3.78, still above the chain's £3.50. The promotion rewards loyalty without undercutting the product's quality message.

## The impact of changing consumer needs

Consumer needs change with tastes, lifestyles, incomes and concerns. When they do, the mix must change, often in more than one element.

| Change in needs | Possible change to the mix |
|---|---|
| More customers want healthier food | Product: lower-sugar options; promotion: highlight ingredients |
| Customers want less packaging waste | Product: refillable containers; price: discount for bringing a container |
| Customers have less time to shop | Place: delivery or click-and-collect; promotion: stress convenience |
| Customers have less to spend | Price: smaller, cheaper pack sizes; product: a basic range |

A business that does not respond risks losing customers to rivals that do. A business that responds too quickly to a short-lived trend risks spending on changes customers soon stop wanting. Weighing those two risks is good evaluation.

## The impact of technology: e-commerce and digital communication

The specification names two technologies here.

**E-commerce** (buying and selling online) affects:

- **Place:** a start-up can sell nationally from one site, or with no shop at all.
- **Price:** customers compare prices in seconds, so prices must be competitive or clearly justified; online sellers may have lower costs, allowing lower prices.
- **Product:** a wider range can be shown online without holding it all in a shop; some products can be personalised when ordered.

**Digital communication** (email, social media, websites, messaging) affects:

- **Promotion:** low-cost ways to reach a target group; the business can see clicks and responses and change messages quickly.
- **Product:** customer reviews and comments tell the business what to improve.
- The risk: rivals use the same channels, and negative reviews spread fast.

### Worked example: is paid social media worth it?

Ivy Lane Candles spends £300 on a month of social media adverts. 2,500 people click through and 2% buy, each spending £24 on average.

1. Customers = 2% × 2,500 = 50
2. Revenue = 50 × £24 = £1,200
3. Revenue per £1 of advertising = £1,200 ÷ £300 = £4

Revenue is four times the advertising cost, but that does not mean profit. Suppose each buyer takes two candles priced at £12 (£24), and each candle costs £10 to make.

4. Candles sold = 50 × 2 = 100, so variable costs = 100 × £10 = £1,000
5. Revenue minus variable costs = £1,200 − £1,000 = £200
6. After paying for the adverts: £200 − £300 = a loss of £100

The adverts build awareness, but they pay for themselves only if some of the 50 customers buy again. A sharp answer always asks what a promotion costs as well as what it brings.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Analyse style):** Analyse the impact of e-commerce on the marketing mix of Kip and Co, a start-up selling handmade pet beds from a market stall. [6]

> Selling online would change Kip and Co's place, because customers across the country could order, not just people visiting the market *(technology to place, in context)*. This could increase sales well beyond the stall's weekly trade *(consequence)*. However, online customers can compare prices with mass-produced pet beds quickly *(technology to price)*, so Kip and Co may need to justify its higher price by promoting the handmade quality with photos and reviews *(link to promotion)*. The beds are bulky, so delivery costs could be high *(product feature)*, which might mean adding a delivery charge, making the total price less competitive *(chain back to price)*.

Each point links a technology to an element of the mix, uses a detail of the business and follows the effect through.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the four Ps one at a time without showing how they affect each other.
- Assuming the cheapest price is always the best response to competition.
- Treating e-commerce as promotion. E-commerce is mainly about place (and price); digital communication is mainly about promotion.
- Saying the mix "must change" when consumer needs change, without saying which element and how.
- Ignoring the cost of a change to the mix.

## Exam technique

- **Outline:** one element plus its effect on the named business.
- **Analyse:** chains that connect elements: "this means... which leads to...".
- **Justify:** compare two possible changes, for example cutting price or spending more on promotion, using the data in the source.
- **Evaluate:** decide which element matters most, or whether a change is worth it, and say what the decision depends on.
