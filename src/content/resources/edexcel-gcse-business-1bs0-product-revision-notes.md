These notes condense content item 2.2.1 Product from Topic 2.2 Making marketing decisions in Theme 2 of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. Worked examples and full explanations are in the [product study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-product/); exam-style questions are in the [product practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-product-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented.

## The specification in one view

- **The design mix:** function, aesthetics, cost.
- **The product life cycle:** the phases; extension strategies.
- **Differentiation:** the importance to a business of differentiating a product or service.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Design mix | The balance of function, aesthetics and cost in a product's design |
| Function | How well the product does its job: performance, reliability, ease of use |
| Aesthetics | How the product looks, feels, sounds, smells or tastes |
| Cost (design mix) | What the product costs to make, which limits the price and profit |
| Product life cycle | The phases a product's sales pass through over time |
| Extension strategy | An action to lengthen a product's life, usually in late maturity or decline |
| Differentiation | Making a product distinct from rivals in a way customers value |
| Unique selling point (USP) | A feature that makes a product stand out (standard term) |

## Design mix: which element leads?

- **Function first:** tools, safety equipment, medical aids.
- **Aesthetics first:** fashion, jewellery, gifts, luxury goods.
- **Cost first:** basic own-label goods, disposable items.
- **Balance of all three:** most consumer electronics and cars.

Changing one element moves the others. Better materials improve function and aesthetics but raise unit cost, so either the price rises or profit per unit falls.

**Quick calculation.** Ledbury Lamps adds a brass finish costing £4 a lamp and raises the price by £10. Contribution per lamp rises by £10 − £4 = **£6**, provided sales do not fall.

## The four phases

| Phase | Sales | Profit | Marketing focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Low, slowly rising | Usually a loss (launch costs) | Awareness, trial |
| Growth | Rising fast | Becomes positive and rises | Wider distribution, loyalty |
| Maturity | Peak, levelling off | Usually highest | Defend share, remind, extend |
| Decline | Falling | Falling | Extend or withdraw |

Remember: profit lags behind sales, and the length of each phase varies by product.

**Spotting the phase from data.** Calculate percentage change year on year: large rises suggest growth, changes near zero suggest maturity, steady falls suggest decline. A product selling 12,000, 12,300 and 12,100 units in three years is in maturity: the changes are +2.5% and about −1.6%.

## Extension strategies

| Method | Example |
|---|---|
| Change the product | A new flavour, size or feature |
| New market | A new age group or a new country |
| New use | Promote a different purpose for the same product |
| New packaging or promotion | A refreshed design or advertising message |
| Price or place change | A price cut, or selling online or in new shops |

**Test for any extension strategy:** extra contribution = extra units × contribution per unit. Compare it with the cost of the strategy, and ask how long the effect will last.

## Why differentiation matters

1. It gives customers a reason to choose this product in a crowded market.
2. It allows a higher price, because customers value the difference.
3. It builds loyalty, so customers are less likely to switch.
4. It helps defend market share in maturity, when competition is fiercest.

**Limits:** it costs money, rivals copy, and it only works if customers value the difference.

**Applied example.** A dog-walking firm that sends owners a photo and route map after every walk is differentiated by service. Busy owners who value that reassurance may pay more and stay loyal, even when a cheaper walker starts up nearby.

## Exam traps

- Naming design mix elements with no link to the product.
- Claiming profit is highest in growth.
- Mistaking a seasonal dip for decline.
- Recommending an extension strategy without comparing extra contribution with its cost.
- Saying differentiation allows higher prices without explaining why customers accept them.

## Self-test

1. Name the three elements of the design mix.
2. Which element of the design mix is likely to matter most for a budget pack of paper towels?
3. In which phase of the product life cycle is profit usually highest?
4. Why is profit often negative in the introduction phase?
5. Give two extension strategies.
6. Define differentiation.
7. Sales of a game were 8,000, 8,200 and 8,100 units in three years. Which phase is it in?
8. A new feature adds £2.50 to unit cost and allows a price rise of £6. What is the change in contribution per unit?
9. An extension strategy costs £15,000 and adds 5,000 units, each with a £2.20 contribution. Calculate the net gain.
10. Give one reason differentiation is important to a business.
11. Give one limit of differentiation.
12. Why might a business not use an extension strategy for a product in decline?

### Answers

1. Function, aesthetics, cost.
2. Cost, because buyers compare prices and expect a basic product that simply works.
3. Maturity.
4. Sales are still low while development and launch promotion costs are high.
5. Any two: change the product; new market; new use; new packaging or promotion; price or place change.
6. Making a product or service distinct from rivals' in a way customers value.
7. Maturity: sales are almost flat (+2.5%, then about −1.2%).
8. £6 − £2.50 = **£3.50** more per unit.
9. 5,000 × £2.20 = £11,000; £11,000 − £15,000 = **−£4,000**, a net loss.
10. It gives customers a reason to choose the product, allows a higher price, or builds loyalty.
11. It costs money, rivals can copy it, or customers may not value the difference.
12. The extra contribution may not cover the cost, or the business may prefer to spend the money on a new product that is replacing it.
