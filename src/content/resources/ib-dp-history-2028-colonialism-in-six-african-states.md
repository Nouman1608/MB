**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 8, "Colonialism in Côte d'Ivoire, Kenya, Mozambique, Nigeria, Senegal and Uganda (1890 to 1980)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies. The guide states that every examination question will require you to refer to **one of the prescribed countries**, and recommends teaching a variety so that all inquiry topics are covered. The six inquiry topics are: methods used to establish colonial authority; methods used to maintain power; the impact of colonial economic policies; social and cultural continuity and change; the impact of colonial rule on different social groups; and the effectiveness of Indigenous resistance.

## The six countries at a glance

| Country | Colonial power | Main model | Key features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Côte d'Ivoire | France | Direct rule, assimilation in theory | Cocoa and coffee; forced labour; Houphouët-Boigny |
| Kenya | Britain | Settler colony | White Highlands; Kikuyu land loss; Mau Mau |
| Mozambique | Portugal | Direct rule, concession companies | Forced labour (*chibalo*); migrant labour to South Africa; liberation war |
| Nigeria | Britain | Indirect rule | Lugard; Native Authorities; Northern and Southern differences |
| Senegal | France | Assimilation | Four Communes; groundnuts; Dakar; Senghor |
| Uganda | Britain | Indirect rule through kingdoms | Buganda; smallholder cotton; Baganda agents |

## 1. Methods used to establish and maintain colonial political authority

- **Assimilation.** France's policy in Senegal gave residents of the Four Communes (Saint-Louis, Gorée, Dakar and Rufisque) some citizenship rights. Blaise Diagne was elected as the first African deputy in the French parliament in 1914. In practice few people were granted citizenship, and most were "subjects" under a separate legal code (the *indigénat*). Portugal's *assimilado* status in Mozambique was open to a very small minority who met standards of education and culture.
- **Direct rule.** In Côte d'Ivoire, French administrators and appointed chiefs worked under a governor within the federation of French West Africa. In Mozambique, large chartered companies ruled parts of the north and centre until the 1930s, then the state took over.
- **Indirect rule.** Frederick Lugard developed the system in Nigeria. The Northern and Southern Protectorates were joined in 1914, and emirs and chiefs were kept as Native Authorities under British officers. In the east, where no chiefs of the northern type existed, Britain appointed "warrant chiefs", which caused resentment. In Uganda the Buganda Agreement of 1900 gave the Kabaka and his chiefs a privileged position, and Baganda administrators were used elsewhere.
- **Settler colonies.** Kenya is the clearest case. Europeans took large areas of the highlands for farms, and Africans were moved to reserves. Settlers demanded political influence and cheap labour.

**Argument to weigh:** "Indirect rule was cheap because it used African institutions." Support: few British officers governed millions. Challenge: it also changed those institutions, creating chiefs with new powers, and left some groups feeling excluded.

## 2. Methods used to maintain power

- **African involvement in colonial administration.** Chiefs, clerks, interpreters, police and soldiers (such as the French *tirailleurs* and the King's African Rifles) carried out much of the work.
- **Legal methods.** Poll and hut taxes, pass systems (the *kipande* in Kenya), native courts and the *indigénat*, which allowed French officials to punish subjects without trial.
- **Internal security.** Colonial police and armies, and in Mozambique the secret police (PIDE) in the 1950s and 1960s.
- **Coercion and violence.** Forced labour in French and Portuguese territories (*corvée* and *chibalo*) until reforms after 1946 in French territories and reforms in the early 1960s in Mozambique. In Kenya, the Emergency (1952 to 1960) included mass detention and abuse, and the killing of eleven detainees at Hola camp in 1959 led to criticism in Britain.

## 3. Impact of colonial economic policies

- **Land and agriculture.** Cash crops: groundnuts in Senegal (the Mouride brotherhood organised much production), cocoa and coffee in Côte d'Ivoire (grown by African planters, but with labour recruited by force for European plantations), cotton in Uganda (smallholders) and in Mozambique (compulsory cotton under concession companies), palm oil, cocoa, groundnuts and tin in Nigeria, and coffee, tea and sisal on Kenyan settler farms. Land alienation was most severe in Kenya.
- **Voluntary and forced involvement.** Taxes made cash essential. Many people chose wage work or cash crops, while others were forced. Migrant labour took men from southern Mozambique to the South African mines under agreements with the Portuguese authorities.
- **Technology and infrastructure.** Railways, such as the Uganda Railway to Lake Victoria (1901), the Lagos to Kano line (1911) and the Dakar-Niger line, linked interiors to ports. Roads and ports served export trade more than local needs.
- **Trade.** Economies became tied to world prices. The Depression of the 1930s hurt cash-crop farmers, and war demand after 1939 increased exports and controls.

## 4. Social and cultural continuity and change

- **Religion.** Mission churches grew through schools and clinics. Africanist or independent churches also arose, such as the Harrist movement in Côte d'Ivoire led by William Wadé Harris from about 1913 and the Aladura churches in Nigeria. In Kenya there were Kikuyu independent schools and churches in the 1920s and 1930s. Islam continued to spread in Senegal and northern Nigeria.
- **Social structures.** Urbanisation in Dakar, Abidjan, Lagos, Nairobi, Kampala and Lourenço Marques (now Maputo), labour migration, and changes in family roles.
- **Indigenous and national identity.** *Négritude*, associated with Léopold Senghor, expressed pride in African culture. Ethnic identities were sometimes strengthened by colonial categories, as with Baganda in Uganda and the regional divisions of Nigeria. Nationalist identity grew through newspapers, schools such as Makerere and political parties.
- **Attitudes.** Colonisers often saw their rule as a civilising mission and treated Africans as inferior, though attitudes differed between countries and officials. Africans responded with adaptation, criticism and rejection.

## 5. Impact of colonial rule on different social groups

- **Colonists.** In Kenya settlers had political and economic privileges. In Mozambique, Portuguese settlement increased after the Second World War. In Côte d'Ivoire French planters held large estates until the 1940s.
- **Indigenous elites.** Mission-educated clerks, chiefs, traders and professionals gained status, and some became nationalist leaders (Houphouët-Boigny, Senghor, Azikiwe, Kenyatta). Others, such as Baganda chiefs, benefited from collaboration.
- **Women.** Taxes and male labour migration increased women's work in farming. Women lost rights where colonial law favoured male household heads. Women took action: the Aba Women's War of 1929 in south-eastern Nigeria opposed taxation and warrant chiefs, and women gave support to Mau Mau.
- **Different ethnic groups.** Colonial policy favoured some groups, such as the Baganda, the northern emirates or the Kikuyu's loss of land compared with others, which affected later politics.

## 6. Effectiveness of Indigenous resistance to colonial rule

- **Political and legal actions.** Parties and associations pressed for rights: the NCNC in Nigeria (1944), the RDA in Côte d'Ivoire (1946), the Kenya African Union (1944), and trade unions. Lawsuits and petitions, and the French reforms of 1946 which ended forced labour and widened representation, show some success.
- **Cultural resistance.** Independent churches, schools, newspapers and the Négritude movement.
- **Day-to-day resistance.** Tax evasion, flight across borders, slow work and avoidance of crops or labour.
- **Armed rebellion.** The Mau Mau rising in Kenya (1952 to 1960) was crushed militarily but led to reform and independence in 1963. In Mozambique, FRELIMO (founded 1962 under Eduardo Mondlane) began an armed struggle in 1964. It ended when the Portuguese regime fell in 1974, and independence followed in 1975.

**Argument to weigh:** "Armed rebellion was the most effective form of resistance." Support: Mozambique and the pressure on Britain in Kenya. Challenge: peaceful political action secured independence in Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, Nigeria and Uganda in 1960 to 1962.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "Evaluate the view that colonial economic policies benefited Africans." Use one country (for example Senegal).

1. Define "benefit" by criteria: income, infrastructure, security, long-term development, fairness.
2. For: groundnut income for peasants, the Dakar-Niger railway, growth of Dakar, schools.
3. Against: dependence on one crop, debt, low prices in the Depression, taxes, the profits taken by French firms, and the limits on African industry.
4. Perspectives: older colonial accounts stressed development; nationalist and dependency accounts stressed exploitation; more recent studies stress mixed outcomes and African choices.
5. Judgement: some groups gained, but the structure favoured the colonial power.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all six colonies as the same.
- Describing Mau Mau without analysing effectiveness.
- Using "assimilation" as if it were a reality for most people.
- Forgetting the role of African agents of colonial rule.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many countries should I study?** The guide says each question refers to one country and recommends a variety. Choose two or three and know them deeply.

**Is Portuguese rule covered?** Yes, in the case of Mozambique.
