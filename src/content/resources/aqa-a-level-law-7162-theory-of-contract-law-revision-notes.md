Condensed notes for the section 3.4 row **Theory of contract law**: "analysis and evaluation of the voluntary nature of a contract and of principles governing contract law", with an outline of five issues. For explanation and evaluation, use the [theory of contract law study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-theory-of-contract-law/).

## The five issues in one line each

1. **Freedom of contract v consumer protection:** autonomy and certainty against inequality of bargaining power.
2. **Offers, invitations to treat and the postal rule:** who controls conclusion of the contract; who bears the risk of communication failure.
3. **Consideration, privity and duress:** consideration marks a bargain; privity follows from it; duress now polices renegotiation.
4. **Exemption clauses:** risk allocation, controlled by incorporation, construction and statute.
5. **Remedies:** damages, equitable remedies and simple statutory consumer remedies.

## Voluntary nature

- Contract obligations are chosen; tort obligations are imposed.
- **Freedom of contract** and **sanctity of contract**: Jessel MR, *Printing and Numerical Registering Co v Sampson* (1875), "utmost liberty of contracting".

## Issue 1

| For freedom | Against / protection |
|---|---|
| Autonomy | Unequal bargaining power |
| Certainty for business | Standard terms not negotiated or read |
| Parties allocate and price risk: *Photo Production v Securicor* (1980) | Atiyah, *The Rise and Fall of Freedom of Contract* (1979) |

**Protection:** CRA 2015 ss9–11, 31 (implied terms not excludable), s62 (fairness), s65 (death or personal injury); UCTA 1977 reasonableness for business contracts.

## Issue 2

**Invitation to treat:** leaves seller free to decide; avoids over-commitment of stock (*Boots*, 1953; *Partridge v Crittenden*, 1968).
**Unilateral offer:** *Carlill* (1893): bank deposit showed intention; accepted by performance.

**Postal rule:** acceptance effective on posting (*Adams v Lindsell*, 1818), even if lost (*Household Fire Insurance v Grant*, 1879).

| Rationale | Limit |
|---|---|
| Offeror chose the post, so bears risk | Excluded by "notice in writing": *Holwell Securities v Hughes* (1974) |
| Certainty for the offeree | Instant methods: receipt rule (*Entores*, 1955) |
| Offeree can act at once | *Brinkibon* (1983): no universal rule; intention and risk |

**Electronic communications:** sender can usually check delivery; most commentators favour a receipt rule; no binding appellate decision on email acceptance.

## Issue 3

- **Rationale:** consideration distinguishes bargains from gifts; "the price for which the promise of the other is bought" (*Dunlop v Selfridge*, 1915); evidence of intention.
- **Sufficient, not adequate:** *Chappell v Nestlé* (1960): courts do not judge the bargain.
- **Privity:** *Tweddle v Atkinson* (1861); *Dunlop v Selfridge* (1915); injustice in *Beswick v Beswick* (1968); reform by Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999.
- **Duress:** *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) v *Williams v Roffey* (1991): practical benefit is consideration if no duress or fraud; *Atlas Express v Kafco* (1989): duress and no consideration.
- **Tension:** *Foakes v Beer* (1884) still governs part payment of debts.

## Issue 4

- **Nature:** clauses that exclude or limit liability; allocate risk.
- **Controls:** incorporation (*Olley*, 1949; *Thornton*, 1971: unusual clauses need clearer notice); construction (ambiguity against the party relying); statute (UCTA ss2–3; CRA ss31, 57, 65).
- **Effectiveness:** strong control of death or personal injury exclusions; business clauses often upheld if reasonable; consumers may not know their rights.

## Issue 5

| Remedy | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Damages | Standard, predictable | Limits: remoteness (*Hadley v Baxendale*, 1854), mitigation, no general distress damages (*Addis*, 1909) |
| Enjoyment contracts | *Jarvis v Swans Tours* (1973) recognises disappointment | Limited category |
| Cost of cure v loss of amenity | *Ruxley v Forsyth* (1996) avoids waste | Claimant may not get what was promised |
| Equitable remedies | Flexible | Discretionary, less certain |
| CRA consumer remedies | Simple, usable without court: ss20, 23, 24, 55, 56 | Depend on awareness and trader compliance |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Freedom of contract | Liberty to choose whether, with whom and on what terms to contract |
| Sanctity of contract | Agreements freely made should be enforced |
| Inequality of bargaining power | One party can dictate terms to the other |
| Postal rule | Acceptance by post effective when posted |
| Privity | Only parties to a contract can enforce it |
| Practical benefit | A real advantage that can count as consideration (*Williams v Roffey*) |
| Economic duress | Illegitimate commercial pressure making a contract voidable |
| Exemption clause | A term excluding or limiting liability |

## Exam traps

- Give the reason for a rule, not just the rule.
- Do not claim the postal rule definitely applies to email.
- Link *Williams v Roffey* to duress: the specification names this relationship.
- In evaluation, distinguish business contracts (freedom) from consumer contracts (protection).
- End with a judgement.

## Self-test

1. Who described the "utmost liberty of contracting"? *Jessel MR in Printing and Numerical Registering Co v Sampson (1875).*
2. Give one argument against freedom of contract. *Inequality of bargaining power, especially for consumers on standard terms.*
3. Why are shop displays invitations to treat? *To leave the seller free to decide whether to sell, avoiding over-commitment.*
4. What showed intention to be bound in *Carlill*? *The statement that money had been deposited with a bank.*
5. Give one rationale for the postal rule. *The offeror chose the post, so should bear the risk; or certainty for the offeree.*
6. How can an offeror exclude the postal rule? *By requiring actual receipt, e.g. "notice in writing" (Holwell v Hughes).*
7. What did *Brinkibon* say about instantaneous communications? *No universal rule; depends on intention, business practice and where risk should lie.*
8. How is consideration linked to privity? *Only someone who gave consideration can sue on the promise.*
9. What did the 1999 Act change? *A third party can enforce a term that expressly allows it or purports to benefit them.*
10. How does *Williams v Roffey* link consideration and duress? *Practical benefit is consideration only if the promise was not obtained by duress or fraud.*
11. Which provision prevents excluding liability for death or personal injury against a consumer? *s65 Consumer Rights Act 2015.*
12. Why are CRA consumer remedies said to be effective? *They are simple and can be used without going to court.*
