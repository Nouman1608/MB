This guide teaches section **3.3.6.1 Identification of functional groups by test-tube reactions** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level, and A-level Paper 3 can test it, including in the practical context of Required practical 6. The [Organic analysis study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-organic-analysis/) covers all of section 3.3.6. This page goes deeper on the test-tube work: the exact reagent, condition and observation for each test, what the chemistry is behind each result, how to avoid false conclusions, and how to plan a sequence of tests to identify an unknown. Recall is in the [Functional group tests revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-functional-group-test-tube-reactions-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Functional group tests practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-functional-group-test-tube-reactions-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| The reactions of functional groups listed in the specification | Identify the functional groups using reactions in the specification |
| Required practical 6: tests for alcohol, aldehyde, alkene and carboxylic acid | Carry out and interpret these tests safely |

The test-tube reactions come from across the specification. The list that follows collects the ones you need.

## The tests at a glance

| Functional group | Reagent | Conditions | Positive result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkene (C=C) | Bromine water | Shake at room temperature | Orange to colourless |
| Primary or secondary alcohol | Acidified potassium dichromate(VI) | Warm | Orange to green |
| Aldehyde | Tollens' reagent | Warm in a water bath | Silver mirror |
| Aldehyde | Fehling's solution | Warm in a water bath | Blue solution to red precipitate |
| Carboxylic acid | Sodium carbonate or hydrogencarbonate solution | Room temperature | Effervescence; CO₂ turns limewater milky |
| Halogenoalkane | Aqueous sodium hydroxide, then nitric acid, then silver nitrate | Warm, then acidify | White, cream or yellow precipitate |

## Alkenes: bromine water

```
CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br
```

The alkene adds bromine across the double bond (electrophilic addition), so the orange colour of the bromine disappears. The solution becomes **colourless**, not "clear" (a coloured solution can be clear). Alkanes do not react under these conditions, so the water stays orange.

## Alcohols: acidified potassium dichromate(VI)

Primary and secondary alcohols are oxidised by acidified dichromate. The orange dichromate(VI) ion is reduced to green chromium(III).

```
Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O
```

A **tertiary** alcohol is not easily oxidised, so the solution stays orange. This test therefore separates tertiary alcohols from the others. It does not distinguish primary from secondary alcohols, and aldehydes also give a green colour. For this reason, use it alongside the aldehyde tests.

## Aldehydes: Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution

Both reagents are mild oxidising agents. An aldehyde is easily oxidised to a carboxylic acid; a ketone is not.

**Tollens' reagent** contains the diamminesilver(I) complex ion, [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, in alkaline solution. It is made by adding sodium hydroxide to silver nitrate to give a brown precipitate, then adding dilute ammonia until the precipitate just redissolves.

```
Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag                       (silver(I) is reduced)
RCHO + [O] → RCOOH                  (aldehyde is oxidised)
```

The silver forms a **silver mirror** on the clean inner surface of the test tube. Warm the tube in a water bath rather than heating it directly with a flame.

**Fehling's solution** contains copper(II) ions complexed in alkaline solution and is blue. The aldehyde reduces Cu²⁺ to copper(I) oxide, a **red precipitate**. A ketone leaves the solution **blue**.

| Compound | Tollens' reagent | Fehling's solution |
|---|---|---|
| Aldehyde | Silver mirror | Blue to red precipitate |
| Ketone | No change | Stays blue |

## Carboxylic acids: carbonate test

Carboxylic acids are strong enough acids to liberate carbon dioxide from carbonates.

```
2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
```

Add the unknown to sodium carbonate solution (or solid). **Effervescence** shows a gas. To prove it is carbon dioxide, bubble it through limewater, which turns milky. Alcohols and esters do not react, so there is no fizzing with them.

## Halogenoalkanes: hydrolysis then silver nitrate

Halogenoalkanes are covalent, so they do not give a precipitate with silver nitrate directly. First release the halide ion:

1. Warm the halogenoalkane with aqueous sodium hydroxide, giving the alcohol and a halide ion.
2. **Acidify with dilute nitric acid**. This removes excess hydroxide, which would otherwise form a brown precipitate of silver oxide and hide the result.
3. Add aqueous silver nitrate.

| Halide | Precipitate |
|---|---|
| Chloride | White, AgCl |
| Bromide | Cream, AgBr |
| Iodide | Yellow, AgI |

Do not use hydrochloric or sulfuric acid for the acidification, since chloride or sulfate ions would also give precipitates with silver ions.

## Other observations in the specification

| Reaction | Observation |
|---|---|
| Ethanoyl chloride with water | Steamy white fumes of hydrogen chloride; a vigorous reaction |
| Ethanoyl chloride with ammonia | White solid and fumes |
| Ethanoic anhydride with water | Slower reaction than the acyl chloride |

These are covered in the Acylation sub-topic. They help to identify an acyl chloride, since few other organic compounds react so vigorously with water at room temperature.

## Planning a sequence of tests

A good identification plan follows these steps.

1. Use a test that gives a clear result for the most distinctive group, such as the carbonate test.
2. Choose tests that do not interfere with each other. Use separate samples for each test.
3. Record an observation for **both** possibilities. Examiners award marks for "no change" as well as for the positive result.
4. State the conclusion in terms of the functional group, then name a compound if the question gives enough data.

## Worked example 1: four unlabelled liquids

Four liquids are known to be hex-1-ene, hexan-1-ol, hexanal and hexanoic acid. Each is tested with the reagents below.

| Test | Hex-1-ene | Hexan-1-ol | Hexanal | Hexanoic acid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium carbonate solution | No fizz | No fizz | No fizz | **Fizzes** |
| Bromine water | **Colourless** | Orange | Orange | Orange |
| Warm with Tollens' reagent | No change | No change | **Silver mirror** | No change |
| Warm with acidified dichromate | No change | **Green** | Green | No change |

The carbonate test picks out hexanoic acid. Bromine water picks out hex-1-ene. Tollens' reagent picks out hexanal. The remaining liquid, which gives green with dichromate but no mirror, is hexan-1-ol.

## Worked example 2: a formula with several possibilities

A compound has formula C₃H₆O. It gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent. Identify the compound and rule out another isomer.

A silver mirror shows an aldehyde, so the compound is **propanal**, CH₃CH₂CHO. An isomer, prop-2-en-1-ol (CH₂=CHCH₂OH), contains an alkene and a primary alcohol. It would decolourise bromine water and turn dichromate green, but would not give a silver mirror. Propanone, also C₃H₆O, is a ketone and would give no mirror either. The Tollens' result is therefore enough to choose the aldehyde.

## Worked example 3: amounts in the carbonate test

0.600 g of ethanoic acid (Mᵣ = 60.0) is added to excess sodium carbonate. Calculate the volume of carbon dioxide at room temperature and pressure (24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹).

```
n(CH₃COOH) = 0.600 ÷ 60.0 = 0.0100 mol
2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
n(CO₂)     = 0.0100 ÷ 2 = 0.00500 mol
V(CO₂)     = 0.00500 × 24.0 = 0.120 dm³ = 120 cm³
```

The stoichiometry is 2 : 1, so half as many moles of gas form as moles of acid.

## Worked example 4: silver mass from a Tollens' test

0.0100 mol of propanal reacts completely with excess Tollens' reagent. Each aldehyde molecule is oxidised by two silver(I) ions. Calculate the mass of silver deposited (Aᵣ Ag = 107.9).

```
n(Ag) = 2 × 0.0100 = 0.0200 mol
mass  = 0.0200 × 107.9 = 2.158 g
```

## Safety and good practice

- Use a water bath for warming, because organic liquids are flammable.
- Wear eye protection. Bromine water and dichromate are hazardous, and dichromate(VI) compounds are toxic.
- Use small quantities and dispose of Tollens' reagent immediately after the test, because it can form explosive compounds on standing.
- Test only small samples when smelling the product.

## Common mistakes

- "Clear" for a decolourised bromine water instead of "colourless".
- Saying dichromate turns green with a tertiary alcohol.
- Expecting dichromate alone to tell an aldehyde from a primary alcohol.
- Omitting the "no change" observation for the negative result.
- Heating Tollens' reagent or Fehling's solution directly with a flame.
- Describing Fehling's positive result as a brown or white precipitate.
- Using sulfuric acid or hydrochloric acid to acidify before silver nitrate.
- Giving the result of the carbonate test as "bubbles" with no gas identified.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish X from Y**: name the reagent, the condition, and what you observe with **each**.
- **Identify**: name the functional group first, then the compound.
- **Explain a result**: link the observation to the chemistry, such as oxidation of an aldehyde.
- **Evaluate a test**: say what other compounds would give the same result.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use one test for everything?**
No. Each reagent detects one group, so you combine tests. Use separate samples of the unknown.

**Why does Tollens' reagent need to be freshly made?**
It slowly forms silver compounds that can be explosive when dry, so it is made as needed and disposed of promptly.

**Why does the silver form a mirror?**
Silver deposits slowly as a smooth layer on a very clean glass surface. On a dirty tube you see a grey or black precipitate.
