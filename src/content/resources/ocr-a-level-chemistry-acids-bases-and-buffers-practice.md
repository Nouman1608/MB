Here you practise section 5.1.3, Acids, bases and buffers, of the OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432) specification, Version 3.1 (May 2026), for first assessment in 2017. The questions test every learning outcome, 5.1.3(a) to (o), from Module 5, Physical chemistry and transition elements. H432 is the full A level. Questions 11 and 12 are longer problems that combine several outcomes.

Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-acids-bases-and-buffers/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-acids-bases-and-buffers-revision-notes/). Course links are in Next steps at the end.

Use Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K, the temperature of all solutions unless stated.

## Questions

**1.** This equilibrium is set up in solution:
H₂PO₄⁻ + CO₃²⁻ ⇌ HPO₄²⁻ + HCO₃⁻

**(a)** State what is meant by a Brønsted–Lowry acid. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the two conjugate acid–base pairs, labelling the acid in each pair. **[2]**

**2.** State the basicity of each acid.

**(a)** H₃PO₄ **[1]**
**(b)** ethanedioic acid, HOOC–COOH **[1]**
**(c)** methanoic acid, HCOOH **[1]**

**3.** Write an ionic equation, with state symbols, for the reaction of dilute sulfuric acid with:

**(a)** zinc metal **[1]**
**(b)** aqueous sodium carbonate **[1]**
**(c)** solid magnesium oxide **[1]**
**(d)** aqueous potassium hydroxide **[1]**

**4.** 25.0 cm³ of 0.180 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is diluted with water to 250 cm³. Calculate the pH of the diluted solution. **[3]**

**5.** **(a)** Calculate the pH of 0.0125 mol dm⁻³ barium hydroxide, Ba(OH)₂. **[3]**
**(b)** At 50 °C, Kw = 5.48 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶. Calculate the pH of pure water at 50 °C and explain whether it is acidic, neutral or alkaline. **[3]**

**6.** Benzoic acid, C₆H₅COOH, is a weak monobasic acid with Ka = 6.46 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

**(a)** Calculate the pH of 0.0450 mol dm⁻³ benzoic acid. **[3]**
**(b)** State the two approximations you used. **[2]**

**7.** A 0.250 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak monobasic acid HR has pH 2.62. Calculate Ka and pKa for HR. **[4]**

**8.** A weak monobasic acid HW has Ka = 3.3 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³. A solution of HW has concentration 0.0200 mol dm⁻³.

**(a)** Use the usual weak acid approximations to calculate the pH. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of HW that your answer to (a) implies has dissociated. Use it to explain why the approximation is unreliable for this acid. **[3]**
**(c)** State whether the true pH is higher or lower than your answer to (a). **[1]**

**9.** 60.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ propanoic acid (Ka = 1.35 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³) is mixed with 30.0 cm³ of 0.180 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Calculate the pH of the buffer solution formed. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how this buffer minimises the pH change when a small amount of dilute hydrochloric acid is added. **[2]**

**10.** A chemist needs a buffer of pH 4.90. She dissolves sodium butanoate, C₃H₇COONa (M = 110.0 g mol⁻¹), in 500 cm³ of 0.0800 mol dm⁻³ butanoic acid (Ka = 1.48 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³). Ignore any volume change. Calculate the mass of sodium butanoate needed. **[4]**

**11.** 25.0 cm³ of 0.120 mol dm⁻³ butanoic acid (Ka = 1.48 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³) is titrated with 0.150 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, and the pH is recorded throughout.

| Indicator | pH range | Colour in acid | Colour in alkali |
|---|---|---|---|
| methyl orange | 3.1–4.4 | red | yellow |
| bromothymol blue | 6.0–7.6 | yellow | blue |
| phenolphthalein | 8.3–10.0 | colourless | pink |

**(a)** Calculate the volume of NaOH needed to reach the equivalence point. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the pH before any NaOH is added. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the pH after 10.0 cm³ of NaOH has been added. **[2]**
**(d)** Choose the most suitable indicator from the table and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(e)** Treating the indicator as a weak acid, HIn, explain the colour change at the end point. **[2]**
**(f)** The NaOH is replaced by aqueous ammonia of the same concentration. Describe how the curve after the equivalence point changes, and explain why no indicator from the table is then suitable. **[2]**

**12.** Blood plasma contains the carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate buffer. For H₂CO₃, take Ka = 7.9 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³.

**(a)** Calculate [H⁺] in blood plasma at pH 7.40. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the ratio [HCO₃⁻] / [H₂CO₃] at pH 7.40. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how this buffer responds when lactic acid enters the blood during exercise. **[2]**
**(d)** A student measures the pH of a plasma sample with a pH meter. Describe how she should calibrate the meter and take a reliable reading. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A proton (H⁺) donor [1]
**(b)** H₂PO₄⁻ (acid) and HPO₄²⁻ (base) [1]; HCO₃⁻ (acid) and CO₃²⁻ (base) [1]
*Examiner insight:* each pair must differ by exactly one H⁺; pairing H₂PO₄⁻ with CO₃²⁻ (the two reactants) is a common way to lose both marks.

**2. (a)** **tribasic** [1]
**(b)** **dibasic** [1]
**(c)** **monobasic** [1]
*Examiner insight:* basicity counts donatable protons, not all hydrogen atoms; counting the C–H hydrogen in methanoic acid gives the wrong answer.

**3. (a)** Zn(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + H₂(g) [1]
**(b)** CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) [1]
**(c)** MgO(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) [1]
**(d)** H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) [1]
*Examiner insight:* leave out spectator ions (Na⁺, K⁺, SO₄²⁻) and keep the insoluble oxide as MgO(s); an equation that is not balanced for charge will not earn the mark.

**4.** New concentration = 0.180 × 25.0 / 250 = 0.0180 mol dm⁻³ [1]
HCl is a strong acid, so [H⁺] = 0.0180 mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = –log(0.0180) = **1.74** [1]
*Examiner insight:* dilution is a common trap; a pH from the undiluted 0.180 mol dm⁻³ cannot gain the final mark.

**5. (a)** [OH⁻] = 2 × 0.0125 = 0.0250 mol dm⁻³ [1]
[H⁺] = (1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴) / 0.0250 = 4.00 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **12.40** [1]
**(b)** [H⁺] = √(5.48 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 2.34 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **6.63** [1]
**Neutral**, because [H⁺] = [OH⁻] in pure water [1]
*Examiner insight:* in (b) "acidic because pH is below 7" is wrong; credit goes to linking neutrality to [H⁺] = [OH⁻].

**6. (a)** Ka = [H⁺]² / [C₆H₅COOH] [1]
[H⁺] = √(6.46 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0450) = 1.70 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **2.77** [1]
**(b)** [H⁺] = [C₆H₅COO⁻], because the dissociation of water is negligible [1]
[C₆H₅COOH]equilibrium ≈ 0.0450 mol dm⁻³, because dissociation is so small [1]
*Examiner insight:* "the acid is weak" is not an approximation; say what is assumed equal to what.

**7.** [H⁺] = 10⁻²·⁶² = 2.40 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]
Ka = (2.40 × 10⁻³)² / 0.250 [1]
Ka = **2.30 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³** [1]
pKa = –log(2.30 × 10⁻⁵) = **4.64** [1]
*Examiner insight:* give Ka its units, mol dm⁻³, and give pKa none.

**8. (a)** [H⁺] = √(3.3 × 10⁻³ × 0.0200) = 8.12 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **2.09** [1]
**(b)** Percentage = (8.12 × 10⁻³ / 0.0200) × 100 = **41%** [1]
The approximation assumes [HW]equilibrium ≈ 0.0200 mol dm⁻³, i.e. very little dissociation [1]
But 41% has dissociated, so [HW]equilibrium is much less than 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ and the assumption fails [1]
**(c)** **Higher** (the approximation overestimates [H⁺]) [1]
*Examiner insight:* name the specific approximation that fails; a general comment that "approximations are inaccurate" earns little.

**9. (a)** n(propanoic acid) = 0.0600 × 0.200 = 0.0120 mol; n(OH⁻) = 0.0300 × 0.180 = 0.00540 mol [1]
n(propanoate) = 0.00540 mol; n(acid) left = 0.0120 – 0.00540 = 0.00660 mol [1]
[H⁺] = 1.35 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.00660 / 0.00540 = 1.65 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **4.78** [1]
**(b)** Added H⁺ reacts with the conjugate base: CH₃CH₂COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃CH₂COOH [1]
The equilibrium CH₃CH₂COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃CH₂COO⁻ shifts left, removing most of the added H⁺, so [H⁺] and pH change very little [1]
*Examiner insight:* in (a), subtracting the OH⁻ from the acid is the step most often missed; in (b), name the species that removes H⁺ and state the direction of the shift.

**10.** [H⁺] = 10⁻⁴·⁹⁰ = 1.26 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]
[C₃H₇COO⁻] = Ka × [HA] / [H⁺] = 1.48 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0800 / (1.26 × 10⁻⁵) = 0.0940 mol dm⁻³ [1]
n = 0.0940 × 0.500 = 0.0470 mol [1]
mass = 0.0470 × 110.0 = **5.17 g** [1]
*Examiner insight:* write the rearranged Ka expression before substituting; every later step depends on it.

**11. (a)** n(acid) = 0.0250 × 0.120 = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol; V = 3.00 × 10⁻³ / 0.150 = **20.0 cm³** [1]
**(b)** [H⁺] = √(1.48 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.120) = 1.33 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]
pH = **2.88** [1]
**(c)** 10.0 cm³ is half the equivalence volume, so [HA] = [A⁻] and [H⁺] = Ka [1]
pH = pKa = –log(1.48 × 10⁻⁵) = **4.83** [1]
**(d)** **Phenolphthalein** [1]
Its range, 8.3–10.0, lies within the vertical section for a weak acid–strong base titration (about pH 7–11), where the other two ranges do not [1]
**(e)** HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻; HIn is colourless and In⁻ is pink [1]
Added OH⁻ removes H⁺, so the equilibrium shifts right and the pink In⁻ form appears at the end point [1]
**(f)** After equivalence the pH rises only slowly and levels off lower, so there is no sharp vertical section [1]
An indicator needs a sharp pH change across its range at equivalence, so none is suitable for a weak acid–weak base titration [1]
*Examiner insight:* an indicator choice without a reason linking its range to the vertical section is unlikely to gain the second mark in (d).

**12. (a)** [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷·⁴⁰ = **3.98 × 10⁻⁸ mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(b)** Ka = [H⁺][HCO₃⁻] / [H₂CO₃], so [HCO₃⁻] / [H₂CO₃] = Ka / [H⁺] [1]
= (7.9 × 10⁻⁷) / (3.98 × 10⁻⁸) = **20** (to 2 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** H⁺ from the lactic acid reacts with HCO₃⁻ to form H₂CO₃; H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ shifts left [1]
The H₂CO₃ formed is converted to CO₂ and H₂O and the CO₂ is breathed out, so blood pH stays within 7.35–7.45 [1]
**(d)** Calibrate with buffer solutions of known pH, e.g. pH 4, 7 and 10 [1]
Rinse the electrode with deionised water between solutions and blot dry [1]
Immerse the bulb, stir gently and record the value only when the reading is steady, at constant temperature [1]
*Examiner insight:* in (d), each mark needs a separate practical point; "use the meter carefully" earns nothing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using –log[OH⁻] as the pH of a strong base instead of going through Kw.
- Not doubling [OH⁻] for Ba(OH)₂ and other group 2 hydroxides.
- Stating "the acid is weak" instead of the two specific approximations.
- Describing buffer action without naming the reacting species and the direction of shift.
- Choosing an indicator whose range lies outside the vertical section.
- Giving units for pH or pKa, or leaving them off Ka.
