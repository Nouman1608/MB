This set covers Depth Study 3, "Germany: development of dictatorship, 1918–45", and concentrates on the
parts of the specification that the Depth Studies practice set does not: Stresemann's recovery, the Munich
Putsch and the reorganisation of the party, Nazi policies towards women, the young and the Churches,
racial persecution, the Home Front and opposition during the war. It uses the three question styles of the
Marlbridge Paper 1 materials: what impression an extract gives, explaining two effects, and a "how far do
you agree" essay. Every extract is an **original passage written for this set** in the style of a
historian's interpretation; none is quoted from a published book.

## Section A

**Extract A** (original passage written for this set)

> "Stresemann did not so much cure Germany's illness as find a doctor willing to pay for the medicine.
> The new currency, the settlement over reparations and the handshake at Locarno all bought calm; but
> the calm rested on dollars lent from across the Atlantic, and it would last only as long as the
> lenders' patience."

## Answers

**1.** The author gives the impression that the recovery was **real but borrowed**: the metaphor of a doctor
"willing to pay for the medicine" suggests Germany's problems were eased by others' money rather than
solved, and the list of the currency, reparations and Locarno shows that the author accepts these brought
"calm" [3]. The author also gives the impression that the recovery was **fragile and temporary**, because it
"rested on dollars lent from across the Atlantic" and would last only "as long as the lenders' patience",
pointing ahead to the withdrawal of American loans after 1929 [3]. **[6]**

**2.** The Dawes Plan **made reparations payments manageable**: annual payments were reduced and set to
rise gradually as the economy recovered, and the French and Belgian troops left the Ruhr by 1925, so
industry in Germany's most important industrial region could produce normally again [4]. It also **brought
large American loans** into Germany, which funded new factories, public buildings and services; output
rose and unemployment fell, but the economy became dependent on short-term loans from the USA that could
be recalled at short notice [4]. **[8]**

**3.** The author gives the impression that the Putsch was **a complete failure as a rising**: the word
"fiasco" and the detail that it "collapsed under police fire in a single morning" stress how quickly and
badly it went [3]. However, the author also gives the impression that it was **important for Hitler's
future**, because he gained "a national audience", through the publicity of his trial, and "a lesson he
never forgot", the decision to seek power by legal means rather than force [3]. **[6]**

**4.** The Putsch's failure **led to a change of strategy**: Hitler decided that the party must win power
through elections, so after the party was refounded in 1925 he built a national organisation, with regional
leaders (Gauleiters), a youth movement and the SS, ready to fight elections across Germany [4]. It also
**gave Hitler time and publicity**: his trial in 1924 was reported nationally, and during his
imprisonment at Landsberg he wrote *Mein Kampf*, which set out his ideas and strengthened his position as
the party's unchallenged leader, confirmed at the Bamberg Conference of 1926 [4]. **[8]**

**5.** The author gives the impression that Nazi policies aimed above all to **keep women in the home as
wives and mothers**, describing motherhood as "a German woman's highest service" and noting that "loans,
medals and speeches", such as marriage loans and the Mother's Cross, all promoted it [3]. The author also
gives the impression that the policies were **contradicted by economic needs**, since the factories of the
late 1930s, short of workers because of rearmament, "pulled the other way", drawing women back into paid
work [3]. **[6]**

**6.** Nazi youth policies **brought young people under the regime's influence**: membership of the Hitler
Youth and the League of German Maidens grew rapidly and was made compulsory by law, so many young people
spent their free time on drill, camping and political lessons that taught loyalty to Hitler and Nazi
racial ideas [4]. They also **changed schools**: teachers had to join the Nazi Teachers' League, textbooks
were rewritten, and subjects such as race studies and more physical education were introduced, preparing
boys for military service and girls for motherhood [4]. **[8]**

**7.** Kristallnacht **brought violent, open persecution**: during the night of 9–10 November 1938,
synagogues were burned, Jewish shops and homes were attacked, around 30,000 Jewish men were sent to
concentration camps and many Jews were killed [4]. It also **led to the exclusion of Jews from economic
life**: Jews were made to pay a huge collective fine, Jewish businesses were forced to close or be sold,
and many Jews who could do so tried to leave Germany [4]. **[8]**

**8.** Allied bombing **caused mass death and homelessness**: raids on cities such as Hamburg in 1943 and
Dresden in 1945 created firestorms that killed tens of thousands of people and destroyed huge numbers of
homes, forcing evacuations and adding to shortages [4]. It also **disrupted the war economy and daily
life**: attacks on factories, railways and fuel supplies damaged production and transport, and constant
raids meant nights in shelters, while the regime used the destruction in propaganda to call for greater
sacrifice [4]. **[8]**

**9.** There is a strong case that recovery was genuine: the Rentenmark (November 1923) ended
hyperinflation, the Dawes Plan (1924) eased reparations and brought American loans, industrial output rose,
and abroad the Locarno Treaties (1925), membership of the League of Nations with a permanent seat on its
Council (1926) and the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) restored Germany's status, while support for extremist
parties fell [6]. Against this, the recovery was limited and fragile: it depended on short-term American
loans that were recalled after the Wall Street Crash; farmers struggled with low prices; the middle classes
had not recovered their savings lost in 1923; nationalists attacked Locarno and the Young Plan (1929) as
accepting Versailles; and coalition governments remained unstable [6]. A strong answer reaches a
supported judgement, for example that the recovery was genuine in the short term but not secure, because
it rested on foreign loans and did not remove the Republic's political weaknesses [4]. **[16]**

**10.** There is a strong case for the statement: after von Schleicher's government failed, von Papen
persuaded President von Hindenburg to appoint Hitler chancellor on 30 January 1933, with Papen as
vice-chancellor, believing that with only three Nazis in the cabinet they could control him; Hindenburg,
who had refused to appoint Hitler in 1932, agreed [6]. However, Hitler could only be considered because the
Nazis had become the largest party in the Reichstag, winning 230 seats in July 1932 and 196 in November,
after the Depression drove up unemployment; Nazi propaganda, the SA and Hitler's own campaigning had built
that support, and the use of Article 48 decrees had already weakened democracy [6]. A strong answer weighs
the intrigue of January 1933 against the mass support that made it possible, for example judging that the
actions of Papen and Hindenburg were the immediate cause but not the main one [4]. **[16]**

**11.** There is a case for propaganda: Goebbels's Ministry of Popular Enlightenment and Propaganda
controlled newspapers, radio, film and the arts; cheap radios carried Hitler's speeches into homes; the
Nuremberg rallies and the 1936 Berlin Olympics showed a strong, united Germany; and the Hitler Youth spread
Nazi ideas among the young, so many Germans came to accept or support the regime [6]. On the other hand,
terror was arguably more effective at preventing opposition: the SS under Himmler, the Gestapo, informers
and concentration camps such as Dachau made open resistance very dangerous, while censorship, book burning
and popular policies such as falling unemployment and Strength Through Joy also secured acceptance [6]. A
strong answer reaches a supported judgement, for example that propaganda and terror worked together, with
propaganda winning support from many and terror silencing those it did not persuade [4]. **[16]**

**12.** There is a strong case for the statement: the Edelweiss Pirates and the White Rose Group were small;
White Rose leaders including Hans and Sophie Scholl were executed in February 1943 after distributing
leaflets in Munich; members of an Edelweiss Pirates group were publicly hanged in Cologne in 1944; and the
July Bomb Plot of 1944, led by Colonel von Stauffenberg, failed to kill Hitler and was followed by thousands
of arrests and many executions, so the regime survived until military defeat in 1945 [6]. However,
opposition did achieve something: it showed that not all Germans supported Hitler; the White Rose leaflets
were copied and spread; Edelweiss Pirates helped deserters and escaped prisoners and refused to join the
Hitler Youth; and the July Plot showed that even senior army officers had turned against Hitler [6]. A
strong answer weighs practical results against the courage and symbolic importance of opposition before
reaching a supported judgement on how much was achieved [4]. **[16]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- In the extract questions, describing what you know about the topic instead of explaining the impression
  the author's own words create.
- Explaining only one effect, or naming effects without showing how each followed from the event.
- In the Munich Putsch effects question, describing the events of November 1923 instead of what changed
  afterwards.
- Writing a one-sided essay that ignores the second bullet point or brings in no information of your own.
- Getting the chronology of 1932–33 wrong: the July and November elections, then Schleicher, then Hitler's
  appointment.
