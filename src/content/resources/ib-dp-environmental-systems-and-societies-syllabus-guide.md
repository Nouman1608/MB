This guide covers the full syllabus structure of **IB Diploma Programme
Environmental Systems and Societies (ESS)**, for first assessment 2026,
at both Standard Level (SL) and Higher Level (HL). It complements the
[subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-subject-guide/)
and the [assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-assessment-revision-notes/)
already on the site by setting out what is actually taught -- the
concept-based foundation, the eight numbered syllabus topics, the
HL-only lenses, and the experimental programme.

ESS is built around a **concept-based syllabus**. All students --
SL and HL -- share a foundations unit introducing three unifying
concepts (perspectives, systems and sustainability), one internally
assessed individual investigation, and a collaborative sciences
project. The SL course runs to 100 recommended teaching hours of
syllabus content; the HL course extends this to 190 hours, with the
extra depth concentrated in the later topics and in three HL-only
lenses that do not exist for SL students at all.

## Syllabus coverage

**IB DIPLOMA PROGRAMME ENVIRONMENTAL SYSTEMS AND SOCIETIES -- SYLLABUS CONTENT**

Recommended teaching hours are shown as SL / HL.

- **Topic 1: Foundations** (16 / 35 hours) -- the starting point for
  both levels, built around the three unifying concepts:
  - **1.1 Perspectives** (3 hours) -- differing individual and
    collective worldviews on environmental issues.
  - **1.2 Systems** (5 hours) -- systems theory as a tool for
    understanding environmental structures and processes.
  - **1.3 Sustainability** (8 hours) -- resource management and the
    idea of sustainability as central to the whole course.
- **Topic 2: Ecology** (22 / 26 hours).
- **Topic 3: Biodiversity and conservation** (13 hours, both levels).
- **Topic 4: Water** (12 / 25 hours).
- **Topic 5: Land** (8 / 15 hours).
- **Topic 6: Atmosphere and climate change** (10 / 23 hours).
- **Topic 7: Natural resources** (10 / 18 hours).
- **Topic 8: Human populations and urban systems** (9 / 15 hours).
- **HL lens a: Environmental law** (5 hours, HL only).
- **HL lens b: Environmental and ecological economics** (7 hours, HL only).
- **HL lens c: Environmental ethics** (5 hours, HL only).
- **Experimental programme** (50 hours, both levels): 30 hours of
  practical work (including the collaborative sciences project) plus
  10 hours of scientific investigation, providing the evidence base
  for the individual assessment.

The syllabus's own subtopic detail below Topic 1 is not itemised in
the public subject brief this guide is sourced from -- only topic
names, structure and teaching hours are published there. Topic-level
detail (Topics 2-8 subtopic breakdowns) sits in the IB's internal
subject guide, distributed through the Programme Resource Centre, not
in the public brief, so it is not reproduced here.

## Why Topic 1 gets disproportionate attention

Topic 1's three concepts -- perspectives, systems and sustainability --
are not a one-off introduction: the specification explicitly says they
are "revisited throughout the course," meaning every later topic is
implicitly assessed against how well a student can apply these three
lenses, not just recall content. A student who treats Topic 1 as a
short warm-up unit rather than the interpretive framework for
everything that follows tends to write descriptive rather than
analytical answers on Papers 1 and 2, because they lack the vocabulary
(carrying capacity, ecological footprint, environmental value systems,
positive/negative feedback) that the specification expects to see
threaded through answers on water, land, atmosphere or resources.

## The HL-only lenses

HL students study the same eight core topics as SL students, but with
greater depth and breadth throughout, and then apply three additional
lenses that SL students never encounter:

- **Environmental law** -- how legal frameworks are used to address
  environmental issues, at local, national and international scale.
- **Environmental and ecological economics** -- how economic tools and
  models (including the valuation of ecosystem services) are applied
  to environmental decision-making.
- **Environmental ethics** -- the range of value systems and ethical
  positions that shape how individuals and societies respond to
  environmental issues.

Because these lenses require HL students to reprocess content they
already studied through the core topics -- explaining, for example, a
water-scarcity issue through a legal lens, an economic lens and an
ethical lens in turn -- they are best revised topic-by-topic alongside
the core content they extend, rather than as a separate block at the
end of the course.

## The experimental programme

Fifty hours, shared by SL and HL, split into practical work (30 hours,
including the group-based collaborative sciences project) and
scientific investigation (10 hours). This underpins the internally
assessed individual investigation, in which each student designs and
carries out their own fieldwork-based enquiry, gathers primary data,
and writes an evidence-based report capped at 3,000 words. Because the
collaborative sciences project sits inside the practical-work hours
rather than being separately assessed, its main value to a student's
final grade is the data-handling and investigative skills it builds
for the individual investigation, not marks of its own.

## How to approach it

Start by mapping the eight topics against their teaching hours: topics
with a large HL-hours gap relative to SL (Topic 6, Atmosphere and
climate change, nearly doubles from 10 to 23 hours; Topic 4, Water,
roughly doubles from 12 to 25) signal where HL students should expect
the most additional depth, and therefore where revision time is most
efficiently weighted. Because Topic 1's three concepts recur across
every other topic, revise them first and explicitly practise using
their vocabulary when summarising each later topic, rather than
revising Topic 1 in isolation and moving on. HL students should treat
each of the three lenses as a way of re-reading content they already
know, not as new factual material -- for each core topic, ask "what
would a legal, economic and ethical analysis of this issue look like?"
as a standing revision question. For the individual investigation,
because it is scored against a fixed evidence-based-report structure
capped at 3,000 words, drafting a clear, testable research question
early and checking it against the assessment criteria before
collecting data saves far more marks than trying to rescue an
unfocused question after the fact.
