This guide covers hormones and pheromones in the biological approach of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). The 2027 guide lists **chemical messengers** as a term in the biological approach ("the role of one or more chemical messengers in human behaviour") and again under interpersonal relationships in the human relationships context. Hormones are chemical messengers, so the hormone sections (cortisol and oxytocin) apply directly. The guide does not name pheromones, so the pheromone section is extension material on chemical signals that act between individuals. The [biological approach study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-biological-approach/) introduces the approach and the basic difference between a hormone and a pheromone. This page goes further: how hormones act, two hormone studies you can use as examples, the evidence for and against human pheromones, and how to turn that evidence into a Section A answer or a 15-mark Section C response. The 2019 course, which listed hormones and pheromones as a topic, had its last examination session in November 2026.

## Hormones: chemical messengers in the blood

A **hormone** is a chemical released by an endocrine gland into the bloodstream, which carries it to target cells with matching receptors. Compared with a neurotransmitter, a hormone is slower to act, travels further and has longer-lasting, more widespread effects. That difference matters in an exam answer: a neurotransmitter explanation is about signals across a synapse, while a hormone explanation is about a body-wide state that changes how a person thinks or acts over minutes, hours or days.

Three hormones are useful examples for the IB course:

| Hormone | Released by | Behaviour it is linked to |
|---|---|---|
| Cortisol | adrenal cortex, at the end of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis | the stress response; memory |
| Oxytocin | made in the hypothalamus, released by the posterior pituitary | bonding, trust and social behaviour |
| Testosterone | mainly the testes and ovaries, with a small amount from the adrenal glands | dominance, competition and aggression |

The table only shows a link. For any hormone you write about, state the mechanism (where it is released and what it acts on) and then the specific behaviour, supported by a named study.

## Cortisol and memory: Newcomer et al. (1999)

**Aim.** To find out whether cortisol at levels like those produced by major stress impairs verbal declarative memory.

**Method.** Healthy adult volunteers were randomly allocated to take a high dose of cortisol, a low dose, or a placebo tablet for four days. The study was double-blind. Memory was measured by asking participants to recall a short prose passage, and they were tested before, during and after the treatment period.

**Findings.** The high-dose group recalled less of the passage than the placebo group, while the low-dose group showed no clear impairment. When treatment stopped, memory performance returned to normal.

**Conclusion.** High cortisol levels can impair verbal declarative memory, and the effect appears to be reversible. A likely mechanism is cortisol acting on receptors in the hippocampus, a region involved in forming declarative memories.

**Evaluation.** Because cortisol was manipulated and participants were randomly allocated, the study supports a causal claim, which correlational studies of stressed people cannot. Double-blind procedures reduce expectancy effects. On the other hand, taking cortisol tablets is not the same as experiencing real stress, which involves other hormones and the person's appraisal of events, so ecological validity is limited. The study tested only one kind of memory, and the participants were healthy adults, so generalising to older people or to chronically stressed groups needs other evidence.

## Oxytocin and trust: Kosfeld et al. (2005)

**Aim.** To test whether oxytocin increases trust between people.

**Method.** Male participants were given oxytocin or a placebo through a nasal spray and then played a trust game for real money. As "investors", they chose how much to transfer to an anonymous "trustee"; the amount was multiplied, and the trustee could choose whether to return some of it. A second condition used the same choices but against a computer that returned money at random, so the decision involved risk without trusting a person.

**Findings.** Investors given oxytocin transferred more money than those given a placebo. In the condition against the computer, oxytocin made no difference.

**Conclusion.** Oxytocin appeared to increase willingness to trust another person, not willingness to take risks in general.

**Evaluation.** The control condition is a strength, because it rules out a simple increase in risk-taking. However, the sample was all male, and a nasal spray may not show how oxytocin works when the body releases it naturally. Later reviewers, notably Nave, Camerer and McCullough (2015), concluded that attempts to replicate the effect of oxytocin on trust have been inconsistent, so the original finding should be presented as influential but not settled. De Dreu and colleagues (2011) also found that oxytocin increased favouritism towards the participant's own ethnic in-group, which suggests its effect depends on who the other person is. That makes "the trust hormone" an oversimplified label.

## Pheromones: the human debate

A **pheromone** is a chemical signal released by one member of a species that affects the behaviour or physiology of another member of the same species. The term was introduced by Karlson and Lüscher in 1959. Researchers sometimes distinguish **releaser** pheromones, which trigger an immediate behaviour such as mating, from **primer** pheromones, which cause slower physiological change such as altering reproductive cycles.

The question for the IB course is whether humans have pheromones at all. Evidence that is often used:

- **McClintock (1971)** reported that women living together in a college residence showed increasingly synchronised menstrual cycles. Later studies, including Yang and Schank (2006), did not find synchrony, and the original statistics have been questioned.
- **Stern and McClintock (1998)** collected odourless compounds from the armpits of women at different phases of the cycle and applied them above the upper lip of other women. Depending on the phase of the donor, the recipients' cycles were shortened or lengthened, which looks like primer-pheromone action.
- **Wedekind et al. (1995)** asked men to wear the same T-shirt for two nights; women then rated the smell of the shirts. Women tended to prefer the odour of men whose immune-system genes (the major histocompatibility complex, MHC) were different from their own, a preference that would produce offspring with a wider range of immune genes. The pattern was reversed in women taking oral contraceptives.
- **Hare et al. (2017)** tested two much-publicised candidate chemicals, androstadienone and estratetraenol, and found that they did not change how participants judged the gender or attractiveness of faces.

**Evaluating the evidence.** Wyatt (2015) argued that no human pheromone has yet been chemically identified, partly because candidate molecules were chosen without first showing which chemical in a body odour causes a reliable effect. Body odour preferences, as in Wedekind's study, may also be learned or cultural rather than an innate pheromone response. Human behaviour is strongly shaped by cognition and context, which makes a single chemical's effect hard to isolate; participants' expectations can also produce an effect. A balanced answer says that some findings are consistent with human chemical signalling, but the case for human pheromones remains unproven.

## Worked example: a Section A answer

**Question:** "Explain the role of one chemical messenger in human behaviour, and give an example." **[4]**

1. **Name the messenger and the behaviour** in the first sentence: cortisol and verbal memory.
2. **Explain the mechanism** in two sentences: cortisol is released by the adrenal cortex at the end of the HPA axis during stress, and it binds to receptors in the hippocampus.
3. **Give an example and explain it** (Newcomer et al., 1999): healthy adults given a high dose of cortisol for four days, in a double-blind, placebo-controlled design, recalled less of a prose passage, and recall returned to normal after treatment.
4. **Link back** in a final sentence: the example shows that a hormone produced by stress can change a cognitive behaviour, which is the role the question asks about.

Paper 1 Section A is marked on how detailed your knowledge of the content is and on whether your example is relevant and explained. The details of the study are not assessed, so the example needs only enough detail to make the point. The question does not require evaluation, so time spent criticising the study earns little here. Save evaluation for Section C.

## Turning the topic into a Section C response

Section C (15 marks) offers two concept-based extended-response questions, each from a different context. A question such as "Evaluate the role of chemical messengers in human behaviour, with reference to causality" needs relevant research, links to the concept and a judgement. Newcomer et al. (1999) and Kosfeld et al. (2005) work well together, because both are experiments that support causal claims, yet both raise the same questions: does an administered dose behave like the body's own hormone, and do findings from one sample generalise? A response on pheromones should go further still and weigh evidence that is genuinely contested: present the strongest supporting study, the failures to replicate and Wyatt's argument, and reach a conclusion about how far the evidence goes.

## Common mistakes

- Calling oxytocin "the love hormone" and treating that as an explanation, with no mechanism or study.
- Describing a hormone study as if it showed a hormone *causes* everyday behaviour, when the dose was artificial.
- Using animal pheromone research (such as bombykol in moths) as evidence about humans without saying why it may not generalise.
- Presenting human pheromones as an established fact, or presenting McClintock's synchrony finding without the failures to replicate.
- Confusing a hormone with a pheromone: one acts inside the body that produced it, the other between individuals.

## Exam technique

**Describe** asks for a detailed account of a study or mechanism. **Explain** asks why or how: link the hormone to the behaviour with a cause. **Evaluate** and **discuss** require strengths, limitations and a judgement, so prepare one methodological strength and one limitation for each study. **To what extent** needs a conclusion about degree, which suits the pheromone debate well. Only use study details you are sure of; describing a finding in words is better than inventing a figure.
