This guide goes deeper into **sub-topic 1.6 Urban Settlements**, part of
Theme 1 Population and Settlement in Cambridge O Level Geography (2217),
2026 series (Version 3). For the whole theme in outline, start with the
[Population and Settlement study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-population-and-settlement/).
When you are ready to test yourself, use the
[Urban Settlements revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-urban-settlements-revision-notes/)

## What 1.6 asks you to do

The syllabus sets two learning outcomes for this sub-topic:

1. **Describe and give reasons for the characteristics of, and changes in,
   land use in urban areas.** The further guidance names the land use zones
   (the Central Business District, residential areas, industrial areas and
   the rural–urban fringe) in countries at different levels of economic
   development, and the effect of change in land use and rapid urban growth,
   including **urban sprawl**.
2. **Explain the problems of urban areas, their causes and possible
   solutions.** The named problems are different types of pollution (air,
   noise, water, visual), inequality, housing issues, traffic congestion and
   conflicts over land use change.

A **case study of an urban area or urban areas** is required. The syllabus
allows you to use one city for land use and a different city for urban
problems, so choose the examples you know in most detail.

Sub-topic 1.7 Urbanisation is closely linked but has a different focus: 1.7
is about *why* cities grow so fast and the impact of that growth on rural and
urban areas, while 1.6 is about *how space inside the city is organised* and
the problems of living in it.

## Land use zones and the reasons for their location

Most land use patterns can be explained by two linked ideas: **accessibility**
and **land value**. The centre of a city is usually where transport routes
meet, so it is the most accessible point. Many users compete for this small
area, so land values (and rents) are highest there and fall with distance
from the centre. Users that need customers or workers from the whole city and
can afford high rents locate centrally; users that need a lot of cheap space
locate further out.

| Zone | Typical characteristics | Reasons for its location |
|---|---|---|
| **CBD** | Shops (especially high-order and comparison goods), offices, banks, hotels, entertainment; tall buildings; few residents; busy by day, quiet at night | Most accessible point; highest land values, so only high-profit users can afford it and build upwards |
| **Inner city** (HIC) | Older, high-density housing (often terraced), old factories and warehouses, some derelict or redeveloped land | Built when the city was smaller, close to early industry, railways, rivers or docks, so workers could walk to work |
| **Suburbs** | Lower-density, newer detached and semi-detached housing with gardens; local shopping parades, schools, parks | Cheaper land further out, made reachable by buses, trains and private cars |
| **Industrial areas** | Old industry near the centre, on rivers, canals and railways; modern industrial estates and business parks on the edge | Old industry needed water and rail transport; modern firms want large cheap sites, room to expand and access to ring roads and motorways |
| **Rural–urban fringe** | Mix of urban and rural uses: out-of-town retail parks, airports, sewage works, golf courses, new housing estates, farmland | Large areas of cheaper land, good road access, and away from residential areas for noisy or unpleasant uses |

### Cities at different levels of development

Do not assume every city looks like a high-income city. In many cities in
low- and middle-income countries the pattern is different:

- The **CBD** often combines modern office blocks with older markets or
  bazaars.
- **High-income housing** is frequently close to the CBD or along a main
  road leading out of it, where services, security and short journeys are
  available.
- **Squatter settlements** (informal settlements) grow on the edge of the
  city and on land nobody else wants: steep hillsides, flood plains, beside
  railway lines or next to rubbish dumps.
- **Industry** often lines the main roads and railways leading out of the
  city, or sits beside ports.

So in a high-income city the wealthiest often live furthest from the
centre, whereas in many lower-income cities the poorest live on the
periphery.

## Changes in land use and urban sprawl

Urban land use is never fixed. Common changes include:

- **Deindustrialisation and redevelopment.** Old inner-city factories and
  docks close, leaving derelict **brownfield** sites that are redeveloped for
  offices, flats and leisure. London's former Docklands, redeveloped from the
  1980s with offices at Canary Wharf, is a well-known example.
- **Gentrification.** Wealthier people buy and renovate older inner-city
  housing, which can raise prices and push lower-income residents out.
- **Decentralisation.** Shops and offices move out to retail parks and
  business parks on the fringe, where land is cheaper and parking is easier.
- **Urban sprawl.** The built-up area spreads outwards onto **greenfield**
  land, often in low-density housing and roads.

The syllabus asks specifically about the **effects of urban sprawl**:

| Effects of urban sprawl | Examples |
|---|---|
| On the environment | Loss of farmland, woodland and wildlife habitats; more impermeable surfaces, so faster runoff and flood risk |
| On transport | Longer journeys, more car dependence, more congestion and air pollution |
| On rural settlements | Villages become commuter settlements; house prices rise beyond the reach of local people; village services may close because residents shop in the city |
| On the city | Cost of extending water, sewerage, electricity and roads over a wider area |

Responses include **green belts** (protected rings of open land around a city
where building is restricted), building on brownfield land first, higher-density
housing, and planned **new towns** or satellite cities. Navi Mumbai, planned
from the early 1970s across the harbour from the island city of Mumbai, was
designed to take pressure off the crowded city centre.

## Urban problems, causes and solutions

Learn each problem as a chain: **problem → cause → solution → limitation**.

| Problem | Typical causes | Possible solutions |
|---|---|---|
| **Air pollution** | Vehicle exhausts, factories, burning waste, domestic fuels | Cleaner public transport, emission standards, moving heavy industry out, low-emission zones |
| **Noise pollution** | Traffic, aircraft, construction, factories | Noise barriers beside major roads, flight-path controls, zoning |
| **Water pollution** | Untreated sewage, industrial discharge, rubbish in drains and rivers | Sewage treatment works, fines for polluters, waste collection |
| **Visual pollution** | Litter, graffiti, derelict buildings, advertising hoardings, overhead cables | Redevelopment of derelict sites, planning controls, clean-up schemes |
| **Inequality** | Wealth gaps between areas; poorer areas with weaker schools, health care and jobs | Targeted investment in deprived areas, affordable housing, improved transport links |
| **Housing issues** | Population growth faster than housing supply; high land prices; overcrowding | Affordable and social housing, site-and-service and upgrading schemes, new towns |
| **Traffic congestion** | Rising car ownership, narrow old streets, commuting into the CBD | Mass transit, park-and-ride, bus lanes, congestion charging, ring roads |
| **Conflicts over land use change** | Competing demands for the same land, e.g. housing vs green space, offices vs existing homes | Planning consultation, zoning, compensation, protecting green belts |

## Worked example 1: a resource-based question

*A sketch map shows an industrial area next to a railway and a river close to
the centre of a city, and a newer industrial estate beside a ring road on the
city edge. Suggest reasons for the location of each industrial area.* [4]

**Strong answer.** The older industrial area is close to the river and the
railway because, when it was built, heavy raw materials and finished goods
were moved by water and rail [1], and workers needed to live within walking
distance, so it grew near the early, central part of the city [1]. The newer
estate is on the ring road because modern firms move goods by lorry and need
fast road access [1], and land on the city edge is cheaper and available in
large plots with room to expand and to park [1].

Notice that every reason is tied to something shown on the map. "Suggest"
means apply your knowledge to the resource, not recite a list.

## Worked example 2: planning a case-study answer

*For a named urban area, explain the causes of traffic congestion and describe
attempts to reduce it.* [7]

A good plan for London might read:

- **Causes:** a large commuting workforce travelling into central London; a
  street pattern laid out long before motor vehicles; delivery vehicles,
  taxis and buses competing for road space.
- **Attempts:** the Congestion Charge, introduced in central London in 2003,
  which makes drivers pay to enter the zone during charging hours; an
  extensive Underground, bus and rail network; the Docklands Light Railway
  serving the redeveloped Docklands; cycle lanes.
- **Evaluation:** charging reduces traffic in the zone but critics argue it
  affects small businesses and lower-income drivers; public transport
  investment is expensive and takes years to build.

Add reliable figures from your textbook. Detail **specific to the place**
lifts a case-study answer; a generic answer that fits any city will not.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a land use zone without giving a **reason** for its location.
- Assuming the high-income city model applies everywhere and forgetting
  that the poorest residents often live on the edge of lower-income cities.
- Confusing **urban sprawl** (outward spread of the built-up area) with
  **urbanisation** (a rising proportion of people living in towns and
  cities).
- Listing urban problems with no **cause** and no **solution**.
- Giving a solution with no comment on how well it works.
- A case study with only the city's name and no place-specific detail.

## Exam technique for 1.6

Paper 1 questions are structured: short, resource-based parts come first and
longer free-response parts, including case-study parts, follow. Use the
command words precisely:

- **Identify** or **State**: a short, direct answer; no explanation needed.
- **Describe**: say what is there or what it is like (for example, building
  height, density and land use of a zone). Use evidence from the resource.
- **Explain**: give reasons and make the links clear, often with "because"
  or "so".
- **Suggest**: apply what you know to an unfamiliar place in the resource;
  several answers can be valid.

For land use questions, name the zone, give a characteristic, then give
the reason, linked to accessibility or land value.
