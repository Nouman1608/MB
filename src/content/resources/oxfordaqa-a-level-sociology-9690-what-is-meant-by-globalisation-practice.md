These questions cover content point **3.2.1.2 What is meant by globalisation?** of OxfordAQA International A-level Sociology (9690), Version 1.1, assessed in the Unit 3 People and development exam. All countries, towns and figures in the items are fictional.

Revise first with the [globalisation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-what-is-meant-by-globalisation/) and the [globalisation revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-what-is-meant-by-globalisation-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by time-space compression. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two aspects of political globalisation named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by grobalisation, using one example. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two differences between the sceptical view and the negative globalist view of globalisation. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which the internet contributes to cultural globalisation. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for the fictional country of Kelvara.

| Indicator (fictional) | 2000 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign investment received (US$ million) | 400 | 2,600 |
| Citizens working abroad with a legal work visa (thousands) | 120 | 150 |
| Households with internet access (%) | 6 | 71 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in foreign investment received between 2000 and 2025. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain why the extent of globalisation may differ between flows of capital, people and communication. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> In the fictional city of Marovik, a global coffee chain opened twelve branches in five years. Its Marovik menu includes a spiced drink based on a local recipe. Meanwhile, three traditional tea houses in the old market have reopened, run by young owners who advertise them on social media as "the real Marovik". Local musicians now record songs that mix the city's folk instruments with electronic beats popular worldwide.

**(a)** Identify two ways in which the item shows the local surviving globalisation. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse how the ideas of glocalisation and hybridisation help explain cultural change in Marovik. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which economic globalisation may have benefited some groups while harming others. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three ways in which globalisation has affected religious beliefs. **[6]**

**10.** Assess Appadurai's argument that global culture is made up of uneven flows rather than simple Westernisation. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that political globalisation has spread democracy, human rights and gender equality across the world. **[12]**

**12.** "Globalisation has been greatly exaggerated." To what extent do you agree? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** Faster transport, communication and flows of money [1] mean that distance and time have less effect on social and economic life, so the world seems smaller (Harvey) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "The world getting smaller" alone is a description, not an explanation; name what speeds up.

**2.** Any two: spread of democracy [1]; spread of human rights [1]; spread of gender equality. **[2]**

**3.** Grobalisation is the drive of corporations and states to impose standardised products, practices or ideas [1] on local areas, replacing local variety (Ritzer) [1]. Example: an identical chain store design and menu rolled out unchanged in every country [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The example must show standardisation imposed from the centre; an adapted local menu would be glocalisation.

**4.** Sceptics doubt that globalisation has gone as far as claimed [1], while negative globalists accept that it has gone far [1]. Sceptics stress that nation states remain powerful and trade is concentrated among rich regions [1], while negative globalists focus on the harmful effects, such as rising inequality and corporate power [1]. **[4]**

**5.** The internet lets people access music, films and news from other countries instantly [1], spreading shared popular culture and global brands across borders [1]. It enables communication through social media and messaging between people in different countries [1], supporting McLuhan's idea of a global village and diaspora links [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** (2,600 - 400) / 400 x 100 [1] = 550% increase [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Foreign investment rose 550%, so flows of capital into Kelvara grew very fast [1]. Legal work abroad rose only from 120,000 to 150,000, a much smaller increase, suggesting people move far less freely because of visas and borders [1]. Internet access rose from 6% to 71%, showing communication flows spread widely [1]. So globalisation is uneven: capital and communication have globalised far more than people, which supports a transformationalist or sceptical reading rather than a single global process [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures for each flow before making the comparison.

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: traditional tea houses reopening [1]; the coffee chain adding a local recipe [1]; local musicians using folk instruments; owners promoting local identity as "the real Marovik". **[2]**
**(b)** The global chain's spiced drink based on a local recipe is glocalisation: a global product adapted to local taste [1], which shows local culture shaping global business rather than simply being replaced [1]. The reopened tea houses show local identity being reasserted as a reaction to global chains [1], and they use global social media to do it, so global tools help the local survive [1]. The music mixing folk instruments with global electronic beats is hybridisation (Nederveen Pieterse) [1], producing a new form that is neither purely local nor purely global, which challenges the cultural imperialism view [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use a different detail from the item for each idea, and say what each shows about cultural imperialism.

**8.** Global trade moved manufacturing to lower-wage countries [1], raising incomes for many in the global middle, especially in Asia, as shown in Milanovic's elephant curve [1]. The same shift cost jobs in older industrial regions of rich countries [1], so the lower-middle classes there gained least, which helps explain resentment of globalisation [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Migration and diasporas carry religions to new countries [1], so faiths become global communities with places of worship on every continent [1]. Satellite television and online preaching let teachers reach followers across borders [1], spreading movements faster than missionaries could in the past [1]. Global flows can challenge traditions [1], producing fundamentalist reactions that defend beliefs in a rigid form (Giddens) [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Appadurai identifies five scapes: ethno-, media-, techno-, finance- and ideoscapes [1].
- The flows move at different speeds and in different directions, creating disjunctures [1].
- Application: non-Western media such as Bollywood or K-pop reach Western audiences [1].
- Ideas such as "democracy" are reinterpreted locally within ideoscapes [1].
- This challenges Schiller's cultural imperialism thesis of one-way US influence [1].
- Counter: US and European corporations still own much of the global media and technology [1].
- Ritzer's grobalisation shows standardisation still spreading through corporations [1].
- Judgement: flows are uneven, but uneven does not mean equal; Western power remains strongest in some scapes, such as finance [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Political globalisation spreads norms of democracy, human rights and gender equality [1].
- Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) set rights claimed for everyone [1].
- The International Criminal Court (working from 2002) can try individuals for the gravest crimes [1].
- CEDAW (1979) and the Beijing Platform (1995) made gender equality a global norm [1].
- Fukuyama argued liberal democracy had no serious rival after the Cold War [1].
- Application: own-society example of a law on women's rights or elections linked to a global norm [1].
- Counter: authoritarian governments persist and some have strengthened [1].
- States sign treaties but interpret them selectively, so norms spread more than practice [1].
- Some powerful states have not joined the International Criminal Court, limiting its reach [1].
- Some governments reject "global" rights as Western, an ideoscape disjuncture [1].
- Transformationalist view: states remain central but now act within global rules [1].
- Judgement: norms are widely spread, but their enforcement is uneven and depends on state power [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Hyperglobalists and neo-liberals (Ohmae) claim a borderless world with declining states [1].
- Sceptics, Hirst and Thompson, argue trade is concentrated among a few rich regions [1].
- They note pre-1914 openness was comparable, so "globalisation" is not new [1].
- They call the pattern internationalisation, with states still in control [1].
- Evidence for sceptics: borders, passports and visas restrict people strongly [1].
- Own-society application: visa limits on citizens seeking work abroad [1].
- Evidence against: capital moves instantly across borders [1].
- Global supply chains place stages of production in different countries [1].
- Giddens and Harvey: intensification and time-space compression are qualitatively new [1].
- Cultural flows through the internet reach far more people than before 1914 [1].
- Own-society application: global streaming, social media or brands in daily life [1].
- Political norms such as human rights are widely adopted, if unevenly [1].
- Transformationalists: change is real but uneven, reshaping rather than ending states [1].
- Negative globalists accept the extent but stress harms, a different debate from the sceptics [1].
- Analysis: the answer depends on which flow is measured [1].
- Judgement: exaggerated for people and for some political claims, but not for capital and communication [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Organise the answer by type of flow; it makes the judgement on "extent" much easier to justify.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a sceptic a negative globalist.
- Giving glocalisation examples with no adaptation.
- Treating all types of globalisation as equally advanced.
- Ignoring the figures in a data question or failing to show working.
- Leaving out own-society examples when the question asks for them.
