## What HR management is for

**Human resource management (HRM)** is the function that plans for, recruits, trains, rewards, organises and maintains the people a business needs. A **human resource objective** is a specific target for the workforce, set so that the people support the corporate objectives. If the corporate aim is growth, HR objectives may concern recruiting more staff; if the aim is premium service, they may concern training and engagement.

## The value of setting HR objectives

- **Link people to strategy**: the workforce is often a business's largest cost and its main source of competitive advantage, so it needs direction.
- **Give targets** that can be measured and reviewed.
- **Coordinate with other functions**: operations needs the right skills, marketing needs staff who represent the brand, finance sets the pay budget.
- **Guide decisions** on recruitment, training, pay and organisation.
- **Support motivation**: staff who understand what is expected, and are involved in setting it, tend to be more committed.
- **Reduce costs** of high turnover, absence and poor performance.

**Limits:** objectives about people are harder to measure than money or output; short-term cost pressure may squeeze HR budgets; targets set without consulting staff may cause resentment; and the labour market is outside the business's control.

## The main HR objectives

### Employee engagement

**Engagement** means employees are committed to the business, care about its success and put in discretionary effort, rather than just turning up. It is usually measured through surveys, absence, turnover and productivity. Engaged staff are more productive, give better customer service and are more likely to stay. Objectives might be "raise the engagement score from 68% to 75% within a year".

### Talent development

**Talent development** is identifying staff with potential and giving them the experience and training to take on bigger roles. It builds a pipeline of future managers, so the business can promote from within rather than recruit externally. It helps retain ambitious staff, though it costs money, and developed staff may still leave.

### Training

**Training** gives employees the skills and knowledge to do their present job or new tasks, through induction, on-the-job coaching, courses or e-learning. A training objective might be "every employee completes 20 hours of training a year". Training raises productivity and quality, helps staff cope with new technology and supports safety. Its costs are direct (fees, materials) and indirect (time away from work), and trained staff may leave for better pay.

### Diversity

**Diversity** means a workforce that includes people of different ages, genders, ethnicities, abilities and backgrounds. Objectives may cover recruitment, pay and promotion fairness. Benefits: wider pool of talent, better understanding of diverse customers, different ideas that support innovation, a good reputation, and meeting legal duties on equal treatment. Difficulties: changing recruitment practices, tackling unconscious bias, and the cost of adjustments.

### Alignment of employee and employer values

This means that employees' beliefs and priorities match the business's mission and culture, for example a charity employing staff who believe in its cause. Alignment is helped by careful recruitment, clear communication of values, and consistent behaviour by managers. The result is stronger commitment and a more consistent customer experience. Misalignment, such as a business claiming to be environmentally responsible while staff see waste ignored, causes cynicism and turnover.

### Number, skills and location of employees

This is **workforce planning**: ensuring the right number of people, with the right skills, in the right places at the right time. It depends on forecast demand, productivity, the skills required (which technology changes), and the labour market. A business may be opening new sites, closing others or moving to remote working.

*Worked example.* Cobalt Contact Centre expects 120,000 customer calls to be handled each month. One agent handles 2,000 calls a month.

- Agents needed = 120,000 ÷ 2,000 = **60**.
- It has 52 agents, so it is **8 short**.
- Recruiting 8 more, or raising productivity by training and better software, can close the gap. If productivity rose from 2,000 to 2,308 calls per agent, 52 agents could handle 52 × 2,308 = 120,016 calls, so the gap would close without recruiting. HR must also consider skills (language, product knowledge) and location (office sites, or remote working to reach a wider labour market).

## Methods of achieving HR objectives: soft and hard HRM

| | Soft HRM | Hard HRM |
|---|---|---|
| View of employees | Valuable assets whose commitment and development create competitive advantage | A resource and a cost, to be used as efficiently as other resources |
| Style | Involvement, communication, development, trust, empowerment | Control, close supervision, clear targets, performance management |
| Typical practices | Training and career development, consultation, teamwork, job enrichment, flexible working | Tight targets, performance-related pay, flexible or temporary contracts to match demand, monitoring of output |
| Management assumption | Staff want to contribute and can be trusted (a positive view of workers) | Staff need direction and incentives (a more sceptical view) |
| Strengths | Higher engagement, loyalty and innovation; lower turnover | Clear accountability; low labour cost; quick response to demand changes |
| Weaknesses | Costly and slow; needs managers who share power | Low morale, high turnover, little commitment; may harm quality |
| Suits | Skilled, creative or service roles; tight labour markets | Routine, low-skill work; short-term or seasonal jobs |

Most businesses combine both: a call centre might use hard methods (call-time targets, monitoring) with soft ones (coaching, career paths). The mix reflects the type of work, the labour market and the business's culture and objectives.

### Matching methods to objectives

| Objective | Soft methods | Hard methods |
|---|---|---|
| Engagement | Consultation, recognition, involvement | Targets with incentives |
| Talent development | Mentoring, career planning | Competitive selection for training places |
| Training | Broad development programmes | Training tied to specific job tasks |
| Diversity | Inclusive culture, mentoring | Targets for recruitment and pay monitoring |
| Number and location | Flexible working by agreement | Temporary or zero-hours contracts, relocation |

### Worked example: is training worth it?

Brook Engineering has 40 production employees, each making 300 units a month. A training programme costs £600 per employee and is expected to raise productivity by 5%. Each extra unit earns £12 contribution.

- Cost = 40 × £600 = **£24,000**.
- New output per employee = 300 × 1.05 = 315 units, an extra 15 each, so an extra 15 × 40 = **600 units** a month.
- Extra contribution = 600 × £12 = **£7,200** a month = **£86,400** a year.
- The return is 86,400 ÷ 24,000 = 3.6 times the cost in the first year, if the 5% gain holds.

The calculation is only as good as the forecast: if productivity rose by only 1%, the gain would be one-fifth as large (£17,280), which would not repay the £24,000 cost in the first year. Employees may also leave and take their skills elsewhere, and the training may have other benefits, such as fewer accidents, that are not counted.

## Influences on setting HR objectives

- **Corporate strategy and objectives**.
- **Labour market conditions**: shortages of skills make retention and training more important; high unemployment makes recruiting easier.
- **Technology**: automation changes the numbers and skills required; remote working changes where people work.
- **Legal and ethical influences**: equality, health and safety, employment law, fair treatment, wellbeing.
- **Competition**: rivals' pay and conditions affect the ability to attract staff.
- **Culture and leadership style**.
- **Finance available**.

## Technique for exam questions

- Identify which objective the case concerns and whether the approach is soft or hard.
- Support points with evidence, for example high turnover shows engagement problems.
- Evaluate by considering costs against benefits, and what the decision depends on, such as the type of work and the labour market.

## Common mistakes

- Treating soft HRM as "being nice" and hard HRM as "being cruel" instead of describing different views of the workforce.
- Listing the objectives with no link to the business.
- Ignoring the cost of training and engagement.
- Forgetting that objectives need a measure and a time limit.
- Assuming one approach suits every business.
- Overlooking the effect of the labour market.
