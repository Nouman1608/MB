## What you must be able to do

- Describe the trends in **atomic radius, first ionisation energy and melting point** from Na to Ar.
- **Explain** the radius and ionisation energy trends.
- **Explain** melting points in terms of **structure and bonding**.

## Key data

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius / nm | 0.186 | 0.160 | 0.143 | 0.117 | 0.110 | 0.104 | 0.099 | not quoted |
| First IE / kJ mol⁻¹ | 496 | 738 | 578 | 789 | 1012 | 1000 | 1251 | 1521 |
| Melting point / °C | 98 | 650 | 660 | 1410 | 44 | 115 | –101 | –189 |
| Structure | Metallic | Metallic | Metallic | Giant covalent | P₄ | S₈ | Cl₂ | Atoms |

## Atomic radius

- **Trend:** decreases from Na to Cl.
- **Reason:** nuclear charge increases; electrons are added to the same shell; shielding is similar; the outer electrons are pulled closer.

## First ionisation energy

Definition: the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms. X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.

- **General trend:** increases across the period.
- **Reason:** nuclear charge increases, radius decreases, same shell, similar shielding, so stronger attraction to the outer electron.

| Exception | Values | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Mg to Al (drops) | 738 to 578 | Al's outer electron is in a 3p sub-level, higher in energy than 3s, and slightly shielded by 3s² |
| P to S (drops) | 1012 to 1000 | S has two electrons paired in one 3p orbital; electron pair repulsion makes one easier to remove |

Model answer structure for an exception:
1. Give the configurations or name the sub-level.
2. State what differs (higher energy sub-level, or paired electrons).
3. Conclude: less energy needed.

## Melting point

| Elements | Structure | What is overcome on melting | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na, Mg, Al | Giant metallic | Attraction between metal ions and delocalised electrons | Increases: higher ion charge, more delocalised electrons, smaller ions |
| Si | Giant covalent | Many strong covalent bonds | Highest in the period |
| P₄, S₈, Cl₂ | Simple molecular | Weak London forces between molecules | S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂; larger molecule gives stronger forces |
| Ar | Monatomic | Very weak London forces between atoms | Lowest |

Molecular masses: P₄ 124, S₈ 256, Cl₂ 71, Ar 40 (atom).

### Melting-point graph

Rise from Na to a maximum at Si, then a sharp fall to P, and low values for P, S, Cl and Ar. The fall is from a giant structure (Si) to simple molecular (P).

## Model explanations

**Mg melts at a higher temperature than Na.** Mg²⁺ ions are smaller and more highly charged, and each atom gives two delocalised electrons, so the attraction between ions and electrons is stronger and more energy is needed.

**Si has a very high melting point.** Giant covalent structure; many strong covalent bonds must be broken.

**S melts higher than Cl.** S₈ molecules are larger, so the London forces are stronger.

**Atomic radius falls across Period 3.** More protons, same shell, similar shielding.

## Linking the three properties

| Trend across the period | Cause |
|---|---|
| Radius decreases | Nuclear charge increases; same shell; similar shielding |
| First ionisation energy generally increases | The same cause: stronger attraction to the outer electron |
| Melting point rises to Si, then falls | Structure changes from giant metallic to giant covalent to simple molecular |

Radius and ionisation energy follow the same underlying cause, so you can reuse the same reasoning. Melting point depends on structure and bonding, not on radius alone.

## Quick answers to common prompts

- **Explain the trend in first ionisation energy**: nuclear charge up, same shell, radius down, attraction up.
- **Explain the dip at Al**: 3p electron, higher in energy than 3s.
- **Explain the dip at S**: paired electrons in a 3p orbital repel.
- **Explain why Si has the highest melting point**: many strong covalent bonds in a giant lattice.
- **Explain why S₈ melts higher than P₄**: more electrons, stronger London forces.

## Exam traps

- Breaking covalent bonds when simple molecular elements melt.
- "Intermolecular bonds" instead of forces.
- Explaining Mg to Al with "extra shell".
- "Half-filled sub-level" rather than electron pair repulsion for P to S.
- Forgetting to say that outer electrons are in the same shell.
- Comparing metals without ionic charge, size and delocalised electrons.
- Treating silicon as simple molecular.
- Stating that Ar has a high ionisation energy because it has "a full octet" without nuclear charge and radius.

## Self-test

1. Describe the trend in atomic radius from Na to Cl.
2. Why does atomic radius decrease across the period?
3. Define first ionisation energy.
4. Which element has the highest first ionisation energy in Period 3?
5. Why is the first ionisation energy of Al lower than that of Mg?
6. Why is the first ionisation energy of S lower than that of P?
7. Why does Al have a higher melting point than Na?
8. Why does silicon have the highest melting point in Period 3?
9. Why does S melt at a higher temperature than P?
10. What kind of force is overcome when chlorine melts?
11. Which has a lower melting point, Cl₂ or Ar? Why?
12. Which structure type is Na, Mg and Al?

### Answers

1. It decreases.
2. Nuclear charge rises while electrons are added to the same shell with similar shielding.
3. The energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms.
4. Argon.
5. Al's outer electron is in a 3p sub-level, higher in energy than 3s.
6. In S two electrons are paired in one 3p orbital, so repulsion makes removal easier.
7. Al³⁺ ions are more highly charged and smaller, with three delocalised electrons per atom, so the metallic bonding is stronger.
8. It is giant covalent, so many strong covalent bonds must be broken.
9. S₈ is a larger molecule than P₄, so the London forces are stronger.
10. Weak London forces between Cl₂ molecules.
11. Argon is lower, because single atoms have fewer electrons and weaker London forces.
12. Giant metallic lattices.
