Alkanes, section 4.1.2 of the OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432) specification, Version 3.1 (May 2026), for first assessment in 2017, is the focus of this set. Every outcome of this Module 4 (Core organic chemistry) section, 4.1.2(a) to (g), is tested. Module 4 appears in two of the examined components, Unified chemistry (03) and Synthesis and analytical techniques (02). The last two questions are longer, mixed-outcome problems.

## Questions

**1.** Ethane, CH₃CH₃, is the second member of the alkane homologous series.

**(a)** State what is meant by a saturated hydrocarbon. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe how the orbitals overlap to form the C–C σ-bond in ethane. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the two CH₃ groups can rotate relative to each other. **[1]**

**2.** This question is about pentane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃.

**(a)** State the H–C–H bond angle at carbon 3. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of electron pairs, why the arrangement around carbon 3 has this angle. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how you would show the 3-D arrangement of the four bonds around carbon 1 in a drawing. **[1]**

**3.** Use these boiling points: nonane 151 °C; hexane 69 °C; 3-methylpentane 63 °C; 2,2-dimethylbutane 50 °C.

**(a)** Explain why nonane has a higher boiling point than hexane. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain the order of boiling points of the three isomers of C₆H₁₄. **[3]**

**4.** Heptane does not react with dilute hydrochloric acid or aqueous sodium hydroxide at room temperature. Explain why alkanes such as heptane react with so few reagents. **[3]**

**5.** Pentadecane, C₁₅H₃₂, is one of the alkanes in diesel fuel.

**(a)** Write an equation, with whole-number coefficients, for the complete combustion of pentadecane. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation, with whole-number coefficients, for the incomplete combustion of pentadecane to carbon monoxide and water. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why carbon monoxide from a faulty engine or heater is dangerous. **[2]**

**6.** 30.0 cm³ of a gaseous alkane, X, needs exactly 195 cm³ of oxygen for complete combustion. All gas volumes share one temperature and pressure; water is liquid.

**(a)** Write a general equation for the complete combustion of an alkane CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. **[1]**
**(b)** Deduce the molecular formula and name of X. **[2]**
**(c)** State the volume of carbon dioxide formed. **[1]**

**7.** Methane reacts with bromine when the mixture is exposed to ultraviolet radiation. One product is bromomethane, CH₃Br.

**(a)** Name the type of reaction and mechanism. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the equation for the initiation step and name the type of bond fission. **[2]**
**(c)** Write the two propagation steps that form bromomethane. **[2]**
**(d)** Give one termination step whose product is ethane. **[1]**

**8.** Pentane reacts with chlorine in UV radiation.

**(a)** Name the three structural isomers of C₅H₁₁Cl that can form. **[3]**
**(b)** Some C₅H₁₀Cl₂ also forms. Write two propagation steps to show how C₅H₁₀Cl₂ is made from C₅H₁₁Cl. **[2]**

**9.** A student reacts 3.60 g of 2,2-dimethylpropane, C(CH₃)₄, with chlorine in UV radiation. After separation, 3.73 g of 1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane, C₅H₁₁Cl, is obtained. Use M(C₅H₁₂) = 72.0 g mol⁻¹ and M(C₅H₁₁Cl) = 106.5 g mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Explain why only one monochloro isomer can form from 2,2-dimethylpropane. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage yield of 1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons, linked to the mechanism, why the yield is below 100%. **[2]**

**10.** A portable gas heater burns propane, C₃H₈. Use M(C₃H₈) = 44.0 g mol⁻¹ and M(CO) = 28.0 g mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Write the equation for the complete combustion of propane. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the equation for the incomplete combustion of propane to carbon monoxide and water, with whole-number coefficients. **[1]**
**(c)** In a poorly ventilated room, 0.660 g of propane burns entirely by the reaction in (b). Calculate the mass of carbon monoxide formed. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why a carbon monoxide detector is recommended in rooms with such heaters. **[2]**

**11.** 2-Methylbutane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃, reacts with bromine in UV radiation.

**(a)** Deduce the number of different monobromo isomers that can form. Explain your answer. **[2]**
**(b)** Give the two propagation steps leading to 2-bromo-2-methylbutane, (CH₃)₂CBrCH₂CH₃. **[2]**
**(c)** A by-product with molar mass 142.0 g mol⁻¹ (Ar: C 12.0, H 1.0) is detected. Identify it, and write an equation to show how it forms. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate radical bromination as a way to make pure 1-bromo-2-methylbutane. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** It contains carbon and hydrogen only, and has only single carbon–carbon bonds [1]
**(b)** The orbitals overlap directly between the two carbon nuclei, on the line joining them [1]
**(c)** Rotation about the bond axis does not change the direct overlap, so the σ-bond stays intact [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), "directly between the bonding nuclei" is the key idea; any mention of sideways overlap describes a π-bond instead.

**2. (a)** **109.5°** [1]
**(b)** Carbon 3 has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs [1]; they repel and get as far apart as possible: tetrahedral [1]
**(c)** Draw two bonds as plain lines in the page, a bold wedge for the bond pointing out at the viewer and a hashed wedge for the bond pointing back [1]
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) needs two separate ideas, the count of bonding pairs and the repulsion; "tetrahedral because it has four bonds" gives only one of them.

**3. (a)** Nonane has more electrons and a longer chain, so more surface contact between molecules [1]; this gives stronger London forces (induced dipole–dipole interactions) [1]; more energy is needed to overcome these intermolecular forces, so the boiling point is higher [1]
**(b)** The isomers have the same number of electrons [1]; more branching makes molecules more compact, with fewer points of contact: hexane > 3-methylpentane > 2,2-dimethylbutane [1]; London forces weaken, so less energy is needed and boiling point falls in that order [1]
*Examiner insight:* Name London forces and say they act between molecules; an answer about C–C bonds breaking earns nothing for the reasoning.

**4.** The C–C and C–H bonds have high bond enthalpies, so a lot of energy is needed to break them [1]. Carbon and hydrogen have very similar electronegativities, so the σ-bonds have very low polarity [1]. There are no δ+ or δ− sites to attract the H⁺ ions (electrophiles) or OH⁻ ions (nucleophiles) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Bond enthalpy and polarity are two separate ideas; an answer built only on "strong bonds" covers just one of them, however well it is written.

**5. (a)** **C₁₅H₃₂ + 23O₂ → 15CO₂ + 16H₂O** (or doubled) [1]
**(b)** **2C₁₅H₃₂ + 31O₂ → 30CO + 32H₂O** [1]
**(c)** CO is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin and reduces oxygen transport [1]. It is colourless and odourless, so it goes unnoticed [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b) one molecule of fuel needs 15½ O₂, which does not meet the whole-number instruction; double every coefficient, then recount the oxygen atoms on both sides.

**6. (a)** CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + ((3n + 1)/2)O₂ → nCO₂ + (n + 1)H₂O [1]
**(b)** Volume ratio O₂ : X = 195 ÷ 30.0 = 6.5, so (3n + 1)/2 = 6.5 and n = 4 [1]; X is **C₄H₁₀, butane** [1]
**(c)** 4 × 30.0 = **120 cm³** [1]
*Examiner insight:* At the same temperature and pressure, gas volumes are in the same ratio as moles; show the ratio 6.5 so the deduction is visible.

**7. (a)** Substitution by a radical mechanism: **radical substitution** [1]
**(b)** **Br₂ → 2Br•** [1]; **homolytic fission** [1]
**(c)** CH₄ + Br• → CH₃• + HBr [1]; CH₃• + Br₂ → CH₃Br + Br• [1]
**(d)** **CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The specification requires a dot on every radical; CH₄ + Br → CH₃ + HBr does not show which species are radicals.

**8. (a)** **1-chloropentane** [1]; **2-chloropentane** [1]; **3-chloropentane** [1]
**(b)** C₅H₁₁Cl + Cl• → C₅H₁₀Cl• + HCl [1]; C₅H₁₀Cl• + Cl₂ → C₅H₁₀Cl₂ + Cl• [1]
*Examiner insight:* "4-chloropentane" and "5-chloropentane" are the same compounds as 2- and 1-chloropentane numbered from the wrong end; listing them as extra isomers is incorrect.

**9. (a)** All twelve hydrogen atoms are on equivalent CH₃ groups attached to the same central carbon, so substitution of any one gives the same product [1]
**(b)** n(C₅H₁₂) = 3.60 ÷ 72.0 = 0.0500 mol, so the theoretical n(C₅H₁₁Cl) = 0.0500 mol [1]; actual n = 3.73 ÷ 106.5 = 0.0350 mol [1]; yield = 0.0350 ÷ 0.0500 × 100 = **70.0%** [1]
**(c)** Further substitution turns some product into C₅H₁₀Cl₂ and more highly substituted compounds [1]; termination steps form other products such as C₁₀H₂₂ from two C₅H₁₁• radicals [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Loss during transfer" is not linked to the mechanism; name the side reactions.

**10. (a)** **C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O** [1]
**(b)** **2C₃H₈ + 7O₂ → 6CO + 8H₂O** [1]
**(c)** n(C₃H₈) = 0.660 ÷ 44.0 = 0.0150 mol [1]; n(CO) = 3 × 0.0150 = 0.0450 mol [1]; mass = 0.0450 × 28.0 = **1.26 g** [1]
**(d)** CO binds to haemoglobin and cuts oxygen transport [1]; it is colourless and odourless, so only a detector warns of it [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), each propane gives three CO; leaving out that ratio gives 0.42 g, so show the mole step on its own line.

**11. (a)** **Four** [1]. The two CH₃ groups on carbon 2 are equivalent; carbon 2 (CH), carbon 3 (CH₂) and carbon 4 (CH₃) are each different, so there are four distinct sets of hydrogen atoms [1]
**(b)** CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ + Br• → (CH₃)₂C•CH₂CH₃ + HBr [1]; (CH₃)₂C•CH₂CH₃ + Br₂ → (CH₃)₂CBrCH₂CH₃ + Br• [1]
**(c)** C₁₀H₂₂, formed when two C₅H₁₁• radicals combine [1]; for example 2(CH₃)₂C•CH₂CH₃ → (CH₃)₂C(CH₂CH₃)C(CH₂CH₃)(CH₃)₂ [1]
**(d)** It gives a mixture of four monobromo isomers, plus dibromo compounds and C₁₀H₂₂ [1]; separation is needed and the yield of the wanted isomer is low, so it is a poor route [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" expects a judgement; describing the mixture without concluding on the method is incomplete.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing σ overlap as sideways, or saying single bonds cannot rotate (question 1).
- Stating 109.5° without the four bonding pairs and their repulsion (question 2).
- Talking about bonds breaking on boiling, or giving branched isomers "fewer electrons" (question 3).
- Giving only bond strength for low reactivity and forgetting polarity (question 4).
- Leaving fractional coefficients when whole numbers are asked for (questions 5 and 10).
- Missing radical dots, or writing H• rather than HBr in the first propagation step (question 7).
- Counting the same isomer twice by numbering from the other end of the chain (questions 8 and 11).
- Giving no judgement in an "evaluate" answer (question 11).
