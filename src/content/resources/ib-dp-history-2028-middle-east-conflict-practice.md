> **HL only. These are original questions written for Marlbridge** to practise Regional study 9 of Africa and the Middle East. They are not reproduced exam questions, and the real Paper 3 has essay questions only. Source extracts are constructed for practice. The topic is contested: aim for balanced, evidence-based answers. See the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-middle-east-conflict/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-middle-east-conflict-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short answers

**1.** Explain why the Sykes-Picot agreement caused resentment among Arab nationalists. **[3]**

**2.** State two provisions of the 1939 British White Paper on Palestine. **[2]**

**3.** Identify two outcomes of the Six-Day War of 1967. **[2]**

## Section B: source and argument

*Constructed for practice, not a real source:* "The partition plan of 1947 was not rejected because it was unfair on paper. It was rejected because neither community believed the other would keep to it, and because no outside power was willing to enforce it." (a historian's view, paraphrased)

**4.** Explain the argument made in this extract. **[3]**

**5.** Explain one way in which a historian could challenge this view. **[3]**

**6.** Examine the reasons why the Oslo Accords of 1993 did not lead to a lasting peace. **[6]**

## Section C: essay questions

**7.** To what extent was the creation of the state of Israel in 1948 the result of the policies of Britain? **[15]**

**8.** Evaluate the view that the Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty of 1979 was the most successful attempt to reach a peaceful settlement in the Arab-Israeli conflict. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark for the agreement (a secret deal in 1916 between Britain and France to divide Ottoman Arab lands into zones of influence). One for the contrast with wartime British promises to Sharif Hussein in the Hussein-McMahon correspondence and the Arab Revolt. One for the effect: the later mandates were seen as betrayal of independence and fed Arab nationalism and distrust of Britain and France. **[3]**

**2.** One mark each for two of: limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years; further immigration needed Arab consent; restrictions on land sales to Jews; a promise of an independent Palestine within ten years. **[2]**

**3.** One mark each for two of: Israel took Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights; Arab air forces were destroyed; more Palestinians became refugees or came under occupation; UN Security Council Resolution 242 was passed; settlement in occupied territory began; Nasser's prestige fell. **[2]**

**4.** One mark for the claim that fairness was not the main issue; one for the cause identified (mutual distrust of each community); one for the second cause (no enforcement by outside powers such as Britain or the UN). **[3]**

**5.** One mark for a challenge, up to two for development. For example: Arab leaders rejected the principle of partition, seeing it as unfair because Jews, a minority, were given more than half the land; Arab states and Palestinian leaders openly opposed it; the plan included Arab populations in the Jewish state; or evidence from British and UN records shows that attempts at enforcement were limited but planning for violence by both sides began early. **[3]**

**6.** Up to 6 marks. Credit reasons: final status issues (Jerusalem, refugees, borders, settlements) postponed; continued settlement building; violence by opponents on both sides such as Hamas attacks and the Hebron massacre of 1994; the assassination of Rabin in 1995; weak trust and uneven power; the limited role of outside mediators. Credit the view that the accords did achieve mutual recognition and some self-rule. Level guide: 1 to 2 for description, 3 to 4 for partial examination, 5 to 6 for balanced examination with a conclusion. **[6]**

**7.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding the claim and defining "result of the policies of Britain";
- up to 4 for evidence of British responsibility (the Balfour Declaration, the Mandate, immigration policy, the failure to find a solution, handing the issue to the UN in 1947 and withdrawing in May 1948);
- up to 4 for other factors (Zionist organisation and Jewish immigration, the Holocaust, the UN partition vote and the superpowers, Arab divisions and military outcomes of the war);
- up to 4 for evaluation of perspectives (including New Historians) and a judgement, for example that British policy created the framework and the pressure but the outcome was shaped by many actors. **[15]**

**8.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for setting criteria for "successful" (ending war, lasting peace, addressing core issues, fairness to all parties);
- up to 4 for the 1979 treaty (Sinai returned, Egypt recognised Israel, peace has lasted, Carter's mediation);
- up to 4 for comparison with other attempts (Oslo 1993, Camp David 2000, the Arab Peace Initiative 2002) and limits of 1979 (Palestinian issue left unresolved, Sadat's assassination, isolation of Egypt in the Arab world);
- up to 4 for a reasoned judgement that is consistent with the evaluation. **[15]**

## Model paragraph and feedback

Here is a model paragraph for Question 8, followed by points on how to improve a draft.

*Model paragraph:* "The Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty of 1979 is often regarded as the most successful peace agreement in the Arab-Israeli conflict because it has lasted. Egypt, the largest Arab state, recognised Israel, and Israel withdrew from Sinai. By the criterion of ending war between the two states, it was a success. However, if success means solving the conflict between Israelis and Palestinians, the treaty did not achieve this, as the question of the occupied territories remained. This essay argues that the treaty was the most successful in producing a lasting peace but not in resolving the core Palestinian issue."

*Feedback points:*

- The paragraph sets criteria for "success" and applies them, which is the central skill of an evaluation.
- Use dates and names for each agreement and give both sides' views of it.
- Be careful with wording. Use neutral terms and attribute disputed claims to the group that makes them.
- Do not write a list of peace attempts without comparing them.
