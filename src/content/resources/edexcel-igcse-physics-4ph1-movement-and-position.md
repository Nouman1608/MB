This guide goes deep on sub-topic **1(b) Movement and position**,
statements 1.3 to 1.10 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in
Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. None of these statements carries a "P", so all of
them can be examined on both Paper 1P and Paper 2P. For the whole of
Topic 1 in overview, see the
[Forces and Motion study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-forces-and-motion/).

## What the specification asks

- **1.3** plot and explain distance−time graphs
- **1.4** know and use average speed = distance moved ÷ time taken
- **1.5** practical: investigate the motion of everyday objects such as toy
  cars or tennis balls
- **1.6** know and use acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken,
  a = (v − u) ÷ t
- **1.7** plot and explain velocity−time graphs
- **1.8** determine acceleration from the gradient of a velocity−time graph
- **1.9** determine the distance travelled from the area between a
  velocity−time graph and the time axis
- **1.10** use v² = u² + (2 × a × s)

The relationships in 1.4 and 1.6 are "know and use", and both appear in the
specification's Appendix 7 list of relationships that are not provided in
the examination, so they must be learned.

## Speed, velocity and acceleration

**Speed** is how fast something moves; **velocity** is speed in a stated
direction. On a straight-line journey you can treat them alike, but a
negative velocity means motion in the opposite direction.

**Average speed** is the total distance moved divided by the total time
taken. It is not the average of the speeds. A car that drives 30 km at
60 km/h (taking 0.50 h) and then 30 km at 30 km/h (taking 1.0 h) covers
60 km in 1.5 h, an average speed of 40 km/h, not 45 km/h.

**Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity. In a = (v − u) ÷ t,
u is the initial velocity and v the final velocity. If v is less than u,
the acceleration is negative: the object is decelerating.

## Distance−time graphs (1.3)

Distance is on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis.

| Shape of the line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| horizontal | stationary: distance is not changing |
| straight, sloping upwards | constant speed |
| steeper straight line | greater constant speed |
| curve getting steeper | speeding up (accelerating) |
| curve getting less steep | slowing down (decelerating) |

The **gradient** of a distance−time graph is the speed. For a straight
section, gradient = change in distance ÷ change in time. For a curve, the
speed at one instant is the gradient of the **tangent** drawn at that
point. Choose two points on the tangent far apart to reduce reading errors.

**Plotting.** Put time on the x-axis, label both axes with quantity and
unit, choose a scale that uses more than half the grid in each direction,
plot points with small crosses, and draw a single smooth curve or ruled
line of best fit. Do not join the points dot to dot.

## Velocity−time graphs (1.7 to 1.9)

Velocity is on the vertical axis.

| Shape of the line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| horizontal | constant velocity (zero acceleration) |
| straight, sloping up | constant (uniform) acceleration |
| straight, sloping down | constant deceleration |
| curve getting less steep | acceleration decreasing, as when approaching terminal velocity |
| line on the time axis | stationary |

The **gradient** is the acceleration (1.8). The **area** between the line
and the time axis is the distance travelled (1.9). Split the area into
rectangles (base × height) and triangles (½ × base × height). For a curved
graph, count the squares under the line and multiply by the distance one
square represents.

Do not confuse the two graph types. A horizontal line on a distance−time
graph means stationary; on a velocity−time graph it means moving at
constant velocity.

## Worked example 1: reading a velocity−time graph

A bus starts from rest and accelerates uniformly to 12 m/s in 8.0 s. It
travels at 12 m/s for 20 s, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 6.0 s.

```
acceleration (first stage) = (12 − 0) / 8.0 = 1.5 m/s²
deceleration (last stage)  = (0 − 12) / 6.0 = −2.0 m/s², so 2.0 m/s²

distance = area under the graph
  first stage:  ½ × 8.0 × 12 = 48 m
  second stage: 20 × 12      = 240 m
  last stage:   ½ × 6.0 × 12 = 36 m
  total = 324 m

average speed = 324 / 34 = 9.5 m/s (2 s.f.)
```

*Commentary.* The total time is 8.0 + 20 + 6.0 = 34 s. The average speed is
less than 12 m/s because the bus spends part of the journey speeding up and
slowing down. The deceleration is quoted as a positive number with the word
"deceleration", or as −2.0 m/s² for "acceleration"; both are correct.

## Using v² = u² + 2as (1.10)

This equation links final speed v, initial speed u, acceleration a and
distance s with no time in it. Use it when a question gives (or asks for)
a distance but says nothing about time. It applies only when the
acceleration is constant.

**Worked example 2: braking to rest.** A car travelling at 25 m/s brakes
with a constant deceleration of 5.0 m/s². Calculate the braking distance.

```
v = 0, u = 25 m/s, a = −5.0 m/s²
0² = 25² + 2 × (−5.0) × s
0 = 625 − 10s
s = 625 / 10 = 62.5 m, so about 63 m
```

**Worked example 3: free fall.** A ball is dropped from rest and falls
1.8 m. Ignoring air resistance, its acceleration is 10 m/s². Its speed on
reaching the ground:

```
v² = 0² + 2 × 10 × 1.8 = 36
v = √36 = 6.0 m/s
```

*Commentary.* Write down u, v, a and s before substituting, with signs. The
most common slip is forgetting to square u, or forgetting the square root
at the end.

## The practical: motion of everyday objects (1.5)

A typical investigation is a toy car rolling down a ramp, or a tennis ball
dropped or rolled along the floor.

**Toy car down a ramp, measured by hand**

1. Mark a start line and, with a metre rule, mark distances along the
   ramp or floor, for example every 0.50 m.
2. Release the car from rest at the start line (do not push it).
3. Use a stopwatch to time how long it takes to reach each mark; repeat
   each run three times and calculate a mean time.
4. Plot distance against time; calculate average speed = distance ÷ time
   for each section.

**Improving the measurements.** Human reaction time adds an error to each
stopwatch reading, which matters most for short times such as a tennis
ball falling 1–2 m. Better methods are light gates connected to a data
logger, or filming the object beside a metre rule and stepping through the
video frame by frame. Repeating and averaging reduces the effect of random
errors; releasing from the same point in the same way keeps the test fair.

**Variables.** In an investigation of how ramp height affects the car's
average speed, the independent variable is the ramp height, the dependent
variable is the average speed (from measured distance and time), and
control variables include the car, the ramp surface and the release
point.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the gradient of a velocity−time graph as speed. It is
  acceleration.
- Calculating the area under a distance−time graph. That area is not a
  distance; the distance is read straight off the vertical axis, and area
  is used only on velocity−time graphs.
- Averaging two speeds instead of dividing total distance by total time.
- Using (u − v) instead of (v − u), which gives the wrong sign.
- Using v² = u² + 2as when the acceleration is not constant.
- Using times read off a graph axis in minutes without converting to
  seconds.

## Exam technique with command words

- **Describe** the motion shown on a graph: give each stage in order with
  values from the graph, for example "accelerates from rest to 12 m/s in
  8 s".
- **Explain** a feature of a graph: link the shape to the physics, for
  example "the gradient is constant, so the acceleration is constant".
- **Determine** from a graph: show the values read off and the
  calculation; a quantitative answer is required.
- **Plot**: label axes with units, choose a sensible scale, plot accurately
  and draw a line of best fit.
