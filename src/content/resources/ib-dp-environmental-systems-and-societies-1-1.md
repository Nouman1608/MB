Subtopic 1.1 Perspectives opens the Foundation topic of IB Diploma Programme Environmental Systems
and Societies (ESS), first assessment 2026. The subject brief names perspectives as one of the
course's three key concepts, alongside systems and sustainability, so the ideas here return in every
later topic and in the case study of Paper 1. This guide goes deeper than the
[perspectives, systems and sustainability study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-foundation-perspectives-systems-sustainability/):
it separates values from perspectives, explains each position on the environmental value system
(EVS) spectrum in policy terms, looks at how political, economic and Indigenous worldviews shape
decisions, and shows how to write about a stakeholder's perspective for marks.

The brief does not print learning outcomes for 1.1, so the scope below follows the usual teaching of
this subtopic; check it against your school's copy of the ESS guide.

## What 1.1 asks you to do

- Explain the difference between values, perspectives and worldviews.
- Describe an environmental value system and the factors that shape it.
- Place individuals, groups and policies on the EVS spectrum, with evidence.
- Explain how historical events and new evidence have changed environmental perspectives.
- Analyse how different perspectives lead to different decisions about the same issue.

## Values, perspectives and worldviews

These three words are related but not interchangeable:

- A **value** is a quality or principle a person thinks important, such as fairness, economic growth,
  freedom or care for living things.
- A **perspective** is how a person sees one particular issue, for example a new airport runway.
- A **worldview** is the wider framework of beliefs and values through which a person interprets the
  world as a whole.

Values feed perspectives; a set of values that hangs together is a **value system**. An
**environmental value system** is the part of a value system that concerns the natural world.

Two contrasting kinds of value are especially useful:

- **Instrumental value**: something is valued for what it provides to people (timber, pollination,
  tourism income).
- **Intrinsic value**: something is valued for itself, whether or not people benefit.

Spotting which kind of value a speaker uses is often the quickest route to labelling their EVS.

## The EVS as a system

An EVS can be modelled with systems language. Inputs include family upbringing, religion, culture,
education, media, peers, economic circumstances and direct experience of the environment. These are
processed through the person's values, and the outputs are decisions, actions, purchases, votes and
arguments. Because the inputs change through life, an individual's EVS can change too: someone who
loses a home to flooding may move from trusting engineering solutions to supporting wetland
restoration.

## The spectrum in policy terms

The spectrum has three broad positions, each with sub-positions. It is most useful when you can say
what each would actually propose.

| Position | Sub-position | Core belief | Typical policy on a river that floods a town |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ecocentric | Deep ecologist | Nature has intrinsic value equal to or above human interests | Let the river reclaim its floodplain; move people out |
| Ecocentric | Self-reliance soft ecologist | Small, local communities living within local limits | Community-run wetlands upstream; local food and energy |
| Anthropocentric | Environmental manager | Manage nature for long-term human benefit through regulation | Planning rules to stop floodplain building; taxes or grants for natural flood management |
| Technocentric | Cornucopian | Human ingenuity and markets will overcome limits | Higher concrete walls, pumps and new development behind them |

Two cautions earn marks. First, most people and organisations sit between positions, and they may
take different positions on different issues. Second, a label is only credited when it is supported
by evidence from the source, such as the speaker's words, the solution they favour or the kind of
value they use.

## Worldviews beyond the spectrum

### Political and economic systems

Economic and political systems shape what a society treats as a problem and which solutions seem
acceptable. Market-based economies tend to favour price signals, such as carbon pricing, tradable
permits or payments for ecosystem services. Systems with stronger state planning tend to favour
direct regulation and state-run projects. Neither system is automatically more or less
environmentally damaging: outcomes depend on the policies chosen and how they are enforced.

### Indigenous and local worldviews

Many Indigenous worldviews see people as part of the natural world, with responsibilities to land,
water and other species across generations. Avoid treating them as one view: they differ between
peoples and places. Some of these ideas have entered law. Ecuador's 2008 constitution recognised
rights of nature, and in 2017 New Zealand passed legislation recognising the Whanganui River as a
legal person, following a long campaign by Māori iwi. In exam terms these examples show a perspective
changing the rules that govern decisions.

## How events changed perspectives

Perspectives shift when events make harm visible or show that cooperation can work. Learn a few
examples well enough to explain the change each caused:

| Year | Event | Shift in perspective |
|---|---|---|
| 1956 | Minamata disease in Japan officially identified; later traced to mercury waste from a chemical plant | Industrial pollution seen as a direct threat to human health |
| 1962 | Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* | Public concern about pesticides and the wider effects of chemicals |
| 1970s | Chipko movement in India, villagers protecting trees from felling | Local, often women-led, action defending forests and livelihoods |
| 1972 | UN Conference on the Human Environment, Stockholm | Environment placed on the international political agenda |
| 1984 | Bhopal gas disaster, India | Debate about corporate responsibility and industrial safety in lower-income countries |
| 1992 | UN Earth Summit, Rio de Janeiro | Sustainable development adopted as a shared international aim |
| 2011 | Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident | Several countries reconsidered nuclear power |
| 2015 | Paris Agreement | Near-universal commitment to limit warming |

Media, NGOs and scientific reports act as channels for these shifts. A modern example is the
growth of youth climate strikes from 2018, which shows how social media can spread a perspective
quickly.

## Worked example: an annotated answer

*Original question.* A government proposes a hydroelectric dam that would flood 30 km² of forest used
by a local community. **Discuss how two different environmental value systems might respond to the
proposal. [7]**

> A **technocentric** perspective would support the dam because it treats engineering as the solution
> to energy demand: hydroelectricity is low-carbon once built and provides reliable power for growth.
> A technocentrist would expect resettlement and fish passes to manage the side effects.
>
> An **ecocentric** perspective would oppose it, because the flooded forest has intrinsic value and
> the community depends on it. A self-reliance soft ecologist would prefer small-scale, locally
> controlled energy, such as micro-hydro, that does not displace people.
>
> An **environmental manager** might accept the dam only with conditions: a full environmental impact
> assessment, compensation for the community and minimum river flows downstream.
>
> Overall, the decision depends on whose values carry most weight in the planning process, so
> including the local community's perspective changes the likely outcome.

*Why it scores well:* each EVS is named, linked to a belief, and linked to a specific response. A
third view adds balance, and the conclusion addresses "discuss" by weighing the views.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling a speaker without quoting evidence from the source.
- Confusing anthropocentric with technocentric. Both centre on people, but technocentrists trust
  technology to remove limits, while environmental managers accept limits and regulate.
- Treating "ecocentric" as meaning "anti-human".
- Listing historical events without saying how perspectives changed.
- Describing Indigenous worldviews as a single, uniform view.

## Exam technique

- **Identify / state**: name the EVS.
- **Outline**: name it and give one supporting reason.
- **Explain**: give the reason and the link to the evidence ("this is technocentric because...").
- **Discuss / evaluate / to what extent**: compare at least two perspectives and reach a judgement.

In Paper 1, the case study often contains quotations from stakeholders. Underline the words that show
values (intrinsic or instrumental), then decide the position.
