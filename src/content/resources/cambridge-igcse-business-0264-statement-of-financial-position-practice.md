## Questions

### Section A: short answer

**1.** Define the term **current asset**. **[2]**

**2.** Identify whether each item is a non-current asset, a current asset, a non-current liability or a current liability.

**(a)** A delivery van. **[1]**

**(b)** Money owed to suppliers for goods bought on credit. **[1]**

**(c)** Money owed by customers who bought on credit. **[1]**

**(d)** A bank loan repayable in seven years. **[1]**

**3.** A business has current assets of $62,000 and current liabilities of $48,500. Calculate its working capital. **[2]**

**4.** Define the term **trade payables**. **[2]**

**5.** A business has total assets of $410,000 and current liabilities of $55,000. Calculate its capital employed. **[2]**

**6.** Outline why an overdraft is classified as a current liability. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Brennan Garden Centre has the following items on its statement of financial position.

| Item | $ |
|---|---|
| Land and buildings | 250,000 |
| Equipment | 38,000 |
| Vehicles | 22,000 |
| Inventory | 31,000 |
| Trade receivables | 4,000 |
| Cash | 7,000 |
| Trade payables | 26,000 |
| Overdraft | 12,000 |
| Bank loan repayable in 15 years | 90,000 |

**(a)** Calculate total assets. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate total liabilities. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate working capital. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate capital employed. **[1]**

**(e)** Explain whether Brennan Garden Centre should be concerned about its working capital. **[3]**

**8.** Brennan Garden Centre wants to build a greenhouse costing $20,000. It can pay using its overdraft or take a five-year bank loan.

**(a)** Using your answer to Question 7(c), calculate working capital after the greenhouse is built if it is paid for with the overdraft, and if it is paid for with the bank loan. **[2]**

**(b)** Recommend and justify which method Brennan Garden Centre should use. **[4]**

**9.** The table shows Cosgrove Shoes' current assets and current liabilities at the end of two years.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | 40,000 | 41,000 |
| Trade receivables | 15,000 | 39,000 |
| Cash | 20,000 | 3,000 |
| Trade payables | 30,000 | 33,000 |
| Overdraft | 0 | 8,000 |

**(a)** Calculate working capital in each year. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what has caused Cosgrove's cash to fall, and suggest one action it could take. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why a supplier might look at a business's statement of financial position before agreeing to sell to it on credit. **[3]**

**11.** Explain one problem a business may have if its working capital is very large. **[2]**

**12.** Outline the difference between a statement of financial position and a statement of profit or loss. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Cash, or an item the business owns [1] that is expected to be turned into cash within a year, such as inventory or trade receivables [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **(a)** Non-current asset [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Current liability (trade payables) [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Current asset (trade receivables) [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** Non-current liability [1]. **[1]**

**3.** 62,000 - 48,500 [1] = **$13,500** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Money the business owes to its suppliers [1] for goods or services bought on credit, usually due within a year [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Capital employed = total assets - current liabilities [1] = 410,000 - 55,000 = **$355,000** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** An overdraft can be called in by the bank at short notice and is used for short-term needs [1], so it is treated as an amount owed that is due within a year [1]. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Non-current assets = 250,000 + 38,000 + 22,000 = 310,000; current assets = 31,000 + 4,000 + 7,000 = 42,000 [1]. Total assets = 310,000 + 42,000 = **$352,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Current liabilities = 26,000 + 12,000 = 38,000 [1]. Total liabilities = 38,000 + 90,000 = **$128,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** 42,000 - 38,000 = **$4,000** [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** 352,000 - 38,000 = **$314,000** [1]. **[1]**

**(e)** Working capital is positive but only $4,000 [1]. Most current assets are inventory ($31,000), such as plants and garden goods that may take time to sell, and cash is only $7,000 [1]. If sales are slow, for example in winter, Brennan may struggle to pay its suppliers and overdraft, so it should be concerned [1]. **[3]**

**8.** **(a)** Overdraft: current liabilities rise to 58,000, so working capital = 42,000 - 58,000 = **-$16,000** [1]. Bank loan: current assets and liabilities do not change, so working capital stays at **$4,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Brennan should use the bank loan [1]. The greenhouse will be used for many years, so long-term finance matches its life [1]. Using the overdraft would make working capital negative (-$16,000), so Brennan would not have enough current assets to cover its short-term debts [1]. The drawback is the interest on the loan over five years, but this is better than risking being unable to pay suppliers [1]. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** Year 1: (40,000 + 15,000 + 20,000) - 30,000 = **$45,000** [1]. Year 2: (41,000 + 39,000 + 3,000) - (33,000 + 8,000) = 83,000 - 41,000 = **$42,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Trade receivables have risen from $15,000 to $39,000, so customers who bought on credit have not yet paid [1], so cash that should have come in is still owed by customers, and cash has fallen from $20,000 to $3,000 and an overdraft has appeared [1]. Cosgrove could ask customers to pay more quickly, for example by shortening its credit period [1] or offering a small discount for early payment, which would turn receivables back into cash [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Working capital fell by only $3,000, which hides the problem. Looking inside the total, at receivables and cash, is the analysis that earns the marks.

**10.** The supplier wants to be sure it will be paid [1]. The statement shows the business's current assets, especially cash, compared with its current liabilities [1], so the supplier can judge whether the business is likely to have enough money to pay its trade payables when they fall due [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Money may be tied up in too much inventory or in customers who owe money [1], so it is not being used to earn a return, for example by buying equipment or repaying loans [1]. **[2]**

**12.** A statement of financial position shows what a business owns and owes at one point in time [1], whereas a statement of profit or loss shows revenue, costs and profit over a period [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping trade receivables and trade payables.
- Classing an overdraft as non-current.
- Leaving the overdraft out of current liabilities.
- Saying working capital is "fine" because it is positive, without looking at cash.
- Forgetting that financing with a long-term loan does not change working capital.
