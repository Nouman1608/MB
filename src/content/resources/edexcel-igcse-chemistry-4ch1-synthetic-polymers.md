This guide teaches sub-topic 4(h) Synthetic polymers from Topic 4, Organic chemistry, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). It covers points 4.44 to 4.50. Points 4.44 to 4.47 (addition polymers and their disposal) can be examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2. Points 4.48C to 4.50C carry a C reference (printed in bold in the specification), so condensation polymerisation, polyesters and biopolyesters are assessed on **Paper 2 only**. This guide labels them.

## Addition polymers (4.44)

A **polymer** is a very large molecule made of many repeating units. The small molecules that join up to make it are called **monomers**. In **addition polymerisation**, the monomers each contain a **C=C double bond**. Under high pressure and with a catalyst, the double bonds open, and the monomers join end-to-end to form a long chain of single bonds. **No other product is formed**, so the polymer has the same empirical formula as the monomer.

```
  H   H                 H   H
  |   |                 |   |
  C = C      →      ( - C - C - )n
  |   |                 |   |
  H   H                 H   H
 ethene                poly(ethene)
```

The **n** shows that a large number of units (often thousands) are joined. The brackets show the **repeat unit**, and the bonds at the ends of the bracket continue through the chain.

## Drawing repeat units (4.45)

The repeat unit has the **two carbon atoms of the original double bond in the chain**, now joined by a single bond, with each carbon keeping its other two groups. Everything else on those carbons becomes a side group.

| Monomer | Polymer | Repeat unit |
|---|---|---|
| Ethene, CH₂=CH₂ | Poly(ethene) | -[CH₂-CH₂]ₙ- |
| Propene, CH₂=CHCH₃ | Poly(propene) | -[CH₂-CH(CH₃)]ₙ- |
| Chloroethene, CH₂=CHCl | Poly(chloroethene) (PVC) | -[CH₂-CHCl]ₙ- |
| Tetrafluoroethene, CF₂=CF₂ | Poly(tetrafluoroethene) (PTFE) | -[CF₂-CF₂]ₙ- |

**Method in steps: from monomer to repeat unit.**

1. Draw the monomer with the C=C in the middle and the other groups above and below.
2. Change the double bond to a single bond.
3. Draw open bonds (extending bonds) from each of the two carbons through the brackets.
4. Add n outside the bracket.

**Displayed repeat unit of poly(propene):**

```
    H   H
    |   |
( - C - C - )n
    |   |
    H   CH3
```

The methyl side group is on one carbon. A common mistake is to put the extra group into the chain or to leave the double bond in the repeat unit; the repeat unit of an addition polymer has no C=C.

## Deducing the monomer from a repeat unit (4.46)

To work backwards, **take the two carbon atoms in the chain**, add a double bond between them, and remove the open bonds.

**Worked example 1.** The repeat unit is -[CH₂-CHCl]-. The two chain carbons are CH₂ and CHCl. Put a double bond between them: CH₂=CHCl, which is chloroethene.

**Worked example 2.** The repeat unit is -[CH₂-CH(CH₃)]-. The two chain carbons are CH₂ and CH(CH₃). The monomer is CH₂=CHCH₃, propene.

**If the repeat unit is longer.** For a section of chain such as -CH₂-CH₂-CH₂-CH₂-, the repeat unit is the smallest section that repeats: -CH₂-CH₂-, so the monomer is ethene. If it has four chain carbons with different groups, as in -CH₂-CHCl-CH₂-CHCl-, it is two repeat units of -CH₂-CHCl-, so the monomer is again chloroethene.

## Mass calculations for addition polymers

Because nothing is lost in addition polymerisation, the mass of polymer equals the total mass of monomer used. The relative molecular mass of the polymer is the mass of the repeat unit times n.

| Monomer | Mr of repeat unit | Polymer with n = 1,000: Mr |
|---|---|---|
| Ethene | 28 | 28,000 |
| Chloroethene | 62.5 | 62,500 |
| Tetrafluoroethene | 100 | 100,000 |

**Worked example 3.** A sample of poly(chloroethene) has a relative molecular mass of 62,500. The repeat unit C₂H₃Cl has Mr = 24 + 3 + 35.5 = 62.5. So n = 62,500 / 62.5 = **1,000** repeat units.

**Worked example 4.** 5.0 kg of chloroethene is polymerised completely. Mass of PVC = **5.0 kg**, because no other product forms. The monomer, 5,000 / 62.5 = 80 mol.

## Problems with disposal (4.47)

Addition polymers are very useful because they are cheap, light and unreactive. This also creates problems when they are thrown away.

- **Inertness and non-biodegradability.** The polymer chains contain strong C-C and C-H bonds, so they are unreactive. Microorganisms cannot break them down, so they are **not biodegradable**. Plastic therefore lasts for hundreds of years in landfill, takes up space and litters land and sea, where it harms wildlife.
- **Toxic gases when burned.** Burning gets rid of plastic and can release energy, but some polymers produce **toxic gases**. Burning PVC gives hydrogen chloride (a corrosive, toxic gas), and incomplete combustion of any plastic can give carbon monoxide and soot. Burning also releases carbon dioxide.

**Worked example 5.** Poly(chloroethene) burns: C₂H₃Cl + 2.5O₂ → 2CO₂ + H₂O + HCl. From 62.5 g of the repeat unit, 36.5 g of hydrogen chloride forms, so **1 kg** of PVC gives 1,000 x 36.5 / 62.5 = **584 g** of hydrogen chloride. This is why PVC needs controlled incineration with gas cleaning.

Other approaches (outside the main points required): recycling (melting and re-moulding), reducing use and re-using. Each has costs, such as sorting and the lower quality of recycled plastic.

## Condensation polymers: polyesters (4.48C and 4.49C, Paper 2 only)

In **condensation polymerisation**, two different monomers join, and a small molecule (here **water**) is lost every time a link forms. A **polyester** forms from a **dicarboxylic acid** (two -COOH groups) and a **diol** (two -OH groups). Each ester link forms from one -COOH and one -OH, with the loss of one H₂O.

Take **ethanedioic acid**, HOOC-COOH, and **ethane-1,2-diol**, HOCH₂CH₂OH:

```
n HOOC-COOH + n HOCH2CH2OH  →  [ -OC-CO-O-CH2-CH2-O- ]n + 2n H2O
```

The repeat unit is **-OC-CO-O-CH2-CH2-O-**. It contains two ester links, -CO-O-, one at each end of the unit, and each link formed with the loss of one water molecule.

**Worked example 6.** Mr of ethanedioic acid = 90; Mr of ethanediol = 62. For each repeat unit: 90 + 62 = 152, minus two molecules of water (2 x 18 = 36) gives **116**, which matches the repeat unit C₄H₄O₄ (48 + 4 + 64 = 116). For a chain of n = 100 repeat units, 100 molecules of each monomer (total mass 100 x 152 = 15,200) lose 200 molecules of water (200 x 18 = 3,600), leaving a polymer of mass about 11,600 (ignoring the end groups).

**Key differences from addition polymerisation:** two different monomers, each with two functional groups; a small molecule (water) is lost; the polymer chain contains ester links (-COO-).

## Biopolyesters (4.50C, Paper 2 only)

Some polyesters, called **biopolyesters**, are **biodegradable**. The ester links can be broken down by microorganisms, so the plastic rots away. They are often made from plant materials and can reduce the problem of plastic waste. They may cost more, and they need the right conditions (such as industrial composting) to break down.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving the double bond in the repeat unit of an addition polymer.
- Forgetting the side groups (CH₃, Cl, F) in the repeat unit.
- Naming the monomer after the polymer without the double bond (for example, calling the monomer "ethane").
- Writing that polymers are "not biodegradable because they are plastic". Say that microorganisms cannot break the strong bonds.
- Writing that burning plastics "makes carbon dioxide" as the only problem. Name the toxic gas (hydrogen chloride from PVC, carbon monoxide).
- Forgetting the water produced in condensation polymerisation.
- Saying that addition polymerisation loses a small molecule.
- Mixing up the two types: addition uses one monomer with a C=C; condensation (polyester) uses two monomers with two functional groups each.

## Exam technique

Draw the repeat unit with open bonds and the brackets, with n. For monomer questions, identify the two chain carbons and restore the double bond. For disposal questions, link each problem to its cause (strong bonds, toxic combustion products). Mark 4.48C to 4.50C as Paper 2 only in your notes.
