**Pakistan into the 21st Century: from Independence to 2022 is topic 3.1.2 of OxfordAQA
International GCSE Pakistan Studies 9236, a compulsory topic examined in Paper 1, Section B.** It
covers how Pakistan's constitution and political system developed after 1947, the governments from
Pervez Musharraf to 2022, Pakistan's international relations, and women's empowerment. Questions
range from multiple choice and short answers to extended responses that ask you to explain and
reach a judgement.

## What challenges and opportunities did Pakistan face in 1947?

Challenges included an unfavourable boundary award, the refugee crisis, the delayed division of
assets, the dispute over the waters of the Indus tributaries, the princely states (above all
Kashmir) and building a government from scratch in Karachi. Opportunities included a strong sense
of national purpose, agricultural land in Punjab and Sindh, and international recognition. As
**Governor-General**, Jinnah organised the new administration, appealed for refugee relief, founded
the State Bank (1948) and set out a vision of a tolerant state, but he died in September 1948.

## How did Pakistan's constitution develop?

| Document | Key features |
|---|---|
| Objectives Resolution, 1949 | Sovereignty belongs to Allah; democracy and Islamic principles; protection for minorities; later made part of the constitution |
| 1956 Constitution | Islamic Republic; parliamentary system; President must be a Muslim |
| 1962 Constitution | Presidential system under Ayub Khan; indirect elections |
| 1973 Constitution | Federal parliamentary system; two-house parliament; Islam the state religion; still in force, with amendments |

**President and Prime Minister:** under the 1973 Constitution the Prime Minister leads the government
and is answerable to the National Assembly, while the President is head of state. Amendments have
shifted power between them several times. **Federalism** means power is shared between the federal
government and the provinces, with a Senate giving each province equal representation.

## What democratic concepts and parties should you know?

**Transparency** means government decisions are open to scrutiny; **the rule of law** means
everyone, including leaders, is subject to the law and courts; **accountability** means officials can
be questioned and removed. Major parties include the Pakistan Muslim League factions (descended from
the All-India Muslim League), the Pakistan People's Party (founded 1967 by Zulfikar Ali Bhutto) and
Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (founded 1996 by Imran Khan), alongside regional and religious parties.
Parties organise voters, form governments and act as opposition.

## Why do Urdu, regional languages and sport matter?

The 1973 Constitution names **Urdu** as the national language. It links people across provinces and
carries a rich literary tradition. **Regional languages**, including Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashto,
Balochi, Saraiki and Brahui, are central to provincial identity and culture; respecting them
supports national integration, while past disputes, such as the Bengali language movement, show the
risks of ignoring them. **Sport** builds national pride: cricket (the 1992 World Cup win), field
hockey (Olympic gold medals) and squash (Jahangir Khan and Jansher Khan) are major examples.

## What happened in Pakistani politics from 1999 to 2022?

**Pervez Musharraf (1999–2008).** He took power in October 1999 and became President in 2001.
*Achievements:* strong economic growth in the mid-2000s, a local government system that devolved
power to districts, expanded media freedom with private television channels, and reserved seats
for women in assemblies. *Challenges:* joining the US-led campaign in Afghanistan after September
2001 brought aid but also militant violence; his dismissal of the Chief Justice in 2007 sparked the
Lawyers' Movement; he declared an emergency in November 2007 and resigned in 2008.

**Asif Ali Zardari (President 2008–13).** The **18th Amendment (2010)** removed the President's power
to dissolve the National Assembly, devolved many subjects to the provinces, renamed the North-West
Frontier Province as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and added a right to free education for children aged 5
to 16. The **19th Amendment** changed the process for appointing senior judges. The **2013
elections** were the first in which one elected government that had completed its term handed power
to another.

**PML-N government (2013–18).** Under Nawaz Sharif, priorities included energy projects to end
load-shedding, the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) and military operations against militancy
after the 2014 Peshawar school attack. Sharif was disqualified by the Supreme Court in 2017.

**2018 elections and 2018–22.** Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf won the most seats and Imran Khan became
Prime Minister. Policies included the Ehsaas social-protection programme, health insurance cards,
the tree-planting drive and an IMF support programme in 2019. The government was removed by a
no-confidence vote in April 2022.

**Covid-19.** The National Command and Operation Centre coordinated the response, with targeted
"smart" lockdowns, emergency cash payments through Ehsaas and a vaccination campaign from 2021. The
pandemic hurt daily-wage earners, closed schools and slowed the economy.

## How have Pakistan's international relations developed?

- **India:** Kashmir remains the core dispute; periods of dialogue have been broken by crises, such as the 2019 tensions after the Pulwama attack and India's change to Kashmir's status in August 2019; the Kartarpur Corridor (2019) was a gesture of goodwill.
- **China:** an "all-weather" partner; military co-operation, including the jointly developed JF-17 aircraft; CPEC investment in roads, power, Gwadar port and agriculture.
- **Muslim world:** close ties with Turkey; Saudi Arabia and the UAE provide financial support and employ many Pakistani workers whose remittances support the economy.
- **USA:** a key ally after 2001, giving aid but also tension over drone strikes and the 2011 Abbottabad raid; the Afghan war brought violence and refugees to Pakistan.
- **Multinational bodies:** the 2008 world economic crisis led to IMF support, with further programmes since; Pakistan is one of the largest contributors of troops to UN peacekeeping missions.

## How far have women been empowered in Pakistan?

**Benazir Bhutto** became Prime Minister in 1988, the first woman elected to lead a Muslim-majority
country. Women have since served as Speaker of the National Assembly and Foreign Minister. **Dr
Sania Nishtar**, a cardiologist, founded the health think-tank Heartfile and later led the Ehsaas
poverty programme, which paid cash support directly to women. **Legislation** includes the
Protection of Women Act (2006), the Protection against Harassment of Women at the Workplace Act
(2010) and the 2016 law against so-called honour killings. **By 2022**, girls' school enrolment and
women's political representation had risen, but literacy, workforce participation and legal
protection still lagged, especially in rural areas.

## Frequently asked questions

**What did the 18th Amendment do?**
It strengthened parliament and the Prime Minister by removing the
President's dissolution power, devolved many responsibilities to the provinces, renamed NWFP as
Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and made education for 5- to 16-year-olds a right.

**Why was the 2013 election important?**
It was the first transfer of power from one elected
government that had served a full term to another.

**What is CPEC?**
The China–Pakistan Economic Corridor, launched in 2015, a programme of Chinese
investment in transport, energy and Gwadar port.
