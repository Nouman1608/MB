## What 6.3 asks you to understand

The syllabus lists two points:

- the factors that may affect the ratios of two businesses
- the problems of inter-business comparison

Questions usually give you a set of ratios for two businesses and ask you to explain a difference, suggest why a comparison may mislead, or advise someone (an owner, investor or lender) which business looks stronger. You must use the ratio formulas printed in the syllabus; no other version is accepted.

## Why compare with another business at all

A gross profit margin of 30% means little on its own. Compared with last year it shows a trend; compared with a similar business it shows whether the result is good for that kind of trade. A comparison can reveal that a business pays too much for its goods, takes too long to collect debts or holds too much inventory. The skill being tested is judging how far the other business really is a fair yardstick.

## Worked example: two hardware companies

Kestrel Hardware Limited and Lumen Hardware Limited each have revenue of $600,000 for the year. They sell similar goods in the same town.

| | Kestrel Hardware | Lumen Hardware |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 600,000 | 600,000 |
| Cost of sales | 420,000 | 390,000 |
| Gross profit | 180,000 | 210,000 |
| Expenses (Lumen's include rent of 30,000) | 108,000 | 144,000 |
| Profit before interest | 72,000 | 66,000 |
| Loan interest | 0 | 6,000 |
| Profit for the year | 72,000 | 60,000 |
| Capital employed | 600,000 | 320,000 |
| Current assets (inventory) | 90,000 (60,000) | 150,000 (55,000) |
| Current liabilities | 45,000 | 75,000 |
| Credit sales | 60,000 | 480,000 |
| Trade receivables | 4,000 | 66,000 |

Kestrel owns its premises, bought many years ago, and is financed by issued shares of 300,000 and reserves of 300,000. Lumen rents its shop and is financed by shares of 150,000, reserves of 70,000 and a loan of 100,000. Lumen belongs to a buying group that gives members lower purchase prices. Kestrel sells mainly for cash to the public; Lumen sells mainly on credit to builders.

| Ratio | Kestrel Hardware | Lumen Hardware |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 180,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 30.00% | 210,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 35.00% |
| Profit margin | 72,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 12.00% | 60,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 10.00% |
| ROCE | 72,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 12.00% | 66,000 ÷ 320,000 × 100 = 20.63% |
| Current ratio | 90,000 : 45,000 = 2.00 : 1 | 150,000 : 75,000 = 2.00 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 30,000 : 45,000 = 0.67 : 1 | 95,000 : 75,000 = 1.27 : 1 |
| Trade receivables turnover | 4,000 ÷ 60,000 × 365 = 24.33 days | 66,000 ÷ 480,000 × 365 = 50.19 days |

Remember that ROCE uses profit for the year **before** interest, so Lumen's numerator is 60,000 + 6,000 = 66,000.

### Linking each difference to a factor

- **Gross profit margin.** Lumen's buying group lowers its cost of sales by 30,000, so it earns 5 percentage points more gross profit on the same revenue. The factor is **size and buying power**, not better selling.
- **Profit margin.** Lumen's lead disappears below gross profit. It pays rent of 30,000 that Kestrel does not, because Kestrel **owns its premises**, and it pays 6,000 interest because of **how it is financed**. Its other expenses are also higher.
- **ROCE.** Lumen earns less profit but uses far less capital: it has no premises in its statement of financial position. Kestrel's premises are also shown at **historic cost** from many years ago. Two businesses with very different asset bases will show very different returns, even with similar trading.
- **Current ratio.** Identical at 2.00 : 1, yet the make-up differs. Kestrel's current assets are two-thirds inventory, so its acid test is only 0.67 : 1. Lumen's current assets are mostly trade receivables.
- **Trade receivables turnover.** Lumen's 50.19 days against Kestrel's 24.33 days comes from **cash or credit selling**: builders expect credit, shoppers pay at the till. On its own it does not prove poor credit control at Lumen; you would compare it with the credit period Lumen allows.

The conclusion is not "Lumen is better" or "Kestrel is better". Each result has a structural cause that management cannot change quickly.

## Factors that may affect the ratios of two businesses

Learn the factors grouped by the ratios they move.

| Factor | Ratios most affected | How |
|---|---|---|
| Type of goods or service | Gross profit margin, inventory turnover | Fresh food sells fast at low margins; furniture sells slowly at higher margins |
| Pricing policy | Gross profit margin, profit margin | Low prices for high volume against high prices for fewer sales |
| Size and buying power | Gross profit margin, trade payables days | Bulk buying earns lower prices and longer credit |
| Location | Profit margin | Rent, wages and passing trade differ between sites |
| Cash or credit sales | Trade receivables days, acid test | Credit sales create receivables; cash sales do not |
| Method of finance | ROCE, profit for the year | Loans add interest and raise capital employed |
| Owned or rented premises; age of assets | Profit margin, ROCE | Rent is an expense; owned assets increase capital employed; old assets have low book values and small depreciation |
| Accounting policies | Profit margin, ROCE, current ratio | Depreciation method, inventory valuation and the allowance for irrecoverable debts are choices |

### Accounting policies in figures

Suppose both companies buy new shop fittings for $40,000. Kestrel uses straight line at 10% of cost; Lumen uses reducing balance at 20%. In the first year Kestrel charges 4,000 and Lumen 8,000. Lumen's profit is 4,000 lower purely because of its policy, which would reduce its profit margin by 4,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 0.67 percentage points. Before comparing profit margins, an examiner expects you to ask whether the businesses use the same policies.

## Problems of inter-business comparison

1. **Information may not be available.** Sole traders and partnerships do not publish their accounts. Even published summaries may leave out credit sales, credit purchases or opening inventory, so some ratios cannot be calculated.
2. **Different accounting policies**, as shown above, so the same transactions give different figures.
3. **Different year-end dates.** A garden centre with a year end in March shows high inventory before the spring rush; one with a September year end shows little. Liquidity and inventory ratios then differ for timing reasons alone.
4. **The businesses may not be truly alike** in size, range of products, location, premises or how they are financed.
5. **Historic cost.** Assets bought at different dates are recorded at prices of different years, which distorts ROCE.
6. **An untypical year.** A one-off event, such as a flood or a large single order, can make one year's ratios unrepresentative.
7. **Non-financial aspects are ignored**, such as staff skill, reputation, customer loyalty or the state of the local economy.
8. **Ratios show results, not causes.** They tell you where to look, but more information is needed to explain them.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Kestrel's directors say Lumen is better managed because its ROCE is 20.63% against Kestrel's 12.00%. Evaluate this view.* [6 marks]

> Lumen's ROCE is higher, which suggests it earns more profit on the capital invested in it. **(uses the figures)** However, Lumen rents its shop, so its capital employed of 320,000 does not include premises, while Kestrel's 600,000 includes premises it owns. **(factor 1, applied)** Kestrel's premises are at historic cost, so even its figure may not reflect their value today. **(problem)** Kestrel also earns a higher profit margin, 12.00% against 10.00%, because it pays no rent or interest. **(counter-evidence)** The two companies may also use different accounting policies, such as depreciation methods. **(problem)** So the higher ROCE is largely explained by how each business is structured and financed, and it does not prove Lumen is better managed. **(judgement)**

Each bold label marks a creditable step. Notice that the answer uses the case figures, gives points on both sides, and ends with a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Declaring one business "better" from a single ratio.
- Naming a factor without saying which ratio it affects and in which direction.
- Treating a higher trade receivables figure as poor management without considering cash or credit selling.
- Forgetting to add interest back when calculating ROCE.
- Listing problems in general terms without linking them to the two businesses in the question.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain** a difference: state the ratio values, name the factor, say how it changes the ratio.
- **Compare**: give both businesses' figures and say which is higher or lower and by how much.
- **Suggest** reasons: give reasons that fit the case details, such as credit customers or rented premises.
- **Evaluate** or **Discuss** a comparison: points for and against, then a supported judgement.
- **Advise** an investor or lender: a clear recommendation tied to the ratios that matter to that party, with a limitation of the comparison.
