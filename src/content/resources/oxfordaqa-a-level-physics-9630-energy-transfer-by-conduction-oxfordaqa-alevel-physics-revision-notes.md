## The two equations

**Rate of energy transfer by conduction = kAΔθ/L**

**Rate of energy transfer = UAΔθ, where U = k/L**

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate | energy transferred per second | W | multiply by time for energy |
| k | thermal conductivity of the material | W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ | material property only |
| A | area at right angles to the flow | m² | cm² × 10⁻⁴; mm² × 10⁻⁶ |
| Δθ | temperature difference across the layer | K or °C | a difference, so either unit |
| L | thickness of slab or length of rod | m | mm × 10⁻³ |
| U | U-value of one layer | W m⁻² K⁻¹ | low U = good insulator |

Rearranged forms: k = (rate × L)/(AΔθ); L = kAΔθ/rate; Δθ = (rate × L)/(kA); L = k/U.

## Condensed recall

- Conduction transfers energy through a material without bulk movement of the material. Vibrating particles pass energy to neighbours; in metals free electrons carry energy too, so metals conduct far better.
- Gases conduct poorly because their particles are widely spaced. Insulators work by trapping still air so that convection cannot occur.
- The equation holds in the **steady state**, when temperatures no longer change. The same rate then passes every cross-section.
- In a lagged rod or uniform slab in the steady state, temperature falls **linearly** from hot to cold. Δθ/L is the temperature gradient.
- An unlagged rod loses energy from its sides, so the rate falls along the rod and the profile curves.
- k belongs to a material; U belongs to a layer of a given thickness. Doubling the thickness halves U.
- **Parallel surfaces only:** walls, windows, roof and door at the same Δθ. Total rate = (U₁A₁ + U₂A₂ + …)Δθ. Layers in series are not required.
- The surface with the largest UA contributes most to the total loss and is the best one to improve.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Conduction | Energy transfer through a material from hotter to colder regions without bulk movement |
| Thermal conductivity, k | Rate of energy transfer per unit area per unit temperature gradient; unit W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ |
| Temperature gradient | Temperature difference divided by thickness, Δθ/L; unit K m⁻¹ |
| Steady state | Condition in which the temperature at every point stays constant with time |
| U-value | Rate of energy transfer per unit area per kelvin difference for one layer; U = k/L |
| Lagging | Insulation round the sides of a rod or pipe that stops energy escaping sideways |
| Parallel surfaces | Separate surfaces sharing the same inside and outside temperatures, whose losses add |

## Proportional reasoning

| Change (all else fixed) | Effect on rate |
|---|---|
| Double the area | × 2 |
| Double the thickness | × 1/2 |
| Double the diameter of a rod | × 4 |
| Double the temperature difference | × 2 |
| Material with three times the k | × 3 |
| Double length and double diameter of a rod | × 4/2 = × 2 |

## Exam traps

- Diameter used as radius, or area left in cm².
- Thickness left in mm. A 6.0 mm pane has L = 6.0 × 10⁻³ m.
- k and U confused. Check the unit before substituting.
- "High U-value is a good insulator": wrong, it is a poor one.
- A rate in W given when the question asked for energy in J or kWh.
- U-values added directly instead of the products UA.
- Assuming a linear temperature profile for an unlagged rod.
- Converting a temperature difference by adding 273. A difference of 15 °C is 15 K.

## Quick self-test

1. State what is meant by thermal conductivity.
2. Derive the unit of k from the conduction equation.
3. A slab of area 10 m² and thickness 0.20 m has k = 0.80 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ and a temperature difference of 12 K. Find the rate of energy transfer.
4. Find the U-value of a 0.15 m layer of material with k = 0.045 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
5. A wall of area 25 m² has U = 0.40 W m⁻² K⁻¹. Find the rate of loss when Δθ = 15 K.
6. A lagged rod is replaced by one of the same material with twice the length and three times the cross-sectional area. Δθ is unchanged. By what factor does the rate change?
7. A window of area 4.0 m² (U = 2.5 W m⁻² K⁻¹) and a wall of area 20 m² (U = 0.50 W m⁻² K⁻¹) share a temperature difference of 10 K. Find the total rate of loss.
8. How much energy passes in 1.0 hour at a steady 150 W?
9. Explain why the temperature falls linearly along a lagged rod in the steady state.
10. Material P has U = 0.20 W m⁻² K⁻¹ and material Q has U = 0.50 W m⁻² K⁻¹ for the same thickness. Which is the better insulator?
11. What thickness of foam with k = 0.030 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ gives U = 0.20 W m⁻² K⁻¹?
12. Explain why fibreglass insulation is a poor conductor even though glass itself conducts better.

### Answers

1. The rate of energy transfer by conduction per unit area per unit temperature gradient through a material.
2. k = rate × L/(AΔθ), so unit = W × m/(m² × K) = W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
3. Rate = 0.80 × 10 × 12/0.20 = 480 W.
4. U = 0.045/0.15 = 0.30 W m⁻² K⁻¹.
5. Rate = 0.40 × 25 × 15 = 150 W.
6. Rate ∝ A/L = 3/2, so it rises by a factor of 1.5.
7. Window: 2.5 × 4.0 × 10 = 100 W; wall: 0.50 × 20 × 10 = 100 W; total 200 W.
8. Energy = 150 × 3600 = 5.4 × 10⁵ J.
9. No energy leaves through the sides and no point is gaining energy, so the same rate passes every cross-section; with constant k and A, the temperature gradient must be the same everywhere.
10. P, because a lower U-value means a smaller rate of loss per square metre per kelvin.
11. L = k/U = 0.030/0.20 = 0.15 m.
12. Most of its volume is still air trapped between the fibres. Air has a very low conductivity, and the fibres stop it circulating, so convection cannot carry energy across.
