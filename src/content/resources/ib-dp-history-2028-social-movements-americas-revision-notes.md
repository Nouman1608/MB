**HL only.** These notes support Paper 3 (Regional study, the Americas) in IB DP History for first assessment 2028. Half of the study is the African American civil rights movement; the other half is **one other movement**. These notes use the Chicano movement and the United Farm Workers (UFW). The [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-social-movements-americas/) gives the explanations, and the [practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-social-movements-americas-practice/) let you rehearse.

## Key terms

- **Jim Crow**: system of racial segregation in the US South.
- **Non-violent direct action**: protest such as boycotts, sit-ins and marches without violence.
- **SCLC, SNCC, CORE, NAACP**: main civil rights organisations.
- **Black Power**: call for Black pride, self-determination and community control.
- **COINTELPRO**: FBI programme to disrupt radical groups.
- **Voting Rights Act (1965)**: ended barriers to Black voting.
- **Aztlán**: mythic Chicano homeland, symbol of heritage.
- **Chicano**: Mexican American identity associated with activism.
- **Boycott**: refusal to buy or use goods or services as protest.

## Civil rights: emergence

| Factor | Points |
|---|---|
| Political | Disenfranchisement; Southern Democrats blocking Congress; WWII and Cold War pressures; Truman's order |
| Social | Jim Crow; *Plessy*; lynching; Emmett Till (1955) |
| Economic | Sharecropping; mechanisation; Great Migration |
| Ideas | Black church; NAACP legal strategy; Gandhian non-violence; "Double V" |

## Civil rights: methods

- Non-violent protest: Montgomery (381 days), Greensboro, Freedom Rides, Birmingham, March on Washington (about 250,000), Selma. King as leader.
- Radical activism: Malcolm X (self-defence, Black pride); Carmichael (Black Power); Panthers (community programmes, patrols; targeted by COINTELPRO).
- Women: Ella Baker (SNCC, group-centred leadership), Fannie Lou Hamer (MFDP), Angela Davis.
- Grassroots groups: NAACP, CORE, SNCC, SCLC, churches. Freedom Summer.

## Civil rights: extent of change

| Area | Achievement | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Courts | *Brown* (1954) | Slow implementation |
| Legislation | Civil Rights Act (1964); Voting Rights Act (1965); Fair Housing Act (1968) | Enforcement and resistance |
| Social and cultural | End of legal segregation; Black Arts; pride | De facto segregation; northern riots (1965 to 1967) |
| Economic | Growth of Black middle class | Poverty and wealth gaps |

## Second movement: Chicano movement and UFW

- Emergence: discrimination and segregated schools; low-paid farm labour outside labour law; civil rights example; heritage and Catholic social teaching.
- Methods (non-violent): Chávez and Huerta; NFWA (1962); Delano strike (1965); march to Sacramento (1966); grape boycott; fasts.
- Methods (militant and cultural): Tijerina and the land-grant raid (1967); "Corky" Gonzales and the Crusade for Justice; Brown Berets; school walkouts (1968); Chicano Moratorium (1970); La Raza Unida.
- Women: Dolores Huerta; Chicana feminism.
- Change: bilingual education law, MALDEF, California Agricultural Labor Relations Act (1975), more Latino officials, Chicano studies; limited economic gains; UFW weakened in the 1980s.

## Debates

- Leaders (King) or grassroots (Baker)?
- Radicals: helped or hindered?
- Was change real or limited, legal or lasting?
- Federal action: response to protest or leadership?
- "Long civil rights movement" (wider time frame and issues).

## Essay skeleton

1. State the argument and your line.
2. Use two or three factors or methods, ranked.
3. Add a perspective.
4. Compare the two movements if the question allows.
5. Judge.

## Worked example: planning an answer

Question: "To what extent did grassroots organisation, rather than national leadership, drive the success of the civil rights movement?"

1. Line: grassroots work built the movement and national leaders gave it focus and access to power.
2. Grassroots evidence: Ella Baker and SNCC, local women in Montgomery, Freedom Summer volunteers, church networks, voter registration in Mississippi.
3. National leadership evidence: King and the SCLC, the March on Washington, Johnson's use of Selma to pass the Voting Rights Act.
4. Perspective: the "long civil rights movement" view stresses local, long-running struggles; older accounts focus on King and Washington.
5. Judgement: neither alone was enough; local organisation made protest possible and national leaders turned it into law.
6. Test the line against the second movement: the UFW also combined local organising (Delano) with national leaders (Chávez and Huerta) and a national boycott.

## Common mistakes

- Making King the only leader.
- Describing events without evaluating.
- Thin evidence on the second movement.

## Self-test

1. What did *Brown v. Board of Education* decide?
2. Why was the Montgomery Bus Boycott significant?
3. What did the Voting Rights Act (1965) do?
4. Who was Ella Baker?
5. Name two methods used by the UFW.
6. Give one limit on the change achieved by the civil rights movement.

**Answers:** 1. That segregation in public schools was unconstitutional. 2. It lasted 381 days, ended bus segregation in Montgomery and made King a national figure. 3. Ended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to protect Black voting. 4. An organiser who helped found SNCC and favoured local, group-centred leadership. 5. Any two: strikes, boycotts, marches, fasts. 6. For example, de facto segregation in housing and schools, or continuing poverty.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use a different second movement?**
Yes. Choose one with strong evidence, such as the American Indian Movement or second-wave feminism.

**Do I need to compare the movements?**
Only if the question asks, but comparison can help judgement.
