## Section A

**1.** Define the term "recharge" as used in drainage basin hydrology. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between throughfall and stemflow. **[2]**

**3.** Name **two** outputs of the drainage basin system and explain why evapotranspiration counts as an output. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why a line of springs often forms where a permeable rock lies above an impermeable rock. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between infiltration and percolation. **[2]**

**6.** The table shows rainfall reaching the ground during one 20 mm storm under four types of vegetation in the fictional Marrow basin.

| Vegetation | Throughfall plus stemflow (mm) |
|---|---|
| Conifer plantation | 13.0 |
| Deciduous woodland, summer | 15.0 |
| Deciduous woodland, winter | 18.4 |
| Grassland | 19.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the interception loss under the conifer plantation as a percentage of the rainfall. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest reasons for the differences in interception loss shown in the table. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Recharge is the addition of water to the saturated zone or groundwater store [1], mainly by percolation of water from the soil above [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Throughfall is rain that drips from leaves or falls through gaps in the canopy to the ground [1]. Stemflow is water that runs down branches, stems and trunks to the ground [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Two outputs from evaporation, evapotranspiration and river discharge [1]. Evapotranspiration is an output because the water leaves the basin as vapour to the atmosphere [1], crossing the system boundary rather than moving between stores within the basin [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Water percolates down through the permeable rock until it reaches the impermeable rock below [1]. It cannot pass downwards, so the water table is held up and water moves sideways along the boundary [1]. Where this boundary meets the ground surface, often along the foot of a hill, water emerges as a series of springs along the same level [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Infiltration is the movement of water from the ground surface into the soil [1]. Percolation is the downward movement of water from the soil into the rock beneath [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Interception loss = 20 - 13.0 = 7.0 mm [1]; 7.0 / 20 x 100 = 35% [1].

**(b)** Conifers keep their needles all year and have a dense canopy with a large surface area, so they hold more water that then evaporates [1]. Deciduous trees intercept much less in winter because they have lost their leaves (loss of 1.6 mm, or 8%, against 5.0 mm, or 25%, in summer) [1]. Grassland has a low, small leaf area, so it stores little water and almost all rain reaches the ground [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Hours 3 and 4 [1].

**(b)** Hour 3: 24 - 16 = 8 mm; hour 4: 20 - 13 = 7 mm [1]. Total = 8 + 7 = 15 mm [1].

**(c)** As rain continues, pores near the surface fill with water [1], so there is less space for more water to enter and the soil approaches saturation [1]. Raindrop impact can also pack fine particles into surface pores, sealing the surface and slowing entry of water [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** The water table is shallowest in March, at 3.2 m [1], and deepest in September, at 9.0 m [1], a range of 5.8 m, rising again from September to January [1].

**(b)** In the wetter, cooler winter, precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, so soils reach field capacity and water percolates down [1], recharging the aquifer and raising the water table [1]. In summer, higher temperatures increase evapotranspiration, so little water percolates and recharge stops [1]. Groundwater continues to drain to the river as baseflow (and may be abstracted), so the water table falls until autumn rains return [1].

**(c)** The stream flows in January, March and May [1], when the water table is less than 6.0 m below the surface and so lies above the stream bed [1]. Groundwater then feeds the channel as baseflow; from July to November the water table is below the bed, so the channel dries, behaving like a winterbourne [1]. **[10]**

**9.** Stores differ in how long they hold water: interception and surface stores hold it for minutes or hours, soil water for days to weeks, and groundwater for months or longer [1]. Water passing through short-residence stores reaches the channel quickly as overland flow [1], producing a rapid rise in discharge soon after rain [1]. Water passing through soil as throughfall arrives later [1]. Water that percolates into groundwater is released slowly as baseflow [1], so a basin with large long-residence stores shows a delayed, smaller response and keeps flowing between storms [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Dense vegetation increases interception storage, so less rain reaches the ground [1] and more is lost directly as interception loss by evaporation [1]. Throughfall and stemflow delay the arrival of water at the surface, slowing the response [1]. Roots and leaf litter open up the soil, raising infiltration capacity [1], so overland flow is reduced and throughflow and percolation are increased [1]. Transpiration draws down soil water storage, leaving space to absorb the next rainfall [1]. Evergreen forest has these effects all year, whereas deciduous woodland intercepts and transpires much less in winter [1]. Sparse cover, such as grassland or bare soil, gives little interception, lower infiltration and more overland flow [1]. **[8]**

**11.** For the view: groundwater is usually the largest store of fresh liquid water in a basin [1]. It supplies baseflow, which keeps rivers flowing between storms and through dry seasons [1]. Its slow release smooths out river regimes and reduces flood peaks in permeable basins [1]. Springs and wells depend on it, so it supports water supply [1].

Against the view: in basins on impermeable rock, groundwater storage is small and soil water and surface stores dominate the response [1]. Soil water controls whether rain infiltrates or runs off, so it decides how flashy the response is [1]. Interception decides how much rain reaches the ground at all, and can return a large share of light rainfall to the atmosphere [1]. Channel storage and lakes or wetlands can be large and buffer floods [1].

Judgement: importance depends on the timescale considered, since groundwater dominates low flows over months while soil and surface stores dominate the storm response over hours [1]. It also depends on the basin's geology, so the statement is most true for permeable basins such as chalk or sandstone and least true for impermeable ones [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating interception loss as the throughfall figure instead of subtracting it from the rainfall.
- Describing water table data without quoting the highest and lowest values and the months they occur.
- Explaining recharge without linking it to the balance between precipitation and evapotranspiration.
- Treating "groundwater" as the answer to every question without explaining the flow (baseflow) that links it to the river.
- Writing a one-sided answer to a "discuss" question with no judgement.
