## What the specification lists

- Explain cash-flow.
- Distinguish a cash-flow forecast from a cash-flow statement, and cash-flow from profit.
- Explain the purpose of a forecast and a statement.
- Calculate, interpret and analyse forecasts and statements, and the impact of changes in costs and revenue.
- Evaluate the impact and usefulness of forecasts and statements.
- Evaluate strategies to overcome forecast cash-flow problems.

## Core recall

### Meaning

- **Cash-flow:** money moving into and out of a business over a period.
- Inflows: customer payments, loans received, asset sales. Outflows: suppliers, wages, rent, loan repayments, equipment.
- Net cash flow = inflows − outflows. Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.

### Forecast and statement

| | Forecast | Statement |
|---|---|---|
| Time | Future | Past |
| Basis | Estimates | Actual cash |
| Purpose | Plan; warn of shortage; support loans | Report cash sources and uses |
| Layout | Monthly receipts, payments, net flow, balances | Operating, investing, financing activities |

### Cash against profit

| | Profit | Cash |
|---|---|---|
| Credit sales | Counted when sold | Counted when paid |
| Equipment | Depreciated over its life | Paid in full when bought |
| Loan | Not income | Inflow when received |
| Depreciation | A cost | Not a cash movement |

A profitable business can run out of cash (slow payers, stock build-up, equipment purchases).

### Building a forecast

1. Work out receipts month by month (allow for credit terms).
2. List payments (stock, wages, overheads, loans, equipment).
3. Net flow = receipts − payments.
4. Closing balance = opening + net flow; carry forward.
5. Spot the lowest balance and when it occurs.

### Cash-flow statement outline

Profit before tax + depreciation − increase in receivables − increase in inventory + increase in payables = cash from operating activities. Add investing (asset purchases) and financing (loans, dividends) to get net change; add opening cash.

### Effect of changes

- Lower revenue: with credit sales, the effect arrives late and lasts several months.
- Higher costs: felt in the month they are paid.
- Always recalculate the balances.

### Strategies for shortages

| Strategy | Cost or risk |
|---|---|
| Overdraft or loan | Interest; may be refused or withdrawn |
| Lease instead of buy | Higher total cost |
| Faster payment from customers (discounts, factoring) | Discounts; fees |
| Delay paying suppliers | Damaged relationships; lost discounts |
| Cut stock | Stock-outs |
| Sale and leaseback | Future rent; lose asset |
| Cut or delay spending | May harm sales |

Match the strategy to the cause: a one-off purchase suggests leasing; a recurring gap suggests an overdraft or faster collection.

### Usefulness

- Forecast: early warning, planning, loan support; limited by estimates and need for updating.
- Statement: shows real sources of cash; historic.

## Quick worked reminder

Marlow Bikes (invented): opening £8,000; closing balances £7,000, £7,000, £6,000, £0, £0 and −£5,000 (January to June) after an April equipment purchase of £12,000. If sales in March to May fall 10%, June closes at −£14,400. Leasing the equipment at £1,000 a month turns June into +£4,000. Hartwell Joinery: profit before tax £60,000 gives cash from operations of £54,000, but after buying equipment (£40,000), a loan (£20,000) and dividends (£10,000), cash rises only £24,000.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cash inflow | Money received by the business |
| Cash outflow | Money paid out by the business |
| Net cash flow | Inflows minus outflows for a period |
| Opening balance | Cash at the start of the period |
| Closing balance | Cash at the end of the period |
| Overdraft | Borrowing from a bank account up to an agreed limit |
| Credit terms | Agreement to pay after a set period |
| Receivables | Money owed to the business by customers |
| Payables | Money the business owes to suppliers |

## Exam traps

- Cash is not profit: use the date money moves.
- Opening balance = previous closing balance.
- Depreciation is not a cash outflow.
- A loan is a cash inflow, not revenue.
- Link each strategy to the cause of the shortage.

## Self-test

1. Define cash-flow.
2. Distinguish between a forecast and a statement.
3. Explain why a profitable business may run out of cash.
4. Opening balance £6,000; receipts £24,000; payments £21,000. Find the closing balance.
5. Net cash flow is −£5,000 and the opening balance is £3,000. Find the closing balance.
6. Give two inflows other than sales.
7. A sale of £50,000 is made on credit; costs of £30,000 are paid at once. State profit and net cash flow for the month.
8. Why is depreciation not shown in a cash-flow forecast?
9. Give one disadvantage of delaying payment to suppliers.
10. When is leasing a good response to a cash shortage?
11. Give one limit of a cash-flow forecast.
12. Profit before tax is £40,000; depreciation £8,000; receivables up £10,000; inventory down £5,000; payables up £3,000. Calculate cash from operating activities.

### Answers

1. The movement of money into and out of a business over a period.
2. A forecast estimates future cash movements; a statement reports actual past movements.
3. Customers may pay late, stock may build up, or equipment may be bought, so cash leaves before it comes in.
4. £6,000 + £24,000 − £21,000 = **£9,000**.
5. £3,000 − £5,000 = **−£2,000** (an overdraft).
6. Any two: a loan received, a share issue, sale of an asset, interest received.
7. Profit = £20,000; net cash flow = **−£30,000**.
8. It is an accounting cost, but no cash leaves the business.
9. It may damage supplier relationships or cause loss of discounts.
10. When the shortage comes from a large one-off equipment purchase.
11. It rests on estimates, which may be wrong, especially for sales.
12. £40,000 + £8,000 − £10,000 + £5,000 + £3,000 = **£46,000**.
