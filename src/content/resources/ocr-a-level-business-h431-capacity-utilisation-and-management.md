## Core meanings

**Capacity** is the maximum output a business can produce in a given period with its existing resources, such as premises, machines and staff. **Capacity utilisation** shows how much of that capacity is actually being used.

Capacity utilisation (%) = (actual output ÷ maximum possible output) × 100

The specification asks you to calculate it but does not print a formula, so learn this standard form. Always state the time period: the same business may have different capacity per day, per month and per year.

**Capacity management** is planning and controlling capacity so that it matches demand as closely as possible, at the lowest cost and without losing quality or customers. It covers forecasting demand, deciding how much capacity to hold, and choosing how to change it.

Two states are easy to name:

- **Under-utilisation** (spare capacity): the business uses less than it could. Fixed costs are spread over fewer units.
- **Over-utilisation** (over capacity): demand exceeds the normal maximum, so the business runs at over 100% for a short period using overtime, extra shifts or reduced maintenance. This cannot be kept up for long.

## Worked example 1: calculation, cost and profit

Orrin Print (invented) can produce 12,000 units a month. Fixed costs are £48,000 a month and variable costs are £6 a unit. It sells each unit for £11.

| Output | Capacity utilisation | Fixed cost per unit | Profit or loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9,000 | 75% | £5.33 | −£3,000 |
| 10,200 | 85% | £4.71 | +£3,000 |
| 11,400 | 95% | £4.21 | +£9,000 |
| 12,000 | 100% | £4.00 | +£12,000 |

Workings for 9,000 units: utilisation = 9,000 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 75%. Fixed cost per unit = £48,000 ÷ 9,000 = £5.33. Profit = (£11 × 9,000) − (£48,000 + £6 × 9,000) = £99,000 − £102,000 = −£3,000.

Each unit contributes £11 − £6 = £5. Break-even output = £48,000 ÷ £5 = 9,600 units, which is 9,600 ÷ 12,000 = **80%** utilisation. Below 80% Orrin makes a loss. Interpreting this: the business needs to keep utilisation above 80% to cover its costs, and every extra 1,200 units (10% of capacity) adds £6,000 to profit.

**Impact on stakeholders.** Low utilisation may mean short-time working or redundancy for employees, lower returns for owners and weaker bargaining with suppliers because orders are small. Customers may benefit from fast delivery because there is spare capacity.

**Why 100% is not always the aim.** At 100% there is no spare capacity for a rush order, no time for planned maintenance, and a breakdown or sickness cuts output at once. Overworked staff and machines also cause errors and accidents. Many businesses aim for around 85% to 90%, depending on the industry, but this figure is a guide, not a rule from the specification.

## Worked example 2: seasonal demand

Orrin's monthly demand varies through the year.

| Season | Monthly demand | Utilisation |
|---|---|---|
| Winter | 6,000 | 50% |
| Spring | 7,500 | 62.5% |
| Summer | 9,000 | 75% |
| Autumn (pre-Christmas) | 13,500 | 112.5% |

Utilisation in autumn: 13,500 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 112.5%. The business cannot make 13,500 units in a month on its normal capacity, so it faces 1,500 units of unmet demand or must find extra capacity.

In winter, 50% utilisation means Orrin loses money: at 6,000 units, profit = (£5 × 6,000) − £48,000 = −£18,000. Analysis of the pattern:

- Average utilisation across the four seasons is (50 + 62.5 + 75 + 112.5) ÷ 4 = 75%, which looks acceptable but hides a winter of heavy loss and an autumn of lost sales.
- The business faces a choice between holding capacity for the peak (expensive in winter) and holding capacity for the average (losing autumn sales).
- Stock can bridge the gap: building stock in spring and summer lets the factory run at a steadier rate, if the goods do not go out of date and the cost of storing them is lower than the profit gained.

## Capacity management

Good capacity management reduces both lost sales and idle resources. It matters because:

- fixed costs are paid whether or not capacity is used, so spare capacity raises unit cost;
- too little capacity loses customers, delays orders and damages reputation;
- plans for staff, stock and maintenance all depend on expected output.

It has an effect on stakeholders. Customers want reliable delivery; employees want stable hours; suppliers want predictable orders; shareholders want efficient use of assets.

## Worked example 3: recommending a change in capacity

Orrin's demand has grown to a steady 15,000 units a month. The directors have three options.

**Option A: do nothing.** Output is limited to 12,000 units. Profit = £5 × 12,000 − £48,000 = £12,000. Demand of 3,000 units a month is lost.

**Option B: add a night shift.** Capacity rises by 4,000 to 16,000, with extra fixed costs of £9,000 a month (supervisors and lighting). Utilisation at 15,000 units = 15,000 ÷ 16,000 × 100 = 93.75%. Profit = £5 × 15,000 − (£48,000 + £9,000) = £75,000 − £57,000 = £18,000. This is £6,000 more than option A.

**Option C: subcontract the extra 3,000 units** at £8.50 a unit. Contribution on those units = (£11 − £8.50) × 3,000 = £7,500, so profit = £12,000 + £7,500 = £19,500.

**Comparison.** At 15,000 units subcontracting adds £7,500 and the night shift adds £6,000. The break-even point between them is where £5 × x − £9,000 = £2.50 × x, so x = £9,000 ÷ £2.50 = 3,600 extra units a month. If demand stays under 3,600 units above 12,000, subcontracting is cheaper; above that, the night shift is better.

**Judgement.** If the demand of 15,000 is permanent, the night shift gives more control over quality and delivery and gains more as demand rises, so it is likely to be the better long-term choice. If demand is uncertain, subcontracting avoids new fixed costs and can be stopped quickly, but gives less control over quality and may reveal methods to a competitor. A sensible recommendation might be to subcontract first and add the shift if demand holds for six months.

## Other ways to change capacity

- **Increase:** buy or rent more space or machines; add shifts or overtime; hire temporary staff; subcontract; use technology to raise output per hour.
- **Reduce:** sell or close part of the site; reduce shifts; make staff redundant, which is costly and damages morale; rent out spare space; move to a smaller site.
- Increasing capacity needs finance and takes time. Reducing capacity brings redundancy costs and may lose skills that are hard to recover.

## Improving capacity management

- **Better forecasting:** use sales history, orders and market research to predict demand.
- **Flexible staffing:** annualised hours, part-time and temporary staff, and multi-skilled workers who can move between tasks.
- **Smoothing demand:** off-peak discounts, booking systems and seasonal products.
- **Planned maintenance:** carry out maintenance in quiet periods to avoid breakdowns at peak times.
- **Use of stock:** produce for stock in quiet periods for products with a long shelf life.
- **Flexible equipment and layouts:** machines that can switch between products reduce idle time.

Each has a cost. Forecasting can be wrong, flexible contracts may reduce commitment, and discounts reduce revenue per unit.

## Evaluating

For a recommendation question, structure your answer like this.

1. Calculate current utilisation and say what it shows.
2. Decide whether the problem is spare capacity or lack of capacity.
3. State the options and compare their cost, speed and control.
4. Consider how permanent the change in demand is.
5. Consider stakeholders: staff, customers, suppliers, owners.
6. Make a judgement, justified by the figures and the objectives of the business.

## Common mistakes

- Using the wrong base: utilisation is actual output divided by **maximum** output, not by current demand.
- Saying that 100% utilisation is always best.
- Treating an average utilisation figure as proof that there is no problem when demand is seasonal.
- Recommending a new factory for a short-lived increase in demand.
- Forgetting that fixed costs rise when capacity rises.

## Exam technique

Show the formula, the working and the percentage with a unit. A calculation question is often followed by "analyse" or "evaluate", so say what the figure means for cost, profit or customers. Link every recommendation to the case, such as the length of the demand increase and the cash available.
