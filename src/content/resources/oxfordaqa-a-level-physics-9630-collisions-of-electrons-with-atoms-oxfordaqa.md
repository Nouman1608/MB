## The electronvolt in practice

An electron accelerated from rest through a potential difference V gains kinetic energy eV. In electronvolts, that energy is simply the number of volts: through 12 V an electron gains 12 eV, which is 12 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.92 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.

This makes the electronvolt the natural unit for collisions: if electrons are accelerated through 6.0 V and the first energy gap in an atom is 4.8 eV, you can see at once that excitation is possible.

## Energy levels and what collisions do

An atom's electrons can only have certain energies. The levels are negative, because energy must be supplied to remove an electron to zero (ionisation). The lowest level is the **ground state**.

When a free electron collides with an atom in its ground state:

| Electron's kinetic energy | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Less than the first energy gap | Elastic collision: the atom is not excited and the electron keeps its energy |
| At least one gap, but less than the ionisation energy | The atom may be excited to any level the electron can reach; the electron keeps the rest |
| At least the ionisation energy | The atom may be ionised; the energy left over is shared as kinetic energy |

The free electron can give **part** of its energy, because it carries on with whatever is left. A photon behaves differently: it is absorbed whole, so it can only excite an atom if its energy exactly matches a gap (or exceeds the ionisation energy).

### Worked example 1: an electron beam through a gas

Atoms of a gas have levels at −10.0 eV (ground state), −5.2 eV, −3.6 eV and −1.8 eV. Electrons accelerated through 6.0 V pass through the gas.

```
gap ground → −5.2 eV: 10.0 − 5.2 = 4.8 eV   (possible: 6.0 > 4.8)
gap ground → −3.6 eV: 10.0 − 3.6 = 6.4 eV   (not possible: 6.0 < 6.4)
electron's energy after exciting the atom: 6.0 − 4.8 = 1.2 eV
```

The excited atoms de-excite from −5.2 eV to the ground state, emitting photons of 4.8 eV:

```
E = 4.8 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 7.68 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
λ = hc / E = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ / 7.68 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.59 × 10⁻⁷ m
```

That is 259 nm, in the ultraviolet. Raising the accelerating voltage above 6.4 V would allow the −3.6 eV level to be reached, and new lines would appear.

### The fluorescent tube, as a chain of energy changes

1. The applied p.d. gives free electrons kinetic energy.
2. Collisions **excite** mercury atoms; some collisions **ionise** atoms, freeing more electrons and keeping the current flowing.
3. The excited atoms de-excite, emitting mostly **ultraviolet** photons.
4. The **phosphor coating** absorbs each ultraviolet photon, which excites its atoms to a high level. They de-excite in several smaller steps, emitting **visible** photons of lower energy.

A common examination question asks why the coating is needed: without it most of the emitted energy would be invisible ultraviolet.

## Line spectra as evidence

A low-pressure gas, excited by a current, emits light only at certain wavelengths. Each line is one photon energy:

hf = E1 – E2

Because the levels are fixed, only fixed energy differences, and so only fixed wavelengths, appear. A continuous spectrum would be expected if the atom could have any energy. The line spectrum is therefore evidence that energy levels are **discrete**.

The same evidence appears in reverse. When white light passes through a cool gas, dark absorption lines appear at exactly the wavelengths the gas emits when hot, because photons are absorbed only when their energy matches a gap.

### Worked example 2: lines from atomic hydrogen

Hydrogen's lowest levels are −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV and −0.85 eV.

```
n = 3 → n = 2: ΔE = 3.40 − 1.51 = 1.89 eV = 3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
λ = hc / ΔE = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.58 × 10⁻⁷ m (red)

n = 4 → n = 2: ΔE = 3.40 − 0.85 = 2.55 eV → λ = 488 nm (blue-green)
n = 2 → n = 1: ΔE = 13.6 − 3.40 = 10.2 eV → λ = 122 nm (ultraviolet)
```

Transitions that end on n = 2 fall in the visible range. Transitions to the ground state release much larger energies and lie in the ultraviolet.

### Counting the possible lines

An atom excited to level n can return to the ground state by many routes. Every pair of levels gives one line, so the number of different lines from the top n levels is n(n − 1)/2. From n = 4: 4 × 3/2 = **6 lines** (4→3, 4→2, 4→1, 3→2, 3→1, 2→1).

## X-rays

### Structure and operation of the tube

- An **evacuated** glass tube, so electrons are not stopped by gas molecules.
- A **heated filament** (the cathode) releases electrons by thermionic emission. The filament current controls how many electrons are released per second.
- A **high p.d.**, typically tens of kilovolts, accelerates the electrons to the **anode**, a target of a metal such as tungsten.
- Only a small fraction of the electrons' energy becomes X-rays; the rest heats the target, which must be **cooled**.

### Two parts of the spectrum

- The **continuous spectrum** comes from electrons decelerating in the target. The shortest wavelength corresponds to an electron giving all of its energy eV to a single photon: λ(min) = hc/eV.
- The **characteristic line spectrum** comes from the target atoms. An incident electron knocks an electron out of an inner level; an electron from a higher level falls into the vacancy and emits a photon equal to the energy difference. The line wavelengths depend only on the target metal.

### Worked example 3: changing the tube settings

A tube is operated at 30 kV and then at 60 kV.

```
30 kV: λ(min) = hc / eV = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 30 × 10³) = 4.14 × 10⁻¹¹ m
60 kV: λ(min) = 2.07 × 10⁻¹¹ m
```

| Change | λ(min) | Characteristic lines | Intensity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher tube voltage | Shorter (halves when V doubles) | Same wavelengths | Greater at all wavelengths |
| Higher filament current | Unchanged | Same wavelengths | Greater, same shape |
| Different target metal | Unchanged | Different wavelengths | Depends on the metal |

### Medical applications

- **Imaging**: bone absorbs X-rays more than soft tissue, so bones appear as lighter shadows on the detector. CT scanning builds cross-sectional images from many X-ray views.
- **Therapy**: high-energy X-ray beams are directed at a tumour to destroy cancer cells.
- X-rays ionise tissue, so exposure is kept as low as possible and staff are shielded.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a free electron needs exactly the gap energy to excite an atom. It needs at least that much.
- Forgetting that the electron keeps the energy left over after an excitation.
- Using the energy of a level instead of the difference between two levels in hf = E1 – E2.
- Mixing eV and J in one calculation.
- Saying the phosphor "converts ultraviolet into light" with no mention of absorption and re-emission at lower photon energy.
- Thinking a higher filament current gives shorter-wavelength X-rays. Only the tube voltage changes λ(min).
- Saying the characteristic lines shift when the voltage changes.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** line spectra as evidence: discrete levels, photon energy equals a level difference, so only certain frequencies appear.
- **Calculate** a wavelength from levels: find the difference in eV, convert to joules, then use λ = hc/ΔE. Keep three significant figures.
- **Describe** the X-ray tube: filament, vacuum, high p.d., target and cooling, each with its purpose.
- **Compare** electron and photon excitation: partial energy transfer for electrons, all-or-nothing for photons.
