## Questions

**1.** State the meaning of exports and of imports, and give one example of a service export. **[3]**

**2.** Country D has a GDP of 200 billion, exports of 70 billion and imports of 64 billion.
**(a)** Calculate exports as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate exports plus imports as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why a country with a high figure in (b) is more exposed to events abroad. **[2]**

**3.** Explain two consumer benefits of international trade. **[4]**

**4.** In a day, Country X can produce either 12 bikes or 24 helmets, and Country Y either 10 bikes or 40 helmets. At present each country uses half of its day on each good.
**(a)** Work out what one bike costs, in helmets, in each country. **[2]**
**(b)** State which good each country should specialise in, and why. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the extra output of each good from specialisation. **[2]**

**5.** A firm has fixed costs of 60,000 and variable costs of 3 per unit. It sells 10,000 units at home and could sell 25,000 if it exported.
**(a)** Calculate average cost at each output. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why exporting lowers average cost. **[2]**

**6.** Country M sends 35% of its exports to Country N. Sales to N fall by 20% because of a recession there.
**(a)** Calculate the fall in M's total exports, assuming other sales are unchanged. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one benefit of global interdependence. **[2]**

**7.** "Free trade is always better for a country than protecting its industries." Evaluate this statement. **[8]**

**8.** Country A and Country B form a free-trade agreement. Before it, A paid a 6% tariff on machines that cost 8,000 each.
**(a)** Explain what a free-trade agreement is. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the price of a machine before the agreement, and the saving for a firm buying 40 machines. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one drawback of the agreement for Country A. **[2]**

**9.** The currency of Country A is the tano. The table shows the market for tano against the ruvi.

| Ruvi per tano | Quantity demanded (million) | Quantity supplied (million) |
|---|---|---|
| 1.20 | 40 | 100 |
| 1.10 | 55 | 85 |
| 1.00 | 70 | 70 |
| 0.90 | 85 | 55 |
| 0.80 | 100 | 40 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium exchange rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the quantity of tano demanded rises as the rate falls. **[2]**
**(c)** Foreign demand for Country A's exports rises, adding 30 million to demand at every rate. State the new equilibrium rate and the percentage by which the tano has moved. **[3]**

**10.** Explain two reasons why a currency might depreciate. **[4]**

**11.** The kelm falls from 2.5 to 2.0 zorn per kelm.
**(a)** Work out the percentage fall in the kelm's value. **[1]**
**(b)** A machine part from the zorn country costs 500 zorn. Calculate its price in kelm before and after the fall. **[2]**
**(c)** An exporter sells 12,000 units at a fixed price of 50 zorn. Calculate its revenue in kelm before and after the fall. **[2]**
**(d)** State one reason why the exporter's profit may rise by less than its revenue. **[1]**

**12.** "A fall in the value of Country A's currency benefits its economy." Evaluate this statement. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Exports are goods and services that a country sells to buyers abroad [1]. Imports are goods and services that its residents and firms buy from abroad [1]. Example: foreign tourists staying at a hotel in the country, or a bank selling services abroad [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Definitions are stronger when they name both goods and services and say which country is buying or selling. An example of a physical good does not answer a question that asks for a service.

**2. (a)** 70/200 x 100 = **35%** [1].
**(b)** (70 + 64)/200 x 100 = **67%** [1].
**(c)** A large share of output depends on trade [1], so a fall in foreign demand or a rise in import prices has a bigger effect on income and jobs [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the sum before the answer. A bare "67%" gives the reader nothing to follow if the arithmetic has slipped, while a short line of working makes the method visible.

**3.** First benefit: imports add competition [1], which pushes prices down and raises quality [1]. Second benefit: trade widens choice [1], so consumers can buy goods the country cannot make, such as tropical fruit in a cold climate [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each benefit needs a reason, not only a name. "Lower prices" earns less than "lower prices because foreign rivals force local firms to cut costs".

**4. (a)** Country X: 24 helmets/12 bikes = **2 helmets per bike** [1]. Country Y: 40/10 = **4 helmets per bike** [1].
**(b)** Country X specialises in bikes, because it gives up fewer helmets per bike [1]. Country Y specialises in helmets, because it gives up fewer bikes per helmet (0.25 against 0.5) [1].
**(c)** Before: X makes 6 bikes and 12 helmets, Y makes 5 bikes and 20 helmets, so the totals are 11 bikes and 32 helmets. After: 12 bikes and 40 helmets. Gains: **1 bike** [1] and **8 helmets** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Opportunity cost must be stated per unit of one good, with the other good named. Quoting "2" with no unit is unclear.

**5. (a)** At 10,000 units: 3 + 60,000/10,000 = **9** [1]. At 25,000 units: 3 + 60,000/25,000 = **5.4** [1].
**(b)** Fixed costs do not change with output [1], so spread over more units they add less to each unit's cost [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Explanations of economies of scale gain from naming which cost is spread. Saying only "it is cheaper to make more" repeats the question.

**6. (a)** 0.35 x 20% [1] = **7%** [1].
**(b)** Interdependence gives access to goods and markets that a country lacks [1], so firms can sell more and households can buy a wider range, for example imported inputs the country cannot produce [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Calculate" questions on shares want the multiplication shown. The common slip is to quote the 20% as the fall in all exports.

**7.** For free trade: lower prices and more choice for consumers [1], because foreign competition forces domestic firms to cut costs [1]. Also specialisation raises total output [1]. For protection: infant industries may need temporary shelter until they grow large enough to compete [1], and a sudden flood of imports can destroy jobs that take years to replace [1]. Judgement: the statement is too strong because "always" ignores cases such as new industries or short-term unemployment [1]. Free trade is usually better in the long run when workers can retrain and the protected firm would otherwise stay inefficient [1]. It depends on how large and how lasting the job losses are [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers weigh both sides and reach a conclusion that responds to the word "always". An answer listing four points with no judgement stays descriptive.

**8. (a)** An agreement between two or more countries [1] to remove tariffs and other barriers on trade between them [1].
**(b)** Price with tariff: 8,000 x 1.06 = **8,480** [1]. Tariff per machine: 8,000 x 0.06 = 480 [1]. Saving for 40 machines: 40 x 480 = **19,200** [1].
**(c)** Domestic machine makers in A now face cheaper imports from B [1], so some may lose sales and jobs [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A drawback must be a consequence for Country A, with a reason. A general comment such as "it is not fair" does not show how A is affected.

**9. (a)** **1.00 ruvi per tano** [1].
**(b)** A lower rate makes Country A's goods cheaper for foreign buyers [1], so they buy more and need more tano [1].
**(c)** New demand is 70, 85, 100, 115, 130 [1]. It equals supply at 1.10 (85 = 85) [1]. Change = (1.10 - 1.00)/1.00 x 100 = **10% appreciation** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* When asked to find a new equilibrium, show the shifted column. Answers that guess the new rate without working are hard to follow if wrong.

**10.** First reason: interest rates fall at home [1], so foreign savers move funds elsewhere, which lowers demand for the currency [1]. Second reason: residents buy more imports [1], which means more of the currency is sold to pay for them, raising supply [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason should name the curve that shifts and in which direction. Saying "people lose confidence" without linking it to demand or supply is incomplete.

**11. (a)** (2.0 - 2.5)/2.5 x 100 = **-20%** [1].
**(b)** Before: 500/2.5 = **200 kelm** [1]. After: 500/2.0 = **250 kelm** [1].
**(c)** Revenue in zorn = 12,000 x 50 = 600,000. Before: 600,000/2.5 = **240,000 kelm** [1]. After: 600,000/2.0 = **300,000 kelm** [1].
**(d)** The firm may use imported parts, whose cost in kelm rises [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Check which currency the rate is quoted in before dividing or multiplying. The rate here is zorn per kelm, so zorn amounts are divided by it.

**12.** Benefits: exports become cheaper abroad [1], so volumes may rise, helping exporters and jobs [1]. Local goods become cheaper than imports, which helps firms facing import competition [1]. Costs: imports become dearer [1], so households face a higher cost of living and firms using imported materials face higher costs [1]. Judgement: the gain depends on how responsive foreign demand is to price [1] and on how much the economy relies on imported inputs [1]. Over a longer period, firms may adjust and the effect may fade, so the overall effect is positive only if demand is elastic enough to outweigh higher costs [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say who gains and who loses, then judge using elasticity or time. Stating only that exports rise treats a depreciation as simply good.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the rate when the answer needs multiplying, because the currency pair was not read carefully.
- Dividing a change by the new figure rather than the starting one.
- Giving a benefit of trade with no reason, such as "more choice" and nothing more.
- Treating all exports as hit by a fall in sales to one partner.
- Saying depreciation makes imports cheaper.
- Drawing or describing currency demand as upward sloping.
- Describing a free-trade agreement as free trade with every country.
- Finishing an evaluation by repeating points without reaching a judgement.
