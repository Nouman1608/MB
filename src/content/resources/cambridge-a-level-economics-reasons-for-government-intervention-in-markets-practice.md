## Questions

**1.** State the two characteristics of a public good. **[2]**

**2.** A fictional village has 250 households. Each values new footpath lighting at 18 currency units (CU). The lighting costs 3,000 CU.

**(a)** Calculate the total benefit to the village. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the net benefit of providing the lighting. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why a private firm is unlikely to provide the lighting. **[3]**

**3.** For each good, state whether it has the characteristics of a public good, with a reason. **[3]**

- a car park with a ticket barrier
- a sea wall that protects every home in a bay
- a cinema

**4.** In a fictional market for dental check-ups, quantity is in thousands of check-ups and price is in CU. Perceived demand is P = 60 − 3Q. Demand if consumers were fully informed is P = 72 − 3Q. Supply is P = 6 + 3Q.

**(a)** Find the market equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the quantity and price if consumers were fully informed. **[2]**
**(c)** State the under-consumption. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain one reason why consumers might underestimate the benefit of a check-up. **[2]**

**5.** In a fictional market for an energy drink, quantity is in thousands of cans and price is in CU. Perceived demand is P = 48 − 2Q. Demand if consumers were fully informed is P = 36 − 2Q. Supply is P = 8 + 2Q.

**(a)** Find the market equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the quantity and price if consumers were fully informed. **[2]**
**(c)** State the over-consumption. **[1]**
**(d)** State where the informed demand curve lies relative to perceived demand. **[1]**

**6.** Explain two reasons why a government might set a maximum price in a market. **[4]**

**7.** A fictional city has a market for student flats. Demand is Qd = 900 − 6P and supply is Qs = 4P − 100, where Q is the number of flats and P is rent in CU a week.

**(a)** Work out the equilibrium rent and number of flats. **[2]**
**(b)** The government sets a maximum rent of 80 CU. Calculate the shortage. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the change in the total weekly rent paid. **[2]**

**8.** A fictional country has a market for a staple grain. Demand is Qd = 800 − 10P and supply is Qs = 6P − 160, in tonnes, with P in CU per tonne.

**(a)** Work out the market equilibrium. **[2]**
**(b)** The government sets a minimum price of 75 CU. Calculate the surplus. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost of buying the whole surplus at 75 CU. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the change in consumer spending on the grain. **[2]**

**9.** In a fictional market for a harmful drink, a minimum price raises the price from 8 CU to 10 CU. Quantity bought falls from 300 to 270 units.

**(a)** Work out the price elasticity of demand (PED). **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in consumer spending. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what the result suggests about how well the policy cuts consumption of a demerit good. **[2]**

**10.** Assess whether a government should provide a public good, such as flood defences, itself rather than leave provision to the market. **[8]**

**11.** In Country A the price of a staple food has risen sharply and the government is considering a maximum price. Evaluate this policy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** It is non-excludable: non-payers cannot be stopped from using it [1]. It is non-rival: one person's use does not reduce what is available to others [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give each feature a short explanation, because naming the term alone leaves it unclear whether you know what it means.

**2. (a)** 250 × 18 = **4,500 CU** [1]
**(b)** 4,500 − 3,000 = **1,500 CU** [1]
**(c)** Lighting is non-excludable, so a firm cannot stop non-payers using it [1]. Households can free-ride, hoping others will pay, so few will pay voluntarily [1]. The firm cannot earn enough revenue to cover 3,000 CU, so the market supplies none [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong explanation links the three steps in order: non-excludable, free-riding, no revenue. A list of the terms with no chain earns less.

**3.** The car park does not: the barrier makes it excludable [1]. The sea wall does: everyone in the bay benefits, so it is non-excludable and non-rival [1]. The cinema does not: it is excludable and rival, because a seat can be filled [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Verdicts need the reason attached. A bare yes or no does not show the characteristic.

**4. (a)** 60 − 3Q = 6 + 3Q, so 54 = 6Q [1]. Q = **9 thousand** and P = 6 + 27 = **33 CU** [1]
**(b)** 72 − 3Q = 6 + 3Q, so 66 = 6Q, so Q = **11 thousand** [1]. P = 6 + 33 = **39 CU** [1]
**(c)** 11 − 9 = **2 thousand check-ups** [1]
**(d)** Benefits appear later, such as avoiding future problems [1], so consumers undervalue it now and buy less than if they had full information [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the cause of imperfect information to the quantity bought. A statement that people ignore the check-up is only half the point.

**5. (a)** 48 − 2Q = 8 + 2Q, so 40 = 4Q [1]. Q = **10 thousand** and P = 8 + 20 = **28 CU** [1]
**(b)** 36 − 2Q = 8 + 2Q, so 28 = 4Q, so Q = **7 thousand** [1]. P = 8 + 14 = **22 CU** [1]
**(c)** 10 − 7 = **3 thousand cans** [1]
**(d)** It lies below and to the left of perceived demand [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtract in the right direction for a demerit good. The market quantity is larger, so over-consumption is market quantity minus informed quantity.

**6.** First reason: to keep an essential good affordable for low-income households [1], because a price below equilibrium lets more of them buy it [1]. Second reason: to limit price rises where a few sellers could charge a high price or where a shortage has pushed the price up [1], which holds down the cost of living [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Develop each reason with a further step. Two reasons stated with no explanation leave the analysis missing.

**7. (a)** 900 − 6P = 4P − 100, so 1,000 = 10P [1]. P = **100 CU** and Q = 900 − 600 = **300 flats** [1]
**(b)** At 80: Qd = 900 − 480 = 420 [1] and Qs = 320 − 100 = 220, so the shortage is **200 flats** [1]
**(c)** Before: 100 × 300 = 30,000 CU. After: 80 × 220 = 17,600 CU [1]. Change = **−12,400 CU a week** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use quantity supplied, not quantity demanded, for the rent bill, because only 220 flats are actually let at the controlled rent.

**8. (a)** 800 − 10P = 6P − 160, so 960 = 16P [1]. P = **60 CU** and Q = **200 tonnes** [1]
**(b)** At 75: Qd = 800 − 750 = 50 [1] and Qs = 450 − 160 = 290, so the surplus is **240 tonnes** [1]
**(c)** 240 × 75 = **18,000 CU** [1]
**(d)** Before: 60 × 200 = 12,000 CU. After: 75 × 50 = 3,750 CU [1]. Change = **−8,250 CU** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A minimum price above equilibrium cuts what consumers buy, so spending is price times quantity demanded, not quantity supplied.

**9. (a)** Percentage change in price = 2 ÷ 8 × 100 = 25%, and in quantity = −30 ÷ 300 × 100 = −10% [1]. PED = −10 ÷ 25 = **−0.4** [1]
**(b)** Before: 8 × 300 = 2,400 CU. After: 10 × 270 = 2,700 CU. Spending rises by **300 CU** [1]
**(c)** Demand is inelastic [1], so quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises, and consumption is cut only a little while sellers gain revenue [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the percentage changes before dividing, and keep the sign. An answer of 0.4 with no sign and no working hides both the method and the direction of the change.

**10.** Knowledge: a public good is non-excludable and non-rival [1], so free-riders mean a private firm cannot earn revenue and the market provides none [1]. Analysis: if benefit to society exceeds cost, provision raises welfare [1]. Funding by compulsory tax removes the free-rider option [1]. The state can pay a private firm, so it need not produce the good [1]. Evaluation: the government has to estimate how much people value the good [1], and the money has an opportunity cost in other uses [1]. The case is strongest for goods close to pure public goods, and weaker where people can be excluded and charged, so a judgement should depend on how pure the good is [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that is supported by a condition, such as how pure the good is, reads as evaluation. A list of drawbacks with no conclusion does not.

**11.** Knowledge and analysis: a maximum price is set below the market price [1]. It makes the food cheaper for households who obtain it [1], which helps low-income families most because food takes a large share of their spending [1]. At the lower price, quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls [1], so a shortage appears [1]. Evaluation: the shortage can lead to queues, black markets and lower quality [1]. Some farmers may stop producing, which makes the shortage worse over time because supply is more elastic in the long run [1]. The gain depends on who gets the food, and those who cannot buy lose [1]. Alternatives such as raising incomes of the poor may reach the aim more directly [1]. A final judgement should say when the policy works best: short-lived, with rationing for fairness [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention both gainers and losers and then reach a conclusion. An answer that only lists the shortage is a one-sided account.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating a good as public simply because the state provides it.
- Naming one characteristic of a public good rather than both.
- Dividing the wrong way when finding under-consumption or over-consumption.
- Using the quantity demanded where the quantity supplied is the one traded.
- Forgetting to substitute the controlled price into both Qd and Qs.
- Applying a ceiling above equilibrium or a floor below it.
- Writing a list of evaluation points and no overall judgement.
- Losing the sign on a price elasticity of demand and not showing percentage changes.
