These notes cover Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239). They follow the Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239) syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028, Version 3 (published December 2025), section 4 (Details of the assessment) and the assessment objectives. Component 4 is externally marked by Cambridge International, is taken only on an A Level route, and is worth 50% of the A Level.

Keep the [9239 subject hub](/boards/cambridge/a-level/global-perspectives/) and the [9239 checklist](/checklists/cambridge/a-level/global-perspectives/) open as you revise, and test yourself with the [Component 4 practice questions](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report-practice/).

## The rules at a glance

| Item | What the syllabus prints |
|---|---|
| Total marks | 85: research report 75, research log 10 |
| Length | Up to 5000 words; work beyond 5000 words is not marked |
| Word count | An accurate word count must be clearly stated |
| Not counted | The title and bibliographical references; the log is also outside the limit |
| Form | Continuous prose; introduction, main body, conclusion; sub-headings allowed |
| Title | Must take the form of a question |
| Audience | A non-specialist reader |
| References | Bibliography required; full references for any quotations; one system used consistently (Harvard, Chicago or APA are named as common) |
| AO weighting in Component 4 | AO1 65%, AO2 20%, AO3 15% |
| Submitted | Report, research log, research proposal form (proposal not externally marked) |
| Other | Electronic submission; Declaration of Authenticity; plagiarism detection software used |

Critical Path skills mapped to Component 4 in the syllabus: Deconstruction, Reconstruction, Reflection and Communication. Collaboration is not part of this component.

## The eleven assessment criteria

You are assessed on your ability to:

1. use a research log to record, plan and reflect on research (AO1, AO2)
2. reflect on research findings and decisions (AO2)
3. analyse your research question (AO1)
4. use appropriate research methods (AO1)
5. select and analyse relevant concepts (AO1)
6. use and assess sources to support arguments and perspectives (AO1)
7. build and compare contrasting perspectives (AO1)
8. reflect on how alternative perspectives have influenced your argument (AO2)
9. make judgements supported by evidence (AO1)
10. reflect on your conclusions (AO2)
11. structure the report effectively, use accurate terminology and complete, appropriate citation and referencing (AO3).

The level descriptors examiners use are not printed in the syllabus; your teacher will confirm them.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Research question | The single question the report answers; it must lead to a debate |
| Key concepts | Terms or ideas relevant to the topic (e.g. gender and feminism for a project on women in poetry) |
| Perspective | "A coherent world view which is a response to an issue": argument, evidence and assumptions, possibly shaped by context |
| Argument | One or more reasons leading to a conclusion |
| Assumption | A reason that is not stated but is still part of an argument |
| Primary evidence | First-hand information: documents, texts, artefacts, observations, interviews, surveys |
| Secondary evidence | Specialists' arguments and opinions that reflect on or synthesise primary evidence |
| Provenance | A source's author and where or when it was written |
| Research methods | The ways you gather and analyse evidence |
| Methodology | The study of why particular methods suit specific academic subject areas |
| Research log | A separate record of information, plans, findings, decisions and reflection |
| Research proposal | A form outlining question, concepts, debate and example sources, approved by your teacher |
| Plagiarism | Passing off someone else's work or ideas as your own |

## Research methods at a glance

The syllabus lists these methods. The strengths and limitations are general research points, not syllabus wording.

| Method | What it gives you | Strength | One limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Review of secondary literature | The main arguments in the field | Expert depth; shows where specialists disagree | You inherit the authors' choices and assumptions |
| Close analysis of texts | Detailed reading of language and meaning | Precise evidence from the text itself | Readings can be contested |
| Interviews, focus groups, ethnography | Qualitative insight into views and experiences | Depth; follow-up questions | Small samples; interviewer influence |
| Statistical or numerical data analysis | Patterns across many cases | Scale; comparison | Data may not measure what you need |
| Surveys, experiments | Your own quantitative data | Control over questions | Sampling bias; shallow answers |
| Mixed methods | Several angles on the question | Weaknesses of one method offset by another | Harder to do well within 5000 words |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs question.** "Urban farming" is a topic. "Should cities give public land to urban farming?" is a question.
- **Open vs loaded question.** A loaded question already assumes its answer ("Why is X harmful?"). An open one lets perspectives compete.
- **Methods vs methodology.** Methods are what you did. Methodology is why those methods suit your subject, and how they shape what you can conclude.
- **Primary vs secondary.** Who produced the evidence, and how close it is to the original situation.
- **Report vs log.** The report argues; the log records and reflects. The log is separate and must not be used to stretch the word limit.
- **Proposal vs report.** The proposal is reviewed by your teacher and submitted, but not externally marked.
- **Summary vs judgement.** A summary repeats both sides. A judgement weighs them and says which is stronger and why.
- **Reflection on perspectives vs reflection on limits.** AO2 wants both: how other views changed your thinking, and the scope and limits of your own research.
- **Teacher support vs teacher input.** Teachers can monitor progress and teach referencing; they must not teach your topic, research for you, or correct drafts.

## Report plan: a typical structure

The syllabus requires an introduction, main body and conclusion. One way to organise 5000 words (your teacher will confirm what suits your project):

1. **Introduction.** State the question. Explain why it is worth asking and how it developed. Define the key concepts for a non-specialist.
2. **Methods and methodology.** What you did, how, and why it suits the question and discipline. Name the limits early.
3. **Perspective A.** Arguments, evidence, assumptions and context. Assess the sources.
4. **Perspective B.** The same treatment, then compare directly with A: where do they disagree, and why?
5. **Further perspective or synthesis.** Only if the evidence supports one.
6. **Evaluation.** Weigh the perspectives using evidence quality and reasoning.
7. **Conclusion.** A clear, supported answer. Reflect on how alternative perspectives changed your view, and on the scope and limits of your research. Suggest further research.
8. **Bibliography and word count.**

**Mini plan for a sample question:** "Should cities give public land to urban farming?"
- Concepts: food security, land value, community.
- Perspective A: food and community benefits (secondary literature, a local interview).
- Perspective B: economic cost of land and housing need (planning data).
- Judgement: depends on land type; vacant land favours A, land zoned for housing favours B.
- Reflection: started strongly for A; housing data shifted the view; small local sample limits the claim.

## Quick self-test

1. How many marks are given to the research report and to the research log?
2. What is the maximum length of the report, and what happens to words beyond it?
3. Which two things does the syllabus exclude from the word count?
4. Name the four things a research proposal should outline.
5. Is the research proposal externally marked?
6. Give the syllabus definition of a perspective.
7. What is the difference between research methods and methodology?
8. Name three referencing systems the syllabus mentions.
9. List the three items submitted for each candidate.
10. Give two things a teacher must not do.
11. What is the AO weighting for Component 4?

### Answers

1. Report 75 marks; log 10 marks; 85 in total.
2. 5000 words; work beyond 5000 words is not marked.
3. The title and bibliographical references.
4. The research question, the relevant key concepts, an outline of the debate, and examples of sources.
5. No. It is reviewed internally and submitted, but not externally marked.
6. A coherent world view which is a response to an issue, made up of argument, evidence and assumptions, and possibly influenced by context.
7. Methods are the ways you collect and analyse evidence; methodology is the study of why particular methods suit particular subject areas.
8. Harvard, Chicago and APA.
9. The Cambridge Research Report, the research log, and the research proposal form.
10. Any two of: teach specific topics or give their own views on them; research for the candidate; correct notes or drafts; prepare any part of the report.
11. AO1 65%, AO2 20%, AO3 15%.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Titles written as statements or topic labels, not questions.
- Loaded questions that only allow one perspective, so "compare contrasting perspectives" cannot be met.
- No explanation of how and why the question developed, even though the log shows it changed.
- Methods named but not justified, and no comment on methodology or its impact on the findings.
- Sources summarised one by one instead of assessed for credibility and placed in perspectives.
- Perspectives presented side by side but never compared directly.
- Conclusions that sit on the fence without a judgement the evidence supports.
- Reflection limited to "I learned a lot", with no account of how views changed or of the report's limits.
- Logs that record dates and tasks but not decisions or reasons.
- Inconsistent referencing, missing bibliography entries, or no stated word count.
