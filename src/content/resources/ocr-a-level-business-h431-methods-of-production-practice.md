## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between job production and batch production. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by flow production. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** advantage of cell production. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Alder Lighting makes a lamp. It could use job, batch or flow production.

| | Job | Batch | Flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed cost per year (£) | 15,000 | 50,000 | 240,000 |
| Variable cost per lamp (£) | 60 | 40 | 22 |

**(a)** Calculate the unit cost of job production at an output of 5,000 lamps. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the unit cost of batch production at an output of 20,000 lamps. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the unit cost of flow production at an output of 40,000 lamps. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the output at which batch production becomes cheaper than job production. **[2]**

**(e)** Evaluate which method Alder Lighting should choose if it expects to sell 8,000 lamps a year but hopes to reach 40,000 within three years. **[8]**

**5.** Alder's packing department has 10 workers, each paid £90 for a 480-minute day. Packing one lamp involves four tasks that take 5, 10, 9 and 6 minutes. Alder introduces a division of labour, which cuts the total time per lamp by 20% when workers specialise. Because tasks cannot be perfectly balanced, the line is 90% efficient.

**(a)** Calculate the daily output before the change. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the daily output after the change. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the labour cost per lamp before and after, to the nearest penny. **[2]**

**(d)** Evaluate the impact of the division of labour on Alder Lighting and its employees. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Job production makes a single, one-off item to a customer's order [1]. Batch production makes a group of identical items together before changing the process for the next group [1]. Job is more flexible and tailored but has a higher unit cost, while batch has lower unit costs but time is lost in changeovers [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Continuous production of standardised items [1] along a line, often highly automated, so that work moves from one stage to the next without stopping [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Cells are small teams with the skills to complete a product, so workers are multi-skilled and more motivated by responsibility [1]; less work is moved between departments, so stock in progress and delays fall [1]; the cell can switch between similar products more easily than a long production line [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** (15,000 + 60 × 5,000) ÷ 5,000 [1] = 315,000 ÷ 5,000 = **£63.00** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** (50,000 + 40 × 20,000) ÷ 20,000 [1] = 850,000 ÷ 20,000 = **£42.50** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** (240,000 + 22 × 40,000) ÷ 40,000 [1] = 1,120,000 ÷ 40,000 = **£28.00** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** 15,000 + 60q = 50,000 + 40q [1]; 20q = 35,000, so q = **1,750 lamps** [1]. **[2]**

**(e)** At 8,000 lamps: job (15,000 + 480,000) ÷ 8,000 = £61.88; batch (50,000 + 320,000) ÷ 8,000 = £46.25; flow (240,000 + 176,000) ÷ 8,000 = £52.00, so batch is cheapest at expected sales [2].
At 40,000 lamps: flow costs £28.00 against batch £41.25 (50,000 + 1,600,000 ÷ 40,000) [1], a saving of £13.25 a lamp, or £530,000 a year (13.25 × 40,000) [1].
Break-even between batch and flow: 50,000 + 40q = 240,000 + 22q, so q = 190,000 ÷ 18 = 10,556 lamps [1]. Flow is only cheaper above this level.
Risks: flow needs £240,000 of fixed costs a year even if sales stay at 8,000, so it would be the more expensive method until demand grows [1].
Judgement: start with batch, which is cheapest now and keeps flexibility, and move to flow when sales are sure to pass about 10,600 lamps a year [1]. A later switch would need investment and planning, so Alder should keep monitoring demand [1]. **[8]**

**5. (a)** Total time = 5 + 10 + 9 + 6 = 30 minutes [1]. Output = 10 × 480 ÷ 30 = **160 lamps** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** New time per lamp = 30 × 0.80 = 24 minutes [1]. Perfectly balanced output = 4,800 ÷ 24 = 200 [1]. At 90% efficiency = **180 lamps** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Wages = 10 × 90 = £900. Before: 900 ÷ 160 = **£5.63** [1]. After: 900 ÷ 180 = **£5.00** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Benefits: output rises by 12.5% (180 ÷ 160) with no extra workers [1]; labour cost per lamp falls by 63p, about 11% [1]; workers become faster through repetition, and less training is needed for each task [1], and lower costs help Alder to compete on price or raise profit.
Costs: repetitive tasks may reduce motivation and raise absence and errors [1]; if one worker is absent, the line may slow or stop [1]; workers have a narrower range of skills, which reduces flexibility and their opportunities [1].
Stakeholders: customers may benefit from lower prices and the owners from higher profit [1].
Judgement: the gains are significant, but Alder should use job rotation or team working to reduce the risks of boredom, and monitor quality and absence [1]. The benefit depends on selling the additional 20 lamps a day [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out fixed costs when calculating unit cost.
- Saying flow production is cheapest at every volume.
- Forgetting the efficiency factor when output is balanced across tasks.
- Ignoring the effect of specialisation on employees.
