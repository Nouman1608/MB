These questions test the section 3.2 row on the rules of criminal law: where the rules come from, the burden and standard of proof, the roles of judge and jury, specific and basic intent, and choosing and applying the right offence.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the burden and standard of proof in a criminal trial, giving an authority. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the source of the offence of murder and the source of the offence of theft. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between a legal burden and an evidential burden. **[2]**

**4.** Name **two** defences for which the defendant bears the legal burden of proof and state the standard. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between specific intent and basic intent offences, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how the roles of the judge and the jury are divided in deciding whether a defendant has committed an attempt. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain, using **one** example, how judges have developed the meaning of words in a criminal statute. **[4]**

**8.** Hamid is charged with murder. He says he acted in self-defence. Explain who must prove what in relation to his defence, and to what standard. **[4]**

**9.** Jo is charged with murder and pleads diminished responsibility. Explain how the burden of proof on this defence differs from the burden for self-defence and suggest a reason for the difference. **[5]**

**10.** Ravi throws a bottle at Tom in a bar, intending to frighten him. It cuts Tom's head, breaking both layers of skin. Explain which non-fatal offence Ravi is most likely to have committed, working down from the most serious possible offence. **[6]**

**11.** Explain why the absence of a criminal code matters for a student applying the rules of criminal law. **[5]**

**12.** Lou takes a friend's bicycle without asking, intending to return it next week. He is stopped by police. Explain how the rules on the source and elements of theft would be applied to decide whether he is guilty of theft. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the offence [1] beyond reasonable doubt, as established in *Woolmington v DPP* (1935) [1].

**2.** Murder is a common law offence, defined by judges in case law [1]; theft is a statutory offence under s1 Theft Act 1968 [1].

**3.** A legal burden is a duty to prove a matter to the required standard [1]; an evidential burden is only a duty to raise enough evidence for the issue to be left to the jury, after which the other side must disprove it [1].

**4.** Insanity [1] and diminished responsibility [1], which the defendant must prove on the balance of probabilities [1].

**5.** A specific intent offence can be committed only with intention [1], for example murder or s18 [1]. A basic intent offence can be committed with recklessness as well as intention [1], for example s20 or battery [1].

**6.** The judge decides whether there is evidence on which the defendant's act could be found to be more than merely preparatory [1]. If so, the question whether it actually was is left to the jury [1], under s4(3) Criminal Attempts Act 1981 [1].

**7.** In s20 Offences Against the Person Act 1861 the word "inflict" was once thought to require a direct application of force [1]. In *R v Ireland; R v Burstow* (1997) the House of Lords held that "inflict" does not require an application of force [1], so psychiatric injury caused by a campaign of stalking could be inflicted GBH [1]. This shows judges adapting a Victorian statute to modern circumstances without Parliament amending it [1]. (Credit equivalent development, such as *Gomez* on "appropriation".)

**8.** Hamid bears only an evidential burden: there must be some evidence that he used force in self-defence [1] before the judge leaves it to the jury [1]. Once raised, the prosecution must disprove self-defence [1] beyond reasonable doubt; if the jury is not sure he was not acting in self-defence, he must be acquitted [1].

**9.** For diminished responsibility, Jo bears the legal burden [1] and must prove the defence on the balance of probabilities under s2(2) Homicide Act 1957 [1]. For self-defence the defendant bears only an evidential burden and the prosecution must disprove it beyond reasonable doubt [1]. A reason for the difference is that the evidence of an abnormality of mental functioning lies mainly with the defendant and his own medical experts [1], and the defence does not deny the killing but seeks to reduce murder to manslaughter [1].

**10.** Start with s18: a cut breaking both layers of skin is a wound (*JCC v Eisenhower*, 1984) [1], but s18 requires an intention to cause GBH, and Ravi intended only to frighten, so s18 fails [1]. Move down to s20: a wound is enough for the actus reus [1]. The mens rea is intention or recklessness as to some harm (*R v Mowatt*, 1968); throwing a bottle at someone suggests he foresaw that some harm might result, which the jury could find [1]. If he did not foresee any harm, s47 is the alternative, since it needs only the mens rea of the assault, which he had by intending to frighten (*R v Savage*, 1991) [1]. Conclusion: s20 is most likely, with s47 as the fallback [1].

**11.** Without a code, the rules are spread across many statutes of different dates and a large body of case law [1]. For common law offences such as murder there is no statutory definition, so the student must define the offence from cases [1]. Even statutory offences depend on judicial interpretation, so the statute alone is not enough [1]. Rules can change through new decisions, so the student must use the current leading authority [1]. The Law Commission's Draft Criminal Code of 1989 was never enacted, so this position continues [1].

**12.** Theft is defined by s1 Theft Act 1968, so each element is taken from ss2–6 [1]. Taking the bicycle is an appropriation under s3, as it is an assumption of the owner's rights [1]. The bicycle is property under s4 [1] and belongs to another under s5 [1]. Dishonesty under s2 and *Ivey* would be for the jury [1], but the key problem is s6: an intention to return the bicycle next week is borrowing, which is intention to permanently deprive only if the borrowing is equivalent to an outright taking, so Lou is unlikely to be guilty of theft (*R v Lloyd*, 1985) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the burden of proof without naming *Woolmington*.
- Saying a defendant must prove self-defence.
- Calling s20 a specific intent offence.
- Jumping to a conclusion on the offence without testing the more serious one first.
