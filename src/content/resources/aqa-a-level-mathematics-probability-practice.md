These questions cover **Section M: Probability** (M1 to M3) of the **AQA A-level Mathematics
(7357) specification**, version 1.3, for A-level exams from June 2018 onwards. Section M is listed
under Paper 3. A calculator is required in every 7357 paper. Questions 8, 10 and 11 are
extended problems. Give answers to 3 significant figures unless told otherwise.

## Questions

**1.** Events A and B are mutually exclusive, with P(A) = 0.3 and P(B) = 0.5.

**(a)** Find P(A ∪ B). **[1]**
**(b)** Find P(A′ ∩ B′). **[1]**

**2.** For events C and D, P(C) = 0.6, P(D) = 0.25 and P(C ∩ D) = 0.15.

**(a)** Determine whether C and D are independent. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Find P(C ∪ D). **[1]**

**3.** A college records how 250 students travel to college.

| | Bus | Car | Walk | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 12 | 48 | 22 | 60 | 130 |
| Year 13 | 42 | 38 | 40 | 120 |
| Total | 90 | 60 | 100 | 250 |

One student is chosen at random.

**(a)** Find the probability that the student walks. **[1]**
**(b)** Given that the student travels by car, find the probability that the student is in Year 13. **[2]**
**(c)** Determine whether the events "travels by bus" and "is in Year 12" are independent. **[3]**

**4.** For events A and B, P(A) = 0.45, P(B) = 0.3 and P(A | B) = 0.5.

**(a)** Find P(A ∩ B). **[1]**
**(b)** Find P(A ∪ B). **[2]**
**(c)** Find P(B | A). **[1]**
**(d)** Find P(B′ | A′). **[2]**

**5.** A box contains 7 green and 5 yellow counters. Two counters are taken at random, without replacement.

**(a)** Show that the probability that both counters are the same colour is 31/66. **[3]**
**(b)** Given that both counters are the same colour, find the probability that they are both green. **[2]**

**6.** Events A, B and C are such that A and B are independent, and C is mutually exclusive with both A and B. P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4 and P(C) = 0.15.

**(a)** Find P(A ∩ B). **[1]**
**(b)** Find the probability that none of A, B and C happens. **[2]**
**(c)** Find P(C | B′). **[2]**

**7.** A machine makes bolts. Each bolt is defective with probability 0.04. Bolts are packed in boxes of 10.

**(a)** Find the probability that a box contains no defective bolts. **[2]**
**(b)** State an assumption you made in part (a), other than the probability being 0.04. **[1]**
**(c)** Defective bolts tend to be made in runs, when the machine drifts out of adjustment. Explain the likely effect of this on your answer to part (a). **[2]**

**8.** On any day, P(rain) = 0.3 at a bakery. It sells out of bread with probability 0.2 on a rainy day and 0.65 on a dry day.

**(a)** Find the probability that the bakery sells out on a given day. **[2]**
**(b)** Given that the bakery sells out, find the probability that it rained. **[2]**
**(c)** Assuming days are independent, find the probability that the bakery sells out on both of two consecutive days. **[2]**
**(d)** Comment on the assumption in part (c). **[1]**

**9.** The mass, X grams, of honey in a jar is modelled by X ~ N(500, 8²). A jar is rejected if X < 488 or X > 515.

**(a)** Find the probability that a jar is rejected. **[2]**
**(b)** Three jars are chosen independently. Find the probability that at least one is rejected. **[2]**
**(c)** Given that a jar is rejected, find the probability that it is underweight. **[2]**

**10.** A spinner gives a score X with the probability distribution below.

| x | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P(X = x) | 0.1 | 0.3 | k | 2k |

**(a)** Find the value of k. **[2]**

The spinner is spun twice, and the spins are independent. E is the event "the total score is 5" and F is the event "the first score is 2".

**(b)** Find P(E). **[3]**
**(c)** Find P(F | E). **[2]**
**(d)** Determine whether E and F are independent. **[2]**

**11.** An archer shoots three arrows. On the first arrow she hits the gold with probability 0.3.

Model A: every arrow hits the gold with probability 0.3, independently.
Model B: after a gold, the next arrow hits the gold with probability 0.4; after a miss, the next arrow hits the gold with probability 0.25.

**(a)** Using Model A, find the probability that exactly one arrow hits the gold. **[2]**
**(b)** Using Model B, find the probability that exactly one arrow hits the gold. **[4]**
**(c)** Using Model B, find the probability that the first arrow hit the gold, given that exactly one arrow hit the gold. **[2]**
**(d)** Give one reason why Model B may be more realistic than Model A. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** P(A ∪ B) = 0.3 + 0.5 = **0.8** [1]
**(b)** P(A′ ∩ B′) = 1 − 0.8 = **0.2** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A follow-through mark is usually allowed in (b) if P(A ∪ B) in (a) is wrong.

**2. (a)** P(C) × P(D) = 0.6 × 0.25 = 0.15 [1]
This equals P(C ∩ D) = 0.15, so **C and D are independent** [1]
**(b)** P(C ∪ D) = 0.6 + 0.25 − 0.15 = **0.7** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The conclusion mark needs both numbers shown and compared; "they are independent" with no product earns nothing.

**3. (a)** P(walks) = 100/250 = **0.4** [1]
**(b)** Car column total is 60, of whom 38 are Year 13 [1]
P(Year 13 | car) = 38/60 = **19/30 = 0.633** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** P(bus) = 90/250 = 0.36 and P(Year 12) = 130/250 = 0.52 [1]
P(bus) × P(Year 12) = 0.36 × 0.52 = 0.1872, while P(bus ∩ Year 12) = 48/250 = 0.192 [1]
0.1872 ≠ 0.192, so the events are **not independent** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b) a denominator of 250 or 120 loses the method mark at once; "given car" means divide by the car total.

**4. (a)** P(A ∩ B) = P(B) × P(A | B) = 0.3 × 0.5 = **0.15** [1]
**(b)** P(A ∪ B) = 0.45 + 0.3 − 0.15 [1] = **0.6** [1]
**(c)** P(B | A) = 0.15 / 0.45 = **1/3** [1]
**(d)** P(A′ ∩ B′) = 1 − 0.6 = 0.4 and P(A′) = 0.55 [1]
P(B′ | A′) = 0.4 / 0.55 = **8/11 = 0.727** (3 s.f.) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Part (d) needs P(A′ ∩ B′) on top, not P(B′) × P(A′); multiplying assumes independence, which (a) shows is false here.

**5. (a)** P(G, G) = 7/12 × 6/11 = 42/132 [1]
P(Y, Y) = 5/12 × 4/11 = 20/132 [1]
P(same) = 42/132 + 20/132 = 62/132 = **31/66** [1]
**(b)** P(both green | same) = (42/132) / (62/132) [1] = **21/31** [1]
*Examiner insight:* On a "show that", give the intermediate value 62/132 before 31/66; jumping straight to the given answer risks the final mark.

**6. (a)** P(A ∩ B) = 0.5 × 0.4 = **0.2** [1]
**(b)** P(A ∪ B) = 0.5 + 0.4 − 0.2 = 0.7, and C does not overlap A or B [1]
P(none) = 1 − 0.7 − 0.15 = **0.15** [1]
**(c)** C lies wholly outside B, so P(C ∩ B′) = 0.15, and P(B′) = 0.6 [1]
P(C | B′) = 0.15 / 0.6 = **0.25** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A labelled Venn sketch with C as a separate circle lets method marks be given even if one region value is wrong.

**7. (a)** P(no defectives) = 0.96¹⁰ [1] = **0.665** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(b)** Bolts are defective **independently** of each other [1]
**(c)** Defectives cluster in a few boxes rather than spreading across many [1]
so more boxes contain none, and the probability is likely to be **higher** than 0.665 [1]
*Examiner insight:* The second mark in (c) needs a direction (higher or lower); "the answer would change" is not enough.

**8. (a)** P(sells out) = 0.3 × 0.2 + 0.7 × 0.65 [1] = 0.06 + 0.455 = **0.515** [1]
**(b)** P(rain | sells out) = 0.06 / 0.515 [1] = **0.117** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** P(both days) = 0.515² [1] = **0.265** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(d)** Rainy days tend to follow rainy days, so sales on consecutive days are probably not independent [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the numerator is the single path "rain and sells out" (0.06), not P(sells out | rain) = 0.2; using 0.2 / 0.515 is a common method-mark loss.

**9. (a)** P(X < 488) = 0.06681 and P(X > 515) = 0.03040 [1]
P(rejected) = 0.06681 + 0.03040 = **0.0972** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(b)** P(at least one rejected) = 1 − (1 − 0.09720)³ [1] = **0.264** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** P(underweight | rejected) = 0.06681 / 0.09720 [1] = **0.687** (3 s.f.) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write each statement, e.g. P(X < 488), before its calculator value; a wrong bare decimal earns no method mark.

**10. (a)** 0.1 + 0.3 + k + 2k = 1 [1]
3k = 0.6, so **k = 0.2** [1]
**(b)** Totals of 5 come from (1, 4), (4, 1), (2, 3), (3, 2) [1]
P(E) = 2(0.1 × 0.4) + 2(0.3 × 0.2) = 0.08 + 0.12 [1] = **0.2** [1]
**(c)** P(F ∩ E) = P(2, 3) = 0.3 × 0.2 = 0.06 [1]
P(F | E) = 0.06 / 0.2 = **0.3** [1]
**(d)** P(F) = 0.3 = P(F | E) [1], so **E and F are independent** [1]
*Examiner insight:* List the ordered pairs in (b); a missed pair loses the accuracy mark, but listed working allows follow-through in (c) and (d).

**11. (a)** P(exactly one) = 3 × 0.3 × 0.7² [1] = **0.441** [1]
**(b)** P(G, M, M) = 0.3 × 0.6 × 0.75 = 0.135 [1]
P(M, G, M) = 0.7 × 0.25 × 0.6 = 0.105 [1]
P(M, M, G) = 0.7 × 0.75 × 0.25 = 0.13125 [1]
Total = **0.371** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** P(first gold | exactly one) = 0.135 / 0.37125 [1] = **0.364** (3 s.f.) [1]
**(d)** Confidence after a gold or frustration after a miss means arrows are unlikely to be independent [1]
*Examiner insight:* Reusing 0.3 for later arrows in Model B treats it as Model A and loses every path mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating "independent" or "not independent" without both P(A) × P(B) and P(A ∩ B) written as numbers.
- Using the grand total instead of the row or column total for a conditional probability from a table.
- Forgetting that the second draw has one fewer item when sampling without replacement.
- Reversing a conditional on a tree.
- Writing model critiques that name no assumption and give no direction of effect.
- Rounding to 3 s.f. part-way through and carrying the rounded value forward.
