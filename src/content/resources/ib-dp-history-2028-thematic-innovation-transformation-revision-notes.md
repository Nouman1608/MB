Revision notes for the thematic study **Innovation and transformation (from 750 CE onwards)** in IB DP History (first assessment 2028), for SL and HL. Four inquiry questions, sixteen lines of inquiry, no prescribed examples. See the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-thematic-innovation-transformation/) and the [practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-thematic-innovation-transformation-practice/).

## Paper 2 in one box

| Part | Task | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| Section A | Analyse a concept, illustrated by an example from the thematic study | 6 |
| Section B (a) | Short response on one example | 4 |
| Section B (b) | Essay using examples from at least two regions | 15 |

Total 25 marks, 1 hour 45 minutes.

## Definitions

- **Innovation:** the introduction of something new in a specific context, such as an original idea, method or technology.
- **Transformative:** bringing a major change to the form or function of aspects of a society.
- Types in the guide: technological (industrial revolutions, mass production and capitalism, the digital revolution); intellectual (Scientific Revolution, Golden Age of Islam, Enlightenment); cultural, social, religious and artistic.

## The four inquiry questions and lines of inquiry

| Inquiry question | Lines of inquiry |
|---|---|
| Why did new innovations emerge? | Social factors; economic factors; political factors; environmental factors |
| How did the innovations transform societies? | Economic change; political change; environmental change; cultural change |
| How were the innovations resisted? | Resistance from established authorities; forms of popular resistance; resilience of traditional ideas; competing innovations |
| How did the innovations affect people's lives? | Experiences of innovators; experiences of social elites; experiences of women; experiences of marginalised groups |

| Example | Key facts |
|---|---|
| Printing in Europe | Gutenberg, about 1450; spread of books; Reformation from 1517; censorship |
| Abbasid Golden Age | Baghdad; translation movement; paper; patronage by caliphs such as al-Ma'mun |
| Song China (960 to 1279) | Paper money, printing, gunpowder, commercial growth |
| British Industrial Revolution | Steam, cotton mills, coal; Luddites 1811 to 1816; factory towns |
| Meiji industrialisation | State-led railways and factories; end of samurai privileges |
| Green Revolution | High-yield wheat and rice from the 1960s; fertiliser and irrigation; uneven benefits |

## Quick evidence by line of inquiry

**Emergence**

- Social: scholarship in Baghdad; literate merchants in Europe.
- Economic: capital, coal and markets in Britain; commercial economy in Song China.
- Political: Meiji state promotion; Abbasid patronage.
- Environmental: fear of famine behind the Green Revolution; coal availability.

**Transformation**

- Economic: factory production; higher yields but more dependence on inputs.
- Political: print and the Reformation; new classes pressing for reform.
- Environmental: pollution; falling water tables.
- Cultural: libraries and learning; standardised languages and higher literacy.

**Resistance**

- Authorities: licensing and censorship of print; debate over the Ottoman restrictions on Arabic-script printing.
- Popular: Luddites (1811 to 1816) and the harsh response.
- Traditional ideas: continuing craft and rural values.
- Competing innovations: canals against railways; old seeds against new.

**Lives**

- Innovators: gains and losses (Watt and Boulton).
- Elites: gains for merchants; loss of status for the Japanese samurai.
- Women: mill work (for example at Lowell); unequal benefit in the Green Revolution.
- Marginalised groups: enslaved labour behind cotton; child workers.

## Section A and B(a)

**Section A.** Define the concept in a line, use one specific example, analyse, then judge. For continuity and change, show what changed and what continued; for cause and consequence, separate actors from conditions.

**Section B (a).** Choose one example. Identify it, describe it, then explain how it relates to the question. Four marks reward effective explanation.

## Section B (b): the essay

1. State a line of argument in the opening.
2. Use examples from at least two regions.
3. Organise by theme or argument, not by example.
4. Make connections explicit: similarity, difference, pattern, exception.
5. Judge.

## Worked example: a short plan

Question: "Evaluate the extent to which economic factors explain why innovations emerged."

- Thesis: economic conditions were necessary, but political and social factors decided where and when innovations took off.
- Paragraph 1 (Europe): Britain's capital, coal and markets made machines profitable.
- Paragraph 2 (Asia): the Meiji state, not private capital alone, created the first factories.
- Paragraph 3 (Middle East): patronage and scholarship drove Abbasid innovation in a culture of translation.
- Conclusion: economic factors set the conditions; political and social choices explain the form and timing.

## Common mistakes

- Treating innovation as always beneficial.
- Describing an invention without its effects.
- Using two examples from one region.
- Overstating contested claims, such as the extent of the Ottoman printing ban.

## Self-test

1. Define innovation using the guide.
2. What makes an innovation transformative?
3. Who were the Luddites?
4. Name two lines of inquiry under "How were the innovations resisted?"
5. Name one environmental effect of the Green Revolution.
6. Which date is usually given for Gutenberg's printing in Europe?

**Answers.**

1. The introduction of something new in a specific context.
2. It brings a major change to the form or function of aspects of a society.
3. English textile workers who destroyed machines between 1811 and 1816 to protect wages and conditions.
4. Any two of: resistance from established authorities; forms of popular resistance; resilience of traditional ideas; competing innovations.
5. For example, falling water tables from intensive irrigation, or greater use of fertiliser.
6. About 1450.
