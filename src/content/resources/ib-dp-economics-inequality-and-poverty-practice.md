## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between income inequality and wealth inequality. **[3]**

**2.** Distinguish between equality and equity, using one example. **[3]**

**3.** (calculator-free) In Ostrel, a fictional country, the shares of national income received by each fifth of households, poorest first, were 5, 9, 14, 22 and 50 (%) before a reform and 7, 11, 16, 23 and 43 (%) after it.

**(a)** Find the cumulative share of income held by the poorest 60% of households before and after the reform. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the change shows about the Lorenz curve and about income inequality. **[3]**

**4.** (calculator-free) In Dorrin, a fictional country, the absolute poverty line is 10,000 dorrs a year, and the relative poverty line is 50% of median income. The median income is 30,000 dorrs. Four households earn: P 9,500; Q 12,000; R 16,000; S 14,500.

**(a)** Identify which households are in absolute poverty and which are in relative poverty. **[3]**
**(b)** Next year the median rises to 36,000 and household R earns 16,800. The absolute line is unchanged. Explain whether household R is in poverty. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the difference between a single indicator and a composite indicator of poverty, giving one example of each. **[4]**

**6.** Explain two difficulties in measuring poverty. **[4]**

**7.** Explain two causes of economic inequality. **[6]**

**8.** Examine the possible effects of high income inequality on economic growth and on standards of living. **[8]**

**9.** (calculator-free) Three taxes in a fictional country take these amounts from a household:

| Tax | Tax paid at income 10,000 | Tax paid at income 40,000 |
|---|---|---|
| X | 1,000 | 8,000 |
| Y | 1,500 | 4,800 |
| Z | 1,000 | 4,000 |

**(a)** Classify each tax as progressive, regressive or proportional, showing the average rates. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain which tax would do most to reduce inequality. **[2]**

**10.** (HL only, calculator allowed) In Karmen, a fictional country, the income shares of the five quintiles, poorest first, are 4, 8, 13, 20 and 55 (%).

**(a)** Calculate the cumulative income shares. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how you would construct the Lorenz curve from these data. **[2]**
**(c)** State the share held by the poorest 60% and comment on it. **[2]**

**11.** (HL only, calculator allowed) Ostrel taxes personal income as follows: nothing on the first 12,000; 25% on income from 12,000 to 36,000; 45% on income above 36,000.

**(a)** Calculate the total tax and the average tax rate for a person earning 30,000. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the total tax and average tax rate for a person earning 60,000, and state what your two average rates show. **[3]**
**(c)** Ostrel also has a 15% sales tax. Calculate the tax paid by a household that spends 14,000 on taxed goods. **[1]**

**12.** In Tarvane, a fictional country with high relative poverty, evaluate transfer payments, a minimum wage and universal basic income as ways of reducing poverty. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Income is a flow received over a period, such as wages and benefits [1]. Wealth is a stock of assets less debts at a point in time [1]. Income inequality is the uneven spread of the first, wealth inequality the uneven spread of the second; a high earner who owns no assets shows the two can differ [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs both items addressed side by side; defining only one earns little.

**2.** Equality means similar economic outcomes for people or groups [1]. Equity means fairness, which is normative, so people hold different views [1]. Example: equal pay for all workers gives equal outcomes, but some may judge it unfair if hours or effort differ widely [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that treat "equity" as a synonym for "equality" lose the contrast the command term asks for.

**3. (a)** Before: 5 + 9 + 14 = **28%** [1]. After: 7 + 11 + 16 = **34%** [1].
**(b)** The poorest 60% hold a larger share, so the Lorenz curve after the reform lies above the old one at that point [1]. The cumulative shares at 20, 40, 60 and 80% are all higher (7, 18, 34, 57 against 5, 14, 28, 50) [1]. The curve is closer to the line of equality, so income inequality has fallen, although it is still below the line, so not equal [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing cumulative shares at several points supports "closer to the line"; one point alone is thin evidence.

**4. (a)** Relative line = 0.5 x 30,000 = 15,000 [1]. Absolute poverty: **P only** (9,500 is below 10,000) [1]. Relative poverty: **P, Q and S** (below 15,000) [1].
**(b)** New relative line = 0.5 x 36,000 = 18,000, and 16,800 is below it, so R is in relative poverty [1]. R is above the absolute line of 10,000, so not in absolute poverty [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Stating the line you are comparing against makes each judgement checkable; bare lists of household letters are easy to mark wrong.

**5.** A single indicator uses one measure [1], for example an international poverty line [1]. A composite indicator combines several measures into one index [1], for example the Multidimensional Poverty Index, covering health, education and living standards [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming an example without saying what it measures does not show understanding.

**6.** Difficulty one: poverty lines are arbitrary [1], so moving the line slightly changes how many people count as poor [1]. Difficulty two: prices and needs differ across regions and household sizes [1], so one money threshold treats unlike households alike [1]. (Other valid: informal income, infrequent surveys, income sharing inside households.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each difficulty needs an explanation sentence; a list of labels gives the marker little to credit.

**7.** Cause one, discrimination: employers pay or hire some groups less for equal work [1], lowering those groups' incomes [1], widening gaps with others [1]. Cause two, differences in human capital: workers with more education and training are more productive [1], so earn higher wages [1], leaving the low-skilled behind [1]. (Any two causes from the guide's list with mechanism.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A cause, a mechanism and a link to incomes are three separate steps; "discrimination causes inequality" alone is a label.

**8.** Growth, harmful: poorer households may be unable to afford education or health care [1], wasting potential output and limiting growth [1]. Growth, helpful: higher rewards for effort and risk, and more saving by richer households, may support investment [1], so the link is not one-way [1]. Standards of living: average income can rise while the poorest gain little [1], so averages overstate how most people live, and poor health and housing lower living standards [1]. Judgement: the effect depends on how large inequality is and on whether it reflects effort or unequal opportunity [1], so a balanced view is better than a claim that inequality always harms or always helps [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Examine" rewards uncovering assumptions; a reasoned conclusion that is conditional gains more than an unsupported one.

**9. (a)** X: 10% at 10,000 and 20% at 40,000, rising, so **progressive** [1]. Y: 15% and 12%, falling, so **regressive** [1]. Z: 10% and 10%, so **proportional** [1].
**(b)** Tax X takes a larger share from higher incomes [1], so it narrows the gap between after-tax incomes most, particularly if the revenue funds support for low earners [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Classify by average rate, not by amount paid; richer households pay more under all three taxes.

**10. (a)** 4, 12, 25, 45, 100 (%) [2].
**(b)** Put cumulative % of households (20 to 100) on the horizontal axis and cumulative % of income on the vertical axis [1]. Plot (0, 0) and the five points, join them, and add the 45-degree line of equality [1].
**(c)** The poorest 60% hold **25%** [1], well below 60% on the line of equality, so the curve sags a long way below it and income is unequally distributed, especially with the richest 20% holding 55% [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A construction answer should name both axes as cumulative percentages and include the line of equality.

**11. (a)** Income in the 25% band = 30,000 − 12,000 = 18,000, and tax = 18,000 x 0.25 [1] = **4,500** [1]. Average rate = 4,500 ÷ 30,000 x 100 = **15%** [1].
**(b)** 12,000 to 36,000: 24,000 x 0.25 = 6,000, and above 36,000: 24,000 x 0.45 = 10,800, so total tax = **16,800** [1], average rate = 16,800 ÷ 60,000 x 100 = **28%** [1]. The average rate rises from 15% to 28% as income rises, so the income tax is progressive [1].
**(c)** 14,000 x 0.15 = **2,100** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Calculate" asks for the stages of working; a correct figure with no working risks losing method credit.

**12.** Transfer payments: cash benefits raise low incomes quickly [1]; they are paid for by taxes, and means-tested versions can weaken incentives to work [1]; targeting them at low-income households makes them more effective against relative poverty than universal payments of the same cost [1]. Minimum wage: raises pay for low earners without direct spending [1]; if above the market wage it can cut jobs or hours, so some low-income households may lose [1]; it helps only those in work, not the unemployed [1]. Universal basic income: simple, no stigma or means test [1]; very costly, since it goes to everyone, and needs high taxes or cuts elsewhere [1]; its effect on poverty depends on the payment level relative to the poverty line [1]. Comparison: the policies differ in cost, speed and targeting [1]. Judgement: a mix is likely to work better than one policy, depending on the labour market and public finances [1]; a clear criterion such as effect on those in deepest poverty supports the verdict [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* In extended answers, credit tends to follow analysis and a justified judgement rather than the number of policies listed; the real paper is marked with markbands.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a Lorenz curve without naming both axes as cumulative percentages.
- Comparing curves from a single point instead of several points.
- Mixing up absolute and relative poverty when incomes and the median both change.
- Calling a tax progressive because the amount paid rises, not the average rate.
- Using the marginal rate in place of the average rate.
- Giving a final figure with no working in a "calculate" question.
- Listing policies without weighing costs, speed and side effects.
- Giving a one-sided answer to "examine" or "evaluate" questions.
