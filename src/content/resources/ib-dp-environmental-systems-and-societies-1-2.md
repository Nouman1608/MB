Subtopic 1.2 Systems is the second part of the Foundation topic of IB Diploma Programme
Environmental Systems and Societies (ESS), first assessment 2026. Systems is one of the three key
concepts the subject brief says run through the whole course, and it gives the course its name.
The [perspectives, systems and sustainability study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-foundation-perspectives-systems-sustainability/)
defines the core vocabulary and works a lake water budget. This guide goes further: why ESS uses a
systems approach at all, how to read and draw Sankey diagrams, how to calculate residence time, how
to build and label feedback loops, how tipping points and resilience behave in real ecosystems, and
how to evaluate a model in an exam answer.

The brief does not print learning outcomes for 1.2, so the scope below follows the usual teaching of
this subtopic; check it against your school's copy of the ESS guide.

## What 1.2 asks you to do

- Explain the systems approach and why it suits environmental issues.
- Represent a system with storages, flows and a boundary, distinguishing transfers from
  transformations.
- Classify systems as open, closed or isolated, at different scales.
- Explain steady-state equilibrium and the roles of negative and positive feedback.
- Explain tipping points, resilience and emergent properties.
- Evaluate the use of models.

## Why a systems approach?

A **reductionist** approach studies parts in isolation, such as a single species or one pollutant.
A **systems** (holistic) approach studies how the parts interact. Environmental problems usually cut
across disciplines: overfishing involves fish biology, ocean chemistry, markets, law and culture at
once. Seeing the whole system helps you find **leverage points** (where a small change has a large
effect) and **unintended consequences** (where solving one problem creates another).

## Storages, flows and diagram conventions

- **Storages** are drawn as boxes, sometimes sized to show relative stock.
- **Flows** are arrows. Label each with the process (evaporation, feeding, harvesting), not just
  "in" or "out".
- The **boundary** is drawn as a line around the system; it is a choice made by the person modelling
  it.

Flows are either **transfers** (a change of location, with no change of form) or
**transformations** (a change of form, state or energy type). Transformations can be grouped as:

- matter to matter: sugars built into cellulose in a growing tree
- energy to energy: light energy converted to chemical energy in photosynthesis
- matter to energy: burning coal releasing heat.

### Sankey diagrams

A **Sankey diagram** shows flows as arrows whose width is proportional to the size of the flow. They
make losses obvious.

**Worked example 1 (fictional data).** A coal-fired power station receives 1,000 MJ of chemical
energy in fuel. It delivers 380 MJ of electrical energy, loses 570 MJ as heat in cooling water and
50 MJ as heat in flue gases.

```
Check: 380 + 570 + 50 = 1000 MJ   (energy is conserved)
Efficiency = useful output / input × 100
           = 380 / 1000 × 100 = 38 %
Share lost in cooling water = 570 / 1000 × 100 = 57 %
```

On the Sankey, the cooling-water arrow would be about one and a half times as wide as the electricity
arrow. The outputs always add up to the input: if they do not, a flow has been missed.

## Open, closed and isolated systems at different scales

| System | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A pond | Open | Water, nutrients, organisms and energy cross the boundary |
| Planet Earth | Close to closed | Sunlight enters and heat leaves; matter exchange with space is negligible |
| A sealed glass terrarium | Approximately closed | Light enters and heat leaves, but no matter |
| The whole universe | Isolated (in theory) | Nothing outside it to exchange with |

The same place can be modelled at different scales: a leaf, a tree, a forest, a drainage basin, the
biosphere. Choosing the scale and the boundary decides which flows count as inputs and outputs.

## Residence time

**Residence time** is the average time a unit of matter spends in a storage. For a storage in steady
state:

`residence time = size of storage ÷ rate of flow in (or out)`

**Worked example 2 (fictional data).** An aquifer holds 2,400 million m³ of water. Recharge from
rainfall is 60 million m³ per year, and an equal amount leaves to springs and rivers.

```
Residence time = 2400 / 60 = 40 years
```

If a city then pumps out an extra 20 million m³ per year:

```
Total outputs = 60 + 20 = 80 million m³ per year
Net change    = 60 − 80 = −20 million m³ per year
Years until the storage halves (to 1200) = 1200 / 20 = 60 years
```

A long residence time explains why groundwater pollution can persist for decades and why
over-abstraction is easy to miss at first.

## Feedback loops in practice

Build every feedback loop as a chain, then ask whether the final step pushes the first change further
or back.

**Negative feedback example: plant growth and carbon dioxide.**
More CO₂ in the air → faster photosynthesis in some plants → more carbon stored in biomass → less CO₂
added to the air than otherwise. The response reduces the original change.

**Positive feedback example: forest dieback.**
Drought and clearing reduce forest cover → less transpiration → less moisture recycled into rain
downwind → drier forest and more fires → further loss of forest. The response amplifies the original
change.

Real systems contain both kinds at once. Climate, for example, includes the ice–albedo positive
feedback and the negative feedback by which a warmer Earth radiates more energy to space. The overall
outcome depends on which loops dominate.

## Tipping points and alternative stable states

A **tipping point** is the threshold at which a system shifts to a different stable state. Three
widely studied examples:

- **Shallow lakes.** Nutrient enrichment can switch a clear lake dominated by rooted plants into a
  turbid lake dominated by algae. Reducing nutrients to the earlier level often does not switch it
  back, because the new state has its own reinforcing feedbacks.
- **Coral reefs.** Overfishing of grazers, nutrient run-off and bleaching can shift a reef to one
  dominated by seaweed.
- **Rainforest to savanna.** Repeated drought and clearing could push parts of a rainforest towards a
  drier, more open state through the dieback feedback above.

Features to mention: little visible change before the threshold; positive feedback drives the shift;
the new state is self-reinforcing; recovery may need much more than reversing the original pressure.

## Resilience

Resilience is the ability of a system to absorb disturbance while keeping its structure and
functions. It tends to be higher where there is:

- high biodiversity, so other species can fill a lost role
- large storages, such as deep soil or big seed banks
- rapid reproduction, so populations recover quickly
- connection to other areas, so organisms can recolonise.

Human activity often lowers resilience: monocultures remove diversity, erosion removes soil storage
and fragmentation cuts connections. High resilience is not always desirable; a degraded state, such as
an invasive-dominated grassland, can also be resilient and hard to restore.

**Emergent properties** arise from interactions and cannot be predicted from the parts alone, such as
the stability of a food web or the climate of a region shaped by its forest.

## Evaluating models

Any model can be evaluated with the same checklist:

- **Purpose:** what question is it built to answer?
- **Simplifications:** which storages and flows are left out?
- **Assumptions:** what is assumed to stay constant?
- **Data:** how reliable are the inputs?
- **Testing:** has it been checked against observations?
- **Range:** how far ahead or how far beyond its data is it being used?

Worked judgement: a box-and-arrow model of a forest's carbon storages is easy to understand and shows
where carbon goes, but cannot predict how fast the forest will respond to drought; a computer model
can, but only as well as its assumptions about rainfall and tree mortality.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing flows without naming the process.
- Calling a change of location a transformation.
- Calling a bad outcome "positive feedback" without checking whether the change is amplified.
- Writing residence time without units.
- Saying resilient systems "never change".
- Evaluating a model with only "it is simplified".

## Exam technique

"Draw" and "construct" questions need labelled storages, process-named flows and a boundary. "Explain"
feedback questions need the full chain and the direction of the final effect. "Evaluate" a model
needs strengths, limitations and a conclusion about its usefulness for the stated purpose.
