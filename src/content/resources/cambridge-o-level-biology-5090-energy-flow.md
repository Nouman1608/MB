The syllabus asks you to understand that the Sun is the principal source of energy for most biological systems; to explain why most life depends on photosynthesis; to describe the flow of energy through food chains and webs; to construct and interpret food chains and webs; to use the terms producer, consumer, herbivore, carnivore and decomposer; to explain why energy transfer between trophic levels is inefficient and why food chains usually have fewer than five trophic levels; to explain why it is more energy efficient for humans to eat crop plants than livestock fed on crops; and to construct and interpret pyramids of numbers, biomass and energy.

## The Sun and photosynthesis

The **Sun is the principal source of energy input to most biological systems**. Light energy from the Sun is captured by green plants, algae and some bacteria in **photosynthesis**, which transfers it into chemical energy stored in carbohydrates such as glucose. All other organisms get their energy, directly or indirectly, from this stored energy. Animals cannot make food from light, so they eat plants or other animals. Therefore **most forms of life are completely dependent on photosynthesis**: without it there would be no organic compounds for food and no oxygen for aerobic respiration.

(A few ecosystems, for example around deep-sea vents, use other energy sources, which is why the Sun is described as the source for **most** biological systems.)

## Feeding relationships

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Producer | An organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually using energy from sunlight, by photosynthesis |
| Consumer | An organism that gets its energy by feeding on other organisms |
| Herbivore | An animal that eats plants (a primary consumer) |
| Carnivore | An animal that eats other animals |
| Decomposer | An organism that gets its energy from dead or waste organic matter (bacteria and fungi) |
| Trophic level | A feeding level in a food chain |

### Food chains

A **food chain** shows the transfer of energy from one organism to the next. The arrows point in the direction of energy flow, from the organism being eaten to the one that eats it.

grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk

| Organism | Trophic level | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Grass | 1 | Producer |
| Grasshopper | 2 | Primary consumer, herbivore |
| Frog | 3 | Secondary consumer, carnivore |
| Snake | 4 | Tertiary consumer, carnivore |
| Hawk | 5 | Quaternary consumer, carnivore |

### Food webs

A **food web** is a network of interconnected food chains. Most organisms eat more than one kind of food, and are eaten by more than one kind of predator, so food webs are more realistic than single chains. To interpret a web, follow the arrows. If one population changes, the effects spread, for example if the number of frogs falls then the number of grasshoppers may rise and the number of snakes may fall.

### Worked example 1: reading a food web

*In a pond food web, algae are eaten by water fleas and by tadpoles. Water fleas are eaten by small fish. Tadpoles are eaten by small fish and by dragonfly larvae. Small fish are eaten by herons.*

- Producer: algae.
- Herbivores (primary consumers): water fleas and tadpoles.
- Carnivores: small fish, dragonfly larvae and herons.
- A food chain with four trophic levels: algae → tadpole → small fish → heron.
- If the small fish die out, the heron loses a food source, and the water fleas and tadpoles may increase because fewer are eaten.

## The flow of energy

Energy enters a food chain as light and is stored as chemical energy in the producers. It is transferred when organisms are eaten. At each stage, a large proportion of the energy is lost to the environment as **heat**, mainly from respiration. The energy does not return to the producers. In this sense energy **flows** through an ecosystem, whereas nutrients are recycled (see nutrient cycles).

### Why energy transfer is inefficient

Only a small fraction of the energy at one trophic level is passed on to the next. Typically, about 10 per cent is transferred. The rest is lost because:

- much of the energy is released as **heat in respiration**, to power movement, growth, keeping warm and other processes;
- some parts of the food are **not eaten**, such as bones, roots, shells and fur;
- some food is eaten but **not digested** and is lost as **faeces**;
- some energy is lost in **excretion**, for example in urine;
- dead organisms and waste are used by **decomposers**, so the energy does not pass to the next consumer.

### Why food chains are short

Because only about 10 per cent of the energy is passed to the next level, there is very little energy left after four or five stages. There is not enough energy to support another group of organisms, so food chains usually have **fewer than five trophic levels**.

### Worked example 2: energy transfer

*A field of grass receives 1 000 000 kJ of light energy per square metre per year. The grass stores 10 000 kJ. A herbivore eating the grass takes in energy, of which 1 200 kJ is stored in its body.*

1. Percentage of light energy stored by grass = 10 000 divided by 1 000 000 x 100 = 1 per cent.
2. Percentage transferred from grass to herbivore = 1 200 divided by 10 000 x 100 = 12 per cent.
3. Energy lost between grass and herbivore = 10 000 - 1 200 = 8 800 kJ.

The main point of the calculation is that most energy is not stored in the next trophic level. The method for efficiency is: energy passed on divided by energy available x 100.

## Why eating plants is more efficient

Crops are producers. When people eat plants directly, they receive energy from the first trophic level. If the crops are first fed to livestock, and people then eat the meat, there is an extra trophic level. At each stage about 90 per cent of the energy is lost, so far less energy reaches the person. A given area of land can feed many more people if the crop is eaten directly than if it is used to feed animals.

### Worked example 3: comparing the two routes

*A field of wheat produces 40 000 kJ of energy per year. If people eat the wheat, assume that 100 per cent of it is available. If the wheat is fed to cattle, assume that 10 per cent of the energy ends up in beef.*

Energy available to people from the wheat = 40 000 kJ.
Energy available through beef = 40 000 x 0.10 = 4 000 kJ.

People can obtain ten times as much energy from the same field by eating the crop. This is why plant-based diets use less land per unit of energy.

## Pyramids

A **pyramid** shows the amount of something at each trophic level. The producers form the base, with the first consumers above, and so on.

| Type of pyramid | What each bar shows | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pyramid of numbers | Number of organisms | Can be an odd shape (for example, one large tree supports thousands of insects) |
| Pyramid of biomass | Dry mass of living material | Usually a pyramid; better than numbers |
| Pyramid of energy | Energy stored per area per unit time | Always a pyramid, because energy is lost at each stage |

To construct a pyramid: draw a horizontal bar for each trophic level, to the same scale, with the producers at the bottom.

### Worked example 4: constructing a pyramid of numbers

*Number of organisms in 100 square metres: grass 10 000, grasshoppers 1 000, frogs 40, snakes 5.*

The bars decrease from the base: 10 000, 1 000, 40, 5. The ratio of grass to grasshoppers is 10 : 1, and grasshoppers to frogs is 25 : 1. The numbers of organisms fall because there is less energy available at each level. A pyramid of numbers can sometimes be inverted: a single oak tree supports many insects, so the first bar is smaller than the second. A pyramid of biomass or energy for this example is not inverted.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing arrows in food chains from the eater to the food. They show the direction of energy transfer.
- Saying that energy is "used up" or "destroyed". It is transferred to the environment as heat.
- Saying that energy is recycled. Nutrients are recycled; energy flows.
- Giving "predators are lazy" or "animals eat less" as reasons for short chains. Energy loss is the reason.
- Forgetting that decomposers are part of the energy flow and nutrient cycling.
- Describing a pyramid of numbers as always pyramid-shaped.
- Writing that 90 per cent of the energy is "wasted". It is lost as heat, in faeces, and in uneaten parts.

## Command words

- **Construct**: draw a food chain, food web or pyramid to scale.
- **Interpret**: read information from a web or pyramid.
- **Explain**: give reasons for energy loss or short chains.
- **Describe**: outline the flow of energy.
