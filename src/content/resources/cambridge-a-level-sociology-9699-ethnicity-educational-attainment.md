Section 6.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) explains why educational attainment differs between ethnic groups. The syllabus lists four strands: racism in schools; cultural explanations for patterns in ethnicity and educational attainment; ethnicity and subcultures; and the relationship between ethnicity, social class and gender. It sits in topic 6, Education and inequality, and is examined on Paper 3 – Education.

The [ethnicity, gender and educational attainment overview](/resources/a-level-sociology-9699-ethnicity-gender-and-educational-attainment/) covers Coard, Gillborn, Gillborn and Youdell, Sewell, Mirza, Francis and Archer, and studies of intersectionality. This page adds further evidence on each strand, the debate between those who find teacher racism and those who doubt it, and international material that lets you apply the topic beyond Britain.

## Start with the pattern, carefully

Ethnic differences in attainment are not a single "minority gap". In England, for example, official data have long shown some groups, such as Chinese and Indian pupils, achieving above the national average, and others, including Black Caribbean, Gypsy/Roma and Irish Traveller pupils, achieving below it. White British pupils eligible for free school meals are among the lower-attaining groups. Two consequences follow for your answers:

- Any explanation must account for **variation between minority groups**, not just a minority–majority gap.
- Ethnicity is **entangled with class**: some groups have very different class profiles, so raw comparisons can mislead.

Patterns differ by country, so if you use evidence from your own society, describe it in the same careful way.

## Racism in schools: the evidence

**Teacher expectations in primary schools.** Cecile Wright, in *Race Relations in the Primary School* (1992), observed multi-ethnic primary classrooms in England. Teachers often assumed Asian pupils had poor English, addressed them in simplified language and left them out of discussions; Black Caribbean boys were expected to misbehave and were disciplined more often and more harshly. The study shows racism operating through routine classroom judgements, not open hostility.

**The sceptical position.** Peter Foster (1990) studied a multi-ethnic comprehensive and argued that he found little evidence of teacher racism; he suggested that some earlier studies inferred racism from interactions that could be explained in other ways. This gives you a genuine methodological debate: ethnographers who find racism are accused of seeing what they expect, while sceptics are accused of missing subtle, institutional processes.

**Institutional racism.** The strongest version of the racism argument does not depend on individual teachers' attitudes. Setting, exclusions, exam tiers and curriculum content can disadvantage some groups even if every teacher means well. This is why critical race theory focuses on outcomes and routines rather than intentions.

## Cultural explanations: three generations of argument

1. **Deficit accounts.** Carl Bereiter and Siegfried Engelmann (1966) described the language of poor Black American children as deficient and proposed intensive pre-school language drills. This is the same cultural deprivation logic examined in class debates, and it draws the same criticism: Labov showed non-standard dialects are rule-governed and capable of complex reasoning.
2. **Supportive-culture accounts.** Some groups' cultural resources help attainment. Tariq Modood (2004) used the idea of **ethnic capital**: high educational aspirations and family and community networks within some minority groups help children succeed even when the family's class position is modest.
3. **Responses to minority status.** John Ogbu distinguished **voluntary minorities**, who migrated by choice and tend to see education as a route upward, from **involuntary minorities**, incorporated through slavery or conquest, who may distrust institutions that have historically excluded them. Signithia Fordham and Ogbu (1986) argued that some Black American students faced "the burden of acting white": academic effort could be seen by peers as abandoning Black identity.

**Evaluation of cultural accounts.** They explain variation between groups better than racism alone, but they risk treating each group as having one culture, and Ogbu's model has been criticised for underplaying differences within groups and the role of schools. The **Swann Report**, *Education for All* (1985), rejected IQ as an explanation for ethnic differences, gave weight to socio-economic factors and called for education that reflected Britain's diversity in all schools.

## Ethnicity and subcultures

The overview explains Sewell's four responses. Two more studies show that minority pupils' subcultures are not simply anti-education.

- **Mary Fuller** (1984) studied Black girls in a London comprehensive. They were **pro-education but anti-school**: they valued qualifications, worked hard, but did not seek teachers' approval and kept friendships with less studious peers. They used success to reject the negative labels they faced.
- **Máirtín Mac an Ghaill**, in *Young, Gifted and Black* (1988), studied Black and Asian students. The African-Caribbean "Rasta Heads" and the Asian "Warriors" resisted school in different ways, while the "Black Sisters" took a pro-education, anti-school stance similar to Fuller's girls.

These studies support a key analytical point: a pupil's reaction to racism or labelling depends on gender, peer group and aims, so the same pressure produces different outcomes.

## Ethnicity, class and gender together

To analyse the three together, ask three questions of any evidence:

1. **Is the gap still there when class is held constant?** If it shrinks, class explains part of it.
2. **Does the gap differ for boys and girls?** Fuller and Mac an Ghaill suggest girls and boys of the same group respond to schooling differently.
3. **Does class work the same way for every group?** Studies of middle-class Black families in Britain suggest class resources do not fully protect children from racism.

**Multicultural versus anti-racist education.** Policy responses mirror the explanations. Multicultural education adds minority cultures to the curriculum to raise self-esteem; anti-racist education targets institutional practices. Maureen Stone (1981) argued that Black pupils did not suffer from low self-esteem and that multicultural lessons could distract from teaching core skills. Anti-racists argue multiculturalism ignores power.

## Worked example: Question 3 style

*Quotation:* "Ethnic differences in attainment are caused by racism in schools." Give two arguments against this view.

> **Argument 1.** The view cannot explain variation between minority groups. Chinese and Indian pupils in England have long achieved above the national average in the same school system that is accused of racism. Modood's concept of ethnic capital suggests that family aspirations and community networks help explain these patterns, so factors outside school matter.
>
> **Argument 2.** Evidence for teacher racism is contested. Foster (1990) found little evidence of racism in the multi-ethnic comprehensive he studied and argued earlier ethnographies over-interpreted classroom interactions. If racism is difficult to demonstrate consistently, it is unsafe to treat it as the cause of all ethnic differences, although institutional processes such as setting may still play a part.

## Worked essay plan

*"Cultural factors are more important than school processes in explaining ethnic differences in attainment." Evaluate this view.*

1. Describe the pattern carefully, including variation between groups and links to class.
2. For cultural factors: deficit accounts (and their criticism), ethnic capital (Modood), Ogbu's voluntary and involuntary minorities, Fordham and Ogbu.
3. For school processes: Wright (1992), Coard, Gillborn's institutional racism, setting and exclusions.
4. Methodological debate: Foster versus ethnographers who find racism.
5. Subcultures as a bridge: Fuller and Mac an Ghaill show cultural responses *to* school processes.
6. Intersection: class and gender.
7. Judgement: cultural and school factors interact; neither alone explains all groups.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "ethnic minorities" as one group.
- Assuming a racism argument requires individually racist teachers; institutional accounts do not.
- Presenting Ogbu's categories as fixed facts rather than a debated model.
- Ignoring class when comparing groups.

## Exam technique

- In **Explain** questions, link each cultural factor to a specific mechanism that affects attainment.
- In **Give** (arguments against), use variation between groups and contested evidence as two distinct lines.
- In **Evaluate** essays, show awareness of how attainment is measured and grouped.
