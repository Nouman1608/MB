This guide goes deeper into section **3.7.3 Analysing the existing internal position of a business to assess strengths and weaknesses: overall performance** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **how to analyse data other than financial statements to assess the strengths and weaknesses of a business**, where the data should include operations, human resource and marketing data, analysed over time or in comparison with other businesses
- **the importance of core competences**
- **assessing short- and long-term performance**
- **the value of different measures of assessing business performance**, to include Elkington's Triple Bottom Line (Profit, People, Planet).

The companion guide on financial ratio analysis covers the accounts. This page covers everything else that tells you how a business is really performing. All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why data other than accounts matter

Financial statements are a record of results. They say little about why results occurred or what will happen next. A business can report a healthy profit while its best staff leave, its quality slips and its customers drift away. Non-financial data often give an early warning, because profit usually falls after these indicators worsen, not before.

## Operations data

| Measure | Calculation | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Productivity | Output ÷ number of inputs (for example output per employee) | How efficiently resources are used |
| Capacity utilisation | Actual output ÷ maximum output × 100 | High: spreading fixed costs; too high: no room for demand or maintenance. Low: wasted resources |
| Unit cost | Total cost ÷ units produced | Cost competitiveness |
| Quality | Defect rate, returns, warranty claims | Whether the product is reliable |
| Delivery | Percentage of orders delivered on time and in full | Reliability to customers |

## Human resource data

| Measure | Calculation | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Labour turnover | Leavers in a year ÷ average staff × 100 | High: low morale, poor pay or poor management; costs of recruitment and training |
| Absenteeism | Days lost ÷ days available × 100 | Motivation, health and safety, stress |
| Labour productivity | Output ÷ employees | Skills, motivation, equipment |
| Training and development | Hours per employee | Investment in capability |
| Staff satisfaction | Survey results | Culture and engagement |

## Marketing data

| Measure | Calculation | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Market share | Firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100 | Competitive strength |
| Sales growth | Change in sales ÷ earlier sales × 100 | Demand for products |
| Customer satisfaction and complaints | Survey scores; complaints per 1,000 sales | Whether the offer meets needs |
| Customer retention | Customers kept ÷ customers at start × 100 | Loyalty and repeat business |
| Brand awareness | Percentage of target customers who recognise the brand | Effect of promotion |

### Over time and against others

As with ratios, a single figure tells you little. **Over time**, you see direction: is turnover rising or falling? **Against other businesses**, you see relative strength: 90% on-time delivery may be good in one industry and poor in another. Always check the data are measured in the same way.

## Worked example: Thornby Logistics

| Measure | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Average employees | 250 | 260 |
| Leavers | 30 | 52 |
| Parcels delivered (million) | 4.0 | 4.2 |
| Maximum capacity (million) | 5.0 | 5.0 |
| On-time delivery | 96% | 91% |
| Market share | 10.0% | 8.75% |

- Labour turnover: 30 ÷ 250 × 100 = **12%**; 52 ÷ 260 × 100 = **20%**.
- Output per employee: 4,000,000 ÷ 250 = **16,000**; 4,200,000 ÷ 260 = **16,154**, a rise of about 1%.
- Capacity utilisation: 4.0 ÷ 5.0 = **80%**; 4.2 ÷ 5.0 = **84%**.

**Analysis.** Output and utilisation have risen slightly, so on the surface operations look stronger. But labour turnover rose from 12% to 20%, so a fifth of staff are leaving. The likely effect is higher recruitment and training costs, more inexperienced staff and weaker service, which fits the fall in on-time delivery from 96% to 91%. Market share fell from 10% to 8.75% even though sales grew, because the whole market grew faster (it rose from £300m to £360m, a 20% increase against 5% growth for Thornby). **Judgement.** The business is growing but losing ground. Without further data on why staff are leaving it is hard to judge, but the evidence suggests that people issues are the main weakness, and that profit may suffer in later years.

## Core competences

A **core competence** is something a business does especially well that gives it a lasting advantage and is hard for rivals to copy. It is a bundle of skills, knowledge, technology and processes, not a single product.

A competence can be called "core" if it:

- is valued by customers and contributes to the benefits they see
- is difficult for competitors to imitate
- can be used across several products or markets.

Examples: a retailer's fast and accurate stock replenishment; a brewer's recipes and know-how; a software firm's ability to design simple interfaces.

**Why they matter for strategy and performance:**

- they are a strength to build on when choosing which markets and products to pursue
- they support a competitive advantage that profit rests on
- they tell the business what to keep in house and what it might outsource
- they warn against diversifying into activities where the business has no advantage.

**Limits:** a competence can become outdated if markets or technology change, and a business may over-rely on one competence. Identifying it is a matter of judgement.

## Short-term and long-term performance

| | Short-term | Long-term |
|---|---|---|
| Typical measures | Profit this year, cash flow, sales this quarter | Market share, brand strength, innovation, staff capability, return on investment |
| Risk of over-emphasis | Cutting training, research and maintenance to lift this year's profit | Ignoring cash flow and survival now |

A business can look strong in the short term while its long-term position weakens. A drop in research spending raises this year's profit but may lead to a lack of new products in three years. Conversely, investing heavily may cut profit this year and be the right decision. Assessing performance means asking whether the short-term result was achieved in a way that protects the future. Link back to short-termism in section 3.7.1.

## Measuring performance: different measures and their value

| Measure | Value | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Profit | Clear and comparable; shows the ability to reward owners | Ignores timing, risk, social impact |
| Return on capital employed | Relates profit to investment | Based on accounting values |
| Market share | Shows competitive standing | Can be bought through price cuts that harm profit |
| Customer satisfaction | Predicts repeat sales | Surveys can be biased |
| Employee measures | Early warning of problems | Hard to link directly to profit |
| Triple Bottom Line | Wider view of success | Hard to measure and compare |

### Elkington's Triple Bottom Line

John Elkington argued that business success should be judged on three "bottom lines":

1. **Profit**: economic performance, including financial return.
2. **People**: social performance, including the treatment of employees, customers and communities, health and safety, and fair pay.
3. **Planet**: environmental performance, including energy use, emissions, waste and resource use.

**Value:** it recognises that a business depends on its workforce, its customers and the environment; it can guard against actions that harm reputation; and it matches growing pressure from stakeholders and regulation.

**Limits:** profit is easy to measure, while people and planet are harder to quantify and combine, so a business may pick measures that flatter it. The three may conflict, for example a cleaner process may cost more. Some critics argue that a business that does not make profit cannot survive to benefit people or the planet at all.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse data:** quote the figures, calculate the change, and then explain what could have caused it and what effect it will have.
- **Assess performance:** use at least one financial and one non-financial measure; consider short and long term; recommend what additional data you would want.
- **Evaluate a measure:** weigh what it shows against what it hides.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a table of figures without interpreting it.
- Treating market share growth as automatically good, even if bought with losses.
- Assuming a high labour turnover is always a problem; in some industries, such as seasonal work, it is normal.
- Confusing a core competence with a product.
- Writing about the Triple Bottom Line as if it replaced profit.
