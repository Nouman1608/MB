This guide teaches section **3.4.2 Mensuration and calculation** of the AQA GCSE Mathematics (8300) specification (content references G14 to G23). It goes deeper than the topic-level [geometry and measures study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-geometry-and-measures/). Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-mensuration-and-calculation-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-mensuration-and-calculation-practice/).

Mensuration means measuring: lengths, areas and volumes. Calculation questions appear on all three papers, so keep answers exact (in terms of π or as surds) where the question asks, and round only at the end.

## Bearings and scale (G15)

A **bearing** is measured clockwise from north and written with three figures (for example 065°). To find the **back bearing**, add or subtract 180°.

**Example 1.** The bearing of B from A is 065°. The bearing of A from B is 065° + 180° = **245°**.

On a scale drawing, a scale of 1 : 50 000 means 1 cm on the map is 50 000 cm = 500 m on the ground. So 6.4 cm on the map is 6.4 × 0.5 = **3.2 km**.

## Areas and volumes of standard shapes (G16)

| Shape | Area |
|---|---|
| Triangle | ½ × base × perpendicular height |
| Parallelogram | base × perpendicular height |
| Trapezium | ½ × (a + b) × h, where a and b are the parallel sides |

**Prism volume = area of cross-section × length.**

**Example 2.** A trapezium has parallel sides 7 cm and 11 cm and height 5 cm. Area = ½ × (7 + 11) × 5 = **45 cm²**. A prism whose cross-section is a triangle of base 6 cm and height 4 cm and length 10 cm has volume ½ × 6 × 4 × 10 = **120 cm³**.

## Circles, sectors and solids (G17, G18)

- Circumference = 2πr = πd; area = πr².
- Cylinder: volume = πr²h; curved surface area = 2πrh.
- Sphere: volume = 4/3 πr³; surface area = 4πr².
- Cone with slant height l: volume = 1/3 πr²h; curved surface area = πrl. Pythagoras links r, h and l.
- Pyramid: volume = 1/3 × base area × height.

**Example 3.** A cylinder has radius 4 cm and height 10 cm. Volume = π × 4² × 10 = 160π = **503 cm³** (3 s.f.). Curved surface area = 2π × 4 × 10 = 80π = **251 cm²**.

**Example 4.** A cone has radius 3 cm and height 4 cm. Slant height = √(3² + 4²) = 5 cm. Volume = 1/3 × π × 9 × 4 = 12π = **37.7 cm³**. Curved surface area = π × 3 × 5 = 15π = **47.1 cm²**.

**Example 5.** A sphere has radius 6 cm. Volume = 4/3 × π × 216 = 288π = **905 cm³**. Surface area = 4π × 36 = 144π = **452 cm²**.

**Sectors.** A sector with angle θ and radius r has:

- arc length = θ/360 × 2πr
- area = θ/360 × πr²

**Example 6.** A sector has radius 9 cm and angle 80°. Arc length = 80/360 × 2π × 9 = 4π = **12.6 cm**. Area = 80/360 × π × 81 = 18π = **56.5 cm²**.

For composite shapes, split into standard parts, find each area or volume, then add or subtract.

## Similar shapes (G19)

Similar shapes have the same angles and corresponding sides in the same ratio, the **scale factor** k.

Both tiers: lengths in similar figures are in ratio k. If a model is 1/20 of the real size, a model height of 8 cm represents 160 cm.

**Higher tier only:** areas are in ratio **k²** and volumes in ratio **k³**.

**Example 7 (Higher tier only).** Two similar cylinders have heights 5 cm and 10 cm. The smaller has volume 40 cm³. The scale factor is 2, so the volume of the larger is 40 × 2³ = **320 cm³**. Its surface area is 4 times that of the smaller.

## Pythagoras and trigonometry (G20, G21)

In a right-angled triangle, the **hypotenuse** is opposite the right angle. **Pythagoras:** a² + b² = c². The trigonometric ratios are:

sin θ = opposite ÷ hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse, tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacent.

Use SOH CAH TOA to choose the ratio. Put the calculator in degrees.

**Example 8.** A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 10 cm and the side opposite angle θ is 7 cm. sin θ = 7/10, so θ = sin⁻¹(0.7) = **44.4°**.

**Example 9.** A ladder 10 m long makes 35° with the ground. Height reached = 10 × sin 35° = **5.74 m**.

**Exact values (both tiers):**

| θ | 0° | 30° | 45° | 60° | 90° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| sin θ | 0 | 1/2 | √2/2 | √3/2 | 1 |
| cos θ | 1 | √3/2 | √2/2 | 1/2 | 0 |
| tan θ | 0 | √3/3 | 1 | √3 | not defined |

### Higher tier only: three dimensions

Break a 3D problem into right-angled triangles, draw each separately and apply Pythagoras or trigonometry.

**Example 10 (Higher tier only).** A cuboid measures 6 cm by 8 cm by 10 cm high. The diagonal of the base is √(6² + 8²) = 10 cm. The space diagonal is √(10² + 10²) = √200 = **14.1 cm**. The angle between the space diagonal and the base satisfies tan θ = 10 ÷ 10 = 1, so θ = **45°**.

## Sine rule, cosine rule and area (G22, G23: Higher tier only)

For any triangle with sides a, b, c opposite angles A, B, C:

- **Sine rule:** a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. Use it when you have a side and its opposite angle, plus one more angle or side.
- **Cosine rule:** a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A. Use it for two sides and the included angle, or three sides.
- **Area** = ½ab sin C (two sides and the included angle).

**Example 11 (Higher tier only).** In triangle ABC, A = 40°, B = 65° and a = 8 cm. Then b = 8 × sin 65° ÷ sin 40° = **11.3 cm**.

**Example 12 (Higher tier only).** Two sides are 7 cm and 9 cm with an included angle 60°. The third side: c² = 49 + 81 − 2 × 7 × 9 × cos 60° = 130 − 63 = 67, so c = **8.19 cm**. Area = ½ × 7 × 9 × sin 60° = **27.3 cm²**.

**Example 13 (Higher tier only).** To find an angle from three sides 5, 6 and 7 (angle opposite 7): cos A = (5² + 6² − 7²) ÷ (2 × 5 × 6) = 12 ÷ 60 = 0.2, so A = **78.5°**.

## Choosing a method

| You know | Use |
|---|---|
| Right-angled, two sides, need third | Pythagoras |
| Right-angled, one side and an angle, or two sides and need an angle | SOH CAH TOA |
| Any triangle: two angles and a side | Sine rule |
| Any triangle: two sides and the included angle | Cosine rule (or area formula) |
| Any triangle: three sides | Cosine rule |

## Common errors

- Writing a bearing with fewer than three figures, or measuring anticlockwise.
- Using the slant height for volume of a cone instead of the perpendicular height.
- Using diameter in place of radius in circle formulae.
- (Higher tier only) Using k² for volumes or k³ for areas; areas use k² and volumes use k³.
- Calculator left in radians.
- Using sin⁻¹ when the question needs an exact value.
- Applying the sine rule to a pair that is not opposite.
