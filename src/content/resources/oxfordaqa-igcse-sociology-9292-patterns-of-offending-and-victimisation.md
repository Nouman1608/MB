This guide covers the final content statement of **3.2.2.4** in Paper 2 of OxfordAQA International
GCSE Sociology (9292): "**Patterns of offending and victimisation by: gender; social class; age;
ethnicity; explanations of these patterns.**" The [Socialisation study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-socialisation/)
records that 3.2.2.4 is not covered on that page, and the
[research methods (Paper 2) study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-research-methods-paper2/)
covers how crime is measured. This page covers what the patterns are and how sociologists explain
them. It describes patterns in general terms; exact figures vary by country and year, so use data from
a society you know when you can.

## Two cautions before the patterns

1. **Official statistics record only some crime.** They depend on what is reported, recorded and
   prosecuted. A pattern in official data may partly reflect who is policed, not only who offends.
   Victim surveys and self-report studies help to check this.
2. **Patterns intersect.** Gender, class, age and ethnicity combine. Following **Crenshaw's** idea of
   intersectionality (from 3.2.1), a young, working-class man from a minority ethnic group may be
   affected by several patterns at once.

## Gender

**Offending pattern.** In almost every society, men make up the large majority of recorded offenders
and of prisoners, especially for violent crime. Women's recorded offending is lower and more often
involves property offences such as shoplifting.

**Explanations:**

- **Gender socialisation and masculinity.** Boys are often socialised to be tough, competitive and
  risk-taking. **Connell's** idea of **hegemonic masculinity**, the dominant ideal of what it means to
  be a man, helps explain why some men use violence to prove status.
- **Social control of women.** **Heidensohn** (1985) argued that women are controlled more closely at
  home, in public and at work, leaving fewer opportunities to offend.
- **The chivalry thesis.** **Pollak** (1950) argued that a male-dominated justice system treats women
  more leniently, so their crimes are under-recorded. Critics reply that women who break gender norms
  may be judged more harshly, facing **double deviance**: punished for the crime and for being an
  "unfeminine" woman.
- **Changing roles.** **Freda Adler** (1975) argued that as women gained more freedom, their offending
  would rise. This "liberation thesis" is disputed.

**Victimisation pattern.** Women are far more likely to be victims of domestic abuse and sexual
violence, much of it hidden from official statistics. Men are more often victims of violence in
public and by strangers, and most victims of homicide worldwide are men, while women who are killed
are far more likely to be killed by a partner or family member.

## Social class

**Offending pattern.** Working-class people, especially young men from poorer areas, are
over-represented in official statistics for street and property crime. Crimes of the middle and upper
classes, such as white-collar and corporate crime, are under-recorded.

**Explanations:**

- **Strain theory.** **Merton** (1938) argued that society encourages everyone to pursue goals such as
  wealth, but not everyone has legitimate means to achieve them. Some people respond by
  **innovation**: using illegal means to reach approved goals.
- **Subcultural theory.** **Albert Cohen** (1955) argued that working-class boys who fail at school
  experience **status frustration** and form subcultures that reward delinquency instead.
- **Social bonds.** **Hirschi** (1969) argued that people with strong attachments, commitments and
  involvement in conventional life have more to lose and offend less.
- **Marxist view.** Laws and policing focus on working-class crime, while crimes of the powerful are
  less often detected or prosecuted.
- **Labelling.** **Cicourel** (1968) found that police and juvenile justice officers held a picture of
  the "typical delinquent" that matched working-class youths, so these young people were more likely
  to be defined as delinquent.

**Victimisation pattern.** People in poorer areas are more likely to be victims of burglary and
violence, while having fewer resources to protect themselves.

## Age

**Offending pattern.** Recorded offending is highest among people in their late teens and early
twenties, then declines with age. This is often called the **age-crime curve**.

**Explanations:**

- **Peer groups and status.** Young people spend more time with peers and may seek status through
  risk-taking.
- **Less to lose.** Following Hirschi, young people have fewer commitments such as jobs, mortgages and
  children; as these grow, offending tends to fall.
- **Visibility and policing.** Young people spend more time in public spaces and attract more police
  attention; moral panics, as studied by **Stanley Cohen**, often focus on youth.

**Victimisation pattern.** Young people are also more likely to be victims of violent crime. Older
people are less often victims of most crimes, but may be targeted by fraud and scams, and may report
greater fear of crime than their risk suggests.

## Ethnicity

**Offending and criminal justice pattern.** In some societies, certain minority ethnic groups are
over-represented in stop and search, arrests and prison populations relative to their share of the
population. In England and Wales, for example, Black people have been more likely than white people to
be stopped and searched, and minority ethnic groups are over-represented in prison; the Lammy Review
(2017) examined this over-representation in the criminal justice system.

**Explanations:**

- **Institutional racism.** The **Macpherson Report** (1999), the inquiry into the police
  investigation of the racist murder of Stephen Lawrence in London in 1993, concluded that the
  Metropolitan Police was institutionally racist.
- **Stereotyping and moral panics.** **Hall and colleagues**, in *Policing the Crisis* (1978), argued
  that a 1970s moral panic about "mugging" linked street crime to young Black men. **Gilroy** described
  a "myth of black criminality".
- **Socio-economic disadvantage.** Some minority groups experience higher rates of poverty and
  unemployment, linking ethnicity to class-based explanations.
- **Marginalisation.** Groups that feel excluded from mainstream society may be more likely to come
  into conflict with the police.

**Victimisation pattern.** Minority ethnic groups are more likely to experience racially motivated
crime and harassment, which may be under-reported where trust in the police is low.

## Worked example 1: a short-answer question

*Explain **one** reason why men commit more recorded crime than women. [3 marks]*

> One reason is gender socialisation (1). Boys are often brought up to be tough and to take risks,
> and Connell's idea of hegemonic masculinity suggests that some men use aggression to prove their
> status (1), so men are more likely to commit violent crimes such as assault (1).

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

*Discuss how far official statistics give an accurate picture of who commits crime. Use examples from
your own society or a society you know about. [12 marks]*

- **Accurate in part:** consistent patterns by gender and age across many societies; victim surveys
  often confirm the gender pattern for violence.
- **Inaccurate in part:** under-recording of white-collar, corporate and domestic crime; Cicourel and
  labelling; institutional racism (Macpherson); chivalry thesis.
- **Application:** a named society, for example who is stopped by police there.
- **Judgement:** statistics reveal real patterns but are shaped by reporting, recording and policing.

## Common mistakes

- Treating official statistics as a complete picture of crime.
- Describing a pattern with no explanation, or an explanation with no sociologist.
- Forgetting victimisation, which is half of the specification statement.
- Writing about ethnicity in a way that implies a group is naturally more criminal, instead of
  analysing policing, disadvantage and labelling.
- Ignoring intersections between gender, class, age and ethnicity.
