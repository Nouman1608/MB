This set practises key question 3.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *How has globalisation
affected inequalities between societies?* Parts (a) to (f) follow the six-part shape the syllabus
describes for Paper 1 Question 3; parts (g) to (k) give extra practice on migration, ecology and
theory.

---

## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *brain drain* **[2]**
- (ii) *remittances* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** pull factors that encourage migration. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** reasons why migrants may face negative perceptions in receiving societies.
**[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways climate change may increase inequalities between societies. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss feminist explanations of global inequality. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that migration benefits receiving societies more than sending societies.
**[14]**

### Extra practice (3.3.1 to 3.3.3)

**(g)** Explain **two** ways migration increases cultural diversity in receiving societies. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain dependency theory. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** ways pollution may affect inequalities between societies. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** economic consequences of migration for receiving societies. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain Wallerstein's world-systems theory. **[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** The loss of skilled and educated workers from a country through emigration (1), for
example doctors and nurses trained at home moving abroad for higher pay (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Money that migrants send back to family or communities in their home country (1),
often used for food, schooling, housing or small businesses (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: jobs; higher wages; safety and political stability; better
education; healthcare; joining family members already there.

**(c) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each (identify + explain):

- **Scapegoating** — migrants may be blamed for unemployment, low wages or housing shortages that
  have wider economic causes.
- **Media representation** — negative or exaggerated reporting can create fear and stereotypes of
  migrants as a threat.
- **Cultural difference** — unfamiliar languages, religions or customs may be seen as a threat to
  national identity.
- **Political campaigns** — some politicians use migration as an issue to win support.
- **Competition for resources** — in areas of poverty, residents may feel they compete with migrants
  for jobs and services.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:

- **Unequal exposure** — poorer countries are often in regions facing drought, floods and storms, so
  they suffer more damage.
- **Unequal ability to adapt** — richer countries can afford flood defences, irrigation and disaster
  relief; poorer ones cannot.
- **Dependence on farming** — where many people rely on agriculture, crop failure directly reduces
  income and food supply.
- **Threat to territory** — low-lying states such as Tuvalu and the Maldives face rising seas despite
  producing few emissions.
- **Climate migration** — people forced from their homes may become poorer and face hostility
  elsewhere.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear, developed discussion with examples and evaluation; 4–6 some
explanation; 1–3 basic points.

- Feminists argue global inequality is shaped by **patriarchy** as well as by class and nation.
- **Global assembly lines:** TNCs employ young women in export factories at low wages, as the Rana
  Plaza disaster (2013) highlighted.
- **Migration:** women migrate as domestic and care workers, often with few rights.
- **Unpaid work and environment:** where women collect water, fuel and food, environmental damage adds
  to their workload.
- **Ownership:** women have limited access to land, credit and property in many societies.
- **Evaluation:** factory work can give women independent income and status; Marxists argue class and
  global capitalism are the main causes; women's experiences differ by class and region
  (intersectionality).

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed and evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

- *Receiving societies gain:* workers fill labour shortages in health care, construction and
  agriculture; migrants pay taxes; skilled migrants arrive already trained; cultural diversity and new
  businesses.
- *But receiving societies may also face:* claimed pressure on housing and services in some areas;
  tensions and negative perceptions.
- *Sending societies gain:* remittances support families, education and local businesses; returning
  migrants bring skills and savings; emigration can reduce local unemployment.
- *But sending societies lose:* brain drain of doctors, nurses and engineers whose training they paid
  for; communities left with mainly older people and children.
- *Marxist view:* migration serves capital in core countries by providing cheap labour, widening
  global inequality.
- *Conclusion:* the balance varies with the skills of migrants and the size of remittances, but
  receiving societies often gain trained workers at little cost, supporting the view. Credit a
  reasoned judgement.

**(g) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Languages and religions** — migrants bring their languages and faiths, adding places of worship
  and community organisations (1 + 1).
- **Food, music and festivals** — new cuisines, music and celebrations become part of everyday life
  for the wider population (1 + 1).
- **Hybrid identities** — children of migrants combine cultures, creating new forms of identity
  (1 + 1).

**(h) [4]** Dependency theory, associated with Andre Gunder Frank, is a Marxist-influenced explanation
of global inequality (1). It argues that rich "metropolis" countries developed by taking surplus —
raw materials, cheap labour and profit — from poor "satellite" countries (1). Underdevelopment is
therefore caused by the global capitalist system (1), not by poor countries being at an earlier stage
of development (1).

**(i) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Exported waste** — hazardous and electronic waste shipped from richer to poorer countries harms
  the health of those who dismantle it (1 + 1).
- **Extraction** — mining and oil production in poorer regions poison local land and water while the
  products are consumed elsewhere (1 + 1).
- **Weak regulation** — TNCs may locate polluting factories where environmental laws are weak, moving
  health costs to poorer societies (1 + 1).

**(j) [4]** Two consequences, 2 marks each:

- **Filling labour shortages** — migrants take jobs in sectors that lack workers, supporting economic
  growth (1 + 1).
- **Tax and spending** — working migrants pay taxes and spend money, supporting public services and
  businesses (1 + 1).
- **Lower wages in some sectors** — some employers may use migrant labour to keep wages down, which
  Marxists link to the reserve army of labour (1 + 1).

**(k) [4]** Immanuel Wallerstein saw the world as a single capitalist economic system (1) divided into
a **core** of rich industrial states (1), a **periphery** of poorer countries supplying raw materials
and cheap labour (1), and a **semi-periphery** in between; wealth flows from the periphery to the core,
maintaining global inequality (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need case studies in (d)?** They are not required, but naming an example such as Tuvalu makes
an explanation more convincing.

**In (f), should I discuss migrants themselves?** Briefly, if it supports your judgement, but the
question asks about receiving and sending societies.
