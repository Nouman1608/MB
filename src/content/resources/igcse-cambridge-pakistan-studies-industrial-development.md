**Industrial development is Section 5 of Paper 2 in Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies 0448.** It
defines primary, secondary and tertiary industry, studies ten named processing and manufacturing
industries plus tourism, and asks why each is located where it is, how the formal and informal
sectors contribute, and how government promotes industrial growth. Good answers link each industry
to a named place and to specific location factors.

## Where does industry fit in 0448 Paper 2?

Industry depends on the raw materials of Sections 2 and 4 (minerals, cotton, sugar cane) and on the
power supply of Section 3. Its products feed into trade (Section 6), its location depends on
transport (Section 7), and its jobs link to employment (Section 8). Questions often ask you to
connect these sections, for example explaining why cotton textile mills cluster near both
cotton-growing areas and the port of Karachi.

## What do the key industrial terms mean?

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Raw material | Natural material used to make something, e.g. raw cotton, limestone |
| Processed | Changed in form, e.g. cotton ginned and spun into yarn |
| Refined | Impurities removed, e.g. crude oil into petrol, cane juice into white sugar |
| Manufactured | Made into a finished product, e.g. cloth into shirts |
| Value-added | Extra value gained at each stage of processing |
| Infrastructure | Basic facilities: roads, railways, power, water, telecommunications |
| Services | Activities that help people rather than make goods: banking, transport, health |
| Primary industry | Extracting natural resources: farming, fishing, mining, forestry |
| Secondary industry | Processing and manufacturing |
| Tertiary industry | Providing services, including tourism |

**Large-scale** industry uses many workers, heavy machinery and large capital in factories.
**Small-scale** industry uses fewer workers in small units. **Cottage** industry is carried out in
homes, often by family members using simple tools.

| Industry | Main locations | Key location factors | Market |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cotton textiles | Faisalabad, Karachi, Lahore, Multan | Raw cotton nearby; labour; port for exports and imported machinery | Domestic and major export |
| Sugar refining | Near cane fields in central Punjab, Sindh and the Peshawar valley | Cane is bulky and loses sugar if not crushed quickly | Mainly domestic |
| Cement | Near limestone and gypsum deposits in the Salt Range, the north-west and near Hyderabad and Karachi | Heavy raw materials; power | Domestic and export |
| Fertiliser | Near gas fields in Sindh and on gas pipelines in Punjab | Natural gas as raw material and fuel | Domestic |
| Iron and steel | Pakistan Steel Mills at Pipri, near Port Qasim, Karachi | Imported iron ore and coking coal arrive by sea | Domestic |
| Oil refining | Karachi, near Rawalpindi (Attock) and near Multan | Imported crude at the coast; local crude in the Potwar; markets | Domestic |
| Sports goods | Sialkot | Skilled workforce, tradition, export links | Mainly export |
| Surgical instruments | Sialkot | Skilled metalworkers in small units | Mainly export |
| Brick | On the edge of most towns | Clay soils, cheap labour, local demand | Domestic |
| Crafts | Throughout, e.g. carpets, pottery, embroidery | Traditional skills, cottage production | Domestic and export |

## What is the difference between the formal and informal sectors?

The **formal sector** is registered with government, pays taxes, keeps accounts and offers regular
wages, often with some protection for workers. The **informal sector** is unregistered: street
vendors, roadside mechanics, small workshops, domestic workers. The informal sector provides cheap
services and many jobs for people without qualifications, needs little capital, and recycles
materials. Its disadvantages are low and irregular pay, no job security, poor working conditions,
use of child labour, and lost tax revenue. Both sectors matter: many cotton garments and sports
goods pass through small informal workshops before reaching formal-sector exporters.

## How does government promote industrial growth?

- Building infrastructure: power, roads, ports, industrial estates.
- Setting up export processing zones (EPZs) with tax and customs concessions. The Karachi EPZ is the best-known example; others include Sialkot and Gwadar.
- Industrial estates such as SITE in Karachi and the Hattar estate in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.
- Loans and credit for small and cottage industry; training institutes; tax holidays; incentives for foreign investment.

**Why build EPZs?** To attract foreign investment, earn foreign exchange, create jobs and bring in
new technology. Their characteristics: secure fenced sites, good infrastructure, duty-free import
of raw materials and machinery, and production mainly for export.

## Can global communications create service jobs?

Call centres and IT services need fast internet links, reliable power and English-speaking,
computer-literate workers. Pakistan's large young workforce and lower wage costs are advantages.
Barriers include power cuts, uneven broadband outside big cities, and competition from other
countries. Assess feasibility by weighing these.

## Is tourism a feasible route to development?

**Natural attractions:** mountains and valleys such as Hunza, Swat and Naran–Kaghan; peaks of the
Karakoram including K2; coastal beaches. **Cultural attractions:** Mohenjo-daro, Taxila, the
Lahore Fort and Badshahi Mosque, Sufi shrines. Tourism creates jobs in hotels, transport and
guiding, earns foreign exchange and adds to GNP and GDP. It is limited by poor access to remote
areas, concerns about security, seasonal demand, and the risk of environmental damage.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Sialkot so important?**
It has a long tradition of skilled small-scale manufacturing and
is the centre of Pakistan's sports goods and surgical instrument industries, which are mostly
exported.

**Why is the steel mill near Karachi?**
Iron ore and coking coal are mostly imported, so a coastal
site near Port Qasim cuts transport costs.

**What is value-added?**
The increase in value when a raw material is processed. Exporting cloth or
garments rather than raw cotton earns more foreign exchange.
