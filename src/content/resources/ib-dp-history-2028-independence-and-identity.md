The focused study **Independence and identity** is one of the five focused studies in IB DP History (first assessment 2028). Two examples are studied, and both are required: **the Haitian Revolution (circa 1780 to 1825)** and **Kenyan independence (1945 to 1978)**. There are three inquiry questions, each with three points of grounding content for each example. The study is assessed through paper 1, in which you use three sources to address an inquiry question, so this guide gives the historical context and shows how to use it with sources. It applies to SL and HL.

## How paper 1 uses this study

Paper 1 (1 hour 15 minutes, 24 marks) uses sources A, B and C and one inquiry question. The questions are always: explain how the content of sources A and B can be used (6 marks); analyse how the context of source C influences how it can be used (6 marks); and examine how the perspectives in all the sources can be used (12 marks). You will not be asked to write about the grounding content directly. You need it to understand the sources.

## Inquiry question 1: What prompted the emergence of the independence movement?

### Haiti

The grounding content names French colonial rule, slave resistance, maroonage and Vodou, and the influence of the French Revolution.

Saint-Domingue, the western third of the island of Hispaniola, was France's richest colony in the eighteenth century, producing large amounts of sugar and coffee. By 1789 its population was around half a million, and a great majority were enslaved people, many of them born in Africa. Above them were free people of colour (many of mixed ancestry, some wealthy and some slave-owners) and white colonists, who were divided between wealthy planters and poorer whites. Slave society was harsh and brutal, with high mortality that required constant imports of new captives.

Resistance was constant. **Maroonage** refers to flight from plantations and the formation of communities of escaped people in remote areas, as well as to shorter absences. **Vodou** was a religion that drew on African traditions and gave enslaved people a shared spiritual and social life. The leader Makandal had been executed in 1758 after a campaign of poisonings, and became a symbol of resistance. The ceremony at Bois Caïman in August 1791 is closely associated with the start of the great revolt, and historians continue to debate details of it.

The French Revolution of 1789 added a political spark. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen raised the question of who counted as a citizen. Free people of colour demanded equal rights; white colonists wanted their own autonomy; and the divisions between them weakened colonial authority.

### Kenya

The grounding content names British colonial rule, the resistance of the Kenya African Union, and the influence of the Second World War.

Kenya was a British colony (the East Africa Protectorate became the Colony of Kenya in 1920). European settlers took large areas of the fertile highlands, often called the White Highlands, which was a major grievance for the Kikuyu, whose land had been taken. Africans were subject to taxes, pass laws and restrictions on growing cash crops and on political organisation. The **Kenya African Union (KAU)**, formed in 1944 and led from 1947 by Jomo Kenyatta, called for land reform, political representation and an end to racial discrimination.

The Second World War had a large effect. Many Kenyans served in the King's African Rifles and saw service in Africa and Asia, and they returned with new skills, wider experience and expectations. At the same time, the war accelerated economic change and urban growth, and the post-war world saw independence movements in Asia, such as in India in 1947. Those who came back often found few opportunities, and resentment increased.

## Inquiry question 2: How was independence achieved?

### Haiti

The grounding content names the war for freedom in Saint-Domingue (1791) and the abolition of slavery (1793), the independence struggle (1794 to 1804), and the roles of Toussaint Louverture and Jean-Jacques Dessalines.

The revolt of August 1791 spread through the northern plain. In 1793, facing war with Britain and Spain, the French commissioner Léger-Félicité Sonthonax proclaimed freedom for enslaved people in the north, and in February 1794 the revolutionary government in Paris abolished slavery in all French territories. **Toussaint Louverture**, a formerly enslaved man who had joined the rebels, changed sides to the French republic in 1794, and became the colony's most powerful figure. He defeated British forces and by 1801 controlled the entire island.

Napoleon Bonaparte, who wanted to restore French control and slavery in the Caribbean, sent a large expedition under his brother-in-law, General Leclerc, in 1802. Toussaint was seized by treachery and deported to France, where he died in 1803. Resistance continued under **Jean-Jacques Dessalines** and other commanders. Disease (especially yellow fever) devastated the French army, and the rebels won a decisive victory at Vertières in November 1803. Independence was proclaimed on 1 January 1804.

### Kenya

The grounding content names the Mau Mau Uprising (1952 to 1960), the Lancaster House Conferences, self-government and multi-party politics (1960 to 1963), and the role of Jomo Kenyatta.

The Mau Mau movement, drawn largely from the Kikuyu, with Embu and Meru supporters, used oaths and guerrilla war in the forests, and attacked settlers and also Africans who were seen as collaborating with the British. In October 1952 the British declared a state of emergency and arrested Kenyatta and others. Kenyatta was tried at Kapenguria and imprisoned. The British response included large military operations, villagisation and detention camps in which abuses were widespread. Historians disagree about the numbers killed, and about how much Mau Mau forced political change. A common view is that the uprising was militarily defeated but changed British thinking about the cost of holding Kenya.

In 1960 the emergency ended and the first Lancaster House Conference in London set the course for African majority rule. Elections followed, and two parties emerged: the Kenya African National Union (KANU), associated with Kenyatta and the larger groups, and the Kenya African Democratic Union (KADU), which favoured regional government. A second conference was held in 1962. Internal self-government began in June 1963 with Kenyatta as prime minister, and independence came on 12 December 1963.

## Inquiry question 3: How, and with what challenges, was a new identity formed?

### Haiti

The grounding content names the constitution (1801) and the Declaration of Independence (1804), continuing social inequalities established during French colonial rule, and the independence debt (1825).

The 1801 constitution, issued by Toussaint's assembly, declared that slavery was abolished forever and made Toussaint governor for life, while Saint-Domingue remained part of the French empire. The 1804 Declaration of Independence proclaimed a break with France. The new state took the Indigenous name Haiti, and its early constitutions stressed freedom and, in 1805, defined its citizens as "black", regardless of skin tone. Inequalities remained. The plantation economy persisted, a military and landowning elite emerged, and divisions between a lighter-skinned elite and the black majority became a source of long-term conflict.

In 1825 King Charles X of France recognised Haiti's independence in return for an indemnity of 150 million francs, backed by a naval presence. The sum was reduced to 90 million in 1838. To pay it, Haiti borrowed from French banks. Historians argue that the debt held back the country's development for generations.

### Kenya

The grounding content names the independence constitution (1963) and the move to a one-party political system, education and the promotion of cultural diversity, and land reforms.

The 1963 independence constitution created a regional (majimbo) system, but this was soon replaced by a strongly centralised state. KADU dissolved in 1964 and Kenya became a republic with Kenyatta as president. A second party, the Kenya People's Union, formed in 1966 under Oginga Odinga and was banned in 1969, which left KANU as the only effective party; one-party rule was made legal in 1982. Education expanded, and the self-help (harambee) movement built schools. National unity was emphasised, and Swahili was promoted alongside English. Land reform was a central issue: schemes funded by Britain and others transferred former settler land to Africans, but many Kenyans criticised the process for favouring the elite and those close to the government.

## Using sources

- Written sources for Haiti include the 1801 constitution, Toussaint's letters, the 1804 declaration and French official reports. A French planter's account and a Haitian general's proclamation will carry very different perspectives.
- For Kenya, sources include Mau Mau oral testimony, colonial reports, Kenyatta's speeches and later academic work such as studies of the detention camps.
- Always ask who wrote the source, for whom and why. A British colonial report on Mau Mau, for example, tends to emphasise criminality, while a participant's memoir tends to emphasise land and freedom.

## Linking to the concepts

- **Cause and consequence:** long-term conditions (plantation slavery, land theft) and short-term triggers (the French Revolution, the emergency of 1952).
- **Continuity and change:** political independence came, but inequalities in land and wealth continued.
- **Perspectives:** colonists, free people of colour, enslaved people; settlers, loyalists, Mau Mau fighters.
- **Significance:** Haiti as the only successful slave revolt to found a state, and Kenya's place in decolonisation.

## Common mistakes

- Treating Toussaint Louverture as the leader who declared independence (that was Dessalines).
- Treating Mau Mau as a single united organisation without complexity.
- Describing events instead of using sources.
- Forgetting that the two examples differ in the role of violence and in the type of colonial rule.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need dates for paper 1?** Dates help you place sources, but the questions ask how to use the sources, not for a chronology.

**Is the debt of 1825 part of the course?** Yes, it is named in the grounding content for Haiti.
