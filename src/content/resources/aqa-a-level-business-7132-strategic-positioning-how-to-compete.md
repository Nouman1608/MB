This guide goes deeper into section **3.8.2 Strategic positioning: choosing how to compete** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.8 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **how to compete in terms of benefits and price**, with strategic positioning to include **Porter's low cost, differentiation and focus strategies**
- **influences on the choice of a positioning strategy**
- **the value of different strategic positioning strategies**
- **the benefits of having a competitive advantage**
- **the difficulties of maintaining a competitive advantage**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## Positioning: benefits and price

Where section 3.8.1 asks "which markets and products?", strategic positioning asks "how will we win customers in them?" Customers choose by comparing the **benefits** a product offers with its **price**. A business can:

- offer **lower prices** than rivals for acceptable benefits
- offer **greater benefits** that justify a higher price
- serve a **narrow group** better than rivals who try to serve everyone.

These choices correspond to Michael Porter's generic strategies.

## Porter's generic strategies

Porter's matrix considers two questions: is the **competitive advantage** based on low cost or on uniqueness, and is the **scope** broad (the whole market) or narrow (a segment)?

| | Competitive advantage: lower cost | Competitive advantage: uniqueness |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad market** | Cost leadership (low cost) | Differentiation |
| **Narrow segment** | Cost focus | Differentiation focus |

### Low cost (cost leadership)

The business becomes the lowest-cost producer in the industry. It can then either charge the lowest price and win market share, or charge average prices and earn higher margins.

*How:* economies of scale, standardised products, tight cost control, efficient processes and technology, low-cost locations, bulk purchasing and minimal frills.

*Benefits:* can survive price wars; high barriers to entry for rivals; strong position against powerful buyers and suppliers.

*Risks:* a rival with even lower costs (for example from a new technology or overseas production); the business may neglect quality, and customers may leave if quality falls; cost cutting can damage staff morale and supplier relations; rivals can copy low-cost methods.

### Differentiation

The business offers products that customers see as unique and valuable, and can therefore charge a **premium price**.

*How:* superior quality, design, brand image, innovation, service, reliability, exclusivity.

*Benefits:* higher margins; customer loyalty and lower price sensitivity; protection from rivals' price cutting; defence against substitutes.

*Risks:* the premium may not cover the cost of the extra features; customers may decide the difference is not worth it, especially in a recession; rivals can imitate; image can be damaged by one scandal.

### Focus

The business concentrates on a **narrow segment**, such as a particular customer group, region or product type, and serves it better than broad competitors. Focus can be based on **cost** (low prices in a niche) or **differentiation** (unique products for a niche).

*Benefits:* detailed knowledge of customers; less direct competition from large rivals; can be profitable with a small market; loyal customers.

*Risks:* the niche may be small or shrinking; larger rivals may enter; customer tastes can change; dependency on one segment.

### "Stuck in the middle"

Porter warned that a business with neither the lowest cost nor a distinct, valued difference earns poor returns. It cannot compete on price, and customers see no reason to pay more. A business is likely to do better by choosing a clear position and committing to it.

## Worked example: three footwear makers

| | Low cost (Tamsin Footwear) | Differentiation (Aldric & Rowe) | No clear position (Brightwell Shoes) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price per pair | £40 | £70 | £55 |
| Cost per pair | £34 | £55 | £50 |
| Profit per pair | £6 | £15 | £5 |
| Profit margin | 15.0% | 21.4% | 9.1% |
| Pairs sold a year | 100,000 | 40,000 | 50,000 |
| Annual profit | £600,000 | £600,000 | £250,000 |

**Analysis.** Both clear strategies earn the same total profit, but in different ways: Tamsin earns a thin margin on high volume; Aldric & Rowe earns a high margin on lower volume. Brightwell is in the middle, with neither the lowest cost nor a strong brand, and earns far less.

**Test of resilience.** Suppose a price war forces Tamsin to cut its price 10% to £36. Profit per pair falls from £6 to £2, so annual profit falls from £600,000 to **£200,000**, a fall of two-thirds, unless volume rises. Now suppose Aldric & Rowe's costs rise 10% to £60.50 per pair. Its profit per pair falls to £9.50, so annual profit is **£380,000**, a fall of 37%. Both are vulnerable, but the low-cost firm is more exposed to price cutting, while the differentiator is more exposed to cost rises and to a fall in the appeal of its brand.

**Judgement.** Neither strategy is better in general. The right one depends on the market, the firm's capabilities and the customers' priorities.

## Influences on the choice of positioning

| Influence | Effect |
|---|---|
| Customers' priorities | Price-sensitive customers favour low cost; customers who value quality or status favour differentiation |
| Market structure | A mass market with similar products favours cost leadership; fragmented markets offer niches for focus |
| Firm's resources and competences | Scale, buying power and process skill favour cost; design, brand, and research favour differentiation |
| Competitors' positions | Avoid a crowded position; look for gaps |
| Industry economics | High fixed costs and economies of scale favour size and low cost |
| Technology | Cheap technology can lower costs or enable customisation |
| Ownership and objectives | A plc seeking rapid growth may pursue volume; a family firm may prefer a protected niche |
| The economy | A recession pushes buyers to cheaper options |

## The value of different strategies

| Strategy | Value | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Low cost | Resilience in price wars; volume | Needs scale; easily undercut; quality risk |
| Differentiation | Margin, loyalty | Higher costs; imitation; recession sensitivity |
| Focus | Specialist knowledge; less rivalry | Small market; larger rivals can follow |

## Competitive advantage

A **competitive advantage** is something that lets a business consistently beat rivals in winning customers or earning profits, such as lower costs, a stronger brand, better technology, patents or a loyal customer base.

**Benefits:**

- higher profit margins or market share
- loyal customers who are less likely to switch
- ability to attract finance and talented staff
- a stronger position against buyers, suppliers and new entrants (see Porter's five forces in section 3.7.7)
- more freedom to invest in innovation.

**Difficulties of maintaining it:**

- **Imitation:** rivals copy products, processes and prices. The easier the copying, the shorter the advantage.
- **Technological change:** a new technology can wipe out a cost or quality lead.
- **Changing customer tastes:** what customers value changes, so a strength today may be irrelevant tomorrow.
- **Complacency:** success can create inertia; a business that stops improving loses its edge.
- **New entrants and global competition:** firms with lower costs or fresh ideas appear.
- **Cost of maintaining the lead:** continuing investment in research, brand and efficiency is expensive.
- **Staff and knowledge leaving:** skills and know-how depend on people.

A lasting advantage is based on things that are hard to copy: patents, strong brands built over time, unique skills (core competences) and economies of scale.

## Positioning and the customer

Positioning begins with the customer. A useful way to compare options is to map price against perceived benefit.

| Position | Price | Benefit | Typical customer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low cost | Lowest in the market | Adequate | Price-sensitive |
| Differentiation | Premium | Unique and highly valued | Quality- or status-conscious |
| Focus | Depends on the niche | Tailored to the niche | A narrow group with particular needs |
| Middle | Average | Average | No clear group |

Firms in the middle are vulnerable from both sides: low-cost rivals take price-sensitive customers and differentiators take those who value quality.

## Combining strategies

Some businesses pursue both low cost and differentiation, for example by using scale to deliver a good-quality product cheaply. This can work, but it is hard to do and rivals can imitate parts of it. Porter warned that trying to do everything weakens the message to customers and strains the organisation. In an examination, judge whether a combined strategy is realistic for the firm's resources.

## Keeping the advantage

Ask four questions of any advantage: is it valuable to customers, rare, hard to copy, and supported by the organisation (people, processes, culture)? An advantage that passes all four is likely to last longer. One that passes only the first is likely to be copied.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** the strategy from evidence in the case: price level, cost structure, product features, the size of the target group.
- **Analyse** the effect on profit, customers and risk.
- **Evaluate** by considering the business's circumstances and the sustainability of the advantage.
- Use the numbers: compare margins and volumes and say what each means.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing low cost with low price. A cost leader may charge average prices.
- Assuming differentiation always justifies a high price.
- Treating focus as simply being small.
- Forgetting that an advantage can be copied.
- Recommending a strategy without linking it to the business's resources.
