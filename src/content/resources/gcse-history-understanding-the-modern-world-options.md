This guide covers **Paper 1: Understanding the Modern World** of AQA
GCSE History (8145) in more depth than a general course overview --
specifically, the named period study and wider world depth study options
AQA offers, since the [course structure guide](/resources/gcse-history-course-structure/)
covers only the two-component shape of the qualification and does not
itemise these named choices.

## Where this fits in 8145

Paper 1 is tested through a mixture of short-answer questions,
source-based tasks and extended writing, split into two equally weighted
sections: Section A (a period study) and Section B (a wider world depth
study). Every centre teaching AQA GCSE History selects one option from
each section -- so two schools studying the same specification may sit
the same exam paper format while revising entirely different historical
content.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA GCSE HISTORY (8145) — PAPER 1: UNDERSTANDING THE MODERN WORLD**

**Section A: Period studies** (choose one) —

- **AA. America, 1840–1895: Expansion and Consolidation** — westward
expansion, conflict with Plains Indians, the American Civil War and its
aftermath, and the consolidation of the nation through to 1895.
- **AB. Germany, 1890–1945: Democracy and Dictatorship** — the growth
and collapse of Weimar democracy, the rise of the Nazi Party, the
establishment of Hitler's dictatorship, and the experiences of Germans
under Nazi rule.
- **AC. Russia, 1894–1945: Tsardom and Communism** — the end of Tsarist
rule, Lenin's Bolshevik government, and Stalin's dictatorship and
modernisation of the USSR.
- **AD. America, 1920–1973: Opportunity and Inequality** — the economic
boom of the 1920s, the Depression and New Deal, and post-war society
including the Civil Rights movement.

**Section B: Wider world depth studies** (choose one) —

- **BA. Conflict and Tension: The First World War, 1894–1918** — the
alliance system, Anglo-German rivalry, the outbreak and stalemate of the
war, and its ending.
- **BB. Conflict and Tension: The Inter-War Years, 1918–1939** — the
Treaty of Versailles, the League of Nations, and the escalation of
tension leading to the outbreak of the Second World War.
- **BC. Conflict and Tension Between East and West, 1945–1972** — the
origins and development of the Cold War, the Berlin Wall, the Cuban
missile crisis, and the move towards détente.
- **BD. Conflict and Tension in Asia, 1950–1975** — the causes and
course of the Korean War and the Vietnam War.
- **BE. Conflict and Tension in the Gulf and Afghanistan, 1990–2009** —
the 1990 Gulf War, the war on Al-Qaeda following 11 September, and the
2003 Iraq War.

## How to approach it

Because each option covers roughly half a century of change, the most
useful first step is building a simple timeline for your specific
period or depth study before attempting thematic revision -- knowing
the sequence of events makes it far easier to explain causation and
consequence later. Every option AQA offers rewards the same two
recurring skills regardless of content: explaining *why* things
happened and changed (causation) and analysing *how far* things changed
over time (change and continuity), so build revision notes around these
two questions for your specific period rather than around isolated
facts. For period studies especially, examiners consistently reward
answers that link political, economic, social and cultural strands of
change together, rather than treating them as separate categories. For
wider world depth studies, since these focus more tightly on the causes,
events and outcomes of specific conflicts, practise structuring answers
around short-term triggers versus longer-term underlying causes, since
exam questions frequently ask students to weigh the relative importance
of different causes.

Each option also has an internal three-part structure worth knowing in
advance. Period studies such as AB Germany move through a first phase
covering the establishment or collapse of a political system, a middle
phase covering a period of crisis or upheaval, and a final phase
covering the lived experience of ordinary people under the resulting
system -- so a strong revision plan covers all three phases rather than
concentrating only on the most dramatic middle section. Wider world
depth studies such as BB The Inter-War Years follow a similar three-part
pattern: the immediate aftermath of a prior conflict, an international
institution or diplomatic effort intended to preserve peace, and the
breakdown of that effort into renewed conflict. Recognising this shared
shape across different options makes it much faster to orient yourself
if you ever need to sanity-check revision notes or past-paper questions
against the specification.

## Worked example: applying causation to any option

Whichever period or depth study you are revising, a reliable way to
structure a causation answer is to sort the causes you know into three
categories -- long-term (underlying conditions built up over years or
decades), short-term (events in the run-up to the outcome being
explained), and the trigger (the specific event that precipitated
it) -- and then explain how the categories interact rather than listing
them in isolation. For example, in BB The Inter-War Years, the
long-term cause of German resentment at the Treaty of Versailles, the
short-term cause of the Depression's economic impact on Germany, and the
trigger of Hitler's appointment as Chancellor in January 1933 all need
to be connected to explain the collapse of Weimar democracy convincingly
-- naming all three without showing how they reinforced each other tends
to cap marks at the lower half of the top level.

## Common mistakes

Revising a period or depth study option your school did not actually
choose -- always confirm your exact options (for example, "AB Germany"
rather than "AC Russia") before using any revision resource. Treating
Section A and Section B as requiring the same style of answer, when
period studies test a broader sweep of political, economic and social
change while depth studies test a tighter focus on the causes and course
of specific conflicts. Writing narrative accounts of events without
addressing the specific historical concept the question is testing,
such as causation, consequence, or change and continuity.

## Quick revision checklist

- Confirm exactly which period study (AA-AD) and which wider world
depth study (BA-BE) your school teaches for Paper 1.
- Build a timeline of key events for each of your two chosen options
before attempting thematic revision.
- Practise linking political, economic, social and cultural change for
your period study.
- For your depth study, practise weighing short-term triggers against
longer-term causes.
- Use the [Historic Environment study resources](/resources/gcse-history-historic-environment-revision-notes/)
separately -- that content sits within Paper 2, not Paper 1.
