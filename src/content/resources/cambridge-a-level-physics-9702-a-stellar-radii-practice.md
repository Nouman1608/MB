## Section A

**1.** State Wien's displacement law. **[1]**

**2.** The spectrum of a star has its peak at a wavelength of 380 nm. Calculate the surface temperature of the star. **[1]**

**3.** Calculate the wavelength at which a star of surface temperature 3200 K emits most strongly. **[1]**

**4.** A star has radius 9.0 × 10⁸ m and surface temperature 6500 K. Calculate its luminosity. **[2]**

**5.** Star A has twice the radius of star B and half its surface temperature. Calculate the ratio (luminosity of A) ÷ (luminosity of B). **[2]**

**6.** Suggest why a radius calculated using Wien's law and the Stefan-Boltzmann law is only an estimate. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** The star in Question 2 has a luminosity of 9.0 × 10²⁷ W.

**(a)** Calculate T⁴ for the star. **[1]**

**(b)** Estimate the radius of the star. **[2]**

**(c)** The radius of the Sun is 7.0 × 10⁸ m. Express the star's radius as a multiple of the Sun's radius. **[1]**

**8.** A star is at a distance of 1.2 × 10¹⁸ m from the Earth. The radiant flux intensity of its radiation at the Earth is 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ W m⁻², and its spectrum peaks at 580 nm.

**(a)** Calculate the luminosity of the star. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate its surface temperature. **[1]**

**(c)** Estimate its radius. **[2]**

**9.** Two stars have the same luminosity. The spectrum of star A peaks at 300 nm and that of star B at 900 nm.

**(a)** Calculate the ratio (surface temperature of A) ÷ (surface temperature of B). **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the ratio (radius of A) ÷ (radius of B). **[2]**

**10.** The Sun has a radius of 7.0 × 10⁸ m and a surface temperature of 5800 K.

**(a)** Calculate the luminosity of the Sun. **[2]**

**(b)** In the far future the Sun may become a red giant with a radius 100 times larger and a surface temperature of 3000 K. Calculate the factor by which its luminosity would change. **[2]**

**11.** White dwarf stars have very high surface temperatures but very low luminosities. Explain how this is possible. **[2]**

**12.** Describe how the black-body spectrum of a star (intensity against wavelength) changes if its surface temperature rises from 4000 K to 8000 K. **[3]**

**13.** The luminosity of a star is known exactly, but its surface temperature is found with an uncertainty of ±4 %. Determine the percentage uncertainty in the calculated radius. **[2]**

**14.** Star P appears red and star Q appears blue-white. The two stars have the same radius. State and explain which star has the greater luminosity. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The wavelength at which a black body (star) emits maximum power is inversely proportional to its thermodynamic (surface) temperature [1].

**2.** T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ ÷ 380 × 10⁻⁹ = 7600 K [1].

**3.** λ_max = 2.9 × 10⁻³ ÷ 3200 = 9.1 × 10⁻⁷ m (910 nm) [1].

**4.** L = 4πσr²T⁴ = 4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (9.0 × 10⁸)² × 6500⁴ [1] = 1.0 × 10²⁷ W [1].

**5.** L ∝ r²T⁴ [1]; ratio = 2² × (½)⁴ = 4 ÷ 16 = 0.25 [1].

**6.** Stars are not perfect black bodies (or: the surface temperature varies over the star), so the temperature from Wien's law is approximate [1].

**7. (a)** T⁴ = 7630⁴ = 3.4 × 10¹⁵ K⁴ [1].

**(b)** r = √(L/(4πσT⁴)) = √(9.0 × 10²⁷ ÷ (4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 3.4 × 10¹⁵)) [1] = 1.9 × 10⁹ m [1].

**(c)** 1.9 × 10⁹ ÷ 7.0 × 10⁸ = 2.8 [1].

**8. (a)** L = 4πd²F = 4π × (1.2 × 10¹⁸)² × 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ [1] = 1.1 × 10²⁸ W [1].

**(b)** T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ ÷ 580 × 10⁻⁹ = 5000 K [1].

**(c)** r = √(1.09 × 10²⁸ ÷ (4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 5000⁴)) [1] = 4.9 × 10⁹ m [1].

**9. (a)** T ∝ 1/λ_max, so T_A/T_B = 900 ÷ 300 = 3 [1].

**(b)** Same L, so r ∝ 1/T² [1]; r_A/r_B = (1/3)² = 1/9 (0.11) [1].

**10. (a)** L = 4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (7.0 × 10⁸)² × 5800⁴ [1] = 4.0 × 10²⁶ W [1].

**(b)** L ∝ r²T⁴, so factor = 100² × (3000 ÷ 5800)⁴ [1] = 10⁴ × 0.0716 = 720 (an increase) [1].

**11.** The power emitted per unit area (σT⁴) is very high because of the high temperature [1], but L = 4πσr²T⁴ and the radius is very small, so the total surface area, and so the luminosity, is small [1].

**12.** The intensity increases at every wavelength (the curve lies higher everywhere) [1]; the peak moves to a shorter wavelength, halving from about 725 nm to about 363 nm [1]; the total power (area under the curve) increases by a factor of 2⁴ = 16 [1].

**13.** r ∝ T⁻² when L is fixed [1], so the percentage uncertainty in r is 2 × 4 % = ±8 % [1].

**14.** Star Q is more luminous [1]: its spectrum peaks at a shorter wavelength, so by Wien's law its surface temperature is higher, and with equal radii L ∝ T⁴ [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving λ_max in nanometres when calculating a temperature.
- Using T² instead of T⁴ in the Stefan-Boltzmann law, or forgetting to take the square root for r.
- Substituting the star's distance from the Earth for its radius.
- Keeping σ and 4π in ratio questions, adding avoidable arithmetic.
- Assuming the most luminous star must be the hottest.
- Ignoring the factor of two when finding the uncertainty in r from the uncertainty in T.
