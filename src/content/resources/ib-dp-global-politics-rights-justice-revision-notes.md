The [full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-syllabus-guide/) confirms all three
thematic studies are compulsory for every student, at both SL and HL. These notes cover **Rights and
justice**, alongside the
[Peace and conflict revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-peace-conflict-revision-notes/)
already on the site.

## Why this theme is universal

Unlike the geography course's teacher-selected options, all three DP Global Politics thematic studies
(Rights and justice, Development and sustainability, Peace and conflict) are compulsory for every
student. Rights and justice is therefore just as high-value to revise thoroughly as Peace and
conflict, since Paper 2's extended-response questions can draw on any of the three.

## Applying the four core concepts

Rights and justice is not revised as a stand-alone content block -- it is revised through the same
four concepts that structure the whole course: **power** (who defines and enforces rights), 
**sovereignty** (a state's authority to determine rights within its own borders, and the tension this
creates with universal rights claims), **legitimacy** (whether a rights claim or its denial is widely
accepted as valid), and **interdependence** (how rights issues in one place create pressure or
obligations elsewhere, for example through international human rights law).

## Worked example: applying the four concepts to a rights issue

A question asks students to analyse a contemporary human rights issue using the course's core
concepts.

```
Power:            which actors (a state, an international body, a
                   civil society movement) hold the power to define or
                   enforce the right in question
Sovereignty:       does the state where the issue occurs claim
                   exclusive authority to handle it internally, and
                   how does this interact with international human
                   rights frameworks
Legitimacy:        is the state's position, or the rights claim being
                   made against it, widely accepted as valid by other
                   states and international institutions
Interdependence:   how does this issue create pressure, obligations,
                   or consequences for actors beyond the state where
                   it occurs
```

Structuring an answer around all four concepts explicitly -- rather than describing the rights issue
in isolation -- is the assessed skill this thematic study specifically rewards, and the same structure
applies equally to Development and sustainability or Peace and conflict.

## Building a case-study bank

Because the specification is explicitly built around "contemporary real-world examples," keep a
running log of rights and justice case studies as the course progresses, each tagged to which of the
four core concepts it best illustrates -- current, live examples tend to be more persuasive in an
extended-response answer than well-known but dated cases the whole cohort is also using.

## Engagement project connections

Because the internally assessed engagement project rewards direct, personal engagement with a
political issue, Rights and justice is a common and productive choice of topic — a student might
engage with a local rights campaign, a legal case, or an advocacy organisation's work. Where the
engagement project draws on Rights and justice, use the same four core concepts to structure the
written report's analysis, since consistency between taught content and the project's own framing
strengthens the analytical depth examiners look for.

## Comparing across the three thematic studies

Because Paper 2 may combine or contrast content from more than one thematic study in a single
extended-response question, practise explicitly comparing a Rights and justice example with one from
Development and sustainability or Peace and conflict — for instance, discussing how a conflict
situation (Peace and conflict) also raises rights violations (Rights and justice) affecting the same
population. This kind of cross-theme fluency, using the same four core concepts throughout, reflects
the genuinely interconnected way real political issues rarely respect the syllabus's own theme
boundaries.

## Exam traps

- Describing a rights issue in isolation without explicitly applying power, sovereignty, legitimacy or
  interdependence.
- Treating Rights and justice as less important than Peace and conflict, when all three thematic
  studies are equally compulsory and examinable.
- Using only dated, textbook case studies instead of current, real-world examples the specification
  explicitly expects.
- At HL, forgetting that the global political challenges extension expects genuine synthesis across
  all three thematic studies, not treatment of Rights and justice as fully separate from the others.

## A note on assessment structure

Rights and justice, like the other two thematic studies, is examined through Paper 2's
extended-response format: Section A tests understanding of core concepts using an example from the
theme, and Section B combines a short response with an essay response. Because the same format applies
across all three thematic studies, practising this structure once with Rights and justice content
builds a technique directly transferable to Development and sustainability and Peace and conflict as
well.

## Self-test

1. Which four concepts structure every thematic study in this course, including Rights and justice?
2. Why is Rights and justice just as important to revise as Peace and conflict?
3. What tension does "sovereignty" create for rights issues specifically?
4. What kind of case studies does the specification explicitly favour?
5. What does HL's global political challenges extension require in relation to the three thematic
   studies?

**Answers:** 1. Power, sovereignty, legitimacy and interdependence. 2. Because all three thematic
studies (Rights and justice, Development and sustainability, Peace and conflict) are compulsory for
every student and can appear in Paper 2's extended-response questions. 3. The tension between a
state's claimed authority to handle rights issues within its own borders and universal or
international human rights claims that may conflict with that authority. 4. Contemporary, real-world
examples, ideally current ones followed as the course progresses rather than only dated textbook
cases. 5. Genuine synthesis of material across Rights and justice, Development and sustainability and
Peace and conflict, rather than treating them as separate, unconnected topics.
