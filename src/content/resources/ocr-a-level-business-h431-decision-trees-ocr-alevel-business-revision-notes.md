## What the specification lists

- Nature and purpose of decision trees.
- Calculate and interpret probabilities, in decimals and percentages.
- Calculate and interpret economic outcomes and expected values.
- Evaluate the outcomes shown; evaluate how a business should respond.
- Assess the importance of the information shown and the usefulness of a tree.

## Core recall

### Conventions

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Square | Decision node: the business chooses |
| Circle | Chance node: outcomes depend on probabilities |
| Number on a decision branch | Cost of that option |
| Number at a branch end | Payoff |

- Probabilities at each chance node add up to 1 (100%).
- Work from right to left: EV at chance nodes, best option at decision nodes.

### Formulas (standard forms)

- Expected value (EV) at a chance node = sum of (probability × payoff).
- Net gain of an option = EV − cost of the option.
- Economic outcome of a branch = payoff − costs paid along that path.
- EV of economic outcomes = net gain (same answer by a second route).

### Interpreting

- Highest net gain = best option on average.
- Also report: the worst economic outcome, the chance of a loss and the range from best to worst.
- A probability of 0.3 means about 30 in 100 similar cases, not a promise.

### Switching probability

- Write the net gain of the riskier option in terms of p; set it equal to the other option's net gain; solve for p.
- If the estimate is close to the switching point, the recommendation is fragile.

### Value of information (test markets, research)

- A test is worth paying for only if it may change the decision.
- Value of the information ≈ EV with the test (before its cost) − EV without it.
- If the test costs more than this, it lowers EV, but may still be chosen to cut the worst-case loss.

### Responding to a tree

1. Check the worst case against reserves.
2. Test sensitivity of probabilities and payoffs.
3. Question the source of the numbers and any bias.
4. Consider more information if the choice is close.
5. Add qualitative factors.
6. Use investment appraisal too, since a tree ignores timing.

### Usefulness

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Sets out options, outcomes and probabilities | Probabilities and payoffs are estimates |
| Makes assumptions visible | EV is an average, not a result |
| Weighs risk explicitly | Real outcomes are more varied than the branches |
| Handles sequences of decisions | Ignores timing of cash flows |
| Allows sensitivity checks | Ignores qualitative factors; false precision |

Most useful: costly one-off decisions with evidence-based probabilities. Least useful: new markets where probabilities are guesses.

## Quick worked reminder

Westerby Foods (invented): Option 1 costs £120,000, 0.6 × £300,000 and 0.4 × £80,000, so net gain £92,000; economic outcomes +£180,000 or −£40,000. Option 2 costs £50,000, 0.7 × £140,000 and 0.3 × £60,000, so net gain £66,000; outcomes +£90,000 or +£10,000. Option 2 becomes better if the chance of high demand falls below about 0.48.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Decision tree | A diagram comparing options under risk using probabilities and payoffs |
| Decision node | A point where the business chooses between options |
| Chance node | A point where outcomes depend on probability |
| Probability | The estimated likelihood of an outcome, from 0 to 1 |
| Expected value | The probability-weighted average of the payoffs |
| Economic outcome | The payoff of a branch minus the costs paid to reach it |
| Net gain | Expected value minus the cost of the option |
| Sensitivity analysis | Testing how a result changes when an input changes |

## Exam traps

- Subtract the cost of the option.
- Check that probabilities at each node add to 1.
- Work right to left in multi-stage trees.
- Always mention the worst case and its likelihood.
- Do not call EV the profit the business will make.
- Use the case to judge how reliable the probabilities are.

## Self-test

1. What does a square node show?
2. Two of three branches have probabilities 0.45 and 0.35. What is the third?
3. Write 0.08 as a percentage.
4. An option costs £40,000 with outcomes £100,000 (0.7) and £20,000 (0.3). Find the EV and net gain.
5. Find the economic outcome of each branch in question 4.
6. What is the chance of a loss in question 4?
7. In which direction do you work through a two-stage tree?
8. Option A's net gain is 150,000p − 30,000. Option B's is £45,000. Find the probability at which they are equal.
9. A test costs £8,000. EV with the test, before its cost, is £61,000; EV without it is £56,000. Is the test worth it on expected value?
10. Why might a business pay for a test that lowers expected value?
11. Give two limitations of decision trees.
12. When is a decision tree most useful?

### Answers

1. A decision: the business chooses between options.
2. 1 − 0.45 − 0.35 = **0.2**.
3. **8%**.
4. EV = 70,000 + 6,000 = **£76,000**; net gain **£36,000**.
5. +£60,000 and −£20,000.
6. **30%** (0.3).
7. Right to left.
8. 150,000p = 75,000, so **p = 0.5**.
9. No: the information is worth £61,000 − £56,000 = £5,000, less than its £8,000 cost.
10. To reduce the worst-case loss, if the business could not survive a failed launch.
11. Any two: estimates of probabilities and payoffs; EV is an average; ignores timing; ignores qualitative factors; false precision.
12. For costly, one-off decisions where probabilities can be based on evidence.
