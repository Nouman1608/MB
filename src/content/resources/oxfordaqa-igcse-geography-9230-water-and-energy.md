This guide covers section 3.2.3.1, Water and energy, in Section C (Global issues) of Unit 2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. In Section C, students study **either** Water and energy resources **or** Population and communication, so you only need this theme if your class chose the water and energy option. The key ideas are: "Demand for water resources is rising globally but supply can be insecure, which may lead to conflict" and "Different strategies can be used to increase water supply", with the same pair of ideas for energy. This page goes deeper than the unit-level material and gives four worked examples. If your class studied different examples, keep the same headings.

## What the specification lists

**Water**

- Areas of surplus (security) and deficit (insecurity); global inequalities in supply and consumption; global patterns.
- Reasons for rising consumption; factors affecting availability; impacts of insecurity.
- Strategies to increase supply: diverting supplies and increasing storage, dams and reservoirs, transfers and desalination; **an example of a large-scale water transfer scheme** with advantages and disadvantages.
- A sustainable future: water conservation, groundwater management, recycling, grey water; **an example of a local scheme in an LIC or NEE**.

**Energy**

- Areas of surplus and deficit; global inequalities; global distribution.
- Reasons for rising consumption; factors affecting supply; impacts of insecurity.
- Strategies: renewable and non-renewable sources; **an example of fossil fuel extraction** with advantages and disadvantages.
- A sustainable future: carbon footprints, energy conservation; **an example of a local renewable energy scheme in an LIC or NEE**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Water security | Having reliable access to enough safe water |
| Water insecurity | Lacking reliable access to enough safe water |
| Surplus and deficit | More than needed and less than needed |
| Abstraction | Taking water from rivers, lakes or underground |
| Aquifer | Underground rock that stores water |
| Desalination | Removing salt from sea water |
| Grey water | Used household water from sinks and showers that can be reused |
| Energy security | Having reliable access to affordable energy |
| Renewable | Energy sources that are replaced naturally, such as wind and solar |
| Non-renewable | Sources that will run out, such as coal, oil, gas |
| Carbon footprint | The amount of carbon dioxide released by an activity or person |

## Water: surplus and deficit

**Global patterns.** Water is unevenly spread. There are large surpluses in places with high rainfall and large rivers, such as parts of Canada, Brazil, Russia and South-East Asia. Deficits (insecurity) occur in dry regions such as North Africa and the Middle East, and in densely populated areas where demand is high relative to supply, such as parts of South Asia and northern China.

There are two kinds of shortage. **Physical scarcity** means that there is not enough water naturally, as in deserts. **Economic scarcity** means that water exists but people cannot afford or reach it, as in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa, where the lack of pipes and wells, not rainfall, limits access.

**Global inequalities.** Consumption is very unequal. People in HICs may use hundreds of litres a day at home, while many people in LICs have access to only a few tens of litres. More than two billion people do not have safely managed drinking water at home.

**Reasons for rising consumption.**

- **Economic development:** more industry, power stations and irrigation, and more households with taps, washing machines and flush toilets.
- **Rising population:** more people mean more drinking water, food and goods.
- Rising living standards change diets, with more meat, which needs more water to produce.

**Factors affecting water availability.**

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Climate | Low or unreliable rainfall; high evaporation |
| Geology | Permeable rocks store water in aquifers; impermeable rocks give surface water but little groundwater |
| Pollution of supply | Sewage, industry and farm chemicals make water unsafe |
| Over-abstraction | Taking water faster than it is replaced lowers water tables and dries rivers |
| Limited infrastructure | No pipes, wells or treatment plants |
| Poverty | People cannot pay for water, treatment or equipment |

**Impacts of water insecurity.**

- **Waterborne disease and pollution:** diarrhoea, cholera and typhoid spread where people drink unsafe water.
- **Food production:** less water for irrigation reduces harvests.
- **Industrial output:** industries such as manufacturing and power need water.
- **Conflict:** where demand is greater than supply and rivers cross borders, countries may compete, as around the Nile, the Jordan and the Tigris and Euphrates.

## Water strategies

| Strategy | How it works | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dams and reservoirs | Store water in wet periods for use in dry ones | Reliable supply; also power and flood control | Costly; flood land; displace people; reduce flow downstream |
| Water transfers | Move water in canals or pipes from areas of surplus to deficit | Brings water to cities and farms in dry regions | Very expensive; affect source area; may cause conflict |
| Desalination | Removes salt from seawater | Reliable in coastal dry areas | High energy use and cost; brine waste |

### Example: a large-scale water transfer, South-North Water Transfer, China

Northern China, including Beijing and Tianjin, is dry and densely populated, with a large demand for water. The south, in the Yangtze basin, has far more. The government built a system of canals to move water from south to north, along planned eastern, middle and western routes. The eastern and middle routes are in operation. The middle route carries water from the Danjiangkou reservoir on the Han River, a tributary of the Yangtze, more than a thousand kilometres to Beijing and Tianjin.

- **Advantages:** supplies water to tens of millions of people in the north; supports industry and farming; reduces the pressure on groundwater that has been over-used.
- **Disadvantages:** very expensive, costing tens of billions of dollars; hundreds of thousands of people were relocated to build the reservoir and canals; less water in the Han River and the source area; risks of pollution along the route; water in the north may remain costly.

Check these against your own notes. A common alternative is the Lesotho Highlands Water Project.

## Water: towards a sustainable future

- **Conservation:** fixing leaks, water meters, efficient appliances, drip irrigation.
- **Groundwater management:** limiting abstraction to the rate at which aquifers refill.
- **Recycling and grey water:** reusing water from showers and sinks for toilets and gardens.

### Example: a local scheme in an LIC or NEE, sand dams in Kitui, Kenya

Kitui is a dry area of eastern Kenya where rivers flow only after rain. Communities build **sand dams**, small concrete walls built across seasonal river beds. When the river flows, sand collects behind the wall and water is stored within the sand, protected from evaporation. Villagers dig wells or pipes to reach the water through the dry season.

- **Advantages:** low cost; built and maintained by local people with simple tools; water is available close to home, saving women and children hours of walking; water is cleaner because sand filters it; helps farming and small gardens.
- **Disadvantages:** needs a suitable river bed; can take years to fill with sand; yields are limited and may not supply a large population.

## Energy: surplus and deficit

**Global patterns.** Energy consumption per person is highest in HICs and rising fast in NEEs such as China and India. Producers of fossil fuels are concentrated in a few regions, such as the Middle East, Russia and North America, so many countries have to import energy and are insecure. Hundreds of millions of people still lack access to electricity, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa.

**Reasons for rising consumption.**

- **Economic development:** industry, transport and power need energy.
- **Rising population:** more people use more energy.
- **Technology:** more appliances, vehicles and devices.

**Factors affecting energy supply.**

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Physical | Resources are located in particular places; wind, sun and rivers differ by region |
| Cost of exploitation and production | Deep or remote fields are expensive to develop |
| Technology | Allows extraction from difficult places and use of renewables |
| Political | Governments, conflict, sanctions and agreements can restrict or cut off supply |

**Impacts of energy insecurity.**

- Exploration in **difficult and environmentally sensitive areas**, such as the Arctic and deep sea.
- **Economic and environmental costs** of higher prices, pollution and habitat loss.
- **Food production** suffers when fuel and fertiliser costs rise.
- **Industrial output** falls when energy is not available.
- **Conflict** where demand exceeds supply.

## Energy strategies

| Type | Sources | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-renewable | Coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear | Reliable, large quantities, established technology | Run out; fossil fuels release carbon dioxide; nuclear waste |
| Renewable | Biomass, wind, hydro, tidal, geothermal, wave, solar | Do not run out; low carbon in use | Some are intermittent; high set-up cost; may affect landscape or wildlife |

### Example: fossil fuel extraction, oil in the Niger Delta, Nigeria

Oil has been extracted in the Niger Delta since the late 1950s.

- **Advantages:** oil provides most of Nigeria's export earnings and a large share of government revenue; it creates jobs; and it supplies fuel and money for development.
- **Disadvantages:** oil spills and gas flaring have polluted water, soil and air; farming and fishing, the main livelihoods of many local people, have suffered; local communities have received little of the wealth, which has fuelled unrest and sabotage; there is also corruption.

Check against your own notes. Alternatives include oil sands in Alberta (Canada) or North Sea oil.

## Energy: towards a sustainable future

- **Individual use and carbon footprints:** people can cut their footprint by using less electricity, travelling less by car and eating less meat.
- **Energy conservation:** designing buildings and transport efficiently, for example insulation and public transport; reducing demand; using technology to burn fossil fuels more efficiently.

### Example: a local renewable energy scheme in an LIC or NEE, micro-hydro in Nepal

In the hills of Nepal, many villages are far from the national grid. Communities have built small hydroelectric schemes that divert part of a mountain stream through a pipe to a turbine.

- **Advantages:** provides electricity for lighting, mobile phones and small workshops or mills; cheap to run once built; no fuel needed; uses local water; reduces use of kerosene and wood.
- **Disadvantages:** output falls in the dry season; floods and landslides can damage equipment; building costs are high for poor villages and often rely on donor help; limited power.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain why water supply can be insecure in an area with a growing population. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "A growing population needs more water for drinking, farming and industry, so demand rises. If rainfall stays the same or is unreliable, supply cannot keep up. People may also take water from rivers and aquifers faster than they refill, so water tables fall and rivers dry up. Where there are few pipes or treatment plants, or where people are poor, water may be unsafe or unreachable."

The answer links demand, supply, over-abstraction and infrastructure.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating all shortage as physical.** Economic scarcity is also important.
- **Listing strategies with no advantages and disadvantages.** The specification asks for both.
- **Quoting examples with no detail.** Name the place and give two or three facts.
- **Calling nuclear power renewable.** It is non-renewable.
- **Saying renewable energy has no disadvantages.** Cost, intermittency and local impacts matter.
- **Mixing up water conservation and increasing supply.** Conservation reduces demand.
