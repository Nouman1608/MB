These questions practise key question 5.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What factors help
to explain differences in educational achievement and experience?* Set A follows the six-part (a)–(f)
structure the syllabus describes for each Paper 2 question; Set B gives extra practice on ethnicity,
gender and global differences.

---

## Set A — Explaining achievement

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *compensatory education* **[2]**
- (ii) *self-fulfilling prophecy* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** linguistic factors that may affect educational achievement. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** home or community factors that may affect the achievement of
working-class pupils. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** reasons why girls may achieve more highly than boys in many countries. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the role of institutional racism in explaining ethnic differences in educational
achievement and experience. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that in-school factors are the main cause of class differences in
educational achievement. **[14]**

## Set B — Extra practice

**(g)** Explain **two** ways an ethnocentric curriculum may affect minority ethnic pupils. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** ways anti-school subcultures may affect achievement. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** reasons why girls may have less access to education in some societies. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** limitations of cultural deprivation explanations. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** ways access to digital resources may cause global differences in achievement. **[4]**

**(l)** Discuss labelling theory as an explanation of gender differences in education. **[8]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Programmes that give extra resources or support to disadvantaged children (1) to make
up for disadvantages at home, such as Operation Head Start in the USA or Sure Start in the UK (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** A prediction that comes true because people act on it (1), for example a pupil
labelled as weak who receives less challenging work and then performs poorly (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: use of the restricted code rather than the elaborated code
(Bernstein); learning in a second language; vocabulary at home differing from the language of
textbooks and examinations; teachers misjudging ability because of accent or dialect.

**(c) [6]** Three factors, 2 marks each (point plus development):
- **Material deprivation**: no quiet study space, devices or internet; poor diet affects health and
  concentration.
- **Parental interest**: Douglas (1964) linked achievement to parental interest; parents working long
  hours may have less time to support homework.
- **Cultural capital**: middle-class children arrive with the knowledge and confidence schools reward
  (Bourdieu); working-class children may lack this.
- **Values**: Sugarman (1970): immediate gratification and fatalism may discourage staying on.
- **Language**: restricted code may disadvantage pupils in a school using the elaborated code.
- **Community**: few local role models with higher education.

**(d) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each:
- **Changing ambitions**: Sharpe found girls' priorities shifting from marriage towards careers.
- **Job opportunities**: more professional jobs open to women motivate girls to gain qualifications.
- **Socialisation**: girls may be encouraged in study habits such as persistence and organisation.
- **Equal-opportunity policies** and campaigns to encourage girls into all subjects.
- **Laddish subcultures**: some boys see hard work as unmasculine (Mac an Ghaill, 1994).
- **Teacher expectations**: girls may be expected to work hard, boys labelled as disruptive.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, both sides with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic.
- **Explaining:** discrimination built into policies rather than individual prejudice; Gillborn and
  Youdell (2000): educational triage placed some minority pupils outside the group targeted for
  success; setting and exclusion practices; ethnocentric curriculum; few minority role models; Coard
  (1971) on labelling of West Indian pupils.
- **Experience:** feeling excluded from the curriculum; higher exclusion rates in some groups.
- **Evaluation:** cannot explain why some minority groups achieve above average; home, class and
  material factors also matter; pupils may resist labels (Fuller, 1984).

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For in-school factors:*
- Labelling: Becker's ideal pupil; Rist (1970); Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968).
- Setting and streaming place working-class pupils lower; Hargreaves (1967) on anti-school
  subcultures.
- Selective and private schooling favour those who can pay or move.
- Marxists: the hidden curriculum and myth of meritocracy.

*For out-of-school factors:*
- Material deprivation: digital access, diet, study space, tuition.
- Cultural capital (Bourdieu) and parental interest (Douglas, 1964).
- Linguistic factors (Bernstein).
- Compensatory education exists because home disadvantage starts before school.

*Evaluation:* factors interact; labels often reflect class background; cultural deprivation may blame
families unfairly; material factors explain why labels stick.

*Conclusion:* in-school factors matter but usually work on top of home and material disadvantage.
Credit a reasoned judgement.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: pupils may feel their culture and history are ignored, lowering
motivation; examples and texts may be unfamiliar, making work harder; it may reinforce a sense of not
belonging; teachers may undervalue knowledge pupils bring from home.

**(h) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: status comes from breaking rules rather than working, so effort
falls; truancy and exclusion mean missed learning; conflict with teachers reinforces negative labels;
pressure on others in the group not to work.

**(i) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each: poverty, with families prioritising sons; early marriage;
safety concerns about travel to school; lack of separate facilities; laws or rules restricting girls'
schooling; cultural norms valuing domestic roles for girls.

**(j) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each: blames working-class or minority culture rather than poverty
or schools; ignores material reasons for attitudes; ignores in-school factors; assumes one culture is
superior.

**(k) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: pupils without internet cannot access online learning; schools in
poorer countries may lack computers; teachers may lack digital training; pupils without access fell
behind during school closures.

**(l) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *For:* teachers may label boys as disruptive, leading to exclusions and lower sets; girls labelled
  hard-working may receive encouragement; subject labels steer choices (science as masculine).
- *Against:* gender patterns also reflect job opportunities and socialisation outside school; pupils
  may reject labels; labelling cannot explain global access barriers for girls.
- *Evaluation:* labelling helps explain experience inside school but is one factor among several.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the same study for class and ethnicity?** Yes, if you explain how it applies; labelling
studies can support both.

**How many explanations should a (f) answer cover?** Enough to show both sides: at least two
in-school and two out-of-school factors, followed by a judgement.

**Should I give figures for achievement gaps?** Only if you are certain of them. A clear explanation
earns more than a doubtful statistic.
