This guide teaches section **3.1.5.2 Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). The specification asks you to draw and interpret distribution curves for different temperatures. It is a skill topic: the curve is always drawn the same way, and the marks are for the details. Learn each feature and the reason for it. ## What the curve shows

Particles in a gas move at many different speeds and so have many different kinetic energies. They collide and exchange energy all the time, but the overall pattern of energies at a fixed temperature stays the same. The **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** is the graph of this pattern.

- **x-axis:** energy of the molecules (kinetic energy).
- **y-axis:** number of molecules with that energy (or the fraction of molecules).

### The features of the curve

```
 number of
 molecules
   ▲      ___
   │     /   \
   │    /     \__
   │   /         \___
   │  /              \_____________ (never reaches axis)
   │ /                        ↑
   └/─────────┬──────────────┴──────► energy
   0         Eₐ
```

| Feature | Meaning and reason |
|---|---|
| Curve starts at the origin | No molecules have zero energy. All molecules are moving |
| A single peak | The most probable energy: more molecules have this energy than any other |
| The peak is to the left of the mean | The curve is asymmetrical. The mean energy is higher than the most probable energy, because the tail on the right pulls the mean up |
| The curve does not touch the x-axis at high energy | There is no maximum energy. A very few molecules have very high energy |
| Area under the curve | The total number of molecules. This does not change unless molecules are added or removed |
| Area to the right of Eₐ | The number of molecules with energy equal to or greater than Eₐ. Only these can react on collision |

Because the area is the total number of molecules, the area under every curve for the same sample of gas is **the same**, whatever the temperature.

## Showing the activation energy

Draw a vertical line at the activation energy Eₐ on the energy axis. The area under the curve **to the right** of the line is the number of molecules able to react when they collide. At ordinary temperatures it is a small part of the whole area: most collisions are unsuccessful.

## The effect of temperature

When the temperature increases, the molecules have more energy on average. The distribution changes in four ways:

1. The peak moves to the **right** (higher most probable energy).
2. The peak is **lower**.
3. The curve is **broader**: it spreads to a wider range of energies.
4. The **total area stays the same**, because the number of molecules is the same.

Because the curve is lower at the peak but the area is the same, the high-energy tail must be larger. The result is that the area to the right of Eₐ is **greater** at the higher temperature.

```
 number of
 molecules
   ▲   ,-.
   │  /   \     T₁ (lower temperature)
   │ /  ,--.\
   │/  /    `\.__   T₂ (higher temperature)
   │  /         `-.____
   └─/──────────┬─────────────► energy
                Eₐ
```

At T₂ the peak is lower and further right, and the T₂ curve lies above the T₁ curve to the right of Eₐ.

### Drawing it

1. Label the axes.
2. Draw the T₁ curve from the origin with a peak and an asymmetric tail that gets very close to the x-axis but does not touch it.
3. Draw the T₂ curve starting at the origin, with a lower and further right peak, and ensure that the high-energy tail is higher than that of T₁.
4. Make sure the two curves cross once after the peak of T₁. Curves must **not** touch the axis or each other at the far right.
5. Mark Eₐ with a vertical line and shade the areas to the right of it.

### Worked example 1: interpreting the diagram

On the diagram for T₁ and T₂, which curve represents the higher temperature, and how can you tell?

The curve with the lower peak at a higher energy is for the higher temperature (T₂). The molecules are spread over a wider range of energies, and a larger area lies to the right of Eₐ.

### Worked example 2: comparing the numbers

For a sample of 1.00 × 10²² molecules, 0.5% have energy equal to or greater than Eₐ at 300 K, and 4.0% have energy at least Eₐ at 340 K. Compare the numbers.

```
At 300 K:  0.005 × 1.00 × 10²² = 5.0 × 10¹⁹ molecules
At 340 K:  0.040 × 1.00 × 10²² = 4.0 × 10²⁰ molecules
ratio = 4.0 × 10²⁰ ÷ 5.0 × 10¹⁹ = 8
```

The number of molecules able to react is eight times greater. The total number of molecules is the same, so the areas under the two curves are the same, but the area beyond Eₐ is eight times larger for the higher temperature. These figures are an illustration, not measured values.

### Worked example 3: why the peak falls

Explain why the peak is lower at a higher temperature although the total number of molecules is the same.

The area under the curve is constant. If the curve extends to higher energies, so that more of the area lies at the high-energy end, the area at the peak must reduce. The peak therefore falls and moves right.

### Worked example 4: sketch for three temperatures

A sketch of the distribution at 400 K should have a peak further to the right and lower than at 300 K. If the question asks for three temperatures, each higher temperature has a lower peak that is further to the right, and a heavier tail. The curves cross each other to the right of the peak of the lowest-temperature curve.

## Using the diagram to explain rate (preview)

A higher temperature gives more molecules with energy of at least Eₐ, so more collisions are successful and the rate increases. This is developed in the next subtopic.

## Common mistakes

- Curve starting above zero on the y-axis or not starting at the origin.
- Curve touching the x-axis at the right-hand end, or turning upward.
- A symmetrical curve like a normal distribution.
- Curves of different area for different temperatures.
- Moving the peak to the right but not lowering it.
- Putting Eₐ on the y-axis.
- Shading the area to the left of Eₐ.
- Writing "the curve shifts to the right" without saying the peak is lower and the curve is broader.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Sketch**: label both axes, start at the origin, make the curve asymmetric and show Eₐ.
- **Interpret**: refer to the areas to the right of Eₐ at each temperature.
- **Explain** features: state what the area represents, why the curve starts at the origin and why it never touches the axis.
- **Compare**: say which curve has the greater proportion of molecules above Eₐ.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the x-axis show speed or energy?**
In AQA questions it is energy. A curve for speed has a similar shape.

**Why is the curve not symmetrical?**
Energy cannot be less than zero but has no upper limit, so the distribution has a long tail to the right.

**Is the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution only for gases?**
The specification refers to molecular energies in gases, so use it for gas-phase explanations.
