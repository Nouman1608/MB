## Questions

**1.** State two properties of ATP that make it suitable as the energy currency of cells. **[2]**

**2.** Outline three processes in cells that are supplied with energy by ATP. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between cell respiration and gas exchange. **[3]**

**4.** Compare aerobic and anaerobic cell respiration in humans. **[4]**

**5.** Germinating seeds of mass 3.0 g were placed in a respirometer containing a carbon dioxide absorbent. A bead in a capillary tube of internal radius 0.60 mm moved 18 mm in 15 minutes at 15 °C, and 39 mm in 15 minutes at 25 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of oxygen taken up at 15 °C. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake at 25 °C in mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹, to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the carbon dioxide absorbent was needed. **[2]**

**6.** Yeast was added to glucose solutions of different concentrations and the carbon dioxide released in 5 minutes was measured.

| Glucose / % | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ / cm³ | 0.5 | 4.0 | 7.5 | 11.0 | 11.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate of carbon dioxide release at 4% glucose, with units. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the trend shown by the data. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons for the small change between 4% and 8%. **[2]**

**7.** (HL only)
**(a)** State what happens to NAD, and to the substrate, when hydrogen is removed from the substrate in respiration. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why removing hydrogen from a substrate counts as oxidation. **[2]**

**8.** (HL only)
**(a)** Outline the four main events of glycolysis. **[4]**
**(b)** State the net yield of ATP and reduced NAD from one glucose molecule in glycolysis. **[2]**

**9.** (HL only) Blood lactate was measured in an athlete: 1.0 mmol dm⁻³ at rest, 12.0 mmol dm⁻³ immediately after a sprint, and 4.0 mmol dm⁻³ after 10 minutes of recovery.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in lactate from rest to immediately after the sprint. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the conversion of pyruvate to lactate allows glycolysis to continue. **[3]**

**10.** (HL only)
**(a)** State the two products of anaerobic respiration in yeast. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why bread dough rises when yeast is active. **[2]**
**(c)** State which stage of the pathway differs between yeast and humans. **[1]**

**11.** (HL only)
**(a)** State how many carbon dioxide molecules are released in the link reaction and Krebs cycle from one glucose molecule. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the Krebs cycle, naming the 6C and 4C intermediates. **[3]**
**(c)** The oxygen supply to a tissue is cut off. Explain why the electron transport chain stops and why the Krebs cycle slows. **[6]**

**12.** (HL only) The energy released from carbohydrate is 17 kJ g⁻¹ and from lipid is 37 kJ g⁻¹ (data supplied).

**(a)** Calculate the mass of each needed to release 4000 kJ, to the nearest gram. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the chemical composition of the two substrates to account for the difference in energy per gram. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why fatty acids cannot be used in anaerobic respiration. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Soluble, so it moves around the cell [1]; hydrolysis releases a small, usable amount of energy [1]; quickly regenerated from ADP and phosphate (other valid properties accepted). **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct properties are needed; saying "it stores energy" is vague and gains less credit than naming a property such as solubility or rapid regeneration.

**2.** Active transport across membranes using pumps [1]; synthesis of macromolecules such as proteins or DNA (anabolism) [1]; movement of the cell or its parts, for example chromosomes during mitosis [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" needs three separate processes, each with a clear example; listing three types of active transport counts as one idea.

**3.** Respiration is a chemical process in cells [1] that releases energy from carbon compounds to make ATP [1]; gas exchange is the physical movement (diffusion) of oxygen and carbon dioxide across a surface [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A "distinguish" answer must state both sides. Writing "breathing" for respiration is a common way to lose the mark.

**4.** Aerobic needs oxygen, anaerobic does not [1]; aerobic can use carbohydrate and lipid, anaerobic uses carbohydrate only [1]; aerobic gives a much higher ATP yield [1]; aerobic produces carbon dioxide and water, anaerobic produces lactate (other valid points: aerobic uses mitochondria as well as the cytoplasm) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A "compare" answer needs a point from both processes in the same sentence or row; describing each separately may leave the comparison unstated.

**5. (a)** Volume = π × 0.60² × 18 [1] = **20.4 mm³** [1]
**(b)** Volume = π × 0.60² × 39 = 44.1 mm³; rate = 44.1 / 15 / 3.0 [1] = **0.980 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** It absorbs the carbon dioxide produced by respiration [1], so the movement of the bead shows only the oxygen taken up [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula and the substituted values; an answer with the wrong unit or without "per gram" can lose the final mark even if the working is right.

**6. (a)** 11.0 / 5 = **2.2 cm³ min⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** The rate rises as glucose concentration rises from 0 to 4% (from 0.1 to 2.2 cm³ min⁻¹) [1]; above 4% it levels off, with only a small rise to 2.3 cm³ min⁻¹ at 8% [1].
**(c)** The number of yeast cells or enzyme molecules is limiting [1]; enzymes are saturated with substrate, or another factor such as temperature or pH is limiting [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A description of a trend scores best when it quotes values with units from the table; explanations should name the limiting factor, not just say "it stops increasing".

**7. (a)** NAD is reduced [1]; the substrate is oxidised [1].
**(b)** Hydrogen is a proton plus an electron [1], so removing it takes an electron from the substrate, and oxidation is loss of electrons [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Define oxidation as electron loss; "gain of oxygen" is not the definition the course uses for this topic.

**8. (a)** Phosphorylation: two ATP are used to add phosphate groups to the sugar [1]; lysis: the 6C sugar splits into two 3C molecules [1]; oxidation: hydrogen is removed and NAD is reduced [1]; ATP formation: ATP is made (four in total) and pyruvate is formed [1].
**(b)** Net yield is **2 ATP** [1] and **2 reduced NAD** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Net" means the ATP used is subtracted; quoting 4 ATP without saying 2 are used is a frequent way to lose the mark.

**9. (a)** (12.0 − 1.0) / 1.0 × 100 [1] = **1100%** [1]
**(b)** Glycolysis needs NAD [1]; without oxygen reduced NAD cannot pass its hydrogen to the electron transport chain [1]; pyruvate takes up the hydrogen and becomes lactate, so NAD is regenerated and glycolysis continues [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* For percentage change, the denominator is the starting figure, not the final one; the explanation should link NAD regeneration to the continuation of glycolysis, not just to "getting rid of pyruvate".

**10. (a)** Ethanol and carbon dioxide [1].
**(b)** Fermentation by the yeast releases carbon dioxide [1]; bubbles of the gas are trapped and swell the dough [1].
**(c)** The conversion of pyruvate, which regenerates NAD [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both products. Naming only ethanol, or giving lactate, does not score.

**11. (a)** **6** [1]
**(b)** An acetyl group (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) to form citrate (6C) [1]; there are four oxidations (dehydrogenations) and two decarboxylations, releasing carbon dioxide and forming ATP and reduced NAD [1]; oxaloacetate is regenerated so the cycle continues [1].
**(c)** Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor, so without it electrons cannot leave the last carrier [1]; the carriers stay reduced and electron flow stops [1]; no more protons are pumped, the gradient falls and ATP synthase stops making ATP [1]; reduced NAD cannot be oxidised, so NAD is not regenerated [1]; the link reaction and Krebs cycle need NAD for their oxidations [1]; so they slow as reduced NAD builds up [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A long explanation question rewards a chain of cause and effect; name the terminal acceptor, the stalled carrier flow and the lack of NAD, rather than saying the cycle "needs oxygen".

**12. (a)** Carbohydrate: 4000 / 17 = **235 g** [1]; lipid: 4000 / 37 = **108 g** [1]
**(b)** Lipids contain less oxygen per gram [1] and more hydrogen and carbon that can be oxidised [1]; more oxidation gives more energy transferred to ATP production [1].
**(c)** Anaerobic respiration is glycolysis followed by conversion of pyruvate [1]; fatty acids are broken into 2C acetyl groups that enter as acetyl-CoA, not through glycolysis [1]; they can enter only the Krebs cycle, which depends on oxygen through the chain [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the correct rounding for each value stated in the question; compare "per gram" explicitly instead of saying fat is "bigger" or "denser".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing breathing or gas exchange when the question asks about respiration.
- Giving energy values or ATP counts without stating "net" or per glucose.
- Dividing by the final value rather than the starting value in percentage change.
- Omitting units, or "per gram", from a rate.
- Describing a graph or table without quoting figures.
- Mixing up the anaerobic products of humans and yeast.
- Placing oxygen use in the Krebs cycle rather than at the end of the chain as terminal electron acceptor.
- Describing reduced NAD as "making" ATP directly, with no mention of the proton gradient.
- Saying fats cannot be respired anaerobically without explaining that they bypass glycolysis.
