Quick recall for section 3.1 "Business finance – sources" of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1), Issue 2, February 2026. Worked examples and an annotated answer are in the [sources of finance study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-business-finance-sources/); questions are in the [sources of finance practice set](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-business-finance-sources-practice/). Figures below are invented, in dollars.

## 1. The specification list

- **3.1.1 The need for finance:** short-term needs, long-term needs, to start up or expand.
- **3.1.2 Internal sources:** personal savings, retained profit, selling assets.
- **3.1.3 External sources:** overdraft and trade payables; loan capital, share capital, including stock market flotation (public limited companies); venture capital; crowdfunding.

## 2. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Internal source | Finance from within the business or its owner |
| External source | Finance from people or organisations outside the business |
| Personal savings | The owner's own money put into the business |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business rather than paid to owners |
| Selling assets | Raising cash by selling something the business owns |
| Overdraft | Permission from a bank to take more out of an account than is in it, up to a limit |
| Trade payables | Amounts owed to suppliers who have supplied goods on credit |
| Loan capital | Money borrowed for an agreed period and repaid with interest |
| Share capital | Money raised by a company selling shares to its owners |
| Stock market flotation | A public limited company offering its shares to the general public on a stock market |
| Venture capital | Investment, usually in a young high-growth business, in return for part-ownership |
| Crowdfunding | Raising money from many people, each giving a modest sum, usually online |
| Security | An asset a lender can claim if a loan is not repaid |

These meanings are standard teaching; the specification names the sources without defining them.

## 3. Sources at a glance

| Source | Internal or external | Best for | Main cost or drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal savings | Internal | Start-up | Limited amount; owner's money at risk |
| Retained profit | Internal | Expansion by a profitable business | Less paid to owners; not available to a start-up |
| Selling assets | Internal | Raising cash from unused assets | Asset is lost; a quick sale may get a low price |
| Overdraft | External | Short-term gaps | High interest; bank can ask for repayment quickly |
| Trade payables | External | Short-term inventory | Supplier goodwill at risk if payment is late |
| Loan capital | External | Long-term assets | Interest; fixed repayments; may need security |
| Share capital | External | Long-term growth of a company | Shared ownership, control and dividends |
| Flotation | External | Very large expansion by a plc | High costs; loss of control; takeover risk |
| Venture capital | External | Risky high-growth start-ups | Investor takes part of the business and expects high returns |
| Crowdfunding | External | Start-ups with an appealing idea | Uncertain; may raise nothing if the target is missed |

## 4. Choosing checklist

Use these five tests on any case:

1. **Cost:** interest, or a share of future profits?
2. **Amount:** can this source raise enough?
3. **Time:** does the length of the finance match the length of the need?
4. **Control:** will new owners gain a say?
5. **Risk:** must repayments be made whatever happens?

Then check the **legal form**: only companies issue shares, and only public limited companies can float on a stock market.

## 5. Key calculations

**Interest cost of a loan.** A loan of 18,000 is repaid at 900 a month for 24 months. Total repaid = 900 × 24 = 21,600. Interest = 21,600 − 18,000 = **3,600**, which is 3,600 ÷ 18,000 × 100 = **20%** of the loan.

**Ownership after a share issue.** A company with 12,000 shares issues 3,000 new shares to an investor. Investor's stake = 3,000 ÷ 15,000 × 100 = **20%**.

**Value implied by venture capital.** 80,000 for 25% of a business implies a value of 80,000 ÷ 0.25 = **320,000**.

**Overdraft interest.** Overdrawn by 6,000 for 3 months at 2% a month: 6,000 × 0.02 × 3 = **360**.

## 6. Exam traps

- Recommending retained profit for a business that has not yet made a profit.
- Calling an overdraft long-term finance.
- Saying share capital must be repaid. It has no repayment date.
- Suggesting share capital for a sole trader or partnership without a change of legal form.
- Saying a private limited company can float on a stock market. It must first become a public limited company.
- Writing "crowdfunding is free". Backers may expect a reward, a product or a share, and campaigns take time and money.
- Listing sources without linking them to the need and the business in the case.

## 7. Answer frames

- **Explain** one advantage of retained profit: no interest to pay → lower costs than a loan → more of future profit stays with the owners.
- **Explain** one disadvantage of venture capital: investor takes a share of ownership → owners lose some control → decisions may favour fast growth over the founder's aims.
- **Justify** a choice of source: identify the need and its length → test each option on cost, control and risk using the case → recommend one and say why the other is weaker here.

## Quick self-test

1. Give two internal sources of finance.
2. Which type of business can use stock market flotation?
3. State one reason a start-up cannot use retained profit.
4. Why is an overdraft unsuitable for buying a building?
5. What is security for a loan?
6. A loan of 10,000 is repaid at 470 a month for 24 months. Calculate the interest.
7. A company with 8,000 shares issues 2,000 more to a new investor. What percentage does the investor own?
8. Give one advantage of trade payables as a source of finance.
9. Give one benefit of venture capital other than the money itself.
10. A crowdfunding campaign aims for 12,000. 300 backers give 30 each. Has it reached its target?
11. Give one drawback of selling assets to raise finance.
12. Why might a bank be more willing to lend to an owner who also uses personal savings?

### Answers

1. Any two of: personal savings, retained profit, selling assets.
2. A public limited company.
3. It has no past profit to keep in the business.
4. It is meant for short-term needs; the bank can ask for repayment at short notice and interest is high, while a building is a long-term asset.
5. An asset the lender can claim if the loan is not repaid.
6. 470 × 24 = 11,280; interest = 11,280 − 10,000 = 1,280.
7. 2,000 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = 20%.
8. It is free short-term finance: the business can use the goods before paying for them.
9. Advice, experience or business contacts from the investor.
10. No: 300 × 30 = 9,000, which is 3,000 short.
11. The business loses the use of the asset, or a quick sale may bring a low price.
12. The owner is risking their own money too, which shows commitment and reduces the amount the bank must lend.
