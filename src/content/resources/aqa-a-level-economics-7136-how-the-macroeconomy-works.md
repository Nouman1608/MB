This guide covers **3.2.2 How the macroeconomy works: the circular flow of income, aggregate demand/aggregate
supply analysis and related concepts** in AQA A-level Economics (7136). Its six parts are 3.2.2.1 The circular
flow of income, 3.2.2.2 Aggregate demand and aggregate supply analysis, 3.2.2.3 The determinants of aggregate
demand, 3.2.2.4 Aggregate demand and the level of economic activity, 3.2.2.5 Determinants of short-run aggregate
supply and 3.2.2.6 Determinants of long-run aggregate supply.

The [study guide on how the macroeconomy works](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-aggregate-demand-supply/) sets
out each model. This page shows how to **use** them on a problem: tracing an event through the circular flow,
sizing it with the multiplier, deciding which curve moves, and judging the outcome with the right AS curve. All
countries and figures are invented. Revise with the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-how-the-macroeconomy-works-revision-notes/)
and practise with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-how-the-macroeconomy-works-practice/).

## The circular flow as a balance

National income measures the value of the economy's output in a period, and in the circular flow **income =
output = expenditure**. Money leaves the flow as **withdrawals** (saving, taxation, imports) and enters as
**injections** (investment, government spending, exports). National income is in equilibrium when planned
injections equal planned withdrawals.

**Worked example (invented).** In Country C injections are fixed at 400 billion. Withdrawals rise with income:

| National income | Injections (I + G + X) | Withdrawals (S + T + M) | Pressure on income |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 400 | 250 | rising |
| 1,200 | 400 | 300 | rising |
| 1,400 | 400 | 350 | rising |
| 1,600 | 400 | 400 | **equilibrium** |
| 1,800 | 400 | 450 | falling |

Below 1,600, more is injected than withdrawn, so spending exceeds output and firms expand. Above 1,600, the
reverse holds. If injections rise to 450, the equilibrium moves to 1,800, where withdrawals also reach 450:
national income rises by 200 from a 50 rise in injections. That magnified effect is the **multiplier**.

The specification is careful on two points. **Equilibrium income need not be full employment income**: Country C
could settle at 1,600 with many workers unemployed. And **nominal income** can rise because prices rise, so **real
national income** is the indicator of economic performance.

## The multiplier from the marginal propensity to consume

The **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)** is the fraction of an extra unit of income that is spent on
consumption. The multiplier is:

**k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC)**

AQA requires the multiplier to be calculated **only from the MPC**.

**Worked example (invented).** Country C's government spends an extra 20 billion on rail improvements. The MPC
is 0.8.

- k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 1 ÷ 0.2 = **5**
- Final change in national income = 20 × 5 = **100 billion**

The rounds explain why: contractors and workers receive 20 billion and spend 0.8 of it (16 billion); those
receivers spend 0.8 of that (12.8 billion); then 10.24 billion, and so on. The successive rounds add up to 100
billion.

**Why the MPC sets the size.** With an MPC of 0.6, k = 2.5 and the same spending adds only 50 billion. The more of
each extra unit of income that is spent rather than withdrawn, the larger each round and the larger the final
effect.

**Evaluating the multiplier.** The full effect takes time to build; if the economy is near capacity, more
spending raises prices rather than real output; extra government borrowing may raise interest rates and reduce
private investment; and a fall in confidence can lower the MPC. The multiplier works **in reverse** too: a
fall of 10 billion in exports with k = 5 cuts national income by 50 billion.

## The determinants of aggregate demand

AD = C + I + G + (X − M). For each component, know what drives it:

| Component | Main determinants |
|---|---|
| Consumption | disposable income, interest rates, wealth (house and share prices), confidence, credit availability |
| Investment | interest rates, expected demand and profits, business confidence, technology, the cost of capital goods |
| Government spending | policy decisions; also automatic changes in benefit spending over the cycle |
| Exports | incomes abroad, the exchange rate, relative prices and quality |
| Imports | domestic incomes, the exchange rate, relative prices and quality |

**Saving versus investment.** Saving is income not spent on consumption; investment is spending on capital goods.
They are done by different people for different reasons: households save to provide for the future, firms invest
when they expect profits. A rise in saving does not automatically produce a rise in investment. The determinants
of saving include interest rates, confidence about future income, wealth and the availability of credit.

**The basic accelerator.** Investment depends partly on the **rate of change** of output, not its level. If demand
for a firm's output rises, it needs more machines, so investment rises sharply; if demand merely stops growing,
investment can fall even though output stays high. AQA does not require accelerator calculations, but you should
be able to explain why investment is the most volatile component of AD.

## Which curve moves?

A change in the **price level** is a movement along AD and AS. Anything else shifts a curve:

- **AD shifts** when consumption, investment, government spending or net exports change for a reason other than the
  price level.
- **SRAS shifts** with production costs: money wage rates, raw material prices, business taxation and productivity.
- **LRAS shifts** with the economy's productive capacity: technology, productivity, attitudes, enterprise, factor
  mobility, economic incentives and institutions such as a banking system that channels funds to business
  investment.

## Two views of aggregate supply

**The vertical LRAS.** The position of the vertical long-run AS curve represents the **normal capacity level of
output**. A rise in AD raises output only temporarily; in the long run it raises the price level.

**The Keynesian AS curve.** It has three ranges: a horizontal section where there is large spare capacity, a
rising section as bottlenecks appear, and a vertical section at full capacity. The same rise in AD has different
effects depending on where the economy is:

| Starting point | Effect of a rise in AD |
|---|---|
| Deep recession (horizontal range) | real output and employment rise; little or no rise in the price level |
| Approaching capacity (rising range) | both output and prices rise |
| Full capacity (vertical range) | only the price level rises |

Which view you apply is an evaluation point: if an extract describes high unemployment and idle factories, the
Keynesian horizontal range supports a strong output response to higher AD.

## Demand-side and supply-side shocks

**Demand-side shock (invented).** A recession in Country C's main trading partners cuts exports by 15 billion. AD
shifts left. With an MPC of 0.75, k = 4, so national income falls by up to 60 billion. Output falls, cyclical
unemployment rises and inflationary pressure eases.

**Supply-side shock (invented).** World oil prices double. Firms' costs rise, so SRAS shifts left. The price level
rises **and** real output falls, a combination sometimes called stagflation. Because the shock pushes inflation and
unemployment the wrong way at once, demand-side policy faces a dilemma: raising AD worsens inflation, cutting it
worsens unemployment.

The specification asks you to understand "how global economic events can affect the domestic economy": both
examples start abroad and reach the domestic economy through trade and costs.

## Common errors

- Calculating the multiplier as 1 ÷ MPC.
- Treating saving and investment as the same thing.
- Shifting AD for a change in the price level.
- Shifting LRAS for a change in costs such as wages, which shifts SRAS.
- Claiming a rise in AD always raises real output, without reference to spare capacity.
- Describing an oil price rise as a demand-side shock.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), substitute, then multiply by the initial change in spending.
- **Explain with a diagram:** label the price level and real national output on the axes, and the curves (AD₁,
  AD₂, SRAS, LRAS) and equilibria.
- **Analyse:** trace the chain through the components of AD or costs to output, employment and prices.
- **Evaluate:** consider spare capacity, the size of the MPC, time lags, confidence and which AS curve fits the
  situation.
