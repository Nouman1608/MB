All rates, countries and data are invented, including rates quoted for real currencies. ## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Under a floating exchange rate system, which **one** of the following would be most likely to cause the pound to appreciate? **[1]**

A: UK residents take more holidays abroad

B: Foreign firms increase their investment in UK factories

C: The Bank of England cuts interest rates relative to other countries

D: UK inflation rises faster than inflation abroad

**2.** The exchange rate is £1 = €1.15. What is the euro price of a UK good priced at £80? **[1]**

A: €69.57

B: €80.00

C: €92.00

D: €95.15

**3.** A central bank is holding its currency at a fixed rate. At that rate there is excess supply of its currency. Which **one** action would help to maintain the peg? **[1]**

A: Selling its own currency in exchange for foreign currency

B: Cutting interest rates

C: Buying its own currency using foreign currency reserves

D: Removing controls on currency leaving the country

**4.** Distinguish between a devaluation and a depreciation of a currency. **[2]**

**5.** The exchange rate moves from £1 = $1.40 to £1 = $1.25.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the value of the pound against the dollar. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the dollar price of a £30 UK product before and after the change. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the pound price of a $21 US product before and after the change. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**6.** With the aid of a diagram, explain how a recession in a country's main export market is likely to affect its floating exchange rate. **[4]**

**7.** Country Lanor fixes its currency, the lan, at $0.50 per lan. After a fall in confidence, the quantity of lan demanded at the peg is 300 million a day and the quantity supplied is 360 million a day.

**(a)** Calculate the excess supply of lan at the peg. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the value in dollars of the reserves the central bank must use each day to hold the peg. **[2]**

**(c)** The central bank has reserves of $1.2 billion. For how many days could it hold the peg by this method alone? **[1]**

**(d)** Explain one other way the central bank could defend the peg. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **two** causes of a depreciation of a floating currency. **[4]**

**9.** In Country Morra, imports make up 25% of the consumer price index basket. After a depreciation, import prices measured in Morra's currency rise by 8%.

**(a)** Calculate the direct effect on the inflation rate, in percentage points, if importers pass on only half of the rise to consumers. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why importers might not pass on the full rise. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why a depreciation might not improve a country's current account balance in the short run. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a small, open economy that trades mainly with one large partner should fix its exchange rate against that partner's currency. **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that a depreciation of its currency will benefit an economy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. Inward investment raises demand for pounds. Options A, C and D all reduce demand for pounds or raise their supply. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]: 80 × 1.15 = €92.00. Option A divides instead of multiplying. **[1]**

**3.** C [1]. Buying its own currency absorbs the excess supply. Options A, B and D would all add to the downward pressure. **[1]**

**4.** A devaluation is a deliberate lowering of a fixed exchange rate by the government or central bank [1]. A depreciation is a fall in a floating exchange rate caused by changes in market demand and supply [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** (1.25 − 1.40) ÷ 1.40 × 100 [1] = **−10.7%**, a depreciation of 10.7% [1].

**(b)** Before: 30 × 1.40 = $42.00 [1]. After: 30 × 1.25 = $37.50 [1].

**(c)** Before: 21 ÷ 1.40 = £15.00 [1]. After: 21 ÷ 1.25 = £16.80 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The pound price of the US product rises by 12%, not 10.7%, because the base is different. Check direction: a weaker pound makes imports dearer.

**6.** Incomes in the export market fall, so demand for the country's exports falls [1]. Foreign buyers need less of the country's currency, so the demand curve for the currency shifts to the left [1]. A correctly labelled diagram: vertical axis the price of the currency in another currency, horizontal axis the quantity of the currency, with D shifting left [1]. The exchange rate falls from its original equilibrium to a lower one and the quantity traded falls: a depreciation [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Excess supply is 360 − 300 = **60 million lan a day** [1].

**(b)** The central bank buys 60 million lan [1] at $0.50 each, using 60 × 0.50 = **$30 million** of reserves a day [1].

**(c)** Reserves last 1,200 ÷ 30 = **40 days** [1].

**(d)** It could raise interest rates [1], which attracts financial inflows from savers abroad and increases demand for the lan at the peg [1]. Alternative: impose controls limiting currency leaving the country, which reduces supply of the lan. **[6]**

**8.** Cause 1: a fall in interest rates relative to other countries [1] makes the country's bank deposits and bonds less attractive, so savers move money abroad, reducing demand for the currency and raising its supply [1]. Cause 2: higher inflation than trading partners [1] makes exports less competitive and imports more attractive, so demand for the currency falls and supply rises over time [1]. Other valid causes: speculation that the currency will fall; political uncertainty; falling demand for exports; lower inward investment. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Price rise passed on: 8% × 0.5 = 4% on imported items [1]. Effect on the index: 0.25 × 4 = **1.0 percentage point** [1].

**(b)** In a competitive market, importers may absorb part of the rise by cutting their profit margins to avoid losing customers to domestic producers [1]; they may also expect the depreciation to be temporary, or have bought currency in advance at the old rate [1]. **[4]**

**10.** After a depreciation, imports cost more in the home currency and exports cost less in foreign currency [1]. In the short run demand for imports and exports is often price inelastic, because contracts are fixed and buyers take time to find substitutes [1]. Import volumes therefore fall only a little while each unit costs more, so spending on imports may rise [1]. Export volumes rise only slowly, so export revenue in home currency may not rise enough to offset this, and the current account may worsen before it improves [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Definition of a fixed system and how it is maintained, using reserves and interest rates [1].
- For: certainty about prices for exporters and importers, which is valuable when most trade is with one partner [1].
- For: lower risk encourages investment from that partner [1].
- For: inflation discipline, since the economy cannot rely on depreciation to restore competitiveness [1].
- Against: interest rates must be used to defend the peg, so they cannot be set to suit domestic inflation or unemployment [1].
- Against: large reserves are needed, which have an opportunity cost [1].
- Against: if the rate is set too high, exports become uncompetitive and a deficit can persist [1].
- Against: speculators may attack the peg if they doubt it can be held [1].
- Depends on whether the two economies face similar shocks: if their cycles move together, the loss of independent interest rates matters less [1].
- Judgement: a peg is more attractive the more trade is concentrated on the partner and the more alike the two economies are, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Exports become cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer, improving price competitiveness [1].
- Net exports rise, increasing AD, output and employment [1].
- The current account may improve, if demand for exports and imports is price elastic [1].
- In the short run elasticities are low, so the current account may worsen first [1].
- Dearer imports of consumer goods raise inflation and reduce real incomes [1].
- Dearer imported inputs raise firms' costs, which can offset the price advantage of exporters [1].
- If the economy is near full capacity, extra AD adds mainly to inflation rather than to output [1].
- Effects differ between groups: exporters gain, importers, consumers and foreign travellers lose [1].
- The cause matters: a fall caused by a loss of confidence may signal deeper problems and raise borrowing costs [1].
- Judgement: beneficial mainly when there is spare capacity, trade is price elastic and inflation is low, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers tie the verdict to the economy's starting point, for example its spare capacity and inflation rate.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing when you should multiply, or the reverse, in conversions.
- Giving the percentage change of the wrong currency.
- Shifting the wrong curve on the currency diagram.
- Forgetting to multiply the excess supply by the peg rate when finding reserves.
- Claiming depreciation always improves the current account.
- Two-sided answers with no final judgement.
