This guide covers section **4.3.4 Global industries and companies (multinational corporations)** in Unit 4 (Global business) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. A **multinational corporation (MNC)** is a business that owns or controls operations in more than one country. The section asks you to weigh the benefits and costs of MNCs for host countries, to understand the ethical issues that arise from operating across borders, and to explain how MNCs can be controlled. All businesses, countries and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Global business study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-global-business/). Condensed notes are in the [Global industries and companies revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-global-industries-and-companies-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Global industries and companies practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-global-industries-and-companies-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **The impact of MNCs:** on the local economy (local labour, wages, working conditions and job creation; local businesses; the local community and environment) and on the national economy (economic growth, FDI flows, balance of payments, technology and skills transfer, consumers, business culture, tax revenues).
2. **International business ethics:** stakeholder conflicts; environmental considerations (emissions and waste disposal, sustainability); supply chain considerations (pay and working conditions, exploitation of labour and child labour); marketing considerations (misleading product labelling, inappropriate marketing activities).
3. **Controlling MNCs:** the power of the MNC, political influence, legal control, consumer pressure, pressure groups, social media and self-regulation.

## Impact of MNCs on the local economy

**Worked example 1.** Nexus Motors invests $120m in a new plant in Orvia, creating 1,500 jobs. The average local wage is $6,000 and Nexus pays $9,000.

- Investment per job = 120m / 1,500 = **$80,000**.
- Wage bill = 1,500 x 9,000 = **$13.5m**, which is **50% above** the local average (9,000 / 6,000 - 1).
- Nexus buys $20m a year of parts from local suppliers.
- Orvia Motors, a local rival with $40m of sales, loses 15% of its sales: 0.15 x 40m = **$6m**, leaving $34m.
- Nexus earns $20m profit, and with corporation tax at 25% it would pay $5m a year. But a five-year tax holiday was agreed, so the government gives up 5 x 5m = **$25m** over the period.

The same plant can therefore be a gain and a loss at once.

| Area | Possible benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Local labour, wages and conditions** | Jobs; higher wages; training; better safety standards | Wages may still be low relative to the MNC's profits; pressure on staff; workers may be laid off if the MNC relocates |
| **Local businesses** | New customers (suppliers, services); technology spreads; more competition lifts standards | Local rivals lose sales or close; suppliers become dependent on one buyer; the MNC may import parts instead of buying locally |
| **Local community and environment** | Better infrastructure; community projects; spending in local shops | Pollution, congestion, loss of land and farming; strain on housing and services; cultural change |

## Impact of MNCs on the national economy

| Area | Possible benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic growth** | Extra output and incomes; multiplier effects | Profits sent abroad; economy may depend on a few firms |
| **FDI flows** | Capital, equipment and technology come in | Inflow may reverse if the MNC leaves; foreign ownership of key industries |
| **Balance of payments** | Exports from the plant earn foreign currency; imports of goods are replaced | Profits sent home (outflow); the plant may import many components |
| **Technology and skills transfer** | New methods, management skills and training spread to local firms | The MNC may keep research and key skills at home |
| **Consumers** | More choice, lower prices, better quality | Local brands lose out; consumers pushed towards global tastes |
| **Business culture** | Better management practices, wider outlook | Local ways of working undermined; clash of values |
| **Tax revenues** | Corporation tax, income tax on workers, indirect taxes | Tax holidays, subsidies and transfer of profits to low-tax countries can reduce the benefit |

In the Nexus example, $25m of tax is forgone, but the government gains income tax on a $13.5m wage bill, spending in the local economy, and technology. Whether the deal was good depends on what would have happened without the plant, and on the unquantified benefits such as skills.

## International business ethics

An MNC operates in countries with different laws, wages and expectations. **Ethics** here means doing what is right even when the law does not require it, or when the law differs between countries. **Stakeholder conflicts** arise because shareholders want profit while workers, host communities, governments and customers have different interests. For example, relocating a plant lowers costs for shareholders but removes jobs at home.

**Environmental considerations.** *Emissions and waste disposal:* a poor country may have weaker rules, tempting an MNC to dump waste cheaply or emit more than it could at home. *Sustainability:* using resources in a way that they will still be available for future generations. An MNC that cuts down forest or uses scarce water for production may hurt the local community and its own long-term supply.

**Supply chain considerations.** MNCs buy from thousands of suppliers who may be hard to monitor.

- *Pay and working conditions:* suppliers may pay below a living wage, require long hours or ignore safety.
- *Exploitation of labour and child labour:* children working instead of attending school, or forced and bonded labour, are serious abuses.

**Worked example 2.** A supplier to Nexus pays $2.50 an hour for a 10-hour day, six days a week: 2.50 x 10 x 6 = **$150** a week. An independent estimate of a living wage is $190 a week, so the shortfall is $40, which is **21%** of the living wage. Nexus's pressure to cut prices may have contributed. If Nexus pays $0.05 a unit more to the supplier, the supplier could raise wages, but Nexus's own margin would fall; the ethical choice has a cost.

**Marketing considerations.** *Misleading product labelling* includes unclear ingredients, false health claims or labels in the wrong language. *Inappropriate marketing* includes advertising unsuitable products to children, promoting baby milk in a way that discourages breastfeeding where clean water is scarce, or using culturally offensive images.

**Consequences of unethical behaviour:** loss of reputation, boycotts, legal action, loss of talented staff and lower long-term profit. **Benefits of ethical behaviour:** loyalty, trust, easier recruitment and a licence to operate in the host country. Ethical choices can cost more in the short term.

## Controlling MNCs

MNCs can be very large, sometimes with more revenue than the host country's government. The factors that affect how far they can be controlled include:

| Factor | How it works | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Power of the MNC** | Size, global reach and ability to move production or threaten to leave | Gives MNCs bargaining power over governments, especially small ones |
| **Political influence** | Lobbying, links with politicians and campaign funding | Governments may be pressed to relax rules or grant concessions |
| **Legal control** | Laws on labour, the environment, competition, tax and consumer protection, with fines | Weak enforcement in poorer countries; MNCs can use the best lawyers; laws differ between countries |
| **Consumer pressure** | Customers choose or avoid brands based on conduct | Only works if customers know and care, and have alternatives |
| **Pressure groups** | Campaigns, investigations and lawsuits | Lack of funds; MNCs can respond with public relations |
| **Social media** | Spreads news of abuses quickly and widely, with large reputational damage | Misinformation; attention fades |
| **Self-regulation** | MNC sets its own codes of conduct and audits suppliers | Depends on honesty; may be used for public relations ("greenwashing") |

The most effective control combines several: laws set minimum standards, pressure groups and social media expose breaches, consumers punish bad conduct and the MNC's self-regulation gives day-to-day control.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the impact of an MNC as simply good or bad. Say for whom, in what area and over what period.
- Confusing the local economy (a region or community) with the national economy (the whole country).
- Counting profits that are sent abroad as a benefit to the host country.
- Listing ethical issues without saying who is affected and what the business would gain or lose.
- Assuming that laws alone control MNCs; enforcement and power matter.
- Writing about only one method of control.

## Exam technique

For **explain**, choose one effect (for example, tax revenue) and show its size or direction using a figure from the source. For **analyse**, set out the chain: "the MNC buys from local suppliers, which increases their sales and jobs, but makes them dependent on one customer". For **discuss** and **assess**, weigh benefits and costs for the host country, and give a judgement that depends on factors such as the MNC's tax deal, what the alternative would be, and the strength of local laws.
