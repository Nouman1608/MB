## Why marketing matters: three linked ideas

The first three bullets in 4.2.1 form a chain.

1. **Satisfying customer needs**: offering the right product, at a price customers accept, where and when they want it. Customers whose needs are met buy, and do not return goods.
2. **Building customer relationships**: contact after the sale, such as answering questions, handling complaints well, personalised offers or a members' scheme. Customers feel known and valued.
3. **Keeping customer loyalty**: customers who return again and again. Loyal customers cost less to serve than new ones, who must be found through promotion, and they recommend the business to others.

### Worked example 1: what loyalty is worth

Hartwell Garden Centre has 300 regular customers, each spending an average of 1,000 a year. Usually 70% stay from one year to the next (210 customers). After it starts a members' club with advice evenings and birthday offers, 80% stay (240 customers).

- Extra customers kept: 240 − 210 = 30.
- Extra yearly revenue: 30 × 1,000 = **30,000**.

If the club costs 9,000 a year to run, it pays for itself in revenue terms several times over, before counting the new customers that loyal members recommend. The figure is revenue, not profit, so a full judgement would deduct the cost of the goods those customers buy.

## Market orientation and product orientation

| | Market orientation | Product orientation |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Customer needs, found by research | The product the business can make |
| Main question | "What do customers want?" | "How do we sell what we make?" |
| Strength | Products match demand, so less risk of failure | Can lead to new ideas customers had not imagined; uses the firm's skills |
| Weakness | Research costs money; following customers may lead to copying rivals | The product may not match what people want |

Most businesses mix the two. A specialist engineering firm may be product-oriented in design but still ask customers which features matter.

## Market share and market analysis

Market share is one business's sales as a percentage of the total market's sales. The specification gives no formula; the standard one is sales of the business ÷ total market sales × 100. **Market analysis** means studying the market's size, growth, competitors' shares and trends, so the business can set objectives and spot threats.

### Worked example 2: reading a market table

The paint market in one region (sales in thousands of dollars):

| | Year 1 sales | Year 1 share | Year 2 sales | Year 2 share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Firm A | 600 | 30% | 648 | 27% |
| Firm B | 500 | 25% | 552 | 23% |
| Kilnworth Paints | 300 | 15% | 384 | 16% |
| All others | 600 | 30% | 816 | 34% |
| **Total market** | **2,000** | **100%** | **2,400** | **100%** |

What the analysis shows:

- The market grew by (2,400 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **20%**.
- Kilnworth's sales grew by (384 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = **28%**, faster than the market, so its share rose by one percentage point.
- Firms A and B both sold more, yet lost share, because they grew more slowly than the market.
- "All others" grew from 30% to 34%: small firms are winning customers. That is a warning that the market is becoming more competitive.

The lesson: higher sales do not always mean a higher share. Always compare a firm's growth with the market's growth.

## Niche and mass marketing

**Niche marketing** targets a small, clearly defined group with specialised needs. **Mass marketing** aims a standard product at most of the market.

### Worked example 3: two strategies for a bag maker

Tessaly Bags could:

- **Niche:** sell 2,000 handmade climbing chalk bags a year at 85 each, with a cost per bag of 50. Profit = 2,000 × (85 − 50) = **70,000**.
- **Mass:** sell 20,000 basic sports bags at 22 each, with a cost per bag of 16. Profit = 20,000 × (22 − 16) = **120,000**.

Mass looks better on these figures, but the comparison needs judgement. The mass market has many large competitors, so the 20,000 sales are far less certain and a price war could cut the 6 profit per bag. The niche has fewer rivals, loyal customers and a higher profit per bag, but it is small and could shrink if climbing becomes less popular. A small business with skilled makers may be better suited to the niche.

## Responding to changes in the market

| Change named in 4.2.2 | Example | Possible responses |
|---|---|---|
| Changing customer needs | Customers want products with less packaging | Redesign packaging; promote the change |
| Changing spending patterns | Average weekly spending on takeaway meals in a town falls from 52 to 46, a fall of 11.5% | Smaller, cheaper portions; meal deals; cut costs |
| Increased competition | A large chain opens a branch nearby | Better service; loyalty scheme; focus on a niche the chain ignores |

A strong answer says **why** the response fits. Cutting prices against a large chain may fail, because the chain can usually go lower; offering something the chain does not, such as personal service, may work better.

## Market segmentation: targeting customers

Segmentation divides a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics, so the business can target the right product, price and promotion at each. The specification names five bases.

| Basis | Question it answers | Example of targeting |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Where do customers live or work? | A delivery service limited to one city's postcodes |
| Demographics | What are their characteristics, such as gender, family size or occupation? | Family-sized packs for households with children |
| Lifestyle | How do they live and what do they value? | Vegan ranges for customers who avoid animal products |
| Income | How much can they spend? | A budget range and a premium range of the same product |
| Age | How old are they? | Holidays aimed at retired travellers |

### Building a segment profile

Bases are usually combined. Solace Travel targets "people aged 60 and over (age), with high incomes (income), who enjoy culture and walking (lifestyle), living within two hours of its home airport (location)". A clear profile lets the business choose:

- **product**: small-group guided tours with comfortable hotels;
- **price**: premium, as the segment can afford it;
- **promotion**: printed brochures and partnerships with walking clubs rather than video apps.

Benefits: less wasted promotion, products that fit needs, chance to charge more. Drawbacks: research costs, and ignoring other segments may miss sales.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** market share and changes: show the formula and use the total market as the base.
- **Explain** why loyalty matters: statement plus two expansion points.
- **Analyse** a response to change: the change → the response → why it suits this business → the effect.
- **Justify** niche versus mass: compare figures, competition and the firm's size and skills, then decide.

## Common mistakes

- Saying sales growth means share growth without checking the market.
- Confusing market orientation with simply doing market research.
- Calling any small business "niche". Niche refers to the target market, not the firm's size.
- Listing segmentation bases without showing how the business would target the segment.
