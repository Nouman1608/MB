## Ozone and why it matters

Ozone, O₃, forms naturally in the upper atmosphere (the stratosphere). Ultraviolet radiation splits some oxygen molecules into atoms, and each oxygen atom combines with another oxygen molecule.

```
O₂ → 2O•                 (ultraviolet radiation)
O• + O₂ → O₃
```

Ozone is beneficial there because it **absorbs ultraviolet radiation** from the Sun. Ultraviolet radiation can damage living tissue and cause skin cancer, so a thinner ozone layer allows more of it to reach the surface. Ozone at ground level is a different matter: it is a pollutant. The specification is about ozone in the upper atmosphere.

## Chlorofluorocarbons and why they were used

CFCs are compounds containing only carbon, chlorine and fluorine, for example CCl₃F and CCl₂F₂. They were widely used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants and solvents because they are:

- chemically unreactive (stable),
- non-flammable,
- non-toxic,
- volatile enough to be easy to use as gases or in liquid form.

Their stability is the problem. They do not react with water, oxygen or the other gases in the lower atmosphere, so they are not removed there. They are carried slowly upwards and reach the stratosphere, where ultraviolet radiation is stronger.

## Forming chlorine atoms

In the stratosphere, ultraviolet radiation provides enough energy to break the **C–Cl** bond in a CFC. The bond splits so that each atom keeps one electron of the bonding pair. This is **homolytic fission**, and it gives free radicals.

```
CCl₂F₂ → •CClF₂ + Cl•        (ultraviolet radiation)
```

The C–Cl bond breaks in preference to the C–F bond because it is weaker (mean bond enthalpy about 346 kJ mol⁻¹ against about 467 kJ mol⁻¹). The C–F bonds stay intact.

A **radical** is a species with an unpaired electron. It is shown with a dot, Cl•. Radicals are very reactive because they seek to pair the unpaired electron.

## The catalytic cycle

A chlorine atom attacks an ozone molecule, and the chlorine is regenerated.

```
Step 1:  Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂
Step 2:  ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl•
```

Adding the two equations and cancelling species that appear on both sides (Cl• and ClO•) gives the overall change:

```
2O₃ → 3O₂
```

Chlorine does not appear in the overall equation, and it is unchanged at the end. That is the definition of a **catalyst**: it speeds up a reaction, provides a different pathway and is not used up. One chlorine atom can therefore destroy many thousands of ozone molecules before it is removed. It is removed only when it takes part in a termination step, such as combining with another radical.

```
Cl• + •CH₃ → CH₃Cl        (a termination step)
```

Note that the chain looks like the free-radical substitution in alkanes: radicals form (initiation), react in repeating steps (propagation) and are lost (termination). Here the repeating steps are the two ozone-destroying equations.

## What counts as "the hole"

The "hole in the ozone layer" is an area of the stratosphere, especially over Antarctica, where the ozone concentration falls much lower than normal in spring. The specification asks only that you say that chlorine atoms contribute to it. Describe it as a thinning of the layer, not as a literal hole.

## Research and legislation

The specification asks you to appreciate that results of research by **different groups** provided evidence for legislation.

- In the 1970s, chemists proposed that CFCs could release chlorine atoms in the stratosphere and destroy ozone.
- Measurements from the 1980s showed that ozone levels over Antarctica had dropped sharply.
- Evidence from several independent groups (laboratory studies of the reactions, satellite and ground-based measurements) pointed to the same cause.
- International agreements, such as the Montreal Protocol of 1987, then banned or phased out CFCs as solvents and refrigerants.
- Chemists developed **chlorine-free** replacements, for example hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), which contain C–F bonds but no C–Cl bonds and so cannot release chlorine atoms.

Agreement between independent sources is what makes evidence persuasive: it reduces the chance that one experiment or one instrument is at fault. Also note that replacements may have their own drawbacks. Some HFCs are greenhouse gases, so chemists continue to look for better alternatives.

## Worked example 1: writing the initiation equation

**Question.** Write an equation for the formation of chlorine atoms from CCl₃F and explain why the C–F bond is not broken.

```
CCl₃F → •CCl₂F + Cl•       (ultraviolet radiation)
```

The C–Cl bond is weaker than the C–F bond, so ultraviolet radiation breaks it first. A dot is needed on both products, and the condition (ultraviolet) is part of the answer.

## Worked example 2: combining the steps

**Question.** Show that chlorine atoms are a catalyst for the decomposition of ozone.

```
  Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂
  ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl•
  --------------------------------
  2O₃ + Cl• + ClO• → 3O₂ + ClO• + Cl•
  cancel Cl• and ClO• from both sides:
  2O₃ → 3O₂
```

Chlorine is used in step 1 and regenerated in step 2, so it is not consumed.

## Worked example 3: scale of the effect

A model assumption: 1.00 g of CCl₂F₂ (Mᵣ = 121.0) releases one chlorine atom per molecule, and each chlorine atom takes part in 1000 complete cycles. Each cycle destroys two ozone molecules (Mᵣ of O₃ = 48.0). Find the mass of ozone destroyed.

```
n(CCl₂F₂)         = 1.00 ÷ 121.0 = 0.008264 mol
n(Cl•)            = 0.008264 mol
n(cycles)         = 0.008264 × 1000 = 8.264 mol
n(O₃ destroyed)   = 2 × 8.264 = 16.53 mol
mass              = 16.53 × 48.0 = 793 g
```

The numbers are an illustration of catalysis and not real atmospheric data, but they show why a small mass of CFC matters: one gram affects the equivalent of hundreds of grams of ozone.

## Worked example 4: explaining why a replacement is safer

**Question.** The refrigerant CH₂FCF₃ replaces CCl₂F₂. Explain why it does not deplete the ozone layer in the same way.

CH₂FCF₃ contains no chlorine and no C–Cl bonds. Ultraviolet radiation cannot release chlorine atoms from it. The C–F bonds are too strong to break under stratospheric ultraviolet radiation, so no radical catalyst forms.

## Common mistakes

- Saying CFCs destroy ozone directly. Chlorine atoms from CFCs are the destroyers.
- Breaking the C–F bond in the initiation step.
- Writing Cl⁻ instead of Cl•, or leaving out the unpaired-electron dot.
- Writing the overall equation with Cl• and ClO• in it.
- Writing ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl• without balancing: check that there are three O atoms on the left from O₃ plus one from ClO•, giving four O atoms in 2O₂.
- Confusing ozone depletion with global warming.
- Forgetting to state ultraviolet radiation as the condition for the bond to break.
- Saying CFCs are harmful because they are reactive. They are harmful because they are **unreactive** in the lower atmosphere, so they reach the stratosphere.

## Exam technique

- **Use equations to explain**: write both propagation steps with dots on the radicals, then combine them.
- **Explain catalysis**: say chlorine is regenerated, and give the overall equation without chlorine.
- **Explain the role of evidence**: refer to independent research groups and how their results agreed.
- **Compare replacements**: link the absence of C–Cl bonds to the absence of chlorine atoms.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is ozone good in the upper atmosphere but a pollutant at ground level?**
In the upper atmosphere it absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation before it reaches living things. At ground level it damages lungs and plants, so it is harmful there. The specification concentrates on the beneficial role.

**Why does the C–F bond not break?**
It is stronger than the C–Cl bond, so the available ultraviolet radiation does not have enough energy to break it.

**Do the equations need state symbols?**
Not unless the question asks. The radical dots matter much more.
