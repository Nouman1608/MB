## Section A

**1.** Define *drought*. **[2]**

**2.** State the **two** causes of drought listed in the syllabus. **[2]**

**3.** State what is meant by *desalination*. **[1]**

**4.** Name **one** strategy for managing drought that can be used during the drought itself. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** The table shows rainfall in a grazing region. The normal annual rainfall is 500 mm. *The data are
invented for practice.*

| Year | Rainfall / mm |
|---|---|
| 1 | 480 |
| 2 | 360 |
| 3 | 290 |
| 4 | 260 |

(a) Calculate the percentage below normal in Year 4. **[2]**
(b) Calculate the mean rainfall for Years 1 to 4. **[1]**
(c) Use the data and the definition of drought to explain whether the region experienced a drought. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how drought in the grazing region could lead to soil erosion and desertification. **[3]**

**7.** Describe **three** impacts of drought on the natural environment. **[3]**

**8.** A city reservoir holds 9 million m³. The city uses 45 000 m³ per day.
(a) Calculate how many days the reservoir would last with no rain. **[1]**
(b) A water-conservation campaign reduces use by 20%. Calculate the new number of days. **[2]**
(c) Suggest **one** reason why the actual number of days may be lower than calculated. **[1]**

**9.** Explain how an increase in global temperatures can cause drought. **[2]**

**10.** Describe how monitoring and warning can reduce the impacts of drought on farmers. **[3]**

**11.** Explain **two** limitations of using dams and reservoirs to manage drought. **[4]**

**12.** Explain why famine is more likely in some countries than others during a drought. **[2]**

## Section C

**13.** A dry coastal country is choosing between desalination and greater use of aquifers and wells to
increase water supply. Discuss the benefits and limitations of each. **[6]**

**14.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of emergency water supplies and international aid for managing
the impacts of a severe drought. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A period of dry weather **[1]** that is longer or more severe than normal **[1]**.

**2.** Lack of rain **[1]**; climate change (increase in global temperatures and in extreme weather events)
**[1]**.

**3.** Removing salt from sea water to produce fresh water. **[1]**

**4.** Any one: emergency water supplies; water conservation; international aid. **[1]**

**5.** (a) 500 - 260 = 240 mm **[1]**; 240 / 500 x 100 = **48%** below normal **[1]**.
(b) (480 + 360 + 290 + 260) / 4 = 1 390 / 4 = **347.5 mm**. **[1]**
(c) Rainfall was below the normal 500 mm in every year and fell each year to 260 mm **[1]**, so the dry period
was both longer and more severe than normal, which fits the definition of drought **[1]**.

**6.** Grass dies and livestock overgraze what is left, leaving bare soil **[1]**; dry, loose soil is blown
away by wind or washed away when rain returns (soil erosion) **[1]**; as fertile topsoil is lost and plants
cannot regrow, the land becomes desert-like (desertification) **[1]**.

**7.** Any three: rivers, lakes and wetlands dry up **[1]**; loss of habitats **[1]**; death of organisms such
as fish and plants **[1]**; increased risk of wildfires **[1]**; soil erosion **[1]**; decrease in air quality
from dust and smoke **[1]**. **[max 3]**

**8.** (a) 9 000 000 / 45 000 = **200 days**. **[1]**
(b) New use = 45 000 x 0.8 = 36 000 m³ per day **[1]**; 9 000 000 / 36 000 = **250 days** **[1]**.
(c) Any one: evaporation from the reservoir in hot weather; leaks; demand rises in hot weather; not everyone
follows the campaign. **[1]**

**9.** Higher temperatures increase evaporation from soils, rivers and reservoirs and transpiration from
plants **[1]**, so water is lost faster than it is replaced, and extreme dry spells become longer and more
intense **[1]**.

**10.** Rainfall, soil moisture and reservoir levels are measured, and satellites show vegetation health
**[1]**; because drought builds up slowly, warnings can be given months ahead **[1]**, so farmers can plant
drought-tolerant crops, store fodder or sell livestock before they die **[1]**.

**11.** Two explained limitations, two marks each. Dams are very expensive to build **[1]**, so low-income
countries may need loans or aid **[1]**. The reservoir floods land, destroying habitats and sometimes homes
**[1]**, and in a hot drought much water is lost by evaporation, so the reservoir may still run dry **[1]**.

**12.** Countries with more money can import food and transport it to drought areas **[1]**, while poorer
countries with weak transport, conflict or dependence on their own harvests may be unable to replace failed
crops **[1]**.

**13.** **[6]** One mark per point, up to six, with both strategies covered. Desalination: supply does not
depend on rainfall **[1]**; suits a coastal country with unlimited sea water **[1]**; but is very expensive and
energy-intensive **[1]**, and brine waste harms marine life **[1]**. Aquifers and wells: cheaper and ground
water loses little to evaporation **[1]**; but over-pumping lowers the water table and wells can dry up,
because recharge in a dry country is slow **[1]**; near the coast, over-pumping can draw salt water into the
aquifer **[1]**. Conclusion: aquifers are cheaper in the short term, but desalination is more reliable over a
long drought if the country can afford it **[1]**. **[max 6]**

**14.** **[6]** One mark per point, up to six. Emergency water supplies: quickly provide drinking water,
preventing dehydration and disease **[1]**; but tankers and bottled water are expensive **[1]** and hard to
deliver to remote areas **[1]**; they are short-term only **[1]**. International aid: provides food, water and
money when a country cannot cope, helping prevent famine **[1]**; but it may arrive late because drought
builds slowly and is noticed late **[1]**, and long-term reliance can create dependence **[1]**. Conclusion:
both save lives during severe drought but must be combined with monitoring and increased supply **[1]**.
**[max 6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining drought as "no rain" or "little rain" without comparing with normal.
- Calculating percentage below normal by dividing by the actual rainfall.
- Saying rainwater harvesting solves a drought without noting that rain is needed first.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the syllabus require a named drought case study?**
The 2027-2029 syllabus does not name case studies; questions use source material, so practise applying the
listed causes, impacts and strategies to unfamiliar places.

**Is desertification the same as drought?**
No. Drought is a period of dry weather; desertification is a possible long-term impact in which land
becomes desert-like.
