## Condensation polymerisation (3.3.12.1)

In **condensation polymerisation** each monomer has **two** reactive functional groups. When two
monomers join, a small molecule is lost, usually water. Because every monomer has a group at each end,
the chain can keep growing in both directions.

Compare this with addition polymerisation, where a C=C bond opens and nothing else is produced. In a
condensation polymer the main chain contains **linkages** (ester or amide groups), not just C–C bonds.

The specification names three combinations:

| Monomers | Linkage formed | Type of polymer |
|---|---|---|
| dicarboxylic acid + diol | ester, –COO– | polyester |
| dicarboxylic acid + diamine | amide, –CONH– | polyamide |
| amino acids | amide, –CONH– | polyamide (polypeptide) |

### Polyesters: Terylene

Terylene is made from **benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid** and **ethane-1,2-diol**. The –COOH of one
monomer reacts with the –OH of the other. The OH from the acid and the H from the alcohol leave as water,
and an ester link forms.

```
n HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH  +  n HO–CH₂CH₂–OH
        →  –[ CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–CH₂CH₂–O ]ₙ–  +  2n H₂O
```

Here C₆H₄ is a benzene ring joined at positions 1 and 4. The repeating unit contains **one residue of
each monomer** and one complete ester link, plus the bonds that continue to the next units.

**Typical uses of Terylene:** fibres for clothing (often blended with cotton) and plastic drinks bottles.

### Polyamides: nylon 6,6 and Kevlar

Nylon 6,6 is made from **hexanedioic acid** and **hexane-1,6-diamine**. The –COOH reacts with –NH₂,
water is lost, and an amide link forms. The name "6,6" comes from the six carbon atoms in each monomer.

```
n HOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOH  +  n H₂N–(CH₂)₆–NH₂
        →  –[ CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₆–NH ]ₙ–  +  2n H₂O
```

Kevlar is made from **benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid** and **benzene-1,4-diamine**. Its repeating unit is
–[CO–C₆H₄–CO–NH–C₆H₄–NH]–.

**Typical uses:** nylon 6,6 for clothing, carpets and ropes; Kevlar for bulletproof vests and body armour,
and for ropes and protective clothing where great strength for low weight is needed.

In the laboratory, nylon 6,6 is usually made with **hexanedioyl dichloride** in place of the acid. The
acyl chloride is more reactive, and HCl is lost instead of water. This links to the reactions of acyl
chlorides with primary amines in 3.3.9.2.

### Polyamides from amino acids

An amino acid has an –NH₂ group and a –COOH group on the same molecule, so **one** monomer is enough. The
–NH₂ of one molecule reacts with the –COOH of the next, losing water.

```
n H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH   →   –[ NH–CH(CH₃)–CO ]ₙ–  +  n H₂O
     2-aminopropanoic acid
```

When different amino acids join, the polymer is a polypeptide. Proteins and amino acid structure are
covered in 3.3.13.

**Worked example 1.** Draw the repeating unit of the polyester made from butanedioic acid and
propane-1,3-diol, and state what else forms.

- Write the acid: HOOC–CH₂CH₂–COOH. Write the diol: HO–CH₂CH₂CH₂–OH.
- Remove OH from one end of the acid and H from one end of the diol; join C to O. Do the same at the other
  end.
- Repeating unit: **–[CO–CH₂CH₂–CO–O–CH₂CH₂CH₂–O]–**, with the n outside the brackets.
- The other product is **water**.

### Repeating units from a section of chain

1. Find the linkages (–CO–O– or –CO–NH–) along the chain.
2. Find the pattern that repeats. For a two-monomer polymer it contains **two** linkages' worth of
   atoms: one diacid residue and one diol or diamine residue.
3. Cut through the middle of a linkage (between C and O, or C and N) at each end, so the unit begins with
   the CO or the O/NH. Draw extension bonds and put n outside.

### Monomers from a section of chain

1. Break every ester or amide link between C and O, or between C and N.
2. Add **OH** to each C=O carbon you cut, giving –COOH.
3. Add **H** to each O or N you cut, giving –OH or –NH₂.

**Worked example 2.** A section of a polymer is
–NH–(CH₂)₄–NH–CO–(CH₂)₈–CO–NH–(CH₂)₄–NH–CO–(CH₂)₈–CO–. Give the repeating unit and the monomers.

- The links are –CO–NH–, so this is a polyamide.
- Repeating unit: **–[NH–(CH₂)₄–NH–CO–(CH₂)₈–CO]–**.
- Breaking the C–N bonds and adding H to N and OH to C gives **H₂N–(CH₂)₄–NH₂** (butane-1,4-diamine) and
  **HOOC–(CH₂)₈–COOH** (decanedioic acid).

**Worked example 3.** A sample of nylon 6,6 has an average Mr of 4.52 × 10⁴. How many repeating units are
in an average chain? (Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0)

- Repeating unit formula: C₁₂H₂₂N₂O₂. Mr = 12(12.0) + 22(1.0) + 2(14.0) + 2(16.0) = 226.0.
- Check: Mr(hexanedioic acid) + Mr(hexane-1,6-diamine) − 2 × Mr(H₂O) = 146.0 + 116.0 − 36.0 = 226.0.
- Number of units = 4.52 × 10⁴ / 226.0 = **200**.

**Worked example 4.** Calculate the percentage atom economy for making nylon 6,6 from hexanedioic acid and
hexane-1,6-diamine, taking two water molecules lost per repeating unit.

- Atom economy = 226.0 / (146.0 + 116.0) × 100 = **86.3%**.
- It is below 100% because water is a by-product. Addition polymerisation has an atom economy of 100%.

### Intermolecular forces between condensation polymers

All polymer chains have **van der Waals forces** between them. Condensation polymers also have stronger
forces, because their linkages are polar.

- **Polyamides** (nylon 6,6, Kevlar) form **hydrogen bonds** between chains. The H on the N–H group of
  one chain is attracted to a lone pair on the O of a C=O group on a neighbouring chain. N–H is very polar
  because N is much more electronegative than H.
- **Polyesters** (Terylene) have **permanent dipole–dipole forces** between the polar C=O groups of
  neighbouring chains. There is no H bonded to O or N in the ester link, so there is no hydrogen bonding
  between the chains.
- **Kevlar** has flat, rigid chains because of the benzene rings. The chains line up parallel and form
  many hydrogen bonds with each other. That is why Kevlar fibres are so strong.

Stronger forces between chains mean the polymer needs more energy to melt and is stronger as a fibre.
This is why polyamides and polyesters make good fibres, while polyalkenes such as poly(ethene), which
have only van der Waals forces, are softer.

## Biodegradability and disposal (3.3.12.2)

### Why polyesters and polyamides can be hydrolysed

Ester and amide links contain a polar **C=O** bond, so the carbon is **δ+**. Nucleophiles such as water
or hydroxide ions can attack this carbon, and the link breaks. This is **hydrolysis**, the reverse of the
condensation reaction. It happens faster with an acid or alkali. Because these polymers can be broken
down like this, they are **biodegradable**.

- **Acid hydrolysis** of a polyester gives the dicarboxylic acid and the diol. Acid hydrolysis of a
  polyamide gives the dicarboxylic acid and the **ammonium salt** of the diamine, because the amine
  groups are protonated.
- **Alkaline hydrolysis** (eg with NaOH) gives the **carboxylate salt**, plus the diol from a polyester
  or the diamine from a polyamide.

**Worked example 5.** Terylene is boiled with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Name the organic products.

- The ester links are hydrolysed. The diol is released as **ethane-1,2-diol**.
- The acid groups end up as their sodium salt in alkali: **sodium benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate**.

### Why polyalkenes cannot be hydrolysed

A polyalkene chain contains only C–C and C–H bonds. These bonds are strong and **non-polar**, so there is
no δ+ carbon for a nucleophile to attack. Polyalkenes are therefore **chemically inert** and
**non-biodegradable**. (PVC has polar C–Cl bonds, but its main chain is still made of C–C bonds.)

### Methods of disposal

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Landfill | Simple; handles mixed waste without sorting | Uses up land; polyalkenes remain for a very long time; waste of a resource |
| Incineration | Reduces volume; heat released can generate electricity | Releases CO₂, a greenhouse gas; burning PVC releases toxic gases such as HCl, which must be removed |
| Mechanical recycling | Saves raw materials (crude oil) and energy; less landfill | Polymers must be collected, sorted by type and cleaned; quality may fall each time |
| Feedstock recycling | Polymer is broken down into monomers or other chemicals for new products | Needs energy and specialist plant |

When you evaluate a method, give an advantage **and** a disadvantage, each tied to the chemistry.

## Common errors

- Drawing a condensation repeating unit with only one monomer residue when there are two monomers.
- Forgetting to show water (or HCl) as the second product in the equation.
- Leaving –OH or –H on the ends of a repeating unit; the unit ends with extension bonds.
- Writing the amide link as –CO–NH₂– or the ester link as –CO–OH–.
- Saying polyesters form hydrogen bonds between chains; the ester link has no N–H or O–H.
- Saying polyalkenes cannot be hydrolysed because "their bonds are weak"; C–C and C–H are strong and
  non-polar.
- Giving the acid, not the salt, as a product of alkaline hydrolysis.
- Listing "recycling is good" without a reason or a disadvantage.
