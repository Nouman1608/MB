## Section A -- short questions

**1.** Explain who is an occupier for the purposes of the Occupiers' Liability Acts, with an authority. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the special duty owed to child visitors under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957. **[3]**

**3.** Explain when an occupier is not liable for the faulty work of an independent contractor. **[4]**

**4.** State two differences between the duty owed under the 1957 Act and the duty owed under the 1984 Act. **[2]**

**5.** Explain who may sue in private nuisance, with an authority. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the relevance of malice in private nuisance, with one case. **[3]**

**7.** Explain the defence of prescription in private nuisance. **[3]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by a continuing trespass, with an example. **[2]**

## Section B -- scenario and essay questions

**9.** *Original scenario.* Greenleaf Garden Centre has a pond with a low wall and a sign reading "Danger: deep water". Ella, aged six, wanders away from her mother and falls into the pond. Later that day, Raj, a self-employed electrician called to fix a faulty light, is electrocuted by a live wire he was told about. That evening, Jack, aged 14, climbs a locked gate to skateboard in the empty car park and is injured falling into an unguarded trench that the owners knew local teenagers often crossed. Advise Greenleaf on its liability to each claimant. **[14]**

**10.** *Original scenario.* Moorside Bakery has operated in a mainly residential street for 25 years. Last year it began baking through the night, and noise and smells now disturb Mina, who owns a house next door, and her lodger Ola. Mina's neighbour, who is in dispute with Mina, also plays loud music late every night to annoy her. Advise Mina and Ola. **[10]**

**11.** *Original scenario.* Fernway Chemicals stores large drums of a corrosive solvent at its works. A drum is punctured by an intruder, and the solvent flows onto a neighbouring farm, poisoning crops. A farm worker is also burned. Advise the farmer and the farm worker on any claim under Rylands v Fletcher. **[9]**

**12.** "The Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 strikes a fair balance between protecting trespassers and protecting occupiers." Evaluate this statement. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** An occupier is a person with a sufficient degree of control over the premises (1). There can be more than one occupier, as in Wheat v E Lacon and Co (1966), where both the brewery and the manager occupied the pub (1). **[2]**

**2.** Under s2(3)(a) an occupier must be prepared for children to be less careful than adults (1). Features that attract children into danger may be allurements, as with poisonous berries in Glasgow Corporation v Taylor (1922) (1). But an occupier may expect parents to supervise very young children (Phipps v Rochester Corporation, 1955) (1). **[3]**

**3.** Under s2(4)(b) the occupier is not liable if it was reasonable to entrust the work to a contractor (1), the occupier took reasonable steps to check the contractor was competent (1), and, where the work allows, checked that the work was properly done (1). In Haseldine v Daw (1941) the occupier was not liable for a negligent lift repair because checking technical work needed expertise, whereas in Woodward v Mayor of Hastings (1945) an icy step was easy to check (1). **[4]**

**4.** Any two of: the 1957 duty is owed to all lawful visitors, whereas the 1984 duty arises only if the s1(3) conditions are met (1); the 1957 Act covers property damage, while the 1984 Act covers only personal injury (s1(8)) (1); exclusion is expressly allowed under the 1957 Act but the 1984 Act is silent. **[2]**

**5.** Only a person with a proprietary interest in the affected land, such as an owner or tenant (1), as held in Hunter v Canary Wharf (1997), where family members without an interest could not sue (1). **[2]**

**6.** Malice can make otherwise reasonable conduct an unreasonable interference (1). In Christie v Davey (1893) a neighbour who banged on the party wall to disrupt music lessons committed a nuisance (1), and in Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett (1936) firing guns to disrupt fox breeding was a nuisance because it was done to cause harm (1). **[3]**

**7.** A defendant may acquire a right to commit a nuisance by carrying it on for 20 years (1). Time runs only from when the activity became an actionable nuisance to the claimant (1), as in Sturges v Bridgman (1879), where the noise affected the doctor only once he built his consulting room (1). **[3]**

**8.** A trespass that continues for as long as something remains on the claimant's land, giving a fresh cause of action each day (1), such as the buttresses left on the claimant's land in Holmes v Wilson (1839) (1). **[2]**

**9.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Ella is a lawful visitor, so the 1957 Act applies (1).
- s2(3)(a): occupiers must expect children to be less careful (1); a pond may be an allurement (Glasgow Corporation v Taylor) (1).
- A written sign may not make a six-year-old reasonably safe (s2(4)(a)) (1); but a garden centre may expect a parent to supervise a young child (Phipps) (1).
- Raj is a visitor carrying out his calling, s2(3)(b) (1); he can be expected to guard against risks incidental to it (Roles v Nathan) (1); he was warned of the live wire, so Greenleaf is probably not liable (1).
- Jack is a trespasser, so the 1984 Act applies (1).
- s1(3): Greenleaf knew of the trench (1) and that teenagers came into the vicinity (1); an unguarded trench is a risk it may reasonably be expected to protect against (1).
- Unlike Tomlinson, the danger comes from the state of the premises, not from skateboarding alone (1).
- Damages may be reduced for Jack's contributory negligence (1). **[14]**

**10.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Mina, as owner, has a proprietary interest and can sue (1); Ola, a lodger with no interest in land, cannot (Hunter v Canary Wharf) (1).
- Locality: a mainly residential street, so night baking is more likely unreasonable (Sturges v Bridgman) (1).
- Duration and timing: continuous, night-time interference is weighty (De Keyser's Royal Hotel) (1).
- Prescription: the bakery has operated for 25 years, but the night baking began only last year (1), so 20 years of actionable nuisance have not run (1).
- Public benefit of supplying bread is not a defence (1).
- Neighbour's music: the purpose is to annoy, so malice makes it unreasonable (Christie v Davey) (1).
- Remedies: an injunction, possibly limiting hours, and damages (1).
- Conclusion: Mina has claims against both; Ola has none in private nuisance (1). **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Fernway brought and kept the solvent on its land for its own purposes (1).
- A corrosive solvent is likely to do mischief if it escapes (1).
- Storing large quantities is arguably a non-natural use (Rickards v Lothian; Transco) (1).
- The solvent escaped to the farm (1), and crop damage is of a foreseeable type (Cambridge Water) (1).
- Defence: act of a stranger, since an intruder caused the escape (Rickards v Lothian) (1); it fails if the intrusion was foreseeable and Fernway could have prevented it (1).
- The farm worker cannot claim for personal injury under Rylands (Transco) (1), and has no interest in land, so must sue in negligence instead (1). **[9]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- Knowledge (up to 6): Herrington; s1(3) conditions; s1(4) duty; s1(5) warnings; s1(6) consent; s1(8) no property damage; Ratcliff v McConnell; Tomlinson v Congleton BC; Keown v Coventry Healthcare.
- Analysis (up to 3): explain how the conditions limit the duty to occupiers who know of both the danger and the likely presence of trespassers.
- Evaluation (up to 6): for the statement, occupiers are protected from liability for obvious risks and for claimants' own reckless activities (Tomlinson), and trespassers chose to enter. Against, child trespassers may not appreciate risks, and the activity-versus-premises distinction (Keown) can deny children a remedy; the exclusion of property damage and uncertainty over exclusion clauses are criticised. A reasoned conclusion. **[15]**
