This guide goes deeper into **3.2.5.3 Shapes of complex ions**, part of section 3.2.5 Transition metals in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 3 (Inorganic 2 and Physical 2) and available again in the synoptic Unit 5. The subtopic asks you to predict the shape of a complex from its ligands, recognise when a complex can exist as stereoisomers, and know three named examples: cisplatin, Tollens' reagent and complexes of chloride ions.

## What the specification asks for

The statements in 3.2.5.3 are:

- transition metal ions commonly form octahedral complexes with small ligands (for example H₂O and NH₃)
- octahedral complexes can display cis–trans isomerism (a special case of E–Z isomerism) with monodentate ligands, and optical isomerism with bidentate ligands
- transition metal ions commonly form tetrahedral complexes with larger ligands (for example Cl⁻)
- square planar complexes are also formed and can display cis–trans isomerism
- cisplatin is the cis isomer
- Ag⁺ forms the linear complex [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, as used in Tollens' reagent.

Everything here builds on two ideas from 3.2.5.1 and 3.2.5.2: the **co-ordination number** (the number of co-ordinate bonds, not the number of ligands) and the difference between **monodentate** and **bidentate** ligands.

## Step 1: find the co-ordination number

Shape follows from the co-ordination number. Count donor atoms, not ligands:

| Ligand | Donor atoms per ligand |
|---|---|
| H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻ | 1 (monodentate) |
| H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ (ethane-1,2-diamine, often written "en") | 2 (bidentate) |
| C₂O₄²⁻ (ethanedioate) | 2 (bidentate) |
| EDTA⁴⁻ | 6 (multidentate) |

So [Cr(C₂O₄)₃]³⁻ has three ligands but a co-ordination number of **6**, and [Cu(EDTA)]²⁻ has one ligand but a co-ordination number of **6**.

## Step 2: match the co-ordination number to a shape

The co-ordinate bonds are pairs of electrons, and they repel one another. The ligands take up positions that keep the bonding pairs as far apart as possible, just as in the shapes of simple molecules.

| Co-ordination number | Shape | Bond angle | When it occurs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | octahedral | 90° (180° between opposite ligands) | small ligands such as H₂O and NH₃, and complexes of bidentate or multidentate ligands |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | larger ligands such as Cl⁻ |
| 4 | square planar | 90° | certain complexes, notably platinum(II) complexes such as cisplatin |
| 2 | linear | 180° | Ag⁺ with two NH₃ ligands |

**Why size matters.** Six small, uncharged ligands fit round a metal ion. Chloride ions are larger, and only four fit, so the co-ordination number falls from 6 to 4 and the shape changes from octahedral to tetrahedral. For example:

```
[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺   octahedral, co-ordination number 6
[CuCl₄]²⁻      tetrahedral, co-ordination number 4
```

**Two shapes for four ligands.** A co-ordination number of 4 can be tetrahedral or square planar. You cannot tell which from the formula alone, so a question will either tell you, or name a familiar example. If a four-co-ordinate complex is said to show cis–trans isomerism, it must be square planar, because a tetrahedral complex cannot (see below).

## Stereoisomerism in complexes

Stereoisomers have the same formula and the same bonds, but a different arrangement of atoms in space. Complexes show two kinds.

### Cis–trans isomerism

This is the special case of E–Z isomerism you meet with alkenes, applied to a metal centre. It needs two identical ligands that can sit either next to each other or opposite each other.

- **Cis**: the two identical ligands are at **90°** to each other (adjacent positions).
- **Trans**: the two identical ligands are at **180°** to each other (opposite positions).

It occurs in:

- **octahedral** complexes of the type [MA₄B₂], with monodentate ligands, for example [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺
- **square planar** complexes of the type [MA₂B₂], for example [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂].

It cannot occur in a **tetrahedral** complex. Every position in a tetrahedron is next to every other one, with all angles 109.5°, so there is no "opposite" position and only one arrangement of [MA₂B₂] exists.

### Cisplatin

[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] is square planar. In the **cis** isomer, the two chloride ligands are next to each other at 90°; in the trans isomer they are opposite each other at 180°. **Cisplatin is the cis isomer.** It is used as an anticancer drug; how it acts is covered in section 3.3.13.3, in the [amino acids and proteins study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-amino-acids-and-proteins/).

```
      Cl        NH₃                 Cl        NH₃
        \      /                      \      /
          Pt                            Pt
        /      \                      /      \
      Cl        NH₃                H₃N        Cl

   cis isomer (cisplatin)          trans isomer
   Cl–Pt–Cl angle 90°              Cl–Pt–Cl angle 180°
```

### Optical isomerism

Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images. In this subtopic they arise in **octahedral complexes with bidentate ligands**. Three bidentate ligands wrapped round a metal ion form a "propeller" that can twist one way or the other, and the two forms are mirror images that cannot be superimposed. Examples: [Ni(H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)₃]²⁺ and [Cr(C₂O₄)₃]³⁻.

A bidentate ligand such as ethane-1,2-diamine or ethanedioate is short. Its two donor atoms can only reach **adjacent** positions (90° apart), never opposite ones. That fact explains which isomers can exist.

A square planar complex cannot show optical isomerism, because the plane containing the metal and all four donor atoms is a plane of symmetry. The same applies to the trans isomer of an octahedral complex such as trans-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺: it has a plane of symmetry, so it is not chiral.

## Linear complexes and Tollens' reagent

Silver(I) forms [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺: two co-ordinate bonds from nitrogen lone pairs, arranged at **180°**, so the complex is **linear**. This is the active species in Tollens' reagent, which aldehydes reduce to a silver mirror (section 3.2.5.5 explains the redox chemistry). Linear complexes cannot show cis–trans or optical isomerism.

## Worked example 1: shape, charge and isomers

For each complex, give the oxidation state of the metal, the co-ordination number, the shape and the number of stereoisomers.

**(a) [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺**

1. Charge: Co + 4(0) + 2(−1) = +1, so cobalt is **+3**.
2. Six monodentate ligands: co-ordination number **6**, **octahedral**.
3. Type [MA₄B₂]: the two Cl⁻ ligands can be at 90° (cis) or 180° (trans).
4. Neither isomer is chiral: each has a plane of symmetry. Total: **2 stereoisomers**.

**(b) [Cr(C₂O₄)₂(H₂O)₂]⁻**

1. Charge: Cr + 2(−2) + 2(0) = −1, so chromium is **+3**.
2. Co-ordination number: 2 × 2 + 2 × 1 = **6**, **octahedral**.
3. The two water ligands can be trans (opposite) or cis (adjacent).
4. The trans form has a plane of symmetry. The cis form has none, so it exists as a pair of optical isomers. Total: **3 stereoisomers** (one trans, two cis).

**(c) [Ni(H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)₃]²⁺**

1. Charge: Ni + 3(0) = +2, so nickel is **+2**.
2. Co-ordination number 3 × 2 = **6**, **octahedral**.
3. Three bidentate ligands: a pair of optical isomers. Total: **2 stereoisomers**.

## Worked example 2: an annotated model answer

*Question: Platinum(II) forms the complex [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]. Explain why it exists as two isomers, whereas the tetrahedral complex [MA₂B₂] of a different metal exists as only one. (3 marks)*

> The platinum complex is square planar, so the two Cl⁻ ligands can be adjacent at 90° (cis) or opposite at 180° (trans) [1]. These are cis–trans stereoisomers because the arrangement in space differs but the bonds are the same [1]. In a tetrahedral complex all four positions are adjacent to one another (all angles 109.5°), so any arrangement of two A and two B ligands is the same [1].

The answer names the shape, gives the two angles, and explains the tetrahedral case through geometry rather than just stating it.

## Common mistakes

- Taking the number of ligands as the co-ordination number, so calling [Cr(C₂O₄)₃]³⁻ "three-co-ordinate" or trigonal planar.
- Giving 90° as the bond angle in a tetrahedral complex, or 109.5° in a square planar one.
- Claiming that a tetrahedral complex shows cis–trans isomerism.
- Saying cisplatin has the two ammonia ligands opposite each other. In the cis isomer both pairs of identical ligands are adjacent.
- Forgetting that the cis form of [M(en)₂X₂] is chiral, and so counting only two stereoisomers instead of three.
- Drawing a bidentate ligand spanning opposite positions of an octahedron.
- Describing [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ as bent. It is linear, 180°.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** or **give**: a shape name or bond angle is enough; no reasoning needed.
- **Deduce**: show the arithmetic for the charge or co-ordination number, then the conclusion.
- **Draw**: show the metal at the centre, every donor atom, and the correct geometry. For an octahedral complex use wedge and dashed bonds or a clear perspective sketch, label cis or trans, and draw mirror images side by side with a dashed mirror line.
- **Explain**: link the shape to the cause, such as ligand size, the plane of symmetry, or the absence of an "opposite" position in a tetrahedron.
