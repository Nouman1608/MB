The ark is Country A's currency and the bel is Country B's; rates are quoted in bels per ark.

## Key terms (2a, 2d, 2e)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Floating rate | Set by market demand and supply |
| Fixed rate | Set officially and defended by the central bank |
| Managed rate | Market-determined but with official intervention |
| Appreciation | Market-driven rise in a floating currency |
| Revaluation | Official rise in a fixed or target rate |
| Depreciation | Market-driven fall |
| Devaluation | Official fall in a fixed or target rate |
| Capital flight | Rapid outflow of funds after a loss of confidence |
| Competitive depreciation | Lowering the rate to win price competitiveness, which others may copy |

Market words (appreciation, depreciation) go with floating rates. Official words (revaluation, devaluation) go with fixed or managed rates.

## Intervention (2b)

| Tool | Effect on the rate | Cost or limit |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign currency transactions | Buying the currency with reserves raises it; selling it lowers it | Reserves run out when defending a high rate |
| Interest rates | A higher rate attracts funds and raises it; a lower rate does the reverse | Higher rates slow growth and tie policy to the rate |
| QE | Lowers yields and tends to lower the rate | Aimed mainly at spending; the fall is a by-product |

To lower the rate the central bank sells its own currency, cuts interest rates or uses QE. QE is not a tool for raising the rate.

**Systems at a glance.**

| | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Certainty; discipline on inflation | Reserves needed; interest rates tied; speculative attack |
| Floating | Automatic adjustment; policy freedom | Volatile; imported inflation after a fall |
| Managed | Some stability and some flexibility | Needs reserves and judgement; markets may test it |

**Peg check.** Demand for arks is Qd = 100 − 20R and supply Qs = 40 + 20R. Equilibrium: 60 = 40R, so R = 1.50. A peg at 1.80 gives Qd = 64 and Qs = 76. The excess supply is 12 billion arks. Reserves needed: 12 × 1.80 = 21.6 billion bels.

## Floating rate factors (2c)

Method in steps:

1. Name the factor and the shift it causes: demand for the currency, supply of it, or both.
2. State the direction: rate up if demand rises or supply falls.
3. Say why: export buyers, investors or speculators.
4. Add a limit or a time frame.

| Factor | Effect on currency |
|---|---|
| Higher interest rates than abroad | Up |
| Lower inflation than abroad | Up over time |
| Current account deficit | Down |
| Stronger economy and prospects | Usually up |
| Capital flight | Down |
| Expected rise or fall | Self-fulfilling |
| Fall in a commodity price (exporter) | Down |

**PPP quick form.** Rate = price level of basket abroad ÷ price level at home. If a basket costs 90 arks and 180 bels, PPP is 2.00 bels per ark. Relative PPP: the currency of the country with higher inflation should depreciate by about the gap. With inflation of 3% in A and 5% in B, the ark should rise by 1.05 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 1.94%.

## Effects of a change (2f)

| Area | After a depreciation | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Current account | Exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer: balance improves if the sum of the two price elasticities of demand is above 1 | Short-run elasticities low |
| Capital and financial accounts | Cheaper domestic assets attract inflows; expected further falls prompt outflows | Expectations |
| Growth | Net exports rise, AD shifts right, multiplied | Spare capacity needed |
| Employment | Jobs in exporters and import-competing firms | Labour intensity, lags |
| Inflation | Imported goods and inputs dearer; demand-pull from higher AD | Pass-through, import share |
| FDI | Lower foreign-currency cost of entry | Stability and returns matter more |

An appreciation has the opposite effects.

**Marshall-Lerner and the J-curve.** A depreciation improves the current account when PED for exports plus PED for imports is above 1. Below 1, the balance worsens. In the short run elasticities are low (contracts and habits), so the balance worsens first and then improves: a J-shape over time.

**J-curve sketch.** Vertical axis: current account balance. Horizontal axis: time since the depreciation. The line starts at zero, dips below it, bottoms out, then climbs above zero.

```
balance
  +              _____
                /
  0 ---\       /
        \_____/
  -
        time after depreciation
```

**Marshall-Lerner mini-example.** Short-run elasticities of 0.2 and 0.3 sum to 0.5: worse. Long-run elasticities of 0.9 and 0.6 sum to 1.5: better.

**Capital account chain.** Depreciation → assets cheaper to foreigners → inflow. But expected further falls → investors sell now → outflow. The result depends on expectations.

**Quick calculation.** Net exports rise by 30 million arks. MPW is 0.4, so the multiplier is 2.5 and GDP rises by 75 million, if there is spare capacity.

**Inflation quick form.** Imports are 15% of the basket and import prices rise 10%. Direct effect: 0.15 × 10 = 1.5%.

## Competitive depreciations (2g)

- Chain: A devalues → exports gain → B loses market share → B devalues → gains cancel.
- Consequences: dearer imports, inflation, uncertainty, trade barriers, weaker incentive to raise productivity, volatile markets.
- Evaluation: it can work if retaliation is slow and elasticities are high, but if every country does it, nobody gains relative to the others.
- Gains are temporary and fall mainly on the country that is slow to retaliate.

## Method: analysing a change in the rate

1. State what happened and why (official decision or market force).
2. Give the direct effect on export and import prices, with a number if the data allows.
3. Apply elasticities and the time period to the current account.
4. Trace the knock-on effect through aggregate demand, output, jobs and prices.
5. Weigh the effects and say which dominates, and under what conditions.

**Percentage reminder.** The ark moves from 2.50 to 2.00 bels. A 600-ark good costs 1,500 bels, then 1,200: a fall of 20% for buyers abroad. The ark itself has fallen by (2.00 ÷ 2.50 − 1) = 20%.

## Evaluation checklist

- Time period (J-curve) and size of elasticities
- Spare capacity or full employment
- Import share of production and consumption
- Retaliation and global conditions
- Whether quality, not price, drives sales
- Which groups gain: exporters, tourists; lose: importers, consumers abroad

## Quick self-test

1. The ark falls from 1.25 to 1.00 bels. State the percentage change in the ark and in the bel (in arks).
2. A basket costs 90 arks and 180 bels. What is the PPP rate?
3. Price elasticities are 0.35 (exports) and 0.45 (imports). Does the Marshall-Lerner condition hold?
4. A 250-ark good sells at 3.00 then 2.40 bels per ark. Find the bel price each time and the percentage change.
5. Inflation is 3% in A and 5% in B. What does relative PPP imply for the ark?
6. A bel saver has 80,000 bels. Deposit rates are 4% in A and 1.5% in B, and the start rate is 2.00. Compare outcomes if the end rate is 1.95.
7. Distinguish devaluation from depreciation.
8. Net exports rise by 30 million and MPW is 0.4. Find the change in GDP.
9. Imports are 15% of the basket, and import prices rise 10%. Find the direct effect on prices.
10. Name two ways a central bank can defend a peg.

### Answers

1. Ark: 1.00 ÷ 1.25 − 1 = −20%. Bel in arks: from 0.80 to 1.00 = +25%.
2. 180 ÷ 90 = 2.00 bels per ark.
3. 0.35 + 0.45 = 0.80, below 1. No: the balance worsens.
4. 750 and 600 bels; a fall of 20%.
5. The ark should appreciate by about 1.94%.
6. Deposit in A: 40,000 arks become 41,600, then 41,600 × 1.95 = 81,120 bels. Staying: 81,200. The saver loses 80 bels. Break-even end rate is 81,200 ÷ 41,600 = 1.952.
7. Devaluation is an official cut in a fixed or target rate; depreciation is a market fall in a floating rate.
8. 30 ÷ 0.4 = 75 million.
9. 1.5%.
10. Buy the currency with reserves; raise interest rates.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up devaluation with depreciation, or revaluation with appreciation.
- Claiming a depreciation automatically fixes a deficit, without elasticities or timing.
- Quoting the Marshall-Lerner condition as a single elasticity rather than the sum.
- Saying exports "rise" without saying they are cheaper in foreign currency.
- Reading the direction of a rate quote the wrong way round.
- Treating the percentage fall of one currency as the percentage rise of the other.
- Stating a chain of reasoning in one sentence with no link between steps.
- Ending an evaluation with "it depends" and no reason.
