These questions cover section 3.3.1 Progressive and stationary waves (3.3.1.1 to 3.3.1.3) of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408), including Required practical 1. Section A has short questions; Section B has structured questions. Learn the content first with the [progressive and stationary waves study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-progressive-and-stationary-waves/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-progressive-and-stationary-waves-revision-notes/). Take g = 9.81 m s⁻² and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by:

**(a)** the wavelength of a progressive wave **[1]**
**(b)** the frequency of a wave. **[1]**

**2.** A sound wave of frequency 680 Hz travels through air at 340 m s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate its wavelength and period. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the phase difference, in radians, between two points on the wave 0.125 m apart. **[2]**

**3.** Describe the difference between a transverse wave and a longitudinal wave, giving one example of each. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why the polarisation of light is evidence that light is a transverse wave. **[2]**

**5.** Unpolarised light passes through two Polaroid filters. The second filter is rotated through 360°. Describe what an observer sees. **[2]**

**6.** State two differences between a stationary wave and a progressive wave. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A string of length 0.75 m and mass per unit length 2.4 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹ is held under a tension of 60 N.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of the first harmonic. **[3]**
**(b)** State the frequency of the second harmonic and the number of nodes, including the ends. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the tension needed for a first-harmonic frequency of 150 Hz. **[2]**

**8.** A microwave transmitter faces a metal plate. A probe moved along the line between them passes through 8 successive minima over a distance of 4.9 cm.

**(a)** Calculate the wavelength and frequency of the microwaves. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the minima do not fall to zero signal. **[1]**

**9.** Two points on a progressive wave oscillate with period 20 ms. One reaches its maximum displacement 4.0 ms after the other. Calculate their phase difference in radians. **[2]**

**10.** In Required practical 1, a student keeps the vibrating length at 0.60 m and changes the tension.

| T / N | 2.0 | 4.0 | 6.0 | 8.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| f₁ / Hz | 37.3 | 52.7 | 64.5 | 74.5 |

**(a)** Describe how the student sets the tension and identifies the first harmonic. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain which graph the student should plot to test whether f₁ ∝ √T. **[1]**
**(c)** Use the data to find the mass per unit length of the string. **[3]**

**11.** A loudspeaker emitting sound of frequency 850 Hz faces a wall. A microphone finds that adjacent quiet points are 0.20 m apart. Calculate the speed of sound. **[2]**

**12.** Explain how a stationary wave forms on a string fixed at both ends, and why there is a node at each end. **[3]**

**13.** A stationary wave on a string has wavelength 0.80 m. Point A is 0.10 m from a node. Point B is 0.10 m beyond the next node. State and explain the phase relationship between A and B, and compare their amplitudes. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The distance between two adjacent points oscillating in phase (such as two adjacent crests) [1].
**(b)** The number of complete oscillations passing a point per second [1].

**2. (a)** λ = c/f = 340/680 = **0.50 m** [1]; T = 1/f = **1.47 × 10⁻³ s** [1].
**(b)** x/λ = 0.125/0.50 = 0.25 [1]; Δφ = 0.25 × 2π = **π/2 rad** [1].

**3.** In a transverse wave the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer [1]; in a longitudinal wave they are parallel to it [1]. Examples: light (transverse) and sound (longitudinal) [1].

**4.** A polarised wave oscillates in only one plane [1]. Only transverse waves have oscillations perpendicular to their direction of travel that can be restricted in this way, so light must be transverse [1].

**5.** The brightness varies between a maximum and zero [1]: maximum when the filters' axes are parallel, zero when they are at 90°, so it falls to zero twice in a full rotation [1].

**6.** Any two of these, one mark each: a stationary wave does not transfer energy; its amplitude varies with position, from zero at nodes to maximum at antinodes; all points between adjacent nodes are in phase [1] [1].

**7. (a)** √(T/μ) = √(60 / 2.4 × 10⁻³) = 158 m s⁻¹ [1]; f₁ = 158 / (2 × 0.75) [1] = **105 Hz** [1].
**(b)** **211 Hz** [1]; **3** nodes [1].
**(c)** f ∝ √T, so T = 60 × (150/105.4)² [1] = **122 N** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Ratios avoid recalculating everything. Doubling the frequency would need four times the tension.

**8. (a)** 8 minima span 7 node spacings: 7 × λ/2 = 4.9 cm [1], so λ = **1.4 cm** [1]; f = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 0.014 = **2.1 × 10¹⁰ Hz** [1].
**(b)** The reflected wave has a smaller amplitude than the incident wave, so they do not cancel completely [1].
*Examiner insight:* Counting minima rather than spacings gives 8 × λ/2 and a wrong wavelength.

**9.** Δφ = 2π × (4.0/20) [1] = **0.40π rad = 1.26 rad** [1].

**10. (a)** The string runs over a pulley to a mass hanger; T = mg [1]. The vibration generator is driven by a signal generator whose frequency is adjusted [1] until the string shows a single loop with maximum amplitude at the centre [1].
**(b)** f₁ against √T, which should be a straight line through the origin if f₁ ∝ √T [1].
**(c)** Gradient = (74.5 − 37.3) / (2.83 − 1.41) = 26.3 Hz per √N [1]. Gradient = 1/(2l√μ), so √μ = 1/(2 × 0.60 × 26.3) [1]; μ = **1.0 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹** [1].

**11.** Quiet points are nodes λ/2 apart, so λ = 0.40 m [1]; c = fλ = 850 × 0.40 = **340 m s⁻¹** [1].

**12.** A wave travelling along the string reflects at the fixed end, so two waves of the same frequency travel in opposite directions [1]. They superpose, producing points of permanent cancellation (nodes) and maximum amplitude (antinodes) [1]. The ends cannot move, so they must be nodes [1].

**13.** A and B are on opposite sides of a node (nodes are 0.40 m apart, so A is in one loop and B in the next) [1], so they are in antiphase, a phase difference of π rad [1]. Both are 0.10 m from a node, so they have the same amplitude [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using node spacing as the full wavelength.
- Counting minima instead of spacings.
- Taking the hanging mass as the tension without multiplying by g.
- Stating that doubling tension doubles frequency.
- Claiming stationary waves transfer energy.
- Mixing centimetres and metres in phase difference or c = fλ.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you find a wavelength from a stationary wave?**
Measure the distance between adjacent nodes, or across several and divide, then double it, because adjacent nodes are half a wavelength apart.

**Why plot f against √T rather than f against T?**
The first-harmonic frequency is proportional to the square root of tension, so f against √T gives a straight line through the origin, which is easy to test and whose gradient gives the mass per unit length.
