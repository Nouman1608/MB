These questions practise the "Research methods and methodology" section of Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, where candidates "must explain their choice and use of appropriate research methods, and consider the methodology which has informed this". For the component as a whole, see the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The Research Report is coursework, so these questions practise the method decisions behind it; they are not past-paper questions. All projects, countries and data are fictional. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [research methods study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-research-report-methods/) and the [research methods revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-research-report-methods-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Name **two** of the qualitative research methods the syllabus gives as examples. **[2]**

**2.** Classify each question as empirical, interpretive or normative. **[3]**

- **(i)** "Has the number of languages spoken in a region declined since 1950?"
- **(ii)** "How are climate activists portrayed in television news?"
- **(iii)** "Should endangered languages be taught compulsorily in schools?"

**3.** State **two** ethical conventions that apply when research involves interviewing people. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** A candidate's question is "How fairly do national newspapers represent refugees?"

**(a)** Suggest an appropriate main method and justify it. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the methodology part of the methods section should say. **[2]**

**5.** A candidate writes: "For my literature review I read lots of articles online." Rewrite this as a stated search strategy. **[3]**

**6.** A survey asks: "Don't you agree that plastic bag bans are good for the planet?" Identify **two** problems with the question and rewrite it. **[3]**

**7.** A candidate compares youth unemployment in two fictional countries:

| Country | Youth unemployment | Age range | How measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arlen | 14% | 15-24 | National labour force survey |
| Besk | 22% | 16-29 | Registered benefit claimants |

She concludes: "Youth unemployment is much worse in Besk." Explain why this comparison is unsafe and what she should do. **[4]**

**8.** A candidate's national data show that a town's new library led to more book loans, but her interviews with eight residents suggest most of them use it mainly for internet access. Explain how she should treat this in her report. **[4]**

**9.** Read this extract from a methods section. Identify which of the five questions a methods section should answer are missing, and explain why each matters. **[4]**

> "I used a survey and interviews. The survey had 50 responses and I interviewed four people."

**10.** A candidate interviewed six shop owners in one street about a pedestrian zone. Her conclusion says: "Pedestrian zones damage small businesses." Rewrite the conclusion so that it matches the limits of her method. **[3]**

**11.** A candidate asks "Do biodegradable plastic bags break down faster than conventional ones in garden soil?" She plans an experiment. State what she should vary, what she should measure and **two** things she should hold constant, and give one limit of the experiment for answering a wider question about environmental benefit. **[5]**

**12.** "A Cambridge Research Report on a 'should' question needs no primary research." Evaluate this claim. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: ethnography [1]; interviews [1]; focus groups [1]; close analysis of texts is also accepted as a qualitative approach [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (i) empirical [1]; (ii) interpretive [1]; (iii) normative [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Any two: informed consent [1]; anonymity or confidentiality [1]; the right to withdraw [1]; avoiding harm or distress [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Close analysis of texts: a sample of newspaper articles [1], because the question asks how refugees are represented, which requires examining language, framing and images in the coverage itself [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** That questions about representation are normally studied in media and cultural studies through close reading of texts [1], and that this shows how coverage frames refugees but not how readers respond to it, so conclusions are about the texts, not their effects [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example: "I searched a university library database and two news archives using the terms 'X' and 'Y' [1], included studies and reports from 2010 onwards from at least three regions [1], and grouped them by the perspective they support [1]." **[3]**

**6.** It is a leading question, inviting agreement [1]; "good for the planet" is vague, so respondents may interpret it differently [1]. Rewrite, for example: "How far do you support or oppose a ban on single-use plastic bags in shops? (strongly support / support / neither / oppose / strongly oppose)" [1]. **[3]**

**7.** The two figures cover different age ranges [1] and are measured differently: a labour force survey counts people looking for work, whereas claimant counts include only those registered for benefits [1], so the difference may reflect definitions rather than real conditions [1]. She should find data using the same definition, such as figures from an international body that standardises measures, or limit her conclusion and explain the problem [1]. **[4]**

**8.** She should not discard either result [1]. The data show a rise in loans; the interviews suggest a different main use among the people she spoke to [1]. Because eight interviews cannot show how common that use is, she should treat the conflict as a finding: loans may have risen while internet access is the main reason some residents visit [1]. She could suggest data on computer use in the library to test this [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Any two, 2 marks each: how exactly the methods were carried out is missing, such as who was surveyed and how they were chosen [1], which matters because the reader cannot judge representativeness [1]; why the methods suit the question and subject is missing [1], so the methodology is not addressed as the syllabus requires [1]; limits and their effect on conclusions are missing [1], so the reader cannot tell how far the findings can be trusted [1]. **[4]**

**10.** For example: "The six shop owners I interviewed on one street [1] reported that the pedestrian zone reduced their trade, which suggests that some small businesses may be harmed [1]; data on sales across several zones would be needed to show whether this is typical [1]." **[3]**

**11.** Vary the type of bag (biodegradable or conventional) [1]. Measure the change in mass or visible breakdown over a set period [1]. Hold constant, any two: soil type, moisture, temperature, depth of burial, size of sample [1] [1]. Limit: faster breakdown in one soil does not show overall environmental benefit, which also depends on manufacture, other environments and what the bags break down into [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Supports the claim: normative questions turn on arguments about values, which a literature review can examine [1].
- Supports: secondary data on outcomes, such as results where a policy was tried, may be more extensive than anything a candidate could collect [1].
- Supports: the syllabus says research can be based on "primary and/or secondary evidence", so primary research is not required [1].
- Challenges: primary evidence can test whether published claims hold in a local context [1].
- Challenges: interviews can supply perspectives missing from the literature, such as those of people directly affected [1].
- Challenges: methodology depends on the subject area; some fields expect first-hand evidence [1].
- Judgement: the claim is broadly right that primary research is not required, but wrong if read as "never useful"; the choice should follow from the question and subject [1].
- The answer explains that whichever is chosen must be justified in the methods section [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing a method because it is familiar rather than because it fits the question type.
- Search strategies with no inclusion criteria.
- Rewriting a leading survey question with a different leading question.
- Comparing statistics without checking definitions and age ranges.
- Discarding a result that conflicts with another instead of discussing it.
- Conclusions that claim more than the sample and method can support.
