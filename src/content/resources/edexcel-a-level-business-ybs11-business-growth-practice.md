## Sources

> **Source 1: Sable Brewing plc**
>
> Sable is a large brewer. It plans a takeover of Tern Cider, a rival cider
> producer that sells to many of the same pubs. Tern has 8 million shares at
> a market price of $2.50 and made a profit for the year of $2.1m. Sable
> offers $3.00 per share. It will pay 25% of the price from cash and borrow
> the rest at 6% interest. The directors of Tern say they are opposed to the
> bid.
>
> **Source 2: Bellwether Catering**
>
> Bellwether provides meals for offices and schools. It has grown rapidly by
> opening new kitchens. Average cost per meal and annual sales are:
>
> | Year | Meals | Total cost ($) | Sales ($) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | 100,000 | 950,000 | 1,100,000 |
> | 2 | 220,000 | 1,804,000 | 3,000,000 |
> | 3 | 400,000 | 3,640,000 | 4,800,000 |
>
> Customers take 50 days to pay. Staff report that messages between the
> head office and kitchens are often late, and some kitchens have
> repeated orders for ingredients.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "hostile takeover". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the premium Sable is offering and the total cost of the takeover. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate Sable's annual interest cost on the loan. **[4]**

**4.** Explain **one** type of integration this takeover represents. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why Sable wants to take over Tern. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse the financial risks to Sable of this takeover. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "diseconomies of scale". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate Bellwether's average cost per meal in each year. **[4]**

**9.** Calculate the trade receivables Bellwether has in Year 2 and Year 3 and the increase. Use sales and 50 days of credit. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse why Bellwether might be at risk of overtrading. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss how Bellwether could reduce the problems of internal communication. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether Bellwether should continue to grow organically or look for a takeover. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A takeover in which the board of the target firm opposes the bid [1] and the bidder offers directly to the target's shareholders to buy a controlling share [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Premium = (3.00 / 2.50 - 1) x 100 [1] = **20%** [1]. Cost = 8 million x $3.00 [1] = **$24m** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Cash paid = 25% x $24m = $6m, so loan = $18m [1]. Interest = 6% x 18m [1] = **$1.08m** a year [2]. (Allow 2 marks for the correct answer with units.) **[4]**

**4.** The takeover is **horizontal integration** [1] because Sable and Tern are in the same industry and at the same stage of production, both supplying drinks to pubs [1]. It increases Sable's market share and reduces the number of competitors [1], which gives it more bargaining power with pubs and may allow it to raise prices or gain economies of scale [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Sable could remove a rival [1], which gives it a larger market share in supplying pubs [1]. With fewer competitors, Sable may have more power to set prices [1] and could gain a new product (cider) to sell to the same customers using its existing distribution network [1]. *(Any one reason with development.)* **[4]**

**6.** Sable must borrow $18m, so it pays $1.08m a year in interest [1], which is greater than half of Tern's current $2.1m profit [1]. If Tern's profit falls after the takeover, for example because staff leave in a hostile bid, Sable may not earn enough to cover the interest [1]. A 20% premium means Sable is paying more than the market value of Tern ($20m), so it must gain synergies to justify the extra $4m [1]. If the expected savings do not appear, Sable's profit and share price could fall [1], and with high debt its ability to invest in other projects is reduced [1]. **[6]**

**7.** A rise in average cost per unit [1] when a business grows beyond its most efficient size [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Year 1 = 950,000 / 100,000 = **$9.50** [1]. Year 2 = 1,804,000 / 220,000 = **$8.20** [1]. Year 3 = 3,640,000 / 400,000 = **$9.10** [1]. All three correct with units [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Year 2 receivables = 3,000,000 x 50/365 = **$410,959** [1]. Year 3 receivables = 4,800,000 x 50/365 = **$657,534** [1]. Increase = 657,534 - 410,959 [1] = **$246,575** [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Bellwether's sales grew 60% between Year 2 and Year 3 (3.0m to 4.8m) [1], and it has to pay for staff, food and new kitchens before customers pay [1]. Customers take 50 days to pay, so receivables rise by $246,575 [1], which must be financed from overdrafts or loans [1]. If cash runs short, it cannot pay suppliers, who may stop deliveries [1], so even though sales are rising, Bellwether could fail, which is the danger of overtrading [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **Actions:** Bellwether could set up an intranet or regular video briefings so that the head office and kitchens share the same information [1]; appoint kitchen managers with authority to place orders within a budget, reducing the need for messages [1]; set clear reporting lines and a single ordering system, so that orders are not repeated [1].
**Limits:** new systems cost money and training time [1]; delegation could lead to inconsistent standards [1]; communication problems may continue if staff are not motivated to use the system [1].
**Judgement:** a single ordering system is likely to give the quickest benefit, because it deals directly with the repeated orders in the source [1]. Over time, delegation and a flatter structure help most as the business keeps growing, but only if managers are trained [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **Organic growth:** Bellwether's average cost fell from $9.50 to $8.20 between Years 1 and 2, showing that growth can bring economies of scale [1]; organic growth is cheaper and keeps control [1]; it lets Bellwether solve its communication problems before getting bigger [1]. But the average cost then rose to $9.10 in Year 3, suggesting diseconomies of scale [1], and fast growth is already straining cash through overtrading [1].
**Takeover:** buying another caterer would add customers and kitchens at once [1] and might bring experienced managers [1]; but it would need large funding at a time when cash is under pressure [1]; it would add another culture and system to a business already struggling with communication [1].
**Judgement:** Bellwether should continue to grow organically, but more slowly, because its communication problems, rising unit costs and cash strain show that it is already growing too quickly to manage [1]. A takeover could make each of these problems worse [1]. The choice would change if Bellwether could finance a deal safely and the target had strong systems and cash [1]. It should first fix communication, shorten the 50-day credit terms and bring costs per meal back towards $8.20 [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the old market price instead of the offer price for the cost of a takeover.
- Forgetting to subtract the cash paid before calculating the interest on the loan.
- Calculating receivables with cost rather than sales.
- Naming diseconomies of scale without a cause from the source.
- Choosing between organic and inorganic growth without using the figures.
