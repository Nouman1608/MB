Section 3.2.3.3 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification has one content point, "Supply-side policies". The additional guidance names the policies you must know: "investment in education and training, lower direct taxes, lower taxes on business profits, trade union reform and privatisation/de-regulation". It also asks for "the advantages and disadvantages of supply-side policies" and "how supply-side policies can be used to help achieve government objectives". This guide takes each named policy in turn, shows the chain by which it raises productive capacity, and gives a drawback specific to that policy, which is what lifts an evaluation above a generic list.

The section sits in 3.2 How the economy works, assessed on Paper 2, with no tiers. Figures are invented. ## The idea: shifting the economy's limits outwards

Every economy has a maximum output it can produce with its resources, shown by the production possibility curve you met in the economic foundations topic. Fiscal and monetary policy change how much of that capacity is **used**, by changing spending. Supply-side policies try to move the limit itself **outwards**, by improving:

- the **quantity** of labour (more people willing to work, or to work longer);
- the **quality** of labour (skills and productivity);
- the amount and quality of **capital** (investment in machinery and technology);
- the **efficiency of markets** (competition and flexibility).

When capacity grows, real GDP can rise without excess demand pushing up prices. That is why supply-side policies can help several objectives at once.

## The named policies, one by one

### Investment in education and training

**Chain:** better schooling, apprenticeships and retraining → workers gain skills → output per worker rises, and workers can move into growing industries → higher productive capacity and less structural unemployment.

**Worked example (invented).** A call centre's 42 staff handle 1,260 calls a day, or 1,260 ÷ 42 = **30 calls per worker**. After a training course they handle 1,386 calls, or **33 per worker**: productivity has risen by 3 ÷ 30 × 100 = **10%**.

**Specific drawback:** the benefits take years to arrive, the government pays the cost now, and courses only help if they teach skills employers actually need.

### Lower direct taxes

**Chain:** lower income tax → workers keep more of each extra pound earned → stronger incentive to take a job, work extra hours or seek promotion → larger and more willing labour force.

**Worked example (invented).** A worker is offered 5 extra hours at £14 an hour, £70 before tax. If 40% of extra earnings goes in tax and deductions, they keep £42. If that falls to 30%, they keep **£49**, which makes the extra hours more worthwhile.

**Specific drawback:** some people may not respond, or may even work fewer hours because they can reach the income they want sooner. Tax revenue falls, and the largest cash gains go to the highest earners, which can widen inequality.

### Lower taxes on business profits

**Chain:** a lower corporation tax rate → firms keep more of their profit → more money to reinvest in machinery, research and training, and the country becomes more attractive to foreign firms deciding where to locate → more capital and higher productivity.

**Worked example (invented).** A firm makes £2 million profit. At a 25% tax rate it keeps £1.5 million; at 19% it keeps **£1.62 million**, £120,000 more that could fund investment.

**Specific drawback:** firms may pay the extra profit to shareholders instead of investing it. Lower revenue may mean cuts to public services, and other countries may cut their rates too, cancelling the advantage.

### Trade union reform

**Chain:** laws that limit union powers, for example requiring secret ballots before strikes → fewer days lost to strikes and more flexible pay and working practices → firms can adjust to changes in demand more easily and costs are lower → more output and investment.

**Specific drawback:** workers lose bargaining power, so wages and conditions may worsen. This can increase inequality and reduce motivation, and so productivity.

### Privatisation and deregulation

**Privatisation** sells state-owned firms to private owners. **Chain:** private owners seek profit → pressure to cut costs, improve services and innovate; access to private investment funds → greater efficiency.

**Deregulation** removes rules that restrict competition. **Chain:** fewer barriers to entry → new firms enter → more competition lowers prices and raises quality.

**Specific drawbacks:** a privatised firm with no competitors may become a private monopoly that raises prices, so it needs a regulator. Job losses are common as firms cut costs. Removing rules can also weaken protection for consumers, workers or the environment.

## How supply-side policies help the objectives

| Objective | Most relevant policies | Link |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | All of them | More productive capacity raises real GDP in the long run |
| Full employment | Education and training; lower direct taxes | Training tackles structural unemployment; lower taxes increase the incentive to work |
| Price stability | Training, competition, lower business taxes | Higher productivity lowers costs, easing cost-push inflation; capacity keeps up with demand |
| Balance of payments | Training, lower business taxes, deregulation | Lower costs and better quality make UK goods more competitive abroad |

## Advantages and disadvantages: how to weigh them

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Can help growth, jobs, inflation and the balance of payments together | Long time lags, often years |
| Raise living standards permanently rather than for one year | Costly to the government or reduce tax revenue |
| Make firms more competitive internationally | Results uncertain: people and firms may not respond |
| Avoid the inflation risk of boosting demand | Some policies widen inequality or reduce job security |

Use four questions to evaluate a specific policy: **How long** will it take? **How much** will it cost? **How certain** is the response? **Who gains and who loses?**

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain how privatisation could improve the efficiency of an industry. **[3]**

*Model answer:* When a state-owned firm is sold to private owners, its managers must earn a profit for shareholders [identifies the change in incentive]. This gives them a reason to cut waste, control costs and improve services to win customers [develops the chain]. If the industry is also opened to competition, rival firms add further pressure, so output is produced at lower cost per unit, which is greater efficiency [links to the key term].

## Common mistakes

- Treating a cut in interest rates or extra spending to boost demand as a supply-side policy.
- Describing a policy without saying how it raises productive capacity.
- Offering supply-side policy as a quick fix for a recession.
- Confusing privatisation (a change in ownership) with deregulation (a change in rules).
- Giving generic disadvantages ("it costs money") for every policy instead of the drawback specific to that policy.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: name the policy, the factor of production or market it improves, and the effect on capacity or the named objective.
- **Evaluate** questions: compare at least two policies, use the four questions above, and decide which suits the problem in the case. A structural skills shortage points to training; weak investment points to lower business taxes.
