This guide covers **Component 5: Speaking**, the optional component of Cambridge IGCSE Urdu as a Second
Language (0539). It is distinct from the site's existing guides to
[Paper 1: Reading and Writing](/resources/igcse-cambridge-urdu-paper-1-reading-writing/) and
[Paper 2: Listening](/resources/igcse-cambridge-urdu-paper-2-listening/), both compulsory written and
audio components -- Speaking is assessed separately, conducted and marked in Urdu by the candidate's own
teacher, and results in its own separate endorsement rather than contributing to the overall IGCSE grade.

## Where this fits in 0539

Because Speaking is optional and separately endorsed rather than graded alongside Paper 1 and Paper 2,
not every candidate takes it -- a centre chooses whether to offer it, and a candidate's overall IGCSE grade
is unaffected either way. For candidates who do take it, however, it is the only component that assesses
spontaneous spoken production rather than reading, writing, or listening comprehension.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE URDU AS A SECOND LANGUAGE (0539) -- COMPONENT 5: SPEAKING**

- No written question paper -- the test is conducted and assessed entirely in Urdu, in person, by a
  teacher/examiner at the candidate's own centre
- Worth 60 marks in total, internally marked by the centre and externally moderated by Cambridge
  International
- Part 1, Presentation (2-3 minutes): the candidate selects a topic in advance, related to Urdu-speaking
  society or culture, and gives a prepared outline on the day of the exam, optionally supported by a small
  card of prompt headings and 3-4 illustrative materials such as maps, statistics or photographs
- Part 2, Discussion (3-4 minutes): the examiner discusses the candidate's presentation with them,
  requiring the candidate to explain and justify opinions with natural, unscripted responses
- Part 3, General Conversation (3-4 minutes, worth 20 marks): a conversation covering at least two topics
  unknown to the candidate in advance, drawn from the syllabus's general topic areas
- Separately endorsed with a grade from 1 (high) to 5 (low), rather than contributing marks to the overall
  IGCSE grade for Urdu as a Second Language
- Speaking tests take place before the main written examination series, with a recorded sample of
  performances sent to Cambridge International for external moderation

## How to approach it

Because Part 1's topic is chosen by the candidate in advance, it rewards genuine preparation -- selecting a
topic with enough depth to sustain 2-3 minutes of spoken Urdu and to support a further 3-4 minutes of
follow-up discussion in Part 2. Part 3's general conversation, by contrast, cannot be prepared for with a
memorised script, since the candidate does not know which topics will come up -- broad, regular spoken
practice across a range of everyday subjects is the only reliable preparation.

## Choosing a strong Part 1 presentation topic

A good presentation topic connects a personal interest to something genuinely related to Urdu-speaking
society or culture -- for example, a favourite regional cuisine, a family tradition, a well-known festival,
or a piece of Urdu literature or poetry -- rather than a generic topic with only a loose connection to Urdu
culture specifically. Choosing a topic the candidate can speak about confidently and in enough depth to
sustain both the initial 2-3 minute outline and the follow-up discussion in Part 2 matters more than
choosing a topic that sounds impressive but is difficult to discuss fluently under exam conditions.

## Part 2 discussion: moving beyond the prepared outline

Part 2 specifically tests the ability to respond naturally, not to recite further prepared material --
examiners are trained to ask genuine follow-up questions that probe a candidate's opinions and reasoning,
so a candidate who has only memorised their Part 1 presentation without preparing to discuss and defend
its content in unscripted Urdu will struggle here. Practising answering "why" and "what do you think"
style questions about a chosen topic, out loud and without a script, is the most direct preparation for
this part.

## Worked example: preparing for the unpredictable general conversation

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official past
paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - identify the syllabus's general topic areas covered by
Part 3 (confirm the exact list with a teacher, since it can be
updated between syllabus versions).

Step 2 - for each general topic area, prepare a small bank of
useful vocabulary and 2-3 opinion phrases in Urdu, rather than
full scripted answers.

Step 3 - practise being asked questions on a topic without
advance warning, ideally with a teacher or fluent speaker playing
the role of examiner.

Step 4 - focus practice on natural, spontaneous responses rather
than word-perfect recitation, since Part 3 specifically rewards
the ability to sustain unscripted conversation.
```

## Common mistakes

Preparing a Part 1 presentation so tightly scripted that the candidate struggles to respond naturally when
the examiner asks unexpected follow-up questions in Part 2. Choosing a Part 1 topic too broad or too
narrow to sustain the required speaking time. Assuming Part 3's general conversation can be memorised in
advance, when its central purpose is to test genuinely spontaneous spoken Urdu. Forgetting that Speaking is
separately endorsed and does not affect the overall Paper 1/Paper 2 IGCSE grade, which can lead candidates
to under-prepare for it relative to its own separate outcome.

## Quick revision checklist

- Choose a Part 1 topic with genuine depth and a clear connection to Urdu-speaking society or culture.
- Practise discussing and justifying opinions on the chosen topic out loud, not just reciting the prepared
  outline.
- Build a broad vocabulary bank across the syllabus's general conversation topic areas for Part 3, rather
  than scripting full answers.
- Rehearse responding to unexpected follow-up questions with a teacher or fluent speaker before the exam.
- Confirm with a teacher whether the centre offers Component 5, since it is optional and separately
  endorsed rather than compulsory.
