This guide covers **Rights and Justice**, one of three compulsory
thematic studies for IB Diploma Programme Global Politics, first
assessment 2026. All three thematic studies are compulsory at both SL
and HL, as confirmed by the [full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-syllabus-guide/), unlike the
teacher-selected options found in some other DP individuals and
societies subjects.

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Rights and Justice sits alongside [Peace and
Conflict](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-peace-conflict/) and
Development and Sustainability as one of the three compulsory
thematic studies. Because all three are equally examinable in Paper
2's extended-response questions, Rights and Justice should not be
deprioritised relative to the other two. The HL Global political
challenges extension (Paper 3) is separate: it is built on cases each
HL student researches independently, connected to eight topic areas
(borders, environment, equality, health, identity, poverty, security and technology).

## Applying the four core concepts

Rights and Justice is not revised as a stand-alone block of content --
it is examined through the same four core concepts that structure the
whole course, introduced in "Understanding power and global politics":
**power** (which actors hold the authority to define or enforce a
right), **sovereignty** (a state's claimed authority to determine
rights within its own borders, and the tension this creates with
universal rights claims), **legitimacy** (whether a rights claim, or
its denial, is widely accepted as valid by other states and
institutions), and **interdependence** (how a rights issue in one
place creates pressure or obligations elsewhere, including through
international human rights law). A strong answer names which concept
it is applying at each stage, rather than describing a rights issue
and leaving the connection to the four concepts implicit.

## Worked example: applying the four concepts to a rights issue

Consider a question asking students to analyse a contemporary human
rights issue using the course's core concepts. A methodical answer
works through each concept in turn: which actors -- a state, an
international body, a civil society movement -- hold the power to
define or enforce the right in question; whether the state where the
issue occurs claims exclusive sovereign authority to handle it
internally, and how this interacts with international human rights
frameworks; whether the state's position, or the rights claim being
made against it, is widely accepted as legitimate by other states and
international institutions; and how the issue creates interdependence
-- pressure, obligations, or consequences for actors beyond the state
where it occurs. Structuring an answer around all four concepts
explicitly, rather than describing the rights issue in isolation, is
the assessed skill this thematic study specifically rewards, and the
same structure applies equally to Peace and Conflict or Development
and Sustainability.

## Building a case-study bank

Because the guide is explicitly built around "contemporary
real-world examples," keep a running log of rights and justice case
studies as the course progresses, each tagged to which of the four
core concepts it best illustrates. Current, live examples tend to be
more persuasive in an extended-response answer than well-known but
dated cases the whole cohort is also likely to be using, since
examiners see the same handful of textbook examples repeatedly.

Anchor the log to real, named frameworks and institutions rather than
generic descriptions: the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights
and the wider UN human rights system; international bodies such as the
International Court of Justice (ICJ), the UN Human Rights Council
(UNHRC), the UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) and the
International Criminal Court (ICC); regional human rights courts such
as the European Court of Human Rights, the Inter-American Court of
Human Rights and the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights; the
"Responsibility to protect" (R2P) norm; and civil society organisations
such as Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International. Naming the
specific treaty, institution or case a claim rests on, rather than
referring only to "an international body" or "a rights framework" in
general terms, is itself part of what Paper 2 rewards.

## Engagement project connections

Because the internally assessed engagement project rewards direct,
personal engagement with a political issue, Rights and Justice is a
common and productive choice of topic -- a student might engage with a
local rights campaign, a legal case, or an advocacy organisation's
work. Where the engagement project draws on Rights and Justice, use
the same four core concepts to structure the written report's
analysis, since consistency between taught content and the project's
own framing strengthens the analytical depth examiners look for.

## Comparing across the three thematic studies

Because Paper 2 may combine or contrast content from more than one
thematic study in a single extended-response question, practise
explicitly comparing a Rights and Justice example with one from Peace
and Conflict or Development and Sustainability -- for instance,
discussing how a conflict situation also raises rights violations
affecting the same population, or how unequal development outcomes
themselves raise questions of distributive justice. This kind of
cross-theme fluency, using the same four core concepts throughout,
reflects the genuinely interconnected way real political issues rarely
respect the syllabus's own theme boundaries.

## Assessment structure

Rights and Justice, like the other two thematic studies, is examined
through Paper 2's extended-response format: candidates answer one
essay question from Section A (a set of thematic-study-specific
questions, one per thematic study) and one essay question from Section
B (a set of integrating questions requiring candidates to draw links
across all three thematic studies and the core topics). Because
the same two-section format applies across all three thematic studies, practising
this structure once with Rights and Justice content builds a technique
directly transferable to Peace and Conflict and Development and
Sustainability as well.

## How to approach it

Treat Rights and Justice with the same priority as the other two
compulsory thematic studies, since Paper 2 can draw on any of them.
Practise structuring answers explicitly around power, sovereignty,
legitimacy and interdependence rather than describing a rights issue
in general terms, and build a current, named case-study bank rather
than relying on generic or dated examples. At HL, note where a rights
case you know connects to one of the eight HL topic areas (equality or
identity, for example), as a possible starting point for the
independent case research the Global political challenges extension
requires.
