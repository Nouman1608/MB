## Why materials cycle

Energy flows through an ecosystem and is lost as heat, but the **materials** that organisms are made of are limited. Atoms of carbon and nitrogen are used again and again. The cycles show the routes that these atoms take between the air, the soil, plants, animals and decomposers. Without recycling, the nutrients would be locked up in dead material and living things would run out.

## 4.10 The carbon cycle

Carbon is found in carbon dioxide in the air (about 0.04% of the air), in the organic compounds of living things (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids), in dead material and in fossil fuels.

### The four stages the specification names

| Stage | What happens | Effect on atmospheric carbon dioxide |
|---|---|---|
| **Photosynthesis** | Plants and algae take in carbon dioxide and convert the carbon into glucose and then other organic compounds | Removes it |
| **Respiration** | All living organisms, including plants, release carbon dioxide as they release energy from glucose | Adds it |
| **Decomposition** | Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) feed on dead organisms and waste, and respire, releasing carbon dioxide | Adds it |
| **Combustion** | Burning of fossil fuels and wood releases the carbon in them as carbon dioxide | Adds it |

### The route of a carbon atom

1. A carbon atom in carbon dioxide in the air is taken in by a leaf and built into glucose by **photosynthesis**.
2. It becomes part of the plant's tissues, such as cellulose and starch.
3. When a herbivore **eats** the plant, the carbon passes into the animal's body. It continues along the food chain in the same way.
4. At each level some carbon returns to the air as carbon dioxide by **respiration**.
5. When plants and animals die, or produce waste, **decomposers** feed on them. Their respiration returns carbon dioxide to the air.
6. Carbon in fossil fuels (formed over millions of years from the remains of dead organisms) is returned to the air by **combustion**.

### Key idea: balance

Over a long time, photosynthesis removes about as much carbon dioxide as respiration, decomposition and combustion add, so the percentage in the air stays fairly constant. Burning fossil fuels at a high rate adds carbon dioxide faster than it is removed. This links to the greenhouse effect in sub-topic 4(d).

### Worked example 1: describing the carbon cycle

*Describe how carbon in the carbon dioxide of the air becomes part of a fox, and how it returns to the air.*

1. Carbon dioxide diffuses into a plant leaf and is used in **photosynthesis** to make glucose. [Carbon enters the producer.]
2. The glucose is converted into other carbon compounds in the plant.
3. A rabbit **eats** the plant. [Carbon passes to a primary consumer.]
4. A fox **eats** the rabbit. [Carbon passes to a secondary consumer.]
5. The fox **respires**, releasing carbon dioxide into the air.
6. When the fox dies, **decomposers** break down its body and release carbon dioxide by respiration.

A good answer names each process in bold terms and states where the carbon is at each step (carbon dioxide, glucose, then proteins and fats).

### Worked example 2: a carbon budget

*In a year, the plants in a woodland take in 900 units of carbon by photosynthesis. The plants release 380 units by respiration. Calculate the net carbon taken in by the plants, and say what happens to it.*

```
net uptake = 900 − 380 = 520 units
520 ÷ 900 × 100 = 57.8% of the carbon taken in remains in the plant
```

The remaining 520 units form new plant tissue (growth). Some of that is passed to animals and decomposers when leaves fall or are eaten. If the woodland is growing, this store increases. If the net uptake is zero, the woodland is in balance. The calculation shows why growing forests are a **store** of carbon.

### Worked example 3: what happens if decomposers are removed?

*Explain what would happen to the carbon cycle if all decomposers died.*

1. Dead plants and animals and waste would not be broken down.
2. Carbon would stay locked in dead material and not return to the air as carbon dioxide.
3. The carbon dioxide in the air would be used up by photosynthesis and not replaced at the same rate, and nutrients would no longer be returned to the soil.
4. Eventually plants could not grow, so the ecosystem would collapse.

## 4.11B The nitrogen cycle (Paper 2 only)

Nitrogen gas (N₂) makes up about 78% of the air, but plants and animals **cannot use it directly**. Living things need nitrogen to make **amino acids** and then **proteins** (and DNA). Plants take nitrogen from the soil as **nitrate ions** through their roots. Animals get nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.

### The four groups of bacteria and decomposers

| Group | What it does | Starting material → product |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** | Convert nitrogen gas into nitrogen compounds that plants can use | Nitrogen gas → ammonia / ammonium compounds. Some live free in the soil. Some live in **root nodules** of legumes (such as peas and beans) |
| **Decomposers** | Break down dead organisms, waste and urea | Proteins and urea → ammonia / ammonium compounds |
| **Nitrifying bacteria** | Convert ammonium compounds into nitrates | Ammonium compounds → nitrite → nitrate (they need oxygen) |
| **Denitrifying bacteria** | Convert nitrates into nitrogen gas | Nitrate → nitrogen gas (in waterlogged soil with little oxygen) |

The specification says specific names of bacteria are **not required**. Use the group names above.

### The route of nitrogen through the cycle

1. **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** change nitrogen from the air into ammonium compounds. Legumes benefit from the bacteria in their root nodules, and the bacteria receive carbohydrate from the plant.
2. **Nitrifying bacteria** in the soil convert ammonium compounds into **nitrates**.
3. **Plants** absorb nitrates through their roots and use them, with carbohydrates, to make **amino acids and proteins**.
4. **Animals** eat plants (or other animals) and use the amino acids to make their own proteins.
5. **Decomposers** break down dead plants and animals and waste such as urea, and release ammonium compounds back to the soil.
6. **Denitrifying bacteria** convert nitrates into nitrogen gas, which returns to the air.

### Worked example 4: explaining crop rotation

*Farmers often grow peas in one year and wheat in the next. Explain how the pea crop helps the wheat.*

1. Pea plants have **nitrogen-fixing bacteria** in their root nodules.
2. The bacteria convert nitrogen gas into ammonium compounds, which nitrifying bacteria convert into nitrate.
3. When the pea plants die or are ploughed in, decomposers release more nitrogen compounds into the soil.
4. The wheat can take up more nitrate and make more protein, so it grows better without as much fertiliser.

### Worked example 5: waterlogged soil

*A field is flooded for several weeks. The farmer finds that the soil has lost nitrate. Explain why.*

1. Waterlogged soil contains little oxygen.
2. Conditions there favour **denitrifying bacteria**.
3. Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrates into nitrogen gas, which escapes into the air.
4. So the nitrate that plants need falls, and crop growth is poorer.

## Comparing the two cycles

| Feature | Carbon cycle | Nitrogen cycle |
|---|---|---|
| Gas in the air | Carbon dioxide (about 0.04%) | Nitrogen (about 78%) |
| Can plants use the gas directly? | Yes, by photosynthesis | No, only as nitrate from the soil |
| Role of decomposers | Release carbon dioxide | Release ammonium compounds |
| Bacteria needed | Decomposers | Fixing, nitrifying, denitrifying |

## Common mistakes

- Saying plants take in nitrogen gas from the air.
- Saying plants do not respire. Plants respire all the time, and they also photosynthesise.
- Leaving out decomposers, or saying they "produce" energy.
- Mixing up nitrification (ammonium to nitrate) with denitrification (nitrate to nitrogen gas).
- Writing "bacteria" without naming the type of bacteria and what it does.
- Saying burning wood does not add carbon dioxide.
- Saying fossil fuels are "burnt by respiration". The process is combustion.
- Describing the cycles as a sequence of events in a fixed order. The cycles show linked pathways.

## Self-check

1. Name the process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
2. Name three processes that add carbon dioxide to the air.
3. (Paper 2 only) Name the bacteria that convert ammonium compounds into nitrates.
4. (Paper 2 only) Where do nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in a pea plant?
5. (Paper 2 only) Why can plants not use nitrogen gas directly?

**Answers:** (1) Photosynthesis. (2) Any three of: respiration, decomposition, combustion. (3) Nitrifying bacteria. (4) In the root nodules. (5) They cannot convert nitrogen gas into nitrates or amino acids. They absorb nitrate ions through their roots.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the carbon cycle include feeding?**
Yes. Carbon passes along food chains when organisms eat one another, and returns to the air by respiration and decomposition.

**Do I need the names of the bacteria in the nitrogen cycle?**
No. The specification says specific names are not required. You need the roles of the groups.

**Is the nitrogen cycle on Paper 1?**
No. Point 4.11B is bold with a B reference, so it is Paper 2 only.
