These questions test the section 3.1 row on the criminal courts and lay people: classification of offences, the appeal system, sentencing of adult offenders, magistrates and juries. Section A tests recall and short explanation; Section B asks you to apply the rules to short scenarios and to evaluate juries.

---

## Section A

**1.** Identify the three classes of criminal offence and the court or courts in which each is tried. **[3]**

**2.** State **two** purposes of sentencing that a court must have regard to when sentencing an adult offender. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the role of the legal adviser in a magistrates' court. **[2]**

**4.** State the eligibility requirements for jury service under the Juries Act 1974. **[3]**

**5.** Explain when a majority verdict may be accepted from a jury. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the difference between an appeal from the magistrates' court to the Crown Court and an appeal by way of case stated. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Darius is charged with theft of goods worth a modest sum from a shop. He intends to plead not guilty. Explain how the court in which he will be tried is decided. **[5]**

**8.** Lena is convicted of a s20 offence in the Crown Court. She believes the trial judge wrongly admitted evidence. Advise Lena on how she can appeal and what the appeal court must decide. **[5]**

**9.** Explain the role of lay magistrates in criminal cases, other than trying summary offences. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how a judge in the Crown Court decides the sentence for an adult offender who pleads guilty to an either-way offence. **[6]**

**11.** A juror in a long fraud trial searches online for the defendant's previous convictions and tells the other jurors what she found. Explain the legal consequences of her conduct and what this shows about the jury system. **[6]**

**12.** "Juries are the best way of deciding guilt in serious criminal cases." Evaluate this statement. **[12]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Summary offences are tried only in the magistrates' court [1]; either-way offences may be tried in the magistrates' court or the Crown Court [1]; indictable-only offences are tried only in the Crown Court [1].

**2.** Any two of: punishment of offenders; reduction of crime, including by deterrence; reform and rehabilitation of offenders; protection of the public; reparation by offenders to persons affected by their offences (s57 Sentencing Act 2020) [1] [1].

**3.** The legal adviser is a qualified lawyer who advises lay magistrates on law, practice and procedure [1], but must not take part in deciding guilt or sentence, which is for the magistrates alone [1].

**4.** Aged 18 to 75 [1]; registered on the electoral register [1]; ordinarily resident in the UK for at least five years since the age of 13, and not disqualified [1].

**5.** The judge first asks for a unanimous verdict [1]; after the jury has deliberated for at least two hours, a majority of 11–1 or 10–2 may be accepted (s17 Juries Act 1974) [1].

**6.** An appeal to the Crown Court is open only to the defendant [1] and is a full rehearing of the case on its facts, or an appeal against sentence [1]. An appeal by way of case stated is available to either the prosecution or the defence [1] and is on a point of law only, heard by the Divisional Court of the King's Bench Division [1].

**7.** Theft is triable either way [1]. Darius first appears in the magistrates' court, where he indicates his plea at plea before venue [1]. Because he indicates not guilty, there is an allocation hearing at which the magistrates decide whether the case is suitable for summary trial, taking into account the seriousness of the offence and whether their sentencing powers would be adequate [1]. A low-value theft is likely to be accepted for summary trial [1], but Darius may still elect trial by jury in the Crown Court [1].

**8.** Lena may appeal against conviction to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) [1], but she needs leave to appeal, from the trial judge or the Court of Appeal [1]. Wrongful admission of evidence is a possible ground [1]. The Court of Appeal will ask whether the conviction is unsafe under s2 Criminal Appeal Act 1968 [1]; if it is, it may quash the conviction or order a retrial, and if not it will dismiss the appeal [1].

**9.** Lay magistrates decide preliminary matters in all criminal cases, including bail or remand in custody [1] and applications for legal aid [1]. They hold allocation hearings for either-way offences and send indictable-only cases to the Crown Court [1]. They issue search and arrest warrants [1]. They sit with a judge in the Crown Court to hear appeals from the magistrates' court, and with extra training sit in the youth court [1].

**10.** The judge must follow the relevant Sentencing Council guideline unless that would be contrary to the interests of justice [1]. The judge assesses the offender's culpability and the harm caused to identify a starting point and category range [1]. The sentence is adjusted upwards for aggravating factors, such as relevant previous convictions [1], and downwards for mitigating factors, such as remorse or good character [1]. Credit is then given for the guilty plea, up to one third if it was entered at the first stage [1]. The judge has regard to the five purposes of sentencing in s57 Sentencing Act 2020 and checks the final sentence is proportionate [1].

**11.** Research into the case by a juror is a criminal offence under the Juries Act 1974, as amended by the Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015 [1], and disclosing it to other jurors spreads the prejudice [1]. The juror may be discharged and, if the jury is contaminated, the whole jury discharged and a retrial ordered [1]. Jurors have previously been jailed for contempt for internet misconduct, as in *Attorney General v Fraill* (2011) [1]. The episode shows that jurors may be influenced by material outside the evidence [1], and that because deliberations are secret (*R v Mirza*, 2004) such misconduct may go undetected unless another juror reports it [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **For:** juries allow public participation and give legitimacy to verdicts in serious cases [1]. Jury equity allows a jury to acquit where applying the law would be unjust, protected since *Bushell's Case* (1670) [1], as in *R v Ponting* (1985) [1]. Random selection should produce an impartial cross-section, and the judge must explain the law openly [1]. Research by Cheryl Thomas (2010) found no evidence that juries discriminated against ethnic minority defendants [1]. **Against:** jury equity can produce perverse verdicts, such as *R v Kronlid* (1996) [1]. Because deliberations are secret and no reasons are given, errors cannot be identified or corrected (*Mirza*) [1]. Jurors may be influenced by the media (*R v Taylor and Taylor*, 1993) [1] or by internet research (*Fraill*) [1]. Bias can occur in the jury room (*Sander v UK*, 2000), and some jurors misunderstand directions [1]. **Alternatives and conclusion:** Parliament already allows judge-alone trials where there is a danger of jury tampering (s44 Criminal Justice Act 2003; *R v Twomey*, 2009) [1]. A reasoned conclusion, for example that juries remain justified for serious cases but need safeguards against misconduct [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not identifying the class of offence before explaining the trial court.
- Forgetting that leave is needed for a Court of Appeal appeal against conviction.
- Using "unfair" instead of the statutory word "unsafe".
- Listing jury points without cases or research, or without a conclusion.
