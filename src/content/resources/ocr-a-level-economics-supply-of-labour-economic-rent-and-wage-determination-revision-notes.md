## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply of labour | Hours or people offered to employers at each possible wage |
| Wage elasticity of supply (EWS) | % change in labour supplied ÷ % change in wage |
| Monetary factors | Wage, overtime, bonuses, cash benefits, tax |
| Non-monetary factors | Satisfaction, hours, risk, location, status, security, training |
| Transfer earnings | Minimum payment to keep a worker in the job (next best alternative) |
| Economic rent | Earnings above transfer earnings |
| MRP | Extra revenue from the last worker hired |
| ACL / MCL | Average / marginal cost of labour |
| Monopsony | A single (or dominant) buyer of labour |
| Bilateral monopoly | Monopsony employer facing a single union |
| Wage differential | Gap in pay between jobs, groups or regions |

## Supply: factors and elasticity

- A wage change is a movement along the supply curve. Population, migration, participation, training costs, pay elsewhere and non-monetary conditions shift the curve.
- EWS is higher with short training times, high mobility, spare labour, long time periods and few entry barriers.
- Short run: the workforce is mostly fixed (overtime, poaching). Long run: training, migration and new entrants.

**Worked reminder.** Wage up from £9.00 to £9.90, supply from 500 to 515 workers.

```
Wage  +10%    Supply +3%
EWS = 3 / 10 = 0.3  (inelastic)
```

## Individual labour supply

| Effect | Wage rise causes | Hours |
|---|---|---|
| Substitution | Leisure dearer, work more attractive | Up |
| Income | Same income in fewer hours, buy more leisure | Down (if leisure is normal) |

**Diagram in words.** Leisure on the horizontal axis, income on the vertical. A wage rise pivots the budget line to a steeper slope. Substitution effect: a move along the original indifference curve to less leisure. Income effect: a shift to a higher indifference curve, with more leisure. Net effect = sum of the two.

- Low wages: substitution dominates, so the curve slopes upward.
- High wages or a target income: the income effect can dominate, giving a backward-bending section.
- Evaluate: fixed contract hours, taxes, overtime rules, family commitments, and the fact that the market curve still slopes upward as new workers enter.

## Economic rent and transfer earnings

**Method for a linear supply curve** w = a + bL at equilibrium (L, w):

```
Total earnings    = w x L
Economic rent     = 0.5 x (w - a) x L     (triangle above supply)
Transfer earnings = total - rent          (area under supply)
```

*Example.* Supply w = 3 + 0.01L and wage £13. Then L = 1,000. Total = 13,000, rent = 0.5 x 10 x 1,000 = 5,000, transfer earnings = 8,000. Check: 5,000 + 8,000 = 13,000.

*Individual.* A person earns £40,000 and could earn £28,000 elsewhere. Transfer earnings are £28,000 and rent is £12,000, which is 30% of earnings.

- More inelastic supply means a larger share of earnings is rent. Perfectly inelastic: all rent. Perfectly elastic: all transfer earnings.
- Stronger demand lifts the wage, and rent grows with it.
- Better alternative jobs raise transfer earnings.

## Competitive labour market

Assumptions: many employers and workers, homogeneous labour, perfect information, mobility, wage takers. The firm hires while MRP is at least the wage, so wage = MRP at the margin.

| Workers | MRP (£) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 24 |
| 2 | 20 |
| 3 | 16 |
| 4 | 12 |
| 5 | 8 |

At a wage of £14 the firm hires 3 workers (the 4th adds only £12).

**Market equilibrium.** MRP: w = 24 - 0.02L. Supply: w = 4 + 0.03L.

```
24 - 0.02L = 4 + 0.03L  ->  20 = 0.05L  ->  L = 400
w = 4 + 12 = £16   (check: 24 - 8 = 16)
```

Shifts: more demand (higher MRP) raises wage and employment; less supply (for example a new licence) raises the wage and cuts employment.

## Trade unions

Method in steps:

1. Read employment from the demand curve at the union wage.
2. Read the number willing to work from the supply curve.
3. Unemployment = supply minus demand.
4. Compare wage bill (wage x employment) before and after.
5. Use the midpoint method for elasticity if the change is big.

*Example, same market, union wage £19.*

```
Demand: (24 - 19)/0.02 = 250 employed
Supply: (19 - 4)/0.03  = 500 willing   -> 250 unemployed
Wage bill: 16 x 400 = 6,400  ->  19 x 250 = 4,750 (falls)
Midpoint: L -46.2%, w +17.1%  ->  elasticity = -2.69 (elastic)
```

When demand is elastic, the union's gain per worker comes at a larger loss of jobs. When inelastic, the wage bill rises. Evaluate: union density, strike threat, capital substitution, productivity gains and whether the union represents insiders only.

## Monopsony

- The monopsonist faces the supply curve (ACL). MCL is above ACL and steeper, because a higher wage must go to all workers.
- Hire where MCL = MRP, then pay the wage shown on the supply curve for that employment level.

*Example, same curves.* Total cost of labour = 4L + 0.03L^2, so MCL = 4 + 0.06L.

```
4 + 0.06L = 24 - 0.02L  ->  L = 250
Wage = 4 + 0.03 x 250 = £11.50
MRP  = 24 - 5 = £19.00   (MCL = 4 + 15 = 19)
Gap (MRP - wage) = £7.50
```

Against competition (400 at £16): employment is 37.5% lower and the wage 28.1% lower.

**Policy twist.** A binding wage floor at £16 makes the supply curve flat at £16 for the firm. Demand at £16 is (24 - 16)/0.02 = 400 workers and supply is also 400, so employment rises from 250 to 400. A floor above £19 cuts employment below 250.

## Bilateral monopoly

- Employer pushes to the monopsony outcome (here £11.50 and 250). The union pushes for a higher wage.
- Theory gives a **range**, not a single point. The result depends on bargaining power, strike and lock-out threats, information and the cost of failing to agree.
- A union may offset monopsony power and raise both wages and employment, but an over-aggressive union can cut jobs.

## Wage differentials

| Reason | Link |
|---|---|
| Compensating differentials | Risky or unsocial work pays more |
| Human capital and skills | Higher MRP and costly training |
| Scarcity, barriers to entry | Licences, rare talent, inelastic supply |
| Demand and productivity differences | Different MRP by sector |
| Unions | Bargaining premium |
| Monopsony, discrimination | Pay below MRP |
| Region, sector, information | Cost of living, public-sector scales, mobility |

*Example.* £22 an hour for dangerous night work versus £16.50 for daytime: a premium of (22 - 16.50) ÷ 16.50 = 33.3%.

## Usefulness of the models

- Competitive MRP: best for many employers and measurable output; weak for public services and team output.
- Monopsony: best where one big employer dominates a small labour market or a specialist occupation.
- Union and bilateral models: best where collective bargaining is strong.
- Regional differences come from mobility and living costs; occupational differences from skills, licensing and output measurement.
- Judgement: no single model fits every sector. State which one fits the case and why.

## Must-know distinctions

- Movement along supply versus shift of supply.
- Substitution effect versus income effect.
- Economic rent versus transfer earnings.
- ACL (the wage) versus MCL (what the extra worker costs the monopsonist).
- Wage elasticity of supply versus wage elasticity of demand.
- Short-run versus long-run supply.

## Quick self-test

1. Wage rises 8% and labour supplied rises 12%. Calculate EWS.
2. A person earns £55,000 and her next best job pays £44,000. State her economic rent.
3. Give two non-monetary factors that affect supply.
4. A firm with the MRP table above faces a wage of £14. How many workers does it hire?
5. In the competitive market, what are the wage and employment if supply becomes w = 8 + 0.03L?
6. In which direction does the income effect move hours after a wage rise?
7. In the monopsony example, what is the gap between MRP and the wage?
8. Why can a wage floor raise employment under monopsony?
9. Why does bilateral monopoly have no unique outcome?
10. Job A pays £18 and job B pays £24 because it is dangerous. Calculate the premium.

### Answers

1. 12 ÷ 8 = **1.5**.
2. 55,000 - 44,000 = **£11,000**.
3. Any two: job satisfaction, hours, risk, location, status, security, training.
4. MRP of 3rd worker is £16 (at least 14), 4th is £12 (below 14): **3 workers**.
5. 24 - 0.02L = 8 + 0.03L gives L = 320, w = 8 + 9.6 = **£17.60 and 320 workers** (check 24 - 6.4 = 17.60).
6. **Down** (more leisure, if leisure is normal).
7. 19 - 11.50 = **£7.50**.
8. The floor makes the marginal cost of labour equal to the floor wage up to the supply limit, so hiring more no longer raises the wage for everyone; employment rises until MRP equals the floor.
9. Two powers meet, so the wage lies in a range set by bargaining strength.
10. (24 - 18) ÷ 18 = **33.3%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "demand" or "supply shifts" when only the wage has changed.
- Treating all earnings as economic rent.
- Reading the monopsony wage off the MCL curve instead of the supply curve.
- Drawing the diagram but not naming the axes, curves and equilibrium points.
- Not following a union wage through to unemployment and the wage bill.
- Saying a wage floor always cuts jobs without separating competitive markets from monopsony.
- Describing the substitution and income effects without saying which dominates and why.
- One-sided answers to "evaluate" questions: no judgement on which model fits which sector.

## Frequently asked questions

**What shifts the supply curve of labour?**
A change in the wage causes a movement along the supply curve. The curve shifts with population, migration, participation, training costs, pay in other jobs and non-monetary conditions.

**What is a bilateral monopoly in the labour market?**
A bilateral monopoly has a single buyer of labour (a monopsonist) and a single seller (a union covering the whole workforce). Theory gives a range of possible wages rather than a single point, and the result depends on bargaining power, strike and lock-out threats, information and the cost of failing to agree.

**Can a minimum wage raise employment?**
In a monopsony labour market, a binding wage floor makes the supply curve flat for the firm at that wage, so it can raise both the wage and employment. A floor set too high cuts employment instead.

**How useful are wage determination models?**
The OCR A Level Economics (H460) notes show that no single model explains all pay. Competitive theory works best where output is measurable, while monopsony, unions and public-sector pay scales explain pay in other regions and occupations.
