## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gas exchange | Diffusion of oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out of it across the alveolar surface |
| Breathing (ventilation) | Muscle movements that move air into and out of the lungs |
| Respiration | Chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from glucose (not breathing) |
| Inspired air | Air breathed in |
| Expired air | Air breathed out |
| Rate of breathing | Number of breaths per minute |
| Depth of breathing | Volume of air taken in per breath |

## 9.1.4 and 9.1.6 Structures to identify

**Airway, in order:**

`larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli (with associated capillaries)`

- **Larynx**: voice box at the top of the trachea.
- **Trachea**: windpipe, with rings of cartilage.
- **Bronchi**: two branches, one to each lung.
- **Bronchioles**: many small branching tubes.
- **Alveoli**: air sacs in clusters, wrapped in **capillaries**.
- **Lungs**: two spongy organs in the thorax.

**Thorax wall and floor:**

- **Ribs**: bony cage around the lungs.
- **External intercostal muscles**: outer layer between the ribs.
- **Internal intercostal muscles**: inner layer, nearer the lungs.
- **Diaphragm**: dome-shaped muscular sheet below the lungs.

## 9.1.1 Four features of gas exchange surfaces

| Feature | Effect |
|---|---|
| Large surface area | More gas diffuses at once |
| Thin surface | Short diffusion distance |
| Good blood supply | Removes oxygen, brings carbon dioxide: keeps the gradient steep |
| Good air supply | Ventilation refreshes alveolar air: keeps the gradient steep |

The syllabus list stops at these four for gas exchange surfaces in general.

## 9.1.5 Alveoli: characteristics and role

**Characteristics:** very many alveoli (large total area); walls one cell thick; capillary walls one cell thick; moist lining; dense capillary network; well ventilated.

**Role, in steps:**

1. Oxygen dissolves in the moist lining.
2. Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air (high concentration) into the blood (low concentration).
3. Red blood cells carry the oxygen away.
4. Carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood (high concentration) into alveolar air (low concentration).
5. Carbon dioxide is breathed out.

## 9.1.3 Inspired versus expired air

| | Inspired | Expired | Reason for the change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | ~21% | ~16% | Diffuses into blood; used in aerobic respiration |
| Carbon dioxide | ~0.04% | ~4% | Made in respiration; diffuses from blood into alveoli |
| Nitrogen | ~78% | ~78% | Not used by the body |
| Water vapour | variable | saturated | Evaporates from moist alveolar lining |
| Temperature | surroundings | near body temperature | Warmed by the body |

**Method in steps (carbon dioxide):**

1. Set up two tubes with **equal volumes** of limewater or hydrogencarbonate indicator.
2. Draw inspired air through one tube; breathe expired air through the other.
3. Time how long each takes to change: limewater goes **milky**, hydrogencarbonate indicator goes from **red/orange to yellow**.
4. The expired-air tube changes much sooner: more carbon dioxide.

**Worked reminder.** Limewater went milky after 20 s with expired air and 300 s with inspired air. Rate = 1 ÷ time, so the expired-air rate is 300 ÷ 20 = **15 times** faster. Faster change means more carbon dioxide. Always compare times from tubes with equal volumes of the same solution.

**Water vapour:** breathe on a cold mirror; condensation forms. **Temperature:** compare a thermometer in breathed-out air with room temperature.

**Safety:** limewater is a moderate hazard; wear eye protection and never suck back. Use your own clean mouthpiece.

## 9.1.7 Breathing mechanism

| | Breathing in | Breathing out (at rest) | Forced breathing out |
|---|---|---|---|
| External intercostals | contract | relax | relax |
| Internal intercostals | relax | relax | contract |
| Ribs | up and out | down and in | pulled down and in further |
| Diaphragm | contracts, flattens | relaxes, domes | relaxes, domes |
| Thorax volume | increases | decreases | decreases further |
| Thorax pressure | falls below atmospheric | rises above atmospheric | rises further |
| Air flow | in | out | more air out |

**Method in steps (any breathing explanation):** muscle → ribs or diaphragm → **volume** → **pressure** → direction of air flow. Always pair volume with pressure, in that order.

**Model reminder.** In a bell-jar model the rubber sheet is the diaphragm, the jar is the thorax, the balloons are the lungs and the Y-tube is the trachea and bronchi. Pull the sheet down: volume inside rises, pressure falls, air enters and the balloons inflate. Push it up: the reverse. The model's rigid jar cannot show the ribs or intercostal muscles moving, so it only models the diaphragm.

## 9.1.8 Physical activity and breathing

**Measuring:** rate = breaths counted × (60 ÷ seconds counted). Depth by spirometer, or chest expansion with a tape measure.

**Fair test:** same person, same exercise, same duration; record at rest, straight after, then each minute until back to resting; repeat.

**Explanation chain:**

1. Muscles contract more → respire faster.
2. More carbon dioxide produced; more oxygen needed.
3. Blood carbon dioxide concentration rises → **detected by the brain**.
4. Brain increases **rate and depth** of breathing.
5. More oxygen in, more carbon dioxide out.

Small worked reminder: 11 breaths in 15 s → 11 × 4 = **44 breaths per minute**.

## 9.1.9 Protection

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| Goblet cells | Secrete mucus |
| Mucus | Sticky; traps pathogens and particles |
| Ciliated cells | Cilia beat, moving mucus up to the throat to be swallowed |

Result: pathogens and particles are kept away from the alveoli. If the cilia stop beating, mucus collects in the airways and is harder to clear, so pathogens and particles are more likely to reach the lungs.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Breathing vs respiration**: a physical process moving air vs chemical reactions in cells.
- **Gas exchange vs breathing**: diffusion across the alveoli vs movement of air by muscles.
- **External vs internal intercostals**: external raise the ribs (in); internal lower them in forced breathing out.
- **Rate vs depth**: breaths per minute vs volume per breath.
- **Mucus vs cilia**: mucus traps; cilia move.
- **Limewater vs hydrogencarbonate indicator**: limewater shows carbon dioxide only by turning milky; the indicator changes colour (red/orange to yellow) as carbon dioxide rises.

## Quick self-test

1. State the percentage of oxygen in atmospheric air.
2. Name the structure between the trachea and the bronchioles.
3. Give two characteristics of the alveoli that make diffusion fast.
4. Why does expired air contain more water vapour than inspired air?
5. What happens to the diaphragm when you breathe in?
6. Which muscles contract during forced breathing out?
7. Expired air contains about 4% carbon dioxide and inspired air about 0.04%. How many times more is that?
8. A student counts 12 breaths in 20 s. Calculate the breathing rate.
9. What does the brain detect that causes faster breathing during exercise?
10. A pie chart shows atmospheric air. What angle represents oxygen at 21%?
11. A red blood cell measures 10.5 mm on a photomicrograph at ×1500. Calculate its actual diameter in µm.
12. State the role of goblet cells.

### Answers

1. About **21%**.
2. **Bronchi** (one bronchus to each lung).
3. Any two: large total surface area; thin walls (one cell thick), so short diffusion distance; moist lining; dense capillary network; well ventilated.
4. Water **evaporates** from the moist lining of the alveoli into the air.
5. It **contracts** and **flattens** (moves down).
6. The **internal intercostal muscles**.
7. 4 ÷ 0.04 = **100 times**.
8. 12 × (60 ÷ 20) = **36 breaths per minute**.
9. A rise in the **carbon dioxide concentration** of the blood.
10. 21 ÷ 100 × 360 = **75.6°**.
11. 10.5 mm = 10 500 µm; 10 500 ÷ 1500 = **7 µm**.
12. They **secrete mucus**, which traps pathogens and particles.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing "thin" or "large surface area" with no effect on diffusion attached.
- Writing that the lungs pull or suck air in, instead of linking volume and pressure.
- Saying "pressure increases" when breathing in, by reversing the volume-pressure link.
- Saying the diaphragm moves up on breathing in.
- Giving the internal intercostals a role in quiet breathing in.
- Stating that inspired air contains no carbon dioxide.
- Explaining exercise breathing only as "more oxygen needed", missing carbon dioxide detected by the brain.
- Giving a breathing rate per 15 s or 30 s when the question asks for per minute.
- Writing that cilia trap dust, or that cilia "sweep mucus down" to the lungs.
- Confusing breathing with respiration in an explanation.
