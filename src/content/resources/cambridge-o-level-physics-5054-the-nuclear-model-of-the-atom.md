## 5.1.1 The structure of the atom

An atom has a tiny, dense, **positively charged nucleus** at its centre.
**Negatively charged electrons** orbit the nucleus. Almost all of the
atom's volume is the space through which the electrons move. A neutral
atom has **equal numbers of protons and electrons**, so the positive and
negative charges cancel.

| Particle | Where | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | Nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| Neutron | Nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| Electron | Orbiting the nucleus | −1 | Very small (about 1/2000) |

## The alpha-particle scattering experiment

The experiment was carried out by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden working
with Ernest Rutherford, who published the nuclear model in 1911. A narrow
beam of **alpha particles** (positively charged and fast-moving) was fired
at a **very thin gold foil** inside an evacuated chamber. A detector that
could be moved round the foil recorded how many alpha particles were
scattered through each angle.

Two details are worth knowing for "describe" questions:

- the chamber was **evacuated** because alpha particles are absorbed by a
  few centimetres of air
- the foil was **very thin** so that each alpha particle would pass close
  to at most one nucleus

**Observations and what each shows.** This mapping is the core of the
subtopic and is examined directly.

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| **Most** alpha particles passed **straight through** with little or no deflection | The atom is **mostly empty space**, and the nucleus is **very small** compared with the atom |
| **A small number** were **deflected through large angles** | The nucleus is **positively charged**: it repels the positive alpha particles that pass close to it |
| **A very few** **bounced back** towards the source | The nucleus contains **most of the mass** of the atom: only a massive, concentrated nucleus could turn a fast alpha particle back |

The rarity of the large deflections is itself evidence that the nucleus is
small: an alpha particle has to pass very close to the nucleus to be
deflected strongly, and few do.

**Model answer (3 marks): "Explain how the experiment shows the nucleus is
positively charged."** "The alpha particles are positively charged. Some
were deflected through large angles, which shows they were repelled by a
strong force. Like charges repel, so the nucleus must be positively
charged."

## 5.1.2 The nucleus

The nucleus is made of **protons and neutrons**, together called
**nucleons**.

- **Proton number (atomic number) Z** — the number of protons in the
  nucleus. It decides which element the atom is.
- **Nucleon number (mass number) A** — the total number of protons and
  neutrons in the nucleus.
- **Number of neutrons** N = A − Z.

### Nuclide notation

A **nuclide** is a particular type of nucleus, specified by its numbers of
protons and neutrons. It is written

```
 A
   X        A = nucleon number (top), Z = proton number (bottom),
 Z          X = chemical symbol
```

In text this appears as, for example, ²³₁₁Na.

**Worked example 1.** Find the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons
in a neutral atom of each nuclide.

| Nuclide | Protons (Z) | Neutrons (A − Z) | Electrons (neutral atom) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ²³₁₁Na | 11 | 23 − 11 = 12 | 11 |
| ⁵⁶₂₆Fe | 26 | 56 − 26 = 30 | 26 |
| ²³⁵₉₂U | 92 | 235 − 92 = 143 | 92 |
| ¹⁹⁷₇₉Au | 79 | 197 − 79 = 118 | 79 |

### Ions

An atom becomes an **ion** when it gains or loses electrons. The nucleus is
unchanged.

- **Losing** electrons → more protons than electrons → **positive ion**.
- **Gaining** electrons → more electrons than protons → **negative ion**.

**Worked example 2.** A sodium atom (Z = 11) loses one electron: it now has
11 protons and 10 electrons, so it is a positive ion with charge +1. A
chlorine atom (Z = 17) gains one electron: 17 protons and 18 electrons, a
negative ion with charge −1.

Ionisation never changes Z or A, so the element and nuclide stay the same.

### Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the **same element** — the same proton number —
with **different numbers of neutrons**, and so different nucleon numbers.
An element may have more than one isotope.

| Element | Isotopes | Protons | Neutrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | ¹₁H, ²₁H, ³₁H | 1 | 0, 1, 2 |
| Carbon | ¹²₆C, ¹³₆C, ¹⁴₆C | 6 | 6, 7, 8 |
| Chlorine | ³⁵₁₇Cl, ³⁷₁₇Cl | 17 | 18, 20 |

Isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons in their neutral
atoms, and so the same chemical behaviour, but different masses. Some
isotopes are unstable and radioactive — carbon-14 is one — which leads into
[subtopic 5.2 Radioactivity](/resources/cambridge-o-level-physics-5054-radioactivity/).

**Worked example 3 (combining ideas).** State the numbers of protons,
neutrons and electrons in the ion ³⁷₁₇Cl⁻.

```
protons   = Z = 17
neutrons  = 37 − 17 = 20
electrons = 17 + 1 = 18   (one gained, giving the − charge)
```

## Common mistakes

- Writing the nucleon number as the number of neutrons. A counts protons
  **and** neutrons.
- Swapping the top and bottom numbers in nuclide notation. The larger
  number (A) goes on top.
- Saying isotopes have different numbers of protons. They have the same
  proton number; only the neutron number differs.
- Saying a positive ion has gained protons. It has lost electrons; the
  nucleus does not change.
- Matching the wrong conclusion to an observation in the scattering
  experiment, especially linking "most went straight through" to the
  nucleus being positive.
- Saying alpha particles bounced off electrons. Electrons are far too light
  to deflect an alpha particle significantly.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe** the atom: say where each particle is and its charge.
- **Describe how the experiment provides evidence**: pair each observation
  with its conclusion, one sentence each, using "most", "a few" and "very
  few" precisely.
- **Define** Z and A: "the number of protons in the nucleus" and "the
  number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus".
- **Explain** an isotope: same proton number, different neutron number,
  same element.
- **Calculate** neutrons: show A − Z with the numbers substituted.
