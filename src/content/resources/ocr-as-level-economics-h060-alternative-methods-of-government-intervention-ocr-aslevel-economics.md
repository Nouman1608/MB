## The methods and what each targets

| Method | Main problem it targets | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Taxation (indirect) | External costs, overconsumption | 1 |
| Subsidies | External benefits, underconsumption | 2 |
| Price controls (maximum and minimum prices) | Affordability; low or unstable producer incomes | 3 |
| Buffer stock systems | Unstable prices in markets with inelastic supply | 4 |
| Tradable pollution permits | External costs from emissions | 5 |
| Government expenditure, public/private partnerships | Underprovision of goods with wide social benefits | 6 |
| Legislation and regulation | Harmful activities, poor standards | 6 |
| Information provision | Information failure | 6 |
| Competition policy | Market power | 6 |

The worked examples use one invented market: demand **Qd = 300 − 10P** and supply **Qs = 20P − 60**, with P in pounds and Q in thousand units. Without intervention, 300 − 10P = 20P − 60 gives **P = £12** and **Q = 180**.

## 1. Indirect taxation

A specific tax of £t per unit raises firms' costs, so supply shifts up (left) by £t at every quantity.

**Worked example 1: a £3 tax.** New supply: Qs = 20(P − 3) − 60 = 20P − 120.

- 300 − 10P = 20P − 120 gives **P = £14**, **Q = 160**.
- Consumers pay £2 more (£12 to £14). Producers receive £14 − £3 = £11, which is £1 less.
- Tax revenue = £3 × 160 = **£480 thousand**.
- Consumers bear two-thirds of the tax because demand is less price elastic than supply here.

If the tax equals the marginal external cost, output falls towards the social optimum and the external cost is **internalised**: the price now reflects the full cost to society. Taxes also raise revenue that can fund other policies.

**Limits:** the external cost is hard to measure; inelastic demand means quantity falls little; taxes on necessities can be regressive; high taxes encourage smuggling or avoidance.

## 2. Subsidies

A subsidy of £s per unit lowers firms' costs, so supply shifts down (right) by £s.

**Worked example 2: a £1.50 subsidy.** New supply: Qs = 20(P + 1.5) − 60 = 20P − 30.

- 300 − 10P = 20P − 30 gives **P = £11**, **Q = 190**.
- Consumers pay £1 less; producers receive £11 + £1.50 = £12.50, which is 50p more.
- Cost to government = £1.50 × 190 = **£285 thousand**.

Set equal to the marginal external benefit, a subsidy moves output up to the social optimum.

**Limits:** opportunity cost of the spending; firms may come to depend on it; the right size is hard to judge; some of the subsidy goes to buyers who would have bought anyway.

## 3. Price controls

**Maximum price** (a ceiling set below equilibrium) aims to keep a good affordable.

**Worked example 3: a maximum price of £10.** Qd = 300 − 100 = 200; Qs = 200 − 60 = 140. **Shortage of 60 thousand units.** Buyers who obtain the good gain, but some go without; queues, waiting lists and black markets can appear.

**Minimum price** (a floor set above equilibrium) aims to raise producers' incomes or discourage consumption.

**Worked example 4: a minimum price of £15.** Qd = 300 − 150 = 150; Qs = 300 − 60 = 240. **Surplus of 90 thousand units.** Unless the government buys or destroys the surplus, sellers will try to undercut the floor.

**Limits:** both controls stop the price from rationing; their effects grow with the elasticity of demand and supply; they need enforcement.

## 4. Buffer stock systems

A buffer stock agency sets a **price band** and buys or sells the good to keep the price inside it. It suits goods that can be stored and whose supply varies with the weather, such as grains.

**Worked example 5: a band of £10 to £14.** Demand stays Qd = 300 − 10P.

- **Bumper harvest:** supply becomes Qs = 20P + 30. The free price would be £9, below the floor. At £10, Qd = 200 and Qs = 230, so the agency **buys 30 thousand units** and stores them.
- **Poor harvest:** supply becomes Qs = 20P − 150. The free price would be £15, above the ceiling. At £14, Qd = 160 and Qs = 130, so the agency **sells 30 thousand units** from store.

In this example, stocks bought in a good year exactly cover the shortfall in a bad year.

**Limits:** storage costs and waste; the agency runs out of stock after several poor years or out of money after several good ones; a band set too high encourages persistent overproduction and growing stockpiles.

## 5. Tradable pollution permits

The government sets a **cap** on total emissions and issues permits, each allowing one tonne. Firms that pollute less than their permits allow can sell the spare ones.

**Worked example 6.** Firms A and B each emit 100 tonnes. The cap is 120 tonnes, so each receives 60 permits and must cut 40 tonnes. Cutting a tonne costs A £20 and B £50.

- Without trading: 40 × 20 + 40 × 50 = **£2,800**.
- With trading: A cuts 80 tonnes (£1,600) and sells its 40 spare permits to B, which then cuts nothing. Total cost = **£1,600**, a saving of £1,200 for the same total emissions.
- Any permit price between £20 and £50 benefits both firms.

Permits fix the **quantity** of pollution with certainty and let the market find the cheapest way to meet it. **Limits:** setting the cap, monitoring emissions, permits given away too generously, and firms moving to countries without a cap.

## 6. The other methods

- **Government expenditure:** the state provides or funds goods with large external benefits, such as schools, vaccination programmes or roads. Provision can be free at the point of use, but it has an opportunity cost and may be less efficient than private provision.
- **Public/private partnerships:** private firms build or run projects, such as a hospital, and are paid by the government over many years. They bring private expertise and spread the cost, but long contracts can prove expensive and inflexible.
- **Legislation:** laws ban or require actions, such as banning sales of a product to children.
- **Regulation:** rules and standards enforced by an agency, such as emission limits, food safety standards or licensing. Simple to understand, but costly to monitor, and firms have no incentive to do better than the standard.
- **Information provision:** campaigns, labels and published data correct information failure; cheap and preserves choice, but often ignored.
- **Competition policy:** a competition authority investigates mergers and anti-competitive behaviour, keeping prices closer to the competitive level; investigations are slow and judgements contested.

## 7. Evaluating effectiveness

Use five tests for any method:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Does it reach the target output or price? |
| Cost | What is the budget and administration cost, and the opportunity cost? |
| Information | Can the authority measure the problem and set the right level? |
| Side effects | Shortages, surpluses, black markets, firms relocating? |
| Equity | Who gains and who pays? |

A combination often works best: a tax on a harmful good alongside information provision and a subsidy for a cleaner substitute.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting demand instead of supply for an indirect tax or subsidy.
- Drawing a maximum price above equilibrium, where it has no effect.
- Forgetting that a subsidy has a cost to the government.
- Calling permits a tax: they cap quantity and let the price adjust.
- Evaluating with "it might not work" instead of naming why.

## Exam technique

- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** show the shift or the control line, both quantities, and any shortage, surplus, revenue or subsidy area.
- **Calculate:** shift the supply equation by the tax or subsidy, solve for the new equilibrium and work out revenue or cost.
- **Evaluate:** apply the five tests, compare with an alternative method, and reach a judgement that depends on the market and the problem.
