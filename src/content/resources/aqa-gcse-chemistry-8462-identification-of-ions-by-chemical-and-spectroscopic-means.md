This guide covers **4.8.3 Identification of ions by chemical and spectroscopic means (chemistry only)** of the
AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016. It sits in topic 4.8, Chemical
analysis, and is assessed on Paper 2 in the separate chemistry qualification. It is for both tiers, and there is no
Higher tier only statement. Required practical 7 (using tests to identify ions in unknown ionic compounds)
belongs here.

Use the [identification of ions revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-analysis-revision-notes/)
for quick recall and the [identification of ions practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-identification-of-ions-by-chemical-and-spectroscopic-means-practice/)
to test yourself. The [chemical analysis study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-analysis/)
covers the whole topic; this page goes deeper on ion tests.

## 4.8.3.1 Flame tests

Flame tests identify some **metal ions (cations)**. A clean wire loop (or a splint) is dipped into the compound
(or its solution) and held in a blue Bunsen flame. The flame colour is the clue.

| Metal ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| Lithium, Li⁺ | **Crimson** |
| Sodium, Na⁺ | **Yellow** |
| Potassium, K⁺ | **Lilac** |
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | **Orange-red** |
| Copper, Cu²⁺ | **Green** |

- Clean the loop in hydrochloric acid and heat until there is no colour, between tests.
- If a **mixture of ions** is present, some colours can be **masked**. Sodium's strong yellow can hide others,
  such as potassium's lilac.
- Magnesium and aluminium ions give no colour. Other flame colours are not required.

## 4.8.3.2 Metal hydroxides

**Sodium hydroxide solution** identifies some metal cations. Add a few drops to a solution of the compound, then
add excess.

| Ion | With a few drops of NaOH | With excess NaOH |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminium, Al³⁺ | **White** precipitate | Precipitate **dissolves** (colourless solution) |
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | **White** precipitate | Stays |
| Magnesium, Mg²⁺ | **White** precipitate | Stays |
| Copper(II), Cu²⁺ | **Blue** precipitate | Stays |
| Iron(II), Fe²⁺ | **Green** precipitate | Stays |
| Iron(III), Fe³⁺ | **Brown** precipitate | Stays |

The precipitates are **insoluble metal hydroxides**. Only aluminium hydroxide dissolves in excess sodium
hydroxide. You do not need equations for the formation of sodium aluminate.

**Balanced equations** to produce the insoluble hydroxides. Ionic equations are shorter:

- Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s)
- Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)
- Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)
- Al³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Al(OH)₃(s)
- Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Mg(OH)₂(s)

A full equation includes the spectator ions: copper(II) sulfate + sodium hydroxide gives copper(II) hydroxide and
sodium sulfate.

CuSO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) + Na₂SO₄(aq)

### Telling three white precipitates apart

Aluminium, calcium and magnesium all give white precipitates. Use the **excess** test to find aluminium. To
separate calcium from magnesium, use a **flame test**: calcium gives an orange-red flame, magnesium gives none.

## 4.8.3.3 Carbonates

Carbonates react with **dilute acids** to form **carbon dioxide gas**. Identify the gas with **limewater** (it
turns milky).

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

## 4.8.3.4 Halides

Halide ions in solution produce **precipitates with silver nitrate solution** in the presence of **dilute nitric
acid**.

| Halide ion | Precipitate | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| Chloride, Cl⁻ | Silver chloride | **White** |
| Bromide, Br⁻ | Silver bromide | **Cream** |
| Iodide, I⁻ | Silver iodide | **Yellow** |

Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

Nitric acid is added to **remove other ions** such as carbonate that would also form a precipitate with silver
ions, and so could give a false positive.

## 4.8.3.5 Sulfates

Sulfate ions in solution produce a **white precipitate** with **barium chloride solution** in the presence of
**dilute hydrochloric acid**.

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

The hydrochloric acid removes carbonate ions, because barium carbonate is also a white precipitate.

### Required practical 7

You are given unknown single ionic compounds and use the tests in 4.8.3.1 to 4.8.3.5 to decide which ions are
present. A tidy approach is to record every observation in a table.

### Worked example 1: unknown compound A

*A white solid A is dissolved in water. A flame test is lilac. Dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate solution
give a cream precipitate. Identify A.*

- Lilac flame: **potassium**.
- Cream precipitate with silver nitrate: **bromide**.
- A is **potassium bromide**.

### Worked example 2: unknown compound B

*A blue solid B dissolves in water. Sodium hydroxide gives a blue precipitate. Barium chloride and dilute
hydrochloric acid give a white precipitate. Identify B.*

- Blue precipitate with sodium hydroxide: **copper(II)**.
- White precipitate with barium chloride: **sulfate**.
- B is **copper(II) sulfate**.

### Worked example 3: a mixture that masks

*A solution gives a yellow flame. Sodium hydroxide gives a white precipitate that stays in excess, and the
flame test with a cobalt blue glass shows lilac as well. What can be said?*

Sodium gives a yellow flame, which can mask lilac. The extra evidence suggests both **sodium and potassium**.
(The white precipitate shows calcium or magnesium may be present as well; more tests are needed.)

## 4.8.3.6 Instrumental methods

Elements and compounds can be detected and identified using **instrumental methods**. They are **accurate,
sensitive and rapid**.

Advantages compared with the chemical tests:

- **Rapid:** results in minutes.
- **Sensitive:** can detect very **small amounts** of a substance.
- **Accurate:** less depends on judging a colour by eye.
- Can analyse **small samples** (important in forensic work).
- Can **measure concentration** as well as identify.

Disadvantages: the equipment is **expensive**, needs **training**, and results usually have to be compared with
reference data.

## 4.8.3.7 Flame emission spectroscopy

**Flame emission spectroscopy** is an instrumental method for analysing **metal ions in solutions**.

1. The sample is put into a **flame**.
2. The light given out is passed through a **spectroscope**.
3. The output is a **line spectrum** that can be analysed to **identify the metal ions** in the solution and to
   **measure their concentrations**.

Each metal ion gives its own pattern of lines. You compare the sample's lines with a **reference set** in the
same form (chart or table).

### Worked example 4: interpreting a spectrum

The table shows the lines present (✓) in the spectrum of a sample and in reference spectra.

| Line position | Li | Na | K | Cu | Sample |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ✓ | | | | |
| 2 | | ✓ | | | ✓ |
| 3 | | | ✓ | | ✓ |
| 4 | | | | ✓ | |
| 5 | | ✓ | | | ✓ |
| 6 | | | ✓ | | ✓ |

The sample has lines at 2 and 5 (sodium) and at 3 and 6 (potassium). It contains **sodium and potassium** ions.
A flame test would only show the yellow of sodium, so this instrumental method finds more.

## Exam technique

- Always give the **reagent**, the **observation** and the **conclusion** in order.
- Include the **acid** with silver nitrate and barium chloride, and say why it is added.
- For aluminium, mention that the precipitate **dissolves in excess**.
- When comparing instrumental and chemical methods, write "faster, more sensitive and more accurate", then give a
  disadvantage for balance.
- Use the **ionic equation** with state symbols when asked for a balanced equation.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a flame colour for the wrong ion (lithium crimson, calcium orange-red).
- Forgetting to add excess sodium hydroxide, so aluminium is not separated from magnesium and calcium.
- Writing "white solid" rather than "white precipitate".
- Leaving out nitric acid or hydrochloric acid in the halide and sulfate tests.
- Mixing up the colours of silver halides (white, cream, yellow).
- Naming Fe²⁺ as brown or Fe³⁺ as green. Iron(II) is green, iron(III) is brown.
