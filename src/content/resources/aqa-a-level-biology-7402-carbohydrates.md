This guide goes deeper into **3.1.2 Carbohydrates** from the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), first teaching 2015. The sub-topic covers monosaccharides, disaccharides, the α- and β-isomers of glucose, the polysaccharides glycogen, starch and cellulose, and the biochemical tests using Benedict's solution and iodine in potassium iodide. It is examined at AS and A-level.

The [monomers, polymers and carbohydrates study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-monomers-polymers-and-carbohydrates/) already lists the disaccharides, describes each polysaccharide, sets out the three tests and works colorimetry and chromatography examples. This page goes further in four places: how to draw and number glucose so you can show 1,4 and 1,6 bonds, how to build a full-mark structure-to-function explanation, why storing glucose as a polymer matters for water potential, and how to make the Benedict's test semi-quantitative without a colorimeter. Condense it with the [3.1.2 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-carbohydrates-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1.2 practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-carbohydrates-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

- Glucose, galactose and fructose are common monosaccharides.
- A condensation reaction between two monosaccharides forms a glycosidic bond.
- Maltose (glucose + glucose), sucrose (glucose + fructose) and lactose (glucose + galactose).
- Glucose has two isomers, α-glucose and β-glucose, and you must know their structures.
- Glycogen and starch are formed from α-glucose; cellulose from β-glucose.
- The basic structure and functions of glycogen, starch and cellulose, and how structure relates to function in animal and plant cells.
- Tests with Benedict's solution (reducing and non-reducing sugars) and iodine/potassium iodide (starch).

## Drawing and numbering glucose

You are expected to recognise and draw the two isomers. Build the drawing in steps.

1. Draw a hexagon with **one oxygen atom** at the top right corner. The other five corners are carbons 1 to 5.
2. Number clockwise from the carbon to the right of the ring oxygen: carbon 1 is at the right, then 2 and 3 along the bottom, carbon 4 at the left and carbon 5 at the top left, next to the oxygen.
3. Carbon 6 sits **outside** the ring as a CH₂OH group attached to carbon 5, pointing up.
4. Add an H and an OH to carbons 1 to 4.
5. On carbon 1: in **α-glucose** the OH points **down** (below the ring); in **β-glucose** it points **up** (above the ring). Everything else is identical.

Numbering lets you name the bonds precisely.

| Bond | Carbons joined | Where it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| α-1,4 glycosidic | Carbon 1 of one α-glucose to carbon 4 of the next | Main chains of amylose, amylopectin and glycogen |
| α-1,6 glycosidic | Carbon 1 of one α-glucose to carbon 6 of another | Branch points in amylopectin and glycogen |
| β-1,4 glycosidic | Carbon 1 of one β-glucose to carbon 4 of the next, alternate units inverted | Cellulose |

The 1,6 bond explains branching: carbon 6 sits outside the ring, so a chain can sprout sideways from it.

## Building a full-mark structure-to-function answer

Mark schemes for "explain how the structure of X suits its function" reward a chain of four links: **feature → property → consequence → function**. Stopping after the feature or the property is the usual reason for losing marks.

| Molecule | Feature | Property | Consequence | Function served |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Starch | Amylose coils into a helix | Compact | Much glucose stored in little space | Energy store in plant cells |
| Starch / glycogen | Large polymer | Insoluble; does not diffuse out of cells | No effect on water potential; stays where it is stored | Storage without osmotic problems |
| Glycogen | More 1,6 branches than amylopectin | Many free ends | Enzymes hydrolyse many ends at once, releasing glucose rapidly | Energy store for animals, which have high metabolic rates |
| Cellulose | β-glucose, alternate units inverted | Straight, unbranched chains | Many hydrogen bonds form between parallel chains, making microfibrils | Tensile strength of plant cell walls |

### Worked example 1: the osmotic argument with numbers

A liver cell needs to store glucose. Compare two options: 1,000 free glucose molecules, or one glycogen molecule made of 1,000 glucose units.

- Free glucose: 1,000 dissolved solute molecules. Glucose is soluble, so these lower the water potential of the cytoplasm. Water would enter the cell by osmosis, and glucose could also leave by diffusion through carrier proteins.
- Glycogen: 1 large molecule, insoluble, so it adds almost no solute particles. Water potential is unaffected and the stored glucose cannot diffuse out.

The energy content is the same, because the same 1,000 glucose units can be released by hydrolysis. That is the whole reason for storing glucose as a polymer: the store is osmotically inactive. Writing "starch is insoluble, so it is a good store" without the water potential link earns little.

## Why animals cannot digest cellulose

Enzymes are specific to the shape of their substrate (3.1.4.2). The enzymes that hydrolyse starch and glycogen fit α-glycosidic bonds. Because alternate β-glucose units in cellulose are inverted, the bonds have a different shape and these enzymes do not fit them. This is why cellulose passes through the human gut largely undigested, even though it is made of glucose.

## Reducing and non-reducing sugars: reading the results carefully

All monosaccharides are reducing sugars. Maltose and lactose are reducing; sucrose is non-reducing.

Two consequences are often tested:

1. **You cannot use Benedict's to show maltose has been hydrolysed.** Maltose and its product, glucose, are both reducing, so both give a positive result. A change in the amount of precipitate may hint at it, but the test alone cannot prove hydrolysis.
2. **A mixture can be positive before and more positive after acid hydrolysis.** If a sample contains glucose and sucrose, the first test is positive. After boiling with acid and neutralising, the sucrose has been split into glucose and fructose, so there is more reducing sugar and more precipitate.

## Making the Benedict's test semi-quantitative

The colour sequence (blue, green, yellow, orange, brick red) gives a rough ranking. Two methods improve on it without a colorimeter.

- **Time to first colour change.** Heat equal volumes in a water bath at a fixed temperature and time how long each takes to turn from blue. More reducing sugar gives a shorter time.
- **Mass of precipitate.** After heating for a fixed time, filter each tube, dry the precipitate and weigh it. More reducing sugar gives more precipitate.

For any method, control the volume of sample, the volume and concentration of Benedict's solution (use it in excess), the temperature of the water bath and the heating time.

### Worked example 2: precipitate mass

A student filters and dries the precipitate from glucose standards and one unknown. All values are invented for practice.

| Glucose (g dm⁻³) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dry precipitate (mg) | 0 | 18 | 37 | 55 | 74 |

The unknown gave 46 mg. It lies between 4 g dm⁻³ (37 mg) and 6 g dm⁻³ (55 mg). Interpolating: 4 + (46 − 37) ÷ (55 − 37) × 2 = 4 + 9 ÷ 18 × 2 = **5.0 g dm⁻³**.

The student then boiled a second portion of the unknown with acid, neutralised it and repeated the test: 74 mg, equivalent to 8 g dm⁻³. The increase shows a non-reducing sugar, such as sucrose, was also present. The data cannot give its concentration directly, because different sugars may reduce Benedict's solution by different amounts per gram.

### Iodine in a time-course

Iodine/potassium iodide turns blue-black with starch and stays yellow-brown without it. In an amylase investigation, drops of the reaction mixture are added to iodine on a spotting tile at regular intervals. The time until the iodine no longer turns blue-black is the time for the starch to be hydrolysed. It links this sub-topic to the [enzymes required practical](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-enzymes-and-required-practical-1/).

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a test: name the reagent, any heating, and the colour change from what to what.
- **Explain** a structure: use the four-link chain and say "α-1,4" or "β" precisely.
- **Draw** glucose: ring oxygen, carbon 6 outside the ring, OH on carbon 1 down for α, up for β.
- **Suggest** a result: apply which sugars are reducing, and remember to neutralise after acid hydrolysis.

## Common mistakes

- Placing the CH₂OH group on carbon 1, or drawing the ring oxygen in the wrong place.
- Saying glycogen is "more branched, so it stores more energy". Branching speeds up release; it does not add energy.
- Saying starch is a good store "because it is insoluble" without water potential.
- Claiming Benedict's can show that maltose has been hydrolysed.
- Saying humans cannot digest cellulose "because it is too strong", rather than because the enzymes do not fit β-linked units.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds?**
An α-1,4 bond joins carbon 1 of one α-glucose to carbon 4 of the next, forming chains. An α-1,6 bond joins carbon 1 to carbon 6 of another unit, forming branch points.

**Why is glucose stored as starch or glycogen rather than as glucose?**
Glucose is soluble and would lower the water potential of the cell and could diffuse out. Starch and glycogen are insoluble, so they store glucose without osmotic effects.

**Can the Benedict's test show how much sugar is present?**
Only roughly by colour. Timing the first colour change or weighing the dried precipitate makes it semi-quantitative; a colorimeter gives a more precise value.

**Why can't humans digest cellulose?**
The enzymes that hydrolyse starch fit α-glycosidic bonds. Cellulose is made of β-glucose with alternate units inverted, so those enzymes do not fit its bonds.
