This guide covers section **2.3.5 External influences** in Unit 2 (Managing business activities) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business, Issue 1. External influences are the forces outside a business that it cannot control but must respond to. The specification asks you to explain their effects and how a business can best respond. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Managing Business Activities study guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-business-managing-business-activities/). Condensed notes are in the [External influences revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-external-influences-revision-notes/) and questions in the [External influences practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-external-influences-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Economic influences:** the effect on businesses of, and how they can best respond to, changes in the rate of inflation, exchange rates (appreciation and depreciation), interest rates, taxation and government spending, and the business cycle.
2. **Legislation:** the effects of consumer protection, employee protection, environmental protection, competition policy, health and safety, and intellectual property rights (copyright, patents and trademarks).
3. **The competitive environment:** the effects of competition in terms of competitor numbers, size and behaviour; ways for a small business to compete in a competitive market.

Each influence has two parts to every answer: the **effect** (what happens to costs, revenue or demand) and the **response** (what the business can sensibly do). Strong answers also say how big the effect is for this particular business.

## Inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level. For a business it raises costs: materials, energy, rent and wages. It also changes customers' spending power.

**Worked example 1.** Brannock Joinery spends $240,000 a year on timber and had a profit of $60,000. Timber prices rise by 6%.

- Extra cost = 6% x 240,000 = **$14,400**.
- If Brannock cannot raise its prices, profit falls to 60,000 - 14,400 = **$45,600**, a fall of **24%**.

The 6% cost rise has produced a 24% fall in profit, because profit is a small fraction of the cost base. Responses include raising prices (if demand is inelastic), switching suppliers, agreeing fixed-price contracts, cutting waste or improving productivity. Each has a risk: higher prices can lose customers, and cheaper timber could harm quality. High inflation also makes planning harder and encourages wage claims.

## Exchange rates

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another. If a currency **appreciates**, it buys more foreign currency; if it **depreciates**, it buys less.

**Worked example 2.** A European buyer pays Brannock in euros. Brannock sells a cabinet for EUR 120. At $1 = EUR 0.90 it receives 120 / 0.90 = **$133.33**. If the dollar depreciates to $1 = EUR 0.80, the same sale brings in 120 / 0.80 = **$150.00**.

Now consider an importer. Brannock buys EUR 60,000 of fittings. At $1 = EUR 0.90 these cost 60,000 / 0.90 = **$66,667**. At $1 = EUR 0.80 they cost 60,000 / 0.80 = **$75,000**, an extra $8,333.

| | Currency appreciates | Currency depreciates |
|---|---|---|
| Exporters | Prices abroad rise, so exports become less competitive | Prices abroad fall, so exports become more competitive |
| Importers | Imported materials cost less | Imported materials cost more |
| Tourism | Domestic holidays cost visitors more | Domestic holidays cost visitors less |

Responses include invoicing in the home currency, hedging through forward contracts, buying materials from home suppliers, or producing in the country where the business sells. The effect on a particular firm depends on whether it exports, imports or both, and on how much its prices are tied to the foreign currency.

## Interest rates

The **interest rate** is the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. A rise in rates raises the cost of loans and overdrafts, and reduces consumer spending, especially on big items bought on credit.

**Worked example 3.** Brannock has a $200,000 loan with a variable rate. At 4%, interest is $8,000 a year. At 6%, interest is $12,000, which is **50% more**. A 2 percentage point rise has raised the interest bill by half.

A business with high borrowing (a highly geared firm) feels this most. Responses include fixing the rate, paying down debt, delaying investment or relying more on retained profit. Falling interest rates have the opposite effect, lowering borrowing costs and boosting spending. They can encourage investment, but also make exports less competitive if the currency rises.

## Taxation and government spending

**Taxation** includes corporation tax on profits, income tax and VAT or sales taxes. Higher corporation tax leaves less profit to reinvest: on a pre-tax profit of $80,000, a rate of 25% takes $20,000, but 28% takes $22,400, leaving $57,600 instead of $60,000. Higher income tax cuts consumers' disposable income and spending. Higher sales taxes raise prices and may reduce demand.

**Government spending** can create demand for businesses. A new road scheme helps construction firms, and extra health spending helps medical suppliers. Cuts have the reverse effect. Responses include widening the customer base, targeting government contracts, adjusting pricing or cutting costs.

## The business cycle

The **business cycle** is the pattern of rising and falling economic activity: boom, slowdown, recession (or slump) and recovery. In a boom, demand is high, but costs and wages rise and capacity becomes stretched. In a recession, demand falls, particularly for luxuries, and unemployment rises.

Income elasticity of demand (YED) shows how sensitive a product is to incomes. If incomes rise by 4% and YED is +2.0, demand rises by 4 x 2.0 = **8%**. A product with a YED of -0.5 is an inferior good, so demand falls by 4 x 0.5 = **2%**. A firm selling luxury cars is therefore exposed to the cycle, while a budget supermarket may gain in a recession. Responses include diversifying, building cash reserves in booms, offering lower-priced ranges in recessions and keeping fixed costs flexible.

## Legislation

| Law | Effect on business | Possible response |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer protection (goods must be as described, of satisfactory quality; clear pricing and labelling) | Costs of testing, accurate labelling and returns; protects reputation | Quality assurance; clear information; reliable complaints handling |
| Employee protection (minimum wage, contracts, discrimination, working hours) | Higher wage costs; HR administration; fair treatment | Training; efficient rotas; good HR practice |
| Environmental protection (emissions, waste, pollution) | Costs of cleaner technology; fines for breaches; marketing benefit | Invest in green technology; recycling; sustainable sourcing |
| Competition policy (preventing cartels, abuse of dominant position, anti-competitive mergers) | Limits price fixing and some takeovers; protects smaller rivals | Avoid collusion; seek approval for mergers |
| Health and safety | Costs of training, equipment and inspections; fewer accidents and claims | Risk assessments; safety training |
| Intellectual property (copyright, patents, trademarks) | Protects inventions and brands from copying; costs of registering and defending | Register patents and trademarks; monitor infringement |

Legislation usually adds costs, but it also builds trust, protects staff and gives legal protection to a business's own ideas. A **patent** protects an invention, a **trademark** a name or logo, and **copyright** original written, musical or artistic work. Note that a larger business spreads compliance costs more easily than a small one, so legislation can be a heavier burden on small firms.

## The competitive environment

Competition affects a business through three features of its rivals.

- **Numbers:** many competitors, as in a market for takeaway food, force businesses to keep prices low and to differentiate. Few competitors, as in a market for aircraft, give more pricing power.
- **Size:** large rivals can use economies of scale to charge lower prices and spend heavily on advertising. A small rival may be more flexible and closer to customers.
- **Behaviour:** aggressive price-cutting, heavy promotion, rapid product launches or takeovers all force a response, whereas rivals that avoid price competition allow more stable profits.

**Worked example 4.** In a market worth $10m, shares are 30%, 24%, 18%, 14% and 14%. The leader's sales are 30% x 10m = $3.0m and the smallest rival's are $1.4m. The leader is more than twice the size of either small rival, so a price war by the leader would be hard for smaller firms to follow.

**Ways for a small business to compete:** target a niche market; offer excellent personal service; differentiate by quality or design; build local reputation; use flexibility and speed to respond to customers; use online selling to reach more customers at low cost; form partnerships or buying groups.

## Common mistakes

- Describing an effect without a response, or a response without explaining why it fits the business.
- Mixing up appreciation and depreciation: appreciation makes exports dearer for foreign buyers.
- Saying a rise in interest rates always hurts a business: a firm with no debt may benefit from higher returns on savings.
- Writing about legislation as only a cost; it also protects the business.
- Listing competitors without saying whether they are many or few, large or small, aggressive or passive.

## Exam technique

For **explain** questions give one effect and develop it using a number or detail from the source. For **analyse**, build a chain: "interest rates rise, so loan repayments rise, so profit falls and the firm may delay investment". For **discuss** and **assess**, weigh which influence matters most to this business at this time, and say what would change your view, such as whether the business exports, is highly geared or sells luxury goods.
