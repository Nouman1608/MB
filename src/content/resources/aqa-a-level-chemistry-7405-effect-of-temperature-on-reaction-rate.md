## Rate of reaction

**The rate of reaction is the change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time.**

```
rate = change in concentration ÷ time        units: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
```

Rate can also be measured as a change in mass, volume of gas or colour per unit time. A fast reaction is complete in a short time. In many experiments a quantity proportional to rate is used: for a reaction that is timed until a fixed amount of product forms, **rate ∝ 1 ÷ time**.

## The effect of temperature

Raising the temperature increases the rate of almost every reaction. There are **two** reasons, and the second is much more important.

1. **The particles move faster**, so they collide **more frequently**. This effect is real but small.
2. **A greater proportion of the particles have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy.** More collisions are therefore successful.

### Why a small increase gives a large effect

Look at a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. The activation energy lies in the high-energy tail, where the curve is already low. When the temperature rises, the whole distribution shifts to the right and the tail gets bigger. A small shift in the curve produces a large proportional increase in the area to the right of Eₐ, because the area is small to begin with.

### Worked example 1: a full-mark explanation

Explain why increasing the temperature by 10 °C approximately doubles the rate of many reactions.

The particles have more kinetic energy and collide more often, but this effect on its own is small. The main reason is that the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve shifts to higher energy and the tail broadens, so the proportion of molecules with energy equal to or greater than the activation energy rises sharply. A larger fraction of collisions is successful, so the frequency of successful collisions, and hence the rate, increases greatly.

### Worked example 2: how small is the collision effect?

The collision frequency in a gas rises roughly in proportion to the square root of the absolute temperature. From 298 K to 308 K:

```
√(308 ÷ 298) = √1.0336 = 1.017
```

The collision frequency increases by only about 1.7%. If this were the only effect, a 10 K rise would increase the rate by under 2%. Experiments show a rise of around 100% for many reactions, so the increase must come mainly from the greater proportion of successful collisions.

(The square-root relationship is background and is not examined as a calculation. Use it only to appreciate the relative sizes.)

## Reading rate data

Required practical 3 often uses the reaction of sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid, which forms a fine precipitate of sulfur:

Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + SO₂(g) + S(s) + H₂O(l)

A conical flask stands on a cross marked on paper. The time for the cross to disappear is recorded. Since the same amount of sulfur is needed to hide the cross each time, **rate ∝ 1 ÷ time**.

### Worked example 3: processing data

| Temperature / °C | Time for cross to disappear / s | 1000 ÷ time / s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 85 | 11.8 |
| 30 | 43 | 23.3 |
| 40 | 22 | 45.5 |
| 50 | 11 | 90.9 |
| 60 | 6 | 166.7 |

The ratio of the relative rates for each 10 °C step is 1.98, 1.95, 2.00 and 1.83. The rate approximately doubles for each 10 °C rise, then the effect is a little smaller at higher temperatures. A graph of 1000 ÷ time against temperature is a curve that rises more and more steeply, not a straight line.

## Planning the practical

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Independent variable | Temperature of the reaction mixture |
| Dependent variable | Time for the cross to disappear |
| Control variables | Concentrations and volumes of both solutions, the flask and the cross, the observer |
| Method | Warm the thiosulfate solution in a water bath to the required temperature, add acid, start the clock, swirl, and stop when the cross vanishes |
| Repeats | Repeat each temperature and average |
| Safety | Sulfur dioxide is toxic and can trigger asthma, so work in a fume cupboard or a well-ventilated room, and use eye protection |

### Limitations and improvements

- The end point (the cross vanishing) is a subjective judgement. Use the same observer, or use a light sensor.
- The temperature changes during the reaction. Use a water bath and measure the temperature at the start and the end, then use the mean.
- The reagents are mixed at a temperature different from the water bath. Equilibrate both solutions before mixing.
- Timing uncertainty (starting and stopping the clock) is a large fraction of a short time: ±0.5 s on 6 s is 8%, but on 85 s it is only 0.6%.

## What temperature does not change

Raising the temperature does **not** change the activation energy, the enthalpy change, or the total number of molecules. It changes the energy distribution and the frequency of collisions.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that molecules "move faster so collide more often" as the only reason, with no mention of the activation energy.
- Writing that the activation energy is lowered by heating.
- Saying that "more molecules have energy greater than the activation energy" without "equal to or".
- Failing to link the explanation to the tail of the distribution.
- Using Celsius for a calculation that needs kelvin.
- Writing that rate and time are directly proportional (rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time).
- Not controlling the concentration in the practical.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain** why temperature increases rate: give both effects, state which is more important and use the distribution.
- **Sketch**: draw two curves with the same area and show Eₐ.
- **Evaluate** a method: name a specific limitation, say how it affects the result and propose a change.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is rate not simply doubled when the collision frequency doubles?**
Rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions, which depends on both the collision frequency and the proportion with enough energy.

**Do all reactions double in rate for a 10 °C rise?**
No. This is only a rule of thumb for many reactions near room temperature. The effect depends on the activation energy.

**Is it correct to say that heating raises the energy of every particle?**
No. The average energy rises and the distribution changes, but individual particles still have a range of energies.
