## The meaning of national income

**National income** is the total value of the goods and services an economy produces over a period, normally a year, which is also the total income generated by that production. It is a **flow**, not a stock: it records what is produced or earned during the year, not what is owned at a point in time.

Production, income and spending are three views of the same total.

- **Output view:** the value of final goods and services produced.
- **Income view:** the wages, rent, interest and profit paid to the factors of production that made that output.
- **Expenditure view:** the spending on that final output by households, firms, government and foreign buyers, less spending on imports.

In principle all three give the same figure, because every unit of output sold is someone's spending and is paid out to someone as income. In practice the three estimates differ slightly because the data come from different sources, and statistical offices publish an adjustment to reconcile them.

## Measuring output without double counting

Only **final** goods and services are counted. Intermediate goods, which are used up in making other goods, are already included in the price of the final product. One way to avoid double counting is to add only the **value added** at each stage of production.

### Worked example 1: value added in a shirt

| Stage | Sales value (cu) | Bought from previous stage (cu) | Value added (cu) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cotton farmer | 40 | 0 | 40 |
| Spinner (yarn) | 90 | 40 | 50 |
| Weaver (cloth) | 150 | 90 | 60 |
| Tailor (shirt) | 260 | 150 | 110 |
| **Total** | 540 | | **260** |

Adding every sale gives 540 cu, which counts the cotton four times. Adding value added gives 260 cu, the same as the value of the finished shirt. The 260 cu is also the total of the incomes paid out along the chain.

### What else is left out

- **Transfer payments**, such as pensions and unemployment benefits, are excluded from the income view, because no good or service is produced in return. Counting them would add income that matches no output.
- **Second-hand sales** were counted when the good was first produced. Only the dealer's service, the margin, is new output.
- **Purely financial transactions**, such as buying existing shares, move ownership of assets but create no new output.

## GDP, GNI and NNI

| Measure | What it records |
|---|---|
| **GDP** | Value of output produced within the country's borders in a year, whoever owns the factors of production |
| **GNI** | GDP plus net primary income from abroad: the income earned by the country's residents, wherever they produce |
| **NNI** | GNI minus depreciation (capital consumption) |

**Net primary income from abroad** = primary income received from abroad (profits, interest, dividends and wages earned abroad by residents) minus primary income paid abroad (to foreign owners of factors used in the country). It can be positive or negative.

### When GDP and GNI differ

- A country whose mines and factories are largely foreign-owned sends much of the profit abroad. Net primary income is negative, so **GNI is below GDP**: much of what is produced at home is not income for residents.
- A country whose residents own large overseas investments receives profit and interest from abroad. Net primary income is positive, so **GNI is above GDP**.

When a question asks which measure better shows the **income** of a country's people, GNI is usually the stronger answer. When it asks about **production** or activity inside the country, GDP is.

## Market prices and basic prices

A value at **market prices** is what buyers pay. It includes taxes on products and is reduced by subsidies on products. A value at **basic prices** is what producers receive.

```
Basic prices = market prices - taxes on products + subsidies on products
```

The adjustment matters because product taxes go to the government, not to producers, and subsidies add to producers' receipts. Comparing GDP at market prices between two years can mislead if product tax rates changed in between: market-price GDP would rise after a sales tax increase even with no extra output.

## Gross values and net values

**Gross** values are before any allowance for **depreciation**, the fall in the value of the capital stock as machinery, vehicles and buildings wear out or become obsolete. **Net** values deduct it.

```
Net = gross - depreciation
```

Net figures show the output or income available for consumption and new investment **without running down** the capital stock. A country with rising GDP but much faster rising depreciation may not be adding to its productive capacity as quickly as the gross figure suggests.

## Worked example 2: a full data table

Country X (invented figures, billion cu, one year):

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Consumer spending (C) | 520 |
| Investment (I) | 140 |
| Government spending on goods and services (G) | 180 |
| Exports (X) | 210 |
| Imports (M) | 230 |
| Primary income received from abroad | 45 |
| Primary income paid abroad | 70 |
| Depreciation | 95 |
| Taxes on products | 90 |
| Subsidies on products | 15 |

```
GDP at market prices = 520 + 140 + 180 + 210 - 230 = 820
Net primary income    = 45 - 70 = -25
GNI at market prices = 820 - 25 = 795
NNI at market prices = 795 - 95 = 700

Market-to-basic adjustment = -90 + 15 = -75
GDP at basic prices = 820 - 75 = 745
GNI at basic prices = 795 - 75 = 720
NNI at basic prices = 700 - 75 = 625
```

The two adjustments are independent, so they can be made in either order: NNI at basic prices is 625 whether depreciation or the product-tax adjustment is removed first. Keep three steps separate in your head: **domestic to national** (add net primary income), **gross to net** (subtract depreciation) and **market to basic** (subtract product taxes, add subsidies).

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain the difference between Gross Domestic Product and Gross National Income. **[4]**

> GDP is the **total value of output produced within a country's borders** in a year *(1: GDP defined)*, including output from factors owned by foreigners *(1: ownership irrelevant to GDP)*. GNI is GDP **plus net primary income from abroad** *(1: GNI defined)*, so it adds income such as profits earned abroad by residents and subtracts income paid to foreign owners of factors used in the country; it measures the income of residents rather than output within the borders *(1: what the adjustment means)*.

## Common mistakes

- Including transfer payments or second-hand sales in national income.
- Adding every sale in a production chain instead of value added.
- Treating the market-to-basic step as removing **all** taxes. It removes taxes on products only.
- Subtracting subsidies instead of adding them when moving to basic prices.
- Using depreciation to move from GDP to GNI.
- Getting the sign of net primary income wrong when more income is paid out than received.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: write each step with its label and keep units (for example, billion cu).
- **Define** questions on GNI must include "net primary income from abroad"; on NNI, "minus depreciation".
- **Explain** a difference between two measures by naming the adjustment and what it represents economically.
- In data responses, comment on what a gap between GDP and GNI says about foreign ownership or overseas assets.
