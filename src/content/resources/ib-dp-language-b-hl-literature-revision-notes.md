These revision notes cover the **HL literature strand** of IB Diploma Programme Language B: reading
two literary works, discussing an extract and linking it to the prescribed themes, with the focus on
Identities, Social organization and Sharing the planet. They follow the IB Language B subject brief
for first assessment 2020, which remains the examined course until the new syllabus is first
assessed in May 2029, so they apply to the 2026, 2027 and 2028 sessions. Everything here is **HL
only**. The notes are in English; apply each point to your target-language works.

## Facts from the brief

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Literary works at HL | Two, "originally written in the target language" -- required at HL only |
| HL language expectation | Extend the "range and complexity" of language you use and understand |
| Individual oral | Internal assessment, 25% of the final grade, 30 marks |
| SL oral stimulus | A visual image clearly relevant to one or more themes |
| HL oral stimulus | "An excerpt from one of the two literary works studied" |
| Recommended teaching hours | 240 at HL (150 at SL) |

The brief does not give the oral's format, timings or criteria. Your teacher will share them.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Narrator | The voice that tells the story. Not the same as the author. |
| First-person narration | Told by "I", a character inside the story; limited to what they know and feel. |
| Third-person narration | Told from outside ("she", "he"); can be close to one character or see many. |
| Direct characterisation | The text tells you what a character is like ("She was proud"). |
| Indirect characterisation | You infer it from actions, speech, appearance or others' reactions. |
| Setting | Time and place; often reflects mood, class or a society's rules. |
| Imagery | Language that appeals to the senses, including simile and metaphor. |
| Simile / metaphor | A comparison with "like" or "as" / a comparison stated as if it were true. |
| Personification | Giving human qualities to an object, place or idea. |
| Symbol | An object or image that stands for a larger idea. |
| Motif | An image, object or phrase that recurs across a work. |
| Irony | A gap between what is said and what is meant, or expected and what happens. |
| Tone | The attitude the voice takes: tender, bitter, detached, mocking. |
| Turning point | A moment after which a character or situation cannot go back. |
| Prescribed theme | One of the five course themes in the brief (e.g. Identities). |
| Literary theme | An idea a work explores (e.g. belonging, loss). Link it to a prescribed theme. |

## Method in steps

> **Discussing an extract: L-S-A-C-L**
>
> 1. **Locate** -- where it sits in the work; what comes before and after.
> 2. **Summarise** -- one or two sentences, no more.
> 3. **Analyse** -- two or three features: feature, short quotation, effect.
> 4. **Connect** -- to a character's development or a motif across the work.
> 5. **Link** -- to a prescribed theme and one of its possible questions.

> **Preparing each work**
>
> 1. Reading log: one row per chapter (events, relationships, one short quotation, theme link).
> 2. Character map and timeline, with chapter numbers.
> 3. Key-passage bank: 10 to 12 passages per work, each with a theme link.
> 4. Theme map: which passages answer which possible question.
> 5. Target-language phrase bank for discussing literature, practised aloud.

## Theme links at a glance

| Theme | Guiding principle (brief) | Recommended topics | What to look for in a work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identities | "Explore the nature of the self and what it is to be human." | lifestyles; health and well-being; beliefs and values; subcultures; language and identity | belonging, divided loyalties, names and language, belief |
| Social organization | "Explore the ways in which groups of people organize themselves, or are organized, through common systems or interests." | social relationships; community; social engagement; education; the working world; law and order | schools, workplaces, families as systems; rules, power, resistance |
| Sharing the planet | "Explore the challenges and opportunities faced by individuals and communities in the modern world." | the environment; human rights; peace and conflict; equality; globalization; ethics; urban and rural environment | nature and land, town and country, injustice, conflict, moral choices |

Possible questions to keep ready:

- **Identities:** "What constitutes an identity?"; "How do language and culture contribute to form
  our identity?"
- **Social organization:** "What is the individual's role in the community?"; "What role do rules
  and regulations play in the formation of a society?"
- **Sharing the planet:** "What environmental and social issues present challenges to the world, and
  how can these challenges be overcome?"; "What challenges and benefits does globalization bring?"

## Worked reminder

> *Original text written for Marlbridge*
>
> The new road reached the village in spring. By summer, the children had stopped learning the
> names of the birds. They learned the bus times instead.

- **Feature:** contrast between two kinds of knowledge, "the names of the birds" and "the bus
  times".
- **Effect:** the road brings access but costs a link with nature. The flat, short final sentence
  makes the loss sound ordinary, which makes it sharper.
- **Link:** Sharing the planet, *urban and rural environment*; the first possible question about
  environmental and social challenges.

## Must-know distinctions

| Distinction | Remember |
|---|---|
| Summary vs analysis | Summary says what happens. Analysis says how the language makes it mean something. |
| Narrator vs author | A narrator's opinion is a character's opinion until the work shows otherwise. |
| Character's view vs the work's view | A work can present a view in order to criticise it. |
| Literary theme vs prescribed theme | "Loss" is a literary theme; Identities is a prescribed theme. Name both. |
| Technique vs effect | "Metaphor" is a label. "It makes the city seem hungry" is an effect. |
| SL vs HL oral | SL: visual image. HL: excerpt from one of your two literary works. |
| Describing a culture vs stereotyping it | "In this village, as the novel shows..." not "People from there always..." |

## Phrase bank (learn the target-language equivalents)

- Locating: "This extract comes just after..." / "Earlier in the work..."
- Analysing: "The metaphor suggests..." / "The repetition of... emphasises..."
- Connecting: "This is the first time that..." / "This foreshadows..."
- Linking: "This links to Identities because..." / "It answers the question of..."
- Hedging: "This could be read as..." / "It seems that..."
- Concession: "Although she agrees, her tone implies..."

## Quick self-test

1. How many literary works does the brief require at HL, and what condition must they meet?
2. What is the individual oral stimulus at SL, and at HL?
3. What weighting and mark total does the brief give the individual oral?
4. List the five steps of the extract method.
5. "The station swallowed the last travellers." Name the technique and give its effect.
6. A first-person narrator calls her brother lazy. Why should you not simply state that the brother
   is lazy?
7. A scene shows workers voting to strike. Name the prescribed theme and a recommended topic.
8. Which theme asks "What constitutes an identity?"
9. "He slammed the door without a word." Is this direct or indirect characterisation? What does it
   suggest?
10. Improve this sentence for HL range: "The writer says the city is bad."
11. What is the difference between a symbol and a motif?
12. Why does the brief's wording mean you must revise both works?

### Answers

1. Two, originally written in the target language.
2. SL: a visual image clearly relevant to one or more themes. HL: an excerpt from one of the two
   literary works studied.
3. 25% of the final grade, 30 marks; it is internally assessed.
4. Locate, summarise, analyse, connect, link.
5. Personification (or metaphor): the station is shown as a creature that eats people, which makes
   the place seem threatening and the travellers powerless.
6. It is the narrator's opinion. Check whether the rest of the work supports or undermines it.
7. Social organization; *the working world* (also *social engagement* or *law and order*).
8. Identities.
9. Indirect: we infer anger, or refusal to talk, from his action.
10. For example: "Although the city offers work, the writer presents it as hostile, which suggests
    that progress comes at a cost."
11. A symbol stands for a larger idea; a motif is anything that recurs across the work. A recurring
    symbol is both.
12. The HL stimulus is "an excerpt from one of the two literary works studied", so it can come from
    either.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Spending most of the time retelling the plot around the extract.
- Naming a technique ("there is a metaphor") with no explanation of its effect.
- Reading out long quotations instead of picking a few words and commenting on them.
- Treating a narrator's or character's opinion as the author's.
- Linking to a theme in one vague sentence without naming a topic or possible question.
- Choosing a theme the extract does not support, because it was the one prepared.
- Revising one work thoroughly and the other hardly at all.
- Using only simple sentences and "says" or "shows", which limits the range HL expects.
- Making sweeping claims about a culture that the text does not support.
