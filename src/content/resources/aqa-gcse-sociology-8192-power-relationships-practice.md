## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of the following is one of Walby's six structures of patriarchy?

A. The mass media
B. Patriarchal relations of production
C. The education system
D. Organised religion

**[1]**

**2.** In Walby's theory, the direct control of women by their fathers or husbands is called:

A. Public patriarchy
B. Private patriarchy
C. Patriarchal culture
D. Male domination of the state

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "patriarchy". **[2]**

**4.** Describe what Walby meant by "double standards" in relation to sexuality. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** way in which social class can affect power relationships at work. **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** way in which disability can affect power relationships. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Hartsfield Council (a fictional local authority): staff by grade and sex, 2025**

| Grade | Women | Men | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Senior management | 6 | 14 | 20 |
| Middle management | 45 | 35 | 80 |
| Administrative and support | 320 | 80 | 400 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of senior managers who are women. **[1]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain how the data could support Walby's idea of public patriarchy. **[3]**

**8.** Read the item.

> Sara and Dev both work full time. Dev earns more and decides how most of the household money is spent. Sara does most of the cooking, cleaning and childcare. When Sara suggested Dev should do more, he said, "That's just how my parents did it."

Using the item, explain **two** of Walby's structures of patriarchy. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which age can affect power relationships. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a Marxist and a functionalist would each interpret unequal power between men and women. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that equality laws have removed patriarchy from UK society. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that social class is more important than gender in shaping power relationships. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B. Patriarchal relations of production [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* the other options are agencies of socialisation you meet elsewhere; Walby's list is paid work, relations of production, culture, sexuality, male violence and the state.

**2.** B. Private patriarchy [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "private" is the home; "public" is the workplace and public life.

**3.** Male domination of society [1], in which men hold more power than women in areas such as the family, work, politics and culture [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the second mark needs where or how the domination happens.

**4.** The same sexual behaviour is judged differently in men and women [1]: for example, men with multiple partners are often admired by other men, while women with multiple partners are frequently condemned [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the example from the specification's summary secures the second mark.

**5.** Employers and managers usually control decisions about pay, hours and jobs [1], while employees, especially those in routine or insecure jobs, have little say [1]. This means owners and managers can shape workers' income and security, which Marxists see as the central power relationship, although trade unions can help workers act collectively [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* explain who holds the power and how it is used, not just "the rich have more power".

**6.** Disabled people may face physical barriers and employer attitudes that limit access to work [1], so decisions about services and support are often made for them rather than with them [1]. This reduces their influence over their own lives, though campaigns led to the Disability Discrimination Act 1995, which shifted some formal power [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* a law or campaign is useful evaluation but the marks are mainly for the mechanism.

**7. (a)** 6 ÷ 20 × 100 = 30% [1]
**(b)** Women make up most of the administrative and support staff, 320 out of 400, which is 80% [1], but only 30% of senior managers [1]. This fits public patriarchy because women have access to paid work but are concentrated in lower-status, lower-paid grades, while men hold most senior posts [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* calculate a figure for the lower grade too; comparing two percentages is stronger than describing one.

**8.** First, patriarchal relations of production: Sara does most of the cooking, cleaning and childcare even though she works full time [1], so Dev benefits from her unpaid labour in the home, as Walby argued [1]. Second, patriarchal culture: Dev justifies the arrangement with "that's how my parents did it" [1], showing social expectations that apply different standards to men and women continue to shape behaviour [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* paid work is also arguable (Dev earns more); any two structures linked to item details can score.

**9.** First, children have little formal power [1], because adults such as parents and teachers make most decisions about their lives, including where they live and go to school [1]. Second, older workers may face ageism [1], so they may find it hard to be re-employed after redundancy and have less power in the labour market, although older people's higher voting turnout can give them political influence [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* young workers on insecure contracts and lower pay is another valid point.

**10.** A Marxist would see unequal power between men and women as secondary to class [1], arguing that sexism divides workers and benefits the ruling class, for example through cheap or unpaid female labour [1]. A functionalist would see men's and women's different roles as complementary [1], as Parsons described instrumental and expressive roles in the family, so the difference is functional rather than a sign of domination [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Marxists say it is capitalism's fault" is too vague; explain how class benefits.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Patriarchy means male domination of society [1].
- Laws such as the Equal Pay Act 1970, the Sex Discrimination Act 1975 and the Equality Act 2010 give women legal protection [1].
- Women's entry into higher education and professions shows real change [1].
- Liberal feminists argue laws and changing attitudes bring gradual progress [1].
- Walby argues the state is less patriarchal than before [1].
- But Walby says equal opportunities laws are seldom enforced and women generally receive lower wages [1].
- Paid work: women's opportunities are still restricted by cultural values about wives and mothers [1].
- Patriarchal relations of production: men still benefit from women's unpaid domestic labour, which laws do not reach [1].
- Male violence and double standards in sexuality are not removed by equality laws [1].
- Walby's shift from private to public patriarchy shows patriarchy has changed form rather than disappeared [1].
- Critics note that class and ethnicity mean some women have gained far more than others [1].
- Judgement: laws have reduced formal discrimination, but informal and domestic structures remain, so patriarchy has been reshaped, not removed [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers show which of Walby's structures laws can reach and which they cannot.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Marxists see class, rooted in ownership of the means of production, as the key power relationship [1].
- Owners and managers control jobs, pay and conditions [1].
- Wealth gives informal power through donations, lobbying and media ownership [1].
- Marxists argue gender divisions weaken working-class unity and benefit the ruling class [1].
- Feminists argue gender is the key power relationship across all classes [1].
- Walby's six structures show male domination at work, at home, in culture and in the state [1].
- Male violence and unpaid domestic labour affect women of every class [1].
- Women are under-represented in senior posts, even in middle-class professions [1].
- Weber suggests power has several sources, including status and party, not class alone [1].
- Race, age, disability and sexuality also shape power and overlap with class and gender [1].
- A wealthy woman may hold more power than a working-class man, showing class can outweigh gender in some settings [1].
- Judgement: class and gender both shape power and interact, so neither is simply "more important"; the answer depends on the setting [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* comparison questions reward direct weighing; avoid two separate essays on class and gender.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing Walby's structures without linking them to the item.
- Forgetting the private to public shift.
- Treating equality laws as proof that inequality has ended.
- Writing only about gender when the question names another factor.
- Giving a percentage without the working.
