## Section A

**1.** Name the four stages of water treatment. **[4]**

**2.** State the key difference between bioaccumulation and biomagnification. **[2]**

**3.** Name the vector responsible for spreading malaria. **[1]**

## Section B

**4.** A coastal region builds a desalination plant using reverse osmosis to supplement its potable
water supply.

**(a)** Briefly describe how reverse osmosis desalination works. **[2]**
**(b)** Discuss one benefit and one limitation of using desalination as a water source. **[4]**

**5.** Agricultural fertiliser runoff enters a lake, and over the following weeks the lake develops a
thick algal bloom, and fish begin dying.

**(a)** Name the process being described. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain, as a causal chain, how the fertiliser runoff leads to the fish dying. **[4]**

**6.** A government is comparing strategies to control malaria and cholera in a rural region.

**(a)** State one control strategy specific to malaria and one specific to cholera. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why these two diseases require different control strategies. **[3]**

**7.** A country builds a new multipurpose dam on a major river.

**(a)** State two uses of the dam from the list covered in this topic. **[2]**
**(b)** For one use named, state a corresponding limitation. **[2]**

**8.** Overfishing has severely depleted wild fish stocks in a coastal fishing community, which turns
to marine aquaculture as an alternative.

**(a)** Define marine aquaculture. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this shift could help address the underlying problem of overfishing. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the difference between a "store" and a "transfer" in the water cycle, giving one
example of each. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Screening, sedimentation, filtration, chlorination [4].

**2.** Bioaccumulation is a toxin building up within a single organism over time; biomagnification is
that concentration increasing further as it passes up a food chain from prey to predator [2].

**3.** The female Anopheles mosquito [1].

**4. (a)** Reverse osmosis forces salt water through a semi-permeable membrane under pressure, which
allows water molecules through while blocking salt and other dissolved substances, producing fresh
water on the other side [2].
**(b)** Benefit: desalination provides a water source independent of rainfall, increasing water
security in regions with limited fresh water availability [1–2]. Limitation: desalination requires
significant energy input, making it costly to operate and potentially environmentally impactful
depending on the energy source used [1–2].

**5. (a)** Eutrophication [1].
**(b)** Fertiliser runoff introduces excess nutrients into the lake (nutrient enrichment) [1], which
causes rapid, excessive growth of algae on the water's surface (an algal bloom) [1]. The dense algal
bloom blocks sunlight from reaching plants below the surface, and as the algae eventually die and
decompose, the decomposition process consumes dissolved oxygen in the water [1], depleting oxygen
levels to the point where fish and other aquatic organisms cannot survive [1].

**6. (a)** Malaria: personal protection (such as bed nets) or vector control (such as insecticide
spraying) [1]. Cholera: sanitation, safe potable water supply, or vaccination [1].
**(b)** Malaria is a vector-borne disease transmitted by mosquitoes, so its control strategies target
the mosquito vector and prevent bites [1–2]. Cholera is a waterborne disease spread through
contaminated water and poor sanitation, so its control strategies instead target water quality and
hygiene rather than an insect vector — the two diseases spread by entirely different mechanisms and
therefore require different control approaches [1].

**7. (a)** Any two of: flood control, hydro-electric power, irrigation, storage, transport,
recreation, tourism, fish farming [2].
**(b)** For example, hydro-electric power provides a renewable energy source, but the dam's
construction can disrupt river ecosystems and displace downstream communities [2]. (Any correctly
paired use-and-limitation combination from the syllabus gains full credit.)

**8. (a)** Marine aquaculture is the farming of marine species, such as fish, crustaceans or seaweeds,
in captivity, rather than harvesting them from the wild [2].
**(b)** Farming marine species in captivity reduces the community's dependence on catching wild fish
directly from already-depleted stocks [1–2], allowing wild populations a greater opportunity to
recover over time while the community's demand for seafood continues to be met through farmed
sources instead [1–2].

**9.** A store is a place where water is held for a period of time, such as ice sheets/glaciers, the
oceans, or ground water [2]. A transfer is the movement of water from one store to another, such as
precipitation (water moving from the atmosphere to the surface) or evaporation (water moving from a
surface store to the atmosphere) [2].

## A note on exam technique for this topic

Question 7 illustrates the study guide's core advice for multipurpose dam questions: use the full list
of named uses as a structure, pairing each benefit named with its corresponding documented limitation,
rather than only listing benefits. Question 6 rewards keeping malaria and cholera control strategies
strictly separate by transmission mechanism — vector control belongs to malaria, sanitation-based
measures belong to cholera — since mixing the two up is the most common error the study guide flags
for this sub-topic. Question 9 rewards the same store-versus-transfer discipline the study guide recommends for revising the water cycle: learning both as two separate, linked lists, rather than trying to hold the whole cycle as a single undifferentiated diagram.
