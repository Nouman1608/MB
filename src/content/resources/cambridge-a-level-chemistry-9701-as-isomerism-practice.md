## Answers

**1.** Compounds with the same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space [1].

**2. (a)** Chain isomerism [1].

**(b)** Positional isomerism [1].

**(c)** Functional group isomerism [1].

**3.** Restricted rotation about the C=C (because of the π bond) [1]; each carbon of the C=C bonded to
two different atoms or groups [1].

**4.** C2, the central carbon, bonded to H, CH₃, NH₂ and COOH [1].

**5.** Pent-2-ene [1] and 1-chloropropene [1]. (Pent-1-ene has a =CH₂ end and 2-methylbut-2-ene has two
CH₃ groups on one carbon, so neither shows cis/trans isomerism.)

**6. (a)** Pentane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ [1]; 2-methylbutane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ [1]; 2,2-dimethylpropane,
C(CH₃)₄ [1].

**(b)** 2,2-dimethylpropane [1]: it is the most branched and most compact, so it has the fewest points of
contact between molecules and the weakest instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces to overcome [1].

**7. (a)** Propanoic acid, CH₃CH₂COOH [1]; methyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₃ [1]; ethyl methanoate,
HCOOCH₂CH₃ [1].

**(b)** Functional group isomerism [1].

**(c)** Add sodium carbonate (or sodium hydrogencarbonate) solution [1]; the carboxylic acid gives
effervescence of CO₂ (turns limewater milky), while the esters give no effervescence [1].

**8. (a)** 1-bromobutane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br [1]; 2-bromobutane, CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃ [1];
1-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Br [1]; 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CBr [1].

**(b)** 2-bromobutane, whose C2 carries H, Br, CH₃ and C₂H₅ [1]; the two enantiomers are
non-superimposable mirror images of each other [1].

**9.** The C=C contains a π bond formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals [1]. Rotation about the C=C
would break this overlap, so rotation is restricted [1]. Each carbon of the C=C carries two different
groups (H and CH₃), so the CH₃ groups can be fixed on the same side (cis) or on opposite sides (trans)
[1].

**10. (a)** C4, the carbon bonded to Cl (it carries H, Cl, CH₃ and CH=CHCH₃) [1].

**(b)** There is restricted rotation about the C=C because of the π bond [1], and each C=C carbon carries
two different groups: C2 has H and CH₃, C3 has H and CHClCH₃ [1].

**(c)** Four [1].

**11. (a)** No: C1 carries H, CH₃ and two identical ring paths [1].

**(b)** Yes: C3 carries H, CH₃, a ring path that reaches the C=O after one carbon and a ring path that
reaches it after three carbons, so all four groups differ [1].

**(c)** No: C1 carries H, OH and two identical ring paths [1].

**(d)** No: C4 lies on the symmetry line of the ring, so its two ring paths are identical [1].

**12.** 3-chloropropene, CH₂=CHCH₂Cl [1]; 2-chloropropene, CH₂=CClCH₃ [1]; cis-1-chloropropene [1];
trans-1-chloropropene [1] (ClCH=CHCH₃ is the only one with two different groups on each C=C carbon).

**13. (a)** Butanal, CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO [1]; 2-methylpropanal, (CH₃)₂CHCHO [1]; butanone, CH₃COCH₂CH₃ [1].

**(b)** Butanal and 2-methylpropanal: chain isomerism [1]. Butanal and butanone: functional group
isomerism [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining restricted rotation without mentioning the π bond or the sideways overlap of p orbitals.
- Missing isomers by not working systematically, or listing the same isomer twice under two names.
- Calling a carbon chiral when two of its groups are identical further along the chain or ring.
- Giving cis/trans isomers for an alkene with a =CH₂ or =C(CH₃)₂ end.
- Forgetting that stereoisomers of one structural isomer must be counted separately when the question
  says "including stereoisomers".

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I check a ring carbon for chirality?**
Walk round the ring in both directions from the carbon. If the two paths meet the same atoms in the same
order, they are identical and the carbon is not chiral; if they differ, they count as two different
groups.

**How many stereoisomers does a molecule with one chiral centre and one cis/trans C=C have?**
Four: the cis and trans forms each exist as a pair of enantiomers.
