## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a city proper and a metropolitan area. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between gross density and net residential density. **[2]**

**3.** Define the peak land value intersection. **[1]**

**4.** Describe two differences between the core and the frame of a CBD. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why comparisons of urbanisation between countries can be unreliable. **[2]**

**6.** Outline what is meant by vertical zoning in a CBD. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A district covers 8 km squared and has 96,000 residents. Housing occupies 4.8 km squared.

**(a)** Calculate the gross density. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the net residential density. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why a planner might prefer the net figure when deciding where to build a new school. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows population density along a transect from a city centre.

| Distance from centre (km) | 0 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Density in 1990 (people per km squared) | 25,000 | 15,000 | 9,000 | 5,400 | 3,240 |
| Density in 2020 (people per km squared) | 18,000 | 14,000 | 10,500 | 7,500 | 5,000 |

**(a)** Show that the 1990 gradient is exponential. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how the gradient changed between 1990 and 2020. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest two reasons for this change. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how the CBD can migrate over time. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why models based on a single CBD may not fit an urban region such as a megalopolis. **[3]**

## Section C

**11.** With reference to named examples, compare the characteristics of urban areas in countries at different levels of economic development. **[10]**

**12.** "Models of urban structure are of limited use for understanding real cities." To what extent do you agree? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A city proper is the area governed by the city's own administration [1]; a metropolitan area is the wider region including the built-up area and surrounding places linked to it by daily commuting, so it is usually much larger [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Gross density divides population by the total area of a district, including roads, parks and industry [1]; net residential density divides population by the area used only for housing [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The most accessible point in the city, usually in the CBD where main routes meet, with the highest land values [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two, for example: the core contains department stores, flagship shops and head offices, while the frame contains car parks, warehouses and transport terminals [1]; land values and building heights are highest in the core and lower in the frame [1]; the core is busiest for pedestrians, the frame for vehicles and servicing [1]. Maximum [2]. **[2]**

**5.** Countries use different definitions of urban, based on minimum population, density, administrative status or employment [1], so the same settlement might count as urban in one country and rural in another [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Different uses occupy different floors of the same building [1], with shops at street level where footfall is greatest and offices or housing on the floors above [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 96,000 / 8 = **12,000 people per km squared** [1].

**(b)** 96,000 / 4.8 = **20,000 people per km squared** [1].

**(c)** The net figure shows how concentrated families are in the areas where they actually live [1], so it better indicates where demand for school places will be greatest within the district [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Each 3 km step multiplies density by the same factor: 15,000 / 25,000 = 0.6, 9,000 / 15,000 = 0.6 and 5,400 / 9,000 = 0.6 [1], so density falls by a constant proportion rather than a constant amount, which is exponential [1].

**(b)** The gradient became flatter [1]: central density fell from 25,000 to 18,000, while density at 12 km rose from 3,240 to 5,000 [1].

**(c)** Any two, for example: suburbanisation as households moved outwards for space [1]; better roads and public transport made outer areas accessible [1]; conversion of central housing to offices and shops [1]; new housing estates or informal settlements at the edge [1]. Maximum [2]. **[6]**

**9.** The CBD expands towards higher-status districts, where shops and offices take over attractive buildings: the zone of assimilation [1]. On the opposite side, older premises are abandoned or used for lower-value activities: the zone of discard [1]. Changes in transport, such as a new station, can also shift the point of greatest accessibility and pull the CBD towards it [1]. **[3]**

**10.** A megalopolis contains many cities and centres whose built-up areas and commuter belts have merged [1], so there is no single point of maximum accessibility around which land use is arranged [1]; people commute between several centres, so patterns of land value and density form multiple peaks [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Growth rates:** cities in many lower-income countries, such as Lagos or Dhaka, grow fast through migration and natural increase, while many in high-income countries grow slowly or have stopped, such as Tokyo [1] [1].
- **Housing:** informal settlements, such as Dharavi in Mumbai, are much more common in lower-income cities [1].
- **Economy:** a large informal sector in lower-income cities; finance and knowledge sectors dominant in world cities such as London [1] [1].
- **Spatial structure:** wealth often decreases outwards in Latin American cities, while in many US cities poorer districts are nearer the centre [1].
- **Density:** central densities often remain very high in lower-income cities; gradients have flattened in car-oriented cities [1].
- **Primacy:** primate cities such as Bangkok or Lima are common where development has been centralised [1].
- **Similarities:** high CBD land values and the rise of edge developments occur in many cities [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, level of development explains much of the variation, but history, physical site and planning make each city distinctive [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Uses of models:** they simplify, allow comparison and generate testable predictions [1]; bid-rent theory explains the CBD and density gradients in many cities [1].
- **Burgess:** fits parts of early twentieth-century Chicago, but assumes flat land and a single centre [1].
- **Hoyt:** explains sectors along transport routes and attractive land, as in coastal high-status districts [1].
- **Multiple nuclei:** fits car-based cities and urban regions better [1].
- **Latin American model:** adapts the ideas to cities with peripheral informal settlements [1].
- **Limits:** physical site, history, planning controls and inequality distort the patterns, as in Rio de Janeiro where favelas sit beside wealthy districts [1] [1].
- **Change over time:** models describe cities at one stage and age quickly as transport and economies change [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, agree only partly: models are limited as descriptions of any one city but useful as frameworks for explaining departures from them [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "to what extent" questions on models reward testing each model against a named city rather than describing models in turn.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating net density with the total area.
- Describing a density change without quoting values.
- Comparing cities without stating the criteria used.
- Describing models without saying where they fit and fail.
