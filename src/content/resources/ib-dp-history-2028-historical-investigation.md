The **historical investigation** is the internal assessment (IA) of IB DP History for first assessment in 2028. You investigate a historical topic of your choice. You formulate an inquiry question, identify and choose sources, synthesise information, and evaluate sources to produce a response. Your teacher marks it and the IB moderates the marks externally. The task, word limit and criteria are the same at SL and HL, but the weighting differs. This guide explains what the guide requires, how each criterion works and how to plan the investigation.

## The task at a glance

| Feature | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Time | About 20 hours of teaching and working time |
| Weighting | 30 per cent of the final assessment at SL; 20 per cent at HL |
| Total marks | 24 |
| Word limit | No more than 2,200 words (references, headings and bibliography do not count) |
| Structure | One document with three sections and a bibliography |
| Sources | A maximum of seven historical sources |
| Work | Individual; group work is not allowed |
| Skill emphasis | Asking questions |

The word count must be stated clearly at the start of the document. If the work goes over the limit, the marker stops reading at the limit and assesses only what comes before it.

## The three sections and the criteria

| Section | Suggested words | Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Historical inquiry question | 400 | A | 6 |
| 2. Sources and perspectives | 400 | B | 6 |
| 3. Synthesis and evaluation | 1,400 | C | 12 |
| Total | 2,200 | | 24 |

The word allocations are suggestions only. Criterion C carries half the marks, so a response that spends most of its words on sections 1 and 2 will be unbalanced.

## Choosing a topic

The topic is your choice, and your teacher must approve it before you begin. Teachers should not choose topics for students. The guide sets few limits:

- there are **no regional constraints**: you may choose any part of the world, including regions you have not studied;
- the topic **need not be connected to the syllabus**;
- there are **no date constraints**: you may study the period before 750 CE;
- a **contemporary** topic (roughly the past two decades) is allowed, but it must be treated historically, which means that you can study causes but not consequences that cannot yet be judged;
- you need access to **sufficient and appropriate sources**, and the topic must be manageable within the time and the word limit;
- you must be aware of **ethical considerations**, such as sensitivity and confidentiality.

A good topic is one that interests you and about which there are different views and enough sources. A topic is too broad if you cannot cover it in about 1,400 words of analysis. For example, "the Roman Empire" is too broad, while "the reasons for the rapid spread of the Antonine Plague in Roman cities" would need sources with different interpretations. (Both are invented illustrations, not recommended questions.)

## Section 1: the historical inquiry question (criterion A, 6 marks)

You are assessed on how well you formulate an appropriate and conceptual question. The question must be:

- **focused**: narrow enough for the word limit;
- **analytical**, not descriptive or narrative, so it leads to a judgement;
- **historical**: it asks about the past and uses historical thinking, including diverse perspectives;
- **conceptual**: it is guided by a concept.

The four specified concepts (cause and consequence, continuity and change, perspectives, significance) are a useful starting point, but you may use another concept, such as power, identity or revolution. In the section you should:

1. **state the question clearly**;
2. **explain why it is appropriate** for historical inquiry (for example, that it leads to analysis and invites different perspectives, or how a contemporary topic is treated historically);
3. **explain how the question is conceptual** and how that concept informs the inquiry.

For the top level (5 to 6 marks), the explanation is full and the references to the concept are precise and detailed, with a clear account of how the concept shapes the inquiry. At the lowest levels the question is unclear, the work is descriptive and any reference to a concept is vague or generic.

**Examples of weaker and stronger questions** (invented for illustration):

- Weak: "What was the Haitian Revolution?" (descriptive; no concept)
- Better: "To what extent was the influence of the French Revolution the main cause of the Haitian Revolution between 1789 and 1804?" (cause and consequence; a judgement is required)
- Better: "How far did life for ordinary Japanese farmers change in the first twenty years after the Meiji Restoration?" (continuity and change, and a clear scope)

You are encouraged to redraft your question as you learn more, so allow time for this.

## Section 2: sources and perspectives (criterion B, 6 marks)

You are assessed on how you identify and choose sources that offer **diverse perspectives**. You choose **no more than seven** sources. More than seven is likely to be self-penalising, because you cannot explain them all in section 2 or use them all in section 3.

In this section you should:

- explain how your selection of sources provides diverse historical perspectives;
- explain how those perspectives will help you answer your inquiry question.

The guide says there is no prescribed format, but recommends organising your response around **perspectives**, not giving a source-by-source account. Refer to specific sources as needed. A strong section might say: "Two sources represent the view of the colonial administration, two represent the view of those who resisted, and three are later historians' interpretations. Together they allow me to compare ...". Make sure the sources you choose in section 2 are the ones you use in section 3. The guide calls this alignment a crucial element.

A **source** is any object historians can use (written, visual, diagrammatic, multimodal or material). **Evidence** is the information you take from it. Keep these terms distinct.

At the middle levels (3 to 4 marks), you explain how the sources provide diverse perspectives and partly explain how these help with the question. At the top levels (5 to 6 marks), the explanation is well developed and clear. At the lowest levels (1 to 2 marks), you identify or describe sources with limited reference to how they will be used.

## Section 3: synthesis and evaluation (criterion C, 12 marks)

This is the largest section. You **synthesise** evidence from the sources to support an analytical response, **evaluate** the sources as appropriate, and reach a **reasoned judgement**.

- **Synthesis** means combining evidence from different sources to build an argument. You do not have to use every source equally.
- **Evaluation** means considering how context (such as author, date, audience and purpose) shapes how a source can be used to answer the question. Not every source needs separate evaluation, but targeted evaluation should inform your argument.
- **Judgement** is your answer to the question. It must be consistent with your analysis.

The levels differ in the following ways. At 1 to 3 marks the work is generalisation and assertion, with limited reference to sources and no clear judgement. At 4 to 6 marks it is mostly descriptive, with some analysis and minimal source evaluation. At 7 to 9 it is mostly analytical, supported by evidence, with partial evaluation and a consistent judgement. At 10 to 12 it is sustained analysis focused on the question, fully supported by synthesised evidence, with evaluation integrated into the argument and a reasoned judgement.

A practical structure is to organise paragraphs by argument or theme, not by source. Each paragraph can make a claim, bring in evidence from two or more sources, comment on the reliability or usefulness of those sources in the context of the claim, and link back to the question.

## Referencing, bibliography and integrity

Reference all sources accurately, using any standard system. The guide says accurate referencing is required but not assessed. Include a bibliography of the (maximum seven) sources, which is not counted in the word limit and not assessed. Your work must be your own. Your teacher will read one draft and give advice but will not edit it. The next version you hand in must be the final version. You must confirm authenticity. The same work cannot be used for both the IA and the extended essay.

## A suggested plan

1. **Weeks 1 to 2:** explore interests, read widely, draft possible questions, discuss with your teacher, get approval.
2. **Weeks 3 to 5:** search for sources and perspectives; keep notes on origin, date, audience and purpose; narrow the question.
3. **Weeks 6 to 7:** choose your sources (up to seven) and write sections 1 and 2.
4. **Weeks 8 to 10:** plan and write section 3; submit a draft for one round of advice.
5. **Weeks 11 to 12:** revise, check the word count, references and bibliography, and submit the final version.

(This timeline is only a suggestion; your school sets its own deadlines.)

## Common mistakes

- A question that is too broad or descriptive.
- Naming a concept but not explaining how it informs the inquiry.
- A source-by-source summary in section 2.
- Choosing sources that do not provide different perspectives.
- Using more than seven sources.
- Describing events in section 3 instead of arguing.
- Evaluating sources in isolation from the argument ("this source is biased" with no link to the question).
- A judgement that does not follow from the analysis.
- Exceeding 2,200 words.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I choose a topic from my own country's history?** Yes. The guide encourages topics of personal and local interest.

**Is the IA the same at SL and HL?** Yes. The requirements, criteria and word limit are the same. Only the weighting differs.

**Can I change my question during the project?** Yes. The guide encourages redrafting as you learn more.
