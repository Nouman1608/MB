## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a biodegradable pollutant and a persistent pollutant. **[2]**

**2.** Outline why a BOD sample is sealed and kept in the dark for five days. **[2]**

**3.** Define *limiting nutrient*. **[1]**

**4.** State two ways in which microplastics enter the oceans. **[2]**

**5.** Outline how acid mine drainage harms aquatic organisms. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why lakes may remain eutrophic for years after external phosphorus inputs have been reduced. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) Water samples were taken from four sites on a fictional river. A dairy processing plant discharges between Sites 1 and 2.

| Site | Distance below plant / km | DO day 0 / mg L⁻¹ | DO day 5 / mg L⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (upstream) | −1 | 9.4 | 8.2 |
| 2 | 0.5 | 7.1 | 1.3 |
| 3 | 3 | 6.0 | 2.8 |
| 4 | 8 | 8.5 | 6.9 |

**(a)** Calculate BOD₅ for each site. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the pattern of BOD₅ downstream of the plant. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the Site 2 result may underestimate the true BOD. **[2]**
**(d)** State the purpose of Site 1. **[1]**

**8.** (Data response) Two fictional rivers flow into the same lake.

| River | Phosphate / mg L⁻¹ | Discharge / m³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| North | 0.30 | 15 |
| East | 0.90 | 2 |

**(a)** Calculate the phosphate load of each river in kg per day. **[3]**
**(b)** A council proposes treating only the East river because it is "more polluted". Evaluate this proposal using your answers. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why loads measured only in dry weather may underestimate the phosphate entering the lake. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how warm water discharged from a power station can reduce the diversity of invertebrates in a river. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how mercury released into Minamata Bay led to disease in people living around it. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate strategies for reducing plastic pollution in the oceans, with reference to the three levels of pollution management. **[6]**

**12.** "Point-source water pollution has largely been solved; non-point pollution is now the main challenge." To what extent do you agree? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A biodegradable pollutant can be broken down by decomposers into simpler substances [1]; a persistent pollutant breaks down very slowly or not at all, so it accumulates in the environment and organisms [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Sealing stops oxygen entering from the air, and darkness stops photosynthesis producing oxygen [1], so the fall in dissolved oxygen is due only to microorganisms respiring as they decompose organic matter [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The nutrient in shortest supply relative to the organisms' needs, which therefore restricts their growth [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two: breakdown of larger plastic items by sunlight and waves [1]; fibres shed from synthetic clothing in wastewater [1]; tyre wear washed off roads; industrial pellets lost in transport; microbeads from cosmetics. **[2]**

**5.** Water flowing through exposed sulfide minerals becomes acidic, lowering river pH below what many organisms tolerate [1]; the acid dissolves metals such as iron, aluminium or copper, which are toxic to fish and invertebrates [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Phosphorus from earlier inputs is stored in the lake-bed sediments [1]. When bottom water loses oxygen, this phosphorus is released back into the water (internal loading) [1], so algal growth continues to be fuelled, keeping oxygen low and releasing more phosphorus, a positive feedback [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Site 1: **1.2**; Site 2: **5.8**; Site 3: **3.2**; Site 4: **1.6 mg L⁻¹** (2 marks for all four, 1 mark for two or three correct) [2].
**(b)** BOD rises sharply just below the plant (from 1.2 to 5.8 mg L⁻¹) because organic waste such as milk residues enters the river [1]. It then falls downstream (3.2 at 3 km, 1.6 at 8 km) as decomposers break down the organic matter [1] and the waste is diluted by tributaries, so less remains to decompose [1].
**(c)** Dissolved oxygen fell to 1.3 mg L⁻¹ by day 5, so oxygen may have run low before the end [1]; microbial respiration would then slow or stop, so the sample should have been diluted first [1].
**(d)** It is a control, showing the river's BOD before the discharge [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** North: 0.30 g m⁻³ × 15 m³ s⁻¹ = 4.5 g s⁻¹ [1]; × 86 400 = **388.8 kg per day** [1]. East: 0.90 × 2 = 1.8 g s⁻¹ × 86 400 = **155.5 kg per day** [1].
**(b)** The East river has the higher concentration, but the North river delivers about 2.5 times the load because its discharge is much greater [1]; treating only the East river would leave most of the phosphate entering the lake, so the proposal is poorly targeted [1].
**(c)** Rain increases discharge [1] and washes fertiliser and soil-bound phosphate off fields, often raising concentration as well, so most of the annual load may arrive during storms [1]. **[7]**

**9.** Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen [1]. Higher temperature raises the metabolic rate of invertebrates, increasing their oxygen demand [1]. Species that need cold, well-oxygenated water, such as stonefly larvae, die or leave, so only tolerant species remain and diversity falls [1]. **[3]**

**10.** A chemical factory released mercury compounds into the bay [1]. Mercury was converted to methylmercury, which is fat-soluble and persistent [1]. It bioaccumulated in organisms and biomagnified along the food chain to fish and shellfish [1]. People who ate the seafood received high doses and developed severe neurological disease (Minamata disease) [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative points, one mark each:
- Level 1 (change the activity): bans or charges on single-use plastics and design for reuse cut the amount produced [1].
- Level 1 tackles the cause and prevents microplastic formation, so it is the most effective long term [1].
- Level 2 (control release): better waste collection, deposit-return schemes, filters on washing machines and drains [1].
- Level 2 needs investment, especially in countries with limited waste infrastructure [1].
- Level 3 (clean-up): beach cleans and ghost-net removal help locally but cannot recover dispersed microplastics [1].
- Judgement: combine all three, but prioritise Level 1, because clean-up treats only the visible fraction [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so use these points as a guide:
- Point sources are single, identifiable outlets, so they can be regulated by permits and monitored [1].
- Sewage treatment and discharge limits have greatly reduced point-source pollution in many countries, e.g. phosphorus cuts that improved Lake Erie after 1972 [1].
- But many lower-income countries still discharge untreated sewage and industrial waste [1].
- Point sources from mining and industry can still cause serious incidents [1].
- Non-point pollution from farm fertiliser, livestock and urban runoff comes from many small sources [1].
- It is hard to measure, varies with rainfall and cannot easily be traced to one polluter [1].
- Non-point runoff drives major problems such as the Gulf of Mexico dead zone and the return of blooms in Lake Erie [1].
- Solutions such as buffer strips and precision fertiliser use depend on many individual landowners [1].
- Judgement: the statement is largely true for high-income countries but not globally, where point-source control is still incomplete [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reporting the day-5 oxygen reading as BOD.
- Forgetting the × 86 400 step when converting a load to a daily value.
- Comparing rivers by concentration only.
- Evaluating plastic strategies without saying which management level each belongs to.
- Agreeing or disagreeing with a statement without considering different countries.
