These notes cover the "different types of data" row of section 3.7 in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification: the usefulness of qualitative and quantitative data, and of official and non-official statistics. Read the [different types of data study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-different-types-of-data/) for full explanations and a worked source assessment, then practise with the [different types of data practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-different-types-of-data-practice/). The [research methods revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods-revision-notes/) cover all of section 3.7.

## What you must be able to do

**Assess the usefulness** of qualitative and quantitative data and of official and non-official statistics. "Assess" means judge: give strengths and limitations for a stated purpose and reach a view.

## The usefulness checklist: PVRRDSD

- **P**urpose: collected for this aim or another?
- **V**alidity: a true picture?
- **R**eliability: repeatable?
- **R**epresentativeness: who is included or missing?
- **D**efinitions: how were key terms defined?
- **S**ource: who produced it, and could they be biased?
- **D**ate: still relevant?

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Data | Information collected for research |
| Quantitative data | Numerical data: counts, percentages, rates |
| Qualitative data | Descriptive data: words, meanings, observations |
| Official statistics | Numerical data produced by government and its agencies |
| Non-official statistics | Numerical data produced by others, such as charities, universities or businesses |
| Census | Survey of the whole population, every ten years in the UK |
| Dark figure of crime | Crime that is not reported or not recorded |
| Social construct | Something shaped by social decisions rather than simply existing |
| Bias | Anything that distorts results |
| Selective use of data | Choosing only evidence that supports an argument |
| Trend | General direction of data over time |
| Personal documents | Diaries, letters and autobiographies |

## Quantitative versus qualitative

| | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| Shows | What, how many, how often | Why, how it feels, what it means |
| Strengths | Patterns, trends, comparison, reliability | Depth, meaning, validity |
| Limitations | Loses meaning; depends on definitions | Hard to compare; small samples; interpretation bias |
| Example | Divorce rate by year | Interview transcript with a divorced parent |

## Official statistics

- **Producers:** government and agencies; the ONS is the main producer.
- **Examples:** registrations of births, marriages and deaths; the census; exam results; exclusions; police recorded crime; the Crime Survey for England and Wales.
- **Hard to dispute:** registration data, which must be legally recorded.
- **Shaped by decisions:** recorded crime (victims report, police record) and exclusions (head teachers decide).
- **Strengths:** national coverage; regular; free; standardised; show trends.
- **Limitations:** definitions change; unrecorded events (dark figure); political presentation; little on causes.
- **Interactionist view:** statistics are social constructs.

## Non-official statistics

- **Producers:** universities, charities, pressure groups, think tanks, polling companies, businesses.
- **Strengths:** cover topics the state ignores; different definitions can challenge official pictures (Townsend's measure found far more poverty than the state standard).
- **Limitations:** smaller samples; less consistent over time; producer may have an interest in the result.

## Matching data to purpose

| Purpose | Most useful data |
|---|---|
| Has divorce become more common since 2000? | Official statistics on divorces |
| Why do couples divorce? | Qualitative interviews |
| How much crime is not reported? | Victim surveys, compared with recorded crime |
| How do pupils feel about setting? | Qualitative interviews or open questions |
| Do exam results differ by gender? | Official exam statistics |
| How common is hidden homelessness? | Non-official surveys by researchers or charities |

## Mini plan for an extended answer

*"Evaluate the usefulness of official statistics for studying divorce."*

1. Define official statistics; divorces are legally recorded, so counts are accurate.
2. Strength: national, annual figures show long-term trends and allow comparison by age or length of marriage.
3. Strength: free, quick and standardised.
4. Limitation: legal changes alter what is counted, so trends can reflect law rather than behaviour.
5. Limitation: figures miss separations without divorce and unhappy marriages that continue ("empty shell" marriages).
6. Limitation: numbers show how many, not why couples divorce.
7. Alternative: interviews give reasons and experiences.
8. Judgement: highly useful for scale and trends; weak for causes, so combine with qualitative data.

## Exam traps

- **Trap:** "official statistics are facts". **Fix:** many depend on decisions by people.
- **Trap:** "non-official statistics are biased". **Fix:** check who produced them and how.
- **Trap:** "qualitative data are just opinions". **Fix:** they are systematic records of meanings and behaviour.
- **Trap:** no purpose stated. **Fix:** always say "useful for..."
- **Trap:** "biased" with no explanation. **Fix:** name the interest or the missing group.

## Quick self-test

1. Give one example of quantitative data about families.
2. Give one example of qualitative data about education.
3. Who is the main producer of UK official statistics?
4. Why are registration statistics usually seen as accurate?
5. What is the dark figure of crime?
6. Why do interactionists call some official statistics social constructs?
7. Give two strengths of official statistics.
8. Give two producers of non-official statistics.
9. Give one reason non-official statistics might be biased.
10. Why might a change in definitions be a problem for trend data?
11. What is selective use of data?
12. What type of data would best show why young people join gangs, and why?

### Answers

1. The number of divorces per year, or the percentage of lone-parent households.
2. An interview transcript or observation notes about classroom behaviour.
3. The Office for National Statistics (ONS).
4. Because births, marriages and deaths must be legally registered.
5. Crime that is not reported to or not recorded by the police.
6. Because the figures depend on decisions by victims, police and officials.
7. Any two of: national coverage; collected regularly; free; standardised; show trends.
8. Any two of: universities, charities, pressure groups, think tanks, polling companies, businesses.
9. The producer may have an interest in the result, such as a charity wanting to show its cause is urgent.
10. Figures before and after the change are not measuring the same thing, so the trend may be false.
11. Choosing only the evidence that supports an argument.
12. Qualitative data, such as unstructured interviews, because they show meanings and motives in young people's own words.
