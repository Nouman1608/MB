This guide follows the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification, version 1.2, for exams from May/June 2026 onwards (first teaching 2024, first examined 2026). It covers part of section 3.4, Preparation of financial statements (topic 4): how accounting concepts shape the figures, the advantages and disadvantages of sole traders, partnerships and limited companies, and sole traders' income statements and statements of financial position for service and trading businesses. The specification lists Paper 2 as assessing content 3 to 5. All amounts are in dollars.

## What the specification asks for

| Specification wording | You must be able to |
|---|---|
| Use of concepts in preparing statements | Record other payables and receivables, depreciation (straight line and reducing balance), disposals, provision for doubtful debts, irrecoverable debts, income due, income received in advance, goods taken for own use and inventory |
| Advantages and disadvantages of different organisations | Compare sole traders, partnerships and limited companies |
| Sole traders | Prepare statements from ledger accounts and from a trial balance, with adjustments, for service and trading businesses, and comment on them |

The specification says that the FIFO, AVCO and LIFO methods of inventory valuation will not be examined. Inventory is valued at cost or net realisable value (section 3.3).

## Concepts behind the adjustments

Each adjustment applies a concept. Knowing which one lets you explain a treatment, not just perform it.

| Adjustment | Concept | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Other payables (expense owing) | Accruals | Add to the expense; liability in current liabilities |
| Other receivables (expense paid in advance) | Accruals | Deduct from the expense; asset in current assets |
| Income due / received in advance | Accruals, realisation | Add or deduct so that income belongs to the year earned |
| Depreciation | Going concern, historic cost, consistency | Spreads cost over use; a non-cash expense; keep the same method year to year |
| Irrecoverable debt | Prudence | Expense; trade receivables reduced |
| Provision for doubtful debts | Prudence | Only the change in the provision is charged |
| Inventory at cost or net realisable value | Prudence | Lower figure used, so profit is not overstated |
| Goods taken for own use | Business entity | Drawings at cost, not an expense |

Nerys Mallory runs Cobham Canvas Supplies. The year ends on 31 December 2025.

Adjustments:

1. Closing inventory at cost is 11,200. It includes goods that cost 900 but can only be sold for 600 after costs.
2. Electricity owing is 420.
3. Insurance paid in advance is 600.
4. Rent received includes 500 for next year.
5. An irrecoverable debt of 500 is to be written off, then the provision set at 5% of remaining trade receivables.
6. The vehicle is depreciated by the straight line method (residual value 4,000, five years). The fittings are depreciated by the reducing balance method at 20%.
7. Nerys took goods that cost 700 for her own use. They are still in purchases.

## Worked example 2: a service business with ledger accounts

Lowenna Delacourt runs Delacourt Piano Tuning (year end 30 June 2026). A service business has no inventory, so there is no cost of sales and the income statement starts with revenue and goes straight to expenses.

Ledger accounts show how the adjustments arise:

```
Insurance account                      Van running costs account
Paid        1,800 | Income st  1,350   Paid        4,200 | Income st  4,550
            | Other rec. c/d    450    Owing c/d     350 |
```

```
Disposal of van account
Van at cost     15,000 | Provision for depreciation  12,300
Profit to income st 700 | Bank (proceeds)              3,400
                15,700 |                             15,700

Provision for doubtful debts account
Balance c/d     288 | Balance b/d              180
                    | Income statement          108
                288 |                          288
```

Fee revenue in the trial balance is 46,800. Fees of 900 are due, and 1,200 was received in advance, so revenue is 46,800 + 900 - 1,200 = 46,500. Wages are 24,000, an irrecoverable debt is 300, and the 4% provision applies to receivables of 7,500 - 300 = 7,200. Equipment depreciation is 25% of a net book value of 6,000, which is 1,500.

| Income statement (extract) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 46,500 |
| Profit on disposal of van | 700 |
| Total income | 47,200 |
| Wages | 24,000 |
| Van running costs | 4,550 |
| Insurance | 1,350 |
| Irrecoverable debt | 300 |
| Increase in provision for doubtful debts | 108 |
| Depreciation: equipment | 1,500 |
| Total expenses | 31,808 |
| **Net profit** | **15,392** |

The disposal account compares net book value (15,000 - 12,300 = 2,700) with proceeds of 3,400. Proceeds above net book value mean a profit. A loss would be an expense.

## Goods taken for own use

The owner and the business are separate (business entity). Taking stock is drawings, valued at cost. Debit drawings, credit purchases. This lowers cost of sales, so gross profit rises, while capital falls by the same cost through drawings.

## Comparing sole traders, partnerships and limited companies

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | Owner keeps all profit and all control; quick decisions; simple to start | Unlimited liability; capital limited to the owner's own funds and borrowing; business ends or depends on one person |
| Partnership | More capital and skills; workload and risk shared | Partners usually share unlimited liability; disagreements; profit is shared |
| Limited company | Limited liability for shareholders; shares can raise large sums; continues if owners change | More rules to follow; owners may lose control to other shareholders; setting up costs more |

Match points to the case. A sole trader wanting to raise a large sum for a second site could consider a company for the capital, but the answer should weigh the loss of control and extra rules.

## A method for any trial balance question

1. Read every adjustment first and mark which accounts it touches.
2. Work out each adjusted figure on rough paper: expense charge, income earned, depreciation, change in provision, closing inventory.
3. Build the income statement from revenue down, then add any income and deduct expenses.
4. Build the statement of financial position, placing each balance under non-current assets, current assets, current liabilities or non-current liabilities.
5. Roll capital forward: opening capital plus net profit less drawings. If net assets differ from closing capital, look for an adjustment entered once instead of twice.

## Commenting on the statements

The specification asks you to comment, so numbers alone do not earn full credit. Say what the figure shows, then why it matters to the owner. Useful angles:

- Profit against drawings: if drawings exceed net profit, capital is falling, so the owner is taking out more than the business earns.
- Gross profit against revenue: a low or falling share may mean selling prices are too low or purchase costs are rising.
- Net current assets: positive figures suggest short-term debts can be paid, but check whether the cash sits in inventory that may not sell quickly.
- Long-term borrowing: a loan repayable in several years is a non-current liability and has less effect on short-term cash.

## Common errors

- Charging the whole provision for doubtful debts to the income statement instead of the change.
- Treating goods taken for own use as an expense or leaving them in cost of sales.
- Adding a prepaid expense to the expense instead of deducting it.
- Valuing inventory at selling price or at cost when net realisable value is lower.
- Forgetting that net profit is added to capital and drawings deducted.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which accounting concepts explain the year-end adjustments?**
Accruals explains expenses owing and paid in advance, depreciation draws on going concern, historic cost and consistency, prudence explains irrecoverable debts, provisions for doubtful debts and valuing inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value, and business entity explains treating goods taken by the owner as drawings.

**How do you calculate profit or loss on disposal of a non-current asset?**
Compare the net book value of the asset (cost less accumulated depreciation) with the proceeds of sale. If proceeds exceed net book value there is a profit on disposal; if they are lower there is a loss, which is shown in the income statement.

**What are the advantages of a limited company over a sole trader?**
A limited company gives its shareholders limited liability, can raise larger amounts of capital through shares and loans, and continues if an owner leaves. A sole trader keeps all the profit and makes quick decisions alone, but has unlimited liability and limited sources of finance.

**What should you write when asked to comment on a sole trader's financial statements?**
Use the figures to make a judgement, for example comparing gross and net profit margins or comparing drawings with net profit. If drawings exceed net profit, the owner's capital falls, which is a point a marker would expect you to state and explain.
