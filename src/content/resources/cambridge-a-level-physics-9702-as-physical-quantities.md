This guide covers subtopic **1.1 Physical quantities** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series. It is the shortest subtopic in the syllabus, but estimates are often tested in the multiple-choice paper, and the idea that a quantity is "a number and a unit" underlies every table, graph and calculation you write. ## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL PHYSICS 9702 — AS Level, Topic 1.1**

Candidates should be able to:

1. understand that all physical quantities consist of a numerical magnitude and a unit;
2. make reasonable estimates of physical quantities included within the syllabus.

## A quantity is a number multiplied by a unit

Writing l = 2.5 m means the length is 2.5 multiplied by one metre. The unit is not a label stuck on the end; it is a factor in the value. Three consequences follow.

**1. Changing the unit changes the number, not the quantity.** A speed of 72 km h⁻¹ and a speed of 20 m s⁻¹ are the same quantity:

```
72 km h^-1 = 72 x (1000 m) / (3600 s) = 20 m s^-1
```

Substitute the unit as if it were an algebraic term, and the conversion looks after itself.

**2. Units obey algebra.** In p = F/A with F = 600 N and A = 0.040 m², the units divide just like the numbers: 600 N ÷ 0.040 m² = 15 000 N m⁻² = 15 000 Pa. If the units of your answer do not come out as the unit of the quantity you were asked for, the method is wrong.

**3. Dividing a quantity by its unit gives a pure number.** If l = 2.5 mm, then l / mm = 2.5 and l / m = 2.5 × 10⁻³. This is exactly why the syllabus's practical section asks for table headings and graph axes in the form *quantity / unit*: the column headed `I / mA` contains numbers such as 34, meaning I ÷ mA = 34, so I = 34 mA. The practical section also accepts "I (mA)" or "I in mA", but not a bare "mA" or "I mA", because those do not state both the quantity and its unit.

### Quantities with no unit

A ratio of two quantities of the same kind, such as efficiency, strain or a refractive index, has units that cancel. It is still a physical quantity; its value is a pure number, and writing "efficiency = 0.35 J" is wrong. A percentage is the same pure number multiplied by 100.

### Numbers without units lose marks

An answer of "4.2" to "calculate the resistance" is incomplete. Give the unit with every final answer, and carry units through the working of a "show that" question so the evidence is complete.

## Making reasonable estimates

The syllabus asks for estimates of quantities "included within the syllabus". An estimate is a value to one, or at most two, significant figures that is believable for the situation. Two skills are tested: knowing typical values, and combining them in a short calculation.

### Typical values worth knowing

| Quantity | Reasonable estimate |
|---|---|
| Mass of an adult | 70 kg |
| Mass of an apple | 0.1 kg |
| Height of a room | 3 m |
| Walking speed | 1.5 m s⁻¹ |
| Speed of a car on a motorway | 30 m s⁻¹ |
| Speed of sound in air | 300–340 m s⁻¹ |
| Density of water | 1000 kg m⁻³ |
| Density of air | 1.2 kg m⁻³ |
| Atmospheric pressure | 1 × 10⁵ Pa |
| Wavelength of visible light | 400–700 nm |
| Diameter of an atom | 10⁻¹⁰ m |
| Diameter of a nucleus | 10⁻¹⁵–10⁻¹⁴ m |
| Mains supply voltage (many countries) | 230 V |

Do not memorise these as exact data. Use them as anchors: if a calculated walking speed comes out at 15 m s⁻¹, something has gone wrong.

### Worked example 1: mass of air in a classroom

Estimate the mass of air in a classroom.

```
dimensions:  8 m x 6 m x 3 m  ->  V = 144 m^3
density of air ~ 1.2 kg m^-3
m = rho V = 1.2 x 144 = 173 kg  ->  about 200 kg
```

Many students guess "a few kilograms". The estimate shows why a room full of air has a mass comparable with two or three adults.

### Worked example 2: pressure under a standing person

```
weight ~ 700 N (70 kg x 9.81 N kg^-1, rounded)
area of one shoe sole ~ 0.28 m x 0.10 m = 0.028 m^2
two shoes: A ~ 0.056 m^2
p = F / A = 700 / 0.056 = 1.25 x 10^4 Pa  ->  about 1 x 10^4 Pa
```

Each assumption is stated, so the examiner can follow the reasoning even if your shoe size differs.

### Worked example 3: useful power of a student running upstairs

A 60 kg student runs up a flight of stairs 3 m high in 4 s.

```
gain in GPE = mgh = 60 x 9.81 x 3 = 1770 J
power = 1770 / 4 = 440 W  ->  about 400 W
```

### Choosing between options in a multiple-choice estimate

Options are usually spread over several powers of ten, for example 10⁻² J, 1 J, 10² J, 10⁴ J. You do not need precision: work to one significant figure, round values freely (g ≈ 10 m s⁻², π ≈ 3), and choose the option closest to your answer. Spending long on exact arithmetic is the commonest way to waste time on these items.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the unit as decoration.** Converting 2.5 mm to metres by "moving the decimal point" without writing the factor 10⁻³ leads to errors with squared and cubed units.
- **Writing table headings as "Current (A)" in one column and "V" in the next.** Use one consistent convention, with both quantity and unit in every heading.
- **Giving an estimate to four significant figures.** An estimate built from rounded assumptions cannot justify more than one or two.
- **Estimating without stating assumptions.** In a written estimate, each assumed value is part of the answer.
- **Forgetting that ratios have no unit.** Attaching a unit to efficiency or strain is marked wrong.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Estimates** are often tested as Paper 1 multiple-choice items; in structured questions you may be asked to "determine" or "suggest" a value. State each assumed value with its unit.
- **State** a unit: one precise answer, such as "N m⁻²", nothing more.
- **Calculate** or **determine**: show the substitution with units, give the final answer with its unit and a sensible number of significant figures.
- **Show that**: give every step, including the unit of the final value, so the result is proved rather than asserted.
- **Suggest** a reasonable value: one value, with a brief justification, is enough.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does Cambridge 9702 write table headings as "quantity / unit"?**
Because a quantity divided by its unit is a pure number, which is what is written in the table or on the graph axis. A heading such as `t / s` means each entry is t ÷ s.

**How accurate should an estimate be?**
One or two significant figures. The aim is the right order of magnitude, with each assumption reasonable for the situation.

**Does every physical quantity have a unit?**
Every quantity has a magnitude and a unit, but for a ratio of like quantities, such as efficiency or strain, the units cancel and the value is a pure number.
