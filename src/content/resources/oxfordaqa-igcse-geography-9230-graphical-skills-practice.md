## Section A

**1.** Name the type of map in which areas are shaded in darker tones for higher values. **[1]**

**2.** What does an isoline join? **[1]**

**3.** State what is meant by a desire line. **[1]**

**4.** Name the graph that is best for showing the relationship between two variables. **[1]**

**5.** Why do the bars in a histogram touch each other? **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A survey asks 90 pupils how they travel to school. The results are: walk 36, bus 27, car 18, cycle 9.

**(a)** Calculate the angle for each category if the data are shown on a pie chart. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the length of each section if the data are shown on a divided bar that is 9 cm long. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest, with a reason, why a bar chart might be a better choice than a pie chart if the survey was repeated in five different schools. **[2]**

**7.** On a contour map, point A is at 250 m and point B is at 300 m. The distance between them is 2.5 km.

**(a)** Calculate the gradient between A and B, giving your answer as a ratio (1 in ...). **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how the contour lines would look on the map along a section with a steeper gradient. **[1]**

**8.** On an isobar map, the 1008 hPa and 1012 hPa lines are 4 cm apart. Point P lies between them, 1.5 cm from the 1008 hPa line. Estimate the pressure at P. **[2]**

**9.** The table shows the percentage of the population in each age group in an invented country.

| Age group | Percentage of population |
|---|---|
| 0 to 14 | 20 |
| 15 to 64 | 63 |
| 65 and over | 17 |

**(a)** Calculate the dependency ratio, as the number of dependants per 100 people of working age (15 to 64). Give your answer to the nearest whole number. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the likely shape of the population pyramid for this country. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **one** problem this age structure might cause for the government. **[2]**

**10.** A map of the regions of a country uses these classes for population density: under 25, 25 to 49, 50 to 99, 100 and over (people per km²). A region has 4 800 people in an area of 60 km².

**(a)** Calculate the population density of the region. **[1]**

**(b)** State which class it belongs to. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why a choropleth map should use density rather than total population. **[2]**

**11.** On a map of the migration of refugees, a flow line is drawn 1 mm wide for every 1 000 people. A flow of 12 500 people is to be shown.

**(a)** Calculate the width of the line. **[1]**

**(b)** The flow lines of two routes are 3 mm and 9 mm wide. Compare the two migration flows. **[2]**

**12.** On a map using proportional circles, a city of 2 million people is shown by a circle with a radius of 1 cm. The area of the circle is proportional to the number of people. Calculate the radius of the circle for a city of 8 million people. **[2]**

**13.** A dot map uses one dot to represent 5 000 people. A district has 42 000 people. How many dots should be drawn, to the nearest whole dot? **[1]**

**14.** Suggest an appropriate type of graph for each set of data and give a reason for each choice. **[4]**

- (i) Monthly temperature over one year at a weather station.
- (ii) The percentage of land used for different purposes in a town.

## Answers

**1.** Choropleth map [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Places of equal value (for example height, temperature or pressure) [1]. **[1]**

**3.** A straight line drawn between an origin and a destination to show movement, such as migration or commuting [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Scattergraph (scatter graph) [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Because the data are continuous, with no gaps between the classes [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** The total is 90, so each pupil is 360 ÷ 90 = 4 degrees [1]. Walk 36 × 4 = 144 degrees; bus 27 × 4 = 108 degrees [1]. Car 18 × 4 = 72 degrees; cycle 9 × 4 = 36 degrees [1]. (Check: 144 + 108 + 72 + 36 = 360.) **[3]**

**(b)** Each pupil is 9 ÷ 90 = 0.1 cm [1]. Walk 3.6 cm; bus 2.7 cm; car 1.8 cm; cycle 0.9 cm (all four correct) [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** A bar chart is better because it allows the categories to be compared easily between schools [1]. Five pie charts would be hard to compare, and pie charts with different totals are difficult to read [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Vertical change = 300 - 250 = 50 m [1]. 2.5 km = 2 500 m, so gradient = 50 ÷ 2 500 = 1 in 50 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The contour lines would be closer together [1]. **[1]**

**8.** The gap between the lines is 4 cm, which represents 4 hPa, so 1 hPa per cm [1]. Pressure at P = 1008 + 1.5 = 1009.5 hPa [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** Dependants = 20 + 17 = 37% [1]. Dependency ratio = 37 ÷ 63 × 100 = 58.7, so about 59 per 100 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The pyramid is fairly straight-sided with a moderate base, because 20% are under 15 [1], and a wide top with a significant proportion in the oldest groups, because 17% are aged 65 and over, typical of an HIC with a low birth rate and long life expectancy [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Any one problem, developed: a large number of elderly people means high spending on pensions and healthcare [1], while a relatively small working population pays the taxes to support them [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** 4 800 ÷ 60 = 80 people per km² [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 50 to 99 [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Totals depend on the size of each region, so large regions would appear to have more people even if they are sparsely populated [1]. Density allows fair comparison between regions of different size [1]. **[2]**

**11. (a)** 12 500 ÷ 1 000 = 12.5 mm [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** The 9 mm line represents 9 000 people and the 3 mm line 3 000 people [1]. So the first route carries three times as many people as the second [1]. **[2]**

**12.** City 8 million is 4 times the population of 2 million, so the area is 4 times as large [1]. The radius is therefore the square root of 4 times larger, so 2 × 1 = 2 cm [1]. **[2]**

**13.** 42 000 ÷ 5 000 = 8.4, so 8 dots [1]. **[1]**

**14.** (i) Line graph [1], because the data are continuous over time and a line shows the trend [1]. (ii) Pie chart (accept divided bar) [1], because it shows parts of a whole, and the percentages add up to 100 [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating pie chart angles without checking that they add to 360.
- Writing a gradient as a decimal when the question asks for a ratio.
- Using the total, not the density, for a choropleth map.
- Doubling the radius of a proportional circle when the area should double.
- Forgetting units (cm, mm, hPa, per km²).
- Choosing a graph without a reason.
