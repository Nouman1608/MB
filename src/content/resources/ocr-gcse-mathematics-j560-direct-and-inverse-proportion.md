This guide covers section 5.02, Direct and inverse proportion, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Mathematics J560 specification, Version 2.1 (August 2026), for first assessment in 2017. The first two columns, simple and more formal proportion problems, are content for **both tiers**. Proportion to a power or root of another quantity is **Higher tier only** and is labelled where it appears. Growth and decay (5.03) is not covered here.

The [direct and inverse proportion and growth and decay guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-mathematics-direct-and-inverse-proportion-and-growth-and-decay/) introduces 5.02 alongside 5.03 with one example of each case. This page goes further on 5.02 alone: testing a table for proportion, best buys, two-step currency conversion, "workers and days" inverse problems, the effect of scaling one variable, and finding a power law from data. ## What the specification asks

| Ref | Statement | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 5.02a | Solve simple problems involving quantities in direct proportion, including algebraic proportions (equality of ratios); currency conversion | Both tiers |
| 5.02a | Solve more formal problems where y ∝ x; recognise that if y = kx, where k is a constant, then y is proportional to x | Both tiers |
| 5.02a | Formulate equations and solve problems involving a quantity in direct proportion to a power or root of another | Higher tier only |
| 5.02b | Solve simple word problems involving quantities in inverse proportion (for example, if speed is doubled, time is halved) | Both tiers |
| 5.02b | Solve more formal problems where y ∝ 1/x; recognise that if y = k/x, then y is inversely proportional to x | Both tiers |
| 5.02b | Formulate equations and solve problems involving a quantity in inverse proportion to a power or root of another | Higher tier only |

## Spotting the type of proportion

| Test | Direct proportion | Inverse proportion |
|---|---|---|
| One doubles | The other doubles | The other halves |
| What stays constant | The ratio y/x | The product xy |
| Zero | x = 0 gives y = 0 | Neither quantity can be 0 |

**Worked example 1.** Decide whether each table shows direct proportion, inverse proportion or neither.

```
Table P:  x  2   5   8        y/x: 7, 7, 7          direct (y = 7x)
          y  14  35  56

Table Q:  x  2   4   10       xy: 40, 40, 40        inverse (y = 40/x)
          y  20  10  4

Table R:  x  1   2   3        y/x: 5, 3.5, 3        neither
          y  5   7   9        xy: 5, 14, 27
```

Table R increases steadily, but it starts at 5, not 0, so y is not proportional to x. A straight-line graph of a direct proportion passes through the origin.

## Direct proportion in context

### Equality of ratios and the unitary method

If y ∝ x, then y₁/x₁ = y₂/x₂. The unitary method finds the value for one unit first; the scaling method uses the multiplier between the two x values. Use whichever gives easier numbers.

**Worked example 2 (best buy).** Rice is sold in a 400 g bag for £2.60 and a 750 g bag for £4.65. Which bag is better value?

```
cost per 100 g:  400 g bag:  2.60 ÷ 4   = £0.65
                 750 g bag:  4.65 ÷ 7.5 = £0.62
```

The **750 g bag** is better value, because 100 g costs less. Compare like with like: cost per 100 g, or grams per £1, but not one of each.

### Currency in two steps

**Worked example 3.** Use the invented rates £1 = €1.15 and €1 = $1.08. Convert £250 to dollars, then convert $54 back to pounds.

```
£250 → €:   250 × 1.15 = €287.50
€ → $:      287.50 × 1.08 = $310.50

$54 → €:    54 ÷ 1.08 = €50
€ → £:      50 ÷ 1.15 = £43.48 (to the nearest penny)
```

Multiply when moving in the direction of the rate; divide when going back. A sense check helps: one pound buys more than one euro, so the euro amount is larger.

## Formal direct proportion: y = kx

**Worked example 4.** y is directly proportional to x. When x = 24, y = 18. Find y when x = 40, and x when y = 27.

```
y = kx
18 = 24k,  so k = 0.75
y = 0.75x
x = 40:  y = 30
y = 27:  27 = 0.75x, so x = 36
```

Write the equation with the value of k in it before using it. A fractional or decimal k is common.

## Inverse proportion in context

The classic structure is a fixed total of work, distance or quantity shared out.

**Worked example 5.** 4 identical pumps empty a tank in 9 hours.

```
total work = 4 × 9 = 36 pump-hours
6 pumps:     36 ÷ 6 = 6 hours
3 hours:     36 ÷ 3 = 12 pumps
```

More pumps take less time, so the answer to "how long for 6 pumps" must be under 9 hours. If it goes the wrong way, you have used direct proportion. The same reasoning gives "6 workers take 15 days, so 9 workers take 6 × 15 ÷ 9 = 10 days", assuming everyone works at the same rate.

## Formal inverse proportion: y = k/x

**Worked example 6.** p is inversely proportional to q. When q = 1.5, p = 12. Find p when q = 4, and q when p = 0.6.

```
p = k/q
12 = k/1.5,  so k = 18
p = 18/q
q = 4:    p = 4.5
p = 0.6:  0.6 = 18/q, so q = 18 ÷ 0.6 = 30
```

## Power and root proportion (Higher tier only)

The equations become y = kxⁿ or y = k/xⁿ, including y = k√x.

**Worked example 7 (Higher tier only).** y is directly proportional to the cube of x. When x = 3, y = 54. Find y when x = 5, and x when y = 16.

```
y = kx³
54 = 27k,  so k = 2
y = 2x³
x = 5:   y = 2 × 125 = 250
y = 16:  x³ = 8, so x = 2
```

**The effect of scaling (Higher tier only).** Without finding k you can say what happens when one quantity changes.

| Relationship | x is multiplied by | y is multiplied by |
|---|---|---|
| y ∝ x³ | 2 | 2³ = 8 |
| y ∝ √x | 4 | √4 = 2 |
| y ∝ 1/x² | 3 | 1/3² = 1/9 |
| y ∝ 1/√x | 9 | 1/3 |

**Worked example 8 (Higher tier only).** Find the relationship from this table.

```
x  1   2   4
y  3   12  48
```

Doubling x multiplies y by 4 each time, which is 2², so y ∝ x². Then 3 = k × 1², so k = 3 and **y = 3x²**. Check: 3 × 4² = 48.

## Common mistakes

- Using direct proportion when one quantity rises as the other falls.
- Comparing cost per gram for one item with grams per pound for another.
- Multiplying instead of dividing when converting back to the original currency.
- Finding k and then not writing the equation, so the wrong formula is used.
- Treating a steady increase that does not start at 0 as direct proportion.
- (Higher tier only) Applying the power to k as well as x, or forgetting the root when solving for x.
- (Higher tier only) Saying "y doubles" when x doubles in y ∝ x².

## Exam technique

- **Formal questions** ("y is directly proportional to x²"): write the equation, find k, write the equation with k, then use it. Each step usually earns a mark.
- **"Which is better value?"**: show both comparable values and give a clear conclusion.
- **"Explain"** for proportion: name the constant (ratio or product) or describe what happens when one quantity doubles.
- **Assumptions**: in work problems, say that everyone works at the same rate if the question asks what you assumed.
