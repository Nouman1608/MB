## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a dispositional factor affecting collective behaviour in the
specification?
A Deindividuation
B Social loafing
C Culture
D Morality **[1]**

**2.** Define the term *prosocial behaviour*. **[1]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by deindividuation. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** conditions that make deindividuation more likely. **[2]**

**5.** Outline **one** difference between social loafing and deindividuation. **[2]**

**6.** In a school charity car wash, each student in a group of twelve washes cars more slowly than
when they did a car on their own. Identify the factor and explain the students' behaviour. **[3]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Describe **one** study of deindividuation and state what it shows. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how culture can affect collective behaviour. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why deindividuation does not always lead to antisocial behaviour. Refer to research in
your answer. **[4]**

**10.** Ellie and Sam are in a large, noisy crowd at a protest. When some protesters begin throwing
bottles, Ellie joins in but Sam refuses. Use your knowledge of dispositional factors to explain the
difference in their behaviour. **[4]**

**11.** A festival organiser wants to reduce antisocial behaviour. Suggest **two** changes, explaining
how each would work, using your knowledge of factors affecting collective behaviour. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss social explanations of antisocial behaviour in crowds. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **D** [1].

**2.** Behaviour that benefits other people, such as helping or sharing [1].

**3.** A psychological state in which a person in a crowd loses their sense of individual identity and
self-awareness [1], and so feels less personally responsible for their actions, weakening normal
restraints [1].

**4.** Any two, [1] each: anonymity (for example masks, uniforms, darkness, large crowds); high
arousal (for example noise, excitement, alcohol). Maximum 2.

**5.** Any one difference, explained [2]: social loafing is reduced **effort** in a group because
individual contributions cannot be identified [1], whereas deindividuation is reduced
**self-awareness and responsibility**, which can lead to behaviour the person would not normally do
[1].

**6.** **Social loafing** [1]. In a group of twelve, each student's contribution cannot easily be
identified [1], so each puts in less effort, expecting others to do the work or feeling their own
effort makes little difference [1].

**7.** Award up to 4. For example, Zimbardo (1969): female students were made anonymous with hoods and
lab coats or made identifiable with name labels [1]; they were asked to give electric shocks (not
real) to another person [1]; anonymous participants gave shocks for about twice as long [1]. This
shows anonymity increases antisocial behaviour through deindividuation [1]. Accept Diener's
Halloween study or Mann's analysis of baiting crowds, described accurately.

**8.** One mark per point, up to 4:
- Cultures differ in the norms they set for how crowds should behave, for example at festivals,
  matches or protests [1].
- In collectivist cultures people see group goals as their own [1].
- So social loafing tends to be lower in collectivist cultures, as a review by Karau and Williams
  found [1].
- A shared identity in a crowd can produce prosocial behaviour, such as survivors helping strangers in
  emergencies [1].
Maximum 4.

**9.** One mark per point, up to 4:
- Deindividuation makes people follow the norms of the situation or group more closely [1].
- Johnson and Downing dressed participants in outfits resembling nurses' uniforms or Ku Klux Klan robes
  [1].
- Participants in nurse-like uniforms gave fewer shocks than those in their own clothes [1].
- So if the group's norms are prosocial (for example a charity event in fancy dress), deindividuation
  can increase prosocial behaviour [1].
Maximum 4.

**10.** One mark per point, up to 4:
- Ellie may have a more aggressive or impulsive **personality**, so she is more likely to join
  antisocial behaviour [1] when the crowd provides the opportunity [1].
- Sam may have strong **moral** principles about violence [1], which he still applies even though he is
  anonymous in a large crowd, so he refuses [1].
Maximum 4.

**11.** Two changes, each [1] for the change and [1] for explaining the factor it addresses:
- Increase identifiability with better lighting, cameras or visible stewards [1], reducing anonymity
  and so deindividuation [1].
- Limit alcohol sales or provide quiet areas [1], lowering arousal, which reduces deindividuation [1].
- Promote positive group norms, such as messages from performers about looking after each other [1],
  so that deindividuated crowd members follow prosocial norms [1].
Maximum 4.

**12.** Award up to 6, for description and evaluation of social explanations:
- **Deindividuation:** anonymity and arousal reduce self-awareness and responsibility, leading to
  antisocial behaviour [1].
- Supported by Zimbardo's hooded participants and Diener's trick-or-treaters [1].
- But Johnson and Downing show deindividuation follows group norms, so it does not always produce
  antisocial behaviour [1].
- **Social loafing:** in large crowds individuals contribute less to preventing harm, so antisocial
  acts by a few may go unchallenged [1].
- **Culture:** cultural norms and individualism or collectivism affect how crowds behave [1].
- Limitation: much evidence comes from artificial laboratory tasks, and social explanations cannot
  explain why individuals in the same crowd act differently, which needs dispositional factors [1].
Maximum 6.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating social loafing and deindividuation as the same thing.
- Describing a study without saying what it shows about crowd behaviour.
- In scenario questions, naming factors without linking them to the people described.
- Writing only about antisocial behaviour when the question asks about prosocial behaviour too.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the specification name any studies of crowd behaviour?**
No. It names the factors; studies such as Zimbardo's and Johnson and Downing's are supporting
evidence you can use.

**Is anonymity a factor in crowd behaviour or conformity?**
Both: in 3.2.2 it is listed as a social factor affecting conformity, and in crowds it is a cause of
deindividuation.
