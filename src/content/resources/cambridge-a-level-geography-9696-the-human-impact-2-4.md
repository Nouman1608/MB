This guide covers **2.4 The human impact** in Topic 2, Atmosphere and weather, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content is:

- **The enhanced greenhouse effect and global warming:** the evidence, possible causes and atmospheric impacts.
- **Case study:** an urban area which shows the effects of human activity on climate: **temperature (heat island), humidity, precipitation and winds**.

It is examined in Paper 1, both in Section A resource questions and in the Atmosphere and weather structured question in Section B, where the case study is often the final, highest-mark part. The [2.4 human impact revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-atmosphere-human-impact-revision-notes/) explain the greenhouse mechanism and outline London as an urban case study. This guide focuses on how to use evidence, how to weigh causes, and how to handle urban climate data. Practise with the [2.4 human impact practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-human-impact-2-4-practice/).

## The natural and the enhanced greenhouse effect

The natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth habitable: gases such as water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide let incoming shortwave radiation through but absorb part of the outgoing longwave radiation and re-radiate it, including back towards the surface. The **enhanced greenhouse effect** is the extra warming caused when human activity raises the concentration of these gases, so more longwave radiation is absorbed and the surface and lower atmosphere warm. **Global warming** is the resulting rise in global mean temperature.

## The evidence

Good answers group evidence by type and say what each shows:

- **Instrumental temperature records:** thermometer records from land stations and ships, combined into global averages, show warming since the late nineteenth century, with each recent decade warmer than the last. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) assessed the global surface temperature in 2011 to 2020 as about 1.1°C above 1850 to 1900.
- **Atmospheric composition:** continuous measurements at Mauna Loa in Hawaii since 1958 show carbon dioxide rising every year, from about 315 parts per million (ppm) at the start of the record to over 420 ppm in recent years, compared with about 280 ppm before industrialisation.
- **Ice cores:** air bubbles trapped in Antarctic and Greenland ice record past carbon dioxide levels and temperatures, showing that present concentrations are far above those of previous interglacial periods covered by the cores.
- **The cryosphere:** most mountain glaciers are retreating, Arctic summer sea ice has declined in extent since satellite records began in the late 1970s, and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass.
- **Sea level:** global mean sea level is rising, through thermal expansion of warming water and melting land ice.

The **strength** of the evidence comes from several independent sources pointing the same way. Its **limitations** are worth mentioning in evaluation: early instrumental records are sparse in the southern hemisphere and over oceans, urban growth can bias some station records (which is why scientists adjust for it), and proxy records such as ice cores have uncertainties in dating.

## Possible causes

The syllabus says "possible causes", which invites you to weigh human and natural factors.

**Human causes:**

- **Carbon dioxide** from burning coal, oil and gas for energy, transport and industry; from cement manufacture; and from **deforestation**, which releases stored carbon and removes a carbon sink.
- **Methane** from livestock, rice paddies, landfill sites and leaks from coal mining and oil and gas extraction.
- **Nitrous oxide** mainly from nitrogen fertilisers.
- **Halocarbons** such as CFCs and their replacements, powerful greenhouse gases although their concentrations are small.

**Natural factors that can change climate:**

- variations in solar output;
- large volcanic eruptions, whose sulphate aerosols cool the climate for a year or two;
- long-term changes in the Earth's orbit, which pace ice ages over tens of thousands of years.

**Weighing them:** solar output has not risen over recent decades while temperatures have; volcanic eruptions cause short-lived cooling, not sustained warming; and orbital changes act far too slowly to explain warming over a century. The IPCC concluded that human influence has unequivocally warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land. A strong answer explains why natural factors cannot account for the recent trend rather than simply dismissing them.

**Feedbacks** amplify the initial change. Warmer air holds more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas. Melting snow and ice expose darker land and sea, lowering albedo so more radiation is absorbed. These help explain why the Arctic is warming faster than the global average.

## Atmospheric impacts

Keep impacts focused on the atmosphere, as the syllabus does:

- **Higher temperatures**, with more frequent and intense heatwaves and fewer cold extremes.
- **Changes in precipitation:** warmer air can hold about 7% more water vapour per 1°C of warming, so heavy rainfall events tend to become more intense. Some dry regions may become drier while wet regions get wetter, though patterns vary.
- **Changes in storms:** the strongest tropical cyclones are expected to become more intense, with heavier rainfall, although changes in their total number are less certain.
- **Shifts in circulation:** changes to the position of wind belts and storm tracks, and to the behaviour of jet streams, are possible; these are areas of active research with more uncertainty than temperature changes.

## The urban climate case study

Choose one real urban area and learn located evidence for each of the four elements the syllabus names. Organise notes as **effect, causes, evidence and variations**:

| Element | Typical urban effect | Main causes |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Warmer than surrounding rural areas: the urban heat island, strongest at night | Heat storage in buildings and roads, low albedo, little evaporation, waste heat, trapped longwave radiation |
| Humidity | Relative humidity usually lower | Rapid drainage, little vegetation, higher temperatures |
| Precipitation | Possible increase in convectional rain and thunderstorms, especially downwind | Extra heating and uplift, condensation nuclei from pollution |
| Winds | Lower mean speeds, but strong gusts and channelling between tall buildings | Surface roughness, canyon effect |

## Worked example: describing an urban heat island transect

*Night-time air temperatures (°C) were recorded along a fictional transect on a calm, clear night, from a city centre to the countryside. A large park lies 3 km from the centre.*

| Distance from centre (km) | 0 | 2 | 3 (park) | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 14.0 | 13.2 | 11.0 | 12.1 | 10.8 | 9.5 | 9.0 |

**Heat island intensity** = urban maximum - rural value = 14.0 - 9.0 = **5.0°C**.

**Description:** temperature falls with distance from the centre, from 14.0°C to 9.0°C over 10 km. The fall is gentle near the centre (0.8°C in the first 2 km) and steeper at the urban edge (1.3°C between 4 and 6 km and between 6 and 8 km), flattening in the countryside. The park is an anomaly: at 11.0°C, it is 2.2°C cooler than the point 1 km nearer the centre.

**Explanation:** dense buildings and roads in the centre store more heat by day and release it at night; the park has vegetation and soil moisture, higher evapotranspiration and an open sky view, so it cools more quickly. Calm, clear conditions maximise the contrast; a windy, cloudy night would reduce it.

## Annotated model answer

*"With reference to an urban area you have studied, explain how human activity affects humidity and precipitation." [6]*

> In my case study city, most rain falls on impermeable roofs and roads and drains rapidly into sewers, so less water is available for evaporation **(cause)**. With few trees and little open water, evapotranspiration is low, so the air holds less vapour, and because the city is warmer, relative humidity is lower still **(effect on humidity)**. However, heat stored in buildings strengthens convection on summer afternoons **(cause)**, and pollution particles act as condensation nuclei **(cause)**, so thunderstorms can be more frequent downwind of the centre **(effect on precipitation)**. These effects are smaller and harder to measure than the heat island, because rainfall varies naturally from storm to storm **(evaluation)**.

In the exam, replace "my case study city" with the name of your city and add located detail, such as a named district or a measured difference.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the enhanced greenhouse effect with ozone depletion.
- Listing evidence without saying what each type shows.
- Treating "possible causes" as only human causes, with no evaluation of natural factors.
- Describing impacts on sea level or ecosystems when the question asks for atmospheric impacts.
- Writing the urban case study with only temperature, omitting humidity, precipitation and winds.
- Claiming the heat island is strongest at midday; it is usually strongest at night.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** data: quote values, the overall trend, rates of change and anomalies.
- **Explain**: link each human activity to a change in the energy budget or water budget.
- **Evaluate** or **assess**: weigh strength of evidence, compare human and natural causes, or judge which urban effect is largest and most certain.
