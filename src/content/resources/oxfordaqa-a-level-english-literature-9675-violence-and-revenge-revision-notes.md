Condensed recall for one aspect named in the 9675 Unit 1 content: "the significance of violence and
revenge", applied to the Section B essay. The task itself is explained in the
[Unit 1 Section B study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-1-section-b/);
the full discussion of this aspect is in the
[violence and revenge study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-violence-and-revenge/).

## The idea in one box

- Section B is **one essay, 25 marks, closed book**, with no printed extract.
- Older revenge tragedy: public violence, revenge as duty, a bloody final act.
- Later tragedy: violence **offstage, in the past, threatened or turned inward**; revenge shrinks to
  **spite, humiliation, blackmail or sabotage**, or vanishes.
- The specification's reminder applies: an absent aspect can be as significant as a present one.

## Four forms of violence

| Form | What it looks like | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Blows, shots, assault | Is it staged, heard or reported? |
| Psychological or verbal | Cruelty, exposure, humiliation | Who holds power through words? |
| Institutional or economic | Dismissal, poverty, empire, mapping | Who is the unseen agent? |
| Against the self | Suicide, self-destruction | Is it defeat, protest or control? |

## Play by play

**Death of a Salesman**
- Ben's sparring with Biff: the "never fight fair" lesson as the play's view of competition.
- Willy's planned suicide: the rubber tube; the offstage car crash heard, not seen.
- Willy accuses Biff of spite; Biff's grievance dates from the Boston discovery.
- Economic violence: Howard dismisses Willy without a blow being struck.

**A Streetcar Named Desire**
- Poker night: the radio thrown out, Stella struck, Stella's return.
- Stanley's revenge for Blanche's contempt: exposing her past, the bus-ticket "present".
- The rape at the climax, presented by Stanley as fated.
- Past violence: the young husband's suicide after Blanche's public cruelty.

**Hedda Gabler**
- General Gabler's pistols: inheritance, danger, a joke shot at Brack.
- Burning the manuscript as a figurative killing of a "child".
- Two offstage deaths: Lovborg's sordid one, reported; Hedda's own, heard.
- Brack's knowledge of the pistol becomes blackmail; her suicide ends his power.

**Waiting for Godot**
- Estragon beaten nightly by unseen attackers, for no stated reason.
- Pozzo's whip and rope on Lucky: violence as a relationship.
- Two considered hangings from the tree.
- No wrong named, no avenger: violence without plot.

**Translations**
- Renaming places as cultural violence.
- Yolland's disappearance; Lancey's threats to livestock and homes.
- Small resistance: moved poles, the burning camp.
- Hugh's 1798 memory: heroic violence questioned.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Use it to say |
|---|---|---|
| Revenge tragedy | Genre built on a wrong, delay and repayment | What the later play keeps or abandons |
| Offstage action | An event heard or reported, not shown | Why the imagined act weighs more |
| Retrospective violence | A past act that shapes the present | How the past traps the protagonist |
| Displacement | Feeling expressed through an object or another target | How anger finds a safe outlet |
| Spite | Petty, deliberate wish to hurt | How revenge shrinks in domestic tragedy |
| Blackmail | Power gained by threatening exposure | How Brack controls Hedda |
| Sound effect | A noise written into the stage directions | How a death reaches the audience |
| Tragicomedy | Mixed tragic and comic elements | How *Godot* makes suffering absurd |

## Essay shape in five steps

1. Decide your argument in one sentence: what violence **means** in your play.
2. Choose three moments: one staged, one offstage or past, one of revenge or spite.
3. For each, analyse method: stage directions, sound, report, dialogue.
4. Weigh one alternative view (for example, that the violence is senseless, or deserved).
5. End on the audience: pity, fear, unease or complicity.

## Exam traps

- Listing incidents instead of arguing what they show.
- Forgetting that offstage, reported and threatened violence is still method.
- Assuming every tragedy needs an avenger.
- Moral labels ("brute", "villain") in place of analysis of power.
- Evidence from only one part of the play.

## Self-test

1. Quote the phrase from the 9675 Unit 1 content that names this aspect.
2. Give two ways in which later tragedy changes the role of violence.
3. What lesson does Ben give Biff when they spar, and why is it significant?
4. How is Willy's death presented to the audience?
5. What does Stanley do on poker night?
6. Why can Stanley's treatment of Blanche be read as revenge?
7. What does Hedda do to Lovborg's manuscript, and how does she describe it?
8. How does Brack gain power over Hedda?
9. Who beats Estragon, and why?
10. What does Captain Lancey threaten in *Translations*?
11. Give one form of resistance by the community in *Translations*.
12. Why might the absence of revenge in *Waiting for Godot* be significant?

## Answers

1. "The significance of violence and revenge".
2. Any two: it happens offstage or in the past; it turns inward against the self; it becomes
   economic or institutional; revenge shrinks to spite or disappears.
3. Never to fight fair with a stranger; it shows the ruthless view of success that shapes Willy's
   values.
4. Offstage, through sound, after the rubber tube has warned of suicide earlier in the play.
5. He throws the radio out of the window and hits Stella.
6. He has overheard her contempt for him and responds by exposing her past to Mitch and giving her a
   bus ticket home as a birthday present.
7. She burns it, speaking of it as Thea and Lovborg's child.
8. He recognises the pistol used in Lovborg's death and can expose her.
9. Unseen attackers; no reason is given, which makes the violence part of the human condition rather
   than a crime with a motive.
10. To shoot the livestock and then evict families and level their homes if Yolland is not found.
11. Any one: moving the surveyors' poles; the burning of the army camp.
12. There is no named wrong and no avenger, so suffering has no plot or meaning to resolve it; the
    absence becomes the play's comment on the human condition.
