## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. define the term isotope in terms of numbers of protons and neutrons;
2. understand the notation for isotopes with the mass (nucleon) number as a superscript and the atomic (proton) number as a subscript before the symbol;
3. state that, and explain why, isotopes of the same element have the same chemical properties;
4. state that, and explain why, isotopes of the same element have different physical properties, **limited to mass and density**.

## Defining an isotope precisely

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

A full-mark definition needs both halves: *same number of protons* and *different numbers of neutrons*. "Same element, different mass" is not enough, because it does not say which particle differs.

| Isotope | Proton number | Mass number | Neutrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ¹H (hydrogen-1) | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| ²H (deuterium, D) | 1 | 2 | 1 |
| ³H (tritium, T) | 1 | 3 | 2 |
| ¹²C | 6 | 12 | 6 |
| ¹³C | 6 | 13 | 7 |
| ¹⁴C | 6 | 14 | 8 |

## Reading and writing the notation

The mass number is written as a superscript and the proton number as a subscript, both before the symbol, as in ³⁷₁₇Cl. Because the symbol already fixes the proton number, the subscript is often left out and the isotope written as ³⁷Cl or chlorine-37.

The same notation extends to ions, with the charge at the top right: ³⁷₁₇Cl⁻ has 17 protons, 20 neutrons and 18 electrons.

### Worked example: is it an isotope?

Which of these pairs are isotopes of each other?

| Pair | Protons | Neutrons | Isotopes? |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⁴⁰₁₈Ar and ⁴⁰₂₀Ca | 18 and 20 | 22 and 20 | No: different proton numbers, so different elements |
| ⁵⁴₂₆Fe and ⁵⁶₂₆Fe | 26 and 26 | 28 and 30 | Yes |
| ³⁵₁₇Cl and ³⁵₁₇Cl⁻ | 17 and 17 | 18 and 18 | No: same isotope, one an atom and one an ion |

The first pair is a classic trap: equal mass numbers do not make atoms isotopes. The third pair shows that isotopes differ in neutrons, not electrons.

## Why isotopes have the same chemical properties

Build the explanation in three linked steps:

1. Isotopes of an element have the **same number of protons**, so a neutral atom of each has the **same number of electrons**.
2. These electrons have the **same electronic configuration**, including the same number of outer-shell electrons.
3. Chemical reactions involve the **electrons**, especially those in the outer shell: they are transferred or shared when bonds form. Neutrons are in the nucleus, carry no charge and take no part in bonding.

So ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl both gain one electron to form Cl⁻, both form NaCl with sodium, and both form HCl with hydrogen. Deuterium oxide, D₂O, reacts with sodium in the same way that H₂O does, giving sodium deuteroxide and deuterium gas, D₂.

Examiners look for the word *electrons* and the link to configuration. Writing only "they have the same proton number" does not explain anything about reactions.

## Why isotopes have different physical properties

The syllabus limits this to **mass** and **density**. Both follow from the different number of neutrons.

### Mass

Each extra neutron adds about one unit of mass to an atom while leaving its size almost unchanged, because the size of an atom is set by its electrons. A ³⁷Cl atom is about 37/35 times as massive as a ³⁵Cl atom.

Molecules containing different isotopes therefore have different relative molecular masses:

| Molecule | Relative molecular mass |
|---|---|
| ¹H₂¹⁶O | 2 × 1 + 16 = 18 |
| ²H₂¹⁶O (D₂O) | 2 × 2 + 16 = 20 |
| ³⁵Cl₂ | 70 |
| ³⁵Cl³⁷Cl | 72 |
| ³⁷Cl₂ | 74 |

(These use mass numbers as approximate relative masses, which is accurate enough for comparisons.)

### Density: gases

At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules. One mole of any gas occupies 24.0 dm³ at room conditions, so density in g dm⁻³ = molar mass ÷ 24.0.

**Worked example.** Compare the densities of ³⁵Cl₂ and ³⁷Cl₂ gas at room conditions.

1. ³⁵Cl₂: 70 ÷ 24.0 = 2.92 g dm⁻³.
2. ³⁷Cl₂: 74 ÷ 24.0 = 3.08 g dm⁻³.
3. Ratio: 74 ÷ 70 = 1.06, so ³⁷Cl₂ is about 6 % denser.

The density ratio equals the mass ratio, because the number of molecules in each volume is the same.

### Density: liquids and solids

In a liquid or solid the molecules are packed together, and the space each one takes up depends on its electrons. D₂O and H₂O molecules have the same electron arrangement and almost the same size, so they pack into almost the same volume.

**Worked example.** Water (H₂O) has a density of about 1.00 g cm⁻³. Predict the density of D₂O, assuming its molecules take up the same volume.

1. Same volume per molecule means density is proportional to the mass of each molecule.
2. Predicted density = 1.00 × 20 ÷ 18 = 1.11 g cm⁻³.
3. The measured value is about 1.10 g cm⁻³, close to the prediction, which supports the idea that the molecules are the same size.

A striking result follows: an ice cube made from D₂O sinks in ordinary liquid water, because D₂O ice is denser than liquid H₂O.

## Putting it together: a model answer

*Question:* Compare the chemical and physical properties of ¹H₂O and ²H₂O.

*Answer:* They have the same chemical properties, because ¹H and ²H atoms both have one proton and so one electron, in the same configuration, and reactions involve electrons, not neutrons. They have different physical properties: ²H has one more neutron than ¹H, so a ²H₂O molecule has a greater mass (Mr 20 compared with 18). The molecules are almost the same size, so ²H₂O is denser.

Each point the examiner wants is there: same electrons and configuration, reactions involve electrons, extra neutrons, greater mass, greater density with the reason.

## Common mistakes

- Defining isotopes as "atoms with different mass numbers" without mentioning protons and neutrons.
- Treating atoms with the same mass number, such as ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca, as isotopes.
- Saying isotopes have the same chemical properties "because they are the same element", which restates the question.
- Claiming isotopes have different numbers of electrons.
- Listing physical properties outside the syllabus limit when asked to explain; keep to mass and density.
- Writing that a heavier isotope's atoms are larger. Extra neutrons add mass, not size.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: give both halves (same protons, different neutrons) in one sentence.
- **State that and explain why**: state the property first, then give the reason through electrons or neutrons.
- **Compare** two species: give one similarity and one difference, each with its reason.
- **Calculate** a density ratio: use relative masses, and for gases use the molar volume given on the data sheet.
