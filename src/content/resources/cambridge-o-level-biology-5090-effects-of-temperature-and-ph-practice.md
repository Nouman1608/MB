## Section A

**1.** A student mixes amylase with starch and tests drops of the mixture with iodine solution every 30 seconds. Which result shows that all the starch has been broken down?

A: iodine turns blue-black

B: iodine stays orange-brown

C: the mixture turns brick-red

D: the mixture turns cloudy white

**[1]**

**2.** Which change happens to an enzyme when it is denatured?

A: It is used up in the reaction.

B: Its active site changes shape permanently.

C: Its molecules lose kinetic energy.

D: It becomes complementary to more substrates.

**[1]**

**3.** State **two** ways in which the progress of an enzyme-catalysed reaction can be followed. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why an enzyme kept at 4 °C works again when warmed to its optimum temperature. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction increases as temperature rises from 15 °C to 35 °C. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** A student investigated the effect of pH on amylase at 35 °C. The table shows the time taken for starch to disappear.

| pH | Mean time (s) |
|---|---|
| 4 | 240 |
| 5 | 90 |
| 6 | 60 |
| 7 | 150 |
| 8 | starch still present after 600 s |

**(a)** Calculate the rate (1000 ÷ time) at pH 5 and pH 6. Give your answers to 1 decimal place. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the effect of pH on the activity of this amylase. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why there was no measurable reaction at pH 8. **[2]**

**(d)** State **three** variables the student should have kept constant. **[3]**

**7.** A student plans to investigate the effect of temperature on amylase activity.

**(a)** Explain why the student should place the amylase and starch in the water bath separately before mixing them. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe a suitable control and explain its purpose. **[2]**

**(c)** The student uses temperatures of 20, 40 and 60 °C. Suggest how the method could be improved to find the optimum temperature more precisely. **[2]**

**8.** Three repeats at 40 °C gave times of 18 s, 21 s and 52 s.

**(a)** Identify the anomalous result. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate a mean time that excludes the anomaly. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest one possible cause of the anomaly. **[1]**

**9.** Explain, in terms of shape and fit, why the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction falls above the optimum temperature. **[4]**

**10.** The protease pepsin works in the stomach. Other proteases work in the duodenum. Suggest and explain how their optimum pH values differ. **[3]**

**11.** A student says: "At 70 °C the enzyme has been killed, so cooling it will bring it back to life." Explain what is wrong with this statement. **[3]**

**12.** Describe how Benedict's solution could be used to show that amylase has produced reducing sugar from starch. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Brick-red is the Benedict's result for reducing sugar, not an iodine result.

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Measure how quickly the substrate is used up, such as testing for starch with iodine [1]; measure how quickly a product forms, such as testing for reducing sugar with Benedict's solution or collecting a gas [1]. **[2]**

**4.** At 4 °C the molecules have little kinetic energy, so effective collisions are rare, but the enzyme is not denatured [1]; its active site keeps its shape, so on warming collisions increase and activity returns [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster [1], so enzyme and substrate collide more frequently [1], giving more effective collisions and more enzyme-substrate complexes per second [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** pH 5: 1000 ÷ 90 = 11.1 [1]. pH 6: 1000 ÷ 60 = 16.7 [1].

**(b)** Activity rises from pH 4 to a peak at pH 6, the optimum in this investigation [1]. It falls again at pH 7 (time 150 s) [1], and there is no measurable activity at pH 8 [1].

**(c)** At pH 8 the shape of the active site has changed so much that the enzyme is denatured [1], so starch no longer fits and no enzyme-substrate complexes form [1].

**(d)** Temperature [1]; volume or concentration of amylase [1]; volume or concentration of starch [1]. Also accept the volume of iodine or the sampling interval in place of any one. **[10]**

**7. (a)** So that the reaction starts at the temperature being tested [1]; if they were mixed cold and then warmed, part of the reaction would happen at a lower temperature, making the time inaccurate [1].

**(b)** Use boiled (denatured) amylase with starch [1], to show that the starch is broken down only by active enzyme [1].

**(c)** Test more temperatures between 20 °C and 60 °C [1], with smaller intervals, such as every 5 °C around the fastest result [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** 52 s [1].

**(b)** (18 + 21) ÷ 2 = 19.5 s [1].

**(c)** For example, a sample taken late, the water bath not at 40 °C, or the end point misjudged [1]. **[3]**

**9.** High temperature makes the enzyme molecule vibrate, breaking the bonds that hold its shape [1]. The active site changes shape [1], so the substrate no longer fits and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form [1]. The enzyme is denatured, which is permanent [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Pepsin has a low (acidic) optimum pH [1] because the stomach contains hydrochloric acid [1]. Proteases in the duodenum have a higher optimum pH, because conditions there are alkaline [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Enzymes are proteins, not living things, so they cannot be killed [1]. At 70 °C the enzyme is denatured: the shape of its active site has changed [1]. Denaturation is permanent, so cooling does not restore the active site or activity [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Take a sample of the mixture and add Benedict's solution [1]. Heat it in a hot water bath [1]. A colour change from blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red shows reducing sugar is present [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying enzymes are killed instead of denatured.
- Explaining rate changes with "more energy" but no collisions.
- Plotting or describing time as if it were rate.
- Forgetting to keep pH constant in a temperature investigation.
- Claiming an exact optimum from widely spaced readings.
