These questions test the section 3.2 row on fatal offences against the person: murder, the two partial defences, unlawful act manslaughter and gross negligence manslaughter. In Section B, work through each offence's elements in order and conclude.

---

## Section A

**1.** Define the actus reus of murder. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by implied malice, giving an authority. **[2]**

**3.** State the effect of a successful plea of loss of control or diminished responsibility. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the "normal person" test in the defence of loss of control. **[3]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by a "recognised medical condition" in diminished responsibility, and whether acute voluntary intoxication qualifies. **[3]**

**6.** Explain what makes an unlawful act "dangerous" for unlawful act manslaughter. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Nadia stabs Omar intending to frighten him, not to injure him. Omar is taken to hospital, where he receives treatment that is careless but not unusual, and dies. Explain whether Nadia caused Omar's death. **[5]**

**8.** Paul sets fire to a shop at night intending to destroy it. He knows a caretaker usually sleeps above it. The caretaker dies. Explain whether Paul has the mens rea for murder. **[5]**

**9.** Rosa has been abused by her partner Sam for years. One evening Sam threatens to "finish her off". Two hours later, while he sleeps, she kills him with a hammer. Advise Rosa on the defence of loss of control. **[8]**

**10.** Tariq, who has been diagnosed with severe depression, kills his neighbour after a minor dispute. He had also drunk heavily that evening. Advise Tariq on diminished responsibility. **[6]**

**11.** Uma, a nurse, fails to check a patient's medical notes and gives a drug to which the patient is known to be allergic. The patient dies. Advise whether Uma is guilty of gross negligence manslaughter. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the law on unlawful act manslaughter. **[12]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The unlawful killing of a reasonable creature in being [1], under the King's peace, with the defendant's conduct causing the death [1].

**2.** Implied malice is an intention to cause grievous bodily harm, which is sufficient mens rea for murder even without an intention to kill [1], as held in *R v Vickers* (1957) [1].

**3.** The defendant is not acquitted but convicted of manslaughter instead of murder [1], so the judge has discretion in sentencing instead of imposing the mandatory life sentence [1].

**4.** The jury asks whether a person of the defendant's sex and age [1], with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint [1], and in the circumstances of the defendant, might have reacted in the same or a similar way (s54(1)(c)); circumstances relevant only to the defendant's general capacity for tolerance or self-restraint are excluded (s54(3)) [1].

**5.** A recognised medical condition is a physical or psychiatric condition accepted in medical classification, such as depression or schizophrenia [1], which must give rise to the abnormality of mental functioning [1]. Acute voluntary intoxication does not qualify (*R v Dowds*, 2012) [1].

**6.** The act is dangerous if all sober and reasonable people would inevitably recognise that it must subject the victim to the risk of some harm, albeit not serious harm (*R v Church*, 1966) [1]. The test is objective; the defendant need not have recognised the risk [1]. The reasonable person has the knowledge the defendant had or would have gained during the act (*R v Watson*, 1989) [1].

**7.** Factual causation: but for the stabbing, Omar would not have needed treatment and would not have died (*R v White*, 1910) [1]. Legal causation: the stab wound must be an operating and substantial cause of death (*R v Smith*, 1959) [1]. Medical treatment breaks the chain only if it is so independent and so potent that the original wound becomes insignificant (*R v Cheshire*, 1991) [1], as where treatment was "palpably wrong" and the wound had nearly healed in *R v Jordan* (1956) [1]. Careless but ordinary treatment does not break the chain, so Nadia caused Omar's death [1].

**8.** Paul did not aim to kill or seriously injure, so there is no direct intention [1]. The question is oblique intention: the jury is entitled to find intention if death or serious injury was a virtual certainty as a result of his actions [1] and he appreciated that this was the case (*R v Woollin*, 1998) [1]. Setting fire to a building at night knowing someone usually sleeps there could make serious injury virtually certain, and he knew of the caretaker [1]. The jury may, but need not, find intention, so murder is possible; if not, unlawful act manslaughter based on arson is the alternative [1].

**9.** Under s54 Coroners and Justice Act 2009 Rosa must have lost self-control [1]; this need not be sudden (s54(2)), which helps where there is a delay, as in long-term abuse cases [1]. But a two-hour gap and an attack on a sleeping man may suggest a considered decision, and the defence is excluded if she acted in a considered desire for revenge (s54(4)) [1]. Qualifying trigger: a fear of serious violence from Sam (s55(3)) is likely after his threat [1]; years of abuse may also be things said and done amounting to circumstances of an extremely grave character giving her a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged (s55(4)) [1]. Normal person test: a woman of her age with normal tolerance and self-restraint, in her circumstances including the history of abuse, might have reacted in the same or a similar way [1]. Once there is sufficient evidence, the prosecution must disprove the defence beyond reasonable doubt (s54(5)–(6)) [1]. If loss of control fails, diminished responsibility could be considered if the abuse caused a recognised medical condition [1].

**10.** Tariq must prove on the balance of probabilities an abnormality of mental functioning arising from a recognised medical condition [1]; severe depression is likely to qualify [1]. It must substantially impair his ability to understand his conduct, form a rational judgment or exercise self-control; "substantially" means important or weighty (*R v Golds*, 2016) [1]. It must provide an explanation for the killing, being a significant contributory factor (s2(1B)) [1]. His heavy drinking is not itself a recognised medical condition (*R v Dowds*, 2012) [1], so the jury must ask whether the depression substantially impaired him even taking the drink into account (*R v Dietschmann*, 2003) [1].

**11.** Uma, as a nurse, owes a duty of care to her patient [1] and breached it by failing to check the notes [1]. Giving a drug to which the patient is known to be allergic creates a risk of death [1], which would be serious and obvious to a reasonable nurse with the information available at the time (*R v Rose*, 2017) [1]. The drug caused the death [1]. The jury must decide whether her breach was so bad as to be criminal (*R v Adomako*, 1995); failing to make a basic check on a known allergy may well be gross, so she is likely to be guilty [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Description:** the defendant must commit a criminal unlawful act (*R v Lamb*, 1967) [1] that is objectively dangerous (*R v Church*, 1966) [1] and causes death, with only the mens rea of the unlawful act (*DPP v Newbury and Jones*, 1977) [1]. **Criticisms:** it breaks the principle of correspondence, because the defendant need not foresee death or even harm [1]. It is a very wide offence, so a single punch causing an unforeseeable death carries the same label as much more culpable killings, raising fair labelling concerns [1]. The objective test can be harsh where the defendant could not appreciate the risk, although *R v Dawson* (1985) limits it to what the reasonable person would know [1]. It cannot be committed by omission (*R v Lowe*, 1973), which can seem arbitrary [1]. **Defence of the law:** a person who chooses to commit a dangerous crime accepts the risk of the consequences [1]. The objective test and the requirement of a criminal act limit liability [1], and *R v Kennedy (No 2)* (2007) prevents liability where the victim freely chooses to take a risk [1]. **Reform:** the Law Commission has recommended restructuring homicide into a ladder of offences [1]. A reasoned conclusion [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Concluding on murder without considering implied malice or oblique intention.
- Forgetting the considered desire for revenge exclusion in delayed loss of control cases.
- Treating intoxication as a recognised medical condition.
- Not explaining why an act is objectively dangerous.
