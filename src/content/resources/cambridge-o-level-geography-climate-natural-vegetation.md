This guide goes deeper into **subtopic 2.5 Climate and Natural
Vegetation** of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series
(Version 3). The [Natural Environment study
guide](/resources/o-level-geography-natural-environment/) introduces
the two climates in a paragraph; here each factor, each layer of the
ecosystem and the deforestation chain is explained in the detail an
extended answer needs. Afterwards, use the [climate and natural
vegetation revision
notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-climate-natural-vegetation-revision-notes/)
and the [climate and natural vegetation practice
questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-climate-natural-vegetation-practice/).

## What 2.5 covers

- The characteristics of **two climates**: equatorial and hot desert,
  described by temperature (mean of the hottest month, mean of the
  coolest month, annual range) and precipitation (total and seasonal
  pattern).
- The factors that explain them: **latitude, pressure systems, winds,
  distance from the sea, altitude and ocean currents**, with
  convection and relief rainfall.
- Drawing, describing and interpreting **climatic graphs**.
- The characteristics of **tropical rainforest** and **hot desert**
  ecosystems: vegetation, soils, animals and how they are linked.
- The **causes and effects of deforestation** of tropical rainforest.

Keep the names straight: *equatorial* and *hot desert* are climates;
*tropical rainforest* and *hot desert* are ecosystems.

## The equatorial climate and why it happens

Equatorial climates lie roughly within 5 to 10 degrees of the Equator,
for example in the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and much of
Indonesia. Typical features: temperatures of about 26 to 28 °C in
every month, an annual range of only 1 to 3 °C, and over 2,000 mm of
rain a year with no true dry season.

- **Latitude**: the midday Sun is high in the sky all year, so
  insolation is intense and there is no cool season. Day length is
  close to 12 hours throughout the year.
- **Pressure and winds**: heated air rises, creating a belt of low
  pressure where the trade winds converge. Rising air cools,
  water vapour condenses, and towering cumulonimbus clouds form.
- **Convection rainfall**: this happens almost every day. Mornings are
  often clear, the ground heats up, and heavy thunderstorms follow in
  the afternoon.
- **Relief rainfall**: where moist air is forced over mountains it
  cools and condenses, so windward slopes are wetter still.
- **Altitude** can modify the climate: temperature falls by roughly
  6 °C for every 1,000 m, which is why equatorial highlands are much
  cooler than nearby lowlands.

## The hot desert climate and why it happens

Hot deserts are found mainly between about 15 and 30 degrees north
and south of the Equator, near the Tropics: the Sahara around the
Tropic of Cancer, and the Namib and Atacama on the west coasts of
southern Africa and South America. Typical features: under 250 mm of
rain a year, falling unreliably; very hot summers (monthly means of
30 °C or more); cooler winters; and a large **diurnal (daily)** range,
because clear skies let the ground heat strongly by day and lose heat
rapidly at night.

- **Pressure**: air that rose at the Equator sinks again near the
  Tropics, forming the subtropical high-pressure belt. Sinking air
  warms and holds its moisture, so clouds rarely form.
- **Winds**: the trade winds that blow out from these high-pressure
  areas cross land before reaching much of the Sahara, so they carry
  little moisture.
- **Distance from the sea**: the interior of a large continent is far
  from sources of moisture.
- **Ocean currents**: cold currents offshore (the Benguela Current
  beside the Namib, the Humboldt or Peru Current beside the Atacama)
  cool the air above them. Cool air cannot hold much water vapour and
  is stable, so it gives fog rather than rain.
- **Relief**: mountains can create a rain shadow; the Andes block moist
  air from the east before it reaches the Atacama.

## Worked example: describing a climatic graph

An illustrative equatorial station shows monthly temperatures between
26 and 28 °C and rainfall between 100 mm (August) and 300 mm (April),
with a total of 2,520 mm.

A strong description:

> Temperatures are high all year, ranging only from 26 °C in June and
> July to 28 °C in March, April and October, an annual range of 2 °C.
> Rainfall is heavy, totalling 2,520 mm, and every month receives at
> least 100 mm. There is a wetter period peaking at 300 mm in April and
> a less wet period with a minimum of 100 mm in August, but no dry
> season.

Notice the pattern: overall statement, maximum and minimum with months,
the range or total, then any seasonal variation. If asked to
*explain*, attach a factor to each feature (high Sun angle → high
temperatures; low pressure and convection → rain in every month).

## The tropical rainforest ecosystem

**Structure.** The forest is layered. **Emergents** rise above the
rest, up to about 50 m. Below them the dense **canopy** forms an
almost continuous roof that intercepts most of the light and rain.
The **understorey** contains younger trees and shrubs adapted to
shade, and the **forest floor** is dark, with little undergrowth.

**Plant adaptations.**

- *Drip tips* on leaves shed water quickly, preventing the growth of
  fungi and algae.
- *Buttress roots* support very tall trees in shallow soil.
- *Lianas* (woody climbers) and *epiphytes* (plants growing on other
  plants) reach the light without growing a trunk of their own.
- *Thin, smooth bark* is enough, because there is no cold season to
  protect against.

**Soils and nutrient cycling.** Rainforest soils are deep and often
red from iron oxides, but they are **poor in nutrients**. Heavy rain
washes nutrients downwards (leaching), so most nutrients are stored in
the vegetation itself. Leaf litter decomposes within weeks in the hot,
wet conditions and is taken up quickly by shallow roots. This rapid
cycle is the key to understanding why clearing the forest is so
damaging.

**Animals.** Very high biodiversity, with many species living in the
canopy where food is plentiful: for example, monkeys with gripping
tails, sloths, tree frogs and brightly coloured birds.

## The hot desert ecosystem

**Plant adaptations** are all about obtaining and saving water.

- *Long tap roots* reach groundwater; *wide, shallow roots* catch
  brief rainfall before it evaporates.
- *Small, waxy leaves or spines* reduce water loss by transpiration.
- *Succulent stems or leaves* (as in cacti in the Americas) store
  water.
- *Ephemerals* survive as seeds, then germinate, flower and set seed
  within weeks of rain.

**Soils** are thin, sandy or stony, with little organic matter because
plants are sparse. Strong evaporation can draw salts to the surface.

**Animals** avoid the heat and save water: many are nocturnal or live
in burrows; the fennec fox loses heat through its large ears; the
camel can go long periods without drinking and has wide feet for soft
sand.

## Deforestation of tropical rainforest

Use the Amazon as your named example. Its main causes:

- **Cattle ranching**, the largest single cause of clearance in the
  Brazilian Amazon.
- **Commercial farming**, especially soya.
- **Logging** of valuable hardwoods.
- **Mining**, such as the Carajás iron-ore mine in Pará state.
- **Road building**, such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway begun in the
  1970s, which opens the forest to settlers.
- **Hydroelectric dams**, such as Belo Monte on the Xingu River, which
  flood forest.

**Effects** form a chain. Removing the canopy exposes soil to heavy
rain, so it is eroded and leached; with the nutrient store gone, soils
become infertile within a few years. Eroded soil silts rivers and
raises flood risk. Habitats and species are lost. Less transpiration
can reduce local rainfall. Burning and decay release carbon dioxide,
adding to the enhanced greenhouse effect. Indigenous peoples lose
land and ways of life, although clearance also brings jobs, export
income and roads, so effects should be weighed.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the climate "tropical rainforest climate" when asked for the
  syllabus name, *equatorial*.
- Saying deserts are "always hot": nights can be cold, and the large
  diurnal range is a key feature.
- Listing adaptations without the condition they respond to.
- Describing deforestation causes but not linking them to the
  nutrient cycle when explaining soil decline.
- Climatic graph descriptions with no figures, months or units.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a climate: values with months and units, the range,
  the total and the seasonal pattern.
- **Explain** a climate: one factor per feature, with the process
  (rising air → cooling → condensation → rain).
- **Explain** an adaptation: name it, then say *how* it helps survival
  in that climate.
- **Describe the effects** of deforestation: give a chain of
  consequences, and include people as well as the environment.
- Long answers reward **named, place-specific detail**, so attach your
  points to the Amazon and to a named desert such as the Sahara or the
  Namib.
