This guide covers section 1.1, Business objectives, in Topic 1 (Business activity and influences on business) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Point 1.1.1 says businesses can have several objectives, financial (survival, profit, sales, market share, financial security) and non-financial (social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence and control). Point 1.1.2 asks why aims and objectives change as businesses evolve: market conditions, technology, performance, legislation and internal reasons.

Both papers can draw on all the content. Paper 1 is set on a small business of up to 49 employees with a local or national market; Paper 2 on a large business of more than 250 employees with a multinational or global market. That difference matters here, because the objectives that make sense depend on the business. The overview of 1.1 and 1.2 together is in the [business objectives and types of organisation guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-business-objectives-and-types-of-organisation/). Companion pages: the [business objectives revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-business-objectives-revision-notes/) and the [business objectives practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-business-objectives-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars.

## 1. Which objectives fit which business

The specification lists the objectives; your marks come from matching them to the business in the case.

| Objective | Most likely to matter when | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Survival | A business is new, or trading conditions are poor | Without enough revenue and cash to keep going, no other objective can be met |
| Profit | The business is established, or owners and shareholders expect a return | Profit rewards owners and pays for growth |
| Sales | A business is building a customer base or launching a product | More sales spread costs and build recognition |
| Market share | A large business faces strong rivals | A bigger share gives more influence over prices and suppliers |
| Financial security | The owner relies on the business for income | A steady income and reserves protect against a bad year |
| Social objectives | The owners or customers care about the community or environment | They can build reputation and loyalty as well as doing good |
| Personal satisfaction | A small owner-run business | The owner may value enjoying the work above earning more |
| Challenge | An owner starting something new or difficult | Testing oneself is a reason to run a business at all |
| Independence and control | A sole trader or family business | Being your own boss may matter more than growth |

A Paper 1 owner is often driven by survival, independence and personal satisfaction. A Paper 2 business answers to shareholders, so profit and market share usually lead, with social objectives that protect its reputation across many countries.

## 2. Measuring progress against an objective

An objective is useful only if the business can tell whether it has been met. The specification gives no formulas for this section; the ones below are the standard forms.

### Worked example 1: a café over three years

Hazel Street Café opened three years ago.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 84,000 | 120,000 | 141,000 |
| Total costs | 88,000 | 108,000 | 118,500 |
| Profit or loss | −4,000 | 12,000 | 22,500 |

- **Year 1: survival.** A loss of 4,000. The objective was simply to keep trading, and the owner's savings covered the loss.
- **Year 2: sales.** Growth = (120,000 − 84,000) ÷ 84,000 × 100 = **42.9%**. The café reached a profit of 12,000.
- **Year 3: market share.** The local café market is worth 1,500,000, so the share is 141,000 ÷ 1,500,000 × 100 = **9.4%**. If the target was 10%, it needed 0.10 × 1,500,000 = 150,000 of revenue, so it fell 9,000 short.

The objective moved as the business grew: survive, then grow sales, then win share. That is the pattern 1.1.2 asks you to explain.

## 3. When objectives conflict

Objectives can pull against each other, and many "evaluate" answers turn on this.

### Worked example 2: sales against profit

A juice bar sells 20,000 drinks a year at 5.00. Each drink costs 2.50 in ingredients and cups, and fixed costs are 40,000. It cuts the price to 4.50 to raise sales and sells 23,000.

| | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 100,000 | 103,500 |
| Variable costs | 50,000 | 57,500 |
| Fixed costs | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| Profit | 10,000 | 6,000 |

Sales volume rose by 15% and revenue by 3.5%, but profit fell by 40%. The sales objective was met at the cost of the profit objective.

Other common conflicts:

- **Social objectives against profit.** Paying suppliers more or using recycled packaging raises costs now, though it may win customers later.
- **Independence against growth.** Growing fast may need outside investors, who will want a say in decisions.
- **Market share against financial security.** Spending heavily to win share can leave little in reserve.

## 4. Why objectives change (1.1.2)

Each reason needs a chain: what changed, what it does to the business, and the new objective.

- **Market conditions.** A new competitor opens nearby, so sales fall; the business moves from growth to survival and sets a target to hold its customer numbers.
- **Technology.** Online ordering becomes cheap to set up, so the business can reach customers beyond walking distance; it adds an objective for online sales.
- **Performance.** Profit beats its target two years running, so the owners raise the target or add a market share objective; if results fall short, the target may be cut to something reachable.
- **Legislation.** A new law requires safer packaging or higher minimum pay, so costs rise; the profit target may be lowered, or a new objective set to meet the rule by a date.
- **Internal reasons.** A new owner or manager has different priorities; an owner nearing retirement may replace growth with financial security.

The causes often combine. A business that performs well may move into new markets, which changes the market conditions it faces, which changes its objectives again.

## 5. Annotated model answers

The specification's command-word table says "explain" means "a statement of fact, with two further expansion points", with no context; "analyse" is an extended answer placed in context; "evaluate" reaches a supported conclusion.

**Explain one reason why a business may change its objectives.**
*A change in market conditions can lead to new objectives (statement). For example, a recession may reduce demand, so sales and profit fall (expansion 1). The business may then change its objective from growth to survival, cutting costs to keep trading (expansion 2).*

**Analyse why Hazel Street Café changed its objective from survival to sales growth.** A strong answer uses the case: the loss of 4,000 in Year 1 turned into a profit in Year 2 (performance); with survival no longer in doubt, the owner could aim to grow revenue, which rose 42.9%; higher sales then spread the fixed costs of the premises over more customers, which helps profit too.

**Evaluate whether profit is the most important objective for a small business.** For: without profit the owner cannot draw an income, and the business cannot pay for new equipment. Against: in Year 1 survival matters more, and many owners value independence or personal satisfaction above extra profit. Judgement: profit matters most once survival is secure, but for this owner it depends on what they value and the stage the business has reached; say which, using the case.

## Common mistakes

- Listing objectives without saying which one fits the business described.
- Treating survival as an objective only for failing businesses; it is the first objective of most start-ups.
- Confusing sales (the business's own figure) with market share (its share of the whole market).
- Giving a reason for change with no new objective at the end of the chain.
- Writing an "evaluate" answer with no conclusion, or one that does not use the case.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do small and large businesses have different objectives?**
A small owner-run business often values survival, independence and personal satisfaction. A large company answers to shareholders, so profit and market share usually come first.

**Can a business have more than one objective at once?**
Yes. The specification says businesses can have several objectives, and they can conflict, as when a price cut raises sales but lowers profit.
