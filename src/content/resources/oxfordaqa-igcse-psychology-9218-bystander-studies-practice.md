## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following was a finding of Piliavin's subway study?
A Participants alone reported smoke more often than those in groups
B Larger groups of passengers did not help less than smaller groups
C Supporters helped a jogger in a rival team's shirt most often
D Most first helpers were women **[1]**

**2.** Identify the independent variable in Latané and Darley's smoke study. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why Piliavin's subway study is described as a field experiment. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the role of the confederates in the smoke study. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** ethical issues raised by Piliavin's subway study. **[2]**

**6.** Outline what Levine's Study 2 added to the findings of Study 1. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Describe Latané and Darley's smoke study. Refer to the method and results. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the results of Piliavin's study support the idea that the cost of helping affects
bystander intervention. **[3]**

**9.** Describe Levine's football supporter study and state what it shows about bystander behaviour.
**[5]**

**10.** Compare the smoke study and the subway study in terms of control and ecological validity.
**[4]**

**11.** A researcher wants to replicate Levine's study using supporters of two rival basketball teams
at a university.
**(a)** Identify the independent variable and the dependent variable. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest **one** way the researcher could deal with the ethical issue of deception. **[2]**

**12.** Evaluate the research into bystander behaviour by Piliavin, Latané and Darley, and Levine.
**[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1].

**2.** Whether the participant was alone, with two passive confederates, or with two other naive
participants (the presence of others) [1].

**3.** It took place in a natural setting, a real subway train [1], but the researchers manipulated the
independent variable, such as whether the victim appeared drunk or carried a cane [1].

**4.** They pretended to be participants filling in the questionnaire [1] and, when the smoke appeared,
noticed it but stayed passive and carried on working, to see whether their calm would affect the real
participant [1].

**5.** Any two, [1] each: no informed consent; no right to withdraw; no debriefing; deception;
possible distress to passengers. Maximum 2.

**6.** When supporters were made to think of themselves as football fans in general [1], they helped the
jogger in a rival team's shirt as well as their own team's, showing that the group boundary for
helping can be widened [1].

**7.** One mark per point, up to 4:
- A laboratory experiment with male university students filling in a questionnaire [1].
- Smoke was pumped into the room through a vent and researchers recorded whether participants reported
  it [1].
- Conditions: alone, with two passive confederates, or with two other naive participants [1].
- 75% of participants alone reported the smoke [1].
- Only 10% reported it with passive confederates; in 38% of the three-person groups someone reported it
  [1].
Maximum 4.

**8.** One mark per point, up to 3:
- The drunk victim was helped spontaneously less often (19 of 38 trials) than the cane victim (62 of 65)
  [1].
- Helping a drunk person carries higher costs, such as the risk of being sick on, attacked or
  embarrassed [1].
- So bystanders weighed the costs against the costs of not helping, which supports the arousal:
  cost-reward model [1].
Maximum 3.

**9.** Description, up to 4: Manchester United supporters completed questionnaires about their team
[1]; on the way to another building they saw a jogger (confederate) fall and appear hurt [1]; the
jogger wore a Manchester United, Liverpool or plain shirt [1]; he was helped most often in the
Manchester United shirt, and much less often in the others [1]. What it shows [1]: people are more
likely to help a victim who shares their group identity (similarity to the victim). Maximum 5.

**10.** Control, up to 2: the smoke study had high control because every participant experienced the
same room, smoke and task [1], whereas the subway study had less control because passengers, crowd
sizes and conditions varied between trials [1]. Ecological validity, up to 2: the subway study had
high ecological validity because real passengers responded to an apparently real emergency in
everyday life [1], whereas the smoke study took place in an artificial laboratory, so behaviour may
not reflect real emergencies [1].

**11. (a)** IV: the team shirt the victim wears (own team, rival team or plain) [1]. DV: whether
(or how quickly) the participant helps [1].
**(b)** Any one, described [2]: debrief participants fully afterwards, explaining the deception and
why it was needed [1], and offer them the right to withdraw their data [1]; or gain approval from an
ethics committee [1] and only use a staged fall that is mild and unlikely to cause distress [1].

**12.** Award up to 6, for balanced evaluation linked to the studies:
- Together the studies show three different factors (presence of others, cost of helping, similarity)
  with different methods, which strengthens the overall explanation [1].
- Piliavin and Levine used field experiments, giving high ecological validity [1].
- The smoke study's high control allows a cause-and-effect conclusion about the presence of others [1].
- The findings conflict on group size: the smoke study shows a bystander effect but the subway study
  does not, suggesting the effect depends on how ambiguous the emergency is [1].
- Samples were limited (male students; male football supporters; one city's passengers), which
  limits generalisation [1].
- All three raise ethical issues, especially deception and, in the subway study, lack of consent and
  debriefing [1].
Maximum 6.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up the figures from different studies.
- Saying the subway study found diffusion of responsibility.
- Describing a study with no results, or results with no procedure.
- Evaluation points that could apply to any study ("it was unethical") without saying why.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much detail do I need for each study?**
Enough to describe the method, the key manipulation, the main result and the conclusion, plus one
strength and one weakness.

**Do I need exact percentages?**
Accurate figures add precision, but a correct pattern of results (for example "most helped the cane
victim, about half helped the drunk victim") is the essential point.
