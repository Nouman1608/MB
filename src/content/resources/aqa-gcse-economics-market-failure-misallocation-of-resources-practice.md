## Questions

**1.** Define the term market failure. **[2]**

**2.** A small bakery in a made-up town has a private cost of £0.80 per loaf. Smoke from its oven costs neighbours £0.20 per loaf. It bakes 5,000 loaves a week. Calculate the social cost per loaf and the total weekly external cost. **[3]**

**3.** For each situation, state whether it is a positive or negative externality, and whether it arises from production or consumption.

**(a)** Dust from a quarry settles on nearby farms.
**(b)** A firm pays for apprentices' training and other local firms later hire some of them.
**(c)** Fans leave litter in the streets after a match.
**(d)** A person has a flu jab and so is less likely to pass flu to colleagues. **[4]**

**4.** In Town Z, producing a tonne of chemicals has a private cost of £50. The external cost is £18 per tonne. Output is 600 tonnes a month.

**(a)** Calculate the social cost per tonne. **[1]**
**(b)** Work out the total external cost each month. **[1]**
**(c)** Express the external cost as a percentage of the social cost, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **two** costs to society of a misallocation of resources. **[4]**

**6.** A firm's private cost is £40 per unit and its external cost is £15 per unit. It makes 800 units. Explain why the market is likely to produce too much of this good, and state the total external cost. **[4]**

**7.** A tram line in a made-up city gives each rider a private benefit of £9 a trip. Because roads are less crowded, other people gain an external benefit of £3 per trip. There are 8,000 trips a month.

**(a)** Calculate the social benefit per trip. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the total external benefit each month. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the market is likely to provide too few trips. **[3]**

**8.** A government puts a tax of £5 per unit on a good. Before the tax the price was £14 and 9,000 units were sold. After the tax the price paid by buyers is £16.50 and 7,200 units are sold.

**(a)** Calculate how much of the tax each unit is paid by buyers, and how much by producers. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in quantity sold. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the government's revenue from the tax. **[1]**
**(d)** State what must be true of the good for this tax to improve the allocation of resources. **[1]**

**9.** The government of a made-up country gives a subsidy of £3 per unit for 1,200 units of a service that has a positive externality.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of the subsidy. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how the subsidy affects the price and the quantity of the service. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** problem with using a subsidy. **[2]**

**10.** A river is polluted by factories. Compare a tax on each unit of output with a limit on the amount of waste that each factory may release, and decide which is better. **[8]**

**11.** Four food vans operate near a stadium in a made-up town. Each van sells 3,000 meals a month. Packaging from each meal causes litter that costs the council £0.15 to clear.

**(a)** Calculate the total monthly external cost of the litter. **[2]**
**(b)** The council introduces a charge of £0.15 per meal. Explain how this could improve the allocation of resources. **[3]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether a charge is the best way for the council to deal with the litter. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Market failure is the inability of the market system [1] to allocate resources efficiently, so there is misallocation [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that say only "the market fails" or "prices are too high" repeat the term and show no understanding. Naming resource allocation is the idea the definition needs.

**2.** Social cost = private cost + external cost [1] = 0.80 + 0.20 = **£1.00 per loaf** [1]. External cost = 0.20 × 5,000 = **£1,000 a week** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the addition and the multiplication as separate lines, and put the pound sign on both answers. A bare number with no unit loses clarity.

**3. (a)** Negative, production [1].
**(b)** Positive, production [1].
**(c)** Negative, consumption [1].
**(d)** Positive, consumption [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each part needs both labels. Ask who causes the effect (the producer or the consumer), and then whether the third party is helped or harmed.

**4. (a)** 50 + 18 = **£68** [1].
**(b)** 18 × 600 = **£10,800** [1].
**(c)** 18 ÷ 68 [1] × 100 = **26.5%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* For a percentage, divide by the right base. The base here is social cost, not private cost, and the question says where to round.

**5.** Any two costs, each with an explanation, 2 marks each. For example: resources are wasted [1] because land, labour and capital used for over-produced goods could have made something worth more, so the opportunity cost is a better use forgone [1]. Third parties suffer harm such as pollution or ill health [1] and nobody pays for it through the price [1]. Other acceptable answers include too little of a beneficial good being provided, and public money spent on clean-up that cannot be used elsewhere. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a cost earns less than explaining it. Say who loses and what they lose, and use the word opportunity cost where it fits.

**6.** The firm counts only its private cost of £40 [1], but the true cost to society is £55 because of the £15 external cost [1]. The price is therefore too low, so more is produced than is best for society [1]. Total external cost = 15 × 800 = **£12,000** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the reasoning in order: cost ignored, price too low, output too high. A bare statement of "over-production" with no chain gets less credit.

**7. (a)** 9 + 3 = **£12** [1].
**(b)** 3 × 8,000 = **£24,000** [1].
**(c)** Riders consider only their own benefit of £9 [1], so demand is lower than the benefit to society of £12 [1]. Fewer trips are bought than is best for society, so the market provides too few [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Direction matters. A positive externality leads to under-provision, and a mix-up with the negative case is an easy way to lose several marks at once.

**8. (a)** Buyers pay 16.50 − 14 = **£2.50** of the tax [1], so producers pay 5 − 2.50 = **£2.50** [1].
**(b)** (9,000 − 7,200) ÷ 9,000 [1] × 100 = **20%** [1].
**(c)** 5 × 7,200 = **£36,000** [1].
**(d)** The good has a negative externality, and the tax is close to the size of the external cost per unit [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), the change in the buyers' price is the buyers' share. Compare the price after the tax with the price before it, and give the producers' share as the remainder.

**9. (a)** 3 × 1,200 = **£3,600** [1].
**(b)** The subsidy lowers the cost of supplying each unit, so supply increases [1]. The price falls and the quantity rises [1].
**(c)** Any one problem, with an explanation: it costs taxpayers £3,600 [1], and that money has an opportunity cost because it could be spent on something else [1]. Alternatives are that the external benefit may be measured wrongly, or that the producer may keep the subsidy as extra profit. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A problem with no consequence is a weaker point. "It costs money" improves when you say whose money it is and what else it could do.

**10.** Tax: makes the polluter pay for each unit, raising cost [1], so supply falls and output falls [1]. Firms keep the freedom to choose how to cut costs, and the government gains revenue [1]. The tax needs the external cost to be estimated well, and firms may pass it on to consumers [1]. Limit: sets a maximum that every factory must meet, backed by fines [1]. It gives a more certain result when the harm is serious [1], but it is less flexible and gives no income, and policing it costs money [1]. Judgement: a reasoned choice that depends on context, such as a limit where the pollution is dangerous and a tax where harm rises steadily with output, supported by one reason [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison earns more when each method is set against the other on the same point, such as certainty, flexibility or revenue. A final choice with no reason attached is less convincing.

**11. (a)** 0.15 × 3,000 = £450 per van [1]. × 4 = **£1,800 a month** [1].
**(b)** The price of a meal does not include the litter cost, so social cost is above private cost [1]. A charge of £0.15 adds the external cost to the cost of each meal [1]. Prices rise, fewer meals and less packaging are sold, and output moves towards the level that is best for society [1].
**(c)** For the charge: it makes those who cause the litter pay [1] and it raises revenue that can pay for clearing it [1]. Against: the council needs to be sure that £0.15 is the right figure [1], and vans might move or customers might litter elsewhere [1]. Close with a short judgement, such as that the charge suits this case if the litter cost is measured well. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* For an evaluation part, give points on both sides and then a short decision. Referring to the stadium and the vans keeps the points about this case, not about any charge.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining an externality as any cost to a firm, rather than a cost or benefit to a third party.
- Writing that a negative externality leads to under-production.
- Calculating a percentage with the wrong base.
- Giving no unit, or giving a result such as 1,000 without saying it is £1,000 a week.
- Saying a tax removes pollution, when it reduces output.
- Naming an intervention but not explaining how it changes cost, price or quantity.
- Evaluating by repeating the explanation, with no limit, cost or alternative.
- Leaving out the opportunity cost of public money.
