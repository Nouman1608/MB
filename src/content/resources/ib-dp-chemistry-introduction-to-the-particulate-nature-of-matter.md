## Structure 1.1.1 Elements, compounds and mixtures

### The three kinds of matter

- An **element** contains atoms of just one kind, all with the same atomic number. No chemical reaction can split it into simpler substances. Examples: neon, O₂, sulfur (S₈), copper.
- A **compound** contains atoms of two or more different elements that are chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. Water is always two hydrogen atoms to one oxygen atom. Getting the elements back out requires a chemical reaction.
- A **mixture** is made of two or more elements or compounds that are not chemically bonded to each other, in no fixed ratio. Because there are no bonds between the components, physical methods can separate them.

Elements and compounds are both **pure substances**. A mixture is not.

### How their properties differ

| Property | Element or compound | Mixture |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | Fixed | Variable |
| Melting and boiling points | Sharp, at one temperature (at a given pressure) | Usually over a range of temperatures |
| Properties compared with the parts | A compound's properties are quite different from those of its elements | Each component keeps its own properties |
| Separation | A compound needs a chemical reaction | Physical methods are enough |

Take hydrogen and oxygen. A balloon of both gases is a mixture in any proportions. Ignite it and they react to form water, a compound that is liquid at room temperature and is always H₂O.

### Homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures

- A **homogeneous** mixture has the same composition throughout and only one phase. You cannot see separate parts. Examples: air, a sugar solution, brass.
- A **heterogeneous** mixture does not have a uniform composition. You can see (or detect with a microscope) more than one phase. Examples: sand in water, oil floating on vinegar, granite.

Alloys such as brass are treated as mixtures: despite their metallic bonding, the proportions of the metals can vary.

### Worked example 1: classifying substances

Classify each of the following: (a) ozone, O₃; (b) magnesium oxide; (c) seawater; (d) chalk powder stirred into water.

1. (a) O₃ contains only oxygen atoms: an **element**, even though it is molecular.
2. (b) Magnesium and oxygen are chemically bonded in a fixed 1:1 ratio, so magnesium oxide is a **compound**.
3. (c) Seawater is water with dissolved salts in variable amounts. It is uniform throughout: a **homogeneous mixture**.
4. (d) Chalk does not dissolve, so solid particles are suspended in the liquid and settle out: a **heterogeneous mixture**.

### Separation methods

Each method uses a difference in a physical property between the components.

| Method | Property it uses | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Solvation (dissolving) | Solubility in a chosen solvent | Solvent particles surround the particles of a solute, taking one component into solution and leaving others behind |
| Filtration | Particle size: solid vs dissolved | Holds back an insoluble solid (residue) while the solution passes through (filtrate) |
| Evaporation | Volatility | Removes a solvent to leave a dissolved solid behind |
| Recrystallization | Solubility changing with temperature | Purifies a solid: dissolve in the minimum of hot solvent, cool, collect the crystals; soluble impurities stay in solution |
| Distillation | Boiling point | Simple distillation recovers a solvent from a solution; fractional distillation separates miscible liquids with different boiling points |
| Paper chromatography | Distribution between a stationary phase and a mobile phase | Separates and identifies the components of a small sample, such as a mixture of dyes |

### Worked example 2: purifying a reaction product by recrystallization

A chemist makes benzoic acid. The crude solid contains some sand and a small amount of a coloured impurity that is soluble in water. Benzoic acid is much more soluble in hot water than in cold water. Describe how to obtain pure, dry crystals.

1. Add **just enough hot water** to dissolve the crude solid (solvation), so that as much product as possible crystallizes later.
2. **Filter while hot** to remove the sand, so benzoic acid does not crystallize in the funnel.
3. **Cool the filtrate** slowly. Benzoic acid's solubility falls and crystals form; the small amount of coloured impurity stays dissolved.
4. **Filter** to collect the crystals, then **wash** them with a little cold water to remove the solution left on their surface.
5. **Dry** the crystals, for example in a warm oven or a desiccator.

A pure sample melts sharply, so a melting point checks purity.

### Worked example 3: paper chromatography

Pigments extracted from a flower petal are spotted on a pencil base line on chromatography paper and run in a solvent. The solvent front travels 6.8 cm. Two spots appear, at 2.04 cm and 4.42 cm from the base line. Find each retardation factor, Rf.

1. Rf = (base line to centre of spot) ÷ (base line to solvent front).
2. First spot: 2.04 ÷ 6.8 = **0.30**.
3. Second spot: 4.42 ÷ 6.8 = **0.65**.
4. Compare with known pigments run in the same solvent and paper. A matching Rf suggests the same substance.

Draw the base line in pencil (ink would separate) and keep the spots above the solvent level.

## Structure 1.1.2 Kinetic molecular theory and changes of state

The **kinetic molecular theory** models matter as particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are always moving. The strength of the attractions between the particles, compared with their kinetic energy, decides the state.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | Ordered lattice, particles touching | Particles touching but disordered | Widely spaced, no order |
| Movement | Vibration about a fixed point | Particles slide past each other | Fast, random straight-line motion between collisions |
| Shape and volume | Fixed shape, fixed volume | Takes the shape of its container, fixed volume | Fills its container |
| Compressibility | Almost none | Very little | Easy, because of the large spaces |
| Attraction between particles | Strongest | Intermediate | Very weak |

### State symbols

Add a state symbol after every formula in an equation:

- (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas
- (aq) aqueous: dissolved in water

Example: Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s). Physical changes take state symbols too, for example Br₂(l) → Br₂(g).

### Names of the changes of state

| Process | What happens | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | a solid becomes a liquid | endothermic |
| Freezing | a liquid becomes a solid | exothermic |
| Vaporization | a liquid becomes a gas | endothermic |
| Condensation | a gas becomes a liquid | exothermic |
| Sublimation | a solid becomes a gas directly, with no liquid stage | endothermic |
| Deposition | a gas becomes a solid directly, with no liquid stage | exothermic |

**Vaporization** has two forms. **Evaporation** happens only at the surface of a liquid and at any temperature: the fastest-moving particles escape. **Boiling** happens throughout the liquid, at one temperature for a given pressure, with bubbles of vapour forming inside it.

Changes that separate particles need energy to overcome the attractions between them, so they are endothermic; the reverse changes release energy.

Frost forming on a cold windscreen is deposition; solid carbon dioxide turning straight into gas is sublimation.

## Structure 1.1.3 Temperature and kinetic energy

### Temperature in kelvin

Temperature's SI unit is the **kelvin**, symbol K. Temperature in kelvin is a measure of the **average kinetic energy** of the particles in a sample: if the kelvin temperature doubles, the average kinetic energy doubles. Not every particle has the same energy; the temperature reflects the average.

One kelvin is the same size step as one degree Celsius, so converting only shifts the zero:

- T (K) = T (°C) + 273.15
- T (°C) = T (K) − 273.15

Many questions round this to 273. Kelvin values are written without a degree sign and are never negative. 0 K is absolute zero.

### Worked example 4: converting temperatures

Use these values: nitrogen boils at −196 °C; tungsten melts at 3422 °C; gallium melts at 302.9 K.

1. Nitrogen: −196 + 273.15 = **77.15 K** (77 K to the nearest kelvin).
2. Tungsten: 3422 + 273.15 = **3695.15 K** (3695 K).
3. Gallium: 302.9 − 273.15 = **29.75 °C**, so it would melt in your hand.

### Worked example 5: does the average kinetic energy double?

A sealed sample of gas is heated from 27 °C to 327 °C. Does the average kinetic energy of the particles double? What if it is heated from 10 °C to 20 °C instead?

1. Convert to kelvin: 27 °C = 300.15 K; 327 °C = 600.15 K.
2. Ratio: 600.15 ÷ 300.15 = 2.00 (to 3 significant figures). So **yes, the average kinetic energy doubles** (very nearly).
3. For 10 °C → 20 °C: 283.15 K → 293.15 K. Ratio = 293.15 ÷ 283.15 = 1.035.
4. The average kinetic energy rises by only about **3.5%**, although the Celsius number doubled. Use kelvin for ratios.

### Heating and cooling curves

When a pure solid is heated steadily, the temperature rises, then stays constant while it melts, rises again, then stays constant while it boils.

- **Sloping sections:** the energy supplied increases the average kinetic energy of the particles, so the temperature rises.
- **Flat sections (plateaus):** the energy overcomes the attractions between particles, raising their potential energy, not their kinetic energy. No temperature rise happens until every particle has changed state.
- The boiling plateau is usually longer, because separating particles into a gas needs more energy than loosening them into a liquid.

A cooling curve shows the same features in reverse, with plateaus at the condensation and freezing temperatures. A **mixture** shows no sharp plateau: it melts or freezes over a range of temperatures. That is why a melting point is a quick test of purity.

### Worked example 6: reading a heating curve

A pure substance is heated from −20 °C at a steady rate. The temperature rises to 17 °C and stays there for 3 minutes, rises to 118 °C and stays there for 11 minutes, then rises again. Use these data.

1. The first plateau is the **melting point: 17 °C = 290.15 K**. Solid and liquid are both present along it.
2. The second plateau is the **boiling point: 118 °C = 391.15 K**. Liquid and gas are both present.
3. At 50 °C the substance is a **liquid**, so its state symbol would be (l).
4. The boiling plateau is longer because vaporization needs more energy than melting.

## Common errors

- Calling O₂ or O₃ a compound because it is a molecule. A molecule of one element is still an element.
- Calling an alloy a compound. It is a mixture, despite the metallic bonding.
- Writing (aq) for a pure liquid such as ethanol. Use (l); (aq) means dissolved in water.
- Confusing evaporation (surface, any temperature) with boiling (throughout the liquid).
- Saying temperature rises during melting. It stays constant on the plateau.
- Calculating a ratio of kinetic energies from Celsius values.
