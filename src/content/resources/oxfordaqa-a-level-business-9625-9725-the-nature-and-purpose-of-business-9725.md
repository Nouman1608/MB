This guide covers section **3.1.1.1 The nature and purpose of business** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business specification (9725), Version 1.1, for International AS exams May/June 2027 onwards. It is first-year (International AS) content in section 3.1 Business and markets, which the specification assesses in Unit 1. If you are completing the older 9625 specification, revise from that document instead, because its first topic is organised differently.

The specification groups the content under three headings:

- **Entrepreneurs**: reasons why people set up in business (financial and non-financial motivations); characteristics of an entrepreneur (risk taker, resilient, hard working, creative, passionate, innovative, adaptable); challenges of being an entrepreneur and starting up a business; how the role of the entrepreneur may change as the business grows.
- **Business objectives**: typical objectives (survival, profit, growth, cash flow, social); the features of effective objectives (Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time specific: SMART); the value of setting business objectives.
- **Business plans**: the business plan; its key elements (ownership, objectives, target market and market analysis, competitor analysis, unique selling point, financial analysis); the benefits and limitations of a business plan; the meaning and significance of competitiveness.

## Entrepreneurs

### Why people start businesses

Motivations are either **financial** or **non-financial**, and most founders have a mix.

- Financial: the hope of earning more than a salary; building something valuable enough to sell; the lack of a well-paid job locally.
- Non-financial: wanting to be in charge of one's own work; pursuing an interest; flexible hours to fit family life; wanting to solve a problem in the community.

In a case study, look for clues to the motive, because it predicts behaviour. A founder driven by independence may refuse outside investment even when it would speed growth.

### The seven characteristics

9725 names seven characteristics. Note that this list includes **hard working** and **creative**, which some other specifications leave out.

- **Risk taker**: commits money and security to an uncertain idea.
- **Resilient**: keeps going after setbacks such as a failed product or a lost customer.
- **Hard working**: early stages usually mean long hours with little help.
- **Creative**: generates ideas for products, promotions or solutions to problems.
- **Passionate**: enthusiasm persuades customers, staff and lenders.
- **Innovative**: turns ideas into new products or processes that actually work in the market.
- **Adaptable**: changes direction when evidence shows the plan is not working.

Creativity and innovation are related but not identical: creativity is having the idea, innovation is putting it into practice successfully.

### Challenges of being an entrepreneur and starting up

- Finance is hard to obtain without a trading history or assets to offer as security.
- Customers must be persuaded to try an unknown business.
- Cash runs short because spending on stock, equipment and premises comes before sales.
- The founder may lack expertise in finance, law, marketing or managing people.
- Income is uncertain, and the founder carries the personal stress of the risk.

### How the role changes as the business grows

| Stage | Entrepreneur's main role |
|---|---|
| Start-up | Does most tasks personally: producing, selling, buying, record keeping |
| Early growth | Recruits staff and starts to delegate; still involved in daily operations |
| Established | Leads managers, sets objectives and strategy; less hands-on |
| Mature or sale | May appoint a professional chief executive, step back, or sell the business |

The skills that made the start-up succeed, such as doing everything personally, can hold the business back later if the founder will not delegate.

## Business objectives

### The typical objectives

- **Survival**: continuing to trade, the first aim for most new businesses and for any business in a crisis.
- **Profit**: revenue exceeding costs; once survival is secure, owners usually expect a return.
- **Growth**: increasing sales, market share, the number of outlets or the size of the workforce.
- **Cash flow**: making sure enough cash comes in to pay bills on time; essential because a business can be profitable on paper yet unable to pay its suppliers.
- **Social**: benefiting society, for example by creating jobs for disadvantaged groups or reducing environmental harm.

### SMART, as 9725 defines it

The specification gives SMART as **Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time specific**. "Accountable" means a named person or team is responsible for achieving the objective.

Testing an objective: "Grow the café chain" fails every test. "The operations director will open four new cafés, taking the total from 10 to 14, by 31 December next year" passes: it is specific (new cafés), measurable (four), accountable (the operations director), realistic if finance is available, and time specific (a date).

### The value of setting objectives

Objectives focus effort and help coordinate departments; they let owners measure performance and hold managers to account; and they give a basis for choosing between options. Their value falls if they are unrealistic (demotivating), ignored, or not reviewed when conditions change.

## Business plans

A business plan is a document that sets out what a business intends to do, how it will do it and what results it expects. Founders prepare one before starting; established businesses prepare them for major projects.

### The key elements, with a worked example

**Saffron Lane Tiffins** plans to deliver home-cooked lunches to offices.

| Element | What it covers | Saffron Lane's plan |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Legal form and who owns and controls the business | A private limited company owned by two founders, 50% each |
| Objectives | What the business aims to achieve, ideally SMART | Survive the first year; reach 250 lunches a day within 12 months |
| Target market and market analysis | Who the customers are; size and trends of the market | Office workers within 3 km; about 40 offices; demand for healthy lunches rising |
| Competitor analysis | Who the rivals are, their strengths and weaknesses | Two sandwich shops and a national delivery app; neither offers fresh cooked meals delivered in reusable boxes |
| Unique selling point (USP) | What makes the business different from rivals | Freshly cooked regional meals in returnable boxes, ordered by 10am |
| Financial analysis | Start-up costs, forecasts of sales, costs, profit and cash flow | Start-up costs £18,000; price £7; forecast break-even at about 120 lunches a day |

**A critical look.** The plan is clear on its USP and market. Its weakness is the financial analysis: 250 lunches a day is a forecast based on a small survey, and if only half the 40 offices order regularly, revenue could fall well short. A careful reader would ask for a cash-flow forecast showing how long the founders can survive at lower sales.

### Benefits of a business plan

- Forces the founder to research the market and the competition before committing money.
- Needed to persuade banks and investors to provide finance.
- Sets objectives and forecasts against which actual results can be compared.
- Helps communicate the idea to partners, staff and suppliers.

### Limitations of a business plan

- Forecasts for a new business rest on assumptions that may be wrong.
- Market conditions change, so a plan can quickly go out of date.
- Preparing a detailed plan takes time and may need paid advice.
- A plan can give false confidence, making the founder slow to react when reality differs.

## Competitiveness

Competitiveness is the ability of a business to attract customers away from rivals and keep them. A business can compete on **price**, which depends on keeping costs low, or on **non-price** factors such as quality, design, reliability, service, brand and convenience. Its significance:

- A competitive business can win market share, charge more or both, which supports profit and growth.
- An uncompetitive business loses customers, has to cut prices, sees margins shrink and may not survive.
- Competitiveness is relative: it can fall even if the business does nothing wrong, because rivals improve.

The USP in a business plan is the founder's claim about where the business's competitiveness will come from.

## Exam technique

- **Short-answer questions**: give precise definitions and, where asked, one applied example.
- **Calculation questions**: show working and units; in this section these may involve forecasts in a business plan.
- **Extended responses**: link characteristics, objectives or plan elements to the case, analyse the consequences and reach a supported judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Listing all seven characteristics without linking any to the case.
- Writing "Achievable" instead of "Accountable" in SMART.
- Treating a business plan as a guarantee of finance or success.
- Giving the elements of a plan but not judging their quality.
- Confusing creativity (the idea) with innovation (making it work).
- Describing competitiveness only in terms of price.
