## Condensed recall

- **Free trade:** trade between countries without government barriers.
- Barriers: **tariffs** (taxes on imports), **quotas** (limits on quantity), **non-tariff barriers** (rules, paperwork, checks, subsidies to home firms).
- Free trade mostly benefits **consumers** and **exporters**; protection mostly benefits **firms and workers competing with imports**.
- Three levels of agreement: free-trade agreement → customs union (plus a common external tariff) → single market (plus common rules and free movement of goods, services, capital and people).
- The **EU** is a customs union and single market of 27 states, and as a whole the UK's largest trading partner.
- UK timeline: joined the EEC on 1 January 1973; referendum on 23 June 2016; left on 31 January 2020; Trade and Cooperation Agreement from 1 January 2021.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Protectionism | Using barriers to protect domestic producers from foreign competition |
| Non-tariff barrier | Any obstacle to trade other than a tariff, such as border checks or different product rules |
| Common external tariff | The same tariff charged by all members of a customs union on goods from outside |
| Rules of origin | Rules that decide whether a good was made mainly within an agreement and so trades tariff-free |
| Retaliation | One country imposing barriers in response to another's |
| Infant industry | A new industry with high costs that may need temporary protection |
| Strategic industry | An industry seen as vital to national security, such as energy or food |
| Dumping | Selling exports abroad below their cost of production |

## Arguments in one table

| For free trade | Against free trade |
|---|---|
| Lower prices and more choice for consumers | Job losses in industries that cannot compete |
| Specialisation raises world output | Infant industries may need protection to grow |
| Larger markets and economies of scale for exporters | Strategic industries may need protecting |
| Competition drives efficiency and innovation | Dumping and very low standards abroad can be unfair |
| Cheaper imported inputs for firms | Tariffs raise government revenue |
| Avoids retaliation against exports | Domestic industries in some regions may decline |

## Who gains, who loses: a method

For any change in barriers, check five groups:

1. **Consumers:** prices and choice.
2. **Exporters:** access to foreign markets.
3. **Firms competing with imports:** competition at home.
4. **Workers:** jobs in exporting versus import-competing industries.
5. **Government:** tariff revenue, and any retaliation.

## Rules of origin in one example

Members P and Q have a free-trade agreement. P charges 5% on shoes from outside; Q charges 15%. Without rules of origin, outside shoes could enter P and move to Q tariff-free, avoiding Q's tariff. So only shoes made mainly in P or Q trade tariff-free, and firms must prove origin. A customs union, with one common external tariff, does not need these checks between members.

## The EU and the UK since leaving

- The Trade and Cooperation Agreement keeps trade in goods between the UK and the EU free of tariffs and quotas where goods meet its rules of origin.
- The UK left the customs union and single market, so there are now customs declarations, checks and separate product rules: **non-tariff barriers** that add costs, especially for small firms.
- The UK sets its own tariffs and makes its own agreements, for example with Australia and New Zealand.

## Worked calculations

**1. Does a tariff protect home producers?** An imported jacket costs £40 and a UK-made jacket £45. With a 25% tariff, the import costs 40 × 1.25 = **£50**, so the UK jacket is now cheaper. Remove the tariff in a trade agreement and the import is again £5 cheaper.

**2. Tariff revenue.** A 10% tariff on £2 million of imports raises **£200,000** for the government.

**3. Tariff on an input.** A UK firm imports 20,000 motors a year at £350 each. A 12% tariff adds 0.12 × 350 = £42 per motor, or **£840,000** a year; each motor now costs **£392**.

**4. Comparing two members' tariffs.** A £30 outside good costs 30 × 1.05 = **£31.50** in a member with a 5% tariff and 30 × 1.20 = **£36** in a member with a 20% tariff. This price gap is why rules of origin are needed in a free-trade area.

## Exam traps

- A free-trade agreement, a customs union and a single market are different levels of integration.
- Under the Trade and Cooperation Agreement, most UK–EU trade in goods is tariff-free; the main new costs are checks and paperwork.
- A tariff raises prices for **firms buying inputs** as well as for consumers.
- Protection can bring retaliation against a country's exports.
- "The EU is the UK's largest trading partner" refers to the EU as a whole, not to any one member.

## Self-test

1. Define free trade.
2. Give one example of a non-tariff barrier.
3. What does a customs union add to a free-trade agreement?
4. What does a single market add to a customs union?
5. Why do free-trade areas need rules of origin?
6. Give two arguments for free trade.
7. Explain the infant industry argument.
8. A £60 imported good faces a 15% tariff. What is its new price?
9. A 5% tariff applies to £12 million of imports. How much revenue does it raise?
10. In which year did the UK join the European Economic Community?
11. Give one new cost UK exporters to the EU have faced since 2021.
12. Give one benefit to the UK of being outside the EU customs union.

### Answers

1. Trade between countries without barriers such as tariffs and quotas imposed by governments.
2. Any one: border checks, customs paperwork, different product standards, subsidies to domestic firms.
3. A common external tariff on goods from outside the union.
4. Common rules on products and free movement of goods, services, capital and people.
5. Members charge different tariffs on outside goods, so without rules goods could enter through the member with the lowest tariff and move on tariff-free.
6. Any two: lower prices, more choice, specialisation, larger markets and economies of scale, competition and efficiency, cheaper inputs.
7. A new industry has high costs until it grows, so it may need temporary protection until it can compete with established foreign firms.
8. 60 × 1.15 = £69.
9. 0.05 × 12,000,000 = £600,000.
10. 1973.
11. Any one: customs declarations, border checks, meeting separate product rules, proving rules of origin.
12. It can set its own tariffs and make its own trade agreements with other countries.
