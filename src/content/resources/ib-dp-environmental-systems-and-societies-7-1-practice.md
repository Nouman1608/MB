## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between natural capital and natural income, using a fishery as an example. **[2]**

**2.** A fictional wetland, Marsh Q, provides the following. Classify each as a provisioning, regulating, supporting or cultural service: (i) reeds cut for thatching; (ii) storage of floodwater after storms; (iii) birdwatching tourism; (iv) decomposition that recycles nutrients. **[4]**

**3.** Table 1 shows the wood biomass of a fictional plantation with no felling.

| Date | 1 Jan 2020 | 1 Jan 2021 | 1 Jan 2022 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biomass (t) | 12,000 | 12,540 | 13,100 |

**(a)** Calculate the sustainable yield for 2020 and for 2021. **[2]**
**(b)** From 2022 the owner fells 700 t a year. Assuming the yield stays at the 2021 value, calculate the biomass at the start of 2025. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the biomass may in fact fall faster than your answer to (b). **[2]**

**4.** A fictional fish stock grows logistically with a carrying capacity of 50,000 t and an intrinsic growth rate of 0.4 per year. Use: increase = r × N × (1 − N ÷ K).

**(a)** Calculate the maximum sustainable yield. **[1]**
**(b)** State the stock size at which it occurs. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the natural increase when the stock is 10,000 t. **[2]**
**(d)** A quota of 5,000 t is kept when the stock is 10,000 t. Calculate the change in the stock in the first year. **[1]**
**(e)** Suggest **two** reasons why managers might set the catch below the MSY. **[2]**

**5.** A fictional country holds 2,400 Mt of a metal ore reserve and extracts 80 Mt a year.

**(a)** Calculate the reserves-to-production ratio. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the ratio if extraction rises to 120 Mt a year. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain **two** reasons why the ratio in (a) may underestimate how long the reserve will last. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why water from some aquifers should be treated as non-renewable natural capital. **[2]**

**7.** Outline the argument of the tragedy of the commons. **[3]**

**8.** Explain, with reference to the work of Elinor Ostrom, why some common-pool resources are managed sustainably. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate the use of individual transferable quotas to manage a fishery. **[4]**

**10.** Distinguish between use values and non-use values of a tropical forest, giving **two** examples of each. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how technocentric and ecocentric value systems might lead to different management of a mangrove coast. **[6]**

**12.** To what extent is maximum sustainable yield a useful basis for managing fisheries? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Natural capital is the stock that produces income, here the fish population [1]; natural income is the yield it produces, here the yearly increase in fish that can be caught without reducing the stock [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Calling the fish caught "natural capital" reverses the terms and earns nothing.

**2.** (i) Provisioning [1]; (ii) regulating [1]; (iii) cultural [1]; (iv) supporting [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Flood storage is regulating, not supporting; supporting services underpin other services, such as nutrient cycling and soil formation.

**3. (a)** 2020: 12,540 − 12,000 = **540 t** [1]; 2021: 13,100 − 12,540 = **560 t** [1]
**(b)** Net change = 560 − 700 = −140 t a year [1]; 13,100 − 3 × 140 = **12,680 t** [1]
**(c)** As felling removes more than the increase, the stock gets smaller [1], and a smaller stock (fewer, younger trees) usually grows more slowly, so each year's increase falls and the gap with the harvest widens [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) subtracting 700 t a year without adding the yield gives 11,000 t; the stock still grows while it is felled.

**4. (a)** 0.4 × 50,000 ÷ 4 = **5,000 t per year** [1]
**(b)** **25,000 t** (half of K) [1]
**(c)** 0.4 × 10,000 × (1 − 10,000 ÷ 50,000) [1] = 0.4 × 10,000 × 0.8 = **3,200 t** [1]
**(d)** 3,200 − 5,000 = **−1,800 t** [1]
**(e)** Any two: stock estimates are uncertain, so the true stock may be smaller than thought [1]; environmental variation such as a poor breeding year can cut the increase [1]; catching at MSY leaves no margin, and a stock pushed below K ÷ 2 declines faster each year [1]; by-catch and illegal fishing add to the real catch [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (e) needs reasons tied to risk; "to be sustainable" restates the question.

**5. (a)** 2,400 ÷ 80 = **30 years** [1]
**(b)** 2,400 ÷ 120 = **20 years** [1]
**(c)** Any two: higher prices can make lower-grade ore economic, adding to reserves [1]; new technology can recover more from deposits [1]; new deposits may be discovered [1]; recycling can reduce the need for new extraction [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Prices change" is not enough; say which way and why it changes reserves.

**6.** Some aquifers filled thousands of years ago and receive little or no recharge today [1], so any abstraction reduces the stock permanently on a human timescale, as with mining a mineral [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Abstraction faster than recharge also earns the second mark if explained as running down the stock.

**7.** A common-pool resource is open to many users [1]. Each user gains the whole benefit of taking a little more, while the cost of depletion is shared among all users [1]. So each acts rationally in their own interest, and the combined effect is overexploitation of the resource [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The key mark is the contrast between private benefit and shared cost.

**8.** Ostrom studied fisheries, forests and irrigation systems and found many sustainably managed by their users [1]. Success depended on conditions such as clear boundaries for the resource and its users [1], users taking part in making rules suited to local conditions [1], and monitoring with graduated sanctions so cheating is detected and punished [1]. Local conflict resolution and outside recognition of the right to organise also earn credit. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name at least two conditions and explain how each prevents overuse; a list of principles without effect is limited to 2 marks.

**9.** For: each holder owns a share of the catch, so there is less incentive to race to catch fish before others [1]; holders gain from a healthy stock because their quota's value rises, encouraging long-term thinking [1]. Against: quotas can be bought up by large firms, so small fishers and communities lose access [1]; the system only works if the total allowable catch is set correctly and catches are monitored [1]. A judgement weighing these against the fishery's situation earns credit within the 4 marks. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs both strengths and limitations; a one-sided answer caps at 2.

**10.** Use values: any two, such as timber or food harvested (direct use) [1], and carbon storage or water regulation (indirect use) [1]; option value of medicines not yet discovered also earns credit. Non-use values: existence value, knowing rare species survive [1], and bequest value, keeping the forest for future generations [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Ecotourism is a use value; placing it under non-use is a common slip.

**11.** Indicative points, up to 6.
- Technocentric: values the coast mainly for what it provides to people and trusts technology and management to replace lost services [1].
- It might allow conversion of some mangrove to aquaculture for income, using sea walls to replace storm protection [1].
- It might value mangroves through markets, such as payments for carbon storage [1].
- Ecocentric: sees intrinsic value in the ecosystem and favours protection and small-scale, low-impact use [1].
- It might prefer community management and restoration of cleared areas, accepting lower short-term income [1].
- Balanced comment: in practice, managers often combine both, such as protected zones alongside sustainable harvesting, and the choice depends on who holds power and who bears the costs [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each value system needs a concrete management action; describing the value systems in general terms earns little.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9. Real essays are marked holistically, so treat these as a guide.
- MSY is the largest catch that can be taken year after year, at about half the carrying capacity [1].
- Useful: it gives a clear, quantitative target that links catch to the stock's natural increase [1].
- Useful: it recognises that a fished stock can be productive, balancing food and jobs with conservation [1].
- Limitation: stock size and growth rate are uncertain, so the target may be set too high [1].
- Limitation: the model treats one species alone, ignoring predators, prey and by-catch [1].
- Limitation: environmental change, such as warming seas, shifts K and r over time [1].
- Limitation: once a stock falls below half of K, a quota at MSY causes accelerating decline, as the northern cod collapse and the 1992 moratorium showed [1].
- Political pressure to protect jobs tends to push quotas up, not down [1].
- Judgement: MSY is useful as an upper limit, but safer management sets catches below it, uses precautionary margins and adds tools such as no-take reserves [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers use a case study as evidence and end with a conditional judgement, not "MSY is good and bad".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the harvest the sustainable yield, or forgetting that the stock grows while it is harvested.
- Using MSY as if it applies at every stock size.
- Treating the reserves-to-production ratio as a fixed end date.
- Classifying flood storage as supporting rather than regulating.
- Describing value systems without saying how they change a management decision.
- Essays with no case study and no final judgement.
