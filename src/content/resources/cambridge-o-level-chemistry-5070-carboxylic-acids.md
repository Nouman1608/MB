Section 11.7 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has three learning outcomes. You must be able to describe the reactions of carboxylic acids with metals, bases and carbonates, "including names and formulae of the salts produced"; describe the formation of ethanoic acid by oxidation of ethanol "with acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII)" and "by bacterial oxidation during vinegar production"; and describe the reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol using an acid catalyst to form an ester. 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so every outcome is examined for every candidate.

The broader [Alcohols and Carboxylic Acids guide](/resources/alcohols-and-carboxylic-acids/) shows these reactions for ethanoic acid. Because 5070 says "carboxylic acids" in general, this guide extends them to methanoic, propanoic and butanoic acids, shows how to build salt formulae from ion charges, and adds the observations and tests that structured questions ask for.

## Carboxylic acids are weak acids

A carboxylic acid contains the –COOH group. In water it releases hydrogen ions, but only partially. In section 7.1 terms, it is a **weak acid**: partially dissociated in aqueous solution.

```
CH3COOH(aq) ⇌ CH3COO–(aq) + H+(aq)
```

So carboxylic acids show all the typical reactions of acids, but **more slowly** than a strong acid such as hydrochloric acid of the same concentration, because there are fewer H⁺ ions in the solution.

**Worked example.** Equal volumes of ethanoic acid and hydrochloric acid, both of concentration 1.0 mol/dm³, are added to identical pieces of magnesium ribbon. Compare what is seen and explain the difference.

*Answer.* Both produce bubbles of hydrogen and the magnesium dissolves, but the bubbles form more slowly with ethanoic acid [1]. Ethanoic acid is a weak acid, only partially dissociated, so its solution has a lower concentration of H⁺ ions than hydrochloric acid, which is completely dissociated [1]. If the magnesium is in excess, both acids eventually give the **same volume** of hydrogen, because they contain the same number of moles of acid molecules; only the rate differs.

## Salts of carboxylic acids

The salt is named from the metal and the acid, with **-oic acid** changed to **-oate**.

| Acid | Formula | Negative ion | Ion formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methanoic acid | HCOOH | methanoate | HCOO⁻ |
| Ethanoic acid | CH₃COOH | ethanoate | CH₃COO⁻ |
| Propanoic acid | CH₃CH₂COOH | propanoate | CH₃CH₂COO⁻ |
| Butanoic acid | CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH | butanoate | CH₃CH₂CH₂COO⁻ |

Each carboxylate ion has a charge of **1−**. To write a salt formula, balance the charges, exactly as for inorganic salts.

**Worked example 1.** Write the formulae of sodium propanoate and calcium ethanoate.

*Answer.* Sodium is Na⁺ (1+) and propanoate is CH₃CH₂COO⁻ (1−), so one of each: **CH₃CH₂COONa**. Calcium is Ca²⁺ (2+), so two ethanoate ions are needed: **(CH₃COO)₂Ca**.

The brackets matter: "CH₃COO₂Ca" would mean one ion with an extra oxygen atom.

## The three reactions

### With reactive metals

```
acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
2CH3COOH + Mg → (CH3COO)2Mg + H2
2HCOOH + Zn → (HCOO)2Zn + H2
```

Observations: bubbles (effervescence), the metal gets smaller and eventually disappears. The gas gives a "pop" with a lighted splint.

### With bases

```
acid + base → salt + water
CH3CH2COOH + NaOH → CH3CH2COONa + H2O
2CH3COOH + CuO → (CH3COO)2Cu + H2O
```

With copper(II) oxide, a black solid dissolves on warming to give a blue solution. With an alkali such as sodium hydroxide there is no visible change, so an indicator is needed to follow the reaction.

### With carbonates

```
acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2
2CH3CH2COOH + CaCO3 → (CH3CH2COO)2Ca + H2O + CO2
```

Observations: fizzing; the gas turns limewater milky.

**Worked example 2.** Butanoic acid reacts with potassium carbonate. Name the salt and write the balanced equation.

*Answer.* Salt: **potassium butanoate**, CH₃CH₂CH₂COOK. Potassium carbonate is K₂CO₃, which supplies two K⁺ ions, so two acid molecules are needed:

```
2CH3CH2CH2COOH + K2CO3 → 2CH3CH2CH2COOK + H2O + CO2
```

## Making ethanoic acid by oxidising ethanol

Oxidation adds oxygen to ethanol and removes hydrogen. The syllabus names two methods.

**1. Acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII).** Ethanol is warmed with the purple oxidising agent. The [O] symbol stands for oxygen supplied by the oxidising agent:

```
CH3CH2OH + 2[O] → CH3COOH + H2O
```

The potassium manganate(VII) changes from **purple to colourless** as it oxidises the ethanol. This is the same colour change used in section 6.4 to identify a redox reaction.

**2. Bacterial oxidation during vinegar production.** Bacteria use oxygen from the air to oxidise the ethanol in wine or cider, producing ethanoic acid. Vinegar is a dilute solution of ethanoic acid.

```
C2H5OH + O2 → CH3COOH + H2O
```

This is why fermentation (section 11.6) must exclude air: once oxygen gets in, the ethanol can be oxidised and the liquid turns sour.

**Worked example 3.** Describe **two** chemical tests that would distinguish ethanol from aqueous ethanoic acid. [4]

*Answer.*

- Add sodium carbonate (or magnesium carbonate): ethanoic acid fizzes and gives a gas that turns limewater milky [1]; ethanol gives no fizzing [1].
- Warm with acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII): ethanol turns it from purple to colourless [1]; ethanoic acid leaves it purple, because it has already been oxidised [1].

## Esterification

A carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol, using an **acid catalyst**, to form an **ester** and **water**.

```
carboxylic acid + alcohol → ester + water
CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH → CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O     (ethyl ethanoate)
CH3CH2COOH + CH3OH → CH3CH2COOCH3 + H2O     (methyl propanoate)
```

The –OH of the acid and the H of the alcohol's –OH leave together as water; the two remaining parts join through –COO–. The ester is named alcohol part first (-yl), then acid part (-oate). For the full naming method, see [naming organic compounds](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-naming-organic-compounds/).

**Worked example 4.** Name the ester formed from butanoic acid and ethanol and write the equation.

*Answer.* Ethyl butanoate.

```
CH3CH2CH2COOH + CH3CH2OH → CH3CH2CH2COOCH2CH3 + H2O
```

## Common mistakes

- Naming the salt with "-oic": it is sodium ethan**oate**, not "sodium ethanoic".
- Missing brackets in salts of 2+ metals, such as (CH₃COO)₂Mg.
- Forgetting carbon dioxide in the carbonate reaction, or hydrogen in the metal reaction.
- Saying carboxylic acids react faster than hydrochloric acid of the same concentration; they are weak acids and react more slowly.
- Writing the oxidation of ethanol as combustion, with CO₂ as a product.
- Leaving out water as a product of esterification, or the acid catalyst as a condition.
- Saying potassium manganate(VII) turns purple; it starts purple and becomes colourless.

## Exam technique: the command words

- **Describe** (reaction): reactants, products with names, and observations such as fizzing.
- **Name**: salts end in -oate; esters are "alkyl alkanoate", alcohol part first.
- **Write** (formula or equation): balance charges in salts, then atoms in the equation.
- **Explain** (oxidation): ethanol gains oxygen and loses hydrogen, forming ethanoic acid.
