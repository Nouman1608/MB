## What the specification lists

- The need for accounts to give a "true and fair" view.
- The need to comply with Generally Accepted Accounting Practice (GAAP); knowledge of specific rules is not required.
- The conventions: consistency, going concern, accruals (matching), materiality, objectivity, prudence (conservatism), realisation.
- Apply the conventions; evaluate their usefulness, impact and importance.

## Core recall

### True and fair

- **True:** free from material error; records what happened.
- **Fair:** unbiased; does not mislead users.
- Users: owners, lenders, investors, tax authority, employees, suppliers.

### GAAP

- Rules and standards for preparing accounts.
- Gives comparability, confidence, legal compliance and less scope for manipulation.
- Cost: time and expense; rules may not suit every business.

### The seven conventions

| Convention | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Consistency | Same methods each year; disclose changes | Keep one depreciation method |
| Going concern | Assume the business continues | Stock valued at cost, not break-up value |
| Accruals (matching) | Record revenue and costs in the period they relate to | Accrue unpaid electricity; prepay insurance |
| Materiality | Only items big enough to affect decisions matter | Stapler expensed; press capitalised |
| Objectivity | Rely on verifiable evidence | Record cost from the invoice |
| Prudence | Do not overstate profit or assets; recognise likely losses early | Provide for a doubtful debt; stock at lower of cost and net realisable value |
| Realisation | Record revenue when earned (sale or delivery) | December order delivered in January counts in January |

### Key calculations

- **Prepayment:** amount paid × months not used ÷ 12.
- **Accrual:** cost incurred but unpaid, added to the expense.
- **Stock valuation:** lower of cost and net realisable value (selling price less selling costs).
- **Provision for doubtful debt:** reduces receivables and profit.
- **Accruals profit:** revenue earned less costs incurred, whatever cash moves.

### Usefulness

| Convention | Useful because | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Consistency | Year-to-year comparison | May block a better method |
| Going concern | Sensible asset values | Judgement about the future |
| Accruals | True profit for the period | Profit differs from cash |
| Materiality | Avoids clutter | Judgement on size |
| Objectivity | Reliable evidence | Ignores uncertain value such as brand |
| Prudence | Avoids over-optimism | Can understate profit |
| Realisation | Stops early revenue | May delay credit for a good year |

### Impact and importance

- Affects reported profit, tax, dividends and bonuses.
- Lenders and investors rely on consistent, prudent accounts.
- More important for large businesses with outside users, and where managers could flatter results.
- Conventions can conflict: prudence against accruals.

## Quick worked reminder

Penrose Printing (invented): sales earned £180,000 (£8,000 unpaid); cash costs £142,800. Cash profit £29,200. Accruals: electricity accrual £2,400 added and £2,700 of insurance carried forward as a prepayment, so costs are £142,500 and profit £37,500, which is £8,300 higher. Stock cost £6,000 with NRV £9 × 500 = £4,500 gives a £1,500 write-down; a £6,000 doubtful debt gives a total profit reduction of £7,500.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| True and fair view | Accounts that are free from material error and unbiased |
| GAAP | Generally Accepted Accounting Practice |
| Accrual | A cost incurred but not yet paid |
| Prepayment | A cost paid in advance for a later period |
| Net realisable value | Selling price less the cost of selling |
| Provision | An amount set aside for a likely future loss |
| Going concern | The assumption that the business will continue trading |
| Break-up value | The value of assets if sold off separately |

## Exam traps

- Prudence is "do not overstate", not "always understate".
- Accruals is about timing, not caution.
- Realisation is about when revenue counts, not when cash arrives.
- Do not state a convention without applying it to the figures.
- Link to the true and fair view when evaluating.

## Self-test

1. Explain "true and fair".
2. Why does GAAP matter to investors?
3. State the consistency convention.
4. State the going concern convention.
5. A business pays £4,800 rent on 1 July for 12 months; its year ends 31 December. How much is the prepayment?
6. Stock cost £9,000; net realisable value is £7,200. At what value should it be shown, and what is the write-down?
7. Receivables are £45,000, of which £3,500 is doubtful. What is the prudent value?
8. Which convention says a sale is recorded on delivery?
9. Give one example of a material and an immaterial item.
10. Which convention is breached if a business changes depreciation method each year to get the profit it wants?
11. Give one limit of the prudence convention.
12. Sales earned £95,000, of which £12,000 is unpaid; costs paid £70,000; wages of £3,000 accrued. Calculate accruals profit.

### Answers

1. Accounts that are free from material error (true) and prepared without bias so as not to mislead (fair).
2. It makes accounts comparable and reliable, so investors can compare businesses and trust the figures.
3. Use the same accounting methods from year to year, and disclose and explain any change.
4. Assume the business will continue to trade for the foreseeable future.
5. Paid £4,800 for 12 months; 6 months fall in the next year: **£2,400**.
6. Lower of cost and NRV: **£7,200**; write-down **£1,800**.
7. £45,000 − £3,500 = **£41,500**.
8. Realisation.
9. Material: a £14,000 machine in a small business. Immaterial: a £40 stapler.
10. Consistency (and it risks breaching true and fair).
11. It can understate profit and assets, and so give a misleading view in the other direction.
12. Costs = £70,000 + £3,000 = £73,000; profit = £95,000 − £73,000 = **£22,000**.
