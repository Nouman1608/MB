This guide covers **Component 2: Macroeconomics (H460/02)**, one of
three equally weighted components (each worth 33.33% of the full A
Level) in OCR A Level Economics (H460), for first assessment 2021. It
sits alongside Microeconomics (H460/01) and Themes in Economics
(H460/03), the latter a fully synoptic paper drawing on both
Microeconomics and Macroeconomics content.

## Syllabus coverage

**OCR A LEVEL ECONOMICS H460 -- COMPONENT 2 MACROECONOMICS**

- **1. Aggregate demand and aggregate supply** -- 1.1 the circular flow
  of income, including injections, leakages and methods of measuring
  national income; 1.2 aggregate demand and its components, the
  relationship between aggregate demand and price level, and shifts of
  the AD curve; 1.3 aggregate supply and its relationship with price
  level in the short and long run; 1.4 the interaction of aggregate
  demand and supply and macroeconomic equilibrium; and 1.5 the
  multiplier and accelerator, including calculating average and
  marginal propensities to consume, save and withdraw, the size of the
  national income multiplier, and output gaps.
- **2. Economic policy objectives** -- 2.1 economic growth, including
  calculating growth rates and GDP per capita; 2.2 development,
  including alternative measures such as the Human Development Index;
  2.3 employment, including measures of unemployment; 2.4 inflation,
  including measuring it via the Consumer Prices Index; 2.5 the
  balance of payments and its components; 2.6 trends in UK
  macroeconomic performance over the last 20 years; 2.7 income
  distribution and welfare, including Gini coefficients and the Lorenz
  curve; and 2.8 the Phillips Curve, including the natural rate of
  unemployment (NAIRU).
- **3. Implementing policy** -- 3.1 fiscal policy (government budgets,
  types of taxation, budget deficits/surpluses, discretionary policy
  versus automatic stabilisers, crowding out, and the Laffer curve);
  3.2 monetary policy (interest rates, money supply, inflation
  targets, quantitative easing); 3.3 supply-side policy (privatisation,
  deregulation, investment in infrastructure/education/training, and
  labour market flexibility); and 3.4 policy conflicts and trade-offs
  between objectives.
- **4. The global context** -- 4.1 international trade and its
  patterns over time; 4.2 exchange rates, including their determination
  and the effects of changes; 4.3 globalisation, international
  competitiveness, and comparative advantage; and 4.4 trade policies
  and negotiations, including protectionism, trading blocs, and the
  role of the World Trade Organisation.
- **5. The financial sector** -- 5.1 money and interest rates,
  including the creation and supply of money; 5.2 the role of the
  financial sector in promoting economic development, including the
  Harrod-Domar model and microfinance; and 5.3 financial regulation,
  including the role of a central bank and international bodies such
  as the IMF and World Bank.

## Why the five sub-topics form one connected argument

The specification's own framing of this component matters: it states
that students "will learn how the macroeconomy functions on both a
domestic and global level and develop an understanding of the
objectives, limitations and conflicts of macroeconomic policies." That
last phrase -- conflicts of policy -- is the thread that runs through
every sub-topic here. Aggregate demand and supply (section 1) sets up
the model; policy objectives (section 2) defines the targets a
government aims for; implementing policy (section 3) shows the tools
available; and section 3.4 explicitly requires evaluating when those
tools conflict with each other -- for example, when a policy that
boosts growth also raises inflation, or when reducing unemployment
conflicts with controlling the balance of payments. The global context
and financial sector sections then extend the same policy-conflict
logic to an international and monetary setting.

The marginal propensity to consume (MPC) in an economy is 0.75. Find
the value of the national income multiplier.

multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 1 ÷ 0.25 = **4**

This means an initial injection of £10 million into the economy would
ultimately generate £40 million of total extra national income, once
the multiplier effect works through successive rounds of spending --
a calculation that appears frequently in questions combining sections
1.5 (the multiplier) and 3.1 (fiscal policy, where government spending
is the initial injection).

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a shift in aggregate demand and a movement along it as
  the same thing** -- a change in the price level causes a movement
  along the AD curve; a change in any of AD's components (consumption,
  investment, government spending, net exports) for a reason other
  than price shifts the whole curve.
- **Confusing fiscal and monetary policy**, or describing supply-side
  policy only as "cutting taxes" without addressing its actual aim of
  shifting long-run aggregate supply through structural reform.
- **Evaluating policy in isolation from its trade-offs.** Section 3.4
  is explicitly about conflicts between objectives, so an answer that
  only lists the benefits of a policy without discussing what it might
  worsen elsewhere in the economy misses a core assessment objective
  of this component.
- **Misreading the Phillips Curve as a fixed, permanent relationship**
  between inflation and unemployment, rather than distinguishing the
  short-run trade-off from the long-run vertical curve at the natural
  rate of unemployment.
- **Ignoring the global context when answering a domestic policy
  question**, when in reality exchange rates, international
  competitiveness and trade policy frequently interact with domestic
  fiscal and monetary decisions in exam scenarios.

## How to approach it

Revise sections 1 and 2 together first, since aggregate demand and
supply is the model that every policy objective in section 2 is
ultimately measured against -- inflation, unemployment and growth are
all outcomes read off the same AD/AS diagram. Once that foundation is
solid, treat section 3 (implementing policy) as the natural next step,
explicitly practising policy-conflict evaluation questions rather than
only questions that ask you to describe a single policy's effect.
Since Component 3 (Themes in economics) is fully synoptic across both
Microeconomics and Macroeconomics, revise the global context and
financial sector sections with an eye to how they connect back to
microeconomic content such as market structures and exchange-rate
determination, rather than treating Macroeconomics as entirely
self-contained.

## Frequently asked questions

**What topics are in OCR A Level Economics Component 2 Macroeconomics?**
Component 2 (H460/02) covers five areas: aggregate demand and aggregate supply, economic policy objectives, implementing policy, the global context, and the financial sector.

**How much is the macroeconomics component worth in OCR A Level Economics?**
Component 2 is one of three equally weighted components, each worth 33.33% of the full OCR A Level Economics (H460) qualification.

**What is supply-side policy?**
Supply-side policy aims to shift long-run aggregate supply through structural reform. Describing it only as "cutting taxes" misses this aim.

**Why do exchange rates matter in a domestic policy question?**
Exchange rates, international competitiveness and trade policy often interact with domestic fiscal and monetary decisions. For example, an interest rate cut can weaken the currency, which affects import prices, inflation and the balance of payments.
