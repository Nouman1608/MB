Sub-topic 4.1 The atmosphere is the foundation for the rest of Topic 4 in the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029. It has three statements: the structure and composition of the atmosphere (4.1.1), a definition of climate (4.1.2) and the natural greenhouse effect (4.1.3). Each later sub-topic depends on it: climate change builds on the greenhouse effect, ozone depletion needs the location of the ozone layer, and acid rain needs the idea of gases reacting in the air. The content is short, so examiners expect precise answers.

## What the syllabus asks for

- **4.1.1(a)** The names and relative order of the layers: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere and thermosphere.
- **4.1.1(b)** The location of the ozone layer in the lower stratosphere.
- **4.1.1(c)** The percentage composition of clean air: 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, plus carbon dioxide, argon and water vapour.
- **4.1.2** Climate is the weather conditions in a location based on the weather over many years.
- **4.1.3** The five steps of the natural greenhouse effect.

The word "limited to" in the syllabus is important. You need the names and order of the layers, not their heights or temperatures, so spend your time on getting the order and the ozone layer's position right.

## The layers of the atmosphere

Starting at the Earth's surface and moving upwards, the layers are:

1. **Troposphere**: the layer we live in. It holds most of the air and nearly all of the water vapour, so clouds and weather form here.
2. **Stratosphere**: above the troposphere. Its **lower part contains the ozone layer**.
3. **Mesosphere**: above the stratosphere.
4. **Thermosphere**: the outermost of the four layers named in the syllabus.

A memory aid built from the first letters, T S M T, can help: "**T**all **S**tudents **M**ake **T**ables". In a diagram question you may be given a column with the layers unlabelled; label from the bottom up and mark the ozone layer just above the boundary between the troposphere and the stratosphere.

### Why the location of the ozone layer matters

Ozone in the lower stratosphere absorbs much of the Sun's ultraviolet radiation before it reaches the surface. This is why the loss of ozone, covered in sub-topic 4.4, increases the radiation reaching people, crops and other organisms. A common error is to place the ozone layer in the troposphere or to describe it as the outer edge of the atmosphere.

## The composition of clean air

| Gas | Approximate percentage by volume | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 78% | Largest component |
| Oxygen | 21% | Needed for respiration and combustion |
| Argon | Just under 1% | Unreactive |
| Carbon dioxide | About 0.04% | Small share, but a greenhouse gas |
| Water vapour | Varies from place to place | Higher in warm, humid air; a greenhouse gas |

The syllabus gives exact figures only for nitrogen and oxygen, so those are the ones to quote. The other three make up the remaining 1% or so between them. Notice that two of the minor gases, carbon dioxide and water vapour, are named greenhouse gases in sub-topic 4.2. A tiny percentage can still have a large effect, which is a helpful idea when you later explain why rising carbon dioxide matters.

"Clean air" means air without pollutants. Polluted air also contains gases such as sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen, which appear in sub-topic 4.3.

## Weather and climate

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a particular time: today's temperature, rainfall, cloud and wind. **Climate** is defined in the syllabus as the weather conditions in a location based on the weather over many years. Climate is usually described using averages, for example the mean temperature of each month and the mean total rainfall for each month, calculated from many years of records.

The distinction matters when you read data. "It rained 45 mm in the town yesterday" describes weather. "July is usually the wettest month in this town" describes climate. Later in the topic, **climate change** means a long-term change in these average conditions, not one unusual week.

## The natural greenhouse effect

The syllabus lists five steps. Learn them in order.

1. **Solar radiation passes through the Earth's atmosphere.**
2. **Some solar radiation is absorbed by the land and oceans, heating the Earth.**
3. **Some solar radiation is reflected back into space**, for example by clouds, ice and snow.
4. **Some solar radiation is absorbed and re-emitted back to the Earth's surface by greenhouse gases and clouds.**
5. **This natural process warms the Earth enough to support life.**

The important word in step 4 is *re-emitted*: greenhouse gases absorb radiation and send energy back towards the surface. They do not act as a solid lid or a mirror. Without the natural greenhouse effect, the average surface temperature would be well below freezing and most life as we know it could not exist. The problem studied in sub-topic 4.2 is the **enhanced** greenhouse effect, caused by extra greenhouse gases from human activities, not the natural effect itself.

## Worked example 1: gases in a room

*A practice calculation using the syllabus percentages.* A classroom measures 7 m by 6 m by 3 m.

1. Volume of air = 7 × 6 × 3 = 126 m³.
2. Nitrogen = 126 × 0.78 = 98.28 m³, about 98.3 m³.
3. Oxygen = 126 × 0.21 = 26.46 m³, about 26.5 m³.
4. Other gases = 126 − 98.28 − 26.46 = 1.26 m³, about 1.3 m³.

The answer shows how small the share of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour is: barely more than one cubic metre in the whole room.

## Worked example 2: using records to describe climate

*Figures invented for practice.* July rainfall in a town over five years was 210 mm, 185 mm, 240 mm, 160 mm and 205 mm.

1. Total = 210 + 185 + 240 + 160 + 205 = 1,000 mm.
2. Mean = 1,000 ÷ 5 = 200 mm.
3. Range = 240 − 160 = 80 mm.

A single July (the weather) could be anywhere between 160 mm and 240 mm, but the mean of 200 mm is closer to a description of the climate. Five years is a short record; the syllabus wording "over many years" explains why a longer record gives a more reliable picture.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Describe the natural greenhouse effect.* **[4]**

> Solar radiation passes through the atmosphere and some is absorbed by the land and oceans, heating the Earth. ✔
> Some of the radiation is reflected back into space by clouds and ice. ✔
> Greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide and water vapour, and clouds absorb some radiation and re-emit it back towards the Earth's surface. ✔
> This keeps the Earth warm enough to support life. ✔

Each sentence matches one syllabus step and uses the key verbs: passes through, absorbed, reflected, re-emitted.

## Common mistakes

- Putting the stratosphere below the troposphere, or naming only three layers.
- Placing the ozone layer in the troposphere or the upper atmosphere.
- Swapping the percentages of nitrogen and oxygen.
- Defining climate as "the weather in a place", without "over many years".
- Saying greenhouse gases "reflect" heat. They absorb and re-emit it.
- Describing the greenhouse effect as harmful; it is the enhanced effect that causes climate change.

## Exam technique

**Describe** questions on structure need names in the correct order and the position of the ozone layer. **Define** questions on climate need the syllabus phrase about many years. **Describe** questions on the greenhouse effect are usually marked step by step, so number the steps or write one sentence for each. If a diagram is provided, refer to its arrows and labels.
