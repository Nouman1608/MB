The sociocultural approach explains behaviour by looking at the social groups people belong to and the culture they live in. Instead of asking what happens in the brain or in the mind of an isolated individual, it asks how other people, shared norms and cultural meanings shape what individuals think, feel and do. In IB Diploma Programme Psychology this is one of the core approaches, and you are expected to use theories and research to explain behaviour, evaluate that research and write about it in the examination.

## Where this fits in the specification

The sociocultural approach sits in the core of the course, next to the biological and cognitive approaches. It is built on a few principles: humans are social animals who have a need to belong; the cultural and social environment affects behaviour and cognition; and people's behaviour is influenced by the groups they identify with. Content is usually organised into individual and group behaviour, cultural origins of behaviour and cognition, and cultural influences on individual attitudes, identity and behaviours. The approach is examined through short-answer and extended-response questions that ask you to describe, explain, discuss or evaluate. ## Individual and group behaviour

### Social identity theory

Social identity theory, developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner, says that part of our self-concept comes from the groups we belong to. It describes three processes. **Social categorisation** sorts people into groups, **social identification** is adopting the identity of the group, and **social comparison** compares our group (the in-group) with others (out-groups). To maintain a positive social identity we tend to favour the in-group, which can lead to **in-group bias** and discrimination against out-groups.

The **minimal group paradigm** (Tajfel and colleagues, 1971) tested this. Schoolboys were divided into groups on trivial grounds, such as a supposed preference for the paintings of one of two artists, and then gave points to others identified only by group. They tended to allocate more to members of their own group, even though the groups were meaningless. It suggests that mere categorisation is enough for bias, but the participants were all boys of one age in one country, the setting was artificial, and the task may have encouraged them to guess what the researcher expected.

### Social cognitive theory

Albert Bandura's social cognitive theory explains that people learn by observing models. Attention, retention, reproduction and motivation (shaped by reinforcement, including vicarious reinforcement) determine whether an observed behaviour is imitated. Learning is also affected by **reciprocal determinism**: the person, their behaviour and the environment influence each other, and by **self-efficacy**, belief in our ability to succeed. In the Bobo doll experiments (Bandura, Ross and Ross, 1961), children who watched an adult behave aggressively towards an inflatable doll were more likely to behave aggressively towards it than children who saw a non-aggressive model, and boys were more likely to imitate aggression than girls. Evaluation points are the artificial laboratory setting, whether hitting a doll shows real aggression, the ethical issue of encouraging aggression in children, and the use of a small sample from one nursery.

### Stereotypes

A **stereotype** is a generalised belief about a group of people. Stereotypes can form through **illusory correlation**, where two infrequent things are wrongly seen as linked, as shown by Hamilton and Gifford (1976), and through social categorisation, media and cultural learning. Stereotypes can affect behaviour: in Steele and Aronson (1995) African American students did worse on a test of verbal ability when it was described as diagnostic of ability, and the researchers explained this by **stereotype threat**, anxiety about confirming a negative stereotype. You should be able to explain how stereotypes affect those who hold them and those they target.

## Cultural origins of behaviour and cognition

**Culture** is the set of attitudes, behaviours, norms and values shared by a group and passed from one generation to the next. The process of learning the rules of your own culture is **enculturation**. Researchers describe cultures in terms of **cultural dimensions**. Hofstede's work on employees of one multinational company in many countries produced dimensions such as power distance, individualism versus collectivism, masculinity versus femininity, uncertainty avoidance and, later, long-term orientation. In **individualist** cultures people see themselves as independent and give priority to personal goals; in **collectivist** cultures people see themselves as part of a group and give priority to group goals (Markus and Kitayama, 1991, argued that this affects the self-concept). Critics say that the dimensions treat nations as uniform, rely on data from a single company, and risk reinforcing stereotypes.

Cultural norms also shape behaviour that appears universal. Cohen and Nisbett (1996) compared white male students from the northern and southern United States and found that southerners reacted more strongly to an insult, in line with their culture of honour. This shows how culture can affect emotion and aggression.

## Cultural influences on individual attitudes, identity and behaviours

**Acculturation** is the process of change that occurs when people from different cultures are in continuous contact. John Berry proposed four strategies, depending on whether a person keeps their own culture and whether they take part in the new culture: **integration** (both), **assimilation** (adopt the new, abandon the old), **separation** (keep the old, avoid the new) and **marginalisation** (neither). **Acculturative stress** describes the psychological strain of adapting. Research suggests integration tends to be linked to better wellbeing, but this depends on the attitudes of the host society, and the model may over-simplify the experience.

## Evaluating sociocultural research

When evaluating, consider these points together:

- **Generalisability and sampling**: many studies use students or one cultural group, so findings may not apply elsewhere.
- **Ecological validity**: laboratory studies may not show real-life behaviour.
- **Cultural bias**: theories developed in Western societies may be imposed on other cultures (an *etic* approach), while an *emic* approach studies a culture from within.
- **Ethics**: deception, harm to participants and consent, particularly when children or vulnerable groups are involved.
- **Causation**: many studies are correlational or quasi-experimental, so culture cannot easily be isolated as the cause.
- **Alternative explanations**: biological or cognitive factors may play a part, and a sociocultural account should not be treated as the only one.

## Worked example

Consider a fictional replication of a minimal group study. Eight participants each divide 10 points between one in-group member and one out-group member. The points given to the in-group member are 6, 7, 5, 8, 6, 7, 6 and 5.

1. Total in-group points: 6 + 7 + 5 + 8 + 6 + 7 + 6 + 5 = 50. Mean = 50 / 8 = 6.25.
2. Each out-group allocation is 10 minus the in-group share: 4, 3, 5, 2, 4, 3, 4 and 5. The total is 30 and the mean is 30 / 8 = 3.75.
3. A fair split would be 5 and 5. The in-group receives 6.25 - 5 = 1.25 points more than fair on average, and the difference between in-group and out-group means is 6.25 - 3.75 = 2.5 points.
4. Interpretation: the results fit social identity theory because participants favoured the in-group, even though the groups were arbitrary. Two participants chose an even 5 and 5 split, which shows individual differences.
5. Evaluation: with only eight participants and no statistical test the result cannot be generalised, and demand characteristics may have led participants to favour the in-group.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a study without saying how it supports or challenges the theory.
- Treating culture and nationality as the same thing, or assuming everyone in a culture behaves alike.
- Listing evaluation points (such as small sample) without explaining their effect on the conclusion.
- Confusing social identity theory with social cognitive theory.
- Mixing up the four acculturation strategies.

## Exam technique

IB command terms matter. **Describe** needs a detailed account; **explain** needs reasons or causes; **discuss** needs a balanced review of several points; **evaluate** needs strengths and limitations with a judgement. In an extended answer, state the argument, use two or three relevant studies with accurate aims, method, findings and conclusion, and link them back to the question, since a list of studies does not earn credit. Only describe studies you can recall accurately.

## Check your understanding

1. What are the three processes of social identity theory? *Social categorisation, social identification and social comparison.*
2. What is in-group bias? *Favouring members of one's own group over out-group members.*
3. Which concept did Steele and Aronson use to explain their findings? *Stereotype threat.*
4. Name Berry's four acculturation strategies. *Integration, assimilation, separation and marginalisation.*
5. Give one criticism of Hofstede's cultural dimensions. *For example: the data came from one company, and nations are treated as uniform.*
6. What is the difference between emic and etic approaches? *Emic studies a culture from within; etic applies external concepts across cultures.*
7. Eight participants give an in-group mean of 6.5 points out of 10. What is the out-group mean? *3.5 points.*

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three processes in social identity theory?**
Tajfel and Turner's social identity theory describes social categorisation, social identification and social comparison between the in-group and out-groups.

**What did the minimal group paradigm show?**
Tajfel and colleagues (1971) found that schoolboys divided into groups on trivial grounds tended to give more points to their own group, suggesting mere categorisation can produce in-group favouritism.

**What are Berry's four acculturation strategies?**
They are integration (keeping your own culture and taking part in the new one), assimilation, separation and marginalisation.

**What is the difference between emic and etic approaches?**
An emic approach studies a culture from within, while an etic approach applies external concepts across cultures, which risks imposing Western theories on other societies.
