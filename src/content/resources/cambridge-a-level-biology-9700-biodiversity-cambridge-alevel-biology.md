This guide teaches section 18.2 Biodiversity from Topic 18, Classification, biodiversity and conservation, in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It covers all six learning outcomes. This is A Level content, examined on Paper 4; the fieldwork methods and statistics are also favourite contexts for Paper 5 planning and analysis questions. The formulae for the Lincoln index, Spearman's rank correlation, Pearson's linear correlation and Simpson's index of diversity are provided in the exam, but you must know how to use them.

The [Classification, biodiversity and conservation study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-classification-biodiversity-and-conservation/) summarises each method with one example. This page adds the planning detail examiners ask for: how to randomise, which abundance measure to choose, when each correlation test is valid, and how to interpret D. ## Ecosystem and niche (18.2.1)

- An **ecosystem** is a relatively self-contained, interacting community of organisms, together with the environment in which they live and with which they interact. A rock pool, a lake and a forest are all ecosystems.
- A **niche** is the role of a species in an ecosystem: where it lives, what it feeds on, what feeds on it, when it is active, and how it interacts with other organisms and with abiotic factors.

## Three levels of biodiversity (18.2.2)

| Level | What is assessed | Example of high diversity |
|---|---|---|
| Ecosystems and habitats | Number and range of different ecosystems and habitats in an area | A region with forest, grassland, river and wetland |
| Species | **Species richness** (number of species) and **relative abundance** (how evenly individuals are spread among species) | A reef with many fish species, none dominant |
| Genetic | Variation within each species: number of different alleles of each gene | A wild cereal population with many alleles for disease resistance |

Species richness alone can mislead. Two meadows each with 10 species are not equally diverse if one is 90% a single grass species. That is why Simpson's index includes relative abundance.

## Random sampling (18.2.3)

You cannot count every organism, so you take samples. **Random sampling** gives every part of the area an equal chance of being sampled, so:

- the sample is **representative** of the whole area;
- the results are not **biased** by the person choosing "interesting" or easy spots;
- statistical tests, which assume random samples, can be used.

**Method in steps**

1. Lay two tape measures at right angles along two edges of the area to form axes.
2. Use a random number generator to produce a pair of coordinates.
3. Place the quadrat at that position; repeat for the planned number of samples.
4. Keep sampling until a **running mean** of the measurement levels off, showing that more samples would not change the result much.

Random sampling suits an area that looks uniform. Where conditions change across an area (for example from a shaded wood edge to open grass), **systematic sampling** along a transect is used instead, because the aim is to see how distribution changes along that gradient.

## Sampling methods (18.2.4)

### Frame quadrats

A square frame of known area, such as 0.25 m² or 1 m². Choose the measure to suit the organism:

| Measure | How | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Density | Count individuals, divide by quadrat area | Large, easily separated plants or slow animals |
| Frequency | Percentage of quadrats in which the species is present | Quick surveys; species hard to count |
| Percentage cover | Estimate the percentage of the quadrat area covered by the species | Grasses and mosses, where individuals cannot be separated |

### Line and belt transects

- **Line transect**: a tape across the area; record every organism touching the line at set intervals. Quick, but records presence only.
- **Belt transect**: quadrats placed along the line, either touching each other (continuous) or at regular intervals (interrupted). Gives abundance data at each point, which can be paired with abiotic readings such as light intensity, soil moisture or pH at the same point.

### Mark-release-recapture

For mobile animals. Capture a sample, count and mark them (n₁), release them, wait for them to mix back into the population, capture a second sample (n₂) and count the marked individuals in it (m₂).

**N = (n₁ × n₂) ÷ m₂**

**Worked example.** 35 woodlice are caught under logs and marked with a dot of non-toxic paint on the underside. A day later 42 are caught, of which 6 are marked.

N = (35 × 42) ÷ 6 = 1470 ÷ 6 = **245 woodlice**.

**Assumptions to state**: marks are not lost and do not harm the animal or make it more visible to predators; marked animals mix fully with the population; no births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples; every animal has an equal chance of capture in each sample.

## Correlation (18.2.5)

Both tests give a coefficient between **−1** (perfect negative correlation) and **+1** (perfect positive correlation), with 0 meaning no correlation. A correlation never proves that one variable causes the change in the other.

| Test | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Pearson's linear correlation (r)** | Continuous data from a normally distributed population; a scatter graph suggests a linear relationship; at least five pairs, ideally ten or more |
| **Spearman's rank correlation (rₛ)** | Data not normally distributed, or ordinal (or convertible to ranks); scatter graph suggests the values increase or decrease together; more than five pairs, ideally 10 to 30; individuals sampled at random |

### Worked example: Spearman's rank

Eight quadrats were placed at random in a damp field. Soil moisture and the number of rush plants were recorded.

| Quadrat | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Soil moisture / % | 12 | 18 | 25 | 31 | 36 | 44 | 52 | 60 |
| Rushes | 2 | 5 | 4 | 9 | 12 | 10 | 15 | 18 |
| Rank moisture | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| Rank rushes | 1 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 5 | 7 | 8 |
| D² | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 |

1. ΣD² = 4; n = 8.
2. rₛ = 1 − (6 × 4) ÷ (8³ − 8) = 1 − 24 ÷ 504 = 1 − 0.048 = **0.95**.
3. The critical value supplied for 8 pairs at p = 0.05 is 0.738. Since 0.95 > 0.738, reject the null hypothesis: there is a **significant positive correlation** between soil moisture and the number of rushes.

### Worked example: Pearson's, partly completed

Six pairs of readings give distance from a footpath (x, in m) and percentage of bare ground (y). You are given Σxy = 770, x̄ = 7.0, ȳ = 21.3, sₓ = 3.74 and sᵧ = 6.80.

r = (Σxy − n x̄ ȳ) ÷ ((n − 1) sₓ sᵧ) = (770 − 6 × 7.0 × 21.33) ÷ (5 × 3.74 × 6.80) = (770 − 896) ÷ 127.2 = **−0.99**.

A strong negative correlation: bare ground decreases with distance from the path, consistent with trampling (a biotic factor caused by humans), though the test itself cannot show cause.

## Simpson's index of diversity (18.2.6)

**D = 1 − Σ(n ÷ N)²**, where n is the number of individuals of each type and N is the total of all individuals.

**Worked example.** A stream sample contains six species with 15, 15, 10, 5, 3 and 2 individuals (N = 50).

| n | 15 | 15 | 10 | 5 | 3 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| n ÷ N | 0.30 | 0.30 | 0.20 | 0.10 | 0.06 | 0.04 |
| (n ÷ N)² | 0.0900 | 0.0900 | 0.0400 | 0.0100 | 0.0036 | 0.0016 |

Σ(n ÷ N)² = 0.2352, so D = 1 − 0.2352 = **0.765** (3 s.f.).

**Significance of D.** D lies between 0 and 1.

- **Close to 1**: high diversity: many species, with individuals spread fairly evenly among them.
- **Close to 0**: low diversity: few species, or one or two species dominate.

Evenness matters as well as richness. Two ponds each have two species of 50 individuals in total. Pond A has 25 and 25: D = 1 − (0.25 + 0.25) = **0.50**. Pond B has 45 and 5: D = 1 − (0.81 + 0.01) = **0.18**. Same richness, different diversity.

## Common mistakes

- Defining niche as "where an organism lives". That is its habitat; a niche is its role.
- Saying random sampling makes results "accurate". It removes bias and makes the sample representative.
- Placing a transect randomly across an obvious gradient, or random quadrats when the question asks about change along a gradient.
- Forgetting the Lincoln index assumptions, or giving N as a decimal of animals.
- Using Pearson's test on ranked or skewed data.
- Concluding that one variable **causes** the other from a correlation coefficient.
- Squaring n instead of n ÷ N in Simpson's index, or forgetting "1 −".

## Exam technique

- **Explain** random sampling: representative + avoids bias + allows statistics.
- **Describe** a method: equipment, how positions are chosen, what is recorded, how many samples, and what is kept the same.
- **Use** a formula: show substitution, then the answer with sensible significant figures.
- **State the significance** of D: link the value to number of species *and* evenness.
