## Section A – Short-answer and explanation questions

**1.** Distinguish between equality of opportunity and equality of outcome. **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by "parentocracy"? **[2]**

**3.** Describe the difference between contest mobility and sponsored mobility. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what Boudon meant by the secondary effects of social stratification. **[4]**

**5.** Explain two consequences of educational under-achievement for society. **[6]**

**6.** Read the item. The data are invented.

> In the fictional region of Ostrava Vale, researchers followed 800 pupils who all gained top grades at 16. Of 300 from professional families, 255 entered university. Of 500 from routine-manual families, 300 entered university.

**(a)** Calculate the university entry rate for each group and the gap between them in percentage points. Show your working. **[3]**
**(b)** Using Boudon's concepts, explain what the data suggest. **[3]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why expanding higher education may not increase relative social mobility. **[6]**

**8.** Read the item.

> Lena and Tomas both achieve excellent results at a fictional state school. Lena's parents are doctors; Tomas's father is a bus mechanic. Lena's parents pay for an unpaid internship in a city law firm, and arrange a family friend to advise on her university application. Tomas is offered a well-paid apprenticeship at the bus depot and accepts it.

Using the item, explain two ways in which background may shape mobility even when attainment is equal. **[6]**

## Section B – Arguments against a view and essays

**9.** "Modern education systems are meritocratic."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**10.** "Education is the main influence on a person's life chances."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that education promotes social mobility. **[26]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that differences in educational achievement between social classes reflect differences in ability and effort. **[26]**

## Answers

**1.** Equality of opportunity means everyone has a genuinely equal chance to compete and succeed [1]; equality of outcome means results or rewards are broadly equal between groups [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A system in which a child's educational success depends on the wealth and wishes of parents [1] rather than on the child's own ability and effort (Brown, 1990) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Contest mobility treats success as an open race [1] with late, reversible selection, so late developers can still succeed [1]. Sponsored mobility selects an elite early [1], and those chosen are prepared for high status by the existing elite (Turner, 1960) [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Secondary effects are class differences in educational choices [1] among pupils who have reached the same level of attainment [1]. Pupils from less advantaged backgrounds weigh the costs, risks and social distance of continuing differently [1], so they are less likely to stay on even with equal grades, which widens class gaps beyond those caused by attainment [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Consequence 1: wasted talent [1]. If able pupils leave education early, the economy loses skills it needs [1], lowering productivity, which functionalists and social democrats see as a cost to all [1].
Consequence 2: weaker social cohesion [1]. Groups who feel shut out of opportunity may lose trust in institutions [1], and their children inherit fewer resources, so inequality is reproduced across generations [1]. **[6]**

**6. (a)** Professional: 255 ÷ 300 × 100 = 85% [1]. Routine-manual: 300 ÷ 500 × 100 = 60% [1]. Gap: 85 − 60 = 25 percentage points [1].
**(b)** All pupils had equal top grades, so the gap cannot be a primary effect of attainment [1]. It reflects secondary effects: different choices at equal attainment [1], which Breen and Goldthorpe explain as relative risk aversion, since a routine-manual family can maintain its position without university [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Reason 1: degrees are partly positional goods (Hirsch, 1976) [1]. As more people gain them, employers raise entry requirements [1], so advantaged groups keep their lead and relative chances are unchanged [1].
Reason 2: advantaged groups benefit most from new places [1]. Middle-class families have the cultural capital and money to take up expanded provision first [1], so expansion can raise all groups' participation while preserving the gap, as Goldthorpe's stable relative mobility suggests [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Way 1: social and cultural capital [1]. Lena's internship and the family friend's advice give contacts and knowledge of how elite routes work [1], advantages that do not show in exam results but shape access to high-status careers [1].
Way 2: secondary effects and risk aversion [1]. For Tomas, a well-paid apprenticeship already secures a respected position in his family's class, so university's costs and risks weigh more [1]; for Lena, university is needed simply to avoid downward mobility (Breen and Goldthorpe) [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Argument 1: class background still predicts outcomes [1]. Halsey, Heath and Ridge (1980) found service-class boys far more likely to reach university than working-class boys [1]. Bourdieu explains this through cultural capital, which schools reward as if it were ability [1]. So success reflects origins, not merit alone [1].
Argument 2: parents can buy advantage [1]. Brown (1990) describes a shift to parentocracy [1], seen in private schooling, tutoring and moving into catchment areas of high-performing schools [1]. Where money shapes results, rewards do not follow ability plus effort [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Argument 1: the occupational structure matters as much as education [1]. Much absolute mobility came from the growth of professional and managerial jobs [1]; if good jobs are not created, more qualifications produce credential inflation rather than better life chances (Dore; Collins) [1]. So the economy, not education, may be the main influence [1].
Argument 2: family background shapes life chances directly [1]. Inheritance, housing and parental contacts affect income and security regardless of qualifications [1], and Boudon's secondary effects show background shapes educational choices themselves [1]. Education may therefore transmit rather than determine life chances [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **Knowledge (AO1):** definitions of absolute, relative, intergenerational and intragenerational mobility; functionalist role allocation; Goldthorpe on rising absolute and stable relative mobility; Saunders (1996); Halsey, Heath and Ridge (1980); Boudon (1974); Breen and Goldthorpe (1997); Brown (1990); Hirsch (1976); Collins (1979).
- **Application (AO2):** contest and sponsored systems; scholarship or widening-access schemes in a named country; credential inflation in a crowded graduate market.
- **Analysis and evaluation (AO3):** distinguish individual mobility stories from group patterns; separate the role of education from changes in the occupational structure; note that measured ability may itself reflect class; consider how far policy can reduce secondary effects.
- **Judgement:** for example, education is necessary for individual mobility but does little on its own to change relative chances.

**Level guidance:** top answers use the absolute–relative distinction to build a sustained argument with evidence on both sides; middle answers describe arguments without weighing them; low answers make general claims about "hard work".

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For the view:** functionalist meritocracy; Saunders's argument that ability and effort outpredict class; open examinations and anonymous marking; some working-class pupils achieve highly.
- **Against the view:** material deprivation and cultural capital (Bourdieu); Boudon's secondary effects; parentocracy and private advantage (Brown); labelling and setting within schools; class shaping measured ability itself.
- **Evaluation:** effort and ability are real, but their development is socially shaped; separate primary and secondary effects; use evidence from more than one society.
- **Judgement:** for example, ability and effort explain individual differences within classes better than differences between them.

**Level guidance:** top answers interrogate the meaning of "ability" and "effort" and reach a reasoned conclusion; middle answers list factors; low answers assert one side.
