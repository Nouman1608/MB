It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Collision theory study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-collision-theory/) and the [Collision theory revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-collision-theory-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Define activation energy. **[2]**

**2.** State the two conditions that must be met for two particles to react when they collide. **[2]**

**3.** A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen can be kept in a sealed vessel at room temperature without reacting. Explain why, in terms of collisions. **[3]**

**4.** A reaction profile shows reactants at an enthalpy of 0 kJ mol⁻¹, a peak at +85 kJ mol⁻¹ and products at −120 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** State the activation energy of the forward reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** State the enthalpy change of the forward reaction. **[1]**

**(c)** State, with a reason, whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the activation energy of the reverse reaction. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a reaction with a large negative enthalpy change may nevertheless be very slow. **[2]**

**6.** Reaction A has an activation energy of 45 kJ mol⁻¹ and reaction B has an activation energy of 110 kJ mol⁻¹. The reactions are carried out at the same temperature and concentration.

**(a)** State which reaction has the greater rate. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain your answer. **[2]**

**7.** Sketch a labelled reaction profile for an endothermic reaction. Show the reactants, products, the activation energy and the enthalpy change. **[4]**

**8.** A student says: "Most collisions do not lead to a reaction because the particles do not collide often enough." Evaluate this statement. **[3]**

**9.** In a gas the rate of collisions between molecules at room temperature is of the order of 10⁹ per second for each molecule, yet many reactions are very slow. Explain this. **[2]**

**10.** Distinguish between the frequency of collisions and the frequency of successful collisions. **[2]**

**11.** Two reactions have the same collision frequency at 298 K. Reaction X has Eₐ = 60 kJ mol⁻¹ and reaction Y has Eₐ = 80 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain which one proceeds faster. **[3]**

**12.** Suggest why a spark is needed to start the reaction between methane and oxygen but the reaction continues once started. **[3]**

**13.** A student writes the following about a reaction with a high activation energy: "Heating the reactants gives the particles more energy, so the activation energy gets lower." Identify the error and write a correct statement. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The minimum energy [1] that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The particles must collide [1]; the collision must have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Hydrogen and oxygen molecules collide constantly [1], but the activation energy of the reaction is high [1]; at room temperature almost no collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so the particles bounce apart without reacting [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** **85 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** **−120 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** Exothermic, because the products are at a lower enthalpy than the reactants [1]

**(d)** The reverse reaction goes from the products to the peak: 85 − (−120) [1] = **205 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[5]**

**5.** The activation energy may be high [1]; so few collisions have enough energy to be successful, even though the overall change releases energy [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Reaction A [1]

**(b)** A smaller activation energy means a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than it [1]; so there are more successful collisions per second in reaction A [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Axes labelled enthalpy and progress of reaction [1]; reactants at a lower level than products [1]; a peak above both, with an arrow from the reactants to the peak labelled Eₐ [1]; an upward arrow from the reactants to the products labelled ΔH (positive) [1]. **[4]**

**8.** The statement is wrong [1]. Collisions are extremely frequent [1]; most are unsuccessful because the particles have energy less than the activation energy [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Only a very small fraction of collisions involve particles with energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1]; so the rate of successful collisions is much smaller than the total collision frequency [1]. **[2]**

**10.** Collision frequency is the number of all collisions per second [1]; successful collisions are only those in which the particles have energy at least equal to the activation energy [1]. **[2]**

**11.** Reaction X is faster [1]. A larger proportion of the particles has energy of at least 60 kJ mol⁻¹ than of at least 80 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; so a higher proportion of the collisions is successful, giving more successful collisions per second [1]. **[3]**

**12.** The spark supplies energy so that some molecules have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1]; the reaction is exothermic and releases heat [1]; this heat raises the energy of neighbouring molecules so that more of their collisions are successful and the reaction spreads [1]. **[3]**

**13.** The error is that heating does not change the activation energy [1]; the correct statement is that heating gives the particles more kinetic energy, so a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the (unchanged) activation energy [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "minimum" out of the definition.
- Confusing activation energy with enthalpy change.
- Writing "greater than" without "equal to".
- Claiming that collisions are rare, rather than that few have enough energy.
- Not labelling axes and arrows on a profile diagram.
