This guide goes deeper on **7.1 Organisational structure**, the first subtopic of A Level topic 7 (Human resource management) in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content, so it is assessed on the A Level papers and builds on AS topic 2. The syllabus content is:

- **7.1.1** The relationship between business objectives and organisational structure: the purpose and attributes of a structure, such as flexibility, meeting the needs of the business, allowing for growth and development, and encouraging intrapreneurship.
- **7.1.2** Types of structure (functional, hierarchical (flat and narrow), matrix): advantages and disadvantages; why some organisations are structured by product and others by function or geographical area; reasons and ways structures change, for example growth or delayering; features of a formal structure (levels of hierarchy, chain of command, span of control, responsibility, authority, delegation, accountability, centralised, decentralised).
- **7.1.3** Delegation and accountability: their relationship; the processes of accountability; the impact of delegation.
- **7.1.4** Control, authority and trust: span of control and levels of hierarchy; authority and responsibility; conflicts between control and trust when delegating.
- **7.1.5** Centralisation and decentralisation: their impact on a business.
- **7.1.6** Line and staff: examples, distinctions and conflicts.

## Structure follows objectives

A structure is a means to an end. Match it to what the business is trying to achieve:

| Objective | Structural features that help |
|---|---|
| Cut costs | Fewer layers, wider spans, centralised purchasing |
| Innovate | Cross-functional project teams (matrix), freedom for intrapreneurs, decentralised decisions |
| Expand internationally | Divisions by geographical area, local managers with authority |
| Consistent quality and brand | Centralised standards and policies, clear chain of command |
| Fast customer response | Decentralised decisions near the customer, short chains of command |

When a case describes a change in objectives, ask whether the structure still fits. A firm that has moved from cost-cutting to innovation but keeps a tall, centralised hierarchy has a mismatch.

## Reading an organisation chart

**Worked example.** Kestrel Outdoor's chart:

- Managing director (1)
- Three directors: operations, marketing, finance (3)
- Operations director → 4 factory managers; marketing director → 2 managers; finance director → 2 managers (8 managers)
- Each factory manager → 6 supervisors; each other manager → 3 team leaders (24 + 12 = 36)
- Each supervisor → 10 operatives (240)

From this you can state:

- **Levels of hierarchy**: 5 (managing director down to operatives).
- **Spans of control**: managing director 3; operations director 4; factory manager 6; supervisor 10.
- **Chain of command** for an operative: supervisor → factory manager → operations director → managing director, so a suggestion passes through three intermediate levels before reaching the top.
- **Total staff shown**: 1 + 3 + 8 + 36 + 240 = 288.

The managing director's span of 3 is narrow; supervisors' spans of 10 are wide. Spans often widen lower down, where work is more routine.

## Choosing between structures

**By function, product or geographical area?**

- **Function** suits a firm with one main product range: specialists work together, and there is no duplication.
- **Product** suits a firm with distinct ranges that need different expertise or face different markets; each division can focus on its customers, but some functions (such as marketing) are duplicated.
- **Geographical area** suits a firm operating in regions with different languages, laws or tastes; local managers respond to local conditions.

Many large organisations combine these, for example product divisions that each have their own functional departments.

**Matrix in practice.** A software firm creates a team to build a new payroll app, drawing a programmer, a designer, a marketer and an accountant from their departments. Each reports to their department head for professional standards and to the project manager for the app. This shares scarce skills and speeds innovation, but staff may receive conflicting instructions. It works best when the two managers agree priorities in advance and staff know who decides what.

## Why and how structures change

**Growth**: a business with one founder may start with everyone reporting to the owner. As staff numbers rise, the owner's span becomes unmanageable, so departments and managers are added. Further growth into new products or countries may lead to divisions.

**Delayering**: removing a layer of management to cut costs and shorten the chain of command.

**Worked example.** Lindley Insurance removes its layer of 12 regional supervisors, each paid $46 000 a year. The 60 team leaders below them take on extra duties for an extra $4 000 a year each.

- Saving = 12 × 46 000 = $552 000.
- Extra cost = 60 × 4 000 = $240 000.
- Net annual saving = **$312 000**.

But: the managers above now each oversee far more team leaders, team leaders' workloads rise, and there are fewer promotion steps. A strong evaluation weighs the saving against the risk of overload, lower morale and weaker supervision, and asks whether staff have the training to take on extra decisions.

## Delegation that works

Delegation passes authority to a subordinate, but the manager remains **accountable** for the outcome. For delegation to succeed:

1. **Authority must match responsibility**: a supervisor responsible for meeting a deadline needs authority to approve overtime.
2. **Objectives must be clear**: the subordinate needs to know what a successful result looks like.
3. **The subordinate needs the skills**: training may come first.
4. **Monitoring must be proportionate**: enough to catch serious problems, not so much that delegation becomes meaningless.

**Accountability processes** include agreed targets, regular reports, appraisal, audits and review meetings. These let the manager remain accountable without controlling every step.

### The control–trust dilemma

The more a manager checks, the less the subordinate feels trusted; the less the manager checks, the greater the risk of costly errors. Factors that justify more trust: experienced staff, low-risk decisions, good accountability processes. Factors that justify more control: new staff, high-risk decisions (safety, large sums of money), legal requirements.

## Centralisation and decentralisation: deciding which decisions go where

Few businesses are wholly one or the other. The useful question is *which decisions* to centralise.

**Worked example: a chain of 40 pharmacies.**

| Decision | Centralise or decentralise? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Buying medicines from suppliers | Centralise | Bulk-buying discounts; consistent safety standards |
| Legal compliance and dispensing rules | Centralise | High risk; must be consistent |
| Staff rotas | Decentralise | Branch managers know local staff and busy times |
| Local promotions and community events | Decentralise | Local knowledge |
| Opening a new branch | Centralise | Large investment; strategic |

The impact of decentralisation includes faster local decisions, more motivated branch managers and senior managers freed for strategy, against a risk of inconsistency and harder control. Technology can shift the balance: real-time sales data lets head office monitor decentralised branches more easily.

## Line and staff: managing the conflict

**Line** managers (such as a production manager) have direct authority over the main operations. **Staff** specialists (such as HR, IT, legal, finance) advise and support.

Conflicts often arise when a staff department sets rules that line managers see as obstacles. For example, HR may require a lengthy recruitment process while a production manager needs workers urgently. Ways to reduce conflict: involve line managers when designing procedures, agree service standards (such as filling vacancies within a set time), and make clear who has final authority on each type of decision.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Evaluate whether a growing restaurant chain should adopt a flat organisational structure.*

> A flat structure has few levels and wide spans of control, so decisions pass through fewer people and can be made faster, which suits restaurants where managers must respond quickly to customers. *(Knowledge and application.)* It also lowers management costs and gives restaurant managers more authority, which may motivate them and encourage intrapreneurial ideas such as local menus. *(Analysis.)* However, as the chain grows, area managers with wide spans may not be able to visit each restaurant often, so quality and brand consistency could suffer. *(Counter-argument.)* A flat structure is likely to suit the chain if restaurant managers are experienced and good accountability processes, such as weekly performance reports, are in place; if not, an extra layer of area managers may be needed as it grows. *(Judgement with conditions.)*

## Common mistakes

- Saying a manager "delegates accountability".
- Treating a wide span of control as always better.
- Calculating delayering savings without the extra cost of redistributed duties.
- Recommending centralisation or decentralisation for everything, rather than for particular decisions.
- Describing line and staff without explaining how they conflict.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Explain**: show how a structural feature affects communication, cost or motivation in the case.
- **Analyse**: trace the chain from structure to decision speed, control or morale, and then to objectives.
- **Evaluate**, **Assess** or **To what extent**: weigh advantages against disadvantages for the specific business and judge, stating conditions.
- **Advise** or **Justify**: recommend a structure or a change, using evidence from the case such as size, objectives and staff skills.
