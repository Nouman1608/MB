This guide covers section 2.5 **Price determination** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus has two parts. **Market equilibrium** asks for the "definition, drawing and interpretation of demand and supply schedules and curves used to establish equilibrium price and sales in a market". **Market disequilibrium** asks you to use the same tools "to identify disequilibrium prices and shortages (demand exceeding supply) and surpluses (supply exceeding demand)".

The combined [supply and equilibrium guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-supply-and-equilibrium-and-disequilibrium-prices/) introduces both ideas. This page goes further into four things that guide only touches on: how much is actually bought and sold when the price is wrong, how the size of a shortage or surplus changes with the price, what disequilibrium looks like in a real market, and how to read all of this from a diagram. All markets and figures are invented.

## 2.5.1 Market equilibrium

**Equilibrium** is a position from which there is no tendency to change. In a market it is reached when the **quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied**. The price at which this happens is the **equilibrium price** (also called the market-clearing price), and the amount bought and sold is the **equilibrium sales** or equilibrium quantity.

Why is there no tendency to change? At this price every buyer who is willing and able to pay it can buy, and every seller who is willing and able to sell at it can sell. Nobody is left in a queue and no stock is left unsold, so no buyer needs to offer more and no seller needs to accept less.

### Worked example 1: a schedule

The weekly market for school bags in invented Town P:

| Price ($) | 10 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 18 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded | 260 | 230 | 200 | 170 | 140 | 110 |
| Quantity supplied | 50 | 90 | 130 | 170 | 210 | 250 |

Equilibrium price is $16, where both quantities are 170. Equilibrium sales are 170 bags a week, and total spending (which is also sellers' total revenue) is 16 × 170 = $2,720.

### Reading equilibrium from a diagram

Price goes on the vertical axis and quantity per week on the horizontal axis. The demand curve D slopes downwards and the supply curve S slopes upwards. Equilibrium is where they cross. Draw a dashed line from the crossing point across to the price axis (label it Pe, here $16) and down to the quantity axis (label it Qe, here 170). If the curves are drawn from a schedule, read the values from the scale rather than guessing.

### Worked example 2: two equations

Sometimes the data come as equations. For bottled water in invented Country M: Qd = 300 − 20P and Qs = 30P − 50.

1. At equilibrium Qd = Qs, so 300 − 20P = 30P − 50.
2. Collect terms: 350 = 50P, so P = 7.
3. Substitute: Qd = 300 − 140 = 160 and Qs = 210 − 50 = 160. Both match, which checks the answer.

Equilibrium is a price of $7 and sales of 160 bottles. Always substitute back into **both** equations: if they do not give the same quantity, there is an arithmetic slip.

## 2.5.2 Market disequilibrium

A **disequilibrium price** is any price at which quantity demanded and quantity supplied are not equal.

- A price **below** equilibrium gives a **shortage** (excess demand): quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied.
- A price **above** equilibrium gives a **surplus** (excess supply): quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded.

Measure each one as the **gap** between the two quantities at that price.

### Worked example 3: the size of the gap

Using the school bag market:

| Price ($) | Qd | Qs | Gap | Position | Quantity actually sold |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 230 | 90 | 140 | Shortage | 90 |
| 14 | 200 | 130 | 70 | Shortage | 130 |
| 16 | 170 | 170 | 0 | Equilibrium | 170 |
| 18 | 140 | 210 | 70 | Surplus | 140 |
| 20 | 110 | 250 | 140 | Surplus | 110 |

Two patterns stand out.

- **The further the price is from equilibrium, the bigger the gap.** Each $2 away from $16 adds 70 to the gap, because demand changes by 30 and supply by 40 for every $2, and the two effects add together.
- **Sales are lower at any disequilibrium price than at equilibrium.** When there is a shortage, buyers can only buy what sellers offer, so sales equal quantity supplied. When there is a surplus, sellers can only sell what buyers want, so sales equal quantity demanded. In both cases the **smaller** of the two quantities is what is traded. Equilibrium is the price at which the most bags change hands.

This also changes revenue. At $20 sellers take 20 × 110 = $2,200 and are left with 140 unsold bags; at $12 they take 12 × 90 = $1,080 and turn away 140 buyers. Both are below the $2,720 earned at equilibrium.

### What disequilibrium looks like

| Shortage | Surplus |
|---|---|
| Queues and waiting lists | Unsold stock building up in warehouses |
| Goods sold out quickly; empty shelves | Perishable goods (fruit, fish, flowers) thrown away |
| Sellers rationing, for example "two per customer" | Special offers and discounts to clear stock |
| Buyers offering more than the asking price | Sellers cutting output for the next period |

On a diagram, draw a horizontal line at the disequilibrium price. The shortage or surplus is the horizontal distance between the two curves along that line. At a price below Pe the line meets S first, then D: that distance is the shortage. At a price above Pe it meets D first, then S: that distance is the surplus.

### How the market moves back to equilibrium

**From a surplus.** Unsold stock pushes sellers to cut prices. As the price falls, quantity demanded extends (a movement down D) and quantity supplied contracts (a movement down S). The gap narrows until it closes at $16.

**From a shortage.** Buyers compete for scarce goods and sellers see they can charge more. As the price rises, quantity supplied extends and quantity demanded contracts until they match.

In both cases **neither curve shifts**. Only the price changes, and the quantities move along the curves. This is the key difference between 2.5 and 2.6: in 2.6 a curve shifts and creates a new equilibrium.

How quickly this happens depends on the market. Prices at a fruit market change within hours; prices printed in a catalogue or fixed by contract may stay wrong for months, so shortages or surpluses last longer. Where a government fixes a price by law, a shortage or surplus can last indefinitely; maximum and minimum prices belong to section 2.11, the mixed economic system.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a price above equilibrium a shortage. High price, surplus; low price, shortage.
- Giving the shortage as a single quantity (for example "90") instead of the gap (230 − 90 = 140).
- Saying sales at a disequilibrium price equal quantity demanded in every case. Sales equal the smaller quantity.
- Shifting a curve to explain the return to equilibrium. The adjustment is movement along both curves.
- Forgetting to label Pe and Qe, or reading equilibrium off the wrong column.
- Writing "demand rises" when a falling price causes quantity demanded to extend.

## Exam technique for 2.5

- **Define** equilibrium in market terms: "the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied". Add that there is no tendency for price to change.
- **Identify** a disequilibrium from data: state the price, both quantities, the gap and its name.
- **Calculate**: set out the subtraction or the equation steps so that method can be credited.
- **Explain** a return to equilibrium as a chain: surplus, stock builds up, sellers cut prices, demand extends and supply contracts, gap closes.
- **Analyse** with a diagram: label axes, D, S, Pe and Qe, and mark the disequilibrium price with its two quantities. Refer to the labels in your writing.
- **Discuss** questions in this area often ask whether a market will reach equilibrium. Consider how quickly prices can change, whether goods are perishable and whether the price is fixed by law or contract.
