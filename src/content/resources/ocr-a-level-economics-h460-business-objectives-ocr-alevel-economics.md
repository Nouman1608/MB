This guide covers **Business objectives**, the third content section of Component 01, Microeconomics, in the OCR *A Level Economics (H460)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026), for first assessment in 2021. The section has three topics: 3.1 Business objectives (maximising and non-maximising objectives, and the principal-agent problem), 3.2 Costs and economies of scale, and 3.3 Revenue and profit. Exam questions often combine them: you are given a cost and revenue table and asked which output a firm would choose under different objectives. This guide works through one such table in full. The firm and every figure are invented.

For condensed recall use the [business objectives revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-business-objectives-ocr-alevel-economics-revision-notes/), and for exam-style work the [business objectives practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-h460-business-objectives-ocr-alevel-economics-practice/). ## The firm: Kestrel Kitchens

Kestrel Kitchens makes chilled ready meals. Output (Q) is in thousand meals a week, and money values are in £ thousand. To sell more, it must cut its price: P = 20 − Q. Fixed costs are £24 thousand a week.

| Q | Price (AR) | TR | MR | VC | MC | TC | AC | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 19 | 19 | 19 | 6 | 6 | 30 | 30.00 | −11 |
| 2 | 18 | 36 | 17 | 10 | 4 | 34 | 17.00 | 2 |
| 3 | 17 | 51 | 15 | 15 | 5 | 39 | 13.00 | 12 |
| 4 | 16 | 64 | 13 | 22 | 7 | 46 | 11.50 | 18 |
| 5 | 15 | 75 | 11 | 31 | 9 | 55 | 11.00 | 20 |
| 6 | 14 | 84 | 9 | 42 | 11 | 66 | 11.00 | 18 |
| 7 | 13 | 91 | 7 | 56 | 14 | 80 | 11.43 | 11 |
| 8 | 12 | 96 | 5 | 74 | 18 | 98 | 12.25 | −2 |
| 9 | 11 | 99 | 3 | 95 | 21 | 119 | 13.22 | −20 |
| 10 | 10 | 100 | 1 | 119 | 24 | 143 | 14.30 | −43 |

MR and MC are the changes in TR and TC from the previous row.

## Reading the cost side (3.2)

- **Fixed costs** (24) do not change with output in the short run: rent, insurance, the kitchen's equipment. **Variable costs** rise with output: ingredients, packaging, hourly labour.
- **Marginal cost** falls from 6 to 4 and then rises steadily. Once more workers share a fixed kitchen, each extra worker adds less output: the **law of diminishing returns**, which raises MC from the third thousand meals onwards.
- **Average cost** falls while MC is below it and rises once MC is above it, so MC cuts AC at its minimum (11.00, between outputs 5 and 6).
- This is the **short run**, because one factor, the kitchen, is fixed. In the **long run** Kestrel could build a second kitchen; whether its average cost then falls depends on economies and diseconomies of scale (below).

## Reading the revenue side (3.3)

- **Average revenue** equals price. **Marginal revenue** is below price because, to sell one more unit, the firm cuts the price on all units.
- MR falls by 2 for every extra thousand meals, and TR is highest where MR approaches zero, at Q = 10.
- **Profit** = TR − TC. A negative figure is a loss.

## Choosing output under each objective (3.1)

| Objective | Rule | Kestrel's output | Price | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Profit maximisation | MR = MC (produce while MR ≥ MC) | 5 | £15 | 20 |
| Sales revenue maximisation | MR = 0 | 10 | £10 | −43 |
| Revenue maximisation with a profit floor of 15 | Highest TR with profit ≥ 15 | 6 | £14 | 18 |
| Sales volume maximisation | Largest output with profit ≥ 0 | 7 | £13 | 11 |
| Profit satisficing (target 15) | Any output meeting the target | 4, 5 or 6 | £16 to £14 | 18, 20, 18 |

**Why 5 maximises profit.** Moving from 4 to 5, MR (11) exceeds MC (9), so profit rises by 2. Moving from 5 to 6, MR (9) is below MC (11), so profit falls by 2. Profit therefore peaks at 5.

**Why unconstrained revenue maximisation fails.** At Q = 10, TR is the highest in the table, but the loss of £43 thousand a week could not be sustained. In practice a revenue-maximising firm must satisfy a profit constraint, which is why the constrained row matters.

**Growth maximisation** has no single output rule. A firm aiming to grow may accept lower profit now, for example choosing output 6 or 7 to win market share, as long as it keeps enough profit to fund expansion. **Utility maximisation** by managers has no fixed rule either: output reflects what gives them pay, status or an easier working life.

## Accounting, normal and supernormal profit

The table uses **accounting costs**: payments the firm actually makes. Economists add **normal profit**, the minimum return needed to keep the owner's resources in the business, which is an opportunity cost.

Suppose Kestrel's owner could earn £12 thousand a week running a rival firm.

- At Q = 5, accounting profit is 20. Normal profit is 12. **Supernormal profit** = 20 − 12 = **8**.
- If accounting profit fell below 12, the business would be making less than normal profit in the long run, and the owner would have reason to leave the industry.
- Sales volume maximisation "subject to at least normal profit" now means accounting profit of at least 12. Output 7 (profit 11) no longer qualifies, so the answer becomes **6**.

Always check whether a question's cost figures already include normal profit.

## The principal-agent problem in the table

Kestrel is bought by a larger group whose shareholders (principals) appoint managers (agents). If managers' bonuses depend on sales revenue, they gain from moving output towards 10 even though shareholders lose profit. Even with a profit floor of 15, the managers' preferred output (6) earns 2 less profit than the shareholders' preferred output (5). Asymmetric information makes this hard to detect: shareholders cannot easily tell whether lower profit reflects market conditions or managerial choice. Linking pay to profit or long-run share value narrows the gap.

## Non-maximising objectives

- **Social welfare.** If Kestrel were a social enterprise supplying meals to schools, it might set the lowest price that covers costs, choosing output 7 (price £13) or even accepting losses funded by grants.
- **Corporate social responsibility.** Suppose sourcing free-range ingredients adds £1 thousand to variable cost per thousand meals at every output. MC rises by 1 at every output, so MR = MC still gives Q = 5 (MR 11 versus MC 10 for the fifth thousand, and MR 9 versus MC 12 for the sixth), but profit falls by 5 to **15**. Whether this pays depends on whether customers are willing to pay more for ethical sourcing, which would shift AR and MR upwards.

## Economies of scale and objectives

If Kestrel builds a second kitchen, **internal economies of scale** such as bulk buying of ingredients, specialised machinery and cheaper finance may lower long-run average cost. Growth until the **minimum efficient scale** (the lowest output at which long-run average cost is minimised) can therefore support profit. Beyond some size, **diseconomies of scale**, such as poor communication and weaker motivation across sites, raise average cost. A growth-maximising firm risks expanding into diseconomies. **External economies**, such as a local supply of trained chefs, lower costs for all firms in an area.

## Factors influencing the choice of objective

- **Ownership and control:** owner-managers are closer to profit maximisation; large public companies with dispersed shareholders leave room for revenue, growth and utility goals.
- **Competition:** intense competition forces firms towards profit maximisation to survive.
- **Stage of development:** a new firm may prioritise survival or growth; a mature firm, profit and dividends.
- **Legal form and mission:** charities and social enterprises pursue welfare.
- **Stakeholder pressure:** customers, staff and investors can push firms towards CSR.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the output with the highest price or highest revenue as profit maximising.
- Reading MR and MC from the wrong row: they describe the move *into* that row.
- Forgetting that sales volume maximisation needs profit of at least zero, or normal profit if stated.
- Treating normal profit as zero accounting profit when the question gives an opportunity cost.
- Assuming CSR always lowers long-run profit.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show TR − TC row by row and state the units (£ thousand, thousand meals).
- **Explain:** give the rule (MR = MC, MR = 0, profit ≥ 0) before the answer.
- **Evaluate:** compare objectives using the data, then judge which the firm is most likely to pursue, naming the deciding factor, such as who controls the firm.
