These notes cover **section 3.2.3.2 Monetary policy** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: "what is meant by monetary policy", how it can be used to achieve "the government objective of controlling inflation" and how it can be used "to achieve other government economic objectives". They set out the separate channels through which interest rates work, which the shorter [fiscal and monetary policy revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-fiscal-and-monetary-policy-revision-notes/) list in one sequence. Full explanations are in the [monetary policy study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-monetary-policy/); test yourself with the [monetary policy practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-monetary-policy-practice/). Figures are invented unless a real UK fact is stated.

## Condensed recall

- **Monetary policy:** using interest rates (and the money supply) to influence spending in the economy.
- **Who:** the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee sets the Bank Rate.
- **Target:** set by the government, 2% CPI inflation. The Bank chooses how to meet it.
- A higher Bank Rate → higher rates on loans, mortgages and savings → less spending → lower total demand → less upward pressure on prices.
- A lower Bank Rate → cheaper borrowing → more spending → more output and jobs, with a risk of inflation.
- The full effect takes many months, often more than a year, so the Bank acts on **forecasts**.
- Interest rates work best against **demand-pull** inflation.

## The five channels of a rate rise

| Channel | What happens | Effect on demand |
|---|---|---|
| Borrowing | Loans, credit and variable-rate mortgages cost more | Consumer spending falls |
| Saving | Higher reward for saving | More income saved, less spent |
| Investment | Projects funded by loans become less profitable | Business investment falls |
| Confidence and house prices | Dearer mortgages cool the housing market; people feel less wealthy | Spending falls |
| Exchange rate | Higher returns attract money from abroad, so the pound strengthens | Imports cheaper; exports dearer; import prices fall |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bank of England | The UK's central bank, responsible for monetary policy |
| Monetary Policy Committee | The Bank's committee that votes on the Bank Rate |
| Bank Rate | The Bank of England's main interest rate, which influences the rates banks charge and pay |
| Variable-rate mortgage | A home loan whose interest rate moves with market rates |
| Fixed-rate mortgage | A home loan whose rate is fixed for a set period |
| Time lag | The delay between a policy change and its full effect |
| Quantitative easing | Creating money to buy bonds, used by the Bank of England from 2009 when interest rates were already very low |

## Winners and losers

| From a rate **rise** | From a rate **cut** |
|---|---|
| Gain: savers, people living on interest from savings, importers | Gain: borrowers, home buyers, firms planning investment, exporters if the pound falls |
| Lose: variable-rate borrowers, firms with big loans, exporters (if the pound rises) | Lose: savers, people living on interest from savings |

## Other objectives and conflicts

- **Growth and employment:** cut rates → more borrowing and spending → firms produce more and hire. Conflict: inflation may rise; imports may rise.
- **Balance of payments:** raise rates → less spending on imports. Conflict: the pound may strengthen, making exports dearer, and growth slows.
- **Controlling inflation** with higher rates conflicts with growth and full employment.

## Worked calculations

**1. Mortgage interest.** An interest-only mortgage of £240,000. At 2.5%: 0.025 × 240,000 = **£6,000** a year. At 4.5%: **£10,800**. Extra cost **£4,800** a year.

**2. Savings interest.** £15,000 saved. At 1.5%: **£225** a year. At 4%: **£600** a year.

**3. Real interest rate.** Savings pay 4% while inflation is 6%. Real rate ≈ 4 − 6 = **−2%**: the savings lose purchasing power.

**4. An investment decision.** A firm borrows £80,000 for a machine that will earn £4,000 a year, a 5% return. At 5% interest the loan costs **£4,000**, so the project only breaks even; at 8% it costs **£6,400**, so the firm loses £2,400 a year and does not invest.

## Limits

- Time lags of many months.
- Low confidence: people may not borrow even at low rates.
- One rate for every region.
- Banks may not pass on the full change.
- Many mortgages are fixed for several years, which slows the effect.
- Little direct effect on cost-push inflation from world prices.

## Exam traps

- The Bank of England, not the government, sets the Bank Rate.
- Interest rates are monetary, not fiscal, policy.
- Finish the chain: total demand, then prices (inflation) or output and jobs (growth and employment).
- A rate rise does not hurt everyone: savers gain.
- On trade questions, mention the effect on the exchange rate.

## Self-test

1. What is the Bank Rate?
2. Why does the government set the inflation target but leave interest-rate decisions to the Bank of England?
3. Distinguish between a fixed-rate and a variable-rate mortgage.
4. Give two channels through which a rise in interest rates reduces spending.
5. Why does a rise in UK interest rates tend to strengthen the pound?
6. Why might a rate rise take a long time to affect households with fixed-rate mortgages?
7. A £120,000 interest-only mortgage moves from 3% to 5%. Find the rise in yearly interest.
8. Savings pay 3% and inflation is 1%. What is the approximate real interest rate?
9. Name two groups who gain from a cut in interest rates.
10. Explain one conflict between controlling inflation and achieving full employment.
11. Why are interest rates less effective against cost-push inflation?
12. Why might a cut in interest rates fail to raise spending in a recession?

### Answers

1. The Bank of England's main interest rate, which influences the rates banks charge on loans and pay on savings.
2. So that decisions are less likely to be shaped by short-term political pressures, such as cutting rates before an election.
3. A fixed rate stays the same for a set period; a variable rate moves with market interest rates.
4. Any two: borrowing costs more; saving is more rewarding; investment becomes less profitable; house prices and confidence fall; a stronger pound.
5. Higher returns attract savers from abroad, who buy pounds to deposit in the UK, raising demand for the currency.
6. Their rate stays the same until the fixed deal ends.
7. 0.05 × 120,000 − 0.03 × 120,000 = 6,000 − 3,600 = £2,400.
8. About 3 − 1 = 2%.
9. Any two: borrowers, home buyers, firms planning investment, exporters if the pound falls.
10. Raising rates to cut inflation reduces spending, so firms cut output and jobs, raising unemployment.
11. The cause is higher costs, such as world energy prices, which interest rates cannot change; they work only by reducing demand, which costs output and jobs.
12. If confidence is low, households and firms may not want to borrow or spend, whatever the interest rate.
