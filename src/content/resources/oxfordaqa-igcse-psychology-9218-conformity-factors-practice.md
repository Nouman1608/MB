These questions cover the conformity factors in **3.2.2 Social influence** from Paper 2 of OxfordAQA
International GCSE Psychology (9218): group size, anonymity, task difficulty, personality and
expertise, with Asch's research.

---

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following would be expected to **reduce** conformity?
A Increasing the majority from one person to three
B Making the task more difficult
C Allowing participants to give their answers privately
D Making all members of the majority agree **[1]**

**2.** Define *informational social influence*. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why Asch included trials on which the confederates gave the correct answer, and a
condition in which participants judged the lines alone. **[2]**

**4.** Identify the social factor that Asch tested by making the comparison lines closer in length, and
state the effect on conformity. **[2]**

**5.** Outline how personality can affect conformity. **[2]**

**6.** In an invented replication of Asch's study, 15 participants each completed 12 critical trials.
There were 54 conforming answers in total. Calculate the percentage of critical trials on which
participants conformed. Show your working. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Explain how group size affects conformity. Refer to Asch's research in your answer. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why giving answers anonymously reduced conformity in Asch's research. **[3]**

**9.** Zara is a keen birdwatcher. On a school trip, her classmates all say that a distant bird is a
crow, but Zara is sure it is a rook and says so. Later, in a maths lesson on a topic she finds very
hard, she copies the answer most of the class gives. Use your knowledge of dispositional and social
factors to explain Zara's behaviour in **both** situations. **[6]**

**10.** A company wants staff to give honest opinions about a new policy. At present, opinions are given
aloud in meetings of twelve people. Suggest **two** changes the company could make, and explain how
each would reduce conformity. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **one** strength and **one** limitation of Asch's line-judgement task as a way of
studying conformity. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss the view that conformity is caused more by social factors than by dispositional
factors. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1].

**2.** Conforming because you believe the group's judgement is correct, usually when you are uncertain
[1].

**3.** To provide a baseline or control for comparison [1]: participants made almost no errors when
alone (and on neutral trials), so errors on critical trials could be attributed to group pressure rather
than difficulty seeing the lines [1].

**4.** Task difficulty [1]; conformity increased [1].

**5.** Some people are consistently more likely to conform because of stable traits [1], for example
those with low self-esteem or a high need for social approval are more sensitive to group pressure
[1].

**6.** 15 × 12 = 180 critical trials [1]; 54 ÷ 180 × 100 = 30% [1].

**7.** One mark per point, up to 4:
- Conformity increases as the size of the majority increases, up to a point [1].
- In Asch's variations, conformity was about 3% with one confederate, about 13% with two and about 32%
  with three [1].
- Beyond three, there was little further increase [1].
- A larger unanimous majority creates more normative pressure, because disagreeing with more people is
  more uncomfortable [1].
Maximum 4.

**8.** In Asch's task the correct answer was obvious, so conformity was mainly normative: people agreed
publicly to avoid standing out [1]. When answers were written privately, the group could not see the
participant disagree [1], so the pressure to fit in was removed and conformity fell [1].

**9.** Award up to 6:
- Birdwatching: Zara has **expertise** (a dispositional factor) in identifying birds [1].
- She is confident in her own judgement, so informational influence from the group is weak [1], and
  she feels able to disagree, so she resists normative pressure [1].
- Maths: the topic is **difficult** for her (task difficulty, a social factor) [1].
- She is uncertain, so she looks to the majority for the right answer: informational social influence
  [1].
- She also lacks expertise in this topic, so she is more likely to assume the majority knows better,
  and the large class majority adds pressure (group size) [1].
Maximum 6.

**10.** Two changes, each [1] for the change and [1] for the explanation:
- Collect opinions anonymously, for example through a private online survey [1], removing the
  audience and so the normative pressure to agree publicly [1].
- Use smaller discussion groups [1], so that each person faces a smaller majority, which creates less
  pressure to conform [1].
- Ask one person to argue against the policy or invite dissent first [1], breaking the majority's
  unanimity, which reduces conformity [1].
Maximum 4.

**11.** Strength, up to 2: the task was unambiguous with a clear correct answer [1], so any conformity
showed the power of group pressure rather than genuine uncertainty, and variables could be controlled
[1]. Limitation, up to 2: the task was trivial and had no real consequences [1], so it may not
reflect conformity on important decisions in everyday life (low ecological validity) [1].

**12.** Award up to 6, for a balanced discussion:
- Social factors: Asch's variations show conformity changes greatly with group size, anonymity and task
  difficulty while participants stay similar [1] [1].
- This suggests the situation has a strong causal effect on conformity [1].
- Dispositional factors: about a quarter of Asch's participants never conformed, despite facing the
  same pressure, which shows individual differences [1].
- Personality traits such as low self-esteem, and expertise in the task, help explain these
  differences [1].
- Conclusion: both matter; social factors set the level of pressure, and dispositional factors affect
  how each person responds [1].
Maximum 6.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying anonymity increases conformity.
- Explaining factors without naming normative or informational influence.
- In scenario questions, explaining only one of two situations when the question asks for both.
- Forgetting to multiply participants by trials before calculating a percentage.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know the exact percentages from Asch's variations?**
Accurate figures add precision, but the pattern (a sharp rise up to three, then a levelling off) is the
essential point.

**Is unanimity in the specification?**
It is not named as a separate factor, but it explains why group size has its effect and is useful in
evaluation.
