## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain the purpose of Ansoff's matrix. **[2]**

**2.** Tolmere Bikes sells bicycles to households. Identify the Ansoff quadrant for each strategy: **(a)** selling its bicycles to delivery companies; **(b)** a range of cycling clothing for its existing customers; **(c)** a spring price promotion; **(d)** opening a chain of cafés for office workers. **[4]**

**3.** Distinguish between related and unrelated diversification. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why market penetration may be risky for a business that already has a large share of a saturated market. **[3]**

**5.** A strategy costs £180,000 and is forecast to add £48,000 to profit each year. Calculate the payback period. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Ollerton Pottery makes hand-painted tableware sold through UK department stores. It plans to sell the same range in the USA through a distributor. Evaluate the level of risk of this strategy for Ollerton Pottery and its stakeholders. **[9]**

**7.** Sunnyhill Juices sells bottled fruit juice to UK supermarkets. Its objective is to raise annual profit by £70,000 within three years. It has £250,000 to invest.

| Option | Cost | Forecast extra annual profit |
|---|---|---|
| A: more advertising and promotions in existing supermarkets | £90,000 | £30,000 |
| B: a smoothie range for the same supermarkets | £150,000 | £45,000 |
| C: export the existing juices to Ireland | £200,000 | £70,000 |
| D: open juice bars in city centres | £350,000 | £80,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the payback period for each option. **[3]**

**(b)** Recommend and justify a strategy for Sunnyhill Juices, using Ansoff's matrix and the data. **[9]**

**8.** Calverley Tea sells loose-leaf tea through its website. It plans to launch a range of tea-making machines for its existing customers. Analyse **two** ways in which Calverley Tea could reduce the risk of this product development. **[6]**

**9.** Using the information in Question 7, assess the usefulness of Ansoff's matrix to Sunnyhill Juices compared with the financial data. **[8]**

**10.** Drayford Engineering makes gearboxes for farm machinery. It is considering a move into servicing wind turbines for energy companies, using its engineers' gearbox skills. Analyse the risks of this strategy for **two** of Drayford's stakeholder groups. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** It sets out the growth options open to a business by whether its products and markets are existing or new [1], and shows how risk rises the further a business moves from what it knows [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **(a)** Market development [1]. **(b)** Product development [1]. **(c)** Market penetration [1]. **(d)** Diversification [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Both involve new products in new markets [1]. Related diversification shares skills, technology or customers with the existing business, such as a brewery buying pubs [1]; unrelated diversification has no link, so it is usually riskier because no knowledge transfers [1]. **[3]**

**4.** In a saturated market most potential customers already buy, so growth must come from rivals' customers [1]. With a large share already, rivals may respond with price cuts or heavy promotion [1], so the business may gain little extra share while its margins fall [1]. **[3]**

**5.** 180,000 ÷ 48,000 [1] = **3.75 years** (3 years 9 months) [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Classification: market development, because the product is unchanged but the customers are new [1].
Sources of risk: Ollerton does not know US shoppers' tastes, prices or rivals [1]; it depends on a distributor it does not control, so poor service would harm its brand [1]; costs of shipping and exchange-rate changes may cut margins [1].
Reducing risk: using a distributor that already knows the market lowers the knowledge gap [1], and Ollerton keeps its familiar product and production methods [1].
Stakeholders: employees may gain more work and security if exports succeed, but face pressure if large orders stretch capacity [1]; owners risk the cost of samples, marketing and stock if US sales are weak [1].
Judgement: the risk is moderate rather than high, because the product is proven in the UK and a distributor reduces the market knowledge gap; it would rise sharply if Ollerton had to fund large stocks in the USA before orders were confirmed [1]. **[9]**

**7. (a)** A: 90,000 ÷ 30,000 = **3.0 years**; B: 150,000 ÷ 45,000 = **3.33 years** [1]. C: 200,000 ÷ 70,000 = **2.86 years** [1]. D: 350,000 ÷ 80,000 = **4.38 years** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Classification: A is penetration, B product development, C market development, D diversification [1].
D can be rejected: it costs £350,000, more than the £250,000 available, and needs retail skills Sunnyhill lacks [1].
C alone meets the £70,000 target, costs £200,000 and has the fastest payback [1]; but it puts most of the money into one market Sunnyhill does not know, with exporting risks such as distribution and exchange rates [1].
A and B together cost £240,000 and add £75,000, meeting the target [1]; both use the supermarkets Sunnyhill already supplies, so the market knowledge gap is small [1]; they spread the risk across two strategies [1]. However, supermarkets may not give shelf space to a new smoothie range, and the payback on B is the longer at 3.33 years [1].
Recommendation: A and B together, because they meet the target with lower risk by building on existing customer relationships; if supermarket buyers will not stock smoothies, C becomes the better option [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Test the product with existing customers before launch [1], for example by sending prototypes to a sample of regular buyers [1], so faults or a lack of demand are found before large stocks are bought [1].
Buy the machines from an established manufacturer under Calverley's brand rather than designing them [1]; this reduces the technical risk, because Calverley lacks engineering skills [1], although it lowers the margin and gives less control over quality [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Useful: the matrix classifies the four options and shows that D, diversification, carries the most risk because both product and market are new [1]. It highlights that C involves an unfamiliar market, prompting Sunnyhill to research Ireland before investing [1]. It reminds managers that A and B rely on customers they already know [1].
Limitations: it cannot show which option meets the £70,000 target or fits the £250,000 budget; only the financial data shows that D is unaffordable and that A and B together meet the target [1]. It gives no payback figures, so it cannot show that C recovers its cost fastest [1]. Its risk ranking is general: exporting the same product to a nearby English-speaking market may be less risky than its "new market" label suggests [1].
Judgement: the matrix is useful for identifying the type of risk in each option [1], but the financial data is more useful for the final choice, so the two should be used together [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Employees: engineers' gearbox skills give a head start, but wind-turbine work may require climbing, travel and new safety training [1], so some staff may be unwilling or unable to do it [1]; if the venture takes investment and management time away from the core gearbox business, jobs there could also be at risk [1].
Lenders: if Drayford borrows to buy vehicles and equipment, the bank's repayment depends on winning contracts in a market where Drayford has no record [1]; energy companies may require experience Drayford lacks, so revenue could be slow to build [1]; this is related diversification, though, so the risk is lower than for an unrelated move, because existing skills transfer [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a new market by place rather than customer group.
- Calling any small product change "product development".
- Recommending a quadrant without using the costs and paybacks given.
- Describing risk in general terms without saying who bears it.
- Assessing usefulness without comparing the matrix with other information.
