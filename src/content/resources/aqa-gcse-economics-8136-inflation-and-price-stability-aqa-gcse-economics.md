Section 3.2.2.4 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification covers five content points: "What is inflation?", "Measures of inflation", "Causes of inflation", "Consequences of inflation" and "Government policies to manage inflation". The additional guidance names the Consumer Price Index (CPI), "simple calculations using CPI figures", "cost-push and demand-pull inflation" and "the consequences of inflation to different groups within the economy". This guide explains how a price index is built from the ground up, follows each cause through as a chain of reasoning, and shows how to judge who gains and who loses.

The section sits in 3.2 How the economy works, assessed on Paper 2, with no tiers. Figures are invented unless a real UK fact is stated. ## What inflation is, and what it is not

Inflation is a sustained rise in the **general** level of prices. The rate of inflation is the percentage increase in that price level over a period, normally twelve months. Three distinctions earn marks:

- A rise in the price of one product, such as coffee, is not inflation. Inflation is about the average across everything households buy.
- A **lower rate** of inflation does not mean lower prices. If inflation falls from 5% to 2%, prices are still rising, only more slowly.
- **Price stability** does not mean zero inflation. The UK government has set the Bank of England a target of 2% CPI inflation. If inflation is more than one percentage point above or below the target, the Governor of the Bank must write an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why and what the Bank will do.

## How a price index is built

The ONS constructs the CPI in four steps. Understanding them makes the calculations easy.

1. **Survey spending.** Data on household spending show what a typical household buys.
2. **Choose a basket.** A representative basket of goods and services is selected, and its prices are collected regularly from shops and online.
3. **Weight each item.** Items take up different shares of spending, so each gets a weight. A price change in something households spend a lot on moves the index more.
4. **Compare with a base year.** The index is set to 100 in the base year, and every later value is a percentage of that year's prices.

### Worked example: a three-category index (invented)

| Category | Weight | Price index this year (base = 100) |
|---|---|---|
| Food | 0.5 | 104 |
| Transport | 0.3 | 110 |
| Clothing | 0.2 | 95 |

Weighted index = (0.5 × 104) + (0.3 × 110) + (0.2 × 95) = 52 + 33 + 19 = **104.0**.

Prices are 4.0% higher than in the base year, even though clothing became cheaper. The fall in clothing prices counts for less because clothing has the smallest weight. This is why one price falling does not stop inflation.

## Calculations with CPI figures

**Rate of inflation.** CPI rises from 120.0 to 126.0. Rate = (126.0 − 120.0) ÷ 120.0 × 100 = **5.0%**.

**Working back to an earlier price.** An item costs £63 after a year of 5% inflation. Last year's price = 63 ÷ 1.05 = **£60**. Dividing by 1.05 is correct; taking 5% off £63 (which gives £59.85) is a common slip.

**Real wages.** A worker's weekly pay rises from £500 to £520, a 4% rise, while the CPI rises 5%. In last year's prices the new wage is worth 520 ÷ 1.05 = **£495.24**, so real pay has fallen by about 1%. A pay rise below inflation is a cut in what the worker can buy.

## Causes of inflation as chains

**Demand-pull inflation** happens when total spending in the economy grows faster than the economy can increase output.

Lower interest rates or tax cuts → households and firms spend more → firms cannot raise output fast enough, especially near full capacity → shortages appear and firms raise prices → the general price level rises.

**Cost-push inflation** happens when firms' costs of production rise and they pass the increase on to customers.

World energy prices rise → transport, heating and manufacturing costs rise across many industries → firms raise prices to protect their profit margins → the general price level rises.

Other sources of cost-push inflation include a fall in the exchange rate (which makes imported materials dearer), higher indirect taxes and wage rises that are not matched by higher productivity.

**The wage-price spiral.** The two causes can feed each other. Prices rise, so workers ask for higher pay to protect their real wages; higher pay raises firms' costs, so they raise prices again. This is why the Bank of England pays close attention to pay growth.

## Consequences for different groups

| Group | When inflation is high or unexpected |
|---|---|
| Savers | Lose if the interest rate is below inflation: savings earning 3% when inflation is 5% buy less at the end of the year |
| Borrowers | Gain, because the real value of the money they owe falls; this is reversed if interest rates rise to fight inflation |
| Workers | Lose if pay rises lag behind prices; strong unions may protect real pay better than weak ones |
| People on fixed incomes | Lose purchasing power unless pensions or benefits rise with prices |
| Firms | Face rising costs and uncertainty; must reprint price lists and change menus and websites; find planning investment harder |
| Exporters | Lose competitiveness if UK prices rise faster than those of rivals abroad |
| The government | Receives more tax as prices and incomes rise, but pays more on benefits and pensions that rise with prices |

The skill AQA rewards is to **name the group** and explain the mechanism. "Inflation is bad for everyone" earns little.

## Government policies to manage inflation

| Policy | How it reduces inflation | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Monetary policy: higher Bank Rate | Borrowing costs more and saving pays more, so spending falls | Slower growth, higher unemployment; hurts borrowers |
| Fiscal policy: higher taxes or lower spending | Less disposable income and less government demand reduce total spending | Unpopular; may slow growth; cuts may harm public services |
| Supply-side policies: training, competition, investment | Higher productivity lets output keep up with demand and holds down costs | Slow to take effect; can be costly |

Match the policy to the cause. Demand-side policies work best against demand-pull inflation. Against cost-push inflation caused by world energy prices, a higher interest rate cannot lower the cost of oil; it can only reduce demand, which risks a recession.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain how an increase in VAT could cause inflation. **[3]**

*Model answer:* VAT is an indirect tax added to the price of most goods and services [identifies the cost]. An increase raises the cost to firms of selling their products, and firms pass most of it on in higher prices [develops the chain]. Because VAT applies across many products at once, the general price level rises, which is cost-push inflation [links to the general price level and names the type].

## Common mistakes

- Treating a fall in the inflation rate as a fall in prices.
- Calling a single product's price rise "inflation".
- Working out a percentage change from the later CPI figure instead of the earlier one.
- Saying savers always lose. They lose only when the interest rate is below inflation.
- Recommending an interest-rate rise for every type of inflation without noting its limits against cost-push inflation.

## Exam technique

- **State** questions need one precise point, for example "a sustained rise in the general price level".
- **Calculate** questions: write the formula, show the substitution and give the unit (per cent or pounds).
- **Explain** questions: build a chain with "so" and "because", and end at the general price level or at the named group.
- **Evaluate** questions: identify the cause of the inflation in the case first, weigh at least two policies against it, and give a judgement that depends on that cause.
