Every Depth Study question on Paper 1 follows the same three-part structure: a
6-mark part (a) that asks what impression an extract gives, using an attributed
historian's interpretation rather than a primary source; an 8-mark part (b) that
asks you to explain two effects of something; and a 16-mark part (c) essay that
asks how far you agree with a stated view, with bulleted prompts to help shape
your answer and a requirement to bring in information of your own. The questions
below practise all three parts across several topics.

## Question 1 — the Weimar Republic's early problems, 1919–23

**Extract 1**: From a history of interwar Germany, published in 1998.

> "The new republic was condemned before it had a chance to prove itself:
> forced to accept a peace it had not made, propped up by a constitution that
> invited instability, and battered within its first years by risings from
> left and right alike."

**1(a)** What impression does the author give about the Weimar Republic's early
problems? You must use Extract 1 to explain your answer. **[6]**

**1(b)** Explain **two** effects of the hyperinflation of 1923 on Germany. **[8]**

**1(c)** "The Weimar Republic was doomed from the moment it was created in
1919." How far do you agree with this statement? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- the Treaty of Versailles and the "stab in the back" myth
- the Weimar constitution

and any other information of your own.

## Question 2 — the growth of Nazi support, 1929–33

**Extract 2**: From a history of the Weimar Republic's collapse, published in
2004.

> "It was not that the Nazis suddenly became more persuasive after 1929; it
> was that mass unemployment made millions of Germans willing, for the first
> time, to listen to what they were saying."

**2(a)** What impression does the author give about why Nazi support grew
after 1929? You must use Extract 2 to explain your answer. **[6]**

**2(b)** Explain **two** effects of the Depression on the Nazi Party's
electoral support between 1929 and 1932. **[8]**

**2(c)** "The Depression alone explains the growth of Nazi support between
1929 and 1933." How far do you agree with this statement? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- Nazi propaganda and the role of the SA
- fear of communism among businessmen and the middle class

and any other information of your own.

## Question 3 — Hitler's consolidation of power, 1933–34

**Extract 3**: From a biography of Hitler, published in 1991.

> "Within eighteen months of taking office, Hitler had turned a chancellorship
> hedged about with constitutional limits into a personal dictatorship that
> answered to no one."

**3(a)** What impression does the author give about how completely Hitler's
power changed between 1933 and 1934? You must use Extract 3 to explain your
answer. **[6]**

**3(b)** Explain **two** effects of the Enabling Act of March 1933 on
Germany. **[8]**

**3(c)** "The Night of the Long Knives was the most important step in
Hitler's consolidation of power." How far do you agree with this statement?
**[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- the Reichstag Fire and the Decree for the Protection of People and State
- the death of Hindenburg and the army's oath of loyalty

and any other information of your own.

## Question 4 — the USA in the 1920s

**Extract 4**: From a history of the United States between the wars,
published in 2010.

> "For the first time, ordinary American families could own the products of
> mass production on credit, and for much of the decade it seemed the
> prosperity would never end."

**4(a)** What impression does the author give about prosperity in the USA in
the 1920s? You must use Extract 4 to explain your answer. **[6]**

**4(b)** Explain **two** effects of mass production and hire purchase on the
American economy in the 1920s. **[8]**

**4(c)** "Most Americans shared in the prosperity of the 1920s." How far do
you agree with this statement? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- farmers and workers in declining industries such as coal and textiles
- discrimination faced by black Americans and recent immigrants

and any other information of your own.

## Answers

**1(a)** The author gives the impression that the Republic's problems were
**inherited rather than self-inflicted**: it did not choose the peace treaty
it had to accept, and its founding constitution already carried a weakness
("invited instability") before any government had even acted [3]. The
phrase "battered... from left and right alike" also gives the impression
that the threat was not from one direction but came at the Republic from
opposing extremes simultaneously [3].

**1(b)** Hyperinflation **destroyed savings**: by late 1923 money saved by
the middle class was worthless, wiping out a lifetime of thrift for many
families [4]. It also **discredited the Republic itself**, since the
currency collapsed on its watch and many blamed the government's decision to
print money to fund passive resistance in the Ruhr, permanently alienating a
middle class that might otherwise have supported democracy [4].

**1(c)** There is a strong case the Republic was fatally weakened from the
start: it was blamed for the Treaty of Versailles and the "stab in the back"
myth denied it legitimacy before it had governed at all, while proportional
representation produced unstable coalitions and Article 48 allowed rule by
decree, both built into the constitution itself [6]. Against this, the
Republic did survive the Spartacist rising, the Kapp Putsch and hyperinflation,
and enjoyed genuine stability and growth between 1924 and 1929 under
Stresemann, suggesting its problems were serious but not inevitably fatal
[6]. A strong answer weighs the inherited weaknesses against this recovery
before reaching a supported judgement, rather than treating 1919's problems
as proof of an inevitable collapse fourteen years later [4].

**2(a)** The author gives the impression that Nazi support grew not because
the Nazis' message changed but because **circumstances made people more
receptive to a message that already existed** — unemployment made people
"willing... to listen" rather than making the Nazis newly persuasive [3].
This places the explanation for Nazi growth on economic conditions rather
than on any change in Nazi tactics or ideology [3].

**2(b)** The Depression **swelled the pool of angry, desperate voters** the
Nazis could appeal to: unemployment rose above six million, and Chancellor
Brüning's deflationary policies made conditions worse while discrediting
democratic government itself [4]. It also **pushed fearful businessmen and
the middle class towards the Nazis**, since they were seen as the strongest
available force against communism, bringing the party crucial funding [4].

**2(c)** The Depression was clearly central: without mass unemployment,
the Nazis had won only 2.6% of the vote in 1928, and their support only
became a mass movement once economic collapse gave their message an
audience [6]. However, the Depression alone cannot fully explain the scale
of Nazi success — their modern propaganda machine under Goebbels, Hitler's
personal campaigning, and the SA's visible organisation on the streets all
converted economic anger into votes more effectively than any rival party
managed [6]. A strong answer concludes that the Depression created the
opportunity, but Nazi organisation and tactics were what let them exploit it
more successfully than other parties facing the same crisis [4].

**3(a)** The author gives the impression that the change was **both rapid
and total**: "within eighteen months" stresses the speed, while "answered to
no one" stresses that no constitutional limit survived the process at all
[3]. This gives the impression of a complete transformation rather than a
gradual or partial shift in how Germany was governed [3].

**3(b)** The Enabling Act **removed the Reichstag as a check on Hitler**,
giving him the power to make laws without its approval for four years, which
was the legal basis for everything that followed [4]. It also **led directly
to the destruction of other parties and organisations**: trade unions were
banned and rival parties dissolved within months, since nothing in the
Reichstag could now stop this happening [4].

**3(c)** There is a strong case for the Night of the Long Knives: murdering
Röhm and the SA leadership removed the one internal threat still capable of
challenging Hitler, and secured the loyalty of the army, which had feared SA
ambitions and whose support Hitler needed above all [6]. However, the
Reichstag Fire Decree and the Enabling Act arguably mattered more, since
they supplied the **legal machinery** — suspending civil liberties and
enabling rule without the Reichstag — without which Hitler could not have
acted against the SA, or anyone else, without resistance in the first place
[6]. A strong answer judges which step removed the last *effective* check on
Hitler's power, rather than simply ranking the events by how dramatic they
were [4].

**4(a)** The author gives the impression that 1920s prosperity was both
**widely shared** ("ordinary American families") and, for most of the
decade, **expected to continue indefinitely** ("seemed... would never end")
[3]. This gives an impression of broad-based confidence rather than
prosperity confined to a small wealthy minority [3].

**4(b)** Mass production **cut the cost of consumer goods dramatically**,
above all through Ford's assembly line, bringing cars and appliances within
reach of ordinary families for the first time [4]. Hire purchase **let
demand run ahead of income**, since consumers could buy goods on credit
before they could actually afford them, fuelling a boom that depended on
continued borrowing [4].

**4(c)** Prosperity was real for many: rising factory wages, new consumer
goods, and the sense of a modern, confident economy touched a broad swathe
of urban and suburban America [6]. However, it was far from universal:
farmers suffered from overproduction and collapsing prices throughout the
decade, older industries such as coal and textiles declined, and black
Americans and recent immigrants faced discrimination, low wages and were
first to lose work when conditions tightened [6]. A strong answer concludes
that prosperity was genuine but unevenly spread, rather than either
accepting or dismissing the statement wholesale [4].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering the (a) part as if it asks what the extract's author is *right or
  wrong* about, rather than what impression the extract's own words give.
- Giving only one effect in the (b) part, or naming an effect without
  explaining how it followed from the cause named in the question.
- Treating the Depression, or any single factor, as the only cause of Nazi
  growth, rather than weighing it against organisation and propaganda.
- Writing the (c) essay as a one-sided argument rather than reaching a
  balanced, supported judgement that uses the bulleted prompts and brings in
  further information of your own.
- Describing events in the (c) essay without linking them back to the
  statement being judged.
