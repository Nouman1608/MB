## Section A

**1.** State **three** things you should consider when assessing the usefulness of a historical source. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between the **reliability** and the **usefulness** of a source. **[3]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain **three** problems faced by the Weimar Republic between 1919 and 1923. **[9]**

**4.** Explain why the Nazi Party's support grew between 1929 and 1933. **[9]**

**5.** Explain how Hitler consolidated power between January 1933 and August 1934, referring to **three** events. **[9]**

**6.** Explain **three** reasons for the economic boom in the USA in the 1920s, and **two** reasons why not everyone shared in it. **[10]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three: the **origin** — who produced it, when and in what circumstances [1]; the **purpose** — why it was produced and for whom [1]; the **content** — what it says and whether that is corroborated by other evidence and by your own knowledge [1]; its **limitations** — what it does not tell you [1].

**2. Reliability** concerns whether a source gives an **accurate and unbiased account** [1]. **Usefulness** concerns **what a historian can learn from it, for a specified enquiry** [1]. A source can be **highly unreliable yet very useful** — a piece of Nazi propaganda is not an accurate account of events but is excellent evidence of what the regime wanted people to believe [1].

**3.** Any three, 3 marks each: **The Treaty of Versailles** — the Republic was **blamed for signing it**, and the "November criminals" and "stab in the back" myth undermined its legitimacy from the start [1] [1] [1]. **Political extremism** — the **Spartacist rising of 1919** on the left and the **Kapp Putsch of 1920** and Munich Putsch of 1923 on the right [1]; the government was forced to rely on the Freikorps to survive, which meant depending on men hostile to the Republic [1] [1]. **Hyperinflation of 1923** — the French occupation of the Ruhr and the government's decision to support passive resistance by printing money **destroyed the currency** [1]; savings were wiped out, and the **middle class in particular was ruined and permanently alienated** [1] [1]. **The constitution itself** — proportional representation produced **unstable coalitions**, and Article 48 allowed rule by decree [1] [1] [1].

**4.** The **Wall Street Crash and the Depression** were decisive: American loans were withdrawn, unemployment rose above six million, and the **Weimar coalition governments failed to agree on a response** [1] [1]. **Chancellor Brüning's deflationary policy** made conditions worse and he ruled by decree, discrediting democracy itself [1]. The Nazis offered **simple, confident solutions and scapegoats** — the Treaty, the November criminals, the Jews and the communists [1] [1]. **Fear of communism** drove businessmen and the middle class towards the Nazis as the strongest anti-communist force, and brought funding [1] [1]. Nazi **propaganda and organisation** under Goebbels, with Hitler's air campaigning and mass rallies, was far more modern than any rival's [1]. The **SA** provided visible strength and street presence, appealing to the unemployed young [1].

**5.** **The Reichstag Fire (February 1933)** — blamed on the communists, it allowed Hitler to secure the **Decree for the Protection of People and State**, suspending civil liberties and permitting the arrest of communist deputies [1] [1] [1].
**The Enabling Act (March 1933)** — passed with the communists excluded and the SA intimidating deputies, it gave Hitler the power to **make laws without the Reichstag for four years**, the legal basis of the dictatorship [1] [1]. Trade unions were then banned and other parties dissolved by July [1].
**The Night of the Long Knives (June 1934)** — Röhm and the SA leadership were murdered [1]; this **removed the internal threat from the SA and secured the loyalty of the army**, which had feared SA ambitions [1]. On Hindenburg's death in August, Hitler **merged the offices of Chancellor and President and had the army swear a personal oath to him** [1].

**6.** **Reasons for the boom**, 2 marks each: **mass production** techniques, above all Ford's assembly line, cut costs so dramatically that consumer goods came within reach of ordinary families [1] [1]. **Hire purchase and easy credit** let consumers buy cars and appliances before they could afford them, so demand ran ahead of income [1] [1]. **Republican policies** of low taxation, laissez-faire and **tariffs such as Fordney-McCumber** protected American industry from foreign competition [1] [1]. (Also accept: new industries and advertising; the confidence generated by the war.)
**Reasons why some did not share in it**, 2 marks each: **farmers** suffered from **overproduction and collapsing prices**, worsened by foreign tariff retaliation, so rural incomes fell throughout the decade [1] [1]. **Older industries such as coal and textiles declined**, and **black Americans and recent immigrants faced discrimination**, low wages and were the first to lose work [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Judging a source unreliable and therefore concluding it is not useful.
- Describing Weimar's problems without explaining why they threatened the Republic.
- Treating the Depression as the only cause of Nazi growth.
- Listing 1920s boom factors without explaining the mechanism.
