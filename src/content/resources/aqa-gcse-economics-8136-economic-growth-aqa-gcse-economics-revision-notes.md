These notes condense **section 3.2.2.2 Economic growth** from the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification, first taught from September 2017 (Paper 2, no tiers). Full explanations and worked examples are in the [economic growth study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-economic-growth-aqa-gcse-economics/); test yourself with the [economic growth practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-economic-growth-aqa-gcse-economics-practice/). All countries and figures are invented.

## Core recall

- **Economic growth**: an increase in the output of goods and services over time, measured by the percentage change in real GDP.
- Growth is one of the four principal government objectives, with full employment, price stability and a balanced balance of payments.
- A fall in real GDP for two quarters in a row is commonly called a recession.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| GDP | Total value of goods and services produced in a country in a period, at that period's prices |
| Nominal GDP | GDP measured at current prices, not adjusted for inflation |
| Real GDP | GDP adjusted for inflation, showing changes in quantity of output |
| GDP per capita | GDP divided by population: average output per person |
| Productive capacity | The maximum output an economy can produce with its resources |
| Productivity | Output per worker or per hour worked |
| Investment | Spending by firms on capital such as machinery and buildings |
| Recession | A fall in real GDP, commonly for two quarters in a row |

## Formulas

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Growth rate | (new − old) ÷ old × 100 |
| Real GDP | nominal GDP ÷ (1 + inflation as a decimal) |
| Quick real growth | nominal growth − inflation (an estimate) |
| GDP per capita | GDP ÷ population |

**Mini example.** Country Dene: nominal GDP 500 billion → 530 billion; inflation 2%; population 25 million.
- Nominal growth = 30 ÷ 500 × 100 = 6%.
- Real GDP = 530 ÷ 1.02 = 519.6 billion; real growth = 3.9%.
- Nominal GDP per capita in year 2 = 530 billion ÷ 25 million = 21,200.

## Why growth matters

- Higher living standards on average.
- More jobs, lower unemployment.
- More tax revenue without higher tax rates; less spent on benefits.
- Confidence for firms to invest.

## Causes

**Demand-side (using spare capacity):** more consumer spending, investment, government spending or exports.

**Supply-side (raising capacity):** more workers; better-skilled workers through education and training; more and better capital; new technology.

Demand-side growth stops when spare capacity runs out, and then mainly raises prices. Lasting growth needs capacity to rise.

## Benefits and costs

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Higher incomes and living standards | Inflation if demand outruns capacity |
| More jobs | Pollution, emissions, use of finite resources |
| More tax revenue for services | Gains may be uneven, widening inequality |
| Profits for firms to reinvest | Congestion and housing pressure |

## Policies

- **Monetary:** lower interest rates → cheaper borrowing → more spending and investment. Risk: inflation.
- **Fiscal:** more government spending or lower taxes → more demand. Risk: more government borrowing.
- **Supply-side:** education, training, infrastructure, incentives to invest → more capacity. Risk: slow and costly.

## GDP per capita: limits as a measure of living standards

- An average, so it hides inequality.
- Ignores unpaid work.
- Ignores quality of life: pollution, hours worked, health.
- Misses the hidden economy.

## Evaluation frame for 9-mark growth questions

1. **Point for**: a benefit of growth or of the policy, with a chain of reasoning.
2. **Point against**: a cost, a delay or a group that loses out.
3. **Depends on**: the type of growth (spare capacity or new capacity), how fast it is, and how the gains are shared.
4. **Judgement**: a clear answer to the question, with the condition that would change it.

Example judgement: "Growth from higher productivity benefits most people because it raises incomes without causing inflation, but if it brings more pollution, the cost falls on people who may not share the gains."

## Exam traps

- Using nominal GDP to judge real growth.
- Mixing billions and millions when dividing by population.
- Thinking a larger GDP means higher living standards.
- Forgetting that real GDP per capita falls if population grows faster than real GDP.
- One-sided answers on the benefits of growth.
- 9-mark answers with no judgement.

## Self-test

1. Define economic growth.
2. Distinguish GDP from real GDP.
3. Why is GDP per capita a better guide to living standards than GDP?
4. Country E has GDP of 360 billion and a population of 12 million. Find GDP per capita.
5. Country F's nominal GDP rises from 600 billion to 640 billion with 5% inflation. Find real growth to one decimal place.
6. Real GDP grows 3% and population grows 1%. Does real GDP per capita rise or fall?
7. Give two supply-side causes of growth.
8. Give one demand-side cause of growth.
9. Why might growth cause inflation?
10. State one environmental cost of growth.
11. How do lower interest rates encourage growth?
12. Give one reason GDP per capita may overstate the living standards of a typical person.

### Answers

1. An increase in the output of goods and services produced by an economy over time, measured by the change in real GDP.
2. GDP is measured at current prices; real GDP is adjusted for inflation.
3. It divides output by population, showing average output per person rather than the size of the economy.
4. 360 billion ÷ 12 million = 30,000.
5. Real GDP = 640 ÷ 1.05 = 609.5 billion; real growth = 9.5 ÷ 600 × 100 = 1.6%.
6. It rises, by about 2%.
7. Any two: more workers, better training, more capital investment, new technology.
8. Any one: more consumer spending, more investment, more government spending, more exports.
9. If spending rises faster than the economy's capacity, firms raise prices rather than output.
10. Any one: pollution, carbon emissions, using up finite resources.
11. Borrowing is cheaper, so consumers spend more and firms invest more, raising output.
12. A few very high incomes can raise the average, while most people earn less (it hides inequality).
