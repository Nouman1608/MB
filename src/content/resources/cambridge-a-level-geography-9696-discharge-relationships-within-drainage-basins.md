This guide covers **1.2 Discharge relationships within drainage basins** in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus asks for the **components of hydrographs (storm and annual)** and the **influences on hydrographs**, which it splits into two groups:

- **Climate:** precipitation type and intensity, temperature, evaporation, transpiration, evapotranspiration and antecedent moisture.
- **Drainage basin characteristics:** size and shape, drainage density, porosity and permeability of soils, rock type, slopes, vegetation type and land use.

Hydrographs are one of the most common resources in Paper 1 Section A, so this subtopic rewards accurate reading of graphs as much as knowledge. Use the [1.2 discharge relationships revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-discharge-relationships-revision-notes/) for recall, and test yourself with the [1.2 discharge relationships practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-discharge-relationships-within-drainage-basins-practice/).

## The storm hydrograph, component by component

A storm hydrograph shows how discharge at one point changes after a single rainfall event, usually with rainfall plotted as a bar chart on the same time axis.

| Component | What it shows | What controls it |
|---|---|---|
| Baseflow | Discharge before the storm, fed by groundwater | Groundwater store, rock type, season |
| Rising limb | The increase in discharge as storm water arrives | Speed of overland flow and throughflow |
| Peak discharge | The maximum discharge | Total rain, intensity, basin size and shape |
| Lag time | Time from peak rainfall to peak discharge | Distance and speed of flow routes |
| Falling (recession) limb | The decrease after the peak | Drainage of soil and groundwater stores |
| Stormflow (quickflow) | Discharge above baseflow during the event | Overland flow and fast throughflow |

On many hydrographs a sloping line is drawn from the start of the rising limb to a point on the falling limb, separating **baseflow** below from **stormflow** above. Baseflow often rises slightly during and after a storm because some rain percolates and recharges groundwater. If you are asked to describe a hydrograph, comment on each component in turn and quote values with units.

## The annual hydrograph

An **annual hydrograph** plots discharge across a whole year, either as daily values or as monthly means (a regime). Its components are different from a storm hydrograph:

- the **high-flow season**, when discharge is consistently above average;
- the **low-flow season**, when the river depends mainly on baseflow;
- **individual flood peaks**, visible as spikes on a daily record but hidden in monthly means;
- the **timing** of the highest and lowest flows, which links to the climate.

A daily annual hydrograph for a river in a temperate maritime climate shows many short spikes in the wetter months superimposed on high baseflow, and lower, smoother flow in the drier season. A monthly regime for the same river shows only the seasonal pattern. Always check which kind of graph you are given before describing it.

## Climatic influences explained

- **Precipitation type:** rain reaches the ground and can flow at once. **Snow** is stored on the surface and released only when it melts, so a snowfall may produce no hydrograph response until days or weeks later. Rapid melt, or rain falling on a snowpack, can then produce a very high peak.
- **Precipitation intensity:** when intensity exceeds the infiltration capacity, overland flow is generated, giving a steep rising limb and high peak. The same total falling gently over a longer period infiltrates more.
- **Temperature:** high temperatures increase evaporation and transpiration, so less water is left to reach the channel; summer storms often produce smaller peaks than winter storms of similar size. Temperatures below freezing can turn the ground surface into an impermeable layer, so rain or meltwater runs straight off.
- **Evaporation, transpiration and evapotranspiration:** these outputs remove water before it becomes discharge. Their effect on a single storm is small; on an annual hydrograph they are a major reason for low summer flows.
- **Antecedent moisture:** the amount of water already in the soil before the event. A wet basin has little spare storage, so saturation-excess overland flow is widespread.

## Basin characteristics explained

- **Size:** a larger basin collects more water, so peak discharge is usually higher, but water travels further, so lag time is longer.
- **Shape:** in a compact basin, water from all parts reaches the outlet at about the same time, giving a high, sharp peak; an elongated basin spreads arrival over time.
- **Drainage density:** total channel length divided by basin area. A high density means water has only a short distance to travel over or through the ground before reaching a channel, so the response is fast.
- **Porosity and permeability:** these are different properties. **Porosity** is the proportion of a soil or rock made up of pore spaces; **permeability** is the ability to let water pass through. Clay has a high porosity but a low permeability, because its pores are tiny and poorly connected, so it holds water but transmits it very slowly and encourages overland flow. Sandy soils combine moderate porosity with high permeability.
- **Rock type:** permeable rocks such as chalk and many sandstones absorb water and release it as baseflow. Impermeable rocks such as granite or shale (except along joints) force more water to flow over or near the surface.
- **Slopes:** steep slopes increase the speed of overland flow and throughflow and reduce the time for infiltration.
- **Vegetation type:** forest intercepts and transpires more than grassland; evergreen cover has an effect all year, deciduous cover mainly in the growing season.
- **Land use:** urban surfaces, compacted farmland and drained fields speed up the response; woodland and wetlands slow it.

## Worked example 1: reading a storm hydrograph

*A fictional gauging station recorded peak rainfall at 06:00. Discharge was 4 cumecs at 06:00 and rose to a peak of 34 cumecs at 14:00. Calculate the lag time and the mean rate of rise of the rising limb.*

Lag time = 14:00 - 06:00 = **8 hours**.
Rise in discharge = 34 - 4 = 30 cumecs.
Mean rate of rise = 30 / 8 = **3.75 cumecs per hour**.

A second station on a neighbouring basin rose from 4 to 22 cumecs between 06:00 and 18:00: lag time 12 hours and a rate of rise of 18 / 12 = 1.5 cumecs per hour. The first basin is much flashier, so you would look for steeper slopes, impermeable rock, wetter antecedent conditions or more urban land in its description.

## Worked example 2: drainage density

*Basin A has a total stream length of 84 km and an area of 60 km². Basin B has 45 km of streams in 50 km². Calculate and compare their drainage densities.*

Basin A: 84 / 60 = **1.4 km per km²**.
Basin B: 45 / 50 = **0.9 km per km²**.

Basin A has more channel per unit area, which usually indicates impermeable rock or soil (water cannot soak away, so it cuts more channels). Expect basin A to have the shorter lag time and higher peak for similar rainfall.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain how porosity and permeability influence the shape of a storm hydrograph." [6]*

> Porosity is the share of pore space in a soil or rock, while permeability is how easily water passes through it **(defines both)**. On a highly permeable rock such as chalk, rain infiltrates and percolates into groundwater **(process)**, so it reaches the channel slowly as baseflow, giving a long lag time and a low, broad peak **(effect on hydrograph)**. A clay soil may be porous but has low permeability, because its pores are very small and poorly connected **(distinguishes the terms)**. Once its surface pores fill, further rain cannot enter, so overland flow develops **(process)** and the hydrograph has a steep rising limb, short lag and high peak **(effect)**.

The answer scores because it uses both terms correctly, avoids treating them as the same thing, and links each property to a named feature of the graph.

## Common mistakes

- Treating porosity and permeability as one property.
- Describing an annual hydrograph with storm hydrograph vocabulary, such as "lag time".
- Saying a larger basin always has a "flashier" hydrograph: its peak may be higher, but its lag time is longer.
- Forgetting that temperature can work in two ways: high temperatures increase evapotranspiration, while frozen ground increases runoff.
- Quoting a lag time from the start of rainfall rather than from **peak** rainfall.
- Listing factors without saying whether each increases or decreases peak discharge and lag time.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a hydrograph: quote baseflow, peak, the times of peak rainfall and peak discharge, and the lag time, with units.
- **Compare** two hydrographs: write comparative sentences ("basin X peaks 4 hours earlier and 12 cumecs higher than basin Y"); two separate descriptions do not make a comparison.
- **Explain** influences: link factor, process and effect on the graph in one chain.
- **Suggest** reasons for an unfamiliar hydrograph: use the information given in the resource (rock, land use, rainfall) before adding your own ideas.
- In a Section B extended answer, group influences into climatic and basin characteristics, as the syllabus does, and judge which matter most in the case you are discussing.
