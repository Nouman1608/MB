## 8.11 and 8.12 Ionisation energy and reactivity

**Use these first ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹):** Li 520, Na 496, K 419; Mg 738, Ca 590, Sr 550,
Ba 503.

Going down either group:

1. Each element has one more occupied shell, so the atomic radius increases.
2. Inner shells shield the outer electron from the nucleus.
3. Nuclear charge rises, but the larger distance and extra shielding outweigh it.
4. The outer electron is held less tightly, so removing it takes less energy.

Group 1 metals react by losing one electron (M → M⁺ + e⁻) and Group 2 metals two (M → M²⁺ + 2e⁻).
These are removed more easily down the group, so reactivity increases from Li to K and Mg to Ba. In redox terms the metals are reducing agents, and their oxidation
number rises from 0 to +1 or +2. For Group 2 the first and second ionisation energies both fall down
the group.

## 8.13 Reactions with oxygen, chlorine and water

| Reaction | Group 1 (Li to K) | Group 2 (Mg to Ba) |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | 4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O (oxide). Sodium in plenty of oxygen gives mainly the peroxide, 2Na + O₂ → Na₂O₂. Potassium gives the superoxide, K + O₂ → KO₂ | 2M + O₂ → 2MO. Magnesium burns with a brilliant white flame |
| Chlorine | 2M + Cl₂ → 2MCl, white solids | M + Cl₂ → MCl₂, white solids |
| Water | 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂. Li fizzes steadily; Na melts into a ball and darts about; K also ignites the hydrogen with a lilac flame | M + 2H₂O → M(OH)₂ + H₂. Mg reacts very slowly with cold water but glows with steam: Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂. Ca fizzes steadily, leaving a cloudy white suspension; Sr and Ba react faster |

Oxygen has oxidation number −2 in an oxide, −1 in a peroxide and −½ in a superoxide.

## 8.14 Oxides and hydroxides

The oxides are basic. With water they give hydroxides, which is why the solutions are alkaline:

- Li₂O + H₂O → 2LiOH and CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (the second is strongly exothermic).
- Magnesium oxide is only slightly soluble, so the solution is only weakly alkaline.

With dilute acid, oxides and hydroxides both give a salt and water:

- Na₂O + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O; BaO + 2HNO₃ → Ba(NO₃)₂ + H₂O
- KOH + HNO₃ → KNO₃ + H₂O; Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
- Ionic forms: O²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O and OH⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O

With dilute sulfuric acid, the calcium, strontium and barium compounds can stop reacting early,
because a coating of poorly soluble sulfate forms on the solid.

## 8.15 Solubility of Group 2 hydroxides and sulfates

| Compound | Trend down the group (Mg → Ba) |
|---|---|
| Hydroxides | Solubility **increases**: Mg(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble, Ba(OH)₂ dissolves most |
| Sulfates | Solubility **decreases**: MgSO₄ is soluble, BaSO₄ is insoluble |

The trends run in opposite directions. That is why barium ions are used to test for sulfate (8.19),
and why aqueous sodium hydroxide gives a white precipitate with Mg²⁺(aq) but little or none with
dilute Ba²⁺(aq).

## 8.16 Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates

| Salt | Group 1 (Na, K) | Lithium | Group 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbonate | No decomposition at Bunsen temperatures | Li₂CO₃ → Li₂O + CO₂ | MCO₃ → MO + CO₂ |
| Nitrate | 2MNO₃ → 2MNO₂ + O₂ | 4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂ | 2M(NO₃)₂ → 2MO + 4NO₂ + O₂ |

**The explanation in four links:**

1. The carbonate and nitrate ions are large, with a spread-out electron cloud.
2. A cation that is small and highly charged (high charge density) polarises that cloud, pulling electron
   density towards itself.
3. This weakens the bonds inside the anion, so less heat is needed to break it into CO₂, or NO₂ and O₂.
4. Down a group the charge stays the same but the cation gets larger, so it polarises less and the
   salt is **more** stable.

Group 1 salts are more stable than Group 2 salts because M⁺ carries a smaller charge and is larger
than the M²⁺ ion in the same period. Li⁺ is very small, so lithium salts decompose the way Group 2
salts do.

**Worked example 1.** 4.23 g of strontium nitrate is heated until it has fully decomposed. Calculate
the total volume of gas at room temperature and pressure, and the mass of solid left.

```
Mr Sr(NO₃)₂ = 87.6 + 2(14.0) + 6(16.0) = 211.6
n = 4.23 / 211.6 = 0.0200 mol
2Sr(NO₃)₂ → 2SrO + 4NO₂ + O₂
n(NO₂) = 0.0400 mol; n(O₂) = 0.0100 mol; total gas = 0.0500 mol
volume = 0.0500 × 24.0 = 1.20 dm³
mass SrO = 0.0200 × 103.6 = 2.07 g
```

## 8.17 Flame colours

Heat from the flame promotes electrons to higher energy levels. When they fall back to lower
levels they release the energy as light. Each energy gap has a fixed size, so each element emits
particular frequencies and gives its own colour.

| Ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| Li⁺ | red (crimson) |
| Na⁺ | yellow-orange |
| K⁺ | lilac |
| Ca²⁺ | brick red (orange-red) |
| Sr²⁺ | crimson red |
| Ba²⁺ | pale (apple) green |

Magnesium compounds give no flame colour, because the energy released lies outside the visible range.

## 8.18 Practical procedures

**Flame test.** Clean a nichrome wire by dipping it in concentrated hydrochloric acid and heating it
until the flame shows no colour. Dip it in the acid again, then into the solid, and hold it in the
hottest part of a non-luminous Bunsen flame.

**Thermal decomposition patterns.** Heat equal amounts (in moles) of each salt in identical boiling
tubes over the same flame.

- Carbonates: pass the gas into a fixed volume of limewater and time how long it takes to turn
  cloudy. Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky.
- Nitrates: time how long it takes for brown gas to appear, or test for oxygen with a glowing splint,
  which relights. Nitrogen dioxide is brown and acidic; it turns damp blue litmus red. Use a fume
  cupboard, since NO₂ is toxic.
- Take the delivery tube out of the limewater before you stop heating, so liquid is not sucked back.

## 8.19 Tests for ions

| Ion | Test | Positive result and equation |
|---|---|---|
| CO₃²⁻ / HCO₃⁻ | Add a dilute acid and pass any gas into limewater | Effervescence; the limewater goes cloudy (milky). CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O; HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O; Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O |
| SO₄²⁻ | Acidify with dilute HCl, then add BaCl₂(aq) | A white precipitate forms. Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) |
| NH₄⁺ | Warm with sodium hydroxide solution | Gas turns damp red litmus blue and gives white smoke with HCl fumes. NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O; NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl |

The acid in the sulfate test removes carbonate ions, which would otherwise give a white barium
carbonate precipitate.

## 8.20 and 8.21 Titrations and Core Practical 3

- c (mol dm⁻³) = n ÷ V, with V in dm³. Mass concentration (g dm⁻³) = c × M.
- Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in alkali, orange at the end point.
- Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in alkali.

**Core Practical 3 outline.** Pipette 25.0 cm³ of the hydrochloric acid into a conical flask with two
or three drops of indicator. Fill a burette with sodium hydroxide of known concentration. Run in alkali,
swirling, then add it dropwise near the end point. Repeat until you have concordant titres (typically
within 0.10 cm³) and average only those.

**Worked example 2.** 25.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid needs 0.150 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Titres: rough 21.20;
then 20.80, 20.55, 20.65 cm³.

```
Concordant: 20.55 and 20.65 → mean = 20.60 cm³ (20.80 is 0.15 from 20.65)
n(NaOH) = 0.150 × 20.60/1000 = 3.09 × 10⁻³ mol = n(HCl)
c(HCl) = 3.09 × 10⁻³ / 0.0250 = 0.124 mol dm⁻³
mass concentration = 0.1236 × 36.5 = 4.51 g dm⁻³
```

## 8.22 and 8.23 Uncertainty and Core Practical 4

**Making a standard solution.** Weigh the solid acid by difference. Dissolve it in a small volume
of distilled water in a beaker. Transfer it to a volumetric flask, rinsing the beaker and glass rod
into the flask. Fill to the mark so that, viewed at eye level, the bottom of the meniscus touches the graduation line.
Stopper and invert several times.

**Reducing uncertainty:** use larger masses and larger titres; rinse the burette and pipette with
the solution they will hold; remove the funnel and fill the burette jet; read meniscuses at eye level
against a white tile.

**Percentage uncertainty** = (uncertainty ÷ value measured) × 100. A titre uses two burette readings,
so double the reading uncertainty. Add the percentage uncertainties to estimate the overall
percentage uncertainty.

**Worked example 3.** 2.95 g of butanedioic acid (HOOCCH₂CH₂COOH, Mr 118.1, diprotic) is made up to
250 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions need a mean of 23.65 cm³ of NaOH. Use these uncertainties: balance ±0.005 g
per reading, flask ±0.2 cm³, pipette ±0.04 cm³, burette ±0.05 cm³ per reading.

```
n(acid) in flask = 2.95 / 118.1 = 0.02498 mol; in 25.0 cm³ = 2.498 × 10⁻³ mol
n(NaOH) = 2 × 2.498 × 10⁻³ = 4.996 × 10⁻³ mol (1 : 2)
c(NaOH) = 4.996 × 10⁻³ / 0.02365 = 0.211 mol dm⁻³
% uncertainties: mass 0.01/2.95 = 0.34%; flask 0.08%; pipette 0.16%; titre 0.10/23.65 = 0.42%
total ≈ 1.0%, so c = 0.211 ± 0.002 mol dm⁻³
```

The mass and the titre dominate, so increasing either would improve the result most.

## Common errors

- Saying the nuclear charge falls down a group. It rises; distance and shielding win.
- Writing Na₂O for sodium burnt in plenty of oxygen without mentioning the peroxide, or forgetting
  that potassium gives KO₂.
- Reversing the solubility trends: hydroxides become more soluble, sulfates less soluble.
- Explaining thermal stability by "the metal gets bigger" without linking cation size to
  polarisation of the anion.
- Giving sodium nitrate the Group 2 equation. Sodium and potassium nitrates give the nitrite and
  oxygen only.
- Averaging every titre, including the rough one or one that is not concordant.
- Using one burette reading's uncertainty for a whole titre.
