This guide goes deeper into section **3.6.1 Continuous and discontinuous variation** of the OxfordAQA International
GCSE Biology (9201) specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered and
any part of the specification may be assessed on either paper.

The specification text for this subtopic is short. It states the causes of variation: **genetic variation**,
different characteristics as a result of mutation or reproduction, and **environmental variation**, different
characteristics caused by an organism's environment (acquired characteristics). The title of the subtopic also
names two kinds of variation, continuous and discontinuous, so this guide explains both, with data work that
questions on this topic commonly use.

The existing [Variation, natural selection and the origin of species study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-variation-natural-selection-and-the-origin-of-species/)
covers 3.6.1 and 3.6.2 together. This page concentrates on variation and adds new worked examples: a grouped frequency
table, blood groups and genetically identical plants grown in different conditions. Afterwards, use the
[Variation, natural selection and the origin of species revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-variation-natural-selection-and-the-origin-of-species-revision-notes/)
and the [Continuous and discontinuous variation practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-continuous-and-discontinuous-variation-practice/).

## What 3.6.1 asks you to know

| Content | Detail |
|---|---|
| Variation | Differences between individuals of the same species |
| Genetic variation | Different characteristics as a result of mutation or reproduction |
| Environmental variation | Different characteristics caused by an organism's environment (acquired characteristics) |

## Causes of variation

**Variation** means differences between individuals. The specification gives two causes.

### Genetic variation

Genetic variation is due to differences in the genes (alleles) that individuals inherit. It arises from:

- **mutation**: a change in the genetic material, which can create a new allele;
- **reproduction**: in sexual reproduction, offspring receive a mixture of alleles from two parents, so each
  individual (except identical twins) has a different combination.

Genetic variation can be passed on to the next generation.

### Environmental variation

Environmental variation is caused by an organism's **environment**. The differences are called **acquired
characteristics**. Examples:

- a plant growing in shade is taller and paler than one in sunlight;
- an animal on a poor diet is smaller than one with plenty of food;
- a scar, or a suntan.

Acquired characteristics are not changes to the genes, so they are not usually passed on to children.

### Both together

Most characteristics are affected by both. A person's height is influenced by the genes they inherit and by nutrition
and health while growing up. A plant's leaf size is influenced by its genes and by water, light and minerals.

### Worked example 1: separating the two causes using genetically identical plants

Invented data. Cuttings were taken from one spider plant, so they are genetically identical. Five were grown in bright light
and five in dim light. The length of the longest leaf was measured after eight weeks.

| Cutting | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bright light (cm) | 38 | 41 | 36 | 40 | 35 |
| Dim light (cm) | 24 | 27 | 22 | 25 | 27 |

```
mean, bright = (38 + 41 + 36 + 40 + 35) ÷ 5 = 190 ÷ 5 = 38.0 cm
mean, dim    = (24 + 27 + 22 + 25 + 27) ÷ 5 = 125 ÷ 5 = 25.0 cm
difference = 38.0 − 25.0 = 13.0 cm
percentage difference = 13.0 ÷ 38.0 × 100 = 34.2 %
```

Interpretation: the plants have the same genes, so the 13.0 cm difference between the groups is due to the environment
(light) and not to genetic differences. The small differences within each group may be due to other
environmental variation (position, soil) because there can be no genetic variation between clones.

## Continuous variation

**Continuous variation** is variation in a characteristic that can take any value within a range, with no distinct
categories. Examples include height, mass and leaf length. Typically:

- the data are measured, not counted into categories;
- most individuals have values near the middle of the range, and fewer have extreme values;
- a frequency histogram has a bell shape;
- the characteristic is usually affected by many genes together and by the environment.

### Worked example 2: a grouped frequency table of heights

Invented data. The heights of 40 students were put into groups.

| Height (cm) | 145 up to 150 | 150 up to 155 | 155 up to 160 | 160 up to 165 | 165 up to 170 | 170 up to 175 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 2 | 6 | 12 | 11 | 6 | 3 |
| Mid-point (cm) | 147.5 | 152.5 | 157.5 | 162.5 | 167.5 | 172.5 |

```
total students = 2 + 6 + 12 + 11 + 6 + 3 = 40
sum of (frequency × mid-point) =
  2 × 147.5  = 295.0
  6 × 152.5  = 915.0
  12 × 157.5 = 1890.0
  11 × 162.5 = 1787.5
  6 × 167.5  = 1005.0
  3 × 172.5  = 517.5
  total      = 6410.0
estimated mean = 6410.0 ÷ 40 = 160.25 cm   (about 160 cm)
modal class = 155 up to 160 cm (the highest frequency, 12)
```

Interpretation: most students are in the middle groups and the frequencies fall on both sides, giving a bell-shaped
histogram. The mean is an **estimate** because the actual heights within each group are not known. Because the data
are continuous, the histogram bars touch each other.

## Discontinuous variation

**Discontinuous variation** is variation in which individuals fall into distinct categories with no intermediates.
Examples: human blood groups (A, B, AB, O), a pea plant with purple or white flowers, being male or female, and
having cystic fibrosis or not. Typically:

- the data are counted in categories;
- the characteristic is controlled by one gene or a small number of genes, with little or no effect of the
  environment;
- the data are shown in a **bar chart** with gaps between the bars.

### Worked example 3: blood groups

Invented data. The blood groups of 50 people were recorded.

| Blood group | A | B | AB | O |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of people | 20 | 7 | 3 | 20 |

```
total = 20 + 7 + 3 + 20 = 50
A  = 20 ÷ 50 × 100 = 40 %
B  = 7 ÷ 50 × 100 = 14 %
AB = 3 ÷ 50 × 100 = 6 %
O  = 20 ÷ 50 × 100 = 40 %
```

Interpretation: each person belongs to exactly one group, and there are no in-between values, so this is
discontinuous variation. A bar chart with gaps between bars displays it correctly. A line graph or bell-shaped curve
would be wrong because there is nothing between the categories.

## Comparing the two kinds

| | Continuous | Discontinuous |
|---|---|---|
| Values | Any value in a range | Distinct categories |
| Examples | Height, mass, leaf length | Blood group, flower colour, sex |
| Data type | Measured | Counted in categories |
| Graph | Histogram (bars touching) or line graph | Bar chart (gaps between bars) |
| Usual causes | Many genes and the environment | One or few genes, little environmental effect |

## Choosing the right graph

- A **bar chart** with gaps for categories (discontinuous data).
- A **histogram** with touching bars for grouped measurements (continuous data).
- A **line graph** to show how one measured variable changes with another measured variable.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that all variation is genetic, or that all variation is environmental. Many characteristics have both causes.
- Saying acquired characteristics are inherited. They are not usually passed on.
- Confusing a histogram with a bar chart.
- Describing continuous variation as "no variation" or discontinuous variation as "a small number of variations".
- Forgetting that mutation and reproduction are the two sources of genetic variation in the specification.
- Treating clones as having genetic differences.
- Using total range when asked for the mean or the modal class.

## Exam technique

- **Identify the cause**: if the individuals are genetically identical, differences must be environmental; if the
  environment is the same, differences are likely to be genetic.
- When describing a graph of continuous data, mention the **shape** (most in the middle, few at the extremes).
- For grouped data, use mid-points to estimate a mean and say it is an estimate.
- Choose a bar chart for categories and a histogram for continuous ranges, and say why.
