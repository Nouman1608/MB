## Four sub-topics, four vocabulary sets

| Sub-topic | Core vocabulary focus |
|---|---|
| **3.1 My Studies** | Subject names, opinions on subjects, timetables, the school day |
| **3.2 Life at School/College** | Facilities, uniform, rules, comparing school experiences |
| **3.3 Education Post-16** | Further/higher education routes, qualifications |
| **3.4 Jobs, Careers, Ambitions** | Part-time jobs, professions, future intention language |

Do not confuse 3.1 (subjects, opinions on them) with 3.2 (facilities, rules, routine) — reading and
listening tasks test this distinction directly by asking which sub-topic a detail belongs to.

## Why this theme is different from Themes 1 and 2

Theme 1 is present-focused and personal; Theme 2 is present-focused but outward-looking (issues,
places). Theme 3 is the only theme built around the **future** — plans, ambitions, and things not yet
true. Treat future-tense and "would like to" structures as essential vocabulary here, not optional
extras layered onto present-tense description.

## The structures that raise the grade

- **Future tense** — direct statements of intention: *I will study..., I will continue...*
- **"Would like to" / conditional** — more tentative aspiration: *I would like to become..., if I
  passed my exams, I would...*
- **Comparatives** — *this subject is more useful than..., a stable career matters more to me than...*
- **Opinion + justification** — *my favourite subject is... because...* — always paired, never a bare
  preference alone.
- **Past tense for reflection** — occasionally needed when a task asks you to look back at school
  experience before turning to future plans.

## Moving between tenses in one response

Many Theme 3 tasks want a present-tense description (current studies) **and** a future-tense plan
(post-16 route, career ambition) in the same piece of writing. Practise this tense-switching
deliberately, since maintaining correct verb agreement while moving from present to future within one
paragraph is exactly where marks are commonly lost.

## Reading and listening: two layers

Distinguish a stated **fact** (a specific subject or job named) from an expressed **opinion or
aspiration** (why the candidate likes a subject, or what they hope to do). A passage naming three
subjects and one ambition is really giving you four separate pieces of information, not one flat list
— read for both the fact and the opinion attached to it.

## Worked example

Task: *Write about your studies and your plans for after you finish school.*

```
Weak:    I study many subjects. I want a good job.
         (no opinion, no justification, one tense only)

Strong:  favourite subject + reason (present) -- my favourite
           subject is science because I find experiments interesting
         post-16 plan (future) -- after school, I will continue my
           studies at college
         career ambition + justification (would like to / because)
           -- I would like to become a doctor because I want to help
           people
         conditional reflection -- if I study hard, I will achieve
           my goal
```

A specific subject with a justified opinion, a concrete post-16 plan, and a justified career ambition
is a reliable structure for the strongest Theme 3 responses.

## Where this fits in the exam

Theme 3 is assessed across the same Paper 1 (Reading, Grammar and Meaning) and Paper 2 (Writing) that
draw on all three themes, each 1 hour 45 minutes and worth 70 marks (50% of the GCSE). There is no
dedicated Theme 3 paper, so this content must be exam-ready alongside Theme 1 and Theme 2, not
revised as an isolated final block.

## Exam traps

- Naming a subject or job with no opinion or reason attached — an easy lost opportunity for
  justification marks.
- Answering a future-plans task using only the present tense.
- Confusing "my studies" (3.1: subjects, opinions) with "life at school" (3.2: facilities, routine).
- Losing verb agreement when switching from present-tense description to future-tense plans within
  the same response.
- Giving a career ambition with no justification, when justification is what separates a strong
  answer from a bare list.

## Building active, not passive, vocabulary

Recognising future-tense and "would like to" forms when reading is not the same as producing them
correctly and quickly in your own writing or speech. For each sub-topic, practise producing full
sentences from memory rather than only matching vocabulary to translations, since the writing and
speaking components require active production under time pressure.
