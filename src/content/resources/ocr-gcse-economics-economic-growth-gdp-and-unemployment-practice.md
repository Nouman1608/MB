There are no tiers in this specification. All data is invented for practice: the countries and regions named here do not exist, and each label (Country A to Country E) is used for one economy only. ## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by Gross Domestic Product (GDP). **[2]**

**2.** Country A's real GDP rises from 1,600 billion units in year 1 to 1,656 billion units in year 2. Calculate the rate of economic growth. **[2]**

**3.** In year 1 Country B has a real GDP of 900 billion units and a population of 12 million. In year 2 its real GDP is 936 billion units and its population is 12.48 million.

**(a)** Calculate GDP per capita in year 1. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why GDP per capita in year 2 is no higher than in year 1, although GDP has grown. **[3]**

**4.** Annual growth in Country C's real GDP was: year 2, 2.4%; year 3, -1.8%; year 4, -0.6%; year 5, 1.9%; year 6, 3.4%. Real GDP in year 2 was 500 billion units.

**(a)** Identify the years in which output fell. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate real GDP in year 4, to one decimal place. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one limit of using GDP growth alone to judge how well off people are. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how more education and training could raise a country's economic growth. **[4]**

**6.** Evaluate whether economic growth always benefits a country. **[8]**

**7.** In Country D, 24.6 million people are employed and 1.4 million are unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate to one decimal place. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the Claimant Count measures unemployment, and why it may give a different figure from the number of people who want a job. **[4]**

**9.** Identify the type of unemployment (cyclical, frictional, seasonal or structural) in each case.

**(a)** A beach cafe worker is out of work every winter. **[1]**
**(b)** A person leaves one job and spends three weeks looking for another. **[1]**
**(c)** A factory worker is made redundant because the economy has slumped and spending has fallen. **[1]**
**(d)** A steelworker's town loses its only steelworks and local employers want skills the worker does not have. **[1]**

**10.** Country E has two regions. Region East has a labour force of 1.5 million, of whom 135,000 are unemployed. Region West has a labour force of 4.5 million, of whom 108,000 are unemployed.

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate in each region. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the national rate and explain why it gives a misleading picture of the country. **[2]**
**(c)** Each unemployed person costs the government 10,500 units a year in benefits and lost tax. Calculate the total annual cost of the unemployed in both regions. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate the consequences of unemployment for individuals, regions and the government. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** GDP is the money value [1] of final output made inside a country over a set period, such as a year [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong definition names both what is counted (value of output) and the boundaries (a country, a period). "How rich a country is" is too vague.

**2.** Growth = (1,656 - 1,600) / 1,600 x 100 [1] = **3.5%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division by the old figure. A bare answer is risky, because one slip leaves nothing to reward.

**3. (a)** GDP per capita = GDP / population = 900 billion / 12 million [1] = **75,000 units per person** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** GDP grew by (936 - 900) / 900 = 4% [1]. Population grew by (12.48 - 12) / 12 = 4% [1]. Output and population rose at the same rate, so GDP per capita is unchanged at 75,000 (936 billion / 12.48 million) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use figures from the question in an explain answer. Writing only "population grew faster" would be wrong here, because the rates are equal.

**4. (a)** **Years 3 and 4** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Year 3 = 500 x (1 - 0.018) = 491.0 [1]. Year 4 = 491.0 x (1 - 0.006) [1] = 488.054, so **488.1 billion units** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** GDP growth says nothing about how income is shared [1], so most people may not be better off even when growth is high (or: it ignores pollution and unpaid work) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In a chained calculation, apply each year's growth in turn. Starting from the wrong year, or adding the percentages together, is a common slip. A limit needs a reason attached, not just a name.

**5.** Education and training raise workers' skills [1]. Skilled workers produce more per hour, so productivity rises [1]. Higher productivity raises the economy's potential output, which means higher growth [1]. Evaluation: the effect takes years, and training costs money that could be spent elsewhere, so growth is not immediate [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis needs linked steps ("so", "which leads to"). A list of benefits does not build a chain.

**6.** Benefits: growth raises incomes and creates jobs [1]. Higher output brings more tax revenue, which can fund schools and hospitals [1]. Living standards, such as health care and education, can improve [1]. Costs: the gains can go mostly to the richest, so inequality widens [1]. Higher output can mean more pollution [1] and faster use of non-renewable resources, which threatens sustainability for future generations [1]. Evaluation: the answer depends on how growth is produced and shared; clean, widely shared growth benefits most people [1]. Judgement: growth does not always benefit a country, but it can when policies share the gains and protect the environment [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluate answer weighs both sides and ends in a supported judgement. Covering economic, social and environmental points gives breadth, but the judgement must follow from the points you made.

**7.** Labour force = 24.6 + 1.4 = 26.0 million [1]. Rate = 1.4 / 26.0 x 100 [1] = 5.38..., so **5.4%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing 1.4 by 24.6 gives 5.7%, which is a different measure. Check that the denominator includes the unemployed.

**8.** The Claimant Count counts the number of people claiming unemployment-related benefits [1], using government benefit records [1]. It may differ from the number who want work because some unemployed people do not claim, for example due to savings or a partner's earnings [1], so it can understate unemployment (or: changes in benefit rules alter who can claim) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question has two parts, how it measures and why it differs. Make sure each gets its own sentence.

**9. (a)** **Seasonal** [1]. **(b)** **Frictional** [1]. **(c)** **Cyclical** [1]. **(d)** **Structural** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Match the key detail in the scenario, such as winter, three weeks, spending fallen or skills not wanted, to the type.

**10. (a)** East: 135,000 / 1,500,000 x 100 [1] = **9.0%** [1]. West: 108,000 / 4,500,000 x 100 [1] = **2.4%** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** National: (135,000 + 108,000) / 6,000,000 x 100 = **4.05%** (about 4.1%) [1]. This average hides the gap: East has 9.0%, nearly four times West's rate, so East's hardship is understated [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Total unemployed = 243,000 [1]. Cost = 243,000 x 10,500 = **2,551.5 million units** a year [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In calculations with several regions, label each result clearly. The comment should use the numbers rather than just say regions differ.

**11.** Individuals: lost income lowers living standards and can cause debt [1]. The longer the spell, the more skills fade and health and confidence suffer [1]. Regions: unemployed people spend less, so local shops lose sales and may close [1], and high unemployment can drive away new firms and young people [1]. Government: it pays more in benefits and receives less tax [1], and the opportunity cost is the schools or hospitals the money could have funded [1]. Evaluation: the damage depends on the type and length of unemployment; short frictional unemployment does little harm, while long structural unemployment in one region does the most [1]. Judgement: the consequences are most severe for individuals and regions facing long-term structural unemployment, and the government's costs grow with them [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The question names three groups, so cover each one. Using the type of unemployment to decide which consequence is worst turns description into evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the new GDP figure when finding growth.
- Mixing up GDP and GDP per capita, or ignoring population.
- Using the employed as the denominator of the unemployment rate.
- Rounding too early in a chained calculation.
- Describing a determinant of growth without explaining the link to output.
- Only naming a limit of the data, without saying why it matters.
- Writing about costs of growth with no benefits, or the reverse, in an evaluate answer.
- Treating all unemployment as the same type.
