This guide goes deeper on **5.1 Business finance**, the first subtopic of AS Level topic 5 (Finance and accounting) in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. The syllabus content is:

- **5.1.1 The need for business finance**: reasons why businesses need finance to start up, to grow and to survive; the distinction between short-term and long-term need for finance; the difference between cash and profits; business failure as a consequence of lack of finance (bankruptcy, liquidation and administration).
- **5.1.2 Working capital**: the meaning and importance of working capital; managing trade receivables and trade payables; the distinction between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure.

The [combined guide to business finance and sources of finance](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-business-finance-and-sources-of-finance/) defines each term. This page works through the situations case studies actually present: a profitable firm short of cash, a growing firm stretching its working capital, and a manager deciding how to treat customers and suppliers. Revise with the [Business finance revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-business-finance-revision-notes/) and practise with the [Business finance practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-business-finance-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why finance is needed: a start-up budget

**Fenwick Bike Repairs** is about to open. The owner lists what must be paid before the first customer pays:

| Item | Cost | Type of need |
|---|---|---|
| Workshop fit-out and tools | $18 000 | Long term (assets used for years) |
| Van | $14 000 | Long term |
| Opening inventory of parts | $5 000 | Short term (turns into sales within weeks) |
| Three months' rent and wages before revenue builds up | $12 000 | Short term |
| **Total** | **$49 000** | |

The long-term items ($32 000) should be matched with long-term finance, such as the owner's capital or a loan repaid over several years. The short-term items ($17 000) can be covered by short-term finance, such as an overdraft, because they will be recovered from sales fairly soon. Matching the length of the finance to the length of the need is the key principle.

Once open, the business will need finance for different reasons:

- **To grow**: a second workshop, more staff, larger inventory.
- **To survive**: covering a slow winter season, a large bad debt, or a sudden rise in costs.

## Cash and profit: which transactions affect which

Profit compares revenue with the costs of a period. Cash records money actually received and paid. Many transactions affect one but not the other at the time they happen.

| Transaction | Effect on profit this period | Effect on cash this period |
|---|---|---|
| Sale on 60 days' credit | Increases | None until the customer pays |
| Buying inventory for cash, not yet sold | None until it is sold | Decreases |
| Receiving a bank loan | None | Increases |
| Repaying a loan | None (only the interest is a cost) | Decreases |
| Buying a machine for cash | Little this period (the cost is spread over its life) | Large decrease |
| Owner puts in savings | None | Increases |

This explains how a business can be profitable but run out of cash, or have plenty of cash while making a loss (for example, after receiving a loan).

### Why growing firms run short of cash

**Worked example.** Linden Office Furniture sells desks on 60 days' credit. Each month it must pay for materials and wages in the same month it makes the desks. It doubles its monthly sales from $40 000 to $80 000. Costs are 75% of sales.

- Monthly costs rise from $30 000 to $60 000, paid immediately.
- Revenue from the extra sales does not arrive for two months.
- In each of the first two months after the increase, extra cash out is $30 000 while extra cash in is zero: a gap of $60 000 to finance before the new revenue starts to arrive.

Linden's profit rises (an extra $10 000 a month), yet unless it arranges finance it may be unable to pay wages. Expanding faster than the finance available is a common cause of failure among profitable businesses.

## Business failure from lack of finance

When a business cannot pay its debts as they fall due, it is **insolvent**. The syllabus names three outcomes:

| Outcome | Applies to | What happens | Who is affected most |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bankruptcy | Individuals, such as sole traders and partners | The person's assets, including personal ones if liability is unlimited, are used to pay creditors | The owner and their family |
| Liquidation | Companies (and businesses being closed) | The business stops trading; its assets are sold and the money is shared among creditors | Employees lose jobs; creditors may be paid only part of what they are owed |
| Administration | Companies | An administrator takes control to try to rescue the business or sell it as a going concern, while creditors are held back | Some jobs may be saved; shareholders often lose their investment |

**Warning signs** in a case include: a growing overdraft, suppliers demanding cash on delivery, late payment of wages or taxes, and a few large customers who pay slowly.

## Working capital: the cycle

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. It is easier to understand as a cycle:

**Cash → buy materials (inventory) → make and sell on credit (trade receivables) → customers pay (cash)**

The longer this cycle takes, the more working capital the business needs.

**Worked example.** Brackley Kitchens holds materials for 30 days, takes 20 days to make each kitchen, and gives customers 45 days' credit. It pays its own suppliers after 30 days.

- Days from paying for materials to receiving cash: 30 + 20 + 45 − 30 = **65 days**.
- If daily costs are $2 000, Brackley needs about 65 × 2 000 = $130 000 to finance the cycle.
- If it cuts customer credit to 30 days, the gap falls to 50 days and the finance needed to $100 000, releasing $30 000.

### Too little or too much?

- **Too little**: bills cannot be paid on time, discounts are lost, suppliers refuse credit, and the business may become insolvent.
- **Too much**: cash is tied up in idle inventory or unpaid invoices instead of earning a return elsewhere, such as in new equipment.

The right level depends on the business. A supermarket selling for cash and paying suppliers later needs little; a construction firm paid in stages needs a lot.

## Managing trade receivables and trade payables

Every method involves a trade-off:

| Action | Cash benefit | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Shorten credit given to customers | Cash arrives sooner | Customers may buy from rivals offering longer credit |
| Offer a discount for early payment | Faster cash | Lower revenue on each sale |
| Credit-check new customers | Fewer bad debts | Some sales lost |
| Use debt factoring | Immediate cash for invoices | A fee reduces the amount received |
| Take longer to pay suppliers | Cash stays in the business longer | Supplier goodwill falls; may lose discounts or face stricter terms |

**Worked example on discounts.** A customer owes $20 000 in 60 days. The business offers 2% off for payment in 10 days. It receives $19 600 fifty days earlier, losing $400. If the business is paying 12% a year on its overdraft, borrowing $20 000 for 50 days costs about 20 000 × 0.12 × 50 ÷ 365 ≈ $329. The discount costs more than the interest saved, so it is only worth offering if cash is urgently needed or the customer is a bad-debt risk.

## Capital and revenue expenditure: why the distinction matters

- **Capital expenditure** buys non-current assets used for more than a year: a delivery van, an extension, new software licences bought outright.
- **Revenue expenditure** covers running costs: fuel, wages, rent, repairs and maintenance.

Classifying correctly matters for two reasons. First, **financing**: capital expenditure usually needs long-term finance, while revenue expenditure should be covered by regular income. Second, **profit measurement**: treating a $30 000 machine as a cost of one year would understate that year's profit and overstate later years'.

Tricky cases: repairing a roof is revenue expenditure; replacing the whole roof with a larger one that adds space is capital expenditure.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Analyse why a profitable business might still fail.*

> Profit is revenue minus costs, but revenue can be recorded before cash arrives, for example when goods are sold on 60 days' credit. *(Knowledge.)* If the business must pay wages and suppliers immediately, cash can run out even though each sale makes a profit. *(Analysis: timing difference.)* This is especially likely when it grows quickly, because the costs of extra output are paid long before the extra revenue is received. *(Developed chain.)* If suppliers or the bank then refuse further credit, the business cannot pay its debts as they fall due and could be forced into administration or liquidation. *(Consequence linked to syllabus terms.)*

## Common mistakes

- Using "cash" and "profit" as if they mean the same.
- Saying administration always closes the business.
- Applying "bankruptcy" to a limited company.
- Suggesting a long-term loan to cover a short gap in cash, or an overdraft to buy a building.
- Recommending longer credit for customers without mentioning the cash cost.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Define**: precise meaning, such as working capital = current assets − current liabilities.
- **Calculate**: show working and units (dollars, days).
- **Explain**: give the reason and its effect on cash or profit.
- **Analyse**: trace the chain from a decision (such as offering credit) to cash, survival or growth.
- **Evaluate** or **Advise**: judge a working-capital policy for the business in the case, weighing cash against sales and goodwill.
