This guide covers sub-topic **1.3 Factors of production** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content. The syllabus lists five points: the nature and definition of factors of production, land, labour, capital and enterprise (1.3.1); the difference between human capital and physical capital (1.3.2); rewards to the factors of production (1.3.3); division of labour and specialisation (1.3.4); and the role of the entrepreneur in contemporary economies, risk and organisation of the other factors of production (1.3.5). The Topic 1 introduction also asks you to consider the advantages and disadvantages of specialisation in the use of resources. All businesses and figures below are invented.

## 1.3.1 What counts as each factor

**Factors of production** are the resources used to produce goods and services. Classifying them correctly is where many marks are won or lost, because everyday meanings mislead.

| Factor | Economic meaning | Common misclassification |
|---|---|---|
| Land | All natural resources: ground, seas, rivers, minerals, forests, fish stocks, the climate | Thinking it means only a plot of ground |
| Labour | The physical and mental effort of people in production | Counting the machines workers use |
| Capital | Goods made by people and used to produce other goods and services | Counting money or shares as capital |
| Enterprise | Taking risks and organising the other three factors to produce | Treating every manager as an entrepreneur |

**Money is not capital in this sense.** A bank balance cannot produce anything; it can be used to buy capital goods such as tools or vehicles. Economists separate the *finance* that buys capital from the *capital goods* themselves.

**The same item can be different factors in different uses.** A truck used by a farmer to move crops is capital. The same type of truck bought by a family for holidays is a consumer good, not a factor of production.

**Quantity and quality.** An economy's productive potential depends on how much of each factor it has and how good each one is: the skills of its workers, the fertility of its soil, the age of its machinery. This links directly to shifts of the production possibility curve in 1.5.

## 1.3.2 Human capital and physical capital

**Physical capital** is the stock of man-made goods used in production: machines, buildings, roads, computers, delivery vans. **Human capital** is the stock of skills, knowledge, experience and health embodied in workers, which raises their productivity.

| Feature | Physical capital | Human capital |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Tangible equipment and structures | Skills and knowledge inside people |
| Built up by | Investment in capital goods | Education, training, experience, health care |
| Can be sold separately? | Yes | No: it stays with the person |
| Wears out by | Depreciation through use and age | Skills becoming outdated; ill health; retirement |

**Worked example 1: investing in human capital.** A mobile phone repair technician pays 4,800 currency units (cu) for an advanced course. Afterwards she can repair newer models, raising her earnings by 900 cu a year. Over 8 years of working life in which the skill stays relevant, she gains 900 × 8 = 7,200 cu, or 2,400 cu more than the course cost. The investment is worthwhile only if the skill stays in demand: if those models are replaced after three years, she recovers only 2,700 cu.

The two forms of capital are often **complements**. A hospital's new scanner (physical capital) produces little without trained radiographers (human capital), and the radiographers are less productive without the equipment.

## 1.3.3 Rewards to the factors

| Factor | Reward | Nature of the reward |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Rent | Payment for the use of natural resources |
| Labour | Wages | Payment for effort, usually agreed in advance |
| Capital | Interest | The return to those who provide capital |
| Enterprise | Profit | The residual left after all other factors are paid; can be negative |

Profit differs from the other rewards. Rent, wages and interest are usually contracted before production happens. Profit is what is left over, so it is uncertain. That uncertainty is the reason for 1.3.5: the entrepreneur bears the risk that the residual is a loss.

**Worked example 2: dividing revenue.** A small fruit-juice firm has annual revenue of 320,000 cu. It pays 150,000 cu in wages, 30,000 cu in rent for its unit and orchard, 20,000 cu in interest on a loan for its pressing equipment, and 85,000 cu for fruit, bottles and energy bought from other firms. Profit = 320,000 − 150,000 − 30,000 − 20,000 − 85,000 = **35,000 cu**, the reward to enterprise. If revenue fell by 15% to 272,000 cu with costs unchanged, profit would be −13,000 cu: the other factors are still paid, but the entrepreneur makes a loss.

## 1.3.4 Division of labour and specialisation

**Specialisation** is concentrating on a narrow range of tasks or products. **Division of labour** is specialisation by workers: a production process is split into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.

**Worked example 3: a phone repair shop.** Four technicians each carry out whole repairs: diagnosis, replacing parts, testing and packaging. Each completes 6 repairs a day, so the shop completes 24. The owner reorganises the work: one technician diagnoses, two replace parts and one tests and packs. Output rises to 36 repairs a day.

- Rise in output: (36 − 24) ÷ 24 × 100 = **50%**.
- With a daily wage bill of 4 × 80 = 320 cu, labour cost per repair falls from 320 ÷ 24 = **13.33 cu** to 320 ÷ 36 = **8.89 cu**.

**Why output rises**

- Workers become faster and more skilled through repetition.
- No time is lost moving between tasks and tools.
- Each worker can be matched to the task they do best.
- Specialised equipment becomes worthwhile when one person uses it all day.

**Disadvantages**

- Repetitive work can be boring, lowering motivation and quality.
- A worker with a narrow skill finds it harder to change jobs if demand for that skill falls.
- Interdependence: if the one diagnostic technician is absent, the whole shop slows.
- Standardised output can reduce variety.

**Specialisation beyond the workplace.** Firms specialise in particular products, regions in particular industries, and countries in particular goods. In each case specialisation raises output but creates dependence on **exchange**, which in turn relies on money and markets, and on others continuing to supply what the specialist does not produce.

## 1.3.5 The entrepreneur in contemporary economies

The syllabus defines the entrepreneur's role by two functions.

1. **Organisation.** Bringing together land, labour and capital, deciding what to produce and how, and combining the factors efficiently.
2. **Risk.** Committing resources before knowing whether customers will buy. Wages, rent and interest must be paid whatever happens; the entrepreneur receives whatever profit or loss remains.

**Worked example 4: launching a product.** An entrepreneur plans a meal-kit delivery service. Before the first sale she signs a lease (rent), hires drivers and cooks (wages) and borrows to buy refrigerated vans (interest). If demand is strong, the business earns a profit; if not, she still owes the rent, wages and interest. Her reward is uncertain precisely because the other factors' rewards are not.

**Why entrepreneurs matter in a modern economy**

- They introduce new products and methods, which drives productivity growth.
- They create jobs and competition, pushing existing firms to improve.
- They respond to changing consumer wants faster than a central planner could.

**Is enterprise a separate factor?** In large public companies, organisation is done by salaried managers while risk is borne by shareholders. The two functions can be split between different people, which is one reason some economists treat enterprise as a special kind of labour. For 9708 answers, define it by the two functions the syllabus names.

## Common mistakes

- Calling money or shares "capital" in the factor of production sense.
- Using "land" to mean only ground.
- Giving "profit" as the reward to capital, or "interest" as the reward to enterprise.
- Listing advantages of the division of labour with no disadvantages.
- Describing the entrepreneur only as "a person who starts a business", without risk and organisation.
- Treating human capital as something a firm owns.

## Exam technique

- **Define:** use the economic meaning of each factor, with one example.
- **Distinguish:** for human and physical capital, give one difference in form and one in how each is built up.
- **Explain:** for specialisation, follow the chain from the task to the productivity gain to the lower cost per unit.
- **Assess** or **Evaluate:** weigh the gains from specialisation against motivation, flexibility and dependence, and reach a judgement about the case given.
