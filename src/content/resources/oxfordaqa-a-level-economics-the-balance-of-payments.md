## The three accounts

The balance of payments is a statement of the payments that pass between a country's residents and everyone abroad during a year or quarter. A payment received from abroad is a credit and a payment sent abroad is a debit.

- **Current account:** exports and imports of goods and of services, primary income (wages, profits, interest and dividends earned across borders) and secondary income (transfers such as gifts, aid and remittances).
- **Capital account:** small transfers of capital and the sale or purchase of non-produced assets.
- **Financial account:** flows of money for investment, such as FDI, portfolio investment and bank lending.

Presentation differs between sources. Here the financial account shows net inflows as positive, so the accounts add to zero once a balancing item (net errors and omissions) is included. Check which sign convention your textbook uses.

### Worked example 1: Country K's balance of payments

Figures are in billions of K-units, and GDP is 650.

| Item | Credits | Debits | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods | 180 | 228 | -48 |
| Services | 70 | 40 | +30 |
| Primary income | 30 | 24 | +6 |
| Secondary income | 10 | 24 | -14 |

1. Current account balance = -48 + 30 + 6 - 14 = **-26**, a deficit.
2. As a share of GDP: -26 / 650 × 100 = **-4.0%**.
3. The capital account is +2. The financial account has FDI +13, portfolio investment +10 and other flows -2, so it is +21.
4. The accounts so far sum to -26 + 2 + 21 = -3. Net errors and omissions must be **+3** to make the total zero.

The central idea: the whole balance of payments always balances. A current account deficit is paid for by net inflows on the capital and financial accounts, meaning foreigners lend to the country or buy its assets. When people say "a deficit", they usually mean the current account unless told otherwise.

## Deficits and surpluses: why they matter

### A persistent current account deficit

A deficit is not harmful in itself. It matters through its cause, its size and how it is financed.

- **Rising foreign claims.** Each year of deficit adds to what the country owes abroad, through new loans and the sale of assets. Interest, profits and dividends then flow out, which worsens the primary income balance in later years.
- **Dependence on inflows.** If lenders lose confidence, the funds stop. The country may have to raise interest rates to attract money, or let the currency fall, or cut spending sharply.
- **Weak competitiveness.** A deficit caused by costly or low-quality exports points to a structural problem and lost jobs in traded industries.
- **Leakage from demand.** Imports exceeding exports reduce AD, other things equal, which can slow growth.

### Worked example 2: is a deficit sustainable?

Suppose Country K runs a current account deficit of 4% of GDP every year, and nominal GDP grows 5% a year. After many years, net foreign liabilities settle at deficit / growth = 4% / 5% = **80% of GDP**. A steady deficit therefore need not explode, provided growth holds up and lenders keep lending. If growth falls to 2%, the same deficit would head towards 4 / 2 = 200% of GDP. This is a simplified model that ignores changes in asset prices, but it shows why a deficit is judged against income, not in absolute terms.

### A persistent surplus

A surplus is not simply good. It can mean weak domestic demand and low consumption. It may lift AD and inflation when exports boom, and it can create tension with trading partners who run the matching deficits. The currency may also be pushed up, which erodes competitiveness.

**Evaluation:** judge a deficit or surplus by its size relative to GDP, how long it lasts, what causes it (temporary or structural, such as imports of capital goods that raise future output) and how easily it is financed.

## FDI and portfolio investment

**Foreign direct investment (FDI)** is money put into productive assets in another country, for instance by setting up a plant or acquiring a majority share in a company there. The investor takes a lasting interest and some say in how the business is run. **Portfolio investment** is the purchase of financial assets such as shares and bonds, with no control, in search of a return.

| | FDI | Portfolio investment |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Control and long-term presence | Financial return |
| Time horizon | Long, hard to reverse | Short or long, easy to sell |
| Stability | Relatively stable | Can reverse quickly |
| Example | A foreign firm builds a plant | A fund buys government bonds |

### Consequences for the country receiving investment

- **Benefits:** extra capital, new technology and management methods, jobs, higher productivity, possibly more exports, tax revenue and a financial-account inflow that funds a current account deficit.
- **Costs:** profits are sent abroad (a debit on primary income), local firms may be squeezed out, control of key industries may pass overseas, and jobs may disappear if the investor leaves. Large portfolio inflows can push up the exchange rate and then reverse suddenly.

For the country sending funds, investors earn returns that come back as primary income credits and gain access to markets. The cost is less investment and employment at home.

### Worked example 3: a foreign plant in Country K

A foreign firm invests 120 million K-units in a plant. Each year it exports 30 million of output, imports 12 million of inputs and sends 10 million of profit abroad.

1. Year of investment: financial account +120 million.
2. Each later year: current account effect = +30 - 12 - 10 = **+8 million**.
3. The 120 million inflow is a one-off, but it creates a claim on the country. The yearly profit outflow of 10 million continues for as long as the plant operates, and it is already included in the +8 million.

The conclusion is that FDI helps the current account most when it earns export revenue or replaces imports, and less when its profits leave and its inputs are imported. Productivity and employment gains may matter more than the account effect.

### Worked example 4: a portfolio reversal

Country K's current account deficit is 26 billion. Portfolio flows swing from +10 to -6, a swing of 16 billion. Unless other inflows rise, a financing gap of 16 billion opens. It can be met by using foreign exchange reserves, by raising interest rates to attract funds, or by a falling exchange rate. This is why portfolio flows can destabilise an economy that relies on them.

## Policies to deal with balance of payments problems

Policies fall into two groups, and the group tells you the mechanism.

| | Expenditure-reducing | Expenditure-switching |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Cut total spending so that fewer imports are bought | Move spending from imports to home output |
| Tools | Higher taxes, lower government spending, higher interest rates | A lower exchange rate, tariffs, quotas, export subsidies |
| Main risk | Slower growth and higher unemployment | Higher import prices, retaliation, cost-push inflation |

### Worked example 5: expenditure-reducing policy

In Country K an extra 1 of income adds 0.3 to imports, so the marginal propensity to import (MPM) is 0.3. The government wants to cut imports by 8 billion.

1. Income must fall by 8 / 0.3 = **26.67 billion**.
2. With a multiplier of 1.8, AD must be cut by 26.67 / 1.8 = **14.81 billion**.

The cut slows the economy. Output falls by far more than the import saving, so correcting the deficit this way is costly when the economy has spare capacity.

### Worked example 6: why elasticities decide whether switching works

Country K's exports are 250 billion and imports are 268 billion (goods and services), so the trade balance is -18. The exchange rate falls 10%. Export prices in K-units stay the same, and import prices in K-units rise 10%.

| | Exports | Imports | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic: export PED 1.2, import PED 0.4 | 250 × 1.12 = 280 | 268 × 1.10 × 0.96 = 283.01 | -3.01 |
| Inelastic: export PED 0.5, import PED 0.2 | 250 × 1.05 = 262.5 | 268 × 1.10 × 0.98 = 288.90 | -26.40 |

In the elastic case, the balance improves by about 15. In the inelastic case the balance worsens by about 8.4, because the import bill rises faster than export volume. Elasticities tend to be lower in the short run, since buyers need time to find alternatives, so the balance can worsen before it improves. Supply elasticity matters too: exporters must be able to raise output.

A tariff of 15% on goods imports with import PED 0.8 cuts volume by 0.8 × 15 = 12%. The goods import bill, before tariff, falls from 228 to 228 × 0.88 = 200.64. Retaliation or a lack of home substitutes would weaken this, and higher prices fall on consumers and on firms that import inputs.

**Supply-side policies**, such as investment in skills and infrastructure, work slowly but lift productivity and quality. They improve competitiveness without cutting living standards, and they raise the elasticity of supply.

## Effects on other policy objectives

| Policy | Helps | Hurts |
|---|---|---|
| Higher taxes, lower spending, higher rates | Lower inflation | Growth, employment, possibly investment |
| Lower exchange rate | Export-led growth and jobs | Imported inflation, lower real incomes for importers |
| Tariffs and quotas | Protects domestic jobs | Prices, choice, trade relations, efficiency |
| Supply-side measures | Growth, productivity | Cost, long delay |

For a surplus, the reverse applies: expansionary policy, a higher exchange rate or lower trade barriers reduce the surplus but may raise inflation or hurt exporters.

**Evaluation chain.** Match the policy to the cause. If the deficit comes from an overheating economy with high inflation, expenditure-reducing policy fixes both problems. If the economy is in recession, the same policy conflicts with growth and employment, and switching or supply-side measures fit better. Ask also how big the deficit is, how elastic trade is and how quickly the policy works.

## Common errors

- Saying the balance of payments is in deficit overall. The accounts sum to zero, so say which account.
- Treating every deficit as bad without looking at its cause, size or financing.
- Confusing FDI with portfolio investment, or saying FDI is always better.
- Expecting a weaker currency to fix a deficit automatically. Say what happens if demand is inelastic.
- Listing policies without saying whether they cut spending or switch it.
- Forgetting the side effects on inflation, growth and unemployment.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between FDI and portfolio investment?**
FDI is investment in productive assets abroad with a lasting interest and some control, such as building a plant. Portfolio investment is buying financial assets such as shares and bonds for a return, with no control, and it can reverse quickly.

**When does a current account deficit matter?**
A deficit matters through its cause, size and financing. It adds to foreign claims, so income flows out later, it depends on lenders' confidence, and it may reflect weak competitiveness.

**Why can a currency depreciation worsen the trade balance at first?**
Elasticities tend to be low in the short run, so import volumes fall little while import prices rise. The import bill can then rise faster than export earnings until buyers find alternatives.

**What are the side effects of correcting a deficit?**
Higher taxes, lower spending or higher interest rates cut growth and employment, a lower exchange rate adds imported inflation, tariffs raise prices and risk retaliation, and supply-side measures are costly and slow.

**What are the benefits and costs of FDI for the receiving country?**
FDI can bring capital, technology, jobs, higher productivity, exports and tax revenue, and it finances a deficit. Costs include profits sent abroad, local firms being squeezed out and loss of control over key industries.
