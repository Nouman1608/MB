This guide covers section **3.3.4 Influences on business decisions** in Unit 3 (Business decisions and strategy) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. Earlier sections looked at what a business could do. This one looks at the forces inside and around the business that shape what it chooses to do: its culture, the people with an interest in it, and its sense of what is right. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Business decisions and strategy study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-business-decisions-and-strategy/). Condensed notes are in the [Influences on business decisions revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-influences-on-business-decisions-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Influences on business decisions practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-influences-on-business-decisions-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Corporate culture:** strong and weak cultures; the classification of cultures as power, role, task and person; how culture is formed; the difficulties in changing an established culture.
2. **Stakeholder model versus shareholder model:** internal and external stakeholders; stakeholder objectives; the stakeholder view (consider all stakeholders in decisions and objectives) and the shareholder view (focus on returns through share price and dividends); the potential for conflict between profit-based and wider objectives.
3. **Business ethics:** the ethics of strategic decisions and the trade-off between profit and ethics; pay and rewards; corporate social responsibility (CSR).

## Corporate culture

**Corporate culture** is the shared values, beliefs, habits and ways of working in an organisation: "the way we do things here". It affects how decisions are made, how staff treat customers, how quickly the business adapts and how well people work together.

A **strong culture** is widely shared and clearly understood by staff, so behaviour is consistent and employees are often loyal and motivated. A **weak culture** has little shared identity: different departments follow different habits, and control depends more on rules and supervision. A strong culture is helpful only when it fits the strategy. A strong culture based on caution can block innovation.

### Handy's classification

| Culture | Key feature | Typical business | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Power | Control by one person or a small group at the centre; decisions are made by those in power | Small family firm, entrepreneur-led start-up | Quick decisions; clear direction | Depends on one leader; staff have little say |
| Role | Rules, procedures and defined job descriptions; authority from the position | Civil service, large bank | Predictable and stable; clear responsibilities | Slow to change; bureaucratic |
| Task | Teams formed to solve problems or complete projects; expertise matters more than position | Consultancy, advertising agency, engineering projects | Flexible; innovative; good for teamwork | Can be costly; teams may compete for resources |
| Person | Individuals are at the centre; the organisation exists to serve their interests | Partnership of doctors or lawyers, a co-operative | Highly valued staff; high satisfaction | Hard to manage; individuals may not follow a common goal |

Businesses rarely fit one type exactly. A business may be role-based in finance and task-based in product design.

### How culture is formed

Culture develops from the **founder's values** and style; the **history** of the business and its stories of past successes; the **leadership** and what managers visibly reward; the **industry** and its norms (a hospital differs from an advertising agency); the **size** and structure; the **recruitment** of people who fit; and **rewards**, systems and symbols such as dress codes, office layout and rituals.

### Difficulties in changing culture

Culture is hard to change because it is deeply embedded and often unwritten. Long-serving staff may resist, as new ways threaten their status or habits. Managers who benefit from the old way may block change. Change takes years, not months, and early success can be lost if leaders slip back. A merger is a good example: two strong but different cultures can clash, so productivity falls and good staff leave. Managers can ease change by explaining the reasons, involving staff, changing reward systems, and leading by example. Section 3.3.6 returns to resistance to change.

## Stakeholder model versus shareholder model

**Stakeholders** are the individuals and groups with an interest in a business. **Internal** stakeholders (owners, managers, employees) work within it. **External** stakeholders (customers, suppliers, lenders, the local community, the government, pressure groups) are outside.

| Stakeholder | Typical objective |
|---|---|
| Shareholders | Rising share price and dividends |
| Managers | Salary, bonuses, status, job security |
| Employees | Fair pay, secure jobs, good conditions |
| Customers | Quality, value, safe products |
| Suppliers | Regular orders, prompt payment |
| Lenders | Repayment with interest |
| Local community | Jobs, minimal pollution and traffic |
| Government | Tax revenue, legal compliance, employment |

The **shareholder model** holds that the business exists to maximise returns to its owners. Decisions should raise the share price and dividends, and other stakeholders matter only so far as they help the business earn profit. The **stakeholder model** holds that a business should consider the interests of all stakeholders. In the long term, it argues, a business that treats staff, customers and communities well is likelier to succeed.

**Worked example 1.** Haldane Plc made a profit for the year of $2.4m.

| Use of profit | Shareholder model | Stakeholder model |
|---|---|---|
| Dividends | 80% = $1,920,000 | 50% = $1,200,000 |
| Staff training and bonuses | 0 | $400,000 |
| Community and environmental projects | 0 | $300,000 |
| Retained for investment | $480,000 | $500,000 |
| **Total** | **$2,400,000** | **$2,400,000** |

Under the stakeholder model, shareholders receive $720,000 less in dividends, which is 37.5% lower than under the shareholder model. In return, Haldane might gain a more motivated workforce, a better reputation and fewer conflicts with the community. Whether this pays off depends on the time frame: it may lower returns this year but protect them in the long run.

**Potential for conflict.** Shareholders may want higher dividends, while employees want higher pay and the community wants lower emissions, and all draw on the same profit. Managers may be pulled between the short-term demands of investors and the long-term needs of other stakeholders. Resolving conflict involves compromise, negotiation, and ranking stakeholders by their power and interest in the decision.

## Business ethics

**Ethics** are moral principles about right and wrong in behaviour. Strategic decisions involve a **trade-off** between profit and ethics: choices that raise profit may be less ethical, and ethical choices may raise costs or cut profit.

**Worked example 2.** Haldane could switch to a cheaper clothing supplier whose factory has poor safety and long hours. This saves $180,000 a year on revenue of $12m.

- Profit rises from $2.4m to 2.4m + 0.18m = **$2.58m**, an increase of **7.5%**.
- Suppose the practice is exposed and 3% of revenue ($12m x 0.03 = **$360,000**) is lost. At a gross margin of 40%, the lost profit is 0.40 x 360,000 = **$144,000**.
- The net gain is 180,000 - 144,000 = only **$36,000**, before legal costs and damage to staff morale.

So an unethical choice may bring only a small gain and a big risk. Ethical behaviour can also lead to **competitive advantage**: customers may pay more for Fairtrade or ethically sourced goods, and good employers attract and keep staff. However, ethical standards cost money, and some firms may take advantage of weak regulation.

### Pay and rewards

Pay is an ethical issue as well as a motivational one. Questions include whether top managers' pay is justified in relation to that of workers, whether workers are paid a living wage, and whether pay is fair across genders and roles. If Haldane's chief executive receives $900,000 and the average employee $30,000, the pay ratio is 900,000 / 30,000 = **30:1**. High executive pay can be defended as the reward for responsibility and results, and as the price of attracting talent. It can be criticised if it is large compared with staff pay, or if it rewards short-term share price gains at the cost of long-term health. Rewards linked to ethical targets and long-term performance help to align managers with stakeholders.

### Corporate social responsibility (CSR)

**CSR** means a business acting responsibly towards society and the environment beyond what the law requires: reducing emissions, sourcing fairly, supporting local communities, treating staff well and being open about its performance. **Benefits:** stronger brand and customer loyalty, easier recruitment, fewer conflicts, and sometimes cost savings (for example from saving energy). **Costs:** the money spent, which may reduce short-term profit; the risk of **greenwashing** (claiming more than is true), which damages trust; and the difficulty of measuring the return. A shareholder-model business may support CSR only if it improves long-term profit, while a stakeholder-model business treats it as part of its purpose.

## Common mistakes

- Listing Handy's four types without linking one to the business in the source.
- Treating "strong culture" as automatically good.
- Confusing stakeholders (all interested parties) with shareholders (owners of shares).
- Claiming that the stakeholder model ignores profit: it pursues profit alongside other aims.
- Saying that ethics always lowers profit; it can raise profit through reputation and loyalty.
- Describing CSR in general terms with no cost or benefit for the business in the question.

## Exam technique

For **explain** and **analyse** questions, apply the concept to the business's own context, for instance "a role culture, with its strict procedures, is likely to be slow to respond to new competitors". In **discuss** and **assess** questions about conflict or ethics, weigh the short-term gain against the long-term risk, name the stakeholder groups affected and give a judgement that depends on the business's circumstances, such as how much its customers care about ethical sourcing or how exposed its brand is.
