This guide covers subtopic **29.2, Characteristic organic reactions**, from Topic 29, An introduction to A Level
organic chemistry, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level
content. The single learning outcome is short, but the two terms it names are the labels for almost every
A Level organic mechanism in Topics 30 to 34, so using them precisely earns marks across the whole paper. The
overview page [A Level Organic Chemistry: Naming, Mechanisms and Aromatic Shape](/resources/a-organic-chemistry-naming-mechanisms-and-aromatic-shape/)
introduces both terms; this page shows you how to **classify** any reaction you are given and how to **outline**
each mechanism for examples not covered there. Afterwards, use the
[characteristic reactions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-characteristic-organic-reactions-revision-notes/)
and the [characteristic reactions practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-characteristic-organic-reactions-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

Candidates should be able to **understand and use** the following terminology associated with types of
organic mechanisms:

- (a) **electrophilic substitution**
- (b) **addition–elimination**

"Understand and use" means you must be able to define each term, recognise when a reaction is of that type,
and apply the term correctly when describing a mechanism.

## Reading a mechanism name

Every mechanism name has two parts. Get both right and the label follows from the reaction.

**Part 1: the attacking species.**

- **Electrophile**: an electron-pair **acceptor**, attracted to regions of high electron density. Often a
  positive ion (NO₂⁺, CH₃CO⁺) or the positive end of a polarised molecule.
- **Nucleophile**: an electron-pair **donor**, with a lone pair, attracted to a δ+ atom. Examples: H₂O, OH⁻,
  NH₃, ROH, RNH₂.
- **Radical**: a species with an unpaired electron.

**Part 2: what happens overall.**

- **Substitution**: one atom or group is **replaced** by another.
- **Addition**: two species combine to form **one** product; a π bond is used up.
- **Elimination**: a small molecule or ion is **removed**, often forming a π bond.

## Electrophilic substitution

**Definition:** a reaction in which an **electrophile** attacks an electron-rich centre and **replaces** an atom
or group (usually a hydrogen atom on a benzene ring).

Why arenes react this way:

- the delocalised π system is **electron-rich**, so it attracts electrophiles;
- substitution **restores** the delocalised system after it is briefly disrupted, while addition would destroy
  it. The ring keeps its aromatic stability.

The pattern always has three stages:

1. **generate the electrophile** (often needs a catalyst or a strong acid);
2. the π system **attacks the electrophile**, forming a positively charged intermediate in which delocalisation
   is partly disrupted;
3. the intermediate **loses H⁺**, restoring the delocalised ring.

### Worked example 1: outlining Friedel–Crafts acylation

*Outline the mechanism for the reaction of benzene with CH₃COCl in the presence of AlCl₃ and heat.*

**Generating the electrophile:**

    CH₃COCl + AlCl₃ → CH₃CO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻

**Step 1:** a curly arrow from the delocalised ring to the carbon of CH₃CO⁺. The intermediate has a positive
charge spread over five ring carbons; the sixth carbon now carries both H and COCH₃.

**Step 2:** a curly arrow from the C–H bond back into the ring. H⁺ is lost and the ring is restored, giving
phenylethanone, C₆H₅COCH₃.

**Catalyst regenerated:**

    H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → HCl + AlCl₃

**Name of mechanism:** electrophilic substitution. The full treatment of nitration and bromination is in
[Arenes and Halogenoarenes](/resources/a-arenes-and-halogenoarenes/).

## Addition–elimination

**Definition:** a two-step mechanism in which a **nucleophile adds** to a carbonyl carbon, forming an
intermediate, and then a small molecule or ion is **eliminated**, re-forming the C=O bond. The overall
change replaces one group on the carbonyl carbon by another.

Why acyl chlorides react this way:

- the carbonyl carbon is bonded to **two electronegative atoms**, O and Cl, so it is strongly **δ+** and
  attracts nucleophiles;
- the C=O π bond can accept the electron pair temporarily, so the nucleophile **adds** first;
- **Cl⁻ is a good leaving group**, so when the C=O re-forms, Cl⁻ is **eliminated**; loss of H⁺ from the
  nucleophile completes the reaction, giving HCl overall.

### Worked example 2: outlining the reaction with ammonia

*Outline the mechanism for the reaction of ethanoyl chloride with ammonia.*

**Step 1 (addition):** a curly arrow from the lone pair on N of NH₃ to the δ+ carbonyl carbon; at the same time
a curly arrow from the C=O π bond to the O atom. The intermediate has O⁻ and NH₃⁺ on the same carbon, with Cl
still attached.

**Step 2 (elimination):** a curly arrow from the lone pair on O⁻ back to re-form C=O; a curly arrow from the
C–Cl bond to Cl, which leaves as Cl⁻. H⁺ is then lost from the NH₃⁺ group.

**Products:** ethanamide and HCl:

    CH₃COCl + NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + HCl

With excess ammonia, the HCl reacts further to give ammonium chloride. The same two steps, with H₂O, ROH,
C₆H₅OH or RNH₂ as the nucleophile, give a carboxylic acid, an ester, a phenyl ester or an N-substituted amide.
See [Carboxylic Acids and Acyl Chlorides](/resources/a-carboxylic-acids-and-acyl-chlorides/).

## How to classify a reaction you are given

Work through three questions:

1. **What is being attacked?** A benzene ring, a C=C, a C=O bonded to a leaving group, a C=O without a leaving
   group, or a saturated carbon bonded to a halogen?
2. **What attacks it?** An electrophile, a nucleophile or a radical?
3. **What is the overall change?** Is something replaced, added or removed?

| What is attacked | Attacked by | Overall | Mechanism name |
|---|---|---|---|
| benzene ring | electrophile | H replaced | **electrophilic substitution** |
| C=O with a leaving group (–COCl) | nucleophile | Cl replaced, via an intermediate | **addition–elimination** |
| C=C | electrophile | atoms added across | electrophilic addition (AS) |
| C=O without a leaving group (aldehyde, ketone + HCN) | nucleophile | atoms added across | nucleophilic addition (AS) |
| sp³ carbon bonded to halogen | nucleophile | halogen replaced, in one or two steps | nucleophilic substitution (AS) |
| alkane C–H, with UV | radical | H replaced | free-radical substitution (AS) |

### Worked example 3: classifying reactions

| Reaction | Classification | Key reason |
|---|---|---|
| phenol + Br₂(aq) → 2,4,6-tribromophenol + 3HBr | electrophilic substitution | ring H atoms replaced by Br; Br₂ polarised by the electron-rich ring |
| CH₃COCl + CH₃OH → CH₃COOCH₃ + HCl | addition–elimination | alcohol O adds to C=O, then Cl⁻ and H⁺ are lost |
| benzenediazonium ion + phenoxide ion → azo compound | electrophilic substitution | the diazonium ion is the electrophile; it replaces H at position 4 |
| CH₃CHO + HCN → CH₃CH(OH)CN | nucleophilic addition | no leaving group, so nothing is eliminated |
| CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻ | nucleophilic substitution | sp³ carbon; no C=O, so no addition step |

The ethanoyl chloride and bromoethane reactions both swap a halogen for an oxygen group. They are still
different mechanisms: only the acyl chloride has a C=O that can accept the nucleophile first.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling acyl chloride reactions "nucleophilic substitution".** The overall change is a replacement, but the
  mechanism is addition–elimination. Use the syllabus term.
- **Calling arene reactions "electrophilic addition".** The ring is restored; the product has the same ring.
- **Mixing up electrophile and nucleophile** in a definition: the electrophile *accepts* an electron pair.
- **Omitting the H⁺ loss** in electrophilic substitution, or the re-formation of C=O in addition–elimination.
- **Curly arrows from atoms or positive charges.** Arrows start at a bond or a lone pair.
- **Leaving out the electrophile-generating equation** when asked for a full mechanism.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **State** or **name the type of reaction / mechanism**: give both words, e.g. "electrophilic substitution",
  "addition–elimination".
- **Define**: one sentence naming the attacking species and the overall change.
- **Describe the mechanism** or **outline**: curly arrows, intermediate, charges, lone pairs and all products.
- **Explain** why a compound reacts by a given mechanism: link the structure (electron-rich ring; strongly δ+
  carbonyl carbon with a good leaving group) to the attacking species.
