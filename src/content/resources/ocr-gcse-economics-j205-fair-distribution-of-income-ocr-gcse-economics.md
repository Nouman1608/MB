| Statement | What you must do |
|---|---|
| Explain what is meant by the distribution of income, including different types of income and the difference between income and wealth | Describe how income is shared, name its sources, separate flows from stocks |

| Evaluate the causes of differences in the distribution of income and wealth and the consequences for an economy | Explain why gaps arise, judge which causes matter most, weigh the effects |

## Distribution, and why "fair" is a judgement

The **distribution of income** describes how a country's total income is shared between households or individuals. If every household received the same, the distribution would be perfectly equal. No real economy is like that.

"Fair" is not the same as "equal". Economists and voters disagree about what fairness means:

- **Fair as more equal outcomes**: large gaps are unfair in themselves, especially if some households cannot afford essentials.
- **Fair as equal opportunity**: differences are acceptable if everyone has a real chance to gain skills and well-paid work.
- **Fair as reward for contribution**: people who work longer, train harder or take risks deserve more.

Because these views conflict, a statement such as "the distribution is unfair" is a **value judgement**. In the exam, show you know this by saying what kind of fairness you mean.

## Types of income, linked to their source

| Income | Source | Earned by working? |
|---|---|---|
| Wages and salaries | Selling labour | Yes |
| Profit | Running a business (enterprise) | Yes, with risk |
| Rent | Owning land or property | No |
| Interest | Owning savings or lending money | No |
| Dividends | Owning shares in companies | No |
| Benefits and state pensions | Transfers from the government | No |

Three of these, rent, interest and dividends, come **from owning wealth**. That link is the key to understanding why inequality in wealth produces inequality in income.

## Worked example 1: a household's income

The Mensah household receives, in one year:

- Joel's salary: £28,000
- Dividends on Joel's shares: £1,200
- Rent from a flat they let: £6,600
- Ama's part-time wages: £11,500
- Government benefits: £1,300

Total gross income = 28,000 + 1,200 + 6,600 + 11,500 + 1,300 = **£48,600**.

Income from work = 28,000 + 11,500 = £39,500, which is 81% of the total. Income from owning wealth (dividends and rent) = £7,800, or 16%.

## Worked example 2: net wealth, including negative wealth

Net wealth = value of assets − value of liabilities (debts)

| | Household A | Household B |
|---|---|---|
| House | £320,000 | Rents, owns no property |
| Shares | £60,000 | None |
| Savings | £20,000 | £2,500 |
| Pension fund | £90,000 | None |
| Mortgage | −£140,000 | None |
| Other debts | Car loan −£8,000 | Credit card −£3,200 |
| **Net wealth** | **£342,000** | **−£700** |

Household B has **negative net wealth**: it owes more than it owns. Many young adults and low-income renters are in this position.

## How wealth feeds income

Now see what each household's wealth earns in a year:

- Household A: dividends at 3% on £60,000 = £1,800, plus interest at 4% on £20,000 = £800. That is **£2,600** a year before anyone works.
- Household B: pays 20% interest on its £3,200 credit card debt = **£640** a year.

Household A's wealth produces income that can be saved and turned into more wealth; Household B's debt drains income. Over many years the gap widens even if both earn the same wages. This is why wealth is usually distributed **more unequally** than income, and why inheritance matters.

## Reading distribution data

Data is often given for tenths or fifths of households. In an invented Country D, households are split into ten equal groups.

| Year | Share of income: richest tenth | Share of income: poorest tenth | Ratio (per household) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 24% | 3.0% | 8 times |
| Year 10 | 30% | 2.5% | 12 times |

Because each tenth has the same number of households, the ratio of shares equals the ratio of income per household: 30 ÷ 2.5 = **12**. The gap has widened. To analyse it, say how much the ratio has changed, then suggest a cause from the list below.

## Causes of differences: going deeper

- **Skills and qualifications.** Scarce skills command higher wages. If technology raises demand for skilled workers and reduces it for routine work, the gap between them grows.
- **Wealth and inheritance.** As shown above, assets produce income, and they are passed on.
- **Age.** People usually earn and save more in middle age; young adults and many retired people have lower incomes. Some differences at any moment simply reflect where people are in their lives.
- **Region.** Wages and house prices differ between regions, so home-owners in high-price areas gain more wealth.
- **Hours and employment.** Part-time work, insecure contracts and unemployment cut income.
- **Discrimination.** Lower pay or fewer opportunities for some groups with the same skills.
- **Government policy.** Progressive taxes and benefits narrow gaps; cuts to them can widen gaps.

**Evaluating causes.** Ask which cause the case study points to, and whether it reflects choices and life stage (less worrying) or barriers to opportunity (more worrying).

## Consequences for an economy

| Consequence | Chain | Counter-argument |
|---|---|---|
| Lower spending | Poorer households spend almost all extra income; richer ones save more, so concentrating income can weaken demand | Savings can fund investment |
| Wasted potential | Low-income children may not afford education or training, so skills and future growth suffer | Free state education reduces this |
| Government costs | More people need benefits; less is raised in income tax | Taxes on high incomes can fund support |
| Incentives | Some inequality rewards effort, training and enterprise, which can raise growth | Very large gaps may not reflect effort |
| Social cohesion | Large visible gaps may reduce trust and create tension | Hard to measure |

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether differences in wealth are a more important cause of income inequality than differences in skills.*

"Wealth produces income without work: rent, interest and dividends go to those who own property, savings and shares. **[mechanism]** Because wealth can be inherited and its income reinvested, the gap grows over generations. **[development]** However, for most households, wages are the largest source of income, so differences in skills and qualifications explain most of the gap between, for example, a cleaner and a doctor. **[counter-argument]** Overall, skills matter more for differences in income among working households, but wealth matters more at the very top and over long periods, so the answer depends on which part of the distribution is being considered." **[judgement with a condition]**

## Common mistakes

- Saying a high income means high wealth, or the reverse.
- Forgetting debts when calculating net wealth, or treating a mortgage as an asset.
- Treating "fair" as meaning "equal".
- Listing causes without explaining how each changes income.
- Ignoring the benefits of some inequality when evaluating consequences.

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: define the term and add an example from the case study.
- **Calculate**: set out assets and liabilities in two lists; label income by source.
- **Analyse** distribution data: compare shares, calculate a ratio, state whether gaps are widening.
- **Evaluate**: weigh at least two causes or consequences, then judge which matters most in the context and why.
