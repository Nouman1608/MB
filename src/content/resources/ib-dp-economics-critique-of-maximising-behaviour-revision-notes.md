## Definitions to know cold

| Term | Short definition |
|---|---|
| Rational consumer | Compares costs and benefits, chooses consistently, maximises utility |
| Utility | Satisfaction gained from consuming |
| Perfect information | Full knowledge of prices, quality and outcomes |
| Rule of thumb | A simple shortcut for deciding quickly |
| Anchoring | A first number or reference point sways later judgements |
| Framing | The wording or presentation changes the choice made |
| Availability | Judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind |
| Bounded rationality | Limited information, time and thinking power restrict decisions |
| Bounded self-control | Knowing the better long-run choice but not acting on it |
| Bounded selfishness | Caring about fairness and others, not only own gain |
| Imperfect information | Missing, wrong or uneven knowledge about a choice |
| Choice architecture | How options are arranged and described |
| Nudge | A change in presentation that steers choice without banning or repricing |
| Satisficing | Aiming for a satisfactory outcome, not the best possible |

## Biases: what each one breaks

- **Rule of thumb** breaks full comparison of options. Quick and often fine.
- **Anchoring and framing** break consistency: identical facts give different choices.
- **Availability** breaks accurate probability judgement: vivid events feel likely.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Bounded rationality vs bounded self-control | The first is about limits on working out the best choice; the second is about failing to follow a choice already known to be best |
| Bounded selfishness vs bounded self-control | The first is about caring for others; the second is about patience with oneself |
| Anchoring vs framing | Anchoring uses a reference number; framing uses the wording of the same facts |
| Imperfect information vs bounded rationality | The first is missing facts; the second is limited ability to use the facts available |
| Nudge vs ban or legal requirement | A nudge leaves every option open; a ban or legal requirement removes the option to do otherwise |
| Default vs restricted choice | A default keeps all options but starts you on one; restricted choice cuts the list |

## Method in steps: explain how a behaviour departs from rational choice

1. Name the assumption of rational choice involved (rationality, utility maximisation or perfect information).
2. Name the specific behaviour (for example availability).
3. Link the behaviour to the context given in the question.
4. State the outcome: the person chooses something other than the utility-maximising option.
5. Add a judgement if the command term needs it.

## Worked reminders

**Anchoring (invented).** "Was 90, now 54" feels like a cut of (90 - 54) / 90 = 40%. If the usual price was 60, the real saving is 6 / 60 = 10%.

**Availability (invented).** Perceived risk 3% a year against a true 1 in 10,000, loss 40,000. Perceived expected loss = 0.03 x 40,000 = 1,200. True expected loss = 0.0001 x 40,000 = 4. Cover costing 90 is over-bought.

**Bounded self-control (invented).** Choosing 50 today over 60 in 4 weeks, yet 60 in 56 weeks over 50 in 52 weeks, is a reversal. The rational model predicts one pattern in both pairs.

**Default (invented).** 10,000 members: opt-in 20% = 2,000, opt-out 75% = 7,500, so the default adds 5,500 participants.

## Nudge theory and choice architecture

- A good nudge is easy and cheap to avoid.
- Three forms: default (opt-out), restricted (fewer options) and mandated (you must make an active choice before going on).
- The guide also lists consumer nudges in sub-topic 2.7 (HL only), where they are weighed against other government tools.

**Strengths:** low cost, keeps freedom, targets bias. **Limitations:** may fade, hard to measure, ethical doubts about influence, may not change strong preferences. Evaluate by comparing with a tax, a ban or an information campaign.

## Business objectives: what to say about each

| Objective | Meaning | Why a firm might choose it |
|---|---|---|
| Profit maximisation | Output where MR = MC | Owner pressure, survival |
| CSR | Accepting costs for social or environmental aims | Reputation, values, regulation risk |
| Market share | Priority on size of sales relative to rivals | Build a brand, deter entry, scale economies |
| Satisficing | Meet a target profit, not the maximum | Managers with limited information and effort |
| Growth | Expand sales or revenue | Managers' status, long-run strength |

**Quick numbers (invented).** For P = 100 - 2Q and TC = 200 + 20Q: profit is maximised at Q = 20 (profit 600); revenue peaks at Q = 25 (profit 550); a 400 profit target is met for Q between 10 and 30.

**Evaluating the profit-maximisation assumption:** it predicts well in tough competition, less well where managers have discretion, information is poor or other goals matter.

## Judging the critique itself

For AO3 you need a view on how far behavioural economics weakens the rational model.

- **Supports the critique:** biases and bounded behaviour are systematic, so they predict real choices that the model misses, such as under-saving or over-insuring.
- **Defends the model:** it is a simplification that still predicts average behaviour in many markets, especially where choices are repeated and feedback is quick, so errors get corrected.
- **Practical limits:** behavioural findings come from particular settings, so a result in one group may not carry over to another. There is no single behavioural theory that replaces the rational model.
- **Policy link:** if biases cause harm, a nudge may improve outcomes at low cost, but it relies on a judgement about what is better for the chooser.

End with a short conclusion that takes a side and says when it would change.

## Quick self-test

1. State the three assumptions of the rational consumer model.
2. Name the three biases the guide lists.
3. Distinguish anchoring from framing.
4. A shop says "Was 200, now 140; usually sold at 150". Calculate the headline discount and the real saving as percentages.
5. Which bound explains a person who plans to save but does not?
6. A responder in a pot-splitting game refuses 8 out of 50 and gets nothing. Which behaviour is this?
7. State the three forms of choice architecture.
8. 5,000 customers; 10% join with opt-in and 60% with opt-out. How many extra join under the default?
9. Why is a ban not a nudge?
10. Name the four alternative business objectives the guide lists.
11. A firm needs 300 profit and has options giving 280, 310 and 420. Which would a satisficer accept first?
12. Give one limitation of nudges.

### Answers

1. Rationality, utility maximisation, perfect information.
2. Rule of thumb, anchoring and framing, availability.
3. Anchoring: an initial number pulls judgement. Framing: wording of identical facts changes the choice.
4. Headline (200 - 140) / 200 = **30%**. Real saving (150 - 140) / 150 = **6.67%** (6.7%).
5. Bounded self-control.
6. Bounded selfishness (concern for fairness, at a personal cost).
7. Default, restricted, mandated.
8. 0.60 x 5,000 - 0.10 x 5,000 = 3,000 - 500 = **2,500**.
9. It removes the option to choose otherwise; a nudge leaves all options open.
10. CSR, market share, satisficing, growth.
11. The 310 option (the first that meets the target).
12. Effects may fade (or: ethical concerns, hard to measure).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a bias without naming which assumption of rational choice it contradicts.
- Writing "people are irrational" with no link to the case in the question.
- Confusing the three "bounded" ideas.
- Calling a ban or a tax a nudge.
- Showing only one side when the command term is discuss or evaluate; advice for AO3 questions is to weigh strengths against limitations and then judge.
- Treating satisficing as laziness rather than a limited-information, target-based aim.
- Giving real-world claims you cannot support; use the example given or a clearly hypothetical one.
- Forgetting to show working in percentage and expected-value calculations.

## Frequently asked questions

**What assumptions does the rational consumer model make?**
Standard theory assumes rationality, meaning people compare benefits and costs and choose consistently; utility maximisation, meaning they pick the option giving the most satisfaction; and perfect information about prices, qualities and consequences.

**What biases are in the IB DP Economics guide?**
The guide lists rules of thumb, anchoring and framing, and availability. Each breaks part of the rational model, such as full comparison of options, consistency of choices or accurate judgement of probability.

**What are the three forms of choice architecture?**
Default choice is the option that applies unless you act, restricted choice offers only some options, and mandated choice requires an active choice with no pre-set option.

**Is satisficing the same as laziness?**
No. Satisficing is a target-based aim: a firm sets a level of profit it finds acceptable rather than seeking the maximum, often because information is limited or owners and managers want different things.
