Revise first with the ['traditional' media study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-traditional-media/) and the ['traditional' media revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-traditional-media-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by agenda-setting. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two forms of communication technology listed in the specification for 'traditional' media. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by concentration of media ownership. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two news values that make events more likely to be reported. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which 'traditional' media may represent older people. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, from a fictional content analysis of 400 speaking roles in prime-time drama on a fictional television channel.

| Group (fictional) | Speaking roles |
|---|---|
| Women, all ages | 152 |
| Women aged over 50 | 24 |
| Men, all ages | 248 |
| Men aged over 50 | 72 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of women's roles played by women aged over 50, and the percentage of men's roles played by men aged over 50. Give answers to the nearest whole number. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain how the data could support Tuchman's idea of symbolic annihilation. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> The fictional media company Corvane owns twelve newspapers, a national television channel, a film studio and a chain of cinemas. Last year one of its newspapers published an investigation into poor safety at a factory owned by Corvane's chairman. The newspaper's editor was replaced a month later. Corvane says the editor left for "budget reasons" and that its journalists are free to report what they choose.

**(a)** Identify two forms of media integration shown in the item. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse how Marxist and pluralist sociologists would interpret the relationship between ownership and content at Corvane. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which the telegraph changed social life. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three criticisms of the hypodermic syringe model. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how 'traditional' media have represented ethnic minority groups. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the cultural effects approach to the relationship between media and audiences. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent have 'traditional' media been used to spread propaganda and disinformation? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** The media's power to influence which issues audiences think are important [1] through what they choose to cover most, so they shape what people think about rather than exactly what they think (McCombs and Shaw) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: post [1]; telegraph [1]; telephone. **[2]**

**3.** A situation in which a small number of media corporations [1] own a large share of newspapers, broadcasters or film companies [1], which Marxists argue narrows the range of views available (Bagdikian) [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Negativity: bad news such as disasters or conflict is more likely to be reported [1], because it is dramatic and attracts audiences [1]. Reference to elite people: stories involving the powerful or famous are favoured [1], so the actions of leaders and celebrities receive more coverage than those of ordinary people (Galtung and Ruge) [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Older people may be shown as frail, confused or dependent [1], reinforcing stereotypes of old age as decline [1]. Older people, especially older women, may be largely invisible in prime-time roles [1], which suggests that their lives are less valued (symbolic annihilation) [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Women: 24 / 152 x 100 = 16% [1]. Men: 72 / 248 x 100 = 29% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Women have only 152 of 400 speaking roles, fewer than men's 248 [1], showing women are under-represented overall [1]. Only 16% of women's roles go to women over 50, against 29% of men's roles to men over 50 [1], so older women are almost absent, supporting Tuchman's idea that media "annihilate" groups by leaving them out [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: horizontal integration (twelve newspapers) [1]; cross-media ownership (newspapers, television and film) [1]; vertical integration (film studio and cinemas). **[2]**
**(b)** Marxists would see the editor's removal after the investigation as owners using allocative control [1] to protect the chairman's business interests, so content serves the owning class [1]. The range of outlets owned by one company shows concentration that narrows the views audiences receive [1]. Pluralists would accept Corvane's claim that journalists choose content [1], arguing that the investigation was published at all, which shows operational independence [1], and that one editor's departure may reflect budget decisions rather than censorship, so the item alone does not prove control of content [1]. **[6]**

**8.** The telegraph carried messages almost instantly over long distances [1], separating communication from transport, as Carey argued [1]. This enabled news agencies and national markets to share information fast [1], helping create standard time and nationwide news on the same day [1]. **[4]**

**9.** It treats audiences as passive [1], ignoring the different ways people interpret messages (Hall's encoding/decoding) [1]. Evidence such as Bandura's Bobo doll study comes from artificial settings [1], so it may not show real-world effects [1]. It ignores social context and opinion leaders [1], which the two step flow model shows filter media influence [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Minorities are often linked to crime, conflict or threat [1].
- Hall and colleagues: the 1970s "mugging" panic racialised street crime [1].
- Van Dijk: press reporting reproduced racist assumptions [1].
- Minorities are under-represented in positive or powerful roles [1].
- Cohen: moral panics create folk devils, often from minority groups [1].
- Application: own-society example of coverage of a minority, migrant or religious group [1].
- Counter: representation has diversified with more minority journalists and characters [1].
- Analysis: patterns reflect news values and who controls newsrooms [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Cultural effects: repeated messages over time shape what audiences see as normal [1].
- Gerbner's cultivation theory: heavy viewers overestimate crime [1].
- The "mean world syndrome" shows long-term effects on beliefs [1].
- Neo-Marxists link this to hegemony: media make ruling ideas seem common sense [1].
- Application: own-society example of repeated stereotypes in TV or film [1].
- Strength: explains slow, cumulative influence that experiments miss [1].
- Hall's encoding/decoding allows dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings [1].
- Morley's *Nationwide* study found readings varied by class [1].
- Effects over long periods are hard to measure and separate from other influences [1].
- Uses and gratifications sees audiences as choosing media for their needs [1].
- Pluralists argue diverse outlets reduce any single cultural effect [1].
- Judgement: persuasive for long-term framing, but audiences are more active than it assumes [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Propaganda is organised persuasion, often by states [1].
- Nazi Germany used the "people's receiver" radio to reach most homes [1].
- RTLM radio in Rwanda (1994) broadcast incitement during the genocide [1].
- Wartime posters and newsreels mobilised populations [1].
- Own-society application: state-controlled broadcasting or wartime media [1].
- Herman and Chomsky: filters produce consent in market media without direct censorship [1].
- Gatekeeping and news values shape what is presented as true [1].
- Misinformation spreads through errors and unchecked rumours; corrections reach fewer people [1].
- Counter: public service broadcasting aims to inform, educate and entertain [1].
- Professional journalism and fact-checking limit disinformation [1].
- Pluralists: competition exposes false claims [1].
- Investigative journalism has exposed state and corporate wrongdoing [1].
- Audiences can take oppositional readings (Hall) [1].
- Own-society application: a case where media exposed or corrected false claims [1].
- Analysis: extent depends on whether media are state-controlled, commercial or public service [1].
- Judgement: traditional media have been powerful propaganda tools, especially under authoritarian control, but are not inherently so [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate state propaganda, commercial bias and accidental misinformation; the judgement depends on which you mean.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing agenda-setting with gatekeeping.
- Treating a single item as proof of owner control.
- Describing audience models without evidence.
- Using representation claims with no change over time.
- Leaving out own-society examples where the question asks for them.
