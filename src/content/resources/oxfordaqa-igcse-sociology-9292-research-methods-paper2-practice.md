## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** ways in which sociologists measure social class. **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by a **victim survey**? **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** ethical issues that arise when researching young children. **[2]**

**4.** What is meant by **content analysis**? **[2]**

**5.** Identify **one** strength and **one** limitation of using retrospective questions to study social mobility. **[2]**

**6.** What is meant by **self-identification** when recording a person's ethnicity? **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain **two** problems with using a person's occupation to measure their social class. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why a researcher studying intersectionality might need a large stratified random sample. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why self-report studies may be more useful than police statistics for studying illegal drug use among young people. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** difficulties sociologists face when researching wealthy and powerful groups. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a sociologist could use content analysis to study gender socialisation in children's television programmes. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of official statistics for studying gender inequality in employment. You should refer to examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[12]**

**13.** Evaluate the usefulness of ethnography for studying how institutions such as prisons or hospitals control the people inside them. **[12]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: occupational scales (such as NS-SEC in the United Kingdom); income; wealth; subjective class (asking people which class they belong to); educational level as an indicator.

**2. [2]** A survey asking a sample of people which crimes they have experienced as victims (1), usually over a set period such as the past year, whether or not they reported them to the police (1).

**3. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: informed consent from both the child and a parent or guardian; children may not fully understand what the research involves; power imbalance, so children give answers they think adults want; protecting wellbeing and responding to any disclosure of harm; confidentiality and anonymity.

**4. [2]** A method of analysing media or documents (1) by systematically counting or interpreting how often and how particular groups, themes or behaviours appear (1).

**5. [2]** Strength, 1 mark: cheap and quick, since one survey can collect information about two generations. Limitation, 1 mark: relies on memory, so respondents may recall a parent's job inaccurately or describe it in a better light.

**6. [2]** Recording a person's ethnicity according to how they define it themselves (1), usually by choosing from a list of categories or describing it in their own words, rather than the researcher deciding (1).

### Section B

**7. [4]** Two problems, 2 marks each (1 for the problem, 1 for explanation):
- **People without a current job** [1]: unemployed, retired, student and unpaid carers cannot easily be placed on a scale based on occupation [1].
- **Ignores wealth** [1]: the very rich may live on income from property or shares, so occupation misses the ownership that Marxists see as the basis of class [1].
- **Household classification** [1]: when partners have different occupations, it is unclear which one defines the household's class [1].
- **Same job title, different conditions** [1]: two people with the same occupation may have very different pay and security, so the scale may group unequal people together [1].

**8. [4]** Intersectionality (Crenshaw) means class, gender, ethnicity and other differences combine [1]. To analyse combined groups, such as working-class women from a particular minority, the sample must contain enough people in each combination [1]. Stratifying the sampling frame by the relevant characteristics ensures each group is represented in proportion [1], and a large sample reduces the chance that small subgroups are too few to generalise from reliably [1].

**9. [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each:
- **No victim to report it** [1]: drug use is a "victimless" crime, so police statistics record only the users who are caught, often by targeted stop and search [1].
- **Shows who offends, not who is caught** [1]: anonymous self-report questionnaires can reveal how common drug use is across classes and ethnic groups, testing whether police statistics reflect policing patterns rather than real behaviour [1].
- **Anonymity encourages honesty** [1]: young people may admit offences confidentially that they would never admit to the police [1].

**10. [4]** Two difficulties, 2 marks each:
- **Access** [1]: elites can refuse to take part and use gatekeepers such as assistants or lawyers to block researchers [1].
- **Controlled self-presentation** [1]: the powerful are used to managing their image, so interviews may give a polished, less valid account [1].
- **Legal and financial power** [1]: organisations can threaten legal action over critical findings, which may discourage research [1].
- **Researcher status** [1]: a student or junior researcher may struggle to be taken seriously by senior executives or officials [1].

**11. [4]** Select a **sample** of programmes, such as the most-watched children's programmes over a set period [1]. Draw up **categories** to count, for example how often male and female characters appear, their jobs, who leads and who cares for others [1]. Record the frequency of each category in a coding sheet, giving **quantitative, reliable** data that another researcher could repeat [1]. Add **qualitative** analysis of how characters are portrayed, such as language used to praise girls and boys, and note that this shows content, not how children interpret it [1].

**12. [12]** Mark by levels:

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge of official statistics; strengths and limitations applied closely to gender inequality in employment; relevant example from a named society; clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application; conclusion reached |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; general evaluation, little application |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic points, mainly description |

Indicative content:
- **Strengths**: large-scale, national data on employment rates, pay and occupations by sex; allows comparison over time and between societies; cheap and readily available; fits a positivist approach to measuring patterns such as the glass ceiling. Example: in the United Kingdom, larger employers have published gender pay gap figures since 2017.
- **Limitations**: show the size of a gap, not its causes, such as discrimination, caring responsibilities or occupational segregation; definitions and categories set by governments may not match the sociologist's concepts; may miss informal and unpaid work, which is often done by women and is significant in many societies; organisations may present figures favourably.
- **Alternatives**: interviews or case studies to explore women's experiences of promotion; triangulation.
- **Judgement**: very useful for measuring the extent of inequality, less useful for explaining it.

**13. [12]** Levels as Question 12 (applied to institutions and social control).
Indicative content:
- **Strengths**: ethnography observes daily routines in the natural setting, revealing hidden rules and informal control that official accounts omit; Goffman's *Asylums* (1961), based on fieldwork in a psychiatric hospital, showed how a total institution reshapes identity; gives valid, qualitative data on meanings; suits an interpretivist approach.
- **Limitations**: **access** to prisons and hospitals is controlled by gatekeepers; **ethics**: residents may be vulnerable and unable to give free consent; covert roles involve deception; **observer effect**: staff and residents may behave differently when watched (Hawthorne effect); **reliability** and **generalisability** are low, since one institution may be untypical; time-consuming and possibly risky for the researcher.
- **Alternatives**: interviews with former residents; official documents; triangulation.
- **Judgement**: highly useful for understanding how control works from the inside, but findings need checking against other institutions and methods.
