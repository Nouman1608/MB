Section 10.1 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has seven learning outcomes. You must be able to describe chemical tests for water using anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride and anhydrous copper(II) sulfate; describe how to test the purity of water "using melting point and boiling point"; explain why distilled water rather than tap water is used in practical chemistry; state what substances natural water may contain, which are beneficial and which are potentially harmful; and describe how the domestic water supply is treated. 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so every outcome is examined for every candidate.

The broader [Chemistry of the Environment guide](/resources/chemistry-of-the-environment/) summarises these facts in a few tables. This guide goes further into the reasoning that structured questions reward: telling a test for water apart from a test for purity, using data to judge whether a sample is pure, and linking each treatment stage to the impurity it removes.

## Two different questions: "is water present?" and "is it pure?"

Students often lose marks by mixing these up. They need different tests.

| Question being asked | Test | What a positive result shows |
|---|---|---|
| Is water present in this liquid? | Add anhydrous copper(II) sulfate or anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride | Water is present (it may be mixed with other substances) |
| Is this water pure? | Measure the melting point and boiling point | Pure water melts at exactly 0 °C and boils at exactly 100 °C at atmospheric pressure |

### Chemical tests for water

| Reagent | Colour before | Colour with water |
|---|---|---|
| Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate | white | blue |
| Anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride (often on test paper) | blue | pink |

Both tests work because the anhydrous solid takes up water to form the hydrated compound. These are the same reversible reactions described in section 6.3, where heating a hydrated compound drives the water off again:

```
CuSO4(s) + 5H2O(l) ⇌ CuSO4·5H2O(s)
 white                   blue
```

A positive result only proves that water is **present**. Salt water, fruit juice and dilute acid all turn anhydrous copper(II) sulfate blue, because each contains water. A chemical test for water can never tell you that the water is pure.

### Testing purity with melting and boiling points

A pure substance has a fixed, sharp melting point and boiling point. Dissolved impurities change both values:

- an impurity **lowers** the melting (freezing) point below 0 °C
- an impurity **raises** the boiling point above 100 °C
- an impure sample often melts or boils over a **range** of temperatures rather than at one value

So the method is: heat the sample and measure the temperature at which it boils, or cool it and measure the temperature at which it freezes. Compare the readings with 0 °C and 100 °C.

**Worked example 1.** Four samples of water were tested at atmospheric pressure.

| Sample | Freezing point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 100 |
| B | −2 | 101 |
| C | 0 | 100 to 103 (range) |
| D | −1 | 100.5 |

State which sample is pure water and explain your choice.

*Answer.* Sample A [1]. It freezes at exactly 0 °C and boils at exactly 100 °C, the fixed values for pure water [1]. B and D have lowered freezing points and raised boiling points, so they contain dissolved substances; C boils over a range, which also shows an impurity [1].

The figures in this table are invented for practice, but the pattern is the one examiners test: any reading away from 0 °C or 100 °C, or a range instead of a single value, means the sample is impure.

## Why practical chemistry uses distilled water

Tap water has been treated to make it safe to drink, not to make it pure. It still contains dissolved substances, such as metal compounds picked up from rocks and soil and the chlorine compounds added during treatment. Distillation boils water away from these and condenses it, so distilled water contains far fewer chemical impurities.

The syllabus wants the reason "explained", so link the impurity to a problem in an experiment. For example:

- in a test for chloride ions, any chloride ions already present in tap water could give a white precipitate with acidified silver nitrate even when the substance being tested contains none
- when a solution of exact concentration is made up for a titration, dissolved substances in tap water could react with the solute
- when a salt is crystallised, dissolved substances in tap water would end up as impurities in the crystals

An answer that just says "tap water is dirty" does not score. Say that tap water contains **dissolved chemical impurities** that could **react or interfere** with the substances being tested.

## What natural water contains

Water from rivers, lakes and underground sources is never pure. The syllabus lists seven kinds of substance it may contain, and asks you to sort them into beneficial and potentially harmful.

| Substance | Where it comes from | Beneficial or harmful? |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved oxygen | Air dissolving at the surface; aquatic plants | Beneficial: needed by aquatic life |
| Metal compounds | Rocks and soil the water flows over | Some provide essential minerals; some are toxic |
| Plastics | Litter and waste | Harmful: some plastics harm aquatic life |
| Sewage | Human and animal waste | Harmful: contains harmful microbes that cause disease |
| Harmful microbes | Sewage and animal waste | Harmful: cause disease |
| Nitrates | Fertilisers washed off farmland | Harmful in excess: lead to deoxygenation of water |
| Phosphates | Fertilisers and detergents | Harmful in excess: lead to deoxygenation of water |

Notice that metal compounds appear on both sides. If a question asks for one beneficial and one harmful substance, "metal compounds" can only score for one of them, so choose a different substance for the other.

For nitrates and phosphates, the syllabus requires only the outcome: they "lead to deoxygenation of water and damage to aquatic life". The syllabus states that details of the eutrophication process are not required, so a short, accurate statement is all you need.

## Treating the domestic water supply

Water companies take water from a natural source and treat it in stages. Each stage removes a different kind of substance, and examiners award marks for matching the stage to its purpose.

| Stage | What happens | What it removes |
|---|---|---|
| Sedimentation | Water stands in large tanks so heavy insoluble particles settle to the bottom | Larger insoluble solids |
| Filtration | Water passes through beds of sand and gravel | Smaller insoluble solid particles |
| Carbon treatment | Water passes through carbon, which takes up dissolved substances onto its surface | Substances causing unpleasant tastes and odours |
| Chlorination | Chlorine is added | Kills harmful microbes |

Two points are worth understanding rather than memorising.

1. **Sedimentation and filtration only remove insoluble solids.** Dissolved substances pass straight through a filter, just as dissolved salt passes through filter paper in the laboratory. That is why treated tap water still contains dissolved metal compounds.
2. **Chlorination kills microbes; it does not remove them or any other substance.** Chlorine is added after the solids have been removed so that it acts on the microbes in clear water.

**Worked example 2.** Describe how water from a river is made suitable for drinking. [4]

*Annotated answer.*
"The water is left in tanks so that large solid particles settle out (sedimentation) [1]. It is then filtered through sand and gravel to remove smaller insoluble particles [1]. Carbon is used to remove substances that cause tastes and odours [1]. Finally chlorine is added to kill harmful microbes [1]."

Each mark pairs a named stage with its purpose. Writing "the water is filtered and chlorinated to clean it" names two stages but gives no purpose, so it scores little.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the colour changes. Copper(II) sulfate goes **white to blue**; cobalt(II) chloride goes **blue to pink**. Using hydrated (already blue) copper(II) sulfate gives no change.
- Using a chemical test for water to show that water is pure.
- Writing that an impurity raises the melting point or lowers the boiling point. It is the other way round.
- Saying filtration removes dissolved substances or microbes.
- Saying carbon kills bacteria. Carbon removes tastes and odours; chlorine kills microbes.
- Calling all metal compounds harmful, or calling dissolved oxygen a pollutant.
- Writing a long account of eutrophication when the question asks only for the effect of nitrates and phosphates.

## Exam technique: the command words

- **State**: a short answer, such as "white to blue" or "kills microbes".
- **Describe** (a test): name the reagent, say it must be anhydrous, and give the colour change from start to finish.
- **Describe** (treatment): name each stage in order and say what it removes.
- **Explain** (distilled water): give the reason and the consequence: tap water contains dissolved impurities, which could react with or interfere with the substances in the experiment.
