## Section A

**1.** Define *aggregate demand*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** reason why the AD curve slopes downwards. **[2]**

**3.** State whether each event shifts AD or SRAS, and in which direction. **[4]**

- A sharp fall in world oil prices.
- A fall in consumer confidence.
- A rise in the minimum wage paid by most firms.
- A recession in the country's main export market.

**4.** Define an *inflationary gap*. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the monetarist/new classical LRAS curve is vertical. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** factors that would shift the LRAS curve to the right. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The fictional economy of Corrin has these schedules (real output in billions of corrins). Potential output is 420.

| Price level | AD | SRAS |
|---|---|---|
| 80 | 420 | 370 |
| 85 | 410 | 385 |
| 90 | 400 | 400 |
| 95 | 390 | 415 |
| 100 | 380 | 430 |

**(a)** Identify the short-run equilibrium price level and real output. **[1]**

**(b)** Identify the type and size of the output gap. **[2]**

**(c)** Government spending rises by 25 billion corrins at every price level. Find the new equilibrium and the percentage change in the price level. **[3]**

**(d)** State the output gap after the rise in spending. **[1]**

**8.** Using an AD-AS diagram, explain how a recessionary gap is eliminated in the monetarist/new classical model. **[4]**

**9.** Using a Keynesian AS diagram, explain why an increase in AD may raise real output without raising the price level. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a negative supply shock can lead to both a higher price level and lower real output. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why the size of an increase in AD matters when the economy has a recessionary gap. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss whether a government should act to close a recessionary gap. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The total planned spending on an economy's final goods and services [1] at each price level, made up of C + I + G + (X - M) [1].

**2.** Wealth effect: a higher price level reduces the real value of savings and other assets [1], so households consume less, reducing real output demanded [1]. (Credit the interest rate or international trade effect, similarly explained.)

**3.** Oil prices fall: SRAS shifts right [1]. Consumer confidence falls: AD shifts left [1]. Minimum wage rises: SRAS shifts left [1]. Recession in export market: AD shifts left, as exports fall [1].

**4.** A situation where short-run equilibrium real output is above potential output [1], so resources are over-used and there is pressure on the price level [1].

**5.** In the long run wages and resource prices adjust fully to the price level [1], so output is determined by the quantity and quality of resources and technology, not by the price level [1].

**6.** Any two: an increase in the labour force [1]; better education and training [1]; more capital; improved technology; better institutions.

**7. (a)** Price level **90**, real output **400** [1].

**(b)** Recessionary (deflationary) gap [1] of 420 - 400 = **20 billion corrins** [1].

**(c)** New AD: 445, 435, 425, 415, 405 [1]. AD equals SRAS at price level **95** and output **415** [1]. Price level change = (95 - 90) ÷ 90 × 100 ≈ **5.6%** [1].

**(d)** A smaller recessionary gap of 420 - 415 = **5** [1].

**8.** Diagram with price level and real output axes, AD, SRAS1 and vertical LRAS at Yp, and equilibrium at Y1 below Yp [1]. Unemployment above the natural rate leads to falling money wages and resource prices [1]. Firms' costs fall, so SRAS shifts right to SRAS2 [1]. The price level falls and output rises until long-run equilibrium is restored at Yp [1].

**9.** Keynesian AS curve with a horizontal section, and AD crossing it there at Y1 [1]. On the horizontal section there is a large amount of spare capacity: unemployed workers and idle machines [1]. Firms can expand output without bidding up wages or other costs [1], so when AD shifts right real output rises from Y1 to Y2 while the price level stays the same [1].

**10.** A negative supply shock, such as a rise in energy prices, raises firms' costs [1], so SRAS shifts left [1]. At the new equilibrium the price level is higher and real output lower, so the economy faces rising prices and falling output together [1].

**11.** If the rise in AD is too small, part of the gap and the unemployment remain [1]; if it is too large, output overshoots potential and creates an inflationary gap with rising prices [1].

**12.** For action: in the Keynesian view wages are sticky downwards, so the gap can persist with high unemployment for a long time [1]; unemployment brings lost output, lower incomes and social costs [1]; raising AD on the flatter part of AS can increase output with little inflation [1]. Against action: in the monetarist/new classical view the economy self-corrects as wages and prices fall [1]; policy works with time lags and may take effect after recovery, adding to inflation [1]; overestimating the gap can create an inflationary gap [1]; higher government spending may add to government debt [1]. Judgement: action is more justified when the gap is large and wages adjust slowly, and less so when the gap is small and recovery is already under way [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling AD-AS axes "price" and "quantity".
- Shifting AD when only the price level has changed.
- Forgetting to compare equilibrium output with potential output when naming a gap.
- Giving only one view of the model in a "discuss" answer.
