This set covers optional topic 3.1.3, **Islam in the subcontinent (c700–c1831)**, which is examined
in Section C of Paper 1 as an alternative to the Mughal Empire. Section C uses short and extended
response questions rather than multiple choice, so the short recall items below are for checking
knowledge, while Questions 8 to 13 use the 4, 6 and 9-mark tariffs of the board's specimen paper.
The questions cover all three key developments: the expansion of Islam c700–c1700, Shah Waliullah's
reforms in the 18th century, and Syed Ahmad Barelvi and the revival of Islam in the 19th century.

Learn the content from the [Islam in the Subcontinent study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-islam-in-the-subcontinent/)
and the [Islam in the Subcontinent revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-islam-in-the-subcontinent-revision-notes/).

## Section A — short-answer questions

**1.** Name the Umayyad governor who sent Muhammad bin Qasim to Sindh. **[1]**

**2.** Name the ruler of Sindh defeated by Muhammad bin Qasim. **[1]**

**3.** Give the title of al-Ghazali's best-known work. **[1]**

**4.** Name the place where Syed Ahmad Barelvi was killed in 1831. **[1]**

**5.** State **two** reasons why the teaching of Sufi saints attracted followers in the
subcontinent. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** facts about Shah Waliullah's early life and education. **[2]**

**7.** State **two** aims of Syed Ahmad Barelvi's Jihad movement. **[2]**

## Section B — extended questions

**8.** Identify and explain **two** key beliefs of Syed Ahmad Barelvi. **[4]**

**9.** Identify and explain **two** ways in which al-Ghazali's ideas influenced Islam in the
subcontinent. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse **two** reasons why the Muslim community in the subcontinent was weak and divided by
the early 18th century. **[6]**

**11.** Analyse **two** ways in which Syed Ahmad Barelvi contributed to the revival of Islam in the
subcontinent. **[6]**

**12.** Which was the more important reason for the failure of Syed Ahmad Barelvi's Jihad movement?

- opposition from local Pathan leaders
- the military strength of the Sikh kingdom

Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. **[9]**

**13.** "Sufi saints did more than Muslim rulers to spread Islam in the subcontinent between c700
and c1700."

How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. **[9]**

## Answers

Recall and "state" items score 1 mark per correct answer. "Identify and explain" items score 1 for
each point and 1 for its explanation. The 6- and 9-mark questions are marked by levels, set out
after the answers. Credit other accurate points.

**1.** **Hajjaj bin Yusuf** [1], governor of Iraq under the Umayyad Caliphate. **[1]**

**2.** **Raja Dahir** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** ***Ihya Ulum al-Din*** (*The Revival of the Religious Sciences*) [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **Balakot** [1], in the Kaghan valley, where he died fighting Sikh forces together with Shah
Ismail. **[1]**

**5.** Two reasons, 1 mark each: Sufi saints welcomed people of every background, including
low-caste Hindus, at their lodges (*khanqahs*) [1]; they taught through personal example, kindness
and service to the poor rather than force [1]. Also credit: they used local languages, poetry and
music to explain their message; their shrines became centres of community life. **[2]**

**6.** Two facts, 1 mark each: he was the son of Shah Abdur Rahim, who founded the Madrasa Rahimiya
in Delhi, where he studied and later taught [1]; he performed the Hajj and studied hadith in Madinah
before returning to Delhi [1]. Also credit: he was born in 1703 and learned the Qur'an as a child.
**[2]**

**7.** Two aims, 1 mark each: to free the Muslims of the Punjab and the north-west from Sikh rule [1];
to set up an Islamic state in which the Sharia would be followed [1]. Also credit: to purify the
practice of Islam by removing un-Islamic customs. **[2]**

**8.** Two beliefs, each identified (1) and explained (1):
- strict belief in the oneness of Allah (*tawhid*) [1], so he opposed practices such as excessive
  reverence of saints' tombs that he believed came close to associating others with Allah [1];
- Muslims should follow the Qur'an and the Sunnah of the Prophet (peace be upon him) and reject later
  innovations [1], which is why his movement was called the *Tariqa-i-Muhammadiya*, the path of
  Muhammad (peace be upon him) [1].

Also credit: a belief that jihad was a duty when Muslims were prevented from practising their faith.
**[4]**

**9.** Two ways, each identified (1) and explained (1):
- he showed that Sufism could sit within the Sharia [1], so later Sufi orders in the subcontinent
  taught spiritual devotion alongside obedience to Islamic law [1];
- his *Ihya Ulum al-Din* became a standard text [1], studied in madrasas and by scholars in the
  subcontinent for centuries [1].

Also credit: his criticism of philosophers who put reason above revelation influenced how scholars
in the subcontinent approached Greek philosophy. **[4]**

**10.** Mark by the Analysis (6) levels. Indicative content:
- *Political decline:* after Aurangzeb died in 1707, weak emperors, wars of succession and powerful
  nobles weakened Mughal authority, so Muslims lost the political protection the empire had given
  them.
- *Rival powers:* the Marathas in the south and west and the Sikhs in the Punjab expanded at Mughal
  expense, and invasions such as Nadir Shah's in 1739 humiliated Delhi.
- *Religious division:* there were disputes between Sunnis and Shias and between scholars and those
  who followed customs at shrines; few Muslims could read the Qur'an in Arabic, so many relied on
  others for its meaning. **[6]**

**11.** Mark by the Analysis (6) levels. Indicative content:
- He was a disciple of Shah Abdul Aziz, son of Shah Waliullah, so he carried Shah Waliullah's
  reforming ideas into a mass movement.
- He travelled widely and preached, gaining thousands of followers who promised to give up
  un-Islamic customs.
- His Jihad movement showed Muslims that they could organise to defend their faith, and he was
  remembered as a martyr after Balakot.
- His followers and ideas influenced later movements, including the Ahl-i-Hadith and the scholars
  associated with the Deobandi movement. **[6]**

**12.** Mark by the Evaluation (9) levels. Answers must address both bullet points. Indicative
content:
- *Pathan opposition:* after the Mujahideen took control of Peshawar in 1830, the imposition of the
  Sharia, including taxes and changes to local marriage customs, angered tribal chiefs who resented
  outsiders interfering in their traditions; some turned against the movement and killed its
  officials, forcing Syed Ahmad to withdraw towards the Kaghan valley.
- *Sikh strength:* Ranjit Singh's army was well equipped and trained, with artillery; the Mujahideen
  were fewer and lacked modern weapons; at Balakot in 1831 a Sikh force killed Syed Ahmad and Shah
  Ismail.
- *Judgement:* for example, the loss of local support was more important, because it cost the
  movement its base and left it exposed to the Sikh army. **[9]**

**13.** Mark by the Evaluation (9) levels. Indicative content:
- *Sufi saints:* figures such as Ali Hujwiri in Lahore, Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti in Ajmer and
  Bahauddin Zakariya in Multan drew people to Islam through example, welcome and service; their
  shrines remained centres of faith long after the rulers who controlled the land had gone.
- *Rulers:* Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest brought Sindh under Muslim rule, and mosques and Islamic
  learning followed; later Muslim rulers in Delhi gave land and protection to scholars and Sufis, and
  Aurangzeb promoted the Sharia, for example through the *Fatawa-e-Alamgiri*.
- *Judgement:* for example, rulers created the conditions for Islam to be practised, but conversion
  owed more to the saints. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating al-Ghazali as a scholar of the subcontinent: he taught in Baghdad, and his influence
  reached the subcontinent through his books.
- Mixing up Ahmad Sirhindi (16th–17th centuries, Naqshbandi) with Shah Waliullah (18th century).
- Describing the Jihad movement's battles without explaining why the movement failed.
- Writing about the Faraizi movement, which is not part of this specification.

Approximate centuries and places are enough; focus on how they spread Islam.
