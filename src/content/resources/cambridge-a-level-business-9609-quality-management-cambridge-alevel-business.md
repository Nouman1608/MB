This guide goes deeper into **section 9.2 Quality management** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content, examined through case studies and essays.

The [quality management and operations strategy guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-quality-management-and-operations-strategy/) defines quality control, quality assurance, TQM and benchmarking. This page takes the next step: it shows how to measure what quality costs, how each method changes those costs, and how to judge which approach suits a particular business. Afterwards, use the [quality management revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-quality-management-cambridge-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and the [quality management practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-quality-management-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

| Part | Syllabus wording |
|---|---|
| 9.2.1 Quality control and quality assurance | Quality in terms of meeting customer expectations; the importance of quality; the impact of methods of quality control, of quality assurance and of Total Quality Management (TQM) on a business |
| 9.2.2 Benchmarking | The importance of benchmarking in quality management |

Note the word **impact** appearing three times. The syllabus wants you to explain what each method does to costs, staff, customers and competitiveness, not just to describe it.

## Quality as meeting customer expectations

A product is high quality if it meets the expectations of the customers it is aimed at. Expectations depend on the price paid and the market segment. Customers of a budget airline expect a safe, punctual flight, not free meals; a premium airline's customers expect much more. Both airlines can be "high quality" for their own customers.

Expectations usually cover several dimensions:

| Products | Services |
|---|---|
| Performance: does it do the job? | Reliability: is the service delivered as promised? |
| Reliability: does it keep working? | Responsiveness: how quickly are problems handled? |
| Durability: how long does it last? | Accuracy: is the order or bill correct? |
| Appearance and finish | Courtesy and competence of staff |
| After-sales support | Consistency from one visit to the next |

In a case, identify which dimension the customers care about most. A courier's customers care most about punctuality and accuracy; a furniture maker's customers about durability and finish.

## Why quality matters: the cost of quality

A useful way to show the importance of quality is to split quality-related costs into four groups:

| Group | Examples | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Prevention | Training, supplier checks, better design, documented procedures | Spending to stop faults happening |
| Appraisal | Inspection, testing, quality staff, test equipment | Spending to find faults |
| Internal failure | Scrap, rework, machine downtime caused by faults | Faults found before the customer |
| External failure | Returns, refunds, repairs under guarantee, lost customers, damaged reputation | Faults found by the customer: usually the most expensive |

The central argument of quality management is that money spent on **prevention** usually saves more in failure costs. External failure is especially damaging because it includes lost future sales that never appear in the accounts.

## Quality control: impact on a business

Quality control relies on **inspection and testing** of output. Two choices shape its impact:

- **100% inspection or sampling.** Inspecting every unit catches more faults but is slow and costly; sampling is cheaper but some faulty units pass.
- **End of line or at stages.** Checking only at the end means faults are found after all the materials and labour have been used; checking at stages finds them earlier.

**Positive impact:** protects customers from faulty output; suits products where a fault is dangerous, such as medicines or car brakes; gives clear responsibility to a quality department.

**Negative impact:** inspection adds cost but not value; production workers may feel quality is "someone else's job"; and quality control does not remove the cause of faults, so the same faults recur.

## Quality assurance: impact on a business

Quality assurance sets **standards and procedures for every stage** so that faults are prevented. Typical features include agreed standards with suppliers, written procedures, staff trained in them, and records that allow faults to be traced to their cause. Some businesses seek certification to a recognised quality management standard, such as the ISO 9000 family, which customers may require from suppliers.

**Positive impact:** fewer faults made, so failure costs fall; customers and business buyers trust the product; certification can win contracts.

**Negative impact:** paperwork and procedures cost time and money; procedures can become rigid and slow down change; certification proves that a system exists, not that every product is excellent.

## Total Quality Management: impact on a business

TQM makes quality the responsibility of **every employee in every department**. Its main principles:

- **Customer focus**, including internal customers: the next person in the process.
- **Right first time**, aiming at zero defects.
- **Continuous improvement**, often through small, frequent changes.
- **Employee involvement**, for example through quality circles that meet to solve problems.

**Positive impact:** failure costs fall sharply; fewer inspectors are needed; staff gain responsibility, which links to Herzberg's motivators; quality becomes part of the business's reputation.

**Negative impact:** large training cost; a change of culture can take years; staff may resist extra responsibility or see it as more work for the same pay; and TQM can fail if senior managers do not show commitment.

## Worked example: moving from inspection to TQM at Brenton Pumps

Brenton Pumps makes 8,000 water pumps a year. Current annual quality costs:

```
Inspection (4 inspectors x $26,000)          $104,000
Scrap and rework: 5% of 8,000 = 400 pumps x $120   $48,000
Guarantee repairs: 2% of 8,000 = 160 pumps x $300  $48,000
Total                                         $200,000
```

Under TQM, forecast for year 2: training $60,000; one inspector kept ($26,000); scrap and rework 2% (160 × $120 = $19,200); guarantee repairs 0.5% (40 × $300 = $12,000).

```
Year 2 total: 60,000 + 26,000 + 19,200 + 12,000 = $117,200
Saving:       200,000 - 117,200 = $82,800
```

**Evaluation.** The saving is large, but it rests on forecast defect rates that depend on staff accepting the change. The three inspectors who are no longer needed may resist, and the training cost may continue beyond year 2. On the other hand, the figures understate the benefit: 120 fewer guarantee repairs a year means fewer unhappy customers, which protects future sales.

## Benchmarking and its importance in quality management

Benchmarking compares the business's performance or processes with the best, then uses the gap to drive improvement. A typical sequence:

1. Choose what to benchmark, such as defect rate or delivery time.
2. Choose whom to compare with: another part of the business, a competitor, or a leader in another industry with a similar process.
3. Collect and compare data.
4. Find out *how* the leader achieves better results.
5. Set targets and adapt the methods.
6. Monitor and repeat.

**Worked example: Kelby Couriers.** Kelby compares itself with a leading parcel firm.

| Measure | Kelby | Leader | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parcels delivered on time | 91% | 97% | 6 points |
| Complaints per 1,000 parcels | 8.0 | 2.5 | 5.5 |
| Parcels damaged per 1,000 | 4.0 | 1.0 | 3.0 |

On 500,000 parcels a year, Kelby has 500 × 8.0 = 4,000 complaints; at the leader's rate it would have 500 × 2.5 = 1,250. Closing even half the gap removes about 1,375 complaints. The importance is that the benchmark turns a vague aim ("better service") into specific, evidence-based targets.

**Why benchmarking matters in quality management:** customers judge quality against what rivals offer, so internal standards alone can mislead; it shows the gap with evidence; it supports continuous improvement; and it suggests *how* to improve, not just *that* improvement is needed.

**Limits:** data from competitors can be hard to obtain or compare; copying another business's methods may not fit a different culture or customer base; and a business that only benchmarks will always follow, never lead.

## Annotated model answer

*Assess the importance of quality to Kelby Couriers.*

> "Quality is very important because Kelby's customers judge it on punctuality and care of parcels, and its 91% on-time rate is six points below the leader. Each complaint is an external failure cost: staff time, refunds and, most seriously, lost business customers who can switch to a rival with few switching costs. Improving quality would protect revenue more than cutting price. However, quality improvements cost money, and if Kelby's customers are price-sensitive small senders, a lower price may matter as much. On balance, quality is essential for business customers who send regularly, so its importance depends on Kelby's target market."

It uses the case figures, names the cost category, gives a counter-argument and ends with a condition.

## Common mistakes

- Defining quality as "the best possible product" rather than meeting customer expectations.
- Describing QC, QA and TQM without stating any impact on the business.
- Ignoring external failure costs when judging whether a quality method pays.
- Saying benchmarking means "copying competitors".

## Exam technique

**Explain** the impact of a method by giving a cause and an effect on cost, staff or customers. **Analyse** with a chain from the method to the cost of quality to profit. **Evaluate** and **assess** by weighing implementation costs and staff reactions against reduced failure costs, then judging what success depends on, often management commitment and the type of product.
