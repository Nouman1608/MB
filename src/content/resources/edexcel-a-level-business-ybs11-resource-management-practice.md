Each question uses one of the specification's command words at its published International AS tariff: **define** (2), **calculate** (4), **explain** (4), **analyse** (6), **discuss** (8) and **assess** (10). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Sources

> **Source 1: Tallon Bikes**
>
> Tallon assembles bicycles by batch production. Year 2 maximum capacity is
> 12,600 bikes. Fixed costs are $450,000 a year.
>
> | | Year 1 | Year 2 |
> |---|---|---|
> | Bikes made | 9,600 | 11,340 |
> | Employees | 48 | 54 |
> | Total costs ($) | 1,344,000 | 1,474,200 |
>
> **Source 2: Glenmore Foods**
>
> Glenmore makes jars of sauce and sells 25,000 a month. It uses JIT for
> its fresh tomatoes but holds a buffer of other ingredients. For one key
> ingredient the maximum inventory is 800 kg and the buffer is 200 kg.
> Usage is 100 kg a day and the supplier takes 3 days to deliver. Currently
> 3% of jars are returned as faulty and each replacement costs $2.40. The
> owner is considering Total Quality Management (TQM), which she hopes
> will cut returns to 1%.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "productivity". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate Tallon's labour productivity in Year 1 and Year 2, and the percentage change. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate Tallon's capacity utilisation in Year 2. **[4]**

**4.** Calculate Tallon's average cost per bike in each year. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** advantage to Tallon of its high capacity utilisation in Year 2. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse why Tallon's average cost per bike fell between Year 1 and Year 2. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "buffer inventory". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the reorder level and the order quantity for Glenmore's key ingredient. **[4]**

**9.** Calculate the monthly saving if TQM cuts returns from 3% to 1%. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse one risk to Glenmore of using JIT for fresh tomatoes. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss whether Glenmore should introduce TQM. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether Tallon would be better to remain a batch producer or move to flow production. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Output per unit of input [1] per time period, for example per employee per year [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Year 1 = 9,600 / 48 = **200 bikes per employee** [1]. Year 2 = 11,340 / 54 = **210 bikes per employee** [1]. Change = (210 - 200) / 200 x 100 [1] = **5% increase** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Capacity utilisation = current output / maximum output x 100 [1] = 11,340 / 12,600 [1] x 100 [1] = **90%** [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Year 1 = 1,344,000 / 9,600 = **$140** [2]. Year 2 = 1,474,200 / 11,340 = **$130** [2]. (Award 1 mark per year for the correct method if the answer is wrong.) **[4]**

**5.** Fixed costs of $450,000 are spread over more bikes [1]. Fixed cost per bike falls from $46.88 (450,000 / 9,600) to $39.68 (450,000 / 11,340) [1], a saving of about $7 a bike [1]. This lowers average cost and lets Tallon either widen its profit margin or cut prices to win more orders [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Output rose by 18.1% (9,600 to 11,340) while total costs rose by only 9.7% [1]. Fixed costs of $450,000 were spread across more bikes, so fixed cost per bike fell [1]. Labour productivity rose by 5%, so each employee made more bikes, cutting labour cost per bike [1]. Higher capacity utilisation (90%) means machines and workshops were used more fully [1]. Together, these cut average cost from $140 to $130 [1], a fall of about 7%, which improves Tallon's efficiency and ability to compete on price [1]. **[6]**

**7.** The minimum amount of inventory a business keeps [1] as a safety net against late deliveries or unexpectedly high demand [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Reorder level = buffer + (usage x lead time) = 200 + (100 x 3) [1] = **500 kg** [1]. Order quantity = maximum - buffer = 800 - 200 [1] = **600 kg** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Current returns = 3% x 25,000 = 750 jars, costing 750 x $2.40 = $1,800 [1]. Returns after TQM = 1% x 25,000 = 250 jars, costing 250 x $2.40 = $600 [1]. Saving = 1,800 - 600 [1] = **$1,200 a month** [1]. **[4]**

**10.** With JIT, Glenmore holds very little tomato inventory [1], so any late delivery stops production almost immediately [1]. Tomatoes are fresh and perishable, so Glenmore cannot easily buy extra from elsewhere at short notice or stockpile them [1]. If production stops, 25,000 jars a month of sales could be lost [1], customers may switch to rival sauces [1], and fixed costs still have to be paid, cutting profit [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **For TQM:** returns would fall from 750 to 250 jars, saving $1,200 a month [1]; fewer faulty jars protects Glenmore's reputation and customer loyalty [1]; staff responsibility for quality could raise motivation and cut waste [1].
**Against:** TQM needs training and time, which cost money [1]; a $1,200 monthly saving may not cover those costs for a long time [1]; it needs commitment from all staff and managers or it will fail [1].
**Judgement:** TQM is probably worthwhile if the training cost is recovered within a year or two and reputation is important in the sauce market [1]; the decision depends on the cost of the programme and the owner's ability to engage staff [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **For staying with batch:** batch production allows Tallon to make different models and respond to customer tastes [1]; flow production needs expensive machinery and Tallon's output of 11,340 bikes may not justify the capital cost [1]; a breakdown on a flow line stops everything [1].
**For flow:** unit costs would fall further as fixed costs spread over a larger output [1]; Tallon is at 90% utilisation, so extra demand would need extra capacity, and a flow line could provide it [1]; standardised production can raise productivity beyond 210 bikes per employee [1].
**Judgement:** Tallon is probably better to stay with batch production for now, because the range of models and flexibility are valuable and utilisation is already high [1]. It should consider flow production only if demand becomes high and steady enough to fill a much larger line [1], and if it can finance the investment without harming its liquidity [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating productivity from total cost instead of output and employees.
- Dividing by current output instead of maximum output in capacity utilisation.
- Forgetting to add the lead-time usage to the buffer in the reorder level.
- Giving a general advantage of JIT without linking it to perishable tomatoes.
- Listing arguments for and against TQM without a judgement based on the figures.
