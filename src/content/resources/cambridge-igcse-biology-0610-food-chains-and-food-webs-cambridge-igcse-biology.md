This guide goes deeper into one sub-topic of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: **19.2 Food chains and food webs**, the second part of topic 19, Organisms and their environment. It is a large sub-topic with nineteen learning outcomes. Outcomes 1 to 14 are Core. Outcomes 15 to 19, on pyramids of energy and the efficiency of energy transfer, are Supplement content, so they are **Extended only** (Papers 2 and 4).

The [organisms and their environment study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-organisms-and-their-environment/) defines the terms and gives one example of each pyramid. This page concentrates on the skills: building chains from a description, predicting knock-on effects in a web, drawing pyramids to scale and handling energy calculations. Afterwards, use the [food chains and food webs revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-food-chains-and-food-webs-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/) and the [food chains and food webs practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-food-chains-and-food-webs-cambridge-igcse-biology-practice/).

## Building a food chain from a description

Questions often describe feeding relationships in words. Turn the words into arrows in three steps:

1. Find the **producer**: the organism that photosynthesises. Every chain starts here.
2. Draw an arrow from each organism **to the organism that eats it**. The arrow shows the direction of energy transfer.
3. Label the trophic levels in order.

**Worked example 1.** "On a rocky shore, seaweed is grazed by periwinkles. Crabs eat periwinkles. Gulls eat crabs, and gulls are sometimes caught by large birds of prey."

```
seaweed → periwinkle → crab → gull → bird of prey
producer → primary → secondary → tertiary → quaternary consumer
```

The periwinkle is a **herbivore** (it eats a producer); the crab and gull are **carnivores**. The bird of prey is a **quaternary consumer**, the highest level named in the syllabus.

## Interpreting a food web

In a web, follow every arrow leaving and entering the organism that changes. Ask: who eats it (they lose food), what does it eat (that is eaten less), and who competes with it (they gain or lose food).

**Worked example 2.** A woodland web (described in words):

- oak leaves are eaten by caterpillars and by deer;
- caterpillars are eaten by blue tits and by shrews;
- shrews are eaten by owls and by weasels;
- blue tits are eaten by weasels.

*Question:* A disease kills most of the shrews. Suggest the effects on the caterpillars and the blue tits.

- **Caterpillars increase**, because fewer shrews eat them.
- **Blue tits may decrease**, because weasels lose shrews as food and eat more blue tits; **or** blue tits may increase because there are more caterpillars for them to eat. A good answer gives one effect with its reason, and may mention that both pressures act.

Examiners accept either direction if the reasoning follows the arrows. What they do not accept is a change with no reason.

### Organisms at more than one level

In worked example 2, every chain ending at the weasel has four organisms (oak → caterpillar → shrew → weasel, and oak → caterpillar → blue tit → weasel), so the weasel is a **tertiary** consumer in both. If weasels also ate caterpillars directly, the weasel would be a **secondary** consumer in that chain as well. One organism can occupy two trophic levels, so check each chain separately before naming a level.

## Human impact through food webs

**Overharvesting** is taking a food species faster than it can reproduce. **Introducing a foreign species** means bringing a species into a habitat where it did not live before.

**Worked example 3.** In a sea, sand eels are eaten by puffins and by cod. Fishing boats remove large numbers of sand eels.

- Puffins and cod have less food, so their populations may fall; puffin chicks may starve.
- The plankton that sand eels eat may increase, because fewer are eaten.

For introduced species, use the web to describe the effect: the newcomer may eat a native species, compete with natives for the same food, or have no predator to control its numbers, so its population grows rapidly.

## Pyramids of numbers and biomass

### Drawing to scale

Draw each trophic level as a horizontal bar, **producers at the bottom**, with bar width proportional to the value. Choose a scale, such as 1 cm for 1 000 organisms or 1 cm for 100 g per m². Label each bar with the trophic level.

### Describing the shape

| Situation | Pyramid of numbers | Pyramid of biomass |
|---|---|---|
| grass → rabbits → foxes | normal pyramid | normal pyramid |
| one tree → many insects → birds | narrow base, wide middle | normal pyramid |
| one fox → many fleas | inverted at the top (more fleas than foxes) | normal pyramid |

A **pyramid of biomass** uses the **dry mass** of living material at each level, so a single large producer is shown with its true mass.

**Discuss** questions on outcome 12 want both sides: a pyramid of biomass allows for the size of organisms and is almost always pyramid-shaped, but it needs organisms to be killed and dried to find dry mass, and it shows only the mass present at one moment.

## Extended only: pyramids of energy

A pyramid of energy shows the energy transferred into each trophic level over a period of time, usually in kJ per m² per year. Its advantages:

- it is **never inverted**, because energy is lost at every transfer;
- it shows **energy transferred over time**, not a snapshot;
- it allows the **efficiency** of each transfer to be calculated and compared.

### Worked example 4: efficiency

In a marsh, producers transfer 18 000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹ into new biomass, herbivores gain 1 620 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹ and carnivores gain 113 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹ (illustrative figures).

```
efficiency = energy in higher level ÷ energy in lower level × 100
producers → herbivores: 1 620 ÷ 18 000 × 100 = 9.0 %
herbivores → carnivores:  113 ÷ 1 620 × 100 = 6.98 … = 7.0 % (2 s.f.)
```

### Why transfer is not efficient

Energy fails to reach the next level because parts of organisms are not eaten; food is not all digested, so energy is lost in faeces; energy is lost in excretory products such as urea; and above all, energy is released by respiration and transferred to the environment as heat.

### Why chains have fewer than five levels

With only a small fraction passed on each time, the energy available falls rapidly. Using the marsh figures, a fourth level gaining about 7% of 113 kJ would receive about 8 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹: too little to support a breeding population.

### Crops versus livestock

Humans eating crops are **primary consumers**: one transfer. Humans eating livestock fed on crops are **secondary consumers**: two transfers, and the livestock respire, move and keep warm, losing energy as heat. A given area of crop therefore provides more energy, and feeds more people, if humans eat the crop directly.

## Common mistakes

- Arrows pointing from predator to prey.
- Starting a chain with the Sun. The Sun is not an organism; chains begin with a producer.
- Changes in a web stated without a reason.
- Drawing a pyramid with the producer at the top or bars not to scale.
- Calling a pyramid "inverted" when only one bar is wider than the one below.
- Using fresh mass for biomass; the syllabus pyramid uses dry mass.
- (Extended) Dividing by the higher level in efficiency calculations, or writing that energy is "used up".

## Exam technique for 19.2 command words

- **Construct**: arrows towards the eater, producer first.
- **Interpret** a web: direction of change plus a reason from the arrows.
- **Draw**: horizontal bars, producer at the base, to scale, labelled.
- **Discuss**: advantages and at least one limitation.
- **Explain** (Extended): name the losses (heat from respiration, faeces, uneaten parts, urea) and link them to the number of levels or to food for humans.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are pyramids of energy Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
Pyramids of energy and the reasons for inefficient energy transfer are Supplement content, so they are Extended only. Pyramids of numbers and biomass are Core.

**Which way do the arrows point in a food chain?**
From the organism that is eaten to the organism that eats it, showing the direction in which energy is transferred.

**Why can a pyramid of numbers be inverted?**
Because it counts organisms without allowing for their size: one large producer or host can support many small consumers.
