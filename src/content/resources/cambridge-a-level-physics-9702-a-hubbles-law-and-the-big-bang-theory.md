This guide covers subtopic **25.3 Hubble's law and the Big Bang theory** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (A Level). The broader [Astronomy and Cosmology study guide](/resources/a-physics-astronomy-and-cosmology/) introduces redshift and Hubble's law briefly; this page goes further into how redshift is measured from spectral lines, using the redshift equation in both wavelength and frequency form, the step-by-step argument from redshift to an expanding Universe, and how Hubble's law leads to the Big Bang theory. For quick recall use the [Hubble's law and the Big Bang revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-hubbles-law-and-the-big-bang-theory-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Hubble's law and the Big Bang practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-hubbles-law-and-the-big-bang-theory-practice/).

## What 25.3 asks you to do

The syllabus lists four outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- understand that the lines in the **emission and absorption spectra** from distant objects show an **increase in wavelength** from their known values
- use **Δλ/λ ≈ Δf/f ≈ v/c** for the **redshift** of electromagnetic radiation from a source moving relative to an observer
- explain why redshift leads to the idea that the **Universe is expanding**
- recall and use **Hubble's law v ≈ H₀d** and explain how this leads to the **Big Bang theory** (candidates will only be required to use SI units)

## Spectral lines as markers

Every element produces lines at characteristic wavelengths, measured accurately in the laboratory (see [Energy levels in atoms and line spectra](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-energy-levels-in-atoms-and-line-spectra/)). In the light from a distant galaxy, astronomers find the **same pattern** of lines, so they can identify the elements, but every line is at a **longer wavelength** than its laboratory value. The whole pattern is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum: this is **redshift**.

Because each line shifts by the **same fraction** of its own wavelength, the pattern is recognisable even though no line is where it "should" be. That is the evidence that the shift is due to motion and not to unfamiliar elements.

## The redshift equation

For a source moving away from (or towards) the observer at speed v, much less than the speed of light c:

```
Δλ / λ ≈ Δf / f ≈ v / c
```

- λ (or f) is the wavelength (or frequency) emitted, as measured in the laboratory.
- Δλ is the change in wavelength: observed − laboratory. For a **receding** source Δλ is positive (**redshift**) and the frequency decreases.
- An **approaching** source gives a decrease in wavelength: **blueshift**.
- The "≈" reminds you that the equation is an approximation valid when v is much less than c.

This is the Doppler effect for electromagnetic radiation, the counterpart of the [Doppler effect for sound](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-doppler-effect-for-sound-waves/) met at AS Level.

## Worked example 1: speed from a wavelength shift

A hydrogen line has a laboratory wavelength of 486.1 nm. In the spectrum of a galaxy it is observed at 502.4 nm. Calculate the recession speed of the galaxy.

```
Δλ = 502.4 − 486.1 = 16.3 nm
v = c Δλ / λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 16.3 / 486.1 = 1.0 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹
```

*Annotation.* Δλ and λ are both in nm, so the units cancel and no conversion is needed. Divide by the **laboratory** wavelength, not the observed one (the difference is small here, but using the correct value earns the method mark). The positive Δλ means the galaxy is moving away.

## Worked example 2: the frequency form

A line emitted at a frequency of 4.57 × 10¹⁴ Hz is received from a galaxy at 4.48 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Calculate the galaxy's speed.

```
Δf = 4.57 × 10¹⁴ − 4.48 × 10¹⁴ = 0.09 × 10¹⁴ Hz
v = c Δf / f = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 0.09 / 4.57 = 5.9 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹, away from the Earth
```

*Annotation.* A **decrease** in frequency is a redshift. Work with the size of Δf and state the direction in words.

## From redshift to an expanding Universe

Examiners expect a clear chain of reasoning:

1. Light from almost all distant galaxies is **redshifted**.
2. So almost all distant galaxies are **moving away** from us (receding).
3. The **more distant** the galaxy, the **greater** its redshift, and so the faster it recedes.
4. Galaxies moving apart with speeds proportional to distance means the whole Universe is **expanding**: the distances between galaxies (clusters of galaxies) are increasing.

A common question asks whether this means the Earth is at the centre. It does not. In a uniformly expanding Universe, an observer in **any** galaxy sees all other distant galaxies receding, with speed proportional to distance. Picture dots on a balloon being inflated: every dot sees all the others moving away, the farther ones faster, yet no dot is at the centre of the surface.

## Hubble's law

```
v ≈ H₀ d
```

The recession speed v of a galaxy is (approximately) proportional to its distance d. H₀ is the **Hubble constant**. In SI units, v is in m s⁻¹, d in m and H₀ in **s⁻¹**. Its measured value is about 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹, and a question will give the value to use.

On a graph of v against d, galaxies lie close to a **straight line through the origin**, with gradient H₀. The scatter of real data about the line comes from uncertainties in distance and from galaxies' own motions within their clusters.

**Getting the data.** v comes from redshift (worked examples above). d comes from **standard candles** in the galaxy (see [Standard candles](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-standard-candles/)). Hubble's law combines the two subtopics.

## Worked example 3: distance from redshift

A galaxy's spectrum shows a fractional wavelength shift Δλ/λ = 0.010. Taking H₀ = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹, calculate its distance.

```
v = 0.010 × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 3.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹
d = v / H₀ = 3.0 × 10⁶ / 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 1.3 × 10²⁴ m
```

*Annotation.* Once H₀ is known, a measured redshift alone gives the distance to a galaxy, which is how the most distant galaxies are located.

## How Hubble's law leads to the Big Bang theory

1. Galaxies are moving apart now, with speeds proportional to their distances.
2. Running the expansion **backwards in time**, galaxies were closer together in the past.
3. Because v ∝ d, a galaxy twice as far away is moving twice as fast, so **all** galaxies would have been together at roughly the **same moment**.
4. So the Universe had a **beginning** at a definite time in the past, when all the matter and energy was concentrated in an extremely small, extremely **hot and dense** state.
5. The **Big Bang theory** states that the Universe began in this state and has been expanding (and cooling) ever since.

## Common mistakes

- **Dividing by the observed wavelength** instead of the laboratory wavelength.
- **Calling redshift a colour change**, as if the galaxy "looks redder". It is a shift of every spectral line to longer wavelength.
- **Saying the Earth is at the centre** of the expansion.
- **Writing "v = H₀d" without the idea of proportionality**: the key point for the Big Bang argument is that v is proportional to d.
- **Using H₀ in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹.** This syllabus uses SI units only; use s⁻¹ with metres.
- **Treating the Big Bang as an explosion at one point in space.** Use the syllabus idea: the Universe was in a very small, hot, dense state and has expanded since.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** (what is meant by redshift): an increase in the observed wavelength of spectral lines from a source moving away from the observer, compared with the laboratory values.
- **Calculate** (speed): v = cΔλ/λ, with λ the laboratory value.
- **Explain** (why redshift suggests expansion): all distant galaxies redshifted, so receding; more distant ones recede faster; so the Universe is expanding.
- **State** (Hubble's law): the recession speed of a galaxy is (approximately) proportional to its distance from the observer.
- **Explain** (how Hubble's law leads to the Big Bang): reverse the expansion; all matter together at one time; Universe began in a hot, dense state.
