## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must do |
|---|---|
| Explain what is meant by supply side policy and how it can be used to achieve economic objectives | Define it, give examples, link each to growth, unemployment, inflation or distribution |
| Evaluate the costs, including opportunity cost, and the benefits of supply side policies for the economy | Weigh long-run gains against costs, delays and side effects |

## What supply side policy means

**Supply side policy** is any government measure designed to increase the economy's **productive capacity**: the maximum output it can produce with its resources. It works by raising the **quantity** of resources available (more workers, more capital) or their **quality** and efficiency (skills, technology, better-working markets).

Fiscal and monetary policy mainly change **demand** in the short run. Supply side policy changes what the economy **can supply** in the long run. That is why it can, in principle, raise growth without raising inflation: output grows because capacity grows, not because spending outruns what firms can make.

## Three groups of supply side policy

| Group | Examples | How it raises capacity |
|---|---|---|
| Improving the labour force | Education, apprenticeships, retraining, childcare support, help to move for work | More people able to work; each worker more productive |
| Improving capital and technology | Infrastructure (roads, rail, broadband), grants or tax relief for research and investment | Lower costs for firms; more and better capital per worker |
| Improving incentives and markets | Lower income or corporation tax, cutting unnecessary regulation, encouraging competition | Rewards work, investment and enterprise; pushes firms to cut costs |

Some of these involve government spending (training, infrastructure); others involve the government stepping back (lower taxes, fewer rules). Both aim at the same thing: more output from the economy's resources.

## How supply side policy serves each objective

- **Economic growth**: higher capacity lets output keep rising over the long run.
- **Low unemployment**: training and help to move tackle **structural** unemployment, where skills or location do not match the jobs available. Supply side policy is the main tool for this type, because extra demand does not fix a skills mismatch.
- **Price stability**: lower costs and higher productivity let firms produce more without raising prices, easing **cost-push** pressure.
- **Fair distribution of income**: training can raise the pay of low-skilled workers. But cutting top tax rates can widen gaps, so the effect depends on the policy chosen.

## Worked example 1: an infrastructure upgrade

Country E spends £12bn upgrading its rail freight network. Firms spend £20bn a year moving goods, and the upgrade cuts these costs by 5%.

- Yearly saving for firms = 5% × £20bn = **£1bn**.
- Simple payback period = £12bn ÷ £1bn = **12 years**.
- Opportunity cost: the £12bn could have funded hospitals, schools or a tax cut now.

Lower transport costs let firms cut prices or expand, so output and competitiveness rise. But the benefits arrive over decades while the cost is paid up front, and the project may overrun.

## Worked example 2: childcare support to raise the workforce

Country E spends £2.4bn a year on childcare support. As a result 150,000 parents who were economically inactive take jobs, each producing £38,000 of output a year and paying 25% of it in tax.

- Extra output = 150,000 × £38,000 = **£5.7bn** a year.
- Extra tax revenue = 25% × £5.7bn = **£1.425bn**.
- Net cost to the government = 2.4 − 1.425 = **£0.975bn** a year.

The policy more than doubles its cost in extra output, and recovers well over half its cost in tax. Whether it is worthwhile depends on how many parents would have returned to work anyway: if half would have done so, the extra output is only £2.85bn.

## Worked example 3: a corporation tax cut

Country E cuts corporation tax from 25% to 20%. Company profits are £400bn.

- Revenue before = 25% × £400bn = **£100bn**.
- Revenue after, if profits do not change = 20% × £400bn = **£80bn**, a loss of £20bn.
- For revenue to stay at £100bn, profits would need to reach 100 ÷ 0.20 = **£500bn**, a rise of 25%.

The aim is that lower tax encourages firms to invest more and attracts firms from abroad, raising output and profits over time. But profits are unlikely to rise 25% quickly, so the cut will cost revenue at least in the short run, with an opportunity cost in public services.

## Supply side policy compared with demand-side policy

| | Fiscal and monetary (demand) | Supply side |
|---|---|---|
| Main effect | Changes total spending | Changes productive capacity |
| Speed | Months to a couple of years | Often many years |
| Effect on inflation when expanding | Can raise inflation | Can lower cost pressures |
| Best for | Cyclical unemployment, short-term inflation | Structural unemployment, long-term growth |

A government usually needs both: demand policy to manage the cycle, supply side policy to raise the long-run growth rate.

## Evaluating supply side policies

**Benefits**
- Long-lasting rise in potential output and living standards.
- Can reduce unemployment and inflation at the same time.
- Improves international competitiveness.

**Costs and limits**
- **Opportunity cost**: spending on training or infrastructure is not available for other uses; tax cuts reduce revenue for services.
- **Time lags**: education and infrastructure take years to pay off.
- **Uncertainty**: training may not match the skills firms need; firms may not invest after a tax cut.
- **Distribution**: tax cuts and deregulation can favour high earners or weaken protections for workers and the environment.
- **Deadweight**: some of the money supports activity that would have happened anyway.

**How to judge.** Match the policy to the problem. If unemployment is structural, retraining fits; if firms face high transport costs, infrastructure fits. Consider the time frame and who gains.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the use of spending on apprenticeships to reduce unemployment.*

"Apprenticeships give young people skills employers want, so they become more productive and more employable, which reduces structural unemployment. **[chain to objective]** Higher skills also raise output per worker, so the economy's capacity grows. **[second benefit]** However, the spending has an opportunity cost, such as fewer school repairs, and the benefits take years, since an apprenticeship lasts some time before the worker is fully trained. **[cost and delay]** If unemployment is cyclical, caused by a recession, apprenticeships do little in the short run because firms are not hiring. **[type matters]** So the policy is effective for long-term structural unemployment, especially if courses match local employers' needs, but a weak tool for a sudden rise in cyclical unemployment." **[judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Describing supply side policy as "increasing supply in one market". It is about the whole economy's capacity.
- Claiming it works quickly.
- Forgetting opportunity cost when the question asks for costs.
- Treating all supply side policies as reducing inequality.
- Confusing it with a subsidy to correct an externality (section 3.8).

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: name the policy, then link it step by step to productive capacity and to the objective named.
- **Calculate**: show the cost, the gain and any tax recovered; state the opportunity cost.
- **Evaluate**: consider time lags, cost, effectiveness for the type of problem, and who gains; finish by saying when the policy would work best.
