> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, for practising the skills behind the
> non-exam assessment. They are **not** NEA tasks set by the board, and the sample paragraphs are
> original writing about an invented novel. Your actual NEA text and task are agreed with your
> teacher, and the work you submit must be your own. Use this page to rehearse planning and
> analysis, not to prepare text to copy into your NEA.

Related: [Non-exam assessment study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-9275-non-exam-assessment-route-b/), [Non-exam assessment revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-9275-non-exam-assessment-route-b-revision-notes/)

The NEA is Route B only: one extended response to a substantial whole prose fiction text that is not
a 9275 set text, worth 30 marks and 20% of the qualification. Section A checks the rules and core
skills; Section B gives longer planning and writing tasks.

---

## Section A: rules and skills

**1.** State two requirements the NEA text must meet. **[2]**

**2.** A student proposes writing on a 40-page short story by a well-known author. Explain whether
this is likely to be suitable. **[2]**

**3.** Which assessment objective carries the smallest weighting in the NEA, and what does it
assess? **[2]**

**4.** Rewrite this task so that it invites analysis of the writer's methods: *"Write about the
relationship between the two sisters in the novel."* **[2]**

**5.** Which of these is the stronger thesis? Explain why. **[3]**

- (a) *"The novel has lots of themes, such as family, money and love."*
- (b) *"The novel presents money as the force that tests every family bond, and only the bonds that
  refuse its value survive."*

**6.** Explain why an NEA essay that discusses only the first half of a novel is unlikely to reach the
higher levels. **[2]**

**7.** Give two ways a student can show that their NEA is their own work. **[2]**

## Section B: planning and writing

Read the sample paragraph below, then answer Question 8. The novel it discusses, *The Salt Road*, is
invented for this exercise.

> *The Salt Road* is about a boy called Tomas who leaves his village to work in the city. At first he
> is happy, but then he misses home. He writes letters to his mother. In the end he goes back to the
> village. This shows that home is important.

**8.** Identify three weaknesses in the paragraph and rewrite it as an analytical paragraph that could
reach Level 4 or above. You may invent short quotations from the invented novel. **[10]**

**9.** Plan an NEA-style essay on the task *"How does the writer present the conflict between
tradition and change in your chosen novel?"*, using a novel you have read that is not a 9275 set
text. Your plan should include a thesis, five or six sections with evidence from across the novel,
and a note of where you will use context. **[15]**

**10.** Write the introduction and one developed paragraph for the essay you planned in Question 9.
**[15]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Any two: prose fiction [1]; a substantial whole text [1]; not one of the 9275 prescribed set
texts [1]; similar in quality, scope and challenge to the Paper 1 set texts [1]; from the British Isles
canon or seminal world literature [1]. Maximum 2.

**2.** It is unlikely to be suitable [1], because the NEA requires a substantial whole prose fiction
text similar in scope to the set texts, and a single short story is unlikely to meet that standard;
the student should check with their teacher [1].

**3.** AO3, 4% [1]. It assesses an informed, personal response using appropriate terminology and
coherent, accurate writing [1].

**4.** One mark for a "how" focus and one for directing attention to methods or development. Example:
*"How does the writer present the changing relationship between the two sisters, and how does the
novel's structure shape the reader's view of it?"* [2]

**5.** (b) is stronger [1]. It is arguable, so the essay has something to prove [1], and it links
themes into a single idea that can be traced across the whole novel, whereas (a) is a list [1].

**6.** The level descriptors from Level 3 upwards refer to a response to the whole text [1], so an
essay covering half the novel cannot show the development and patterns across the text that the
higher levels reward [1].

**7.** Any two: keeping notes and drafts [1]; recording sources used [1]; signing the centre's
declaration [1]; writing all of it themselves without copying or using AI-generated text [1].
Maximum 2.

### Section B

**8.** Up to three marks for weaknesses and up to seven for the rewrite.

Weaknesses (any three, 1 mark each): it retells the plot; it treats Tomas as a real person rather than
a construction; it has no quotations; it names no methods; the final point is vague ("home is
important"); there is no argument.

A rewrite at Level 4 or above makes a clear claim, uses short evidence, analyses methods and effects,
and links to the whole text. For example:

> The writer presents Tomas's longing for home through the changing style of his letters. Early
> letters are crowded with city detail, "trams, lights, a hundred voices", and the list conveys
> excitement and overload. By the middle of the novel the letters shrink to a few lines that end with
> questions about the village well, and this structural change lets the reader see his homesickness
> before he admits it. His return in the final chapter is therefore prepared from the beginning: the
> letters work as a record of a self slowly turning back towards home.

Credit: clear claim [1]; short embedded evidence [1]; named methods such as listing or structure [2];
explained effects on the reader [2]; whole-text link [1].

**9.** These are marked against level-style criteria; award up to 15 marks.

- **12–15:** an arguable thesis; five or six sections ordered to build an argument; evidence from the
  beginning, middle and end; methods named for each section; context placed where it explains a
  choice; a conclusion that offers a personal judgement or alternative reading.
- **8–11:** a clear thesis; sections covering the whole text with some evidence; methods identified
  for most sections; some context.
- **4–7:** a general focus; sections that follow the plot; limited methods or context.
- **1–3:** a list of events with little sense of argument.

**10.** Award up to 15 marks.

- **12–15:** the introduction states a thesis and signals the essay's line of argument; the paragraph
  is analytical, with precise short quotations, close analysis of methods, context that explains the
  writer's choices, and a clear link to the whole text and the thesis.
- **8–11:** a clear introduction; a paragraph with relevant quotations and explained methods.
- **4–7:** an introduction that mostly describes the novel; a paragraph with some evidence and
  comments.
- **1–3:** narrative and descriptive writing.

## How to use this page

Work through Section A first. Then use Questions 9 and 10 as rehearsal on a novel you know well that
is not your NEA text, or discuss with your teacher how practice on your own text fits your centre's
rules. Keep your actual NEA writing separate and entirely your own.
