This guide goes deeper into subtopic **10.1 Water** of the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. The broader [Chemistry of the Environment](/resources/chemistry-of-the-environment/) guide summarises the water tests, the substances in natural water and the treatment steps. This page explains the difference between testing for water and testing for pure water, why distilled water matters in practical work, how to sort the substances in natural water, and what water treatment does and does not remove. For quick recall use the [water revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-water-revision-notes/), and practise with the [water practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-water-practice/).

## What 10.1 asks you to do

All seven outcomes in 10.1 are **Core**; there is no Supplement content. You need to:

1. describe the chemical tests for the presence of water using anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride and anhydrous copper(II) sulfate;
2. describe how to test the purity of water using melting point and boiling point;
3. explain why distilled water, not tap water, is used in practical chemistry;
4. state the substances that water from natural sources may contain: dissolved oxygen, metal compounds, plastics, sewage, harmful microbes, nitrates from fertilisers, and phosphates from fertilisers and detergents;
5. state which are beneficial: dissolved oxygen for aquatic life, and some metal compounds as essential minerals;
6. state which are potentially harmful: toxic metal compounds, plastics that harm aquatic life, sewage containing microbes that cause disease, and nitrates and phosphates that lead to deoxygenation and damage to aquatic life (details of eutrophication are **not** required);
7. describe domestic water treatment: sedimentation and filtration, carbon, and chlorination.

## Testing for water

Both chemical tests use an **anhydrous** salt: one that has had its water removed. Adding water turns it back into the hydrated form, which has a different colour.

| Reagent | Colour before (anhydrous) | Colour with water (hydrated) |
|---|---|---|
| Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate | white | blue |
| Anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride | blue | pink |

Cobalt(II) chloride is often used as a paper soaked in the salt and dried. Both changes can be reversed by heating, which drives the water out again; this links to the reversible reactions in subtopic 6.3.

**What these tests do and do not show.** A positive result tells you that **water is present**. It does not tell you that the liquid **is** water, or that the water is pure. Seawater, lemonade and a dilute acid would all turn copper(II) sulfate blue, because they all contain water.

## Testing the purity of water

A pure substance has a **fixed, sharp** melting point and boiling point. At standard atmospheric pressure, pure water:

- **melts at 0 °C**, and
- **boils at 100 °C**.

Dissolved impurities **lower the melting point** and **raise the boiling point**, and the substance melts or boils **over a range** of temperatures rather than at one value.

**Method for the boiling point:** heat the sample gently with a few anti-bumping granules, with the thermometer bulb in the boiling liquid, and read the temperature while it boils. (When the impurities are dissolved solids, the steam above the liquid is just water vapour, so a reading taken in the vapour would not reveal them.) If it boils steadily at 100 °C, the water is pure; if it boils above 100 °C, or the temperature keeps rising as it boils, it contains impurities.

### Worked example: interpreting purity data

*The results below are invented for practice.*

| Sample | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 100 |
| B | −2 | 101 to 103 |
| C | 0 | 100 |

**Question.** Deduce which samples could be pure water, and explain your answer for sample B. [3]

**Answer.** A and C [1]. Sample B melts below 0 °C [1] and boils above 100 °C over a range, so it contains dissolved impurities [1].

*All three samples would turn anhydrous copper(II) sulfate blue. That is why the colour test cannot answer a purity question.*

## Why distilled water is used in practical chemistry

Tap water looks clean but contains **dissolved substances**, such as metal ions and chloride ions, many of them left after treatment or added during it. **Distilled water** contains **fewer chemical impurities**, because distillation leaves dissolved solids behind.

Using tap water in an experiment could:

- give a **false positive** in a test for ions, for example a white precipitate with acidified silver nitrate caused by chloride ions already in the tap water rather than in the sample;
- **react** with the chemicals being used;
- change the **concentration** of a solution being made up.

A full answer to "explain why distilled water is used" names the impurity problem **and** its effect on the result.

## What natural water contains

Water from rivers, lakes and wells is never just water. The syllabus lists seven substances and asks you to sort them.

| Substance | Where it comes from | Beneficial or harmful? |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved oxygen | the air | **beneficial**: needed by fish and other aquatic life |
| Metal compounds | dissolved from rocks; from industry | **both**: some provide essential minerals; some are toxic |
| Plastics | litter and waste | **harmful**: harm aquatic life |
| Sewage | human and animal waste | **harmful**: contains harmful microbes that cause disease |
| Harmful microbes | sewage and animal waste | **harmful**: cause disease |
| Nitrates | fertilisers washed off farmland | **harmful** in excess |
| Phosphates | fertilisers and detergents | **harmful** in excess |

**Nitrates and phosphates.** The syllabus wording is that they **lead to deoxygenation of water and damage to aquatic life**. That sentence is all you need. Details of eutrophication, the step-by-step process, are not required.

**Metal compounds are the "it depends" entry.** If a question asks whether metal compounds are beneficial or harmful, the safe answer is both, with a reason for each: some provide essential minerals for life; others are toxic.

## Treating the domestic water supply

| Stage | What it removes or does |
|---|---|
| Sedimentation | larger insoluble particles settle to the bottom |
| Filtration | smaller insoluble particles are removed as the water passes through a filter, such as layers of sand and gravel |
| Carbon | removes **tastes and odours** |
| Chlorination | **kills microbes** |

Each stage has one job. Sedimentation and filtration deal with **solids**; carbon deals with **tastes and smells**; chlorine deals with **microbes**.

**What treatment does not do.** None of these stages removes **dissolved** salts. Sedimentation and filtration remove only insoluble solids, and chlorination adds a substance. That is why treated tap water is safe to drink but is still not pure water, and why distilled water is used in the laboratory.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the colour tests to show purity.** They only show that water is present.
- **Getting the cobalt(II) chloride colours the wrong way round.** Anhydrous is blue; with water it turns pink.
- **Saying impurities lower the boiling point.** They raise the boiling point and lower the melting point.
- **Saying chlorination "cleans" the water.** It kills microbes; it does not remove solids, tastes or dissolved salts.
- **Saying filtration removes dissolved substances.** Filtration removes only insoluble particles.
- **Writing a full eutrophication sequence.** It is not required; state deoxygenation and damage to aquatic life.
- **Saying all metal compounds are harmful.** Some provide essential minerals.

## Exam technique for 10.1 command words

- **Describe** a test: name the reagent, its colour before, and its colour after.
- **Describe** a purity test: say what you measure and the values for pure water.
- **Explain** distilled water: impurities in tap water, and what they would do to the experiment.
- **State:** short answers such as "kills microbes" or "dissolved oxygen".
- **Deduce:** compare data with 0 °C and 100 °C and say which samples are pure.
