These notes condense section **3.3.5.3 Elimination** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015, with Required practical 5. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level. Full worked examples are in the [Elimination study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-elimination-alcohols/), and exam-style questions are in the [Elimination practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-elimination-alcohols-practice/). Oxidation, the other main reaction of alcohols, is in the [Oxidation of alcohols revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-oxidation-of-alcohols-revision-notes/).

## What you must be able to do

- State that alkenes form from alcohols by **acid-catalysed elimination**.
- Explain that these alkenes can make **addition polymers without crude-oil monomers**.
- **Outline the mechanism** for elimination of water from alcohols.
- Carry out and explain **Required practical 5**: distillation of a product from a reaction.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Elimination | Removal of a small molecule (here H₂O) from a larger one, forming a C=C bond |
| Dehydration | Another name for elimination of water |
| Oxonium ion | Protonated alcohol, R–OH₂⁺ |
| Carbocation | Ion with a positive carbon |
| β-carbon | A carbon next to the carbon bonded to –OH; must carry an H for elimination |
| E/Z isomers | Stereoisomers caused by restricted rotation about C=C with two different groups on each carbon |

## Conditions

Heat with **concentrated H₂SO₄** or **concentrated H₃PO₄** (catalyst). Phosphoric acid gives fewer side products because concentrated sulfuric acid also oxidises some alcohol.

## Mechanism in three arrows

| Step | Arrow from | Arrow to | Species formed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lone pair on O of –OH | H⁺ | R–OH₂⁺ |
| 2 | C–O bond | O | Carbocation + H₂O |
| 3 | C–H bond on a β-carbon | The C–C bond to C⁺ | Alkene + H⁺ (regenerated) |

### Written out for propan-2-ol

```
CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + H⁺   →  CH₃CH(OH₂⁺)CH₃
CH₃CH(OH₂⁺)CH₃      →  CH₃C⁺HCH₃ + H₂O
CH₃C⁺HCH₃           →  CH₂=CHCH₃ + H⁺
```

Both methyl groups are equivalent, so propene is the only product. Propan-1-ol gives propene too: the two alcohols are different, but the alkene is the same.

## Counting the alkenes in steps

1. Find the C–OH carbon.
2. List neighbouring carbons with H.
3. Merge equivalent ones.
4. One position of C=C per different neighbour.
5. Check each alkene for E/Z: both C=C carbons need two different groups.

| Alcohol | Alkenes | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Ethanol | Ethene | 1 |
| Butan-1-ol | But-1-ene | 1 |
| Cyclohexanol | Cyclohexene | 1 |
| 2-methylpropan-2-ol | 2-methylpropene | 1 |
| Pentan-2-ol | Pent-1-ene, (E)- and (Z)-pent-2-ene | 3 |
| 3-methylbutan-2-ol | 3-methylbut-1-ene, 2-methylbut-2-ene | 2 |
| 3-methylpentan-3-ol | 2-ethylbut-1-ene, (E)- and (Z)-3-methylpent-2-ene | 3 |
| 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol | 2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene, 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene | 2 |

**No alkene possible:** methanol (no neighbouring carbon).

## Hydration versus elimination

Reverse reactions, both acid-catalysed, both via carbocations.
**Excess steam → alcohol. Concentrated acid, alkene removed → alkene.**

## Polymers without crude oil

Glucose → (fermentation) → ethanol → (elimination) → ethene → (addition polymerisation) → poly(ethene).
Same polymer as from crude oil; renewable feedstock, but land, farming inputs and distillation energy still count.

## Required practical 5: cyclohexene from cyclohexanol

| Step | Why |
|---|---|
| Heat cyclohexanol with conc. H₃PO₄ and distil | Cyclohexene (b.p. about 83 °C) distils off; cyclohexanol (about 161 °C) stays |
| Shake with Na₂CO₃(aq) in a separating funnel; vent often | Neutralise acid; CO₂ builds pressure |
| Keep the upper layer | Cyclohexene is less dense than water and immiscible |
| Add anhydrous CaCl₂ until clear | Removes water |
| Decant, redistil near 83 °C | Purifies the product |
| Bromine water: orange → colourless | Confirms an alkene |

Safety and apparatus: anti-bumping granules; electric heater, not a flame; thermometer bulb at the side arm; condenser water in at the bottom; never heat a sealed system.

## Exam traps

- "Dilute acid" or "acid" with no concentration.
- Protonation arrow drawn from H⁺ to O.
- Third arrow from the H atom, not the C–H bond.
- Missing an alkene, or counting equivalent β-carbons twice.
- Inventing E/Z isomers for a C=C carbon with two identical groups.
- Organic layer said to be the lower one.
- Percentage yield from masses instead of moles.

## Self-test

1. Name two acids that can catalyse elimination from an alcohol.
2. Write an equation for the elimination of water from propan-1-ol.
3. What is the first step of the mechanism?
4. Where does the H⁺ come from in the final step?
5. Name all the alkenes from butan-2-ol.
6. Why does butan-1-ol give only one alkene?
7. Why does 2-methylpent-2-ene show no E/Z isomerism?
8. Why cannot methanol form an alkene?
9. Which conditions favour hydration rather than elimination?
10. Why is the cyclohexene distillate shaken with sodium carbonate solution?
11. Why is the separating funnel tap opened often?
12. 8.00 g of cyclohexanol (Mᵣ 100.0) gives 4.43 g of cyclohexene (Mᵣ 82.0). Calculate the percentage yield.

### Answers

1. Concentrated sulfuric acid and concentrated phosphoric acid.
2. CH₃CH₂CH₂OH → CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O.
3. A lone pair on the oxygen accepts H⁺, forming R–OH₂⁺.
4. From a C–H bond on a carbon next to the positive carbon.
5. But-1-ene, (E)-but-2-ene and (Z)-but-2-ene.
6. Only one neighbouring carbon (C2) carries hydrogen, so the C=C can form in only one position.
7. One carbon of the C=C (C2) carries two methyl groups.
8. It has only one carbon, so there is no neighbouring carbon to form C=C.
9. Excess steam (water) with the acid catalyst.
10. To neutralise acid carried over in the distillate.
11. To release carbon dioxide pressure.
12. 0.0800 mol in, 0.0540 mol out: **67.5%**.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the acid used up in elimination?**
No. H⁺ protonates the –OH group in the first step and is released in the last step, so it is a catalyst.

**Does elimination always give a mixture?**
Only when the C–OH carbon has two or more different neighbouring carbons carrying H, or when an alkene formed has E/Z isomers.
