## Question 1 — The market mechanism

**(a)** Define equilibrium price and explain what happens if price is set above it. **[3]**

**(b)** Define price elasticity of supply and state one factor that affects it. **[2]**

**(c)** A new, cheaper substitute for coffee appears on the market. Using demand and supply analysis, explain the effect on the equilibrium price and quantity of coffee. **[4]**

**(d)** Explain, with reasons, whether demand for each of the following is likely to be elastic or inelastic: petrol; salt. **[4]**

**(e)** Calculate the price elasticity of supply for a good if a 10% price rise causes a 15% rise in quantity supplied, and state whether supply is elastic or inelastic. **[4]**

**(f)** State one reason why supply might be more elastic in the long run than in the short run. **[3]**

## Question 2 — Elasticity and consumer behaviour

**(a)** Explain, with reasons, whether demand for each of the following is likely to be elastic or inelastic: a particular brand of crisps; foreign holidays. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain what is meant by income elasticity of demand. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the income elasticity of demand (YED) if a 5% rise in income causes an 8% rise in quantity demanded, and classify the good. **[3]**

**(d)** Explain what a negative YED indicates, giving an example. **[3]**

**(e)** Explain the difference between a normal good and an inferior good, using income elasticity of demand. **[4]**

**(f)** State **two** factors, other than income, that could cause a change in demand for a good. **[4]**

## Question 3 — Market failure and government intervention

**(a)** Explain **three** causes of market failure, giving an example of each. **[9]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether an indirect tax is the best way to correct the negative externality caused by tobacco consumption. **[8]**

**(c)** State one alternative policy, other than an indirect tax, that a government could use to correct a negative externality. **[3]**

## Question 4 — Costs, production and factors

**(a)** Explain what is meant by *opportunity cost*, using an example. **[3]**

**(b)** State the four factors of production and the reward corresponding to each. **[4]**

**(c)** State **two** internal economies of scale a large firm might achieve as it expands. **[4]**

**(d)** Explain the difference between economies of scale and diseconomies of scale. **[4]**

**(e)** Explain one reason why a firm might experience diseconomies of scale as it grows very large. **[3]**

**(f)** State one example of a fixed cost and one example of a variable cost for a manufacturing firm. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The **price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied**, so there is no tendency for price to change [1] [1]. Above it there is **excess supply (a surplus)**, so sellers cut prices to clear unsold stock and price falls back towards equilibrium [1].

**(b)** The **responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price**, calculated as %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP [1]. Affected by the **time period, availability of spare capacity, or the ease of storing stock** [1].

**(c)** A cheaper substitute **shifts the demand curve for coffee to the left**, as consumers switch away from coffee [1]; at the original price this creates **excess supply** [1]; the equilibrium **price falls** [1] and the equilibrium **quantity falls** [1].

**(d) Petrol** — **inelastic**: there are **few close substitutes** and most journeys are necessary, so consumers keep buying despite price rises [1] [1]. **Salt** — **inelastic**: it is a **necessity taking a tiny proportion of income** with no substitute, so price changes barely affect the quantity bought [1] [1].

**(e)** PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP = 15 ÷ 10 = **1.5** [2]; since the magnitude exceeds 1, supply is **elastic** [2].

**(f)** In the long run, firms have **more time to adjust their factors of production** (e.g. build new capacity or acquire new machinery), so quantity supplied can respond more fully to a price change than in the short run, when at least one factor is fixed [3].

**2. (a) A particular brand of crisps** — **elastic**: there are **many close substitutes**, so consumers switch brands readily if the price rises [1] [1]. **Foreign holidays** — **elastic**: they are a **luxury taking a large share of income**, and can be postponed or replaced with a domestic holiday [1] [1].

**(b)** Income elasticity of demand measures the **responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in income** [1], calculated as %ΔQd ÷ %Δincome [1].

**(c)** YED = 8 ÷ 5 [1] = **+1.6** [1]; positive and greater than 1, so it is a **normal luxury good** [1].

**(d)** A negative YED means that **as income rises, demand for the good falls** [1] [1]; the good is **inferior**, e.g. bus travel or supermarket own-brand basics, which consumers abandon for better alternatives as they become richer [1].

**(e)** A **normal good** has a **positive** YED — demand rises as income rises [1] [1]. An **inferior good** has a **negative** YED — demand falls as income rises, as consumers switch to preferred alternatives they can now afford [1] [1].

**(f)** Any two: a change in the **price of a substitute or complement good** [1] [1]; a change in **consumer tastes or fashion** [1] [1]; a change in **population** [1] [1]; **advertising** [1] [1].

**3. (a)** Any three, 3 marks each: **Negative externalities** — costs imposed on third parties not reflected in the price, so the good is **overproduced and overconsumed**; e.g. **pollution from a factory** harming local residents' health [1] [1] [1]. **Public goods** — non-excludable and non-rival, so the **free rider problem means the market provides none**; e.g. **national defence or flood barriers** [1] [1] [1]. **Information failure** — consumers **do not know the true costs or benefits** of a good, so they consume the wrong amount; e.g. **smoking**, where the long-term health cost is underestimated, or **education**, whose benefits are underestimated [1] [1] [1]. (Also accept monopoly power, factor immobility, inequality.)

**(b) For an indirect tax** [3]: it **raises the price**, causing a contraction in demand and reducing consumption [1]; it **internalises the external cost**, making the polluter or consumer pay for the harm imposed on others [1]; it **raises government revenue** which can be spent on healthcare or on anti-smoking campaigns [1].
**Against** [3]: demand for tobacco is **highly inelastic and addictive**, so a large tax causes only a **small fall in consumption** — the main effect is a transfer of income to the government [1]; the tax is **regressive**, taking a larger share of income from the poor (who are also more likely to smoke), and a high tax also risks encouraging **smuggling and a black market**, which reduces revenue and puts consumption beyond regulation [1]; the **correct level of tax is very difficult to calculate**, since the external cost cannot be measured precisely [1].
**Judgement** [2]: a tax is **useful but insufficient on its own** [1]; because demand is inelastic it works best **combined with information campaigns, advertising bans and age restrictions**, which shift demand leftwards rather than relying on price alone [1].

**(c)** Any one: **regulation** (e.g. banning sale to under-18s or restricting advertising) [3]; **provision of information** (e.g. public health campaigns) [3]; **direct provision** of substitute goods or support services [3].

**4. (a) Opportunity cost** is the **next best alternative given up** when a choice is made [1] [1]; e.g. spending an hour revising economics means giving up the chance to revise a different subject in that hour [1].

**(b) Land — rent** [1]; **labour — wages** [1]; **capital — interest** [1]; **enterprise — profit** [1].

**(c)** Any two, 2 marks each, all internal economies of scale: **purchasing economies** — buying raw materials in bulk at a lower cost per unit [1] [1]; **technical economies** — large-scale machinery and production techniques lower the cost per unit [1] [1]; **financial economies** — larger firms can borrow more cheaply [1] [1]; **marketing economies** — advertising cost is spread over a much larger output [1] [1]; **managerial economies** — larger firms can afford specialist managers [1] [1]; **risk-bearing economies** — a larger firm can spread risk across a wider range of products or markets [1] [1].

**(d) Economies of scale** are **falling long-run average costs as output rises** [1] [1]. **Diseconomies of scale** are **rising long-run average costs as output rises**, once a firm has grown beyond its most efficient size [1] [1].

**(e)** As a firm grows very large, **communication and coordination between managers and workers can break down**, since decisions must pass through more layers and information can be lost or delayed, raising costs per unit [3].

**(f)** Any valid example, e.g. **fixed cost** — rent on a factory (does not change with output) [1]; **variable cost** — raw materials (changes directly with output) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying elasticity depends on whether the good is "cheap".
- Confusing income elasticity with price elasticity.
- Giving an example of market failure without explaining the mechanism.
- Not addressing inelastic demand when evaluating a tax on tobacco.
- Explaining a market change without stating all four steps: which curve shifts, which direction, the new equilibrium, and the effect on both price and quantity.
- Confusing capital (machinery and equipment) with money in the factors of production.
- Confusing economies of scale (falling costs from growth) with diseconomies of scale (rising costs from excessive growth).
