## Section A

**1.** State what the letters MARPOL stand for. **[1]**

**2.** Name **two** causes of oil pollution that can happen during routine operations rather than accidents. **[2]**

**3.** State how a double-hulled tanker reduces the risk of an oil spill. **[1]**

**4.** Name the strategy that uses floating barriers to stop a slick reaching the coast. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why oil on the shore reduces the growth of seaweeds. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** impacts of oil pollution on a tourist beach. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Sort these six strategies into those that **prevent** oil spills and those that **minimise the impacts** of spills: risk assessments, skimmers, regular maintenance, controlled burning, MARPOL, sorbents. **[2]**

**8.** A tanker spills 12,000 tonnes of oil. The table shows what happened to the oil in the first two weeks. The figures are invented for practice.

| Fate of oil | Percentage of spill |
|---|---|
| Evaporated | 25 |
| Recovered by skimmers | 10 |
| Removed by controlled burning | 15 |
| Dispersed by detergent sprays | 20 |
| Washed ashore | the remainder |

(a) Calculate the mass of oil washed ashore. Show your working. **[2]**
(b) Explain why oil dispersed by detergent sprays can still harm marine organisms. **[2]**

**9.** An oil spill reaches a tropical coast with a coral reef offshore. Explain how the oil could affect:
(a) the coral reef **[2]**
(b) fish living on the reef. **[2]**

**10.** An oil company operates a pipeline across the seabed to an on-shore refinery. Explain how risk assessments and regular maintenance could prevent oil leaking from the pipeline. **[4]**

**11.** Two spills occur on the same day. Spill A is in stormy open ocean, 200 km from land. Spill B is a small, thin slick in a calm, sheltered harbour.
(a) Suggest the most suitable strategy for Spill A and explain your choice. **[2]**
(b) Suggest the most suitable strategy for Spill B and explain your choice. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of prevention strategies compared with strategies that minimise the impacts of oil spills. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships **[1]**.

**2.** Cleaning of tanks at sea **[1]**; leaks or waste water from refineries, or slow leaks from pipelines **[1]**.

**3.** If the outer hull is damaged, the inner hull still holds the oil, so it does not escape **[1]**.

**4.** Booms **[1]**.

**5.** Oil coats the seaweed and blocks light **[1]**, so photosynthesis is reduced and the seaweed grows less or dies **[1]**.

**6.** Sand and rocks are coated with oil and organisms living in the sand die **[1]**; tourists stay away, so local businesses lose income, and clean-up is costly **[1]**.

**7.** Prevention: risk assessments, regular maintenance and MARPOL, all three correct **[1]**. Minimising impacts: skimmers, controlled burning and sorbents, all three correct **[1]**.

**8.** (a) 25 + 10 + 15 + 20 = 70%, so 30% is washed ashore **[1]**; 12,000 × 0.30 = 3,600 tonnes **[1]**.
(b) The oil is not removed; it is broken into droplets that stay in the water **[1]**, where fish, their eggs and filter-feeding animals take them in, and the detergent itself can be toxic **[1]**.

**9.** (a) Oil and droplets smother the corals and block light **[1]**; toxic compounds damage coral tissue, so corals may die **[1]**.
(b) Oil coats the gills of fish, reducing oxygen uptake, and kills eggs and young fish **[1]**; damage to the coral removes the fish's food and shelter, so fish populations on the reef decline **[1]**.

**10.** A risk assessment identifies hazards along the pipeline, such as storms, ships' anchors or areas prone to corrosion **[1]**, so the company can reroute, bury or protect the pipe and prepare emergency shut-off plans **[1]**. Regular maintenance inspects the pipe for corrosion and worn joints **[1]**, so weak sections are repaired or replaced before they leak **[1]**.

**11.** (a) Detergent sprays from aircraft or ships **[1]**, because booms and skimmers fail in rough seas, and the spill is far from coral and shores, so dispersed droplets are diluted in deep water **[1]**. Controlled burning is unsuitable here, because it needs calm conditions and thick oil.
(b) Booms with sorbents or skimmers **[1]**, because calm, sheltered water lets booms hold the thin slick in place while it is soaked up or skimmed, avoiding chemicals near the shore **[1]**.

**12.** One mark per point; at least one benefit and one limitation of each type and a judgement for full marks.
- Prevention benefit: stops oil entering the sea at all, so no organisms are harmed and no clean-up is needed **[1]**.
- Prevention benefit: MARPOL applies to ships worldwide and reduces routine pollution such as tank cleaning, not only accidents **[1]**.
- Prevention limitation: double hulls, maintenance and risk assessments are expensive, and rules are hard to enforce on the open sea, so some spills still happen **[1]**.
- Minimising benefit: booms, skimmers and burning reduce how much oil reaches coasts, coral reefs and wildlife after a spill **[1]**.
- Minimising limitation: each method only works in certain conditions, they recover only part of the oil, and some, such as detergents and burning, cause further pollution **[1]**.
- Judgement: for example, prevention is more effective and cheaper over time, but minimising strategies are still needed because no prevention system is perfect **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 3**: saying a double hull is "stronger" without explaining the inner hull keeps the oil in.
- **Question 7**: placing improved methods such as skimmers under prevention.
- **Question 8(a)**: forgetting to subtract all four fates before converting to tonnes.
- **Question 11**: choosing a strategy without linking it to the sea conditions given.
- **Question 12**: describing strategies without comparing the two types.
