These questions cover the additional higher level part of sub-topic D.2, Electric and magnetic fields, from the IB *Physics guide* (first assessment 2025). Every question is HL only. Use k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻² and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C unless a question gives other data. If a topic is new, read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-physics-electric-potential-energy-potential-and-equipotentials/) first.

## Questions

**1.** A charge of –1.2 nC and a charge of +2.5 nC are 6.0 cm apart in a vacuum. Calculate the electric potential energy of the pair and state what its sign shows. **[2]**

**2.** Define the electric potential at a point in an electric field, and state one property that shows it is a scalar quantity. **[3]**

**3.** A point charge of –8.0 nC is fixed in a vacuum.
**(a)** Calculate the electric potential 0.50 m from the charge. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the work done by an external agent in bringing a charge of +3.0 nC from infinity to this point, and comment on the sign. **[2]**

**4.** Two parallel plates are 15 mm apart with a potential difference of 900 V. The negative plate is at 0 V.
**(a)** Calculate the electric field strength between the plates, away from the edges. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the electric potential at a point 5.0 mm from the negative plate. **[2]**

**5.** An alpha particle (charge +2e, mass 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) is released from rest and accelerated through a potential difference of 1.2 kV.
**(a)** Calculate the kinetic energy gained, in joules. **[2]**
**(b)** State this energy in electronvolts. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the final speed. **[2]**

**6.** An isolated solid conducting sphere of radius 0.12 m carries a charge of +25 nC.
**(a)** Calculate the electric potential at its surface. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the potential 0.30 m from the centre. **[1]**
**(c)** State the potential and the electric field strength at the centre of the sphere. **[2]**

**7.** Two point charges of equal size and opposite sign are fixed a short distance apart.
**(a)** Describe the equipotential halfway between them. **[1]**
**(b)** State the angle between the electric field lines and the equipotential surfaces. Use W = qΔVe to explain why this must be so. **[3]**

**8.** The table gives the electric potential at distances r from a point charge of +2.0 nC in a vacuum.

| r / m | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.30 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ve / V | 180 | 90 | 60 |

**(a)** Use the values at 0.10 m and 0.30 m to estimate the field strength at r = 0.20 m. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the field strength at r = 0.20 m from Ve = kQ/r and E = –ΔVe/Δr. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the estimate in (a) differs from the value in (b). **[2]**

**9.** Three point charges lie in a straight line, 0.10 m apart: +2.0 nC, +2.0 nC and –1.0 nC, in that order.
**(a)** Calculate the total electric potential energy of the system. **[4]**
**(b)** Interpret the sign of your answer in terms of the work done in assembling the system. **[2]**

**10.** A charge of +4.0 nC and a charge of –4.0 nC are 0.60 m apart in a vacuum. Point P lies on the line joining them, 0.20 m from the positive charge.
**(a)** Calculate the electric potential at P. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the work done by an external agent in bringing a +3.0 nC charge from infinity to P. **[2]**
**(c)** State the potential at every point on the plane halfway between the two charges, and explain why the electric field strength there is not zero. **[2]**
**(d)** State the direction of the field at the midpoint of the line. **[1]**

**11.** Two protons are held 2.0 nm apart in a vacuum, then released from rest. The proton mass is 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
**(a)** Calculate the electric potential energy of the pair at release. **[2]**
**(b)** Express this energy in electronvolts. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the speed of each proton when they are very far apart. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why the two speeds are equal. **[2]**

**12.** An electron gains 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ J of kinetic energy in an accelerator. State this energy in electronvolts and the potential difference through which the electron moved from rest. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Ep = kq₁q₂/r = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (–1.2 × 10⁻⁹) × (2.5 × 10⁻⁹) / 0.060 [1] = **–4.5 × 10⁻⁷ J**. The negative sign shows the charges attract, so work must be done to separate them [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative Ep with no comment on its meaning leaves the question half answered.

**2.** The electric potential at a point equals the work done for each coulomb of charge [1] in carrying a small positive test charge in from infinity to that point [1]. It is a scalar because it has size and sign but no direction, so potentials from several charges add as numbers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that leaves out "from infinity" or "per unit charge" is incomplete; include both, and say the test charge is positive.

**3. (a)** Ve = kQ/r = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (–8.0 × 10⁻⁹) / 0.50 [1] = **–1.4 × 10² V** (–144 V) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** W = qΔVe = 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ × (–144) [1] = **–4.3 × 10⁻⁷ J**. The negative sign shows that the field attracts the positive charge, so the agent's work is negative [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the sign of Q through both parts; a positive answer to (b) contradicts the attraction.

**4. (a)** E = V/d = 900 / 0.015 [1] = **6.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The gradient is constant, so Ve = E × 0.0050 [1] = **3.0 × 10² V** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert millimetres to metres before substituting, and say in (b) that the distance is measured from the 0 V plate.

**5. (a)** KE = qΔV = 2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1200 [1] = **3.8 × 10⁻¹⁶ J** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 2 × 1200 = **2400 eV** (2.4 keV) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** ½mv² = 3.84 × 10⁻¹⁶, so v = √(2 × 3.84 × 10⁻¹⁶ / 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷) [1] = **3.4 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting the charge 2e changes both answers. Work in joules before using the mass.

**6. (a)** Ve = kQ/R = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 25 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.12 [1] = **1.9 × 10³ V** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Outside, the sphere acts as a point charge at its centre: Ve = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 25 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.30 = **7.5 × 10² V** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The potential is **1.9 × 10³ V**, the same as the surface [1], and the field strength is **zero** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving zero potential at the centre is a frequent slip. State potential and field separately.

**7. (a)** The plane halfway between them is at **zero potential** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The field lines cross equipotential surfaces at **90°** [1]. No work is done moving a charge along an equipotential because ΔVe = 0, so W = qΔVe = 0 [1]. A field component along the surface would do work on a charge moved along it, so the field must be perpendicular to the surface [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking W = qΔVe to the absence of a field component along the surface is stronger than quoting the 90° rule alone.

**8. (a)** Gradient = (60 – 180) / (0.30 – 0.10) = –600 V m⁻¹ [1], so E ≈ **600 V m⁻¹**, directed away from the charge [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Ve = kQ/r gives –ΔVe/Δr = kQ/r² [1] = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.20² = **4.5 × 10² V m⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The graph of Ve against r is curved, so the gradient is not constant [1]. It is steeper near 0.10 m than at 0.20 m, so the average over the interval is larger than the value at the midpoint [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the curvature of the graph as the cause, not measurement error.

**9. (a)** There are three pairs [1]. Ep for the two +2.0 nC charges = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (2.0 × 10⁻⁹)² / 0.10 = +3.6 × 10⁻⁷ J [1]. Each +2.0 nC with –1.0 nC: –1.8 × 10⁻⁷ J at 0.10 m and –9.0 × 10⁻⁸ J at 0.20 m [1]. Total = **9.0 × 10⁻⁸ J** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** The total is positive, so the external agent does net positive work of 9.0 × 10⁻⁸ J to assemble the system from infinity [1], because repulsion between the like pair outweighs attraction in the unlike pairs [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* List each pair separately before adding. The 0.20 m pair is the one most often left out.

**10. (a)** The distance from the negative charge is 0.40 m [1]. Ve = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (4.0 × 10⁻⁹/0.20 – 4.0 × 10⁻⁹/0.40) [1] = **90 V** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** W = qΔVe = 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 89.9 [1] = **2.7 × 10⁻⁷ J** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The potential is **zero**: every point on the plane is the same distance from both charges, so the two contributions cancel [1]. The field is not zero because potential is a scalar and cancels, while the two field vectors point the same way and add [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** From the positive charge towards the negative charge [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Using a single distance for both charges at P is the commonest slip. Say that potential is a scalar when explaining why zero potential still allows a field.

**11. (a)** Ep = ke²/r = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² / 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ [1] = **1.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 1.15 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **0.72 eV** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** All of Ep becomes kinetic energy of two protons: 2 × ½mv² = Ep [1]. v = √(Ep/m) = √(1.15 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) [1] = **8.3 × 10³ m s⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** The forces on the protons are equal and opposite [1] and the masses are equal, so the accelerations and speeds are equal [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The energy is shared between two particles. Giving all of Ep to one proton overestimates the speed by a factor of √2.

**12.** Energy in eV = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **2.0 × 10⁴ eV** [1]. The electron has charge e, so the potential difference is **2.0 × 10⁴ V** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division by e so the conversion to eV is visible.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dropping the sign of a charge in Ep or Ve, so an attractive system seems to have positive energy.
- Missing one pair when assembling three or more charges.
- Not converting cm, mm or nm to metres.
- Using r² in the potential or potential energy formula.
- Adding potentials as vectors.
- Claiming that zero potential and zero field go together.
- Giving the potential inside a conducting sphere as zero rather than the surface value.
- Giving all the energy to one particle in a two-body release.
