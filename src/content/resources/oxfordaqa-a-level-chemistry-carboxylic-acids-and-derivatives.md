## 3.3.9.1 Carboxylic acids and esters

### Structures

A **carboxylic acid** has the –COOH group: a carbon carrying both a C=O and an O–H. That carbon is C1 of the chain, and the name ends in **-oic acid**. Butanoic acid is CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH.

An **ester** has the –COO– link between two carbon groups. Its name has two words: the alcohol part first (methyl, ethyl, propyl...), then the acid part ending **-oate**. The chain holding the C=O carbon is always the acid part.

### Worked example 1: naming and splitting an ester

Name CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ and give the acid and alcohol that form it.

1. Find the C=O carbon. CH₃CH₂CH₂CO– has four carbons, so the acid part is **butanoate**.
2. The group on the single-bonded oxygen is –CH₂CH₂CH₃, so the alcohol part is **propyl**.
3. Name: **propyl butanoate**. It forms from **butanoic acid** and **propan-1-ol**.

### Weak acids that still release CO₂

Only a small fraction of carboxylic acid molecules ionise in water, which makes them **weak acids**:

`CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH ⇌ CH₃CH₂CH₂COO⁻ + H⁺`

Even so, it releases CO₂ from carbonates: you see fizzing, and the gas turns limewater milky.

### Worked example 2: methanoic acid and magnesium carbonate

Write the equation.

1. Methanoic acid is HCOOH, so the salt contains the methanoate ion, HCOO⁻.
2. Mg²⁺ needs two methanoate ions, so you need two acid molecules.

`2HCOOH + MgCO₃ → (HCOO)₂Mg + CO₂ + H₂O`

### Making esters

Warming a carboxylic acid with an alcohol and an **acid catalyst** (usually a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid) gives an ester and water. The reaction is reversible, so you write ⇌ and the yield stays below 100%.

### Worked example 3: esterification yield

A student heats 5.28 g of butanoic acid with excess propan-1-ol and an acid catalyst, and collects 4.68 g of propyl butanoate. Calculate the percentage yield. (Ar: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0)

1. Equation: CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH + CH₃CH₂CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ + H₂O. The ratio is 1 : 1.
2. Mr of butanoic acid, C₄H₈O₂ = 88.0. Amount = 5.28 ÷ 88.0 = 0.0600 mol.
3. Mr of propyl butanoate, C₇H₁₄O₂ = 130.0. Maximum mass = 0.0600 × 130.0 = 7.80 g.
4. Yield = 4.68 ÷ 7.80 × 100 = **60.0%**.

### Uses of esters

The specification names the common uses: **solvents**, **plasticisers**, **perfumes** and **food flavourings**.

### Oils and fats

Every vegetable oil and animal fat is an **ester of propane-1,2,3-triol**, also called **glycerol**, HOCH₂CH(OH)CH₂OH. Each of its three –OH groups is esterified by a long-chain carboxylic acid, giving three ester links per molecule.

### Hydrolysis of esters

Hydrolysis splits the ester link, in acid or in alkali.

| Conditions | Products from propyl butanoate | Extent |
|---|---|---|
| Heat under reflux with dilute acid | Butanoic acid + propan-1-ol | Reversible |
| Heat under reflux with NaOH(aq) | Sodium butanoate + propan-1-ol | Goes to completion |

`CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃CH₂CH₂COONa + CH₃CH₂CH₂OH`

In alkali the acid ends up as its carboxylate ion, which does not react with the alcohol, so the ester cannot re-form. Add a strong acid afterwards to recover the free acid.

  <figcaption>Acid hydrolysis is reversible; alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion and gives a salt. All boards.</figcaption>

### Soap, glycerol and biodiesel

**Soap.** When an oil or fat is hydrolysed by **alkali**, the products are **glycerol** and **soap**, made up of the salts of long-chain carboxylic acids.

**Biodiesel** consists of several **methyl esters of long-chain carboxylic acids**. To make it, a vegetable oil is reacted with **methanol**, with a **catalyst** present. Each acid chain becomes a methyl ester, and glycerol is released.

### Worked example 4: one fat, two products

Glyceryl tritetradecanoate, (C₁₃H₂₇COO)₃C₃H₅, has Mr 722.0. Separate 28.88 g samples are (a) boiled with excess NaOH(aq) and (b) reacted with methanol and a catalyst. Calculate the maximum mass of sodium tetradecanoate (Mr 250.0), of methyl tetradecanoate (Mr 242.0) and of glycerol (Mr 92.0) from one sample.

1. Amount of fat = 28.88 ÷ 722.0 = 0.0400 mol.
2. (C₁₃H₂₇COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3NaOH → 3C₁₃H₂₇COONa + C₃H₅(OH)₃
3. Sodium tetradecanoate = 3 × 0.0400 = 0.120 mol, so mass = 0.120 × 250.0 = **30.0 g**.
4. (C₁₃H₂₇COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3CH₃OH → 3C₁₃H₂₇COOCH₃ + C₃H₅(OH)₃
5. Methyl tetradecanoate = 0.120 mol, so mass = 0.120 × 242.0 = **29.0 g**.
6. Glycerol = 0.0400 mol in either reaction, so mass = 0.0400 × 92.0 = **3.68 g**.

## 3.3.9.2 Acylation

### Structures

| Family | Functional group | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Acyl chloride | –COCl | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂COCl, pentanoyl chloride |
| Acid anhydride | –CO–O–CO– | (CH₃CH₂CH₂CO)₂O, butanoic anhydride |
| Amide | –CONH₂, or –CONHR from a primary amine | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CONH₂, pentanamide |

An acyl group is R–CO–. Acylation replaces an H on O or N with an acyl group.

### The four nucleophiles

Each of these two reagent families reacts with four nucleophiles: **water, alcohols, ammonia and primary amines**. The specification calls these **nucleophilic addition–elimination** reactions. An acyl chloride releases HCl; an acid anhydride releases a carboxylic acid.

| Nucleophile | Pentanoyl chloride | Butanoic anhydride |
|---|---|---|
| Water | C₄H₉COCl + H₂O → C₄H₉COOH + HCl | (C₃H₇CO)₂O + H₂O → 2C₃H₇COOH |
| Propan-1-ol | C₄H₉COCl + C₃H₇OH → C₄H₉COOC₃H₇ + HCl | (C₃H₇CO)₂O + C₃H₇OH → C₃H₇COOC₃H₇ + C₃H₇COOH |
| Ammonia | C₄H₉COCl + 2NH₃ → C₄H₉CONH₂ + NH₄Cl | (C₃H₇CO)₂O + 2NH₃ → C₃H₇CONH₂ + C₃H₇COONH₄ |
| Ethylamine | C₄H₉COCl + 2C₂H₅NH₂ → C₄H₉CONHC₂H₅ + C₂H₅NH₃Cl | (C₃H₇CO)₂O + 2C₂H₅NH₂ → C₃H₇CONHC₂H₅ + C₃H₇COO⁻ ⁺H₃NC₂H₅ |

C₄H₉ and C₃H₇ are straight chains. The anhydride route makes propyl butanoate without the equilibrium that limited Worked example 3. With ammonia or an amine, a **second** molecule acts as a base and takes up the acid by-product.

### Mechanism: nucleophilic addition–elimination

You must outline this mechanism for **acyl chlorides** with all four nucleophiles. The carbonyl carbon is strongly δ+ because O and Cl both draw electrons from it. The nucleophile attacks with a lone pair on O (water, alcohols) or N (ammonia, amines).

<figure class="topic-figure">
  <img src="https://img.marlbridge.com/aqa-a-level-chemistry-carboxylic-acids-and-derivatives--08-addition-elimination.webp" width="900" height="1210" alt="Step 1: a lone pair on water oxygen attacks the delta-positive carbonyl carbon and the C=O electrons move onto oxygen, giving an intermediate with O minus and O plus. Step 2: a lone pair on O minus re-forms C=O and the C-Cl bond breaks, so chloride leaves. Step 3: chloride removes H plus from the O plus to give HCl and ethanoic acid." loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Curly arrows start at a lone pair or a bond; the AQA name is nucleophilic addition-elimination.</figcaption>
</figure>


### Worked example 5: pentanoyl chloride and ethylamine

A second ethylamine removes a proton to form N-ethylpentanamide and ethylammonium chloride." loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="display: block; width: 100%; height: auto;" /></a>
  <figcaption>The nucleophilic addition–elimination mechanism accounts for the charged intermediate and the second ethylamine molecule needed to neutralise the acid by-product. Select the image to enlarge it.</figcaption>
</figure>
<!-- /marlbridge-diagram:chem-b26-pentanoyl-ethylamine-addition-elimination -->


```
Addition
  arrow 1: lone pair on N of CH3CH2NH2  →  carbonyl C (δ+)
  arrow 2: C=O π bond                   →  O, which becomes O⁻

  intermediate:  C4H9–C(O⁻)(Cl)–N⁺H2CH2CH3

Elimination
  arrow 3: lone pair on O⁻  →  re-forms C=O
  arrow 4: C–Cl bond        →  Cl, which leaves as Cl⁻
  arrow 5: a second CH3CH2NH2 removes H⁺ from N⁺

  products:  C4H9CONHCH2CH3 (N-ethylpentanamide) + CH3CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻
```

Each arrow begins at a bond or a lone pair. The same addition–elimination sequence applies to all four nucleophiles: a lone pair on O attacks for water or an alcohol, and a lone pair on N attacks for ammonia or a primary amine; the C=O π pair moves to O; O⁻ reforms C=O as the C–Cl bond pair leaves; then a proton is transferred from the oxonium/ammonium product. Water gives a carboxylic acid, an alcohol an ester, ammonia a primary amide and a primary amine an N-substituted amide. For water/alcohol reactions the proton transfer may be mediated by solvent or another base; the net by-product is HCl. For ammonia/amine reactions a second nucleophile molecule commonly removes the proton and binds HCl as its ammonium chloride salt. Do not treat free Cl⁻ as the only possible proton acceptor in every medium.

### Aspirin: why industry uses ethanoic anhydride

Aspirin is made by acylating the –OH group of 2-hydroxybenzoic acid:

`HOC₆H₄COOH + (CH₃CO)₂O → CH₃COOC₆H₄COOH + CH₃COOH`

Ethanoic anhydride is preferred to ethanoyl chloride because it:

- is **cheaper**
- is **less corrosive**
- is **less readily hydrolysed**, so it copes better with water and damp air, making storage and handling easier
- reacts **less vigorously**, so the process is easier to control
- produces ethanoic acid, not **corrosive, toxic hydrogen chloride gas**, as its by-product.

1. Filter off the crude solid under reduced pressure.
2. Dissolve it in the **minimum volume of hot solvent**, so the solution is saturated and most product crystallises on cooling.
3. Filter the hot solution to remove any insoluble impurities.
4. Cool the filtrate. Crystals form; the small amounts of soluble impurities remain dissolved.
5. Collect the crystals by filtration under reduced pressure and wash with a little **cold** solvent, which rinses off impure solution without dissolving much product.
6. Dry the crystals.

### Testing purity

Compare its melting point with the data-book value. A **pure** solid melts **sharply**, over a narrow range, at the data-book value. Impurities **lower** the melting point and make it melt over a **wider range**.

### Worked example 6: yield after purification

A student acylates 4.14 g of 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (Mr 138.0) with excess ethanoic anhydride. After recrystallisation, 4.05 g of dry aspirin (Mr 180.0) is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield.

1. Amount of 2-hydroxybenzoic acid = 4.14 ÷ 138.0 = 0.0300 mol.
2. The ratio is 1 : 1, so maximum mass of aspirin = 0.0300 × 180.0 = 5.40 g.
3. Yield = 4.05 ÷ 5.40 × 100 = **75.0%**.

Some product always stays dissolved in the cold solvent.

## Common errors

- Naming an ester backwards. The alcohol part comes first: CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ is butyl ethanoate, not ethyl butanoate.
- Calling a carboxylic acid strong because it fizzes with a carbonate. It is weak because it dissociates only partly.
- Giving the free acid as the product of alkaline hydrolysis. You get its salt.
- Treating fat and soap (or methyl ester) as 1 : 1. Each fat molecule gives three.
- Writing HCl as a product when an acid anhydride reacts. The by-product is a carboxylic acid.
- Using one NH₃ or one amine molecule per acyl chloride. You need two.
- Calling the mechanism nucleophilic substitution, or leaving the charges off the intermediate.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are carboxylic acids weak acids?**
Only a small fraction of carboxylic acid molecules ionise in water, so they are weak acids. They still release carbon dioxide from carbonates, giving fizzing and a gas that turns limewater milky.

**What are esters used for?**
The OxfordAQA 9620 specification names the common uses of esters as solvents, plasticisers, perfumes and food flavourings.

**What are fats and oils made of?**
Every vegetable oil and animal fat is a triester of propane-1,2,3-triol, also called glycerol, with each of its three –OH groups esterified by a long-chain carboxylic acid.

**What is the mechanism for acyl chlorides reacting with nucleophiles?**
It is nucleophilic addition–elimination: a lone pair on the nucleophile attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon and the C=O electrons move on to oxygen, then the C=O re-forms and Cl⁻ leaves, and finally H⁺ is lost. You must be able to outline it with water, alcohols, ammonia and primary amines.
