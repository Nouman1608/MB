## What the current account records

The **balance of payments** records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world. The **current account** covers trade in goods and services and flows of income. Every transaction is either a **credit** (money coming in) or a **debit** (money going out).

| Part | Credit example (UK) | Debit example (UK) |
|---|---|---|
| Trade in goods | A UK-made car sold in Germany | Bananas bought from Costa Rica |
| Trade in services | A French firm insures cargo with a London insurer | A UK family stays in a hotel in Portugal |
| Primary income | Profit earned by a UK firm's factory abroad, sent home | Interest paid to foreign owners of UK bonds |
| Secondary income | Money sent home by UK citizens working abroad | UK government overseas aid |

**Sorting trick.** Ask two questions: is money coming in or going out? Is it payment for a good, a service, income from owning assets or working (primary), or a transfer with nothing in return (secondary)?

## Balanced, surplus, deficit

- **Balanced**: credits = debits, a balance of zero.
- **Surplus**: credits > debits. More money flows in from trade and income than flows out.
- **Deficit**: debits > credits. More flows out than in, so the country must borrow from abroad or sell assets to pay for the difference.

## Worked example 1: the full current account

Country U, one year, £bn:

| Part | Credits | Debits | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods | 380 | 470 | −90 |
| Services | 300 | 180 | +120 |
| Primary income | 140 | 175 | −35 |
| Secondary income | 15 | 40 | −25 |
| **Current account** | **835** | **865** | **−30** |

- The **trade balance** (goods and services) is −90 + 120 = **+£30bn**, a surplus.
- Net income flows (primary + secondary) are −35 − 25 = **−£60bn**.
- So the current account is in **deficit by £30bn** even though trade is in surplus.
- With GDP of £2,400bn, the deficit is 30 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **1.25% of GDP**.

**Finding a missing value.** If you know the current account balance is −£30bn and every figure except services credits, rearrange: services credits = total debits − 30 − other credits = 865 − 30 − 535 = £300bn.

## Analysing export and import data

Country U, exports and imports of goods and services, £bn:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exports | 600 | 630 | 645 | 610 | 660 | 700 |
| Imports | 640 | 660 | 700 | 650 | 690 | 710 |
| Trade balance | −40 | −30 | −55 | −40 | −30 | −10 |
| Exports as % of imports | 93.8 | 95.5 | 92.1 | 93.8 | 95.7 | 98.6 |

What a strong analysis says:

- Exports rose **16.7%** from Year 1 to Year 6; imports rose **10.9%**. Faster export growth narrowed the deficit from £40bn to £10bn.
- The deficit widened sharply in **Year 3** (to £55bn), when imports jumped by £40bn while exports barely grew.
- In **Year 4** both exports and imports fell, which suggests a downturn at home and abroad: households bought fewer imports and foreign buyers bought fewer exports.
- "Exports as % of imports" shows how far export earnings pay for imports: by Year 6 exports covered **98.6%**.

Always state whether figures are for goods, services or both, and whether they are in money or as a share of GDP.

## Causes of deficits and surpluses

| Cause | How it leads to a deficit | Opposite effect (surplus) |
|---|---|---|
| Strong exchange rate | Exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper at home | Weak currency makes exports cheaper |
| Higher inflation than trading partners | Domestic goods become less price competitive | Lower inflation improves competitiveness |
| Fast growth in domestic incomes | Households and firms buy more imports | Slow domestic growth cuts imports |
| Slow growth abroad | Foreign buyers cut demand for exports | Fast growth abroad raises export demand |
| Low productivity or poor quality | Exports lose sales on price or quality | High productivity and quality win sales |
| Lack of natural resources | Fuel or materials must be imported | Resource-rich countries export them |

**Evaluating causes.** Ask whether the cause is **temporary** (a boom at home, a short-lived strong currency) or **structural** (weak competitiveness, lack of resources). Temporary causes tend to correct themselves; structural ones need policy.

## The UK pattern

The UK typically runs a **deficit in trade in goods** and a **surplus in trade in services**, reflecting its strength in finance, insurance, education and business services. Overall, the UK has had a current account deficit in most years for several decades. Use the exam source for any figures.

## Evaluating the importance of the current account to the UK

**Reasons it matters**
- A persistent deficit must be financed by borrowing from abroad or selling UK assets to foreign owners, so future income may flow out as interest and profit.
- It may signal weak competitiveness in industries that export.
- A large deficit could put downward pressure on the pound, raising import prices and inflation.

**Reasons it may matter less**
- A deficit financed by foreign firms investing in UK factories and offices can raise future output.
- If imports are machinery, the deficit may fund investment rather than consumption.
- A deficit of 1-2% of GDP is far easier to sustain than one of 6%.
- Other objectives, such as inflation and unemployment, may be more pressing in a given year.

**Judgement pattern.** The current account matters most when the deficit is large, long-lasting and caused by weak competitiveness. It matters least when it is small and reflects investment that will raise future exports.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether a current account deficit is a problem for an economy.*

"A deficit means more money flows out for imports and income than flows in, so the country must borrow from abroad or sell assets, which adds to future interest and profit payments overseas. **[consequence]** If the deficit is caused by low productivity, firms are losing sales to foreign competitors, so jobs in exporting industries may be lost. **[cause linked to harm]** However, if the deficit reflects strong growth at home, with firms importing machinery to expand, it may raise future output and exports. **[counter-argument]** A small deficit, such as 1% of GDP, is easy to finance. **[size]** So a deficit is a problem when it is large, persistent and caused by weak competitiveness, but not when it is small and funds investment." **[judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Confusing a current account deficit with a government budget deficit.
- Putting tourism in goods rather than services.
- Treating primary income as a "transfer".
- Saying a deficit is always bad.
- Describing data without quoting figures or calculating changes.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: lay out credits, debits and balance for each part; keep signs.
- **Analyse**: quote figures, give growth rates, identify turning points, and link to a cause.
- **Evaluate**: weigh size, persistence and cause, and apply to the UK when asked.
