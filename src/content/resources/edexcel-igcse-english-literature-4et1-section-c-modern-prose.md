This guide goes deeper on one part of Component 1 of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Literature (4ET1): **Section C, Modern Prose**, the last and highest-tariff section of Paper 1. For the
paper as a whole, start with the
[Poetry and Modern Prose study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-poetry-and-modern-prose/).

## What Section C covers

You study **one** novel from the specification's modern prose list and answer **one essay question
from a choice of two** on that text. The section is worth **40 marks**, the largest single tariff on
Paper 1 (about 44% of the paper), and the paper recommends **45 minutes**.

The set list currently has seven titles: *To Kill a Mockingbird*, *Of Mice and Men*, *The Whale
Rider*, *The Joy Luck Club*, *Things Fall Apart*, *Klara and the Sun* and *Western Lane*, the last
two added for first assessment in 2026. Set texts change when Pearson revises the specification, so
confirm the list for your own series.

Section C is the **closed-book** part of Paper 1: you do not have the novel in the exam. That shapes
everything about how you revise.

## The two assessment objectives

- **AO1**: demonstrate a close knowledge and understanding of the text, maintaining a critical style
  and presenting an informed personal engagement. In practice: a clear argument that answers the
  question, supported by precise reference to the whole novel.
- **AO4**: show understanding of the relationship between the text and the contexts in which it was
  written. In practice: explaining how social, historical or cultural circumstances shape what the
  writer shows and why.

AO2 is not one of the objectives for this section, so analysis of the writer's methods earns credit
when it supports your argument, not as a separate list of techniques. "Steinbeck uses foreshadowing"
is only useful if it helps answer the question.

## Key idea 1: an argument across the whole novel

Questions usually focus on a **character**, a **theme**, a **relationship** or a **setting**. A strong
answer tracks that focus across the novel rather than describing one scene. A reliable shape is three
or four moments:

1. **Opening position**: how the character or theme is first established.
2. **Turning point**: an event that changes or tests it.
3. **Development**: how the change plays out.
4. **Ending**: where the novel leaves it, and what the writer wants the reader to conclude.

Each paragraph should begin with a claim that answers the question, not with a plot event.

## Key idea 2: context that explains, not decorates

AO4 is half of the assessment focus, so context needs to appear throughout, not in a final paragraph.
Useful kinds of context for the set novels include:

| Kind of context | Example of how it can work |
|---|---|
| Historical setting | *Of Mice and Men* is set among migrant ranch workers in 1930s California during the Great Depression, which explains why the dream of owning land matters so much |
| Time of writing | *To Kill a Mockingbird* was published in 1960, during the civil rights movement, though it is set in 1930s Alabama under segregation |
| Culture and tradition | *Things Fall Apart* presents Igbo society before and during the arrival of British colonial rule and Christian missionaries |
| Identity and migration | *The Joy Luck Club* explores Chinese American mothers and their American-born daughters |
| Ideas and debates | *Klara and the Sun*, narrated by an artificial "friend", raises questions about technology, love and what makes a person |

A context sentence earns credit when it explains a specific choice in the novel. Ask "why might the
writer show this, given when and where the text was written or set?"

## Key idea 3: quotations for a closed-book exam

Because you cannot check the text, prepare a bank of **short, flexible quotations**: single words or
brief phrases that you can attach to several themes. Fifteen to twenty for your novel, each with a
note of where it appears and which themes it serves, is more useful than a few long passages
half-remembered. Accurate paraphrase of a specific moment also counts as textual reference ("when
Atticus agrees to defend Tom Robinson, he tells his children ...").

## Worked example: planning a theme question

**Question (on *Of Mice and Men*):** How does Steinbeck present loneliness in the novel? [40]

**Overview:** Steinbeck presents loneliness as the normal condition of the ranch, created by the
economic insecurity of 1930s migrant work and by prejudice, and he uses George and Lennie's friendship
to show both the hope and the fragility of escaping it.

**Plan:**

1. **Opening:** George's speech about men who work on ranches having no family or place establishes
   loneliness as typical, and makes the friendship unusual. *Context*: itinerant labourers moved from
   ranch to ranch with no security during the Depression.
2. **Isolation by prejudice:** Crooks is kept apart because of his race; his conversation with Lennie
   shows how isolation damages a person. *Context*: racial segregation and discrimination in 1930s
   America.
3. **Isolation by gender and age:** Candy's fear of being discarded and Curley's wife's lack of a name
   and of anyone to talk to show that loneliness affects anyone without power. *Context*: limited
   roles for women and the lack of support for workers who could no longer work.
4. **Ending:** the death of the dream at the end leaves George alone, as the ranch-hands he described
   at the start, so the structure is circular and the hope of companionship is defeated.

**Sample paragraph (paragraph 2, summarised):**

> Steinbeck uses Crooks to show that loneliness on the ranch is not just bad luck but is imposed by
> prejudice. Crooks lives apart in the harness room, and when Lennie enters, his first reaction is
> defensive hostility, which shows how exclusion has made him wary of others. Yet as they talk, he
> admits how much a person needs someone to be near, revealing that his bitterness covers a longing
> for company. In 1930s America, segregation meant that a Black worker like Crooks could be excluded
> legally and socially, so Steinbeck presents his isolation as a product of the society the novel
> depicts, inviting the reader to see that society as cruel rather than natural.

This paragraph opens with a claim, refers precisely to the text through paraphrase, and connects the
character's situation directly to the historical context.

## Worked example: a setting question

If asked how a writer uses a setting to show a character's state of mind, choose two or three
contrasting moments in the same or related places. In *To Kill a Mockingbird*, for instance, the
Radley house and the courthouse could be tracked from childhood fear to an adult understanding of
injustice. The rule is the same: make each appearance of the setting carry a claim about the
character's development.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling the plot.** Narrative without argument earns limited AO1 credit.
- **Bolted-on context.** A paragraph of history at the end, unconnected to the novel, earns little AO4.
- **Generalising context.** "In those days people were racist" is weaker than naming the specific
  condition and linking it to a moment.
- **One-scene answers.** Focusing on a single memorable episode instead of the whole novel.
- **Misquoting.** An inaccurate quotation is worse than accurate paraphrase.
- **Running out of time.** Section C comes last; arriving with 30 minutes rather than 45 costs the most
  heavily weighted marks on the paper.

## Exam technique and command words

Section C questions may ask **how** a writer presents something, or invite you to **explore** a
character, theme or relationship. Either way, answer the question in your first sentence and keep
returning to its key words. Spend about five minutes planning: an overview, three or four moments
across the novel, and a context link for each. Choose the question that lets you range across the
whole text, not the one about your favourite scene.
