This guide covers subtopic **9.5 Corrosion of metals** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). [Metal Properties and Reactivity](/resources/metal-properties-and-reactivity/) introduces rusting and the three-tube experiment; this guide explains each control in that experiment, matches barrier methods to real objects, and builds the two-part explanation of galvanising that the syllabus asks for, including the electron-loss equation.

## What the syllabus asks

1. **State** the conditions required for the rusting of iron and steel to form **hydrated iron(III) oxide**.
2. **Describe** how barrier methods prevent rusting by **excluding oxygen or water**.
3. **State** some common barrier methods, including **painting, greasing and coating with plastic**.
4. **Explain** sacrificial protection in terms of the **reactivity series** and in terms of **electron loss**.
5. **Describe** the use of **zinc in galvanising** as an example of a barrier method **and** sacrificial protection.

## Corrosion and rusting

**Corrosion** is the gradual destruction of a metal by reaction with substances in its surroundings, such as oxygen and water. **Rusting** is the name used only for the corrosion of **iron and steel**. Other metals corrode but do not rust.

### Conditions and product

Iron rusts only when **both water and oxygen** are present. The product, rust, is **hydrated iron(III) oxide**: iron(III) oxide with water combined in it.

```
iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide
4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) + 2xH₂O(l) → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O(s)
```

The x shows that the amount of water in rust varies. Rusting is an **oxidation**: iron atoms lose electrons to become iron(III) ions, and iron gains oxygen.

Rust is a problem because it is weak and flaky. It falls away from the surface, exposing fresh iron to more oxygen and water, so rusting continues until the object is weakened or eaten through.

### The investigation, step by step

Three iron nails are set up in separate test tubes:

| Tube | Contents | Water? | Oxygen? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Nail in tap water, tube open | Yes | Yes | Rusts |
| B | Nail in boiled water, layer of oil on top, tube stoppered | Yes | No | No rust |
| C | Nail in dry air with anhydrous calcium chloride, tube stoppered | No | Yes | No rust |

Why each step is there:

- **Boiling the water in B** removes dissolved air (and so dissolved oxygen).
- **The oil layer in B** stops oxygen from the air dissolving back into the water.
- **Anhydrous calcium chloride in C** is a drying agent: it absorbs water vapour, keeping the air dry.
- **The stoppers** stop fresh air (with water vapour or oxygen) entering B and C.

Conclusion: only A has both water and oxygen, and only A rusts, so **both are needed**. Examiners often ask for the purpose of one step; give the specific reason, not "to make it fair".

## Barrier methods

A barrier method puts a layer over the iron that **excludes oxygen or water** (or both), so the conditions for rusting are not met.

| Method | Typical object | Why this method suits it |
|---|---|---|
| Painting | Car bodies, bridges, railings | Covers large areas; can be renewed |
| Greasing (or oiling) | Bicycle chains, tools, machine parts | Moving parts would scrape paint off; grease also lubricates |
| Coating with plastic | Wire fences, dish-drying racks, garden furniture | Tough, waterproof layer on objects that are handled or kept outdoors |

**The weakness of every barrier method:** if the barrier is scratched, chipped or worn away, oxygen and water reach the iron and it rusts at that point. The barrier only protects where it is intact.

## Sacrificial protection

Sacrificial protection uses a **more reactive metal** in contact with the iron. Zinc and magnesium are both above iron in the reactivity series.

### In terms of the reactivity series

Because zinc is more reactive than iron, zinc reacts with oxygen and water **in preference to** iron. The zinc corrodes instead, so the iron is protected. The zinc is "sacrificed".

### In terms of electron loss

Rusting begins when iron atoms lose electrons. A more reactive metal has a greater tendency to lose electrons, so it loses them first:

```
Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻     (zinc loses electrons and corrodes)
```

The iron does **not** lose electrons, so it is not oxidised and does not rust. The electrons released by the zinc flow to the iron, so the iron stays as metal.

Sacrificial protection works even where the iron is exposed to air and water, but the sacrificial metal is gradually used up and must be replaced. Blocks of zinc or magnesium are attached to steel structures in water or buried in the ground for this reason.

## Galvanising: both methods at once

**Galvanising** is coating iron or steel with a layer of **zinc**. It protects in two ways:

1. **Barrier:** the zinc layer covers the iron and excludes oxygen and water.
2. **Sacrificial:** if the zinc layer is scratched and the iron exposed, the zinc, being more reactive, still corrodes in preference to the iron.

That second point is what makes galvanising better than paint: a scratched painted surface rusts, but a scratched galvanised surface does not.

### Worked example: a full-mark explanation

*A galvanised steel bucket is scratched, exposing the steel. Explain why the steel does not rust. [3]*

Model answer: "Zinc is more reactive than iron [1]. So the zinc loses electrons (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻) and corrodes instead of the iron [1]. The iron does not lose electrons, so it does not rust [1]."

Annotation: this question is about the **sacrificial** part. Writing "the zinc coating keeps out air and water" scores nothing here, because at a scratch the barrier is broken.

Apply the same reasoning to a coating of a metal **less** reactive than iron: it can act only as a barrier. Once it is scratched, the iron is the more reactive metal, so the iron loses electrons and rusts.

## Common mistakes

- **"Rust is iron oxide."** Write **hydrated iron(III) oxide**.
- **"Iron rusts in air."** Dry air alone is not enough; water must also be present.
- **"Zinc protects iron because zinc is unreactive."** It is the opposite: zinc is **more** reactive.
- **Explaining galvanising with the barrier idea only.** The syllabus wants barrier **and** sacrificial.
- **Giving a sacrificial metal below iron**, such as copper.
- **"Rusting happens to all metals."** Only iron and steel rust; other metals corrode.

## Exam technique

- **State** conditions: water **and** oxygen.
- **Describe** a barrier: name it and say it **excludes oxygen and/or water**.
- **Explain** sacrificial protection: reactivity comparison, then electron loss, then the consequence for the iron.
- **Describe** galvanising: zinc coating, barrier, and sacrificial when scratched.
- **Suggest** a method for an unfamiliar object: consider whether it moves (grease), is large (paint), is handled or outdoors (plastic), or is hard to reach for repainting (sacrificial blocks or galvanising).
