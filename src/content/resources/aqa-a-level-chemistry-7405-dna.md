## The nucleotide

DNA stands for deoxyribonucleic acid. Its monomer is the **nucleotide**, which has three parts:

1. A **phosphate** group (derived from the phosphate ion).
2. A five-carbon sugar, **2-deoxyribose**, a pentose sugar.
3. An organic **base**, one of adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G) and thymine (T).

```
 phosphate – 2-deoxyribose – base
```

The base is bonded to the sugar, and the phosphate is bonded to the sugar. The phosphate is not bonded directly to the base.

The four bases contain nitrogen atoms in rings and have N–H or C=O groups that can take part in hydrogen bonding. Their structures, and those of the sugar and phosphate, are given in the Chemistry Data Booklet, so you do not need to memorise them but you do need to be able to read them.

## A single strand of DNA

Nucleotides join into a long chain. The **phosphate** group of one nucleotide forms a covalent bond with the **2-deoxyribose** of the next nucleotide. The link is formed in a condensation reaction that releases water. The result is a **sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate** backbone, with a base attached to each sugar and pointing out from the chain.

```
 ...–sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate–...
        |                |                |
      base A           base T           base G
```

The sequence of bases along the chain carries the genetic information. The backbone is the same for every strand. Only the order of the bases differs.

The covalent bonds in the backbone are strong, which makes each strand stable and long-lasting.

## The double helix

DNA normally exists as **two strands** twisted around each other in a **double helix**. The two strands are **complementary**: the sequence of bases on one strand determines the sequence on the other.

- The sugar–phosphate backbones are on the outside.
- The bases point inwards and pair up between the strands.
- Each pair is held together by **hydrogen bonds**.

### Base pairing

| Pair | Number of hydrogen bonds |
|---|---|
| Adenine – thymine (A–T) | 2 |
| Guanine – cytosine (G–C) | 3 |

Adenine always pairs with thymine, and cytosine always pairs with guanine.

### Why these pairs?

Hydrogen bonds form between a hydrogen atom attached to nitrogen (N–H) and a lone pair on nitrogen or oxygen (N or O). Each base has hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor groups in a particular pattern. Adenine and thymine have groups that line up to make two hydrogen bonds. Guanine and cytosine have groups that line up to make three. The shapes are such that each pair has the same overall width, so the helix has a regular diameter. Other combinations do not line up, so they do not form a full set of hydrogen bonds.

### Why hydrogen bonds are the right kind of bond

- Individually they are weak, but there are millions of them, so the double helix is stable overall.
- They are weak enough that the strands can **separate** when the cell needs to copy or read the DNA, without breaking the strong covalent backbone.

This is the explanation to give for "how hydrogen bonding between base pairs leads to the two complementary strands": the bases of one strand can form hydrogen bonds only with their specific partners on the other, so the second strand is fixed by the sequence of the first.

## Worked example 1: the complementary strand

**Question.** One strand of DNA has the base sequence A–T–G–C–C–G–T–A. Write the sequence of the complementary strand.

Pair each base: A with T, T with A, G with C, C with G.

```
Strand 1:   A  T  G  C  C  G  T  A
Strand 2:   T  A  C  G  G  C  A  T
```

## Worked example 2: counting hydrogen bonds

**Question.** How many hydrogen bonds hold together the eight base pairs in the sequence in Example 1?

A–T pairs: A–T, T–A, T–A, A–T = 4 pairs × 2 = 8 hydrogen bonds.
G–C pairs: G–C, C–G, C–G, G–C = 4 pairs × 3 = 12 hydrogen bonds.
Total = 8 + 12 = **20 hydrogen bonds**.

## Worked example 3: base composition

**Question.** In a sample of DNA, 30 % of the bases are adenine. Give the percentages of the other three bases.

A pairs with T, so thymine is also 30 %. The remaining 100 − 60 = 40 % is shared equally by cytosine and guanine, which pair with each other: **20 % C and 20 % G**.

## Worked example 4: a longer molecule

A double helix of 1000 base pairs is 40 % G–C pairs. Calculate the total number of hydrogen bonds.

```
G–C pairs = 0.40 × 1000 = 400, each with 3 hydrogen bonds → 1200
A–T pairs = 600, each with 2 hydrogen bonds              → 1200
total hydrogen bonds = 2400
```

A sample with a higher proportion of G–C pairs would have more hydrogen bonds per base pair and so would need more energy to separate the strands.

## Features to compare

| Feature | Backbone | Between strands |
|---|---|---|
| Type of bond | Covalent | Hydrogen bonds |
| Strength | Strong | Weak individually |
| Role | Stable chain | Holds strands together but allows separation |
| Location | Outside of the helix | Inside of the helix |

## Comparing DNA with a protein

| Feature | Protein | DNA |
|---|---|---|
| Monomer | Amino acid | Nucleotide |
| Link between monomers | Peptide link, –CO–NH– | Covalent bond between phosphate and sugar |
| Backbone | Repeating N–C–C unit | Repeating sugar–phosphate unit |
| Side groups | R groups of the amino acids | Bases |
| Information carried by | Order of amino acids | Order of bases |
| Shape held by | Hydrogen bonds, S–S bonds and others between side chains | Hydrogen bonds between base pairs |

Both are polymers built by condensation, and in both the sequence of the side groups carries the information. Setting them side by side helps you to avoid the common slip of describing the bases as part of the backbone.

## Why complementary strands are useful

Because each strand determines the other, either strand can act as a template for making a new partner strand. If the strands are separated, each can pair with new nucleotides according to the pairing rules (A with T, C with G), producing two identical double helices. This is why the specification focuses on complementary pairing. The details of copying DNA are studied in biology, but the chemistry behind them is the hydrogen bonding between specific bases.

## Common mistakes

- Pairing A with G or C with T.
- Stating that the bases pair by covalent bonds, or that the phosphate pairs with the base.
- Saying the backbone is held by hydrogen bonds. The backbone is covalent.
- Writing that G–C has two hydrogen bonds and A–T three.
- Saying the base is attached to the phosphate. It is attached to the sugar.
- Calling the sugar ribose rather than 2-deoxyribose.
- Not stating that the two strands are complementary because of specific hydrogen bonding.
- Describing the double helix as two identical strands. They are complementary, not identical.

## Exam technique

- **Describe a nucleotide**: phosphate, 2-deoxyribose, base, and the order in which they bond.
- **Describe a strand**: covalent bonds between the phosphate of one and the sugar of the next.
- **Explain the double helix**: complementary bases, specific hydrogen bonds, A–T two and G–C three.
- **Calculate**: count pairs and multiply by 2 or 3.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the sugar called 2-deoxyribose?**
It is ribose with one oxygen removed from carbon 2: there is an H instead of an OH at that position.

**Are all the bonds in DNA hydrogen bonds?**
No. The backbone consists of covalent bonds, and only the base pairs are held by hydrogen bonds.

**Why does the number of hydrogen bonds matter?**
More hydrogen bonds between a pair of bases means a stronger attraction, so G–C regions are harder to separate than A–T regions.
