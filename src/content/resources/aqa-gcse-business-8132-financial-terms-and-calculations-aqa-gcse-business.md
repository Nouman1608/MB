## What the specification asks for

| You must be able to | Covered below |
|---|---|
| Understand variable costs, fixed costs and total costs, and the concepts of revenue, costs, profit and loss | Costs, revenue and profit |
| Understand the main investments businesses make, such as machinery, buildings and vehicles, and calculate the average rate of return | Average rate of return |
| Understand break-even output, interpret break-even charts, and identify the break-even level of output and margin of safety | Break-even |
| Evaluate the value of break-even analysis | Value of break-even |

The specification states that students will not be expected to draw break-even charts or use the break-even formula. You must read and interpret them. In its finance content the specification also notes that students will not be given formulae in an exam, so learn the ones below.

## Costs, revenue and profit

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not change with output in the short run, such as rent, insurance and salaries |
| Variable costs | Costs that rise and fall with output, such as materials and packaging |
| Total costs | Fixed costs + total variable costs |
| Revenue | Selling price × quantity sold |
| Profit | Revenue − total costs |
| Loss | When total costs are greater than revenue |

**Total variable costs** = variable cost per unit × output.

### Worked example: why profit rises faster than sales

Ashgrove Mugs sells mugs at £9. Each mug costs £3 in clay, glaze and packaging. Fixed costs are £4,500 a month.

| Mugs sold | Revenue | Total variable costs | Total costs | Profit or loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 600 | £5,400 | £1,800 | £6,300 | −£900 |
| 900 | £8,100 | £2,700 | £7,200 | £900 |
| 1,200 | £10,800 | £3,600 | £8,100 | £2,700 |

Each extra 300 mugs adds £2,700 of revenue but only £900 of cost, because fixed costs do not rise. So profit rises by £1,800 for every 300 mugs. This is why businesses with high fixed costs are so sensitive to the level of sales.

## Average rate of return

Businesses invest in **machinery**, **buildings** and **vehicles**, spending money now for returns over several years. The **average rate of return (ARR)** shows the average yearly profit from an investment as a percentage of its cost.

Standard method (no formula is printed in the specification):

1. Total return = add the returns for every year.
2. Total profit = total return − cost of the investment.
3. Average annual profit = total profit ÷ number of years.
4. ARR = average annual profit ÷ cost of the investment × 100.

### Worked example: choosing between two investments

Coldridge Brewery can buy one of two machines.

| | Machine A | Machine B |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | £60,000 | £45,000 |
| Life | 4 years | 3 years |
| Returns | £21,000 each year | £18,000, £20,000, £22,000 |

**Machine A**

- Total return: 4 × £21,000 = £84,000
- Total profit: £84,000 − £60,000 = £24,000
- Average annual profit: £24,000 ÷ 4 = £6,000
- ARR: £6,000 ÷ £60,000 × 100 = **10%**

**Machine B**

- Total return: £18,000 + £20,000 + £22,000 = £60,000
- Total profit: £60,000 − £45,000 = £15,000
- Average annual profit: £15,000 ÷ 3 = £5,000
- ARR: £5,000 ÷ £45,000 × 100 = about **11.1%**

Machine B has the higher ARR, even though Machine A makes more total profit (£24,000 against £15,000). Machine B is also cheaper, which matters if the brewery would borrow. On the other hand, Machine A lasts longer. The returns are forecasts; if demand for the brewery's beer falls, both ARRs fall. A business usually compares the ARR with the interest rate on borrowing: borrowing at 7% to earn 11.1% makes sense; borrowing at 12% does not.

## Break-even

**Break-even output** is the level of output at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither a profit nor a loss.

**Margin of safety** = actual (or planned) output − break-even output. It shows how far sales could fall before the business makes a loss.

### Finding break-even from a table

Lisle Lamps sells lamps at £12. Variable cost is £4 a lamp and fixed costs are £8,000 a month.

| Output | 0 | 500 | 1,000 | 1,500 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £0 | £6,000 | £12,000 | £18,000 |
| Total costs | £8,000 | £10,000 | £12,000 | £14,000 |
| Profit or loss | −£8,000 | −£4,000 | £0 | £4,000 |

Break-even output is **1,000 lamps**, where revenue and total costs are both £12,000.

If Lisle sells 1,400 lamps, the margin of safety is 1,400 − 1,000 = **400 lamps**. Profit at 1,400: revenue £16,800 − total costs (£8,000 + £5,600) £13,600 = **£3,200**.

### Reading a break-even chart

On a break-even chart, output is on the horizontal axis and costs and revenue in pounds on the vertical axis.

- The **fixed cost line** is horizontal, at the level of fixed costs.
- The **total cost line** starts at the fixed cost level on the vertical axis and rises.
- The **revenue line** starts at zero and rises more steeply.
- **Break-even** is where the revenue and total cost lines cross. Read down to the output axis for the break-even output and across for the revenue at that point.
- To the left of break-even the gap between the lines is a **loss**; to the right it is a **profit**.
- The **margin of safety** is the horizontal distance between break-even output and actual output.

### What happens when things change

Use tables to check. These all start from Lisle Lamps.

| Change | Effect on chart | New break-even |
|---|---|---|
| Price rises to £14 | Revenue line becomes steeper | 800 lamps (revenue £11,200 = total costs £8,000 + £3,200) |
| Fixed costs rise to £10,000 | Total cost line shifts up | 1,250 lamps (revenue £15,000 = total costs £10,000 + £5,000) |
| Variable cost rises | Total cost line becomes steeper | Break-even output rises |

So: higher price, lower break-even; higher costs, higher break-even. A lower break-even point means the business needs to sell fewer units to cover its costs, so it is less risky.

## The value of break-even analysis

**Uses**

- Shows the minimum sales needed, so a start-up can judge whether its plan is realistic.
- Supports a loan application by showing the lender when the business will cover its costs.
- Lets a business test "what if" changes in price or costs before acting.
- The margin of safety shows how much risk the business faces.

**Limitations**

- It assumes all output is sold.
- It assumes price and variable cost per unit stay the same at every level of output, although bulk buying or discounts change them.
- It relies on forecasts, which may be wrong.
- It says nothing about demand: a price rise lowers break-even on paper but may cut sales.
- Fixed costs may change, for example when the business moves to a bigger site.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to multiply variable cost per unit by output.
- Dividing ARR by the total return or by the number of years only, rather than by the cost of the investment.
- Forgetting to subtract the cost of the investment before averaging.
- Giving margin of safety in pounds when the question asks for units, or the reverse.
- Reading break-even from the fixed cost line instead of the total cost line.
- Saying a higher price always increases profit, ignoring the effect on demand.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate:** write each step, label units (£, %, units).
- **Identify from a chart:** state the value with its unit.
- **Explain:** what a figure means for the business, such as the risk shown by a small margin of safety.
- **Evaluate:** compare investments using ARR and other factors, or judge how useful break-even is for this business.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you calculate average rate of return?**
Add the yearly returns, subtract the cost of the investment, divide by the number of years to find average annual profit, then divide by the cost of the investment and multiply by 100.

**Do I need to know the break-even formula for AQA GCSE Business?**
No. The specification states you will not be expected to use the break-even formula or draw break-even charts, but you must interpret charts and identify break-even output and the margin of safety.

**What is the margin of safety?**
The difference between actual or planned output and break-even output. It shows how far sales can fall before a loss is made.
