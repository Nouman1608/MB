## Population change

```
natural increase = birth rate - death rate
population change = natural increase +/- net migration
```

Rates are given **per 1000 people per year**.

**High birth rates** result from: limited contraception, high infant mortality, children needed for labour, religious and cultural norms, low female education and status.

**Falling death rates** result from: better healthcare, sanitation, clean water, food supply, vaccination.

## Demographic Transition Model

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Natural increase | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 High fluctuating | High | High | Low | Remote tribal groups |
| 2 Early expanding | High | **Falling fast** | Very high | Afghanistan, Niger |
| 3 Late expanding | **Falling** | Low | Slowing | Brazil, India |
| 4 Low fluctuating | Low | Low | Low/zero | UK, USA |
| 5 Declining | **Below** death rate | Low | Negative | Japan, Germany |

The death rate falls **before** the birth rate — that gap is what causes rapid growth in stage 2.

## Population pyramids

- **Wide base, narrow top** → high birth rate, high death rate, low life expectancy (LEDC, stages 1–2).
- **Narrow base, wide top** → ageing population, low birth rate (MEDC, stages 4–5).
- Look for **indentations** (war, disease, emigration) and gender imbalance.

## Migration

| Push factors | Pull factors |
|---|---|
| Unemployment, low wages | Jobs, higher wages |
| Poor services, housing | Better healthcare, education |
| War, persecution, disaster | Safety, political stability |
| Drought, crop failure | Better living conditions |

**Impacts** on the source area: labour shortage, ageing population, remittances received, family separation.
**Impacts** on the receiving area: labour supply, cultural diversity, but pressure on housing, services and possible tension.

## Settlement

- **Site** = the land the settlement is built on. **Situation** = its position relative to surroundings. These are frequently confused.
- **Patterns**: nucleated (clustered), dispersed (scattered), linear (along a route).
- **Hierarchy**: isolated dwelling → hamlet → village → town → city → conurbation. As settlements rise in the hierarchy, they become **fewer** but have **larger sphere of influence** and more **high-order services**.

## Exam traps

- Natural increase excludes migration.
- In stage 2 it is the **death rate** falling, not the birth rate.
- Site ≠ situation.
- Give **named examples** — Cambridge geography answers require them.
- A high birth rate does not automatically mean rapid growth; the death rate matters too.

## Self-test

1. Birth rate 32, death rate 9. State the natural increase per 1000.
2. Which DTM stage has the fastest growth, and why?
3. Give two push and two pull factors for rural–urban migration.
4. Distinguish site from situation.
5. What does a wide-based population pyramid indicate?

**Answers:** 1. 32 − 9 = **23 per 1000 per year** (2.3%). 2. Stage 2 — the death rate has fallen sharply through better healthcare and sanitation while the birth rate remains high, creating the widest gap. 3. Push: unemployment, poor services, drought, conflict. Pull: jobs, better healthcare and education, higher wages. 4. Site is the actual land a settlement occupies; situation is its position in relation to the surrounding area. 5. A high birth rate with a young population, typical of DTM stages 1–2 in an LEDC.
