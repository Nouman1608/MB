Learn the content first from the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-physics-photoelectric-effect-matter-waves-and-compton/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-physics-photoelectric-effect-matter-waves-and-compton-revision-notes/). Data for every question: Planck constant 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; speed of light 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; elementary charge 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg. Answer to three significant figures unless told otherwise.

## Questions

**1.** (HL only) A metal has work function 4.30 eV.

**(a)** Calculate its threshold frequency. **[2]**
**(b)** Ultraviolet light of wavelength 220 nm falls on the metal. Find the greatest kinetic energy an emitted electron can have, in J and in eV. **[3]**

**2.** (HL only) Discuss three features of the photoelectric effect that classical wave theory cannot explain. State the wave prediction and the observation for each. **[4]**

**3.** (HL only) Light of frequency 9.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz is shone on a metal. The stopping potential is found to be 1.45 V.

**(a)** Determine the work function of the metal in eV. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the threshold frequency. **[1]**

**4.** (HL only) A student measures the stopping potential Vs for a different metal at four frequencies f.

| f / 10¹⁴ Hz | 5.5 | 6.5 | 7.5 | 8.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vs / V | 0.18 | 0.59 | 1.01 | 1.42 |

**(a)** Find the gradient of the line using the outermost data points. **[2]**
**(b)** Hence determine a value for the Planck constant. **[2]**
**(c)** Determine the threshold frequency and the work function in eV. **[2]**

**5.** (HL only) A metal has work function 1.90 eV. A beam of green light of wavelength 520 nm and power 1.2 mW falls on it.

**(a)** Calculate the number of photons arriving each second. **[2]**
**(b)** On average 1 photon in 400 releases an electron. Calculate the photocurrent. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons in J. **[1]**
**(d)** The green light is replaced by red light of wavelength 700 nm and the same power. Deduce what happens to the current. **[1]**

**6.** (HL only) Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of

**(a)** an electron of kinetic energy 3.0 keV. **[3]**
**(b)** an alpha particle of mass 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and kinetic energy 5.0 MeV. **[2]**

**7.** (HL only) A beam of electrons is directed at a narrow gap of width 1.5 nm. The first intensity minimum is observed at 9.5° from the central axis.

**(a)** Determine the de Broglie wavelength of the electrons. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate their speed. **[2]**
**(c)** The electrons are now sent at twice the speed. Calculate the new angle of the first minimum. **[2]**

**8.** (HL only) Describe an experiment in which electron diffraction is observed, and explain what it shows about matter. **[4]**

**9.** (HL only) A photon of wavelength 0.0720 nm is Compton scattered by an electron through 150°. Use h/mec = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.

**(a)** Calculate the change in wavelength. **[2]**
**(b)** State the wavelength of the scattered photon. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the wavelength increases. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why Compton scattering supports the photon model of light more strongly than the photoelectric effect does. **[2]**

**10.** (HL only) A photon of wavelength 0.0975 nm scatters from a stationary electron and emerges with wavelength 0.1000 nm. Use h/mec = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.

**(a)** Calculate the angle through which the photon is scattered. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the kinetic energy gained by the electron, in eV. **[3]**

**11.** (HL only) An electron and a photon each have wavelength 0.250 nm.

**(a)** Calculate the momentum and the kinetic energy of the electron, in eV. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy of the photon. **[2]**
**(c)** Compare the two energies. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Φ = 4.30 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.88 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. f₀ = Φ/h = 6.88 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = **1.04 × 10¹⁵ Hz** [1].
**(b)** Photon energy = hc/λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ / 220 × 10⁻⁹ = 9.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. Emax = 9.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ - 6.88 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **2.16 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** [1] = **1.35 eV** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the conversion from eV to J before using h; an answer with a unit mix-up usually cannot show the earlier steps were right.

**2.** Any three of the following features, each with its prediction and observation, earn one mark each. The fourth mark is for the photon view.
Threshold: a wave of any frequency should eventually free electrons if intense enough; observed, no emission below f₀ whatever the intensity [1].
Emax: a more intense wave should give electrons more energy; observed, Emax depends on frequency and not on intensity [1].
Delay: a dim wave should need time to accumulate energy; observed, emission is immediate [1].
The photon model fits all three, since one photon of energy hf gives its energy to one electron [1].
*Examiner insight:* Pair each feature with both a prediction and an observation; naming a feature alone usually leaves the argument incomplete.

**3. (a)** Photon energy = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 9.00 × 10¹⁴ = 5.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. Emax = eVs = 1.45 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.32 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. Φ = 5.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ - 2.32 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.65 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = **2.28 eV** [1].
**(b)** f₀ = 3.65 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = **5.50 × 10¹⁴ Hz** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A stopping potential in volts equals Emax in eV numerically, so you can skip the conversion, but state that you are doing it.

**4. (a)** Gradient = (1.42 - 0.18) / ((8.5 - 5.5) × 10¹⁴) [1] = **4.13 × 10⁻¹⁵ V Hz⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** Gradient = h/e, so h = 4.13 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ [1] = **6.61 × 10⁻³⁴ J s** [1].
**(c)** Vs = 0 at f₀ = 5.5 × 10¹⁴ - 0.18 / 4.13 × 10⁻¹⁵ = **5.06 × 10¹⁴ Hz** [1]. Φ/e = gradient × f₀ = 4.13 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 5.06 × 10¹⁴, so **Φ ≈ 2.09 eV** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use two points well separated on the line and show the subtraction; a value near 2.1 eV from sensible working is a fair reading of a graph.

**5. (a)** Photon energy = hc/λ = 3.83 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. N = 1.2 × 10⁻³ / 3.83 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **3.14 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** Electrons per second = 3.14 × 10¹⁵ / 400 = 7.84 × 10¹² [1]. I = 7.84 × 10¹² × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.25 × 10⁻⁶ A** [1].
**(c)** Φ = 1.90 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Emax = 3.83 × 10⁻¹⁹ - 3.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **7.85 × 10⁻²⁰ J** [1].
**(d)** Photon energy at 700 nm = 2.84 × 10⁻¹⁹ J (1.78 eV), which is below Φ, so **there is no emission and the current falls to zero** [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (d), the argument is the comparison of photon energy with Φ; stating that "red light has less intensity" would not answer the question.

**6. (a)** KE = 3.0 × 10³ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁶ J [1]. p = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁶) = 2.96 × 10⁻²³ kg m s⁻¹ [1]. λ = h/p = **2.24 × 10⁻¹¹ m** [1].
**(b)** KE = 5.0 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0 × 10⁻¹³ J; p = √(2mKE) = 1.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ kg m s⁻¹ [1]; λ = **6.43 × 10⁻¹⁵ m** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert keV or MeV to joules first, and write the momentum on its own line before dividing into h.

**7. (a)** sin θ = λ/b, so λ = 1.5 × 10⁻⁹ × sin 9.5° [1] = **2.48 × 10⁻¹⁰ m** [1].
**(b)** p = h/λ = 2.68 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹ [1]; v = p/m = **2.94 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Doubling the speed doubles p and halves λ, so sin θ = 0.165 / 2 = 0.0825 [1], giving **θ = 4.73°** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Halving the angle is the usual slip; halve the sine of the angle, not the angle itself.

**8.** A beam of electrons of known kinetic energy (set by an accelerating voltage) travels in a vacuum [1]. It passes through a narrow gap or a thin crystal whose spacing is comparable with the de Broglie wavelength [1]. A screen or detector records intensity against angle, and shows maxima and minima [1]. A diffraction pattern is a wave property, so this shows matter has a wave nature, and the minima fit λ = h/p [1].
*Examiner insight:* Mention the vacuum and the spacing compared with λ, and link the pattern to a wave property.

**9. (a)** 1 - cos 150° = 1.866 [1]; Δλ = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² × 1.866 = **4.53 × 10⁻¹² m** [1].
**(b)** λf = 7.20 × 10⁻¹¹ + 0.453 × 10⁻¹¹ = **7.65 × 10⁻¹¹ m** [1].
**(c)** The photon gives some of its energy to the electron [1]. Since E = hc/λ, less energy means a longer wavelength [1].
**(d)** The photon behaves as a particle in a collision, exchanging momentum as well as energy [1]. The measured shift fits a formula in the scattering angle, so it is a quantitative test [1].
*Examiner insight:* The negative cosine matters: 1 - cos 150° is greater than 1, not less.

**10. (a)** Δλ = 0.1000 - 0.0975 = 2.50 × 10⁻¹² m [1]. cos θ = 1 - 2.50 / 2.43 = -0.0288 [1]. **θ = 91.7°** [1].
**(b)** hc/λi = 2.04 × 10⁻¹⁵ J [1]; hc/λf = 1.99 × 10⁻¹⁵ J [1]; difference = 5.10 × 10⁻¹⁷ J = **319 eV** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A small change in the constant used shifts the angle in the third figure, so use the value the question gives and keep extra digits until the end.

**11. (a)** p = h/λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / 0.250 × 10⁻⁹ = 2.65 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹ [1]. KE = p²/2m = 3.86 × 10⁻¹⁸ J [1] = **24.1 eV** [1].
**(b)** E = hc/λ = 7.96 × 10⁻¹⁶ J [1] = **4.97 keV** [1].
**(c)** The photon's energy is about 206 times larger than the electron's, although the two have the same momentum [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use p²/2m for the electron and hc/λ for the photon; applying the same energy formula to both is the standard mistake.

## Where marks are usually lost

* Not converting eV to J, or keV and MeV to J, before using the Planck constant.
* Using Emax = hf instead of Emax = hf - Φ.
* Linking intensity to the maximum energy rather than to the number of electrons.
* Stating a classical feature without naming both the prediction and the observation.
* Halving an angle rather than the sine of the angle when the wavelength changes.
* Using the photon energy formula for an electron, or p²/2m for a photon.
* Getting the sign wrong in the Compton factor 1 - cos θ for angles above 90°.
* Rounding the constant h/mec early and losing accuracy in an angle.
* Describing a diffraction experiment without the detector or the gap size.
