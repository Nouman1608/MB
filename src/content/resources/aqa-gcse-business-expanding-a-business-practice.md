## Questions

**1.** Define the term "economies of scale". **[2]**

**2.** Quarrydale Print makes packs of leaflets. Its total cost for 12,000 packs is £33,600.

**(a)** Calculate the average unit cost. **[2]**
**(b)** At 20,000 packs the average unit cost is £2.45. Calculate the total cost. **[2]**

**3.** Explain one purchasing economy of scale that a growing business might gain. **[3]**

**4.** Explain one technical economy of scale that a growing business might gain. **[3]**

**5.** Glenhaven Tea sells loose-leaf tea from two shops and plans to expand through e-commerce. Explain one benefit and one drawback of this method of growth for Glenhaven Tea. **[4]**

**6.** Stonebarrow Cafes opens new cafes. Its weekly figures are:

| Output (drinks) | Total cost |
|---|---|
| 6,000 | £10,500 |
| 15,000 | £24,000 |
| 30,000 | £52,500 |

**(a)** Calculate the average unit cost at each output. **[3]**
**(b)** Interpret your results. **[3]**

**7.** Quarrydale Print packs 50,000 orders a year in its own warehouse at £0.64 per order. A specialist firm offers to do the same for £0.55 per order.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly saving from outsourcing the packing. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse one drawback of outsourcing for Quarrydale Print. **[3]**

**8.** Sandrigg Bakes is franchising its shops. Each franchisee pays an initial fee of £10,000 and then 5% of annual sales revenue. Eight franchisees each have sales revenue of £150,000 in the first year.

**(a)** Calculate the total amount Sandrigg Bakes receives from the eight franchisees in the first year. **[3]**
**(b)** Analyse one disadvantage of franchising for Sandrigg Bakes. **[3]**

**9.** Distinguish between a merger and a takeover. **[4]**

**10.** Harkstead Joinery makes kitchen doors. Its figures are:

| Output (doors a month) | Total cost |
|---|---|
| 800 | £40,000 |
| 1,400 | £63,000 |
| 2,000 | £100,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the average unit cost at each output. **[3]**
**(b)** Analyse two possible reasons for the change in average unit cost between 1,400 and 2,000 doors. **[4]**

**11.** Glenhaven Tea sells 45,000 packs a year at a total cost of £90,000. A rival, Yarnley Teas, sells 15,000 packs at a total cost of £36,000. Glenhaven can take over Yarnley for £400,000 and has £150,000 in cash reserves. It would need a loan for the rest. It expects the combined firm to sell 60,000 packs at a total cost of £114,000. Evaluate whether Glenhaven Tea should take over Yarnley Teas. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Economies of scale are unit cost advantages from growth [1], so the average unit cost falls as output rises [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that say "costs fall" without saying "per unit" or "average" tend to lose the second idea, because total cost does not fall.

**2. (a)** Average unit cost = total cost / output [1] = £33,600 / 12,000 = **£2.80 per pack** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Total cost = average unit cost x output = £2.45 x 20,000 [1] = **£49,000** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the formula, then the substitution. A wrong final number with clear working usually earns more than a bare wrong number. Include the £ sign.

**3.** A larger business orders materials in greater quantities [1]. The supplier gives a lower price per unit as a bulk discount [1], so the average unit cost of each item falls, for example flour costing less per kilogram when bought by the lorry load [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the type, then follow the chain from bulk order to lower price per unit to lower average unit cost. Stopping at "buys in bulk" leaves the chain incomplete.

**4.** A larger business can afford bigger or more specialised machinery [1]. The cost of the machine is spread over many more units [1], so each unit carries less of the cost, for example a high-speed press that prints thousands of leaflets an hour at a lower cost per leaflet [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep technical economies separate from purchasing economies. Technical economies concern production methods and equipment, not supplier discounts.

**5.** Benefit: Glenhaven can reach customers beyond its two shops [1], so sales could rise without opening more premises [1]. Drawback: it needs a website, secure payment, packing and delivery systems, which cost money [1], and it faces many other online tea sellers [1]. Award marks for any one benefit and one drawback, each developed. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Both the benefit and the drawback must be developed and tied to Glenhaven. Naming a point with no explanation usually earns less than explaining one point well.

**6. (a)** £10,500 / 6,000 = £1.75 [1]; £24,000 / 15,000 = £1.60 [1]; £52,500 / 30,000 = **£1.75** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Average unit cost falls from £1.75 to £1.60 as output rises to 15,000 drinks [1], which shows economies of scale [1]. At 30,000 drinks it rises back to £1.75, which suggests diseconomies of scale beyond some output [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Interpretation answers do best when they quote the figures, name the pattern and place the turning point between the outputs given.

**7. (a)** In-house cost = 50,000 x £0.64 = £32,000 [1]. Outsourced cost = 50,000 x £0.55 = £27,500, so the saving is **£4,500** a year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Quarrydale has less control over how orders are packed [1]. A poorly packed order could arrive damaged, and customers would blame Quarrydale [1], which could reduce repeat sales and wipe out the £4,500 saving [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A drawback is analysed when it ends in a consequence for the business. Say what could go wrong and why it matters.

**8. (a)** Revenue share per franchisee = 5% x £150,000 = £7,500 [1]. Income per franchisee = £10,000 + £7,500 = £17,500 [1]. Total = 8 x £17,500 = **£140,000** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Sandrigg has less control over each shop [1]. A franchisee who bakes poor products or serves customers badly can harm the brand name in every shop [1], so sales could fall across the whole business [1]. (Sharing profit with franchisees is another acceptable disadvantage if developed.) **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Take care to apply the 5% to the revenue and then add the one-off fee. Mixing up the two parts is a common cause of lost credit.

**9.** A merger is where two businesses agree to join together to form one [1], usually with both sides consenting [1]. A takeover is where one business buys enough of another to gain control of it [1], which can happen even if the target's managers do not want it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs a clear difference, not two separate definitions. Words such as "agreed" and "control" show the contrast.

**10. (a)** £40,000 / 800 = £50 [1]; £63,000 / 1,400 = £45 [1]; £100,000 / 2,000 = **£50** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Poor communication: with more staff and shifts, instructions about door sizes and finishes may be missed [1], so doors are remade and cost rises [1]. Reduced staff motivation: workers in a bigger workshop may feel less valued [1], so they work more slowly or leave, raising the average unit cost [1]. Coordination issues are also acceptable if developed. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question names the output range, so tie each reason to the rise from £45 to £50. Reasons with no link to the business tend to stay at one mark.

**11.** Points in favour:
- Combined average unit cost would be £114,000 / 60,000 = £1.90, against Glenhaven's current £90,000 / 45,000 = £2.00 [1]. That is a fall of 5%, evidence of economies of scale [1].
- Yarnley's own cost is £36,000 / 15,000 = £2.40, so joining it to Glenhaven's larger output could spread costs and lower unit cost [1].
- A takeover gives quick growth in customers and output [1].

Points against:
- The price is £400,000 and Glenhaven has £150,000, so it must borrow £250,000 [1]. Interest and repayments could outweigh a unit cost saving of ten pence [1].
- The two businesses may have different cultures, and poor communication or coordination could cause diseconomies of scale [1].
- The £1.90 is only an expectation. Actual costs may differ [1].

Judgement [1]: for example, the takeover is worthwhile only if the loan repayments are less than the extra profit from the lower unit cost and added sales; a more cautious option such as e-commerce could be considered first. The judgement must be supported by the evidence above. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluate answer needs both sides in the context of the figures and a conclusion that says what the decision depends on. A one-sided list of advantages usually limits the mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "costs fall" instead of "average unit cost falls".
- Using the wrong order in the formula, output divided by total cost.
- Leaving out units, such as £ per pack.
- Stopping at "buys in bulk" without the lower price per unit.
- Confusing technical economies with purchasing economies.
- Treating diseconomies of scale as certain rather than a risk.
- Describing a method of growth without a benefit and a drawback.
- Using general points that do not refer to the named business.
- Finishing an evaluate answer without a judgement or the condition it depends on.
