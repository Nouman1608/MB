Quick recall for section 4.1, Charge and current, of the OCR Advanced GCE in Physics A specification (H556), Version 3.0 (March 2026), for first assessment in 2017: learning outcomes 4.1.1(a)-(g) and 4.1.2(a)-(c). For worked examples, read the [charge and current study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-h556-charge-and-current/), then test yourself with the [charge and current practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-h556-charge-and-current-practice/).

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electric current | Rate of flow of charge, I = ΔQ/Δt |
| Coulomb | Charge passing a point when 1 A flows for 1 s; 1 C = 1 A s |
| Elementary charge e | 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; electron −e, proton +e |
| Quantised | Net charge only takes values that are whole-number multiples of e |
| Conventional current | Direction positive charge would flow: from + to − in the external circuit |
| Electron flow | The actual direction electrons move in a metal: from − to + |
| Kirchhoff's first law | Sum of currents into a point = sum of currents out |
| Mean drift velocity | Average velocity of charge carriers along a conductor |
| Number density n | Number of free charge carriers per unit volume, m⁻³ |

## Equations

| Equation | Notes |
|---|---|
| I = ΔQ/Δt | time in seconds |
| Q = area under an I-t graph | for a changing current |
| Q = ±Ne | N whole number of electrons gained or lost |
| ΣI in = ΣI out | at any junction |
| I = Anev | carriers of charge e; use I = Anqv for charge q |
| n = (ρ/M) N_A × (free electrons per atom) | number density from bulk data |

## Carriers

- **Metals**: free electrons; positive ions stay fixed in the lattice.
- **Electrolytes**: positive and negative ions move in opposite directions; their contributions add.
- Ion charge = (number of electrons lost or gained) × e, so Cu²⁺ carries 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

## Kirchhoff's first law in steps

1. Mark every current with an arrow, guessing unknown directions.
2. At each junction: total in = total out.
3. Solve one junction at a time.
4. A negative result means the true direction is opposite to the guess.
5. Justify with conservation of charge: charge does not build up at a junction.

## Drift velocity in steps

1. Convert the area to m² (A = πd²/4, d in m).
2. Write n and e (or q).
3. v = I/(Ane).
4. Check the size: in metals, typically fractions of a millimetre per second.

## Proportion with I = Anev

| Change | v |
|---|---|
| Halve A (same I, n) | × 2 |
| Halve d (same I, n) | × 4 |
| n smaller by 10⁶ (same I, A) | × 10⁶ |
| Series wires | same I; thinner or lower-n wire has larger v |

## Materials by n

| Type | n | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Metal | about 10²⁸ to 10²⁹ m⁻³ | good conductor |
| Semiconductor | far smaller | n rises with temperature and light |
| Insulator | extremely small | negligible current |

## Worked reminders

- 3.6 C in 90 s: I = 0.040 A.
- 2.0 mA for 1 hour: Q = 2.0 × 10⁻³ × 3600 = 7.2 C.
- A sphere with charge −4.8 × 10⁻¹⁸ C has gained 30 electrons.
- A current rising from 0 to 0.6 A in 4 s: Q = ½ × 4 × 0.6 = 1.2 C.
- Junction: 3.0 A in, 1.4 A and I out: I = 1.6 A.

## Exam traps

- Minutes or hours left in I = ΔQ/Δt.
- Height of an I-t graph used instead of its area.
- e used for a doubly charged ion.
- Ion contributions subtracted in an electrolyte.
- d in mm, or d instead of A, in I = Anev.
- "Electrons move at nearly the speed of light"; it is the field (signal) that travels fast.
- Kirchhoff's first law stated without conservation of charge when an explanation is asked for.

## Quick self-test

1. Define the coulomb.
2. Calculate the charge passing when 0.25 A flows for 8.0 minutes.
3. How many electrons make up a charge of −8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ C?
4. Explain why a charge of 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ C cannot exist on an object.
5. Name the charge carriers in a metal and in an electrolyte.
6. In which direction do electrons move in a wire relative to conventional current?
7. Currents of 2.5 A and 0.7 A enter a junction and one current leaves. What is it?
8. State the principle on which Kirchhoff's first law depends.
9. Write I = Anev and name each symbol.
10. A wire's diameter is halved at the same current. What happens to the drift velocity?
11. Why does a semiconductor conduct better when warmed?
12. Find v for I = 1.5 A, A = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m², n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³.

### Answers

1. The charge passing a point when a steady current of 1 A flows for 1 s.
2. Q = 0.25 × 480 = **120 C**.
3. 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **500 electrons** (excess).
4. It equals 2.5e; net charge must be a whole-number multiple of e.
5. Metal: free (delocalised) electrons. Electrolyte: positive and negative ions.
6. The opposite direction: electrons move towards the positive terminal.
7. 2.5 + 0.7 = **3.2 A**.
8. Conservation of charge.
9. I current, A cross-sectional area, n number density of carriers, e charge per carrier, v mean drift velocity.
10. A becomes a quarter, so **v becomes four times larger**.
11. More charge carriers are released, so n increases.
12. v = 1.5 ÷ (2.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = **5.5 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹**.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Kirchhoff's first law a statement of conservation of charge?**
Current is charge per second. If more charge entered a junction each second than left it, charge would have to be created or would build up there. In a steady circuit neither happens, so the currents in and out must balance.

**Is I = Anev on the OCR data sheet?**
Check the data, formulae and relationships booklet your centre uses. Either way, learning outcome 4.1.2(b) expects you to use it, and deriving it is a common question.

**Why is drift velocity so small when current starts instantly?**
The electric field that drives the carriers is set up round the whole circuit almost instantly. Every free electron starts to drift at once, so the current starts everywhere even though each electron moves slowly.
