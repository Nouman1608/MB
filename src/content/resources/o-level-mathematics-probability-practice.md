These eleven questions cover single events, relative frequency, expected frequency, selections with and without replacement, Venn diagrams and possibility diagrams. Questions marked *(no calculator)* are in the style of Paper 1. The rest allow a calculator, as in Paper 2. Give probabilities as fractions, decimals or percentages, and never above 1. Cover the answers and write full working before you check.

---

## Questions

**1.** *(no calculator)* A box holds 20 cards: 8 yellow, 5 green and the rest white. One card is taken at random. Find the probability that it is **(a)** white **(b)** not yellow. **[2]**

**2.** In a survey, the relative frequencies of students choosing football, tennis and other sports are 0.35, 0.25 and 0.1. Every other student chose cricket. **(a)** Find the relative frequency of cricket. **(b)** 400 students took part. How many chose tennis? **[2]**

**3.** A bus is early, on time or late. The probability that it is early is x. The probability that it is on time is 4x. The probability that it is late is 0.25. **(a)** Work out the value of x. **(b)** Write down the probability that the bus is not late. **[3]**

**4.** A fair six-sided dice is rolled 240 times. **(a)** How many sixes would you expect? **(b)** How many times would you expect a prime number? **(c)** In one trial the dice showed 52 sixes. Write the relative frequency of a six as a fraction in its simplest form. **[3]**

**5.** *(no calculator)* A bag contains 7 red counters and 3 green counters. Two counters are taken at random without replacement. Find the probability that **(a)** both are red **(b)** one of each colour is taken. **[4]**

**6.** A bag contains 4 red counters and 6 blue counters. Three counters are taken at random without replacement. Find the probability that **(a)** all three are blue **(b)** at least one is red. **[4]**

**7.** In a class of 30 students, 17 play football, 12 play basketball and 7 play neither. **(a)** How many play both? **(b)** A student is chosen at random. Find the probability that they play exactly one of the two sports. **[4]**

**8.** The universal set is ξ = {1, 2, 3, ..., 20}. A is the set of multiples of 3. B is the set of even numbers. **(a)** List the members of A ∩ B. **(b)** Find n(A ∪ B). **(c)** A number is chosen at random from ξ. Find the probability that it is in neither A nor B. **(d)** Find the probability that it is in A but not in B. **[4]**

**9.** Two fair dice are rolled and the two scores are added. Use a possibility diagram to find the probability that the total is **(a)** 8 **(b)** less than 5 **(c)** a prime number. **[5]**

**10.** The probability that a train is late on any school day is 0.2. Whether it is late on one day does not depend on any other day. **(a)** Find the probability that it is on time on both Monday and Tuesday. **(b)** Find the probability that it is late on exactly one of those two days. **(c)** Show that the probability that it is late at least once in three days is 0.488. **(d)** A term has 60 school days. How many late days would you expect? **(e)** State why the assumption of independence may not be realistic. **[8]**

**11.** Box X holds 5 red and 3 blue counters. Box Y holds 4 red and 6 blue counters. **(a)** One counter is taken at random from box X. Find the probability that it is blue. **(b)** A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, a counter is taken from box X. If it lands tails, a counter is taken from box Y. Find the probability that the counter is red. **(c)** Two counters are taken from box X without replacement. Find the probability that both are blue. **(d)** Find the probability that both counters in part (c) are the same colour. **(e)** The experiment in part (b) is carried out 400 times. How many red counters would you expect? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** **(a)** White cards = 20 − 8 − 5 = 7, so P = **7/20** [1]. **(b)** 12 cards are not yellow, so P = 12/20 = **3/5** [1].

*Examiner insight:* in (b), count the cards that are not yellow, or work out 1 − 8/20. Leaving 12/20 unsimplified usually still scores, but check the question for the form it asks for.

**2.** **(a)** 1 − (0.35 + 0.25 + 0.1) = **0.3** [1]. **(b)** 0.25 × 400 = **100** [1].

*Examiner insight:* relative frequencies for all outcomes add up to 1. Marks go when students add the three given values and stop, without subtracting from 1.

**3.** **(a)** x + 4x + 0.25 = 1 [1], so 5x = 0.75 and x = **0.15** [1]. **(b)** P(not late) = 1 − 0.25 = **0.75** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the probabilities of all outcomes add up to 1, and that gives the equation. Students who write 5x = 0.25 forget that the late probability is already a number and belongs on the other side.

**4.** **(a)** 240 × 1/6 = **40** [1]. **(b)** The primes on a dice are 2, 3 and 5, so P = 1/2 and the expected number is 240 × 1/2 = **120** [1]. **(c)** 52/240 = **13/60** [1].

*Examiner insight:* 1 is not a prime number, which is a common slip in (b). Expected frequency is probability × number of trials, and a relative frequency comes from the actual results, so it will differ from 1/6.

**5.** **(a)** 7/10 × 6/9 [1] = **7/15** [1]. **(b)** 7/10 × 3/9 + 3/10 × 7/9 [1] = 42/90 = **7/15** [1].

*Examiner insight:* without replacement, the total and the number of that colour both fall by one for the second counter. In (b), students often find only one of the two orders and lose the final two marks.

**6.** **(a)** 6/10 × 5/9 × 4/8 [1] = 120/720 = **1/6** [1]. **(b)** At least one red is the complement of all blue [1], so P = 1 − 1/6 = **5/6** [1].

*Examiner insight:* "at least one" almost always means 1 minus the probability of none. Listing every case with one, two or three red counters takes far longer and risks a missing case.

**7.** **(a)** 17 + 12 + 7 − 30 [1] = **6** [1]. **(b)** Football only = 17 − 6 = 11 and basketball only = 12 − 6 = 6 [1], so P = 17/30 [1].

*Examiner insight:* fill in the overlap first, then the two "only" regions. A common error is to count the overlap twice, which gives 23/30 instead of 17/30.

**8.** **(a)** A = {3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18} and B = {2, 4, ..., 20}, so A ∩ B = **{6, 12, 18}** [1]. **(b)** n(A ∪ B) = 6 + 10 − 3 = **13** [1]. **(c)** 20 − 13 = 7, so P = **7/20** [1]. **(d)** A but not B = {3, 9, 15}, so P = **3/20** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the numbers in A ∩ B are counted in both sets, so subtract them once when you find n(A ∪ B). Numbers outside both sets still belong to ξ, and forgetting them gives a wrong total in (c).

**9.** The possibility diagram has 36 equally likely outcomes. **(a)** The pairs that total 8 are (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3) and (6,2) [1], so P = **5/36** [1]. **(b)** Totals of 2, 3 and 4 have 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 outcomes [1], so P = 6/36 = **1/6** [1]. **(c)** Totals of 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11 have 1 + 2 + 4 + 6 + 2 = 15 outcomes, so P = 15/36 = **5/12** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the denominator is 36, not 11. Totals are not equally likely, so you cannot treat the eleven possible totals as separate outcomes.

**10.** **(a)** 0.8 × 0.8 = **0.64** [1]. **(b)** 0.2 × 0.8 + 0.8 × 0.2 [1] = **0.32** [1]. **(c)** P(on time all three days) = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 [1] = 0.512 [1]; P(late at least once) = 1 − 0.512 = **0.488** [1]. **(d)** 60 × 0.2 = **12** days [1]. **(e)** A late train one day may be caused by a fault or by weather that continues the next day, so the days are linked [1].

Model plan for the show-that: state the complement, multiply along the branches, subtract from 1. The real exam marks extended answers using the mark scheme for that question, and your working must reach the printed value.

*Examiner insight:* in "show that" parts, write the calculation that leads to the printed value, and do not work backwards from 0.488. The (b) answer needs both orders, since late-then-on-time and on-time-then-late are different outcomes.

**11.** **(a)** **3/8** [1]. **(b)** 1/2 × 5/8 [1] + 1/2 × 4/10 [1] = 5/16 + 1/5 = **41/80** [1]. **(c)** 3/8 × 2/7 [1] = 6/56 = **3/28** [1]. **(d)** Both red = 5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56 = 5/14 [1]; same colour = 5/14 + 3/28 [1] = **13/28** [1]. **(e)** 400 × 41/80 = **205** [1].

In the real exam, a long item like this is marked step by step, so show every product and the sum.

*Examiner insight:* in (b), the two routes to red are added, because they are different ways of getting the same result, and each route multiplies along its branches. A final answer above 1 means you added where you should have multiplied.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not reducing the total for the second pick when there is no replacement.
- Counting the overlap twice in a Venn diagram, or forgetting the members of ξ outside both sets.
- Relative frequencies or probabilities that do not add up to 1.
- Treating 1 as a prime number when finding prime scores.
- Using the 11 possible totals of two dice as the denominator instead of the 36 equally likely outcomes.
- Adding probabilities along a branch when they should be multiplied, or multiplying where you should add the routes.
- Listing only one order in "one of each colour" questions.
- Leaving an answer as a ratio or giving a probability above 1.
