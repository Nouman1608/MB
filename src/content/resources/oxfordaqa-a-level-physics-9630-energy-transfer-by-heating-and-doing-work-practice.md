These questions cover section 3.11.1, Energy transfer by heating and doing work, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), including Required practical 8. It is International A-level content. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [heating and doing work study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-energy-transfer-by-heating-and-doing-work/) or the [heating and doing work revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-energy-transfer-by-heating-and-doing-work-revision-notes/) first.

Use: specific heat capacity of water 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹; specific latent heat of fusion of ice 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹; specific latent heat of vaporisation of water 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹. Assume no energy is lost to the surroundings unless a question says otherwise.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Define the internal energy of a body. **[2]**

**2.** State the first law of thermodynamics, explaining the meaning of each term. **[2]**

**3.** A solid melts at a constant temperature. What happens to the particles' energies during melting?

A: The mean kinetic energy increases.

B: The potential energy increases.

C: Both the kinetic and the potential energy increase.

D: Neither changes, because the temperature is constant.

**[1]**

**4.** A gas in a cylinder is compressed. 250 J of work is done on the gas and 90 J is transferred from the gas to the surroundings by heating. Calculate the change in internal energy of the gas. **[2]**

**5.** Calculate the energy needed to heat 2.5 kg of water from 15 °C to 85 °C. **[2]**

**6.** Calculate the energy needed to boil away 0.40 kg of water already at 100 °C. **[1]**

### Section B

**7.** 0.050 kg of steam at 100 °C condenses on a cold surface and the water formed cools to 30 °C. Calculate the total energy released. **[3]**

**8.** An ice cube of mass 0.030 kg at 0 °C is added to 0.30 kg of water at 25 °C in an insulated cup of negligible heat capacity.

**(a)** Write an energy equation for the final temperature θ, once all the ice has melted. **[2]**

**(b)** Solve it to find θ. **[2]**

**9.** A 60 W heater warms 0.50 kg of a solid. Its temperature rises at 0.060 K s⁻¹, then stays constant for 2500 s, then rises again at 0.040 K s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the solid. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the liquid. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain, in terms of particle energies, why the temperature is constant for 2500 s. **[2]**

**10.** A solar water-heating panel transfers energy to the water flowing through it at a rate of 1.2 kW. Water enters at 18 °C and flows at 0.010 kg s⁻¹. Calculate the outlet temperature. **[3]**

**11.** In Required practical 8, a 1.00 kg aluminium block is heated by a heater with a p.d. of 12.0 V and a current of 3.50 A. The straight part of the graph of temperature against time has a gradient of 0.0465 K s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium from these data. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how energy losses to the surroundings affect the value calculated. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one improvement to the method that reduces this effect. **[1]**

**12.** A car of mass 1200 kg brakes from 20 m s⁻¹ to rest. 60% of its kinetic energy goes to its brake discs, of total mass 8.0 kg and specific heat capacity 450 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the rise in temperature of the discs. **[3]**

**(b)** State whether the energy reaches the discs by heating or by doing work, and explain your answer. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The sum of the randomly distributed kinetic energies and potential energies [1] of the particles in the body [1]. **[2]**

**2.** ΔU = Q + W [1], where ΔU is the change in internal energy, Q the energy transferred to the system by heating and W the work done on the system [1]. **[2]**

**3.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** W = +250 J and Q = −90 J [1]; ΔU = **+160 J** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Q = mcΔθ = 2.5 × 4180 × 70 [1] = **7.3 × 10⁵ J** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Q = ml = 0.40 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = **9.0 × 10⁵ J** [1]. **[1]**

**7.** Condensing: 0.050 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 1.13 × 10⁵ J [1]; cooling: 0.050 × 4180 × 70 = 1.46 × 10⁴ J [1]; total = **1.3 × 10⁵ J** (1.28 × 10⁵ J) [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Energy lost by the warm water = energy to melt the ice + energy to warm the melted ice from 0 °C to θ [1]: 0.30 × 4180 × (25 − θ) = 0.030 × 3.34 × 10⁵ + 0.030 × 4180 × θ [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 31 350 − 1254θ = 10 020 + 125.4θ [1]; θ = 21 330 / 1379.4 = **15 °C** [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** c = P / (m × gradient) = 60 / (0.50 × 0.060) = **2000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** l = 60 × 2500 / 0.50 = **3.0 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹** [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** 60 / (0.50 × 0.040) = **3000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** The energy supplied increases the potential energies of the particles as they separate [1]; their mean kinetic energy does not change, so the temperature stays constant [1]. **[2]**

**10.** P = (Δm/Δt) cΔθ, so Δθ = 1200 / (0.010 × 4180) [1] = 28.7 K [1]; outlet temperature = 18 + 28.7 = **47 °C** [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** VI = mc × gradient, so c = 12.0 × 3.50 / (1.00 × 0.0465) [1] = **903 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Some energy supplied is lost, so the temperature rises more slowly and the gradient is too small [1]; c = VI/(m × gradient) is therefore **too large** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Insulate the block (or start below room temperature and finish the same amount above it) [1]. **[1]**

**12. (a)** Ek = ½ × 1200 × 20² = 2.4 × 10⁵ J [1]; energy to discs = 0.60 × 2.4 × 10⁵ = 1.44 × 10⁵ J [1]; Δθ = 1.44 × 10⁵ / (8.0 × 450) = **40 K** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** By doing work: the friction force of the pads acts on the moving discs, so work is done on them (there is no temperature difference driving the transfer at first) [1]. **[1]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking W as the work done by the gas, so getting the sign of ΔU wrong.
- Missing a stage in a change-of-state problem.
- Forgetting that melted ice must also warm up in a mixing problem.
- Using the total mass instead of the mass flow rate.
- Saying heat losses make the measured specific heat capacity too small.
