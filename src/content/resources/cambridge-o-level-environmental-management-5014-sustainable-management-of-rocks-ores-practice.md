## Section A

**1.** Define a finite resource. **[1]**

**2.** Define sustainable management of resources. **[2]**

**3.** State the difference between reducing and reusing materials. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **three** conditions the syllabus links to successful recycling. **[3]**

**5.** State **one** example of an alternative material that could replace copper. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain why copper ore is classed as a finite resource. **[2]**

**7.** *Invented data.* The table shows steel recycling in a city.

| Year | Steel used (tonnes) | Steel recycled (tonnes) |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 5000 | 2000 |
| 2025 | 5000 | 3200 |

(a) Calculate the recycling rate in 2020 and in 2025.

(b) Calculate the percentage increase in the mass of steel recycled between 2020 and 2025.

(c) Suggest **two** measures the city could have introduced to cause this increase. **[6]**

**8.** Describe how increased efficiency of extraction helps to manage mineral resources sustainably. **[3]**

**9.** A country passes a law requiring all mining companies to recycle their waste water and restore land
after mining. Explain why enforcement is needed for this law to succeed. **[3]**

**10.** Suggest how a school could use education to increase the amount of metal and glass its students
recycle. **[3]**

**11.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of using alternative materials as a sustainable management
strategy. **[6]**

**12.** A government wants to make its supply of a rare metal last as long as possible. Discuss which
**two** strategies from the syllabus would be most effective. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A natural resource that is used up at a faster rate than it is replaced [1].

**2.** The use of strategies that ensure the needs of the present are met [1] without compromising the
ability of future generations to meet their own needs [1].

**3.** Reducing means using less material in the first place [1]; reusing means using an item again for
the same or another purpose [1].

**4.** Accessibility [1]; ease [1]; education [1].

**5.** Any one: plastic (for water pipes) [1]; aluminium (for power cables) [1]; fibre-optic cable (for
communication cables) [1].

**6.** Copper ore forms over millions of years [1], but it is being mined far faster than that, so it is
used up faster than it is replaced [1].

**7.** (a) 2020: 2000 / 5000 x 100 = **40%** [1]. 2025: 3200 / 5000 x 100 = **64%** [1].
(b) 3200 - 2000 = 1200 tonnes [1]; 1200 / 2000 x 100 = **60%** [1].
(c) Any two linked to the syllabus conditions:

- Kerbside collection or more collection points, improving accessibility [1].
- Clearly labelled bins so sorting is easy [1].
- Campaigns or lessons explaining what can be recycled, improving education [1].
- A law setting recycling targets, or a deposit-return scheme [1].

**8.** Any three:

- Better technology recovers more metal from each tonne of ore [1].
- Less of the useful material is left in the waste [1].
- Old waste heaps can be reprocessed, for example by bioleaching [1].
- The deposit lasts longer, so fewer new mines are needed [1].
- Less land is damaged and less waste must be stored [1].

**9.** Any three:

- Without inspections, companies may ignore the law to save money [1].
- Monitoring checks that waste water is really being recycled and land restored [1].
- Fines or loss of licence give companies a reason to obey [1].
- Enforcement needs money and trained staff, so the government must provide them [1].

**10.** Any three:

- Lessons explaining why metals are finite and how recycling reduces mining [1].
- Clear signs on bins showing which items go where [1].
- Assemblies or competitions between classes to recycle the most [1].
- Displays showing how much material the school has recycled [1].

**11.** Benefits, any three:

- Reduces demand for the scarce material, so its reserves last longer [1].
- The alternative may be cheaper or lighter, for example plastic pipes instead of copper [1].
- Less mining of the scarce material means less habitat loss and pollution [1].
- The alternative may be more plentiful or renewable [1].

Limitations, any three:

- The alternative may not perform as well, for example being weaker or less durable [1].
- It may cost more to produce [1].
- It may itself be finite; many plastics are made from petroleum [1].
- Its production may cause its own environmental problems [1].

Maximum 6 marks; a judgement such as "useful if the substitute is plentiful and performs well" is
expected for full marks.

**12.** Up to 6 marks for two strategies justified with benefits and limitations, for example:

- **Recycling** recovers metal from old products, so less new ore is mined [1]; it needs accessible, easy
  collection and education to work [1].
- **Increased efficiency of extraction** gets more metal from each tonne of ore [1], so the reserve lasts
  longer, but the technology is expensive [1].
- **Avoiding resource depletion** by limiting yearly extraction keeps reserves for the future [1], but it
  may reduce income and be hard to agree [1].
- **Alternative materials** reduce demand [1], but a substitute may not exist for every use [1].
- **Legislation and enforcement** could make recycling compulsory [1], but enforcement costs money [1].

Judgement: a reasoned choice of two, for example recycling with efficiency, because together they reduce
both the amount of new ore needed and the amount wasted (credited within the 6 marks).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Incomplete definitions in questions 1 and 2.
- In 7(b), dividing the increase by 3200 instead of the original 2000.
- Treating legislation as enough on its own, with no mention of enforcement.
- Giving benefits only in questions 11 and 12, or giving no judgement.
