## Questions

**1.** Define the term "just in time (JIT) stock control". **[2]**

**2.** State the production process (job, batch or flow) that best matches each business. **[3]**
Business A makes one hand-stitched wedding dress to a bride's own design.
Business B bottles milk on a line that runs all day.
Business C bakes 200 croissants, then resets the ovens for 150 pain au chocolat.

**3.** Wharfe Candles made 960 candles in a week with 8 staff. After buying a pouring machine it made 1,260 candles with 9 staff. Calculate the percentage change in productivity (output per worker). Give your answer to 1 decimal place. **[4]**

**4.** Explain one way that flow production can help a business keep its unit costs low. **[3]**

**5.** Greenfield Garden Centre sells bags of compost. A bar gate stock graph shows: maximum stock 500 bags; reorder level 200 bags; use 25 bags a day; lead time 4 days.
**(a)** State the maximum stock level. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of days from a full delivery until the reorder level is reached. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the stock level, in bags, at the moment a delivery arrives. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the size of each delivery. **[2]**

**6.** Outline one risk to a manufacturer of using JIT stock control. **[2]**

**7.** Hartwood Furniture makes dining tables in batches of 20. The owner is thinking of buying a computer-controlled cutting machine for £80,000. About a third of its sales are made-to-order tables in unusual sizes. Analyse how the machine might affect Hartwood Furniture. **[6]**

**8.** Fenwick Chocolates buys 3,000 kg of chocolate in each of 24 deliveries a year. Supplier P charges £6.20 a kg and delivered late on 6 of the 24 deliveries last year. Supplier Q charges £6.55 a kg and delivered late once. Each late delivery stops production and costs Fenwick £1,500 in lost output.
**(a)** Calculate how much more Fenwick would pay Supplier Q than Supplier P over a year, based on price only. **[4]**
**(b)** Justify which supplier Fenwick should choose. **[6]**

**9.** Petal Post sells flowers online and delivers by courier. Last week a courier delivered 40 bouquets two days late. Analyse the impact of this logistics problem on Petal Post. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss why trust is important in a business's relationship with its suppliers. **[6]**

**11.** Calder Biscuits uses batch production. It makes 300 packs a day at a unit cost of £5.40. Demand has risen to about 600 packs a day and the owner is considering a flow line costing £250,000. The line could make 900 packs a day at a unit cost of £3.90 when running at full capacity. Calder changes its flavours each season.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in unit cost if the flow line is used at full capacity. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Calder Biscuits should buy the flow line. **[8]**

**12.** Ashby Cabinets averages £60,000 of materials in stock and wants to move to JIT, cutting average stock to £15,000. Storage, insurance and waste cost 8% a year of stock value. Its main supplier was late on 3 of the last 20 orders. A late order stops assembly for one day, losing 12 cabinets with £85 profit each. Ashby would place about 40 orders a year.
**(a)** Calculate the yearly saving in holding costs from moving to JIT. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Ashby Cabinets should move to JIT. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Stock control in which materials or parts arrive only when they are needed for production [1], so the business holds very little stock [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition tends to gain credit when it says when the stock arrives and what that means for the amount held.

**2.** A: job production [1]. B: flow production [1]. C: batch production [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Match the clue in the stem: resetting between groups points to batch, a continuous line to flow.

**3.** Productivity before = 960 ÷ 8 = 120 candles per worker [1]. After = 1,260 ÷ 9 = 140 [1]. Change = (140 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 [1] = **16.7%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the working. Using total output instead of output per worker gives a different percentage.

**4.** Flow production makes very large quantities on one line [1]. Fixed costs such as the machinery are spread over many units, so each unit carries a small share [1]. The business can then keep its cost per unit low and may be able to charge a competitive price [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain answers tend to gain credit when a point is followed by two linked expansions. "It is cheaper" alone stays at one point.

**5. (a)** **500 bags** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Fall in stock = 500 − 200 = 300 bags [1]. Days = 300 ÷ 25 = **12 days** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Use during the lead time = 25 × 4 = 100 bags [1]. Stock on arrival = 200 − 100 = **100 bags** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Delivery = 500 − 100 [1] = **400 bags** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) stock keeps falling while the order travels, so take the lead time off the reorder level.

**6.** A late or wrong delivery leaves too little stock [1], so production stops and orders may be late or lost [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Outline answers need two linked points; a bare risk is one.

**7.** Faster cutting could raise productivity, so each worker produces more tables [1]. Computer control gives consistent cuts, improving quality and reducing waste [1]. The £80,000 cost adds a large fixed cost, which is hard to cover if sales are uncertain [1]. A programmable machine can be reset for a new size, which helps with the made-to-order third of sales [1]. However, setting it up for each unusual size takes time and skilled staff [1]. Overall it suits Hartwood if batch orders are large enough to cover the cost [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analyse answers tend to gain credit when points are tied to the business in the stem, such as the batches of 20. General lists stay descriptive.

**8. (a)** Supplier P per delivery = 3,000 × 6.20 = £18,600 [1]. Supplier Q = 3,000 × 6.55 = £19,650 [1]. Difference = £1,050 per delivery [1]. Over 24 deliveries = 24 × 1,050 = **£25,200** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Late deliveries cost Supplier P 6 × 1,500 = £9,000 and Supplier Q 1 × 1,500 = £1,500 [1]. Supplier Q saves 9,000 − 1,500 = £7,500 [1]. Net, Supplier Q is still 25,200 − 7,500 = £17,700 dearer a year [1]. Supplier Q is more reliable, which protects customers and Fenwick's reputation, and this is not counted in the figures [1]. However, a late rate of 25% (6 of 24) for Supplier P is high [1]. I recommend Supplier P, because the price saving is far larger than the cost of lateness, but Fenwick should hold a small buffer and review the choice if late deliveries lose customers [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Justify answers tend to gain credit when they use the figures and end with a clear recommendation.

**9.** Customers receive flowers after the date they wanted, so the bouquets may be worthless to them [1]. Refunding or replacing 40 orders raises costs and cuts profit [1]. Customers may leave poor reviews, and the online business depends on its reputation [1]. Satisfaction falls because customers expected reliability, so repeat orders may fall [1]. A more reliable courier may cost more, but protects future sales [1]. Reviews can also reach people who never ordered [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analyse answers tend to gain credit when the chain of consequences is clear: delay, cost, reputation, satisfaction.

**10.** Trust means believing the supplier will deliver the right quality, on time and at the agreed price [1]. If the business trusts the supplier, it can hold less stock, which saves storage cost [1]. It is more likely to get help in a shortage [1]. Trust can lead to better terms, such as discounts, because both sides want a long relationship [1]. Without trust the business may check every delivery and keep large buffers, adding cost and time [1]. However, trust does not replace checking quality [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Discuss questions have no context, so credit tends to go to developed points. One point only, however long, limits the mark.

**11. (a)** Fall = 5.40 − 3.90 = £1.50 [1]. Percentage = 1.50 ÷ 5.40 × 100 [1] = **27.8%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Unit cost falls by 27.8% at full capacity, which could raise profit per pack or allow lower prices [1]. The batch method makes only 300 packs against demand of 600, so without change Calder cannot meet half the demand [1]. At 600 packs a day the line is used at 600 ÷ 900 = 66.7% of capacity [1]. At that level the fixed costs are spread over fewer packs than at full capacity, so the unit cost is likely to be above £3.90 [1]. The £250,000 cost needs enough sales to repay it [1]. Changing flavours each season reduces flexibility, because a flow line is hard to adjust [1]. Adding batch capacity costs less and keeps flexibility, although unit cost stays high [1]. On balance I would buy the line if demand looks lasting and the flavour range can be limited; if demand is uncertain, adding batch capacity is safer [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluate answers tend to gain credit when they use the numbers, weigh both sides and reach a supported judgement.

**12. (a)** Holding cost now = 60,000 × 0.08 = £4,800 [1]. Under JIT = 15,000 × 0.08 = £1,200 [1]. Saving = **£3,600** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The holding cost saving is £3,600 a year [1]. Late orders: 3 of 20 is 15%, so about 40 × 0.15 = 6 late orders a year [1]. Each costs 12 × 85 = £1,020, so about £6,120 a year [1]. On these figures JIT costs about 6,120 − 3,600 = £2,520 more than it saves [1]. JIT also frees space and reduces waste, which the figures do not count [1]. Late orders could also harm Ashby's reputation [1]. Ashby should not move to JIT with this supplier, because the risk outweighs the saving [1]. A more reliable supplier could reverse this [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluate answers tend to gain credit when the calculated saving is compared with the calculated risk before a judgement is made.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining JIT as holding "no stock".
- Calculating productivity from total output instead of output per worker.
- Ignoring the lead time when finding the stock at delivery.
- Listing technology benefits without balancing them against cost and flexibility.
- Choosing the cheapest supplier without costing late deliveries.
- Leaving out a recommendation in a justify or evaluate answer.
