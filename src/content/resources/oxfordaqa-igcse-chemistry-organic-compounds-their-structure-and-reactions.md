## Families of organic compounds

A **functional group** is the part of a molecule responsible for how it reacts. Compounds sharing one functional group form a **homologous series**: neighbouring members differ by CH₂, and they all react in similar ways.

## 3.10.3.1 a: recognising alcohols

Alcohols contain the **–OH** functional group bonded to a carbon atom. The first three members are:

| Name | Carbon atoms | Structural formula | Molecular formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methanol | 1 | CH₃OH | CH₄O |
| Ethanol | 2 | CH₃CH₂OH | C₂H₆O |
| Propanol | 3 | CH₃CH₂CH₂OH | C₃H₈O |

The name ends in **-ol**; the stem (meth-, eth-, prop-) gives the number of carbon atoms. Ethanol is often written C₂H₅OH.

Displayed formula of ethanol, with every bond drawn:

```
    H   H
    |   |
H - C - C - O - H
    |   |
    H   H
```

**Worked example: spotting the alcohols.** Which of these are alcohols? P: C₄H₉OH. R: CH₃CH₂CH₃. S: CH₃OH. T: CH₃CH₂COOH.

- P ends in –OH attached to a carbon chain: an **alcohol**. You need only recognise it, not name it.
- R has only carbon and hydrogen: a hydrocarbon.
- S is methanol: an **alcohol**.
- T contains –COOH. The O–H here is part of a carboxyl group, so T is a carboxylic acid.

Answer: **P and S**.

## 3.10.3.1 b: properties and uses of alcohols

Methanol, ethanol and propanol share four behaviours.

**1. They dissolve in water to form a neutral solution.** The –OH group does not release H⁺ or OH⁻ ions into the water. Universal indicator stays green, pH 7.

**2. They react with sodium to produce hydrogen.** A small piece of sodium fizzes, gets smaller and disappears, more gently than with water. The gas gives a squeaky pop with a lighted splint. A sodium compound of the alcohol forms.

```
2CH₃OH + 2Na → 2CH₃ONa + H₂
```

**3. They burn in air.** With plenty of oxygen, complete combustion gives carbon dioxide and water, and releases energy.

**4. Uses.** They are used as **fuels** and **solvents**. Ethanol is the main alcohol in **alcoholic drinks**.

**Worked example: balancing the combustion of methanol.**

1. Write the formulae: CH₃OH + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
2. Balance carbon: one C each side, so 1 CO₂.
3. Balance hydrogen: CH₃OH has 4 H, so 2 H₂O.
4. Count oxygen on the right: 2 + 2 = 4. The left already has 1 O in methanol, so O₂ must supply 3 atoms, which is 1½ O₂.
5. Double everything to clear the half:

```
2CH₃OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 4H₂O
```

Check: C 2 = 2; H 8 = 8; O 2 + 6 = 8 and 4 + 4 = 8.

For ethanol the same method gives C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O.

**Worked example: gas volume from sodium.** 0.69 g of sodium reacts completely with excess methanol. Calculate the volume of hydrogen formed at room temperature and pressure. (Ar Na = 23; molar gas volume 24 dm³.)

1. Moles of sodium = 0.69 ÷ 23 = 0.030 mol.
2. The equation shows 2 Na → 1 H₂, so moles of H₂ = 0.030 ÷ 2 = 0.015 mol.
3. Volume = 0.015 × 24 = **0.36 dm³** (360 cm³).

## 3.10.3.1 c: oxidation of ethanol

Ethanol can be **oxidised** to **ethanoic acid**, a carboxylic acid. There are two routes in the specification.

- **Chemical oxidising agents.** Warming ethanol with an oxidising agent converts it to ethanoic acid. A common laboratory choice is acidified potassium dichromate, which turns from orange to green as it reacts.
- **Microbial action.** Microbes use oxygen from the air to oxidise ethanol, which is why opened wine turns sour.

Ethanoic acid is the main acid in **vinegar**.

Overall, for oxidation by oxygen:

```
C₂H₅OH + O₂ → CH₃COOH + H₂O
```

Oxidation keeps the two-carbon chain. Combustion breaks the molecule down completely to carbon dioxide and water.

**Worked example: mass of acid from ethanol.** 6.9 g of ethanol is fully oxidised to ethanoic acid. Calculate the mass of ethanoic acid formed. (Ar H = 1, C = 12, O = 16.)

1. Mr ethanol, C₂H₅OH = 24 + 6 + 16 = 46. Moles = 6.9 ÷ 46 = 0.15 mol.
2. The ratio is 1 : 1, so 0.15 mol of ethanoic acid forms.
3. Mr ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH = 24 + 4 + 32 = 60. Mass = 0.15 × 60 = **9.0 g**.

## 3.10.3.2 a: recognising carboxylic acids

Carboxylic acids contain the **–COOH** functional group. The carbon in –COOH counts towards the name.

| Name | Carbon atoms | Structural formula |
|---|---|---|
| Methanoic acid | 1 | HCOOH |
| Ethanoic acid | 2 | CH₃COOH |
| Propanoic acid | 3 | CH₃CH₂COOH |

Displayed formula of ethanoic acid. The carboxyl carbon has a double bond to one oxygen and a single bond to an O–H:

```
    H   O
    |   ||
H - C - C - O - H
    |
    H
```

Recognise a carboxylic acid from its name (ending **-oic acid**) or formula; you only need to name these three.

## 3.10.3.2 b: reactions and strength of carboxylic acids

**In water.** Carboxylic acids dissolve to give **acidic** solutions. Universal indicator turns orange or red.

**With carbonates.** Like other acids, they react with metal carbonates to give a salt, water and **carbon dioxide**. You see fizzing, and the gas turns limewater milky. The salts take the ending **-oate**: methanoates, ethanoates, propanoates. You must be able to write the balanced equations.

**Worked example: propanoic acid and potassium carbonate.**

1. Each acid molecule gives up one H, and the carbonate ion is 2–, so two acid molecules are needed.
2. The salt is potassium propanoate, CH₃CH₂COOK.

```
2CH₃CH₂COOH + K₂CO₃ → 2CH₃CH₂COOK + H₂O + CO₂
```

3. Check: C 6 + 1 = 7 and 6 + 1 = 7; H 12 = 10 + 2; O 4 + 3 = 7 and 4 + 1 + 2 = 7; K 2 = 2.

Now the amounts. 2.76 g of potassium carbonate reacts with excess propanoic acid. What volume of carbon dioxide forms at room temperature and pressure? (Ar K = 39, C = 12, O = 16.)

- Mr K₂CO₃ = 78 + 12 + 48 = 138. Moles = 2.76 ÷ 138 = 0.020 mol.
- 1 K₂CO₃ gives 1 CO₂, so 0.020 mol CO₂.
- Volume = 0.020 × 24 = **0.48 dm³** (480 cm³).

**With alcohols.** With an **acid catalyst**, a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol to make an **ester** and water. See 3.10.3.3 below.

**Why carboxylic acids are weak acids.** When a carboxylic acid dissolves, only a small fraction of its molecules ionise. Most stay as whole molecules. The forward and backward reactions both happen, so use the ⇌ sign:

```
CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺
```

A **strong acid** such as hydrochloric acid ionises completely. At the **same concentration**, the strong acid releases far more H⁺ ions, and more H⁺ means a lower pH. So, at equal concentrations, the solution of the weak acid has the **higher pH**.

**Worked example: explaining pH data.** A student tests 0.10 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid and 0.10 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid with universal indicator. She records pH 1 and pH 3. Which is which, and why?

1. Hydrochloric acid ionises completely, giving the higher H⁺ concentration and the **lower pH (1)**.
2. Ethanoic acid only partly ionises, giving fewer H⁺ ions and the **higher pH (3)**.

A weak acid is not the same as a dilute acid. "Weak" describes how much the acid ionises. "Dilute" means there is only a small amount of acid in each dm³ of solution.

## 3.10.3.3 a: esters and ethyl ethanoate

Esters contain the **–COO–** functional group, with a carbon atom on both sides. The only ester you must name is **ethyl ethanoate**, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃, made from ethanol and ethanoic acid:

```
C₂H₅OH  +  CH₃COOH  →  CH₃COOC₂H₅  +  H₂O
ethanol    ethanoic acid  ethyl ethanoate  water
```

An acid catalyst is needed. Displayed formula:

```
    H   O       H   H
    |   ||      |   |
H - C - C - O - C - C - H
    |           |   |
    H           H   H
```

**Recognising an ester.** From a name: two words, the second ending in **-oate** (for example methyl propanoate). From a formula: –COO– sits in the middle of the chain, not at the end. Compare CH₃CH₂COOH (ends in –COOH, an acid) with CH₃COOCH₃ (carbon on both sides of –COO–, an ester). Both are C₃H₆O₂, so a molecular formula alone cannot decide.

**Worked example: mass of ester.** 9.0 g of ethanoic acid reacts with excess ethanol. Calculate the maximum mass of ethyl ethanoate. (Ar H = 1, C = 12, O = 16.)

1. Moles of ethanoic acid = 9.0 ÷ 60 = 0.15 mol.
2. The ratio is 1 : 1, so at most 0.15 mol ester.
3. Mr CH₃COOC₂H₅ = 48 + 8 + 32 = 88. Mass = 0.15 × 88 = **13.2 g**.

You will collect less than this. Because the reaction is reversible it never goes to completion, and separating the ester from the mixture loses some.

## 3.10.3.3 b: properties and uses of esters

Esters are **volatile**: they evaporate easily at room temperature. They have **distinctive**, often fruity, **smells**. Together these explain their use as **flavourings** and in **perfumes**: the molecules escape from food or skin and reach your nose.

## Common errors

- Calling C₂H₅OH a base or an alkali because it contains OH. Alcohol solutions are neutral.
- Writing CO₂ and H₂O as the products of oxidising ethanol with an oxidising agent or microbes. That is combustion; oxidation gives ethanoic acid.
- Counting the carbons in propanoic acid as two by forgetting the –COOH carbon.
- Using → instead of ⇌ for the ionisation of a weak acid.
- Saying a weak acid has a higher pH "because it is more dilute". The concentrations are the same; the difference is the degree of ionisation.
- Leaving the acid catalyst out when describing ester formation.
