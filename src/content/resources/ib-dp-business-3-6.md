This guide goes deeper into section 3.6 (Efficiency ratio analysis) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024. The whole section is HL only. You must calculate four efficiency ratios (stock turnover in times or days), interpret them, judge strategies to improve them, and distinguish insolvency from bankruptcy.

The broader [3.5 and 3.6 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-profitability-and-liquidity-ratio-analysis-and-efficiency-ratio/) sets out the formulae with a one-year example. This page compares two years, shows how the day-counts combine into a picture of cash, costs each strategy in money terms and links the ratios to insolvency. Companion pages: the [3.6 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-business-3-6-revision-notes/) and the [3.6 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-business-3-6-practice/). The SL and HL ratios are in the [3.5 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-3-5/).

## The formulae

| Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|
| Stock turnover (times) | cost of sales ÷ average stock |
| Stock turnover (days) | (average stock ÷ cost of sales) × 365 |
| Debtor days | (debtors ÷ total sales revenue) × 365 |
| Creditor days | (creditors ÷ cost of sales) × 365 |
| Gearing ratio | (non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed) × 100 |

Average stock = (opening stock + closing stock) ÷ 2. Capital employed = non-current liabilities + equity.

Note which figure goes on the bottom. Debtors relate to sales, so debtor days use sales revenue. Stock and creditors relate to what the business buys, so they use cost of sales.

## A two-year analysis

### Data: Mossgiel Garden Centre

Mossgiel Garden Centre is invented; all figures are invented and in dollars.

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 1,200,000 | 1,300,000 |
| Cost of sales | 720,000 | 800,000 |
| Opening stock | 100,000 | 140,000 |
| Closing stock | 140,000 | 180,000 |
| Debtors | 120,000 | 160,000 |
| Creditors | 80,000 | 70,000 |
| Non-current liabilities | 150,000 | 400,000 |
| Equity | 450,000 | 460,000 |

### Results

| Ratio | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Average stock | 120,000 | 160,000 |
| Stock turnover (times) | 6.0 | 5.0 |
| Stock turnover (days) | 60.8 | 73.0 |
| Debtor days | 36.5 | 44.9 |
| Creditor days | 40.6 | 31.9 |
| Gearing ratio | 25.0% | 46.5% |

Sample working for Year 2: stock turnover = 800,000 ÷ 160,000 = 5.0 times; debtor days = 160,000 ÷ 1,300,000 × 365 = 44.9 days; gearing = 400,000 ÷ (400,000 + 460,000) × 100 = 46.5%.

### Interpretation, ratio by ratio

* **Stock turnover worsened.** Stock now sits for about 73 days instead of 61. For a garden centre selling live plants, slower turnover raises the risk of stock dying or losing value, as well as tying up cash.
* **Debtor days rose by about 8 days.** Customers, perhaps trade buyers such as landscapers, are taking longer to pay. Cash from sales arrives later.
* **Creditor days fell by about 9 days.** Mossgiel is paying suppliers faster. That could reflect stricter supplier terms or a lost credit arrangement.
* **Gearing nearly doubled.** Long-term borrowing rose from 150,000 to 400,000, so almost half of capital employed is now borrowed. Interest and repayments are fixed commitments whatever sales do.

### Combining the day-counts

Adding stock days and debtor days, then subtracting creditor days, gives a rough number of days for which the business must fund its own trading before cash comes back. This is an interpretive step built from the guide's ratios, not a separate ratio in the guide.

* Year 1: 60.8 + 36.5 − 40.6 = about **57 days**.
* Year 2: 73.0 + 44.9 − 31.9 = **86 days**.

All three day-counts moved the wrong way, so the gap grew by about 29 days. This explains why a business that is growing and profitable can still feel short of cash, and why it may have needed the extra borrowing that pushed gearing up.

## Strategies, costed

The guide asks for possible strategies. Putting a money value on a strategy shows why it matters.

### Reduce debtor days

If Mossgiel brings Year 2 debtor days down to 30, debtors would be 30 ÷ 365 × 1,300,000 = about 106,849. That releases about **53,151** of cash, more than enough to cover a short-term shortfall. Methods: shorter credit terms, early payment discounts, credit checks on new trade customers, and chasing late payers. Risk: strict terms may push trade buyers to rival suppliers, and discounts reduce revenue.

### Increase creditor days

If Mossgiel negotiated a return to 45-day terms, creditors would be about 45 ÷ 365 × 800,000 = 98,630, keeping about **28,630** more cash in the business. Risk: suppliers may refuse, withdraw discounts or put Mossgiel lower down the delivery queue in the busy spring season.

### Improve stock turnover

Methods: order smaller quantities more often, drop slow-selling lines, run promotions on ageing stock, and use sales data to forecast demand. For live plants, just-in-time ordering has limits, because suppliers also face seasons. Risk: running out of popular items in peak season loses sales.

### Reduce gearing

If Mossgiel issued 100,000 of new shares and used the money to repay part of its loan, non-current liabilities would fall to 300,000 and equity would rise to 560,000. Gearing would be 300,000 ÷ 860,000 × 100 = **34.9%**. Risk: new shareholders dilute control and expect dividends. Alternatively, retained profit can repay debt over time, but that means lower dividends.

Whether high gearing is a problem depends on the case. If borrowing financed an asset that earns more than the interest rate, owners benefit. If sales are seasonal or falling, fixed interest raises the risk of not meeting payments.

## Insolvency versus bankruptcy

**Insolvency** is a financial position: a business or person cannot pay debts as they fall due. **Bankruptcy** is a legal status or procedure that can follow insolvency, in which a court or official process deals with the debts. The guide lists the distinction without definitions; the terms used, and which apply to companies or individuals, vary between countries, so keep your answer general.

How the ratios link to insolvency:

* Long stock days and debtor days delay the arrival of cash.
* Short creditor days speed up payments out.
* High gearing adds fixed interest and repayments.

Together these can make a profitable business insolvent: profit is recorded when a sale is made, but bills must be paid in cash. Mossgiel's 86-day gap and 46.5% gearing are warning signs, not proof of insolvency, but they show where the pressure is building.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Recommend one strategy for Mossgiel to improve its efficiency ratios.*

> Mossgiel's most valuable quick win is reducing debtor days, which rose from 36.5 to 44.9. **[uses the data]** Bringing them to 30 would release about 53,151, which eases the cash pressure behind the rise in gearing. **[quantified benefit]** The risk is losing trade customers to rival suppliers, so Mossgiel should offer a small early payment discount rather than simply shortening terms. **[risk and mitigation]** Reducing stock days matters too, but with live plants and seasonal demand it is harder to achieve without lost sales. **[alternative weighed]** I recommend tighter credit control first, provided trade customers are not highly price-sensitive; if they are, improving stock management should come first. **[judgement with condition]**

## Common mistakes

* Using sales revenue for creditor days or stock turnover.
* Using closing stock when opening and closing stock are both given.
* Saying more creditor days is always better; it depends on suppliers' terms.
* Treating high gearing as bad in every case.
* Using insolvency and bankruptcy as if they meant the same thing.

## Exam technique

* **Calculate**: state the formula, show the substitution, and give units (times, days or %).
* **Analyse**: explain the cause and the effect on cash or risk, using case details.
* **Evaluate** or **recommend**: put a value on the strategy where possible and judge it against its risk.
