This guide uses the topic **Education for all** from the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025). The topic is on the topic list in section 3 of the syllabus, and the skills it is used for are set out in section 2 (assessment objectives) and section 4 (details of the assessment). The syllabus says the topic list is for all components, so this topic could be the context for Component 1 Written Exam, or you could choose it for Component 2 Individual Report or Component 3 Team Project. **Knowledge of this topic is not assessed.** This page shows you how to use the topic to practise the skills that are assessed.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-education-for-all-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-education-for-all-practice/). For the whole course, see the [Global Perspectives hub](/boards/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/) and the [0457 checklist](/checklists/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/).

## What "knowledge is not assessed" means for you

The syllabus states: "Knowledge of content is not assessed in this syllabus." In the Written Exam, it says candidates are "not assessed on their prior knowledge and understanding of the topic or global issue presented in the source material". So you will never gain marks for remembering facts about schools around the world.

What you gain marks for is what you **do** with information. If an exam insert is about education, you must find the issue, separate the perspectives, test the evidence and judge the actions. In your coursework, you research education yourself and use that research to build an argument. Studying this topic is practice for those moves.

## Finding issues at global, national and local level

The syllabus defines an **issue** as "an important subject or problem for discussion". A topic is broad; an issue is a specific problem inside it that people disagree about.

**Global issues within Education for all**
- Why some children never start school or leave early.
- Whether being in school means a child is actually learning (access versus quality).
- Whether girls and boys have equal chances to attend and stay in school.
- Who should pay for education: governments, families, charities or businesses.

**National issues**
- Whether school should be free and compulsory, and up to what age.
- Which language children should be taught in.
- The role of private schools alongside state schools.

**Local issues**
- Distance to the nearest school and the cost of getting there.
- A shortage of teachers or books in local schools.
- Whether local schools can include children with disabilities.

**Real-world reference points you can research and cite**
- The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), Article 26, states that everyone has the right to education, that education shall be free at least in the elementary and fundamental stages, and that elementary education shall be compulsory.
- The World Conference on Education for All was held in Jomtien, Thailand, in 1990.
- In 2015, United Nations member states adopted Sustainable Development Goal 4: "Ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all".
- In Pakistan, Article 25-A of the Constitution, added by the Eighteenth Amendment in 2010, requires the state to provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen.

**Evaluating these as evidence.** These documents are strong evidence of what governments have **agreed** or **promised**. They are weak evidence of what actually **happens** in classrooms. A law that makes education free does not prove every child attends. To judge progress you need data, such as enrolment and completion figures, and you should check how recent and how reliable that data is.

## Perspectives on education issues

The syllabus defines a **perspective** as "a viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning". A perspective is more than an opinion: it has reasons behind it.

Take one national issue: **should governments rely on low-fee private schools to help get every child into education?**

- **Supporters** argue that private schools can open quickly where the state has not built enough schools, give parents a choice, and that some parents judge them to offer better teaching.
- **Critics** argue that any fee can keep the poorest families out, that quality varies a great deal, and that education is a right the state should provide directly.
- **A parent** might weigh cost against what they see happening in the local state school.
- **A teacher** might focus on pay, class size and training in each type of school.

The syllabus asks you to consider perspectives "objectively and with empathy" (AO2). That means explaining **why** each group holds its view: their values, their experience, and what they stand to gain or lose. You do not need to agree. In the Individual Report, the syllabus says perspectives "do not have to be opposing perspectives but they must be clearly different".

Another useful issue is **language of instruction**. Some argue children learn best in their home language in early years; others argue that a national or international language gives wider opportunities later. Families, governments and teachers can all hold reasoned views here. Present both fairly.

## Causes and consequences

AO1 asks you to research global issues, "their causes and consequences". For any education issue, separate the two clearly.

**Possible causes of children being out of school** (lines of analysis to research and test with evidence):
- **Cost** — fees, uniforms, books and transport, plus the income a family loses if a child works instead.
- **Distance and safety** — a long or unsafe journey.
- **Supply** — too few schools or teachers.
- **Conflict or disaster** — schools closed or destroyed.
- **Social expectations** — beliefs about who needs education, or early marriage.
- **Disability** — buildings or teaching that do not meet a child's needs.

**Possible consequences**, at different levels:
- **Personal** — fewer choices of work and further study.
- **Local** — fewer trained people such as teachers and nurses in the community.
- **National** — effects on a country's economy and on how informed its citizens are.
- **Global** — slower progress on shared goals such as SDG 4.

**Building a chain.** Strong analysis links steps: *the nearest secondary school is far away → transport costs money → families with less money keep children at home → those children leave with fewer qualifications*. Each arrow is a claim you should support with evidence.

## Judging sources and evidence

For Component 1 the syllabus says you will benefit from understanding these terms. Here they are with education examples:

| Term | Meaning | Education example |
|---|---|---|
| Fact | Can be checked as true or false | "The school has 400 pupils." |
| Opinion | A personal view that cannot be proved | "Our school uniform is too expensive." |
| Claim | A statement put forward as true, which needs support | "Free school meals improve attendance." |
| Value | A belief about what is important or right | "Every child deserves to learn." |
| Prediction | A statement about the future | "Fees will push more children out of school next year." |
| Generalisation | A broad statement from limited cases | "All rural parents keep girls at home." |
| Bias | A one-sided presentation | Reporting only the successes of a scheme |
| Vested interest | The source gains if people accept its view | A school chain praising private schools |

The syllabus also says you should "analyse and evaluate use of evidence and language". Look for loaded words ("disastrous", "miracle") and for numbers that sound bigger than they are. A rise from 68% to 83% is **15 percentage points**, which is about a 22% rise in relative terms. A source that mixes these up may be careless or may be exaggerating.

**Worked example.** A fictional source says: *"A campaign group for free schooling asked 300 parents at the market in the fictional town of Marrowby why a child in their family had left school. 45% said fees. Fees are the main reason children leave school across the country."*

> The source claims that fees are the main reason children leave school nationally. Its evidence is a survey in which 45% of 300 Marrowby parents, that is 135 parents, named fees. This supports the idea that fees matter in Marrowby, but the conclusion goes further than the evidence. The sample comes from one market in one town, so it may not represent rural areas or other regions, and the step from Marrowby to "across the country" is a generalisation. 45% is also less than half, so 55% of parents gave other reasons, which weakens the word "main". The campaign group has a vested interest in showing that fees are harmful, which may have shaped the question it asked. The evidence is useful for showing that fees are one important barrier locally, but it is not reliable enough to support a national claim.

Notice the order: the claim, the evidence, what the evidence does support, then specific weaknesses, then a judgement.

## Courses of action and their effects

The syllabus aims include suggesting "evidence-based courses of action" and considering "the concept of sustainability". When you judge an action, ask:

- **Does it tackle a cause** you identified, or only a symptom?
- **Who benefits, and who might lose out?**
- **What does it cost, and who pays?**
- **Is it sustainable** — will it continue when the first money runs out?
- **What evidence suggests it would work?**
- **Could it have unintended consequences?**

Possible actions on education issues include removing school fees, providing school transport, training and recruiting teachers from local communities, adapting schools for children with disabilities, and evening classes for adults.

**Worked example (evaluating an action).**

> Removing school fees tackles the cost barrier directly, so it is likely to raise enrolment among families with the least money. However, if the government does not also pay for more teachers and classrooms, class sizes may rise, and quality could fall, so more children might attend without learning more. Fees are also only one cost: uniforms, transport and lost family income remain. Removing fees is therefore likely to work best as part of a package with funding for teachers, and its success should be judged by learning results as well as enrolment.

This paragraph moves from effect, to limitation, to judgement. That is what "assess actions" questions reward.

## Individual Report ideas

The syllabus says the issue you choose for Component 2 "should be one that divides opinion". You devise a global research question and use it as your title. The report must be 1500–2000 words, include local and/or national and global perspectives, analyse causes and consequences, consider different courses of action, propose one, cite sources, evaluate sources, and end by answering your own question.

**Possible questions on this topic:**
- Should governments rely on private schools to help achieve education for all?
- Should children be taught in their home language in their first years of school?
- Is getting every child into school more important than improving the quality of teaching?

**Weak versus strong.** "What is education for all?" is not a research question: it asks for description and does not divide opinion. "Should governments rely on private schools to help achieve education for all?" invites different perspectives, has causes and consequences to analyse, and leads to a course of action.

Your teacher will confirm whether your question is manageable. See the [Individual Report guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-global-perspectives-individual-report/) for structure.

## Team Project ideas

For Component 3, teams of two to five choose a topic and identify a **local** issue, research different perspectives, plan and carry out an action, and measure its success. The team writes an Explanation of Research and Planning (300–400 words) and submits Evidence of Action. Each member writes a Reflective Paper (750–1000 words).

**Possible local issues and actions:**
- Younger pupils in a nearby primary school have few books at home → a book collection and a weekly reading-buddy session.
- Some parents cannot read school letters in the school's language → a short translated information sheet, made with the school's permission.
- Adults in the area do not know about free evening literacy classes → an awareness campaign.

**Measuring success** must be planned at the start. For the reading-buddy action, you could record how many sessions took place and ask pupils and teachers short questions before and after. Your teacher will confirm what is realistic and safe. See the [Team Project guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-global-perspectives-team-project/).

## Common errors

- Writing facts about education you remember instead of using the sources in the exam.
- Treating a law or promise (such as free schooling) as proof that every child attends.
- Saying a source is "biased" without naming the interest and quoting the language.
- Mixing up percentages and percentage points.
- Listing causes without linking them to consequences in a chain.
- Proposing an action without saying how it would work, who pays and how success would be measured.
- Choosing an Individual Report question that does not divide opinion.
- Picking a Team Project issue that is global rather than local.
