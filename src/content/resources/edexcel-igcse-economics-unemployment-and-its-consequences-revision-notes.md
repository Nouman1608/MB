## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Unemployment | People able and willing to work, and looking for work, who cannot find a job |
| ILO measure | A survey test used in many countries: no paid work in the reference week, available to start within about two weeks, and have looked for work in the last four weeks or so |
| Labour force | Employed + unemployed |
| Economically inactive | Of working age but not working and not passing the ILO test, such as full-time students and carers |
| Unemployment rate | Number unemployed, divided by the labour force, then multiplied by 100 |

## Formulas and method

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Labour force | Add employed and unemployed |
| Rate | Unemployed ÷ labour force × 100 |
| Number from a rate | Rate ÷ 100 × labour force |
| Change in the number | (New − old) ÷ old × 100 |
| Change in the rate | New rate − old rate, in percentage points |
| Cost to government of one more unemployed person | Benefit + lost income tax + lost sales tax |
| Lost output | Extra unemployed × average output per worker |

### Method in steps: rate questions

1. Write down employed and unemployed.
2. Add them to get the labour force. Do not add the inactive.
3. Divide unemployed by the labour force and multiply by 100.
4. Give the answer with a percentage sign, and show each line of working.

### Worked reminder

Country B (illustrative): 9,200 employed and 800 unemployed, in thousands.
Labour force = 9,200 + 800 = 10,000 thousand.
Rate = 800 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **8%**.

Later the rate is 11% with the same labour force. Number unemployed = 11 ÷ 100 × 10,000 = 1,100 thousand. Rise = 300 thousand, which is 300 ÷ 800 × 100 = **37.5%**. The rate rose by **3 percentage points**.

## The five types at a glance

| Type | Cause | Length | Key phrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Fall in total demand | Medium, tied to the cycle | Fewer sales, so fewer workers |
| Structural | Industry decline or skills mismatch | Long | Skills or location do not match vacancies |
| Seasonal | Time-of-year changes in demand | Short, repeating | Predictable each year |
| Voluntary | Worker rejects available work at the pay offered | Varies | Choice, not lack of jobs |
| Frictional | Time between jobs or entering work | Short | Search and matching delay |

Must-know distinctions:

- **Cyclical or structural?** Cyclical affects many industries together and eases when demand recovers. Structural hits one industry or area and does not ease with demand alone.
- **Seasonal or frictional?** Seasonal repeats because the work itself disappears for part of the year. Frictional is a gap while moving between jobs.
- **Voluntary or frictional?** Voluntary is a refusal of jobs on offer at the going pay. Frictional is waiting while searching or being matched.

### Chains to learn for each type

- **Cyclical:** total demand falls, so firms sell less, so they cut output and make workers redundant, so incomes fall and spending drops again.
- **Structural:** an industry shrinks because of new technology or cheaper imports, so its workers lose jobs, so their skills do not fit the vacancies elsewhere, so the spell is long unless they retrain or move.
- **Seasonal:** demand for the work depends on the time of year, so staff are laid off in the quiet months, so the rate rises and falls in a repeating pattern.
- **Voluntary:** the pay on offer is below what the worker will accept, so the worker stays out of work and keeps looking.
- **Frictional:** workers and vacancies both exist, but information and travel take time, so people are briefly between jobs.

### Worked reminder: seasonal figures

A ski village (illustrative) has a labour force of 2,500. Unemployed: 450 in May, 100 in January. Rates: 450 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = 18% and 100 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = 4%. The gap is 14 percentage points. Because it repeats each year, it is seasonal, and a fall from May to January is not a sign of recovery.

## Consequences: cause, effect, next effect

| Impact | Chain |
|---|---|
| Output | Workers idle, so less produced, so actual output is below productive potential (inside the frontier) |
| Use of scarce resources | Labour is wasted; skills fade over long spells; machines may be idle too |
| Poverty | Benefit is lower than the wage, so household income falls, so living standards drop and the risk of poverty rises |
| Benefit spending | More claimants, so a larger government bill |
| Tax revenue | Fewer people earning and spending, so less income tax and sales tax |
| Consumer confidence | Fear of job loss, so households save and spend less, so firms sell less |
| Business confidence | Weak demand expected, so less investment and hiring |
| Society | Stress, ill health, uneven burdens on regions and groups, possible rise in crime |

### Worked reminder: government cost

Benefit 5,200, lost income tax 2,800, lost sales tax 600 per person.
Cost per person = 5,200 + 2,800 + 600 = 8,600.
For 60,000 people: 60,000 × 8,600 = **516 million**.

### Worked reminder: household income

A household has one earner on 2,000 a month after tax. The earner is made redundant and the benefit is 900. New income = 900. Fall = (2,000 − 900) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **55%**. A fall of this size means the household has to cut spending on non-essentials, and it risks poverty if savings run out.

### Evaluation checklist

- Type of unemployment (short frictional against long structural)
- Size of the rise and how long it lasts
- Who is affected: region, age, industry
- Short run against long run
- How generous benefits are (less poverty, higher government cost)

## Quick self-test

1. Labour force is 4,000 and 300 are unemployed. What is the rate?
2. The rate is 5% and the labour force is 40,000. How many are unemployed?
3. Unemployed workers rise from 30 to 45. What is the percentage change?
4. A worker is paid 1,500 and pays 20% income tax. What is the income after tax?
5. A worker's income after tax of 1,200 is replaced by a benefit of 400. What is the percentage fall?
6. 800 extra workers are unemployed and each would have produced 15,000 a year. What is the lost output?
7. State the type of unemployment for a tour guide out of work every winter.
8. State the type for a miner whose mine closes, in a town with no mining jobs.
9. Explain one way unemployment affects tax revenue.
10. Explain why unemployment can lower business confidence.

### Answers

1. 300 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = **7.5%**.
2. 5 ÷ 100 × 40,000 = **2,000**.
3. (45 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = **50%**.
4. 1,500 × 0.80 = **1,200**.
5. (1,200 − 400) ÷ 1,200 × 100 = **66.7%** (to one decimal place).
6. 800 × 15,000 = **12,000,000**.
7. **Seasonal**.
8. **Structural**.
9. Fewer people earn wages, so less income tax is collected. Their spending falls, so sales tax revenue also falls.
10. Fewer households have secure incomes, so spending falls. Firms expect weak sales, so they delay investment and hiring.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by everyone of working age, which includes the inactive, in place of the labour force.
- Giving a change in points when asked for a percentage change, or the reverse.
- Defining the term with only one part, when a "What is meant by" question needs two separate parts.
- Naming a type without a cause that matches it, for example calling any job loss "cyclical".
- Listing consequences ("output falls, tax falls") with no chain linking cause to effect.
- Forgetting that unemployment benefit and lost tax both affect the government's budget.
- Writing about the economy generally when the question sets a context, such as a named town or industry.
- Giving a calculation answer without working.
- Ending an "Evaluate" answer without a supported judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is unemployment in Edexcel IGCSE Economics?**
Unemployment means people who are able and willing to work, and are looking for work, but cannot find a job. In the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, low unemployment is point c) of section 2.1.1.

**What is the ILO measure of unemployment?**
Under the International Labour Organization measure, a person is unemployed if they have no paid work in the reference week, are available to start within about two weeks, and have actively looked for work in about the last four weeks.

**What is the difference between cyclical and structural unemployment?**
Cyclical unemployment comes from a fall in total demand, affects many industries together and eases when demand recovers. Structural unemployment comes from the decline of an industry or a skills mismatch, is concentrated in certain industries or regions and can last a long time.

**What is the difference between seasonal and frictional unemployment?**
Seasonal unemployment repeats because the work disappears for part of the year, as in tourism or farming. Frictional unemployment is the short time people spend moving between jobs or looking for a first job.
