## Earth structure and plates

Crust, mantle, core. The **lithosphere** (crust and uppermost mantle) is broken into plates that move because of mantle processes: **convection, slab pull, ridge push**.

- **Convection:** heat from the core and from radioactive decay makes mantle rock circulate very slowly, dragging the plates above.
- **Slab pull:** a cold, dense oceanic plate sinking at a subduction zone pulls the rest of the plate behind it. Many geologists regard this as the strongest driving force.
- **Ridge push:** new lithosphere at a mid-ocean ridge sits high and slides down and away from the ridge under gravity.

**Oceanic crust** is thinner, denser and younger; **continental crust** is thicker, less dense and older. This density difference explains why oceanic crust subducts and continental crust usually does not.

## Plate boundaries

| Boundary | Movement | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Divergent | Plates move apart | Mid-ocean ridges, continental rifts; shallow earthquakes, basaltic volcanism |
| Convergent | Plates move together | Subduction: ocean trenches, powerful earthquakes, explosive volcanoes; continental collision builds fold mountains |
| Transform (conservative) | Plates slide past | Strong shallow earthquakes; crust not destroyed |

Located examples help: Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (divergent); the East African Rift is a continental rift; Japan and the Andes lie above subduction zones; the Himalaya result from India colliding with Asia; the San Andreas Fault in California is a transform boundary.

At subduction zones, earthquakes occur along a sloping band within the sinking plate, the **Benioff zone**, so foci become deeper with distance from the trench.

**Hotspots** are volcanoes away from boundaries, above rising mantle plumes beneath moving plates.

Hawaii is the classic example: as the Pacific Plate moves over the plume, older volcanoes are carried away and become extinct, forming a chain that gets older with distance from the active island.

## Earthquakes

- Release stored strain energy when rock slips along a fault.
- **Focus** = point of rupture; **epicentre** = point on the surface above it.
- Waves: P, S and surface waves.
- **Magnitude:** moment magnitude scale, logarithmic. **Intensity:** Modified Mercalli scale, based on observed effects.
- Each whole-number rise means ten times the ground motion amplitude and roughly 32 times the energy.

| Wave | Movement | Speed and path |
|---|---|---|
| P (primary) | Compression, pushing and pulling rock in the direction of travel | Fastest; travels through solids and liquids |
| S (secondary) | Shearing, side to side | Slower; travels through solids only |
| Surface waves | Rolling and side-to-side motion at the surface | Slowest but usually cause most damage |

**Shallow-focus** earthquakes are generally more damaging than deep ones of the same magnitude, because less energy is lost before the waves reach the surface.

## Volcanic hazards

Lava flows, pyroclastic flows, ash fall, gas emissions, lahars, and tsunami for submarine or coastal events. The **Volcanic Explosivity Index** classifies eruption size. Link **magma viscosity and gas content** to eruption style.

| Magma | Silica and viscosity | Eruption style | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basaltic | Low silica, runny | Effusive lava flows; gas escapes easily | Divergent boundaries and hotspots (Iceland, Hawaii); shield volcanoes |
| Andesitic | Intermediate | Explosive; gas trapped until pressure is released | Subduction zones (Soufriere Hills on Montserrat, many Andean volcanoes); composite cones |
| Rhyolitic | High silica, very viscous | Very explosive; may form calderas | Continental settings such as Yellowstone |

The VEI runs from 0 to 8 and is logarithmic, based mainly on the volume of material erupted and the height of the eruption column.

- **Pyroclastic flow:** a fast, extremely hot avalanche of gas, ash and rock that hugs the ground; the most deadly volcanic hazard close to the vent.
- **Lahar:** a volcanic mudflow formed when ash and debris mix with water from rain, melting snow and ice, or a crater lake; it can travel far down valleys.

## Related hazards

Landslides, liquefaction, tsunamis. A tsunami forms when sudden sea-floor displacement moves a large volume of water.

- **Tsunami behaviour:** in the open ocean the waves have very long wavelengths and low height and travel fast; as they reach shallow water they slow and grow much taller. A sudden retreat of the sea can be a natural warning. The 2004 Indian Ocean and 2011 Tohoku (Japan) tsunamis were both generated by very large subduction-zone earthquakes.
- **Liquefaction:** shaking makes loose, water-saturated sediment lose strength and behave like a liquid, so buildings tilt or sink. It caused heavy damage in Christchurch, New Zealand, in 2011.
- **Mass movement (landslides, rockfalls, debris flows):** happens when the forces pulling material downslope exceed the slope's strength. Physical triggers include heavy rain (which raises water pressure in the soil), earthquakes and steep slopes; human factors include deforestation, cutting slopes for roads and adding weight with buildings.

## Model paragraph

*Explain why volcanoes at subduction zones tend to erupt explosively.* "At subduction zones, the descending oceanic plate releases water into the overlying mantle, lowering its melting point and producing magma. As this magma rises through thick continental crust it becomes richer in silica, making it more viscous. Viscous magma traps dissolved gases, so pressure builds until it is released violently, fragmenting the magma into ash and producing pyroclastic flows. By contrast, low-silica basaltic magma at divergent boundaries lets gas escape, giving gentler effusive eruptions."

## Exam traps

- Confusing focus with epicentre.
- Treating magnitude as a linear scale.
- Mixing magnitude (energy) with intensity (observed effects).
- Saying all volcanoes lie on plate boundaries: hotspots do not.
- Saying S waves pass through the outer core: they cannot travel through liquids.
- Forgetting mass movement as a geophysical hazard in its own right.

1. Name the three plate boundary types.
2. Which boundary type involves subduction?
3. Define focus and epicentre.
4. Which scale measures earthquake intensity?
5. By what factor does amplitude change for a 2-unit magnitude rise?
6. What is a hotspot?
7. Why does oceanic crust subduct beneath continental crust?
8. What is slab pull?
9. Which seismic waves travel fastest, and which usually cause most damage?
10. Why are basaltic eruptions usually less explosive than andesitic ones?
11. What is a lahar?
12. Give one physical and one human cause of landslides.

**Answers:** 1. Divergent, convergent, transform. 2. Convergent. 3. Focus: point of rupture; epicentre: point on the surface directly above. 4. Modified Mercalli scale. 5. 100 times. 6. A volcanic area away from boundaries above a rising mantle plume. 7. It is denser than continental crust. 8. The pull of a cold, dense sinking slab on the rest of the plate. 9. P waves are fastest; surface waves usually cause most damage. 10. Low-silica basaltic magma is runny, so gas escapes easily instead of building pressure. 11. A volcanic mudflow of ash and debris mixed with water. 12. Physical: heavy rain or earthquakes; human: deforestation or cutting slopes for roads.
