Crime is behaviour that breaks the law; deviance is behaviour that breaks a society's norms, whether
or not it is illegal. Section 3.5 of AQA GCSE Sociology (8192), examined in Paper 2, asks you to
explain how both are socially constructed (3.5.1), how societies control behaviour (3.5.2), which
factors affect criminal and deviant behaviour and the public debates they create (3.5.3), and how
useful the main sources of data on crime are (3.5.4). The specification names five key
sociologists — Merton, Becker, Heidensohn, Albert Cohen and Carlen — whose ideas you must be able to
describe.

For the paper as a whole, see the
[crime and stratification study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-crime-and-stratification/).

## How are crime and deviance socially constructed?

What counts as crime or deviance varies between **societies**, over **time** and by **situation**.
Killing is murder in peacetime but may be honoured in war; behaviours once legal have been
criminalised and others decriminalised. Laws are made by people with power, so crime is a social
definition rather than a fixed quality of an act.

## What did Merton argue about the causes of crime?

Robert K. Merton ("Social Structure and Anomie", 1938), writing as a functionalist, argued that
American society emphasised **success goals** (wealth) more than the **legitimate means** of
achieving them. People in lower positions in the social structure lack those means, producing
**anomie** — a state where "anything goes". He described five responses:

| Adaptation | Goals | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conformity** | Accept | Accept | Gaining qualifications and a good job |
| **Innovation** | Accept | Reject | Theft or fraud to get money |
| **Ritualism** | Reject | Accept | A low-level clerk who follows rules but has given up ambition |
| **Retreatism** | Reject | Reject | "Dropping out", e.g. drug addiction |
| **Rebellion** | Replace | Replace | Revolutionaries seeking a new society |

**Criticisms:** ignores who makes laws and benefits from them; assumes value consensus; does not
explain why only some people experiencing anomie turn to crime; exaggerates working-class crime and
underplays white-collar crime.

## What did Becker argue?

Howard Becker (*Outsiders*, 1963), an interactionist, argued that an act only becomes deviant when
others **label** it so. Whether a label is applied depends on who commits the act, when and where,
who sees it and the negotiations involved. Agents of social control such as police and courts have
the power to make labels stick. The label becomes a **master status**, others treat the person
accordingly, and the person may come to see themselves that way — a **self-fulfilling prophecy**.
Rejection by others may encourage further deviance, beginning a **deviant career**, completed when
the person joins an organised deviant group with its own subculture.

## How does social control work?

| Formal social control | Informal social control |
|---|---|
| Written rules and laws | Unwritten rules and expectations |
| Police, courts, prisons, government | Family, peers, school, workplace, religion, media |
| Sanctions: arrest, fines, prison | Sanctions: praise, disapproval, gossip, exclusion |

**Perspectives:** functionalists see control maintaining value consensus; Marxists see control
protecting the ruling class; feminists see control maintaining patriarchy; interactionists focus on
how agents of control label people.

## Why do women commit less crime? Heidensohn

Frances Heidensohn (*Women and Crime*, 1985), a feminist, used control theory to argue that
male-dominated, patriarchal societies control women more effectively than men:

- **At home** — women are expected to spend most time on housework and childcare; challenging this
  risks male violence; men's financial power as breadwinners; daughters are more closely supervised
  than sons.
- **In public** — fear of male sexual violence and of losing reputation keeps women out of public
  spaces at night; the idea of "separate spheres".
- **At work** — male-dominated hierarchies and sexual harassment.

**Criticisms:** generalises about all women; claims are not always backed by strong research
evidence.

## Which factors affect criminal behaviour? (3.5.3)

- **Social class** — working-class people are over-represented in official statistics; explanations
  include blocked opportunities (Merton), subcultures (Cohen) and labelling; Marxists stress crimes
  of the powerful.
- **Gender** — men commit more recorded crime; explanations include control (Heidensohn),
  socialisation and the chivalry argument.
- **Ethnicity** — some minority groups are over-represented in stop and search and prison; debate
  centres on discrimination versus deprivation.
- **Age** — offending peaks in the teenage years and early twenties.

## What did Albert Cohen argue?

Albert Cohen (*Delinquent Boys*, 1955), a functionalist, argued working-class boys share mainstream
success goals but, because of **educational failure** (which he linked to **cultural deprivation**)
and poor job prospects, cannot reach them. They suffer **status frustration** and form a
**delinquent subculture** that values stealing, vandalism and truancy; success in the subculture
brings prestige among peers. *Criticism:* some argue working-class youths do not accept mainstream
goals in the first place, but act out of resentment towards those whose values they reject.

## What did Carlen find?

Pat Carlen (*Women, Crime and Poverty*, 1988), a feminist, carried out unstructured interviews with
39 mostly working-class women aged 15 to 46 who had been convicted of crimes, several of them in
prison or youth custody. Using control theory, she argued women are controlled by the promise of
rewards from two "deals": the **class deal** (consumer goods in return for wage labour) and the
**gender deal** (material and emotional rewards from a male breadwinner in return for love and
domestic labour). When these rewards fail, crime becomes a viable alternative. *Criticism:* a small
sample, although it supports the view that crime rises when social control breaks down.

## What public debates surround crime?

The specification names concerns over **violent crime**, **sentencing** (are sentences too lenient
or harsh?), **the treatment of young offenders** (punishment or rehabilitation?), **the prison
system** (does it reduce reoffending?), and **media coverage of crime** (does it exaggerate crime
and create fear or moral panics?).

## How useful is data on crime? (3.5.4)

| Source | How collected | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Police recorded crime** (official statistics) | Crimes reported to and recorded by police | National coverage; regular; shows trends in recorded offences | Many crimes unreported; police discretion in recording; changes in counting rules |
| **Victim surveys** — e.g. the Crime Survey for England and Wales (begun in 1982 as the British Crime Survey) | Asks a large sample about crimes they have experienced | Captures unreported crime | Misses crimes without direct victims, such as corporate crime; relies on memory |
| **Self-report studies** | Ask people which offences they have committed | Reveal hidden offending and offenders never caught | People may lie or forget; usually limited to minor offences |

The **dark figure** of crime is the crime that is unreported or unrecorded, and so does not appear in
official statistics. Victim surveys have generally recorded much more crime than police figures,
and both have shown a long fall in many types of crime since the mid-1990s, though patterns vary by
offence.

**Perspectives on data:** functionalists tend to treat official statistics as broadly showing who
commits crime; Marxists argue statistics reflect policing of the working class while crimes of the
powerful go unrecorded; feminists point out that domestic and sexual violence against women are
heavily under-reported.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which sociologists must I know for Paper 2 crime?** Merton and Becker (3.5.1), Heidensohn (3.5.2),
Albert Cohen and Carlen (3.5.3).

**What is the difference between crime and deviance?** Crime breaks the law; deviance breaks social
norms. Some acts are both, some only one.

**Why is the dark figure important?** Because it means official statistics may misrepresent both the
amount of crime and who commits it.
