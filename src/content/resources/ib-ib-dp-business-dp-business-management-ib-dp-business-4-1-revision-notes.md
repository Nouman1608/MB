## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Marketing | Identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer needs profitably |
| Market orientation | Decisions start from customer needs, found through research |
| Product orientation | Decisions start from what the business can make; buyers are sought afterwards |
| Market share | A business's sales as a percentage of total market sales |
| Market share by value | Based on sales revenue |
| Market share by volume | Based on units sold |
| Market growth | Percentage change in total market size between two periods |
| Market leader | The business with the largest market share |
| Percentage point | The unit for the difference between two percentages |

## Formulae (standard forms)

```
Market share (%) = business sales / total market sales x 100
Market growth (%) = (market size this period - market size last period) / market size last period x 100
```

Use the same measure (value or units) and the same period on top and bottom.

## Orientation: recall

* **Market orientation:** research first; lower risk of unwanted products; research costs time and money; may limit radical innovation.
* **Product orientation:** product first; can lead to breakthroughs and high quality; risk of weak demand.
* **Evidence to look for:** surveys and feedback before design (market); founder or engineers decide features (product).
* **Product orientation can work:** new-to-world technology, strong technical lead, safety- or specification-driven markets.

## Value versus volume

* Value share above volume share: the business charges more than the market average.
* Volume share above value share: the business charges less than the market average.
* Market value growing faster than volume: average prices in the market are rising.

## Share against growth

| | Market growing | Market shrinking |
|---|---|---|
| Share rising | Strong position | Bigger slice of a smaller market |
| Share falling | Rivals capturing more growth | Weak position |

## Worked mini-example

Brindley Biscuits (invented): the market fell from 80 million to 72 million; Brindley's sales fell from 12 million to 11.52 million.

* Market growth = (72 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = **−10.0%**.
* Brindley's share: 12 ÷ 80 × 100 = 15.0%, then 11.52 ÷ 72 × 100 = **16.0%**.
* Brindley's sales fell 4.0%, but its share rose by 1 percentage point because the market fell faster.

Comment: Brindley is winning share, but in a shrinking market, so it should consider whether the market has a future before spending heavily to defend it.

## HL only: importance of share and leadership

**Benefits:** lower unit costs through volume; power with suppliers and retailers; brand trust; influence over price; investor confidence.

**Limits:** share bought with discounts can cut profit; leadership of a narrow segment may mean little in the wider market; shrinking markets; customers who prefer smaller brands; competition authorities may act against very large shares.

**Judgement prompt:** share matters most where costs fall steeply with volume and buyers favour familiar brands; least where buyers value individuality.

## Exam traps

* Value and volume mixed in one calculation.
* Share changes written in percent instead of percentage points.
* Rising sales assumed to mean rising share.
* Market growth confused with business growth.
* Product orientation dismissed as always wrong.
* HL leadership answers with no limits or condition.

## Quick self-test

1. Define market orientation.
2. Give one advantage of product orientation.
3. A business sells 6 million in a market worth 40 million. What is its market share?
4. A market grows from 40 million to 46 million. What is the market growth rate?
5. Using questions 3 and 4, the business's sales rise to 6.44 million. What is its new share?
6. A firm's value share is 8% and its volume share is 12%. What does this suggest about its prices?
7. Why can a business's sales rise while its share falls?
8. Market share rises from 22% to 25%. How should the change be described?
9. Give two benefits of market leadership (HL).
10. Give one reason why market leadership may not be worth pursuing (HL).
11. What evidence in a case would suggest product orientation?
12. When might product orientation succeed?

### Answers

1. An approach in which decisions start from customer needs, identified through market research.
2. Any one: can lead to innovative, high-quality products; uses the firm's technical strengths.
3. 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%.
4. (46 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%.
5. 6.44 ÷ 46 × 100 = 14%.
6. Its prices are below the market average, because it sells a larger share of units than of revenue.
7. The market is growing faster than the business's sales.
8. A rise of 3 percentage points.
9. Any two: lower unit costs; power with suppliers and retailers; brand trust; influence over price; investor confidence.
10. Any one: winning share may require price cuts that reduce profit; the market may be shrinking; customers may prefer smaller brands.
11. Features decided by founders or engineers with little research; sales staff asked to find buyers for an existing product.
12. Where the product is genuinely new, where the firm has a strong technical lead, or where specification and safety drive buying decisions.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between market share by value and by volume?**
Share by value uses sales revenue, while share by volume uses units sold. A business with prices below the market average will have a higher volume share than value share.

**Is market leadership in 4.1 SL or HL?**
The importance of market share and market leadership is HL only in the IB DP Business Management guide, first assessment 2024. Market share and market growth calculations are SL and HL.
