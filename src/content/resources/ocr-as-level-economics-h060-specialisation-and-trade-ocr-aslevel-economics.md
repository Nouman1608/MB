## Specialisation and the division of labour

**Specialisation** is concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services. It can apply to an individual (a dentist), a firm (a company that only makes bicycle brakes), a region (a city known for financial services) or a country.

The **division of labour** is a particular kind of specialisation inside a production process: the process is split into separate tasks, and each worker concentrates on one task.

### Worked example: a sandwich kitchen

Four workers each make complete sandwiches: slicing bread, adding fillings, wrapping and labelling. Each makes 30 an hour, so the kitchen makes 120 an hour.

The manager reorganises: one worker slices, two add fillings, one wraps and labels. Output rises to 180 an hour.

- Output per worker rises from 30 to 180 / 4 = 45 sandwiches an hour, a 50% rise in labour productivity.
- If each worker costs £12 an hour, labour cost per sandwich falls from 48 / 120 = £0.40 to 48 / 180 = £0.27.

The same scarce resources (four workers, one kitchen) now produce more, which is exactly how specialisation addresses scarcity.

### Why productivity rises

- **Practice:** repeating one task builds skill and speed.
- **Less time lost switching** between tasks and tools.
- **Specialist equipment:** a simple task is easier to mechanise.
- **Matching skills to tasks:** workers can do what they are best at.
- **Shorter training:** a single task is quicker to learn.

### Disadvantages

- **Monotony:** repetitive work can lower motivation and raise mistakes or staff turnover.
- **Dependence:** if one stage stops (the slicer is off sick), the whole process slows.
- **Narrow skills:** workers trained for one task may struggle to find work if that task is automated or the industry declines.
- **Standardised products:** less variety than craft production.

## How specialisation helps address scarcity

Scarcity means resources are limited. Specialisation raises output per unit of resource, so more wants can be met from the same resources. In PPC terms, specialisation helps an economy reach, and over time push out, its production possibility curve.

But the gains are not unlimited. Specialisation does not create new resources, and very narrow specialisation can make a worker, firm or region vulnerable: a town built around one factory suffers badly if it closes. A balanced evaluation says specialisation is one of the main ways societies have raised output, while warning that its benefits depend on markets being able to trade the output.

## Why specialisation leads to trade

A specialist produces more of one thing than they need and none of the other things they want. A baker cannot eat only bread, so specialisation only makes sense if people can **exchange** their output for others' output.

**Worked example (invented).** In one day:

| | Loaves | Baskets |
|---|---|---|
| Ana can make | 10 | or 4 |
| Ben can make | 4 | or 8 |

If each splits the day evenly, Ana makes 5 loaves and 2 baskets and Ben makes 2 loaves and 4 baskets: a total of **7 loaves and 6 baskets**. If Ana makes only bread and Ben only baskets, total output is **10 loaves and 8 baskets**. Specialisation adds 3 loaves and 2 baskets, but only if Ana and Ben trade, since each now has none of the other good. The gains from specialisation and the need for exchange go together.

## Barter and money

**Barter** is exchanging goods directly for other goods. It has serious problems:

- **Double coincidence of wants:** Ana must find someone who both has baskets and wants bread.
- **No common measure of value:** every good must be priced in terms of every other good. With 10 goods there are 10 x 9 / 2 = 45 separate exchange rates; with money there are just 10 prices.
- **Indivisibility:** a basket may be worth two and a half loaves, but half a basket is useless.
- **Storing value is difficult:** bread goes stale, so wealth is hard to save.

**Money** is anything generally accepted as payment. It has four functions:

| Function | What it does |
|---|---|
| Medium of exchange | Separates selling from buying, removing the need for a double coincidence of wants |
| Measure of value (unit of account) | Gives every good a price in one unit, so values can be compared |
| Store of value | Allows purchasing power to be saved for later |
| Standard of deferred payment | Allows debts and contracts to be settled in the future |

By lowering the cost and time of exchange, money allows far more specialisation, because each person can sell output for money and buy whatever they need with it. Its store-of-value function is weakened by high inflation, which erodes purchasing power.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain how money makes specialisation easier than it would be under barter. [4]*

> Under barter, a specialist must find someone who has the good they want and who wants what they produce, a double coincidence of wants. This takes time and may be impossible, so people would be forced to produce many goods themselves. Money acts as a medium of exchange: a specialist can sell output to anyone for money and use that money to buy from anyone else. Because exchange is cheaper and quicker, people can afford to concentrate on producing one thing.

*Why it works:* it names the barter problem, names the function of money that solves it, and links the solution back to specialisation.

## Common mistakes

- Treating specialisation and the division of labour as identical.
- Listing advantages without saying why they raise output per worker.
- Forgetting that specialisation needs exchange.
- Confusing the functions of money, for example calling a price tag a store of value.
- Describing barter problems without linking them to the scale of specialisation.

## Exam technique

- **Section A:** multiple-choice items often test the four functions of money or the double coincidence of wants.
- **Section B:** in data questions, calculate the change in output or productivity before explaining it.
- **Section C:** an essay on whether specialisation solves scarcity should weigh output gains against vulnerability and worker costs, ending with a judgement about when the benefits are greatest.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is specialisation the same as comparative advantage?**
No. Specialisation is concentrating on a limited range of output. Comparative advantage explains which goods a country or person should specialise in. Version 3.0 does not name it, but it helps explain the trade between countries covered in Component 2, topic 4.1 International trade.

**Why do workers still accept repetitive jobs?**
Higher productivity can support higher pay, and firms may rotate tasks to reduce monotony, but the trade-off between productivity and job satisfaction is real.
