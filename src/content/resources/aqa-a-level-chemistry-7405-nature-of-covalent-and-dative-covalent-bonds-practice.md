These questions cover section **3.1.3.2 Nature of covalent and dative covalent bonds** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Covalent and dative covalent bonds study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-nature-of-covalent-and-dative-covalent-bonds/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-nature-of-covalent-and-dative-covalent-bonds-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Define each term.

**(a)** Covalent bond **[1]**

**(b)** Dative covalent (co-ordinate) bond **[2]**

**2.** Hydrogen cyanide has the formula HCN. Draw a dot-and-cross diagram showing all the outer electrons. State the number of shared pairs between carbon and nitrogen. **[3]**

**3.** Ammonia reacts with a hydrogen ion to form the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺.

**(a)** Explain how the fourth N–H bond forms. **[3]**

**(b)** State why all four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are identical in length. **[1]**

**4.** The oxonium ion, H₃O⁺, forms when a water molecule accepts a hydrogen ion.

**(a)** Draw the displayed formula of H₃O⁺, showing the dative bond with an arrow and any lone pair. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total number of outer electrons that must be shown in a dot-and-cross diagram of H₃O⁺. **[1]**

**5.** Boron trifluoride, BF₃, reacts with ammonia to form H₃N–BF₃.

**(a)** Explain why BF₃ can accept a lone pair of electrons. **[2]**

**(b)** Draw the displayed formula of the product, showing the dative bond. **[1]**

**6.** The table shows data for three carbon–carbon bonds.

| Bond | Length / pm | Mean bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| C–C | 154 | 347 |
| C=C | 134 | 612 |
| C≡C | 120 | 838 |

**(a)** State the trend in bond length as the number of shared pairs increases. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain this trend. **[2]**

**(c)** The enthalpy of C=C is not twice that of C–C. State what this shows about the second bond. **[1]**

**7.** Aluminium chloride forms a dimer, Al₂Cl₆, in the gas phase.

**(a)** Explain why an AlCl₃ molecule can accept a lone pair. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how two AlCl₃ molecules join to form Al₂Cl₆. **[2]**

**8.** A student draws the formation of the ammonium ion with an arrow from H⁺ to the nitrogen atom of NH₃.

**(a)** Identify the error. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the correct direction is the opposite one. **[2]**

**9.** Carbon monoxide has the structure C≡O.

**(a)** Calculate the total number of outer electrons in a CO molecule. **[1]**

**(b)** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram of CO, using different symbols for electrons from carbon and oxygen, and show the lone pairs. **[3]**

**(c)** State the number of dative bonds in the molecule and which atom donates. **[1]**

**10.** Explain why methane, CH₄, cannot act as a donor in a dative bond to H⁺ under normal conditions, while ammonia, NH₃, can. **[2]**

**11.** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram of the hydroxide ion, OH⁻, and state how many outer electrons are shown in total. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A shared pair of electrons between two atoms [1]

**(b)** A shared pair of electrons [1] in which both electrons are supplied by one atom [1]. **[3]**

**2.** One shared pair between H and C [1]; three shared pairs between C and N, with one lone pair on N, giving ten outer electrons in total (1 + 4 + 5) [1]; the answer is **three** shared pairs between C and N [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Count first. H, C and N bring 1 + 4 + 5 = 10 electrons. A diagram showing any other total cannot be right.

**3. (a)** Nitrogen in NH₃ has a lone pair of electrons [1]; the hydrogen ion has an empty orbital (no electrons) [1]; the nitrogen donates its lone pair to the hydrogen ion to form a shared pair, which is a dative covalent bond [1]

**(b)** Once formed, a dative bond is the same as any other covalent bond between these atoms [1]. **[4]**

**4. (a)** Oxygen bonded to three hydrogens, with two plain lines and one arrow pointing from O to H [1]; one lone pair shown on oxygen [1]

**(b)** **8** electrons (6 from O, 3 from H, less 1 for the positive charge) [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Boron has only six outer electrons in BF₃ (three bonding pairs) [1]; so it has an empty orbital that can take a lone pair [1]

**(b)** H₃N → BF₃, with an arrow from N to B [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Bond length decreases as the number of shared pairs increases [1]

**(b)** More shared pairs mean a greater attraction between the nuclei and the shared electrons [1]; this pulls the nuclei closer together [1]

**(c)** The second bond in the double bond is weaker than the first (or "the extra bond adds less than a full single bond's worth of strength") [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Aluminium has only six outer electrons in AlCl₃ [1]; so it has an empty orbital to accept a lone pair [1]

**(b)** A lone pair on a chlorine atom of one molecule is donated to the aluminium atom of the other molecule [1]; this happens in both directions, so two dative bonds form [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** The arrow points the wrong way (it should be from N to H⁺) [1]

**(b)** The arrow shows the donation of the pair, and the nitrogen supplies both electrons [1]; the hydrogen ion has no electrons to give [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** 4 + 6 = **10** [1]

**(b)** Triple bond with six electrons shown [1]; one lone pair on carbon and one lone pair on oxygen [1]; electrons drawn so that oxygen supplies both electrons of one shared pair and each atom supplies one electron to the other two pairs [1]

**(c)** One dative bond, donated by oxygen [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Methane has no lone pair on carbon, so it has no pair to donate [1]; all of the outer electrons are used in four bonding pairs, giving carbon a full outer shell [1]. Ammonia, by contrast, has a lone pair on nitrogen that can be donated. **[2]**

**11.** Oxygen bonded to hydrogen by one shared pair [1]; three lone pairs on oxygen [1]; square brackets with a charge of 1−, and a total of **8** outer electrons shown (6 + 1 + 1) [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a dative bond without saying that **both** electrons come from one atom.
- Drawing the arrow from acceptor to donor.
- Leaving off lone pairs, brackets or charges on ions.
- Stating that a dative bond is weaker or longer than other bonds after it has formed.
- Explaining that BF₃ is an acceptor without mentioning the six outer electrons.
