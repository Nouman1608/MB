This guide covers subtopic **3.4, Covalent bonding and coordinate (dative covalent) bonding**, from Topic 3, Chemical bonding, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content and the largest subtopic in Topic 3. The broader [Chemical Bonding: Shapes and Intermolecular Forces](/resources/as-chemical-bonding-shapes-and-intermolecular-forces/) guide gives a short overview; this page works through every named molecule, counts σ and π bonds, and shows how bond energy and bond length are used to compare reactivity. Afterwards, use the [covalent and coordinate bonding revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-covalent-bonding-and-coordinate-dative-covalent-revision-notes/) and the [covalent and coordinate bonding practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-covalent-bonding-and-coordinate-dative-covalent-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

The syllabus asks you to:

- define covalent bonding as **electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons**, and describe it in H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, HCl, CO₂, NH₃, CH₄, C₂H₆ and C₂H₄
- understand that Period 3 elements can **expand their octet**, including in SO₂, PCl₅ and SF₆
- describe **coordinate (dative covalent) bonding**, including NH₃ + HCl forming NH₄⁺, and the Al₂Cl₆ molecule
- describe σ bonds (direct overlap of orbitals) and π bonds (sideways overlap of adjacent p orbitals above and below the σ bond), and how they form in H₂, C₂H₆, C₂H₄, HCN and N₂
- use **hybridisation** to describe sp, sp² and sp³ orbitals
- define **bond energy** and **bond length**, and use them to compare the reactivity of covalent molecules

## The definition

The shared pair of electrons sits between the two nuclei. Both positively charged nuclei are attracted to that negative pair, and that attraction is the bond. A complete definition therefore names **the nuclei**, **a shared pair of electrons** and **electrostatic attraction**. "Sharing electrons" on its own describes what happens but is not the definition.

## The named molecules

| Molecule | Bonds | Lone pairs | σ bonds | π bonds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | H–H | none | 1 | 0 |
| Cl₂ | Cl–Cl | 3 on each Cl | 1 | 0 |
| HCl | H–Cl | 3 on Cl | 1 | 0 |
| O₂ | O=O | 2 on each O | 1 | 1 |
| N₂ | N≡N | 1 on each N | 1 | 2 |
| CO₂ | O=C=O | 2 on each O | 2 | 2 |
| NH₃ | 3 × N–H | 1 on N | 3 | 0 |
| CH₄ | 4 × C–H | none | 4 | 0 |
| C₂H₆ | C–C, 6 × C–H | none | 7 | 0 |
| C₂H₄ | C=C, 4 × C–H | none | 5 | 1 |

The rule behind the last two columns: **every single bond is one σ bond; a double bond is one σ and one π; a triple bond is one σ and two π.**

## Expanded octets in Period 3

Period 2 atoms (C, N, O, F) can hold at most eight electrons in their outer shell, because the second shell has only the 2s and 2p orbitals. Period 3 atoms have a third shell that includes **3d orbitals**, and they are larger, so more atoms can fit around them. This allows more than eight electrons around the central atom:

| Molecule | Around the central atom | Electrons around central atom |
|---|---|---|
| PCl₅ | 5 P–Cl bonding pairs | 10 |
| SF₆ | 6 S–F bonding pairs | 12 |
| SO₂ | 2 S=O double bonds and 1 lone pair | 10 |

Phosphorus uses all five outer electrons in bonds in PCl₅; sulfur uses all six in SF₆. Nitrogen, in the same group as phosphorus, cannot form NCl₅, which is a good test of whether you understand why the octet can be expanded.

## Coordinate (dative covalent) bonding

In a coordinate bond, **both electrons of the shared pair come from the same atom**. The donor needs a lone pair; the acceptor needs an empty orbital. It is often drawn as an arrow from donor to acceptor (N→H).

**Ammonia and hydrogen chloride.** When the two gases meet, a white solid, ammonium chloride, forms:

    NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

The H⁺ from HCl has no electrons. The lone pair on nitrogen forms a fourth N–H bond. In NH₄⁺, all four N–H bonds are identical in length and strength; once formed, the coordinate bond cannot be distinguished from the others.

**Al₂Cl₆.** In AlCl₃, aluminium has only six electrons in its outer shell, so it has an empty orbital. At lower temperatures in the vapour, two AlCl₃ units join. A chlorine atom in each unit donates a lone pair to the aluminium atom of the other unit, forming **two coordinate bonds**. Each bridging chlorine is bonded to two aluminium atoms, and each aluminium atom now has four bonding pairs (eight electrons).

The same idea explains analogous species: H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺, where an oxygen lone pair forms the new bond.

## σ and π bonds

A **σ bond** is formed by **direct (end-on) overlap** of orbitals between the two bonding atoms; its electron density lies on the line between the nuclei. A **π bond** is formed by **sideways overlap of adjacent p orbitals**, with electron density **above and below** the σ bond.

How the bonds form in the named molecules:

- **H₂**: two 1s orbitals overlap directly to give one σ bond.
- **C₂H₆**: each carbon is sp³. The C–C σ bond forms from two sp³ orbitals overlapping; each C–H σ bond forms from an sp³ orbital and a hydrogen 1s orbital.
- **C₂H₄**: each carbon is sp². Its three sp² orbitals form σ bonds (one to the other carbon, two to hydrogen atoms). The remaining unhybridised p orbital on each carbon overlaps sideways with the other to form the π bond.
- **HCN**: carbon is sp. One sp orbital forms the σ bond to H; the other forms the σ bond to N. Carbon's two remaining p orbitals overlap sideways with two p orbitals on nitrogen, giving two π bonds at right angles to each other.
- **N₂**: one σ bond from direct overlap, and two π bonds from sideways overlap of two pairs of p orbitals.

Because sideways overlap is less effective than direct overlap, a π bond is weaker than a σ bond. That is why alkenes react by breaking the π bond in addition reactions.

## Hybridisation

Hybridisation mixes an atom's s and p orbitals to give a set of identical hybrid orbitals.

| Hybrid | Orbitals mixed | Number of hybrids | Arrangement | Unhybridised p orbitals | Carbon example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| sp³ | one s + three p | 4 | tetrahedral, 109.5° | 0 | CH₄, C₂H₆ |
| sp² | one s + two p | 3 | trigonal planar, 120° | 1 (forms a π bond) | C₂H₄ |
| sp | one s + one p | 2 | linear, 180° | 2 (form two π bonds) | HCN, CO₂ carbon |

A quick way to identify the hybridisation of a carbon atom: four single bonds means sp³; one double bond means sp²; a triple bond or two double bonds means sp.

### Worked example: counting bonds and hybridisation

**Question.** Propene is CH₂=CH–CH₃. State the number of σ and π bonds, and the hybridisation of each carbon atom. [3]

There are 6 C–H bonds, 1 C–C single bond and 1 C=C double bond. σ bonds: 6 + 1 + 1 = **8**. π bonds: **1**. The two carbons of the C=C are **sp²**; the CH₃ carbon is **sp³**.

## Bond energy and bond length

- **Bond energy**: the energy required to break **one mole** of a particular covalent bond **in the gaseous state**.
- **Bond length**: the **internuclear distance** of two covalently bonded atoms.

More shared pairs between the same two atoms gives a **shorter, stronger** bond:

| Bond | Approximate length / nm |
|---|---|
| C–C | 0.154 |
| C=C | 0.134 |
| C≡C | 0.120 |

**Using these to compare reactivity.** A molecule reacts more readily when the bond that must break is weaker.

- **Nitrogen** is unreactive because the N≡N triple bond has a very high bond energy (944 kJ mol⁻¹ in the data booklet, compared with 158 kJ mol⁻¹ for F–F and 242 kJ mol⁻¹ for Cl–Cl).
- **Hydrogen halides**: bond energy falls H–Cl > H–Br > H–I as the halogen atom gets larger and the bond longer. HI is the easiest to break into its elements on heating.
- **Halogenoalkanes**: C–Cl > C–Br > C–I in bond energy, so iodoalkanes are hydrolysed fastest, even though the C–I bond is the least polar. Bond energy outweighs polarity here.

### Worked example: explaining reactivity from data

**Question.** Bond energies from the data booklet are H–Cl 431 kJ mol⁻¹ and H–I 299 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain which hydrogen halide is more easily decomposed by heat. [2]

HI is decomposed more easily [1] because less energy is needed to break the H–I bond, which is longer and weaker than H–Cl [1].

## Common mistakes

- Defining covalent bonding as "sharing electrons" without the attraction to both nuclei.
- Counting a double bond as two σ bonds.
- Saying a coordinate bond is weaker or different once formed; in NH₄⁺ all four bonds are identical.
- Forgetting that Al₂Cl₆ has **two** coordinate bonds.
- Claiming nitrogen can expand its octet. Only Period 3 (and later) elements can.
- Describing π bonds as overlap "between the nuclei"; π electron density is above and below the bond axis.
- Using polarity alone to predict halogenoalkane reactivity.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a σ or π bond: name the type of overlap and where the electron density lies.
- **State** the number of σ and π bonds: list each bond, then add up.
- **Explain** reactivity: name the bond broken, compare its bond energy or length, and link to the energy needed.
- When describing a coordinate bond, say which atom donates the lone pair and which accepts it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a coordinate bond weaker than an ordinary covalent bond?**
No. Once formed it is a normal shared pair; in NH₄⁺ all four N–H bonds have the same length and strength.

**Why can sulfur form SF₆ but oxygen cannot form OF₆?**
Sulfur is in Period 3 and can expand its octet; oxygen is in Period 2 and is limited to eight outer electrons.
