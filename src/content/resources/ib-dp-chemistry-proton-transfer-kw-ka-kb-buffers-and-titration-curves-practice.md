Data for every question: all solutions are at 298 K, where Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴. Give pH values to 2 decimal places and other answers to 2 or 3 significant figures unless told otherwise.

## Questions

**1.** A cleaning solution has pH 9.65. Calculate its pOH and its [OH⁻]. **[2]**

**2.** Use these values: acid P, Ka = 2.5 × 10⁻³; acid Q, pKa = 4.10; acid R, Ka = 3.2 × 10⁻⁶; acid S, pKa = 2.20. List the four acids from strongest to weakest, showing how you compared them. **[2]**

**3.** Hydrofluoric acid, HF, is a weak acid with pKa = 3.17.

**(a)** Write the Kb expression for the fluoride ion. **[1]**
**(b)** Show that Ka(HF) × Kb(F⁻) = Kw. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate Kb for F⁻. **[1]**

**4.** Calculate the pH of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ aqueous trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N, given Kb = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵. State the assumptions you make. **[4]**

**5.** A 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak monoprotic acid HZ has pH 3.40. Calculate Ka and pKa for HZ. **[3]**

**6.** Three salts are dissolved separately in water: ammonium iodide, potassium carbonate and sodium nitrate.

**(a)** Predict whether each solution is acidic, neutral or basic. **[3]**
**(b)** Write equations for the reactions with water that explain your answers for the ammonium ion and the carbonate ion. **[2]**

**7.** Hydrocyanic acid has Ka = 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁰. Calculate the pH of 0.0750 mol dm⁻³ sodium cyanide solution. **[4]**

**8.** 20.0 cm³ of an aqueous weak base B is titrated with 0.125 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. The pH curve falls steeply at 16.0 cm³. After 8.0 cm³ of acid has been added, the pH is 10.70.

**(a)** Calculate the concentration of B. **[2]**
**(b)** Deduce pKb and Kb for B. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the pH of the base solution before any acid is added. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain, with an equation, why the pH at the equivalence point is below 7. **[2]**

**9.** An indicator is represented as HInd.

**(a)** Write the equilibrium for HInd in water, and explain which colour you see in a strongly acidic solution. **[2]**
**(b)** Use these ranges: bromocresol green 3.8–5.4; phenol red 6.8–8.4; thymolphthalein 9.3–10.5. Select an indicator for titrating aqueous methylamine with hydrochloric acid, giving a reason. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why bromocresol green is unsuitable for titrating a weak acid with sodium hydroxide. **[1]**

**10.** **(a)** Distinguish between the equivalence point and the end point of a titration. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why no indicator gives a sharp end point when ethanoic acid is titrated with aqueous ammonia. **[1]**

**11.** **(a)** State the composition of a basic buffer made from ammonia. **[1]**
**(b)** Write equations to show how this buffer resists a change in pH when small amounts of acid and of alkali are added. **[2]**

**12.** Phenylethanoic acid, C₆H₅CH₂COOH, has Ka = 4.9 × 10⁻⁵. A buffer is made by mixing 50.0 cm³ of 0.160 mol dm⁻³ phenylethanoic acid with 24.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Calculate the amounts, in mol, of the acid and its conjugate base in the buffer. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the pH of the buffer. **[2]**
**(c)** 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol of HCl is added. Calculate the new pH. **[2]**
**(d)** Predict, with a reason, how the pH of the buffer from (b) changes if you double its volume by adding water. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** pOH = 14.00 − 9.65 = **4.35** [1]. [OH⁻] = 10^(−4.35) = **4.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Label which value is pOH and which is [OH⁻]; a correct number under the wrong label does not answer the question.

**2.** Convert to one scale: pKa(P) = 2.60 and pKa(R) = 5.49 [1]. Strongest to weakest: **S > P > Q > R** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the conversions; an order given without comparable values does not show how you decided.

**3. (a)** **Kb = [HF][OH⁻] / [F⁻]** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Ka × Kb = ([H⁺][F⁻] / [HF]) × ([HF][OH⁻] / [F⁻]); [F⁻] and [HF] cancel to leave [H⁺][OH⁻] = Kw [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** pKb = 14.00 − 3.17 = 10.83, so Kb = 10^(−10.83) = **1.5 × 10⁻¹¹** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* In a "show that", write both expressions in full and state what cancels; jumping straight to Kw shows nothing.

**4.** Kb = [(CH₃)₃NH⁺][OH⁻] / [(CH₃)₃N], taking [(CH₃)₃NH⁺] = [OH⁻] and [(CH₃)₃N] ≈ 0.150 [1]. [OH⁻] = √(6.3 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.150) = 3.07 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]. pOH = 2.51 [1]. pH = 14.00 − 2.51 = **11.49** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Stating the two assumptions is part of the method; without them the square root has no justification.

**5.** [H⁺] = 10^(−3.40) = 3.98 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1]. Ka = (3.98 × 10⁻⁴)² ÷ 0.0200 = **7.9 × 10⁻⁶** [1]. pKa = **5.10** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Square [H⁺] because [Z⁻] equals it; using [H⁺] once gives a Ka that is far too large.

**6. (a)** Ammonium iodide: **acidic** [1]. Potassium carbonate: **basic** [1]. Sodium nitrate: **neutral** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** **NH₄⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₃(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)** [1]. **CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HCO₃⁻(aq) + OH⁻(aq)** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ as a product; that species is what links the equation to the pH.

**7.** Kb(CN⁻) = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 1.61 × 10⁻⁵ [1]. [OH⁻] = √(1.61 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0750) = 1.10 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]. pOH = 2.96 [1]. pH = **11.04** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The data give Ka of the parent acid, so convert to Kb first; using Ka directly gives an acidic pH for a basic salt.

**8. (a)** n(HCl) = 0.125 × 0.0160 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol, and B reacts 1 : 1 [1]. [B] = 2.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0200 = **0.100 mol dm⁻³** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 8.0 cm³ is half-equivalence, so pOH = pKb [1]. pKb = 14.00 − 10.70 = **3.30**, Kb = **5.0 × 10⁻⁴** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** [OH⁻] = √(5.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.100) = 7.08 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]. pOH = 2.15, pH = **11.85** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The salt contains BH⁺, which donates a proton to water: BH⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ B(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) [1]. Cl⁻ comes from a strong acid and does not react with water, so the solution is **acidic** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* At half-equivalence a weak base gives pOH = pKb, not pH = pKb; convert 10.70 before you write pKb.

**9. (a)** HInd(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + Ind⁻(aq) [1]. High [H⁺] shifts the equilibrium to the left, so the **colour of HInd** is seen [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **Bromocresol green**: the salt formed is acidic, so the equivalence pH is below 7 and its range 3.8–5.4 lies in the steep section [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** It changes colour between pH 3.8 and 5.4, in the buffer region, **long before the equivalence point**, which is above 7 [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Justify an indicator choice by linking the salt to the equivalence pH; naming the indicator alone does not show the reasoning.

**10. (a)** Equivalence point: acid and base have reacted in the **stoichiometric ratio** [1]. End point: the **indicator changes colour** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The curve has **no steep vertical section**, so the pH, and the indicator colour, change gradually over a large volume [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Define each term on its own; "the end point is when the reaction finishes" describes the equivalence point.

**11. (a)** Aqueous **ammonia with an ammonium salt** such as NH₄Cl [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Added acid: **NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺** [1]. Added alkali: **NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give one equation for added acid and one for added alkali; two equations for the same addition only cover half the question.

**12. (a)** n(acid) at start = 0.0500 × 0.160 = 0.00800 mol; n(OH⁻) = 0.0240 × 0.200 = 0.00480 mol [1]. After reaction: **n(acid) = 0.00320 mol, n(conjugate base) = 0.00480 mol** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** [H⁺] = Ka × n(acid) / n(base) = 4.9 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.00320 ÷ 0.00480 = 3.27 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]. pH = **4.49** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The base converts H⁺ to acid: n(base) = 0.00400 mol and n(acid) = 0.00400 mol [1]. Equal amounts, so pH = pKa = −log₁₀(4.9 × 10⁻⁵) = **4.31** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Acid and conjugate base are diluted equally, leaving their ratio fixed, so **the pH stays almost unchanged** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtract the added OH⁻ from the weak acid before substituting; using the starting amount of acid gives the wrong ratio.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stopping at pOH when the question asks for pH.
- Writing [H₂O] inside a Kb expression.
- Converting Ka to Kb by dividing the wrong way round; Kb = Kw ÷ Ka.
- Using √(Ka × c) for a buffer after partial neutralisation.
- Writing pH = pKb at the half-equivalence point of a weak base titration.
- Predicting pH 7 at equivalence for every titration.
- Giving hydrolysis equations for spectator ions such as Na⁺, K⁺, I⁻ or NO₃⁻.
- Explaining buffer action with only one equation.
- Claiming that dilution changes buffer pH; it reduces capacity instead.
