This guide covers **Core Content Option A: The Nineteenth Century,
1848–1914**, for Cambridge IGCSE World History 0470, 2024–2026 series.
All candidates study Core content in either Option A or Option B (The
Twentieth Century, 1919–1989), each built around six key questions,
plus at least one of five named Depth studies.

## Where this fits in 0470

Option A is one of two alternative Core content routes through 0470 —
a school teaches either Option A or Option B, not both — and it traces
the major political shifts of nineteenth-century Europe and beyond,
from the revolutions of 1848 through to the outbreak of the First World
War in 1914. Assessment is via Paper 1 (Structured Questions) and Paper
2 (Document Questions), which every candidate takes, plus either
Component 3 Coursework or Paper 4.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE WORLD HISTORY 0470 — CORE CONTENT OPTION A: THE
NINETEENTH CENTURY, 1848–1914**

- 1.1 Were the Revolutions of 1848 Important? — the causes, events and
consequences of the wave of European revolutions
- 1.2 How Was Italy Unified? — the process and key figures behind
Italian unification
- 1.3 How Was Germany Unified? — the process and key figures behind
German unification
- 1.4 Why Was There a Civil War in the United States and What Were Its
Results? — the causes, course and consequences of the US Civil War
- 1.5 Why, and With What Effects, Did Nations Gain and Expand Their
Overseas Empires? — the causes and consequences of nineteenth-century
imperial expansion
- 1.6 What Caused the First World War? — the long-term and short-term
causes leading to war in 1914

## How to approach it

Paper 1 (Structured Questions) draws two of its eight Section A
questions from these six Option A key questions (candidates answer two
from Section A plus one from Section B on a Depth study), so all six
key questions are fair game for Paper 1 revision. **Paper 2 (Document
Questions) is different: candidates answer one question on a single
prescribed topic, chosen by Cambridge from these six key questions, and
that prescribed topic changes every exam series.** Revising "all of
Paper 2" by working through every key question's sources is not how
the paper works — only the officially prescribed topic for your
specific exam series appears on Paper 2.

Always confirm your own series' prescribed topic with your teacher or
the current syllabus before treating this table as final — Cambridge
sets it separately for each series. For "why" questions like 1.6,
practise distinguishing long-term causes (structural tensions building
over years) from short-term triggers (a specific event) and explaining
how they interacted, rather than listing causes without connecting
them — this is consistently where the strongest Paper 1 answers
separate themselves from merely accurate ones. The two unification
questions (1.2 and 1.3) reward comparison: understanding why Italy and
Germany followed different paths to unification, despite similar
starting conditions, is a common higher-level exam angle. For whichever
topic is prescribed for your Paper 2 series, practise with real
historical sources on it specifically — judging reliability, usefulness
and provenance — rather than relying on content knowledge alone.

## The Revolutions of 1848 in outline

1.1 asks candidates to weigh the significance of 1848, and the most
useful way in is to understand why the revolutions failed almost as
much as why they broke out. Their causes combined rising nationalism,
liberal demands for written constitutions and representative
government, economic hardship following a run of poor harvests, and
the direct example set by the February 1848 revolution in France. Most
of the revolutions unravelled within a year or two of breaking out —
France's Second Republic gave way to Louis Napoleon's rise well before
1852; the Frankfurt Parliament's constitution collapsed by 1849; Vienna
and the Austrian Empire's uprisings were suppressed by 1849 — though
the specific mechanism varied by country rather than following one
single script. Common recurring factors across France, the German
states and the Austrian Empire include: revolutionary coalitions
splitting between moderate liberals wanting constitutional reform and
more radical elements wanting deeper social change; property-owning
middle classes withdrawing support once revolution threatened property
itself; peasant involvement that was often limited and driven more by
specific agrarian grievances (such as the desire to abolish remaining
feudal dues) than by the liberal-nationalist programme, though this
varied by region and was not uniform non-involvement everywhere; and
conservative monarchies that — in most (not every) case — retained
enough loyal military force to eventually restore the old order,
France's own outcome coming via Louis Napoleon's political rise and
coup rather than a simple military reconquest. The lesson later
nationalist leaders drew from this failure shaped the rest of the
syllabus: unification in both Italy and Germany would be achieved
through diplomacy and deliberate war rather than through parliamentary
revolution.

## Unification compared

1.2 and 1.3 reward direct comparison of Italy and Germany, since both
began the period fragmented and both were unified within roughly a
generation, but by different methods and different leadership. Italian
unification (1859–70) had to overcome Austrian control of the north,
the Papal States physically dividing the peninsula, strong regional
loyalties, and poor communications between states; it succeeded
through a combination of Cavour's Piedmontese diplomacy and French
alliance, Garibaldi's military conquest of the south in the Expedition
of the Thousand, Mazzini's earlier nationalist ideology, and Victor
Emmanuel II's role as the eventual king of a unified Italy. German
unification (1864–71) instead followed Bismarck's deliberate three-part
method: isolate each opponent diplomatically before fighting; fight a
limited war for one defined objective at a time, against Denmark in
1864, Austria in 1866 and France in 1870–71; and stop once that
objective was achieved rather than pursuing total conquest. The Ems
Telegram's role in provoking France into declaring war in 1870 is
worth knowing precisely, because it explains why the southern German
states joined Prussia against a foreign aggressor rather than resisting
annexation, which is why the process ended in a unified German Empire
rather than a purely Prussian conquest of its neighbours.

## Empire and the coming of war

1.5 and 1.6 connect nineteenth-century imperial expansion to the
alliance system that eventually turned a regional crisis into a world
war. By the early twentieth century, Europe had divided into the
Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy (1882) and the
Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia (1907), with Britain's
move away from its earlier policy of "splendid isolation" driven
largely by German naval expansion threatening British naval supremacy.
When explaining the outbreak of war in 1914, distinguish long-term,
underlying causes -- militarism such as the Anglo-German dreadnought
race, the alliance system itself, nationalism including Slav
nationalism in the Balkans and French resentment over Alsace-Lorraine,
and imperial rivalry including the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911 --
from the short-term trigger, the assassination of Archduke Franz
Ferdinand at Sarajevo on 28 June 1914. The strongest answers explain
why the July Crisis escalated into general war within weeks rather
than months: Germany's Schlieffen Plan required a rapid defeat of
France before Russia's army could fully mobilise, so once Russia began
mobilising, German military timetables left almost no room for
diplomacy to intervene.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the six Option A key questions?**
The revolutions of 1848; how Italy was unified; how Germany was unified; the US Civil War and its results; overseas empires; and the causes of the First World War.

**How is IGCSE History 0470 Paper 1 structured?**
Paper 1 (2 hours, 60 marks, 40%) has Section A on the Core Content (eight questions, answer two) and Section B on the Depth Studies (two on each, answer one). Each question is a three-part structured essay worth 4, 6 and 10 marks.

**How should Italian and German unification be revised?**
Comparatively: both began divided and were unified within about a decade, by a leading state (Piedmont, Prussia), diplomacy and war.
