These notes condense **3.2.3 Economic performance** in AQA A-level Economics (7136). For explanations and worked
examples, read the [study guide for this sub-section](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-economic-performance/),
then use the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-economic-performance-practice/).
All figures are invented.

## Growth and the cycle (3.2.3.1)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Short-run growth | Rise in real GDP using existing spare capacity |
| Long-run growth | Rise in productive capacity; the trend rate of growth of real output |
| Economic cycle | Fluctuations of real GDP around its trend: boom, downturn, recession, recovery |
| Positive output gap | Real GDP above productive potential |
| Negative output gap | Real GDP below productive potential |
| Sustainable growth | Growth that does not compromise future generations' ability to meet their needs |

- **Output gap (%)** = (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100.
- Diagrams: PPF (movement to the boundary = short run; shift out = long run) and AD/AS (AD shift with spare
  capacity = short run; LRAS shift = long run).
- **Indicators of phases:** real GDP, inflation, unemployment, investment.
- **Causes of instability:** excessive credit and debt, asset price bubbles, destabilising speculation, animal
  spirits or herding; domestic and global demand-side and supply-side shocks.
- **Benefits of growth:** higher living standards, jobs, tax revenue. **Costs:** pollution, resource depletion,
  inequality, possible inflation and current account deficits.

## Employment and unemployment (3.2.3.2)

- **Claimant count:** people claiming unemployment-related benefits.
- **Labour Force Survey measure:** without a job, actively sought work in the past four weeks, available to start
  in the next two weeks. Usually higher than the claimant count.
- **Voluntary:** choosing not to work at the going wage. **Involuntary:** willing to work at the going wage but
  unable to find a job.

| Type | Cause |
|---|---|
| Seasonal | Regular yearly changes in demand |
| Frictional | Time spent between jobs |
| Structural | Skill or location mismatch after changes in industry |
| Cyclical | Deficient AD in a downturn (negative output gap) |
| Real wage | Real wages above the market-clearing level |

- **Natural rate of unemployment:** unemployment when the labour market is in equilibrium (frictional plus
  structural); determined by supply-side factors.
- **Consequences:** lost income and skills for individuals; lost output, lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending
  for the economy.

## Inflation and deflation (3.2.3.3)

- **Inflation:** sustained rise in the price level. **Deflation:** falling price level. **Disinflation:** falling
  rate of inflation.
- **Demand-pull:** AD outpaces capacity. **Cost-push:** higher costs shift SRAS left.
- **Expectations** feed into wage and price setting.
- **MV = PQ.** Quantity theory: with V and Q stable, a rise in M raises P proportionately (monetarist view).
- **World commodity prices** and inflation in other economies affect UK inflation.
- **Consequences of inflation:** erodes savings and fixed incomes; menu and shoe-leather costs; uncertainty that
  deters investment; loss of international competitiveness if faster than trading partners.
- **Consequences of deflation:** consumers delay purchases; the real burden of debt rises; firms cut output and jobs.
- Deflationary policies reduce AD but need not cause deflation.

## Conflicts (3.2.3.4)

- Positive output gap → inflationary pressure, low unemployment. Negative gap → high unemployment, low inflation.
- **Short-run Phillips curve:** trade-off between unemployment and inflation.
- **Long-run (L-shaped, vertical) Phillips curve:** at the natural rate; no long-run trade-off once expectations
  adjust.
- Supply-side policies that lower the natural rate shift the long-run curve left.
- Monetarist/supply-side view: the main objectives are compatible in the long run.

## Worked reminders (invented)

- Potential 1,500, actual 1,470: output gap = −30 ÷ 1,500 = **−2.0%**.
- M = 300, V = 5, Q = 750: P = 1,500 ÷ 750 = **2.0**. M rises to 330: P = 1,650 ÷ 750 = **2.2**.
- Labour force 25 million, survey unemployment 1.25 million: rate **5.0%**.

## Must-know distinctions

- Short-run versus long-run growth.
- Positive versus negative output gap; negative gap versus negative growth.
- Claimant count versus Labour Force Survey measure.
- Cyclical (AD) versus structural (supply-side) unemployment.
- Deflation versus disinflation versus deflationary policy.
- Short-run versus long-run Phillips curve.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish short-run from long-run economic growth.
2. Potential output is 800 and actual output is 820. Calculate the output gap.
3. Which indicator usually lags the cycle, and why?
4. Name two causes of cyclical instability listed in the specification.
5. Why is the survey measure of unemployment usually higher than the claimant count?
6. Distinguish frictional from structural unemployment.
7. What determines the natural rate of unemployment?
8. M = 400, V = 3, Q = 600. Calculate P.
9. Give one consequence of deflation for a household with a mortgage.
10. Explain the difference between deflation and disinflation.
11. Why does the long-run Phillips curve become vertical?
12. What would shift the long-run Phillips curve to the left?

### Answers

1. Short-run growth uses existing spare capacity; long-run growth increases productive capacity.
2. +20 ÷ 800 × 100 = **+2.5%**, a positive output gap.
3. **Unemployment**: firms wait until they are confident before hiring, and keep trained staff early in a downturn.
4. Any two: excessive credit and debt, asset price bubbles, destabilising speculation, animal spirits or herding.
5. Some unemployed people are not eligible for, or do not claim, benefits but are counted by the survey.
6. Frictional: short spells between jobs. Structural: long-term mismatch of skills or location after industries
   change.
7. **Supply-side factors** such as skills, mobility, job-matching and the incentives to work.
8. P = (400 × 3) ÷ 600 = **2.0**.
9. The **real value of the debt rises**, so repayments take a larger share of income if wages fall.
10. Deflation is a falling price level; disinflation is a falling but still positive rate of inflation.
11. Workers come to expect the higher inflation and demand higher money wages, so real wages and unemployment
    return to the natural rate at a higher inflation rate.
12. A fall in the natural rate of unemployment, for example through supply-side policies that improve skills or
    labour mobility.
