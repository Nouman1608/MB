## Why position predicts behaviour

Members of one group share an outer-electron count, so they react alike. Down a group, each element has one more occupied shell; that explains most trends here.

- **Metals** (left and centre of the table) react by **losing** electrons to form positive ions. Their oxides are basic; soluble ones make alkaline solutions.
- **Non-metals** (right of the table) react with metals by **gaining** electrons to form negative ions. Many non-metal oxides, such as carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide, are acidic.
- **Group 0** atoms start with a complete outer shell and have nothing to gain by reacting.

A single sodium atom is not soft or silvery; those are bulk properties of the metal. Explain with particles, not bulk properties.

## Group 1: the alkali metals

Rubidium and caesium are too reactive for school use, so you predict their behaviour.

**Physical properties.** Group 1 metals are soft, shiny when freshly cut and tarnish quickly in air. They have low densities (lithium, sodium and potassium float on water) and low melting points, which **decrease** down the group.

**Chemical properties.** Each one is stored under oil because it reacts with oxygen and water vapour in the air.

| Reaction | General equation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| With water | metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen | 2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂ |
| With oxygen | metal + oxygen → metal oxide | 4K + O₂ → 2K₂O |
| With chlorine | metal + chlorine → metal chloride (a white solid) | 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl |

With water, lithium fizzes steadily, sodium melts into a ball and darts about, and potassium's hydrogen ignites with a lilac flame. The hydroxide dissolves, leaving an alkaline solution.

**Explaining the trend.** All Group 1 atoms have **one** outer electron, which they lose to form a 1+ ion. Each step down adds a shell, so the outer electron sits further out with more inner shells screening it. The nucleus holds it less tightly, so it is **lost more easily** and the metal is **more reactive**.

### Worked example 1: predicting for caesium

*In a filmed demonstration, equal-sized pieces of Group 1 metals are dropped into water. Times to disappear: Li 75 s, Na 21 s, K 6 s, Rb 2 s. Predict the time for caesium and describe its reaction with water.*

1. Each time is roughly a third of the one before (75 → 21 → 6 → 2 s).
2. Continuing the pattern predicts **less than 1 s** for caesium.
3. Caesium is lower in the group, so its outer electron is lost even more easily than rubidium's. It reacts **explosively** with water.
4. Equation: 2Cs + 2H₂O → 2CsOH + H₂. The solution formed is alkaline.

A prediction longer than 2 s would break the trend.

## Group 7: the halogens

The halogens exist as **diatomic molecules** (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂).

| Halogen | State and colour at room temperature |
|---|---|
| Chlorine | pale green gas |
| Bromine | red-brown liquid (orange in water) |
| Iodine | grey-black solid (purple vapour; brown in water) |

Melting and boiling points **increase** down the group, as bigger molecules attract each other more strongly. Colours darken down the group.

**Chemical properties.** Halogens react with metals to form ionic salts called **halides**, for example 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃. They react with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides such as HCl, which dissolve in water to give acidic solutions.

**Explaining the trend.** A halogen atom has **seven** outer electrons and gains one to form a 1− ion. Lower in the group the atom is bigger and its nucleus is screened by more inner shells, so it pulls an extra electron in less strongly. Electrons are **gained less easily**, so reactivity **decreases** down Group 7. This is the opposite direction to Group 1, because halogens gain electrons while alkali metals lose them.

### Displacement reactions (PAG C1)

A halogen higher in the group pushes a lower halogen out of its salt solution, and you watch for a colour change.

### Worked example 2: bromine water and sodium iodide

*Orange bromine water is added to colourless sodium iodide solution. Predict what you would see, and write the equation.*

1. Bromine is above iodine in Group 7, so bromine is more reactive.
2. Bromine displaces iodine: the solution turns **brown** as iodine forms.
3. Word equation: bromine + sodium iodide → sodium bromide + iodine.
4. Symbol equation: Br₂ + 2NaI → 2NaBr + I₂.

Bromine water added to sodium chloride solution stays orange: bromine is less reactive than chlorine.

## Group 0: the noble gases

Helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon are colourless gases at room temperature. They exist as **single atoms** (monatomic). Boiling points and densities **increase** down the group as the atoms get larger.

They are very unreactive because each atom has a stable **full outer shell** (two electrons for helium, eight for the others), so it does not lose, gain or share electrons, even with another atom of its own kind.

## Transition metals (C4.1c)

The transition metals sit in the central block, between Groups 2 and 3. Iron, copper, nickel and manganese are good examples. Compared with Group 1 metals, they:

| Property | Transition metals (iron, copper) | Group 1 (sodium) |
|---|---|---|
| Melting point | high (iron 1538 °C, copper 1085 °C) | low (98 °C) |
| Density | high (iron 7.87 g/cm³, copper 8.96 g/cm³) | low (0.97 g/cm³) |
| Reactivity | much less reactive; iron rusts slowly, copper does not react with water | reacts vigorously with cold water |
| Ions | ions with **different charges**: Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺ | one ion only: Na⁺ |
| Colour of compounds | often **coloured** | white solids, colourless solutions |
| Catalysts | often used as catalysts (iron, nickel) | not typical catalysts |

**Coloured ions.** Copper(II) sulfate solution is blue; iron(II) compounds are pale green and iron(III) compounds orange-brown. The Roman numeral gives the ion's charge.

**Catalysts.** Iron is the catalyst in the Haber process for ammonia. Nickel catalyses the reaction of alkenes with hydrogen. Manganese(IV) oxide makes hydrogen peroxide break down faster. In PAG C5 and C8 work you can test whether a transition metal compound speeds up a reaction and identify transition metal ions.

### Worked example 3: an empirical formula showing variable charge (CM4.1i)

*A student heats 1.12 g of iron wool in chlorine. The product has a mass of 3.25 g. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: Fe = 56, Cl = 35.5)*

1. Mass of chlorine combined = 3.25 − 1.12 = 2.13 g.
2. Moles of Fe = 1.12 ÷ 56 = 0.020 mol.
3. Moles of Cl = 2.13 ÷ 35.5 = 0.060 mol.
4. Divide by the smaller value: Fe 0.020 ÷ 0.020 = 1; Cl 0.060 ÷ 0.020 = 3.
5. Empirical formula **FeCl₃**, so the iron is present as **Fe³⁺**.
6. Balanced equation: 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃.

Iron also forms FeCl₂, so iron has ions with different charges. The product is heavier than the iron because chlorine has combined with it; likewise a metal oxide weighs more than the metal burned to make it.

## Metals with water and dilute acid (C4.1e)

When a metal reacts with water or acid, its atoms **lose electrons** to form positive ions, for example Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻. The more easily it forms its positive ion, the more vigorous the reaction.

- metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen (the most reactive metals)
- metal + dilute hydrochloric acid → metal chloride + hydrogen

A reactivity series covering the common laboratory metals, most reactive first: **potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, silver**. Copper and silver have so little tendency to form ions that they do not react with dilute hydrochloric acid at all.

### Worked example 4: comparing rates of hydrogen production (PAG C8)

*Calcium is added to cold water and 72 cm³ of hydrogen is collected in 40 s. Magnesium ribbon in cold water gives only about 3 cm³ in 10 minutes. Calculate the mean rate for each and explain the difference.*

1. Calcium: 72 ÷ 40 = **1.8 cm³/s**.
2. Magnesium: 10 minutes = 600 s; 3 ÷ 600 = **0.005 cm³/s**.
3. Equation for calcium: Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂.
4. Calcium forms Ca²⁺ more easily than magnesium forms Mg²⁺, so reacts faster.

For a fair comparison, keep the volume and temperature of the liquid (and the acid's concentration) the same and use similar-sized pieces of metal. Collect gas in a gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder.

## Deducing reactivity from experiments (C4.1f)

A metal higher in the series **displaces** a lower one from its dissolved salt. Zinc in copper(II) sulfate gives zinc sulfate and a brown coating of copper: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. The blue colour fades as Cu²⁺ ions are removed.

Two kinds of result build an order:

1. **Displacement grids (PAG C1, C7).** Count how many salts each metal displaces. The metal that displaces the most is the most reactive.
2. **Temperature rises (PAG C8).** Displacement reactions are exothermic. Using the same mass of each metal powder and the same volume of a salt solution, a larger temperature rise means a more reactive metal.

### Worked example 5: three unknown metals

*Equal masses of metal powders X, Y and Z are added to 25 cm³ portions of copper(II) sulfate solution at 21.0 °C. Final temperatures: X 33.5 °C, Y 21.0 °C, Z 26.0 °C. Put X, Y, Z and copper in order of reactivity.*

1. Rises: X 12.5 °C, Y 0.0 °C, Z 5.0 °C.
2. X and Z displaced copper, so both are more reactive than copper; X gave the larger rise.
3. Y gave no reaction, so Y is less reactive than copper.
4. Order, most reactive first: **X > Z > copper > Y**.

Repeat each test, and use a lidded polystyrene cup to reduce energy loss.

## Common errors

- Saying Group 1 metals "gain a full shell" when they react; they **lose** their single outer electron.
- Explaining the halogen trend with "more shells, so more reactive". For halogens it is the opposite.
- Writing Cl instead of Cl₂ in equations, then failing to balance.
- Giving a transition metal a single fixed charge.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are the noble gases unreactive?**
Each noble gas atom has a stable full outer shell, with two electrons for helium and eight for the others, so it does not lose, gain or share electrons. The noble gases exist as single atoms, and their boiling points and densities increase down Group 0.

**Why are Group 1 metals stored under oil?**
Group 1 metals react with oxygen and water vapour in the air, so they are stored under oil. They are soft, shiny when freshly cut and tarnish quickly in air.

**Which transition metals are used as catalysts?**
Iron is the catalyst in the Haber process, nickel catalyses the reaction of alkenes with hydrogen, and manganese(IV) oxide speeds up the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.

**What is the reactivity series for OCR GCSE Chemistry?**
A series covering the common laboratory metals, most reactive first, is potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, silver. The more easily a metal forms its positive ion, the faster it reacts with water or dilute acid.
