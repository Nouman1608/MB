## Nature and purpose

**Investment** is spending money now on something (equipment, premises, software, training) that will produce a benefit over several years. **Investment appraisal** is the process of using figures and judgement to decide whether a proposed investment is worth making, or which of several proposals is best. It is needed because:

- the money is spent at once but the benefits come later and are uncertain
- funds are limited, so projects compete
- the decision is often hard to reverse
- lenders and shareholders want to see the reasoning.

## The three quantitative methods

| Method | What it tells you | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Payback period | How long it takes to recover the initial cost | Years until cumulative net cash inflows equal the cost |
| Average rate of return (ARR) | The average annual profit as a percentage of the cost | (Total net return ÷ years) ÷ initial cost × 100 |
| Net present value (NPV) | The value today of future cash inflows, less the cost | Sum of (cash flow × discount factor) − initial cost |

**Net return** here means total cash inflows less the initial cost. **Discounting** reflects that £1 received in the future is worth less than £1 today, because money received now could be invested or used. The **discount rate** is the rate used, often related to the cost of borrowing or the return the business could earn elsewhere. Discount factors are normally given in a table in questions.

### Worked example 1: appraising a single project

Calder Print is considering Project A, a new digital press costing £120,000. The expected net cash inflows are:

| Year | Net cash inflow |
|---|---|
| 1 | £30,000 |
| 2 | £35,000 |
| 3 | £45,000 |
| 4 | £40,000 |
| 5 | £30,000 |
| Total | £180,000 |

**Payback.** Cumulative inflows: year 1 £30,000; year 2 £65,000; year 3 £110,000; year 4 £150,000. After 3 years £10,000 is still to recover (£120,000 − £110,000). Year 4's inflow is £40,000, so the fraction of year 4 needed is £10,000 ÷ £40,000 = 0.25. **Payback = 3.25 years**, or 3 years and 3 months.

**ARR.** Total net return = £180,000 − £120,000 = £60,000. Average annual return = £60,000 ÷ 5 = £12,000. **ARR** = £12,000 ÷ £120,000 × 100 = **10%**.

**NPV at 8%.** Discount factors at 8% for years 1 to 5 are 0.926, 0.857, 0.794, 0.735 and 0.681.

| Year | Inflow | Factor | Present value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | £30,000 | 0.926 | £27,780 |
| 2 | £35,000 | 0.857 | £29,995 |
| 3 | £45,000 | 0.794 | £35,730 |
| 4 | £40,000 | 0.735 | £29,400 |
| 5 | £30,000 | 0.681 | £20,430 |
| Total present value | | | **£143,335** |

**NPV** = £143,335 − £120,000 = **+£23,335**. A positive NPV means that, after allowing for the time value of money, the project returns more than the 8% required, so it adds value. At 12% (factors 0.893, 0.797, 0.712, 0.636, 0.567) the present value is £129,175 and the NPV falls to **+£9,175**: still positive, but much smaller, which shows how the answer depends on the discount rate.

## Worked example 2: choosing between projects

Calder Print has £120,000 to spend and must choose between Project A (the press) and Project B, an online ordering platform, also costing £120,000. Project B's inflows are £60,000, £50,000, £30,000, £20,000 and £10,000 in years 1 to 5 (total £170,000).

| Measure | Project A (press) | Project B (platform) |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | 3.25 years | 2.33 years (cumulative £110,000 after 2 years, then £10,000 ÷ £30,000) |
| ARR | 10.0% | 8.3% ((170,000 − 120,000) ÷ 5 ÷ 120,000) |
| NPV at 8% | +£23,335 | +£23,740 |
| NPV at 12% | +£9,175 | +£13,180 |

Reading the table: B pays back faster and has the higher NPV at both rates; A has the higher ARR. B's NPV advantage is tiny at 8% (£405) but larger at 12% (£4,005), because B's cash arrives sooner, so higher discount rates hurt it less.

## Comparing the methods

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | Simple; focuses on risk and liquidity; useful when cash is short or the future is uncertain | Ignores cash after payback and the timing within the period; ignores the time value of money |
| ARR | Uses all cash flows; gives a percentage to compare with a target return | Ignores timing of cash; average hides when the money arrives |
| NPV | Uses all cash flows and the time value of money; gives a money value of the benefit | Needs a discount rate (a judgement); harder to explain; sensitive to estimates |

A business with a cash shortage may stress payback; a business planning for the long term may prefer NPV. Using several methods together gives a fuller view. All depend on forecasts of cash flow, which become less reliable the further ahead they look.

## Qualitative factors

Not everything can be put into numbers. Qualitative factors include:

- **Strategic fit:** does the project support the business objectives?
- **Risk:** how reliable are the forecasts? Is the technology proven?
- **Staff:** skills, retraining, redundancy and morale.
- **Brand and customers:** effect on image and customer satisfaction.
- **Environmental and ethical impact.**
- **Flexibility:** can the project be scaled down or stopped?
- **Legal and external factors:** regulation, competitors' moves, interest rates.

For Calder Print: the press may let it offer new products and keep skilled operators but depends on printing demand holding up; the platform may attract new customers and reduce phone orders but needs staff with web skills and could fail if the system has faults.

## Recommending and justifying an investment

A strong recommendation:

1. States a clear choice.
2. Uses at least two quantitative measures and compares them.
3. Uses qualitative factors to qualify the figures.
4. Considers the risk and the reliability of the estimates.
5. Reaches a judgement and says what would change it.

*Example for Calder Print.* Recommend Project B. It has the faster payback (2.33 against 3.25 years) and the higher NPV at both discount rates (£23,740 against £23,335 at 8%; £13,180 against £9,175 at 12%). The lower ARR (8.3% against 10%) is outweighed because ARR ignores the timing of cash. B's cash comes early, which reduces risk if the forecasts for later years prove too hopeful. Qualitatively, an online platform fits a trend to ordering online, though it needs staff training and carries the risk of technical faults. The press would give a more certain physical capability. The judgement is that B is the better investment, mainly on payback and NPV, but this would change if the platform's early inflows are less certain than the press's, or if Calder's customers do not want to order online.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to subtract the initial cost when calculating NPV.
- Using total return rather than average annual return in ARR.
- Giving a payback period in years but forgetting the fractional year.
- Treating a positive NPV as a certainty.
- Choosing a project on one method only and ignoring qualitative factors.

## Exam technique

Show every step. For payback, give the cumulative figures; for NPV, show the discount factor and present value for each year. In "evaluate" and "recommend" answers, compare methods, mention qualitative factors and give a final judgement.
