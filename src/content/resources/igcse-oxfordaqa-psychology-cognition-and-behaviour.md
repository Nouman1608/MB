This guide covers **Paper 1 Cognition and Behaviour**, one of two
equally-weighted papers in OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology
(9218), first teaching September 2023, first examined May/June 2025.
There is no coursework -- both papers are examined by written
assessment.

## Where this fits in 9218

Cognition and Behaviour introduces how psychologists study internal
mental processes -- memory, perception and the brain -- and pairs
these content topics with a dedicated Research Methods strand, so that
psychological method and content are tested together throughout.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL GCSE PSYCHOLOGY (9218) — PAPER 1 COGNITION
AND BEHAVIOUR**

- 1.1 Memory — models and processes of memory and forgetting
- 1.2 Perception — how the mind interprets sensory information
- 1.3 Biopsychology — how the brain and nervous system relate to
behaviour
- 1.4 Research Methods — the methods psychologists use to investigate
cognition and behaviour, applied across the other three sub-topics in
this paper

## How to approach it

Because Research Methods (1.4) is applied alongside the content
sub-topics rather than assessed as a standalone unit, practise
applying research methods questions directly to memory, perception and
biopsychology scenarios rather than revising methods in isolation --
this pairing is exactly what the paper is built to test. Memory (1.1)
and perception (1.2) both reward being able to describe a model or
process accurately and then evaluate it, so structure revision around
description plus evaluation rather than description alone.
Biopsychology (1.3) introduces biological vocabulary that pairs with
the more behavioural content of Paper 2, so build a clear picture of
how brain structure and function relate to the behaviours studied
elsewhere in the specification. As with any GCSE-level psychology
course, structure notes around named studies and models rather than
loose general knowledge, since exam questions consistently ask
students to describe and evaluate specific evidence rather than give
an unsupported opinion. Reviewing all four sub-topics together toward
the end of preparation, rather than treating each as a closed unit,
helps surface the connections between them that source-based exam
questions often draw on.

## Memory

Memory involves **encoding**, **storage** and **retrieval**. The **multi-store model** proposes three stores:

| Store | Encoding | Capacity | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensory | Modality-specific | Very large | Under 2 seconds |
| Short-term | Mainly acoustic | 5-9 items | 18-30 seconds |
| Long-term | Mainly semantic | Unlimited | Up to a lifetime |

Attention moves information from sensory to short-term; rehearsal moves it to long-term. The serial position curve — better recall of the first and last items — supports the existence of separate stores.

Long-term memory divides into **episodic** (personal events), **semantic** (facts) and **procedural** (skills).

Forgetting is explained by **interference** — proactive (old disrupts new) and retroactive (new disrupts old) — and by **retrieval failure**, where the cues present at encoding are absent at recall.

## Perception

**Sensation** is the raw information the senses receive; **perception** is the brain's interpretation of it.

**Gibson's direct theory** is bottom-up: the optic array supplies sufficient information — motion parallax, texture gradient, optic flow — for accurate perception without inference.

**Gregory's constructivist theory** is top-down: perception is a hypothesis built from prior knowledge and expectation. Illusions such as the Müller-Lyer and Ponzo figures support this, because the brain misapplies depth cues learned from a carpentered environment.

**Depth cues** are monocular (linear perspective, relative size, height in plane, occlusion) and binocular (retinal disparity, convergence).

**Perceptual set** — the tendency to perceive what we expect — is affected by culture, motivation, emotion and context.

## Development and research methods

Piaget explained development through **schemas** modified by assimilation and accommodation, across stages marked by object permanence, egocentrism and conservation.

Research methods: experiments (laboratory, field, natural), observation, self-report and correlation. Key terms are the independent and dependent variables, extraneous variables, and the distinction between **reliability** (consistency) and **validity** (measuring what is intended).

Ethics require informed consent, no deception, right to withdraw, protection from harm, confidentiality and debriefing.

## Worked example

Explain why the Müller-Lyer illusion supports Gregory rather than Gibson.

```
The two lines are physically identical, yet one appears longer.

Gregory: the brain interprets the arrowheads as depth cues -- the
  fins-out figure resembles an inside corner, so it is judged
  further away and therefore larger. Perception is an inference,
  and here the inference is WRONG.

Gibson would predict accurate perception, since the optic array
  contains the true information.

The persistent error is therefore evidence for top-down processing.
```

## Common mistakes

Confusing sensation with perception. Swapping proactive and retroactive interference. Describing Gibson's theory as top-down. Listing depth cues without classifying them as monocular or binocular. Confusing reliability with validity. Concluding cause from a correlation.

## Quick revision checklist

- Describe the three memory processes and the multi-store model, with capacity, duration and encoding.
- Distinguish episodic, semantic and procedural memory.
- Explain interference and retrieval failure.
- Compare Gibson and Gregory, using a named illusion as evidence.
- Classify monocular and binocular depth cues and explain perceptual set.
- Identify variables, distinguish reliability from validity, and apply ethical guidelines.
