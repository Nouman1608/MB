This guide covers **Paper 1 Cognition and Behaviour**, one of two
equally-weighted papers in OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology
(9218), first teaching September 2023, first examined May/June 2025.
There is no coursework -- both papers are examined by written
assessment.

## Where this fits in 9218

Cognition and Behaviour introduces how psychologists study internal
mental processes -- memory, perception and the brain -- and pairs
these content topics with a dedicated Research Methods strand, so that
psychological method and content are tested together throughout.
Paper 1 is a 2-hour written exam worth 100 marks (50% of the GCSE),
split into four 25-mark sections: Memory, Perception, Biopsychology
and Research Methods.

## How to approach it

Research Methods (3.1.4) is assessed as its own dedicated 25-mark
section of Paper 1, and research-methods and data-handling questions
are also woven into the memory, perception and biopsychology
scenarios in the other three sections -- so revise it both as a
standalone topic and as something to apply directly to content
questions. Memory (3.1.1) and perception (3.1.2) both reward being
able to describe a model or process accurately and then evaluate it,
so structure revision around description plus evaluation rather than
description alone. Biopsychology (3.1.3) introduces biological
vocabulary that pairs with the more behavioural content of Paper 2, so
build a clear picture of how brain structure and function relate to
the behaviours studied elsewhere in the specification. As with any
GCSE-level psychology course, structure notes around named studies and
models rather than loose general knowledge, since exam questions
consistently ask students to describe and evaluate specific evidence
rather than give an unsupported opinion. Reviewing all four sub-topics
together toward the end of preparation, rather than treating each as a
closed unit, helps surface the connections between them that
source-based exam questions often draw on.

## Memory

Memory involves **encoding**, **storage** and **retrieval**. The **multi-store model** proposes three stores:

| Store | Encoding | Capacity | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensory | Modality-specific | Very large | Under 2 seconds |
| Short-term | Mainly acoustic | 5-9 items | 18-30 seconds |
| Long-term | Mainly semantic | Unlimited | Up to a lifetime |

Attention moves information from sensory to short-term; rehearsal moves it to long-term. The serial position curve — better recall of the first and last items — supports the existence of separate stores. **Murdock's serial position curve study** provided the evidence for this: presenting participants with word lists and testing free recall, he found the first few words (the **primacy effect**) and the last few words (the **recency effect**) were recalled far more reliably than words from the middle of the list.

Long-term memory divides into **episodic** (personal events), **semantic** (facts) and **procedural** (skills).

The **working memory model** replaces the single short-term store with four active components: the **central executive** (limited-capacity attention controller that coordinates the other components), the **phonological loop** (holds spoken and written material briefly, coded acoustically), the **visuo-spatial sketchpad** (holds visual and spatial information) and the **episodic buffer** (integrates information from the other components and links it to long-term memory). **Baddeley and Hitch** investigated the model using dual-processing (dual-task) studies: combining two tasks that draw on the *same* component (e.g. two visual tasks) disrupted performance far more than combining tasks that draw on *different* components (e.g. one visual, one verbal), evidence that short-term memory is not a single unitary store.

Forgetting is explained by **interference** — proactive (old disrupts new) and retroactive (new disrupts old) — and by **retrieval failure**, where the cues present at encoding are absent at recall.

The **theory of reconstructive memory** holds that recall is an active rebuilding of an event rather than a faithful replay, shaped by schemas and by **'effort after meaning'** — the tendency to iron out unfamiliar or confusing details so that a memory makes sense in terms of what is already known. **Bartlett's *War of the Ghosts* study** supported this: participants recalling an unfamiliar folk story shortened it, omitted culturally unfamiliar details, and changed elements to fit their own expectations. Factors affecting the accuracy of memory include **interference**, **context** and **false memories** — confident but inaccurate recollections that can be created by misleading information or suggestion after the event.

## Perception

**Sensation** is the raw information the senses receive; **perception** is the brain's interpretation of it.

**Gibson's direct theory** is bottom-up: the optic array supplies sufficient information — motion parallax, texture gradient, optic flow — for accurate perception without inference.

**Gregory's constructivist theory** is top-down: perception is a hypothesis built from prior knowledge and expectation. Illusions such as the Müller-Lyer and Ponzo figures support this, because the brain misapplies depth cues learned from a carpentered environment.

Illusions are useful evidence precisely because they reveal different ways perception can go wrong. The specification names four explanations for illusions: **ambiguity**, **misinterpreted depth cues**, **fiction** and **size constancy**. The Müller-Lyer and Ponzo illusions arise from misapplied **depth cues**. Rubin's vase arises from **ambiguity** — the same outline can be read as a vase or as two faces. The Kanizsa triangle arises from **fiction**, where the brain supplies edges that are not actually present in the image. The **Necker cube** also arises from ambiguity, flipping unpredictably between two equally valid three-dimensional interpretations. The **Ames Room** is the canonical example of **size constancy**: the room is actually a distorted trapezoid, built and viewed from a fixed point so it looks like an ordinary rectangular room, so the brain keeps applying its normal assumption that retinal size indicates distance — making two people of equal height standing in different corners appear drastically different in size. Together these examples show perception is an active hypothesis the brain forms and revises, not a passive reading of the image.

**Depth cues** are monocular (linear perspective, relative size, height in plane, occlusion) and binocular (retinal disparity, convergence).

**Perceptual set** — the tendency to perceive what we expect — is affected by culture, motivation, emotion and expectation. Four named investigations provide evidence for these factors: **Gilchrist and Nesberg** found that hungrier participants rated pictures of food as brighter than well-fed participants did, evidence for the effect of **motivation**; **Bruner and Minturn** showed that an identical ambiguous figure was read as the letter "B" or the number "13" depending on whether it appeared in a sequence of letters or of numbers, evidence for the effect of **context/expectation**; **Kunst-Wilson and Zajonc** showed that participants preferred shapes they had been shown too briefly to recognise, even though they could not tell which shapes they had seen, evidence for the effect of **emotion** (a feeling of liking formed without conscious recognition); and **Nisbett and Miyamoto** compared the eye movements and scene recall of participants from different cultural backgrounds, evidence for the effect of **culture** on what is attended to and perceived.

## Biopsychology

The human nervous system divides into the **central nervous system** (brain and spinal cord) and the **peripheral nervous system**, which itself divides into the **somatic** (voluntary, sensory and motor signals to and from the muscles) and **autonomic** (involuntary control of internal organs) systems.

The **endocrine system** works through glands releasing **hormones** into the bloodstream. The autonomic nervous system triggers the **fight or flight response** to a perceived threat, releasing adrenaline and preparing the body for action. The **James-Lange theory of emotion** proposes that physiological arousal comes first and the emotion is our interpretation of that bodily change -- we feel afraid *because* we notice our heart racing, rather than the other way round.

**Neurons** are specialised cells: **sensory neurons** carry signals from receptors to the central nervous system, **relay neurons** connect neurons within the CNS, and **motor neurons** carry signals from the CNS to muscles and glands. **Synaptic transmission** is the process by which neurotransmitters are released from one neuron, cross the synaptic gap, and bind to receptors on the next, producing either **excitation** (increasing the chance the next neuron fires) or **inhibition** (decreasing it); the neurotransmitter is then reabsorbed by the sending neuron (**reuptake**). **Hebb's theory of learning and neuronal growth** proposes that when two neurons repeatedly fire together, the synaptic connection between them strengthens ("cells that fire together, wire together") -- the biological basis of learning.

The brain has four main lobes -- **frontal**, **temporal**, **parietal** and **occipital** -- plus the **cerebellum**, which coordinates movement and balance. **Localisation of function** means specific brain areas are responsible for specific functions: **motor** areas (movement), **somatosensory** areas (touch and body sensation), **visual** areas (sight), **auditory** areas (hearing), and two named **language areas** -- **Broca's area** (speech production) and **Wernicke's area** (language comprehension). **Penfield's study of the interpretive cortex** electrically stimulated the brains of conscious patients during surgery and found that stimulating areas of the temporal lobe could trigger vivid memories and sensations, evidence for localisation of function.

**Scanning techniques** let psychologists study brain function in living people: **CT** scans use X-rays to image brain structure, **PET** scans track a radioactive tracer to show which areas are metabolically active, and **fMRI** scans measure blood-oxygen changes to show which areas are active during a task. **Tulving's 'gold' memory study** used PET scans to show that episodic and semantic memory are processed in different brain regions.

Neurological damage -- for example from a stroke or head injury -- can affect motor abilities and behaviour, depending on which area of the brain is damaged.

## Research methods

Research methods: experiments (laboratory, field, natural), observation, self-report and correlation. Key terms are the independent and dependent variables, extraneous variables, and the distinction between **reliability** (consistency) and **validity** (measuring what is intended). A **null hypothesis** predicts no effect or relationship between variables; an **alternative hypothesis** predicts that there will be one.

- **Sampling:** **random** sampling gives everyone in the target population an equal chance of selection; **opportunity** sampling uses whoever is available (quick, but often unrepresentative); **systematic** sampling takes every nth person from a list; **stratified** sampling selects from subgroups in proportion to their size in the population.
- **Experimental designs:** **independent groups** (different participants in each condition, so no order effects but participant variables differ), **repeated measures** (the same participants in every condition, so order effects must be controlled by **counterbalancing**) and **matched pairs** (participants matched on a relevant characteristic, then split between conditions).
- **Controls:** **standardised procedures** and instructions, and **random allocation** to conditions, reduce the effect of extraneous variables.
- **Data:** **quantitative** (numbers) and **qualitative** (descriptions) data; **primary** data collected by the researcher and **secondary** data collected by others; summarised with the **mean, median, mode and range** and displayed in tables, bar charts and scatter diagrams (for correlations).

Ethics must follow British Psychological Society guidelines, which require dealing appropriately with issues including **informed consent, protection from harm, confidentiality, the right to withdraw and debriefing**. **Deception** is not an outright ban -- it is permissible where it is scientifically justified and unavoidable, provided participants are fully **debriefed** as soon as possible, told the true nature and purpose of the study, and given the opportunity to withdraw their data. Deception is an ethical issue to be *managed* through justification and debriefing, not a rule that can never be broken -- several of psychology's landmark studies, including Milgram's and Asch's, involved deceiving participants about the study's true purpose.

## Worked example

Explain why the Müller-Lyer illusion supports Gregory rather than Gibson.

```
The two lines are physically identical, yet one appears longer.

Gregory: the brain interprets the arrowheads as depth cues -- the
  fins-out figure resembles an inside corner, so it is judged
  further away and therefore larger. Perception is an inference,
  and here the inference is WRONG.

Gibson would predict accurate perception, since the optic array
  contains the true information.

The persistent error is therefore evidence for top-down processing.
```

## Common mistakes

Confusing sensation with perception. Swapping proactive and retroactive interference. Describing Gibson's theory as top-down. Listing depth cues without classifying them as monocular or binocular. Confusing reliability with validity. Concluding cause from a correlation.

## Quick revision checklist

- Describe the three memory processes and the multi-store model, with capacity, duration and encoding.
- Distinguish episodic, semantic and procedural memory.
- Describe the working memory model's four components and Baddeley and Hitch's dual-processing evidence for it.
- Explain interference and retrieval failure, and reconstructive memory including effort after meaning and false memories.
- Compare Gibson and Gregory, using a named illusion as evidence, and name all four explanations for illusions.
- Classify monocular and binocular depth cues and explain perceptual set, with a named study for each factor.
- Describe the divisions of the nervous system, the endocrine system and fight-or-flight, synaptic transmission, brain lobes and localisation of function including the two named language areas.
- Identify variables, distinguish reliability from validity, state the null and alternative hypothesis, and apply BPS ethical guidelines including how deception is justified and managed.

**What is the James-Lange theory of emotion?**
It proposes that physiological arousal comes first and the emotion is our interpretation of that bodily change.

**Is deception banned under BPS ethical guidelines?**
No. Deception is permissible where it is scientifically justified and unavoidable, provided participants are fully debriefed as soon as possible.
