These notes condense learning outcomes 3.1.2(a) to (j) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification (H420, Version 4.1, April 2026, first assessment 2025), including the heart dissection linked to PAG2. For explanations and worked examples, read the [transport in animals study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-transport-in-animals/), then test yourself with the [transport in animals practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-transport-in-animals-practice/).

## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mass transport | Bulk movement of a fluid carrying substances, driven by a pressure difference |
| Open circulation | Blood (haemolymph) is not always in vessels; it bathes the organs |
| Closed circulation | Blood stays inside vessels |
| Double circulation | Blood passes through the heart twice in one complete circuit |
| Hydrostatic pressure | Pressure exerted by a fluid, here blood pushing on capillary walls |
| Oncotic pressure | The tendency of water to move into the blood by osmosis, due to plasma proteins |
| Myogenic | Contraction starts in the muscle itself, without nervous stimulation |
| Stroke volume | Volume of blood pumped by one ventricle in one beat |
| Cardiac output | Volume pumped by one ventricle per minute = heart rate × stroke volume |
| Bohr effect | Higher CO₂ concentration lowers haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen; curve moves right |

## Circulatory systems: 3.1.2(a)-(b)

- Needed because of large size, small SA:V and high metabolic rate.
- **Insects**: open; tubular heart; haemolymph carries nutrients, not oxygen.
- **Fish**: closed, single; pressure drops in the gills, so flow to the body is slow.
- **Mammals**: closed, double; high pressure to the body, lower pressure to the lungs.

## Vessels: 3.1.2(c)

| Vessel | Key structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Artery | Thick elastic and muscle layers, collagen, small lumen | Carries blood at high pressure away from the heart; recoil evens out pressure |
| Arteriole | Mostly smooth muscle | Controls flow to capillary beds |
| Capillary | Endothelium one cell thick, narrow | Exchange; forms tissue fluid |
| Venule | Thin wall | Collects blood from capillaries |
| Vein | Thin wall, wide lumen, valves | Returns low-pressure blood; valves prevent backflow |

## Tissue fluid: 3.1.2(d)

- Arteriole end: hydrostatic > oncotic → fluid forced out.
- Venule end: hydrostatic < oncotic → water returns by osmosis.
- Excess tissue fluid → lymph vessels → returned to the blood.
- Tissue fluid: no red cells, very few proteins. Lymph: more lymphocytes and fatty acids.

## The heart: 3.1.2(e)

- Right side: deoxygenated blood, vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary artery.
- Left side: oxygenated blood, pulmonary vein → left atrium → bicuspid valve → left ventricle → aorta.
- Left ventricle wall thickest: pumps to the whole body.
- Semilunar valves at the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery; tendinous cords hold the AV valves.
- Coronary arteries supply the heart muscle.

## Cardiac cycle: 3.1.2(f)

| Event | Pressure condition | Valve |
|---|---|---|
| AV valves close | ventricle > atrium | start of ventricular systole |
| Semilunar valves open | ventricle > artery | blood ejected |
| Semilunar valves close | artery > ventricle | start of diastole |
| AV valves open | atrium > ventricle | ventricles refill |

cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume

## Heartbeat control: 3.1.2(g)

SAN (right atrium) → excitation over atria → atria contract → non-conducting layer blocks direct spread → AVN delay → bundle of His → Purkyne tissue → ventricles contract from the apex up.

## ECGs: 3.1.2(h)

- P: atrial depolarisation. QRS: ventricular depolarisation. T: ventricular repolarisation.
- Heart rate = 60 ÷ R-R interval (s).
- Tachycardia > 100 min⁻¹; bradycardia < 60 min⁻¹; fibrillation irregular and uncoordinated; ectopic beat early, then a pause.

## Haemoglobin: 3.1.2(i)-(j)

- Four polypeptide chains, four haem groups with Fe²⁺: up to 4 O₂ per molecule, bound reversibly.
- Sigmoid curve: binding of one O₂ makes the next easier.
- CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic anhydrase) → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
- H⁺ + Hb → haemoglobinic acid; oxygen released; pH buffered.
- HCO₃⁻ out of the red cell, Cl⁻ in: chloride shift.
- Bohr effect: more CO₂, curve right, more O₂ unloaded in respiring tissue.
- Fetal Hb: curve left of adult; takes O₂ from maternal blood at the placenta.

## Worked reminders

- Stroke volume 80 cm³, heart rate 65 min⁻¹: cardiac output = 5200 cm³ min⁻¹ = 5.2 dm³ min⁻¹.
- Cardiac output 6.3 dm³ min⁻¹ at 90 min⁻¹: stroke volume = 6300 ÷ 90 = 70 cm³.
- R-R interval 1.25 s: heart rate 48 min⁻¹, bradycardia.

## Exam traps

- "Arteries carry oxygenated blood": the pulmonary artery does not.
- Tissue fluid containing red blood cells or many plasma proteins.
- SAN placed in the left atrium; AVN delay left out.
- Semilunar valves closing at the start of systole (they close at the start of diastole).
- Bohr effect described as increasing affinity.
- Fetal curve drawn to the right of the adult curve.
- ECG rate calculated from one interval on an irregular trace.

## Quick self-test

1. Why does a fish's single circulation limit the rate of supply to its body?
2. Give two differences between an artery and a vein.
3. Why do arterioles have more smooth muscle than arteries?
4. Explain why tissue fluid has very few proteins.
5. Explain how water returns to the capillary at the venule end.
6. Why is the left ventricle wall thicker than the right?
7. What causes the atrioventricular valves to close?
8. Explain the importance of the delay at the AVN.
9. On an ECG, what does the QRS complex show?
10. An ECG has an R-R interval of 0.45 s. Calculate the heart rate and name the condition.
11. Describe the role of carbonic anhydrase.
12. Explain why the Bohr effect is an advantage during exercise.

### Answers

1. Blood loses pressure in the gill capillaries, so it flows to the body slowly at low pressure.
2. Arteries have thicker walls with more elastic tissue and muscle and a narrower lumen; veins have thin walls, a wide lumen and valves.
3. Their smooth muscle contracts or relaxes to control blood flow into capillary beds.
4. Plasma proteins are too large to pass through the gaps in the capillary wall.
5. Hydrostatic pressure has fallen below the oncotic pressure, so water moves back into the blood by osmosis.
6. It pumps blood all round the body, against a much greater resistance than the lungs, so it must produce a higher pressure.
7. Pressure in the ventricles rises above pressure in the atria.
8. It lets the atria finish contracting and the ventricles fill before the ventricles contract.
9. Depolarisation of the ventricles.
10. 60 ÷ 0.45 = **133 beats per minute**: **tachycardia**.
11. It catalyses the reaction of carbon dioxide with water to form carbonic acid in red blood cells.
12. Respiring muscle produces more CO₂, which lowers haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, so more oxygen is released to the muscle.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between hydrostatic and oncotic pressure?**
Hydrostatic pressure is the blood pressure pushing fluid out of the capillary. Oncotic pressure comes from plasma proteins lowering the water potential of the blood, which draws water back in by osmosis.

**Why is haemoglobin's curve S-shaped?**
When the first oxygen binds, the molecule changes shape and the remaining sites bind oxygen more easily, so saturation rises steeply over a middle range of partial pressures.

**Do I need hormonal and nervous control of the heart here?**
No. For 3.1.2(g) the specification asks for the SAN, AVN, Purkyne tissue and the myogenic nature of cardiac muscle. Nervous control of heart rate is covered later in Module 5.
