This guide covers subtopic **11.4, The reactions of chlorine**, from Topic 11, Group 17, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

The [Group 17: The Halogens](/resources/as-group-17-the-halogens/) guide gives the equations for this subtopic. This page goes further into 11.4: how to **interpret** each reaction through oxidation numbers, how to balance a disproportionation equation from the electron changes, why heating changes the product, ionic equations, reacting-mass calculations, and the chemistry of chlorine in water treatment. Condensed notes are in the [11.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-the-reactions-of-chlorine-revision-notes/), and the [11.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-the-reactions-of-chlorine-practice/) test it.

## What 11.4 asks you to do

1. Describe and interpret, **in terms of changes in oxidation number**, the reaction of chlorine with **cold** and with **hot** aqueous sodium hydroxide, and recognise these as **disproportionation** reactions.
2. Explain, including by use of an equation, the use of chlorine in **water purification**, including the production of the active species **HOCl** and **ClO⁻**, which kill bacteria.

## Disproportionation

**Disproportionation** is a redox reaction in which the **same element** is simultaneously oxidised and reduced. To recognise one, assign oxidation numbers to the element in the reactant and in every product. If one product has a higher value and another a lower value than the reactant, the element has disproportionated.

Chlorine is well suited to this. In Cl₂ its oxidation number is 0, midway between −1 (chloride) and the positive values it takes when combined with oxygen.

## Chlorine with cold aqueous sodium hydroxide

Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O

Ionic equation: **Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O**

| Species | Cl₂ | Cl⁻ (in NaCl) | ClO⁻ (in NaClO) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation number of Cl | 0 | −1 | +1 |

One chlorine atom is **reduced** (0 → −1) and the other is **oxidised** (0 → +1). The product NaClO is **sodium chlorate(I)**. The Roman numeral gives the oxidation number of chlorine. A solution of sodium chlorate(I) is used as household bleach.

## Chlorine with hot aqueous sodium hydroxide

3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O

Ionic equation: **3Cl₂ + 6OH⁻ → 5Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻ + 3H₂O**

| Species | Cl₂ | Cl⁻ (in NaCl) | ClO₃⁻ (in NaClO₃) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation number of Cl | 0 | −1 | +5 |

Chlorine is reduced to −1 and oxidised to +5. The product NaClO₃ is **sodium chlorate(V)**.

### Balancing from oxidation numbers

**Worked example.** *Use oxidation numbers to explain the 5 : 1 ratio of NaCl to NaClO₃ in the hot reaction.*

- Each Cl atom that becomes Cl⁻ **gains 1 electron** (0 → −1).
- Each Cl atom that becomes ClO₃⁻ **loses 5 electrons** (0 → +5).
- Electrons lost must equal electrons gained. So for every one Cl atom oxidised to +5, five Cl atoms must be reduced to −1.
- That needs six Cl atoms in total, which is **3Cl₂**, giving **5Cl⁻ + 1ClO₃⁻**.
- Balance charge with OH⁻ (6OH⁻ on the left, to match 6 negative charges on the right) and finish with water: 3Cl₂ + 6OH⁻ → 5Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻ + 3H₂O.

Check: Cl 6 = 5 + 1; O 6 = 3 + 3; H 6 = 6; charge −6 = −6.

Apply the same reasoning to the cold reaction: one Cl gains 1 electron, one Cl loses 1 electron, so the ratio of Cl⁻ to ClO⁻ is 1 : 1.

### Why heating changes the product

The chlorate(I) ion is itself unstable when heated, and it disproportionates further:

3ClO⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻

Chlorine goes from +1 to −1 (in 2Cl⁻, gaining 4 electrons in total) and from +1 to +5 (in ClO₃⁻, losing 4 electrons). So in hot alkali, any chlorate(I) that forms is converted into chloride and chlorate(V). Adding this equation to three times the cold reaction gives the hot reaction.

### A reacting-mass calculation

**Worked example.** 0.300 mol of chlorine reacts completely with hot aqueous sodium hydroxide. Calculate the mass of sodium chlorate(V) formed. (Aᵣ: Na = 23.0, Cl = 35.5, O = 16.0)

From 3Cl₂ → 1NaClO₃: amount of NaClO₃ = 0.300 ÷ 3 = 0.100 mol.

Mᵣ(NaClO₃) = 23.0 + 35.5 + 3(16.0) = 106.5

Mass = 0.100 × 106.5 = **10.7 g** (3 significant figures).

The same reaction also forms 0.300 × 5 ÷ 3 = 0.500 mol of NaCl.

## Chlorine in water purification

Chlorine is added to water supplies to kill bacteria. It reacts reversibly with water:

**Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HOCl**

| Species | Cl₂ | HCl | HOCl |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation number of Cl | 0 | −1 | +1 |

This is also a disproportionation. HOCl is **chloric(I) acid**. It is a weak acid and partially dissociates to give the **chlorate(I) ion**:

**HOCl ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻**

**HOCl and ClO⁻ are the active species that kill bacteria.** Both contain chlorine in the +1 oxidation state and act as oxidising agents.

The position of the second equilibrium depends on the pH of the water. Adding H⁺ (lower pH) shifts it to the left, so more HOCl is present. Removing H⁺ (higher pH) shifts it to the right, so more ClO⁻ is present.

**Worked example: an annotated model answer.** *Explain, using equations, how chlorine purifies water.*

> "Chlorine reacts with water: Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HOCl [1]. HOCl partly dissociates: HOCl ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻ [1]. HOCl and ClO⁻ are the active species, which kill bacteria in the water [1]."

## Three disproportionations compared

| Conditions | Products containing Cl | Oxidation numbers of Cl | Ratio reduced : oxidised |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | HCl, HOCl | −1, +1 | 1 : 1 |
| Cold aqueous NaOH | NaCl, NaClO | −1, +1 | 1 : 1 |
| Hot aqueous NaOH | NaCl, NaClO₃ | −1, +5 | 5 : 1 |

The cold-alkali reaction is the water reaction with the acids neutralised: HCl and HOCl react with NaOH to give NaCl and NaClO. In every case one product contains chloride, and the other contains chlorine in a positive oxidation state bonded to oxygen.

## Common mistakes

- **Giving the same products for hot and cold alkali.** Cold: NaCl + NaClO. Hot: NaCl + NaClO₃.
- **Calling a reaction disproportionation without oxidation numbers.** The syllabus asks you to interpret the changes in oxidation number, so show them.
- **Wrong names.** NaClO is sodium chlorate(I); NaClO₃ is sodium chlorate(V); HOCl is chloric(I) acid.
- **Writing the chlorine–water reaction with a single arrow.** It is reversible.
- **Saying chlorine itself, or HCl, kills the bacteria.** The syllabus names HOCl and ClO⁻.
- **Balancing the hot equation by trial and error and getting 4 : 2 or 3 : 1.** Use the electron count: 5 × (+1 gained) = 1 × (5 lost).

## Exam technique: command words in 11.4

- **Describe**: give conditions (cold or hot) and the balanced equation.
- **Interpret** (in terms of oxidation number): write the oxidation number of chlorine in every species, and say which change is oxidation and which is reduction.
- **Recognise**: name the reaction type, disproportionation, and justify it.
- **Explain** (water purification): give both equations and name HOCl and ClO⁻ as the species that kill bacteria.
