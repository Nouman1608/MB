This guide teaches section 1.1 (What is a business?) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL. The guide lists four content points: the nature of business; the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors; entrepreneurship; and the challenges and opportunities for starting up a business. The [1.1 and 1.2 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-what-is-a-business-and-types-of-business-entities/) gives the core definitions. This page goes further on each point: how a business turns inputs into outputs, how value is added along a chain of production, why the balance between sectors changes, what entrepreneurship really involves, and how to analyse a start-up's risks with numbers.

Recall the key points with the [1.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-1-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [1.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-1-practice/). All businesses and figures below are invented, and money is in dollars. Where the guide gives no definition, the wording is a standard textbook form.

## The nature of business: inputs, process, outputs

A business combines resources to produce goods or services that customers want. It helps to see this as a simple system.

| Stage | What happens | Example for a bakery |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | Resources are acquired: human (staff and their skills), physical (premises, machinery, materials) and financial (money to pay for them) | Bakers, ovens, flour, a bank loan |
| Process | Resources are combined and transformed | Mixing, baking, packing |
| Outputs | Goods or services are sold to customers | Bread and cakes sold in the shop |

Four functions inside a business manage this system. They are also the structure of the rest of the course: human resource management (Unit 2), finance and accounts (Unit 3), marketing (Unit 4) and operations management (Unit 5).

| Function | Role |
|---|---|
| Human resources | Recruits, trains, organizes and motivates staff |
| Finance and accounts | Raises and controls money, and records performance |
| Marketing | Finds out what customers want and persuades them to buy |
| Operations | Turns inputs into outputs efficiently and to the right quality |

The functions depend on each other. A marketing campaign that doubles demand fails if operations cannot produce enough, or if finance cannot pay for extra materials. Seeing these links is what an analytical answer on "the nature of business" looks like.

## Value added along a chain of production

Most products pass through several businesses in different sectors before reaching the customer. Each one adds value: the difference between the price it charges and the cost of the inputs it buys from other firms.

**Worked example 1: a woollen jumper.**

| Stage | Sector | Buys inputs for | Sells for | Value added |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tarnby Sheep Farm produces raw wool | Primary | 2 (feed, veterinary care) | 6 | 6 − 2 = 4 |
| Calder Knitwear Mill spins and knits the jumper | Secondary | 6 (wool) + 5 (dye, energy, design licence) = 11 | 30 | 30 − 11 = 19 |
| Rowan Lane Boutique sells it | Tertiary | 30 (jumper) + 4 (packaging, card fees) = 34 | 65 | 65 − 34 = 31 |

Total value added is 4 + 19 + 31 = 54. Add the 11 of bought-in inputs from outside the chain (2 + 5 + 4) and you reach the final price of 65.

Analysis: the retailer adds the most value per jumper, even though it changes nothing physical. Customers pay for convenience, location, advice and the brand of the shop. The design licence inside the mill's costs is quaternary-sector work: knowledge sold as an input. This shows two exam points at once: one product links all four sectors, and value added depends on what customers are willing to pay for, not on how much physical work is done.

## Sectoral change

The share of output and employment in each sector shifts over time. This is general economic knowledge rather than a point the guide spells out, but it is the reason the sector classification matters to a business.

| Change | Typical causes | What it means for businesses |
|---|---|---|
| Primary to secondary | Mechanisation of farming; growth of factories | Workers move to manufacturing; demand for machinery rises |
| Secondary to tertiary | Rising incomes; manufacturing moved to lower-cost countries | Growth of retail, finance, tourism and health services |
| Growth of quaternary | Digital technology; demand for data and research | Demand for highly skilled staff; firms compete on knowledge |

**Applying it.** A clothing manufacturer in a country where wages are rising may find its costs no longer competitive. It might move production abroad, automate, or shift toward higher value-added design and retail. Each choice links sectoral change to a decision, which is how to use this content point in Paper 1 answers.

## Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship is often treated as a fourth factor of production, alongside land, labour and capital. The entrepreneur is the person who organizes the other three and bears the risk that the venture fails. The reward for that risk is profit, or, for a social entrepreneur, the social impact achieved.

What separates entrepreneurship from simply working in a business:

- **Opportunity recognition**: seeing an unmet need, such as no late-night food option near a hospital.
- **Risk-bearing**: putting personal money, time and reputation at stake without a guaranteed return.
- **Organizing resources**: hiring staff, finding premises and suppliers, raising finance.
- **Innovation**: offering something new or doing something familiar in a new way.

Motives vary. Some entrepreneurs want independence or wealth; others want to solve a social or environmental problem; some have spotted a gap and simply want to fill it. In an answer, linking the motive to the case matters, because it explains later decisions, such as whether the founder will accept outside investors who might take some control.

## Challenges and opportunities for starting up

The existing guide lists the main challenges and opportunities. Here the focus is on using numbers from a case to judge them, which is what a strong analytical answer does.

**Worked example 2: Ondra's Dumpling Van.** Ondra plans a food van serving office workers.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Start-up costs (van, kitchen fit-out, licences, first stock) | 36,000 |
| Ondra's savings | 15,000 |
| Bank loan offered | 15,000 |
| Monthly fixed costs (loan repayment, pitch fee, insurance, fuel) | 3,200 |
| Expected monthly contribution, months 1 to 3 (while building custom) | 2,000 |
| Expected monthly contribution from month 4 | 4,500 |

Step 1, the funding gap: 36,000 − (15,000 + 15,000) = **6,000** still to find before opening.

Step 2, the early cash shortfall: in months 1 to 3, each month loses 3,200 − 2,000 = 1,200, so the van is 3 × 1,200 = **3,600** down by the end of month 3.

Step 3, the recovery: from month 4, each month gains 4,500 − 3,200 = 1,300. After three more months the position is −3,600 + 3,900 = **+300**, so the van recovers its early losses during month 6.

Judgement: Ondra needs about 9,600 more than she has arranged (6,000 + 3,600), not 6,000, or the van could run out of cash before it becomes profitable. The opportunity is real: a recovery within six months is quick, and a van can move pitch if a location fails. But the plan depends on the month 4 contribution forecast. If custom builds more slowly, the cash gap grows. A recommendation would be to secure the extra finance, or a smaller van, before launch.

## Common mistakes

- Classifying a business by its name instead of each activity it carries out.
- Treating value added as profit. Value added still has to pay wages, rent and interest.
- Saying the tertiary sector "adds no value" because nothing physical is made.
- Describing entrepreneurship as "starting a business" without mentioning risk or organizing resources.
- Listing start-up challenges with no link to figures or details in the case.
- Ignoring the timing of cash: a profitable plan can still fail in its first months.

## Exam technique

The IB uses command terms tied to assessment objectives.

- **Define** and **state** (AO1): a precise one- or two-sentence answer. No example is needed unless asked.
- **Explain** and **analyse** (AO2): give the reason or effect and link it to the case, for example "Rowan Lane adds the most value because ...".
- **Discuss**, **evaluate** and **to what extent** (AO3): weigh points on both sides and reach a supported judgement, stating what it depends on.
- **Calculate** (AO4): show the working, then state the result with units.

In Paper 1 the case is based on a pre-released statement, so practise applying 1.1 ideas to an unfamiliar organization rather than memorizing examples.
