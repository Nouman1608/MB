It is AS content for Paper 1 and Paper 2, assumed on Paper 4. For explanations and worked examples, use the [oxygen and carbon dioxide transport study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-transport-of-oxygen-and-carbon-dioxide-cambridge/); for exam-style questions, use the [oxygen and carbon dioxide transport practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-transport-of-oxygen-and-carbon-dioxide-cambridge-practice/).

## Oxygen in one paragraph

Haemoglobin: four polypeptides, four haem groups, each with iron; carries up to **four O₂**. Reversible: Hb + 4O₂ ⇌ HbO₈. Loads where pO₂ is high (lungs), unloads where pO₂ is low (respiring tissue). Red blood cells have no nucleus, leaving more room for haemoglobin.

## The dissociation curve

| Part of curve | pO₂ | What is happening | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shallow start | Very low | Saturation rises slowly | First O₂ binds with difficulty |
| Steep middle | Roughly 2–6 kPa | Small pO₂ change, big saturation change | First O₂ changes Hb shape; others bind more easily |
| Plateau | High | Nearly 100 % | Few binding sites left |

**Importance**

- Lungs (high pO₂, flat part): haemoglobin almost fully saturated; loading still good if pO₂ falls slightly.
- Tissues (low pO₂, steep part): a small fall in pO₂ releases a lot of oxygen where respiration is fastest.

### Worked reminder: oxygen released

Capacity 20 cm³ O₂ per 100 cm³ blood (given value). Saturation 96 % leaving lungs, 66 % leaving tissue.

```
released = 20 × (0.96 − 0.66) = 20 × 0.30 = 6.0 cm³ per 100 cm³
```

## Carbon dioxide: the five routes and reactions

| Step | Where | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Red blood cell | CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃, catalysed by **carbonic anhydrase** |
| 2 | Red blood cell | H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ |
| 3 | Red blood cell | H⁺ + Hb → **haemoglobinic acid (HHb)**; O₂ released; pH buffered |
| 4 | Red blood cell | CO₂ + amine groups of Hb → **carbaminohaemoglobin** |
| 5 | Plasma | HCO₃⁻ carried in solution (most CO₂); some CO₂ dissolved |

### Chloride shift

- HCO₃⁻ diffuse **out** of the red blood cell into plasma.
- Cl⁻ diffuse **in** to balance the charge.
- Importance: keeps the cell electrically neutral, so HCO₃⁻ can keep leaving; HCO₃⁻ does not build up, so CO₂ keeps being converted and taken up.

### In the lungs

- HCO₃⁻ back into red blood cells; Cl⁻ out.
- H⁺ released from HHb; H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O (carbonic anhydrase).
- Carbaminohaemoglobin releases CO₂.
- CO₂ diffuses into alveoli; Hb affinity for O₂ rises; O₂ loads.

## Bohr shift

- **Describe**: high pCO₂ moves the curve **to the right**.
- **Explain**: more CO₂ → more H⁺ → haemoglobinic acid → Hb shape changes → **lower affinity** for O₂.
- **Importance**: more O₂ unloaded in actively respiring tissues (high CO₂); O₂ loads readily in lungs (low CO₂).

### Worked reminder: effect of the shift

At 3 kPa, saturation is 45 % (normal CO₂) and 32 % (high CO₂). Arriving at 97 %.

```
normal:   97 − 45 = 52 percentage points released
high CO₂: 97 − 32 = 65 percentage points released
extra = 13 percentage points of capacity
```

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Partial pressure (pO₂, pCO₂) | Measure of the amount of a gas present, in kPa |
| Percentage saturation | Proportion of haemoglobin binding sites carrying oxygen |
| Affinity | How readily haemoglobin binds oxygen |
| Oxyhaemoglobin | Haemoglobin combined with oxygen |
| Carbonic anhydrase | Enzyme in red blood cells catalysing CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ |
| Haemoglobinic acid | Haemoglobin combined with H⁺ |
| Carbaminohaemoglobin | Haemoglobin combined with CO₂ at its amine groups |
| Chloride shift | Movement of Cl⁻ into red blood cells as HCO₃⁻ leave |
| Bohr shift | Rightward movement of the dissociation curve at high pCO₂ |
| Sigmoid | S-shaped |

## Exam traps

- CO₂ does **not** bind to haem iron; carbaminohaemoglobin forms at amine groups.
- Bohr shift: "lower affinity for oxygen", not "CO₂ takes oxygen's place".
- The curve moves **right**, not down.
- Chloride ions move **into** red blood cells in tissues.
- Explaining the S-shape needs the shape change after the first O₂ binds.
- Plasma carries most CO₂ as HCO₃⁻, not as dissolved gas.
- Percentage points versus percentage of carried oxygen: read what the question asks.

## Self-test

1. How many oxygen molecules can one haemoglobin molecule carry?
2. Name the enzyme in red blood cells that speeds up the reaction of CO₂ with water.
3. Write the equation for the dissociation of carbonic acid.
4. State two effects of H⁺ combining with haemoglobin.
5. Name the compound formed when CO₂ combines directly with haemoglobin.
6. Describe the chloride shift.
7. Explain the importance of the chloride shift.
8. In what form is most CO₂ carried in plasma?
9. Explain why the dissociation curve is steep in the middle.
10. Why is the steep section important in respiring tissue?
11. Describe the Bohr shift.
12. Fully saturated blood carries 20 cm³ O₂ per 100 cm³. Saturation falls from 95 % to 70 %. Calculate the oxygen released per 100 cm³.

### Answers

1. Four.
2. Carbonic anhydrase.
3. H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
4. Oxyhaemoglobin releases oxygen; free H⁺ are removed, so blood pH does not fall much (buffering).
5. Carbaminohaemoglobin.
6. As HCO₃⁻ diffuse out of red blood cells into plasma, Cl⁻ diffuse in.
7. It keeps the cell electrically neutral so HCO₃⁻ can keep leaving, allowing more CO₂ to be converted and taken up.
8. Hydrogencarbonate ions in solution.
9. Once the first oxygen binds, haemoglobin changes shape and the other sites bind oxygen more easily.
10. A small fall in pO₂ causes a large fall in saturation, releasing a lot of oxygen.
11. At high pCO₂ the curve moves to the right: lower saturation at each pO₂.
12. 20 × (0.95 − 0.70) = **5.0 cm³**.
