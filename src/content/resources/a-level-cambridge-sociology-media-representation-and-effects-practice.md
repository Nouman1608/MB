These questions practise Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4, heading 10
*Media representation and effects* (10.1, 10.2 and 10.3). Paper 4 essays carry 35 marks and must
explore contrasting views; warm-ups check the core concepts.

## Warm-up questions

**W1.** Define *moral panic*. **[2]**

**W2.** Outline the hypodermic syringe model of media effects. **[3]**

**W3.** Explain **one** difference between the two-step flow model and the uses and gratifications
model. **[3]**

**W4.** Give **two** ways the media might have a positive effect on behaviour. **[2]**

### Warm-up answers

- **W1.** An exaggerated, media-amplified public reaction to a group or behaviour seen as a threat
  to social values, which often leads to calls for tougher control (Cohen, 1972).
- **W2.** The media inject messages directly into a passive, homogeneous audience, producing
  immediate and similar effects, for example imitation of violence; associated with Bandura's
  Bobo doll experiments. 1 mark per developed feature.
- **W3.** In the two-step flow (Katz and Lazarsfeld, 1955), messages are filtered by opinion
  leaders who influence others; in uses and gratifications (Blumler and McQuail, 1968), individuals
  actively select media to meet their own needs. The first stresses social networks, the second
  individual choice.
- **W4.** Public-health or road-safety campaigns; charity appeals increasing donations; positive
  role models reducing prejudice; pro-social children's programming.

## Essay 1 (10.1)

**"The media represents women in stereotypical ways." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**For:**
- Tuchman (1978) — symbolic annihilation; Mulvey (1975) — male gaze; Ferguson (1983) — "cult of
  femininity" in women's magazines; Wolf (1990) — beauty myth; McRobbie on *Jackie* — romance as the
  goal for girls.
- Women remain under-represented as experts and in news, and older women particularly so.
- Feminist explanations: male control of media industries; advertising's use of women's bodies.

**Against:**
- Gauntlett (2002): more diverse, assertive images of women; audiences select role models.
- Postmodernists: audiences construct identity from many images; stereotypes are increasingly
  parodied.
- New media allow women to produce their own content.

**Evaluation:** distinguish change in representation from continuing inequality in production;
compare different media and societies; reach a judgement.

## Essay 2 (10.1)

**"Moral panics are created by the media to sell news." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

- **For:** sensationalism attracts audiences; Cohen's Mods and Rockers (1972) shows exaggeration,
  prediction and symbolisation; panics around youth, migrants or welfare claimants sell papers and
  attract clicks.
- **Against/alternative explanations:** neo-Marxist view — Hall et al. (1978) saw moral panic as
  serving ruling-class interests in a crisis of hegemony, not merely sales; public concern may be
  genuine; social media mean panics can start with users, not journalists; some sociologists argue
  the concept is overused.
- **Evaluation:** commercial and ideological explanations can combine; consider class, gender,
  ethnic and age targets of panics.

## Essay 3 (10.2)

**"Audiences are active, not passive, users of the media." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**Supporting the view:** uses and gratifications (Blumler and McQuail); two-step flow (Katz and
Lazarsfeld); Hall's encoding/decoding (1973) and Morley's *Nationwide* study (1980); fan cultures
and user-generated content.

**Against the view:** hypodermic syringe evidence (Bandura); cultural effects — cumulative
influence on "common sense" (Gerbner, Glasgow University Media Group); normative model — media
shape what is seen as acceptable; algorithms on new media limit choice.

**Evaluation:** audiences may be active in interpretation but still constrained by what is
available; the strength of effects varies with age, education and social networks.

## Essay 4 (10.3)

**"Violent media leads to violent behaviour." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**For:**
- Bandura's Bobo doll experiments (1961, 1963) — imitation of observed aggression.
- Newson Report (1994) — concern that violent videos harm children.
- Desensitisation and cultivation arguments.

**Against:**
- Charlton's research on St Helena after television arrived in 1995 found no rise in anti-social
  behaviour.
- Gauntlett's "Ten things wrong with the media effects model" (1998): artificial methods, children
  treated as inadequate, violence defined inconsistently.
- Buckingham (1993): children distinguish fiction from reality.
- Correlation is not causation — violent people may choose violent media.

**Evaluation:** effects may be short-term and limited to some vulnerable individuals; social
factors such as family, peers and poverty explain more violence; reach a supported conclusion.

## Essay 5 (10.3)

**"Media sensationalism and stereotyping have a harmful effect on the groups they target."
Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**For:**
- Moral panics turn groups into folk devils (Cohen, 1972) and lead to tougher policing and
  punishment.
- Deviance amplification (Wilkins, 1964; Young, 1971): reaction increases the deviance it reports.
- Hall et al. (1978): racialised coverage of "mugging" shaped how Black youth were policed.
- Jones (2011): demonisation of working-class people justifies cuts to welfare.
- Labelling: stereotyped groups may internalise the label (self-fulfilling prophecy).

**Against:**
- Audiences decode stereotypes in negotiated or oppositional ways (Hall, 1973; Morley, 1980).
- Targeted groups use new media to produce counter-representations and challenge stereotypes.
- Some coverage raises awareness of injustice and leads to positive change.
- Other agencies (police, politicians, employers) may matter more than the media itself.

**Evaluation:** harm is likely where groups lack power to answer back; consider class, gender,
ethnic and age groups separately; reach a judgement on how far the media is the main cause.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I include methodological criticism?** Yes — evaluating experiments, content analysis and
audience studies is one of the most effective forms of AO3 in media essays.

**Can I write about social media in effects essays?** Yes. The syllabus asks about the extent to
which behaviour is influenced by the media generally, and new media evidence is relevant.

**How do I avoid a list of studies?** Group studies under each model or argument and explain what
each one shows about the question.
