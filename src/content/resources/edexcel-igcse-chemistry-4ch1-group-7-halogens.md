## Colours and states (2.5)

| Halogen | Element at room temperature | Colour in aqueous solution |
|---|---|---|
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | Pale yellow-green gas | Very pale green (almost colourless) |
| Bromine, Br₂ | Red-brown liquid; orange-brown vapour | Orange (yellow when dilute) |
| Iodine, I₂ | Grey-black solid; purple vapour on warming | Brown |

Learn both columns: questions about displacement describe the **solution** colours, while questions about the elements describe their appearance at room temperature.

## Explaining the physical trends

Down the group the colour darkens, and the melting and boiling points increase, so the state changes from gas to liquid to solid. The reason comes from covalent bonding (point 1.48): each halogen is a simple molecular substance made of diatomic molecules.

**Model answer.** Explain why bromine is a liquid at room temperature but chlorine is a gas. **[3]**

> Both are made of simple diatomic molecules with weak intermolecular forces between them [1]. Bromine molecules have a higher relative molecular mass (160 compared with 71), so the intermolecular forces between them are stronger [1]. More energy is needed to overcome them, so bromine's boiling point is above room temperature but chlorine's is below it [1].

The covalent bond inside each molecule is not broken when a halogen melts or boils.

## Predicting other halogens (2.6)

Fluorine is above chlorine and astatine below iodine. Extend each trend in the right direction.

**Worked example 1.** Predict the appearance of astatine at room temperature and whether it reacts with potassium iodide solution.

```
colour darkens down the group           -> darker than iodine: black
melting and boiling points increase     -> solid at room temperature
reactivity decreases down the group     -> less reactive than iodine
a less reactive halogen cannot displace a more reactive one
                                        -> no reaction with potassium iodide
```

## Displacement as evidence (2.7)

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide. The halide solutions (potassium chloride, potassium bromide, potassium iodide) are all colourless, so a colour change shows that a reaction has happened and which halogen has formed.

### Writing any displacement equation

1. Check that the halogen added is more reactive than the halogen in the halide. If not, write "no reaction".
2. Write the formulae: the halogen as X₂; the halide as KY or NaY.
3. Balance: one X₂ molecule reacts with two halide ions.
4. For the ionic equation, remove the metal ion, which is unchanged (a spectator ion).

**Worked example 2.** Bromine water is added to sodium iodide solution.

```
bromine above iodine -> bromine is more reactive -> reaction
Br2(aq) + 2NaI(aq) -> 2NaBr(aq) + I2(aq)
ionic:   Br2(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> 2Br-(aq) + I2(aq)
observation: colourless solution turns brown
```

Bromine gains electrons (it is reduced) and iodide ions lose electrons (they are oxidised). These terms come from point 2.20.

### Identifying an unknown halide

**Worked example 3.** A colourless solution contains either potassium chloride, potassium bromide or potassium iodide. A student adds chlorine water to one sample and bromine water to another.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Add chlorine water | Solution turns orange |
| Add bromine water | No change (stays orange-yellow from the added bromine water) |

```
chlorine reacts -> the halide is less reactive than chlorine: bromide or iodide
bromine does not react -> not iodide
orange colour = bromine formed
conclusion: potassium bromide
```

With bromine water, look for a **change** from the colour of the reagent; a solution that is already orange because bromine water was added has not reacted.

### Planning the experiment

**Model answer.** Describe how to use displacement reactions to show the order of reactivity of chlorine, bromine and iodine. **[6]**

> Put a few cm³ of potassium chloride, potassium bromide and potassium iodide solution into separate test tubes [1]. Add a few drops of chlorine water to the bromide and iodide solutions [1], bromine water to the chloride and iodide solutions [1], and iodine solution to the chloride and bromide solutions [1]. Look for a colour change: orange shows bromine formed and brown shows iodine formed [1]. Chlorine displaces both bromine and iodine, bromine displaces only iodine and iodine displaces neither, so the order is chlorine > bromine > iodine [1].

Use a fume cupboard for chlorine water and bromine water, because the halogens are toxic.

## Explaining the trend (2.8C) — Paper 2 only

A halogen atom reacts by **gaining one electron** to fill its outer shell (chlorine 2,8,7 → Cl⁻ 2,8,8). The more easily it attracts an extra electron, the more reactive it is.

**Model answer.** Explain why chlorine is more reactive than bromine. **[4]**

> A bromine atom has one more occupied shell than a chlorine atom (2,8,7), so its outer shell is further from the nucleus [1]. There is more shielding by inner electrons [1]. So the attraction between the nucleus and an incoming electron is weaker in bromine [1]. Bromine gains an electron less easily, so it is less reactive than chlorine [1].

The reasoning matches Group 1 (distance and shielding) but leads to the opposite trend, because metals lose electrons and halogens gain them.

## Common mistakes

- Giving iodine's appearance as "purple solid": the solid is grey-black; the vapour is purple.
- Confusing element colours with solution colours (bromine water is orange, not red-brown).
- Writing "chlorine displaces chloride" or including the same halogen in both reagents.
- Writing Br for bromine in an equation: halogens are diatomic, Br₂.
- Saying iodine is "more reactive because it is heavier".
- Explaining boiling points by "breaking bonds between atoms".

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a displacement: start colour, end colour and what has formed.
- **Explain** a displacement result: name the more reactive halogen and say it displaces the less reactive one.
- **Predict**: state the trend's direction first, then apply it.
- **Explain** the trend (Paper 2): distance, shielding, weaker attraction for an incoming electron.
