These questions cover section **4.1.1 A simple model of the atom, symbols, relative atomic mass, electronic
charge and isotopes** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards
and exams in 2018 onwards (version 1.1): spec points 4.1.1.1 to 4.1.1.7. This content is examined on Paper 1 at
Foundation and Higher Tier. Question 4 uses content the specification marks (HT only) and is labelled
**(Higher)**. Use a periodic table for atomic numbers.

Learn the content first in the [study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-atomic-structure/) and the
[revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-atomic-structure-revision-notes/). For questions on group
trends and the periodic table, use the [topic 4.1 practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-atomic-structure-practice/);
the questions below are different. The course hub is [AQA GCSE Chemistry](/boards/aqa/gcse/chemistry/) and the
[printable checklist](/checklists/aqa/gcse/chemistry/) lists every topic.

## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by **(a)** an atom and **(b)** a compound. **[2]**

**2.** Name the compounds with these formulae.

**(a)** MgCl₂ **[1]**
**(b)** K₂SO₄ **[1]**
**(c)** CaCO₃ **[1]**
**(d)** How many atoms are shown in the formula Ca(OH)₂? **[1]**

**3.** Balance these equations and write one new equation.

**(a)** __Li + __O₂ → __Li₂O **[1]**
**(b)** __Al + __Cl₂ → __AlCl₃ **[1]**
**(c)** Sodium reacts with water to form sodium hydroxide and hydrogen. Write the word equation and the balanced
symbol equation. **[3]**

**4.** (Higher) Chlorine water is added to sodium bromide solution. Bromine and sodium chloride form.

**(a)** Write the ionic equation for the reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the half equation for bromide ions forming bromine. **[2]**
**(c)** Write the half equation for a magnesium atom forming a magnesium ion, Mg²⁺. **[1]**

**5.** Classify each substance as an element, a compound or a mixture: air, sodium chloride, oxygen, sea water,
carbon dioxide, copper. **[3]**

**6.** A student is given four separation tasks.

**(a)** Obtain pure water from water-based ink. Name the technique and explain how it works. **[2]**
**(b)** Liquid A (boiling point 65 °C) and liquid B (boiling point 98 °C) mix completely. Name the technique that
separates them and explain why it works. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how to obtain copper sulfate crystals from copper sulfate solution. **[2]**
**(d)** Name the technique that shows whether a food colouring contains more than one dye. **[1]**

**7.** Models of the atom changed as new evidence was found.

**(a)** Give **two** differences between the plum pudding model and the nuclear model. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the results of the alpha particle scattering experiment led to the plum pudding model being
replaced. **[3]**
**(c)** State how Niels Bohr adapted the nuclear model, and what James Chadwick's work provided evidence for. **[2]**

**8.** The radius of an atom is about 0.1 nm.

**(a)** Write 0.1 nm in metres, in standard form. **[1]**
**(b)** The radius of a nucleus is about 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m. Calculate how many times larger the radius of the atom is
than the radius of the nucleus. **[2]**
**(c)** A museum builds a model atom with a radius of 5 m. Calculate the radius its nucleus should have, in mm. **[2]**
**(d)** State where almost all the mass of an atom is found. **[1]**

**9.** Give the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in each particle.

**(a)** ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺ **[1]**
**(b)** ³²₁₆S²⁻ **[1]**
**(c)** ³⁹₁₉K⁺ **[1]**
**(d)** An atom has 12 protons and 13 neutrons. Identify the element and give its mass number. **[1]**

**10.** Lithium has two isotopes: lithium-6 (7.5%) and lithium-7 (92.5%).

**(a)** Give one similarity and one difference between atoms of the two isotopes. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the relative atomic mass of lithium to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the answer is closer to 7 than to 6. **[1]**

**11.** A sample of magnesium contains 79% magnesium-24, 10% magnesium-25 and 11% magnesium-26.

**(a)** Calculate the relative atomic mass of magnesium to 1 decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Give the electronic structure of a magnesium atom and describe how it is drawn as a diagram. **[2]**
**(c)** A magnesium-26 atom loses two electrons to form an ion. Give the numbers of protons, neutrons and
electrons in the ion. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why magnesium-24 and magnesium-26 atoms have no overall charge. **[1]**

**12.** Electronic structures.

**(a)** Write the electronic structures of nitrogen, aluminium, argon and calcium. **[4]**
**(b)** An atom has the electronic structure 2,8,6. Identify the element and explain how you decided. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) The smallest part of an element that can exist [1]. (b) Two or more elements chemically combined in
fixed proportions [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Fixed proportions" and "chemically combined" are the creditworthy words; "two things
mixed together" describes a mixture and scores nothing.

**2. (a)** **Magnesium chloride** [1]
**(b)** **Potassium sulfate** [1]
**(c)** **Calcium carbonate** [1]
**(d)** 1 + 2 + 2 = **5 atoms** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Spelling of "sulfate" and "chloride" must be clear enough to be unambiguous; "chlorine"
for chloride is a different substance and is not credited.

**3. (a)** **4**Li + O₂ → **2**Li₂O [1]
**(b)** **2**Al + **3**Cl₂ → **2**AlCl₃ [1]
**(c)** sodium + water → sodium hydroxide + hydrogen [1]; correct formulae Na, H₂O, NaOH, H₂ [1];
**2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The balancing mark depends on correct formulae; changing H₂O to H₂O₂ to "make it balance"
loses both the formula and balancing marks.

**4. (a)** Cl₂ and Br⁻ on the left, Cl⁻ and Br₂ on the right, with Na⁺ omitted [1];
**Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂** [1]
**(b)** Correct species, 2Br⁻ → Br₂ [1]; electrons on the correct side: **2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻** [1]
**(c)** **Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Check charge as well as atoms: in (b) the left side is 2-, so the right must also be 2-,
which only works with 2e⁻ on the right.

**5.** Elements: oxygen and copper [1]. Compounds: sodium chloride and carbon dioxide [1]. Mixtures: air and sea
water [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs both substances in that category correct, so one misplaced substance can
cost two marks.

**6. (a)** **Simple distillation** [1]; water boils, the vapour is condensed and collected, and the dyes stay
in the flask [1].
**(b)** **Fractional distillation** [1]; the liquids have different boiling points, so A (lower boiling point)
reaches the top of the column and condenses first [1].
**(c)** Heat the solution to evaporate some water until it is saturated [1]; leave to cool so crystals form,
then filter and dry them [1].
**(d)** **Chromatography** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a) and (b) the technique name earns one mark and the explanation the other; a bare
technique name scores half.

**7. (a)** Any two differences, one mark each [1] [1]: positive charge spread through the atom vs concentrated
in a nucleus; mass spread out vs almost all in the nucleus; electrons embedded in the positive ball vs outside
the nucleus; no empty space vs mostly empty space.
**(b)** Some alpha particles were deflected through large angles [1]; a spread-out positive charge could not do
this, so the plum pudding model could not explain the evidence [1]; the results showed mass and positive charge
concentrated in a small nucleus, so the nuclear model replaced it [1].
**(c)** Bohr: electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances [1]. Chadwick: the existence of **neutrons** in
the nucleus [1].
*Examiner insight:* A "difference" mark needs both sides of the comparison; "the nuclear model has a nucleus"
alone states one model, not a difference.

**8. (a)** **1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m** [1]
**(b)** (1 × 10⁻¹⁰) ÷ (1 × 10⁻¹⁴) [1] = **10 000** (1 × 10⁴) times [1]
**(c)** 5 ÷ 10 000 = 0.0005 m [1] = **0.5 mm** [1]
**(d)** In the **nucleus** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c) the unit conversion carries the second mark; 0.0005 left in metres when mm were
asked for scores the method mark only.

**9. (a)** 13 protons, 14 neutrons, **10 electrons** [1]
**(b)** 16 protons, 16 neutrons, **18 electrons** [1]
**(c)** 19 protons, 20 neutrons, **18 electrons** [1]
**(d)** **Magnesium**, mass number **25** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs all three numbers right; the electron count for an ion is where the mark
is usually dropped.

**10. (a)** Similarity: same number of protons (3) or electrons [1]. Difference: lithium-7 has one more neutron
(4 compared with 3) [1].
**(b)** (6 × 7.5 + 7 × 92.5) ÷ 100 [1] = 6.925 = **6.9** [1]
**(c)** Lithium-7 is far more abundant, so it contributes more to the average [1].
*Examiner insight:* A correct answer with no working still scores both marks in (b), but a wrong answer with no
working scores zero; showing the expression protects the method mark.

**11. (a)** (24 × 79 + 25 × 10 + 26 × 11) ÷ 100 [1] = (1896 + 250 + 286) ÷ 100 = 24.32 [1] = **24.3** [1]
**(b)** **2,8,2** [1]; a nucleus with three shells drawn round it, carrying 2, 8 and 2 electrons from the inside
out [1]
**(c)** 12 protons and 14 neutrons [1]; **10 electrons** [1]
**(d)** Each atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons (12 each), so the charges cancel [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the abundance-weighted expression earns the method mark even if the arithmetic slips;
the final mark needs the 1 d.p. answer asked for.

**12. (a)** Nitrogen **2,5** [1]; aluminium **2,8,3** [1]; argon **2,8,8** [1]; calcium **2,8,8,2** [1]
**(b)** 2 + 8 + 6 = 16 electrons, so atomic number 16 [1]; the element is **sulfur** [1].
*Examiner insight:* For (b) the reasoning mark needs the link from electron total to atomic number; naming
sulfur with no reason scores one mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting atoms in a formula with brackets without multiplying everything inside the bracket.
- Changing a formula, rather than the number in front, to balance an equation.
- (Higher) Half equations with the electrons on the wrong side or with unbalanced charge.
- Calling air or sea water a compound: their parts are not chemically combined.
- Naming a separation technique but not saying which property it relies on.
- Comparing atomic models by describing only one model.
- Saying the scattering experiment showed electron shells: that was Bohr's adaptation.
- Leaving a converted length in metres when the question asks for millimetres.
- Adding electrons to positive ions instead of removing them.
- Dropping the divide-by-100 step, or rounding before the final line, in relative atomic mass.
