## Why an ionic compound must melt or dissolve

Electrolysis is the decomposition of a liquid by passing a direct current through it. The liquid is called the **electrolyte**. The two rods that dip into it are the **electrodes**.

An ionic solid contains positive and negative ions held in a lattice by strong electrostatic forces. The ions vibrate about fixed positions; they cannot drift through the solid, so no current flows. When the compound melts, or dissolves in water, the ions are free to move and the liquid conducts.

The specification names two misconceptions to avoid:

- Ionic solutions do **not** conduct because electrons move through them. The charge is carried by **ions** moving through the liquid. Electrons flow only in the external wires and are transferred at the electrode surfaces.
- An ionic solid does **not** fail to conduct because "electrons cannot move". It fails because its **ions** are fixed in place.

## C3.4a: which electrode, which product

The **cathode** is the electrode joined to the negative terminal of the power supply. The **anode** is joined to the positive terminal.

- **Cations** are positive ions. Opposite charges attract, so cations move to the cathode.
- **Anions** are negative ions. They move to the anode.

Metal ions and hydrogen ions are positive, so with inert electrodes **metals (or hydrogen) form at the cathode**. Non-metal ions such as chloride, bromide, iodide and oxide are negative, so **non-metals form at the anode**.

At the cathode, cations gain electrons. At the anode, anions lose electrons. Describing these changes as reduction and oxidation in terms of electrons is statement C3.3b, which is **Higher Tier only**.

## C3.4b: molten binary ionic compounds

A binary compound contains just two elements. When it is molten, the only ions present are the metal cation and the non-metal anion, so the products follow directly:

- cathode: the **metal**
- anode: the **non-metal** (halogens and oxygen form as diatomic molecules: Cl₂, Br₂, I₂, O₂)

For molten sodium chloride, the specification's own example, sodium metal forms at the cathode and chlorine gas at the anode.

### Worked example 1: predicting products

Predict the products when each molten compound is electrolysed with graphite electrodes.

| Molten compound | Ions present | Cathode | Anode |
|---|---|---|---|
| calcium iodide, CaI₂ | Ca²⁺, I⁻ | calcium | iodine |
| magnesium bromide, MgBr₂ | Mg²⁺, Br⁻ | magnesium | bromine |
| lithium oxide, Li₂O | Li⁺, O²⁻ | lithium | oxygen |

Step 1: split the compound into its ions. Step 2: send the cation to the cathode and the anion to the anode. Step 3: write each product as the element, with halogens and oxygen as diatomic molecules.

### Worked example 2: empirical formula from electrolysis (CM3.4i)

A molten metal iodide is electrolysed until it is used up. The cathode collects 0.80 g of calcium and 5.08 g of iodine is formed at the anode. Use Ar values Ca = 40 and I = 127 to find the empirical formula.

| | Ca | I |
|---|---|---|
| mass (g) | 0.80 | 5.08 |
| mass ÷ Ar | 0.80 ÷ 40 = 0.02 | 5.08 ÷ 127 = 0.04 |
| divide by smaller | 1 | 2 |

The empirical formula is **CaI₂**. This matches the charges: one Ca²⁺ balances two I⁻.

## C3.4c: competing reactions in aqueous solutions

When an ionic compound dissolves in water, the solution contains the compound's ions **and** a small number of hydrogen ions (H⁺) and hydroxide ions (OH⁻) from water. Two cations compete at the cathode and two anions compete at the anode. Only one product usually forms at each electrode.

**At the cathode (with inert electrodes):**

- Metal **below hydrogen** in reactivity (copper, silver): the **metal** is deposited.
- Metal **above hydrogen** in reactivity (sodium, potassium, magnesium): **hydrogen** is given off and the metal ions remain in solution.

**At the anode (with inert electrodes):**

- If **chloride, bromide or iodide** ions are present, the **halogen** forms.
- If no halide is present (for example sulfate or nitrate solutions), **oxygen** forms from hydroxide ions.

### Aqueous sodium chloride

Ions present: Na⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺, OH⁻.

- Cathode: sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so **hydrogen** gas forms.
- Anode: chloride is a halide, so **chlorine** gas forms. In a very dilute solution some oxygen can form as well.
- Left in solution: Na⁺ and OH⁻, which make **sodium hydroxide**. The solution near the cathode turns alkaline.

### Aqueous copper(II) sulfate

Ions present: Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻, H⁺, OH⁻.

- Cathode: copper sits below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it plates out as a pink-brown layer of **copper**.
- Anode: there is no halide, so bubbles of **oxygen** form.
- Left in solution: H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ (sulfuric acid). The blue colour fades as Cu²⁺ ions are removed, and the solution becomes acidic.

### Worked example 3: aqueous predictions

| Solution (inert electrodes) | Cathode | Reason | Anode | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| potassium bromide | hydrogen | K more reactive than H | bromine | halide present |
| copper(II) bromide | copper | Cu less reactive than H | bromine | halide present |
| sodium nitrate | hydrogen | Na more reactive than H | oxygen | no halide |
| lithium iodide | hydrogen | Li more reactive than H | iodine | halide present |

Bromine shows as an orange-brown colour around the anode and iodine as a brown colour; neither bubbles off as a gas from cold solution.

## C3.4d: half equations

Each electrode has its own half equation, which includes the electrons (e⁻) gained or lost. To build one:

1. Write the ion as the reactant and the element as the product.
2. Make the numbers of each atom match.
3. Add e⁻ to whichever side needs it until the total charges are equal.

| Product | Where | Half equation |
|---|---|---|
| chlorine | anode | 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ |
| sodium, from molten NaCl | cathode | Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na |
| oxygen, from aqueous solutions | anode | 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ |
| copper | cathode | Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu |
| hydrogen | cathode | 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ |

### Worked example 4: balancing and ratio (CM3.4i)

Write the half equations for molten calcium iodide and the overall equation.

- Cathode: Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ca
- Anode: 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻

Both half equations involve 2 electrons, so they add directly: **CaI₂ → Ca + I₂**.

When electron numbers differ, multiply first. In aqueous sodium nitrate, hydrogen forms with 2e⁻ per H₂ and oxygen with 4e⁻ per O₂. Doubling the hydrogen half equation gives 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂, so **two molecules of hydrogen form for every molecule of oxygen**. Gas volumes follow the same 2 : 1 ratio. If 46 cm³ of hydrogen is collected, you would expect **23 cm³** of oxygen. A slightly smaller reading is common because oxygen is a little more soluble in water than hydrogen.

## C3.4e and PAG C2: the technique

### Inert electrodes

Graphite and platinum are the usual inert electrodes: they conduct electricity without being changed themselves. A typical set-up for an aqueous solution:

- the solution in a beaker
- a pair of graphite rods, kept apart, wired to a low-voltage **d.c.** supply
- a small test tube over each rod, full of solution and upside down, so any gas pushes the liquid out

For a molten compound, the solid is heated in a crucible until it melts, with the electrodes dipped in. Chlorine and bromine are toxic, so work with them in a fume cupboard or in a well-ventilated room with small quantities.

### Testing the gases (PAG C2)

| Gas | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| hydrogen | hold a burning splint at the tube's mouth | goes out with a squeaky pop |
| oxygen | put a glowing splint into the gas | bursts back into flame |
| chlorine | hold moist blue litmus paper in the gas | goes red, then white as it is bleached |

### Non-inert electrodes

A non-inert electrode takes part in the reaction. The standard case is copper electrodes in copper(II) sulfate solution:

- Anode: the copper electrode **dissolves**. Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻. No oxygen forms.
- Cathode: copper is **deposited**. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.

Copper ions enter the solution at the anode as fast as they leave at the cathode, so the blue colour stays the same. The anode loses mass and the cathode gains the same mass.

### Worked example 5: copper electrode masses

| Electrode | Mass before (g) | Mass after (g) |
|---|---|---|
| cathode | 24.36 | 25.04 |
| anode | 26.10 | 25.42 |

Cathode change: 25.04 − 24.36 = **+0.68 g**. Anode change: 25.42 − 26.10 = **−0.68 g**. The two changes are equal in size, which is what the half equations predict: every copper atom that leaves the anode as Cu²⁺ is replaced by one deposited at the cathode. As a percentage, the cathode gained 0.68 ÷ 24.36 × 100 = **2.79%** of its starting mass.

For reliable masses, rinse each electrode gently with distilled water, dry it fully before reweighing, and avoid knocking off loose copper from the cathode.

## Common errors

- Saying electrons travel through the solution. Ions carry the charge in the electrolyte.
- Mixing up the electrodes. Cathode is negative and attracts cations.
- Writing "sodium" at the cathode for aqueous sodium chloride. Hydrogen forms because sodium is more reactive than hydrogen.
- Writing "sulfur" or "sulfur dioxide" at the anode for copper sulfate. Sulfate ions stay in solution; oxygen forms.
- Leaving the 2 off Cl₂. At the anode, the electrons belong on the right-hand side as products.
- Forgetting that copper electrodes change the anode reaction: the anode dissolves instead of giving oxygen.
- Describing the chlorine test with dry litmus. The paper must be damp.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why must an ionic compound be molten or dissolved to be electrolysed?**
In an ionic solid the ions are held in a lattice and cannot move, so no current flows. When the compound melts or dissolves, the ions are free to move and carry charge to the electrodes.

**What is produced when aqueous sodium chloride is electrolysed?**
Hydrogen forms at the cathode because sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, and chlorine forms at the anode because chloride ions are present. Sodium and hydroxide ions remain, so sodium hydroxide solution forms.

**What is produced at the anode for a sulfate or nitrate solution?**
With no halide ions present, oxygen forms at the anode from hydroxide ions in water. The sulfate or nitrate ions stay in solution, so naming sulfur or sulfur dioxide as the product is an error.

**Which electrode is the cathode?**
The cathode is the electrode joined to the negative terminal of the power supply, and it attracts positive ions (cations). The anode is joined to the positive terminal and attracts negative ions (anions).
