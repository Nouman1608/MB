## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demand-side policy | Policy that tries to change aggregate demand (AD) |
| Fiscal policy | Changes to government spending and taxation |
| Monetary policy | Changes to the price, availability or quantity of money and credit |
| Reflationary | Raises AD (used with spare capacity or high unemployment) |
| Deflationary | Lowers AD (used when demand-pull inflation is above target) |
| Budget deficit | Government spending greater than tax revenue in a year |
| Policy rate | The interest rate the central bank sets |
| Quantitative easing (QE) | Central bank creates money to buy assets, mainly government bonds |
| Lender of last resort | Central bank lends to banks that cannot raise funds elsewhere |

## Map of the instruments

| | Reflationary | Deflationary |
|---|---|---|
| Spending | Increase | Cut |
| Taxation | Cut | Raise |
| Interest rate | Cut | Raise |
| QE | Buy assets | Stop buying; in principle sell |
| Lending criteria | Loosen (higher loan-to-value) | Tighten (lower loan-to-value) |
| Reserve asset requirement | Lower | Higher |

The first two rows are fiscal. The last four are monetary.

## Transmission chains (learn these as sequences)

**Interest rate cut:** lower cost of borrowing, so more consumption and investment; lower payments on variable debt, so more disposable income; weaker incentive to save; possibly lower exchange rate, so higher net exports. Result: AD up, real GDP up, price level up.

**Government spending rise:** injection into the circular flow; multiplier raises income; more tax revenue partly offsets cost.

**Tax cut:** disposable income up; part is saved or spent on imports; smaller first-round injection than a spending rise of the same cost.

**QE:** central bank buys bonds; bond prices up, yields down; cheaper long-term borrowing; more bank reserves; sellers buy other assets, raising their prices; wealth effect on spending.

**Tighter lending criteria:** fewer borrowers qualify; less credit-fuelled spending, especially on housing.

## Formulae and quick calculations

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Multiplier | 1 / (mps + mpt + mpm), the reciprocal of the marginal rate of withdrawal |
| Change in income | Initial injection × multiplier |
| Real interest rate | Nominal rate − inflation rate |
| Inflation rate | (new index − old index) / old index × 100 |
| Bond yield | Annual coupon / bond price × 100 |
| Budget balance | Tax revenue − government spending (negative means deficit) |
| Maximum deposits from reserves | Reserves / required ratio |

### Method: multiplier question

1. Add up the withdrawal rates and invert.
2. Multiply the injection by the multiplier.
3. If the question concerns a tax cut, first multiply by the share of extra income spent at home.
4. If asked about the budget, subtract tax revenue gained (tax rate × change in income).

**Worked reminder.** Country B has mps 0.2, mpt 0.1, mpm 0.2. Multiplier = 1 / 0.5 = 2. A spending rise of 12bn raises income by 24bn. Tax revenue rises by 0.1 × 24 = 2.4bn. Net cost: 9.6bn.

### Method: inflation versus target

1. Calculate the inflation rate from the index.
2. Compare with the target.
3. Say which direction policy should move, and name the instrument.

**Worked reminder.** Index 108.0 then 111.2: (111.2 − 108.0) / 108.0 = 2.96%, about 3.0%. Against a 2% target the central bank would consider raising the rate. Next year's index at target: 111.2 × 1.02 = 113.42.

## Central banks: four roles

1. Implement monetary policy (policy rate, QE, reserve or lending rules).
2. Work towards an inflation target.
3. Banker to the government (accounts, payments, help with issuing debt).
4. Banker to the banks and lender of last resort.

Link the roles: the lender-of-last-resort role protects confidence in the banking system, but moral hazard is the cost.

## Strengths and weaknesses

| Tool | Best point | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal | Direct, can be targeted, multiplier | Lags, deficit and debt, crowding out |
| Interest rates | Fast, reversible, central bank decides | Weak response when confidence is low; blunt; floor near zero |
| QE | Possible when rates are already very low | Uncertain pass-through; may mostly lift asset prices |
| Lending criteria | Targets credit directly | Borrowers may use other lenders |
| Reserve requirements | Limits lending capacity | Banks hold spare reserves; demand for loans may be weak |

## More worked reminders

**Output gap and spending needed.** Country C has potential output of 330bn and actual output of 312bn. The gap is 18bn, which is 18/330 = 5.45% of potential. With a multiplier of 2.5, the spending rise needed is 18 / 2.5 = 7.2bn.

**QE and yields.** A bond pays 5 a year. At a price of 80 the yield is 6.25%. If central bank buying lifts the price to 100, the yield falls to 5.0%. Say it in words: higher price, lower yield, cheaper long-term borrowing.

**Lending criteria.** A flat costs 150,000. A 90% limit gives a loan of 135,000 and a deposit of 15,000. At 75% the loan is 112,500 and the deposit 37,500. Tighter criteria mean a larger deposit, so fewer first-time buyers qualify.

**Reserve asset requirement.** New reserves of 8bn support at most 8 / 0.10 = 80bn of deposits at 10%, and 8 / 0.16 = 50bn at 16%. Raising the ratio reduces the ceiling on lending.

## Fiscal versus monetary: head-to-head

| Feature | Fiscal | Monetary |
|---|---|---|
| Decision maker | Government | Central bank (usually) |
| Speed of decision | Slow (budgets, legislation) | Fast (regular meetings) |
| Speed of impact | Spending can be slow to start | Rate changes feed through over months |
| Targeting | Can target groups, regions, sectors | Mostly economy-wide |
| Side effect | Deficit and debt | Asset prices, exchange rate |
| Political pressure | High | Lower if the central bank is independent |

Use this table to build a judgement paragraph, not to replace one. A table row is a point. A paragraph says why that point matters in the case given.

## Policy mix

Governments and central banks can pull in the same direction or in opposite ones. If both are reflationary, AD rises strongly, and the risk is overheating when there is little spare capacity. If fiscal policy is deflationary (to cut a deficit) while monetary policy is reflationary (to protect growth), the central bank may be offsetting the budget. This is why a good answer asks whether the instruments are being used together.

## Evaluation prompts

- How much spare capacity is there? (Reflation raises output if yes, prices if no.)
- How large are the time lags?
- How high is existing government debt?
- Is the problem demand-side at all?
- How close are interest rates to zero?
- Who gains and loses (savers, borrowers, asset owners, taxpayers)?
- Short run or long run?

## Quick self-test

1. Which two policies make up demand-side policy, and who normally sets each?
2. Name two reflationary monetary instruments.
3. A bond with a coupon of 6 a year has a price of 120. What is the yield?
4. Nominal rate 5.5%, inflation 2.0%. What is the real rate?
5. Withdrawals: mps 0.1, mpt 0.2, mpm 0.1. What is the multiplier?
6. In question 5, how much does income rise for a 5bn spending increase?
7. State one weakness of QE.
8. A bank holds 9bn of liquid assets against 60bn of deposits. The requirement rises to 20%. By how much is it short?
9. Why might a rate cut fail to raise investment?
10. State one reason moral hazard follows from a lender of last resort.

### Answers

1. Fiscal (government) and monetary (central bank, usually).
2. Any two: cutting the policy rate, QE, looser lending criteria, lower reserve asset requirements.
3. 6 / 120 × 100 = 5.0%.
4. 5.5 − 2.0 = 3.5%.
5. 1 / 0.4 = 2.5.
6. 5 × 2.5 = 12.5bn.
7. Any one: unclear pass-through to lending; raises asset prices and favours asset owners; hard to reverse.
8. Required 0.20 × 60 = 12bn; shortfall 12 − 9 = 3bn.
9. Firms expect weak demand, so they do not invest even when borrowing is cheap.
10. Banks may take larger risks if they expect to be rescued.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "interest rates fall so AD rises" with no chain of reasoning.
- Mixing up reflationary and deflationary.
- Using the word "deflation" for deflationary policy.
- Giving one-sided answers when asked to evaluate.
- Quoting a multiplier without the withdrawal rates that make it.
- Forgetting the real versus nominal difference.
- Describing QE as printing notes.
- Ignoring the state of the economy (spare capacity, debt, how low rates already are).
- Listing the central bank's roles without saying what each does.
- Judging the policy without a reason tied to the context.
