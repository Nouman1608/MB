These questions cover section 2.7, The labour market, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. The specification is not tiered. All workers, markets and tax rules are invented and are **not** real UK rates. ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** In a labour market, state who supplies labour and who demands it. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by derived demand for labour, using an example. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** factor that could increase the supply of labour to an occupation. **[2]**

**4.** A worker is paid £11.80 an hour for a 35-hour week, plus 4 hours of overtime at time and a half. Calculate the worker's gross pay for the week. **[2]**

**5.** **(a)** Define net pay. **[1]**
**(b)** State the deduction from gross pay that is not paid to the government. **[1]**

**6.** At a wage of £18 an hour, employers want 300 lab technicians but only 220 are willing to work. Explain what is likely to happen to the wage. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** The table shows the labour market for software testers in an invented city.

| Hourly wage (£) | Demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | 900 | 500 |
| 16 | 800 | 600 |
| 17 | 700 | 700 |
| 18 | 600 | 800 |
| 19 | 500 | 900 |

New software automates some testing, so demand for testers falls by 200 at every wage.

**(a)** State the original equilibrium wage and employment. **[1]**
**(b)** State the new equilibrium wage and employment. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in the wage and in employment. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain who gains and who loses from this change. **[2]**

**8.** In Country C (invented), these rules apply:

- Income tax: first £10,000 tax-free; 20% on income from £10,000 to £40,000; 40% above £40,000.
- National insurance: 12% on income from £10,000 to £40,000; 2% above £40,000.
- Pension: 4% of gross pay.

A worker earns a gross salary of £45,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the worker's income tax. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the worker's national insurance. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the worker's pension contribution. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the worker's annual and monthly net pay. **[2]**

**9.** The worker in question 8 receives a pay rise of £2,000.

**(a)** Calculate the increase in net pay. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why net pay rises by less than gross pay. **[2]**

**10.** Using supply and demand, explain why a qualified accountant usually earns more than a fast-food worker. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a trade union can affect the interaction between workers and employers. **[3]**

**12.** A country has a shortage of lorry drivers. Analyse how wages and employment in this labour market are likely to change, and how the shortage might ease over time. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Workers (households) supply labour [1]; employers (firms and the government) demand it [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In product markets households buy, but here they are the sellers.

**2.** Labour is demanded because of the demand for the product it helps to make [1]. For example, if more people eat out, restaurants need more chefs [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The example must show the link from product demand to labour demand.

**3.** For example, a fall in the cost or length of training [1] means more people can qualify, so more are willing and able to work in the job at each wage [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Better conditions, lower pay in other jobs or migration are also accepted, if developed.

**4.** Basic pay = 35 × 11.80 = £413.00; overtime = 4 × (11.80 × 1.5) = 4 × 17.70 = £70.80 [1]. Gross pay = **£483.80** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Time and a half means 1.5 times the hourly rate, not an extra half-hour.

**5. (a)** Pay after deductions such as income tax, national insurance and pension contributions [1].
**(b)** The pension contribution [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The pension is still a deduction from gross pay, even though the worker keeps it as savings.

**6.** There is a shortage of 80 technicians [1], so employers compete for the scarce workers and the wage rises until demand and supply are equal [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the size of the shortage to show application.

**7. (a)** £17 an hour and 700 testers [1].
**(b)** £16 an hour and 600 testers [1].
**(c)** Wage: (16 − 17) ÷ 17 × 100 = **−5.9%** [1]. Employment: (600 − 700) ÷ 700 × 100 = **−14.3%** [1].
**(d)** Employers gain because they pay lower wages and need fewer testers [1]. Testers lose: 100 fewer are employed and those who remain earn less per hour [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* New demand at £16 is 800 − 200 = 600, matching supply of 600.

**8. (a)** (40,000 − 10,000) × 0.20 = £6,000; (45,000 − 40,000) × 0.40 = £2,000 [1]. Total = **£8,000** [1].
**(b)** (30,000 × 0.12) + (5,000 × 0.02) = 3,600 + 100 = **£3,700** [1].
**(c)** 45,000 × 0.04 = **£1,800** [1].
**(d)** 45,000 − 8,000 − 3,700 − 1,800 = **£31,500** a year [1]; 31,500 ÷ 12 = **£2,625** a month [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Applying 40% to the whole £45,000 gives £18,000 of tax and loses both marks in (a).

**9. (a)** All £2,000 is above £40,000: extra tax 800, extra NI 40, extra pension 80, total £920 [1]. Net pay rises by 2,000 − 920 = **£1,080** [1].
**(b)** Each extra pound is taxed at the higher rate and also has national insurance and pension taken from it [1], so the worker keeps only part of the rise, here 54% [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out only the extra deductions on the extra £2,000; there is no need to repeat the whole calculation.

**10.** Supply of accountants is limited because the job needs years of study and professional exams [1], while many people can work in fast food with little training, so supply is large [1]. Demand for accountants is high because their work is valuable to firms [1]. Low supply relative to high demand gives accountants a higher equilibrium wage [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both supply and demand, and compare the two jobs rather than describing one.

**11.** A trade union bargains on behalf of many workers at once (collective bargaining) [1]. This gives workers more bargaining power than each would have alone [1], so the union may win higher wages or better conditions from employers, though employers may then hire fewer workers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The final mark rewards a consequence of the stronger bargaining position.

**12.** At the current wage, demand for drivers exceeds supply [1], so firms compete for drivers and offer higher wages [1]. The higher wage causes an extension in supply as more qualified drivers return to the job [1], and employment rises towards the new equilibrium [1]. Over time, the higher wage encourages more people to train as drivers, shifting supply to the right [1], which eases the shortage and slows further wage rises; firms might also offer training or better conditions to attract workers [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the short run (movement along supply) from the long run (supply shifts as people train).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling households the buyers in the labour market.
- Shifting curves for a wage change.
- Applying the higher tax rate to the whole income.
- Forgetting the pension in net-pay calculations.
- Mixing up annual and monthly figures.
- Pay comparisons that ignore either supply or demand.
