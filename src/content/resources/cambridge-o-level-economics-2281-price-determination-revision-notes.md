Quick recall for section 2.5 **Price determination** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: market equilibrium, and market disequilibrium with "shortages (demand exceeding supply) and surpluses (supply exceeding demand)". Full explanations are in the [price determination study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-price-determination/); test yourself with the [price determination practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-price-determination-practice/). For supply on its own, see the [supply revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-supply-revision-notes/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Equilibrium | A position with no tendency to change |
| Market equilibrium | Quantity demanded equals quantity supplied |
| Equilibrium price | The price at which the market clears; also called the market-clearing price |
| Equilibrium sales | The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price |
| Disequilibrium price | Any price at which quantity demanded and quantity supplied differ |
| Shortage (excess demand) | Quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied; happens below equilibrium price |
| Surplus (excess supply) | Quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded; happens above equilibrium price |
| Total revenue at equilibrium | Equilibrium price × equilibrium sales; equal to consumers' total spending |

## Three ways to find equilibrium

1. **Schedule**: find the row where the two quantities match.
2. **Diagram**: find where D crosses S; read across to the price axis (Pe) and down to the quantity axis (Qe).
3. **Equations**: set Qd = Qs, solve for P, then substitute into both equations to check Q.

Example: Qd = 120 − 10P and Qs = 20P − 30. Then 120 − 10P = 20P − 30, so 150 = 30P and P = 5. Q = 120 − 50 = 70, and 100 − 30 = 70 checks it.

## Disequilibrium in one table

| Price ($) | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qd | 90 | 80 | 70 | 60 | 50 |
| Qs | 30 | 50 | 70 | 90 | 110 |
| Gap | Shortage 60 | Shortage 30 | 0 | Surplus 30 | Surplus 60 |
| Quantity sold | 30 | 50 | 70 | 60 | 50 |

Rules to remember:

- **Size of the gap** = larger quantity − smaller quantity at that price.
- **Quantity actually sold** at a disequilibrium price = the **smaller** of Qd and Qs. Buyers cannot buy more than is offered; sellers cannot sell more than is wanted.
- Sales are highest at equilibrium. Any other price means fewer units change hands.
- The gap grows the further the price is from equilibrium.

## Adjustment: what moves and what does not

| Starting point | Signal | Price | Demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Surplus | Unsold stock | Falls | Extends (move down D) | Contracts (move down S) |
| Shortage | Queues, sell-outs | Rises | Contracts (move up D) | Extends (move up S) |

Neither curve shifts during adjustment. A shift only happens when a condition of demand or supply changes, which is section 2.6.

Adjustment is fast where prices are easy to change (auctions, street markets) and slow where prices are printed, set by contract or fixed by law.

## Signs of each in a real market

- **Shortage**: queues, waiting lists, sold-out notices, limits per customer, buyers paying above the asking price.
- **Surplus**: rising stock, discounts and clearance sales, perishable goods wasted, firms cutting next period's output.

## Diagram checklist

- Axes labelled Price and Quantity (with a time period).
- D slopes down, S slopes up, both labelled.
- Pe and Qe marked with dashed lines from the crossing point.
- For disequilibrium: a horizontal line at the given price, with Qd and Qs marked where it meets each curve, and the gap labelled "shortage" or "surplus".

## Exam traps

- Swapping shortage and surplus. Low price, shortage; high price, surplus.
- Giving one quantity instead of the gap.
- Saying the demand curve shifts when price falls. It is an extension along the curve.
- Assuming equilibrium means everyone who wants the good gets it. Only those willing and able to pay the equilibrium price buy.
- Calculating revenue at a disequilibrium price with the larger quantity. Use the quantity actually sold.

## Quick self-test

1. Define equilibrium price.
2. In the table above, what is the equilibrium price and quantity?
3. At $4, state the type and size of the disequilibrium.
4. How many units are sold at $7? Explain why.
5. Calculate sellers' total revenue at $3 and at equilibrium.
6. A price is above equilibrium. Describe what happens to price, quantity demanded and quantity supplied as the market adjusts.
7. Solve Qd = 200 − 5P and Qs = 15P − 40 for the equilibrium price and quantity.
8. Give two signs that a market has a shortage.
9. Why are sales lower at any disequilibrium price than at equilibrium?
10. During adjustment from a surplus, does the supply curve shift? Explain.
11. Why might a surplus of fresh fish be cleared more quickly than a surplus of cars?

### Answers

1. The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears and there is no tendency for price to change.
2. Price $5, quantity 70.
3. A shortage of 80 − 50 = 30 units.
4. 50 units. There is a surplus, so sales are limited to the quantity buyers demand.
5. At $3: 3 × 30 = $90. At equilibrium: 5 × 70 = $350.
6. Sellers cut the price to clear stock; quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until they are equal at the equilibrium price.
7. 200 − 5P = 15P − 40, so 240 = 20P and P = 12. Q = 200 − 60 = 140 (check: 180 − 40 = 140).
8. Queues or waiting lists; goods selling out; limits per customer; buyers offering above the asking price (any two).
9. Only the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied can be traded, and that is below the equilibrium quantity.
10. No. Only the price changes, so quantity supplied contracts along the existing curve.
11. Fish is perishable, so sellers must cut the price quickly or lose the stock; cars can be stored, so sellers can wait before cutting prices.
