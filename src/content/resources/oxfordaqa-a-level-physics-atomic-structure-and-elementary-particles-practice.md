These questions cover sections 3.3.1 and 3.3.2 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification, Version 4.4. The content is shared International AS and A-level. Use these data where a question needs them: proton charge 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; proton and neutron mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg each; electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg; 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J; electron rest energy 0.511 MeV; proton rest energy 938.3 MeV.

## Questions

**1.** A neutral atom of copper has proton number 29 and nucleon number 63. Give the proton, neutron and electron counts for a copper ion of charge +2e with the same nucleus. **[3]**

**2.** The nucleus of phosphorus-31 has proton number 15. Calculate its specific charge and compare it with that of the proton. **[4]**

**3.** Describe the Rutherford alpha-particle scattering experiment and explain what its results show about the atom. **[6]**

**4.** Explain how the results of the scattering experiment led physicists to drop the model with positive charge smeared across the whole atom. **[4]**

**5.**

**(a)** Calculate the mass of an electron as a fraction of the mass of a proton. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the specific charge of an electron is about 1800 times that of a proton. **[2]**

**6.**

**(a)** Name the antiparticles of the electron and of the neutrino. **[2]**
**(b)** State the charge and the rest energy, in MeV, of an antiproton. **[2]**

**7.** An electron and a positron, each with kinetic energy 1.20 MeV, collide head-on and annihilate to produce two photons.

**(a)** Calculate the energy of each photon in MeV. **[3]**
**(b)** Give the photon energy in joules. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why two photons are produced rather than one. **[1]**

**8.** A photon of energy 2.00 MeV passes close to a nucleus and produces an electron-positron pair.

**(a)** Calculate the total kinetic energy of the pair and, if shared equally, of each particle. **[3]**
**(b)** State why the same photon could not produce a proton-antiproton pair. **[1]**
**(c)** State the net charge of the pair and why this is consistent with the photon. **[1]**

**9.** A neutron and an antineutron at rest annihilate to form two photons. The neutron rest energy is 939.6 MeV.

**(a)** Calculate the total energy released, in MeV, and the energy of each photon. **[3]**
**(b)** Convert the energy of one photon to joules. **[1]**
**(c)** The Planck constant is 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. Calculate the frequency of one photon. **[2]**
**(d)** State the minimum energy of a photon able to produce a neutron-antineutron pair. **[1]**

**10.** In a scattering investigation, 30 000 alpha particles were counted at different angles.

| Deflection angle | Particles counted |
|---|---|
| 0° to 5° | 29 120 |
| 5° to 30° | 810 |
| 30° to 90° | 65 |
| Above 90° | 5 |

**(a)** Calculate the fraction deflected through more than 90°, and write it as "1 in N". **[2]**
**(b)** State what the counts at the smallest angles show about the atom. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what the particles deflected above 90° show about the nucleus. **[2]**
**(d)** Predict how many of a further 12 000 alpha particles would exceed 90°, and explain why the count may differ. **[2]**

**11.** A chloride ion has proton number 17, nucleon number 35 and charge −e.

**(a)** State its numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate its specific charge, including the mass of the electrons. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the specific charge of the ion with nucleon number 37 and the same charge, and explain why it differs. **[2]**

**12.** A helium-4 nucleus (charge +2e) and a hydrogen-2 nucleus (charge +e) have equal specific charges.

**(a)** Show that the hydrogen-2 nucleus has specific charge 4.79 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the two values are equal. **[1]**
**(c)** State two other properties that distinguish the two nuclei, with values. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Protons = 29 [1]. Neutrons = 63 − 29 = 34 [1]. Electrons = 29 − 2 = 27 [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers usually earn credit for showing the subtraction A − Z and the electron count adjusted for the ion charge, not the neutral atom.

**2.** Charge = 15 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁸ C [1]. Mass = 31 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 5.18 × 10⁻²⁶ kg [1]. Specific charge = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁸ ÷ 5.18 × 10⁻²⁶ = **4.64 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹** [1]. The proton's is 9.58 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹, so the nucleus's value is slightly less than half of it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A compare question needs a stated relationship, such as "half", backed by both numbers, and units must be on the final value.

**3.** A beam of alpha particles from a source is aimed at a thin gold foil [1], in a vacuum so air does not absorb or scatter them [1], with a movable detector counting particles at different angles [1]. Most went straight on with little deflection, which shows the atom is largely empty [1]. A few were deflected through large angles, so a small region of positive charge repels the alpha particles [1]. A very few rebounded, so that region, the nucleus, is also very massive [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Write each observation next to the conclusion it supports; a block of observations followed by a block of conclusions tends to lose that link.

**4.** The earlier model had positive charge spread through the atom with electrons embedded [1]. That model predicts only small deflections of alpha particles [1]. The observation of large-angle deflections and some rebounds contradicted it [1]. A small, dense, positive nucleus explained the results, so the nuclear model replaced it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers state what the old model predicted before saying what was observed, because "contradicted" needs both sides.

**5.** **(a)** 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ ÷ 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ [1] = **5.45 × 10⁻⁴** [1]
**(b)** The charges have equal magnitude [1]. The electron mass is about 1/1800 of the proton mass, so charge ÷ mass is about 1800 times larger [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* For an explain question, say which quantity is equal and which is different; "because it is lighter" alone is incomplete.

**6.** **(a)** Positron [1] and antineutrino [1].
**(b)** Charge −1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, relative charge −1 [1]. Rest energy **938.3 MeV**, the same as the proton [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving the charge as a sign only is risky; give it in coulombs or as a relative charge.

**7.** **(a)** Each particle has 0.511 + 1.20 = 1.711 MeV [1]. Total = 3.422 MeV [1]. Equal and opposite photons: **1.71 MeV each** [1].
**(b)** 1.711 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = **2.74 × 10⁻¹³ J** [1]
**(c)** Momentum must be conserved, and a single photon would leave non-zero total momentum [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is using only the kinetic energy or only the rest energy; show both added before you halve.

**8.** **(a)** Energy for the pair's rest energy = 2 × 0.511 = 1.022 MeV [1]. Kinetic energy = 2.00 − 1.022 = **0.978 MeV** [1]. Each: **0.489 MeV** [1].
**(b)** A proton-antiproton pair needs at least 1876.6 MeV, far above 2.00 MeV [1].
**(c)** The pair has zero net charge, the same as the photon, so charge is conserved [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Not enough energy" is a stronger answer when it is compared with a stated threshold value.

**9.** **(a)** Total = 2 × 939.6 = **1879.2 MeV** [1]. Each photon = **939.6 MeV** [1], because the particles are at rest and the photons have equal energy [1].
**(b)** 939.6 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = **1.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ J** [1]
**(c)** f = E ÷ h = 1.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ [1] = **2.27 × 10²³ Hz** [1]
**(d)** **1879.2 MeV** [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert MeV to joules before using E = hf; mixing MeV with the Planck constant in joule seconds gives an answer wrong by a factor of 10¹³.

**10.** **(a)** 5 ÷ 30 000 = **1.67 × 10⁻⁴** [1], which is **1 in 6000** [1].
**(b)** Nearly all particles (29 120 out of 30 000) were barely deflected [1], so most of the atom is empty space [1].
**(c)** Large deflection needs a strong repulsive force from a concentrated positive charge [1]. Rebounds need the target to be massive, so the nucleus is small, dense and positive [1].
**(d)** 12 000 × 5 ÷ 30 000 = **2** [1]. Scattering is random, so a small count varies from run to run [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the table when you interpret data, and note that tiny counts are subject to chance.

**11.** **(a)** Protons 17, neutrons 35 − 17 = 18, electrons 17 + 1 = 18 [1] [1] [1].
**(b)** Charge = −1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C [1]. Mass = 35 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ + 18 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 5.85 × 10⁻²⁶ kg [1]. Specific charge = **−2.74 × 10⁶ C kg⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ (37 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = **2.59 × 10⁶ C kg⁻¹** (magnitude) [1]. Same charge but two extra neutrons give a larger mass [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Keeping the negative sign and showing that electron mass barely changes the value both help; a bare unsigned number loses the sign.

**12.** **(a)** Specific charge = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ (2 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) [1] = **4.79 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** Each has a charge-to-nucleon ratio of 1 : 2, so charge ÷ mass is the same [1].
**(c)** Charge differs: +3.20 × 10⁻¹⁹ C for helium-4 and +1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C for hydrogen-2 [1]. Mass differs: 6.68 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and 3.34 × 10⁻²⁷ kg [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" needs the unrounded working followed by the stated value; here that is the division line then 4.79 × 10⁷.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using A as the neutron count instead of A − Z.
- Ignoring the sign of the specific charge for negative ions and electrons.
- Using Z protons for an ion's charge instead of the net charge.
- Listing Rutherford observations without linking each to its conclusion.
- Writing "attraction" when the alpha particle is repelled by the nucleus.
- Using a single rest energy as the pair-production threshold.
- Leaving energies in MeV when joules are requested, or using MeV with the Planck constant.
- Giving a vague "not enough energy" with no threshold figure.
