This guide goes deeper into **3.1.11.2 Determination of rate equation** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification. The specification states that "the rate equation is an experimentally determined relationship" and that orders can provide information about the mechanism. You must be able to use concentration–time graphs to deduce the rate of a reaction, use initial concentration–time data to deduce an initial rate, use rate–concentration data or graphs to deduce the order (0, 1 or 2) with respect to a reactant, derive the rate equation from the orders, and use the orders to provide information about the rate-determining (rate-limiting) step. Required practical 8 asks you to measure rate by an initial rate method and by a continuous monitoring method. This is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 4, with practical skills also tested in Unit 5.

The [rate equations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-rate-equations/) covers the whole of section 3.1.11. This page uses one reaction, the acid-catalysed iodination of propanone, to work through every skill in the point, and then adds a second example for the mechanism link. Then use the [determination of rate equation revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-11-2-revision-notes/) and the [determination of rate equation practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-11-2-practice/). For the definitions and the Arrhenius equation, see [rate equations (3.1.11.1)](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-11-1/).

## The reaction used throughout

```
CH₃COCH₃(aq) + I₂(aq) → CH₃COCH₂I(aq) + HI(aq)        catalysed by H⁺(aq)
```

Iodine is brown in solution and the other species are colourless, so the reaction can be followed by the fading colour. The rate equation found by experiment is

```
Rate = k[CH₃COCH₃][H⁺]
```

Iodine does not appear, although it is a reactant, and H⁺ does appear, although it is a catalyst and is not used up. Neither fact could be predicted from the balanced equation. That is why the rate equation must be determined experimentally. The data in the worked examples below are illustrative values chosen to match this rate equation.

## Skill 1: rate from a concentration–time graph

The rate at any moment is the **gradient** of the concentration–time graph at that time, with the sign made positive for a reactant.

- If the graph is a **straight line**, the rate is constant and the gradient can be read directly.
- If it is a **curve**, draw a **tangent** at the chosen time and find its gradient from a large triangle.

### Worked example 1: a straight-line graph

Propanone and acid are in large excess, so their concentrations barely change. [I₂] is measured with a colorimeter:

| t / s | 0 | 60 | 120 | 180 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [I₂] / mol dm⁻³ | 2.00 × 10⁻³ | 1.70 × 10⁻³ | 1.40 × 10⁻³ | 1.10 × 10⁻³ |

[I₂] falls by 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ every 60 s: the graph is a straight line.

```
rate = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ / 60 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
```

The rate does not change as [I₂] falls, so the reaction is **zero order with respect to iodine**. A straight-line concentration–time graph for a reactant always means zero order for that reactant.

For first- and second-order reactants the graph curves, becoming less steep as the reactant is used up, and the tangent method is needed.

## Skill 2: initial rate from initial concentration–time data

The **initial rate** is the gradient of the tangent at t = 0. In practice, measurements over a short time at the start give it, because the curve is nearly straight there.

In a **clock reaction**, each run is timed until a fixed small amount of product has formed. The initial rate is then proportional to **1/t**. If the volumes are adjusted so the total volume stays constant, the concentration of a reactant is proportional to the volume of its solution used.

### Worked example 2: order from clock times

Three runs use different volumes of a reactant solution R, with water added to keep the total volume fixed:

| Volume of R / cm³ | 10.0 | 20.0 | 30.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time to colour change / s | 84 | 42 | 28 |
| 1/t / s⁻¹ | 0.0119 | 0.0238 | 0.0357 |

Doubling the volume of R doubles 1/t; tripling it triples 1/t. The initial rate is proportional to [R], so the reaction is **first order** with respect to R.

## Skill 3: order from rate–concentration data or graphs

| Shape of rate against [X] | Order with respect to X |
|---|---|
| Horizontal line | 0 |
| Straight line through the origin | 1 |
| Upward curve through the origin (rate against [X]² is a straight line) | 2 |

With a table of initial rates, choose two experiments in which **only one concentration changes**, find the concentration factor and the rate factor, and compare.

### Worked example 3: initial rates for the iodination of propanone

| Exp | [CH₃COCH₃] / mol dm⁻³ | [I₂] / mol dm⁻³ | [H⁺] / mol dm⁻³ | Initial rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.40 | 0.0020 | 0.20 | 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 2 | 0.80 | 0.0020 | 0.20 | 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 3 | 0.40 | 0.0040 | 0.20 | 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 4 | 0.60 | 0.0020 | 0.40 | 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ |

- **Propanone** (Exps 1 and 2): concentration × 2, rate × 2, so **first order**.
- **Iodine** (Exps 1 and 3): concentration × 2, rate unchanged, so **zero order**.
- **H⁺** (Exps 1 and 4): two concentrations change. Propanone × 1.5 alone would give rate × 1.5. The rate actually rises × 3.0, so the remaining factor is 3.0 ÷ 1.5 = 2, matching [H⁺] × 2. **First order** in H⁺.

## Skill 4: derive the rate equation and k

```
Rate = k[CH₃COCH₃][H⁺]                    overall order 2
k = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ / (0.40 × 0.20) = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹
```

Check with Exp 4: 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.60 × 0.40 = 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. It agrees, which confirms the orders.

## Skill 5: what the orders say about the mechanism

In a multi-step reaction, the slowest step controls the overall rate. It is the **rate-determining (rate-limiting) step**. The rate equation tells you which species take part in it:

- A species in the rate equation is involved in the rate-determining step (or in a step before it).
- The order with respect to it equals the number of its particles involved up to and including that step.
- A species that is **zero order** reacts only **after** the rate-determining step.
- A catalyst that appears in the rate equation takes part in the rate-determining step and is regenerated later.

For propanone: one propanone and one H⁺ take part in the slow step. Iodine reacts in a later, fast step. So doubling [I₂] cannot speed up the reaction: the iodine is waiting for the slow step to supply the species it reacts with.

### Worked example 4: two halogenoalkanes

Two hydrolysis reactions with hydroxide ions have these rate equations:

```
bromoethane:              Rate = k[CH₃CH₂Br][OH⁻]
2-bromo-2-methylpropane:  Rate = k[(CH₃)₃CBr]
```

For bromoethane, the rate-determining step must involve **one** bromoethane and **one** hydroxide ion, so a single step in which OH⁻ attacks the molecule fits.

For 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, OH⁻ is zero order. The rate-determining step involves only the halogenoalkane, so the mechanism must have at least two steps: the C–Br bond breaks first (slow), and OH⁻ reacts afterwards in a fast step.

When you test any proposed mechanism, check two things: the slow step matches the orders, and the steps add up to the overall equation.

## Required practical 8

**Initial rate method.** Carry out a series of runs, changing the starting concentration of one reactant each time and keeping the others, the total volume and the temperature constant. A clock reaction is convenient: 1/t is proportional to the initial rate, as in Worked example 2.

**Continuous monitoring method.** Follow a single run over time and plot concentration against time. For propanone iodination, either use a colorimeter to record the absorbance of iodine, or remove samples at intervals, stop the reaction by adding sodium hydrogencarbonate to neutralise the acid catalyst, and titrate the remaining iodine with sodium thiosulfate.

Points to evaluate: the water bath must hold the temperature constant, because k depends on T; timing errors are largest for short times; and tangents drawn by eye introduce uncertainty.

## Common mistakes

- Reading orders from the balanced equation.
- Choosing two experiments in which two concentrations change, and crediting the whole rate change to one of them.
- Calling a straight-line concentration–time graph "first order".
- Forgetting that a catalyst can appear in the rate equation.
- Proposing a slow step that contains a zero-order reactant.

## Exam technique

- **"Deduce the order"**: name the experiments used, state both factors, then the order.
- **"Derive the rate equation"**: write it with k and only the species whose order is not zero.
- **"Calculate k"**: give units, worked from the overall order.
- **"Explain what the rate equation shows about the mechanism"**: say which species are in the rate-determining step and how many of each.
