The human development context of IB Psychology (first assessment 2027) has a second strand called **development of self**. The guide lists four areas of study: the role of **attachment**, **enculturation of social norms**, **peer influence** and **childhood experiences** in the development of self. All four are required. The guide does not define "self", so this page uses a working definition: the self is a person's sense of who they are, including their self-concept (the beliefs they hold about themselves), self-esteem (how they evaluate themselves) and identity (the roles and values they identify with). The guide names no required studies, so choose examples you can describe and evaluate.

Related: [developmental psychology study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-developmental-psychology-study-guide/), [brain development](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-brain-development/), [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-development-of-self-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-development-of-self-practice/).

## How to think about the four areas

The four areas move outward from the child: the first close relationship (attachment), the culture the child grows up in (enculturation), the peer group that matters more with age (peer influence) and the overall pattern of early life (childhood experiences). They are not rivals. A good answer shows how they interact and then uses evidence to say which matters most in a given case.

## 1. Attachment

**Attachment** is a strong emotional bond between an infant and a caregiver. John Bowlby proposed that infants are biologically prepared to form attachments, and that early relationships create an **internal working model**: a set of expectations about whether others are reliable and whether the self is worthy of care. This model carries into later relationships and into self-concept.

**Evidence.**
- *Harlow's monkeys:* infant monkeys with a choice between a wire mother that gave milk and a cloth mother with no milk spent most of their time with the cloth mother, which suggested that comfort, not feeding, builds attachment. The study used animals and involved harm, which raises ethical questions.
- *Ainsworth's Strange Situation:* a laboratory procedure in which a child is observed during short separations from and reunions with a caregiver. Children were classified as securely attached (distressed on separation, easily comforted on reunion), insecure-avoidant (little distress, avoid the caregiver on reunion) or insecure-resistant (very distressed, difficult to comfort). Secure attachment was the most common pattern in the original samples.
- *Longitudinal evidence:* in the Minnesota Longitudinal Study, children who had been securely attached in infancy were later rated as more socially competent and more confident in childhood and adolescence than those insecurely attached.

**Link to self.** A securely attached child learns "I am worth caring for and others can be trusted", which supports a positive self-concept and self-esteem. An insecure child may develop expectations of rejection and may see the self as unworthy or others as unreliable.

**Worked example.** Two five-year-olds fall and hurt their knees. Child A runs to the caregiver, is comforted, and goes back to play. Child B hides the pain and avoids contact. Using the internal working model, Child A has learned that distress brings comfort and that the self is worth helping; Child B has learned that showing need leads to rejection. A strong answer adds that the patterns are probabilistic, not fixed, because later relationships can change them.

**Evaluation.**
- *Strengths:* a large body of research, and practical effects on childcare and social policy.
- *Limitations:* the Strange Situation is an artificial, short observation; classification may reflect temperament or cultural norms; the original samples were mainly American, and cultures differ in parenting practices, so attachment categories may not mean the same elsewhere.
- *Determinism:* the claim that infancy determines adult relationships is too strong, since many people change.
- **Concepts:** causality, measurement (classification in a lab procedure), bias (cultural), change.

## 2. Enculturation of social norms

**Enculturation** is the process by which a person learns the norms, values and practices of their own culture, mainly in childhood, through family, school, language, media and community. It is a sociocultural term in the guide, and the learning objective here is its role in the **development of self**.

**Cultural views of the self.** Markus and Kitayama (1991) proposed that cultures encourage different self-construals. In many Western, individualist settings the self is seen as independent and defined by personal traits and goals. In many East Asian and other collectivist settings, the self is more interdependent and defined by relationships and roles. This does not describe every person, but it shows how enculturation shapes what it means to have a self.

**Mechanisms.** Children learn norms by *social learning* (observing and imitating models), *reinforcement* (praise and sanctions), *instruction* (being told rules) and *language* (which words are used about the self and others). Schooling also transmits norms.

**Worked example.** A child praised at school for "standing out" and for personal achievement may develop a self-concept built on individual success. A child praised for helping and fitting in with a group may develop a self-concept built around contribution to the family and class. Neither is better. The point is that the "self" develops differently under different norms.

**Evaluation.**
- *Strengths:* explains why self-concept varies across societies; supported by cross-cultural comparisons and cultural dimensions research.
- *Limitations:* the individualist-collectivist contrast is a simplification; individuals within cultures differ; globalisation mixes cultural messages, so enculturation may be less uniform. Cross-cultural questionnaires may impose Western ideas of the self (an etic bias), so emic research is needed.
- **Concepts:** perspective (etic and emic), bias, measurement, change.

## 3. Peer influence

During childhood and adolescence, peers become a major source of feedback, norms and identity. Peers influence the self through:
- **Social comparison:** judging oneself against friends.
- **Conformity and acceptance:** adopting group norms to belong.
- **Social learning:** copying peers' behaviours and attitudes.
- **Looking-glass processes:** seeing oneself as others see us.

**Evidence.**
- *Gardner and Steinberg (2005):* adolescents, young adults and adults played a driving simulation either alone or with friends watching. Adolescents took more risks when friends were present, while adults did not change as much. This suggests that peer presence affects adolescent decision-making.
- *Group socialisation theory:* Judith Rich Harris argued that, beyond genes, peer groups influence personality and behaviour more than parents do. The claim is controversial and difficult to test, but it draws attention to peer effects.

**Worked example.** A fourteen-year-old who loved drawing stops after classmates mock it, and joins a sports group where she is accepted. Her self-concept shifts from "artist" to "athlete". An answer can use conformity (normative influence), social comparison and the importance of identity in adolescence. A good answer states that supportive peers can also strengthen self-esteem.

**Evaluation.**
- *Strengths:* experimental studies show causal effects of peer presence; applications include peer-led programmes.
- *Limitations:* adolescents choose their friends, so similarity may reflect selection, not influence; most studies use Western samples; lab tasks may not predict real behaviour.
- **Concepts:** causality, change, responsibility (peer pressure and wellbeing).

## 4. Role of childhood experiences

The guide asks for the **role of childhood experiences** in the development of self. This area asks you to weigh how early life shapes who we become, and to consider both harmful and protective experiences.

**Evidence.**
- *Childhood adversity:* in the survey by Felitti and colleagues (1998), adults who reported more adverse childhood experiences, such as abuse, neglect and household dysfunction, were more likely to report health and emotional difficulties. The data are retrospective and self-reported, so memory bias matters, and the study is correlational.
- *Early deprivation:* studies of children adopted from severe institutional care show that prolonged deprivation was linked to attachment and attention problems, but many children recovered, which suggests resilience and plasticity.
- *Self-control:* Mischel's delay-of-gratification studies found that children who waited longer for a larger reward at about age four tended to do better on later measures. A later large study by Watts, Duncan and Quan (2018) found a much smaller link once family background and early cognitive ability were taken into account, which suggests that the early behaviour partly reflects circumstances.

**Interpretation.** Childhood experiences influence the self, but the effect depends on the child's temperament, later relationships and opportunities. Protective factors, such as a supportive adult, can reduce harm.

**Evaluation.**
- *Strengths:* longitudinal designs can follow the same people; findings inform policy and early intervention.
- *Limitations:* correlational designs and confounds (family income, genes), retrospective reports, and the ethics of studying distressing experiences.
- **Concepts:** causality (confounding), change, responsibility, measurement.

## Putting the four areas together

For a Section C response, show interaction. For example: secure attachment (area 1) gives a child confidence to explore, which allows rich peer experience (area 3); the norms of the child's culture (area 2) shape which peer behaviours are valued; and the sum of these experiences, including any adversity (area 4), shapes self-concept. Then evaluate: most evidence is correlational, so causes are uncertain, and each factor is moderated by genes, temperament and later chances to change.

## Common mistakes

- Treating attachment categories as fixed destiny.
- Equating enculturation with acculturation. Enculturation is learning your own culture; acculturation is change after contact between cultures.
- Presenting cultural dimensions as facts about every person.
- Describing a famous study without linking it to the self.
- Forgetting to evaluate causal claims from correlational data.
