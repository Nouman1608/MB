## What a control account is

A business with many credit customers keeps a separate account for each in
the **sales ledger**, and one for each credit supplier in the **purchases
ledger**. A **control account** summarises all of them in one account, using
**totals** from the books of prime entry rather than individual
transactions.

- The **sales ledger control account** shows total trade receivables. It
mirrors the customers' accounts: what is debited to customers is debited to
the control account.
- The **purchases ledger control account** shows total trade payables. It
mirrors the suppliers' accounts.

If every posting is correct, the balance on each control account equals the
total of the balances in the ledger it controls.

## Purposes

1. **Checking arithmetical accuracy** of a ledger. If the control account
balance and the total of the individual balances agree, the ledger is
probably arithmetically correct.
2. **Locating errors.** A difference points to one ledger, not the whole set
of books, which shortens the search.
3. **Providing totals quickly.** Total trade receivables and trade payables
are available for a trial balance and statements without listing every
account.
4. **Helping to prevent and detect fraud.** If someone other than the ledger
clerk prepares the control account, a false entry in a customer's or
supplier's account is more likely to be noticed.
5. **Supporting credit control.** Managers can follow the total owed by
customers and to suppliers from month to month.

## Where each entry comes from

| Entry | Book of prime entry |
|---|---|
| Credit sales | Sales journal |
| Sales returns | Sales returns journal |
| Receipts from credit customers; cash discounts allowed | Cash book |
| Dishonoured cheques; refunds paid to customers | Cash book |
| Irrecoverable debts written off; interest on overdue accounts; contra entries | General journal |
| Credit purchases | Purchases journal |
| Purchases returns | Purchases returns journal |
| Payments to credit suppliers; cash discounts received; refunds from suppliers | Cash book |

**Cash sales and cash purchases never enter a control account.** They do not
pass through the customers' or suppliers' accounts.

How each line was decided:

- A **dishonoured cheque** reverses a receipt: the customer owes the money
again, so it is debited.
- **Interest on overdue accounts** increases what the customer owes, so it
is debited.
- The **refund** to a customer who had overpaid clears that customer's
credit balance: debit the customer, credit bank.
- A **credit balance** in the sales ledger arises when a customer has paid
too much or returned goods after paying. Both opening and closing credit
balances are shown; they are never netted against the debit balances.
- The **debit closing balance**, $25,345, is the balancing figure. The
credit closing balance is given, so it is entered first.

The **contra entry** of $1,200 appears in both accounts. Juma both sells to
and buys from the same business; instead of each paying the other, the
amounts are set off. The entry is debit purchases ledger control, credit
sales ledger control, because it reduces both what Juma owes and what he is
owed.

## A useful check: the mirror rule

Every item in the sales ledger control account has a twin in the purchases
ledger control account on the opposite side:

| Sales ledger control | Purchases ledger control |
|---|---|
| Credit sales (Dr) | Credit purchases (Cr) |
| Returns from customers (Cr) | Returns to suppliers (Dr) |
| Receipts (Cr) | Payments (Dr) |
| Discount allowed (Cr) | Discount received (Dr) |
| Dishonoured cheque received (Dr) | (A dishonoured cheque paid by the business would be Cr) |
| Interest charged to customers (Dr) | Interest charged by suppliers (Cr) |
| Refund paid to customer (Dr) | Refund received from supplier (Cr) |
| Contra (Cr) | Contra (Dr) |

Irrecoverable debts appear **only** in the sales ledger control account.

## Impact of digital recording

When sales and purchases are recorded with accounting software:

- Each invoice, credit note and receipt is posted once and the software
updates the customer's account and the control total at the same time, so
the control account is produced automatically.
- **Arithmetical** errors in adding up a ledger become rare, so the control
account's role as an arithmetic check is smaller.
- **Input** errors still happen: a receipt keyed against the wrong customer
or an invoice entered with the wrong amount changes both the customer's
account and the control total, so they still agree. Review of reports and
checks with customers' statements are still needed.
- Up-to-date totals of trade receivables and payables, and lists of overdue
accounts, are available at any time, which helps credit control and cash
planning.
- Access controls and audit trails in the software replace some of the
fraud-prevention role of a manually kept control account, but only if
passwords and permissions are managed properly.

## Common mistakes

- Including cash sales or cash purchases.
- Netting the opening debit and credit balances into one figure.
- Entering a dishonoured cheque on the credit side of the sales ledger
control account.
- Entering the contra on the same side in both accounts.
- Putting irrecoverable debts or the allowance for irrecoverable debts in the
purchases ledger control account. The allowance is not entered in any
control account at all.
- Writing the closing balances on the wrong side: carried-down figures go on
the opposite side to where they are brought down.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare**: date column, details naming the other account, balances
brought down on 1 January of the next year.
- **State the source**: name the book of prime entry exactly, for example
"sales returns journal", not "returns book".
- **Explain a purpose**: link it to a benefit, for example "a difference
shows the error is in the sales ledger, so less time is spent searching".
- Information may be given as a list in any order; tick each item as it is
posted.
