This guide covers section **3.6.2 Cash flow** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 6, Finance. The specification asks you to understand the **importance of cash to businesses**; the **consequences of cash flow problems** and the effect of **positive cash flow**; **how and why cash flow forecasts are constructed**; how to **complete and interpret sections of a cash flow forecast**, including **cash inflows and outflows, net cash flow and the opening and closing balance**; the **difference between cash and profit**; and how to **evaluate possible solutions to cash flow problems**: rescheduling payments, overdrafts, reducing cash outflow, increasing cash inflow and finding new sources of finance. You are not expected to construct a whole forecast. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Finance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-finance/). ## What is cash flow?

**Cash flow** is the movement of money into and out of a business over a period of time.

- **Cash inflows** (receipts) are money coming in: sales paid for in cash, payments from customers who bought on credit, loans received, money put in by the owner, and the sale of assets.
- **Cash outflows** (payments) are money going out: wages, rent, materials, utilities, loan repayments, equipment purchases and taxes.

**Cash** is money immediately available: notes, coins and the bank balance. It is different from **profit**, which is a measure of revenue minus costs over a period.

## Why cash is important

A business can be profitable and still run out of cash. Without cash, the business cannot:

- pay wages, so staff may leave
- pay suppliers, who may stop supplying
- pay rent, tax and bills on time, risking legal action
- buy stock or materials to make more goods
- grasp new opportunities, such as a special offer from a supplier.

Many businesses that fail are profitable on paper but run out of cash to pay their bills. This is why managers watch cash flow carefully, especially in new and fast-growing businesses.

### Consequences of cash flow problems

- Late payment of suppliers, leading to refusal of credit.
- Inability to pay wages, leading to lower morale and staff leaving.
- Missing chances to buy in bulk or to invest.
- Borrowing at high interest to survive.
- In the worst case, **insolvency**: the business cannot pay its debts and may have to close.

### Effects of positive cash flow

Positive cash flow means more cash is coming in than going out. It allows the business to pay bills on time, take early-payment discounts, invest in new equipment, build a cash reserve and avoid borrowing.

## The difference between cash and profit

| | Cash | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Money available now | Revenue minus total costs over a period |
| Timing | When money is actually received or paid | When sales are made and costs are incurred, even if money has not changed hands |
| Can it be negative? | Yes, if outflows exceed inflows | Yes, if costs exceed revenue (a loss) |
| Effect of credit sales | Cash arrives only when the customer pays | Profit is recorded when the sale is made |

**Worked example 1: profit without cash.** A furniture maker buys wood and pays workers $6,000 in March to make a set of tables. In March it sells the tables for $10,000, but the customer is allowed 60 days to pay.

```
Profit in March = $10,000 - $6,000 = $4,000
Cash in March   = $0 received - $6,000 paid = -$6,000
```

The business is $4,000 better off on paper, but has $6,000 less cash until the customer pays in May. If it has no cash reserve, it may be unable to pay the next month's wages.

Equally, a business can have cash but make a loss, for example when it borrows money, or sells an asset: the cash rises but profit does not.

## Cash flow forecasts

A **cash flow forecast** is a prediction of the cash that will come in and go out of a business over a future period, usually month by month. It is based on expected sales, costs and the timing of payments.

### Why businesses produce forecasts

- To **spot shortages** in advance and arrange finance before they occur.
- To **plan** large purchases and payments.
- To **support applications for loans**, since banks ask to see forecasts.
- To **set targets** and compare actual cash flow against the plan.
- To **avoid surprises** by showing when money will be tight.

### The layout of a forecast

| Item | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Opening balance | Cash at the start of the month (equals the closing balance of the previous month) |
| Cash inflows (receipts) | Total cash coming in during the month |
| Cash outflows (payments) | Total cash going out during the month |
| Net cash flow | Inflows minus outflows |
| Closing balance | Opening balance plus net cash flow |

```
Net cash flow   = cash inflows - cash outflows
Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow
Next month's opening balance = this month's closing balance
```

**Worked example 2: completing a forecast.** A business starts January with $5,000.

| Month | Opening balance | Inflows | Outflows | Net cash flow | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| January | 5,000 | 12,000 | 15,000 | -3,000 | 2,000 |
| February | 2,000 | 14,000 | 13,500 | 500 | 2,500 |
| March | 2,500 | 16,000 | 17,000 | -1,000 | 1,500 |
| April | 1,500 | 11,000 | 12,500 | -1,500 | 0 |
| May | 0 | 9,000 | 10,000 | -1,000 | -1,000 |
| June | -1,000 | 18,000 | 14,000 | 4,000 | 3,000 |

Check each row. January: 12,000 - 15,000 = -3,000; 5,000 + (-3,000) = 2,000. May: 9,000 - 10,000 = -1,000; 0 + (-1,000) = -1,000. June: 18,000 - 14,000 = 4,000; -1,000 + 4,000 = 3,000.

### Interpreting the forecast

- The balance falls from January and reaches zero in April, and **negative** in May (-$1,000), so the business would need an overdraft or other action to pay its bills in May.
- Net cash flow is negative in four of the six months, which signals a pattern of outflows greater than inflows. In June the large inflow ($18,000) restores a positive balance, perhaps from a seasonal peak.
- A negative balance in May does not mean the business is unprofitable. It shows that timing of receipts and payments creates a shortage.

Forecasts have limits. They are only estimates, and sales may be lower than expected, customers may pay late and costs may rise. A new business has no past data, so forecasts are particularly uncertain. Plans should include a safety margin and be updated regularly.

## Solving cash flow problems

The specification asks you to evaluate possible solutions.

| Solution | How it helps | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| Rescheduling payments (asking suppliers for more time, or delaying a purchase) | Keeps cash in the business longer | Suppliers may refuse credit in future or lose goodwill; may lose early-payment discounts |
| Overdraft | Quick, flexible cover for a short-term gap | High interest; can be withdrawn by the bank |
| Reducing cash outflow (cutting costs, delaying purchases, cutting stock) | Reduces outflows | Cutting costs may harm quality or growth |
| Increasing cash inflow (asking customers to pay sooner, offering discounts for prompt payment, selling stock at lower prices, selling unwanted assets) | Brings cash in faster | Discounts reduce profit; customers may dislike pressure |
| New sources of finance (loan, share issue, owner's investment) | Adds cash | Interest or loss of control; takes time |

**Worked example 3: choosing a solution.** The business in Worked example 2 expects to be $1,000 overdrawn in May.

- **Overdraft of $1,000 for 1 month at 15% a year:** interest = $1,000 x 15% x 1/12 = $12.50. This is cheap and quick, as the shortage is small and short-lived.
- **Asking a customer to pay $2,000 early with a 5% discount:** cost = 5% x $2,000 = $100. This solves the problem but is more costly than the overdraft.
- **Delaying a $1,500 payment for a month:** costs nothing, if the supplier agrees.

For a small, short-term gap, the overdraft or a delayed payment is probably best. For a continuing shortage, the business needs longer-term finance, or must deal with the cause, for example by reducing stock.

The solution depends on the cause. A **temporary** problem (seasonal sales) suits short-term finance. A **permanent** problem (costs too high or prices too low) needs the business to change what it does.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing cash with profit. A profitable business can run out of cash.
- Forgetting that the closing balance of one month is the opening balance of the next.
- Reading a negative closing balance as a "loss". It is a shortage of cash.
- Including non-cash items, such as profit, as an inflow.
- Proposing the same solution for every problem, without regard to the size, length and cause.
- Forgetting the drawbacks of each solution.

## Exam technique for this section

For **define** and **state** questions, give the meaning. For **calculate** questions, show net cash flow and closing balance for each month. For **explain** questions, link cash to consequences: "if the business cannot pay its suppliers, they may refuse to deliver, so the business cannot make products". For **evaluate** questions, such as the best way to cover a shortage, compare at least two solutions in terms of cost, speed and risk, with figures from the case, and recommend one.
