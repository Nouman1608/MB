> use your own learned case study where a question asks for a named example.

## Section A

**1.** Name the four types of industry identified in the syllabus.
**[2]**

**2.** For a factory, give one example of each: (a) an input, (b) a
process, (c) a waste output. **[3]**

**3.** State what is meant by a *footloose* industry. **[1]**

**4.** Give two political factors that can attract a factory to an
area. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a soft-drink bottling plant is usually located
near its market. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A company plans a new electronics assembly plant. The table
gives information about three possible sites (illustrative data).

| Site | Mean monthly wage (US$) | Distance to container port (km) | Government incentive |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 900 | 25 | None |
| Q | 300 | 40 | Five-year tax break in export processing zone |
| R | 250 | 600 | None |

**(a)** Calculate how many times higher the wage is at P than at Q.
**[1]**
**(b)** Recommend the best site and justify your choice using the data.
**[4]**
**(c)** Suggest one disadvantage for local people of a factory moving
into an export processing zone. **[2]**

**7.** Many iron and steel works built in the 19th century were on
coalfields, but newer steel works are often at coastal sites.

**(a)** Explain why early steel works were located on coalfields. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest two reasons why newer steel works are located at the
coast. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why high-technology industries often cluster near
universities. **[4]**

**9.** Describe how the waste outputs of an industrial system can harm
the environment. **[4]**

**10.** For a named factory or industrial zone, explain the factors
that have influenced its location. **[7]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Manufacturing, processing, assembly, high technology (all four
for 2 marks; two or three for 1 mark) [2].

**2.** (a) Any input, such as raw materials, components, labour,
energy or capital [1]. (b) Any process, such as smelting, refining,
assembling or packaging [1]. (c) Any waste, such as waste gases, waste
water, heat or scrap [1].

**3.** An industry that is not tied to raw materials or a particular
power source, so it can locate almost anywhere [1].

**4.** Any two [2]: grants or subsidies; tax breaks; cheap land or
rent; planning permission; location in an export processing zone or
special economic zone; trade barriers that encourage production inside
the market.

**5.** The product is mostly water, so it gains weight and bulk in
production [1]; transporting the finished drinks is costly, so it is
cheaper to make them close to the customers [1].

**6. (a)** 900 ÷ 300 = 3 times [1].
**(b)** Site Q [1]. Up to 3 further marks: wages at Q are a third of
those at P (300 compared with 900 US dollars), which matters because
assembly needs many workers [1]; Q is only 40 km from a container
port, so components can be imported and products exported cheaply,
whereas R is 600 km away [1]; the five-year tax break in the export
processing zone reduces costs further [1]. R's wages are slightly lower
but the distance from the port outweighs this.
**(c)** Any one, developed [2]: wages may be low and working
conditions poor; profits may go to the foreign company rather than
the local area; jobs could disappear when the tax break ends and the
company moves; farmland may be lost to the zone; pollution may
increase.

**7. (a)** Coal was the main fuel for smelting iron ore, and large
quantities were used [1]; coal is heavy and bulky, so locating on the
coalfield reduced transport costs [1].
**(b)** Any two, developed, 2 marks each [4]: local coal and iron ore
have run out or become expensive, so raw materials are now imported by
sea [2]; a coastal port is a break-of-bulk point, so ore and coal can
be unloaded straight into the works [2]; flat coastal land gives space
for large modern plants [2]; finished steel can be exported directly
[2].

**8.** Up to 4 marks: universities supply highly qualified graduates
[1]; research carried out in universities can be developed into new
products by firms [1]; staff and students may set up new companies,
often in nearby science parks [1]; clustering allows firms to share
ideas, suppliers and skilled workers (agglomeration) [1].

**9.** Up to 4 marks: waste gases such as sulphur dioxide and carbon
dioxide cause air pollution and add to the enhanced greenhouse effect
[1]; untreated waste water discharged into rivers harms aquatic life
and contaminates drinking water [1]; solid waste and scrap dumped on
land can contaminate soil and spoil the landscape (visual pollution)
[1]; heat released into rivers lowers oxygen levels, harming fish [1];
noise from machinery and traffic disturbs nearby residents [1].

**10.** Levels-style marking.
*Level 1 (1–3 marks)*: simple factors with no named place.
*Level 2 (4–5 marks)*: a named factory or zone with some factors
developed.
*Level 3 (6–7 marks)*: a named factory or zone with several factors
explained using place-specific detail.
Indicative content (Silicon Valley): southern San Francisco Bay Area,
including Palo Alto, Mountain View, Cupertino, Santa Clara and San
Jose; Stanford University supplies graduates and research; venture
capital funds start-ups; agglomeration of firms such as Intel, Apple
and Google allows the sharing of ideas and skilled staff; airports at
San Francisco and San Jose suit light, high-value products; a pleasant
climate attracts workers.

## A note on exam technique for this subtopic

Always tie a location factor to the type of industry: "near the market
*because the product gains weight*" earns the mark, "near the market"
alone often does not. In decision-making questions, quote the data and
explain why the rejected sites are worse.
