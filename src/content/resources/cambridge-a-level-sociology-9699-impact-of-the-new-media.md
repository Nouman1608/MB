Section 9.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *The impact of the new media*,
completes heading 9 of Paper 4. The syllabus lists four points: how the new media is contributing
to globalisation; the new media as a challenge to existing power structures; the debate about
digital optimism versus digital pessimism; and the impact of the new media on social identities and
interpersonal relationships. The key concepts are *Power, control and resistance* and *Social
change and development*.

The [media ownership, control and the new media guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-media-ownership-and-control/)
gives a summary of 9.3. This page adds the concepts and evidence needed for a full essay. Use it
with the [impact of the new media revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-impact-of-the-new-media-revision-notes/)
and the [impact of the new media practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-impact-of-the-new-media-practice/).

## How the new media contributes to globalisation

- **Shrinking distance.** Marshall McLuhan predicted a **global village** in the 1960s, in which
  electronic media would connect the world as closely as a village. Instant messaging, video calls
  and live streaming make that idea far more literal.
- **Global flows of images and ideas.** Arjun Appadurai (*Modernity at Large*, 1996) described
  **mediascapes**: global flows of images and stories that people use to imagine lives elsewhere.
  These feed migration aspirations, consumer tastes and political ideas.
- **Global platforms and markets.** A few platforms operate in most countries, carrying global
  brands, music and entertainment, while also allowing local content to reach global audiences;
  this is a form of **glocalisation**.
- **Diaspora connections.** Migrants keep daily contact with families and follow homeland news,
  sustaining transnational identities.
- **Global movements.** Campaigns can coordinate across borders, linking 9.3 to global social
  movements in 7.3.

**The digital divide.** Pippa Norris's *Digital Divide* (2001) distinguished a **global divide**
between richer and poorer countries, a **social divide** within countries, and a **democratic
divide** between those who use digital resources to participate in public life and those who do
not. Globalisation through new media is therefore uneven: access depends on income, infrastructure,
age, gender and education.

## The new media as a challenge to existing power structures

**Evidence of challenge**
- **Leaks and exposure.** WikiLeaks published large numbers of US diplomatic cables in 2010;
  Edward Snowden's disclosures in 2013 revealed mass surveillance programmes. New media made it
  possible to publish and spread such material worldwide.
- **Networked protest.** Manuel Castells' *Networks of Outrage and Hope* (2012) analysed movements
  such as the Arab Spring and Occupy, arguing that online networks allowed people to share outrage
  and coordinate action outside traditional organisations.
- **Hashtag movements.** #MeToo, which spread widely in 2017, showed social media allowing
  individuals' accounts of sexual harassment to accumulate into public pressure on powerful men
  and institutions.
- **Citizen journalism** bypasses editors and can record abuses as they happen.

**Evidence that power structures survive or adapt**
- **States use new media too.** Governments monitor online activity, shut down internet access
  during protests and spread propaganda.
- **Corporate power.** Christian Fuchs (*Social Media: A Critical Introduction*, 2014), writing
  from a Marxist position, argues that users' activity is a form of unpaid **digital labour**: it
  generates the data and attention that platforms sell to advertisers.
- **Slacktivism.** Evgeny Morozov popularised the term for low-effort online support (liking or
  sharing) that may replace, rather than lead to, sustained political action.
- **Outcomes.** Many networked movements mobilise quickly but struggle to build lasting
  organisations or achieve policy change.

## Digital optimism versus digital pessimism

| Issue | Digital optimists | Digital pessimists |
|---|---|---|
| **Participation** | Clay Shirky's *Here Comes Everybody* (2008): new tools make it easy for groups to form and act without formal organisations | Participation is unequal; a minority produce most content |
| **Information** | Wider access to knowledge and diverse views | Cass Sunstein (*Republic.com*, 2001) warned of **echo chambers** where people hear only like-minded views; misinformation spreads fast |
| **Democracy** | Citizens can hold power to account | Surveillance by states and companies; targeted manipulation |
| **Economy** | Low entry costs let small producers reach global markets | Platforms dominate markets and profit from user data |
| **Equality** | Marginalised groups gain a voice | The digital divide reproduces existing inequalities |

**Evaluating the debate.** Both sides generalise. A more convincing position treats the new media
as a **contested space**: the same tools serve activists and governments, small producers and
monopolies. Outcomes depend on who has access, skills and resources, which links back to class,
gender and global inequality.

## Impact on social identities

- **Presentation of self.** Erving Goffman's *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959)
  described social life as a performance with front and back stages. Online profiles are carefully
  managed front stages; users choose photographs, edit posts and present idealised selves.
- **Context collapse.** Alice Marwick and danah boyd (2011) described how social media merge
  audiences that would normally be kept separate (family, friends, employers), making identity
  management harder.
- **Experiment and choice.** Postmodernists see online spaces as allowing people to try out and
  combine identities, including identities linked to sexuality, fandom or politics that may be
  hidden offline.
- **Identity tourism.** Lisa Nakamura used this term for people adopting another ethnic or gender
  identity online, arguing that it often reproduces stereotypes rather than challenging them.
- **Cultural difference.** Daniel Miller and colleagues' *How the World Changed Social Media*
  (2016), based on comparative ethnography in several countries, found that people use social media
  in very different ways depending on local culture, so there is no single "social media identity".

## Impact on interpersonal relationships

- **Maintaining ties.** Mirca Madianou and Daniel Miller's *Migration and New Media* (2012) studied
  Filipino migrant mothers in the UK and their children at home. They developed the idea of
  **polymedia**: people choose between many media (calls, messages, video) to express different
  kinds of feeling, which helps sustain transnational families but can also intensify conflict
  and surveillance within them.
- **Networked individualism.** Lee Rainie and Barry Wellman's *Networked* (2012) argued that people
  increasingly belong to loose, personal networks rather than tightly bound groups.
- **Weakened intimacy?** Digital pessimists argue constant connection can make face-to-face
  relationships shallower. Cyberbullying and online harassment extend conflict beyond the school or
  workplace.
- **Evaluation.** Effects depend on how media are used: the same app can deepen a long-distance
  relationship or replace local contact.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question focus: evaluate the view that the new media challenge existing power structures.*

> Castells (2012) supports the view, arguing that online networks allowed the Arab Spring to
> spread outrage and coordinate protest outside parties and unions. **[AO1 and AO2]** However,
> the same tools let states track activists and shut down access, and Morozov warns that easy
> online support can substitute for organised action. **[AO3: counter-argument]** Fuchs adds that
> platforms profit from users' activity, so even protest generates revenue for powerful
> corporations. **[AO3: Marxist analysis]** The new media therefore create new opportunities to
> challenge power but also new forms of control, and the balance depends on who has the resources
> to use them. **[AO3: judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Writing a list of optimist and pessimist names without applying them to a question.
- Treating the Arab Spring as proof that social media cause revolutions; it shows they can help
  coordinate protest, alongside many other causes.
- Ignoring the digital divide when discussing globalisation.
- Discussing identity only in terms of young people in wealthy countries.
- Confusing 9.3 (impact of new media) with 10.3 (impact of media on behaviour, such as violence).

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: give a mechanism (for example, context collapse), explain how it works and
  apply it to an example.
- **Evaluate** questions: set optimists against pessimists, then judge using evidence on who uses
  new media and for what.
- Use concepts from elsewhere in Paper 4: glocalisation and hybrid identity (7.1 and 7.2), global
  social movements (7.3), and ownership trends (9.1).
