## The definition to learn word for word

Metallic bonding is the **electrostatic attraction** between the **positive ions** in a
**giant metallic lattice** and a **'sea' of delocalised electrons**.

Four key phrases: *electrostatic attraction* · *positive ions* · *giant metallic lattice* ·
*sea of delocalised electrons*. Each is worth looking for in your answer.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Positive ion | Metal atom that has released its outer electrons |
| Giant metallic lattice | Regular arrangement of positive ions in layers throughout the metal |
| Delocalised electrons | Outer electrons not fixed to any atom; free to move through the lattice |
| 'Sea' of electrons | The delocalised electrons spread throughout the structure |
| Electrostatic attraction | Force between the positive ions and the negative electrons |
| Malleable | Can be hammered or pressed into shape without breaking |
| Ductile | Can be drawn out into wires |

## How the model forms

```
metal atoms → release outer electrons → positive ions in regular layers
                                     → electrons spread as a delocalised 'sea'
bond = attraction between the ions and the electron sea (acts in all directions)
```

Examples: each Na atom gives 1 electron (Na⁺); each Mg atom gives 2 (Mg²⁺); each Al atom
gives 3 (Al³⁺).

## Diagram rules

- Regular rows of circles marked **+** — label them **positive ions**.
- Electrons drawn **between** the ions, not attached to any one — label them **delocalised
  electrons**.
- No negative ions; no electron pairs drawn between two ions.

## Property explanations

**Electrical conductivity**
delocalised electrons → **free to move** through the lattice → **carry charge** when a
voltage is applied. Works in solid and molten metal.

**Malleability and ductility**
layers of positive ions → **slide over each other** when a force is applied → delocalised
electrons **move with them** → attraction **maintained**, bonding not broken → metal changes
shape instead of breaking.

## Comparison grid: who carries charge?

| Structure | Particles that move | When it conducts |
|---|---|---|
| Metallic | Delocalised electrons | Solid and molten |
| Graphite | Delocalised electrons | Solid |
| Ionic | Ions | Molten or aqueous only |
| Simple molecular, diamond, SiO₂ | None | Does not conduct |

## Answer frames

Fill these in from memory before the exam:

- *Describe metallic bonding:* "A giant lattice of ______ ions surrounded by a sea of
  ______ electrons; the bond is the ______ attraction between them."
- *Explain conductivity:* "The ______ electrons are free to ______ through the lattice and
  carry ______."
- *Explain malleability:* "Layers of positive ions ______ over each other; the delocalised
  electrons move with them, so the ______ is maintained and the metal does not ______."

Answers: positive, delocalised, electrostatic · delocalised, move, charge · slide,
attraction (bonding), break.

**Why both outcomes often appear together:** uses of metals such as wires, cables and sheet
roofing need a property from each outcome — conducting *and* being shaped — so a single
question can test the definition, conductivity and malleability or ductility in turn.

## Exam traps

- "Atoms" in the lattice → write **positive ions**.
- "Free ions conduct" → in metals it is **electrons**.
- "Shared electrons" → that is covalent; metallic electrons are **delocalised**.
- "Layers of atoms slide and bonds break" → **layers of ions slide**, **bonds are not
  broken**.
- Explaining ductility separately from malleability with a different reason — it is the
  **same** explanation.
- Using intermolecular forces for a metal — there are no molecules.
- Leaving out "electrostatic" or "attraction" from the definition loses the bond mark.
- For a wiring question, give **both** conductivity and ductility if asked why a metal is
  used *and* can be made into wire.

## Self-test

1. Give the full definition of metallic bonding.
2. What particles make up the lattice of a metal?
3. What does "delocalised" mean?
4. How many electrons does each magnesium atom release into the electron sea?
5. Explain why metals conduct electricity.
6. Do metals conduct when molten? Explain.
7. Define malleable and ductile.
8. Explain why a metal can be hammered into a thin sheet.
9. Why is the bonding not broken when layers slide?
10. Name two other structures on the 0620 course in which charge is carried by ions or
    electrons, and say which carrier each uses.
11. A diagram shows alternating + and − circles. Why is it not a metallic lattice?
12. Suggest why aluminium is used for overhead power cables, in terms of structure.

**Answers**

1. The electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in a giant metallic lattice and a
   sea of delocalised electrons.
2. Positive metal ions (in regular layers), surrounded by delocalised electrons.
3. Not fixed to any one atom or bond; free to move through the structure.
4. Two (forming Mg²⁺).
5. The delocalised electrons are free to move through the lattice and carry charge.
6. Yes; the delocalised electrons are still present and free to move.
7. Malleable: can be hammered into shape. Ductile: can be drawn into wires.
8. Layers of positive ions slide over each other; the delocalised electrons move with them,
   so the attraction is maintained and the metal does not break.
9. The sea of delocalised electrons still surrounds the ions in their new positions, so the
   electrostatic attraction remains.
10. Ionic compounds — ions (when molten or aqueous); graphite — delocalised electrons.
11. It contains negative ions, so it shows an ionic lattice; a metallic lattice has only
    positive ions with delocalised electrons between them.
12. It has delocalised electrons free to move, so it conducts electricity; its layers of
    ions can slide, so it is ductile and can be drawn into cables.
