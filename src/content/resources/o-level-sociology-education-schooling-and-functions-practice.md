These questions practise key questions 5.1 (*What is education and schooling?*) and 5.2 (*What are
the roles and functions of education?*) of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251). Set A follows the
six-part (a)–(f) structure the syllabus describes for Paper 2; Set B gives extra practice on the
theories.

## Set A

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *informal education* **[2]**
- (ii) *role allocation* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of the hidden curriculum. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** limitations of private schools. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways the Marxist view sees education reproducing class inequality. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the strengths and limitations of online learning. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the functionalist view that education is meritocratic. **[14]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Learning that happens outside schools in everyday life — from family, community,
media or work — without a planned curriculum or formal assessment (2).

**(a)(ii) [2]** The functionalist idea that education sorts people by ability and qualifications
into the jobs that best suit their talents (2) (Davis and Moore, 1945).

**(b) [2]** Any two: punctuality enforced by bells and late detentions; obedience to teachers;
wearing uniform; competition for marks; gendered sports or subject choices; respect for hierarchy.

**(c) [6]** Three limitations, 2 marks each: fees exclude poorer families; they may widen inequality
by giving advantaged pupils better resources and contacts; they draw good teachers away from state
schools; they may create social segregation; results may reflect pupils' advantages more than school
quality.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- The **hidden curriculum** teaches obedience and acceptance of hierarchy, preparing working-class
  pupils for subordinate jobs (Bowles and Gintis, 1976).
- **Middle-class culture** in schools rewards the **cultural capital** of middle-class children
  (Bourdieu).
- The **myth of meritocracy** makes failure look like individual fault, legitimating inequality.
- **Material factors** such as access to digital technology favour wealthier pupils.
- **Setting by ability** often places working-class pupils in lower sets.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 developed on both sides; 4–6 some development; 1–3 basic.
- *Strengths:* flexibility of time and place; access to specialist courses and teachers; continued
  learning during closures (for example during the COVID-19 pandemic); self-paced study.
- *Limitations:* the digital divide — poorer and rural pupils lack devices or internet; less social
  interaction and peer support; harder to motivate; teachers less able to monitor understanding;
  more screen time.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with conclusion; 7–10 developed but less
balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*Functionalist case:*
- Durkheim: education transmits shared values and creates social solidarity.
- Parsons: schools judge pupils by universal standards, so status is achieved by merit.
- Davis and Moore (1945): role allocation sorts the most able into the most important jobs.
- Equal opportunities, standardised testing and social mobility support meritocracy.

*Criticisms:*
- Marxists: the myth of meritocracy hides how class background shapes success; cultural capital
  (Bourdieu); hidden curriculum (Bowles and Gintis).
- Material factors — private tuition, digital access, fees — give advantages to wealthier families.
- Feminists: gendered subject choice and expectations limit girls' opportunities in some societies.
- Achievement gaps by class, ethnicity and gender persist.

*Conclusion:* education allows some mobility but is not fully meritocratic; credit a judgement.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Two strengths, 2 marks each: practical skills that employers need; better job prospects
for pupils less suited to academic study; links with workplaces and apprenticeships; supports the
economy.

**(h) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: teachers may expect boys to be better at science and mathematics,
steering girls away; girls may be praised for neatness and quietness rather than ambition; boys may
receive more classroom attention; careers advice may follow gender stereotypes.

**(i) [4]** Selective schools admit pupils based on tests of ability or attainment (2); non-selective
schools admit pupils regardless of ability, usually from a local area (2).

**(j) [4]** Two criticisms, 2 marks each: in many countries girls now outperform boys, so education
may not disadvantage girls in the way feminists claim; the view may ignore class and ethnic
differences among girls; some changes such as equal access to the curriculum have reduced
inequality.

**(k) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each: depends on parents' time, knowledge and income; children
may have fewer chances to socialise with peers and learn to work in groups; access to specialist
teaching, laboratories and examinations may be harder; less outside monitoring of quality or
safeguarding.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which sociologists should I know for 5.2?** Durkheim and Parsons (functionalist), Bowles and Gintis
and Bourdieu (Marxist and cultural capital), and feminist writers on gendered schooling.

**How do I evaluate in (f)?** Set functionalist arguments against Marxist and feminist criticisms and
use evidence about achievement gaps, then judge.

**Is meritocracy the same as equal opportunity?** Closely related: meritocracy means rewards are based
on ability and effort, which requires equal opportunity to succeed.
