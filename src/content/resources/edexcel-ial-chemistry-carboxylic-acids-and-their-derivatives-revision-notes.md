## The four families

| Compound type | Functional group | Suffix | Named example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | -oic acid | CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)COOH, 2-methylbutanoic acid |
| Acyl chloride | –COCl | -oyl chloride | (CH₃)₂CHCOCl, 2-methylpropanoyl chloride |
| Ester | –COO– | alkyl -oate | CH₃CH₂COO(CH₂)₄CH₃, pentyl propanoate |
| Dicarboxylic acid | two –COOH | -dioic acid | HOOCCH₂CH₂COOH, butanedioic acid |

## 15.9 and 15.13 Naming in steps

**Acids and acyl chlorides**

1. Find the longest chain that includes the C=O carbon. That carbon is C1 and takes no number.
2. Add the ending: -oic acid or -oyl chloride.
3. Number branches from C1.

**Esters**

1. Split the ester at the single-bonded oxygen that is joined to the C=O carbon.
2. The side holding the C=O carbon is the acid part: -oate.
3. The other side is the alkyl group from the alcohol: -yl.
4. Write the alkyl word first.

Worked reminder: HCOOCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃. The acid part has one carbon (methanoate); the other chain has six (hexyl). Name: **hexyl methanoate**, from methanoic acid and hexan-1-ol.

**Three kinds of formula**

- Structural: enough to show the arrangement, e.g. CH₃CH₂COCl.
- Displayed: every atom and every bond drawn, including the O–H in an acid.
- Skeletal: zigzag of carbon lines; O, Cl and the H of O–H written in; H on carbon omitted.

## 15.10 Hydrogen bonding

- Acids hydrogen bond to each other through O–H and the C=O oxygen, often as **dimers**.
- Acids have higher boiling temperatures than alcohols, aldehydes, ketones or esters of similar Mr.
- Esters, aldehydes and ketones have no O–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond to each other. They still accept hydrogen bonds from water.
- Short-chain acids are miscible with water. Solubility falls as the non-polar chain gets longer.

Method for any "explain the boiling temperature" answer:

1. Name the intermolecular forces in each substance.
2. Say which is strongest and why (O–H present or not).
3. Link the strength to the energy needed to separate molecules.
4. If the Mr values differ, comment on London forces too.

## 15.11 Making carboxylic acids

| Starting material | Reagents and conditions | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Primary alcohol | excess K₂Cr₂O₇ with dilute H₂SO₄, heat under reflux | RCH₂OH + 2[O] → RCOOH + H₂O |
| Aldehyde | same | RCHO + [O] → RCOOH |
| Nitrile | dilute HCl, heat under reflux | RCN + 2H₂O + HCl → RCOOH + NH₄Cl |

- The dichromate turns from orange to green.
- Distil instead of reflux and you collect the aldehyde, not the acid.
- A nitrile keeps the same number of carbons when it is hydrolysed: propanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CN, gives propanoic acid, CH₃CH₂COOH. Alkaline hydrolysis gives the salt plus NH₃; acidify afterwards to release the acid.

## 15.12 Reactions of carboxylic acids

| Reagent | Product | Observation or note |
|---|---|---|
| LiAlH₄ in dry ether | primary alcohol | RCOOH + 4[H] → RCH₂OH + H₂O |
| NaOH(aq) | salt + water | neutralisation, no visible change |
| Na₂CO₃ or NaHCO₃ | salt + water + CO₂ | effervescence; CO₂ turns limewater milky |
| PCl₅ (dry) | acyl chloride + POCl₃ + HCl | steamy fumes |
| alcohol + conc. H₂SO₄, warm | ester + water | reversible; fruity smell |

Equation pattern with a carbonate: **2**RCOOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2RCOONa + H₂O + CO₂.

## 15.14 Acyl chlorides

| Reacts with | Product | By-product |
|---|---|---|
| water | carboxylic acid | HCl |
| alcohol | ester | HCl |
| concentrated ammonia (2 mol) | amide, RCONH₂ | NH₄Cl |
| primary amine R′NH₂ (2 mol) | N-substituted amide, RCONHR′ | R′NH₃⁺Cl⁻ |

Worked reminder: CH₃CH₂COCl + 2CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₂CH₂CH₃ + CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻. The organic product is **N-propylpropanamide**.

Why acyl chlorides are more reactive: oxygen and chlorine both withdraw electrons from the C=O carbon, so it is strongly δ+ and attracts nucleophiles. Reactions are fast at room temperature and are not reversible.

## 15.15 Ester hydrolysis

| | Acidic | Alkaline |
|---|---|---|
| Reagent | dilute HCl or H₂SO₄ | NaOH(aq) |
| Conditions | heat under reflux | heat under reflux |
| Products | carboxylic acid + alcohol | carboxylate salt + alcohol |
| Extent | reversible, equilibrium | goes to completion |
| To get the free acid | already present | add strong acid afterwards |

Worked reminder: CH₃CH₂COO(CH₂)₄CH₃ + NaOH → CH₃CH₂COONa + CH₃(CH₂)₄OH (sodium propanoate and pentan-1-ol).

## 15.16 Polyesters

- **Condensation polymerisation**: bifunctional monomers join and a small molecule (H₂O, or HCl from a diacyl dichloride) is lost at every link.
- Polyester = diol + dicarboxylic acid, linked by –COO– groups.
- **Terylene**: ethane-1,2-diol + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid. Repeat unit –OC–C₆H₄–COO–CH₂CH₂–O–.

Method for drawing a repeat unit:

1. Draw each monomer with its functional groups at the ends.
2. Take OH from each COOH and H from each alcohol OH.
3. Join the C=O carbon to the diol oxygen.
4. Show one of each monomer residue, with open bonds at both ends.

To go backwards, cut each ester link between C=O carbon and O, then add OH to the carbon and H to the oxygen.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Addition vs condensation polymer**: addition polymers come from C=C monomers and lose nothing; polyesters lose a small molecule at each link.
- **[O] vs [H]**: 2[O] to oxidise a primary alcohol to an acid; 4[H] to reduce an acid to a primary alcohol.
- **PCl₅ test**: any –OH (alcohol or acid) gives steamy fumes. Use NaHCO₃ fizzing to pick out the acid.
- **Acid vs acyl chloride esterification**: the acid route is reversible and needs a catalyst; the acyl chloride route is fast and complete but releases HCl.
- **Amide vs ammonium salt**: one NH₃ becomes part of the amide; the other becomes NH₄Cl.

## Quick self-test

1. Name CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)COOH.
2. Use these boiling temperatures: ethanoic acid 118 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C, methyl methanoate 32 °C. All three have Mr 60.0. Explain the order.
3. Give the reagents and conditions to make propanoic acid from propan-1-ol.
4. Name the product when hexanoic acid reacts with LiAlH₄ in dry ether.
5. Write an equation for methanoic acid reacting with sodium hydrogencarbonate.
6. 0.0400 mol of methanoic acid reacts with excess sodium carbonate. Calculate the volume of CO₂ at room temperature and pressure. (Molar volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹)
7. Name HCOOCH₂CH(CH₃)₂ and name the acid and alcohol it comes from.
8. Name the organic product of pentanoyl chloride with methylamine.
9. State why alkaline hydrolysis of an ester goes to completion.
10. Name the monomers of a polyester with repeat unit –OCH₂CH₂OOC(CH₂)₂CO–.
11. Outline a three-step route from propan-1-ol to propyl propanoate that avoids an equilibrium in the last step.
12. Calculate the volume of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH that neutralises 2.59 g of propanoic acid. (Mr 74.0)

### Answers

1. **2-methylbutanoic acid**.
2. Methyl methanoate has no O–H, so only London forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces act. Propan-1-ol hydrogen bonds through its O–H. Ethanoic acid forms hydrogen-bonded dimers using O–H and C=O, so it needs the most energy to boil.
3. Excess potassium dichromate(VI) acidified with dilute sulfuric acid, heated under reflux.
4. **Hexan-1-ol**.
5. HCOOH + NaHCO₃ → HCOONa + H₂O + CO₂.
6. 2HCOOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2HCOONa + H₂O + CO₂. n(CO₂) = 0.0400 ÷ 2 = 0.0200 mol; volume = 0.0200 × 24.0 = **0.480 dm³**.
7. **2-methylpropyl methanoate**, from methanoic acid and 2-methylpropan-1-ol.
8. **N-methylpentanamide**, CH₃(CH₂)₃CONHCH₃.
9. The hydroxide turns the acid into a carboxylate ion. That ion cannot re-form the ester, so no back reaction takes place.
10. **Ethane-1,2-diol** and **butanedioic acid**.
11. Oxidise some propan-1-ol to propanoic acid (excess acidified dichromate, reflux). Convert the acid to propanoyl chloride with PCl₅. React the acyl chloride with more propan-1-ol at room temperature.
12. n = 2.59 ÷ 74.0 = 0.0350 mol, ratio 1 : 1; volume = 0.0350 ÷ 0.500 = 0.0700 dm³ = **70.0 cm³**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Numbering the C=O carbon in a name, or counting it as a side chain.
- Writing an ester name with the acid part first, or drawing the ester the wrong way round.
- Saying "aldehydes and ketones hydrogen bond to each other"; they only accept hydrogen bonds from water.
- Missing "under reflux" or "excess oxidising agent" when the target is the acid.
- Adding a carbon when a nitrile is hydrolysed: the acid has the same number of carbons as the nitrile.
- Using aqueous LiAlH₄ or leaving out "dry ether".
- Balancing a carbonate equation with one acid molecule instead of two.
- Writing HCl instead of NH₄Cl as the by-product with ammonia.
- Giving the acid, not the salt, as the product of alkaline hydrolysis.
- Drawing a polyester repeat unit with a full monomer and both end groups still attached.
