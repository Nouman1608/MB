## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between urbanisation and urban growth. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by urban metabolism. **[2]**

**3.** Table 1 shows population data for a fictional country.

| Year | 2000 | 2010 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total population (millions) | 40.0 | 46.0 | 52.0 |
| Urban population (millions) | 14.0 | 20.7 | 28.6 |

**(a)** Calculate the urban share in 2000 and in 2020. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage growth of the urban population from 2000 to 2020. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons for the change shown. **[2]**

**4.** A fictional city of 2.0 million people takes in 640,000 m³ of treated water a day. Leaks lose 15% before delivery, and 80% of the water delivered returns as wastewater.

**(a)** Calculate the water delivered per person per day, in litres. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of wastewater produced each day. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** ways the city could reduce its water input without cutting the water each resident uses. **[2]**

**5.** City R has 5.6 million people on 1,400 km². City S has 5.6 million people on 400 km².

**(a)** Calculate the population density of each city. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain which city is likely to use more transport energy per person. **[3]**

**6.** Evaluate the use of a green belt to manage the growth of a city. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how transit-oriented development can reduce a city's environmental impact. **[3]**

**8.** Explain **two** advantages of upgrading an informal settlement in place rather than relocating its residents. **[4]**

**9.** Sunlight of 900 W m⁻² falls on a dark roof of albedo 0.15 and on a cool roof of albedo 0.60.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage reduction in energy absorbed by using the cool roof. **[2]**
**(b)** Outline **one** other strategy that reduces the urban heat island. **[2]**

**10.** Table 2 shows park area in two districts of a fictional city.

| District | Population | Park area (km²) |
|---|---|---|
| North | 200,000 | 3.0 |
| South | 450,000 | 0.9 |

**(a)** Calculate the park area per person in each district, in m². **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the figure for the two districts combined. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the combined figure could mislead planners. **[2]**

**11.** Discuss how different stakeholders might view a proposal to build housing on greenfield land at the edge of a city. **[6]**

**12.** To what extent can urban planning make cities sustainable? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Urbanisation is an increase in the proportion of a population living in urban areas [1]; urban growth is an increase in the number of urban residents or the area of a city [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The key words are proportion versus number; both must appear.

**2.** A city takes in flows of water, food, energy and materials, transforms them through activity, and releases outputs such as waste, wastewater, pollutants and heat [1], in the way an organism takes in resources and excretes wastes, so the flows can be measured and managed [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming inputs and outputs without the idea of transformation earns 1.

**3. (a)** 2000: 14.0 ÷ 40.0 × 100 = **35%** [1]; 2020: 28.6 ÷ 52.0 × 100 = **55%** [1]
**(b)** (28.6 − 14.0) ÷ 14.0 × 100 = **104%** (to 3 s.f.) [1]
**(c)** Any two: rural–urban migration driven by jobs and services in cities [1]; natural increase within cities with young populations [1]; push factors such as mechanised farming or land degradation [1]; reclassification of growing villages as urban [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "People moved to cities" needs a reason to earn the mark.

**4. (a)** Delivered = 0.85 × 640,000 = 544,000 m³ [1]; 544,000,000 L ÷ 2,000,000 = **272 L per person per day** [1]
**(b)** 0.80 × 544,000 = **435,200 m³ per day** [1]
**(c)** Any two: repair leaking pipes, which waste 96,000 m³ a day [1]; treat and reuse wastewater for industry, parks or toilet flushing [1]; harvest rainwater from roofs [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Measures that ask residents to use less, such as hosepipe bans, do not answer the question as set.

**5. (a)** R: 5,600,000 ÷ 1,400 = **4,000 people per km²** [1]; S: 5,600,000 ÷ 400 = **14,000 people per km²** [1]
**(b)** City R [1]: at lower density homes, jobs and shops are further apart, so trips are longer [1], and fewer people live near each stop, so public transport is less frequent and more trips are made by car, raising fuel use per person [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The answer needs the chain density → distance and transit → car use → energy.

**6.** For: it protects farmland, habitats and recreation space near the city [1] and encourages reuse of brownfield land inside the city, keeping it compact [1]. Against: limiting land supply can raise house prices within the belt [1], and development may leapfrog to towns beyond it, increasing commuting distances and emissions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A brief judgement, such as "effective if paired with transport links and enough housing inside the belt", strengthens the answer.

**7.** Concentrating homes and jobs near stations means many people can reach daily needs by public transport or on foot [1]. This reduces car use, cutting fuel use, CO₂ and air pollutants per person [1], and compact growth takes less land at the urban edge, protecting habitats and farmland [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs an environmental outcome, not just "people use the bus".

**8.** Any two developed points, 2 marks each. Residents stay close to their jobs and markets [1], avoiding long, costly commutes from the city edge [1]. Social networks and community support are kept [1], which matter for childcare and informal work [1]. It is often cheaper than building new housing elsewhere [1], so limited funds can improve conditions for more people [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Advantages must be compared with relocation, not stated in isolation.

**9. (a)** Dark: 900 × 0.85 = 765 W m⁻²; cool: 900 × 0.40 = 360 W m⁻² [1]; reduction = 405 ÷ 765 × 100 = **52.9%** [1]
**(b)** For example, planting street trees or green roofs [1], which shade surfaces and cool the air by evapotranspiration [1]. Reducing waste heat from vehicles and air-conditioning also earns credit. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the absorbed values, not the albedo values, for the percentage.

**10. (a)** North: 3,000,000 m² ÷ 200,000 = **15 m²** [1]; South: 900,000 m² ÷ 450,000 = **2 m²** [1]
**(b)** 3,900,000 ÷ 650,000 = **6 m² per person** [1]
**(c)** The average hides a large inequality: South, with most of the people, has far less green space than the average suggests [1], so planners might wrongly conclude that provision is adequate and fail to target new parks where they are needed [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting to convert km² to m² (× 1,000,000) gives answers a million times too small.

**11.** Indicative points, up to 6.
- Developers: profitable, cheap land, easy to build on [1].
- Families seeking housing: more affordable, larger homes [1].
- Existing residents near the site: loss of open space, more traffic and noise [1].
- Farmers and conservation groups: loss of farmland and habitat; ecocentric objections [1].
- City planners: meets housing demand quickly but increases sprawl and car dependence, and new roads and services cost money [1].
- Balanced conclusion: support depends on whether brownfield sites are available and whether transport links are provided [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name each stakeholder and give a reason linked to their interests; a list of names earns little.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9. Real essays are marked holistically, so treat these as a guide.
- Planning shapes land use, density and transport, which drive much of a city's footprint [1].
- Compact, mixed-use, transit-oriented design cuts car use and energy per person [1].
- Examples such as Curitiba's bus rapid transit and corridor development show integrated planning working [1].
- Green infrastructure and cool roofs reduce heat, runoff and some pollution [1].
- Water reuse and leak repair make flows more circular [1].
- Limitation: cities depend on inputs from outside (food, energy, materials), so their footprint extends far beyond their boundaries [1].
- Limitation: planning needs funding, enforcement and political support; rapid growth can outpace it, producing informal settlements [1].
- Limitation: some outcomes, such as rising prices that displace poorer residents, undermine social sustainability [1].
- Judgement: planning can make cities much more sustainable, but full sustainability also needs changes in consumption and in energy and food systems beyond the city [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" requires a judgement; essays that only list strategies cannot reach the top band.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using urbanisation and urban growth interchangeably.
- Unit errors converting km² to m² or m³ to litres.
- Density answers that stop at "more people per km²" without explaining transport effects.
- Green belt or development answers with only one side.
- Stakeholder answers that list groups without their reasons.
- Essays with no named example and no judgement.
