Condensed notes for section 5.4 (Statement of financial position) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029, content statement 5.4.1. Worked examples on classifying items, financing a machine and reading two years are in the [statement of financial position study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-statement-of-financial-position/). Test yourself with the [statement of financial position practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-statement-of-financial-position-practice/). The combined [profit and loss and statement of financial position notes](/resources/igcse-business-0264-profit-and-loss-and-statement-of-financial-position-revision-notes/) also cover section 5.3.

All examples are invented. Money is in dollars.

## What the statement is

- A snapshot of what a business **owns** (assets) and **owes** (liabilities) on one date.
- Compare: the statement of profit or loss covers a **period**.
- You will not be asked to construct one, but you must classify items, calculate from it and decide with it.

## The four elements

| Element | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | Owned and used for more than a year | Land and buildings, machinery, vehicles |
| Current assets | Cash, or will become cash within a year | Inventory, trade receivables, cash |
| Non-current liabilities | Owed, due after more than a year | Bank loans |
| Current liabilities | Owed, due within a year | Trade payables, overdraft |

**One-year rule:** current = within a year; non-current = longer than a year.

**Receivable vs payable:**

- Trade receivables: customers owe *us* (asset).
- Trade payables: *we* owe suppliers (liability).

## Formulae

```
Total assets        = non-current assets + current assets
Total liabilities   = non-current liabilities + current liabilities
Working capital     = current assets - current liabilities   (given in the syllabus)
Capital employed    = total assets - current liabilities     (standard form)
```

### Worked reminder

Haverton Print: machinery 48,000; premises 110,000; inventory 9,000; trade receivables 6,500; cash 3,500; trade payables 8,000; overdraft 4,000; bank loan (6 years) 40,000.

```
Non-current assets  = 110,000 + 48,000      = 158,000
Current assets      = 9,000 + 6,500 + 3,500 =  19,000
Total assets        = 158,000 + 19,000      = 177,000
Current liabilities = 8,000 + 4,000         =  12,000
Total liabilities   = 12,000 + 40,000       =  52,000
Working capital     = 19,000 - 12,000       =   7,000
Capital employed    = 177,000 - 12,000      = 165,000
```

## Working capital: reading it

| Situation | Meaning | Possible action |
|---|---|---|
| Positive, with a sensible amount of cash | Can pay short-term debts | None needed |
| Negative | Current liabilities exceed current assets; may not pay suppliers or bills on time | Reduce inventory, collect receivables faster, use long-term finance instead of overdraft |
| Positive but mostly inventory | Cash may still be short | Sell surplus stock, order less |
| Very large | Money tied up and not earning | Use surplus cash to invest or repay debt |

## Decision checks

- **Financing a long-term asset:** a loan keeps working capital the same; an overdraft cuts it.
- **Rising inventory with falling cash:** stock may not be selling.
- **Rising receivables:** customers may be slow to pay.
- **A new overdraft appearing:** short-term borrowing is filling a cash gap.
- Always add something the statement does not show: interest costs, sales trends, why figures changed.

### Worked reminder: the effect of a choice

Haverton Print (above) buys a $10,000 printer. Paid by overdraft: current liabilities become 22,000, so working capital = 19,000 - 22,000 = -3,000. Paid by a four-year loan: non-current liabilities rise to 50,000 and working capital stays at 7,000. Total assets rise by $10,000 either way, because the printer is a non-current asset.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Asset | Something a business owns |
| Liability | Something a business owes |
| Non-current asset | Asset kept and used for more than a year |
| Current asset | Cash or an asset that will become cash within a year |
| Trade receivables | Money owed to the business by customers who bought on credit |
| Trade payables | Money the business owes suppliers for goods bought on credit |
| Working capital | Current assets minus current liabilities |
| Capital employed | The long-term finance used in the business: total assets minus current liabilities |

## Exam traps

- An overdraft is a **current** liability.
- Include every current liability (payables *and* overdraft) in working capital.
- Positive working capital does not mean plenty of cash; check the cash line.
- Do not mix up "statement of financial position" with "statement of profit or loss".
- When classifying, use the four headings exactly as the syllabus does.

## Quick self-test

1. What does a statement of financial position show?
2. Give two examples of non-current assets.
3. Give two examples of current assets.
4. Is a bank loan repayable in eight years a current or non-current liability?
5. Is an overdraft a current or non-current liability?
6. What are trade receivables?
7. State the formula for working capital.
8. Current assets are $45,000 and current liabilities are $30,000. Calculate working capital.
9. Total assets are $260,000 and current liabilities are $35,000. Calculate capital employed.
10. Non-current liabilities are $80,000 and current liabilities are $22,000. Calculate total liabilities.
11. Why might a business with positive working capital still struggle to pay wages?
12. Why is a bank loan usually better than an overdraft for buying new machinery?

### Answers

1. What a business owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) at one point in time.
2. Any two: land and buildings, machinery, vehicles.
3. Any two: inventory, trade receivables, cash.
4. Non-current.
5. Current.
6. Money owed to the business by customers who bought on credit.
7. Working capital = current assets - current liabilities.
8. 45,000 - 30,000 = $15,000.
9. 260,000 - 35,000 = $225,000.
10. 80,000 + 22,000 = $102,000.
11. Most of its current assets may be inventory or trade receivables, which take time to turn into cash, so cash itself may be low.
12. The machine is used for years, so long-term finance matches it; a loan does not reduce working capital, whereas an overdraft increases current liabilities and can make working capital negative.
