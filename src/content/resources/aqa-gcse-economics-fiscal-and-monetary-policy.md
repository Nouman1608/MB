Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy. Monetary policy is the use of interest rates (and the supply of money) to influence the economy; in the UK it is carried out by the Bank of England. AQA GCSE Economics (8136) sections 3.2.3.1 and 3.2.3.2 ask you to explain how each policy changes income and spending, how each can be used to reach the government's objectives, and what a balanced budget, a surplus and a deficit mean.

Both sections are in 3.2 How the economy works, assessed on Paper 2, with no tiers. Countries and numbers are invented unless a real UK fact is stated. ## What is fiscal policy?

Fiscal policy uses two tools: **government spending** (on health, education, defence, benefits and infrastructure) and **taxation** (income tax, VAT, corporation tax and others). Changing either alters the total amount of spending in the economy.

| Change | Effect on spending | Typical aim |
|---|---|---|
| Raise government spending | Higher demand directly, and households receive more income | More growth, lower unemployment |
| Cut income tax | Households have more disposable income to spend | More growth, lower unemployment |
| Cut government spending | Lower demand | Lower inflation, smaller deficit |
| Raise taxes | Less disposable income, so less spending | Lower inflation, smaller deficit |

**Expansionary** fiscal policy (more spending or lower taxes) raises demand. **Contractionary** fiscal policy (less spending or higher taxes) reduces it.

## How does fiscal policy help achieve the government's objectives?

- **Unemployment and growth:** in a downturn, extra spending on projects such as roads creates jobs directly, and workers spend their wages, raising demand further.
- **Inflation:** if demand is rising too fast, higher taxes reduce spending and ease the pressure on prices.
- **Distribution of income:** progressive taxes and benefits narrow the gap between rich and poor.
- **Externalities and the environment:** taxes on harmful goods and subsidies for beneficial ones change behaviour.

Fiscal policy has limits. Changes to taxes and spending take time to plan and pass. Higher spending may have to be financed by borrowing. Tax cuts may be saved rather than spent.

## What is a balanced budget, a surplus and a deficit?

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balanced budget | Government spending equals government revenue |
| Budget surplus | Revenue is greater than spending |
| Budget deficit | Spending is greater than revenue, so the government must borrow |

### Worked example (illustrative)

Country F's government collects 860 billion units in revenue and spends 910 billion units. Budget balance = 860 − 910 = **−50**, a **deficit** of 50 billion units that must be borrowed.

### Consequences of a deficit

- Borrowing adds to the national debt, and interest must be paid on it, money that cannot be spent on services.
- In a downturn a deficit can support demand and jobs.
- A long-running deficit may require future tax rises or spending cuts.

### Consequences of a surplus

- The government can repay debt or save for the future.
- But a surplus means taking more out of the economy in tax than is spent, which reduces demand and could slow growth.

## What is monetary policy?

Monetary policy changes the cost and availability of money to influence spending. The main tool is the **interest rate**. In the UK, the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee sets the Bank Rate, which affects the interest rates banks charge borrowers and pay savers. The government sets the Bank of England an inflation target measured by the Consumer Price Index.

## How does monetary policy control inflation?

If inflation is above target, the Bank of England can **raise interest rates**:

1. Borrowing becomes more expensive, so households take out fewer loans and mortgages cost more.
2. Saving becomes more attractive, so people spend less.
3. Firms invest less because loans cost more.
4. Total spending falls, so there is less upward pressure on prices and inflation slows.

If inflation is low and the economy is weak, the Bank can **cut interest rates** to encourage borrowing and spending.

## How can monetary policy help other objectives?

- **Growth and employment:** lower interest rates raise spending and investment, so firms produce more and hire more workers.
- **Balance of payments:** higher interest rates reduce spending on imports. Interest rates can also affect the exchange rate, which changes the prices of exports and imports.

The same tool can help one objective and harm another. Raising rates to control inflation can slow growth and increase unemployment, which is a classic conflict between objectives.

## Fiscal or monetary policy: which is better?

| | Fiscal policy | Monetary policy |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | The government | The Bank of England (in the UK) |
| Main tools | Taxes and government spending | Interest rates |
| Speed | Slow to change; needs planning | Can be changed quickly, though effects take time |
| Targeting | Can target particular groups or regions | Affects everyone who borrows or saves |
| Main risk | Higher borrowing and debt | Hurts borrowers; may not work if confidence is low |

In evaluation, say which policy suits the situation. For example, a targeted spending programme may help a region with high unemployment, while an interest-rate rise is a faster way to cool inflation across the whole economy.

## Common errors

- Confusing fiscal policy (taxes and spending) with monetary policy (interest rates).
- Saying the government sets UK interest rates; the Bank of England does.
- Confusing the budget deficit (one year) with the national debt (the total built up over time).
- Describing a policy's intended effect without showing the chain of reasoning.
- Forgetting that a policy aimed at one objective can harm another.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a budget deficit the same as national debt?** No. The deficit is the gap between spending and revenue in one year; the debt is the total amount the government owes from past borrowing.

**Why do higher interest rates reduce inflation?** They make borrowing dearer and saving more rewarding, so spending falls and firms have less room to raise prices.

**Can fiscal policy reduce inequality?** Yes. Progressive taxes and benefits for low-income households redistribute income.

**Which policy works faster?** Interest rates can be changed at short notice, while tax and spending changes take longer to plan, although both take time to affect the economy fully.
