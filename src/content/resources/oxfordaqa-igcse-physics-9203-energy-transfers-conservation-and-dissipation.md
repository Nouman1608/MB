This guide covers **3.2.2 Energy transfers, conservation and dissipation of energy** (statements a to g) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Statement b (the simple pendulum) is marked P. The [energy transfers, efficiency and Sankey diagrams guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-energy-transfers-efficiency-sankey-diagrams-and-energy/) introduces each statement alongside energy resources; this page goes deeper into tracing energy through multi-stage systems, using conservation to find speeds and heights, accounting for dissipated energy, and drawing Sankey diagrams to scale. Quick recall is in the [energy transfers revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-energy-transfers-conservation-and-dissipation-revision-notes/) and exam-style questions are in the [energy transfers practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-energy-transfers-conservation-and-dissipation-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | When a system changes, energy is transferred; a system is an object or group of objects; identify where energy is transferred using kinetic, gravitational potential and elastic potential energy |
| b | A simple pendulum oscillates, transferring energy between kinetic and gravitational potential energy (P) |
| c | Energy can be transferred usefully, stored or dissipated, but cannot be created or destroyed |
| d | Only part of the energy is usually transferred usefully; the rest is dissipated and stored in less useful ways ("wasted") |
| e | Friction and air resistance dissipate energy by heating the surroundings |
| f | Efficiency = useful energy out ÷ total energy in, or useful power out ÷ total power in, as a decimal or percentage |
| g | Draw and interpret Sankey diagrams showing how energy is redistributed with no net change to the total |

## Choosing the system

A **system** is the object or group of objects you decide to study. Defining it first makes energy questions clearer. For a ball thrown upwards, the system "ball and Earth" stores gravitational potential energy; for a car braking, the system "car, brakes and road" includes the heating of the brakes. Energy can move between parts of the system, and some can leave it, for example by heating the air.

## Tracing energy through a multi-stage system

The skill in statement a is to say **when** and **where** energy is transferred, naming the stores involved. Use the three stores the specification names, kinetic (Ek), gravitational potential (Ep) and elastic potential (Ee), and say where energy is dissipated.

**Worked example 1: a bungee jump.**

| Stage | Main energy change |
|---|---|
| Falling before the cord tightens | Ep of the jumper decreases; Ek increases |
| Cord stretching | Ek and Ep decrease; Ee of the cord increases |
| Lowest point | Ek is zero for an instant; Ee is at its maximum |
| Bouncing back up | Ee decreases; Ep and Ek increase |
| Throughout | Air resistance and internal friction in the cord dissipate energy by heating the surroundings, so each bounce is lower |

## The simple pendulum (P)

A simple pendulum is a small mass (the bob) on a string, swinging from a fixed point. It is an example of **oscillating motion**.

- At each end of the swing the bob is momentarily still and at its highest point: Ek = 0, Ep is greatest.
- At the bottom the bob moves fastest and is lowest: Ek is greatest, Ep is least.
- Between, energy is transferring between the two stores.

If no energy were dissipated, the bob would keep rising to the same height. In practice air resistance and friction at the pivot dissipate a little energy each swing, so the amplitude slowly decreases.

**Worked example 2: pendulum speed.** A bob of mass 0.50 kg is released from rest 0.12 m above its lowest point. Take g = 9.8 N/kg and assume no energy is dissipated during one swing.

```
Ep lost = m g h = 0.50 x 9.8 x 0.12 = 0.588 J
Ek at the bottom = 0.588 J
1/2 x 0.50 x v^2 = 0.588
v^2 = 2.352, so v = 1.53 m/s, about 1.5 m/s
```

The mass cancels out: any bob released from 0.12 m would reach the same speed, if nothing is dissipated.

## Conservation: energy cannot be created or destroyed

Statement c is the principle behind every calculation in this section. The total energy after a change equals the total before. If a question says some energy is "wasted", that energy still exists; it has been dissipated, usually by heating the surroundings.

**Worked example 3: a roller coaster with friction.** A roller coaster car of mass 500 kg starts from rest at the top of a 20 m drop. At the bottom its kinetic energy is measured as 85 000 J. Take g = 9.8 N/kg.

```
Ep lost = 500 x 9.8 x 20 = 98 000 J
energy dissipated = 98 000 - 85 000 = 13 000 J
speed at bottom: 1/2 x 500 x v^2 = 85 000, so v^2 = 340, v = 18.4 m/s
efficiency of the transfer = 85 000 / 98 000 = 0.867, about 87%
```

The 13 000 J has not disappeared. Friction between wheels and track and air resistance have transferred it to the surroundings, which become very slightly warmer.

## Dissipation and why "wasted" energy is less useful

Dissipated energy spreads out into the surroundings, usually as a small temperature rise of a large amount of air or material. It is still stored, but it is so spread out that it is hard to use for anything. That is what statement d means by "stored in less useful ways".

Ways to reduce unwanted dissipation include lubricating moving parts (less friction), streamlining (less air resistance) and thermal insulation (less heating of the surroundings). Each reduction increases the efficiency of the device.

## Efficiency

```
efficiency = useful energy out / total energy in
efficiency = useful power out / total power in
```

Multiply by 100 for a percentage. Efficiency has no unit, and it can never be greater than 1 (or 100%), because a device cannot output more energy than it receives.

**Worked example 4: a winch lifting a load.** An electric winch is supplied with 15 000 J of energy and lifts a 120 kg crate through 8.0 m. Take g = 9.8 N/kg.

```
useful energy out = Ep gained = 120 x 9.8 x 8.0 = 9408 J
efficiency = 9408 / 15 000 = 0.627, about 0.63 or 63%
energy dissipated = 15 000 - 9408 = 5592 J
```

The useful output here is calculated from the Ep equation, so the question combines 3.2.1 and 3.2.2.

## Sankey diagrams drawn to scale

A Sankey diagram shows energy flow with arrows whose **widths** are proportional to the amounts of energy. The input arrow enters from the left; the useful output continues to the right; wasted energy bends away, usually downwards. Because energy is conserved, the widths of the outputs add up to the width of the input.

**Worked example 5: drawing a kettle diagram.** A kettle receives 2000 J and transfers 1800 J to the water; the rest heats the kettle body and the air. Use a scale of 1 mm to 20 J.

```
input width  = 2000 / 20 = 100 mm
useful width = 1800 / 20 = 90 mm
wasted width = 200 / 20  = 10 mm
efficiency   = 1800 / 2000 = 0.90 (90%)
```

To **interpret** a diagram, measure each width, convert with the scale, check that the outputs add up to the input, and calculate the efficiency from the useful arrow.

## Common mistakes

- Saying energy is "used up" or "lost". It is transferred or dissipated.
- Giving efficiency greater than 1, or with a unit.
- Using total energy in place of useful energy in the numerator.
- Drawing Sankey arrows whose output widths do not add up to the input.
- Forgetting to square v in ½ m v².
- Stating that a pendulum's energy disappears as it slows; it is dissipated by air resistance and friction.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** the energy transfers: name each store at each stage and the direction of change.
- **Explain** why a device is not 100% efficient: name the dissipating force and the result (heating the surroundings).
- **Calculate**: state the equation, substitute, and give the answer as a decimal or percentage as asked.
- **Draw** a Sankey diagram: state the scale, label each arrow with its energy and type.
