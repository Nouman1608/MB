This guide goes deeper into one section of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017: 3.1.5 Business location. The specification places it in Topic 1, Business in the real world, which can be assessed on either written paper. The wider topic page, [Business location and Business planning](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-business-location-and-business-planning/), introduces each factor. This page shows how the factors interact, how they differ between types of business, and how to turn them into a justified decision.

When you have worked through it, condense it with the [Business location revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-business-location-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [Business location practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-business-location-aqa-gcse-business-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

The specification has one content statement, "Factors influencing the location decision of a business", and says students should "understand the factors that influence where a business is located, including proximity to the market, availability of raw materials, labour, competition and costs."

Two words in that sentence matter. "Including" means the five factors are the core list, but a case study may raise something else, such as transport links or room to expand, and you can use it. "Influence" means no single factor decides the location on its own: your job is to judge which one matters most for the business in front of you.

## Why location is a big decision

A location decision is hard to reverse. Moving means new premises, fitting out, possibly losing staff who cannot travel, and telling customers where you have gone. It also locks in costs for years, because leases often run for a long period. That is why a business weighs the factors before it commits, and why examiners reward answers that think about the future as well as the present: will the market grow, will rents rise, will a rival arrive?

Location affects all four functions of a business:

- **Operations:** how far materials travel in and goods travel out, and whether the site is big enough.
- **Human resources:** whether enough workers with the right skills can reach the site.
- **Marketing:** whether customers can find and reach the business, and how many pass by.
- **Finance:** rent, business rates, transport and wage costs, all of which affect profit.

## The five factors in more depth

### Proximity to the market

The pull of the market is strongest when the customer comes to the business or needs the product quickly. Three types of business feel it most:

- **Services delivered face to face**, such as dentists, gyms and hairdressers. Customers will not travel far for a routine service.
- **Shops that depend on passing trade**, such as sandwich bars and newsagents. Footfall, the number of people walking past, turns directly into sales.
- **Producers of goods that are fresh, fragile or bulky to deliver**, such as bakeries, florists and makers of bottled drinks. A short journey protects quality and keeps delivery costs down.

The pull is weaker for a business that sells online and posts small, light items. It can locate where costs are lower, provided it has good links to couriers.

### Availability of raw materials

A business that uses heavy or bulky inputs, and produces something lighter, saves most by locating near its supply. A sawmill near a forest is a classic case: the logs are heavy, and much of their weight is cut away before the timber is sent on. A business whose inputs spoil quickly, such as a fruit-juice maker, also gains from being close to its growers. When raw materials are light and easy to transport, this factor matters little.

### Labour

Ask three questions about labour: are there enough workers, do they have the right skills, and what wage will the business have to pay? A town with high unemployment may offer plenty of applicants at lower wages. A region where an industry has grown up may offer skilled workers but at higher pay, because many employers compete for them. If the right workers are scarce, the business may have to pay more, train new staff or accept slower growth.

### Competition

Competition can attract or repel. Some businesses gain from being near rivals: customers who want to compare, such as people buying a car or choosing a restaurant, go where there is choice, so a cluster draws more customers than any one business could alone. Others gain from distance: a convenience shop in a new housing estate with no rival nearby captures most local spending. The risk of an empty area is that nobody is there because demand is too low.

### Costs

Location costs include rent or purchase price, business rates, utilities, transport of goods in and out, and wages. Costs tend to be highest where demand is highest, so a busy high street usually costs more than an industrial estate. The real question is whether the extra sales a site brings are bigger than its extra costs.

## How the factors pull against each other

The factors rarely point to the same site. The most common conflicts are:

| Conflict | Example | How to resolve it in an answer |
|---|---|---|
| Market against costs | A busy street brings sales but has high rent | Compare extra revenue with extra cost |
| Raw materials against market | A food producer near farms is far from city customers | Ask which journey is more costly or damaging to quality |
| Labour against costs | A skilled area has high wages; a cheaper area lacks skills | Ask whether the business can train staff or must hire ready-skilled |
| Competition against market | A cluster has customers but also rivals | Ask whether customers want to compare or simply want convenience |

## Worked example 1: a manufacturer

Figures in this example are invented. Calder Stone makes garden paving slabs from crushed rock. The rock is heavy and the slabs are heavy too, so transport matters at both ends. It compares two sites.

| Monthly figure | Site X: near the quarry | Site Y: near the city, its main market |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | £3,000 | £4,800 |
| Transport of rock to site | £1,200 | £4,000 |
| Delivery of slabs to customers | £3,600 | £1,500 |
| Total of these costs | £7,800 | £10,300 |

Site X costs £10,300 − £7,800 = £2,500 a month less, which is £30,000 a year. The rock is heavier than the finished slabs, because some is lost when they are cut, so carrying it to the city costs more than carrying slabs out. A strong answer then checks labour: if Site X is in a remote valley with few workers, the saving may be partly used up by higher wages or recruitment costs. The decision would change if most customers were trade buyers who needed same-day delivery in the city.

## Worked example 2: an annotated paragraph

Question: *Explain one reason why a dental practice might choose a site close to the market.*

Model answer: "Patients usually visit the practice for regular check-ups, so they prefer one they can reach easily from home or work (point). A practice close to a large residential area will therefore attract more patients (development), which increases its revenue and makes it easier to fill appointment slots every day (consequence)."

Why it works: it names the factor, applies it to a dental practice rather than any business, and ends with an effect on revenue. Writing "because it is near customers" alone would repeat the question.

## Exam technique: command words

- **State or identify:** name a factor; no explanation is needed.
- **Explain:** give the factor, then why it matters to this business, then the effect on sales or costs.
- **Analyse:** build a longer chain, for example raw materials → transport cost → unit cost → price or profit.
- **Evaluate or justify:** weigh at least two factors, use any figures, decide which site is better and say what would change your view.
- **Recommend:** choose one option and support it, while acknowledging the main risk.

Use the case study's details. If the business sells online, say why proximity to the market matters less. If it uses bulky inputs, mention transport. Examiners reward application to the business, not general lists.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the five factors as a checklist to write out in full. Choose the two or three that matter most here.
- Saying "near the market" for a business that sells online without explaining what still matters, such as courier access.
- Assuming competitors always harm a business. Clusters can bring in customers.
- Ignoring the future: a cheap site in an area where demand is falling may be a poor long-term choice.
- Comparing only rent. Transport and wages can outweigh it, as Calder Stone shows.
- Ending an evaluation with "it depends" and no decision.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which location factors does AQA GCSE Business (8132) name?**
Proximity to the market, availability of raw materials, labour, competition and costs. The word "including" means a case study may add others, such as transport links.

**Why does an online business care less about proximity to the market?**
Its customers do not visit, so it can choose a cheaper site. It still needs good road and courier links so that orders arrive quickly.

**Can being close to competitors help a business?**
Yes. Where customers like to compare, such as restaurants or car dealers, a cluster attracts more visitors than a single business could, although each business must then win its share.
