This set covers subtopic 7.2, Energy sources -- uses and management, from Topic 7 of IB DP Environmental Systems and Societies, first assessment 2026. Every question suits SL and HL. All data and countries in tables are fictional; take a year as 8,760 hours. Learn the content first in the [7.2 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-7-2/) and the [7.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-7-2-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between primary energy and secondary energy. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** energy sources that are dispatchable and **two** that are variable. **[2]**

**3.** A fictional gas-fired power station receives 1,500 MJ of fuel energy and delivers 825 MJ of electricity.

**(a)** Calculate its efficiency. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy lost as waste heat. **[1]**
**(c)** Outline how combined heat and power could increase the useful energy obtained from the same fuel. **[2]**

**4.** Table 1 shows two fictional wind farm proposals.

| Proposal | Rated capacity (MW) | Capacity factor |
|---|---|---|
| Ridge site | 40 | 0.38 |
| Plain site | 60 | 0.24 |

**(a)** Calculate the annual output of each proposal in MWh. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the proposal with the larger capacity produces less energy. **[2]**

**5.** Outline **two** ways in which an electricity grid can cope with a high share of wind and solar power. **[4]**

**6.** Table 2 shows the energy supply of a fictional country, Country T, in one year.

| Item | Energy (PJ) |
|---|---|
| Total primary energy supply | 2,500 |
| Domestic production | 1,000 |
| Imports from Supplier X | 900 |
| Imports from Supplier Y | 450 |
| Imports from Supplier Z | 150 |

**(a)** Calculate Country T's import dependency. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of its imports that come from Supplier X. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate Country T's energy security. **[3]**

**7.** Explain why switching from petrol cars to electric cars may not greatly reduce a country's CO₂ emissions. **[2]**

**8.** Explain, with reference to **one** named country, how the availability of natural resources influences its choice of energy sources. **[3]**

**9.** A fictional office replaces 200 lights rated at 58 W with LED lights rated at 25 W. The lights are used for 2,500 hours a year.

**(a)** Calculate the energy saved each year in kWh. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the office's total energy use might not fall by this amount. **[2]**

**10.** Distinguish between energy efficiency and energy conservation. **[2]**

**11.** Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of nuclear power for a country that wants to cut its greenhouse gas emissions. **[6]**

**12.** To what extent can a country rely on renewable energy sources to achieve energy security? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary energy is energy in its natural form, such as coal, wind or sunlight [1]; secondary energy is a carrier produced from primary sources, such as electricity or petrol [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An example alone does not define the term; give the meaning, then the example.

**2.** Dispatchable: any two of gas, coal, oil, nuclear, reservoir hydro, biomass, geothermal [1]. Variable: any two of wind, solar, wave [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs both items of the pair correct.

**3. (a)** 825 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **55%** [1]
**(b)** 1,500 − 825 = **675 MJ** [1]
**(c)** The waste heat is captured, for example from cooling water [1], and piped to heat nearby homes or factories, so more of the fuel's energy is used for the same emissions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* CHP does not make the generator itself more efficient; it uses the heat output.

**4. (a)** Ridge: 40 × 8,760 × 0.38 = **133,152 MWh** [1]; Plain: 60 × 8,760 × 0.24 = **126,144 MWh** [1]
**(b)** Output depends on how often and how strongly the wind blows, not only on rated capacity [1]; the ridge site's higher capacity factor (0.38 against 0.24) means its turbines run nearer full power for more of the year, which outweighs its 20 MW smaller capacity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote both capacity factors; "the ridge is windier" without data earns one mark.

**5.** Any two developed points, 2 marks each. Storage such as pumped hydro or batteries [1] stores surplus power when output is high and releases it when output is low [1]. Interconnectors [1] let a region import power when its own wind or sun is weak [1]. A diverse mix [1] smooths output because wind and solar often peak at different times [1]. Demand management [1] shifts flexible use, such as vehicle charging, to times of high supply [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a method earns 1; the second mark needs how it balances supply and demand.

**6. (a)** Net imports = 900 + 450 + 150 = 1,500 PJ; 1,500 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = **60%** [1]
**(b)** 900 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **60%** [1]
**(c)** Country T is fairly insecure because 60% of its supply is imported [1], and 60% of those imports come from one supplier, so a dispute or disruption with Supplier X could cut over a third of total supply (900 ÷ 2,500 = 36%) [1]. Security would depend also on storage, the stability of the suppliers and whether the domestic 40% could be expanded, so a judgement is conditional on information not given [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers use both calculated figures and say what further information would change the judgement.

**7.** Electricity is a secondary carrier, so the emissions depend on how it is generated [1]; if most electricity comes from coal or gas, emissions move from car exhausts to power stations rather than disappearing, although local air quality improves [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Mentioning manufacturing of batteries is valid, but the expected point is the generating mix.

**8.** Iceland [1]: its volcanic geology gives abundant geothermal heat close to the surface, and its rivers and glaciers provide water for hydroelectric power [1], so it generates almost all its electricity from these two sources rather than imported fossil fuels [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Other countries are credited if the resource and the outcome are linked correctly; a generic answer with no named country loses the first mark.

**9. (a)** Saving per light = 58 − 25 = 33 W; 200 × 33 × 2,500 = 16,500,000 Wh [1] = **16,500 kWh** [1]
**(b)** The rebound effect: cheaper lighting may lead staff to leave lights on longer or install more of them [1], so some of the saving is used up by greater use of the service [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by 1,000 converts Wh to kWh; forgetting it gives an answer a thousand times too large.

**10.** Energy efficiency means obtaining the same service with less energy, such as insulating a building [1]; energy conservation means using less of the service, such as heating rooms to a lower temperature [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Saving energy" describes both; the contrast is technology versus behaviour.

**11.** Indicative points, up to 6.
- Very low operational CO₂ emissions, so it can replace coal or gas [1].
- Dispatchable and reliable, providing steady output that complements variable renewables [1].
- Uranium is energy-dense and can be stockpiled, supporting energy security [1].
- Radioactive waste stays hazardous for very long periods and needs secure long-term storage [1].
- High build cost and long construction times delay emission cuts [1].
- Accident risk and public opposition can stop programmes, as Germany's closure of its last plants in April 2023 showed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced answer needs both sides; a list of disadvantages alone caps at 3.

**12.** Indicative points, one mark each up to 9. Real essays are marked holistically, so treat these as a guide.
- Energy security means reliable, affordable access to enough energy [1].
- Domestic renewables reduce import dependency and exposure to fuel price shocks [1].
- Renewables cannot be cut off by a supplier and have no fuel cost [1].
- Some renewables are dispatchable, such as reservoir hydro and geothermal, as Iceland shows [1].
- Wind and solar are variable, so reliability needs storage, interconnectors or backup [1].
- Interconnectors can bring back dependence on neighbours, and panels, turbines and batteries may be imported, along with their minerals [1].
- Suitability depends on geography: not every country has strong wind, sunshine, rivers or geothermal heat [1].
- Large investment and time are needed to build capacity and grids [1].
- Judgement: renewables can greatly improve security where resources suit them, but most countries will rely on a diverse mix with storage and some dispatchable capacity for the foreseeable future [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" needs a stated, conditional judgement; arguing only that renewables are good for the climate misses the security focus.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing power (MW) with energy (MWh), or forgetting the capacity factor.
- Calling electricity a source of energy.
- Calculating import dependency from gross imports when exports are given; use net imports.
- Explaining security only as "using renewables".
- Ignoring the rebound effect in efficiency questions.
- One-sided discussions of nuclear power or renewables.
