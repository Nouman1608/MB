AQA GCSE Economics (8136) is assessed through two written papers, not tiered: Paper 1 (How markets
work, Content 1-6, 1h45, 80 marks, 50%) and Paper 2 (How the economy works, Content 7-11, 1h45, 80
marks, 50%). These notes turn that structure into an exam-day plan, alongside the
[How Markets Work](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-how-markets-work/) and
[Interest Rates](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-interest-rates-revision-notes/) topic resources
already on the site.

## Both papers draw on the whole course, not just their own content block

Although Paper 1 is titled "How markets work" and Paper 2 "How the economy works," both papers
explicitly draw on knowledge from the entire specification, not only their own named content block.
**Exam-preparation priority**: do not revise Paper 1's and Paper 2's content in complete isolation
from each other -- a Paper 1 question can reasonably expect understanding of a macroeconomic concept
introduced in the Paper 2 content block, and vice versa, so keep whole-course revision active for
both papers.

## AO1, AO2 and AO3: know which skill a question is testing

Both papers are marked against three assessment objectives, roughly AO1 35% (knowledge and
understanding), AO2 35% (application of economic concepts and terminology), and AO3 30% (analysis and
evaluation of economic issues and arguments). **Exam-preparation priority**: because AO2 and AO3
together outweigh AO1, revision that is only definition-based -- reciting what a term means without
applying it to a given scenario or evaluating an argument -- leaves the majority of the marks
unpractised.

## Multiple-choice questions still test genuine understanding

Both papers open with a multiple-choice section before moving into calculation, short-answer and
extended-response questions. **Exam-preparation priority**: treat multiple-choice practice as a
genuine test of understanding, not a guessing exercise -- multiple-choice questions on this
specification are frequently built around applying a concept to a scenario, not simple recall, so the
same revision that prepares extended-response answers also strengthens multiple-choice performance.

## Extended-response questions reward a reasoned judgement, not just description

The highest-tariff questions on each paper require an extended response that reaches a justified
conclusion, weighing more than one side of an economic argument. **Exam-preparation priority**:
practise a consistent extended-response structure -- state the economic concept, apply it to the
given context, consider a counterargument or limitation, then reach an explicit judgement -- rather
than describing the concept alone and stopping short of a conclusion.

## Worked practice scenario: a structured extended-response plan

Question: "Discuss whether raising the minimum wage is likely to reduce unemployment among low-paid
workers."

```
Step 1 - state the relevant economic concept:
A minimum wage set above the market equilibrium wage can create
excess supply of labour (more workers willing to work than employers
demand at that wage), which classical economic theory suggests could
raise unemployment among low-paid workers.

Step 2 - apply it to the specific question (not just the theory):
If a minimum wage rise pushes labour costs above what some employers
in low-margin sectors can afford, those employers may reduce hours or
hiring specifically among low-paid roles.

Step 3 - counterargument (AO3 evaluation, not just AO1 description):
However, higher wages can also increase workers' spending power,
raising demand for goods and services, which could support employment
elsewhere in the economy -- and some employers may absorb the cost
through reduced profit margins rather than cutting jobs.

Step 4 - reach a justified conclusion:
The overall employment effect depends on factors such as how far
above equilibrium the new minimum wage is set and how flexible
employers' costs are elsewhere -- a moderate rise is less likely to
cause significant job losses than a large one.
```

This four-step structure keeps the answer from stopping at description (Step 1) by requiring an
application (Step 2), a genuine counterargument (Step 3), and an explicit judgement (Step 4) --
exactly the combination "discuss" is testing, and the same structure any extended-response question
on this specification rewards.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: revise both papers' content together, not as two isolated blocks; practise
  multiple-choice questions as genuine understanding checks, not guesswork; build a bank of the
  four-step extended-response structure (concept, application, counterargument, judgement) and
  rehearse it across different topics.
- **During Paper 1**: be ready to draw on macroeconomic concepts even though the paper is titled
  "How markets work."
- **During Paper 2**: be ready to draw on microeconomic concepts even though the paper is titled "How
  the economy works."
- **On every paper**: for extended-response questions, check your answer includes a counterargument
  and a final judgement, not just a one-sided explanation.

## Self-test

1. Why should Paper 1 and Paper 2 content not be revised in complete isolation from each other?
2. Which two assessment objectives, combined, outweigh AO1?
3. What four elements does a strong extended-response answer include?
4. In the worked scenario, why does the answer include a counterargument rather than stopping after
   Step 2?

**Answers:** 1. Because both papers explicitly draw on knowledge from the entire specification, not
just their own named content block -- a Paper 1 question can reasonably require a concept introduced
under Paper 2's content, and vice versa. 2. AO2 (application) and AO3 (analysis and evaluation),
together roughly 65% of the marks. 3. Stating the relevant economic concept, applying it to the
specific context given, considering a counterargument or limitation, and reaching an explicit
judgement. 4. Because a "discuss" question tests AO3 (evaluation) as well as AO1/AO2 -- an answer that
only applies the theory to reach one conclusion, without weighing a counterargument, has not
demonstrated the evaluative judgement the command word requires.
