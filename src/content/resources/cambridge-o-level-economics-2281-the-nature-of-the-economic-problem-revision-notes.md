These are condensed notes for section 1.1, *The nature of the economic problem* (1.1.1 finite resources and unlimited wants; 1.1.2 economic and free goods), of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [study guide on the nature of the economic problem](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-nature-of-the-economic-problem/). Then try the [practice questions on the nature of the economic problem](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-nature-of-the-economic-problem-practice/). All examples are invented.

## Key definitions

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Economic problem | Resources are finite but wants are unlimited, so choices must be made |
| Scarcity | The situation in which there are not enough resources to satisfy all wants |
| Resources | The factors of production: land, labour, capital and enterprise |
| Wants | Everything people would like to have, beyond what they need to survive |
| Needs | What is essential for survival, such as food, water and shelter |
| Economic good | A good that is scarce, uses scarce resources and has an opportunity cost |
| Free good | A good that is not scarce: enough for everyone at zero price, using no scarce resources, with no opportunity cost |

## The chain to write

Finite resources + unlimited wants → scarcity → choice → every choice means giving something up (the link to opportunity cost in section 1.3).

## Why each side of the problem holds

**Resources are finite because:**

- There is a limited number of workers and hours.
- Land and natural resources are limited in amount.
- Machines and buildings take time and resources to make.
- Enterprise (people willing to organise and take risks) is limited.

**Wants are unlimited because:**

- Satisfying one want leads to another.
- Rising incomes create new wants.
- New products create wants that did not exist before.
- People want better quality as well as more quantity.

## The four contexts in the syllabus

| Who | What is scarce | Example of a forced choice |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Income, time | A student with 50 cu must choose between a textbook and a concert ticket |
| Workers | Time, skills, energy | A nurse chooses between overtime pay and time with family |
| Producers | Land, labour, capital, finance | A clothing firm decides whether to use its sewing machines for shirts or jackets |
| Governments | Tax revenue, borrowing | A government chooses between a new motorway and more teachers |

**Worked reminder.** A small café (invented) has 6,000 cu to spend this year. The owner wants a new coffee machine (4,200 cu), an outdoor seating area (3,500 cu) and a website (1,300 cu). Total wanted: 9,000 cu. The shortfall is 3,000 cu, so the owner must choose. Machine + website = 5,500 cu fits; machine + seating = 7,700 cu does not.

## Economic goods versus free goods

| | Economic good | Free good |
|---|---|---|
| Scarce? | Yes | No |
| Uses scarce resources? | Yes | No |
| Opportunity cost? | Yes | None |
| Examples | Bread, haircuts, electricity, public hospitals | Air in an open field, sunlight, sea water in the open sea |

**The one test:** does providing more of it mean giving up something else? Yes → economic good. No → free good.

**Zero price is not the test.** These have no price to the user but are economic goods:

- A free newspaper handed out at a station (paper, printing and journalists are resources).
- Free medical care from a government clinic (paid for by taxes).
- A free sample in a shop (the firm used resources to make it).

**Category can change with circumstances:**

- Clean air becomes an economic good in a polluted city where filters are bought.
- River water is a free good in a wet, thinly populated area but an economic good in a drought.
- Sand on a desert is free; sand cleaned and bagged for building is economic.

## Exam traps

- Saying the economic problem is "a lack of money". It is a lack of **resources** relative to wants.
- Writing that wants are limited by income. Income limits spending, not wants.
- Answering only for consumers when the question is about producers, workers or governments.
- Classing a government service or free sample as a free good.
- Forgetting that a free good has **no opportunity cost**.
- Mixing needs and wants: wants include needs but go far beyond them.
- In a multiple-choice question, choosing "goods with no price" as the definition of free goods.

## Quick self-test

1. Define the economic problem.
2. What is scarcity?
3. Give **two** reasons why wants are unlimited.
4. Give **two** reasons why resources are finite.
5. Give an example of the economic problem facing a worker.
6. Give an example of the economic problem facing a government.
7. Define a free good.
8. Why is a public hospital an economic good even though patients pay nothing?
9. Identify the free good: (A) tap water, (B) sunlight, (C) a library book, (D) a bus ride paid for by the council.
10. How can a free good become an economic good? Give an example.
11. What is the opportunity cost of using a free good?
12. Explain why scarcity means choices must be made.

### Answers

1. Resources are finite while wants are unlimited, so choices must be made about how resources are used.
2. Not having enough resources to satisfy all wants.
3. Any two: satisfying one want creates another; rising incomes; new products; wanting better quality.
4. Any two: limited workers and time; limited land and natural resources; capital takes resources to build; limited enterprise.
5. A worker has limited hours, so must choose between working more for income and having more leisure or study time.
6. A government has limited tax revenue, so must choose between spending on, for example, health and defence.
7. A good that is not scarce, using no scarce resources, available to all at zero price with no opportunity cost.
8. Doctors, nurses, buildings and medicines are scarce resources paid for by taxes, so providing care means giving up other uses.
9. B, sunlight.
10. When it becomes scarce relative to wants, for example clean air in a polluted city, or river water in a drought.
11. Zero: nothing is given up.
12. Not every want can be met, so decision makers must decide which wants to satisfy and which to go without.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is money a resource in the economic problem?**
No. The resources in the economic problem are the factors of production. Money lets people claim resources, but a shortage of money for one person is a result of scarcity, not the definition of it.

**Can a good be free in one place and economic in another?**
Yes. Water from a large river in a wet region may be a free good, while the same water in a drought, or once treated and piped, is an economic good.
