## Section A

**1.** Define the term *secondary research*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** primary research methods. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between quantitative and qualitative data. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why a business uses a sample rather than surveying the whole population. **[2]**

**5.** Lowick Toys sells board games bought by parents and played by children. Explain why its research may need to include both customers and consumers. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why the question "How often do you shop online?" may produce unreliable data, and suggest an improved version. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Thornby Hotels surveyed 500 guests about a proposed spa. Results:

| Response | Number |
|---|---|
| Would definitely use | 115 |
| Might use | 190 |
| Would not use | 195 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of guests who would definitely use the spa.

**(b)** Calculate the difference in percentage points between "might use" and "would not use".

**(c)** Explain **one** reason why these results may overstate actual use of the spa. **[6]**

**8.** Analyse **two** limitations of using government population statistics to plan the opening of a new children's clothing shop. **[4]**

**9.** Maple & Rye is a small bakery with a limited budget. It wants to know whether customers would buy gluten-free bread. Analyse **two** research methods it could use. **[4]**

**10.** Crossley Phones emailed a satisfaction survey to 4 000 customers and received 320 replies.

**(a)** Calculate the response rate.

**(b)** Explain how non-response might affect the reliability of the results. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways in which a business could analyse 200 open-ended comments from customers. **[4]**

**12.** Glenholm Drinks plans to spend $60 000 on market research before launching a new energy drink nationally. Evaluate whether this spending is justified. **[10]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Research using data that already exists [1], originally collected for another purpose, such as government statistics or trade reports [1].

**2.** Any two: questionnaires or surveys [1]; interviews [1]; focus groups; observation; test marketing.

**3.** Quantitative data is numerical, such as the percentage of people who would buy [1]; qualitative data is descriptive, giving opinions and reasons [1].

**4.** Surveying everyone would be too costly and slow [1], so a smaller group is questioned to give results quickly at lower cost [1].

**5.** Parents are the customers who decide whether to buy [1], so research must find what they value, such as price or educational benefit [1]. Children are the consumers whose enjoyment decides whether the game is played and recommended, so their reactions also matter [1].

**6.** "Often" means different things to different people, so answers cannot be compared [1]. An improved version gives a precise time period [1], for example: "How many times did you buy something online in the last four weeks?" [1].

**7.** **(a)** 115 ÷ 500 × 100 [1] = 23% [1].

**(b)** Might use = 38%, would not use = 39% [1]; difference = 1 percentage point [1].

**(c)** Any one, explained. Respondent bias: guests may say they would use the spa because it sounds appealing [1], but when they see the price they may not pay [1]. Allow sample bias if only current guests were surveyed, ignoring visitors who might come because of the spa.

**8.** Any two, explained. The statistics may be out of date [1], so the number of families with young children in the area may have changed [1]. They may cover a wide area, such as a whole region, rather than the shop's local catchment [1], so they may not show local demand or competition [1]. Allow: they give no information on customers' tastes or spending on clothes.

**9.** Any two, explained. Observation of which products sell out first gives evidence at almost no cost [1], but shows behaviour rather than reasons [1]. A short questionnaire at the counter is cheap and gives direct answers about gluten-free demand [1], but only reaches existing customers, not people who avoid the bakery because it lacks gluten-free products [1]. Allow small taste trials or checking internal sales records of similar products.

**10.** **(a)** 320 ÷ 4 000 × 100 [1] = 8% [1].

**(b)** 92% did not reply, and those who did may differ from those who did not [1]; for example, very satisfied or very unhappy customers are more likely to reply, so the results may not represent typical customers [1].

**11.** Group the comments into themes, such as price, service and delivery [1], then count how many comments fall into each theme to find the most common issues [1]. Look for specific suggestions in the comments [1], which can show how a problem might be solved, not just that it exists [1].

**12.** For: a national launch is costly, so mistakes in flavour, price or packaging could lose far more than $60 000 [1]; the energy drink market is competitive, so research on competitors and target customers helps position the product [1]; focus groups and test marketing could refine the product before full production [1]; research gives evidence that may reassure investors or retailers [1].
Against: $60 000 is a large cost that reduces profit if the drink would have succeeded anyway [1]; research delays the launch, and a rival may launch a similar drink first [1]; results may be unreliable, for example if respondents say they would buy but do not [1].
Judgement: the spending is likely to be justified because the risk of a failed national launch is high [1], but Glenholm should first use cheaper secondary data to narrow down the questions [1]. The judgement depends on the total launch cost and how well Glenholm already knows this market [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating percentages but not stating what they mean for the decision.
- Saying a sample is "biased" without explaining how or in which direction.
- Recommending costly research to a small business without considering its budget.
- Forgetting that surveying existing customers misses potential ones.
- Evaluating research spending without comparing it with the cost of a failed launch.
