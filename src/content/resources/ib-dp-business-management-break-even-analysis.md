## Contribution: per unit and total

Break-even analysis separates costs into fixed costs (they do not change with output, such as rent) and variable costs (they change with output, such as materials). If those terms feel shaky, read the [3.3 guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-costs-and-revenues/) first.

**Contribution per unit** is the selling price per unit minus the variable cost per unit. It is the amount each unit sold adds towards covering fixed costs, and then towards profit.

**Total contribution** is contribution per unit multiplied by the number of units sold. It is also total revenue minus total variable costs.

The difference matters because each answers a different question. Contribution per unit tells you how strong one sale is. Total contribution tells you how much money the whole sales volume has available to pay fixed costs. Profit is total contribution minus fixed costs.

### Running example

Kilbrook Pottery is an invented studio that makes ceramic mugs. All figures below are invented and all money amounts are in dollars.

* Selling price: 40 per mug
* Variable cost: 16 per mug
* Fixed costs: 12,000 per month
* Capacity: 800 mugs per month
* Current output and sales: 650 mugs per month

Contribution per unit = 40 − 16 = **24**.

Total contribution at 650 mugs = 650 × 24 = **15,600**.

Profit = 15,600 − 12,000 = **3,600**.

The studio sells more than it needs to pay its bills, so it makes a profit. How much more? The margin of safety answers that, below.

## Break-even quantity (point)

The break-even quantity is the output at which total revenue equals total costs, so profit is zero. At lower output the business makes a loss. At higher output it makes a profit.

Standard form:

```
Break-even quantity = fixed costs / contribution per unit
```

For Kilbrook Pottery: 12,000 / 24 = **500 mugs**.

The break-even point is often given in revenue terms too: 500 × 40 = 20,000. If the sum is not a whole number, round up, because you cannot sell part of a unit and still break even.

## Profit or loss at any output

Use the contribution route:

```
Profit (or loss) = (output × contribution per unit) − fixed costs
```

At 200 mugs: 200 × 24 = 4,800, then 4,800 − 12,000 = **−7,200**, a loss of 7,200.

At 800 mugs: 800 × 24 = 19,200, then 19,200 − 12,000 = **7,200** profit.

Check this against the long way. At 650 mugs, total revenue is 650 × 40 = 26,000. Total cost is 12,000 + (650 × 16) = 22,400. Profit is 26,000 − 22,400 = 3,600, the same answer.

## The break-even chart

A break-even chart is a graph. Output in units goes along the bottom, and money (cost and revenue) goes up the side. Draw four things.

1. **Fixed cost line.** A horizontal line at 12,000.
2. **Total cost line.** It starts at 12,000 on the vertical axis (output zero) and rises by 16 for every extra mug.
3. **Total revenue line.** It starts at the origin and rises by 40 per mug.
4. **Break-even point.** The point where the total revenue and total cost lines cross.

Values to plot for Kilbrook Pottery:

| Output (mugs) | Fixed costs | Total costs | Total revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 12,000 | 12,000 | 0 |
| 200 | 12,000 | 15,200 | 8,000 |
| 500 | 12,000 | 20,000 | 20,000 |
| 800 | 12,000 | 24,800 | 32,000 |

A rough sketch in words:

```

32,000 |                         / TR
24,800 |                    ___/_ TC
20,000 |              ____X/        X = break-even, 500 mugs
12,000 |-------------/- - - - - - FC
       |
       +------------------------- output
```

On the chart you can read each aspect:

* **Break-even quantity:** drop a vertical line from the crossing point to the output axis.
* **Loss:** the gap between the lines, where total cost is above total revenue, to the left of the crossing point.
* **Profit:** the gap to the right of the crossing point, where revenue is above cost.
* **Margin of safety:** the horizontal distance from the break-even output to the actual output.

Label both axes and every line, and mark the break-even point.

## Margin of safety

Margin of safety is the amount by which actual (or planned) output is above the break-even output. It tells you the fall in sales the business can absorb and still avoid a loss.

```
Margin of safety (units) = actual output − break-even quantity
```

For Kilbrook Pottery: 650 − 500 = **150 mugs**.

You can also give it as a percentage of actual output: 150 / 650 × 100 = **23.1%**. State which base you use, because a percentage of capacity (150 / 800 = 18.75%) would be a different figure. A wide margin means sales can fall without a loss. A narrow margin means a small drop in demand could push the business into loss.

## Target profit output and target profit

**Target profit output** is the output needed to reach a profit the business has chosen.

```
Target profit output = (fixed costs + target profit) / contribution per unit
```

If Kilbrook Pottery wants 6,000 profit: (12,000 + 6,000) / 24 = **750 mugs**. This is under the 800-mug capacity, so it is possible.

**Target profit**, in the way the guide lists it, is the profit made at a chosen output. It uses the profit formula above. At 800 mugs the target profit is 7,200, as shown earlier. The two ideas are inverses. One asks "what output gives this profit?" and the other asks "what profit does this output give?"

## Target price

Target price is the price that must be charged to reach a target profit at a given output.

```
Target price = (fixed costs + variable costs at that output + target profit) / output
```

Suppose Kilbrook Pottery expects to sell 600 mugs and wants a 6,000 profit. Variable costs are 600 × 16 = 9,600. Required revenue is 12,000 + 9,600 + 6,000 = 27,600. Price = 27,600 / 600 = **46**.

Check: 600 × (46 − 16) = 18,000, then 18,000 − 12,000 = 6,000. It works. Whether customers will pay 46 is a separate question.

## Effects of changes in price or cost

The guide asks for these effects on the break-even quantity, profit and margin of safety, using graphical and quantitative methods. Keep output at 650 mugs and change one thing at a time.

| Change | Contribution per unit | Break-even quantity | Profit at 650 | Margin of safety |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| None (base case) | 24 | 500 | 3,600 | 150 |
| Price up to 44 | 28 | 428.6, so 429 | 6,200 | 221 |
| Variable cost up to 20 | 20 | 600 | 1,000 | 50 |
| Fixed costs up to 15,000 | 24 | 625 | 600 | 25 |

Quantitative method: recalculate with the new figure. For the price rise, 12,000 / 28 = 428.6 and profit is 650 × 28 − 12,000 = 6,200. Margin of safety is 650 − 429 = 221.

Graphical method:

* **Higher price:** the total revenue line becomes steeper, so it crosses the total cost line sooner. The break-even point moves left.
* **Higher variable cost:** the total cost line becomes steeper, so the crossing moves right.
* **Higher fixed costs:** the total cost line is lifted by the extra amount and stays parallel to its old position, so the crossing again moves right. The fixed cost line moves up.

Reverse changes move the lines the other way. A price rise may also cut demand, and the table shows only the arithmetic.

## Limitations of break-even as a decision-making tool

The guide lists limitations without naming them, so these are the common ones. Pick those that fit the case.

* **Straight lines.** Revenue and cost lines are drawn straight. Real prices fall with discounts, and bulk buying can cut variable cost per unit.
* **Fixed costs may not stay fixed.** A business that expands output may need another machine or a larger unit, so fixed costs step up.
* **Assumes all output is sold.** Stock left on the shelf has been paid for but earns nothing.
* **One product.** With several products at different prices, splitting fixed costs between them is difficult.
* **Forecast data.** Costs, price and demand are estimates. An error in any of them shifts the break-even point.
* **Static.** The chart shows one moment. Costs change, competitors react and demand moves.
* **Ignores non-financial factors.** Quality, reputation, employee welfare and ethical concerns do not appear on the chart.
* **Does not show demand.** The tool does not say whether customers will buy the break-even quantity at that price.

For an AO3 answer, do not just list. Take one or two limitations and tie them to the case, then say whether the tool is still useful. For example, it helps a start-up see the sales it needs but cannot show whether those sales are realistic.

## Worked example: a short decision

Kilbrook Pottery is thinking about cutting its price to 36 to attract more customers. Fixed and variable costs stay the same.

* Contribution per unit = 36 − 16 = 20.
* Break-even quantity = 12,000 / 20 = 600 mugs, up from 500.
* At 650 mugs, profit = 650 × 20 − 12,000 = 1,000, down from 3,600.
* Margin of safety = 650 − 600 = 50 mugs, down from 150.

The studio would need to sell more than 650 mugs just to equal its present profit. Required output for 3,600 profit = (12,000 + 3,600) / 20 = 780 mugs. That is close to the 800-mug capacity, so the cut looks risky unless demand rises strongly. This is the kind of reasoned use of numbers that the AO4 label points to.

## Common errors

* Using total fixed costs as the contribution. Contribution is price minus variable cost per unit.
* Dividing the fixed costs by the selling price, when the divisor must be the contribution on each unit.
* Putting the total cost line through the origin. It starts at the fixed cost level.
* Mixing margin of safety units and percentages without saying which one you give.
* Leaving out units or the currency.
