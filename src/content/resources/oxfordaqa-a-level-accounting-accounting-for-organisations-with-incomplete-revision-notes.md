## 3.2.4 at a glance

| Specification content | You must be able to |
|---|---|
| Profit where records are insufficient to prepare income statements | Use statements of affairs to find profit from the change in capital |
| Techniques to find missing information | Rebuild figures from control/total accounts; cash and bank accounts; gross profit margin, mark up, profit in relation to revenue and inventory turnover; depreciation; disposal profits and losses; and the financial statements |
| Different recording systems | Weigh the benefits and limitations of single and double entry records |

## Key terms

- **Incomplete records / single entry**: records where transactions are not entered twice, so there is no full ledger or trial balance.
- **Statement of affairs**: sets out every asset and liability on one day; capital is the balancing figure.
- **Total account**: a control account rebuilt in outline to find a missing figure such as credit sales.
- **Balancing figure**: the amount needed to make an account or statement agree; in this topic it is usually the figure you are asked for.
- **Capital introduced**: private funds or property, such as savings or a car, that the owner adds to the business.

## Formulae

| What | Formula |
|---|---|
| Capital | Assets − Liabilities |
| Profit (capital method) | Closing capital − Opening capital + Drawings − Capital introduced |
| Markup % | 100 × Gross profit / Cost of sales |
| Gross profit margin % | 100 × Gross profit / Revenue |
| Margin from markup | markup / (100 + markup) |
| Markup from margin | margin / (100 − margin) |
| Rate of inventory turnover (number of times) | Cost of sales / Average inventory |
| Rate of inventory turnover (days) | 365 × Average inventory / Cost of sales |
| Profit in relation to revenue % | 100 × Profit for the year / Revenue |
| Cost of sales | Opening inventory + Purchases − Closing inventory |
| Expense for the year | Amount paid − accrual at start + accrual at end + prepayment at start − prepayment at end |

The specification points to the OxfordAQA guide "Formulae for accounting ratios" for the ratio formulae.

## Method in steps

### Profit from statements of affairs

1. List assets and liabilities at the start; capital = assets − liabilities.
2. Repeat at the end. An overdraft, an accrual and a loan are liabilities; a prepayment is an asset.
3. Change in capital = closing − opening.
4. Add drawings (cash **and** goods taken). Deduct capital introduced.
5. A negative answer is a **loss** -- say so in words.

### Missing figure from a total account

1. Write the opening balance on its normal side (receivables debit, payables credit).
2. Enter every known item on its correct side.
3. Enter the closing balance on the opposite side.
4. Total the heavier side; the gap is the missing figure.

| Item | Receivables total account | Payables total account |
|---|---|---|
| Receipts / payments by bank | Credit | Debit |
| Contra | Credit | Debit |
| Irrecoverable debts | Credit | -- |
| Interest charged on overdue accounts | Debit | -- |
| Sales returns / purchases returns | Credit | Debit |
| Discounts received | -- | Debit |

### Cash account for missing takings

Opening cash + cash sales = amount banked + payments out of takings + closing cash. Solve for whichever figure is missing.

### Inventory and markup

Cost of sales = Revenue × 100 / (100 + markup). Then expected closing inventory = opening + purchases − cost of sales.

*Reminder (Hollinmoor Pantry):* revenue 60,000 at a markup of 25% gives cost of sales = 60,000 × 100/125 = 48,000 and gross profit 12,000.

### Non-current assets

- Carrying amount at disposal = cost − accumulated depreciation on that asset.
- Proceeds − carrying amount = profit (positive) or loss (negative) on disposal.
- Missing purchase = closing cost − (opening cost − cost of asset sold).
- Missing depreciation charge = opening carrying amount − carrying amount disposed + additions − closing carrying amount.

### Choosing the technique

| You are asked for | Start from |
|---|---|
| Profit when only asset and liability lists exist | Opening and closing statements of affairs |
| Credit sales | Trade receivables total account |
| Credit purchases | Trade payables total account |
| Takings spent before banking, or drawings from the till | Cash account |
| The year-end bank figure, or an unrecorded payment | Bank account (summary of statements) |
| Revenue, cost of sales or gross profit | Markup or gross profit margin |
| Inventory at either date | Cost of sales first, then rate of inventory turnover |
| Profit or total expenses | Profit in relation to revenue |
| Cost of a new asset, depreciation or sale proceeds | Asset at cost and accumulated depreciation figures |
| Capital at the year end | Opening capital + capital introduced + profit − drawings |

### Order of work for a complete set of statements

1. Capital at the start, from the opening statement of affairs.
2. Bank and cash accounts for missing receipts, payments or balances.
3. Total accounts for credit sales and credit purchases; then include any cash transactions.
4. Adjust each expense for accruals and prepayments at both dates.
5. Depreciation, then any profit or loss on disposal.
6. Income statement, then statement of financial position. If it balances, your figures are consistent.

*Reminder:* an accrual at the start of the year was paid this year but belongs to last year, so it is
deducted from the amount paid; a prepayment at the start belongs to this year, so it is added.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Statement of affairs vs statement of financial position**: same layout, different source (valuations and lists vs ledger balances).
- **Markup vs margin**: markup on cost, margin on revenue; markup is always the larger percentage.
- **Inventory turnover times vs days**: times = cost of sales / average inventory; days inverts it and multiplies by 365.
- **Capital introduced vs a loan**: capital introduced comes from the owner and is deducted in the capital method; a loan is a liability.
- **Credit sales vs revenue**: revenue = credit sales + cash sales.

## Single entry vs double entry

| | Single entry | Double entry |
|---|---|---|
| Benefits | Cheap; simple; quick for a tiny cash business | Trial balance check; full statements; control over receivables and payables; supports ratio analysis, budgets and loan applications |
| Limitations | No arithmetic check; errors and fraud harder to spot; profit may be only a total; costly reconstruction each year | Time, skill and cost; some errors still not revealed by the trial balance |

## Quick self-test

1. Opening capital 18,500, closing capital 21,300, drawings 9,600, capital introduced 2,000. Profit?
2. Convert a markup of 20% to a gross profit margin.
3. Convert a gross profit margin of 20% to a markup.
4. Revenue 48,000 at a markup of 50%. Cost of sales and gross profit?
5. Cost of sales 90,000; rate of inventory turnover 9 times. Average inventory?
6. Trade receivables: opening 2,600, closing 3,050, receipts 27,400, irrecoverable debts 350. Credit sales?
7. Trade payables: opening 4,100, closing 3,700, paid 36,900, discounts received 300. Credit purchases?
8. Rent paid 5,400; prepaid 300 at the start and 450 at the end. Rent expense?
9. A machine with carrying amount 6,400 is sold for 7,000. Result on disposal?
10. Opening carrying amount 30,000, additions 8,000, carrying amount of disposal 2,500, closing carrying amount 28,900. Depreciation charge?
11. Revenue 75,000; profit in relation to revenue 8%. Profit for the year?
12. Average inventory 4,800; cost of sales 58,400. Rate of inventory turnover in days?

### Answers

1. 21,300 − 18,500 + 9,600 − 2,000 = **10,400**
2. 20 / 120 = **16.67%**
3. 20 / 80 = **25%**
4. 48,000 × 100/150 = **32,000**; gross profit **16,000**
5. 90,000 / 9 = **10,000**
6. 27,400 + 350 + 3,050 − 2,600 = **28,200**
7. 36,900 + 300 + 3,700 − 4,100 = **36,800**
8. 5,400 + 300 − 450 = **5,250**
9. 7,000 − 6,400 = **600 profit**
10. 30,000 + 8,000 − 2,500 − 28,900 = **6,600**
11. 75,000 × 8% = **6,000**
12. 4,800 / 58,400 × 365 = **30 days**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving goods taken for own use out of drawings in the capital method.
- Adding capital introduced instead of deducting it.
- Treating a bank overdraft as an asset, or forgetting an accrual, in a statement of affairs.
- Putting the closing balance of a total account on the same side as the opening balance.
- Entering a contra in one total account only, or interest charged on the credit side.
- Applying the markup percentage to revenue as if it were a margin.
- Using closing rather than average inventory in an inventory turnover calculation.
- Not reporting a loss clearly when the capital method gives a negative figure.
- Writing generic points about record keeping in an evaluation instead of linking them to the business described.
