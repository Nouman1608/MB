## Why amines are nucleophiles

A nucleophile donates a lone pair to form a new covalent bond to an electron-poor atom. The nitrogen atom of ammonia or an amine has a lone pair, so it can do this. The same lone pair makes amines bases (accepting H⁺), as explained in the Base properties sub-topic. Here the lone pair attacks a δ+ carbon atom instead of a proton. The more available the lone pair, the better the nucleophile, so primary and secondary aliphatic amines are better nucleophiles than ammonia.

## Substitution with halogenoalkanes: the ladder of products

A halogenoalkane R–X has a δ+ carbon. Ammonia attacks it, and the halogen leaves as a halide ion. Because the amine produced is also a nucleophile, the reaction can continue.

For bromoethane, C₂H₅Br:

```
NH₃          + C₂H₅Br → C₂H₅NH₂ (primary amine)     + HBr
C₂H₅NH₂      + C₂H₅Br → (C₂H₅)₂NH (secondary amine) + HBr
(C₂H₅)₂NH    + C₂H₅Br → (C₂H₅)₃N (tertiary amine)   + HBr
(C₂H₅)₃N     + C₂H₅Br → (C₂H₅)₄N⁺Br⁻ (quaternary ammonium bromide)
```

In the presence of excess ammonia or amine, the HBr formed reacts with a second molecule of the base to give an ammonium salt. Each step replaces one more N–H bond with an N–C bond.

| Product | Number of alkyl groups on N | Class |
|---|---|---|
| C₂H₅NH₂ | 1 | Primary amine |
| (C₂H₅)₂NH | 2 | Secondary amine |
| (C₂H₅)₃N | 3 | Tertiary amine |
| (C₂H₅)₄N⁺ Br⁻ | 4 | Quaternary ammonium salt |

### Controlling the outcome

- **Excess ammonia** favours the primary amine, because a halogenoalkane molecule is far more likely to meet an ammonia molecule than an amine molecule.
- **Excess halogenoalkane** drives the sequence all the way to the quaternary ammonium salt.

### The mechanism

For the first step with ammonia (the same pattern applies at every stage):

```
Arrow 1: lone pair on N of NH₃ → the δ+ carbon
Arrow 2: the C–Br bond → onto Br (leaves as Br⁻)
Intermediate: C₂H₅–NH₃⁺ (and Br⁻)
Then a second NH₃ removes H⁺: C₂H₅NH₃⁺ + NH₃ → C₂H₅NH₂ + NH₄⁺
```

The quaternary salt cannot lose a proton (it has no N–H) and has no lone pair, so the sequence stops there.

## Quaternary ammonium salts as cationic surfactants

A quaternary ammonium ion has four carbon groups on nitrogen and a permanent positive charge. If one of the groups is a **long hydrocarbon chain**, the ion is a **surfactant**:

```
CH₃(CH₂)₁₅–N⁺(CH₃)₃  Br⁻
 long non-polar tail    charged head
```

- The charged head is **hydrophilic** (attracted to water).
- The long tail is **hydrophobic** (non-polar, attracted to oil and grease).
- Because the active ion is positive, it is a **cationic** surfactant.

They are used in fabric conditioners and hair conditioners. Fabric and hair surfaces usually carry a negative charge. The positive head is attracted to the surface and the non-polar tails stick out, giving a smooth feel and reducing static.

## Acylation with acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides

The nitrogen lone pair also attacks the C=O carbon in an acyl chloride or acid anhydride. The mechanism is nucleophilic addition–elimination (see the Acylation sub-topic).

```
CH₃COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + NH₄Cl
CH₃COCl + 2CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃ + CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻
(CH₃CO)₂O + CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃ + CH₃COOH
```

The product from ammonia is a primary amide. The product from a primary amine is an **N-substituted amide** (N-ethylethanamide here). Unlike the halogenoalkane reaction, the reaction stops after one acylation, because the amide's nitrogen lone pair is drawn into the C=O group and is no longer nucleophilic enough to react again.

### Mechanism for ethanoyl chloride with a primary amine

```
Step 1 (addition)
  Arrow 1: lone pair on N of RNH₂ → δ+ carbon of C=O
  Arrow 2: C=O pi bond → onto O (O⁻)
  Intermediate: CH₃–C(O⁻)(Cl)–N⁺H₂R
Step 2 (elimination)
  Arrow 3: lone pair on O⁻ → reforms C=O
  Arrow 4: C–Cl bond → onto Cl (leaves as Cl⁻)
  Then H⁺ is removed from N by a second RNH₂, forming RNH₃⁺Cl⁻
Product: CH₃CONHR
```

## Comparing the two types of nucleophilic reaction

| Feature | With a halogenoalkane | With an acyl chloride |
|---|---|---|
| Type of mechanism | Nucleophilic substitution | Nucleophilic addition–elimination |
| Site of attack | δ+ carbon bonded to halogen | δ+ carbon of C=O |
| Intermediate | R–NH₃⁺ (ion), formed as the halide leaves | Tetrahedral, with O⁻ and N⁺ |
| Number of curly arrows (first stage) | Two | Two, then two more for elimination |
| Organic product with a primary amine | Secondary amine | N-substituted amide |
| Does it continue? | Yes, up to the quaternary salt | No, stops at the amide |
| Second mole of amine | Removes H⁺ | Removes H⁺ |

Being able to write both mechanisms, and to say why one continues and the other stops, covers the main reasoning this sub-topic asks for.

## Worked example 1: the equations from methylamine

**Question.** Methylamine reacts with excess iodomethane. Write equations for each stage up to the quaternary salt.

```
CH₃NH₂ + CH₃I → (CH₃)₂NH + HI               dimethylamine
(CH₃)₂NH + CH₃I → (CH₃)₃N + HI               trimethylamine
(CH₃)₃N + CH₃I → (CH₃)₄N⁺ I⁻                 tetramethylammonium iodide
```

## Worked example 2: mass of a quaternary ammonium salt

0.0500 mol of triethylamine (Mᵣ = 101.0) reacts completely with excess bromoethane to give tetraethylammonium bromide, (C₂H₅)₄N⁺Br⁻. Calculate the maximum mass of salt. Aᵣ: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, N = 14.0, Br = 79.9.

```
(C₂H₅)₃N + C₂H₅Br → (C₂H₅)₄N⁺Br⁻           (1 : 1)
Mr of salt = 8(12.0) + 20(1.0) + 14.0 + 79.9 = 209.9
mass       = 0.0500 × 209.9 = 10.5 g
```

## Worked example 3: acylation product and equation

**Question.** Write an equation for the reaction of ethanoyl chloride with phenylamine, and name the organic product.

```
CH₃COCl + 2C₆H₅NH₂ → CH₃CONHC₆H₅ + C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻
```

The organic product is N-phenylethanamide. Two moles of phenylamine are used for each mole of ethanoyl chloride: one acts as the nucleophile and one removes H⁺.

## Worked example 4: predicting the main product

**Question.** Chloroethane is heated with a large excess of ammonia in ethanol. What is the main organic product? What changes if the ammonia is limited?

With a large excess of ammonia, the main product is the primary amine, ethylamine, formed with ammonium chloride. If the halogenoalkane is in excess, the reaction continues to diethylamine, triethylamine and finally tetraethylammonium chloride.

## Common mistakes

- Saying ammonia in excess gives only primary, secondary and tertiary amines. Excess halogenoalkane is what gives a quaternary salt.
- Writing the quaternary ion with a lone pair or an N–H bond.
- Writing the first arrow from the nitrogen atom itself rather than from the lone pair.
- Showing H⁺ loss from the wrong atom in the intermediate.
- Forgetting the second mole of amine in acylation reactions.
- Describing a cationic surfactant without mentioning the long hydrocarbon chain.
- Confusing substitution (halogenoalkane) and addition–elimination (acyl chloride) mechanisms.
- Naming the amide product without the N- prefix for a primary amine.

## Exam technique

- **Mechanism marks**: arrow from the lone pair on N, arrow to the leaving group, charge on the intermediate, loss of H⁺.
- **Explain a mixture**: state that each product is still a nucleophile.
- **Surfactant**: cite the charged head, the hydrocarbon tail and the surface it sticks to.
- **Equations**: balance H and Cl, and include the second mole of amine.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the quaternary salt not react further?**
It has no lone pair on nitrogen (all four positions are bonded), so it is not a nucleophile.

**Why does acylation stop after one step?**
The amide's nitrogen lone pair is delocalised towards the C=O group, so it is much less nucleophilic.

**Are all quaternary ammonium salts surfactants?**
No. Only those with at least one long hydrocarbon chain have the combination of a hydrophobic tail and a charged head.
