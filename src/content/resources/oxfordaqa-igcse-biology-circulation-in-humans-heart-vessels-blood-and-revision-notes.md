## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Circulatory system | heart, blood vessels and blood |
| Double circulation | two circuits: heart to lungs and back, and heart to all other organs and back |
| Atrium | upper chamber; receives blood and pushes it into a ventricle |
| Ventricle | lower chamber; pushes blood out of the heart |
| Coronary arteries | supply the heart muscle with blood |
| Plasma | the fluid part of blood |
| Haemoglobin | red pigment in red cells; combines with oxygen |
| Oxyhaemoglobin | haemoglobin combined with oxygen |
| Fibrinogen | soluble protein changed into fibrin during clotting |
| Fibrin | fibres that form a network and trap cells in a clot |
| Antigen | protein on the surface of a cell |
| Rejection | the recipient's antibodies attacking antigens on a donor organ |

## Heart and circulation (a, b, c, h, i)

- Heart wall is mostly muscle tissue; it pumps blood.
- Four chambers: right atrium, left atrium, right ventricle, left ventricle.
- Atria contract, forcing blood into ventricles. Ventricles contract, forcing blood out. Valves stop back-flow. Valve names are not needed.
- Arteries: heart to organs. Veins: organs to heart.

**Route of blood, in steps**

1. Vena cava
2. Right atrium
3. Right ventricle
4. Pulmonary artery
5. Lungs
6. Pulmonary vein
7. Left atrium
8. Left ventricle
9. Aorta
10. Body organs, then back to the vena cava

| Vessel | To or from | Oxygen |
|---|---|---|
| Vena cava | body to right atrium | low |
| Pulmonary artery | right ventricle to lungs | low |
| Pulmonary vein | lungs to left atrium | high |
| Aorta | left ventricle to body | high |
| Coronary arteries | aorta to heart muscle | high |

**Must-know distinction:** the pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein break the "arteries carry oxygenated blood" pattern. Name by direction (away from or towards the heart), not by oxygen content.

**Why double?** Blood goes back through the heart after the lungs, so it is pumped again before it reaches the other organs.

## Vessels (j, k)

| Vessel | Wall | Other features | Function link |
|---|---|---|---|
| Artery | thick; muscle and elastic fibres | carries blood away from the heart | withstands pressure; stretches and recoils |
| Vein | thinner | often has valves | valves prevent back-flow |
| Capillary | very thin | very narrow; found in organs | substances pass out of and into the blood through the wall |

## Blood (l to q)

| Component | Nucleus? | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma | not a cell | carries carbon dioxide (organs to lungs), soluble products of digestion (small intestine to organs), urea (liver to kidneys) |
| Red blood cell | no | carries oxygen as oxyhaemoglobin, from lungs to organs |
| White blood cell | yes | defence against microorganisms |
| Platelet | no (a cell fragment) | helps blood to clot at a wound |

**Oxygen carriage, in words:** lungs: haemoglobin + oxygen → oxyhaemoglobin. Other organs: oxyhaemoglobin → haemoglobin + oxygen.

**Clotting, in steps**

1. A vessel is cut; platelets gather at the wound and help.
2. A series of enzyme-controlled reactions begins.
3. Fibrinogen is changed to fibrin.
4. Fibrin forms a network of fibres.
5. The network traps blood cells, forming a clot.

## Heart problems and treatments (d, e, f, g)

| Problem | Cause or description | Treatment | One advantage | One disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Irregular heart rate | natural pacemaker cells (right atrium) not keeping a steady rate | artificial pacemaker: electrical device | corrects the rhythm | needs surgery and a power source |
| Coronary heart disease | fatty layers narrow coronary arteries; less oxygen reaches heart muscle | stent holds artery open | restores blood flow without a donor | artery can narrow again; a procedure is needed |
| Stiff valve | tissue stiffens; valve does not open fully | replacement valve | restores one-way flow | see below |
| Leaky valve | valve lets blood flow backwards | replacement valve | restores one-way flow | see below |
| Heart failure | heart cannot pump enough | artificial heart (temporary) or transplant | keeps patient alive while waiting, or rests the heart | clots, infection, power and mechanical failure risks |

**Replacement valves**

| | Biological | Mechanical |
|---|---|---|
| Source | humans or other mammals | manufactured |
| Possible plus | usually less need for lifelong anticoagulant drugs | can last for a long time |
| Possible minus | can wear out and need replacing | blood may clot on it, so anticoagulant drugs are often needed |

## Antigens, transplants and blood groups (r, s, t)

- Antigens are proteins on the surface of cells.
- Transplant: a diseased organ is replaced by a healthy donor organ.
- Rejection: recipient's antibodies attack the donor organ's antigens because they are not recognised as the recipient's own.
- Reduce rejection by (1) choosing a donor with a similar tissue type and (2) giving immune-suppressing drugs.

**Evaluate: mechanical device or transplant**

| | Device | Transplant |
|---|---|---|
| For | no donor wait; no rejection; no immune-suppressing drugs | real organ, no machinery to fail |
| Against | clots, infection, power or part failure | donor shortage; rejection risk; drugs raise risk of infection; major surgery |

| Group | Antigens | Plasma antibodies |
|---|---|---|
| A | A | anti-B |
| B | B | anti-A |
| AB | A and B | none |
| O | none | anti-A and anti-B |

| Recipient | Can receive from |
|---|---|
| O | O |
| A | O, A |
| B | O, B |
| AB | O, A, B, AB |

Group O is the universal donor because its red cells have no A or B antigens. Blood typing is needed so that recipients are never given cells that their antibodies would attack.

## Method boxes

**Heart rate from a pulse count.** Beats ÷ seconds × 60. Example: 31 beats in 20 s → 31 ÷ 20 × 60 = 93 beats per minute.

**Volume pumped per minute.** Volume per beat × beats per minute. Example: 72 cm³ × 75 = 5400 cm³ per minute (5.4 dm³).

**Percentage change.** (new − original) ÷ original × 100. Example: an artery narrows from 3.0 mm to 1.8 mm. (3.0 − 1.8) ÷ 3.0 × 100 = 40 per cent decrease.

**Magnification.** Image size ÷ real size, with both in the same unit. Convert micrometres to millimetres by dividing by 1000. Example: a cell of 5 µm drawn 40 mm wide: 40 ÷ 0.005 = × 8000.

**Speed in a capillary.** Distance ÷ time. Example: a red cell moves 0.9 mm in 4.5 s → 0.2 mm per second.

## Linking ideas for longer answers

- **Coronary heart disease to symptoms:** narrowed coronary arteries mean less blood, so less oxygen and glucose reach the heart muscle, so less energy is released for contraction.
- **Red cell to function:** no nucleus leaves more room for haemoglobin; haemoglobin binds oxygen in the lungs and releases it in the organs.
- **Capillary to exchange:** a thin wall and narrow tube bring blood close to cells, so substances move between blood and cells easily.
- **Antigen to rejection:** a donor organ carries antigens that differ from the recipient's, so the recipient's antibodies treat it as foreign. A closer tissue type means fewer differences.
- **Immune-suppressing drugs to infection:** the drugs weaken the response that causes rejection, but they also weaken defence against microorganisms.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the vessel that brings blood from the lungs to the heart.
2. Which chamber pumps blood out through the aorta?
3. State the function of the coronary arteries.
4. Give two differences between an artery and a vein.
5. A student counts 58 beats in 40 s. Calculate the heart rate.
6. Name the protein that fibrinogen is changed into.
7. A group AB patient needs red cells. Which donor groups are compatible?
8. State two ways of reducing the chance of organ rejection.
9. Give one disadvantage of a mechanical heart valve.
10. A stent opens an artery from 1.8 mm to 3.0 mm. Calculate the percentage increase in diameter.

### Answers

1. Pulmonary vein.
2. Left ventricle.
3. They supply blood, and so oxygen, to the heart muscle.
4. Artery: thick wall with muscle and elastic fibres, carries blood away from the heart. Vein: thinner wall, often has valves, carries blood towards the heart.
5. 58 ÷ 40 × 60 = 87 beats per minute (87.0).
6. Fibrin.
7. O, A, B and AB.
8. Use a donor with a similar tissue type; give drugs that suppress the immune system.
9. Blood can clot on it, so anticoagulant drugs are often needed. (Any sensible disadvantage.)
10. (3.0 − 1.8) ÷ 1.8 × 100 = 66.7 per cent (3 s.f.).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the pulmonary vein "deoxygenated" or naming vessels by oxygen content.
- Writing "the blood" instead of the chamber or vessel by name when tracing the route.
- Saying a stent "unblocks" an artery completely, rather than holding it open.
- Giving a one-sided answer to an evaluate question: both options need an advantage and a disadvantage.
- Writing "the immune system rejects it" without naming antibodies attacking the antigens.
- Mixing up donor and recipient when reading the compatibility table.
- Forgetting the unit conversion in magnification, or using the wrong base for a percentage change.
- Saying platelets "make" the clot, with no mention of fibrinogen changing to fibrin.
