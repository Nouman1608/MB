**This guide covers the (b) question in Paper 2 Drama.** The syllabus states that on each set play
"There is a choice of two questions on each text: either (a) a passage-based question or (b) an
essay question. Candidates must answer one passage-based question and one essay question." It also
states that "Candidates may not take their set texts into the exam room." So the essay is written
from memory, about the whole play, in roughly half of a 90-minute paper. The
[Paper 2 Drama study guide](/resources/igcse-english-literature-paper-2-drama/) covers the paper's
format and why a quotation bank matters; this page teaches how to turn an essay title into a
25-mark argument. Set plays change by year, so the worked example uses an invented play.

## What the essay question asks

An essay question names a focus within the play: a character, a relationship, a theme, or the way
the playwright presents a situation. It may ask "Explore the ways in which…", "How does the playwright
make…?", or ask you directly for your view, for example "To what extent do you sympathise with…?"
or "What do you think…?". Whatever the wording, the essay is a directed argument: it answers the
question asked, using evidence chosen from across the play.

## Step 1: decode the title

Underline three things:

- **The focus:** the character, relationship or theme.
- **The key word:** "memorable", "sympathy", "powerful", "changing", "dramatic". This is your test of
  relevance.
- **The instruction:** "Explore the ways" asks for methods; "To what extent" and "What do you think"
  ask for a judgement that weighs both sides.

Then write a one-sentence answer before planning anything else. If you cannot answer the question in
one sentence, you are not yet ready to plan.

## Step 2: choose four or five moments

An essay is built from moments, not from a retelling. Choose four or five episodes spread across the
play, so that you can show development: the first appearance of a character, a crisis, a turning
point, the final scene. For each moment, recall:

- what happens and why it matters to the question;
- one or two short quotations (a few words each);
- one dramatic method: an entrance, a stage direction, dramatic irony, a soliloquy, a contrast with
  another character, the structure of the scene.

Because the text is not in the room, short quotations learned for flexibility serve best. A
precise reference ("when she tears the letter without reading it") is also valid evidence when exact
words fail you.

## Step 3: build the argument

Arrange the moments so that the argument develops. Three common structures work well:

- **Chronological development:** how the focus changes across the play, ending with your judgement.
- **Contrasting views:** reasons for one reading, then reasons for another, then a resolution. This
  suits "To what extent" and "sympathy" questions.
- **Methods:** grouping evidence by the playwright's techniques. Use this only when every paragraph
  still answers the key word.

Each paragraph should open with a topic sentence that answers the question, not with a plot event.

## Worked example: an invented play

*Harbour Lights* is an invented play used only for this example. Its main character, Agnes, runs a
harbour café. In Act 1 she refuses to sell the café to a developer; in Act 2 she discovers her son
has already signed papers behind her back; in Act 3 she gives a long speech to the empty café at
night; and in the final scene she hands the keys to the developer without a word.

**Question:** To what extent does the playwright make Agnes a sympathetic character?

**One-sentence answer:** The playwright makes Agnes deeply sympathetic by the end, but only after
showing a stubbornness in Act 1 that the audience is invited to question.

**Plan:**

1. *Introduction:* sympathy grows across the play, from frustration to pity and admiration.
2. *Act 1, the refusal:* her blunt dismissal of the developer ("Not while I breathe") presents
   stubbornness; the audience may side with her son, whose worries about money are reasonable.
3. *Act 2, the betrayal:* dramatic irony, because the audience learns of the signed papers before
   she does, so we watch her trust her son and pity her before she knows.
4. *Act 3, the speech to the empty café:* a soliloquy-like speech reveals her memories of her late
   husband; the stage direction of chairs upturned on tables creates loneliness.
5. *Final scene, the silent handover:* the absence of speech from a talkative character is the play's
   strongest statement; sympathy becomes admiration for her dignity.
6. *Conclusion:* the playwright earns our sympathy by first letting us doubt her.

**Model paragraph (point 3):**

> The playwright deepens our sympathy for Agnes in Act 2 by letting the audience learn of her son's
> betrayal before she does. Because we have already seen him sign the papers, every confident line she
> speaks about "our café" becomes painful, and her insistence that he would "never go behind my back"
> is heard by the audience as tragic irony. This structure changes our view of her stubbornness in
> Act 1: what looked like pride now looks like loyalty to a family that has not been loyal to her,
> and I find it the point at which the audience's sympathy turns firmly towards her.

*Why it works:* the topic sentence answers the question; the evidence is short and memorable; the
method (dramatic irony created by structure) is named and explained; and the paragraph connects the
moment to the argument's development across the play.

## Using dramatic methods without a printed passage

In the essay, you cannot analyse a page in close detail, so focus on methods that you can recall and
that operate across scenes:

- **Structure:** where a revelation is placed, how acts begin and end, the order in which the audience
  learns information.
- **Contrast and parallel:** characters who mirror or oppose each other.
- **Recurring props or images:** an object that returns with changed meaning.
- **Stage directions you remember:** silences, exits, lighting at a scene's end.
- **Speech patterns:** how a character's way of speaking changes as they change.

## Timing and planning

Spend about five minutes planning: the one-sentence answer, the moments and the quotations. Then write
four or five paragraphs and a short conclusion. Stop at your planned time, because the other answer
is worth the same 25 marks.

## Common mistakes

- **Character biography.** Telling the character's story from start to finish.
- **Ignoring the key word.** An essay on Agnes that never discusses sympathy.
- **One-sided judgement.** "To what extent" requires both sides weighed.
- **All moments from one act.** The essay must show the play as a whole.
- **Forgetting the play is performed.** Write about what the audience sees and hears.
- **Invented quotations.** If you are unsure of exact words, use a precise reference instead.

## Command words

"Explore" means "write in detail about particular aspects"; in an essay, the aspects are the moments
and methods you have chosen. A question that asks what you think, or how far you agree, rewards a
clear personal judgement (AO4) stated early and supported throughout.
