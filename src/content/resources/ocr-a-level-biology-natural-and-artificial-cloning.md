## Clones: the key idea

A clone is a group of genetically identical individuals, or cells, that come from one parent by asexual reproduction. Asexual reproduction uses mitosis only, with no meiosis and no fertilisation, so offspring carry the parent's alleles. Differences between clones come from the environment, apart from rare mutations. Cloning is "natural" when the organism does it unaided and "artificial" when people intervene.

## 6.2.1(a)(i) Natural clones in plants

Many plants reproduce by **vegetative propagation**. A part of the parent grows into a new, independent plant. The organs involved are modified shoots or leaves:

| Structure | How a new plant forms | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Runner (stolon) | A horizontal stem grows over the surface and puts down roots and shoots at nodes | strawberry, spider plant |
| Rhizome | An underground horizontal stem carries buds, which grow into new shoots | iris, couch grass |
| Stem tuber | A swollen underground stem stores starch and has buds ("eyes") that sprout | potato |
| Bulb | An underground bud with fleshy leaf bases stores food; side bulbs grow into new plants | daffodil, onion |
| Corm | A swollen stem base stores food; new corms form on top of the old one | crocus |
| Sucker | A new shoot grows from a root or from the base of the stem | raspberry |

Vegetative propagation is rapid and needs no pollinator. The weakness is low genetic variation: one disease can affect every clone.

**Horticulture** uses these natural methods on purpose. A grower divides rhizomes, separates bulbs and plants runners to get uniform plants with known flower colour or fruit quality, which seed-grown plants might not give.

## 6.2.1(a)(ii) Taking a cutting

A cutting is a piece of stem, leaf or root that grows into a new plant. Stem cuttings from a plant such as Pelargonium are a simple cloning technique.

1. Choose a healthy, non-flowering shoot and cut it with a clean, sharp blade (to avoid crushing tissue and letting infection in).
2. Cut just below a node. The node region has meristematic cells and tissue that can form new roots.
3. Remove the lower leaves, to cut water loss by transpiration and stop rotting.
4. Optionally dip the cut end in rooting powder, which contains an auxin that promotes root growth.
5. Push the cutting into moist, well-drained compost. Keep it humid, to reduce water loss until roots form.
6. Keep it warm and in the light.

For the practical link, you may dissect plant material to find nodes, vascular tissue and buds. Record a labelled drawing.

### Worked example 1: evaluating a rooting treatment

A student takes 30 cuttings of one plant species for each group. After four weeks, 24 cuttings treated with rooting powder have rooted and 17 untreated cuttings have rooted.

- Percentage rooted with powder: 24 ÷ 30 × 100 = **80%**.
- Percentage rooted without: 17 ÷ 30 × 100 = **56.7%**.
- Difference: 80 − 56.7 = **23.3 percentage points**. Rooting powder gave 41.2% more rooted cuttings than no powder, because (24 − 17) ÷ 17 × 100 = 41.2%.

To evaluate, note that one species and one trial is limited. Cuttings should match in length and node position, share light and temperature, and the trial should be repeated.

## 6.2.1(b)(i) Micropropagation and tissue culture

**Micropropagation** produces large numbers of identical plantlets from a very small piece of tissue, under sterile conditions. **Tissue culture** is the growing of cells or tissues in an artificial medium. Micropropagation relies on the fact that many plant cells are totipotent. A mature plant cell can be stimulated to divide and differentiate into any cell type, given the right signals.

1. **Select** a parent with the desired features and take an **explant**, such as a shoot tip (apical meristem).
2. **Sterilise** the explant, for example with dilute bleach or ethanol, to remove bacteria and fungi. Use aseptic technique throughout.
3. **Place it on a sterile nutrient agar medium** containing sugar (an energy and carbon source), mineral ions, vitamins and plant growth regulators.
4. **Control the hormone balance.** Cytokinin encourages shoot formation and auxin encourages root formation. A high cytokinin-to-auxin ratio gives shoots and a high auxin-to-cytokinin ratio gives roots. Cells may first form a mass of undifferentiated cells called a **callus**.
5. **Multiply.** Shoots are cut up and transferred to fresh medium (sub-culturing). Each cycle multiplies the number.
6. **Root** the shoots on a medium that favours roots, then transfer them to compost and acclimatise them gradually to normal humidity ("hardening off").

Sterility is essential because the sugar-rich medium suits microbes, which would outgrow the plantlets.

### Worked example 2: multiplication rate

One explant gives 6 shoots per four-week culture cycle, and each shoot is subcultured to give 6 more.

- After 4 cycles (16 weeks): 6⁴ = **1296** shoots.
- If 5% of the shoots are lost to contamination, 1296 × 0.95 = 1231.2, so about **1230** plantlets remain.

## 6.2.1(b)(ii) Plant cloning: arguments for and against

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Many identical plants quickly from one parent | Initial cost: equipment, sterile rooms and trained staff |
| Works all year, in a small space | One contamination can ruin a batch |
| Meristem tissue is often free of viruses, so plantlets are disease-free | Clones share the same alleles, so one disease or pest can destroy the entire crop |
| Preserves rare or endangered species and plants that seed poorly | Reduced genetic diversity limits adaptation and future breeding |
| Uniform crops, easier to harvest and sell | Plantlets may fail when moved to soil |
| Helps plants that seed poorly or flower slowly | Occasional unexpected variation (somaclonal variation) |

**Evaluation:** horticulture gains uniform, healthy ornamental stock, and agriculture gains disease-free planting material for crops that propagate vegetatively. Cost and loss of diversity are the main drawbacks, so weigh benefit against risk for a named use.

## 6.2.1(c) Natural clones in animals

The specification's example is **identical (monozygotic) twins**. After a zygote has divided by mitosis to form a small ball of cells, the early embryo splits into two separate groups, each developing into a separate individual. This is **embryo splitting**. The twins share a genome because they come from one fertilised egg.

Other natural animal clones include the identical offspring of the nine-banded armadillo, budding in *Hydra* and parthenogenesis in aphids.

Identical twins are not exactly the same. Mutation and different environments (diet, disease, lifestyle) create differences in phenotype, so genotype does not alone determine phenotype.

## 6.2.1(d)(i) Artificial cloning in animals

### Artificial embryo twinning

1. Eggs and sperm from selected parents are used in vitro fertilisation.
2. The embryo divides to a very early stage (a few cells), when the cells are totipotent.
3. The embryo is split into two or more groups of cells, which are cultured separately in vitro.
4. The embryos are implanted in surrogate mothers, where they develop.

The offspring are identical to each other but, as they come from a sexual cross, not to either parent. This method cannot copy an adult animal that has already proved its worth.

### Enucleation and somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT)

SCNT can clone an existing, adult animal.

1. A **somatic cell** (any body cell, not a gamete) is taken from the animal to be cloned. Its diploid nucleus holds the full genome.
2. An **unfertilised egg** is taken from a donor female and **enucleated**: its own nucleus is removed.
3. The somatic cell nucleus is put into the enucleated egg, by fusing the whole cell to the egg with a small electric pulse or by injecting the nucleus.
4. The egg is stimulated to divide, for example with an electrical or chemical stimulus. It develops in culture to an early embryo.
5. The embryo is implanted into a **surrogate mother** and develops to birth.

The clone's nuclear DNA is identical to the nucleus donor's. Mitochondrial DNA comes from the egg cytoplasm, so the clone is not identical in every respect. The surrogate contributes no DNA but provides the womb environment.

### Worked example 3: SCNT efficiency

In a trial, 150 reconstructed eggs gave 60 embryos, 12 pregnancies and 5 births. 3 of the 5 were alive after a year.

- Embryos per reconstructed egg: 60 ÷ 150 × 100 = **40%**.
- Births per reconstructed egg: 5 ÷ 150 × 100 = **3.3%**.
- Survival of births to one year: 3 ÷ 5 × 100 = **60%**.
- Overall, 3 ÷ 150 × 100 = **2%** produced a surviving animal.

Low efficiency makes the technique costly and raises welfare concerns.

## 6.2.1(d)(ii) Animal cloning: arguments for and against

| Area | Possible benefits | Concerns |
|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | Copy animals with high milk yield, meat quality or disease resistance; more uniform herds | Reduced genetic diversity; disease can affect all clones; very high cost |
| Medicine | Cloned animals producing human proteins in their milk; animals as models of disease; possible source of tissue | Ethical issues over the use of animals; low success rates |
| Conservation | Add individuals to a tiny population; a black-footed ferret was cloned in 2020 from cells frozen in 1988, restoring lost diversity | Does not solve habitat loss |
| Welfare | | Many failed pregnancies and abnormal births; ethical objections to treating animals as products |

### Longevity of cloned animals

Dolly, the first mammal cloned from an adult cell (born 1996), was euthanised at about six and a half after lung disease and arthritis, and her short telomeres led some to ask whether clones age early. A later study of cloned sheep aged 7 to 9, four from Dolly's cell line, found no clear premature ageing, only mild arthritis in some. The evidence is limited, so say that claims about longevity are not settled.

### Worked example 4: genotype and environment

Cattle clones from two embryo-split pairs have milk yields (litres per year): pair 1 6950 and 7310; pair 2 7880 and 7540.

- Pair means: 7130 and 7710 litres.
- Differences within pairs: 360 and 340 litres, which is 5.0% and 4.4% of the pair means.

Clones within a pair share a genotype, so their differences are due to environment. The gap between pair means hints that genotype matters, but two pairs is too few to be sure.

## Common errors

- Saying a SCNT clone is identical to the egg donor or the surrogate. The nuclear DNA matches the nucleus donor.
- Calling embryo splitting "cloning an adult". It copies an embryo.
- Forgetting aseptic technique or the hormones in micropropagation.
- Writing that clones are "exactly identical". The environment still alters phenotype.
- Giving one-sided answers to an evaluation. Include a benefit, a risk and a judgement.
