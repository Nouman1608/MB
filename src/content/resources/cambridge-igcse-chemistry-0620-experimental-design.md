This guide covers subtopic **12.1 Experimental design** of Topic 12, Experimental techniques and chemical analysis, in the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. All of 12.1 is **Core**. It underpins the practical papers (Paper 5, the Practical Test, and Paper 6, the Alternative to Practical), and its terms also appear on the theory papers. For quick recall use the [experimental design revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-experimental-design-revision-notes/), and practise with the [experimental design practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-experimental-design-practice/). The broader overview of Topic 12 is in [Practical Techniques: Titrations, Chromatography and Separation](/resources/practical-techniques-titrations-chromatography-separation/).

## What 12.1 asks you to do

1. **Name appropriate apparatus** for measuring time, temperature, mass and volume, including stop-watches, thermometers, balances, burettes, volumetric pipettes, measuring cylinders and gas syringes.
2. **Suggest advantages and disadvantages** of experimental methods and apparatus.
3. **Describe** six terms: solvent, solute, solution, saturated solution, residue and filtrate.

The practical-assessment section of the syllabus adds the skills that use this knowledge: identifying variables, choosing apparatus and justifying the choice, taking readings to an appropriate precision, recording results and suggesting improvements.

## Matching apparatus to the quantity

| Quantity | Apparatus | Typical use and reading |
|---|---|---|
| Time | **stop-watch** | timing a reaction; the syllabus apparatus list specifies stop-watches that measure to 1 s |
| Temperature | **thermometer** | the listed thermometers have 1 °C graduations, so read to the nearest 0.5 °C (half a division) |
| Mass | **balance** | listed balances have a precision of at least 0.1 g |
| Volume of liquid (fixed amount, accurate) | **volumetric pipette** | delivers one exact volume, such as 25.0 cm³ |
| Volume of liquid (variable amount, accurate) | **burette** | 50 cm³ burettes; read to the nearest half division |
| Volume of liquid (approximate) | **measuring cylinder** | quick; sizes from 10 cm³ to 100 cm³ |
| Volume of gas | **gas syringe** | collects and measures gas produced in a reaction |

### Why the choice matters

Each piece of volume apparatus is a compromise between **accuracy** and **convenience**:

- A **measuring cylinder** is fast and can measure many volumes, but its readings are less accurate. Use it when the volume does not need to be exact, such as adding "about 20 cm³" of acid in excess.
- A **volumetric pipette** is very accurate but measures only **one** volume. Use it when an exact, fixed volume is needed, such as the solution in the flask in a titration.
- A **burette** is accurate for **any** volume within its range, and lets liquid be added a drop at a time. Use it when the volume needed is not known in advance, such as the titre.
- A **gas syringe** measures gas volume directly. Collecting gas by displacing water from an inverted measuring cylinder is an alternative, but it **cannot** be used for gases that dissolve readily in water.

Choosing a smaller measuring cylinder for a small volume improves accuracy: 8 cm³ is measured more accurately in a 10 cm³ cylinder than in a 100 cm³ one, because the graduations are finer.

## Taking readings

- **Read at eye level** to avoid parallax error.
- For liquids, read the **bottom of the meniscus**.
- Read to the **nearest half-scale division** where required: a thermometer with 1 °C divisions is read to 0.5 °C, so record 23.5 °C, not "about 23 °C".
- Record readings with **consistent precision** in a table, with the **unit in the column heading**, not after each number.
- **Burettes read from the top down**: 0.00 cm³ is at the top, and the volume added is final reading minus initial reading.

## Suggesting advantages and disadvantages

Outcome 2 asks you to judge methods and apparatus, usually in an unfamiliar context. A useful set of headings:

| Heading | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Does the apparatus give a reading close to the true value? |
| Precision | Are readings recorded with enough decimal places? |
| Losses | Could gas escape, or solid be lost on transfer? |
| Speed | Will the reading be taken quickly enough as things change? |
| Safety | Is there a hazard, and how is it reduced? |
| Suitability | Can the apparatus measure the size of quantity expected? |

### Worked example 1: comparing two methods

*A student measures the rate of reaction between marble chips and dilute hydrochloric acid. Method A: measure the volume of carbon dioxide every 30 s with a gas syringe. Method B: measure the loss in mass of the flask every 30 s on a balance, with cotton wool in the neck. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of each.*

- **Method A, advantage:** the gas volume is measured directly; the gas syringe gives clear readings at each time.
- **Method A, disadvantage:** gas can escape while the bung is put in at the start, and the syringe can stick or reach its maximum volume if too much gas is made.
- **Method B, advantage:** the flask is not sealed, so there is no delay at the start; the balance gives a reading continuously.
- **Method B, disadvantage:** the mass of carbon dioxide lost can be small, so a balance reading to only 0.1 g gives imprecise readings. (The cotton wool lets the gas out but stops acid spray escaping, so the mass lost is due to the gas alone.)

The structure is the skill: each point names a specific feature and its effect on the results.

## Planning: variables

When you plan an investigation:

- the **independent variable** is the one you change;
- the **dependent variable** is the one you measure;
- **control variables** are kept the same, so the test is fair.

### Worked example 2: identifying variables and apparatus

*Plan an experiment to find how temperature affects the time for magnesium ribbon to react completely with dilute hydrochloric acid.*

- **Independent variable:** temperature of the acid, measured with a **thermometer**. Choose about five values across a sensible range, for example from room temperature up to about 60 °C.
- **Dependent variable:** time for the magnesium to disappear, measured with a **stop-watch**.
- **Control variables:** volume of acid (measured with a measuring cylinder), concentration of acid, length or mass of magnesium ribbon.
- **Repeats:** repeat each temperature and calculate a mean to reduce the effect of random errors.

## The six terms in outcome 3

Learn the syllabus wording:

| Term | Description |
|---|---|
| **Solvent** | a substance that dissolves a solute |
| **Solute** | a substance that is dissolved in a solvent |
| **Solution** | a mixture of one or more solutes dissolved in a solvent |
| **Saturated solution** | a solution containing the maximum concentration of a solute dissolved in the solvent at a specified temperature |
| **Residue** | a substance that remains after evaporation, distillation, filtration or any similar process |
| **Filtrate** | a liquid or solution that has passed through a filter |

Two common slips: a saturated solution is defined **at a specified temperature**, because most solids dissolve more at higher temperatures; and the **residue** stays in the filter paper while the **filtrate** passes through.

Applied example: when sand and salt solution are filtered, **sand** is the residue and **salt solution** is the filtrate. In that filtrate, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a measuring cylinder** when the question needs an exact volume for a titration.
- **Recording a thermometer reading as a whole number** when it should be read to 0.5 °C.
- **Writing units in every cell** rather than in the column heading.
- **Defining a saturated solution without "at a specified temperature"**.
- **Swapping residue and filtrate.**
- **Vague evaluations** such as "human error" or "more accurate" with no reason.

## Exam technique

- **Name:** give the specific apparatus: "volumetric pipette", not "pipette" or "measuring device".
- **Suggest** (advantage or disadvantage): link a feature to its effect on the result.
- **Describe** (a term): use the syllabus wording.
- **Explain** (a choice): say why the alternative is worse.
