## Question set A — How does society control us? (2.2)

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *negative sanction* **[2]**
- (ii) *digital surveillance* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of formal agencies of social control. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** reasons why the police may not be effective in achieving conformity.
**[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways religion may control behaviour. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss why some young people join subcultures. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that informal social control is more effective than formal social control.
**[14]**

## Question set B — What influences our social identity? (2.3)

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *ethnicity* **[2]**
- (ii) *global culture* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** ways age may affect a person's identity. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways gender identity may be changing. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** positive impacts of virtual communities on identity. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss how globalisation may strengthen local or traditional identities. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that social class is the most important influence on identity. **[14]**

## Answers — Question set A

**(a)(i) [2]** A punishment or penalty given for breaking a norm or rule, such as a fine, detention
or disapproval (2).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Monitoring people's behaviour using technology such as CCTV, phone records, internet
activity or facial recognition (2).

**(b) [2]** Any two: government, police, courts, penal system, armed forces.

**(c) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each: many crimes are never reported; limited resources and
officers; low public trust or perceived corruption reduces co-operation; police cannot be present
everywhere; some people do not see laws as legitimate; targeting of some groups may cause
resentment.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each: **religious teachings** set moral rules (for example against
theft or dishonesty); **promise of reward** in an afterlife encourages conformity; **religious
sanctions** such as exclusion from the community; **community disapproval** from fellow believers;
**rituals and routines** such as regular prayer build self-discipline.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 developed and well explained; 4–6 some development; 1–3 basic.
- Sense of belonging and friendship.
- Status and identity that school or work does not give (status frustration).
- Shared interests such as music, fashion or gaming.
- Rejection of adult or mainstream values; resistance.
- Peer pressure and excitement.
- Online subcultures offer easy access and anonymity.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but less
balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.
- *For:* informal control works from birth and is internalised; family and peer sanctions are
  immediate and personal; most people conform because of what others think, not fear of police;
  religion and community shape morality.
- *Against:* informal control may break down in large, anonymous cities; peer groups can encourage
  deviance; serious crimes require police, courts and prisons; digital surveillance has increased
  formal control's reach.
- *Conclusion:* the two work together — informal control governs everyday norms; formal control
  backs it up.

## Answers — Question set B

**(a)(i) [2]** A shared cultural identity based on factors such as language, religion, customs,
history or place of origin (2).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Cultural products, styles and values shared across many countries through global
media, brands and travel (2).

**(b) [2]** Any two: age determines roles and responsibilities (child, elder); expectations about
behaviour and dress; rights such as voting or marriage; respect given to elders; generational
tastes in music or technology.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each: more women in higher education and paid work; men taking a
larger role in childcare; media showing more varied images of men and women; laws promoting equal
rights; decline of jobs based on physical strength changing ideas of masculinity.

**(d) [6]** Three impacts, 2 marks each: people with rare interests or minority identities find
support; diaspora members keep links with their culture; young people can experiment with identity
safely; access to information and role models; participation in campaigns builds confidence.

**(e) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- **Cultural defence:** when global culture feels threatening, people reaffirm language, dress,
  religion or national traditions.
- Global media can spread local culture worldwide, increasing pride (for example regional music or
  film finding international audiences).
- Migrants abroad may become more attached to their heritage.
- **Hybrid identities** keep traditional elements alongside new ones.

**(f) [14]**
- *For:* class shapes income, education, housing, leisure, accent and aspirations; cultural capital
  passed on in families; class strongly affects life chances.
- *Against:* gender, ethnicity, religion and age may matter more in some societies; postmodernists
  argue consumption and lifestyle now shape identity; digital identities and global culture cut
  across class; intersectionality — identities combine.
- *Conclusion:* importance of class varies between societies and individuals; credit a reasoned
  judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use examples from my own country?** Yes — local examples of agencies, subcultures or
identities are credited as application.

**What separates (e) from (f)?** Part (e) asks you to discuss an issue in depth; part (f) asks you to
argue both sides of a debate and reach a judgement.

**How many points do I need for (c) and (d)?** Three, each identified and then explained.
