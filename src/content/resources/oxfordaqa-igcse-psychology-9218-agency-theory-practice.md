These questions cover "Obedience as investigated by Milgram" and "Milgram's Agency theory of social
factors affecting obedience: agency, authority, culture and proximity" in **3.2.2 Social influence**
from Paper 2 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology (9218).

---

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** In which variation of Milgram's research was obedience lowest?
A The study was moved to a run-down office building
B The learner was in the same room as the teacher
C The teacher had to press the learner's hand onto a plate
D The experimenter gave orders by telephone **[1]**

**2.** Define the term *agentic state*. **[1]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by *moral strain*. **[2]**

**4.** Identify the dependent variable in Milgram's baseline study and state how it was measured. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by a legitimate authority, giving an example. **[2]**

**6.** Outline how Milgram's procedure was standardised. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Describe Milgram's baseline study of obedience. Refer to the procedure and the results. **[5]**

**8.** Explain how proximity affects obedience. Refer to **two** of Milgram's variations. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how culture may affect obedience, and outline **one** problem with using replications in
different countries as evidence. **[4]**

**10.** A school's head teacher tells a new teacher, Mr Okafor, to give a whole class a detention for one
student's behaviour. Mr Okafor thinks this is unfair but does it. Use Agency theory to explain his
behaviour. **[4]**

**11.** Identify **two** ethical issues in Milgram's research and explain **one** of them. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate Milgram's Agency theory as an explanation of obedience. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **D** [1]. (Telephone orders: 20.5%; the other options were 47.5%, 40% and 30%.)

**2.** A state in which a person sees themselves as an agent carrying out another person's wishes, and
so no longer feels responsible for their own actions [1].

**3.** The anxiety a person feels [1] when they are carrying out an order that conflicts with their own
sense of right and wrong [1].

**4.** Obedience [1], measured as the maximum voltage the participant gave before refusing to continue
(or whether they reached 450 volts) [1].

**5.** A person whose right to give orders is accepted because of their recognised position in a social
hierarchy [1]; for example, a police officer, a teacher or a scientist in a lab coat at a university
[1].

**6.** Any two, [1] each: every participant had the same experimenter and learner; the same script of
the learner's responses; the same four prods in the same order; the same shock generator and voltage
steps; the same room and setting. Maximum 2.

**7.** One mark per point, up to 5:
- 40 male participants were recruited by newspaper advertisement for a study of memory [1].
- A fixed draw made the participant the teacher and a confederate the learner, in the next room [1].
- The teacher had to give increasingly strong electric shocks (15 to 450 volts) for each wrong answer;
  no real shocks were given [1].
- If the teacher hesitated, the experimenter used standard prods to make them continue [1].
- 65% of participants went to 450 volts [1].
- All went to at least 300 volts, and many showed signs of extreme stress [1].
Maximum 5.

**8.** One mark for the general point, then credit for each variation [4]:
- The closer and more visible the victim, the harder it is to ignore their suffering, so obedience
  falls [1].
- When the learner was in the same room, obedience fell from 65% to 40% [1].
- When the teacher had to press the learner's hand onto a shock plate, it fell to 30% [1].
- Proximity of the authority also matters: when orders came by telephone, obedience fell to 20.5%
  [1].
Maximum 4.

**9.** Culture, up to 2: cultures differ in how much they value respect for authority and hierarchy [1],
and people socialised to value obedience may enter the agentic state more readily [1]. Problem, up to
2: replications often used different procedures, participants or victims [1], so differences in
obedience rates cannot be attributed to culture alone [1].

**10.** One mark per point, up to 4:
- Mr Okafor sees the head teacher as a legitimate authority because of their position in the school
  [1].
- He shifts into the agentic state, seeing himself as carrying out the head teacher's wishes [1].
- He passes responsibility for the unfair detention to the head teacher, so he obeys [1].
- His sense that it is unfair causes moral strain, which he may reduce with a binding factor such as
  "it is the school's policy" [1].
Maximum 4.

**11.** Identify two, [1] each: deception; lack of fully informed consent; not protected from harm
(distress); the right to withdraw made difficult by the prods. Explain one [1]: for example,
participants were deceived about the true aim and believed they were really giving shocks, so they
could not give fully informed consent. Maximum 3.

**12.** Award up to 6, for balanced evaluation:
- Strength: it explains the variations; obedience fell as the victim got closer and authority became
  less legitimate or less close [1].
- Strength: real-world support from Hofling's study, where 21 of 22 nurses were prepared to obey a
  telephone order from an unknown doctor [1].
- Limitation: it cannot explain individual differences, why about a third of participants defied the
  experimenter in the same situation [1].
- Limitation: a dispositional explanation, such as the Authoritarian Personality, is needed alongside
  it [1].
- Limitation: the agentic state is hard to measure separately from obedience, so the explanation can
  be circular [1].
- Limitation: participants' distress suggests they still felt responsible, which does not fit a
  complete agentic shift [1].
Maximum 6.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that real shocks were given.
- Giving variation figures in the wrong place.
- Applying Agency theory without naming the agentic state or legitimate authority.
- Evaluation that only describes more studies without saying what they show about the theory.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many variation figures should I learn?**
Learn enough to support each element: at least one for authority (such as the run-down office) and
two for proximity (such as same room and telephone orders).

**Is Agency theory social or dispositional?**
The specification calls it a theory of social factors affecting obedience, so it is a social
(situational) explanation.
