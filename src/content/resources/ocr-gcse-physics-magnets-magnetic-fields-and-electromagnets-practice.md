## Questions

**1.** Two bar magnets are placed on a bench.

**(a)** Say what happens if two north poles are brought together. **[1]**
**(b)** Say what happens if a north pole and a south pole are brought together. **[1]**

**2.** A steel paper clip hangs from the north pole of a bar magnet. Explain, using the idea of induced magnetism, why the clip does not fall. **[3]**

**3.** A student tests three rods, P, Q and R, using the north pole of a bar magnet.

| Rod | End 1 | End 2 |
|---|---|---|
| P | attracted | attracted |
| Q | repelled | attracted |
| R | no force | no force |

**(a)** Identify the rod that is a permanent magnet and give the reason. **[2]**
**(b)** State what rod P could be made of, if it is not a magnet. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest what rod R could be made of. **[1]**

**4.** Describe how you would plot the magnetic field around a bar magnet using a plotting compass. **[4]**

**5.** A diagram shows the field lines around a bar magnet. Near one pole the lines are 0.5 cm apart. At point B, further away, they are 1.5 cm apart.

**(a)** State which position has the stronger field and explain how you know. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times further apart the lines are at B than near the pole. **[1]**

**6.** A dipping compass has its needle free to turn in a vertical plane. At location X the needle is 70 degrees below the horizontal. At location Y it is 5 degrees below the horizontal.

**(a)** State which location is nearer a magnetic pole of the Earth. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how the behaviour of a dipping compass is related to evidence that the core of the Earth must be magnetic. **[3]**

**7.** A long straight vertical wire passes through a horizontal card. Small plotting compasses are placed on the card around the wire.

**(a)** Describe the pattern of the magnetic field when a large direct current flows in the wire. **[2]**
**(b)** State what happens to the compass needles when the current is reversed. **[1]**

**8.** Plotting compasses are placed on a card at different distances from a current-carrying wire. The Earth's field is the same at each. The table shows how far each needle turns from its original direction.

| Distance from wire (cm) | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turn of needle (degrees) | 55 | 30 | 17 | 9 |

**(a)** Describe the trend shown. **[2]**
**(b)** State what the trend shows about the field strength. **[1]**
**(c)** State what would happen to the turn at 6 cm if the current were increased. **[1]**

**9.** A student winds a wire into a solenoid and connects it to a power supply.

**(a)** State which magnet the shape of the field outside a solenoid resembles. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why coiling the wire makes the field stronger than that of a straight wire. **[2]**
**(c)** State two other changes that would strengthen the field. **[2]**

**10.** A student investigates an electromagnet at a constant current of 1.0 A. The number of paper clips it can pick up is recorded.

| Number of turns | 25 | 50 | 75 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clips lifted | 6 | 12 | 19 | 23 |

**(a)** Describe the pattern in the results. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in clips lifted from 25 turns to 100 turns. **[2]**
**(c)** State two variables that must be controlled. **[2]**

**11.** A scrapyard crane uses a large electromagnet to lift steel scrap and drop it somewhere else. Explain how the electromagnet works, why its core is soft iron and how it could be made to lift a heavier load. **[6]**

**12.** A solenoid has 240 turns spread evenly over 12 cm. It carries a current of 0.8 A.

**(a)** Calculate the number of turns per centimetre. **[1]**
**(b)** The coil is compressed to 8 cm long with the same turns. Calculate the new turns per centimetre and the percentage increase. **[2]**
**(c)** The current is then raised from 0.8 A to 1.4 A. Calculate the percentage increase in current. **[1]**
**(d)** State the effect of both changes on the strength of the magnetic field in the solenoid. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The two magnets repel. [1]
**(b)** They attract. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Writing only "they are pulled together" for like poles loses the mark because the force direction is the answer, so name attract or repel.

**2.** The magnet's field makes the clip an induced magnet [1]. The end of the clip nearest the north pole becomes a south pole [1]. Opposite poles attract, so the force of attraction holds up the clip against its weight [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The explanation needs the idea of an induced pole at the end nearest the magnet, not just the statement that steel is magnetic.

**3. (a)** Rod Q [1]. Only a magnet can repel the north pole, since an induced magnet is always attracted [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Iron or steel (or cobalt or nickel) that is not magnetised [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** A non-magnetic material such as copper, aluminium or plastic [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Attraction alone proves nothing, so an answer naming P as the magnet because it is attracted scores nothing for the reason.

**4.** Place the bar magnet on paper and put a plotting compass near one pole [1]. Mark the positions of both ends of the needle with pencil dots [1]. Move the compass so that its tail end is on the previous dot, and mark the new head end, repeating until the other pole is reached [1]. Join the dots into a smooth line and add an arrow showing the direction the north end of the needle points (towards the south pole); repeat from other starting points [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A method that omits the step of moving the compass along the line is incomplete, and joining the dots without an arrow leaves direction unstated.

**5. (a)** The field is stronger near the pole [1]. The lines are closer together there, and closer lines mean a stronger field [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 1.5 ÷ 0.5 = **3 times** further apart [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "The lines are bigger" is not an explanation; link the spacing to the strength, and do not suggest that three times the spacing means exactly one third of the strength.

**6. (a)** Location X [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** A compass needle is itself a small magnet, so it settles along whatever field surrounds it [1]. The needle is almost vertical near the poles and nearly horizontal near the equator, which matches the field of a bar magnet [1]. A magnetic field is produced by a magnet or a current, so the Earth's core must be magnetic [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Saying "the compass points north so there is a magnet inside" is too vague; reference the dip pattern and the idea that the compass aligns with the field.

**7. (a)** The field forms concentric circles [1] centred on the wire, in planes at right angles to it [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Every needle turns through 180 degrees, so the field direction reverses [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Straight lines or lines pointing outwards from the wire are the usual wrong answers; say circles, not just "round".

**8. (a)** As the distance from the wire increases, the turn of the needle decreases [1], from 55 degrees at 3 cm to 9 degrees at 12 cm [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The field is weaker the further it is from the wire [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** The needle would turn through a larger angle [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote values from the table with units, and describe both the direction of the trend and the data.

**9. (a)** A bar magnet [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Each loop of wire produces its own field [1]. Inside the coil these fields point in the same direction and add together [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any two of: increase the current, add more turns, put the turns closer together, add an iron core [2]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Make the solenoid bigger" does not gain a mark because it can mean longer, which weakens the field per turn.

**10. (a)** More turns means more clips lifted [1]. The number rises from 6 at 25 turns to 23 at 100 turns, roughly in proportion [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (23 − 6) ÷ 6 × 100 [1] = **283% (3 s.f.)** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any two of: the current, the core material, the length of the coil, the size and type of paper clips [2]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Percentage change divides by the original value, so dividing by 23 gives a wrong answer even with correct subtraction.

**11.** Current in the coil produces a magnetic field [1]. The iron core becomes an induced magnet, adding to the field [1]. The steel scrap is attracted because it also becomes an induced magnet [1]. Soft iron is used because it stops being magnetic soon after the current stops [1], so the scrap drops [1]. Increasing the current or adding more turns gives a stronger field and can lift a heavier load [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A long answer needs separate points for how it works, why soft iron and how to improve it; repeating "it is magnetic" gains little.

**12. (a)** 240 ÷ 12 = **20 turns per cm** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 240 ÷ 8 = **30 turns per cm** [1], so the increase is (30 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = **50%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** (1.4 − 0.8) ÷ 0.8 × 100 = **75%** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Both changes make the field stronger [1], because more turns per centimetre and a larger current each increase the field in the solenoid [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction and division so that the examiner can follow each step, and state the direction of the effect, not just "it changes".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing or describing field lines that cross, or have no arrows.
- Saying attraction proves a rod is a magnet.
- Forgetting that an induced magnet is always attracted.
- Writing that the field lines run from south to north outside the magnet.
- Describing the field around a wire as straight lines.
- Quoting no figures from a table when asked to describe a trend.
- Dividing a percentage change by the new value instead of the original.
- Giving "bigger" or "stronger" with no variable named when asked how to change an electromagnet.
- Writing about the compass without saying that it is a small magnet.
