These questions cover section 3.8.1 strategic direction of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-strategic-direction-markets-and-products/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-strategic-direction-markets-and-products-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A UK bakery begins selling its existing bread range through a chain of supermarkets in France. Which part of the Ansoff matrix does this describe? **[1]**

A: Market penetration

B: Market development

C: New product development

D: Diversification

**2.** Which **one** of the following is usually the riskiest strategy in the Ansoff matrix? **[1]**

A: Selling more existing products to existing customers

B: Selling existing products in a new country

C: Launching a new product to existing customers

D: Launching a new product in a new market

**3.** Define "market penetration". **[2]**

**4.** A firm has a 10% share of a market worth £80m. It raises its share to 13%. Calculate the increase in its annual sales. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a firm in a saturated market might choose new product development. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

Hexham Games sells board games in the UK. It is considering three options, shown below. Revenue figures are extra annual revenue if the option succeeds.

| Option | Investment | Extra revenue if successful | Probability of success |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: Advertising campaign to boost sales of current games in the UK | £0.5m | £2.0m | 70% |
| B: Selling current games in Scandinavian countries | £1.0m | £3.5m | 50% |
| C: Developing and selling video games | £2.0m | £5.0m | 40% |

**6.** Identify which Ansoff category each of options A, B and C fits. **[3]**

**7.** Calculate the expected extra revenue for each option and the expected revenue per £1 invested. **[6]**

**8.** Hexham is deciding which option to choose. Analyse **two** reasons why option A might be the most suitable. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **two** risks facing Hexham if it chooses option C. **[4]**

**10.** The directors argue that, because option C has the highest possible revenue, it should be chosen. Assess this view. **[9]**

**11.** Analyse why a family-owned business may prefer market penetration to diversification. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of the Ansoff matrix to a business choosing its strategic direction. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. The product is unchanged but the market (France) is new. **[1]**

**2.** **D** [1]. New products in new markets give the least familiarity and the highest chance of failure. **[1]**

**3.** Selling more of the firm's existing products [1] in its existing markets, for example through advertising, lower prices or wider distribution [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Current sales = 10% × £80m = £8m [1]; new sales = 13% × £80m = £10.4m, so an increase of **£2.4m** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** In a saturated market, sales of existing products can grow only by taking share from rivals [1]. A new product can attract new buyers or give existing customers a reason to buy more [1], using the firm's knowledge of the market and so with less risk than entering an unknown market [1]. **[3]**

**6.** A: market penetration [1]. B: market development [1]. C: diversification [1] (new product in a new market). **[3]**

**7.** A: £2.0m × 70% = **£1.4m** [1]; per £1 invested = 1.4 ÷ 0.5 = **2.8** [1]. B: £3.5m × 50% = **£1.75m** [1]; 1.75 ÷ 1.0 = **1.75** [1]. C: £5.0m × 40% = **£2.0m** [1]; 2.0 ÷ 2.0 = **1.0** [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Reason 1: the lowest cost and highest chance of success [1]. A has the highest probability of success (70%) and the best return per pound (2.8) [1], so the expected gain is large relative to the investment [1]. Reason 2: it uses existing strengths [1]. Hexham already knows its customers, products and competitors in the UK [1], so can start quickly with little new learning [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Risk 1: no experience [1]. Hexham knows board games but not how to design, produce or market video games, so the business may be uncompetitive against specialist firms [1]. Risk 2: cost and low chance of success [1]. £2.0m is the largest investment and the probability of success is only 40%, so there is a 60% chance of losing much of the money [1]. (Credit also: distraction of management; strong rivals.) **[4]**

**10.** For: option C offers the largest extra revenue if it works (£5.0m) [1], and a new product line would reduce reliance on board games, spreading risk [1]. Its expected revenue is the highest (£2.0m) [1]. Against: its expected revenue per £1 invested is the lowest (1.0) [1], so it generates the least for each pound [1]. It is the most likely to fail, with a 60% chance [1]. Revenue is not profit, and costs of development and marketing may exceed the return [1]. Judgement: the directors' view ignores risk and cost [1]; unless Hexham has spare funds and evidence of a strong game idea, A or B offers a better balance [1], and the company could pursue C later. **[9]**

**11.** Family-owned businesses often value stability and control [1], so are less willing to take big risks with the family's wealth [1]. Market penetration uses the family's existing skills and customer relationships [1], is cheaper and can be funded from retained profit, avoiding outside investors who would dilute control [1]. Diversification needs large funds and new expertise [1], and a failure could threaten the whole business and its reputation across generations [1]. **[6]**

**12.** For: the matrix gives a simple framework for classifying growth options [1], and links the choice to risk, so managers can compare options [1]. It encourages managers to consider both products and markets [1]. It shows the trade-off between risk and reward [1]. Against: it ignores finance, resources and capabilities [1]. It does not say how to grow, for example organic growth or takeover [1]. It gives no guidance on competitors' reactions [1], and the boundaries between categories are blurred [1]. Risk rankings are generalisations [1]. Factors: how well the business knows its markets, and whether the matrix is combined with financial appraisal and analysis of the external environment [1]. Judgement: the matrix is a helpful starting point but not a decision tool on its own [1]; the best decisions combine it with investment appraisal and a realistic assessment of resources [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers go beyond naming the box and use the case evidence on market growth, finance and risk to justify a choice.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Misclassifying a strategy by looking at only the product or only the market.
- Treating the risk ranking as fixed.
- Recommending diversification with no discussion of cost or expertise.
- Taking expected revenue as profit.
- Failing to link the choice to objectives or resources.
