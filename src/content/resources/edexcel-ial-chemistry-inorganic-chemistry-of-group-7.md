## 8.24 Physical trends, electronegativity and reactivity

**Use these values:**

| Halogen | Melting temperature / °C | Boiling temperature / °C | State at room temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | −101 | −34 | gas |
| Br₂ | −7 | 59 | liquid |
| I₂ | 114 | 184 | solid |

All three are simple molecular, made of diatomic molecules. Melting or boiling overcomes the London
forces between molecules; it does not break the X–X covalent bond.

Down the group, each molecule has more electrons, so instantaneous dipoles are larger and London forces
are stronger. More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so melting and boiling temperatures rise. Room temperature
(about 20 °C) is above both values for chlorine, between them for bromine and below both for iodine.

**Electronegativity** measures how strongly an atom pulls the shared electron pair of a covalent bond towards
itself. Its value falls down the group. The atoms get bigger and have more inner shells. The extra nuclear charge
is largely cancelled by extra shielding, so the more distant bonding pair is attracted less strongly.

**Reactivity** decreases down the group. A halogen reacts by gaining electrons, so it acts as an oxidising
agent: X₂ + 2e⁻ → 2X⁻. An electron arriving at a larger atom enters a shell further from the nucleus, with
more shielding between them, so it is attracted less. Iodine therefore gains electrons least readily and
is the weakest oxidising agent of the three.

## 8.25 Displacement reactions and colours

Any halogen higher in the group will oxidise the halide ions of one lower down. Learn the colours first.

| | Standard conditions | In aqueous solution | In a non-polar solvent such as cyclohexane |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine | pale green gas | very pale green, almost colourless | very pale green, almost colourless |
| Bromine | red-brown liquid | yellow to orange | orange to red-brown |
| Iodine | shiny grey-black solid | brown (yellow-brown when dilute) | violet |

The reactions that happen:

- Cl₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq)
- Cl₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)
- Br₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)

Bromine does not react with chloride ions; iodine reacts with neither. The halogen is reduced (0 to −1)
and the halide ion oxidised (−1 to 0).

### Worked example 1: reading a displacement grid

A student adds a few drops of each aqueous halogen to potassium halide solutions, then shakes each tube
with cyclohexane and lets the layers separate. Predict what she sees in the top (organic) layer when
bromine water is added to potassium iodide.

1. Bromine is above iodine, so it oxidises iodide: Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂.
2. The product halogen is iodine. Iodine is non-polar, so it moves into the cyclohexane.
3. The organic layer turns **violet**. In water, brown iodine is easily confused with orange bromine;
   the solvent makes the difference clear.

## 8.26 Redox reactions in terms of oxidation number

### Reactions with Group 1 and 2 metals

Halogens oxidise these metals to their ions. For example, 2K + Cl₂ → 2KCl and Ca + Br₂ → CaBr₂. In
the calcium reaction calcium goes from 0 to +2 and each bromine atom goes from 0 to −1. The reactions
become less vigorous from chlorine to iodine, matching the fall in oxidising power.

### Worked example 2: calcium and bromine

What mass of calcium bromide forms if 2.00 g of calcium is heated in excess bromine vapour?

1. n(Ca) = 2.00 ÷ 40.1 = 0.04988 mol.
2. Ca + Br₂ → CaBr₂ is 1 : 1, so n(CaBr₂) = 0.04988 mol.
3. Mr(CaBr₂) = 40.1 + 2(79.9) = 199.9.
4. Mass = 0.04988 × 199.9 = **9.97 g**.

### Chlorine with water

Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO

Chlorine goes from 0 to −1 in HCl and from 0 to +1 in chloric(I) acid, HClO. One element in a single
species both rises and falls in oxidation number, which makes this **disproportionation**.

Chloric(I) acid kills bacteria, so chlorine is used in water treatment to cut the risk of waterborne
disease such as cholera. Against this, chlorine is toxic and must be dosed carefully, and it may combine
with organic material in the water, giving chlorinated organic by-products, some possibly harmful.

### Making bleach: chlorine and cold, dilute alkali

Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O

Chlorine goes from 0 to −1 in NaCl and to +1 in sodium chlorate(I), NaClO. The resulting solution is
household **bleach**.

### Chlorine with hot alkali

3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O

With hot alkali, chlorine goes from 0 to −1 in chloride and to +5 in sodium chlorate(V), NaClO₃. Check
the ratio by counting electrons.

### Worked example 3: an analogous reaction with bromine

Bromine reacts with hot potassium hydroxide in the same way as chlorine does with hot alkali. Write the
equation and show that the electrons balance.

1. Products by analogy: KBr, KBrO₃ (potassium bromate(V)) and water.
2. Equation: 3Br₂ + 6KOH → 5KBr + KBrO₃ + 3H₂O.
3. Oxidation numbers: Br goes 0 → −1 in KBr (five atoms, each gaining one electron: 5e⁻ gained).
4. One Br atom goes 0 → +5 in KBrO₃ (5e⁻ lost).
5. Electrons gained = electrons lost = 5, so the equation balances in redox terms.

Analogous reactions use the chlorine pattern for bromine or iodine, or potassium in place of sodium.

## 8.27 Reactions of halides and hydrogen halides

### Solid halides with concentrated sulfuric acid

All three solid sodium or potassium halides first give the hydrogen halide, seen as steamy fumes in
moist air. For example: KCl + H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HCl. This step is acid–base, not redox.

What happens next depends on how strongly the hydrogen halide reduces sulfuric acid.

| Halide | Further reaction | Sulfur's oxidation number | Observations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chloride | none: HCl cannot reduce H₂SO₄ | stays +6 | steamy fumes only |
| Bromide | 2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O | +6 → +4 | steamy fumes, orange-brown vapour, choking gas |
| Iodide | 2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O; 6HI + H₂SO₄ → 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O; 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O | +6 → +4, 0 and −2 | some steamy fumes, purple vapour, black solid, yellow solid, smell of bad eggs |

So the reducing ability of the hydrogen halides increases HCl < HBr < HI. In a larger halide ion the outermost
electrons are more distant from the nucleus and better shielded, so they are lost more easily.

### Worked example 4: building the hydrogen sulfide equation

Show how 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O balances.

1. Sulfur goes from +6 in H₂SO₄ to −2 in H₂S, so one sulfur atom gains 8 electrons.
2. Each iodine goes from −1 to 0, losing one electron. Eight iodide ions lose 8 electrons, forming 4I₂.
3. Hydrogen: 8 from HI plus 2 from H₂SO₄ = 10; on the right 2 in H₂S plus 8 in 4H₂O = 10.
4. Oxygen: 4 on each side. The equation balances.

### Halide ions with silver nitrate

Add dilute nitric acid, then aqueous silver nitrate. The nitric acid removes ions such as carbonate that
would otherwise give a precipitate with silver ions. Do not use hydrochloric acid: it adds chloride ions.

Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s)

| Ion | Precipitate | Dilute aqueous ammonia | Concentrated aqueous ammonia |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | white precipitate, AgCl | soluble | soluble |
| Br⁻ | cream precipitate, AgBr | insoluble (at most slightly soluble) | soluble |
| I⁻ | yellow precipitate, AgI | insoluble | insoluble |

The ammonia step matters because white and cream look alike.

### Worked example 5: precipitate mass to concentration

A 25.0 cm³ portion of potassium iodide solution is acidified and treated with excess silver nitrate. The
dried yellow precipitate weighs 0.470 g. Find the concentration of the potassium iodide solution.

1. Mr(AgI) = 107.9 + 126.9 = 234.8.
2. n(AgI) = 0.470 ÷ 234.8 = 2.002 × 10⁻³ mol = n(I⁻), since the ratio is 1 : 1.
3. Concentration = 2.002 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0801 mol dm⁻³**.

### Hydrogen halides with ammonia and with water

With ammonia gas, each hydrogen halide gives a white smoke of the ammonium halide:
HBr(g) + NH₃(g) → NH₄Br(s). The same reaction with HCl is used as a test for either gas.

With water, hydrogen halides dissolve readily and ionise to give acids: HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
This is also why they fume in moist air.

### Worked example 6: mixing two gases

60.0 cm³ of hydrogen bromide is mixed with 45.0 cm³ of ammonia at room temperature and pressure. Find
the gas left over and the mass of ammonium bromide formed.

1. The ratio is 1 : 1, so ammonia (45.0 cm³) is limiting.
2. Gas left: 60.0 − 45.0 = **15.0 cm³ of hydrogen bromide**.
3. n(NH₄Br) = 45.0 ÷ 24 000 = 1.875 × 10⁻³ mol; Mr = 14.0 + 4(1.0) + 79.9 = 97.9.
4. Mass = 1.875 × 10⁻³ × 97.9 = **0.184 g**.

The specification's suggested practicals are solid potassium halides with concentrated sulfuric acid
(small amounts, in a fume cupboard, as the gases are toxic) and precipitation reactions of anions.

## 8.28 Predicting fluorine and astatine

Extend each trend one step up or down.

- **Fluorine:** a pale yellow gas with the lowest boiling temperature, the highest electronegativity and the
  strongest oxidising power. It would displace chlorine, bromine and iodine from their halides.
- **Astatine:** a dark solid with the highest melting temperature and the weakest oxidising power. It would
  be displaced by every halogen above it. Silver astatide would be a dark precipitate, insoluble in
  concentrated ammonia. Hydrogen astatide would be the strongest reducing agent of the hydrogen halides.

### Worked example 7: a prediction question

Predict the products when solid sodium astatide is warmed with concentrated sulfuric acid.

1. First, acid–base: NaAt + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HAt.
2. HAt should be a stronger reducing agent than HI, so expect astatine plus reduced sulfur products
   such as SO₂, S and H₂S.

Be careful with fluorine. It is always −1 in compounds because it is the most electronegative element, so
it cannot take the positive oxidation numbers that chlorine has in chlorate(I) and chlorate(V). Predictions
based on chlorine's disproportionation do not carry over to fluorine.

## Common errors

- Saying boiling breaks the X–X bond. It overcomes London forces between molecules.
- Describing iodine in cyclohexane as brown. It is violet; brown is its colour in water.
- Calling the chlorine–water reaction plain reduction. Chlorine is reduced and oxidised, so it is disproportionation.
- Writing NaClO₃ for cold alkali or NaClO for hot alkali.
- Acidifying a silver nitrate test with hydrochloric acid.
- Stating that sulfuric acid is oxidised. It is reduced.
