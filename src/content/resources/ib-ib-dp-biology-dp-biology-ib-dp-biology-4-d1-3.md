This guide teaches section D1.3 (Mutation and gene editing) of the IB *Biology guide*, first assessment 2025. Statements D1.3.1 to D1.3.7 are for SL and HL; D1.3.8 to D1.3.10 are additional higher level and are labelled "HL only". The combined [D1.1 and D1.3 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-biology-dna-replication-mutation-and-gene-editing/) gives the definitions and a coded example of each mutation type. This page goes further: two real human mutations traced from DNA to phenotype, how individual mutagens damage DNA, the evidence that mutation is random, and how a CRISPR edit is actually made and used.

## Gene mutations as structural changes (D1.3.1)

A gene mutation is a change to the base sequence of a gene. The guide asks you to distinguish three structural types:

| Type | Change to the base sequence | Effect on the reading frame |
|---|---|---|
| Substitution | One base replaced by another | Unchanged |
| Insertion | One or more extra bases added | Shifted unless a multiple of three is added |
| Deletion | One or more bases lost | Shifted unless a multiple of three is lost |

## Base substitutions and SNPs (D1.3.2)

A **single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)** is a position in the genome where individuals in a population differ by a single base. SNPs arise from base substitution mutations. Because the genetic code is degenerate (most amino acids have more than one codon), a substitution may leave the amino acid unchanged, change one amino acid, or create a stop codon.

### Worked example: sickle cell anaemia

The gene for the beta chain of haemoglobin has, at its sixth codon, GAG on the coding strand in most people. In the sickle cell allele this is GTG.

1. mRNA codon changes from GAG to GUG.
2. GAG codes for glutamic acid; GUG codes for valine.
3. One amino acid in a chain of 146 is different: a missense substitution.
4. Glutamic acid has a charged, hydrophilic R group; valine has a non-polar, hydrophobic one. At low oxygen concentration the altered haemoglobin molecules stick to each other and form long fibres.
5. Red blood cells become rigid and sickle-shaped, block capillaries and are destroyed early, causing anaemia.

This example shows the full chain of reasoning examiners reward: base change, codon change, amino acid change, change in protein properties, effect on the cell and organism.

## Insertions and deletions (D1.3.3)

Insertions and deletions are more likely than substitutions to stop a polypeptide working.

- **Frameshift.** Adding or removing a number of bases that is not a multiple of three changes every codon downstream. The amino acid sequence after the mutation is altered, and a stop codon usually appears early in the new frame.
- **Major insertions or deletions.** Even in multiples of three, a large change adds or removes many amino acids and usually disrupts folding.

### Worked example: cystic fibrosis

The most common cystic fibrosis allele has three bases deleted from the CFTR gene, which codes for a chloride ion channel in epithelial cell membranes.

1. Three bases is one codon's worth, so the reading frame is preserved.
2. One amino acid, a phenylalanine, is missing from a protein of 1480 amino acids.
3. Even so, the channel protein folds incorrectly and most of it is broken down before reaching the membrane.
4. Chloride ions are not transported, so mucus in the lungs and pancreatic ducts is thick and sticky.

The lesson: an in-frame deletion is not automatically harmless. Whether it matters depends on where it falls in the protein.

## Causes of mutation (D1.3.4)

Mutations arise from errors in replication or repair that are not corrected, and from **mutagens**: agents that increase the mutation rate.

| Mutagen | Type | How it damages DNA |
|---|---|---|
| Ultraviolet light | Radiation | Covalently links adjacent thymine bases on one strand (thymine dimers), so the strand is copied incorrectly |
| X-rays and gamma rays | Ionising radiation | Break the sugar-phosphate backbone, sometimes both strands |
| Benzo[a]pyrene (in tobacco smoke) | Chemical | Converted in the body to a compound that binds to guanine, causing errors when the strand is copied |
| Nitrous acid | Chemical | Alters bases chemically so that they pair with the wrong partner |

## Randomness in mutation (D1.3.5)

The guide makes two points. First, a mutation can occur at any base in the genome, although some bases mutate more often than others; for example, cytosine carrying a methyl group changes to thymine more often than other bases change. Second, no natural mechanism is known that makes a deliberate change to a particular base in order to change a trait.

**Evidence: replica plating.** Joshua and Esther Lederberg grew bacteria on a plate without antibiotic, then pressed a velvet pad onto it and printed the colonies onto several plates containing antibiotic. Resistant colonies grew in the same positions on every printed plate. The resistant cells must therefore have been present on the original plate before any contact with antibiotic. The antibiotic selected existing mutants; it did not cause the mutation that was needed.

## Germ cells, somatic cells and variation (D1.3.6 and D1.3.7)

- A mutation in a **germ cell** (a cell that gives rise to gametes) can be passed to offspring, who then carry it in every cell, including their own germ cells.
- A mutation in a **somatic cell** is passed only to cells descended from that cell by mitosis. It cannot be inherited, but mutations in genes that control the cell cycle can lead to uncontrolled division and a tumour. Cancer usually needs several such mutations in one cell lineage.

Mutation is the original source of all genetic variation, because it is the only process that makes new alleles. Meiosis and fertilisation only reshuffle alleles that already exist. Most mutations are harmful or neutral to the individual, but over many generations the rare beneficial ones give natural selection the variation it acts on.

## HL only: gene knockout (D1.3.8)

To find out what a gene does, researchers change it so that it no longer works, then compare the knockout organism with normal ones. Any difference in structure, physiology or behaviour suggests what the gene's product does. Libraries of knockout organisms, each lacking a different gene, are available for some model species such as mice, yeast and the plant *Arabidopsis*, so researchers can order a knockout line instead of making it.

Two cautions when you evaluate a knockout: other genes may compensate, so no visible difference does not prove the gene has no role; and a gene with several roles can produce a complex phenotype that is hard to interpret.

## HL only: CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (D1.3.9)

The guide does not require the natural role of CRISPR in prokaryotes. You do need to know how the technology edits a gene and one successful use.

1. A **guide RNA** is designed with a sequence of about 20 bases complementary to the target site in the gene.
2. The guide RNA binds to the enzyme **Cas9** and leads it to the matching DNA sequence by base pairing.
3. Cas9 cuts both strands of the DNA at that site.
4. The cell repairs the cut. Simple rejoining of the ends often inserts or deletes a few bases, causing a frameshift that knocks the gene out. If a DNA template with the desired sequence is supplied, the cell can copy it into the gap, making a precise change.

### Example of successful use

In 2023 the UK medicines regulator authorised a CRISPR-based treatment for sickle cell disease and transfusion-dependent beta-thalassaemia. Blood stem cells are taken from the patient and edited outside the body. The edit disrupts a DNA sequence that normally switches off fetal haemoglobin production after birth. The edited cells are returned to the patient, and the red blood cells they produce contain fetal haemoglobin, which carries oxygen and does not form fibres, so sickling is reduced.

**Nature of science.** Editing body cells of a consenting patient raises different questions from editing embryos, where changes would be inherited by future generations who cannot consent. In 2018 the announcement that children had been born from edited embryos was widely condemned by scientists. Because countries regulate gene editing differently, there are international efforts to agree common rules.

## HL only: conserved sequences (D1.3.10)

**Conserved sequences** are identical or similar across a species or a group of species; **highly conserved** sequences have stayed similar over long periods of evolution. Histone proteins are a classic example: histone H4 differs very little between plants and animals.

Two hypotheses account for conservation:

- **Functional requirement.** The gene product must keep a precise structure, so almost any change reduces fitness and natural selection removes it.
- **Slower mutation rate.** The sequence mutates less often in the first place.

One way to distinguish them is to compare silent and non-silent changes. If selection is the cause, silent substitutions (which do not change the amino acid) should still build up while amino-acid changes stay rare. If a low mutation rate is the cause, both kinds should be rare.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a substitution always changes the protein. Degeneracy means many do not.
- Assuming any deletion causes a frameshift. A deletion of three bases keeps the frame.
- Writing that an organism mutates "to adapt" or "because it needs to". Mutation is random; selection is not.
- Saying somatic mutations are inherited.
- Claiming meiosis creates new alleles. It recombines existing ones.
- (HL) Saying Cas9 finds its target by itself. The guide RNA locates the sequence by base pairing.
- (HL) Concluding a gene has no function because its knockout looks normal.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish** mutation types: state the change to bases and to the reading frame for each.
- **Explain** the effect of a named mutation: give the full chain from base to phenotype.
- **Discuss** gene editing: give a benefit, a risk or ethical concern, and a reasoned conclusion.
- **Suggest** hypotheses for conservation: give both, then a way to test between them.
