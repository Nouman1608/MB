These questions cover section 3.4.7 (Infection and response, parts a to j) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201) specification, Version 5.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards, including the required practical on disinfectants and antibiotics. The qualification is untiered. Learn the content first from the [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-infection-immunity-vaccination-and-antibiotics/) or the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-infection-immunity-vaccination-and-antibiotics-revision-notes/). ## Questions

**1.** **(a)** State what is meant by a pathogen. **[1]**
**(b)** Name two types of pathogen. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how bacteria and how viruses make a person ill. **[4]**

**3.** Describe how white blood cells defend the body against pathogens in three different ways. **[6]**

**4.** A culture starts with 1000 bacteria. Each bacterium divides once every 20 minutes. Calculate the number of bacteria after 3 hours. **[3]**

**5.** A child is given a vaccine containing an inactive form of a virus. Explain why the child does not become ill from the vaccine but is protected if the virus enters the body months later. **[4]**

**6.** A town has 600 people. A vaccination programme means that 85% of them are immune to a disease.
**(a)** Calculate how many people are not immune. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the disease spreads less easily than it would if nobody were immune. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a baby too young to be vaccinated is helped by the programme. **[1]**

**7.** Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of a child being vaccinated with the MMR vaccine against measles. **[6]**

**8.** A patient has flu, which is caused by a virus, and asks the doctor for antibiotics.
**(a)** Explain why the doctor refuses. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a different bacterial disease must be treated with an antibiotic chosen for it. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why it is difficult to develop a drug that kills viruses. **[1]**

**9.** A student tests three disinfectants on a lawn of bacteria on agar. Paper discs soaked in each disinfectant, and a disc soaked in sterile water, are placed on the lawn. After incubation the clear zones have these diameters: disinfectant A 14 mm, disinfectant B 22 mm, disinfectant C 6 mm, water 0 mm.
**(a)** Calculate the area of the clear zone for disinfectant B. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times larger the clear zone for B is than for A. Give your answer to 2 decimal places. **[2]**
**(c)** State the conclusion from the three zone sizes. **[1]**
**(d)** State the purpose of the water disc. **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why the lid of the dish should not be completely sealed. **[1]**

**10.** A hospital tests bacterial samples from patients for resistance to an antibiotic. In year 1, 38 of 200 samples were resistant. In year 3, 24 of 160 samples were resistant.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage of samples that were resistant in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in the proportion of resistant samples from year 1 to year 3. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** The hospital introduced rules limiting antibiotic use over this period. Suggest how these rules could explain the change. **[2]**

**11.** A patient has an infection of 3 million bacteria. Three of them carry a mutation that makes them resistant to antibiotic X. The patient takes antibiotic X, which kills all the non-resistant bacteria. The resistant bacteria then divide once every 30 minutes for 4 hours.
**(a)** Calculate the number of resistant bacteria after 4 hours. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why antibiotic X no longer cures this infection. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why new antibiotics are needed, and why antibiotics are not currently used for a mild throat infection. **[2]**

**12.** Plan an investigation to compare how well three antibiotics kill a harmless bacterium on an agar plate. Include how you keep the culture uncontaminated and how you obtain results. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A microorganism that causes infectious disease. [1]
**(b)** Bacteria [1] and viruses [1].
Total **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming two types is easier to credit when you write the plain words bacteria and viruses; a vague answer such as germs is risky because it is not a type.

**2.** Bacteria may produce poisons, called toxins [1], which make the person feel ill [1]. Viruses live and reproduce inside cells [1], which damages the cells [1].
Total **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each pathogen needs its own mechanism. Writing that both simply "cause disease" gives the examiner nothing to credit.

**3.** Phagocytosis: a white blood cell ingests (takes in) pathogens [1] so they are destroyed [1]. Antibodies: white blood cells produce antibodies that are specific to a particular pathogen [1] and destroy that bacterium or virus [1]. Antitoxins: produced by white blood cells [1] to counteract the toxins released by pathogens [1].
Total **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Six marks across three defences usually means two statements for each. Name the defence, then say what it acts on.

**4.** Divisions in 3 hours = 180 ÷ 20 = 9 [1]. Multiplier = 2⁹ = 512 [1]. Number = 1000 × 512 = **512 000** [1].
Total **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* If you only reach the multiplier, you have shown the method but not the final answer. Always multiply by the starting number.

**5.** The vaccine contains an inactive form of the virus, so it cannot cause the disease [1]. It stimulates white blood cells to make antibodies [1]. If the real virus enters later, the correct antibody is made rapidly [1], which destroys the virus before it makes the person ill [1].
Total **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is to say the vaccine "contains antibodies". Say that it stimulates the body to make them.

**6. (a)** Not immune = 15% of 600 [1] = **90** people [1].
**(b)** A large proportion of the population is immune [1], so an infected person is less likely to meet someone who can catch it and the spread is very much reduced [1].
**(c)** Most of the people around the baby are immune, so the pathogen is less likely to reach the baby [1].
Total **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b), "fewer people get it" restates the question. Explain why: infected people meet mostly immune people.

**7.** Advantages (any three): protects the child from measles [1]; prevents the effects of the disease [1]; increases the proportion of immune people so the spread is reduced, which also protects people who cannot be vaccinated [1]. Disadvantages (any two): the child may have a reaction such as soreness or a fever [1]; the vaccine does not work in every child [1]. A judgement that weighs both sides, for example that the benefits outweigh the small risk [1].
Total **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluate means a conclusion as well as two lists. Keep the side effects general, because details of specific vaccines are not needed.

**8. (a)** Antibiotics cannot kill viruses [1]. Viruses live inside body cells, so the antibiotic does not stop them [1].
**(b)** Specific bacteria should be treated by specific antibiotics, because an antibiotic that works on one may not kill another [1].
**(c)** Viruses live and reproduce inside body cells, so a drug that kills them is likely to damage the body's own tissues [1].
Total **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing that antibiotics "do not work on flu" earns less than saying why: they kill bacteria, and viruses live inside body cells.

**9. (a)** r = 22 ÷ 2 = 11 mm [1]. Area = π × 11² = **380.1 mm²** [1].
**(b)** Area of A = π × 7² = 153.9 mm² [1]. Ratio = 380.1 ÷ 153.9 = **2.47** times [1].
**(c)** Disinfectant B was the most effective against this bacterium, and C the least [1].
**(d)** It is a control: it shows the liquid alone does not kill the bacteria [1].
**(e)** Air must be able to enter, otherwise anaerobic bacteria, which may be harmful, could grow [1].
Total **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Give units with area (mm²). When you compare areas, using the unrounded area for B and A avoids rounding drift.

**10. (a)** Year 1: 38 ÷ 200 × 100 = **19%** [1]. Year 3: 24 ÷ 160 × 100 = **15%** [1].
**(b)** Fall = 19 − 15 = 4 [1]. Percentage fall = 4 ÷ 19 × 100 = **21.1%** [1].
**(c)** Less use of the antibiotic means resistant bacteria have less advantage over non-resistant ones, because fewer non-resistant bacteria are killed [1]. So resistant bacteria reproduce less and the proportion that are resistant falls [1].
Total **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A percentage fall is divided by the starting value, not the final one. Dividing 4 by 15 is a common slip.

**11. (a)** Divisions = 4 × 60 ÷ 30 = 8 [1]. Multiplier = 2⁸ = 256 [1]. Number = 3 × 256 = **768** [1].
**(b)** The resistant bacteria survived the antibiotic [1] and reproduced [1], so the population is now the resistant strain, which antibiotic X cannot kill [1].
**(c)** Resistant strains keep appearing, so new antibiotics have to be developed [1]. Not using antibiotics for non-serious infections such as a mild throat infection slows the rate at which resistant strains develop [1].
Total **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Extended explanations are easiest to follow in order: mutation, survival, reproduction, a population of resistant bacteria. Jumping to the conclusion loses the link steps.

**12.** Sterile equipment and aseptic technique so the culture is uncontaminated [1]; bacteria spread evenly on sterile agar to make a lawn [1]; sterile discs of the same size, soaked in equal volumes of each of the three antibiotics [1]; a control disc soaked in sterile water [1]; lid taped loosely, plate upside down, and incubated at a stated temperature such as about 25 °C for the same time [1]; diameter of each clear zone measured and the area calculated, the larger zone showing the more effective antibiotic [1]. Repeating and taking a mean would improve reliability.
Total **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A plan scores best when it controls variables by name (same bacterium, same disc size, same time and temperature) and says what is measured.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying antibiotics treat viral disease, or giving no reason why they do not.
- Writing that bacteria or people "build up resistance" instead of describing mutation, survival and reproduction.
- Mixing up antibodies (act on the pathogen) and antitoxins (counteract the toxin).
- Describing a vaccine as containing living pathogens rather than dead or inactive forms.
- Giving only one side of a vaccination evaluation, or no final judgement.
- Dividing by the wrong value in percentage change, or forgetting that 2 to the power of the number of divisions is the multiplier.
- Comparing clear zones by diameter when the question asks for area, or leaving out mm².
- Planning the practical without a control disc, a stated temperature or a repeat.
