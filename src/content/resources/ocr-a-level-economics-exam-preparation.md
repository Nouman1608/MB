OCR A Level Economics (H460) is linear and untiered, assessed by three compulsory components of equal
weight: Component 01 (Microeconomics), Component 02 (Macroeconomics) and Component 03 (Themes in
economics), each a 2-hour written paper worth 80 marks and one third of the qualification. Component
03 is the distinctive one: it is **synoptic, applying content from both Microeconomics and
Macroeconomics to an unseen theme**. These notes complement the site's guides to
[Microeconomics](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-microeconomics/) and
[Macroeconomics](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-macroeconomics/).

## Component 03 cannot be revised by content alone

A third of this qualification is assessed by applying theory you already have to a theme you have not
seen. There is no additional content list to learn for it. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise
*transfer*. Take an unfamiliar market, industry or policy question from the news and work it through
with the specification's tools -- market failure, elasticity, costs and revenues, aggregate demand and
supply, policy trade-offs. That exercise is closer to what Component 03 asks than any amount of
re-reading notes.

## The other two components stay live for the third

Because Component 03 draws on both micro and macro, neither can be closed off once its own paper is
sat. **Exam-preparation priority**: sequence revision so that Components 01 and 02 are refreshed
shortly before Component 03 rather than revised once and left. The connection between them -- how a
microeconomic distortion has macroeconomic consequences, and how macro policy lands unevenly across
markets -- is exactly what the third paper rewards.

## Three equal components mean no compensation strategy

Each component is one third. **Exam-preparation priority**: distribute revision evenly rather than
concentrating on the area you find more intuitive. Candidates commonly prefer micro or macro
decisively; the papers do not care.

## Two hours for 80 marks is time-rich by A-level standards

At 1.5 minutes per mark there is room for planning. **Exam-preparation priority**: use it. Five
minutes planning an extended answer -- deciding the position, the two strongest arguments, the diagram
and the decisive factor -- produces a structured essay, and structure is what separates a strong
economics answer from a knowledgeable one.

## Diagrams do analytical work at this level

A correctly constructed diagram can establish in seconds what a paragraph struggles to say.
**Exam-preparation priority**: practise the core diagrams to accuracy and speed, and practise
integrating them: state what the diagram shows in the prose, and use the labelled points to carry the
argument forward. A diagram that appears without commentary is decoration.

## Worked routine: applying theory to an unseen theme

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper, theme booklet or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - classify the theme before reading in detail:
Is this fundamentally about market failure? Market structure?
Labour markets? Macroeconomic policy? Trade? The classification
determines which toolkit you reach for.

Step 2 - identify the economic agents and their incentives:
Who acts, what are they maximising, and what constrains them?
Most unseen themes become tractable at this step.

Step 3 - select two tools, not six:
Depth beats breadth. Two well-applied models outperform a survey
of everything vaguely relevant.

Step 4 - apply each tool to the specific theme:
Use the theme's own detail -- the actual market, the actual policy,
the actual figures supplied -- rather than the generic case.

Step 5 - evaluate on a stated criterion, and say what it depends
on:
Efficiency? Equity? Short run or long run? Name the criterion,
then name the factor that would change the conclusion.
```

Step 3 is the discipline that most improves these answers. Under pressure the instinct is to
demonstrate range; the marks are in depth of application.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: practise transferring theory to unfamiliar themes drawn from current events;
  refresh Components 01 and 02 shortly before Component 03 rather than leaving them behind;
  distribute revision evenly across micro and macro; drill the core diagrams for accuracy and speed.
- **During any component**: spend up to five minutes planning each extended answer.
- **On Component 03**: classify the theme, identify the agents and incentives, choose two tools and
  apply them deeply rather than surveying many.
- **In every diagram**: state in the prose what it shows and use its labelled points in the argument.

## Self-test

1. What makes Component 03 different from the other two?
2. Why should Components 01 and 02 be refreshed before Component 03?
3. Why choose two analytical tools rather than several?
4. What does a diagram need in order to earn marks?

**Answers:** 1. It is synoptic: it applies content from both Microeconomics and Macroeconomics to an
unseen theme, so it cannot be prepared for by learning additional content -- only by practising
transfer. 2. Because it draws on both, and the connections between micro distortions and macro
consequences are precisely what it rewards. 3. Because marks come from depth of application to the
specific theme; a survey of loosely relevant models demonstrates range without applying anything
thoroughly. 4. Accurate labelling and integration -- the prose must state what it shows and use its
labelled points to carry the argument.
