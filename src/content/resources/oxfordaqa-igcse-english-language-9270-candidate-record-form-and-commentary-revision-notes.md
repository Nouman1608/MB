**These notes condense the Candidate record form and commentary in the non-exam assessment of
OxfordAQA International GCSE English Language (9270).** The specification says completion of the
form "is an important feature of the assignment and will be considered in the assessment". For full
explanation and model extracts, use the
[Candidate record form and commentary study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-candidate-record-form-and-commentary/);
for the NEA as a whole, see the
[NEA revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-non-exam-assessment-revision-notes/).

## The four requirements

| Requirement | Specification wording | Check |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Source details | "sufficient to enable a moderator to access them"; "at least five sources" | Could a stranger find each one? |
| 2. Choice and process | "how and why they chose their sources and the process(es) they used" | A reason for each source; a named process |
| 3. Note-making example | "one example of their note-making method" | Shows selection, not copying |
| 4. Commentary | Reasons for including or rejecting; organisation; presentational and stylistic features | All three focuses covered |

## Why it is assessed

- Every reading band includes "five sources cited on Candidate record form".
- Reading and writing Bands 4 to 6 refer to "explanation, example and commentary" on the processes;
  Band 3 to "explanation and commentary".
- Top bands: "sophisticated and independent command of the reading and research processes" and "of
  the writing process".

## Source details to record

| Source | Record |
|---|---|
| Book | Author, title, publisher, year, pages |
| Web page | Author or organisation, page title, full address, date accessed |
| Film or documentary | Title, maker or broadcaster, year, part used |
| Interview | Who, role, date, how recorded |
| Literary text | Author, title, edition, poem or chapter |

Mix kinds of source: research texts, literary texts, non-fiction texts, media texts.

## Explaining choice and process

- **Why**: link each source to the task's audience and purpose.
- **Rejected**: say what you left out and why (out of date, too technical, unreliable).
- **How**: name the process (note-making, annotation, colour-coded highlighting, comparison grid)
  and why it suited the material.
- **Led to**: show how the process shaped further research.

## The note-making example

Shows: a reference to the original, notes in your own words, and how you planned to use the point.
Never copied text.

## The commentary's three focuses

1. **Including and rejecting** information and ideas, with reasons.
2. **Organisation**: how and why the writing is ordered.
3. **Presentational and stylistic features**: specific features and how each serves purpose and
   audience.

## Describe or explain?

| Describes | Explains |
|---|---|
| "I used sub-headings." | "…so that busy readers could find the relevant section." |
| "I found five sources." | "…from different kinds of text to balance facts and young people's views." |
| "I wrote formally." | "…because the organisers are professionals and I wanted to be taken seriously." |

Test: does the sentence contain **because** or **so that**?

## Model commentary sentence (original)

"I opened the article with a short case study of a shop whose website brought in new customers,
because busy business readers are persuaded faster by an example than by a definition; I rejected
material on programming languages, since my readers need to know what a website does, not how it is
built."

One sentence covers inclusion, rejection and a reason linked to audience.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Candidate record form | The form recording sources, processes and the commentary |
| Moderator | The board's assessor who checks centre marking |
| Commentary | Explanation of choices made in the final assignment |
| Note-making | Recording selected information in your own words |
| Annotation | Notes written on or beside a source |
| Synthesis | Combining information from several sources |
| Presentational features | Layout devices such as headings and bullet points |
| Stylistic features | Language choices such as register and rhetorical devices |

## Timeline habit

- Start the form when research starts.
- Log sources and decisions with dates.
- Draft the commentary soon after the assignment is finished.
- Check against all four requirements before submission.

## Traps

- Fewer than five sources, or vague details.
- No reasons for choosing sources.
- A copied note-making example.
- A commentary that retells the assignment.
- Only one of the three commentary focuses.
- Leaving the form to the last day.

## Self-test

1. What does the specification say about the importance of the form?
2. How many sources must be listed, and how detailed must the details be?
3. Name two processes the specification gives as examples for gathering information.
4. How many note-making examples must be included?
5. What are the three focuses of the commentary?
6. Which phrase appears in every reading band?
7. What do the top bands say about the reading and writing processes?
8. Why should you explain rejected sources?
9. What should a note-making example show?
10. Rewrite "I used a rhetorical question" as an explaining sentence.
11. Name four kinds of source the specification mentions.
12. When should you start the form?

## Answers

1. It "is an important feature of the assignment and will be considered in the assessment".
2. At least five, with details sufficient to enable a moderator to access them.
3. Any two: note-making, annotation, highlighting.
4. One.
5. Reasons for including or rejecting information and ideas; how and why the writing is organised;
   presentational and stylistic features used to achieve the purpose.
6. "Five sources cited on Candidate record form".
7. They describe "sophisticated and independent command" of the reading and research processes and
   of the writing process.
8. It shows judgement about purpose, audience and reliability.
9. Selection and transformation: notes in your own words, linked to how you will use them.
10. For example: "I opened with a rhetorical question so that readers would immediately think about
    their own experience of the problem."
11. Any four: encyclopaedia, textbook, novel, poem, review, autobiography, film, documentary.
12. When research starts.
