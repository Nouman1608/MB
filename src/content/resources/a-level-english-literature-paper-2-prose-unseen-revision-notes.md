Condensed, exam-focused notes for Paper 2 of Cambridge International A Level English Literature (9695),
2024-2026 series.

## Paper structure

- **Section A: Prose** — a set text; tests narrative technique, character and theme knowledge.
- **Section B: Unseen** — an unfamiliar prose passage; tests close reading and analysis with no prior
  knowledge required or expected.
- Set text list rotates by examination year — always confirm the current set-text grid for your own
  series.

## Section A: preparing the set text

- Build genuine familiarity with the whole novel, not just memorable extracts — track how a theme or
  character develops **chronologically** across the text.
- Strong answers draw on the text as a whole; weak answers treat it as disconnected moments.
- Practise linking narrative technique to its effect on meaning, not just naming the technique.

## Section B: the unseen method

A repeatable three-step method for the unseen passage:

1. **First read** — for overall sense and tone.
2. **Second read** — annotate specific language and structural choices and their effects.
3. **Plan** — a response built around a small number of well-developed points, not a long list of
   surface observations.

- No benefit from memorised quotations or pre-prepared arguments on this section — only timed practice
  on genuinely new extracts builds this skill reliably.
- Never speculate about plot or context beyond what the passage itself provides.

## The technique-then-effect habit

The single most common weakness across both sections is naming a technique ("the writer uses short
sentences") without explaining its effect on the reader. Always pair an observation with a specific,
textually-grounded explanation — for example, short, clipped sentences can slow pace and mirror a
character's hesitant movement, building unease before anything is stated directly. A device named but
left unexplained earns far less credit than the same device linked to a concrete effect on meaning, tone
or characterisation.

## Time management

- Section A rewards depth of textual knowledge built up over the whole course.
- Section B rewards a fast, reliable close-reading method applied to fresh material.
- Rehearse both sections together under full exam timing, not revised separately, so pacing reflects the
  real paper rather than each section practised in isolation — candidates who only revise separately
  often run out of time on the section they find less comfortable.

## Worked comparison: naming vs explaining a technique

Consider two candidate responses to the same short extract, in which a narrator describes a crowded
train station using long, breathless, unpunctuated sentences. A weak response writes: "the writer uses a
long sentence with no punctuation, which shows the busy atmosphere." This names the device and gestures
at an effect but does not explain the mechanism connecting the two. A stronger response writes: "the
unbroken, breathless syntax mimics the narrator's own sensory overload amid the crowd, denying the
reader any pause and so recreating, at the level of the sentence itself, the disorientation the
character experiences." The stronger version specifies exactly how the technical choice produces the
stated effect, rather than simply asserting that an effect exists. Building the habit of asking "how,
specifically, does this produce that effect" after every observation — for both the studied Section A
text and the unseen Section B extract — is the most direct route from a mid-band to a high-band response
on this paper.

## Revising the set text without over-relying on quotations

For Section A specifically, a common trap is preparing a fixed bank of memorised quotations and forcing
them into an answer regardless of the actual question asked, which produces answers that feel prepared
rather than responsive to the specific question. A more resilient approach is knowing the text's overall
shape — its structure, key turning points, and how each major character or theme develops — well enough
to select and adapt relevant textual detail to whatever the question actually asks, rather than
reproducing a fixed set of pre-learned points regardless of fit. This flexibility matters because a
question can approach the same text from many different angles across different exam series, and
candidates who can only answer the specific angle they revised are disadvantaged relative to those who
understand the text's overall architecture.

## A note on assessment objectives

Like Paper 1, Paper 2 is marked against Cambridge's standard assessment objectives for literature —
knowledge and understanding of texts, analysis of language/form/structure, and (particularly relevant
for Section A) understanding of context and connections where genuinely relevant to the specific
question. Section B, being an unseen extract, weights analysis of language, form and structure
especially heavily, since there is no wider text or context to draw on beyond the passage itself —
which is exactly why a fast, reliable close-reading method matters more for that section than
background knowledge ever could.

4. Why is speculating beyond the passage risky in Section B?
5. Why should both sections be timed-practised together rather than separately?

**Answers:** 1. Section B (Unseen). 2. First read for sense/tone, second read to annotate
language/structure, plan before writing. 3. Naming a technique without explaining its effect on the
reader. 4. Section B tests close reading of what's actually on the page — going beyond it isn't
rewarded and can undermine an otherwise accurate reading. 5. So pacing reflects the real paper and
candidates don't run out of time on the section they find less comfortable at the expense of the other.
