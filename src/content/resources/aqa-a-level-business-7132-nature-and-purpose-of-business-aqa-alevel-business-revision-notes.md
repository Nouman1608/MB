Condensed notes on section **3.1.1 Understanding the nature and purpose of business** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The fuller explanations and the profit sensitivity example are in the [nature and purpose study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-nature-and-purpose-of-business-aqa-alevel-business/); exam-style questions are in the [nature and purpose practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-nature-and-purpose-of-business-aqa-alevel-business-practice/).

## Specification content

- Why businesses exist (objectives: profit, growth, survival, cash flow, social and ethical).
- The relationship between mission and objectives.
- Why businesses set objectives.
- The measurement and importance of profit: revenue (turnover, sales), fixed costs, variable costs, total costs.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mission | Broad, qualitative statement of a business's purpose and direction |
| Objective | Measurable target with a time limit that moves the business towards its mission |
| Profit | Revenue − total costs |
| Revenue (turnover, sales) | Price × quantity sold |
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not change with output in the short run (rent, salaries, insurance) |
| Variable costs | Costs that change directly with output (materials, packaging, piece-rate pay) |
| Total costs | Fixed costs + variable costs |
| Profit maximisation | Aiming for the highest possible profit |
| Satisfactory profit | Aiming for a level of profit that is good enough for the owners |
| Social objective | A target for benefit to society, such as local jobs or reduced waste |
| Ethical objective | A target based on what is morally right, such as fair sourcing |

## Why businesses exist: the two-part answer

1. For customers: meets a need or want they will pay for, adding value.
2. For owners: income, return on capital, independence, family security or a social aim.

## Mission → objectives

- Mission says **what** the business is trying to be; objectives say **how far** and **by when**.
- A useful mission rules options out, is believed by staff and stays stable.
- Criticisms: vague, written for publicity, ignored when rewards point elsewhere.
- Answers on the relationship: show the objective follows from the mission and a decision can be judged against both.

## Which objective first?

| Factor | Pushes towards |
|---|---|
| New business | Survival, cash flow |
| Established business | Profit, growth |
| Plc with investor pressure | Profit, dividends |
| Social enterprise | Social objective first |
| Fierce competition or downturn | Survival, cash flow |
| Easy access to finance | Growth |
| Customer and staff expectations | Social and ethical objectives |

Common conflicts: growth versus cash flow; ethics versus short-run profit; profit maximising versus long-term growth.

## Why set objectives

**Benefits:** direction and coordination; motivation; a measure of performance; a basis for decisions; accountability.
**Limits:** unrealistic targets demotivate; fixed targets cause inflexibility; narrow targets distort behaviour.

## Profit: method and analysis

```
Revenue        = price x quantity
Total costs    = fixed costs + variable costs
               = fixed costs + (variable cost per unit x quantity)
Profit         = revenue - total costs
```

Analysis points:

- Profit is a small residual, so a 10% change in price, costs or volume changes profit by much more than 10%.
- A price rise goes straight to profit only if sales volume holds.
- High fixed costs make profit very sensitive to falls in volume.
- Profit is not cash.

## Fixed or variable? Quick classification

| Cost | Usually | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rent, insurance, business rates | Fixed | Paid whatever the output |
| Raw materials, packaging | Variable | Rise with each unit made |
| Salaried managers | Fixed | Paid the same each month |
| Piece-rate or extra-hours staff | Variable | Pay follows output |
| Delivery fuel per order | Variable | More orders, more fuel |

## Exam traps

- "To make a profit" on its own is an incomplete reason a business exists.
- Mission and objectives are not interchangeable.
- Wages can be fixed (salaries) or variable (piece rates or hourly staff whose hours change with output); read the case.
- Revenue is not profit.
- Calculating without interpreting loses the analysis marks.
- Evaluations need a judgement that says what it depends on.

## Self-test

1. State the difference between a mission and an objective.
2. Give one criticism of mission statements.
3. Rewrite "we want to grow" as a measurable objective.
4. Why might a new business put survival ahead of profit?
5. Give one example of a conflict between two business objectives.
6. A firm sells 4,000 units at £15. Variable cost is £6 per unit and fixed costs are £28,000. Calculate profit.
7. In question 6, the price rises to £16.50 and sales stay at 4,000. Calculate the new profit and the percentage change.
8. In question 6, sales fall to 3,600 at the original price. Calculate the new profit.
9. Why does a 10% fall in sales cause a bigger percentage fall in profit?
10. Give two reasons why profit matters to stakeholders other than owners.
11. Why might managers prefer a satisfactory profit to profit maximisation?
12. Give one limitation of setting objectives.

### Answers

1. A mission is a broad statement of purpose; an objective is a measurable, time-limited target that moves the business towards it.
2. Any one: too vague to guide decisions; written for publicity; ignored if rewards point elsewhere.
3. For example: "increase revenue by 15% within two years".
4. It has few customers and little cash, so staying in business comes before maximising returns.
5. For example: growth needs cash, which conflicts with a cash flow objective.
6. Revenue £60,000; total costs 28,000 + 24,000 = £52,000; profit **£8,000**.
7. Revenue £66,000; profit 66,000 − 52,000 = **£14,000**, a rise of 6,000 ÷ 8,000 = **75%**.
8. Revenue £54,000; total costs 28,000 + 21,600 = £49,600; profit **£4,400**.
9. Fixed costs do not fall with sales, so the loss of revenue above variable cost comes straight out of a relatively small profit.
10. Any two: lenders see the ability to repay; employees gain job security or bonuses; government receives tax; retained profit funds investment.
11. Maximising profit may need more risk or decisions they dislike, and they may value growth or status as well.
12. Any one: unrealistic targets demotivate; inflexibility when conditions change; narrow targets encourage harmful behaviour.
