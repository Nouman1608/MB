These questions cover section 7.2, *Globalisation and identity*, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699) syllabus for 2024–2026, assessed in Paper 4 Section A. Paper 4 sets only extended essays; Section A builds the concepts those essays need, and Section B practises paragraphs and essays. All places, groups and companies in the items are fictional.

Revise first with the [globalisation and identity study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-and-identity/) and the [globalisation and identity revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-and-identity-revision-notes/).

## Section A – Concept questions

**1.** Define "hybrid identity". **[2]**

**2.** Define "cultural convergence". **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by "ethnic revitalisation", with an example. **[3]**

**4.** Distinguish between Castells's resistance identity and project identity. **[4]**

**5.** Explain what Thussu meant by "contra-flow" and why it challenges the idea of cultural homogenisation. **[4]**

**6.** Read the item.

> In the fictional country of Serravia, a global streaming service has become the most watched channel among under-25s. At the same time, enrolment in classes teaching the national folk dance has tripled, and a new pop genre mixing folk melodies with electronic beats tops the national charts.

Using the item, identify and explain three different identity responses to globalisation. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how Said's concept of Orientalism helps explain the role of Western ideology in shaping identity. **[4]**

**8.** Explain two criticisms of the cultural imperialism thesis. **[6]**

## Section B – Paragraph and essay questions

**9.** Explain and evaluate the view that globalisation strengthens cultural defence. **[8]**

**10.** Explain and evaluate the claim that hybrid identities are replacing national identities. **[8]**

**11.** "Globalisation is leading to the Westernisation of identity." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that globalisation produces cultural divergence rather than convergence. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** An identity that combines elements of more than one culture [1] into something new rather than choosing between them, as in diaspora identities (Hall) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The process by which different cultures become more alike [1], for example through the spread of global brands, media formats and consumer lifestyles [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The renewal of pride in, and active revival of, an ethnic group's language, customs or traditions [1], often provoked by awareness of what global culture may erode [1]. Example: revival of te reo Māori through Māori-language pre-schools and the Māori Language Act 1987 [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Resistance identity is built by groups who feel devalued or excluded [1] and defends them against dominant forces, as in religious fundamentalism or ethnic nationalism [1]. Project identity builds a new identity [1] that aims to transform society as a whole, as in feminism or environmentalism [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Contra-flows are media flowing from non-Western centres to global audiences [1], such as Indian film or East Asian television and music [1]. They show global culture is not simply exported from the West [1], so convergence does not mean Americanisation alone [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Convergence: the global streaming service's dominance among under-25s suggests shared global media consumption [1], making young people's cultural tastes more like those elsewhere [1].
Ethnic revitalisation or cultural defence: rising enrolment in folk dance classes shows renewed interest in national tradition [1], possibly in response to global culture [1].
Hybridity: the pop genre mixing folk melodies with electronic beats blends local and global forms [1] into something new, as Back found among young people in south London [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Said argued Western culture and scholarship constructed "the East" as backward, irrational and exotic [1], defining the West by contrast as rational and modern [1]. This ideology presents Western identity as the standard [1], so others may internalise, resist or rework the image, shaping identity on both sides [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Criticism 1: audiences are active [1]. Tomlinson (1991) argued people interpret imported media through their own culture [1], so Western content does not automatically produce Western identities [1].
Criticism 2: flows are not one-way [1]. Contra-flows from non-Western media centres reach global audiences (Thussu) [1], and glocalisation adapts global products locally, so cultural influence runs in several directions [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Explanation: cultural defence is protecting a culture against perceived outside threat [1]. Castells argues excluded groups build resistance identities around religion, ethnicity or territory [1]; Giddens sees fundamentalism as a defence of tradition [1]. The more global flows unsettle local life, the stronger defence may become [1].
Evaluation: many people adopt global culture without defending local identity, especially in consumption [1]. Hybridity shows mixing rather than defence (Back) [1]. Defence may arise from local politics rather than globalisation itself [1]. So globalisation can strengthen defence in some groups but not as a general rule [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Explanation: Hall suggested national identities may decline while new hybrid identities take their place [1]. Migration and global media create diaspora identities belonging fully to neither origin nor settlement society [1]. Back found young people in south London mixing music, language and identities [1], suggesting hybridity in cosmopolitan cities [1].
Evaluation: hybridity fits young, urban, mobile groups best [1]. Nationalism and cultural defence remain strong in many societies [1], and racism and inequality limit how freely people mix identities [1]. Hybridity may add to national identity rather than replace it [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For the view (AO1/AO2):** Westernisation as the spread of individualism, consumerism and liberal values; Ritzer's McDonaldization; Schiller's cultural imperialism; global beauty ideals and consumer identities; Said on Western ideology defining the standard.
- **Against the view:** contra-flows (Thussu); active audiences (Tomlinson); glocalisation; resistance identities and cultural defence (Castells, Bruce, Giddens); ethnic revitalisation; hybridity (Back, Hall, Bhabha); selective adoption of Western technology without Western values.
- **Evaluation (AO3):** Westernisation may describe consumption better than deeper values; it varies by age, class and urban or rural setting; non-Western economic powers export culture; use Hall's or Nederveen Pieterse's frameworks.
- **Judgement:** for example, Western ideology shapes consumer identities widely, but identity overall is more mixed and contested than "Westernisation" implies.

**Level guidance:** top answers use the concept of ideology precisely and weigh convergence against several divergent responses with contrasting evidence; middle answers describe Western influence and some counter-points; low answers list global brands.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For divergence:** ethnic revitalisation (te reo Māori, Welsh); cultural defence and resistance identities; glocalisation; contra-flows; Huntington's clash thesis (with criticism).
- **For convergence:** McDonaldization; global brands and media; English as a global language; cultural imperialism.
- **Third position:** hybridisation (Nederveen Pieterse, Back, Bhabha) — neither simple convergence nor divergence.
- **Evaluation:** separate consumption from identity; consider different groups and societies; Barber's view that homogenisation and backlash are linked.
- **Judgement:** for example, convergence in products often coexists with divergence and hybridity in identity.

**Level guidance:** top answers present three positions and reach a reasoned judgement; middle answers set convergence against divergence; low answers assert one side.
