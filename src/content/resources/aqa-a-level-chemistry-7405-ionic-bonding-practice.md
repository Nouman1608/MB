## Questions

**1.** State the charge on the simple ion formed by each element.

**(a)** Calcium **[1]**

**(b)** Sulfur **[1]**

**(c)** Aluminium **[1]**

**2.** Write the formula of each compound.

**(a)** Magnesium nitrate **[1]**

**(b)** Ammonium sulfate **[1]**

**(c)** Aluminium hydroxide **[1]**

**(d)** Iron(III) carbonate **[2]**

**3.** The formula of a compound is Cu₃(PO₄)₂, where PO₄ carries a charge of 3−. Deduce the charge on each copper ion and name the compound. **[2]**

**4.** Define ionic bonding. **[2]**

**5.** Describe the structure of solid sodium chloride. **[3]**

**6.** Explain, in terms of particles, why sodium chloride:

**(a)** does not conduct electricity when solid **[2]**

**(b)** conducts electricity when molten **[2]**

**7.** Explain why a crystal of an ionic compound shatters when struck sharply. **[3]**

**8.** Magnesium oxide melts at about 2850 °C. Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C. Explain this difference. **[3]**

**9.** Potassium bromide and calcium oxide both have giant ionic lattices. Predict which has the higher melting point and explain your answer. **[3]**

**10.** Sodium fluoride and potassium fluoride both contain ions with a single charge. Predict which has the higher melting point and explain your answer. **[3]**

**11.** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram to show the bonding in lithium oxide, showing outer electrons only. **[3]**

**12.** Explain, with reference to the number of ions in the formula, why the formula of aluminium oxide is Al₂O₃. **[2]**

**13.** Give the electron configuration of the sodium ion and name a negative ion that has the same configuration. **[2]**

**14.** The table shows the melting points of four ionic solids.

| Compound | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|
| NaF | 993 |
| NaCl | 801 |
| MgO | 2852 |
| CaO | 2613 |

**(a)** Suggest why the melting point of NaF is higher than that of NaCl. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why the melting point of MgO is higher than that of CaO. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why the melting point of MgO is much higher than that of NaF. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **2+** [1]

**(b)** **2−** [1]

**(c)** **3+** [1]. **[3]**

**2. (a)** **Mg(NO₃)₂** [1]

**(b)** **(NH₄)₂SO₄** [1]

**(c)** **Al(OH)₃** [1]

**(d)** Fe³⁺ and CO₃²⁻ in the lowest ratio that balances charge (2 Fe³⁺ with 3 CO₃²⁻) [1]: **Fe₂(CO₃)₃** [1]. **[5]**

**3.** Two phosphate ions carry 2 × (−3) = −6, so the three copper ions carry +6 in total, which is **+2 each** [1]; the compound is **copper(II) phosphate** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The electrostatic attraction [1] between oppositely charged ions [1]. Accept "in a lattice" as a supporting point. **[2]**

**5.** A giant ionic lattice [1] with a regular three-dimensional arrangement of alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions [1], each ion surrounded by six ions of opposite charge (or "held by electrostatic attraction in all directions") [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** In the solid the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice [1]; so there are no mobile charge carriers [1].

**(b)** When molten the lattice breaks down and the ions are free to move [1]; so they can carry charge through the liquid [1]. **[4]**

**7.** A blow moves a layer of ions by one ion position [1]; ions of the same charge are now adjacent [1] and repel each other, so the crystal splits [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have larger charges than Na⁺ and Cl⁻ [1]; and Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ are smaller ions, so the ions can get closer [1]; the electrostatic attraction is therefore stronger, so more energy is needed to overcome it [1]. **[3]**

**9.** **Calcium oxide** has the higher melting point [1]. Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ have charges of 2+ and 2−, whereas K⁺ and Br⁻ have 1+ and 1− [1]; so the electrostatic attraction between ions is stronger in calcium oxide and more energy is needed to separate them [1]. **[3]**

**10.** **Sodium fluoride** has the higher melting point [1]. Na⁺ is smaller than K⁺ (it has fewer electron shells) [1]; so the ions in sodium fluoride can approach more closely, giving a stronger electrostatic attraction [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Two Li⁺ ions drawn in square brackets with charge 1+, each showing only the full inner shell of two electrons, because the outer shell is empty [1]; one O²⁻ ion in square brackets with eight outer electrons [1]; the charge shown outside each bracket and the electrons from lithium shown as a different symbol from oxygen's own [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Al³⁺ has charge 3+ and O²⁻ has charge 2− [1]; two Al³⁺ (+6) balance three O²⁻ (−6), so the compound is neutral [1]. **[2]**

**13.** **1s² 2s² 2p⁶** [1]; an oxide ion, O²⁻ (accept fluoride, F⁻, or nitride, N³⁻) [1]. **[2]**

**14. (a)** F⁻ is smaller than Cl⁻ [1]; so the ions in NaF can approach more closely and the electrostatic attraction is stronger [1]

**(b)** Mg²⁺ is smaller than Ca²⁺ [1]; so the attraction between Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ is stronger [1]

**(c)** Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have charges of 2+ and 2−, whereas Na⁺ and F⁻ are 1+ and 1− [1]; the larger charges give a much stronger attraction, which more than outweighs any difference in size [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* In a data question, state the trend in the numbers and then give the reason in terms of ionic charge or radius. Do not just restate the data.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Charges not balanced in a formula, or brackets missing for a repeated compound ion.
- Referring to "molecules" or "atoms" when describing an ionic lattice.
- Writing that electrons carry charge in molten sodium chloride, instead of ions.
- Comparing melting points using only one of charge and radius when both matter.
- Leaving out the square brackets and charges in a dot-and-cross diagram.
