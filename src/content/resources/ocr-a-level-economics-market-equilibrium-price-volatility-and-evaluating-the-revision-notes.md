## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Equilibrium | Buyers and sellers want to trade the same quantity at the going price, so nothing pushes the price to change |
| Disequilibrium | Quantity demanded differs from quantity supplied at the current price |
| Surplus (excess supply) | Quantity supplied above quantity demanded, so the price tends to fall |
| Shortage (excess demand) | Quantity demanded above quantity supplied, so the price tends to rise |
| Derived demand | Demand for a good or factor because of demand for what it helps to make |
| Joint supply | One production process yields two or more products |
| Competitive supply | Producers can switch inputs between products, so the goods compete for resources |
| Composite demand | One good is wanted for several uses |
| Volatility | Large and frequent changes in price or output |
| Comparative statics | Comparing two equilibria before and after a change |

## Equilibrium in three markets

| Market | Price is | Buyers | Sellers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product | Price of the good | Consumers | Firms |
| Financial | Interest rate, exchange rate or bond price | Borrowers, investors | Savers, lenders |
| Factor | Wage, rent or return on capital | Firms (derived demand) | Owners of the factor |

**Method in steps (algebra).**

1. Set Qd = Qs.
2. Solve for P.
3. Substitute P into both equations to find Q.
4. Check that both equations give the same Q.

**Worked reminder.** Qd = 540 − 15P and Qs = −60 + 25P.

```
540 − 15P = −60 + 25P  →  600 = 40P  →  P = 15
Q = 540 − 15(15) = 315   (check: −60 + 375 = 315)
At P = 12: Qd = 360, Qs = 240, shortage of 120
At P = 18: Qd = 270, Qs = 390, surplus of 120
```

**Factor market reminder.** Ld = 600 − 20w and Ls = −100 + 30w. Then 700 = 50w, so w = £14 and L = 320. If product demand falls and labour demand becomes Ld = 550 − 20w, then 650 = 50w, so w = £13 and L = 290. The wage falls 7.14% and employment 9.38%. Labour demand is derived, so the shift in the product market is the cause.

**Financial market reminder.** The price of borrowing is the interest rate. More saving shifts loanable funds supply right, which lowers the rate and raises the amount lent. A fall in confidence among borrowers does the opposite to demand.

**Bond prices and yields.** Yield = fixed payment ÷ price. A bond paying £30 a year has a yield of 30/600 = 5.0% at a price of £600 and 30/400 = 7.5% at £400.

## Shifts and the new equilibrium

**Method in steps (diagram).**

1. Draw the original curves and label the equilibrium P0 and Q0.
2. Name the factor and the curve it shifts.
3. Draw the new curve and label P1 and Q1.
4. State the direction of price and quantity, and compare with the original.

| Shift | Effect on price | Effect on quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases | Rises | Falls |

**Worked reminders.** Using the same market as above:

- demand rises by 40 at each price (Qd = 580 − 15P): 640 = 40P, so P = 16 and Q = 340;
- supply rises by 80 at each price (Qs = 20 + 25P): 520 = 40P, so P = 13 and Q = 345.

The supply increase cuts price by 13.33% and raises quantity by 9.52%. The demand increase lifts price by 6.67% and quantity by 7.94%.

**Both curves shift.** Only one of the two results is guaranteed. The other turns on the relative sizes of the shifts, and your answer should say so.

## Related markets

- **Substitutes:** a price rise in one shifts demand for the other right.
- **Complements:** a price rise in one shifts demand for the other left.
- **Joint supply:** more of one product also means more of the other. If demand for beef rises, hide supply rises and the hide price falls.
- **Competitive supply:** a higher price for one product pulls resources away from the other, so its supply shifts left.
- **Derived demand:** a fall in house building cuts demand for bricks and for builders.

Evaluate by asking how close the goods are, how big and lasting the original change is, how quickly the second market can respond, and whether other factors are moving too.

## Disequilibrium

| Causes | Consequences of a surplus | Consequences of a shortage |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden shifts of demand or supply | Unsold stock | Queues and waiting lists |
| Sticky prices (contracts, posted prices) | Falling prices | Resale at high prices |
| Price set above or below equilibrium | Output cuts, waste of perishables | Rationing by luck or connections |

Price signals restore balance in a free market, but only after a time lag.

## Volatility

Price and output are most volatile when:

- demand and supply are both inelastic;
- supply is fixed in the short run, for example by a harvest;
- there are frequent supply or demand shocks;
- supply responds with a lag;
- speculation amplifies moves.

**Worked reminder.** Qd = 42 − P with a fixed supply.

| Supply | Price | Change in output | Change in price |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 12 | - | - |
| 24 | 18 | −20% | +50% |
| 36 | 6 | +20% | −50% |

A 20% change in output produces a 50% change in price. Steeper curves magnify shocks.

**Cobweb:** producers decide this period's output on last period's price. Prices then swing above and below equilibrium. The swings shrink if supply responds less strongly than demand, and grow if it responds more strongly.

## Evaluating the model

| Market | Model works well because | Model works less well because |
|---|---|---|
| Commodities | Standard goods, many traders | Speculation, stocks, producer groups |
| Housing | Explains price pressure from demand | Differences between homes, credit, planning, expectations |
| Transport | Explains peak pricing | Subsidy, regulated fares, congestion |

**Assumptions:** rational behaviour, homogeneous goods, many buyers and sellers, full information, flexible prices, ceteris paribus, no externalities.

**Techniques:** diagrams, schedules, algebra, percentage changes, comparative statics.

**Judgement.** Name the market. Say which assumption fails most. State whether the model still gives a useful direction of change.

**Transport reminder.** A train has 200 seats and demand is Qd = 400 − 20P. The fare that fills it exactly is £10. At a fixed £6, demand is 280, so 80 passengers are turned away or stand. Fixed prices and fixed capacity together produce disequilibrium.

**Evaluation chain (one example).** Claim: the model predicts that a bad harvest raises the price. Reason: supply shifts left along a steep demand curve. Evidence: a fixed supply cut of 20% gave a 50% price rise. Counter-point: a stockpile or imports could soften the rise, and speculation could magnify it. Judgement: the direction is reliable, but the size depends on stocks, trade and expectations.

## Must-know distinctions

- Sliding along a curve (the good's own price) versus a shifted curve (any other cause).
- Surplus versus shortage.
- Joint supply versus competitive supply.
- Derived versus composite demand.
- Predicting direction versus predicting size.

## Quick self-test

1. In your own words, define equilibrium.
2. Qd = 540 − 15P and Qs = −60 + 25P. What are the equilibrium price and quantity?
3. At £12, what is the shortage or surplus?
4. If demand rises by 40 at each price, what is the new equilibrium?
5. A bond pays £30 a year. What is its yield at a price of £400?
6. Demand for beef rises. What happens to the price of hides, and why?
7. Qd = 42 − P and supply falls from 30 to 24. By what percentage does the price change?
8. Name two assumptions of the supply and demand model.
9. Why might the cobweb pattern grow instead of settling?
10. Give one reason why the model fits housing less well than wheat.

### Answers

1. Buyers and sellers want to trade the same quantity at the going price.
2. £15 and 315.
3. A shortage of 120 (Qd 360, Qs 240).
4. £16 and 340.
5. 7.5%.
6. It falls. More cattle are reared, so hide supply shifts right.
7. The price rises from £12 to £18, up 50%.
8. Any two from rational behaviour, homogeneous goods, many buyers and sellers, full information, flexible prices, ceteris paribus.
9. Supply responds more strongly to last period's price than demand responds to this period's price.
10. Each home is different, so no single price or market exists. Credit, planning limits and expectations also matter.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting a curve when the question describes only a change in the good's own price.
- Writing "price goes up" without naming the curve that moved and the reason.
- Giving both price and quantity as certain when demand and supply both shift.
- Naming a related market without saying whether the link is substitute, complement or joint supply.
- Writing "the model is unrealistic" without naming which assumption fails and what difference it makes.
- Describing a shortage without saying what happens next, such as queues or higher prices.
- Using a formula where the working does not show the substitution step.
- Stopping an evaluation at one side of the argument, without a judgement.
