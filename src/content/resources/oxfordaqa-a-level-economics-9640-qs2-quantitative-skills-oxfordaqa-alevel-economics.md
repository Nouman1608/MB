This guide goes deeper into quantitative skill QS2, "calculate, use and understand percentages and percentage changes", from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification. The specification says these skills could be assessed in any of the assessments.

The broader [ratios, percentages, mean, median and quantiles guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-ratios-percentages-mean-median-and-quantiles/) teaches percentage change, percentage points, reverse percentages and successive changes. This page applies those tools to the harder cases economics data produce: rates whose top and bottom both change, growth per head, growth averaged over several years, combined price and quantity changes, and the contribution of one part to a total change. All figures are invented; "cu" means currency units. Condense the page with the [QS2 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs2-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [QS2 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs2-quantitative-skills-oxfordaqa-alevel-economics-practice/).

## Three formulae, one idea

```
Percentage of a total     = part / total × 100
Percentage change         = (new − old) / old × 100
Multiplier for a change   = 1 + (percentage change / 100)
```

Almost every hard percentage question becomes easy once you turn each change into a multiplier: +4% is × 1.04, −15% is × 0.85.

## 1. Rises and falls are not symmetrical

A percentage change is measured against the starting value, so the base changes depending on direction.

```
Price rises from 80 to 100:  (100 − 80)/80 × 100 = +25%
Price falls from 100 to 80:  (80 − 100)/100 × 100 = −20%
```

So after a 20% fall, a 25% rise is needed to get back to the start. After a 40% fall, a rise of 1/0.6 − 1 = 66.7% is needed. This matters when an economy recovers from a recession or a currency recovers from a depreciation: "output fell 10% and then grew 10%" does not mean output is back where it started (0.9 × 1.1 = 0.99, still 1% lower).

## 2. Rates whose top and bottom both change

Many indicators are percentages of something else: the unemployment rate is a percentage of the labour force, and a budget deficit is often reported as a percentage of GDP. When both parts change, work out each rate separately.

### Worked example 1: unemployment

In Country A, 1.8 million people are unemployed out of a labour force of 30 million. A year later, 1.9 million are unemployed out of a labour force of 32 million.

```
Year 1 rate = 1.8 / 30 × 100 = 6.00%
Year 2 rate = 1.9 / 32 × 100 = 5.94%
Change in the rate = 5.94 − 6.00 = −0.06 percentage points
```

The number unemployed rose by about 5.6%, yet the unemployment rate fell, because the labour force grew faster (by about 6.7%). A question that asks whether "unemployment has risen" may have two correct answers; say which measure you mean.

### Worked example 2: the budget deficit

Country B's budget deficit is 36 cu billion when GDP is 900 cu billion. The next year the deficit is 40 cu billion and GDP is 1,000 cu billion.

```
Year 1: 36/900 × 100 = 4.0% of GDP
Year 2: 40/1000 × 100 = 4.0% of GDP
```

The deficit grew by 11.1% in money terms but stayed at 4.0% of GDP. Measured against the size of the economy, nothing changed.

## 3. Growth per head

GDP per capita is GDP divided by population. If GDP and population both grow, use multipliers:

```
Growth in GDP per capita = (1 + GDP growth) / (1 + population growth) − 1
```

### Worked example 3

Real GDP grows by 3% and population by 1%.

```
1.03 / 1.01 = 1.0198
Real GDP per capita grows by about 1.98%
```

Subtracting (3% − 1% = 2%) gives a close approximation when the rates are small. With large rates it is not close: 12% GDP growth with 5% population growth gives 1.12/1.05 − 1 = 6.67%, not 7%.

## 4. Average growth over several years

If a value grows by 20% in total over 5 years, the average annual growth rate is not 20/5 = 4%, because the growth compounds.

```
Average annual multiplier = (total multiplier)^(1/number of years)
(1.20)^(1/5) = 1.0371
Average annual growth = 3.71%
```

Check: 1.0371⁵ = 1.20. The simple division always overstates the average annual rate for growth, because each year's growth applies to a larger base.

## 5. Combined changes in price and quantity

Total revenue (or total expenditure) is price × quantity. If both change, multiply their multipliers.

### Worked example 4

A firm raises its price by 10% and sales fall by 15%.

```
Revenue multiplier = 1.10 × 0.85 = 0.935
Revenue falls by 6.5%
```

Adding the percentages (+10 − 15 = −5%) gives the wrong answer. This calculation is the link between QS2 and price elasticity of demand (QS8): here the quantity change is larger in size than the price change, demand is price elastic over this range, and revenue falls.

## 6. Contribution of a part to a total change

Data-response questions often ask which component "drove" a change. Express each part's change as a percentage of the original total, in percentage points.

### Worked example 5

Country C's GDP rises from 500 to 520 cu billion, which is growth of 4%. Over the same year:

| Component | Year 1 | Year 2 | Change | Contribution to growth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumption | 300 | 315 | +15 | 15/500 = 3.0 percentage points |
| Investment | 80 | 78 | −2 | −0.4 percentage points |
| Government spending | 100 | 105 | +5 | +1.0 percentage points |
| Net exports | 20 | 22 | +2 | +0.4 percentage points |
| Total | 500 | 520 | +20 | 4.0 percentage points |

The contributions add up to the total growth rate: 3.0 − 0.4 + 1.0 + 0.4 = 4.0. Consumption grew by 5% on its own base, but it contributed 3 of the 4 percentage points of growth because it is the largest component. Investment fell by 2.5% on its own base but subtracted only 0.4 points.

## Common mistakes

- Using the new value as the base of a percentage change.
- Assuming a fall and an equal percentage rise cancel out.
- Calling a change in a rate a "percentage change" when it is a change in percentage points.
- Adding percentage changes in price and quantity to get the change in revenue.
- Dividing total growth by the number of years to get an average annual rate.
- Saying a component grew "fastest" when the question asks which contributed most.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the formula with numbers substituted, then the answer to a sensible accuracy (usually 1 or 2 decimal places), with % or "percentage points".
- **Using the data**: quote the figures you used, so the examiner can follow your working even if the final answer slips.
- **Explain** a result: say what the percentage means in context, for example "the deficit is unchanged relative to the size of the economy".
- **Assess** or **evaluate** a claim: test it with more than one measure, such as a level and a rate, and state which is more relevant.
