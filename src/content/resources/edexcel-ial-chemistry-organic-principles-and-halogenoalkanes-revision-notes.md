## Key definitions (10.1 to 10.4)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Addition | Two reactants combine into one product |
| Elimination | A small molecule leaves and a C=C forms |
| Substitution | An atom or group is replaced by another |
| Oxidation | Organic compound gains O or loses H (includes combustion) |
| Reduction | Organic compound gains H or loses O |
| Hydrolysis | Splitting of a bond by water or hydroxide ions |
| Polymerisation | Many monomers join into one long molecule |
| Reaction mechanism | The step-by-step route from reactants to products, showing electron movement |
| Homolytic fission | Bond breaks with one electron to each atom; radicals form |
| Heterolytic fission | Bond breaks with both electrons to one atom; ions form |
| Electrophile | Accepts an electron pair to form a covalent bond |
| **Nucleophile** | **Donates a lone pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond** |

A reaction can carry two labels. Halogenoalkane + water is substitution and hydrolysis. Alkene + hydrogen is addition and reduction.

## Polarity decides the mechanism (10.5)

- Non-polar C–C and C–H in alkanes: homolytic fission, radicals, **free-radical substitution**.
- Electron-rich C=C in alkenes: attracts electrophiles, **electrophilic addition**.
- Polar Cδ+–Xδ− in halogenoalkanes: δ+ carbon attracts nucleophiles, **nucleophilic substitution**.

## Naming and classifying (10.6, 10.7)

- Prefix with fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-; lowest locants; alphabetical order of substituents.
- Skeletal formulae: lines for C–C bonds, halogen written at the end of its line.
- Classify by the number of carbons bonded to the C–X carbon.

| Type | Carbons on the C–X carbon | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 1 (or 0 for CH₃X) | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl, 1-chlorobutane |
| Secondary | 2 | CH₃CHICH₂CH₃, 2-iodobutane |
| Tertiary | 3 | (CH₃)₂CBrCH₂CH₃, 2-bromo-2-methylbutane |

## Reagents table (10.8)

| Reagent | Conditions | Reagent acts as | Product | Example from bromoethane |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KOH(aq) | Heat under reflux | Nucleophile (OH⁻) | Alcohol | Ethanol |
| KOH in ethanol | Heat under reflux | Base (OH⁻) | Alkene | Ethene |
| AgNO₃(aq) in ethanol | Warm | Nucleophile (H₂O) | Alcohol + AgX precipitate | Ethanol + cream AgBr |
| NH₃ in ethanol | Heat, sealed tube, pressure | Nucleophile (NH₃) | Amine | Ethylamine |
| KCN in ethanol | Heat under reflux | Nucleophile (CN⁻) | Nitrile, one more carbon | Propanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CN |

Key equations (R = alkyl group):

- R–X + OH⁻ → R–OH + X⁻
- R–X + OH⁻ → alkene + H₂O + X⁻ (ethanolic)
- R–X + H₂O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻; Ag⁺ + X⁻ → AgX
- R–X + 2NH₃ → R–NH₂ + NH₄X
- R–X + CN⁻ → R–CN + X⁻

## Mechanism checklist (10.9)

### Method in steps: OH⁻ with a primary halogenoalkane

1. Draw the C–X bond with δ+ on C and δ− on X.
2. Draw OH⁻ with its charge and a lone pair on oxygen.
3. First curly arrow: oxygen lone pair towards the δ+ carbon.
4. Second curly arrow: C–X bonding pair moving onto X.
5. Products: the alcohol and X⁻.

### Method in steps: NH₃ with a primary halogenoalkane

1. Same first arrows, but from the lone pair on N of NH₃.
2. Intermediate: R–NH₃⁺ and X⁻.
3. A second NH₃ removes H⁺: arrow from its lone pair to H, arrow from the N–H bond to N.
4. Products: R–NH₂ and NH₄⁺.

SN1 and SN2 labels and mechanisms belong to Unit 4.

## Rate trends (10.10, 10.12, 10.13)

| Compare | Fastest → slowest | Reason to give |
|---|---|---|
| Halogen | R–I > R–Br > R–Cl | C–I is the weakest bond (smallest bond enthalpy), so less energy is needed to break it |
| Structure | Tertiary > secondary > primary | Learn the trend; the explanation is in Unit 4 |

C–Cl is the most polar of the three bonds, yet chloroalkanes react most slowly. So bond enthalpy, not polarity, controls this trend.

Relative rate ∝ 1/time. **Small worked reminder:** if one compound gives a precipitate in 50 s and another in 400 s, 1/t is 0.020 s⁻¹ and 0.0025 s⁻¹, so the first is 400 ÷ 50 = 8 times faster.

## Core Practical 5 summary (10.11)

- Mix each halogenoalkane with ethanol and aqueous silver nitrate at the same temperature (water bath).
- Ethanol is there so the halogenoalkane and the aqueous solution mix.
- Time how long until a precipitate appears. Observe colour: white (Cl), cream (Br), yellow (I).
- Control: temperature, volumes, AgNO₃ concentration, amount of halogenoalkane.
- Use structural isomers to compare 1°, 2°, 3°; use primary chloro-, bromo-, iodoalkanes to compare halogens.

## Core Practical 6 summary (10.14)

(CH₃)₃COH + HCl → (CH₃)₃CCl + H₂O

| Step | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Shake alcohol with concentrated HCl in a separating funnel | Carries out the substitution at room temperature |
| Open the tap often | Releases pressure |
| Discard the lower aqueous layer | The product is less dense than water and sits on top |
| Wash with NaHCO₃(aq) | Neutralises remaining acid (CO₂ is given off) |
| Wash with water | Removes soluble salts |
| Add anhydrous Na₂SO₄ | Removes water; liquid turns from cloudy to clear |
| Distil, collect near 51 °C | Separates pure product from unreacted alcohol |

## Further suggested practicals

- Halogen test: warm with NaOH(aq), acidify with dilute HNO₃, add AgNO₃(aq). Then ammonia: AgCl dissolves in dilute, AgBr in concentrated, AgI in neither.
- 1-bromobutane: heat butan-1-ol with KBr and sulfuric acid, which together form HBr.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Hydroxide in water vs hydroxide in ethanol:** in water it substitutes (alcohol); in ethanol it eliminates (alkene).
- **Water vs hydroxide as nucleophile:** silver nitrate in ethanol relies on H₂O, a weaker nucleophile, so reactions are slow enough to time.
- **Homolytic vs heterolytic:** radicals vs ions.
- **Electrophile vs nucleophile:** accepts vs donates an electron pair.
- **Polarity vs bond enthalpy:** polarity explains why attack happens at carbon; bond enthalpy explains the I > Br > Cl rate order.
- **Ammonia vs cyanide:** both are nucleophiles; only cyanide adds a carbon atom.
- **Primary vs tertiary classification:** count carbons bonded to the C–X carbon, not the total number of carbons in the molecule.
- **Halogen test vs rate test:** the halogen test hydrolyses with NaOH(aq) first and must be acidified; the rate test uses water in ethanol, so no acidifying is needed.

1. Define the term nucleophile.
2. Classify: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br.
3. Classify: CH₃CHClCH₃ + KOH (ethanol) → CH₃CH=CH₂ + KCl + H₂O.

5. Name CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₂CH₃.
6. Give the reagent and conditions to turn 1-bromopropane into butanenitrile.
7. 2.46 g of 1-bromopropane is converted fully into butanenitrile. What mass of nitrile forms? (H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, Br = 79.9)
8. What colour precipitate forms when 1-iodobutane is warmed with silver nitrate in ethanol?
9. Which reacts fastest with aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol: 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane or 1-iodobutane? Give the reason.
10. Why are two moles of ammonia needed per mole of halogenoalkane?
11. In Core Practical 6, why is sodium hydrogencarbonate used rather than sodium hydroxide to wash the product?
12. Which bond type in a molecule leads to free-radical substitution?

### Answers

1. A species that donates a lone pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond.
2. Addition.
3. Elimination (HCl is removed).
4. Tertiary: the C–I carbon is bonded to three carbons.
5. 3-bromopentane.
6. KCN dissolved in ethanol, heated under reflux.
7. M(C₃H₇Br) = 122.9, so n = 2.46 ÷ 122.9 = 0.0200 mol. M(C₄H₇N) = 69.0, so mass = 0.0200 × 69.0 = **1.38 g**.
8. Yellow (AgI).
9. 1-iodobutane. C–I is the weakest of the three carbon–halogen bonds, so it needs the least energy to break.
10. One NH₃ is the nucleophile; the second removes H⁺ from the RNH₃⁺ ion, forming NH₄⁺.
11. Hydroxide ions would hydrolyse some of the 2-chloro-2-methylpropane back to the alcohol. Hydrogencarbonate is a weak base that removes acid without doing this.
12. Non-polar C–H or C–C bonds, as in alkanes.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "KOH" or "alkali" with no solvent, so the examiner cannot tell substitution from elimination.
- Calling ethanolic hydroxide a nucleophile instead of a base.
- Curly arrows that start on an atom or a minus sign rather than a lone pair or a bond.
- Missing the δ+ and δ− on the C–X bond in a mechanism.
- Stopping the ammonia mechanism at RNH₃⁺ without the second ammonia.
- Explaining the I > Br > Cl order by polarity or electronegativity.
- Getting nitrile names wrong by not counting the CN carbon.
- Describing a "white" precipitate for a bromoalkane. AgBr is cream.
- Forgetting to acidify with dilute nitric acid before adding silver nitrate in the halogen test.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a nucleophile in Edexcel IAL Chemistry?**
A nucleophile is a species that donates a lone pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond, as defined in Topic 10A of the Edexcel IAL Chemistry specification (YCH11). Examples include OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃ and H₂O.

**What is the difference between aqueous and ethanolic potassium hydroxide with halogenoalkanes?**
In water, hydroxide ions act as a nucleophile and substitute the halogen to give an alcohol. Dissolved in ethanol, hydroxide ions act as a base and remove H⁺, giving an alkene by elimination.

**How do you classify a halogenoalkane as primary, secondary or tertiary?**
Count the carbon atoms bonded to the carbon carrying the halogen: one (or none) is primary, two is secondary and three is tertiary. Do not count the total number of carbons in the molecule.

**Are SN1 and SN2 mechanisms needed for Edexcel IAL Unit 2?**
No. In Unit 2 you learn the substitution mechanisms of primary halogenoalkanes with hydroxide and ammonia and the tertiary > secondary > primary rate trend. SN1 and SN2 labels and their explanation belong to Unit 4.

**Why is sodium hydrogencarbonate used to wash the product in Core Practical 6?**
Sodium hydrogencarbonate is a weak base that removes leftover acid. Sodium hydroxide would hydrolyse some of the 2-chloro-2-methylpropane back to the alcohol.
