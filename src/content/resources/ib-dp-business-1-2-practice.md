These questions cover section 1.2 (Types of business entities) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL. All organizations and figures are invented, and money is in dollars. Legal details vary between countries, so answers describe features in general terms. Revise first with the [1.2 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-1-2/) and the [1.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-business-1-2-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *limited liability*. **[2]**

**2.** Outline two features of a publicly held company. **[4]**

**3.** Brisk Bakes closes owing 44,000. Its equipment and stock are sold for 19,000.

**(a)** Calculate the shortfall still owed to creditors. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how much of this shortfall the owner must pay if Brisk Bakes is a sole trader, and if it is a privately held company in which she invested 12,000. **[3]**

**4.** Distinguish between a cooperative and an NGO. **[3]**

**5.** State two reasons why a government may own and run an organization. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one tension that a private sector company with a social purpose may face. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Tallis Robotics is a publicly held company with 10,000,000 shares. A rival, Kestrom Industries, already owns 1,800,000 of them.

**(a)** Calculate the minimum number of extra shares Kestrom must buy to own more than half of Tallis Robotics. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain two ways in which being publicly held affects Tallis Robotics' managers. **[4]**

**8.** Ashgrove Joinery is owned and run by its 24 workers as a worker cooperative. Each worker-member has one vote. Analyse the advantages and disadvantages for the workers of owning the business in this way. **[6]**

**9.** Corvane Rail is a public sector company that runs trains across a country, including rural lines with few passengers. Explain two ways in which its objectives may differ from those of a privately owned rail company. **[4]**

**10.** Westfold Water Trust is an NGO that installs water filters in villages. Last year its income was: donations 120,000; a government grant 200,000 (which ends next year); sales of filters to other charities 80,000.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of income that came from the grant. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain one risk this creates for the trust. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one way the trust could reduce this risk. **[2]**

**11.** Joao wants to start Sunfold Solar, which would fit solar panels to the homes of low-income families at below-market prices. He needs 150,000. Two investors have offered the full amount in return for shares and a share of profit. Alternatively, as an NGO, he could apply for grants of up to 90,000 and seek donations. Evaluate whether Sunfold Solar should be set up as a private sector company with a social purpose or as an NGO. **[8]**

**12.** Explain why a bank lending to a small privately held company may ask its owners for a personal guarantee. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The owners of a business are responsible for its debts only up to the amount they have invested [1], so their personal assets are protected if the business fails [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two features, each identified and developed. For example: its shares are traded on a stock exchange [1], so it can raise very large amounts of finance from the public [1]. It must publish detailed financial reports [1], so information is available to rivals and the cost of disclosure is high [1]. (Limited liability, separate legal entity and takeover risk also earn credit.) **[4]**

**3. (a)** 44,000 − 19,000 = **25,000** [1]

**(b)** As a sole trader, in general she has unlimited liability, so she must pay the whole 25,000 from her personal assets [1]. As a privately held company, the company owes the debt; she loses her 12,000 investment [1] but is not personally responsible for the 25,000, which the creditors lose [1]

**4.** A cooperative is owned and controlled by its members, who usually have one vote each [1], and it trades for a surplus that is shared among members or reinvested, so the guide groups it as a for-profit social enterprise [1]. An NGO is independent of government and does not distribute profit to any owners; any surplus is kept for its mission [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any two: the service is essential, such as water or health care [1]; one network serves everyone, so competition is impractical; private owners might stop serving unprofitable areas; the industry is strategically important [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Its investors may want higher profit and dividends [1], which could push it to raise prices or cut the social activity, moving it away from its purpose (mission drift) [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** More than half means more than 5,000,000 shares, so at least 5,000,001 [1]. 5,000,001 − 1,800,000 = **3,200,001** extra shares [1]

**(b)** Takeover threat: because anyone can buy shares on the market, a rival like Kestrom can gain control [1], so managers may spend time and money defending the company, or lose their jobs if it is taken over [1]. Short-term pressure: published results are watched by many shareholders [1], so managers may favour quick profit over long-term investment such as research [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Award up to 6 marks for balanced analysis linked to Ashgrove. For example:

- Advantage: workers share the surplus, so they gain directly when the business does well [1], which can raise motivation and productivity [1].
- Advantage: one member, one vote gives every worker a say [1], so decisions are more likely to protect jobs and working conditions [1].
- Disadvantage: 24 people voting can make decisions slow [1], which could cost Ashgrove orders when a quick response is needed [1].
- Disadvantage: outside investors cannot buy control, so raising large sums for new machinery is harder. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* "analyse" needs the effect of each point, not just the point. Two developed advantages and one developed disadvantage can reach the full mark if linked to the case.

**9.** A public sector company may aim to provide a service to all areas, including rural lines that lose money [1], whereas a private company would usually close loss-making lines to raise profit [1]. It may keep fares affordable to meet government social or environmental goals [1], whereas a private owner would set fares to maximize revenue for shareholders [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Total income = 400,000; 200,000/400,000 × 100 = **50%** [1]

**(b)** Half its income will disappear when the grant ends [1], so unless it is replaced the trust may have to install far fewer filters or cut staff [1]

**(c)** For example: expand filter sales to other charities, which it already earns 80,000 from [1], because earned income is more under its control than grants [1]. (A regular-giving scheme for donors would also earn credit.)

**11.** Award up to 8 marks for a balanced, supported judgement. Credit points such as:

- As a company, the investors provide all 150,000 at once, so Sunfold can start straight away [1].
- But investors expect a share of profit, which could push prices up for low-income families and weaken the mission [1].
- Joao would also share control with shareholders, who might later push for higher-income customers [1].
- As an NGO, grants of up to 90,000 leave at least 60,000 to raise through donations [1], which may take time and may not be achieved [1].
- An NGO keeps any surplus for the mission and may attract donors and volunteers who support the purpose [1].
- Grant funding may not be renewed, so the NGO's future income is less certain [1].
- Judgement: the NGO fits the mission better, so it is the stronger choice if Joao can raise the remaining 60,000 from donations; if he cannot, the company route with an agreement that limits dividends would be the practical option [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* the best answers use the 150,000 and 90,000 figures, compare both forms against the purpose of helping low-income families, and give a judgement with a condition.

**12.** A company has limited liability, so if it fails, the owners lose only what they invested [1]. A small company may have few assets to sell to repay a loan [1], so the bank asks the owners to promise personally to repay, which reduces the bank's risk but removes some of the owners' protection [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating limited liability as meaning owners lose nothing.
- Confusing a public sector company with a publicly held company.
- Calling cooperatives non-profit.
- Listing features without explaining their effect on the organization in the case.
- Leaving an "evaluate" answer without a judgement and a condition.
- Ignoring the case figures when recommending a form.
