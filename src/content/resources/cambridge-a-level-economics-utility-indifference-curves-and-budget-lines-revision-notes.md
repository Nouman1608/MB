## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Utility | Satisfaction from consuming a good |
| Total utility (TU) | Satisfaction from all units consumed in a period |
| Marginal utility (MU) | Extra satisfaction from one more unit: change in TU ÷ change in quantity |
| Diminishing marginal utility | MU falls as more units are consumed in a given time |
| Indifference curve | All combinations of two goods giving equal satisfaction |
| Budget line | All combinations affordable at given income and prices |
| Marginal rate of substitution (MRS) | Amount of one good given up for one more unit of the other, keeping satisfaction constant |
| Substitution effect | Change in quantity from the change in relative price, with real income held constant |
| Income effect | Change in quantity from the change in real income caused by the price change |
| Price effect | Substitution effect plus income effect |
| Giffen good | A good whose quantity demanded rises when its price rises, because a negative income effect outweighs the substitution effect |

## Formulae and results

| Item | Result |
|---|---|
| Marginal utility | MU = change in TU ÷ change in quantity |
| TU and MU | TU rises while MU is positive, peaks when MU = 0, falls when MU is negative |
| Equi-marginal principle | MU of A ÷ price of A = MU of B ÷ price of B |
| Budget line intercepts | Income ÷ price of X, and income ÷ price of Y |
| Budget line slope | Price of X ÷ price of Y (absolute value) |
| Consumer's optimum | MRS = price of X ÷ price of Y, at the highest affordable curve |
| Price effect | Price effect = substitution effect + income effect |

## Method: maximising utility with the equi-marginal principle

1. Work out MU per cu for each unit of each good: MU ÷ price.
2. Buy units in order of highest MU per cu.
3. Stop when the budget is spent.
4. Check that the last unit of each good has about the same MU per cu, and that total spending equals income.
5. If the ratios differ, spend less on the good with the lower ratio.

**Quick reminder.** TU after each unit: 10, 18, 24, 28, 30, 30. MU = 10, 8, 6, 4, 2, 0. For the third unit, MU = 24 − 18 = 6. MU is falling while TU is still rising.

**Quick reminder.** MU of A = 18 and price of A = 3 gives 6. MU of B = 20 and price of B = 5 gives 4. The ratio is higher for A, so the consumer should buy more A and less B until the ratios are equal.

## Method: deriving an individual demand curve

1. State the assumption that each cu has a constant marginal utility, for example 4 utils.
2. For each price, buy a unit while MU is at least 4 × price.
3. Plot price against quantity.

**Quick reminder.** MU of successive units: 20, 16, 12, 8, 4. At a price of 2.50 cu, units with MU of at least 10 are bought: 20, 16 and 12. That gives 3 units. At 1.50 cu, MU must be at least 6, so 4 units. The curve slopes down.

## Method: budget lines

1. Maximum X = income ÷ price of X. Maximum Y = income ÷ price of Y. Join the intercepts.
2. Income change: parallel shift.
3. One price change: pivot on the intercept of the good whose price did not change.
4. Both prices change in the same proportion: parallel shift, equal to a change in income.

**Quick reminder.** Income 200 cu, X at 10 cu, Y at 25 cu. Intercepts: 20 X and 8 Y. Slope = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4. Income rises to 250 cu: 25 X and 10 Y. Price of Y falls to 20 cu: the Y intercept becomes 10 and the X intercept stays at 20.

## Method: splitting a price effect

1. Find the original optimum and the new optimum.
2. Work out the income that would be needed to buy the original bundle at the new prices.
3. Find the compensated point, where a line at the new price ratio touches the original curve. Its income is slightly below the figure in step 2.
4. Substitution effect: original to compensated. Income effect: compensated to new.

**Quick reminder.** Income 90 cu, price of X falls from 3 to 2 cu, Y at 6 cu. Original bundle: 18 X and 6 Y (54 + 36 = 90). Buying the original bundle at the new price would cost 2 × 18 + 36 = 72 cu. The compensated bundle is 24 X and 3.6 Y (48 + 21.6 = 69.6 cu, slightly below 72). The new bundle is 27 X and 6 Y (54 + 36 = 90). Substitution effect = 24 − 18 = +6. Income effect = 27 − 24 = +3. Price effect = +9. X is a normal good.

## Must-know distinctions

| Good | Substitution effect of a price fall | Income effect of a price fall | Demand curve |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | Raises quantity | Raises quantity | Slopes down |
| Inferior | Raises quantity | Lowers quantity, smaller | Slopes down |
| Giffen | Raises quantity | Lowers quantity, larger | Slopes up |

- Substitution effects always work in the opposite direction from the price change.
- Every Giffen good is inferior, but most inferior goods are not Giffen.
- Total utility and marginal utility are different: TU can be rising while MU is falling.
- A pivot is a change in one price; a parallel shift is a change in income or in both prices together.
- Marginal utility theory measures utility in numbers. Indifference curve analysis needs only a ranking of bundles.

## Evaluation chains

- Marginal utility theory: assumes measurable utils and a constant MU of money, so the numbers are unreliable. But it explains why demand curves slope down, so it is a sensible starting point.
- Rationality: habit, advertising and limited information mean real consumers may not equalise MU per cu. The theory is a benchmark for behaviour rather than a description of it.
- Indifference curves: only two goods, no observable curves, fixed tastes and prices. But the method separates income and substitution effects clearly, which the MU approach cannot.
- Giffen goods: theoretically sound, but hard to find in practice, because few goods are both inferior and a large share of spending.

## Quick self-test

1. Define marginal utility.
2. TU is 10, 18, 24, 28 for four units. What is the marginal utility of the fourth?
3. MU of A = 18, price of A = 3. MU of B = 20, price of B = 5. What should the consumer do?
4. Income 200 cu, X at 10 cu, Y at 25 cu. State the intercepts and slope of the budget line.
5. Income rises to 250 cu. What are the new intercepts?
6. The price of Y falls to 20 cu. What happens to the budget line?
7. A consumer moves from 4 X and 20 Y to 5 X and 14 Y on the same indifference curve. Calculate the MRS.
8. At the consumer's optimum, what does the MRS equal if X is 10 cu and Y is 25 cu?
9. A price fall gives a substitution effect of +5 and an income effect of −2. Classify the good and state the price effect.
10. A price rise gives a substitution effect of −3 and an income effect of +5. Classify the good.
11. Give two limitations of indifference curve analysis.

### Answers

1. The extra satisfaction from consuming one more unit.
2. 28 − 24 = **4 utils**.
3. A: 18 ÷ 3 = 6. B: 20 ÷ 5 = 4. **Buy more A and less B.**
4. 200 ÷ 10 = **20 X**, 200 ÷ 25 = **8 Y**. Slope = 10 ÷ 25 = **0.4**.
5. 250 ÷ 10 = **25 X** and 250 ÷ 25 = **10 Y**.
6. 200 ÷ 20 = 10 Y. **It pivots outward on the X intercept (20), with the Y intercept rising from 8 to 10.**
7. 6 Y given up for 1 X: **MRS = 6**.
8. **10 ÷ 25 = 0.4**, the price ratio.
9. The income effect opposes the substitution effect but is smaller: **inferior good, not Giffen**. Price effect = 5 − 2 = **+3**.
10. Price effect = −3 + 5 = +2. Quantity rises when price rises: **Giffen good**.
11. Any two: only two goods, curves cannot be observed, stable tastes assumed, rational consumer assumed, fixed prices and no saving or borrowing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the budget line slope as price of Y ÷ price of X.
- Dividing change in TU by the wrong quantity change, or using TU where MU is asked for.
- Choosing a bundle with the highest MU rather than the highest MU per cu.
- Pivoting a budget line on the wrong intercept after one price changes.
- Not holding real income constant when finding the substitution effect.
- Calling every inferior good a Giffen good.
- Listing limitations with no judgement on how serious each is.
- Saying a consumer is "indifferent" between points on different curves.
