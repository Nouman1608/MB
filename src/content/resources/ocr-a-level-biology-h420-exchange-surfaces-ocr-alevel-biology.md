Larger, more active animals cannot rely on diffusion across their body surface alone, so they have specialised exchange surfaces and ventilation systems. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 3.1.1 you must explain why, describe the features that make an exchange surface efficient, know the tissues of the mammalian airways and how the lungs are ventilated, interpret spirometer data, and explain ventilation and gas exchange in bony fish and insects, along with the dissection and microscopy work in PAG1 and PAG2.

This guide covers learning outcomes 3.1.1(a) to (h) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 3, Exchange and transport. It goes into more detail than the module-wide [exchange and transport study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-exchange-and-transport/). ## Why are specialised exchange surfaces needed? (a)

A single-celled organism has a large surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) and a short diffusion distance to every part of the cell, so diffusion across its surface meets its needs. As an organism gets bigger, volume increases faster than surface area, so SA:V falls. A large multicellular animal also has cells far from the surface and, if it is active, a high metabolic rate that uses oxygen quickly.

**Worked example 1.** Compare three shapes (all lengths in mm).

| Shape | Surface area | Volume | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cube, side 0.1 | 6 × 0.1² = 0.06 | 0.1³ = 0.001 | 60 : 1 |
| Cube, side 10 | 6 × 10² = 600 | 1000 | 0.6 : 1 |
| Flat sheet, 10 × 5 × 0.2 | 2(50 + 2 + 1) = 106 | 10 | 10.6 : 1 |

Making the body 100 times longer on each side cuts SA:V by 100 times. Flattening the body, as in a flatworm, keeps the ratio high and every cell close to the surface, which is why some small animals manage without a gas exchange system.

## What makes an exchange surface efficient? (b)

The rate of diffusion increases with surface area and with the difference in concentration, and decreases as the diffusion distance increases. Efficient surfaces therefore have:

- **a large surface area**: root hair cells extend the surface of the root; millions of alveoli give the lungs a very large total area;
- **a thin barrier**: the alveolar wall and capillary wall are each one flattened (squamous) cell thick;
- **a steep concentration gradient, kept up by blood flow and ventilation**: blood carries oxygen away from the alveolus or gill, and fresh air or water replaces the used medium.

## Tissues of the mammalian gaseous exchange system (c)

| Tissue | Trachea | Bronchi | Bronchioles | Alveoli | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cartilage | C-shaped rings | Irregular plates | None | None | Holds airways open when pressure falls during inspiration; C shape lets the oesophagus expand when food is swallowed |
| Ciliated epithelium | Yes | Yes | Larger ones only | No (squamous epithelium) | Cilia beat to move mucus up towards the throat |
| Goblet cells | Yes | Yes | Few or none | No | Secrete mucus that traps dust and microorganisms |
| Smooth muscle | Yes | Yes | Yes, a large proportion of the wall | No | Contracts to narrow the airway and controls airflow |
| Elastic fibres | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Stretch during inspiration and recoil during expiration |

Cartilage and goblet cells become fewer, and finally absent, further down the airways. Smooth muscle and elastic fibres continue into the bronchioles, and elastic fibres surround the alveoli.

## How are the lungs ventilated? (d)

| | Inspiration | Expiration at rest |
|---|---|---|
| External intercostal muscles | Contract | Relax |
| Rib cage | Moves up and out | Moves down and in |
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens | Relaxes and domes upwards |
| Thorax volume | Increases | Decreases |
| Pressure in the lungs | Falls below atmospheric | Rises above atmospheric |
| Air | Moves in | Moves out |

Expiration at rest is mostly passive, helped by the recoil of elastic fibres. In **forced expiration** the internal intercostal muscles contract to pull the ribs down and in, and abdominal muscles contract to push the diaphragm up.

## Spirometry: lung volumes and oxygen uptake (e)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tidal volume | Volume of air moved in or out in one normal breath |
| Vital capacity | Largest volume that can be breathed out after the deepest possible breath in |
| Breathing rate | Breaths per minute |
| Ventilation rate | Tidal volume × breathing rate, in dm³ min⁻¹ |
| Oxygen uptake | Volume of oxygen absorbed per minute |

In a spirometer the subject breathes from a chamber of oxygen. Soda lime absorbs the carbon dioxide breathed out, so the volume in the chamber falls as oxygen is used. The downward slope of the trace gives the oxygen uptake.

**Worked example 2.** A trace shows 15 breaths in 1.0 minute with a mean peak-to-trough height of 0.48 dm³. Over 4.0 minutes the troughs fall from 3.20 dm³ to 2.40 dm³.

```
ventilation rate = 0.48 × 15 = 7.2 dm³ min⁻¹
oxygen uptake    = (3.20 − 2.40) / 4.0 = 0.20 dm³ min⁻¹
```

During exercise, tidal volume and breathing rate both rise, so ventilation rate and oxygen uptake rise. Vital capacity is a property of the lungs and does not change during one session of exercise.

## Gas exchange in bony fish and insects (f)

### Bony fish

Water is dense and holds far less oxygen than air, so a steady one-way flow over the gills is needed.

1. The mouth opens and the floor of the buccal cavity is lowered, increasing its volume and lowering pressure, so water flows in; the operculum is closed.
2. The mouth closes and the floor of the buccal cavity is raised, increasing pressure. Water is forced over the gills and out as the operculum opens.

Each gill has many **gill filaments**, and each filament carries many **gill lamellae** (gill plates) with a rich capillary supply and a thin surface. Blood flows through the lamellae in the opposite direction to the water: **countercurrent flow**. Blood always meets water that has a higher oxygen concentration than itself, so a diffusion gradient is kept along the whole lamella and the blood leaves with an oxygen concentration close to that of incoming water. With parallel flow, the two would reach equilibrium partway along and diffusion would stop.

### Insects

- Air enters through **spiracles**, pores along the thorax and abdomen that can be opened and closed to reduce water loss.
- **Tracheae**, tubes strengthened by rings of chitin, branch into narrow **tracheoles** that reach individual cells.
- The ends of tracheoles contain **tracheal fluid**. Oxygen dissolves in it and diffuses into the cells. During intense activity, fluid is drawn out of the tracheole ends into the respiring tissue, so more of the tracheole surface is in contact with air and gas exchange speeds up.
- Larger, active insects ventilate the system by **thoracic and abdominal movements** that change the body volume and pump air in and out.

## Practical work (g)-(h)

- **PAG2 dissection.** Fish gill arches can be removed to show the filaments; floating a gill in water separates the filaments so the large surface becomes visible. Insect tracheae can be seen under a microscope as tubes with spiral thickening.
- **PAG1 microscopy.** Prepared slides of lung tissue show thin-walled alveoli and capillaries; trachea sections show cartilage, ciliated epithelium and goblet cells. Draw with clear single lines, no shading, and label tissues.

## Common mistakes

- Saying alveoli have cartilage or cilia.
- Saying the diaphragm "moves up" during inspiration; it contracts and flattens.
- Describing air moving in "because the lungs expand" without the pressure change.
- Explaining countercurrent flow without saying the gradient is kept along the **whole** length.
- Giving tidal volume as the deepest breath; that is vital capacity.
- Calling tracheoles "blood vessels"; the insect system delivers air directly to cells.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a feature of an exchange surface: name it, then link it to diffusion rate (larger area, shorter distance, steeper gradient).
- **Describe** a tissue's distribution: say where it is present and where it is absent.
- **Calculate** from a spirometer trace: show readings from the graph and the time interval before the answer, with units.
- **Compare** fish and insect systems point by point.
