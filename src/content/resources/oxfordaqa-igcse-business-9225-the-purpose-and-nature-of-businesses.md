This guide covers section **3.1.1 The purpose and nature of businesses** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), the first subtopic of Topic 1, Business in the real world. The specification asks you to understand what a business is and why people start one; the difference between goods and services and between needs and wants; the four factors of production; opportunity cost; the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors and how they depend on each other; the meaning of enterprise and the characteristics and objectives of an entrepreneur; and why the business environment keeps changing. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business in the Real World study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-business-business-in-the-real-world/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-the-purpose-and-nature-of-businesses-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-the-purpose-and-nature-of-businesses-practice/).

## What a business is for

A **business** is an organisation that uses resources to produce goods or services that customers want. Its basic purpose is to meet a demand and, in most cases, to earn a reward for doing so. The reward is usually profit, but not always: a charity-run bakery might aim only to cover its costs and train young people.

The specification lists several reasons for starting a business:

- **Producing goods**, such as furniture, shoes or bread.
- **Supplying services**, such as haircuts, repairs or accounting.
- **Distributing products** made by others, for example a wholesaler or a shop.
- **Fulfilling a business opportunity**, such as noticing that nobody sells fresh fruit juice near a new bus station.
- **Providing a good or service to others**, because the owner wants to serve a community need.

All of these start from the same idea: someone spots a way to satisfy customers and organises resources to do it.

## Goods and services, needs and wants

**Goods** are physical products you can touch, such as a laptop or a loaf of bread. They can be stored and sold later. **Services** are activities done for customers, such as a taxi ride or a dental check-up. A service is produced and used at the same moment and cannot be stored.

Many businesses sell both. A restaurant sells goods (the food) and a service (cooking, serving and cleaning).

**Needs** are things people must have to survive, such as food, water, shelter and clothing. **Wants** are things people would like but can live without, such as a games console. Businesses exist to meet both. The line moves: a mobile phone is a want for some people but close to a need for someone whose work depends on it. In the exam, explain your choice rather than just labelling.

## Factors of production

Every business needs resources. Economists group them into four **factors of production**.

| Factor | Meaning | Example for a bakery |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources and the site used | The shop, the ground it stands on, wheat |
| Labour | The people who do the work | Bakers, shop assistants, delivery drivers |
| Capital | Man-made items used to produce | Ovens, mixers, delivery van, cash to invest |
| Enterprise | Organising the other three and taking the risk | The owner who decides what to bake and how |

Each factor earns a reward: land earns rent, labour earns wages, capital earns interest and enterprise earns profit. A common mistake is to treat "capital" as only money. In this context it also covers machinery and equipment.

## Opportunity cost

Resources are limited, so choosing one use means giving up another. **Opportunity cost** is the value of the next best alternative that you give up when you make a choice.

**Worked example 1: a baker's hour.** Mira's bakery can use one oven-hour to make either 5 loaves or 2 cakes. This week she spends 4 oven-hours on cakes.

```
Cakes made = 4 x 2 = 8 cakes
Loaves given up = 4 x 5 = 20 loaves
```

The opportunity cost of making 8 cakes is 20 loaves. If loaves sell for $2 each, the sales given up are 20 x $2 = $40, and the cakes need to bring in more than $40 to make the choice worthwhile.

Opportunity cost applies to money too. A business with $10,000 to spend can buy a delivery van or a new oven, not both. If it buys the van, the opportunity cost is the benefit it would have had from the oven.

## The three sectors

Businesses are grouped by the type of activity they carry out.

| Sector | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracts or grows natural resources | Farming, fishing, mining, forestry, oil drilling |
| Secondary | Makes, processes or builds products from raw materials | Car manufacturing, baking, construction, clothes factories |
| Tertiary | Provides services | Retail, banking, transport, education, tourism |

### How the sectors depend on each other

The sectors are **interdependent**: each relies on the others.

**Worked example 2: a cotton shirt.** A farm (primary) grows cotton. A mill and clothing factory (secondary) turn it into shirts. A shop and a delivery firm (tertiary) sell the shirts to customers. If the farm's harvest fails, the factory has no cotton and the shop has fewer shirts to sell. If the shop closes, the factory loses its buyer. Banks and insurers (also tertiary) support all three stages.

In an exam, name the stage you are describing and explain the link: "the secondary sector depends on the primary sector for raw materials".

## Enterprise and entrepreneurs

**Enterprise** is the willingness to take a risk to set up and run a business. An **entrepreneur** is a person who takes that risk, organises the resources and aims to earn a profit. Not every business owner is an entrepreneur in the full sense: someone who runs a business founded by a parent may be more a manager than a risk-taker.

### Characteristics of an entrepreneur

The specification names four, and each should be explained, not just listed:

- **Hard-working:** a new business often needs very long hours before it earns a profit.
- **Innovative:** coming up with a new product or a better way to do things helps a business stand out.
- **Organised:** planning stock, staff, money and deadlines keeps the business running.
- **Willing to take a risk:** the entrepreneur may lose their own savings if the business fails.

### Objectives of an entrepreneur

People start businesses for different reasons. The specification lists:

- to **be their own boss**
- to have **flexible working hours**
- to **pursue an interest**, such as photography or cooking
- to **earn more money**
- to **identify a gap in the market**, meaning a product or service customers want but nobody supplies
- **dissatisfaction with their current job**.

Different motives lead to different decisions. An entrepreneur who wants flexible hours may keep the business small. One who wants to earn more money may aim to grow quickly. Reading a case study for the owner's motive helps you judge what they should do next.

## The dynamic nature of business

The business world never stands still. A business that does not respond to change can lose customers. The specification names four sources of change:

- **Technology:** online ordering, social media and automated machines can create new products and new ways to sell.
- **The economic situation:** when people have less money to spend, demand for non-essential goods falls.
- **Legislation:** new laws on pay, safety or product standards change costs and how businesses operate.
- **Environmental expectations:** customers and governments increasingly expect businesses to reduce waste and pollution.

**Worked example 3: a corner photographer.** Dani runs a small photo-printing shop. Phone cameras and online printing reduce her print sales by a third (technology). A local downturn makes families cut back on gifts (economic situation). New rules on disposing of chemicals raise her costs (legislation) and customers ask her to use recycled paper (environmental expectations). Dani adapts by adding framing, event photography and a website. A dynamic business changes its offer as conditions change.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a business only as "a place that makes profit". Not-for-profit organisations are businesses too, and the definition should mention meeting customer needs.
- Mixing up capital and money. Capital as a factor of production includes machinery, vehicles and buildings.
- Saying opportunity cost is the money spent. It is the next best alternative given up.
- Listing the sectors without examples, or placing a shop in the secondary sector. Retail is tertiary.
- Treating all entrepreneurs as motivated only by profit.
- Describing change in business without naming the cause: technology, economic situation, legislation or environmental expectations.

## Exam technique for this section

**Define** and **state** questions need a short precise answer, usually with a key word such as "alternative" for opportunity cost. **Explain** questions need a point developed with a reason: an entrepreneur who is innovative might design a product rivals do not offer, which helps the business to stand out. When a case study gives an example, use its details. **Analyse** questions ask for a chain of reasoning, for example how a fall in raw material supply in the primary sector affects a secondary-sector firm and then its customers.
