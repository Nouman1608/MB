## Questions

### Passage A -- blog post (original text written for Marlbridge)

> **Borrowed Wheels**
>
> **Post 40: The hill wins again (for now)**
> Posted 14 March · Tags: commuting, hills, stubbornness
>
> Forty mornings. Forty mornings on my neighbour's old green bicycle, and I still cannot get up
> Castle Hill without stopping at the postbox.
>
> For new readers: in January I sold my car, borrowed Mr Aziz's bike "until spring", and promised to
> blog every ride. Spring is next week. Mr Aziz has stopped asking.
>
> Today's score: Hill 40, Me 0. But I stopped at the postbox, not the bench. Last month it was the
> bench. That is thirty metres of progress, and I will take it.
>
> [Photo: the green bicycle leaning on a red postbox, rain on the handlebars.]
>
> What's your hill? Tell me in the comments. I read every one, usually with a cup of tea and aching
> legs.
>
> 23 comments

**1.** Identify three conventions of a blog in Passage A. Quote or describe each one. **[3]**

**2.** Analyse how the third paragraph ("Today's score...") builds the blogger's persona. **[3]**

### Passage B -- infographic (original text written for Marlbridge)

> [Layout: a square graphic in green and grey. At the top, in very large green figures, "−25%".
> Beneath it, in smaller type, the headline. Below that, four rows of stick-figure icons, one row per
> way of travelling, each ending in a percentage. Three rows are green; the car row is grey. A pull
> quote sits in a speech bubble on the right. A thin grey line of small print runs along the bottom.]
>
> **−25%**
> **Car drop-offs down by a quarter since 2022**
>
> How Tamsford gets to school, 2026:
> Walk 38% · Bus 25% · Car 24% · Cycle 13%
>
> "My legs, my rules." -- Year 9 walker
>
> Source: Tamsford Schools travel survey, March 2026, 1,200 students. Car share in 2022 survey: 32%.

**3.** Identify the most salient element of Passage B and describe the likely reading path. **[2]**

**4.** The car share fell from 32% to 24%.

**(a)** Show that this is a fall of one quarter, and give the fall in percentage points. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the designer made "−25%" the largest element rather than the fall in
percentage points. **[2]**

**5.** Evaluate how far the source line supports the headline "Car drop-offs down by a quarter".
**[3]**

**6.** Analyse how the colour coding and the pull quote shape the reader's view of walking and
driving. **[3]**

### Passage C -- editorial cartoon (original text written for Marlbridge)

> [Single-panel cartoon. A town mayor, drawn with an enormous head and a tiny body, cuts a ribbon
> with giant golden scissors and smiles at the viewer. Behind him, a sign reading "NEW CYCLE LANE"
> points at a painted strip no wider than a tyre, squeezed between a row of parked cars and a huge
> lorry labelled "RUSH HOUR". A cyclist in the strip holds her breath, cheeks puffed out, elbows
> tucked in.]
>
> Caption: **"Mind the gap."**

**7.** Identify two conventions of an editorial cartoon in Passage C and explain the effect of each.
**[4]**

**8.** Explain what the reader needs to know for Passage C to work, and how the caption creates
irony. **[3]**

**9.** A news photograph shows a crowded railway platform at dusk, taken from a high angle, with
hundreds of commuters looking up at a departures board. Write two captions that anchor it in
opposite ways, and explain how each changes its meaning. **[4]**

**10.** Extended task. Plan a guided analysis of Passage B in response to the guiding question:
**How do the visual and verbal features of the infographic present a success story?** Give a thesis
and a paragraph plan with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Extended task. Write one analytical paragraph on Passage C in response to the guiding
question: **How does the cartoon use exaggeration to criticise the new cycle lane?** **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, one mark each with evidence: a **dated post with a numbered title** ("Post 40",
"Posted 14 March") [1]; **tags** ("commuting, hills, stubbornness") [1]; a **comments section**
("23 comments", "Tell me in the comments") [1]. Also creditworthy: first-person voice; the embedded photo.
*Examiner insight:* A convention earns credit only with evidence from the passage.

**2.** The sports-style score "Hill 40, Me 0" turns a daily defeat into a joke, so the blogger seems
self-mocking rather than discouraged. [1] "But" pivots to a small gain: the postbox, not the bench.
[1] "thirty metres of progress, and I will take it" shows a stubborn, cheerful persona that
readers can root for, which fits the tag "stubbornness". [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers name the persona (self-mocking, stubborn) and tie each quotation
to it; weak ones paraphrase the paragraph.

**3.** The most salient element is the very large green **"−25%"** at the top. [1] The eye then
moves down to the headline, across the icon rows, to the pull quote on the right, and ends at the
small grey source line at the bottom. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Salience needs a reason (size, colour, position), not just a name.

**4. (a)** 32 − 24 = 8, and 8 ÷ 32 = 0.25, so the fall is one quarter (25%). [1] In percentage
points the fall is **8 percentage points**. [1]
**(b)** "−25%" is a bigger, rounder number than 8, so the change looks larger and more
impressive. [1] A single bold figure is also quicker to grasp at a glance, which suits an
infographic's convention of little text. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Commenting on how a figure is framed is analysis; repeating the number is not.

**5.** The source line adds credibility: it names the survey, the date and a sample of 1,200
students. [1] But it gives the car **share** (32% to 24%), not the number of drop-offs, and no sample
size for 2022, so if pupil numbers changed the headline's claim about drop-offs may not follow. [1]
It is also printed small and grey at the end of the reading path, so few readers will check it; the
support is **partial**. [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a judgement that weighs what the source line does and does not
support.

**6.** Walking, bus and cycling rows are green, a colour linked with health and the environment,
while the car row alone is grey, which makes driving look dull and out of place. [1] The pull quote
"My legs, my rules." gives walking a young, confident, independent voice. [1] Together, colour and
quote present walking as the attractive choice, so the graphic persuades as well as informs. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Link each visual feature to an effect; naming colours alone is description.

**7.** Any two conventions, with effect, 2 marks each. **Caricature**: the mayor's enormous head and
tiny body [1] suggest vanity and self-importance, more interest in the photo than the lane. [1]
**Labels**: "NEW CYCLE LANE" and "RUSH HOUR" [1] tell the reader what the objects stand for, so the
lorry becomes the danger of peak traffic, not one vehicle. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each convention needs a precise visual detail and its meaning; naming
"exaggeration" without saying what is exaggerated earns little.

**8.** The reader needs to know that councils open new cycle lanes with ceremonies and that some
lanes are criticised as narrow or unsafe. [1] "Mind the gap" is a familiar safety warning about a
dangerous space. [1] Here the only gap is the tiny lane itself, so the warning ironically admits the
lane is the danger, while the mayor smiles. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Without the context the cartoon assumes, the answer is only description.

**9.** Model captions, for example: A: "Record crowds as the new timetable proves popular" [1]; B:
"Stranded: evening trains cancelled again" [1]. With caption A, the crowd reads as success and the
upward gaze as anticipation. [1] With caption B, the same high angle makes the commuters look small
and helpless, and the upward gaze reads as waiting in vain. [1]
*Examiner insight:* The marks come from explaining how the words redirect an unchanged image.

**10.** The real exam is marked holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point. These are
indicative points.

Thesis: the infographic presents a success story by making the change look large and walking look
attractive, while keeping its limits out of sight. [1]

- P1: salience of "−25%" in large green figures sets a positive frame before any detail. [1]
- P2: framing of the figure: a quarter sounds bigger than 8 percentage points. [1]
- P3: colour coding: green for walking, bus and cycling; grey for the car. [1]
- P4: the pull quote "My legs, my rules." adds a confident young voice. [1]
- P5: the icon rows let readers compare modes at a glance, with walking longest. [1]
- P6: reading path ends at the small grey source line, which reports shares, not numbers. [1]
- Conclusion: features combine so the graphic persuades while appearing to inform. [1]

*Examiner insight:* Strong plans are organised by argument; weak plans describe the graphic top to
bottom.

**11.** Marked holistically in the real exam; these are indicative points.

> The cartoon uses exaggeration to show the new cycle lane as a publicity stunt rather than real
> protection. [1] The mayor's enormous head on a tiny body turns him into a figure of vanity [1],
> and his smile at the viewer, not at the lane, suggests he cares about being seen. [1] The giant
> golden scissors inflate a small painted line into a grand occasion. [1] By contrast, the lane is
> shrunk to a strip "no wider than a tyre" [1], squeezed by a lorry labelled "RUSH HOUR", so the
> danger is made as large as the ceremony. [1] The cyclist's puffed cheeks and tucked elbows make
> her discomfort physical and slightly comic. [1] Exaggeration works in both directions, enlarging
> the ceremony and shrinking the safety, so the reader sees the gap between the mayor's claims and
> the cyclist's experience. [1]

*Examiner insight:* The best paragraphs keep returning to the guiding question's key word,
exaggeration, and explain what each exaggeration criticises.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing conventions from memory without quoting or describing them in the passage.
- Describing a visual feature ("the colours are green") without explaining its effect.
- Taking an infographic's headline figure on trust instead of asking how it is framed.
- Ignoring the source line, or mentioning it without judging what it does and does not support.
- Explaining a cartoon's picture without the context the reader needs to get the point.
- Treating the caption and the image separately instead of showing how the words anchor the image.
- In extended tasks, following the text in order instead of arguing towards the guiding question.
