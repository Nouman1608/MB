This set tests section 3.2.3.2 Break-even analysis from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1: calculating and interpreting break-even output, profit or loss at different outputs, margin of safety and contribution, and interpreting and manipulating break-even charts. Every business and figure is invented.

Before you start, the [break-even study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-break-even-analysis-9725/) teaches the method and the [break-even revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-break-even-analysis-9725-revision-notes/) summarise it.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term contribution per unit. **[2]**

**2.** State **one** difference between fixed costs and variable costs. **[2]**

**3.** Ravensworth Rugs sells rugs at £45 each. The variable cost of each rug is £27 and fixed costs are £36,000 a year. Calculate Ravensworth's break-even output. **[3]**

**4.** Ravensworth Rugs (question 3) sells 2,600 rugs a year.

**(a)** Calculate its margin of safety. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate its annual profit. **[2]**

**5.** Dunmore Printing sells 4,500 greetings cards a month, each with a contribution of £3.20. Its fixed costs are £12,000 a month. Calculate total contribution and profit for the month. **[3]**

**6.** Ellerby Candles has fixed costs of £9,500 a month. It sells candles at £14 each and the variable cost is £8.25 a candle. Calculate break-even output, giving your answer as a whole number of candles. **[3]**

**7.** A break-even chart for Fenwick Lamps shows the following values.

| Output (lamps) | 0 | 500 | 1,000 | 1,500 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue (£) | 0 | 10,000 | 20,000 | 30,000 |
| Total costs (£) | 12,000 | 16,000 | 20,000 | 24,000 |

**(a)** State Fenwick's fixed costs. **[1]**

**(b)** State its break-even output. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the variable cost per lamp. **[2]**

**(d)** State the profit at an output of 1,500 lamps. **[1]**

**8.** Explain the effect of an increase in rent on a business's break-even chart and break-even output. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Halvergate Bakes sells loaves at £3.50. The variable cost is £1.40 a loaf and fixed costs are £4,200 a month. The owner wants a profit of £2,100 a month. Calculate how many loaves Halvergate must sell each month to reach this target. **[3]**

**10.** Corriebank Cycles hires out bikes at £30 a hire. The variable cost is £6 a hire and fixed costs are £14,400 a month. It currently makes 900 hires a month.

**(a)** Calculate Corriebank's current break-even output, margin of safety and monthly profit. **[3]**

**(b)** The manager proposes cutting the price to £26, which she expects to raise hires to 1,050 a month. Calculate the new break-even output and monthly profit. **[3]**

**(c)** Using your answers, explain whether Corriebank should cut its price. **[2]**

**11.** Ysolde Tremayne plans to start a kayak hire business on a lake. She will charge £25 a hire. The variable cost is £5 a hire, mainly cleaning and insurance per trip. Fixed costs, including the lakeside lease, will be £9,600 a month. She expects 650 hires a month in the summer but has no trading record, and trade in winter will be much lower. She needs a bank loan to buy the kayaks, and the bank has asked for a business plan.

**(a)** Calculate Ysolde's break-even output and her margin of safety for a summer month. **[3]**

**(b)** Evaluate the usefulness of break-even analysis to Ysolde in starting her business. **[12]**

**12.** Explain **two** limitations of break-even analysis for a business that sells many different products. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** The selling price of one unit minus its variable cost [1]; the amount each unit contributes towards paying fixed costs and then towards profit [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Fixed costs do not change with the level of output, for example rent [1], whereas variable costs change directly with output, for example raw materials [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Contribution per unit = 45 − 27 = £18 [1]. Break-even = 36,000 ÷ 18 [1] = **2,000 rugs** [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 2,600 − 2,000 = **600 rugs** [1].

**(b)** Profit = margin of safety × contribution = 600 × 18 [1] = **£10,800** [1]. (Revenue 117,000 − total costs 106,200 also earns both marks.) **[3]**

**5.** Total contribution = 4,500 × 3.20 = **£14,400** [1]. Profit = 14,400 − 12,000 [1] = **£2,400** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Contribution = 14 − 8.25 = £5.75 [1]. 9,500 ÷ 5.75 = 1,652.2 [1]. Break-even = **1,653 candles**, rounded up because 1,652 would still leave a small loss [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** **£12,000** (total costs at zero output) [1].

**(b)** **1,000 lamps**, where revenue and total costs are both £20,000 [1].

**(c)** (16,000 − 12,000) ÷ 500 [1] = **£8 per lamp** [1].

**(d)** 30,000 − 24,000 = **£6,000** [1]. **[5]**

**8.** Rent is a fixed cost, so the fixed-cost line shifts upwards [1] and the total-cost line shifts up in parallel, keeping the same slope [1]. Contribution per unit is unchanged, so more units are needed to cover the higher fixed costs and break-even output rises [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Contribution = 3.50 − 1.40 = £2.10 [1]. Required output = (4,200 + 2,100) ÷ 2.10 [1] = **3,000 loaves** [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Contribution = 30 − 6 = £24; break-even = 14,400 ÷ 24 = **600 hires** [1]. Margin of safety = 900 − 600 = **300 hires** [1]. Profit = 300 × 24 = **£7,200** [1].

**(b)** New contribution = 26 − 6 = £20; break-even = 14,400 ÷ 20 = **720 hires** [1]. Profit = (1,050 × 20) − 14,400 = 21,000 − 14,400 [1] = **£6,600** [1].

**(c)** Corriebank should not cut the price on these figures: even if hires rise as expected, profit falls by £600 a month [1], and break-even rises by 120 hires, so the business takes on more risk for less reward; the 1,050 figure is also only an estimate [1]. **[8]**

**11. (a)** Contribution = 25 − 5 = £20 [1]. Break-even = 9,600 ÷ 20 = **480 hires a month** [1]. Margin of safety = 650 − 480 = **170 hires** [1].

**(b)** One mark per point below, up to 12. The strongest answers end with a judgement like the one shown.

- Break-even shows Ysolde she needs 480 hires a month before she makes any profit [1], so she knows the minimum level of trade that the lakeside lease commits her to [1].
- The margin of safety of 170 hires means summer trade could fall by about a quarter before losses begin [1], which gives the bank evidence that the plan can survive a poor month [1].
- As a start-up with no trading record, Ysolde has no past figures to show the bank, so a break-even calculation is one of the few ways to show the plan is viable [1], and it can support her loan application [1].
- It lets her test changes quickly, for example the effect of a price cut to attract families or a higher lease cost [1], before committing money [1].
- However, the 650 hires are only an estimate with no trading history behind it [1]; if the true figure is nearer 500, the margin of safety shrinks to 20 hires and the plan looks far riskier [1].
- Winter trade will be much lower but fixed costs such as the lease continue, so a summer-month calculation overstates the position across the year [1]; she should calculate break-even for the whole year or for each season [1].

Judgement: break-even is useful as a starting point and for the bank, but only alongside a monthly cash flow forecast and a cautious sales estimate covering the winter.

**12.** It assumes one selling price and one variable cost per unit, but each product has its own price and variable cost [1], so there is no single contribution per unit to divide into fixed costs, and an average depends on the sales mix [1]. Fixed costs such as rent are shared between products [1], so dividing them between products is arbitrary and can make one product look less profitable than it is [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by price instead of by contribution per unit.
- Rounding break-even down instead of up.
- Reading revenue at break-even when the question asks for output.
- Forgetting that a sales increase changes profit and margin of safety but not break-even output.
- Judging a price cut on break-even alone without comparing profit before and after.
- Evaluating break-even in general instead of using the details of the business in the case.
