These questions cover sub-topic 3.3, Macroeconomic objectives, from the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). Question 8 is an **HL-only calculation**. Figures are invented, and the [1] points in the answers are a revision aid, not IB marks. Revise first with the [3.3 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-3/) or the [3.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-3-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the *natural rate of unemployment*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between disinflation and deflation. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why the official unemployment rate may understate the true level of unemployment. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between demand-pull and cost-push inflation. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between a budget deficit and government debt. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** consequences of high unemployment for the government budget. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The fictional country of Tamsk has a labour force of 28 million, of whom 2.1 million are unemployed. A further 0.7 million people have stopped looking for work because they believe no jobs are available.

**(a)** Calculate the official unemployment rate. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the unemployment rate if discouraged workers were counted as unemployed and as part of the labour force. **[2]**

**8.** (HL only) Tamsk's statisticians use this basket.

| Item | Weight | Price index (base year = 100) |
|---|---|---|
| Food | 35 | 106 |
| Housing | 25 | 120 |
| Clothing | 15 | 100 |
| Transport | 25 | 103 |

**(a)** Construct the weighted price index. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the result differs from a simple average of the four indices. **[2]**

**9.** Tamsk's CPI was 150.0 in Year 1, 165.0 in Year 2 and 169.95 in Year 3.

**(a)** Calculate the inflation rate in Years 2 and 3. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify whether Year 3 shows inflation, disinflation or deflation. **[1]**

**10.** Using a Phillips curve diagram, explain why there may be no long-run trade-off between unemployment and inflation. **[4]**

**11.** Tamsk's government debt is 720 billion tamsks and nominal GDP is 800 billion. Next year the budget deficit is 48 billion and nominal GDP grows by 6%. The interest rate on the debt is 4.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the debt-to-GDP ratio this year and next year. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate this year's interest cost and explain **one** consequence of it. **[2]**

**12.** Discuss whether a government should give priority to reducing unemployment rather than inflation. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The rate of unemployment when the economy is at potential output [1], made up of frictional, seasonal and structural unemployment with no cyclical unemployment [1].

**2.** Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation, so prices still rise but more slowly [1]. Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level [1].

**3.** Discouraged workers who have stopped searching are not counted [1], and underemployed part-time workers who want full-time work count as employed [1].

**4.** Demand-pull inflation occurs when AD rises faster than productive capacity, pulling up the price level [1]. Cost-push inflation occurs when rising costs, such as wages or energy, shift SRAS left [1].

**5.** A budget deficit is the amount by which government spending exceeds revenue in one year (a flow) [1]. Government debt is the total amount owed, built up from past deficits (a stock) [1].

**6.** Tax revenue falls because fewer people earn incomes [1]; spending on unemployment benefits rises [1].

**7. (a)** 2.1 ÷ 28 × 100 = **7.5%** [1].

**(b)** (2.1 + 0.7) ÷ (28 + 0.7) [1] × 100 ≈ **9.8%** [1].

**8. (a)** (35 × 106 + 25 × 120 + 15 × 100 + 25 × 103) ÷ 100 = (3,710 + 3,000 + 1,500 + 2,575) ÷ 100 [1] = **107.85** [1].

**(b)** A simple average gives (106 + 120 + 100 + 103) ÷ 4 = 107.25 [1]. The weighted index is higher because housing, which rose most, has a larger weight than clothing, which did not rise; weights reflect the share of spending on each item [1].

**9. (a)** Year 2: (165 - 150) ÷ 150 × 100 = **10%** [1]. Year 3: (169.95 - 165) ÷ 165 × 100 = **3%** [1].

**(b)** Disinflation: prices still rise, but at a lower rate [1].

**10.** Diagram with inflation on the vertical axis and unemployment on the horizontal axis, a downward-sloping SRPC and a vertical LRPC at the natural rate [1]. Expansionary policy moves the economy along SRPC1 to lower unemployment and higher inflation [1]. Workers come to expect the higher inflation and demand higher wages, so SRPC shifts up to SRPC2 [1]. Unemployment returns to the natural rate at a higher inflation rate, so in the long run there is no trade-off [1].

**11. (a)** This year: 720 ÷ 800 × 100 = **90%** [1]. Next year: debt 768, GDP 848; 768 ÷ 848 × 100 ≈ **90.6%** [1].

**(b)** Interest = 0.045 × 720 = **32.4 billion tamsks** [1]. This money has an opportunity cost: it cannot be spent on health, education or infrastructure, or it requires higher taxes [1].

**12.** For prioritising unemployment: unemployment wastes output, shown by a point inside the PPC [1]; it causes hardship, poorer health and lasting loss of skills, especially for young people [1]; it reduces tax revenue and raises benefit spending [1]. For prioritising inflation: high or unstable inflation creates uncertainty that reduces investment and long-run growth [1]; it erodes savings and fixed incomes [1]; the long-run Phillips curve suggests pushing unemployment below the natural rate only raises inflation [1]. The right priority depends on the type of unemployment: cyclical unemployment can be reduced by raising AD, but structural unemployment needs supply-side measures [1]. Judgement: when unemployment is cyclical and inflation is low, reducing unemployment should come first; when inflation is high and unemployment is near its natural rate, controlling inflation matters more [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the whole population, not the labour force, for the unemployment rate.
- Using a simple average in the weighted price index.
- Calling a lower positive inflation rate "deflation".
- Ignoring the type of unemployment when judging policy priorities.
