## Specification in brief

- Addition polymers form from **alkenes and substituted alkenes**.
- **Repeating unit**: from a monomer, and from a chain section; **monomer** from a chain section.
- **IUPAC names** for addition polymers.
- Addition polymers are **unreactive**: explain why.
- **Intermolecular forces** between polyalkene chains.
- Understanding of polymer production and properties **developed over time**.
- **PVC**: typical uses; effect of a **plasticiser**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monomer | Small molecule that joins with others to form a polymer; here an alkene or substituted alkene |
| Addition polymerisation | Monomers join as their C=C bonds open; the polymer is the only product |
| Repeating unit | The smallest section of the chain that repeats; for addition polymers, two backbone carbons |
| Substituted alkene | Alkene in which one or more H atoms are replaced by another atom or group |
| Extension bonds | Bonds drawn through the brackets showing the chain continues |
| Plasticiser | Small molecules mixed into a polymer that push chains apart and make it flexible |
| Van der Waals forces | Induced dipole–dipole forces between molecules |
| Permanent dipole–dipole forces | Attractions between polar bonds on neighbouring molecules |

## Recall: drawing and deducing

**Monomer → repeating unit**

1. C=C becomes C–C.
2. Substituents stay on their own carbons.
3. Extension bonds, brackets, n.

**Chain → repeating unit → monomer**

1. Find the two-carbon repeat.
2. Restore C=C to get the monomer.

| Monomer | Repeating unit | Polymer name |
|---|---|---|
| CH₂=CHCH₃ | –[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]– | poly(propene) |
| CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | –[CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)]– | poly(but-2-ene) |
| CF₂=CF₂ | –[CF₂–CF₂]– | poly(tetrafluoroethene) |
| C₆H₅CH=CH₂ | –[CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)]– | poly(phenylethene) |
| CH₂=CCl₂ | –[CH₂–CCl₂]– | poly(1,1-dichloroethene) |
| CH₃CH=CHCl | –[CH(CH₃)–CHCl]– | poly(1-chloroprop-1-ene) |

**Chain-length calculation:** number of monomer units = Mr(polymer) ÷ Mr(monomer). Example: poly(chloroethene) of Mr 1.25 × 10⁵ ÷ 62.5 = 2000 units.

## Recall: properties

**Unreactive because**

- the backbone is saturated (no C=C to attack);
- C–C and C–H bonds are strong and almost non-polar.

**Forces between chains**

| Polymer | Forces between chains | Why |
|---|---|---|
| poly(ethene), poly(propene) | van der Waals only | non-polar chains; very many electrons and large contact area, so total force is large |
| PVC | van der Waals + permanent dipole–dipole | polar C–Cl bonds |
| branched vs unbranched poly(ethene) | weaker for branched | branched chains pack less closely |

**Developed over time (poly(ethene))**

- 1933, ICI: high-pressure process → branched, low-density poly(ethene) (films, bags).
- 1950s, Ziegler and Natta catalysts → few branches, high-density poly(ethene) (bottles, pipes); stronger forces, stiffer.

**PVC**

- Rigid (unplasticised): window frames, pipes.
- Plasticised: cable insulation, flooring, waterproof clothing.
- Plasticiser molecules sit between chains → chains further apart → weaker forces → chains slide → flexible.

### Worked reminders: chain to monomer

- –CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)–CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)–: repeat –CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)–, monomer **CH₂=C(CH₃)COOCH₃**, methyl 2-methylpropenoate. Both side groups stay on the same backbone carbon.
- –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)– has a two-carbon repeat –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–, so the monomer is **but-2-ene**, not propene. Propene would give –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–, with alternating CH₂ and CH(CH₃) groups.
- –CHF–CHF–CHF–CHF–: repeat –CHF–CHF–, monomer CHF=CHF, 1,2-difluoroethene; polymer poly(1,2-difluoroethene).

Always count how many backbone carbons the pattern spans before restoring C=C; if the pattern spans four carbons, check whether it is really two identical two-carbon units.


### Worked reminder: one data question, three answers

A poly(chloroethene) sample has average Mr 1.25 × 10⁵. Monomer Mr = 2(12.0) + 3(1.0) + 35.5 = 62.5. Units per chain = 1.25 × 10⁵ ÷ 62.5 = 2000. Number of backbone carbons per chain = 2 × 2000 = 4000. Number of chlorine atoms per chain = 2000 (one per repeating unit).

## Exam traps

- No C=C in the repeating unit.
- But-2-ene's repeating unit has **two** backbone carbons with a CH₃ on each.
- Unreactivity is about **bonds in the chain**, not intermolecular forces.
- Plasticisers weaken **forces between chains**, not covalent bonds.
- Name with brackets: poly(propene).
- The repeating unit has the **same formula** as the monomer.

## Self-test

1. What is the only product of addition polymerisation?
2. Draw the repeating unit of poly(tetrafluoroethene).
3. Give the monomer of –CH₂–C(CH₃)₂–CH₂–C(CH₃)₂–.
4. Name the polymer in question 3.
5. Give two reasons why poly(propene) is unreactive.
6. Name the forces between poly(ethene) chains.
7. Why are these forces strong overall even though each is weak?
8. What extra force acts between PVC chains?
9. Explain how a plasticiser makes PVC flexible.
10. Give one use each of rigid and plasticised PVC.
11. Why is high-density poly(ethene) stiffer than low-density poly(ethene)?
12. A poly(but-2-ene) sample has average Mr 1.12 × 10⁵. How many monomer units are in an average chain?

### Answers

1. **The polymer** (no other product forms).
2. **–[CF₂–CF₂]–** with extension bonds and n.
3. **2-Methylpropene**, CH₂=C(CH₃)₂.
4. **Poly(2-methylpropene).**
5. The chain is **saturated** (no C=C); its C–C and C–H bonds are **strong and almost non-polar**.
6. **Van der Waals** (induced dipole–dipole) forces.
7. Each chain has **very many electrons** and a **large contact area** with neighbours.
8. **Permanent dipole–dipole** forces (from polar C–Cl bonds).
9. Plasticiser molecules **fit between chains**, pushing them apart and **weakening the forces between chains**, so chains **slide** past each other.
10. Rigid: **window frames or pipes**; plasticised: **cable insulation, flooring or waterproof clothing**.
11. Its chains have **few branches**, so they **pack closely**, giving **stronger van der Waals forces** between chains.
12. Mr(C₄H₈) = 56.0; 1.12 × 10⁵ ÷ 56.0 = **2000**.
