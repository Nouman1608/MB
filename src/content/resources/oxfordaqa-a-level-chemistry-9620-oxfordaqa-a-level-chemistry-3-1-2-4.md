It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1, drawn on by Unit 2 (especially in organic chemistry) and available to the synoptic Unit 5. The method is short and reliable once learned, but marks are routinely lost through rounding at the wrong moment.

## What the specification asks for

- Empirical formula is the simplest whole number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
- Molecular formula is the actual number of atoms of each element in a compound.
- The relationship between empirical formula and molecular formula.
- You should be able to calculate empirical formula from data giving composition by mass or percentage by mass, and calculate molecular formula from the empirical formula and relative molecular mass.

## The two formulae and how they are related

| Compound | Molecular formula | Empirical formula | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | CH₃ | 2 |
| Benzene | C₆H₆ | CH | 6 |
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O | 6 |
| Water | H₂O | H₂O | 1 |
| Dinitrogen tetroxide | N₂O₄ | NO₂ | 2 |

The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. That multiplier is Mr ÷ (mass of one empirical formula unit).

For ionic compounds and other giant structures there are no molecules, so the formula used, such as NaCl or SiO₂, is an empirical formula.

## The method

1. Write down the mass (or percentage) of each element.
2. Divide each by its Ar to get a mole ratio.
3. Divide every result by the smallest.
4. If the results are not all close to whole numbers, multiply them all by the same small integer.
5. Write the formula.

For a percentage, treat it as the mass in grams in a 100 g sample. Keep at least three significant figures in step 2 and step 3.

### Recognising awkward ratios

| Decimal after step 3 | Multiply all by |
|---|---|
| about 0.5 (for example 1.50) | 2 |
| about 0.33 or 0.67 (for example 1.33 or 1.67) | 3 |
| about 0.25 or 0.75 (for example 1.25 or 1.75) | 4 |
| about 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 or 0.8 | 5 |

A value such as 1.98 or 3.03 is a whole number with experimental error; a value such as 1.50 or 1.33 is not, and must not be rounded.

## Worked example 1: percentage data and a molecular formula

**Question.** A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Its Mr is 180.0. Find its empirical and molecular formulae. (Ar: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0)

**Method.**

| | C | H | O |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass in 100 g | 40.0 | 6.7 | 53.3 |
| ÷ Ar | 3.33 | 6.70 | 3.33 |
| ÷ smallest | 1.00 | 2.01 | 1.00 |

Empirical formula **CH₂O**; empirical mass = 12.0 + 2.0 + 16.0 = 30.0. Multiplier = 180.0 ÷ 30.0 = 6, so molecular formula **C₆H₁₂O₆**.

## Worked example 2: a ratio that is not whole

**Question.** An oxide of iron contains 70.0% Fe by mass. Find its empirical formula. (Ar: O 16.0, Fe 55.8)

**Method.** Oxygen is 100 − 70.0 = 30.0%.

- Fe: 70.0 ÷ 55.8 = 1.25
- O: 30.0 ÷ 16.0 = 1.875
- Divide by the smaller value: Fe 1.00, O 1.49

The 1.49 is 1.5 within experimental error; it cannot be rounded to 1. Multiply both by 2: Fe 2, O 3, so the empirical formula is **Fe₂O₃**.

## Worked example 3: combustion data

Organic compounds are often analysed by burning them completely. All the carbon ends up in CO₂ and all the hydrogen in H₂O. Any oxygen in the compound is found by difference.

**Question.** Complete combustion of 0.600 g of a compound containing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen gives 1.32 g of CO₂ and 0.720 g of H₂O. Its Mr is 60.0. Find the molecular formula.

**Method.**

1. n(C) = n(CO₂) = 1.32 ÷ 44.0 = 0.0300 mol; mass of C = 0.0300 × 12.0 = 0.360 g
2. n(H) = 2 × n(H₂O) = 2 × (0.720 ÷ 18.0) = 0.0800 mol; mass of H = 0.0800 g
3. Mass of O = 0.600 − 0.360 − 0.0800 = 0.160 g, so n(O) = 0.160 ÷ 16.0 = 0.0100 mol
4. Ratio C : H : O = 0.0300 : 0.0800 : 0.0100 = 3 : 8 : 1

Empirical formula C₃H₈O, empirical mass 60.0, so the molecular formula is also **C₃H₈O**.

The two most common errors are forgetting the factor of 2 in step 2 (each H₂O has two H atoms) and forgetting to look for oxygen by difference.

## Where the Mr comes from

To turn an empirical formula into a molecular formula you need Mr, which a question may supply directly or expect you to obtain:

- from a mass spectrum, using the molecular ion peak (subtopic 3.1.1.2)
- from gas data, using pV = nRT and Mr = m ÷ n (subtopic 3.1.2.3).

## Composition by mass from experiments

Empirical formula data can also come from direct measurement, for example heating a metal in air and weighing the oxide formed. The mass of oxygen is the mass gained. If 1.20 g of magnesium gives 2.00 g of oxide, the oxygen is 0.80 g: Mg 1.20 ÷ 24.3 = 0.0494 mol, O 0.80 ÷ 16.0 = 0.0500 mol, a 1 : 1 ratio, so MgO.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing percentages by atomic number instead of Ar.
- Rounding 1.5 to 2 or 1.33 to 1, rather than multiplying through.
- Rounding the mole values to one significant figure before dividing by the smallest.
- Using the Mr of O₂ (32.0) for oxygen atoms in step 2.
- In combustion questions, using n(H) = n(H₂O), or forgetting oxygen in the compound.
- Giving the molecular formula as the empirical formula without checking Mr.
- Writing a molecular formula with the elements in an odd order; for organic compounds use C, then H, then other elements.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Calculate** or **Determine**: set the working out in a table with a row for each step, so every division is visible.
- **Deduce**: when Mr is given, show the empirical formula mass and the division that gives the multiplier.
- **Show that**: give the ratio to two decimal places before converting to whole numbers.
- **Suggest**: if asked for a possible structure, use the molecular formula and what you know of functional groups.
