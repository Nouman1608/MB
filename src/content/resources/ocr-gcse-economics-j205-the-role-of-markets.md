This guide covers section 2.1, The role of markets, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. It sits under "The role of markets and money" in component J205/01. The specification is not tiered. Every business, place and figure is invented.

The [markets, specialisation, demand and elasticity guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-markets-sectors-specialisation-and-demand-with-price/) introduces 2.1 together with demand. This page goes further on 2.1 alone: types of market, classifying firms by sector along a chain of production, tracing links between factor and product markets, and building an evaluation of specialisation for each group the specification names. Condense it with the [2.1 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-role-of-markets-revision-notes/) and practise with the [2.1 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-the-role-of-markets-practice/).

## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain what is meant by a market;
- explain the features of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors, including the difference between the production of goods and services;
- explain the difference between factor and product markets, including their interdependence;
- evaluate the costs and benefits of specialisation and exchange in markets, including for producers, workers, regions and countries.

## What a market is

A **market** is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to exchange goods, services or factors of production. Buyers and sellers do not need to meet in person.

| Type | Example (invented where named) |
|---|---|
| Physical, local | A Saturday food market in a town square |
| Online | An app where people sell second-hand bikes |
| National | The market for new cars across the whole country |
| Global | The market for crude oil, traded between countries |
| Factor | Employers recruiting electricians in a city |

In every market, the decisions of buyers (demand) and sellers (supply) interact to set a price and a quantity traded. That is why markets are central to answering the economic problem from 1.2.

## The three sectors along a chain of production

| Sector | Feature | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracts or grows natural resources | Raw materials: crops, fish, timber, minerals |
| Secondary | Manufactures or builds, turning materials into finished goods | Goods: clothing, cars, houses |
| Tertiary | Provides services | Services: retail, banking, transport, education |

**Goods** are tangible: you can touch and store them, and they can be made before they are sold. **Services** are intangible: they are usually produced and consumed at the same time, such as a haircut or a bus journey, so they cannot be stockpiled.

**Worked example: a T-shirt (invented figures).** Follow one T-shirt that sells for £12.00.

| Stage | Sector | Sale price (£) | Value added at this stage (£) | Share of final price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cotton farm | Primary | 0.80 | 0.80 | 0.80 ÷ 12 × 100 = 6.7% |
| Factory makes the T-shirt | Secondary | 3.20 | 3.20 − 0.80 = 2.40 | 2.40 ÷ 12 × 100 = 20.0% |
| Shop sells it | Tertiary | 12.00 | 12.00 − 3.20 = 8.80 | 8.80 ÷ 12 × 100 = 73.3% |

The tertiary stage adds the largest share in this example because retail includes rent, staff, marketing and transport. The example also shows that the sectors depend on each other: the shop has nothing to sell without the factory, and the factory has no customer without the shop.

**Classifying tricky cases.** Classify by the **activity**, not the firm. A farm that grows strawberries is primary; if it also runs a farm shop, the shop is tertiary. A mining company's drilling is primary, but its head-office accountants provide a service. In the exam, say which activity you are classifying.

## Factor markets and product markets

- **Product markets** are where goods and services are bought and sold: households buy, firms sell.
- **Factor markets** are where factors of production are bought and sold, such as labour markets and markets for land or machinery: firms buy, households sell their labour.

The two are **interdependent** because the income earned in factor markets is spent in product markets, and firms' demand for factors depends on what they can sell.

**Worked chain: a fall in demand for cars.**

1. *Product market:* consumers buy fewer new cars, so car makers' sales fall.
2. *Factor market:* car makers need fewer workers and less steel, so demand for car workers falls; jobs and overtime are cut.
3. *Back to product markets:* those workers have less income, so they spend less on other goods and services.
4. *Further factor-market effect:* firms selling those goods and services need fewer workers too.

The demand for a factor is linked to demand for the product it helps to make, so a change in one market spreads to the other. In an exam, name both markets and the link between them.

## Specialisation and exchange

**Specialisation** means concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services, or on one task. It only works with **exchange**: a specialist must trade what it makes for what it needs, and money makes this exchange far easier than swapping goods directly.

The specification asks you to evaluate specialisation for four groups. Build a table like this before writing.

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Higher output per worker; lower cost per unit; firms build expertise and a reputation | Dependence on one product; if demand falls, sales collapse; a break in one stage stops the whole process |
| Workers | Become skilled and quick at one task; may earn more as specialists | Repetitive, boring work; narrow skills make it harder to find a new job if the role disappears |
| Regions | Firms cluster near suppliers and skilled workers; the region becomes known for an industry | High risk if that industry declines: many jobs lost at once |
| Countries | Can produce more in total and trade for what they do not make; consumers get wider choice | Dependence on imports of some goods; vulnerability to falling world prices for their main export |

**Worked example: a specialised town (invented).** Caldbridge has 30,000 jobs, and 12,000 of them are at one car plant: 12,000 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **40%**. The plant cuts 3,000 jobs and its local suppliers cut a further 1,500, so 4,500 jobs go, which is 4,500 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **15%** of all jobs in the town. Spending in local shops falls too.

This is the regional cost of specialisation in figures. A real historical case is the decline of coal mining in parts of the UK in the 1980s, when mining areas faced high unemployment because so many jobs depended on one industry.

### Building the evaluation

A balanced judgement on specialisation usually depends on:

- **how varied the demand is:** specialising in a product with stable demand is less risky;
- **how easily workers can retrain** if their job disappears;
- **whether the gains are shared:** lower prices for consumers, or only higher profits for producers;
- **the time scale:** gains in output come quickly; costs of over-dependence may appear years later.

A model conclusion: "Specialisation raises output and lowers costs for producers and countries, so it is beneficial overall. However, the costs fall heavily on workers and regions that rely on a single industry. The benefit is greatest where workers can retrain and the economy has other industries to absorb job losses."

## Common mistakes

- Defining a market as a physical place only.
- Classifying a whole firm when the question asks about a particular activity.
- Saying services are "not real products": they are output, just intangible.
- Mixing up factor and product markets, or describing them without the link between them.
- Evaluating specialisation for producers only when the question names workers, regions or countries.
- Ignoring exchange: specialists depend on trade and money.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the features of a sector:** say what it does and give an example from the context.
- **Factor and product markets:** use one chain with at least three linked steps.
- **Evaluate specialisation:** one benefit and one cost for the group named, then a judgement with a condition.
- Use figures when given: the share of jobs or of the final price is quick application.
