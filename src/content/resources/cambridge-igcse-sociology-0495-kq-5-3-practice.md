These questions practise key question 5.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What factors help to explain differences in educational achievement and experience?* Section A uses short-answer styles; Section B uses the longer Paper 2 styles.

---

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *compensatory education*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *ethnocentric curriculum*. **[2]**

**3.** Define the term *restricted code*. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** reasons for differences in educational achievement between countries. **[2]**

**5.** Give **two** ways in which teachers' expectations may affect pupils. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **two** patterns of educational achievement or experience by gender. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain **three** cultural factors that may affect the educational achievement of working-class pupils. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **three** in-school factors that may affect the educational experience of ethnic minority pupils. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **three** reasons why some boys may underachieve at school. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss the reasons for global differences in educational achievement.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss the strengths and limitations of labelling theory as an explanation of differences in educational achievement.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that cultural factors are the main cause of ethnic differences in educational achievement.

Your answer should include:
- at least three arguments for the view and three arguments against the view
- a conclusion. **[14]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** Extra educational provision or support (1) designed to make up for disadvantages children face at home or in their community, such as Head Start or Sure Start (1).

**2. [2]** A curriculum that reflects the knowledge, history and values of one culture, usually the majority culture (1), so that minority pupils' cultures are ignored or presented negatively (1).

**3. [2]** Bernstein's term for a form of speech with short, simple sentences and a limited vocabulary (1), which relies on shared context and is less suited to the explicit language schools use (1).

**4. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: availability of schools; poverty and school fees; access to education for girls; class sizes; norms and values about education; access to the internet and digital resources.

**5. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: pupils may be placed in higher or lower sets or exam tiers; pupils may live up or down to the label (self-fulfilling prophecy); some pupils may be disciplined more often; pupils may receive more or less attention and encouragement.

**6. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: girls outperform boys in most subjects at school level in many countries; boys are more likely to choose physics, engineering or computing; girls in some societies have less access to schooling; boys are more likely to be excluded from school.

### Section B

**7. [6]** Three factors, 1 for identifying and 1 for developing each:
- **Cultural capital**: middle-class children bring knowledge and attitudes schools reward, so working-class children may feel less at ease (Bourdieu).
- **Linguistic factors**: the restricted code is less suited to the elaborated code of textbooks and exams (Bernstein).
- **Parental interest and expectations**: Douglas linked parental interest to achievement; parents with less experience of education may be less able to support homework or university applications.
- **Immediate gratification**: some argue working-class young people are encouraged to seek earnings now rather than invest in long education. (Credit with evaluation that this may reflect material need rather than values.)
- **Community norms**: in areas where few people go to university, it may seem less realistic.

**8. [6]** Three factors, 1 + 1 each:
- **Labelling and teacher expectations**: stereotypes may lead to lower expectations and more discipline for some groups.
- **Institutional racism**: Gillborn and Youdell found Black pupils more likely to be entered for lower-tier exams.
- **Ethnocentric curriculum**: lessons centred on the majority culture can make minority pupils feel excluded.
- **Role models**: few teachers from the pupil's ethnic group can lower aspirations.
- **Ethnic subcultures**: pupils respond in different ways, for example Fuller's pro-education Black girls, or the range of responses among Black boys described by Sewell.

**9. [6]** Three reasons, 1 + 1 each:
- **Gendered subcultures**: Mac an Ghaill's macho lads saw schoolwork as unmasculine.
- **Socialisation**: boys may be given more freedom and spend less time reading, which suits school less well.
- **Teacher expectations**: teachers may expect less from boys or tolerate disruption, lowering standards.
- **Peer pressure and social control**: boys who work hard may be teased, so effort is hidden or avoided.
- **Changing job market**: the decline of traditional manual jobs may leave some working-class boys unsure what school is for.

**10. [8]** Levels: 7–8 good knowledge, three developed points with evidence, terms used well; 4–6 some knowledge, most points supported, some undeveloped; 1–3 limited, descriptive.
Indicative content:
- **Availability of schools**: in rural areas of low-income countries, long journeys reduce attendance, especially at secondary level.
- **Poverty**: fees, uniforms and lost income from child labour keep children out of school.
- **Girls' access**: early marriage, household duties and safety concerns reduce girls' schooling in some societies.
- **Class sizes**: very large classes limit individual attention and practical work.
- **Norms and values**: beliefs about the value of education and about gender roles.
- **Digital divide**: limited internet access restricts online resources.
- Evaluation: inequalities within countries can be as large as those between them; government policy can raise achievement even where incomes are low.

**11. [8]** Levels as Question 10. Indicative content:
- **Strengths**: shows how school processes, not just home background, create differences; Becker's ideal pupil and Rosenthal and Jacobson's study of teacher expectations show labels affect outcomes; explains self-fulfilling prophecies and anti-school subcultures (Hargreaves).
- **Limitations**: deterministic, since pupils can reject labels (Fuller's Black girls); ignores structural factors such as poverty and class inequality; hard to show that labels cause achievement rather than reflect it; does not explain where teachers' stereotypes come from.
- A balanced answer concludes on how far labelling explains differences.

**12. [14]** Levels: 12–14 excellent knowledge, three developed and evidenced points on each side, balanced, with a conclusion; 8–11 good knowledge, developed points on both sides but less balance; 4–7 some knowledge, simple two-sided or one-sided answer; 1–3 limited and descriptive. A one-sided answer cannot score more than 6.
*For the view:*
- Strong family expectations of education in some groups support high achievement.
- Linguistic factors: pupils learning in a second language may fall behind at first.
- Home and community factors, such as support with homework and community-run classes.
- Peer cultures: some subcultures value education, others reject it.
*Against the view:*
- Material factors: higher poverty rates in some ethnic groups limit resources; class may explain more than culture.
- Institutional racism: Gillborn and Youdell's findings on exam tiers; Coard's earlier work on placement.
- Labelling and teacher expectations lead to more discipline and lower expectations for some groups.
- Ethnocentric curriculum and lack of role models alienate minority pupils.
- Cultural explanations can stereotype and blame families, ignoring variation within groups by class and gender.
*Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that cultural factors play some part but interact with material disadvantage and in-school processes, so they are not the main cause on their own.
