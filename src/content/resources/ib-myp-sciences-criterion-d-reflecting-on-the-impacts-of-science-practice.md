> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** for revision and practice on Criterion D. They are **not** reproduced IB material and they do not copy the structure or mark tariffs of any on-screen examination. Real MYP work is judged against criterion level descriptors (achievement levels 1-8), so the `[n]` marks below are a revision aid, not IB marks.

## Questions

**1.** (Criterion D) Name the factor that the MYP Sciences guide defines as "relates to government or public affairs". **[1]**

**2.** (Criterion D) Identify the main factor (moral, ethical, social, economic, political, cultural or environmental) in each statement.

**(a)** A town argues about whether it is fair to test a new medicine on children. **[1]**
**(b)** A factory's waste is raising the acidity of a river. **[1]**
**(c)** A new law requires all plastic bottles to carry a deposit. **[1]**

**3.** (Criterion D) Read this case.

> *Case: the island of Tamarind Cay.* A disease is spread to people by mosquitoes. Scientists propose releasing large numbers of male mosquitoes that have been made sterile by radiation. The sterile males mate with wild females. The eggs laid by those females do not hatch. The programme is repeated every week for two years. Local fishers and hotel owners are worried that the programme will change the island's wildlife and its tourism.

**(a)** Explain how the release of sterile males reduces the number of mosquitoes. **[3]**
**(b)** Discuss one social implication of the programme. **[3]**
**(c)** Discuss one environmental implication of the programme. **[3]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether the programme should go ahead, and reach a judgment. **[5]**

**4.** (Criterion D) Rewrite this sentence using precise scientific language: "The plants take in the gas from the air and make food using the sun." **[2]**

**5.** (Criterion D)

**(a)** A student writes: "According to the internet, plastic takes 500 years to rot." Explain why this is not a satisfactory way to use a source. **[2]**
**(b)** Write a reference-list entry in a recognised style for this book: author L. Chen, published in 2022, title *Plastics in the sea*, published by Bluewave Books in Lisbon. **[3]**

**6.** (Criterion D) Source 1 is an unsigned blog saying that a fertiliser is "totally safe". Source 2 is a report on the fertiliser from a national environment agency, with data and methods. Compare the reliability of the two sources. **[3]**

**7.** (Criterion D) Discuss and evaluate the use of lithium-ion batteries in electric cars, interacting with the environmental factor. Include how the science is applied, at least two implications, a judgment and the use of scientific language. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Political [1]. **[1]**

**2. (a)** Ethical (or moral) [1].
**(b)** Environmental [1].
**(c)** Political (a law) or economic (a cost to consumers) [1]. **[3]**
*Marking note:* Accept moral for (a), and either political or economic for (c), if it is given a clear reason. The point is to identify the factor from the wording.

**3. (a)** The sterile males mate with wild females [1]. The females lay eggs that do not hatch, so no offspring are produced from those matings [1]. Repeated releases mean that a large proportion of matings are infertile, so the population falls over time [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Any one developed social implication: fewer mosquitoes means fewer cases of the disease, which improves health and reduces absence from work and school [1]. Cause and effect (fewer bites, less transmission) are linked [1]. Who gains or loses is stated, for example families on the island gain, or some residents may distrust the release and refuse the programme [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Any one developed environmental implication: the mosquitoes are food for animals such as bats, birds and fish larvae [1]. If their numbers fall, predators could lose food and populations might change [1]. The effect may be small if other insects replace them, or if only one species is targeted, so the scale of the effect is uncertain [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** Award up to five marks.
- A benefit with evidence (reduced disease) [1].
- A risk or cost (effect on food webs, cost of weekly releases, tourism worry) [1].
- A comparison with an alternative (insecticide spraying, nets, draining standing water) [1].
- A judgment that follows from the weighing [1].
- A condition or further evidence needed (for example monitoring of other species, a trial on a small area first, public information for residents) [1].
**[5]**

**4.** For example: "Plants absorb carbon dioxide from the air [1] and use light energy to make glucose by photosynthesis [1]." **[2]**
*Marking note:* One mark for a precise name for the gas or for the energy and process; the second for the product.

**5. (a)** "The internet" is not a source. The student has not said who wrote it, when or why, so the reader cannot check it [1]. The claim is also not credited to an author or organisation with a reference in the text and in a list [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For example: Chen, L. (2022) *Plastics in the sea*. Lisbon: Bluewave Books. Author [1], year [1], title and publisher or place [1]. **[3]**
*Marking note:* Any recognised style is accepted if it is complete and used consistently.

**6.** Source 2 is likely to be more reliable, because it is written by a named national body that has expertise and is responsible for accuracy [1]. It gives data and methods, so the claim can be checked [1]. Source 1 has no named author, gives no evidence and uses the extreme word "totally", which suggests it may be advertising or opinion [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Award up to eight marks.
- Science applied: a lithium-ion battery stores energy chemically. When it discharges, lithium ions move between the electrodes through an electrolyte, and electrons flow through the external circuit to the motor [1]. It is rechargeable because the process can be reversed [1].
- Implication 1: an electric car produces no exhaust gases at the point of use, so it can improve local air quality in cities [1].
- Implication 2: the electricity may come from burning fossil fuels, so the total carbon dioxide emitted depends on how the electricity is generated [1].
- Implication 3: mining the metals for the batteries uses water and land and can damage habitats [1]. Recycling at the end of the battery's life can reduce the need for new mining [1].
- Evaluation: weighs benefits (no exhaust, lower emissions with renewable electricity) against harms (mining, disposal), and compares with petrol cars or public transport [1].
- Judgment with condition: for example, electric cars are a better environmental choice if the electricity is largely renewable and the batteries are recycled [1].
- Scientific language: terms such as electrolyte, electrodes, ions, emissions, renewable are used correctly [1], and a source is cited in a recognised style (credit this mark where a citation is shown) [1].
Award a maximum of 8. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing what happened without explaining the science.
- Choosing a factor and then discussing a different one.
- Giving only benefits, or only harms.
- Opinions such as "it is bad for the planet" with no cause and effect.
- Using everyday words ("the gas", "the sun") where terms are needed.
- A reference with missing author, year or title.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I choose the factor?**
Choose the one that fits the issue best and that you can develop with cause and effect. You may link it with another.

**What if I disagree with the judgment in the answers?**
A different judgment is acceptable if it follows from the weighing you have shown and is supported by the science.

**Is the number of implications important?**
Depth matters more than number. Two developed implications are better than five single-line points.
