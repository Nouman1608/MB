This guide covers **Topic 1 Commerce and Production**, the first of
six topics in Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100), for examination
2026-2028. This O Level shares an identical six-topic structure with
sibling Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715).

## Where this fits in 7100

Topic 1 introduces the concepts of commerce and production, including
global supply chains, trading costs and the impact of digitalisation,
before the syllabus develops these ideas further in Commercial
operations, Globalisation of trade, Logistics in commerce, Aids to
trade that support commerce, and Sustainability and ethics.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL COMMERCE (7100) — TOPIC 1 COMMERCE AND
PRODUCTION**

- 1.1 Trade and commerce — the meaning of trade and commerce and the
distinction between the two, home trade (retail and wholesale) and
international trade (exports and imports), aids to trade and logistics
in a supply chain, the different types of modern commerce
(traditional, ecommerce, mcommerce and scommerce), and how production,
trade and commerce are interdependent
- 1.2 Production — six sub-sections: sectors of production and
specialisation; in-house production and outsourced production; global
supply chains (including the domestic supply chain); intermediaries in
a supply chain (home trade: wholesaler and retailer; international
trade: agent and factor); the effects of digital technology on
production (automation/robotics, and digital communication with
suppliers such as electronic data interchange); and the costs involved
in trade, commerce and production, including simple cost calculations

## How to approach it

Because this topic rests on precise definitions, practise stating the
differences between trade and commerce, and between the four types of
modern commerce, clearly and with specific examples -- vague or
overlapping definitions are a common source of lost marks here.
Candidates take two components (a multiple-choice paper worth 30% and
a written paper worth 70%), so practise both quick, precise recall for
multiple-choice questions and longer, explanatory answers for the
written paper. Since production, trade and commerce are explicitly
taught as interdependent rather than separate activities, practise
explaining how a change in one (such as a change in production costs)
affects the others, since this kind of linked reasoning is rewarded
in the written paper.

## What commerce actually is

Commerce is everything involved in moving a product from the point of production to the point of consumption. It is not production itself — it is the distribution and the services that make distribution possible.

It divides into two branches:

- **Trade** — the buying and selling of goods, split into home trade (wholesale and retail) and foreign trade (import, export and entrepot).
- **Aids to trade** — the services without which trade could not happen: banking, transport, communication, insurance, warehousing and advertising.

## The chain of production

| Sector | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary (extractive) | Extracting raw materials from nature | mining, quarrying, fishing, farming, forestry |
| Secondary | Turning raw materials into goods, including construction | manufacturing, processing, construction |
| Tertiary | Providing services that support the other two | commercial services and direct services |
| Quaternary | Digital technology | — |

This is a closed, four-sector list. Each of the first three sectors leaves the product still unusable to the consumer in some respect. Primary output is in the wrong **form**, secondary output is in the wrong **place** and often available at the wrong **time**. Tertiary production closes those gaps, which is why commercial services sit within the tertiary sector; the syllabus adds quaternary (digital technology) as a genuinely separate fourth sector.

## Utility

Utility is the ability of a good to satisfy a want, and commerce creates three kinds:

- **Form utility** — changing a product so it becomes more useful, as when timber becomes furniture.
- **Place utility** — moving goods to where the consumer can reach them, from a distant factory to a local shop.
- **Time utility** — making goods available when they are wanted, which is what warehousing achieves by storing seasonal output.

## Specialisation and interdependence

Specialisation means concentrating on one task, product or industry. It raises output, permits mechanisation and economies of scale, and improves quality.

The syllabus requires the advantages and disadvantages of specialisation in three named contexts — **an individual, a factory and a country** — not a general list of "levels." Its costs are equally examinable: repetitive work is demotivating, craftsmanship declines, machinery displaces labour, choice narrows through standardisation, and — most importantly — everyone becomes **interdependent**. A specialised economy cannot function without exchange, which is precisely why commerce exists.

| Context | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Higher output, greater skill | Repetitive, demotivating work; narrow skill set |
| Factory | Mechanisation, economies of scale, improved quality | Heavy dependence on one product; inflexible to change |
| Country | Efficient use of resources; goods for export | Over-dependence on trade and on imports |

**The logical chain worth stating explicitly** in an extended answer: specialisation, at the individual, factory or country level, increases output, quality or efficiency, but it also means an individual, factory or country produces far more of one thing and far less of everything else it needs — creating **interdependence**. Interdependence, in turn, is exactly what makes **exchange** necessary, and commerce (trade plus its aids) is the mechanism that makes exchange possible across distance and time. Tracing this chain explicitly — specialisation → interdependence → the necessity of commerce — earns more than treating specialisation and commerce as two separate topics that happen to sit in the same syllabus section.

## Worked example

A cotton farmer in Punjab sells raw cotton to a textile mill, which weaves cloth and sells it to a wholesaler, who supplies retailers nationwide. Identify the stages and the utilities created.

```
Farmer growing cotton          -> primary production
Mill weaving cloth             -> secondary production, creates FORM utility
Wholesaler storing stock       -> tertiary, creates TIME utility
Transport to retailers         -> tertiary, creates PLACE utility
Retailer selling to consumer   -> tertiary, completes the chain
```

Naming the utility as well as the stage is usually where the extra marks sit.

## In-house and outsourced production

**In-house production** uses an enterprise's own resources; **outsourced production** pays another enterprise to do the work. In-house gives greater control over quality and information; outsourcing gives access to external expertise and can lower costs.

## Global and domestic supply chains

A **global supply chain** sources materials, components or services from other countries; a **domestic supply chain** sources them from within the same country.

## Intermediaries in a supply chain

Home trade: the **wholesaler** and the **retailer**. International trade adds the **agent** (acts on behalf of a buyer or seller without taking ownership of the goods) and the **factor** (takes possession of goods on consignment and sells them on the owner's behalf for a commission).

## Effects of digital technology on production

**Automation**, e.g. robotics in production, and digital communication with suppliers, e.g. **electronic data interchange (EDI)**.

## Costs involved in trade, commerce and production

Ten named types: advertising, bank charges, commission, customs duties, inventory, labour, packaging, transport, utilities and warehousing. Practise simple calculations from given figures — e.g. transport $1,200 + customs duties $80 + packaging $40 = **$1,320** total cost.

*Balance of Trade worked example (goods only):* exports $650m, imports $710m. Balance of Trade = $650m − $710m = **−$60m, a deficit**. Balance of Payments would add services and financial flows to this goods-only figure — the two must not be confused.

## Common mistakes

Describing commerce as including the creation of goods — commerce covers trade and aids to trade (distribution) only, though commercial services do sit within the tertiary sector of production. Placing warehousing under secondary production. Listing advantages of specialisation without the interdependence that follows from it. Substituting "worker" or "region/town" for the syllabus's actual specialisation contexts (individual, factory, country). Confusing place utility with time utility: transport creates place, storage creates time. Saying tertiary production "sells things", which misses commercial versus direct services. Forgetting the fourth (quaternary) sector.

## Quick revision checklist

- Define commerce and set out its two branches with their subdivisions.
- Describe the four sectors of production and give examples of each.
- Define and distinguish form, place and time utility.
- Explain specialisation for an individual, a factory and a country, with advantages and disadvantages.
- Explain why specialisation makes exchange, and therefore commerce, necessary.
- Distinguish in-house from outsourced production, and a global from a domestic supply chain.
- Name the home-trade and international-trade intermediaries.
