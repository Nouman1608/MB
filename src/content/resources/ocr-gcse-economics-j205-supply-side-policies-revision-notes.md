Recall notes for section 3.7 of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), examined in Component 02. The [supply side policies study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-side-policies/) gives the full explanations and worked examples; the [supply side policies practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-side-policies-practice/) test them. All figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

- Explain what is meant by **supply side policy** and how it can be used to achieve **economic objectives**.
- Evaluate the **costs**, including **opportunity cost**, and the **benefits** of supply side policies for the economy.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply side policy | Government measures to increase the economy's productive capacity |
| Productive capacity | The maximum output an economy can produce with its resources |
| Productivity | Output per worker or per hour |
| Infrastructure | Basic networks such as roads, rail, energy and broadband |
| Deregulation | Removing rules that add unnecessary costs to firms |
| Structural unemployment | Long-term mismatch of skills or location with available jobs |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up |
| Time lag | The delay before a policy has its full effect |
| Deadweight | Spending that supports activity that would have happened anyway |

## Three groups

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Labour force | Education, apprenticeships, retraining, childcare support, help to move for work |
| Capital and technology | Infrastructure, research grants, tax relief for investment |
| Incentives and markets | Lower income or corporation tax, less red tape, more competition |

## Policy → objective

| Objective | How supply side policy helps |
|---|---|
| Growth | Higher capacity lets output rise over the long run |
| Low unemployment | Training and mobility tackle **structural** unemployment |
| Price stability | Lower costs and higher productivity ease **cost-push** pressure |
| Fair distribution | Training can lift low pay; but top-rate tax cuts can widen gaps |

**Key contrast:** fiscal and monetary policy shift **demand** in the short run; supply side policy shifts **capacity** in the long run, so growth need not bring inflation.

## Chains to learn

**Retraining → unemployment:** workers gain skills firms want → they can fill vacancies in growing sectors → structural unemployment falls → output rises as idle labour is used.

**Infrastructure → growth:** faster transport and broadband → lower costs and journey times → firms produce and deliver more for the same inputs → capacity and output rise.

**Lower income tax → labour supply:** take-home pay per hour rises → work becomes more rewarding → some inactive people take jobs and others work more hours → the labour force and potential output grow.

## Worked reminders

**Payback.** A £8bn broadband programme saves firms £0.5bn a year: payback = 8 ÷ 0.5 = **16 years**.

**Training gain.** 40,000 workers each produce £3,000 more a year after retraining: extra output = **£120m** a year.

**Tax cut arithmetic.** Corporation tax cut from 30% to 25% on £200bn of profits: revenue falls from £60bn to **£50bn** unless profits grow. To keep £60bn, profits must reach 60 ÷ 0.25 = **£240bn**, up 20%.

## Benefits and costs

| Benefits | Costs and limits |
|---|---|
| Lasting rise in potential output | Opportunity cost of spending or lost tax revenue |
| Growth without extra inflation | Long time lags |
| Lower structural unemployment | Uncertain results: wrong skills, firms may not invest |
| Better competitiveness | May widen inequality or weaken protections |
| | Deadweight: some money supports what would happen anyway |

**Judge by:** matching the policy to the problem, the time frame, the cost, and who gains.

## Exam traps

- Supply side policy is about the **whole economy's** capacity, not one market's supply curve.
- It is **slow**: do not claim quick effects.
- Always state an **opportunity cost** when costs are asked for.
- Not every supply side policy reduces inequality.
- A subsidy to correct an externality belongs to section 3.8, not here.
- Demand-side policy suits cyclical unemployment; supply side suits structural.

## Self-test

1. Define supply side policy.
2. Give one example of a supply side policy from each of the three groups.
3. Explain why supply side policy can raise growth without raising inflation.
4. Which type of unemployment is supply side policy best suited to reduce?
5. A £8bn broadband programme saves firms £0.5bn a year. What is the payback period?
6. A cut in income tax costs the government £6bn a year. State one opportunity cost.
7. Retraining raises the output of 40,000 workers by £3,000 each per year. Calculate the extra output.
8. Corporation tax falls from 30% to 25% on £200bn of profits. How much revenue is lost if profits do not change?
9. Give one reason a supply side policy may fail.
10. Explain how one supply side policy could widen inequality.
11. Explain how childcare support can increase productive capacity.
12. State one difference between supply side policy and fiscal policy used to boost demand.

### Answers

1. Government measures to **increase the productive capacity** of the economy.
2. For example: **retraining** (labour force); **rail upgrade** (capital and technology); **lower corporation tax** (incentives and markets).
3. Output rises because **capacity grows**, not because spending outruns what firms can produce, so there is less pressure on prices.
4. **Structural** unemployment.
5. 8 ÷ 0.5 = **16 years**.
6. For example, the £6bn could have funded **schools, hospitals or infrastructure**.
7. 40,000 × £3,000 = **£120m**.
8. £60bn − £50bn = **£10bn**.
9. Any one: **time lags**; training does not match firms' needs; firms do not invest after a tax cut; projects overrun.
10. A cut in the **top rate of income tax** gives the largest gains to high earners (or deregulation may weaken workers' protections).
11. Parents who were **economically inactive** can take jobs, so the **labour force** and potential output rise.
12. Supply side policy raises **capacity over the long run**; demand-boosting fiscal policy raises **spending in the short run**.
