This guide covers **3.1 Plate tectonics** in Topic 3, Rocks and weathering, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content is:

- **Nature of tectonic plates and their global patterns.**
- **Types of plate boundaries:** divergent (constructive), conservative and convergent (destructive).
- **Processes and associated landforms:** sea floor spreading, subduction, fold mountain building, ocean ridges, ocean trenches and volcanic island arcs.

It is examined in Paper 1, usually with a world map of plates or a cross-section of a boundary. The [3.1 plate tectonics revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-plate-tectonics-revision-notes/) list the layers, the boundary types with named examples and the evidence for plate movement. This guide explains the processes in more depth and works through calculations using sea floor data. Note that tectonic **hazards** belong to the A Level option Hazardous environments, not to 3.1; at AS Level the focus is processes and landforms.

## The nature of tectonic plates

A tectonic plate is a rigid slab of **lithosphere**: the crust together with the uppermost, rigid part of the mantle. Plates move over the **asthenosphere**, a hotter, weaker layer of the upper mantle that can flow very slowly. A plate is therefore not the same thing as the crust, and many plates carry both oceanic and continental crust.

The two types of crust behave differently:

| | Oceanic crust | Continental crust |
|---|---|---|
| Main rock | Basalt (denser) | Granite-type rocks (less dense) |
| Thickness | Thin, mostly about 5 to 10 km | Thick, mostly about 30 to 70 km |
| Age | Young; generally less than about 200 million years | Old; parts are several billion years old |
| Behaviour at convergent boundaries | Dense enough to be subducted | Too buoyant to be subducted in bulk |

The age difference follows directly from sea floor spreading and subduction: oceanic crust is constantly created at ridges and destroyed at trenches, while continental crust survives.

## Global patterns

There are about seven large plates (Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian, sometimes split into Indian and Australian, and Antarctic) and many smaller ones such as the Nazca, Philippine Sea and Juan de Fuca plates. Three patterns are worth describing on a map:

- **Ocean ridges** run through the middle of the Atlantic and Indian Oceans and along the eastern Pacific, forming a connected system tens of thousands of kilometres long.
- **Trenches and volcanic arcs** ring much of the Pacific Ocean, from the Andes through Alaska, Japan and the Philippines to New Zealand.
- **Fold mountain belts** stretch from the Alps to the Himalayas, where continents have collided.

## Why plates move

Older textbooks explain movement mainly by **convection currents** in the mantle dragging plates along. Geologists now place more weight on forces at the plate edges:

- **Slab pull:** cold, dense oceanic lithosphere sinking at a subduction zone pulls the rest of the plate behind it. This is thought to be the strongest force, which is why plates with long subducting edges, such as the Pacific plate, move fastest.
- **Ridge push:** new lithosphere at a ridge is hot and stands high; as it cools and thickens it slides away from the ridge under gravity.

Mantle convection still provides the heat that drives the system. In an answer, mention both views and avoid claiming that convection alone "carries" the plates.

## Divergent (constructive) boundaries and sea floor spreading

At a divergent boundary, plates move apart. Decompression of hot mantle rock beneath the boundary causes partial melting, and basaltic magma rises to fill the gap, forming new oceanic crust. This is **sea floor spreading**. Repeated intrusion and eruption build an **ocean ridge**: a broad, raised submarine mountain range with a central rift valley, cut across by transform faults. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level in Iceland. Where divergence starts within a continent, a rift valley forms, as in East Africa.

The key evidence comes from **palaeomagnetism**. As new basalt cools, iron-rich minerals align with the Earth's magnetic field. The field has reversed many times, so the sea floor carries **symmetrical stripes** of normal and reversed polarity on either side of a ridge. Together with the increase in the age of the sea floor away from the ridge, the stripes show that new crust is created at the ridge and carried outwards.

## Worked example 1: calculating a spreading rate

*Basalt 60 km from the axis of a fictional ridge has been dated at 3 million years old. Calculate the rate of movement of the plate away from the ridge.*

60 km in 3 million years = 20 km per million years.
20 km = 2 000 000 cm, so 2 000 000 cm / 1 000 000 years = **2 cm per year**.

This is the rate on one side (the half-rate). If the other plate moves away at the same speed, the ocean widens by **4 cm per year**.

## Worked example 2: how long to open an ocean?

*An ocean has widened at an average of 2.5 cm per year and is now 5000 km wide. Estimate how long it has been opening.*

5000 km = 500 000 000 cm.
500 000 000 / 2.5 = **200 million years**.

Calculations like this show how slow annual rates add up to ocean-sized changes over geological time.

## Convergent (destructive) boundaries

**Oceanic-continental convergence:** the denser oceanic plate is **subducted** beneath the continental plate. Where it bends down, a deep **ocean trench** forms. As the slab sinks it is heated and releases water into the mantle above, lowering its melting point; magma rises to form a chain of volcanoes on the continent. Sediments and crust are compressed and uplifted into **fold mountains**, as in the Andes, where the Nazca plate is subducted beneath the South American plate.

**Oceanic-oceanic convergence:** the older, colder and denser plate is subducted. The trench forms on the ocean floor, and volcanoes built from rising magma on the overriding plate emerge as a curved line of islands: a **volcanic island arc**. The Mariana Trench, the deepest known point of the ocean at about 11 km, lies beside the Mariana Islands arc; Japan and the Aleutian Islands are other examples.

**Continental-continental collision:** neither plate is dense enough to subduct far, so the crust crumples, thickens and is pushed upwards in **fold mountain building**. The Himalayas formed as the Indian plate collided with the Eurasian plate, and they are still rising. Volcanoes are rare because little oceanic slab is melting beneath them.

## Conservative boundaries

At a conservative boundary, plates slide past one another along a transform fault, and crust is neither created nor destroyed. Friction locks the plates until stress is released suddenly. The San Andreas Fault in California, between the Pacific and North American plates, is the classic example. Landforms are modest: fault scarps, offset streams and linear valleys along the fault.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain the formation of a volcanic island arc." [5]*

> A volcanic island arc forms where two oceanic plates converge **(setting)**. The older, colder and denser plate is subducted beneath the other, creating a deep ocean trench where it bends down **(process and first landform)**. As the slab sinks into the mantle, rising temperatures release water from its rocks, which lowers the melting point of the mantle above and generates magma **(why melting happens)**. The magma rises through the overriding plate and erupts on the sea floor, building volcanoes that eventually rise above sea level **(construction)**. Because the subduction zone is curved, the volcanoes form a curved chain of islands parallel to the trench, such as the Mariana Islands **(shape and example)**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "the crust floats on molten magma". The asthenosphere is mostly solid rock that flows slowly.
- Treating plates and crust as the same thing.
- Claiming continental crust is subducted at collision zones.
- Describing a trench on the continental side of a subduction zone.
- Forgetting that the magnetic stripe pattern is symmetrical about the ridge.
- Putting fold mountains only at continental collisions; they also form at oceanic-continental boundaries.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a plate map: name plates, boundary types and directions of movement, with located examples.
- **Explain** a landform: state the boundary type, the process and the order of events.
- **Calculate**: convert units carefully and say whether a rate is a half-rate or a full spreading rate.
- Cross-section diagrams should show the direction of plate movement, the subducting slab and where melting occurs.
