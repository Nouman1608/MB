This guide covers section 13.1 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists "visible and invisible imports and exports"; "global patterns of, and inequalities in, trade flows"; "factors affecting global trade (including resource endowment, locational advantage, historical factors such as colonial ties, trade agreements and changes in the global market)"; "the role of the World Trade Organization (WTO) and free trade", where candidates "should be able to critically evaluate the impacts of trade on exporting and importing countries"; and "the nature and role of Fairtrade". ## Measuring trade

- **Balance of trade** = value of exports − value of imports. It can be calculated for goods (visible) or for goods and services.
- A **trade surplus** means exports exceed imports; a **trade deficit** means the reverse.
- **Terms of trade index** = (index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100. Above 100 means a unit of exports buys more imports than in the base year; below 100 means it buys fewer.
- **Export concentration**: the share of export earnings from the largest product. A high share means high risk when that product's price falls.

### Worked example

*A fictional country exports goods worth US$40 billion and services worth US$12 billion, and imports goods worth US$46 billion and services worth US$4 billion. Since a base year, its export price index has fallen to 85 and its import price index has risen to 110.*

- Visible balance = 40 − 46 = **−US$6 billion** (a deficit in goods).
- Invisible balance = 12 − 4 = **+US$8 billion** (a surplus in services, perhaps from tourism or shipping).
- Overall balance on goods and services = −6 + 8 = **+US$2 billion**.
- Terms of trade = 85 ÷ 110 × 100 = **77.3**. Each unit of exports now buys about 23% fewer imports than in the base year.

The example shows why a country can earn more from invisibles than its visible trade suggests, and why falling terms of trade can hurt even when export volumes are rising.

## Global patterns and inequalities

- **Concentration:** most world trade flows within and between Europe, North America and East Asia. Much of it is in manufactured goods and services between high- and upper-middle-income economies.
- **Intra-industry trade:** HICs often trade similar goods with each other, such as cars and machinery.
- **Global value chains:** components cross borders several times before final assembly, so trade between East Asian economies has grown rapidly.
- **Marginalised countries:** many LICs, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, have a small share of world trade and export mainly primary commodities.

### Why the inequality persists

1. **Low value added.** Raw commodities earn less than processed goods; most of the value is added where processing, branding and retail happen.
2. **Tariff escalation.** Some importing countries set higher tariffs on processed goods than on raw materials, which discourages processing in the exporting country.
3. **Price volatility.** Commodity prices swing sharply, so export earnings and government revenue are unstable.
4. **Declining terms of trade.** The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis argues that the prices of primary commodities tend to fall over the long term relative to manufactured goods. Commodity booms show this is not constant, but the long-run risk is widely discussed.
5. **Infrastructure and market access.** Landlocked countries face higher transport costs, and poor ports and roads raise the cost of trading.

## Factors affecting global trade: going further

- **Resource endowment:** shapes what a country can export, but endowment alone does not guarantee benefit; the "resource curse" describes countries where resource wealth coexists with slow growth, conflict or weak institutions.
- **Locational advantage:** proximity to large markets or shipping routes, and access to the sea, lower trade costs.
- **Historical factors such as colonial ties:** infrastructure built to move raw materials to ports, shared languages, legal systems and long-standing trade relationships still influence flows.
- **Trade agreements:** levels of integration rise from a free trade area (no tariffs between members, such as the ASEAN Free Trade Area agreed in 1992), to a customs union (plus a common external tariff), to a common market or single market (plus free movement of labour and capital, as in the EU). Agreements create trade between members but can divert it from non-members.
- **Changes in the global market:** containerisation, falling communication costs, the rise of China as an exporter, shifts in demand towards services, commodity price cycles, and renewed tariffs and trade disputes.

## Free trade, protection and the WTO

The case for free trade rests on **comparative advantage**, the idea developed by David Ricardo: countries gain by specialising in what they produce at relatively lower cost and trading for the rest.

| For free trade | Against unrestricted free trade |
|---|---|
| Lower prices and wider choice for consumers | Local producers can be undercut by cheaper imports |
| Access to larger markets for exporters | Infant industries cannot compete with established foreign firms |
| Competition encourages efficiency | Specialisation in commodities locks in low value added |
| Technology and investment spread | Rich countries may keep subsidies while poor ones open their markets |

**Protectionist tools:** tariffs, quotas, subsidies to domestic producers and non-tariff barriers such as complex standards.

**The WTO's role:** setting rules, negotiating reductions in barriers, monitoring members' policies and settling disputes. Developing countries are allowed some **special and differential treatment**, such as longer periods to implement agreements.

## Evaluating impacts on exporting and importing countries

| | Exporting countries | Importing countries |
|---|---|---|
| Economic benefits | Foreign currency, jobs, economies of scale, investment | Cheaper goods, inputs for industry, wider choice |
| Economic costs | Dependence on few products; exposure to price falls | Loss of local industries and jobs; trade deficits |
| Social effects | Jobs in export sectors, but sometimes poor conditions | Consumer gains spread widely; job losses concentrated in places |
| Environmental effects | Mining, plantation and factory impacts at the source | Emissions from transport; impacts "exported" to producers |

To evaluate critically, ask **who** gains (consumers, firms, workers, governments), **where** (which regions), **when** (booms or slumps) and **what kind** of export (commodity, manufactured good or service).

## Fairtrade: nature and role

**Nature:** a certification and labelling system for producers in LICs and MICs. Certified producer organisations receive a minimum price and a premium for community or business investment, and must meet social, labour and environmental standards.

**Role and limits:**

- It reduces risk for small producers when world prices fall.
- The premium funds schools, water supplies or processing equipment chosen by the producers.
- It promotes democratic co-operatives and bans forced and child labour.
- It covers a small share of global trade, and producers often sell only part of their crop on Fairtrade terms.
- Certification has costs, which can exclude the poorest producers.
- It does not change who controls processing, branding and retail, where most value is added.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why many low-income countries gain little from trade. [5]*

> Many LICs export mainly primary commodities such as unprocessed crops or minerals (**export structure**), which earn low value added because processing and branding take place elsewhere (**value added**). Tariff escalation in some markets discourages them from processing goods themselves (**trade barrier**). Commodity prices are volatile, so earnings fall sharply in slumps (**volatility**), and over the long term their terms of trade may decline relative to manufactured imports (**terms of trade**).

## Common mistakes

- Calling tourism or insurance visible trade; they are invisible.
- Confusing the balance of trade with the terms of trade.
- Treating free trade as either wholly good or wholly bad.
- Describing Fairtrade as a government trade agreement.
- Evaluating impacts without saying for whom.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show each step, include units and say what the result means.
- **Describe** a trade map or table: identify major flows, compare values and note anomalies.
- **Evaluate** impacts: use the who, where, when and what-kind questions, and conclude.
