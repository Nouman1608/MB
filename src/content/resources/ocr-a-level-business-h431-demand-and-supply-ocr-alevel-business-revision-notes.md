## Core recall

- **Demand**: the quantity buyers are willing **and able** to buy at each price over a period.
- **Supply**: the quantity sellers are willing and able to offer at each price over a period.
- **Equilibrium**: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears and there is no pressure for price to change.
- Demand slopes down (higher price, fewer buyers); supply slopes up (higher price, more profitable to produce).
- **Own price changes** cause movements along a curve: extension or contraction.
- **Any other determinant** shifts the whole curve: right is an increase, left is a decrease.
- Price adjusts through **rationing**, **signalling** and **incentive**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Effective demand | Desire backed by the ability to pay |
| Extension of demand | More bought because the product's own price fell |
| Contraction of supply | Less offered because the product's own price fell |
| Increase in demand | Curve shifts right: more bought at every price |
| Decrease in supply | Curve shifts left: less offered at every price |
| Substitute | A good used instead of another; its price rise raises demand for the rival |
| Complement | A good used with another; its price fall raises demand for both |
| Excess supply | Quantity supplied above quantity demanded, at a price above equilibrium |
| Shortage (excess demand) | Quantity demanded above quantity supplied, at a price below equilibrium |
| Market clearing | All goods offered at the price are bought |

## Determinants at a glance

**Demand shifters:** income; prices of substitutes and complements; tastes, fashion and trends; advertising; population size and age structure; expectations of future prices; season and weather.

**Supply shifters:** costs of production (labour, materials, energy); technology; number of sellers; indirect taxes and subsidies; weather for farm goods; expectations of future profit.

## Single shifts: what happens to price (P) and quantity (Q)

| Change | Curve | P | Q |
|---|---|---|---|
| Increase in demand | D right | up | up |
| Decrease in demand | D left | down | down |
| Increase in supply | S right | down | up |
| Decrease in supply | S left | up | down |

## Double shifts: one answer is certain

| Change | Certain | Depends on the larger shift |
|---|---|---|
| D right and S right | Q up | P |
| D left and S left | Q down | P |
| D right and S left | P up | Q |
| D left and S right | P down | Q |

**Mini example (invented).** Weekly hire of electric bikes: QD = 500 − 20P, QS = 30P − 100. Equilibrium: 600 = 50P, so P = £12, Q = 260. Demand rises by 100 at every price and supply also rises by 50: QD = 600 − 20P, QS = 30P − 50. Then 650 = 50P, so P = £13, Q = 340. Quantity was certain to rise; price rose here because the demand shift was larger.

## Excesses and shortages

- At a price **above** equilibrium: excess supply. Measure it horizontally between the curves at that price.
- At a price **below** equilibrium: shortage. Measure it the same way.
- Using the bike market at £15: QD = 200, QS = 350, so excess supply = 150. At £8: QD = 340, QS = 140, so shortage = 200.

**Effects on a business.** Excess: unsold stock, storage cost, discounting, waste if perishable, output cuts. Shortage: lost sales, frustrated customers who may switch, chance to raise price or margin, incentive for new entrants. Size of the effect depends on how long the imbalance lasts, whether the product can be stored and whether price can change quickly.

## Price and output decisions

- **Price taker** (many similar sellers): accepts the market price and decides output.
- **Firm with pricing power** (strong brand, few rivals): can raise price when demand rises, or hold price to grow share.
- **Cost rise**: pass on, absorb or split; depends on rivals' costs and how willing buyers are to pay more.
- **Short run vs long run**: capacity is fixed at first, so a demand rise shows up mainly in price; later, output expands.

## Diagram checklist

- Axes: price (vertical), quantity (horizontal).
- Label D, S, and any new curve D1 or S1.
- Mark P and Q at the old and new equilibria with dashed lines to the axes.
- Shortage or excess: horizontal line at the given price, with the gap labelled.
- Finish in words: "price rises from P to P1 and quantity from Q to Q1".

## Exam traps

- Shifting a curve because the product's own price changed.
- Treating a cost change as a demand change.
- Confusing substitutes and complements.
- Showing a shortage above equilibrium.
- In a double shift, stating both P and Q with certainty.
- Listing factors when asked to evaluate, with no judgement on which matters most for the case business.
- Forgetting to link the market change to the firm's own price and output decision.

## Self-test

1. What turns a want into effective demand?
2. A product's own price falls. Is this a shift or a movement, and what is it called?
3. Coffee becomes more expensive. What happens to demand for tea, and why?
4. Printers become much cheaper. What happens to demand for ink cartridges?
5. A new automated line cuts unit costs. Which curve moves, and which way?
6. State the effect on price and quantity of a decrease in supply.
7. Demand rises and supply falls. Which variable changes with certainty?
8. Where on a diagram is a shortage shown?
9. QD = 840 − 25P and QS = 15P − 160. Find equilibrium price and quantity.
10. In question 9, what is the position at a price of £30?
11. Why is excess supply more damaging for a florist than for a hardware shop?
12. Give one reason a firm might not pass on a rise in costs.

### Answers

1. The ability to pay, usually from income.
2. A movement along the demand curve: an extension of demand.
3. Demand for tea increases (shifts right), because tea is a substitute and buyers switch.
4. Demand for cartridges increases, because they are complements to printers.
5. Supply shifts right.
6. Price rises and quantity falls.
7. Price: it rises.
8. Below the equilibrium price, as the horizontal gap between demand (further right) and supply.
9. 1,000 = 40P, so P = £25 and Q = 215.
10. QD = 90, QS = 290, so excess supply of 200.
11. Flowers are perishable, so unsold stock is wasted; hardware can be stored and sold later.
12. Rivals with lower costs would undercut it, or buyers would switch to substitutes, so it would lose too many sales.
