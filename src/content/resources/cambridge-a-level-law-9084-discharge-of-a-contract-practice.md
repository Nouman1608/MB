## Section A -- short questions

**1.** State the entire or strict performance rule and give one case illustrating it. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the doctrine of substantial performance, contrasting two cases. **[4]**

**3.** Explain why the claimant failed in Sumpter v Hedges (1898). **[3]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a divisible contract and how it avoids the strict performance rule. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the effect of tender of performance, with an authority. **[3]**

**6.** Distinguish an actual breach from an anticipatory breach. **[3]**

**7.** Explain why a contract is not frustrated merely because performance has become more expensive. **[3]**

**8.** Explain the effect of frustration at common law before 1943. **[4]**

## Section B -- scenario and essay questions

**9.** *Original scenario.* Arjun agrees to re-roof Megan's house for £12,000, payable on completion. When he has finished, a small section of guttering is misaligned and will cost £400 to correct. Megan refuses to pay anything. On a separate contract, Arjun agreed to build a garden office for Megan for £9,000 on completion; after he had done half the work, Megan locked the site and told him she had changed her mind. Advise Arjun on what he can recover under each contract. **[10]**

**10.** *Original scenario.* On 1 March Stagecraft Ltd agrees to supply lighting for a concert by the singer Tia at a city arena on 1 June, for £20,000. Tia's promoter pays £5,000 on signing. On 10 April Stagecraft announces it has taken a more profitable booking and will not perform. The promoter decides to wait. On 20 May the arena is destroyed by fire. Stagecraft had spent £1,500 on preparation before 10 April. Advise the promoter. **[12]**

**11.** *Original scenario.* Oakridge Foods agrees to ship 500 tonnes of rice from its own port to a buyer, but the government suddenly bans all exports of rice. Oakridge had already sold some of its stock elsewhere, and claims the ban frustrated the contract. The contract contains no clause about export bans. Assess whether the contract is frustrated. **[8]**

**12.** "The doctrine of frustration is so narrow that it rarely helps a party who cannot perform." Evaluate this statement, including reference to the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** A party must perform exactly and completely before the other party is obliged to pay (1). In Cutter v Powell (1795) a seaman who died before the voyage ended recovered nothing under a lump-sum contract (1). **[2]**

**2.** Where performance is complete apart from minor defects, the party can claim the price less the cost of remedying the defects (1). In Hoenig v Isaacs (1952) decorating and furniture work with minor defects was substantially performed, so the price minus the cost of repair was payable (1). In Bolton v Mahadeva (1972) a heating system that heated unevenly and gave off fumes was not substantially performed (1), so nothing was payable: the test depends on the nature of the defects and the cost of remedying them relative to the price (1). **[4]**

**3.** The builder abandoned the work and the landowner completed the buildings himself (1). Payment for partial performance requires a genuine choice to accept the benefit (1); a landowner has no real choice about buildings on his own land, so he did not have to pay for the work, although he paid for the loose materials he chose to use (1). **[3]**

**4.** A divisible contract is one where payment is due for separate parts or stages of performance (1), so each completed part can be paid for even if the whole is not completed, as in Ritchie v Atkinson (1808) with freight payable per ton (1). **[2]**

**5.** Where a party offers to perform in accordance with the contract and the offer is refused, the tender counts as performance (1), so the party is discharged and may sue for breach (1). In Startup v Macdonald (1843) oil delivered late on the last day, but within the agreed time, was wrongly refused and the seller recovered damages (1). **[3]**

**6.** An actual breach is a failure to perform, or defective performance, when performance is due (1). An anticipatory breach is a clear indication, before performance is due, that a party will not perform (1), as in Hochster v De la Tour (1853), where the innocent party could sue at once (1). **[3]**

**7.** Frustration requires performance to become impossible, illegal or radically different (1). Extra cost or delay means performance is harder, not different in kind (1), as in Davis Contractors v Fareham UDC (1956), where a labour shortage made building slower and dearer, or Tsakiroglou v Noblee Thorl (1962), where the Suez closure lengthened the voyage (1). **[3]**

**8.** Frustration ended the contract automatically at the moment of the event (1), so future obligations were discharged (1). Under Chandler v Webster (1904) money already paid could not be recovered, the loss lying where it fell (1). Fibrosa (1943) allowed recovery only for a total failure of consideration (1). **[4]**

**9.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Re-roofing is an entire contract payable on completion, so the strict rule in Cutter v Powell would bar payment (1).
- But the work is complete apart from a minor defect (1); substantial performance applies (Hoenig v Isaacs) (1).
- Arjun can recover £12,000 − £400 = £11,600 (1).
- This is unlike Bolton v Mahadeva, because the defect is small compared with the price (1).
- Garden office: Megan prevented performance by locking the site (1).
- Prevention allows a claim in quantum meruit for work done (Planché v Colburn) (1).
- Alternatively Megan's refusal is a repudiatory breach, which Arjun can accept and sue for damages (1).
- Damages would aim to put Arjun where completion would have put him, his lost profit plus the cost of work done, subject to mitigation (1).
- Conclusion: Arjun recovers £11,600 on the roof and either a reasonable sum or damages on the garden office (1). **[10]**

**10.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Stagecraft's announcement on 10 April is an anticipatory breach (Hochster v De la Tour) (1).
- The promoter could have accepted it and sued at once (1), but chose to affirm and wait (1).
- Affirmation keeps the contract alive for both parties (1).
- The fire on 20 May destroys the venue, making performance impossible (Taylor v Caldwell) (1), and the event is not caused by either party (1).
- As in Avery v Bowden (1855), the frustrating event discharges the contract and the promoter loses the right to sue for the earlier breach (1).
- Under s1(2) of the 1943 Act the £5,000 paid is recoverable and the £15,000 balance ceases to be payable (1).
- The court may allow Stagecraft to retain up to £1,500 for expenses (1), but it is discretionary and the court may refuse given Stagecraft's own repudiation (1).
- The promoter therefore recovers between £3,500 and £5,000 (1).
- Evaluation: had the promoter accepted the repudiation on 10 April, it could have claimed damages for breach; the decision to wait was costly (1). **[12]**

**11.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Supervening illegality, such as a change in the law, can frustrate a contract (1), as in Fibrosa (1943) (1).
- An export ban makes shipping illegal, which is a classic frustrating event (1).
- There is no contractual provision covering it (1).
- But frustration is unavailable if self-induced (1). Selling stock elsewhere is relevant only if it, rather than the ban, caused the failure (1); the ban makes any shipment illegal, so the sale did not cause it (contrast The Super Servant Two, where the party's own choice was the cause) (1).
- Foreseeability: if a ban was reasonably foreseeable, the risk may be treated as allocated to Oakridge (1). **[8]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- Knowledge (up to 6): definition and types (Taylor v Caldwell; Krell v Henry; Fibrosa); limitations (Davis Contractors; Tsakiroglou; Herne Bay; Maritime National Fish; The Super Servant Two); effect at common law (Chandler v Webster; Fibrosa); the 1943 Act s1(2) and s1(3) (Gamerco; BP v Hunt).
- Analysis (up to 3): explain that frustration is narrow because it overrides the parties' bargain and the courts expect commercial parties to allocate risks themselves, for example by force majeure clauses.
- Evaluation (up to 6): for the statement, increased cost, delay and foreseeable risks are excluded, so most practical difficulties do not frustrate; a party who cannot perform is usually in breach. Against, where the doctrine applies the 1943 Act gives flexible, just results, and narrowness promotes certainty and sanctity of contract. A reasoned conclusion, for example that the narrowness is deliberate and largely justified because parties can protect themselves by drafting. **[15]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating s1(2) recovery without first establishing that the contract is frustrated.
- Describing anticipatory breach without saying which choice the innocent party made and its consequences.
- Concluding "substantial performance" without comparing the cost of remedy with the price.
