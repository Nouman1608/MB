## The specification points

1. Explain what is meant by innovation, research and development.
2. Explain the reasons for innovation, research and development within a business and an industry.
3. Explain what is meant by product innovation and process innovation.
4. Explain the process/cycle of innovation, research and development.
5. Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of innovation, research and development to a business.
6. Evaluate the importance of innovation, research and development to a business, the consumer, other stakeholders and the economy.

## Core recall

### Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Invention | Creating a new idea or device |
| Innovation | Commercial use of a new idea: bringing a new or improved product or process to market |
| Pure research | Seeks knowledge with no product in mind |
| Applied research | Aims at a specific commercial use |
| Development | Turns research into a working product or process |
| R&D intensity | R&D spending as a percentage of sales |

### Reasons

- Business: competitive advantage; replace declining products; cut costs; meet changing needs; respond to law or customer concern; support a premium price and brand.
- Industry: rivals innovate; technology-led markets punish standing still; standards and compatibility; patents protect returns in costly sectors.

### Product and process innovation

| | Product | Process |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | What the customer buys | How it is made or delivered |
| Example | New medicine, app | Robot assembly, self-service checkout |
| Main effect | Revenue, brand | Cost, quality, speed |

### The R&D cycle

1. Idea generation.
2. Research and screening.
3. Development (prototypes).
4. Testing (laboratory and market).
5. Protection (patents, trademarks).
6. Commercialisation.
7. Review, feeding the next cycle.

Fewer ideas survive at each stage, so R&D is risky.

### Appraising projects

- Expected benefit = probability of success × benefit if successful.
- Expected net value = expected benefit − R&D cost.
- Break-even probability = R&D cost ÷ benefit if successful.
- Payback = cost ÷ yearly saving or contribution.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Competitive advantage; first-mover gains | High cost; uncertain results |
| Premium prices | Long wait for any return |
| Lower costs, higher quality | Failure wastes money; rivals copy |
| Extends the portfolio | Needs specialist staff |
| Patent protection | Protection is limited and costly |

### Importance

- Business: growth, survival, profit.
- Consumers: better, safer, cheaper products; more choice; new products may cost more at first.
- Stakeholders: employees (skills, or job loss from automation), shareholders (return or risk), suppliers (new orders), communities (jobs, cleaner processes).
- Economy: productivity, living standards, new industries, exports; structural unemployment possible.

## Quick worked reminder

Cobalt Devices (invented): R&D £1.2m; success chance 40%; benefit if successful £900,000 × 5 years = £4.5m. Expected benefit £1.8m; expected net value +£0.6m; break-even probability 26.7%; at 25%, −£0.075m.

Process: robot £250,000; labour £6.00 to £4.20 on 50,000 devices saves £90,000; defects fall from 4% to 1.5% saving £15,000. Total £105,000; payback 2.38 years.

## Reducing the risk of R&D

- Stage the project and review at each stage, stopping early if tests are poor.
- Test with customers (trial marketing) before full launch.
- Share costs and knowledge with a partner, a university or a supplier.
- Protect results with patents or trademarks.
- Keep a portfolio of projects, so that one failure does not threaten the business.
- Judge projects with expected value, break-even probability and payback, but remember that the estimates are uncertain.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Patent | A legal right that stops others copying an invention for a fixed period |
| First-mover advantage | Benefit of being first to market |
| Prototype | A first working model used for testing |
| Commercialisation | Turning an innovation into a product that is sold |
| Payback | Time taken for savings or profit to repay the cost |
| Structural unemployment | Job loss because an industry declines or changes |

## Evaluation prompts

- How large is the cost compared with the firm's cash and capital?
- How likely is success, and what is the break-even probability?
- Could the risk be shared (partner, staged development)?
- How quickly could rivals copy, and are patents available?
- Who gains and who loses among the stakeholders?
- Finish with a judgement using the figures in the case.

## Exam traps

- Invention is not innovation.
- Include process innovation.
- Use probability and payback when figures are given.
- Mention risk and the size of the business.
- Include effects on employees.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish invention from innovation.
2. Give an example of process innovation.
3. Give two reasons a business innovates.
4. Name two stages of the R&D cycle.
5. Chance of success 30%; benefit £2m. Find the expected benefit.
6. R&D costs £500,000. Find the expected net value in question 5 if cost is £500,000.
7. Find the break-even probability.
8. A machine costs £60,000 and saves £15,000 a year. Find the payback.
9. Give one disadvantage of R&D.
10. Why do firms apply for patents?
11. Give one effect of innovation on employees.
12. Give one benefit of innovation to the economy.

### Answers

1. Invention creates a new idea; innovation brings it to market commercially.
2. Any one: robot assembly; new recipe that cuts waste; self-service checkouts.
3. Any two: competitive advantage; replace declining products; cut costs; meet customer needs; premium price.
4. Any two: idea generation; research; development; testing; protection; commercialisation; review.
5. 0.30 × 2,000,000 = **£600,000**.
6. 600,000 − 500,000 = **+£100,000**.
7. 500,000 ÷ 2,000,000 = **25%**.
8. 60,000 ÷ 15,000 = **4 years**.
9. Any one: high cost; uncertain results; long wait for return.
10. To stop rivals copying the invention for a period, protecting the returns.
11. Any one: new skills; job security; or job losses from automation.
12. Any one: higher productivity; new industries; exports; higher living standards.
