## Definitions

**Custom-written software** (also called bespoke software) is written specially for one client to meet that client's exact requirements. The client usually commissions it from an in-house team or an outside developer.

**Off-the-shelf software** is ready-made software developed for a general market and sold or licensed to many users. It may be downloaded, bought as a subscription, or come pre-installed.

## Uses by individuals and organisations

| | Off-the-shelf | Custom-written |
|---|---|---|
| Individuals | Word processors, photo editors, games, web browsers, anti-virus, personal finance apps | Rare, because of cost; for example, a sole trader commissioning a booking app for their business, or specialised assistive software for one user's disability |
| Organisations | Office suites, accounting and payroll packages, email, general stock-control systems | Systems with unusual or very large requirements: an airline's reservations system, a bank's account system, a national tax system, a factory's control software for its own machines |

Many organisations use both: off-the-shelf software for general tasks and custom-written software for the processes that make them different from competitors.

## The four criteria the syllabus names

### Degree of testing

- **Off-the-shelf:** tested by the developer and, after release, by thousands or millions of users. Bugs are reported and fixed in updates, so mature packages are usually reliable.
- **Custom-written:** tested only by the developer and the client before it goes live. Some errors may appear only after use begins. However, it is tested against the client's own data and processes, which general software never is.

### Level of support

- **Off-the-shelf:** large user communities, online forums, help pages, tutorials, training courses and staff who already know the software. Support is generic and the client has little influence over what is fixed or added.
- **Custom-written:** direct support from the developer who knows the system, tailored to the client. But the client depends on that developer: if it stops trading or the key programmer leaves, support may end.

### Cost

- **Off-the-shelf:** development cost is spread across many buyers, so the price per user is low. Costs can still mount through per-user licences, annual subscriptions and paid upgrades.
- **Custom-written:** the client pays the full development cost: analysis, design, programming, testing and training. The upfront cost is high, though there may be no per-user licence fees afterwards.

### Adaptability

- **Off-the-shelf:** designed for typical users, so it may lack features the client needs and include many it never uses. Some packages allow add-ons or settings, but the client cannot change the core program.
- **Custom-written:** built around the client's exact requirements and can be changed as those requirements evolve, provided the client pays for the changes.

### A fifth factor: time

Off-the-shelf software is available immediately. Custom-written software can take months to analyse, write and test, which delays the benefits.

## Worked example 1: comparing costs over five years

Corvane Freight (invented) has 50 office users.

- Off-the-shelf transport package: £240 per user per year. Five-year cost = 50 × 240 × 5 = **£60,000**.
- Custom-written system: £60,000 to develop plus £6,000 a year maintenance. Five-year cost = 60,000 + 5 × 6,000 = **£90,000**.

On cost alone, off-the-shelf wins. But if Corvane grows to 80 users, the package would cost 80 × 240 × 5 = £96,000, more than the custom system. The two options cost the same at 90,000 ÷ (240 × 5) = **75 users**. Add the non-cost criteria: if the package cannot handle Corvane's unusual cross-border paperwork, staff may waste hours on workarounds, which could justify the custom system even at 50 users.

## Worked example 2: an annotated model answer

*Question: A dental practice with three dentists needs software to book appointments and store patient records. Evaluate whether it should buy off-the-shelf or custom-written software.*

> Off-the-shelf dental booking packages already exist and have been tested by many practices, so they are likely to be reliable *(testing)*. They are cheaper because their cost is shared across many buyers, which matters for a small practice *(cost)*, and they can be installed straight away *(time)*. Support is available from the supplier and from other users *(support)*. Custom software could match the practice's exact procedures *(adaptability)*, but three dentists' booking needs are unlikely to be unusual enough to justify the much higher development cost. **Off-the-shelf is more suitable**, unless the practice has a requirement no package meets.

Each sentence names a criterion and applies it to the practice, then the conclusion follows from the comparison.

## Proprietary and open-source software

**Proprietary software** is owned by a company or individual, who keeps the **source code** secret. Users buy a **licence** to use it, which restricts copying, modifying and passing it on. Examples include Microsoft Office and Adobe Photoshop.

**Open-source software** makes its source code publicly available. Anyone may study, modify and redistribute it, under the terms of its licence. Examples include the Linux operating system, LibreOffice and the Firefox browser.

| | Proprietary | Open-source |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Professional support and regular updates; usually polished and well documented; developer is accountable | Often free of charge; can be modified to fit needs; many people can inspect the code for flaws; no dependence on one company |
| Disadvantages | Licence and upgrade costs; cannot be modified; user is tied to the supplier's decisions | Support may rely on community forums; quality and documentation vary; modifying it needs programming skill |

Two points often confused:

- **Open-source is about access to the source code, not price.** Most open-source software is free, but companies can charge for support or distributions.
- **Proprietary software is not always paid for.** Some is given away free while its code stays closed.

Custom-written software is usually proprietary to the client who paid for it, but it can be built on open-source components.

## Common mistakes

- Saying custom software is "better" without a criterion and a scenario.
- Assuming custom software is fully bug-free because it was built to order.
- Treating "open-source" as meaning "free".
- Forgetting ongoing costs: subscriptions for off-the-shelf, maintenance for custom.
- Listing advantages of one option with nothing on the other when asked to compare.

## Exam technique

- Use the syllabus criteria as your structure: testing, support, cost, adaptability, then time.
- **Compare** questions need a point about each option on the same criterion, linked by "whereas".
- **Evaluate** questions need a justified conclusion for the scenario given, naming the deciding factor.
