## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by comparative advantage. **[2]**

**2.** In a month, using all its resources, Country K can make 80 kettles or 40 fans. Country L can make 60 kettles or 20 fans.

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one fan in each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the country that should specialise in fans, and explain why K's higher output of both goods does not alter this. **[2]**
**(c)** State the range of terms of trade, in kettles per fan, at which both countries gain, and explain why. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows price indices for Country M (Year 1 = 100).

| Year | Export prices | Import prices |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | 100 |
| 2 | 105 | 102 |
| 3 | 111 | 120 |

**(a)** Calculate the terms of trade in Year 3 and state whether they have improved or deteriorated since Year 1. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in export volume needed in Year 3, compared with Year 1, to buy the same volume of imports. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[2]**

**4.** Country P is a small importer of tables and takes the world price of 8 as given. Home demand is Qd = 200 − 5P and home supply is Qs = 50 + 5P.

**(a)** Calculate the quantity imported under free trade. **[2]**
**(b)** A tariff of 2 per table is introduced. Calculate the new home price, the quantity imported and the tariff revenue. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the change in consumer surplus and in producer surplus, and the net welfare loss. **[3]**

**5.** Use the market in question 4. Instead of the tariff, the government sets an import quota of 50 tables.

**(a)** Show that the home price becomes 10. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain who receives the area equal to 100 under the quota, and who receives it under the tariff. **[2]**

**6.** Country N exports cheese at a world price of 6. Home demand is Qd = 110 − 5P and home supply is Qs = 30 + 10P. The government pays 2 for every unit exported.

**(a)** Calculate exports before the subsidy. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate exports after the subsidy. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost of the subsidy. **[1]**

**7.** Name the type of trading bloc described in each case.

**(a)** Members remove tariffs on each other's goods but each sets its own tariff on outsiders. **[1]**
**(b)** As in (a), with a common external tariff. **[1]**
**(c)** As in (b), with free movement of labour and capital. **[1]**
**(d)** As in (c), with a single currency. **[1]**

**8.** Explain two roles of the World Trade Organisation. **[4]**

**9.** Explain two costs that a country may face from increased international trade. **[4]**

**10.** A government in a small low-income economy places a 30% tariff on imported electronic goods to protect its new assembly industry. Evaluate whether this is likely to benefit the economy. **[8]**

**11.** Country Z exports a mineral. Its export price index rose from 100 in Year 1 to 130 in Year 2. Its import price index rose from 100 to 104.

**(a)** Use the index numbers to find Country Z's terms of trade in Year 2. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two possible causes of a change in a country's terms of trade. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate the likely effects of this change on Country Z's economy. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** A country has a comparative advantage in a good when it sacrifices less of other output than its trading partner to make an extra unit [1]. It is judged by opportunity cost, not by total output [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the idea of sacrifice. An explanation that only says "produces more" describes absolute advantage instead.

**2. (a)** K: 80 ÷ 40 = 2 kettles [1]. L: 60 ÷ 20 = 3 kettles [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **Country K** [1], because it gives up 2 kettles per fan compared with 3 for L. Higher output of both goods (absolute advantage) is irrelevant, only the opportunity cost matters [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** **Between 2 and 3 kettles per fan** [1], because K must receive more than the 2 kettles it gives up at home, and L must pay less than the 3 it gives up at home [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each division. Writing only "K" with no opportunity costs leaves nothing to credit if the choice is wrong.

**3. (a)** Terms of trade = 111 ÷ 120 × 100 = **92.5** [1], a deterioration because it is below 100 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 100 ÷ 92.5 = 1.081 [1], so about **8.1%** more [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say improved or deteriorated in words beside the figure. In (b), name Year 1 as the comparison so the 8.1% has a clear reference.

**4. (a)** Qd = 200 − 40 = 160 and Qs = 50 + 40 = 90 [1]. Imports = 160 − 90 = **70** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New price = 8 + 2 = **10** [1]. Qd = 150, Qs = 100, imports = **50** [1]. Revenue = 2 × 50 = **100** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Consumer surplus falls by (160 + 150) ÷ 2 × 2 = **310** [1]. Producer surplus rises by (90 + 100) ÷ 2 × 2 = **190** [1]. Net welfare loss = 310 − 190 − 100 = **20** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention the sign or direction of each change. Checking that the loss equals the two triangles, ½ × 2 × 10 twice, confirms the arithmetic.

**5. (a)** Set Qd − Qs = 50: 200 − 5P − (50 + 5P) = 50 [1]. 150 − 10P = 50, so P = **10** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Under the tariff the 100 is government revenue [1]. Under a quota it goes to the holders of the import licences, unless the government sells the licences [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Importers" alone is thin. Say that the price gap between home and world price is captured by whoever is allowed to import.

**6. (a)** At P = 6, Qd = 110 − 30 = 80 and Qs = 30 + 60 = 90 [1]. Exports = **10** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Producers receive 8, so the home price is 8: Qd = 110 − 40 = 70 and Qs = 30 + 80 = 110 [1]. Exports = **40** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 2 × 40 = **80** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The subsidy applies to exports, not to all output. Using the full quantity supplied gives the wrong cost.

**7. (a)** **Free trade area** [1]. **(b)** **Customs union** [1]. **(c)** **Common market** [1]. **(d)** **Monetary union** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the exact term. Each bloc adds one feature to the one before, so learn the order.

**8.** First role, for example providing a forum for negotiating the reduction of trade barriers [1], which helps lower tariffs and increase trade between members [1]. Second role, for example settling disputes between members [1], which allows a country to challenge another's policy through a rules-based process rather than by retaliation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each role needs a development. Two bare names, such as "negotiation" and "disputes", earn less than two roles explained in a sentence each.

**9.** First cost, for example structural unemployment [1]: an industry that cannot compete with imports shrinks, and workers lack the skills to move quickly into other jobs, so unemployment may last [1]. Second cost, for example over-specialisation [1]: a country that relies on one export is exposed to a fall in its price or to a drop in world demand, which reduces its income [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each cost to who bears it and why it lasts. A bare list such as "unemployment, dependence" is only a statement.

**10.** Reason for protection: the industry is new, with high costs at a small scale [1]. The tariff raises the price of imports, so home output rises [1] and, as scale grows, average costs fall, so it may become competitive and the tariff can be removed [1]. This could create jobs and diversify the economy [1]. Against: consumers and firms pay higher prices [1], protected firms may become inefficient with little pressure to improve [1], and other countries may retaliate, or firms using imported parts face higher costs [1]. Judgement: it is more likely to benefit the economy if the tariff is temporary, the industry has real scope for scale economies, and cheaper alternatives such as training support are not available [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion tied to a stated condition, such as how long the tariff lasts, is usually stronger than "it depends" on its own.

**11. (a)** 130 ÷ 104 × 100 = **125** [1], an improvement [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Any two, each with a reason: a rise in world demand for the mineral raises export prices [1]; a fall in import prices, for example from a fall in the price of imported fuel, lowers the import index [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Each import costs fewer exports, so Country Z can buy more imports for the same export volume, which raises real income [1]. Cheaper imported capital goods may help investment [1]. But a rise in export prices may cut sales if demand is elastic [1], and reliance on one mineral leaves the economy exposed if its price falls again [1]. The effect on the trade balance depends on how volumes respond to price, so an improved terms of trade does not guarantee a better current account [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Support the evaluation with the elasticity point and a time frame. Saying an improvement is "good" with no qualification is a statement and not an evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total output rather than opportunity cost to decide comparative advantage.
- Inverting the terms of trade fraction, so the direction of change is the wrong way round.
- Giving the range of terms of trade without explaining why both countries gain.
- Calling quota rent government revenue.
- Leaving out the welfare loss, or forgetting that the tariff revenue is a transfer.
- Listing costs or reasons for protection without explaining the chain of cause and effect.
- Writing "WTO stops protection" without saying how, or noting its limits.
- Concluding an evaluation question with a one-sided view.
