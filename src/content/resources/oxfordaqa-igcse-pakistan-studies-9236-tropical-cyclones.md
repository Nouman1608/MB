Key development 3.2.1.2 of the 9236 specification, Climate, includes "causes of tropical cyclones and
their impacts on the environment and people of Pakistan". This guide explains how tropical cyclones
form, why Pakistan's coast is affected at particular times of year, what damage they cause and how
Pakistan prepares. It belongs to the compulsory topic examined in Paper 2 Section A. For the climate
of Pakistan as a whole, including the monsoon and western disturbances, see the
[Landscape and Natural Resources study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-landscape-and-natural-resources/).

## What a tropical cyclone is

A tropical cyclone is a large, intense system of **very low pressure** that forms over warm tropical
seas, with winds spiralling around a calm centre. Over the Atlantic such storms are called
hurricanes and over the western Pacific typhoons; over the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal they are
called **cyclones**. They bring three main hazards: **strong winds**, **heavy rain** and a **storm
surge**, a rise in sea level pushed onto the coast by the wind and low pressure.

## The conditions needed

Cyclones form only where several conditions occur together.

- **Warm sea water**, generally above about 26.5 °C, to a depth of tens of metres, which supplies heat
  and moisture.
- **Distance from the equator** of at least about 5 degrees of latitude, so the **Coriolis effect**,
  caused by the Earth's rotation, is strong enough to make the air spin.
- **Low vertical wind shear**: winds at different heights must be similar in speed and direction,
  so the storm's structure is not torn apart.
- **Moist air** in the middle of the atmosphere, and an existing area of disturbed weather to start
  the process.

## How a cyclone forms

1. Warm sea water heats the air above it, and water evaporates rapidly.
2. Warm, moist air rises, creating an area of low pressure at the surface.
3. As the air rises it cools and the water vapour **condenses** into towering clouds, releasing
   **latent heat**, which warms the air further and makes it rise faster.
4. Air rushes in at the surface to replace the rising air. The Coriolis effect turns it, so it spirals
   **anticlockwise** in the northern hemisphere.
5. A calm **eye** forms at the centre, where air sinks. Around it the **eye wall** has the strongest
   winds and heaviest rain.
6. Steering winds move the cyclone across the sea. Over land or cooler water it loses its supply of
   heat and moisture, and friction slows the winds, so it weakens.

## Why the Arabian Sea's season is unusual

Cyclones over the Arabian Sea occur mainly in two periods: **before the monsoon (May and June)** and
**after it (October and November)**. During the height of the summer monsoon, strong winds at
different heights create high wind shear, which prevents cyclones from organising. The Arabian Sea
also produces fewer cyclones than the Bay of Bengal, and many that do form track towards Oman, Iran
or the Indian state of Gujarat rather than Pakistan. When a cyclone does reach Pakistan, the most
exposed areas are the low-lying **Indus delta** and coast of **Sindh**, including Karachi, and the
**Makran** coast of Balochistan.

## Examples to know

- **May 1999.** A severe cyclone struck the coast of southern Sindh, around Thatta and Badin. Fishing
  villages were destroyed, many people died, and boats, crops and livestock were lost.
- **Cyclone Yemyin (June 2007).** It crossed the Makran coast of Balochistan. Its heavy rain caused
  severe flash flooding in Balochistan and parts of Sindh, washing away homes, roads and bridges and
  displacing large numbers of people.
- **Cyclone Biparjoy (June 2023).** It made landfall near the border between Sindh and Gujarat. Pakistani
  authorities evacuated tens of thousands of people from coastal districts of Sindh in advance, and
  the loss of life in Pakistan was small.

Learn one example in detail, with its date, area affected and impacts on both people and the
environment.

## Impacts on people

- **Loss of life and injury**, especially from storm surges in low-lying coastal villages.
- **Damage to homes and infrastructure**: houses, roads, bridges, power lines and ports.
- **Livelihoods**: fishing communities lose boats and nets; farmers lose crops and livestock; ports
  such as Karachi and Gwadar may close.
- **Displacement**: people move to relief camps, sometimes for weeks.
- **Health**: contaminated water after floods spreads diseases such as diarrhoea; shortages of food
  and clean water follow.
- **Economic costs** for government and families, from rebuilding and lost income.

## Impacts on the environment

- **Storm surges and seawater flooding** make farmland salty, sometimes for years.
- **Coastal erosion** reshapes beaches and creeks in the delta.
- **Mangrove forests** can be damaged, though healthy mangroves also reduce the force of waves and
  surges.
- **Flash floods** inland erode soils and river banks, especially in dry Balochistan.
- **Some benefit**: heavy rain can refill reservoirs and groundwater in arid areas.

## Why impacts vary

Two cyclones of similar strength can have very different impacts. Explaining why earns marks in
"explain" and "how far" questions.

- **Physical factors.** The height of the land matters most for surges: the flat, low Indus delta
  floods far inland, while the cliffs and higher ground of parts of the Makran coast are less
  exposed. The angle at which a cyclone meets the coast, its speed and the amount of rain it carries
  also matter.
- **Natural protection.** Wide belts of mangroves absorb wave energy; where mangroves have been cut
  for fuel or cleared for development, surges reach villages more easily.
- **Wealth and housing.** Mud and thatch homes in fishing villages are destroyed by winds and water
  that concrete buildings survive, and poorer families have fewer savings to recover.
- **Warning and preparation.** Communities reached by early warnings, with transport and shelters,
  lose far fewer lives. The contrast between 1999 and 2023 is partly a story of better forecasting
  and evacuation.
- **Population.** A cyclone striking near Karachi, a city of many millions, would put far more people
  and property at risk than one striking a remote stretch of coast.

## How Pakistan prepares and responds

- **Forecasting and warnings** by the **Pakistan Meteorological Department**, using satellite images
  to track storms and issue advisories.
- **Evacuation** organised by the **National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA)** and provincial
  disaster management authorities, as before Biparjoy.
- **Fishing bans** and port closures before landfall.
- **Mangrove restoration** along the Sindh coast, a natural barrier against surges.
- **Embankments, shelters and stronger buildings** in exposed coastal areas.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain why tropical cyclones form over the Arabian Sea in May and June rather than in
July and August. **[4]**

```
1  cyclones need warm sea water above about 26.5 C, which the
   Arabian Sea has before the monsoon
2  they also need low vertical wind shear so the storm can organise
3  during the summer monsoon, strong winds at different heights
   create high wind shear
4  this tears developing storms apart, so most cyclones form
   before or after the monsoon months
```

## Common mistakes

- **Saying cyclones form over land.** They form over warm seas and weaken over land.
- **Forgetting the storm surge.** It is often the deadliest hazard on low coasts.
- **Clockwise rotation.** In the northern hemisphere cyclones spin anticlockwise.
- **Listing impacts without separating people and environment**, when the question names both.
