## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL PHYSICS 9702 — AS Level, Topic 3.2**

Candidates should be able to:

1. show a qualitative understanding of frictional forces and viscous/drag forces including air resistance (no treatment of the coefficients of friction and viscosity is required, and a simple model of drag force increasing as speed increases is sufficient);
2. describe and explain qualitatively the motion of objects in a uniform gravitational field with air resistance;
3. understand that objects moving against a resistive force may reach a terminal (constant) velocity.

The word "qualitative" matters. You need to describe and explain; any calculation uses values of drag given in the question, with F = ma from subtopic 3.1.

## Resistive forces

**Friction** acts where two surfaces are in contact. It acts parallel to the surfaces and opposes their relative motion (or the tendency to move). Its work transfers kinetic energy to thermal energy.

**Viscous or drag forces** act on an object moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas). **Air resistance** is drag in air. The model the syllabus asks for is simple:

- drag acts opposite to the velocity of the object relative to the fluid;
- drag is zero when the object is at rest in the fluid;
- drag **increases as speed increases**.

No formula for drag is required. If a question needs a value, it will give one.

## Falling with air resistance: the four stages

Consider a skydiver of mass 75 kg falling from rest, so weight W = 75 × 9.81 = 736 N.

| Stage | Forces | Resultant | Acceleration |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Just released (v = 0) | weight only; drag = 0 | 736 N down | g = 9.81 m s⁻² |
| 2. Speeding up | drag grows with speed | W − D, getting smaller | decreasing |
| 3. Drag = 400 N (an example) | W = 736 N, D = 400 N | 336 N down | 336 ÷ 75 = 4.5 m s⁻² |
| 4. Terminal velocity | D = W = 736 N | zero | zero |

At terminal velocity the skydiver still moves, at a **constant** velocity. Nothing about the forces is "still increasing": drag has risen until it equals weight and then stays there, because the speed no longer changes.

### The velocity-time graph

Velocity (downwards positive) against time is a curve that:

- starts at the origin with a gradient equal to g (the acceleration at the instant of release);
- has a gradient that decreases steadily;
- levels off to a horizontal line at the terminal velocity.

The **gradient at any instant is the acceleration**, so a decreasing gradient shows the decreasing resultant force. The **area under the curve is the distance fallen**. Without air resistance, the graph would be a straight line of gradient g, lying above the curve at all times after release.

### Worked example 1: the parachute opens

The skydiver is at terminal velocity when the parachute opens. At that speed the drag on the open parachute is 1500 N.

```
resultant = W - D = 736 - 1500 = -764 N   (negative: upwards)
a = -764 / 75 = -10.2 m s^-2
```

The skydiver **decelerates** while still moving downwards; the acceleration is upwards. As the speed falls, drag falls, the deceleration decreases, and a new, **lower terminal velocity** is reached when drag again equals 736 N. On the velocity-time graph this appears as a steep drop that curves into a second, lower horizontal line.

A common error is to say the skydiver "moves upwards" when the parachute opens. The velocity stays downwards; only the acceleration is upwards.

## Throwing a ball upwards with air resistance

A 0.15 kg ball (weight 1.47 N) is thrown vertically upwards. At one moment its speed gives a drag of 0.40 N.

```
going up (drag acts down, with weight):   a = (1.47 + 0.40) / 0.15 = 12.5 m s^-2 downwards
coming down (drag acts up):               a = (1.47 - 0.40) / 0.15 = 7.1 m s^-2 downwards
```

So, compared with motion without air resistance:

- the upward deceleration is greater than g, so the ball **reaches a lower maximum height**;
- the downward acceleration is less than g, so the descent takes **longer than the ascent**;
- the ball returns to the thrower's hand at a **lower speed** than it was thrown, because energy has been transferred to the air throughout.

At the top of its flight the ball is momentarily at rest, so the drag is zero and the acceleration is exactly g.

## Terminal velocity in other situations

**Horizontal motion with a constant driving force.** A car of mass 1000 kg has a constant driving force of 2400 N. At 10 m s⁻¹ the total resistive force is 600 N:

```
a = (2400 - 600) / 1000 = 1.8 m s^-2
```

As speed rises, the resistive force rises, the acceleration falls, and the car reaches its maximum (terminal) speed when the resistive force equals 2400 N.

**A ball-bearing in oil.** A steel ball released in a tall tube of oil reaches terminal velocity within a short distance, because the viscous drag in a liquid is large compared with air resistance at the same speed. In a liquid, upthrust also acts upwards, so at terminal velocity weight = upthrust + drag.

**Heavier versus lighter.** Two balls of the same size and shape but different mass fall through air. The heavier one reaches a **higher** terminal velocity, because it must fall faster before the drag grows large enough to balance its larger weight.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying the acceleration is constant until terminal velocity.** It decreases continuously from g.
- **Describing terminal velocity as "no forces acting".** Weight and drag both act; the resultant is zero.
- **Thinking the skydiver rises when the parachute opens.**
- **Forgetting that drag reverses direction** when a thrown ball turns at the top.
- **Quoting a formula for drag.** The syllabus requires only the qualitative model.
- **Missing upthrust** for an object falling through a liquid.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** the motion: say what happens to velocity and acceleration, in order, with the forces.
- **Explain** the motion: link each change to the forces. A complete chain: speed increases → drag increases → resultant force decreases → acceleration decreases → terminal velocity when drag equals weight.
- **Sketch** a velocity-time graph: mark the initial gradient (g), the curve flattening, the terminal velocity and, if a parachute opens, the second lower level.
- **Calculate** the acceleration at an instant: resultant force from the values given, then a = F/m.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the acceleration zero at terminal velocity?**
Yes. The resultant force is zero, so the acceleration is zero and the velocity is constant.

**Why does a skydiver slow down when the parachute opens?**
At the same speed the open parachute has a much larger drag, which exceeds the weight, so the resultant force and acceleration are upwards until a lower terminal velocity is reached.

**Do I need a formula for drag in Cambridge AS Physics?**
No. A simple model where drag increases as speed increases is sufficient.
