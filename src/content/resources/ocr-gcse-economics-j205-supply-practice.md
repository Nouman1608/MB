These questions cover section 2.3, Supply, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. The specification is not tiered. All firms, markets and figures are invented. Revise first with the [2.3 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply/) or the [2.3 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by supply. **[2]**

**2.** The table shows weekly supply of greetings cards by three firms.

| Price (£) | Firm A | Firm B | Firm C |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 10 | 5 |
| 2 | 40 | 30 | 15 |
| 3 | 60 | 50 | 25 |

Calculate market supply at each price. **[3]**

**3.** For each event, state whether there is a movement along or a shift of the supply curve for milk, and its direction.

**(a)** The price of milk rises. **[1]**
**(b)** The cost of cattle feed rises. **[1]**

**4.** Explain how new technology in car factories is likely to affect the supply of cars. **[2]**

**5.** The price of a garden chair rises from £5 to £6 and the quantity supplied rises from 1,000 to 1,050. Calculate the price elasticity of supply. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the supply of hotel rooms in a seaside town is likely to be price inelastic in the short run. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** The table shows the monthly market for coffee beans in an invented region, in kilograms.

| Price per kg (£) | Demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 1,000 | 600 |
| 4 | 900 | 700 |
| 5 | 800 | 800 |
| 6 | 700 | 900 |

A drought reduces supply by 200 kg at every price.

**(a)** State the original equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** State the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in the equilibrium price. **[1]**
**(d)** Analyse the consequences of the drought for consumers and producers. **[4]**

**8.** The government places an indirect tax on sugary drinks. Explain how this affects the supply of sugary drinks. **[3]**

**9.** Describe a perfectly elastic and a perfectly inelastic supply curve, and give an example of a situation where each might apply. **[4]**

**10.** A toy maker sees the price of its best-selling toy rise by 15%. In the first month, its output rises by 3%. After a year, output is 24% higher than before the price rise.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of supply in the first month and after a year. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the two values differ. **[2]**

**11.** Many new firms enter the market for electric scooters. Analyse the effects on consumers and producers. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the importance of price elasticity of supply for consumers and producers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell [1] at each price over a given period of time [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Supply is about sellers. Mixing in buyers' willingness is a frequent confusion.

**2.** £1: 20 + 10 + 5 = **35** [1]. £2: 40 + 30 + 15 = **85** [1]. £3: 60 + 50 + 25 = **135** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the addition at each price; it makes the working easy to credit.

**3. (a)** Movement along the curve: an extension in supply [1].
**(b)** Shift to the left: a decrease in supply [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A cost change shifts the curve; only the good's own price moves along it.

**4.** New technology raises productivity and lowers the cost of producing each car [1], so firms are willing to supply more at every price and the supply curve shifts right [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the mechanism (lower cost per unit), not just the direction.

**5.** % change in price = +20% and % change in quantity = +5% [1]. PES = 5 ÷ 20 = **0.25** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* PES is positive. A negative answer suggests the demand formula has been used by mistake.

**6.** The number of rooms is fixed by the size of existing hotels [1], and building new hotels takes years, so supply cannot rise quickly when prices rise in the holiday season [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the answer to time and capacity, the two main causes of inelastic supply.

**7. (a)** £5 and 800 kg [1].
**(b)** £6 and 700 kg [1].
**(c)** (6 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = **+20%** [1].
**(d)** Consumers pay 20% more per kilogram [1] and buy 100 kg less [1]. Producers whose crops survived receive a higher price, and total revenue rises from 5 × 800 = £4,000 to 6 × 700 = £4,200 [1]; however, growers whose crops failed have less to sell and may lose income [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the market as a whole from individual producers: the average hides winners and losers.

**8.** An indirect tax adds to the cost of each drink sold [1], so producers earn less profit at every price [1] and the supply curve shifts to the left [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The tax is paid by the seller to the government, so it acts like an extra cost per unit.

**9.** A perfectly elastic supply curve is horizontal at one price [1]: any quantity is supplied at that price, as might happen for a product that a very large number of firms can produce at the same cost [1]. A perfectly inelastic supply curve is vertical [1]: the quantity cannot change whatever the price, such as the seats at a sold-out concert [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each curve needs its shape and a fitting example.

**10. (a)** First month: 3 ÷ 15 = **0.2** [1]. After a year: 24 ÷ 15 = **1.6** [1].
**(b)** In the first month the firm is limited by its existing workers and machines [1]; over a year it can hire and train staff and buy more equipment, so supply becomes elastic [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Time is the main reason PES rises. Spare capacity or stocks could also be mentioned.

**11.** More firms means supply increases and the supply curve shifts right [1], so at the old price there is a surplus [1] and the price falls while the quantity sold rises [1]. Consumers gain from lower prices and wider choice [1]. Existing producers may lose market share and earn lower profit per scooter [1], although efficient firms that cut costs may still grow [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Follow the full chain from the shift to the surplus, then to the new price, before discussing who gains.

**12.** For producers, elastic supply lets a firm raise output quickly when demand rises [1], earning extra revenue and winning sales before rivals [1]. With inelastic supply the firm cannot respond, so customers may go to competitors or imports [1]. For consumers, when supply is inelastic a rise in demand mainly pushes up price [1], as with seasonal food or event tickets [1], whereas elastic supply keeps prices more stable [1]. However, making supply more elastic, by holding stocks or keeping spare capacity, costs money [1]. Judgement: PES matters most in markets where demand changes often or suddenly; there, the cost of spare capacity is worth paying, while in stable markets it may not be [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that names the type of market where PES matters most shows genuine evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Negative PES answers.
- Shifts with no explanation of the cost or capacity change behind them.
- Treating a price change as a shift.
- Ignoring the difference between the short run and the long run.
- Discussing consumers only when producers are also named.
