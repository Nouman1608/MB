Sub-topic 4.3 Acid rain in the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029 follows a pattern you will see across Topic 4: formation (4.3.1), impacts (4.3.2), strategies (4.3.3) and a discussion of their benefits and limitations (4.3.4). The content is compact, so marks depend on precise chemistry in the formation answer and on clear cause-and-effect reasoning in the impacts answer.

## How acid rain forms

The syllabus names two pollutant gases and three sources.

### Sulfur dioxide

- **From combustion of fossil fuels that contain sulfur compounds.** Coal and oil contain sulfur; when they burn in power stations, factories and ships, the sulfur combines with oxygen to form sulfur dioxide.
- **From volcanic gases.** Volcanoes release sulfur dioxide naturally, so some acid rain is not caused by people.

### Oxides of nitrogen

- **When nitrogen from the atmosphere reacts with oxygen in the high temperature of vehicle engines.** Air is 78% nitrogen. Normally nitrogen is unreactive, but at the very high temperatures inside an engine it combines with oxygen to form oxides of nitrogen, which leave in the exhaust.

The source of the nitrogen is the air, not the fuel. This is a frequent error: students write that petrol "contains nitrogen".

### Reaction in the atmosphere

Sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen **react with oxygen and water vapour in the atmosphere to form acid rain**. Sulfur dioxide produces sulfuric acid and oxides of nitrogen produce nitric acid, which fall in rain, snow and mist. Because the gases can be carried by wind for long distances before they react and fall, acid rain often lands far from where the pollution was released, sometimes in another country.

Even unpolluted rain is slightly acidic, because carbon dioxide dissolves in it, but acid rain is distinctly more acidic. The pH scale is logarithmic, so each fall of one pH unit means ten times the acidity.

## The six impacts

| Impact | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Acidification of bodies of water | Lakes and rivers become more acidic | Acid rain falls directly and drains in from surrounding land |
| Reduced fish populations | Fewer fish, and some lakes lose fish altogether | Eggs and young fish fail to develop in acidic water; acid water also releases aluminium from soils, which damages fish gills |
| Effect on aquatic food webs | Food chains break down | Acid-sensitive organisms such as some insects, snails and plankton die, so animals that eat them, including fish and birds, lose food |
| Acidification of soils | Soil pH falls | Acid washes out nutrients such as calcium and magnesium and releases aluminium that damages roots |
| Damage to crops and vegetation | Plant defoliation and reduced crop yields | Acid damages leaves directly; with poorer soils and damaged roots, plants grow weakly and lose leaves |
| Damage to buildings | Stone and metal are eaten away | Limestone and marble react with acid; metal structures corrode faster |

Notice the link between soils and water. Acid soils release aluminium, and the aluminium drains into lakes where it harms fish. Using that link in an explanation shows understanding beyond a list.

## The three strategies

### Flue-gas desulfurisation

Waste gases from a power station chimney (the **flue**) are passed through a mixture containing **calcium carbonate** (limestone) or **calcium oxide** (lime). The sulfur dioxide reacts with it and is removed before the gases leave the chimney. The product is a calcium compound that can be used to make plaster, so the waste can have a use.

### Catalytic converters

A catalytic converter is fitted in a vehicle's exhaust system. It contains a catalyst that converts **oxides of nitrogen** back into harmless nitrogen before the exhaust gases leave the vehicle. It tackles vehicle emissions only; it does nothing about sulfur dioxide from power stations.

### Transport policies

Policies reduce the number of vehicle engines running, and so the oxides of nitrogen produced. Examples include better public transport, cycle lanes, charges for driving in city centres, and rules that require cleaner vehicles.

## Benefits and limitations

| Strategy | Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Flue-gas desulfurisation | Removes most of the sulfur dioxide from large sources such as coal-fired power stations; the calcium product can be sold | Expensive to fit and run; needs large amounts of limestone, which must be quarried; reacting limestone releases carbon dioxide; does not remove oxides of nitrogen |
| Catalytic converters | Cut oxides of nitrogen from every vehicle fitted with one | Add cost to vehicles; older vehicles may not have them; benefits are offset if vehicle numbers keep rising; no effect on sulfur dioxide |
| Transport policies | Reduce oxides of nitrogen and also cut congestion and carbon dioxide | Depend on people changing habits; public transport needs investment; may be unpopular |

A general limitation is that acid rain crosses borders. A country that cleans up its own power stations may still receive acid rain from a neighbour, so cooperation between countries makes the strategies more effective. Another is that strategies stop new damage, but acidified lakes and soils take a long time to recover.

## Worked example 1: effect of desulfurisation

*Figures invented for practice.* A coal-fired power station releases 12,000 tonnes of sulfur dioxide a year. Flue-gas desulfurisation equipment removes 90% of it.

1. Sulfur dioxide removed = 12,000 × 0.90 = 10,800 tonnes.
2. Sulfur dioxide still released = 12,000 − 10,800 = 1,200 tonnes.

The equipment is very effective, but 1,200 tonnes is still released each year, and the station's oxides of nitrogen are unaffected.

## Worked example 2: reading lake data

*Figures invented for practice.* A lake downwind of an industrial region was surveyed every ten years.

| Year | Mean pH | Number of fish species |
|---|---|---|
| 1970 | 6.5 | 9 |
| 1980 | 5.8 | 6 |
| 1990 | 5.2 | 3 |
| 2000 | 4.9 | 1 |

A full description uses figures: pH fell by 1.6 units, from 6.5 to 4.9, and the number of fish species fell from 9 to 1. The explanation links the two: as the lake became more acidic, eggs and young of sensitive species failed and aluminium damaged gills, so species disappeared one by one. A pH fall of 1.6 units means the water is about forty times more acidic, because each unit is a factor of ten. A careful answer adds that the data show a relationship but do not prove the cause on their own.

## Common mistakes

- Naming carbon dioxide as the cause of acid rain. The syllabus names sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen.
- Saying the nitrogen comes from fuel. It comes from the air, reacting at high temperature in engines.
- Leaving out the final step of formation: reaction with oxygen and water vapour in the atmosphere.
- Writing that catalytic converters remove sulfur dioxide.
- Treating acid rain as the same problem as the enhanced greenhouse effect or ozone depletion.

## Exam technique

**Describe** the formation by naming each gas, its source and the reaction in the atmosphere, in that order. **Describe** impacts by giving the effect and what is affected; where marks allow, add a reason. **Describe** strategies by naming what each removes and how. **Discuss** needs benefits and limitations, with a judgement that might mention which strategy suits the main source in the question, such as power stations or traffic.
