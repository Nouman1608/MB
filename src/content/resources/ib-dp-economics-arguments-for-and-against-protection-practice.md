## Questions

**1.** Define dumping. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the infant industry argument for trade protection. **[3]**

**3.** (calculator-free) A fictional firm, Orlin Cycles, makes bicycles in Tarvia. Imported bicycles sell for 60. Its average cost is 90 at 5 000 bicycles a year, 75 at 20 000 and 45 at 50 000.

**(a)** Calculate the loss per bicycle and the total loss at 5 000 bicycles, if it sells at the import price. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the smallest tariff, as a percentage of the import price, that would let the firm break even at 5 000 and at 20 000 bicycles. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the tariff should be temporary. **[2]**

**4.** (calculator allowed) The fictional country Pellia imports fabric at a world price of 50 a unit. After a tariff of t per cent, import volume is M = 600 − 8t.

**(a)** Calculate tariff revenue when t = 20. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate revenue when t = 40 and when t = 50. **[2]**
**(c)** State which of the rates 20, 40 and 50 gives the highest revenue. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why revenue falls at the highest rate, and why this matters for a government that relies on tariffs. **[2]**

**5.** (calculator allowed) Pellia exports goods worth 1 200 and imports goods worth 1 500.

**(a)** State the balance on goods. **[1]**
**(b)** Imports fall by 10% after a tariff. Calculate the new balance. **[2]**
**(c)** Pellia's trading partners retaliate and exports fall by 6%. Calculate the balance after retaliation. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain one reason why protection may not remove a deficit permanently. **[2]**

**6.** (calculator allowed) Pellia raises the price of imported cloth from 40 to 46 with a tariff, and buyers purchase 2.5 million metres a year at the new price. The policy saves 120 jobs, each paid 21 000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the tariff as a percentage of the original price. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the extra cost to buyers. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost per job saved. **[1]**
**(d)** Comment on whether the policy is justified on jobs grounds. **[3]**

**7.** Explain two different arguments a government might use to restrict imports of a food product. **[4]**

**8.** (calculator-free) Two fictional countries, Kelmar and Ostia, each choose free trade or protection. The table shows national benefit as (Kelmar, Ostia).

| | Ostia free trade | Ostia protects |
|---|---|---|
| **Kelmar free trade** | (7, 7) | (4, 9) |
| **Kelmar protects** | (9, 4) | (5, 5) |

**(a)** Each country picks the option that gives it more whatever the other does. Identify the outcome. **[2]**
**(b)** Compare it with the free trade outcome and explain what the table shows about retaliation. **[3]**
**(c)** State one reason why the real world may differ from the table. **[1]**

**9.** Explain how a tariff on imported steel could reduce the export competitiveness of a country's car makers. **[4]**

**10.** Mirova is a fictional economically least developed country that earns most of its export revenue from one crop. Explain how the diversification argument could justify protection for a new manufacturing industry. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss whether protecting jobs is a good reason for a country to restrict imports of footwear. **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that free trade is better than protection for an economically least developed country. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Dumping is selling in a foreign market at a price below the cost of production [1], or below the price charged in the producer's home market [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition needs the comparison point; "selling cheaply abroad" without cost or home price leaves the idea incomplete.

**2.** A new industry has high costs because output is small and workers are inexperienced [1]. Established foreign rivals are more efficient, so it cannot compete [1]. Temporary protection lets it grow until it can compete unaided [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" asks for a brief account, so three short linked statements fit better than a long paragraph.

**3. (a)** Loss per bicycle = 90 − 60 = **30** [1]. Total loss = 30 × 5 000 = **150 000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At 5 000: 30 ÷ 60 = **50%** [1]. At 20 000: (75 − 60) ÷ 60 = 15 ÷ 60 = **25%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At 50 000 bicycles the cost (45) is below the import price (60), so protection is no longer needed [1]. A permanent tariff would raise prices and weaken the incentive to cut costs [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In a calculator-free question, show each division so your method can be followed even if the arithmetic slips.

**4. (a)** M = 600 − 8 × 20 = 440 [1]. Revenue = 50 × 0.20 × 440 = **4 400** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At 40: M = 280, revenue = 50 × 0.40 × 280 = **5 600** [1]. At 50: M = 200, revenue = 50 × 0.50 × 200 = **5 000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** **40%** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** At the highest rate imports fall further, so the tax base shrinks faster than the rate rises [1]. A government that depends on tariffs cannot raise revenue endlessly by raising the rate [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The common slip is to use the pre-tariff quantity; find the new quantity first.

**5. (a)** 1 200 − 1 500 = **a deficit of 300** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** New imports = 1 500 × 0.90 = 1 350 [1]. Balance = 1 200 − 1 350 = **a deficit of 150** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** New exports = 1 200 × 0.94 = 1 128 [1]. Balance = 1 128 − 1 350 = **a deficit of 222** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Partners can retaliate, which cuts exports [1], so the gain is partly lost, as part (c) shows. Or the cause of the deficit may be low competitiveness or an overvalued currency, which a tariff does not change [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain one reason" is better answered by developing one reason fully than by naming two briefly.

**6. (a)** 6 ÷ 40 = **15%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 6 × 2 500 000 = **15 000 000** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 15 000 000 ÷ 120 = **125 000** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** The cost per job saved (125 000) is far above the 21 000 wage, by 104 000 [1]. On jobs grounds alone this is poor value, since the money could fund retraining instead [1]. A fuller judgement also counts jobs lost in cloth-using industries and through retaliation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Comment" asks for a reasoned view tied to your numbers; quote the figure from (c).

**7.** Health and safety: imports may carry contamination that consumers cannot spot when they buy [1]; inspecting or restricting them reduces the risk [1]. National security: food is a basic need, and a foreign supplier may cut supply in a crisis [1]; protecting home farmers keeps supply available [1]. Other arguments are acceptable if explained. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two arguments need two different mechanisms; one mechanism under two labels is still one argument.

**8. (a)** Whatever Ostia does, Kelmar gains more by protecting (9 against 7, and 5 against 4) [1]; the same is true for Ostia. The outcome is **both protect, (5, 5)** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Free trade gives (7, 7), so each country is better off than at (5, 5) [1]. Each protects to gain at the other's expense, but the other's response leaves both worse off [1], which shows how retaliation can cancel the gain from protection [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Countries can negotiate and agree to avoid barriers, or the payoffs may be unknown [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the actual numbers from the table; a bare claim that retaliation is bad leaves the comparison unfinished.

**9.** Steel is an input for car makers [1]. A tariff raises the price of imported steel [1] and raises the cost of making cars [1]. Higher costs make exported cars dearer, so fewer are sold abroad [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Write each link in the chain; do not jump from "tariff" to "fewer exports".

**10.** Dependence on one crop means a fall in its world price cuts export earnings and incomes [1]. Protection for a new manufacturing industry can shelter it from imports while it develops [1]. If it succeeds, the country has a second source of output and exports [1], so a price fall in the crop hurts less [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Anchor your answer to the stated context: dependence on one crop is the risk to build on.

**11.** Model plan; real papers are marked against markbands, not point by point. Indicative points:
For: imports can displace footwear workers, and a tariff raises the import price so domestic firms keep sales [1]. Jobs support workers' incomes and local communities [1]. Unemployment also costs the government benefit payments and lost tax [1]. An example, such as a fictional town where most jobs depend on one footwear factory, adds weight when explained [1].
Against: consumers pay higher prices for footwear [1]. Cost per job saved may exceed the wage [1]. Jobs may be lost in shops, importers and exporters, and through retaliation [1]. Resources stay in a less efficient industry [1].
Evaluation: it depends on the cost per job, whether protection is temporary, whether retraining could do the same at lower cost, and whether retaliation is likely [1]. A conclusion that follows, such as "justified only if temporary and the cost per job is close to the wage" [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" asks for a balanced review and a conclusion; a final paragraph that only repeats earlier points does not weigh anything.

**12.** Model plan; real papers are marked against markbands, not point by point. Indicative points:
For protection: infant industries may need shelter to reach scale [1]. Diversification away from a few primary exports reduces risk [1]. Tariffs may be an easy revenue source where income tax is hard to collect [1]. Dumping from larger economies may destroy new firms [1].
For free trade: access to larger markets allows scale economies [1]. Cheaper imported machinery lowers costs [1]. Competition forces firms to improve efficiency [1]. Retaliation would be costly for a country that depends on exports [1].
Evaluation: the answer depends on whether protection is temporary and targeted [1], on the strength of institutions that must run it [1], and on alternatives such as subsidies [1]. A supported judgement, for example "mostly free trade, with limited protection for specific new industries" [1] is needed. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided essay shows knowledge but not weighing; leave time for conditions and a justified judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining dumping as "low prices" with no comparison point.
- Using the pre-tariff quantity, or forgetting to turn a percentage tariff into a per-unit amount, in revenue answers.
- Ignoring retaliation in balance of payments answers.
- Listing arguments instead of weighing them against each other.
- A conclusion that restates the arguments and takes no position.
