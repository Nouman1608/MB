## Structure 1.1.1 Pure substances and mixtures

### Definitions

- **Element:** one type of atom only; cannot be broken down chemically into simpler substances. Can be atoms (Ar), molecules (N₂, P₄) or a giant lattice (diamond).
- **Compound:** atoms of different elements chemically bonded in a **fixed ratio**. Separating it into elements needs a chemical reaction.
- **Mixture:** two or more elements and/or compounds, **not chemically bonded**, in **no fixed ratio**. Separable by physical methods.
- **Homogeneous mixture:** uniform composition, one phase (e.g. vinegar, bronze, filtered air).
- **Heterogeneous mixture:** non-uniform, two or more visible phases (e.g. muddy water, a suspension of flour in water).

### Must-know distinctions

| Element or compound | Mixture |
|---|---|
| Fixed composition | Variable composition |
| Sharp melting and boiling points | Melts and boils over a range |
| Compound's properties differ from its elements' | Components keep their own properties |
| Chemical change needed to separate | Physical methods separate it |

Quick reminders:

- Nitrogen and hydrogen in a cylinder form a mixture in any ratio, separable by liquefying and boiling off each gas. Ammonia, NH₃, made from them has a fixed 1:3 ratio of N to H atoms, a single boiling point, and properties neither gas has, such as a pungent smell and very high solubility in water.
- An alloy is a mixture, even though it has metallic bonding.
- Diatomic and polyatomic molecules of one element (H₂, O₃, S₈) are elements, not compounds.

### Separation methods in one table

| You want to... | Use | Key physical property |
|---|---|---|
| Get one solid into solution, leaving another | Solvation (dissolving) | Solubility in the solvent |
| Remove an insoluble solid from a liquid | Filtration | Solid vs dissolved particles |
| Get a dissolved solid back | Evaporation (or crystallization) | Solvent is volatile, solute is not |
| Purify an impure solid | Recrystallization | Solubility rises with temperature |
| Get pure solvent from a solution | Simple distillation | Large difference in boiling point |
| Separate miscible liquids | Fractional distillation | Different boiling points |
| Separate and identify small amounts of dissolved substances | Paper chromatography | Distribution between stationary and mobile phases |

### Method in steps: choosing a separation method

1. Identify what you want to keep: a solid, a liquid, or several dissolved substances.
2. Find the physical property that differs between the parts: solubility, particle size, boiling point or affinity for a solvent.
3. Is one part insoluble? Dissolve, then filter. Is the wanted part dissolved? Evaporate or crystallize.
4. Two liquids that mix? Fractional distillation. A small sample to identify? Chromatography.
5. Several steps are often combined, for example solvation, then filtration, then evaporation.

### Method in steps: fractional distillation

1. Heat the mixture in a flask fitted with a fractionating column.
2. Vapour condenses and re-vaporizes many times up the column; the component with the lower boiling point reaches the top first.
3. The thermometer at the top stays steady while one pure component distils; collect each fraction in a separate receiver as the reading changes.

### Method in steps: recrystallization

1. Dissolve the impure solid in the **minimum** of **hot** solvent.
2. Filter hot to remove insoluble impurities.
3. Cool so the product crystallizes; soluble impurities stay in solution.
4. Filter, wash with a little **cold** solvent, dry.

### Method in steps: paper chromatography

1. Pencil base line; small, concentrated spots on it.
2. Solvent level **below** the base line; lid on the tank.
3. End the run with the solvent short of the top edge, and mark the furthest point it reached at once.
4. Rf = (base line to spot centre) ÷ (base line to solvent front), so it always lies between 0 and 1.
5. Same Rf as a reference in the same conditions suggests the same substance. A single spot suggests a pure substance (in that solvent).

Worked reminder: the solvent front moves 9.0 cm and a spot moves 2.7 cm, so Rf = 2.7 ÷ 9.0 = 0.30. A second spot at 8.1 cm has Rf = 0.90: it is more attracted to the mobile phase (the solvent) than the first spot is, so it travels further.

## Structure 1.1.2 Kinetic molecular theory

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spacing | Touching, ordered | Touching, disordered | Far apart |
| Motion | Vibration only | Vibration, rotation, translation | Rapid, random translation |
| Shape; volume | Keeps own shape; own volume | Takes container's shape; own volume | Fills whole container |
| Forces between particles | Strong | Weaker | Negligible |

### State symbols

Use (aq) for a solution in water, and (s), (l) or (g) for a solid, liquid or gas. Example: CaCO₃(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Ca(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).

### Changes of state

| Name | Change | Endothermic or exothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | s → l | endothermic |
| Freezing | l → s | exothermic |
| Vaporization (evaporation, boiling) | l → g | endothermic |
| Condensation | g → l | exothermic |
| Sublimation | s → g | endothermic |
| Deposition | g → s | exothermic |

- **Evaporation:** surface only, below the boiling point, the most energetic particles escape, so the remaining liquid cools.
- **Boiling:** throughout the liquid, at a fixed temperature for a given external pressure.
- Evaporation can happen at any temperature at which the liquid exists; boiling happens only once the boiling point is reached.
- Iodine crystals warmed gently in a beaker give a purple vapour that forms crystals again on a cold surface: sublimation, then deposition.

## Structure 1.1.3 Temperature and average kinetic energy

### Key facts

- Temperature in kelvin is a measure of the **average kinetic energy** of the particles. Double the kelvin temperature, double the average kinetic energy.
- One kelvin = one Celsius degree in size. Only the zero point differs.
- 0 K is absolute zero. Kelvin temperatures cannot be negative and have no degree sign.

| Conversion | Formula |
|---|---|
| Celsius → kelvin | T/K = T/°C + 273.15 |
| Kelvin → Celsius | T/°C = T/K − 273.15 |

Worked reminder: a freezer at −18 °C is at −18 + 273.15 = 255.15 K. A temperature change of 30 °C is a change of 30 K: differences are the same on both scales.

### Heating curve in brief

- Slope: energy raises average kinetic energy, so temperature rises.
- Plateau: energy overcomes the attractions between particles; average kinetic energy and temperature stay constant; two states coexist.
- Lower plateau = melting point; upper plateau = boiling point.
- Boiling plateau longer than melting plateau (more energy to separate particles fully).
- Cooling curve: same in reverse; a pure liquid freezes at one temperature, a mixture over a range.

Worked reminder: a pure solid heated steadily shows plateaus at 44 °C and 280 °C. Melting point = 44 + 273.15 = 317.15 K; boiling point = 280 + 273.15 = 553.15 K. At 100 °C the substance is a liquid, (l).

## Quick self-test

1. Classify: (a) bronze; (b) P₄; (c) glucose; (d) oil and water shaken together.
2. Name the change of state when water vapour forms ice crystals directly on a cold surface.
3. Convert −40 °C to kelvin.
4. Convert 400 K to °C.
5. Which method separates water from a copper(II) sulfate solution, keeping the water?
6. A spot moves 4.8 cm when the solvent front moves 6.4 cm. Calculate Rf.
7. Give the state symbol for sodium chloride dissolved in water.
8. The kelvin temperature of a gas rises from 200 K to 600 K. By what factor does the average kinetic energy change?
9. Explain why the temperature is constant while a pure solid melts.
10. Why is a melting point measured over a range a sign of an impure sample?
11. Heating from 0 °C to 273 °C: by roughly what factor does the average kinetic energy increase?
12. Which method would you use to separate two miscible liquids that boil at 65 °C and 118 °C?

### Answers

1. (a) homogeneous mixture (alloy); (b) element; (c) compound; (d) heterogeneous mixture.
2. Deposition.
3. −40 + 273.15 = **233.15 K** (233 K).
4. 400 − 273.15 = **126.85 °C** (127 °C).
5. Simple distillation.
6. 4.8 ÷ 6.4 = **0.75**.
7. NaCl(aq).
8. 600 ÷ 200 = **3**: it triples.
9. The energy supplied overcomes the attractions between particles instead of raising their average kinetic energy, so the temperature does not rise until all the solid has melted.
10. A pure substance melts at one sharp temperature. Impurities spread melting over a range (and usually lower it).
11. 273.15 K → 546.15 K, so the factor is **2** (it doubles).
12. Fractional distillation; the liquid boiling at 65 °C distils first.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a mixture as "chemically combined" or a compound as "two substances mixed". Use the words bonded, fixed ratio and physical separation.
- Classifying an alloy as a compound, or O₂ as a compound.
- Mixing up homogeneous and heterogeneous, or giving an example without saying why (one phase vs more than one).
- Writing "dissolve in hot water" for recrystallization without the word minimum, or forgetting the cooling step.
- Drawing a chromatography base line in ink, or placing it below the solvent level.
- Measuring Rf to the end of the paper instead of to the solvent front.
- Using (aq) for a pure liquid, or leaving state symbols off a physical change.
- Saying the temperature rises during a change of state.
- Comparing kinetic energies with Celsius values; always convert to kelvin first.
- Writing "°K" or giving a negative kelvin temperature.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a homogeneous and a heterogeneous mixture?**
A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition and one phase, such as vinegar or bronze. A heterogeneous mixture is not uniform and has two or more visible phases, such as muddy water.

**How do you choose a separation method?**
Identify what you want to keep and the physical property that differs between the parts. Dissolve then filter for an insoluble solid, evaporate or crystallize for a dissolved solid, use fractional distillation for two miscible liquids, and chromatography for a small sample to identify.

**How do you convert between Celsius and kelvin?**
Add 273.15 to a Celsius temperature to get kelvin, and subtract 273.15 to go back. One kelvin is the same size as one Celsius degree; only the zero point differs.

**What happens on the plateaus of a heating curve?**
On a plateau the energy supplied overcomes the attractions between particles, so the average kinetic energy and temperature stay constant while two states are present. The boiling plateau is usually longer than the melting plateau.
