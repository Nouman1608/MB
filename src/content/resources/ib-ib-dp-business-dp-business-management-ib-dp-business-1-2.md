This guide teaches section 1.2 (Types of business entities) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL. The guide asks for the distinction between the private and the public sectors; the main features of sole traders, partnerships, privately held companies and publicly held companies; the main features of for-profit social enterprises (private sector companies, public sector companies and cooperatives); and the main features of a non-profit social enterprise, the non-governmental organization (NGO). The entity types are listed at AO3, so you must be able to weigh up which form suits a given organization, not just describe each one.

The feature tables are in the [1.1 and 1.2 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-what-is-a-business-and-types-of-business-entities/). This page builds the judgement on top of them: what liability means in money, how to choose a form using clear criteria, why a publicly held company can be taken over, and the trade-offs facing social enterprises. Recall the content with the [1.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-2-revision-notes/) and practise with the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-1-2-practice/). All organizations and figures are invented; money is in dollars. Legal details differ between countries, so features are described in general terms.

## Private and public sector: why the split matters

The private sector is owned by individuals or groups; the public sector is owned and controlled by government. The split affects three things you can use in an answer.

| Aspect | Private sector | Public sector |
|---|---|---|
| Main aim | Usually profit, sometimes with a social purpose | Usually a public service, such as affordable transport or water |
| Who it answers to | Owners and customers | Government, and through it taxpayers and voters |
| Finance | Owners' capital, loans, retained profit | Government funding, plus any revenue it earns |

Governments often keep an organization in the public sector when the service is essential, when one supplier serves everyone (such as a water network), or when a private owner might cut provision to unprofitable areas. Do not confuse the public **sector** with a publicly held **company**: the second is privately owned, by shareholders.

## Liability in numbers

Liability is the owners' responsibility for the debts of the business. Showing it with figures makes an analysis much sharper.

**Worked example 1: Brannock Tiles.** Lena runs Brannock Tiles. Trade falls, and the business closes owing suppliers and the bank 80,000. Its van, stock and equipment sell for 35,000.

- **As a sole trader** (unlimited liability, in general): the 35,000 pays part of the debt. Lena is personally responsible for the other 45,000, so her savings, car or even home may be at risk.
- **As a privately held company** (limited liability): the company, a separate legal entity, owes the debt. If Lena invested 20,000 for her shares, that 20,000 is lost, but creditors cannot claim her personal assets for the remaining 45,000.

The lesson for analysis: limited liability caps the owner's loss at the amount invested, which is why it makes investors and owners more willing to take risks. The cost is the extra paperwork and disclosure that companies face, and lenders may ask a small company's owners for a personal guarantee anyway, which partly removes the protection.

## Choosing a form: use criteria, not lists

When a question asks which entity suits an organization, compare two realistic options against criteria drawn from the case.

| Criterion | Questions to ask of the case |
|---|---|
| Risk and liability | How large are the possible debts? Would the owners lose personal assets? |
| Finance needed | Can the owners fund it themselves, or do they need outside investors? |
| Control | Do the founders want to keep all decisions? |
| Privacy | Would publishing accounts reveal information to rivals? |
| Continuity | Must the organization survive the departure or death of an owner? |
| Purpose | Is profit the main aim, or a social or environmental mission? |

**Worked example 2: Sefton & Okoye Architects.** Two architects plan to work together. They need 30,000, which they can provide themselves. Their main risk is a claim from a client for a design fault.

- A **partnership** is simple, private and lets them share decisions as equals. But in general each partner may be liable for the other's mistakes, and a large client claim could reach their personal assets.
- A **privately held company** limits their liability to their investment and continues if one leaves, but requires registration and published information.

Judgement: because the main risk is a large claim that could exceed what they invested, a privately held company is likely to be better, provided the extra administration is affordable. If they could insure fully against claims, the simpler partnership would become more attractive. Naming that condition is what lifts the answer to evaluation.

## Publicly held companies: control and takeover

Shares in a publicly held company are traded on a stock exchange, so anyone can buy them. This brings three consequences.

1. **Large-scale finance.** Selling shares to the public can raise far more than private owners could provide.
2. **Loss of control.** Each new share issued dilutes existing owners' voting power.
3. **Takeover risk.** A rival can buy enough shares on the market to gain control, possibly against the wishes of the founders and directors.

Because published accounts are open to everyone, managers also face pressure for short-term results each reporting period, which can discourage long-term investment. These points let you argue both sides when a case company considers "going public".

## For-profit social enterprises

A social enterprise trades to achieve a social or environmental aim. The guide's for-profit group contains three types.

| Type | Distinctive feature | Typical tension |
|---|---|---|
| Private sector company with a social purpose | Privately owned; profit is made and partly used for the mission | Investors may push for profit over purpose ("mission drift") |
| Public sector company | Government-owned, run commercially, serving the public interest | Political goals may conflict with efficiency |
| Cooperative | Owned and controlled by members, often one member, one vote | Democratic decisions can be slow; raising large sums is hard |

Cooperatives can be owned by workers, by customers or by producers who sell through them. Equal voting keeps power spread out, but it limits outside investment, because investors cannot buy control.

## The non-profit social enterprise: NGOs

An NGO is independent of government and does not distribute profit to owners. Any surplus is kept for the mission. Its main challenges follow from that:

- **Funding uncertainty**: donations and grants may not be renewed.
- **Accountability**: donors want evidence that money is used well, which adds reporting costs.
- **Measuring success**: impact (children taught, trees planted) is harder to measure than profit.

**Worked example 3: choosing a form for a refill shop.** Mirela wants to open a shop selling food and cleaning products without plastic packaging, to cut waste. She needs 60,000.

| Option | Likely funding | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Private sector company with a social purpose | Investors could supply all 60,000 | They will expect a return, which may push prices up |
| Customer cooperative | 300 members at 100 each = 30,000 | Leaves a 30,000 gap; decisions shared with members |
| NGO | Grants of 25,000, plus donations | Leaves a 35,000 gap unless donations are strong; funding may not recur |

A reasoned recommendation might be the cooperative plus a loan for the remaining 30,000, because members are also loyal customers and the shop earns revenue to repay the loan. The judgement depends on whether 300 members can really be found.

## Common mistakes

- Saying limited liability means owners lose nothing. They can lose what they invested.
- Mixing up the public sector with publicly held companies.
- Calling every social enterprise non-profit. The guide places cooperatives and two kinds of company in the for-profit group.
- Listing features of every type when the question needs a comparison of two.
- Recommending a form without saying what the recommendation depends on.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish** (AO2): state a clear difference between the two items, not two separate definitions.
- **Explain** (AO2): give a feature and its effect on the organization in the case.
- **Discuss**, **evaluate**, **recommend** (AO3): weigh at least two options using criteria from the case, then decide and give a condition.

Section 1.2 is especially useful for HL Paper 3, which is based on a social enterprise, so practise analysing organizations whose aim is not profit.
