## Reactivity 3.1.1 Acids donate protons, bases accept them

In the Brønsted–Lowry model, **acids** donate protons (H⁺ ions) and **bases** accept them. In water a proton bonds to a water molecule's lone pair, giving H₃O⁺. You may write it as H⁺(aq) or H₃O⁺(aq).

**Base or alkali?** An alkali is a soluble base: in water it releases OH⁻ ions, as sodium hydroxide does. Copper(II) oxide is a base, since it neutralizes acids, yet it does not dissolve, so it is not an alkali.

### Worked example 1: spotting the acid and the base

(a) HNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)

(b) HCOOH(aq) + CH₃NH₂(aq) → HCOO⁻(aq) + CH₃NH₃⁺(aq)

Follow the proton: the species that loses H⁺ is the acid; the one that gains it is the base.

(a) HNO₃ → NO₃⁻ loses a proton: **HNO₃ is the acid**. H₂O → H₃O⁺: **water is the base**.

(b) Methanoic acid loses a proton: **HCOOH is the acid**. Methylamine gains it: **CH₃NH₂ is the base**. No water or OH⁻ is involved, so the model reaches beyond aqueous hydroxides.

**Why the definition evolved.** Acidity was once linked to oxygen, until oxygen-free acids such as hydrogen chloride were studied. Arrhenius linked acids to H⁺ and bases to OH⁻ in water; Brønsted–Lowry dropped the need for water; Lewis (Reactivity 3.4) widened it to electron pairs.

## Reactivity 3.1.2 Conjugate acid–base pairs

Two species that differ by exactly one proton form a **conjugate acid–base pair**. Removing H⁺ gives the conjugate base and lowers the charge by one; adding H⁺ gives the conjugate acid and raises it by one.

### Worked example 2: the polyatomic anions

Give the conjugate acid of CO₃²⁻, PO₄³⁻, OH⁻ and CH₃COO⁻.

Add one H⁺ to each: **HCO₃⁻, HPO₄²⁻, H₂O, CH₃COOH**. Likewise SO₄²⁻ gives HSO₄⁻ and NO₃⁻ gives HNO₃.

Every acid–base equation contains two pairs. In example 1(b) they are HCOOH / HCOO⁻ and CH₃NH₃⁺ / CH₃NH₂.

## Reactivity 3.1.3 Species that can be both

A species that can donate or accept a proton is **amphiprotic**. It needs a proton to lose and a lone pair to take one. Water, HCO₃⁻, HSO₄⁻ and H₂PO₄⁻ are examples.

### Worked example 3: hydrogencarbonate both ways

(a) As an acid: HCO₃⁻(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)

(b) As a base: HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → H₂CO₃(aq) + H₂O(l). The H₂CO₃ breaks down to CO₂ and water, so hydrogencarbonates fizz with acids.

Water does this to itself: H₂O(l) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).

**Oxides.** Across period 3, oxides change from basic to acidic. Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) gives an alkali; SO₃(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₄(aq) gives an acid. Aluminium oxide reacts with both acids and bases (amphoteric). Oxides of nitrogen and sulfur from burning fuels form nitric and sulfuric acids in clouds, causing acid rain.

## Reactivity 3.1.4 Measuring acidity: the pH scale

- pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]
- [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ

The guide says these equations are in the data booklet. One pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺], and lower pH means more H⁺. A sketch of pH against [H⁺] is a curve, not a line: steep at low [H⁺], flattening as [H⁺] rises.

### Worked example 4: both directions

(a) [H⁺] = 0.0045 mol dm⁻³: pH = −log₁₀(0.0045) = **2.35**

(b) pH 3.60: [H⁺] = 10⁻³·⁶⁰ = **2.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³**

(c) Hydroiodic acid, 0.040 mol dm⁻³, is fully ionized: pH = 1.40. Dilute it tenfold and pH = 2.40. Each tenfold dilution of a strong acid raises pH by one.

**Measuring pH.** Universal indicator gives an estimate from a colour chart (red strongly acidic, green near neutral, purple strongly alkaline). A pH meter or probe, calibrated with buffer solutions, gives a precise digital value and can log readings throughout a titration.

## Reactivity 3.1.5 The ion product of water, Kw

Water ionizes slightly, so every aqueous solution holds both H⁺ and OH⁻:

Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 298 K

The guide says this equation and its 298 K value are in the data booklet. With the product fixed, more H⁺ means less OH⁻.

- Acidic: [H⁺] > [OH⁻] (pH below 7 at 298 K)
- Neutral: [H⁺] = [OH⁻] (pH 7 at 298 K)
- Basic: [H⁺] < [OH⁻] (pH above 7 at 298 K)

### Worked example 5: pH of an alkali

0.020 mol dm⁻³ KOH at 298 K is fully ionized, so [OH⁻] = 0.020 mol dm⁻³.

[H⁺] = (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴) / 0.020 = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³, so pH = **12.30**.

**Temperature.** Water's ionization is endothermic, so heating shifts it right and Kw rises. Hot pure water has pH below 7 yet is still neutral, because [H⁺] still equals [OH⁻].

## Reactivity 3.1.6 Strength of acids and bases

A **strong** acid or base ionizes completely in water; a **weak** one only partly, setting up an equilibrium.

- Strong acids listed in the guide: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃ and H₂SO₄. Strong bases: group 1 hydroxides.
- Weak acids include carboxylic acids and carbonic acid; weak bases include ammonia and amines.

**Strength is not concentration.** Concentration is how much is dissolved per dm³; strength is the fraction that ionizes. 5 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid is concentrated but weak; 0.001 mol dm⁻³ nitric acid is dilute but strong.

### Worked example 6: how much is ionized?

0.20 mol dm⁻³ of a weak monoprotic acid HA has pH 2.70.

[H⁺] = 10⁻²·⁷⁰ = 2.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³

Percentage ionized = (2.0 × 10⁻³ / 0.20) × 100 = **1.0%**. A strong acid of equal concentration would have pH 0.70.

**In the lab**, a strong acid at the same concentration shows a lower pH, higher conductivity and faster reaction with magnesium or a carbonate, but not more gas in total.

**Direction of equilibrium.** Acid–base equilibria lie towards the weaker acid and weaker base. For HCN(aq) + F⁻(aq) ⇌ CN⁻(aq) + HF(aq), given that HF is the stronger acid, CN⁻ is the stronger base, so the equilibrium lies to the left. A strong acid has a large ionization equilibrium constant; a weak acid's is far below 1.

**Hydrogen halides.** Acid strength rises from HF to HI because the H–X bond gets longer and weaker down group 17. HF is weak; the other three are strong.

## Reactivity 3.1.7 Neutralization

| Base | Example equation |
|---|---|
| Metal oxide | 2HNO₃(aq) + MgO(s) → Mg(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l) |
| Metal hydroxide | CH₃COOH(aq) + KOH(aq) → CH₃COOK(aq) + H₂O(l) |
| Carbonate | H₂SO₄(aq) + Na₂CO₃(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) |
| Hydrogencarbonate | HCOOH(aq) + NaHCO₃(aq) → HCOONa(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) |
| Ammonia | H₂SO₄(aq) + 2NH₃(aq) → (NH₄)₂SO₄(aq) |
| Amine | HBr(aq) + CH₃CH₂NH₂(aq) → CH₃CH₂NH₃Br(aq) |

Ammonia and amines just gain a proton, so no water forms. The ionic equation for a strong acid with a strong base is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). The reaction is exothermic because forming the O–H bond releases energy.

### Worked example 7: parent acid and base

The anion of a salt comes from the acid; the cation comes from the base.

(a) Calcium nitrate: **nitric acid** and **calcium hydroxide** (or oxide or carbonate).

(b) Ammonium sulfate: **sulfuric acid** and **ammonia**.

(c) Sodium propanoate: **propanoic acid** and **sodium hydroxide** (or carbonate or hydrogencarbonate).

Soluble salts are recovered by evaporating some water and crystallizing; insoluble ones by filtration. A metal releasing hydrogen from an acid is a redox reaction (Reactivity 3.2), not neutralization.

## Reactivity 3.1.8 pH curves for strong acid–strong base titrations

A pH curve plots pH against volume of titrant added. The guide limits these to monoprotic neutralizations.

### Worked example 8: building the curve

20.00 cm³ of 0.0800 mol dm⁻³ HNO₃ is titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ KOH.

Amount of acid = 0.0800 × 0.02000 = 1.60 × 10⁻³ mol, reacting 1 : 1, so the **equivalence point** is at 1.60 × 10⁻³ / 0.100 = **16.00 cm³**.

| KOH / cm³ | 0.00 | 8.00 | 15.90 | 16.00 | 16.10 | 30.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pH | 1.10 | 1.54 | 3.56 | 7.00 | 10.44 | 12.45 |

Check: at 15.90 cm³, excess H⁺ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol in 35.90 cm³, so [H⁺] = 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ and pH = 3.56.

Features to sketch and interpret:

- **pH-axis intercept**: the starting pH (1.10). For a strong acid it gives the concentration: 10⁻¹·¹⁰ ≈ 0.080 mol dm⁻³.
- A slow rise while acid is in excess.
- A near-vertical section: pH jumps from about 3.6 to 10.4 for 0.20 cm³ of KOH.
- **Equivalence point** at pH 7 (298 K), mid-way up the vertical section. It is also called the stoichiometric point because acid and base are present in the ratio of the equation.
- The curve then flattens towards the pH of the KOH (13.0) without reaching it.

Adding acid to base gives the mirror image: high start, falling curve.

## Common errors

- Calling OH⁻ the conjugate acid of water. It is water's conjugate base; H₃O⁺ is its conjugate acid.
- Not changing the charge when adding or removing a proton.
- Treating "weak" and "dilute" as the same.
- Defining neutral as pH 7 at any temperature.
- Putting water in the products of acid + ammonia.
- Reading the equivalence point at the top of the vertical section, not its midpoint.
