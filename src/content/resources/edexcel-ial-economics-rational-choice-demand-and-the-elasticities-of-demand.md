## 1. Rational decision making

**Rationality** (1a): consumers aim to maximise utility (satisfaction) from limited income, and firms aim to maximise profit. The assumption lets economists predict behaviour: if the price of a good falls, a utility maximiser buys more.

**Why consumers may not maximise utility** (1b):

- **Herding**: copying others. People queue at the busier of two similar cafés because the crowd seems to signal quality, even if the quiet café suits them better.
- **Habit**: repeating a purchase without reconsidering it, such as buying the same brand of tea each week.
- **Inertia**: staying put because change takes effort. A household pays 60 a month on a default broadband tariff when a 48 deal exists. The saving is 12 x 12 = 144 a year, or 20% of the bill, yet switching "takes two hours", so nothing happens.
- **Poor computational skills**: misjudging value. Pack A is 400 g for 2.80 and pack B is 650 g for 4.29. Per 100 g, A costs 0.70 and B costs 0.66. A shopper who guesses by eye may pick A.
- **Need to feel valued**: paying more for status, attention or recognition, for example a tailor who knows your name, rather than for the product alone.
- **Framing and bias**: the same facts presented differently change choices. A shop charges 100 for cash or 105 by card. Called a "5 card surcharge", it feels like a loss. Called a "5 cash discount", it feels like a gain, though the prices are identical.

**Evaluation**: these behaviours mean a demand curve is a prediction, not a law. Predictions still tend to hold on average because most buyers do respond to price. The rationality assumption is a useful starting point that needs qualifying when decisions are rushed, complex or emotional.

## 2. The demand curve

**Demand** (2a) is the amount of a good that buyers are both willing and able to purchase at each possible price over a stated period, other things equal. Willingness without ability (income) is not demand.

**Movement or shift** (2b):

- A move in the good's **own price** alone gives a **movement along** the existing curve (a price rise contracts quantity demanded; a fall extends it).
- A change in any **other** influence shifts the whole curve. A rightward shift means more is demanded at every price.

**Diminishing marginal utility** (2c): each extra unit consumed in a period adds less satisfaction than the one before. Suppose slices of pizza at one sitting give total utility of 20, 36, 48, 56 and 60 utils.

| Slices | Total utility | Marginal utility | Money value of marginal utility (1 util = 0.50) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 20 | 10 |
| 2 | 36 | 16 | 8 |
| 3 | 48 | 12 | 6 |
| 4 | 56 | 8 | 4 |
| 5 | 60 | 4 | 2 |

A rational consumer buys another slice only while the money value of its marginal utility is at least the price. At a price of 8 the consumer buys 2 slices, at 6 buys 3, at 4 buys 4 and at 2 buys 5. Because marginal utility falls, a lower price is needed to tempt the consumer to buy more, so the individual demand curve slopes downward.

**Shift factors** (2d), each with a chain of reasoning:

- **Price of a substitute falls**: buyers switch away, so demand for this good shifts left. If a streaming service cuts its price, weekly cinema demand might fall from 1,000 to 850 tickets at a price of 10, a 15% fall.
- **Price of a complement rises**: the pair becomes costlier, so demand for the other good shifts left.
- **Real income rises** (income adjusted for price changes): demand for a normal good shifts right; for an inferior good it shifts left.
- **Tastes**: a fashion or health trend shifts demand towards or away from a good.
- **Population size and age distribution**: an ageing population shifts demand right for healthcare and left for school supplies.
- **Advertising**: persuades and informs buyers, shifting demand right if effective.

## 3. Price, income and cross elasticities

**Price elasticity of demand (PED)** shows how strongly quantity demanded reacts when the good's price changes (3a).

PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price

The same pattern gives **YED** (% change in quantity demanded / % change in income) and **XED** (the % change in demand for good A over the % change in the price of good B) (3b). Percentage change = change / original x 100.

**Worked example (PED)**. A florist raises the price of a bouquet from 12.00 to 13.20; weekly sales fall from 80 to 68.

- % change in price = 1.20 / 12.00 x 100 = +10%.
- % change in quantity = -12 / 80 x 100 = -15%.
- PED = -15 / +10 = **-1.5**.

Because price and quantity move in opposite directions, PED for a normal demand curve is negative. Read its **size**: here 1.5.

**Interpreting PED** (3c):

| PED value | Description | Curve |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly price inelastic | Vertical |
| between 0 and -1 | Price inelastic | Steep |
| -1 | Unitary price elastic | Rectangular hyperbola |
| below -1, such as -3 | Price elastic | Flat |
| minus infinity | Perfectly price elastic | Horizontal |

**Determinants of PED** (3d), each with its reasoning:

- **Availability of substitutes**: many close substitutes make it easy to switch, so demand is more elastic.
- **Branding**: strong loyalty reduces willingness to switch, so demand is less elastic.
- **Percentage of total expenditure**: a tiny share of income, such as salt, barely prompts a change; a large share, such as a car, makes buyers sensitive to price.
- **Addictiveness**: dependent users keep buying, so demand is more inelastic.
- **Durability**: a durable good can be repaired or its replacement postponed, so demand is more elastic.

**Total revenue** (3e) = price x quantity demanded. For the florist, revenue was 80 x 12.00 = 960 and becomes 68 x 13.20 = 897.60, a fall of 62.40 (6.5%).

**PED and total revenue** (3g):

| Price change | If demand is elastic | If unitary | If inelastic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price rises | total revenue drops | stays level | total revenue climbs |
| Price falls | total revenue climbs | stays level | total revenue drops |

Inelastic case: a fuel station raises its price from 1.50 to 1.65 (+10%). Sales fall from 8,000 to 7,600 litres (-5%). PED = -5 / 10 = **-0.5**. Revenue rises from 12,000 to 12,540. Now an elastic case: a price cut from 20 to 18 (-10%) lifts sales from 400 to 520 (+30%), giving PED = **-3** and revenue from 8,000 to 9,360.

**PED along a straight-line demand curve** (3f). Take the invented demand curve P = 100 - 2Q. Slope is constant, but PED is not, because the ratio P/Q changes.

| Price | Quantity | PED | Total revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90 | 5 | -9.0 | 450 |
| 70 | 15 | -2.33 | 1,050 |
| 50 | 25 | -1.0 | 1,250 |
| 30 | 35 | -0.43 | 1,050 |
| 10 | 45 | -0.11 | 450 |

Each PED is found as % change in quantity over % change in price for a move away from that point along the line. From 90 to 80, quantity rises from 5 to 10 (+100%) and price falls 11.1%, so PED = -9. Described in words: the upper half of the line is elastic, the midpoint is unitary and the lower half is inelastic. Revenue is highest at the midpoint, where PED is -1.

```
  50 | \ unitary at the midpoint

   0 +---\--- Quantity (0 to 50)
```

**Income elasticity of demand** (3h). Real incomes rise 5% (from 30,000 to 31,500). Gym memberships rise from 1,000 to 1,070 (+7%), so YED = 7 / 5 = **+1.4**. Bus trips fall from 5,000 to 4,900 (-2%), so YED = -2 / 5 = **-0.4**.

| YED | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly income inelastic: demand ignores income |
| between 0 and +1 | Income inelastic: a necessity-type normal good |
| above +1 | Income elastic: a luxury-type normal good |
| infinite | Perfectly income elastic: only a theoretical limit |
| negative | Inferior good: demand falls as income rises |

**Cross elasticity of demand** (3i). If tea bags rise from 2.50 to 2.70 (+8%) and coffee demand rises from 500 to 560 jars (+12%), XED = 12 / 8 = **+1.5**: substitutes. If printer prices rise 5% and ink cartridge demand falls 3%, XED = **-0.6**: complements. A value close to zero suggests the goods are **unrelated**. The larger the positive value, the closer the substitutes; the larger the negative value, the stronger the complementarity.

**Significance** (3j):

- **Firms** use PED to set prices (raise price if inelastic, cut it if elastic), YED to forecast. If incomes grow 3% a year and YED is +2.0, expected demand rises 6%, so 50,000 units become 53,000. A rival's 5% price rise with XED of +2.0 lifts the firm's demand by 10%.
- **Consumers** on low incomes spending on inelastic necessities have little room to avoid price rises.
- **Governments** use PED to forecast the effect of indirect taxes. A 0.50 tax on a good at 5.00 (+10%) with PED of -0.4 cuts sales by 4%, from 100,000 to 96,000, raising 48,000. With PED of -2, sales fall 20% to 80,000 and revenue is 40,000, but consumption falls much further. YED helps planning of services as incomes change.

**Limits**: elasticity estimates rely on past data, hold only for a price range and ignore other changes in the market.

## Common errors

- Calling a change in the good's price a "shift". It is a movement.
- Dividing the change by the new figure instead of the original.
- Giving one elasticity for a whole straight-line demand curve. It changes along the line.
- Dropping the sign and then misreading an inferior good or complement.
- Giving only the revenue effect and not the reason (the PED value).
