## Syllabus checklist

- Approaches drawing on different research methods: case studies, social surveys, ethnography and longitudinal studies.
- The mixed methods approach: triangulation and methodological pluralism.
- The positivist approach: scientific method, objectivity, reliability and value-freedom.
- The interpretivist approach: verstehen, meaning, subjectivity and validity.
- Whether sociology can or should be based on the methods of the natural sciences, and the role of values in research.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Case study | Detailed study of one example (a person, group, institution or event) |
| Social survey | Collecting the same information from a large sample, usually by questionnaire or structured interview |
| Ethnography | Study of a group's way of life from the inside, usually by long-term participant observation |
| Longitudinal study | Research that follows the same sample over a long period |
| Panel attrition | Loss of participants from a longitudinal study over time |
| Triangulation | Using more than one method, data source or researcher to check findings |
| Methodological pluralism | Using several methods to build a fuller picture, each adding different insights |
| Positivism | The view that sociology should copy the natural sciences, studying observable social facts with objective, reliable methods |
| Value-freedom | Keeping the researcher's own values out of research |
| Interpretivism | The view that sociology must understand the meanings behind social action |
| Verstehen | Weber's term for empathetic understanding of an actor's point of view |
| Subjectivity | Recognising that knowledge reflects the perspective of the people studied and the researcher |
| Falsification | Popper's principle that a scientific theory must be capable of being proved false |
| Paradigm | Kuhn's term for the shared framework of assumptions within a science |

## The four research approaches

| Approach | Strengths | Limitations | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Case study | Depth; can test whether a general theory holds in one case; suits rare cases | Cannot be generalised; may reflect researcher's selection | Goffman's *Asylums* (1961), based on fieldwork in one psychiatric hospital |
| Social survey | Large, representative samples; trends and comparison; reliable | Shallow; imposes categories; response rates | The British Social Attitudes survey, run annually since 1983 |
| Ethnography | Rich understanding of meanings in natural settings; high validity | Time, access, small scale; going native; ethics | Sudhir Venkatesh's *Gang Leader for a Day* (2008), years with a Chicago gang |
| Longitudinal study | Shows change and causes over time in the same people | Expensive; attrition; participants change because studied | The UK Millennium Cohort Study, following children born between 2000 and 2002 |

## Mixed methods

- **Triangulation**: checking one set of data against another. Norman Denzin (*The Research Act*, 1970) described triangulation of data, investigators, theories and methods. If findings agree, confidence in validity rises.
- **Methodological pluralism**: each method adds a different piece, such as a survey showing how common a pattern is and interviews showing why it happens.
- **Evaluation**: mixed methods cost more time and money; results may conflict; critics argue that positivist and interpretivist assumptions cannot simply be combined.

## Positivism

- Auguste Comte argued that society could be studied scientifically, like the natural world.
- Society is made of **social facts** that exist outside individuals and can be measured.
- Uses the **scientific method**: hypothesis, data collection, testing, generalisation.
- Aims: **objectivity** (results not shaped by the researcher), **reliability** (repeatable), **value-freedom**.
- Classic example: Durkheim's *Suicide* (1897), using official statistics to link suicide rates to social integration and regulation.
- Prefers: surveys, structured interviews, official statistics, experiments.

## Interpretivism

- People act on meanings, so sociology must understand them, not just measure behaviour.
- Weber: **verstehen**, understanding action from the actor's point of view.
- Accepts **subjectivity**: the researcher interprets the interpretations of participants.
- Aims for **validity**: a true picture of how people experience their world.
- Prefers: unstructured interviews, participant observation, personal documents.
- Criticism: findings are hard to repeat and generalise, and depend on the researcher's interpretation.

## Can and should sociology be scientific?

| Position | Argument |
|---|---|
| Positivist: yes | Social facts can be measured and laws discovered, as in Durkheim's work |
| Interpretivist: no | Humans have consciousness and act on meanings, so methods of the natural sciences do not fit |
| Popper | Science advances by trying to falsify theories; Marxism and some sociology fail this test because they explain away contrary evidence |
| Kuhn (1962) | Natural science itself works within paradigms and changes through revolutions, so it is less objective than positivists assume; sociology has competing perspectives rather than one paradigm |
| Realists (such as Andrew Sayer) | Both sciences study unobservable structures through their effects, so sociology can be scientific without copying laboratory methods |

## The role of values

- **Weber**: values inevitably shape the choice of topic, but research should be conducted as objectively as possible.
- **Positivists**: aim for value-freedom through standardised methods.
- **Alvin Gouldner**: argued that a claim to be value-free is itself a value position and can hide sociologists' commitments.
- **Becker (1967)**: research always takes sides, so be open about whose side you are on.
- **Feminists**: openly committed research can be legitimate, as in Ann Oakley's (1981) argument for non-hierarchical interviewing of women.

## Exam traps

- **Approach versus method.** A social survey is an approach that may use questionnaires or structured interviews; ethnography usually uses participant observation.
- **Triangulation is not "using lots of methods".** Its purpose is to check findings against each other.
- **Interpretivists are not "unscientific" by default.** They argue for a different kind of rigour.
- **Value-freedom has two stages.** Weber accepted values in choosing a topic but not in conducting research.
- **Forgetting the "should" question.** The syllabus asks whether sociology *can* and *should* be scientific.
- **Longitudinal versus repeated surveys.** Longitudinal studies follow the *same* people.

## Self-test

1. What is the main limitation of a case study?
2. Why are longitudinal studies useful for studying cause and effect?
3. What is panel attrition, and why does it matter?
4. Define triangulation.
5. What is the difference between triangulation and methodological pluralism?
6. Name three things positivists aim for in research.
7. What did Weber mean by *verstehen*?
8. Why do interpretivists reject the methods of the natural sciences?
9. What is Popper's principle of falsification?
10. How does Kuhn's idea of paradigms challenge positivism?
11. What did Weber say about values in research?
12. Give one example of an ethnographic study.

### Answers

1. Its findings cannot be generalised to other cases because one example may be unusual.
2. They track the same people over time, so researchers can see which changes come first.
3. The loss of participants over time; those who drop out may differ from those who stay, making the sample less representative.
4. Using more than one method, source or researcher to check the validity of findings.
5. Triangulation uses methods to cross-check findings; pluralism uses them to add different parts of the picture.
6. Any three: objectivity, reliability, value-freedom, generalisability, testing hypotheses.
7. Empathetic understanding of social action from the point of view of the person acting.
8. Humans have consciousness and act on meanings, unlike the objects natural scientists study.
9. A theory is scientific only if it can be tested and potentially shown to be false.
10. It shows natural science also works within shared assumptions that change through revolutions, so science is less purely objective than positivists claim.
11. Values shape the choice of topic, but research itself should be carried out objectively.
12. Venkatesh's *Gang Leader for a Day* (2008), or Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943), or Barker's *The Making of a Moonie* (1984).
