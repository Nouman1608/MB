## 6.1.1 Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Absolute advantage | Produces a good with fewer resources, or more of it from the same resources |
| Comparative advantage | Produces a good at a lower opportunity cost |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up |
| Specialisation | Concentrating production on goods with a comparative advantage |
| Free trade (trade liberalisation) | Trade without tariffs, quotas or other barriers |
| Trading possibility curve (TPC) | The combinations of two goods a country can consume after specialising and trading at given terms |
| Terms of trade | The rate at which exports exchange for imports |

### Method: find the comparative advantage

1. Put the data in one form: output per worker (or per day) or hours per unit.
2. For each country, work out the opportunity cost of one good in terms of the other (output of the other good divided by output of this good, or hours of this good divided by hours of the other).
3. Compare the same good across countries. The lower opportunity cost marks the comparative advantage.
4. Check that each country has the advantage in a different good.

### Worked reminder 1

Country C produces 12 tonnes of rice or 8 metres of fabric per worker-day. Country D produces 6 tonnes of rice or 3 metres of fabric.

| | Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of rice | Opportunity cost of 1 metre of fabric |
|---|---|---|
| C | 8 ÷ 12 = 0.67 fabric | 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5 rice |
| D | 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5 fabric | 6 ÷ 3 = 2 rice |

C has an absolute advantage in both goods (more of each per worker-day). D has the comparative advantage in rice (0.5 is below 0.67). C has the comparative advantage in fabric (1.5 is below 2).

The exchange price of 1 metre of fabric must lie between 1.5 and 2 tonnes of rice. At 1.75 tonnes, C earns 0.25 tonne more than it could get by making rice itself, and D pays 0.25 tonne less than it would give up making fabric itself. Both gain on every unit traded.

## 6.1.2 Benefits of specialisation and free trade

- Output and consumption possibilities are larger than without trade.
- The TPC lies outside the PPC at any terms of trade that differ from the home opportunity cost.
- Consumers gain choice and lower prices; firms gain larger markets, economies of scale and access to cheaper inputs.
- Competition from imports encourages efficiency and innovation.
- Export growth can raise income and jobs.

### TPC quick facts

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Axes | Quantities of the two goods consumed |
| Start point | The country's PPC intercept for the good it specialises in |
| Other end | The amount of the other good that all of that output could buy at the agreed terms |
| Slope | Set by the terms of trade, not by domestic opportunity cost |
| Better terms of trade | The TPC pivots outwards |

Example: Country C could make 80 metres of fabric from 10 workers. At 1.75 tonnes of rice per metre, all fabric could be swapped for 80 × 1.75 = 140 tonnes of rice. Its PPC would allow at most 120 tonnes if everyone made rice. The TPC runs from 80 metres to 140 tonnes, outside the PPC.

## 6.1.3 The terms of trade

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Formula | (Index of average export prices ÷ index of average import prices) × 100 |
| Rise in the index | Improvement: exports buy more imports |
| Fall in the index | Worsening: exports buy fewer imports |
| Base year | 100 |
| Percentage change | (new index − old index) ÷ old index × 100 |

### Worked reminder 2

Export price index 108 and import price index 90 give 108 ÷ 90 × 100 = 120. If the index later falls to 108, the change is (108 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = −10%. A fall of 12 index points is a fall of 10%, not 12%.

Another check: a country sells 200 units at 15 cu and imports cost 12 cu. Exports buy 200 × 15 ÷ 12 = 250 import units.

### Causes of change

| Improves the index | Worsens the index |
|---|---|
| Rising world demand for exports | Falling world demand for exports |
| Rising export prices from inflation or scarce supply | Rising import prices (such as fuel or raw materials) |
| Appreciation of the home currency (with home-currency pricing) | Depreciation of the home currency |
| Falling world prices of imports | New competing suppliers of the export |

Productivity gains can lower export prices, which lowers the index but raises competitiveness.

### Impact chain

1. State the direction of change in the index.
2. Say what it means for the quantity of imports each unit of exports buys.
3. Link to export revenue through the price elasticity of demand: elastic means revenue falls when prices rise; inelastic means revenue rises.
4. Link to the current account, living standards, inflation (imported costs) and competitiveness.
5. Judge: the cause matters. A worsening caused by higher productivity may be good news; one caused by weak world demand is not.

## 6.1.4 Limitations of the theories

| Limitation | Effect on the case for trade |
|---|---|
| Transport and other trade costs | Cut or remove the gains |
| Increasing opportunity costs | Complete specialisation is unlikely; the advantage narrows |
| Imperfect factor mobility | Retraining costs and unemployment |
| Static view | Comparative advantage changes over time; infant industries |
| Dependence on a narrow range of exports | Exposure to price falls and supply shocks |
| Assumes perfect competition, full employment, perfect information and no barriers | Reality differs, so the gains are smaller or uneven |
| Unequal distribution | Some groups and regions lose |
| Environmental costs of extra transport and production | Not counted in the model |

### Evaluation prompts

- How large are the trade costs compared with the gain?
- How easily can workers move to the growing industry, and over what time?
- How diversified are the exports?
- Is the advantage likely to last?
- Who gains and who loses?

## Quick self-test

1. Define comparative advantage.
2. Country C makes 12 tonnes of rice or 8 metres of fabric per worker-day. What is the opportunity cost of 1 metre of fabric?
3. Country D makes 6 tonnes of rice or 3 metres of fabric. Which good does D have a comparative advantage in, compared with C?
4. Between which two values must the price of fabric in rice lie for both C and D to gain?
5. Export price index 108, import price index 90. Calculate the terms of trade.
6. The index rises from 80 to 90. Calculate the percentage change.
7. Export prices fall to 95 and import prices rise to 105 (base 100). Calculate the terms of trade and say whether they improved.
8. State two causes of an improvement in the terms of trade.
9. Why might an improvement in the terms of trade reduce export revenue?
10. What does the TPC show that the PPC cannot?
11. Give two limitations of comparative advantage theory.
12. A country sells 200 units at 15 cu and imports cost 12 cu. How many import units do the exports buy?

### Answers

1. The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
2. 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5 tonnes of rice.
3. Rice: D gives up 0.5 metre of fabric per tonne, C gives up 0.67.
4. Between 1.5 and 2 tonnes of rice per metre.
5. 108 ÷ 90 × 100 = 120.
6. (90 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = 12.5%.
7. 95 ÷ 105 × 100 = 90.5. This is a worsening, since the index is below 100 and fell.
8. For example, higher world demand for exports, a falling price of imported materials, or an appreciating currency.
9. If demand for exports is elastic, the rise in export prices cuts the quantity sold by a larger percentage than the price rise.
10. Consumption possibilities after trade, which can lie outside the PPC.
11. For example, transport costs, increasing opportunity costs, immobile factors, dependence, changing advantage.
12. 200 × 15 ÷ 12 = 250 units.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Working out opportunity cost the wrong way up. Check it by asking what is given up.
- Naming the country that produces more as the one with the comparative advantage.
- Giving a price outside the opportunity cost limits and still claiming both countries gain.
- Putting import prices on top of the terms of trade formula.
- Describing a fall in the index as a fall of that many percent.
- Writing that an improvement is always good without discussing elasticity or the cause.
- Listing limitations without explaining their effect on the gains from trade.
- Drawing a TPC that starts at a different place from the PPC, or leaving the axes unlabelled.
