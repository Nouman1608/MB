## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ILO unemployed | No job, available to start soon, actively searched recently |
| Economically inactive | Of working age, not in work and not counted as unemployed |
| Labour force | Employed + unemployed |
| Underemployment | In work, but with fewer hours or lower skill use than wanted |
| Inflation | Sustained rise in the general price level |
| Deflation | Price level falling |
| Disinflation | Inflation rate falling; prices still rising |
| Deflationary policy | Policy that cuts aggregate demand; may not cause deflation |

## Formulas

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Unemployment rate | (unemployed ÷ labour force) × 100 |
| Participation rate | (labour force ÷ population of working age) × 100 |
| Underemployment rate | underemployed workers ÷ employed × 100 |
| Inflation rate | (new index − old index) ÷ old index × 100 |
| Weighted index | sum of (weight × item index) |
| Real change | (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 |
| Output gap | (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100 |
| Unit labour cost change | (1 + wage rise) ÷ (1 + productivity rise) − 1 |

## Method in steps: ILO rate

1. Find the labour force first: employed plus unemployed.
2. Divide the number unemployed by that total.
3. Convert to a percentage and give the unit.
4. Ask whether jobs changed or people left the labour force.

**Reminder (Country Gamma).** Working-age population 4,900,000. Employed 3,360,000. Unemployed 140,000. Labour force = 3,500,000. Rate = 140,000 ÷ 3,500,000 × 100 = **4.0%**. Participation = 3,500,000 ÷ 4,900,000 × 100 = **71.4%**; inactive = 1,400,000. If 20,000 unemployed stop searching, unemployed = 120,000 and the rate = 120,000 ÷ 3,480,000 × 100 = **3.4%**, with no extra jobs. Underemployment: 252,000 of 3,360,000 employed want more hours, so the rate is **7.5%**.

## Types of unemployment and matching policy

| Type | Cause | Policy | Weakness of policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Negative output gap, weak AD | Fiscal or monetary stimulus | Time lags, inflation near capacity |
| Structural | Skills or location mismatch | Retraining, relocation help | Slow and costly; older workers adapt less |
| Frictional | Job search takes time | Job information, quicker matching | Some search is healthy |
| Seasonal | Yearly pattern of demand | Diversify local industry | Hard to remove entirely |

Links to the model: a negative output gap → cyclical unemployment. Supply-side causes lower normal capacity, so the vertical LRAS curve sits further left. Example of a positive gap: potential 800, actual 824 → (824 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = **+3.0%**.

## Employment: demand side and supply side

* Demand side: higher consumption, investment, government spending or net exports → higher AD → more output → more hiring.
* Supply side: skills, mobility, productivity, wages relative to productivity, regulation, the incentive to work.
* Chain: lower real wage relative to productivity → hiring is cheaper → employment may rise, if demand for output exists.

## Global influences (list, then evaluate)

* Recession abroad → export orders fall → AD shifts left → cyclical unemployment.
* Cheaper foreign production → closure of home industries → structural unemployment.
* Offshoring out; foreign investment in.
* Migration alters labour supply.
* Exchange rate and world commodity prices change competitiveness and costs.
* Evaluation: trade openness, labour flexibility, speed of switching industries, time period.

## Inflation: causes in one table

| Cause | AD/AS effect | Real GDP | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand-pull (spending, money supply, weaker currency boosting exports) | AD right | Up | Usually down |
| Cost-push (wages, commodities, energy, indirect taxes) | SRAS left | Down | Usually up |
| Imported inflation (weaker currency, dearer world commodities) | SRAS left, plus AD right | Mixed | Mixed |

Method for a cost-push calculation: multiply each input's percentage rise by its share of total cost, then add. Example: fuel rises 30% and is 10% of costs, so cost rises 0.10 × 30 = **3.0%**. Wage growth of 5% with productivity growth of 2% gives unit labour costs up 1.05 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = **2.9%**.

**Money supply.** Excessive growth in the money supply increases AD and causes inflation. Link: faster money growth → more spending → AD shifts right → price level rises, more strongly near full capacity.

## Exchange rate and inflation

A fall in the currency raises import prices in domestic currency and lifts export demand. A rise lowers import prices.

**Reminder.** An import costs 80 foreign units. At 1 domestic unit = 1.25 foreign units, the price is 80 ÷ 1.25 = 64. If the currency appreciates to 1.60, the price is 80 ÷ 1.60 = 50, a fall of (50 − 64) ÷ 64 = **−21.9%**. At 1.00 (depreciation) the price is 80, up **25%** on 64.

## Index numbers, real values, disinflation and deflation

* CPI 131.5 → 135.2: (135.2 − 131.5) ÷ 131.5 × 100 = **2.8%**.
* Weights 25%, 35%, 40% with item indices 112, 106, 103: 28.0 + 37.1 + 41.2 = **106.3**.
* Nominal pay +2.2%, inflation 3.5%: 1.022 ÷ 1.035 − 1 = **−1.3%** (real pay falls).
* Disinflation: inflation 4.5%, 3.0%, 1.5% gives price levels 104.5, 107.6, 109.2. Prices rise throughout.
* Deflation: index 98.6 → 97.3 is **−1.3%**. The price level itself has fallen.

## Chains of reasoning to learn

* **Cyclical unemployment:** global recession → fewer export orders → AD shifts left → real GDP falls below potential (negative output gap) → firms cut hours and jobs → unemployment rises. Policy: raise AD, but check the lag and the debt cost.
* **Structural unemployment:** a new technology makes a skill obsolete → workers cannot fill the new vacancies → unemployment stays high even when AD is strong → the normal capacity level of output falls and LRAS sits to the left. Policy: retraining and mobility support, which work slowly.
* **Cost-push inflation:** world commodity price rises → unit costs rise → SRAS shifts left → price level up, real GDP down. Policy dilemma: cutting AD to fight prices deepens the fall in output.
* **Demand-pull inflation:** weaker currency and cheap credit → AD shifts right → output rises towards capacity → spare capacity runs out → the price level rises faster.
* **Exchange rate:** depreciation → dearer imports in domestic currency → higher costs and prices, but cheaper exports → AD rises.

## Evaluation prompts

Use at least one of these in every extended answer.

* Cause: is the problem cyclical or structural? The right policy differs.
* Size: how large is the shift in AD or SRAS, and how much spare capacity exists?
* Time: short run versus long run; how long are the lags?
* Openness: how much of the economy trades, and how much do firms import?
* Other things: what else is changing at the same time?

## Must-know distinctions

* Disinflation is a lower rate; deflation is a lower price level.
* Unemployed means searching; inactive means not searching.
* Unemployment means no work; underemployment means too little work.
* Demand-pull moves AD; cost-push moves SRAS.
* Deflationary policy is a demand-reducing action; deflation is a result.

## Quick self-test

1. Define disinflation.
2. Employed 3,360,000, unemployed 140,000: find the unemployment rate.
3. 20,000 unemployed people stop searching. Find the new rate.
4. A steel plant closes because of cheaper foreign steel. Name the type of unemployment.
5. Nominal pay rises 2.2% and prices rise 3.5%. Find the change in real pay.
6. CPI rises from 131.5 to 135.2. Find inflation.
7. Fuel rises 30% and forms 10% of a firm's costs. Find the rise in costs.
8. Wages +5%, productivity +2%. Find the change in unit labour cost.
9. Currency appreciates, cutting an import's price from 64 to 50. Find the percentage change.
10. A tax rise cuts inflation from 4% to 2%. Is this deflation? Name it.

### Answers

1. A fall in the rate of inflation; the price level still rises.
2. 140,000 ÷ 3,500,000 × 100 = 4.0%.
3. 120,000 ÷ 3,480,000 × 100 = 3.4%.
4. Structural (a skills or location mismatch caused by competition).
5. 1.022 ÷ 1.035 − 1 = −1.3%.
6. 3.7 ÷ 131.5 × 100 = 2.8%.
7. 0.10 × 30 = 3.0%.
8. 1.05 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = 2.9%.
9. (50 − 64) ÷ 64 × 100 = −21.9%.
10. No. Prices still rise, so it is disinflation; the tax rise is a deflationary policy.

## Where marks are usually lost

* Using the working-age population instead of the labour force in the rate.
* Counting people who stop searching as employed.
* Describing disinflation as falling prices.
* Calling every job loss cyclical without mentioning weak demand.
* Drawing AD shifts for a cost-push case, or the reverse.
* Subtracting inflation from nominal growth instead of dividing.
* Omitting the evaluation of a policy: lags, costs, inflation risk, the cause of the unemployment.
* Giving a global influence without linking it to AD, SRAS or LRAS.
