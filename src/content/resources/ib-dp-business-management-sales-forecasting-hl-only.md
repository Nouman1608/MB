## What a sales forecast is

A **sales forecast** is a prediction of the sales a business expects over a future period, stated in units, in revenue, or both. The guide does not define the term, so treat this as a working definition. A forecast is an estimate. It is built from information available today, so it is never a promise.

Forecasts feed other plans. The sales figure is often the starting point for production schedules, purchasing, staffing, marketing budgets and cash flow forecasts.

## Benefits of sales forecasting

Each benefit below works only if the forecast is reasonably close to what happens. Always say how the benefit helps this particular business.

- **Production and stock planning.** If Orsini Surfboards expects to sell 495 boards, it can order materials and schedule workers for that volume. This reduces stockouts and avoids money tied up in unsold stock.
- **Cash flow and finance.** Expected sales revenue tells the business when cash will arrive, so it can plan when to pay suppliers, and whether it needs an overdraft. Lenders and investors usually want to see forecasts before they commit funds.
- **Human resource planning.** Expected volumes show whether to recruit, train, offer overtime or avoid replacing leavers.
- **Marketing planning.** A forecast helps set the promotion budget and timing. It also lets the business judge whether a marketing objective (for example, a share of the market) is realistic.
- **Targets and control.** Forecast figures give managers and sales teams a target. Later, the gap between forecast and actual sales shows where to investigate.
- **Reduced uncertainty and risk.** Planning from evidence is better than planning from guesswork, which makes decisions on capacity, pricing and expansion easier to defend to stakeholders.

## Limitations of sales forecasting

- **It relies on past data.** A trend forecast assumes the past pattern continues. If the market changes direction, the forecast fails.
- **External factors change.** Competitors, consumer tastes, the economy, technology and regulation can all shift demand in ways a trend line cannot see.
- **Unexpected events.** One-off shocks (a supply disruption, a bad review that spreads, a change in weather) are not in any data set.
- **Longer horizons are less reliable.** The further ahead the forecast, the more can change.
- **Poor or small data sets.** A new business or a new product has little history. A handful of points can give a misleading line.
- **Judgement and bias.** Managers may be optimistic to impress owners or lenders. Sales staff may understate the forecast so targets are easy to beat.
- **Cost and time.** Collecting data and analyzing it takes resources. A small business may not be able to afford detailed work.
- **False confidence.** A precise-looking number can lead managers to commit to production, hiring or borrowing before they check the assumptions.

## Simple linear regression as a forecasting tool (HL only)

The guide lists three parts: scatter diagrams, line of best fit, and correlation/extrapolation. The guide does not give a formula for the line. The least-squares form below is the standard form.

1. **Scatter diagram.** Plot time on the horizontal axis (year 1, 2, 3 and so on) and sales on the vertical axis.
2. **Line of best fit.** Draw the straight line that follows the points most closely. In standard form it is y = a + bx, where b is the gradient (the change in sales per year) and a is the value where the line meets the vertical axis. The standard least-squares results are b = Sxy / Sxx and a = ȳ − b·x̄, where Sxy is the sum of (x − x̄)(y − ȳ) and Sxx is the sum of (x − x̄)².
3. **Correlation.** Correlation describes how closely the points follow a straight line. The standard correlation coefficient r lies between −1 and +1. Values near +1 show strong positive correlation, values near −1 show strong negative correlation, and values near 0 show little linear relationship.
4. **Extrapolation.** Extending the line beyond the data to predict a future value. Reading a value inside the data range is interpolation. Extrapolation is the riskier of the two, especially far from the last data point.

### Worked example 1: a strong trend

Orsini Surfboards recorded these annual sales.

| Year (x) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boards sold (y) | 310 | 345 | 372 | 410 | 428 | 465 |

Step 1. x̄ = 3.5 and ȳ = 388.33.

Step 2. Sxy = 531.0 and Sxx = 17.5, summing the products of the deviations from each mean.

Step 3. b = 531.0 / 17.5 = 30.34, so sales rose by about 30 boards per year.

Step 4. a = 388.33 − 30.34 × 3.5 = 282.13.

Step 5. The line is y = 282.13 + 30.34x. For year 7, y = 282.13 + 30.34 × 7 = 494.5, so the forecast is about 495 boards.

Step 6. The correlation coefficient is r = 0.997, a very strong positive correlation. The points sit close to the line, which supports the forecast one year ahead.

Step 7. Extrapolate far ahead and the reliability falls. The same line gives about 737 boards in year 15, but nothing in six years of data shows that the market will keep growing at a steady 30 boards a year for nine more years. Surfboards may saturate the local market, a competitor may enter, or tastes may move.

### Worked example 2: a weak relationship

Dravik Audio sold a portable speaker over six years.

| Year (x) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speakers sold (y) | 240 | 180 | 265 | 170 | 255 | 200 |

The line of best fit is y = 225.33 − 2.00x and r = −0.09. The points zigzag around a mean of about 218 with no clear trend. The line predicts about 211 for year 7, but the correlation is so close to zero that the line explains almost none of the variation. Using this as a firm forecast would be unwise. A better response is to collect more information (for example, what happened in the high and low years) before committing to production.

## Using the benefits and limitations in an evaluation

AO3 marks reward a judgement supported by the case, not a general list. A useful structure is:

1. State a benefit and tie it to the business (name the decision it supports).
2. State a limitation and tie it to the business (name the feature of the market or the data that causes it).
3. Weigh them using factors from the case.
4. Reach a conclusion and justify it.

Factors that often decide the weighing:

- **Stability of the market.** A mature market with steady demand suits forecasting better than a fashion or technology market.
- **Length of the horizon.** A forecast for next month carries less risk than one for five years.
- **Cost of being wrong.** If errors mean large unsold stock or lost customers, the forecast needs more care. If production can be changed quickly, errors cost less.
- **Quality and amount of data.** Many years of data with a high correlation support the forecast more than a few erratic years.
- **Flexibility.** A business with spare capacity, short supplier lead times or a flexible workforce can cope with forecast error more easily.

### Worked example 3: short evaluation

Question: Evaluate the usefulness of the regression forecast to Orsini Surfboards.

Answer outline. The forecast of about 495 boards for year 7 helps the firm plan resin, foam blanks and workshop hours, and it can support a conversation with a lender because it rests on six years of data. The correlation of 0.997 suggests the trend has been steady. However, six points is a small data set, and the line assumes the past rate of growth carries on. A new competitor, a poor summer or a rise in input costs could cut sales below the line. The forecast is also a single figure, so it hides how far real sales could differ from it. Overall, the forecast is useful for next year's plan because the horizon is short and the fit is strong. Orsini should treat it as a central estimate, keep some spare stock and capacity, and check it against actual sales each quarter rather than treat the figure as certain.

## Common errors

- Writing a list of benefits and limitations with no link to the business in the case.
- Claiming a high correlation proves the forecast will be right. It only shows that the past data followed a line.
- Extrapolating many periods beyond the data and presenting the result without comment.
- Saying that forecasts are "inaccurate" without saying why, or what the business could do about it.
- Confusing a sales forecast (sales, in units or revenue) with a cash flow forecast (money in and out). The first feeds the second.
- Giving a verdict with no reason, or ending with "it depends" and no judgement.
