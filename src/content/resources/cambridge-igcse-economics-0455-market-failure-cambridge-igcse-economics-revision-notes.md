## Core idea in one line

Market failure: the free market allocates resources in a way that is not best for society, because decision makers weigh only their private costs and benefits, or because a market cannot work at all.

## Key terms (2.10.1)

| Term | Definition to learn | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Private cost | Cost borne by the consumer or producer making the decision | A factory's wage bill |
| External cost | Cost imposed on third parties, not paid for by the decision maker | Noise from a night-time delivery depot |
| Social cost | Private cost + external cost | Wage bill + noise damage |
| Private benefit | Benefit gained by the consumer or producer making the decision | A student's higher future salary |
| External benefit | Benefit gained by third parties who do not pay | Fewer infections for unvaccinated neighbours |
| Social benefit | Private benefit + external benefit | Salary gain + gains to employers |
| Public good | Non-excludable and non-rival; not supplied by the market because of free riders | Lighthouse, flood defences, street lighting |
| Merit good | Society judges it under-consumed in a free market; benefits underestimated | Education, health care, vaccination |
| Demerit good | Society judges it over-consumed in a free market; harm underestimated | Cigarettes, alcohol |

Rearranging the formulae is common in data questions:

```
External cost   = social cost - private cost
External benefit = social benefit - private benefit
```

Worked reminder: social cost 140, private cost 112, so external cost = 28, which is 28 / 140 x 100 = 20% of social cost.

## Causes (2.10.2): what goes wrong in each area

| Area | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Public goods | Non-payers cannot be excluded, so people free ride; firms cannot earn revenue; the good is not provided |
| Merit goods | Information failure and external benefits; low incomes may also limit buying |
| Demerit goods | Information failure about harm; use often harms others too |
| External costs | Price excludes harm to third parties, so output is too high |
| External benefits | Price-setting ignores gains to third parties, so output is too low |
| Abuse of monopoly power | Single seller restricts output and raises price; little pressure to be efficient |
| Factor immobility | Workers (and other factors) cannot move between jobs or places, so some are idle while shortages persist elsewhere |

### Two kinds of labour immobility

- **Occupational:** lacking the skills or qualifications for different work.
- **Geographical:** unable to move to another area (housing costs, family ties, cost of moving, lack of information about jobs).

## Consequences (2.10.3): learn the direction

- Demerit goods and goods with external costs: **over-consumed**, too many resources.
- Merit goods and goods with external benefits: **under-consumed**, too few resources.
- Public goods: **not provided**.
- Monopoly abuse: **output too low, price too high**.
- Factor immobility: **unemployed resources alongside shortages**.

### Decision rule with numbers

| Situation | Market chooses to consume if | Society would want consumption if |
|---|---|---|
| External cost | Private benefit > private cost | Social benefit > social cost |
| External benefit | Private benefit > price | Social benefit > price |

Worked reminder: a gym membership costs 30 a month. A person's private benefit is 25 and the external benefit (lower health costs for taxpayers) is 10. The person does not join (25 < 30), but social benefit is 35, which is above 30, so society would want them to join: under-consumption.

## Public good or not? Two tests

| Good | Excludable? | Rival? | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| National defence | No | No | Public good |
| Toll motorway | Yes | Only when congested | Not a public good |
| Free public park | Largely no | Largely no, until crowded | Close to a public good |
| School place | Yes | Yes | Merit good |
| Concert ticket | Yes | Yes | Private good |

## Exam traps

- **"Public sector" is not "public good".** A state-run hospital supplies a merit good.
- **Merit good versus external benefit.** A merit good is about the consumer underestimating benefit; an external benefit is about third parties. Many goods have both, so say which you mean.
- **Direction words.** "Market failure" alone earns little; write "over-consumption" or "under-consumption".
- **Third party.** Always name who bears the external cost or gains the external benefit.
- **Syllabus areas only.** 2.10.2 limits examples to public goods, merit and demerit goods, externalities, monopoly abuse and factor immobility.
- **No diagrams needed.** Do not lose time drawing one unless a question explicitly asks.
- **Monopoly is not always failure.** Market failure arises from the *abuse* of monopoly power: restricting output and charging high prices.

## Chains worth memorising

- External cost: firm ignores harm to third parties → price below social cost → too much bought → over-production and over-consumption → too many resources used.
- Merit good: consumers underestimate benefit → demand lower than it should be → under-consumption → too few resources used.
- Public good: non-excludable → free riders → no revenue for firms → market does not supply it.
- Immobility: workers lack skills or cannot move → vacancies unfilled and unemployment continues → output below what resources could produce.

## Self-test

1. Define market failure.
2. State the formula for social benefit.
3. A firm's social cost is 90,000 and its private cost is 72,000. Calculate the external cost.
4. Give the two characteristics of a public good.
5. Why will a private firm not supply a public good?
6. Is a toll bridge a public good? Give a reason.
7. Give one reason why a merit good is under-consumed.
8. Name the consequence of a demerit good left to the free market.
9. Distinguish occupational immobility from geographical immobility.
10. How can abuse of monopoly power cause market failure?
11. A course costs 200. Private benefit is 170 and external benefit is 60. Will the person enrol, and is this best for society?
12. Which areas of market failure lead to under-consumption?

### Answers

1. When the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, so too much or too little of some goods is produced and consumed.
2. Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
3. 90,000 - 72,000 = 18,000.
4. Non-excludable and non-rival.
5. Non-payers cannot be excluded, so people free ride and the firm cannot earn enough revenue.
6. No: people who do not pay the toll can be excluded.
7. Consumers underestimate its benefits (information failure); also accept external benefits ignored, or low income.
8. Over-consumption, so too many resources are used to produce it.
9. Occupational: cannot switch jobs because of skills; geographical: cannot move to a different area.
10. A single seller restricts output and charges a high price, so too few resources are allocated to the product and consumers lose choice.
11. No (170 < 200). Social benefit is 230, above 200, so society would want enrolment: under-consumption.
12. Merit goods and goods with external benefits (and public goods, which are not provided at all).
