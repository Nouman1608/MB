## What HR objectives are

**Human resource management (HRM)** is the management of the people who work for a business: planning how many are needed, recruiting and selecting them, training and rewarding them, and managing their performance and relationships. **HR objectives** are the targets the HR function sets to ensure the business has the right people, with the right skills, working effectively, at an acceptable cost.

HR objectives are set within the **corporate objectives** of the business, such as growth, profit or improved customer service. They are a functional objective: they say what the HR function must achieve to help the business reach its corporate aims.

## Types of HR objective

- **Workforce numbers and skills.** Having the right number of employees with the right skills at the right time. This depends on workforce planning.
- **Labour costs.** Keeping the wage bill and the costs of recruiting and training within budget, for example labour costs as a percentage of revenue.
- **Retention.** Keeping valued staff, measured by labour turnover.
- **Productivity and performance.** Raising output per employee.
- **Motivation and engagement.** Staff who want to do a good job, measured by surveys, absence and turnover.
- **Training and development.** Closing skills gaps and preparing staff for promotion.
- **Flexibility.** Being able to adjust the workforce to changing demand, through part-time, flexible and temporary staff.
- **Legal compliance and fairness.** Meeting the law on pay, safety, equality and contracts, and treating staff fairly.
- **Employer image.** Becoming a place where good applicants want to work.

## How and why objectives are used

- **To give direction.** They tell managers and HR staff what to aim for.
- **To coordinate.** They link HR activities (such as recruitment and training) so that they pull in the same direction.
- **To allocate resources.** Money and time go to the highest-priority aims.
- **To set targets and measure.** Results can be compared with the target.
- **To motivate.** Staff and managers who know the goal can work towards it.
- **To support the corporate strategy.** If the business plans to open ten new sites, the HR objective may be to recruit and train 150 new staff within a year.

Objectives work best when they are **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. "Improve retention" is vague. "Cut labour turnover from 27.5% to 15% within 18 months" is SMART.

## The need for clear objectives

Without clear HR objectives, a business may recruit when it should be training, offer pay rises with no link to performance, or drift into high turnover without noticing. Clear objectives:

- let managers decide between competing uses of money;
- show whether HR activity has worked;
- make it easier to explain pay, training and recruitment policy to staff;
- help the business respond when conditions change, such as a labour shortage or a new law.

## Worked example 1: labour turnover

**Labour turnover** measures the proportion of the workforce that leaves in a period. The specification does not print a formula, so use the standard form:

Labour turnover (%) = (number of employees leaving in a period ÷ average number of employees) × 100

Larkfield Care (invented) runs care homes with an average of 80 staff. In the last year 22 staff left.

Labour turnover = 22 ÷ 80 × 100 = **27.5%**.

The HR director sets an objective to cut turnover to 15% within 18 months. At 80 staff, 15% means 0.15 × 80 = 12 leavers. That is 10 fewer than now. Each leaver costs £3,200 in recruitment, induction and lost output, so the saving is 10 × £3,200 = **£32,000** a year.

A retention programme (pay review and a training plan) costs £18,000 a year. Net benefit = £32,000 − £18,000 = **£14,000**. If the full saving is achieved, the programme pays for itself in 18,000 ÷ 32,000 × 12 = 6.75 months, about 7 months.

Interpretation and evaluation:

- Some turnover is healthy: it brings fresh ideas and removes poor performers. A figure of zero would not be a good target.
- The right target depends on the sector. Care and hospitality tend to have high turnover; a skilled engineering firm should have less.
- The saving depends on turnover really falling to 15%, and not all of the £3,200 per leaver is a cash cost.
- Reasons for leaving matter. If staff leave for better pay, a pay review could help. If they leave because of poor management, pay will not solve it.

## Worked example 2: absence and labour cost

Larkfield employs 80 staff, each expected to work 240 days a year, so the total working days are 80 × 240 = 19,200. Last year 1,440 days were lost to absence.

Absence rate = 1,440 ÷ 19,200 × 100 = **7.5%**.

The target is 5%, which is 0.05 × 19,200 = 960 days. This means 480 fewer days lost. At a cost of £110 a day in sick pay and cover, the saving is 480 × £110 = **£52,800**.

Labour costs as a share of revenue: wages of £2,400,000 on revenue of £4,800,000 is **50%**. A target to keep the ratio at or below 50% as the business grows means that wages must not rise faster than revenue.

These figures show how objectives can be linked: reducing absence and turnover reduces cost, raises productivity and, for a care home, improves the care that residents receive.

## Linking to the business objectives

HR objectives help the business meet its objectives when they:

- **Support growth:** recruiting and training staff for new sites.
- **Support profit:** controlling labour cost and raising productivity.
- **Support quality and reputation:** retaining skilled staff and training them well.
- **Support survival:** keeping within the law and reducing the risk of claims.

They can also conflict with other objectives. A cost-cutting aim (reduce headcount) conflicts with a quality aim (more staff time with customers). A short-term profit target may conflict with a long-term aim to train. Strong answers say how a business resolves such conflicts: by deciding the priority and measuring the effects.

## Evaluating usefulness

**Benefits.** Objectives give clarity and focus, allow measurement, help managers to plan, and show staff what is expected. They help stakeholders: staff know what the business offers; owners see how people costs are managed; customers benefit from better service when staff are trained and settled.

**Limits.**

- Targets can encourage a focus on what is measured. A manager told to cut absence may pressure sick staff to come in.
- Objectives may be unrealistic, so staff lose faith in them.
- Different objectives can conflict (cost against development).
- External factors, such as a shortage of labour in the area, may make a target impossible.
- Too many objectives dilute effort.
- Setting objectives without consultation may reduce commitment.

**Judgement.** HR objectives are most useful when they are few, linked to corporate aims, measurable and agreed with the people who must carry them out. They are less useful in a very small business that has no HR specialist, where informal management may work as well.

## Recommending objectives

For a recommendation question:

1. Identify the main business objective and the main people problem in the case.
2. Choose two or three HR objectives that address the problem.
3. Make them measurable and time-bound.
4. Say how they will be achieved and how much they will cost.
5. Consider conflicts and stakeholder effects.
6. Explain what the recommendation depends on.

For Larkfield, a good recommendation might be to cut turnover to 15% within 18 months and absence to 5% within two years, because the care homes depend on experienced staff and these two measures link to cost and quality. It would depend on discovering why staff leave.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a list of HR activities (recruitment, training) rather than objectives, which are targets.
- Writing objectives that cannot be measured.
- Forgetting the link to corporate objectives.
- Treating low labour turnover as always good.
- Ignoring the conflict between cost and investment in staff.
- Dividing by the wrong base in labour turnover or absence calculations.

## Exam technique

When you explain, define the term and give a business example. When you calculate, state the formula and use the average number of staff. For evaluation, weigh the benefits and limits and give a judgement based on the case, such as the type of business, its size and its labour market.
