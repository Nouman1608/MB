## What the specification limits you to

Section 4.2.2.2 sets clear boundaries, which also tell you what not to spend time on:

- the structure and functioning of the human heart and lungs, "including how lungs are adapted for gaseous exchange";
- blood vessels associated with the heart: **aorta, vena cava, pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein and coronary arteries** only;
- "Knowledge of the names of the heart valves is not required";
- lungs: **trachea, bronchi, alveoli and the capillary network** surrounding the alveoli only;
- the natural pacemaker and artificial pacemakers;
- arteries, veins and capillaries, explaining how structure relates to function;
- "simple compound measures such as rate" and rate calculations for blood flow (MS 1a, 1c).

## How one heartbeat moves blood

The heart is an organ made mostly of muscle tissue, with two **atria** above two **ventricles**. A wall of muscle separates the right side, which carries deoxygenated blood, from the left side, which carries oxygenated blood.

1. Blood flows into both atria: from the vena cava on the right and the pulmonary vein on the left.
2. The atria contract, pushing blood down into the ventricles.
3. The ventricles contract, forcing blood out: the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery, the left ventricle into the aorta.
4. Valves close behind the blood so it cannot flow backwards into the chamber it has just left.

Because the ventricles do the main pumping, their walls are thicker than the walls of the atria. The **left ventricle** wall is thickest of all, because it must pump blood at high pressure around the whole body, while the right ventricle pumps only to the nearby lungs.

## Why a double circulatory system?

The specification states that "the right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs where gas exchange takes place" and "the left ventricle pumps blood around the rest of the body". Blood therefore passes through the heart twice per complete circuit. This arrangement has two advantages you can use in explanations:

- **Higher pressure to the body.** Blood loses pressure as it flows through the narrow capillaries of the lungs. Returning it to the heart to be pumped again means it reaches the body at high pressure, so substances are delivered quickly.
- **No mixing.** Oxygenated and deoxygenated blood are kept apart, so the blood sent to the body carries as much oxygen as possible.

Both matter for an active animal whose cells respire quickly and need a fast supply of oxygen and glucose.

## The coronary arteries and the pacemaker

Heart muscle needs its own oxygen supply for respiration. The **coronary arteries** branch from the aorta and carry oxygenated blood over the surface of the heart into its muscle. This is why their narrowing, in coronary heart disease, is so dangerous.

The **natural resting heart rate** is controlled by "a group of cells located in the right atrium that act as a pacemaker". If this rhythm becomes irregular, an **artificial pacemaker**, an electrical device, can be fitted to correct it.

## The lungs and gaseous exchange

Air passes down the **trachea**, which divides into two **bronchi**, one to each lung. The bronchi branch repeatedly and end in millions of tiny air sacs, the **alveoli**, each surrounded by a **capillary network**.

### Model answer: how are the lungs adapted for gaseous exchange?

> Millions of alveoli give a very large surface area, so more oxygen can diffuse into the blood at the same time. The walls of the alveoli and of the capillaries are each very thin, so the diffusion distance between air and blood is short and diffusion is fast. A dense network of capillaries surrounds each alveolus, and the blood flowing through it continually carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, which keeps the concentration gradients steep. Breathing replaces the air in the alveoli, so the oxygen concentration there stays high and the carbon dioxide concentration stays low.

Each sentence pairs a **feature** with **its effect on diffusion**. That pairing is what turns a description into an explanation.

## Arteries, veins and capillaries: explaining structure

The specification asks you to "explain how the structure of these vessels relates to their functions". Use the pattern *feature, so, function*.

- **Arteries** carry blood away from the heart at high pressure. They have **thick walls with muscle and elastic fibres**, so they can withstand the pressure; the elastic fibres stretch as each surge of blood arrives and recoil afterwards, which helps keep the blood moving.
- **Veins** carry blood back to the heart at low pressure. They have **thinner walls and a wide lumen**, so blood flows with little resistance, and **valves**, so blood cannot flow backwards.
- **Capillaries** exchange substances with the tissues. Their walls are **one cell thick**, so the diffusion distance is short; they are **narrow** and very numerous, so every cell is close to one and blood flows slowly enough for exchange.

### Worked example 1: reading pressure data

These illustrative values show blood pressure in different vessels (arbitrary units):

| Vessel | Aorta | Small artery | Capillary | Small vein | Vena cava |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure | 100 | 80 | 30 | 10 | 2 |

What the data tell you:

- Pressure is highest in the aorta, next to the left ventricle, which explains why arteries need thick, muscular, elastic walls.
- The biggest fall is between small arteries and capillaries: 80 − 30 = 50 units, because blood is forced through huge numbers of very narrow vessels.
- By the veins the pressure is very low, which is why valves are needed to stop backflow.

## Rate calculations for blood flow

A rate is a quantity divided by time. Write the formula, convert units if needed, and give the unit in the answer.

**Worked example 2: pulse rate.** A student counts 26 beats in 20 seconds.

```
Heart rate = 26 / 20 x 60 = 78 beats per minute
```

**Worked example 3: percentage change.** During exercise the heart rate rises from 70 to 154 beats per minute.

```
Percentage increase = (154 - 70) / 70 x 100 = 120%
```

**Worked example 4: converting a flow rate.** Blood flows through an artery at 4.5 dm³ per minute. Give this in cm³ per second.

```
4.5 dm3 = 4500 cm3
4500 / 60 = 75 cm3 per second
```

**Worked example 5: speed in a capillary.** A red blood cell travels 1 mm along a capillary in 2.5 seconds.

```
Speed = 1 / 2.5 = 0.4 mm/s = 400 um/s
```

The slow flow gives more time for oxygen and glucose to diffuse out of the capillary into the tissue.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the route of blood: name chambers and vessels in order and say where oxygenation happens.
- **Explain** an adaptation: feature, then "so", then the effect on function or diffusion.
- **Compare** vessels: write matched pairs ("arteries have thick walls, whereas veins have thin walls"); a list about one vessel does not compare.
- **Calculate**: show the formula and units; one decimal place or two significant figures is usually sensible unless the question says otherwise.

## Common errors

- Defining arteries as vessels that carry oxygenated blood. Define by direction: away from the heart.
- Saying the left ventricle is thicker "because it pumps more blood". Both ventricles pump the same volume; the left pumps at higher pressure, over a longer distance.
- Naming heart valves you do not need, instead of explaining what valves do.
- Saying capillary walls are "thin" instead of "one cell thick".
- Saying blood is oxygenated in the heart; it is oxygenated in the lungs.
- Leaving out "per minute" or the unit on a rate.
