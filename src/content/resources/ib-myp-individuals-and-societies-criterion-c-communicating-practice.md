> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** for revision and practice on Criterion C. They are **not** reproduced IB material and they do not copy the structure or mark tariffs of any on-screen examination. Real MYP work is judged against criterion level descriptors (achievement levels 1-8), so the `[n]` marks below are a revision aid, not IB marks.

Study first: the [Criterion C study guide](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-c-communicating/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criterion-c-communicating-revision-notes/). Also: [IB MYP Individuals and Societies hub](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-individuals-and-societies/) and [printable checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-myp/myp-individuals-and-societies/). All people, places and sources are invented.

## Questions

**1.** (Criterion C) A task reads: "Write a blog post for students at your school explaining why they should recycle." Identify the audience and the purpose. **[2]**

**2.** (Criterion C) Two students begin a letter to the town's mayor.

- Student 1: "Hi Mayor, your town's bins are rubbish lol."
- Student 2: "I am writing to ask the council to increase the number of recycling bins near the school."

Identify which opening is more appropriate and explain why. **[2]**

**3.** (Criterion C) Rewrite this sentence for an audience of Year 7 students: "The proliferation of single-use plastics constitutes a significant environmental hazard." **[2]**

**4.** (Criterion C) These parts of a formal letter are in the wrong order: closing request; sign-off; body paragraph; greeting; purpose paragraph. Write them in the correct order. **[3]**

**5.** (Criterion C) Read the opening of a speech.

> "Friends, classmates, teachers: how many of you walked past a full bin this morning? Today I want to show you three reasons to act, and one simple way to start."

Identify three features that make this an effective opening for a speech. **[3]**

**6.** (Criterion C)

**(a)** A student includes a bibliography but has no citations in the text. Explain why this does not meet the standard for documenting sources. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an in-text citation and a reference-list entry in a recognised style for this book: M. Lopez, 2020, *Waste in cities*, published by Riverside Books in Dublin. **[3]**

**7.** (Criterion C) Plan a poster that informs students about saving water at home. List four elements of the layout and say why each helps the reader. **[4]**

**8.** (Criterion C) A student writes this letter to the council:

> "Dear Sir. u should ban cars from the high street because its bad. my friend said it makes air dirty. Yours, Sam"

Identify four weaknesses in how the letter communicates. **[4]**

**9.** (Criterion C) Rewrite the letter in Question 8 as a formal paragraph. Use this invented evidence: a council air survey in 2024 found that nitrogen dioxide was above the recommended limit on 9 of 12 days measured. Cite the evidence. **[4]**

**10.** (Criterion C) Write the opening paragraph (about 80 words) of a speech to a school assembly persuading students to walk or cycle to school. Then list the order of the rest of the speech in four stages, and give an in-text citation and reference-list entry for one invented source. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Audience: students at the school (accept peers or teenagers) [1]. Purpose: to explain (and persuade) why they should recycle [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Student 2's opening is more appropriate [1]. It is formal and polite, and states the purpose clearly, which suits a letter to an official. Student 1 uses slang and an insulting tone [1]. **[2]**

**3.** For example: "Using plastic once and throwing it away is very harmful to nature." [1] Simpler words ("using plastic once" in place of "single-use plastics") and shorter sentence structure suitable for Year 7 [1]. **[2]**
*Marking note:* One mark for a correct meaning, one for vocabulary and sentence length matched to the audience.

**4.** Greeting; purpose paragraph; body paragraph; closing request; sign-off [3 for all five in order; 2 for four or three in the correct position; 1 for two]. **[3]**

**5.** Any three of: direct address to the audience ("Friends, classmates, teachers") [1]; a rhetorical question that makes listeners think ("how many of you walked past a full bin?") [1]; a relatable, everyday hook [1]; signposting that previews the structure ("three reasons ... and one simple way") [1]; a clear purpose ("to act") [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Citations in the text show which information came from which source [1]. A bibliography alone does not let the reader check each claim, so the sources are not fully documented [1].
**(b)** In-text: (Lopez, 2020) [1]. Reference list: Lopez, M. (2020) *Waste in cities*. Dublin: Riverside Books. [1 for author and year, 1 for title, place and publisher]
**[5]**
*Marking note:* Any recognised style is accepted if it is complete and consistent.

**7.** Any four, each with a reason (1 mark each element and reason pair).
- A clear title, so readers know the topic at a glance [1].
- Headed sections, for example kitchen, bathroom, garden, so that readers can scan [1].
- Short facts or numbers, because posters are read quickly [1].
- Labelled pictures or icons, because they show ideas without words [1].
- A call to action at the bottom, so the purpose is clear [1].
- Sources in small print, to credit the information [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Any four of: informal language and abbreviations ("u", lol-style wording) not suited to a formal letter [1]; no clear purpose or request ("bad" is vague) [1]; no evidence, only "my friend said" [1]; no source is cited [1]; poor sentence structure and punctuation, with capital letters missing [1]; no developed paragraphs or structure, and the greeting is not correct for a named officer [1]. **[4]**

**9.** For example: "Dear Councillor Reyes, I am writing to ask the council to close the high street to cars. An air survey carried out by the council in 2024 found that nitrogen dioxide was above the recommended limit on 9 of the 12 days measured (Millbrook Council, 2024). I therefore urge the council to approve a trial closure." [Formal tone [1]; evidence stated accurately [1]; in-text citation [1]; clear purpose and request, logical order [1].] **[4]**

**10.** Award up to eight marks.
- An opening paragraph in a style suited to a school assembly (direct address, simple clear words, a hook such as a question or a surprising fact) [2].
- The purpose (persuade) is clear in the paragraph [1].
- A four-stage structure listed in a logical order, for example: reasons (health), reasons (environment), a counter-argument and reply, call to action with a challenge [2].
- A correct in-text citation in the paragraph [1].
- A complete reference-list entry with author, year, title and publisher or website [1].
- Consistency of style and use of a recognised convention [1].
**[8]**
*Marking note:* The paragraph can contain a fact from an invented source such as (Hale, 2023). Credit any consistent style.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing in the same register for every audience.
- Not following the format's conventions.
- Citations in the list but not in the text, or in the text but not in the list.
- Vague purpose and no call to action.
- Informal abbreviations in formal writing.
- Mixing referencing styles.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a model length for question 10?**
The question asks for about 80 words for the paragraph, plus the plan and the reference. Quality matters more than length.

**Do I lose marks for spelling and punctuation?**
Accuracy supports the communication of ideas. The descriptors use "effectively and accurately", so errors that confuse the reader will affect the style strand.

**Can I invent a source in practice?**
In practice you can, to rehearse the format. In real work every source must be real and checked.
