This guide covers the **External influences on human resource management** content in the Area of Study **Human resources within the external environment** of the OCR A Level Business (H431) specification, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027. It is numbered 5.14 on this site; the specification does not number its content. The [human resources study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-human-resources/) covers the whole topic. This page goes further on this one heading: it takes each of the eight influences the specification lists, shows how it changes human resource decisions, and works three costed examples to show how to judge the size of an effect. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

The specification has a single statement: learners should be able to **evaluate** the following influences on human resources: social, legal, ethical, environmental, economic, political, technological and international. "Evaluate" means weighing the size and direction of each effect on the business and its stakeholders and reaching a judgement. Description alone is not enough.

## How to evaluate an external influence

The human resource decisions at stake are planning the workforce, recruiting, training, pay, organisation, relations with employees and redundancy. For each influence, ask:

1. **What changes?** (The supply of labour, the cost of labour, the skills needed, the rules.)
2. **How big is it?** Use figures where you can.
3. **How fast is it?** A sudden change is harder to manage than a slow trend.
4. **Can the business respond?** Larger firms with reserves can adapt faster.
5. **Who gains and who loses?** Owners, managers, employees, customers, the community.

## Social influences

Social influences are changes in population, attitudes and lifestyles. Examples:

- **An ageing population** shrinks the pool of young workers and raises the number of employees close to retirement. A business may need to retain older workers, offer phased retirement and plan for the loss of knowledge.
- **Attitudes to work-life balance** push demand for flexible hours, remote working and part-time roles. Offering them widens the recruitment pool but needs new systems.
- **Changing family patterns** and more women in work have increased demand for childcare support and flexible schedules.

Effect: lower recruitment difficulty for firms that adapt; higher turnover for those that do not.

## Legal influences

Law sets minimum standards on contracts, pay, health and safety, equality, data protection and dismissal. New laws raise costs and administration, but they also protect staff and reduce the risk of claims. A business that pays above the legal floor, trains managers and keeps records reduces risk. Compliance is not optional, so the question is the size and timing of cost. A small firm with no human resources department finds it harder to keep up with changes than a large one.

## Ethical influences

Ethics concern what a business believes is right, beyond what is legal. Examples are fair pay (a living wage above the legal minimum), no use of zero-hours contracts where regular hours are needed, honest recruitment advertising and respect for staff privacy. Ethical conduct can improve motivation and reputation and attract workers. It may cost more in the short term. A business that is exposed for poor treatment of staff, for example through social media, risks losing customers and recruits.

## Environmental influences

Environmental concerns affect the skills needed (green jobs, energy management), working patterns (remote working cuts commuting) and recruitment (many applicants prefer environmentally responsible employers). Extreme weather can affect where and when staff can work. A business may need to train staff in new methods or recruit new specialists, which adds cost but may reduce energy costs and improve its image.

## Economic influences

Economic conditions affect the demand for goods and therefore for labour, and the supply of workers.

- **Unemployment:** when it is low, workers are scarce, so recruiting is harder and pay rises faster. When it is high, recruiting is easier and staff are more likely to stay.
- **Inflation:** raises pressure for pay rises.
- **The business cycle:** in a boom, firms recruit; in a recession, they freeze hiring or make redundancies.
- **Interest rates and exchange rates:** these change demand and costs, and so the number of staff a business can afford.

### Worked example 1: low unemployment and recruitment cost

Carrow Warehousing hires 30 pickers a year. When unemployment is high, it receives many applicants and the cost per hire is £900. When unemployment falls, applicants are few and the cost per hire is £1,800. Because rivals pay more, Carrow also adds £0.80 an hour to the wages of the 30 new staff, who each work 37.5 hours a week for 52 weeks.

- Recruitment cost in the high-unemployment year = 30 × £900 = **£27,000**.
- Recruitment cost in the low-unemployment year = 30 × £1,800 = **£54,000**.
- Wage premium = £0.80 × 37.5 × 52 = £1,560 per person; for 30 people = **£46,800**.
- Extra annual cost = (£54,000 − £27,000) + £46,800 = **£73,800**.

Evaluation: the cost of £73,800 is large, but a failure to recruit would cost more if orders went unfilled. Carrow could reduce the cost by raising retention (so it hires fewer people), training existing staff and offering flexible hours. The effect lasts as long as unemployment stays low, so the strategy should assume it is not temporary.

## Political influences

Political influences are decisions by government that affect employment: policy on wages and benefits, taxes on employers, training funding, apprenticeship schemes, immigration rules and public sector pay. A new employer tax raises the cost of each employee. Subsidised training lowers the cost of developing skills. Political change can be fast and hard to predict, so businesses plan for several scenarios.

## Technological influences

Technology changes which skills are needed, how work is done and how many people are required. It also changes how businesses recruit (online platforms), train (e-learning), communicate and monitor performance (software).

### Worked example 2: automation and the workforce

Brannock Packing has 20 packers on £24,000 each. On-costs (employer contributions, pension) add 15%. It is considering a robot packing cell costing £600,000 with running costs of £60,000 a year. After installation it needs 8 packers and 4 technicians on £34,000 each, with the same on-costs.

- Current labour cost = 20 × £24,000 × 1.15 = **£552,000**.
- New labour cost = (8 × £24,000 + 4 × £34,000) × 1.15 = £328,000 × 1.15 = **£377,200**.
- Labour saving = £552,000 − £377,200 = **£174,800**.
- Saving after running costs = £174,800 − £60,000 = **£114,800**.
- Simple payback = £600,000 ÷ £114,800 = about **5.2 years**.

Human resource effects: 12 packer roles are lost (20 − 8) and 4 technician roles are created, so the net loss is 8 posts; Brannock must plan redundancy or redeployment, retrain some packers as technicians, and consult staff. Stakeholders: owners gain from lower costs after five years; remaining staff face change and need training; redundant staff lose income; the community loses jobs. Judgement: the investment is justified only if the plant has a long life, demand is stable and the business handles the workforce change fairly, because poor handling would damage morale and may breach legal duties.

## International influences

International influences include migration of workers, the availability of labour and skills in other countries, wage differences, trade agreements, exchange rates and the ability to locate work overseas.

### Worked example 3: moving work overseas

Verano Support runs a UK call centre with 40 staff paid £23,000 each. It is considering moving the work to a centre overseas, where the same job pays £9,000.

- UK payroll = 40 × £23,000 = **£920,000**.
- Overseas payroll = 40 × £9,000 = **£360,000**.
- Saving = **£560,000**, which is 60.9% of the UK payroll (560,000 ÷ 920,000).

This ignores set-up costs, management time, redundancy payments, lower customer satisfaction if language or time differences cause problems, and exchange rate movements that could raise the overseas wage bill. Stakeholders: owners gain; UK staff lose jobs; customers may notice a change in service; overseas staff gain employment. Ethical and reputational factors also matter: a business announcing redundancies while keeping its brand "British" risks criticism. Judgement: the saving is large, but a careful business would pilot the move with part of the work and monitor customer satisfaction before committing.

## Putting it together

Influences interact. A tight labour market (economic) pushes a business towards automation (technological), which needs new skills (social, educational) and may trigger redundancy rules (legal). The best evaluations pick the **two or three most important** influences for the case, size them, and say which matters most and why.

## Common mistakes

- Listing all eight influences with one line each. The question usually asks for the most important in the case.
- Describing an influence without linking it to a human resource decision.
- Assuming every influence is a threat; some are opportunities.
- Ignoring how quickly the influence changes.
- Not weighing the effect on different stakeholders.

## Exam technique

State the influence, link it to a human resource decision (recruitment, pay, training, redundancy), give a figure or example to show size, then weigh it. Finish with a judgement on which influence matters most and what could change that view.
