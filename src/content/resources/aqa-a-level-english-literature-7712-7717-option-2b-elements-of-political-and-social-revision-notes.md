## The option in one box

- **Three texts**: one **post-2000 prose**, one **poetry** and one further text; **one of the three
  pre-1900**.
- **Open book**: clean copies only, no annotations or added notes.
- **Three answers**: unseen (Section A), one set text (Section B), two other set texts connected
  (Section C).

## AQA's core idea

The texts have "issues of power and powerlessness at their core". They "foreground oppression and
domination", in public and private settings, and protest is "central to each text's structure".
The key word is **elements**.

## Set texts and their strongest elements

| Text | Strongest elements |
|---|---|
| *Harvest* (Crace) | Enclosure of common land; outsiders punished; a narrator complicit in the village's violence |
| *Brotherless Night* (Ganeshananthan) | Civil war in Sri Lanka; militancy and its costs; a young woman's moral choices |
| *The Kite Runner* (Hosseini) | Ethnic hierarchy (Pashtun and Hazara); war and the Taliban; guilt and redemption |
| *Songs of Innocence and of Experience* (Blake) | Child labour; Church and state; contrary states of the soul |
| Harrison, *Selected Poems* | Class and accent; working lives; industrial decline; political anger in strict forms |
| *Hard Times* (Dickens) | Utilitarian education; industrial labour; imagination against facts |
| *A Doll's House* (Ibsen, trans. Meyer) | Gender politics in marriage; law and money; a woman's rebellion |
| *Henry IV Part I* (Shakespeare) | Rebellion; legitimacy of a usurping king; tavern against court |
| *The Handmaid's Tale* (Atwood) | Theocratic control of women's bodies; language as power; the framing epilogue |

## AQA's elements in six groups

1. **Type**: post-modern novel, science fiction, satirical poetry, historical and political drama.
2. **Settings**: real and imagined places and times where power struggles play out.
3. **The struggle**: the powerful and the powerless; pursuit of power, rebellion, warfare; ruling
   classes; corruption, conspiracy, control.
4. **Scale**: the smaller world connected to the larger; human organisation at home, at work, in
   government.
5. **Gender and class**: gender politics and issues of social class.
6. **Form and effect**: structure heightening and perhaps resolving tension; language of the
   created world; comment on a historical society; inviting reflection on our own world.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dystopia | An imagined society organised around oppression |
| Speculative fiction | Fiction imagining a possible future or alternative world |
| Satire | Ridicule exposing folly or abuse of power |
| Utilitarianism | Philosophy judging actions by usefulness; satirised in *Hard Times* |
| Enclosure | Fencing of common land for private use, as in *Harvest* |
| Patriarchy | A social system in which men hold power |
| Hegemony | Power maintained through consent and cultural dominance |
| Polemic | Forceful argument against an idea or institution |
| Contraries | Blake's paired states, innocence and experience |
| Received Pronunciation | The prestige accent Harrison confronts in "Them and [uz]" |

## Unseen checklist

- Who has power, who lacks it, and how do we know?
- Is the setting public, private or both?
- Whose voice tells the story, and is resistance given a voice?
- Which register belongs to the powerful, and which to the powerless?
- Does the structure heighten, resolve or leave open the tension?
- One argument about significance, not a list.

## Text pairings for Section C

- **Gender**: *A Doll's House* with *The Handmaid's Tale*.
- **Class and labour**: *Hard Times* with Harrison, or with Blake's chimney sweepers.
- **Rebellion and legitimacy**: *Henry IV Part I* with *Brotherless Night*.
- **Land and dispossession**: *Harvest* with Blake's "London".
- **Ethnic hierarchy and guilt**: *The Kite Runner* with *Harvest*.

Remember: Section C must use two texts you did not use in Section B.

## Exam traps

- Summarising a text's message without analysing its methods.
- Forgetting the private and domestic forms of power.
- Context as history with no link to the words.
- An unseen answer with no overall reading.
- Reusing the Section B text in Section C.
- Using an edition of *A Doll's House* other than the Meyer translation AQA requires.

## Self-test

1. What three types of text are studied for Option 2B, and what date requirement applies?
2. How does AQA narrow the claim that "all texts are political"?
3. Which edition of *A Doll's House* must be used?
4. Name the three post-2000 prose texts.
5. Which set text satirises utilitarian education?
6. Which Harrison poem confronts the prestige of Received Pronunciation?
7. Which text ends with an academic lecture about the narrator's account?
8. What does "the connection of the smaller world to the larger world" mean?
9. Which set text is a history play about rebellion against a usurping king?
10. What is enclosure, and which set text explores it?
11. Give two settings for power struggles from different set texts.
12. Is *The Handmaid's Tale* a post-2000 text?

## Answers

1. One post-2000 prose text, one poetry text and one further text, one of which must be pre-1900.
2. By defining the set texts as those with power and powerlessness at their core and protest
   central to their structure.
3. The Bloomsbury Methuen Drama edition translated by Michael Meyer.
4. *Harvest*, *Brotherless Night* and *The Kite Runner*.
5. *Hard Times*.
6. "Them and [uz]".
7. *The Handmaid's Tale*, through its Historical Notes.
8. Private or local experience (a home, a workplace, a village) linked to national or political
   structures of power.
9. *Henry IV Part I*.
10. Fencing common land for private use; *Harvest*.
11. Any two: Gilead; Coketown; the village in *Harvest*; Kabul; Jaffna; the Helmers' home; the
    tavern and the court in *Henry IV Part I*.
12. No; it was published in 1985, so it can only be the further text.
