## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define what is meant by **othering**. **[2]**

**2.** Define what is meant by **interpellation**. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by **incapacitation** as an aim of punishment. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what Goffman meant by a **discreditable** identity. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and briefly explain **two** agencies of formal social control. **[4]**

**6.** Identify and briefly explain **two** aims of punishment other than incapacitation. **[4]**

**7.** Identify and briefly explain **two** criticisms of zero-tolerance policing. **[4]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which surveillance may be used to control behaviour. **[8]**

**9.** Explain **two** differences between Althusser's and Gramsci's views of how ruling classes
maintain control. **[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which women may experience distinctive forms of social control.
**[8]**

**11.** Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that punishment mainly serves to
strengthen shared values. **[20]**

**12.** Evaluate the interactionist view that agents of social control create deviance rather than
simply respond to it. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Defining a group as fundamentally different from, and inferior to, "us" [1], which can be used
to justify excluding or controlling them, as Said showed with Orientalism [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Althusser's term for how ideology "hails" individuals [1], so they recognise and accept the
identity or role offered, such as "obedient pupil", as their own [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Removing an offender's ability to commit further crimes [1], for example through
imprisonment or a driving ban [1]. **[2]**

**4.** An identity whose stigma is not immediately visible [1], so the person must manage
information to prevent others discovering it, for example concealing a criminal record [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Two agencies, 2 marks each (identify [1], explain [1]):
- Police [1]: enforce laws through arrest, warnings and investigation [1].
- Courts [1]: decide guilt and impose formal sanctions such as fines or prison [1].
- Prisons and probation [1]: carry out punishment and supervise offenders [1].
- Government and laws [1]: define what is criminal and set penalties [1].
- Other enforcement agencies [1]: regulators or customs enforce specific rules [1].
**[4]**

**6.** Two aims, 2 marks each:
- Retribution [1]: offenders deserve punishment proportionate to the harm caused [1].
- Deterrence [1]: punishment discourages the offender and others from offending [1].
- Rehabilitation [1]: changing offenders through education or treatment to prevent reoffending [1].
- Reparation [1]: offenders repair harm, for example through restorative justice [1].
- Denunciation [1]: expresses society's disapproval of the act [1].
**[4]**

**7.** Two criticisms, 2 marks each:
- Discriminatory targeting [1]: stop-and-search and minor arrests fall heavily on poor and minority
  neighbourhoods [1].
- Damages trust [1]: aggressive enforcement can alienate communities, reducing cooperation with
  police [1].
- Uncertain effect [1]: crime fell in many places without zero tolerance, so its impact is disputed [1].
- Displaces resources [1]: focus on minor offences may neglect serious and white-collar crime [1].
**[4]**

**8.** Two ways, 4 marks each (identify [1], explain [1], develop [1], evidence [1]):
- **Self-discipline** [1]: if people believe they might be watched, for example by CCTV [1], they
  regulate their own behaviour [1]; Foucault's panopticon describes this disciplinary power [1].
- **Data collection and prediction** [1]: digital platforms and states gather data on movements and
  online activity [1], allowing behaviour to be predicted and nudged [1]; Zuboff calls this
  surveillance capitalism [1].
- Credit: the synopticon (Mathiesen); workplace monitoring; facial recognition at borders.
**[8]**

**9.** Two differences, 4 marks each:
- **Role of force** [1]: Althusser gives repressive state apparatuses (police, army, courts) a
  defined role alongside ideological ones [1]; Gramsci emphasises consent through civil society, with
  force held in reserve [1], so hegemony is mainly about winning agreement [1].
- **Room for resistance** [1]: Althusser's interpellation suggests individuals are formed as subjects
  by ideology, which can seem deterministic [1]; Gramsci argues hegemony must be continually won and
  can be challenged [1], allowing counter-hegemonic movements [1].
**[8]**

**10.** Two ways, 4 marks each:
- **Control in the home** [1]: domestic responsibilities and, in some cases, financial dependence
  or violence [1] restrict women's time and freedom [1]; Heidensohn argued women are controlled at
  home more than men [1].
- **Control in public** [1]: fear of harassment or violence [1] leads many women to avoid public
  places at night, and concern for reputation limits behaviour [1]; Heidensohn saw this as a form of
  control that also helps explain women's lower recorded offending [1].
- Credit: control at work through male supervisors and sexual harassment; dress codes; religious or
  family rules on movement.
**[8]**

**11.** Levels of response:
- 17–20: detailed knowledge; sustained application to a named society; balanced evaluation;
  justified conclusion.
- 13–16: good knowledge and application; some evaluation.
- 9–12: reasonable knowledge; limited evaluation.
- 5–8: basic, descriptive.
- 1–4: fragments.

Indicative content. *For:* Durkheim's view that punishment reaffirms the collective conscience;
denunciation expresses shared disapproval; public trials and media coverage unite law-abiding
citizens; restitutive law in complex societies restores relationships; restorative justice
reintegrates offenders into the community. *Against:* Marxists argue punishment protects ruling-class
property and falls mainly on the working class; Foucault argues modern punishment disciplines bodies
and minds through surveillance; deterrence, incapacitation and rehabilitation are separate aims;
interactionists show punishment can label offenders and increase deviance; feminists note gendered
treatment; in divided societies punishment may express the values of a dominant group, not shared
ones. *Application:* reward examples of punishments from a named society. *Conclusion:* supported
judgement. **[20]**

**12.** Levels of response as for Question 11.

Indicative content. *For:* Becker (1963): deviance is created by rule-makers and enforcers who label
behaviour; labelling can produce a deviant master status and a self-fulfilling prophecy; Goffman
shows stigma pushes people towards others who share it; Stanley Cohen shows moral panics and
policing amplify deviance; selective policing of certain neighbourhoods or groups creates patterns
in statistics. *Against:* some acts (such as serious violence) are condemned in most societies,
suggesting control responds to real harm; functionalists see control as protecting shared values;
Marxists argue labelling theory ignores the structural sources of crime and the power behind law;
left realists argue victims experience real crime that needs a response; labelling may sometimes
deter rather than amplify. *Conclusion:* supported judgement, for example that control shapes which
deviance is recorded and how it develops but does not create all deviance. **[20]**
