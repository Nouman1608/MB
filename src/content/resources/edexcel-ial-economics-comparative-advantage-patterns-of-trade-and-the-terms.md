## 1a. Benefits and costs of specialisation and trade

Specialisation means a country concentrates its resources on the goods it produces at lowest opportunity cost, then trades for the rest.

Benefits:
- Output rises because resources move to their best use.
- Larger markets allow economies of scale, so unit costs fall.
- Consumers gain lower prices, more variety and access to goods that cannot be made at home.
- Competition from imports pressures domestic firms to cut costs and innovate.

Costs:
- Overdependence on a few exports exposes a country to price swings and demand shocks.
- Workers in declining industries lose jobs, and retraining takes time (structural unemployment).
- Imports can undercut domestic firms, and fragile infant industries may never get started.
- Transport and the extra output can add environmental costs.

## 1b. Absolute and comparative advantage

**Absolute advantage** means making a good with fewer resources, or making more from the same resources, than a rival. **Comparative advantage** means giving up less of other goods to make it. Trade depends on the second, not the first.

### Worked example 1: gains when one country is better at everything

Output per worker-year. Country P has 100 workers and Country Q has 200 (illustrative).

| | Rice (tonnes) | Bicycles |
|---|---|---|
| Country P | 8 | 4 |
| Country Q | 5 | 1 |

Step 1, absolute advantage: P is better at both goods.

Step 2, opportunity costs. P: 1 bicycle costs 8 ÷ 4 = 2 rice. Q: 1 bicycle costs 5 ÷ 1 = 5 rice. Bicycles are cheaper in P, so P has the comparative advantage in bicycles. Q gives up 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2 bicycles per rice against P's 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5, so Q has it in rice.

Step 3, output before trade. P splits workers 50/50: 400 rice and 200 bicycles. Q splits 100/100: 500 rice and 100 bicycles. World: 900 rice and 300 bicycles.

Step 4, full specialisation. P makes 100 × 4 = 400 bicycles. Q makes 200 × 5 = 1,000 rice. World output is 1,000 rice and 400 bicycles: 100 more of each.

Step 5, the mutually beneficial price. Both countries gain only if one bicycle trades for between 2 and 5 rice. Take 4 rice. P sells 100 bicycles for 400 rice and consumes 300 bicycles and 400 rice (100 more bicycles than before). Q consumes 600 rice and 100 bicycles (100 more rice). Neither is worse off. A price nearer 2 favours Q, nearer 5 favours P.

### Assumptions of the theory

- Two countries and two goods (the model is simplified).
- Constant opportunity costs, so complete specialisation is possible.
- No transport costs and no trade barriers.
- Factors move freely between industries within a country but not between countries.
- Full employment, perfect information and similar quality goods.
- Prices reflect opportunity costs; no externalities.

### Limitations

Each assumption that fails limits the theory:
- Transport and trade costs shrink or erase the gain. If shipping costs absorb the whole gain (in example 1, worth 100 bicycles to P), trade no longer benefits P.
- Opportunity costs usually rise as a country specialises, so the gain stops short of full specialisation.
- Factors are not perfectly mobile, so workers displaced from one industry may stay unemployed.
- Barriers, subsidies and exchange rate moves distort relative prices.
- The model is static. Comparative advantage changes (section 2), and protecting an infant industry may build a future advantage the model cannot see.
- Gains are not shared equally, and the distribution depends on the price agreed.
- Specialising in primary products exposes a country to volatile prices.

**Evaluation.** The theory shows that trade can raise world output even when one country is better at everything. It is weakest as a guide for a developing economy with one export, where the risks of dependence and the chance to build new advantages matter more than today's opportunity costs.

## 2. Patterns and volume of world trade

A country's pattern of trade is which goods it trades and with whom. Its volume is how much. Five influences from the specification:

1. **Emerging economies.** Fast growth, large labour forces and rising productivity let them take a growing share of world exports and become big import markets.
2. **Changing comparative advantage.** Investment, education, technology and capital accumulation shift opportunity costs, so a country moves from primary goods to manufactures to services. Rising wages can erode a low-cost advantage.
3. **Trading blocs and bilateral agreements.** Removing barriers between members raises trade between them. How blocs create or divert trade is a separate topic.
4. **Relative exchange rates.** Appreciation raises export prices abroad and lowers import prices; depreciation does the reverse.
5. **Protectionism.** Higher barriers cut trade in the affected goods; falling barriers raise it.

### Worked example 2: a changing share of world trade

Country E (emerging, illustrative). World goods exports rise from 20,000 to 26,000 over ten years; E's exports rise from 600 to 1,430 (same currency units).

- Share of world exports: 600 ÷ 20,000 = 3.0%, then 1,430 ÷ 26,000 = 5.5%.
- Growth: E, (1,430 − 600) ÷ 600 = 138.3%; world, 6,000 ÷ 20,000 = 30%.
- E's exports grew over four times as fast as the world's, so its share almost doubled.

Reasons might include rising productivity, a fall in domestic costs relative to partners, new trade agreements or a competitive exchange rate. The numbers show the change; you must explain why.

### Worked example 3: exchange rate and protection

An exporter in Country A sells a machine at 400 A-units. At 0.50 foreign units per A-unit the foreign price is 200. A appreciates to 0.55, so the price is 400 × 0.55 = 220, up 10%. If the price elasticity of demand for the machine is −1.5, volume falls 15% and foreign-currency revenue changes by 1.10 × 0.85 = 0.935, a 6.5% fall.

If Country B cuts its tariff on a good from 20% to 5%, a 100-unit import price falls from 120 to 105, down 12.5%. With price elasticity of demand −1.2, import volume rises 15%.

## 3. The terms of trade

**Index = 100 × export price index ÷ import price index.**

A rise means each unit of exports buys more imports (an improvement); a fall means fewer (a deterioration). Both indices need the same base year and currency.

### Worked example 4: calculating and interpreting

Country A, base year = 100 (illustrative).

| Year | Export prices (index) | Import prices (index) | Terms of trade index |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | 100 | 100.0 |
| 2 | 110 | 104 | 110 ÷ 104 × 100 = 105.8 |
| 3 | 110 | 125 | 110 ÷ 125 × 100 = 88.0 |

From year 2 to 3 the index fell (88.0 − 105.8) ÷ 105.8 = 16.8%. Export prices were unchanged, so dearer imports caused the whole deterioration.

### Influences on the terms of trade (3b)

- **Export and import prices.** Directly set the index. Commodity price swings move it fast.
- **Relative inflation.** If domestic inflation is 6% and partners' is 2%, domestic export prices rise relative to import prices, which improves the index in the short run. It also cuts competitiveness (1.06 ÷ 1.02 gives a 3.9% relative rise).
- **Relative productivity.** Faster productivity growth lowers unit costs and export prices, which tends to lower the index while raising competitiveness.
- **Relative labour costs.** Unit labour cost = wage ÷ output per worker. If wages rise 6% and productivity 10%, unit labour cost changes by 1.06 ÷ 1.10 = 0.964, down 3.6%. Cheaper exports may lower the index.
- **The exchange rate.** Suppose export prices are fixed in domestic currency and import prices in foreign currency. An 8% appreciation cuts the import index from 125 to 125 ÷ 1.08 = 115.7. The index rises from 88.0 to 110 ÷ 115.7 × 100 = 95.0. Depreciation does the opposite.

### Effects of a change (3c)

**Export revenues.** Revenue is price times quantity, so the effect depends on price elasticity of demand for exports. A firm sells 1,000 units at 50 (revenue 50,000). Prices rise 10% to 55.
- Elastic demand (−1.8): volume falls 18% to 820. Revenue is 55 × 820 = 45,100, down 9.8%.
- Inelastic demand (−0.4): volume falls 4% to 960. Revenue is 52,800, up 5.6%.

**Living standards.** An improvement raises real income because the same exports buy more imports. If import prices rise from 50 to 56, 50,000 of export revenue buys 892.9 units instead of 1,000, 10.7% fewer.

**Balance of trade.** Exports are 1,000 units at 50 and imports 900 units at 50, a surplus of 5,000. Import prices rise 12% and import demand has elasticity −0.5, so volume falls 6% to 846. Imports cost 846 × 56 = 47,376. The surplus shrinks to 2,624. Inelastic import demand makes a deterioration worsen the balance.

**Evaluation.** The effect depends on the elasticities, the time horizon, and why the index moved. A fall caused by productivity gains may raise export volumes and employment. A fall caused by weak commodity prices usually just cuts income.

## Common errors

- Inverting the formula (import over export).
- Saying a rise in the index always raises export revenue.
- Treating the terms of trade as the same as competitiveness.
- Mixing currencies or base years.
- Naming a factor without saying which way prices move.
