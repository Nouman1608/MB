## Questions

**1.** Define the term competitive advantage. **[2]**

**2.** Identify three of the five forces in Porter's Five Forces model. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the nature and purpose of Porter's Five Forces model. **[4]**

**4.** Gorsefield Bakery sells plain loaves at £1.80 each, with a unit cost of £1.35, and sells 90,000 a year. Tinsley Artisan Bakers sells hand-shaped loaves at £4.20 each, with a unit cost of £3.15, and sells 20,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate Gorsefield's gross profit per loaf and its gross margin. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the annual gross profit of each bakery. **[2]**

**5.** Vellan Print Studio prints leaflets and menus for small local firms. There are 14 print firms within 20 miles, with similar prices. Three online printers advertise nationally at lower prices. A basic digital press costs about £18,000. Vellan has 400 clients and none provides more than 4% of its revenue. Two wholesalers supply about 90% of the paper in the area, and one has raised prices by 8% this year.

Analyse the competitive forces facing Vellan, using Porter's Five Forces. **[6]**

**6.** Explain the nature and purpose of Porter's generic strategies. **[4]**

**7.** Identify the generic strategy that each business is following. **[3]**

**(a)** A firm sells one design of folding chair at the lowest price in the country, using one huge factory.
**(b)** A firm makes luxury dog beds, sold only through independent pet boutiques.
**(c)** A firm sells designer watches to all age groups through shops and online, backed by a strong brand.

**8.** Gorsefield Bakery (see question 4) runs one automated plant, buys flour in bulk and sells plain loaves mainly to supermarkets. It plans a stone-baked sourdough range at £3.40 a loaf, made on a slower process with a unit cost of £2.70. Evaluate Gorsefield's current and proposed position using Porter's generic strategies. **[8]**

**9.** Evaluate the usefulness of Porter's Five Forces model to a business thinking of entering a new market. **[8]**

**10.** Evaluate the usefulness of Porter's generic strategies to a business. **[8]**

**11.** Using your answer to question 5, recommend and justify a generic strategy for Vellan Print Studio. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A feature of a business that lets it outperform its rivals [1], such as lower costs or something customers value, leading to more customers or higher margins [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition on its own is short; the second mark usually needs the source or the result of the advantage, not a repeat of the word "advantage".

**2.** One mark for each of three correct forces from: rivalry among existing competitors; threat of new entrants; threat of substitutes; bargaining power of buyers; bargaining power of suppliers [3]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Identify questions need names only. Writing a full explanation of each force uses time that the later questions need.

**3.** Nature: a framework that examines five competitive pressures within an industry [1]. Purpose: to judge how attractive and profitable the industry is [1]. Strong forces push prices down or costs up, which squeezes profit [1]. A business can use this to decide whether to enter or stay, or what barriers or strategy to use [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Nature and purpose" questions reward separating what the model is from what it is used for.

**4. (a)** Gross profit per loaf = 1.80 − 1.35 = **£0.45** [1]. Gross margin = 0.45 ÷ 1.80 × 100 = **25%** [1].
**(b)** Gorsefield: 0.45 × 90,000 = **£40,500** [1]. Tinsley: (4.20 − 3.15) × 20,000 = 1.05 × 20,000 = **£21,000** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula and the working. A final figure that is wrong can still earn credit for a correct method, but only if the working is visible.

**5.** Any three forces, each worth two marks: evidence from the case, then an effect on profit. For example: rivalry is strong, because 14 firms with similar prices compete locally [1], which pushes prices down [1]. Substitutes are strong, because the online printers offer a close alternative at a lower price [1] that limits what Vellan can charge [1]. Supplier power is strong, because two wholesalers control 90% of paper and one has raised prices by 8% [1], which raises Vellan's costs [1]. (Buyer power is weak, as no client exceeds 4% of revenue. New entrants are a moderate threat, as an £18,000 press is a low barrier.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis answers gain credit when evidence is followed by an effect on profit. Naming a force with no case evidence earns little.

**6.** Nature: they are broad routes to competitive advantage, namely cost leadership, differentiation and focus [1]. Cost leadership means the lowest cost in the market; differentiation means a valued difference; focus means a narrow segment [1]. Purpose: to help a business choose a clear route to competitive advantage [1] and align its operations, marketing and finance behind it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the strategies without explaining them is a common way to stop at one mark.

**7. (a)** Cost leadership [1]. **(b)** Focus (differentiation focus) [1]. **(c)** Differentiation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Look for scope as well as source. Part (b) is narrow, while part (c) targets the whole market.

**8.** Currently Gorsefield follows cost leadership: one automated plant, bulk buying and low prices [1]. Its margin of 25% on £1.80 gives £0.45 per loaf, so it relies on volume [1]. The sourdough range is differentiation: a higher price of £3.40 for a distinct product [1]. Its margin is £0.70 per loaf (20.6%), higher per loaf but lower as a percentage [1]. Risks: the slower process may not suit the automated plant, so costs and complexity rise [1]; a premium product in the same brand as cheap loaves may confuse customers [1]; supermarket buyers may resist the higher price [1]. Judgement: the move is worthwhile only if it is run as a separate, clearly branded range and volumes are sufficient; otherwise it risks weakening the cost advantage that currently works [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs a judgement that depends on a condition from the case, not just "it depends".

**9.** For: it provides a structured check of rivalry, entry barriers, substitutes, buyers and suppliers [1], so it shows how profitable the new market might be [1] and where the entrant will face its strongest pressure [1]. Against: it is a snapshot, and technology or regulation can change the forces [1]; it describes the industry rather than the entrant's own strengths [1]; judging how strong each force is involves opinion [1]; it does not say what to do [1]. Judgement: it is a useful first filter before entering, but only alongside other tools and forecasts [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A generic list of pros and cons scores less than points tied to entering a market. Link at least one point to the entrant's position.

**10.** For: simple and forces a clear choice [1]; links to competitive advantage [1]; helps align functions behind one aim [1]. Against: many firms mix strategies successfully [1]; four boxes oversimplify, because differentiation can mean many things [1]; the model does not say how to build the needed capability [1]; it does not show whether the market is profitable [1]. Judgement: most useful for diagnosing what a business is doing, and best used with Five Forces [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that states when the model helps most (for instance, for a firm with limited resources) reads as a judgement.

**11.** Recommend differentiation focus: a local, service-led offer for small firms [1]. Cost leadership is unsuitable: online printers already offer lower prices [1], and a local firm lacks the scale to beat them [1]. Differentiation focus fits because local clients value quick turnaround and design help [1] and 400 small clients give a stable base [1]. Risk: clients may still switch if the price gap grows, so Vellan should check that clients will pay a premium [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A justified recommendation names the strategy, then uses case evidence both to rule out an alternative and to support the choice.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a single business's strengths instead of the industry in Five Forces answers.
- Listing forces without case evidence or an effect on profit.
- Treating all forces as equally important and not ranking them.
- Mistaking a low price for cost leadership.
- Ignoring scope when identifying focus.
- Giving margin as an amount rather than a percentage, or the reverse.
- Evaluating a model with points that would fit any model.
- Omitting a final judgement in "evaluate" and "recommend" answers.
