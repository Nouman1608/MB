Large plants need a vascular system to carry water and mineral ions up from the roots and the products of photosynthesis to where they are used or stored. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 3.1.3 you must explain the need for transport, describe xylem and phloem and where they lie in roots, stems and leaves, explain transpiration and the factors that affect it, estimate transpiration rates with a potometer, explain how water enters and moves through the plant, describe adaptations to dry and wet habitats, and explain translocation.

This guide covers learning outcomes 3.1.3(a) to (f) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 3, Exchange and transport, with the practical work in PAG1, PAG2, PAG5 and PAG11. It adds detail to the module-wide [exchange and transport study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-exchange-and-transport/). Use the [transport in plants revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-transport-in-plants-ocr-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) for recall and the [transport in plants practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-transport-in-plants-ocr-alevel-biology-practice/) to test yourself.

## Why plants need a transport system (a)

Leaves and stems have a relatively large surface area, but a large plant still has a small SA:V overall, and its roots and leaves are far apart. Roots cannot photosynthesise and leaves cannot absorb water from soil, so each part depends on substances made or absorbed elsewhere. Plants have a lower metabolic rate than active animals, but diffusion alone is far too slow over the distances involved.

## Xylem and phloem (b)

| Tissue | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Xylem vessel | Dead cells joined end to end with no end walls, forming continuous tubes; walls thickened with lignin in rings or spirals; pits in the walls | Carries water and mineral ions up the plant; lignin strengthens and waterproofs; pits allow sideways movement |
| Sieve tube element | Living cells with sieve plates (perforated end walls); no nucleus and few organelles | Carries assimilates such as sucrose in solution, up or down |
| Companion cell | Nucleus, dense cytoplasm, many mitochondria; linked to sieve tube by plasmodesmata | Supplies ATP and carries out active loading for the sieve tube |

### Where the tissues are

| Organ | Arrangement |
|---|---|
| Root | Xylem in a central star or X shape; phloem in the spaces between its arms; surrounded by the endodermis |
| Stem | Vascular bundles in a ring near the outside; xylem on the inner side, phloem on the outer side, cambium between |
| Leaf | Vascular bundles in the midrib and veins; xylem towards the upper surface, phloem towards the lower |

The central position in the root resists pulling forces as the plant moves; the ring in the stem resists bending.

**Practical (PAG1, PAG2).** Thin sections of stem or root are stained and viewed under a light microscope; a lignin stain such as phloroglucinol colours lignified xylem walls red. Cutting a celery stalk that has stood in coloured water, transversely and longitudinally, shows the dye in the xylem bundles and the lignin spirals along their length.

## Transpiration (c)

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through the stomata. It is a consequence of gas exchange: stomata must open to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis, and water vapour diffuses out at the same time.

| Factor | Effect on rate | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | Higher light, faster | More stomata open |
| Temperature | Higher, faster | Water molecules gain kinetic energy, so evaporation and diffusion are faster; warm air holds more water vapour, so the gradient is steeper |
| Humidity | Higher, slower | Less difference in water vapour concentration between leaf air spaces and air |
| Air movement | More wind, faster | Moist air near the leaf is blown away, keeping the gradient steep |
| Soil water | Low, slower | Stomata close when the plant is short of water |

### Estimating rate with a potometer

1. Cut a leafy shoot under water, at a slant, to stop air entering the xylem.
2. Fit it into the potometer under water and seal the joints with petroleum jelly so the apparatus is airtight and watertight.
3. Dry the leaves, let the shoot settle, then introduce one air bubble into the capillary tube.
4. Record the distance the bubble moves in a fixed time; reset it with the reservoir and repeat.
5. Change one factor at a time, such as air movement with a fan at different distances.

A potometer measures **water uptake**, which is close to, but not the same as, the rate of transpiration: a little water is used in photosynthesis or to keep cells turgid.

**Worked example 1.** The capillary radius is 0.50 mm and the bubble moves 32 mm in 4.0 minutes.

```
volume = πr²l = π × 0.50² × 32 = 25.1 mm³
rate   = 25.1 / 4.0 = 6.3 mm³ min⁻¹
```

Dividing by the total leaf area lets shoots of different sizes be compared.

## How water moves through the plant (d)

**Into the root.** Root hair cells have a lower water potential than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis.

**Across the root cortex.**

- **Apoplast pathway**: through cell walls and the spaces between cells. Water is pulled along by cohesion, without crossing a membrane.
- **Symplast pathway**: through the cytoplasm of cells, passing from cell to cell through plasmodesmata, down a water potential gradient.

At the **endodermis**, a waterproof band in the cell walls (the Casparian strip) blocks the apoplast pathway, so water must enter the cytoplasm. Endodermal cells actively move mineral ions into the xylem, which lowers the water potential there and draws water in.

**Up the xylem.** Water evaporating from leaves creates a **transpiration pull**. Water molecules are held together by hydrogen bonds (**cohesion**) and to the lignified walls of the xylem (**adhesion**), so a continuous column of water is pulled up under tension: the **transpiration stream**.

**Out of the leaf.** Water moves from xylem into mesophyll cells, evaporates from their cell walls into the air spaces, and diffuses out through stomata down a water vapour concentration gradient.

## Adaptations to water availability (e)

| Plant | Habitat | Adaptations |
|---|---|---|
| Marram grass (xerophyte) | Sand dunes | Leaves roll up with stomata on the inner surface; stomata sunk in pits; hairs trap moist air; thick waxy cuticle on the outer surface |
| Cacti (xerophyte) | Deserts | Leaves reduced to spines, cutting the surface area; thick fleshy stems store water and photosynthesise; thick waxy cuticle; extensive roots |
| Water lily (hydrophyte) | Fresh water | Stomata on the upper surface of floating leaves; large air spaces keep leaves afloat and supply submerged parts with oxygen; thin or absent cuticle; little lignified support tissue |

Each xerophyte feature either traps a layer of humid air (reducing the gradient) or reduces the surface from which water evaporates.

## Translocation (f)

**Translocation** is the movement of assimilates, especially sucrose, in the phloem from **sources** (such as photosynthesising leaves, or storage organs when they are used) to **sinks** (such as roots, meristems, fruits). It needs energy.

1. At the source, companion cells use ATP to pump hydrogen ions out by active transport.
2. Hydrogen ions diffuse back in through co-transporter proteins, carrying sucrose with them.
3. Sucrose moves into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata. This is **active loading**.
4. The water potential in the sieve tube falls, so water enters from the xylem by osmosis, raising the hydrostatic pressure.
5. At the sink, sucrose is removed and used in respiration or converted to starch, so water potential rises and water leaves; hydrostatic pressure there is lower.
6. The solution flows by **mass flow** from high to low pressure, from source to sink.

## Common mistakes

- Saying transpiration is "water loss by evaporation" without mentioning water vapour diffusing through stomata.
- Saying xylem vessels are living cells, or that phloem carries only downwards.
- Describing the apoplast pathway as "through the cytoplasm".
- Explaining cohesion and adhesion the wrong way round.
- Claiming a potometer measures transpiration exactly.
- Describing translocation as passive diffusion of sucrose.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a factor's effect on transpiration: link it to stomata opening or to the water vapour gradient.
- **Describe** a potometer method: include cutting under water, sealing, and controlling other variables.
- **Calculate** a rate: show the volume calculation (πr²l) before dividing by time.
- **Explain** an adaptation: name the feature, then its effect on water loss or gain.
