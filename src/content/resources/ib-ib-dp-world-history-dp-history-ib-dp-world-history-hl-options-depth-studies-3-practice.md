Section A checks precise knowledge of late Qing China, Japan between 1912 and 1941, and China under Deng. Section B builds explanation and then full essays; allow about 50 minutes each for questions 11 and 12.

---

## Section A

**1.** Identify **three** terms of the Treaty of Nanjing (1842). **[3]**

**2.** State **two** reasons why the Self-Strengthening movement failed to protect China. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** reforms of the Qing New Policies after 1901. **[3]**

**4.** Outline **two** features of Taishō democracy. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** economic reforms introduced in China after 1978. **[2]**

**6.** Outline **two** reasons for the Tiananmen Square protests of 1989. **[2]**

---

## Section B

**7.** **(a)** Explain why the Hundred Days' Reform of 1898 failed. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **two** consequences of the Boxer Uprising for the Qing dynasty. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Explain why the Wuchang uprising of October 1911 led to the end of the dynasty. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain the role of Sun Yat-sen in the 1911 Revolution. **[3]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain how the Great Depression weakened party government in Japan. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain the significance of the Mukden Incident of 1931. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why Japan decided to go to war with the USA in 1941. **[4]**

**11.** "The rise of militarism in Japan between 1912 and 1937 was caused mainly by economic problems." To what extent do you agree with this statement? **[15]**

**12.** Evaluate the success of Deng Xiaoping's reforms in China between 1978 and 1997. **[15]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: Hong Kong ceded to Britain; five treaty ports opened to British trade and residence (Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, Shanghai); an indemnity paid to Britain; a "fair and regular" tariff on trade; the abolition of the Cohong monopoly of merchants at Guangzhou. **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: it borrowed Western technology without changing China's political institutions or education; it depended on regional leaders such as Li Hongzhang rather than a national programme; funds were diverted and corruption was common; conservative opposition at court limited it; defeat by Japan in 1894-95 showed its failure. **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: abolition of the civil service examinations (1905); a modern New Army; new schools and study abroad; plans for a constitution and provincial assemblies (1909); reform of law and administration; campaigns against opium. **[3]**

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: cabinets led by the majority party in the Diet, beginning with Hara (1918); universal male suffrage (1925); growth of labour and women's movements; a lively press and mass culture; co-operation with the Western powers in the Washington system. **[2]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: the household responsibility system in agriculture; Special Economic Zones such as Shenzhen (1980) open to foreign investment; growth of township and village enterprises; gradual price reform and private enterprise; opening to foreign trade and technology. **[2]**

**6.** Any two, 1 mark each: inflation and corruption linked to economic reform; demands by students and intellectuals for political freedom and dialogue; the death of the reformer Hu Yaobang in April 1989, which became a focus for mourning and protest; workers' grievances in Beijing; the international attention of Gorbachev's visit in May 1989. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: the reforms were issued too quickly, in over a hundred days of edicts, alarming officials (1); they threatened the interests of conservative officials, the examination system and the Manchu elite (1); the Guangxu Emperor had little real power compared with the Empress Dowager Cixi (1); Cixi's coup (September 1898) imprisoned the emperor and executed reformers, while Kang Youwei fled (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed consequence: the Boxer Protocol (1901) imposed a vast indemnity and allowed foreign troops in Beijing, deepening financial crisis and humiliation; the court's support for the Boxers and flight from Beijing destroyed its prestige; Cixi was forced to adopt the New Policies, which ultimately weakened the dynasty; Russia's occupation of Manchuria led towards the Russo-Japanese War. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: New Army units in Wuchang mutinied and took the city (1); provinces across central and southern China declared independence from Beijing within weeks (1); provincial assemblies and gentry elites, angered by railway nationalization, supported the revolution (1); the court recalled Yuan Shikai, who negotiated the abdication of Puyi in return for the presidency (February 1912) (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 1 mark each: he founded the Tongmenghui (1905) and provided the ideology of the Three Principles of the People; he organized earlier failed uprisings and raised funds abroad; he was in the USA when the Wuchang uprising began, and became provisional president only briefly before giving way to Yuan Shikai. **[3]**

**9.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks: the collapse of US demand for silk devastated rural incomes (1); unemployment and poverty discredited the parties, which were seen as linked to big business (*zaibatsu*) and corruption (1); nationalists and the army argued that expansion in Asia would solve economic problems (1); acts of political violence, such as the shooting of Hamaguchi (1930), intimidated civilian politicians (1). **[4]**
**(b)** 2 marks for each developed point: officers of the Kwantung Army acted without government approval, showing that the army could make policy; the conquest of Manchuria and creation of Manchukuo (1932) were popular at home, strengthening the army's position; Japan left the League of Nations (1933) after the Lytton Report, isolating it diplomatically; it began a sequence of aggression leading to war with China in 1937. **[4]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks: Japan depended on imported oil, and the US freezing of assets and oil embargo (1941) threatened to halt its war in China (1); the USA demanded Japanese withdrawal from China, which the army refused (1); Japan aimed to seize the resources of South-East Asia, including Dutch East Indies oil, which meant war with Britain and the USA (1); leaders gambled that a surprise strike at Pearl Harbor would cripple the US Pacific Fleet and force a negotiated peace (1). **[4]**

**11.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. For: the Depression, the collapse of silk prices and rural distress made expansion attractive and discredited the parties; dependence on imported resources made Manchuria seem vital. Against: the Meiji Constitution's structure allowed the army and navy to act independently and topple cabinets; nationalist ideology and emperor worship; assassinations and coups (1930, 1932, 1936); resentment of Western racial and naval policies; the success of the Manchurian invasion; weak civilian leadership. A strong answer weighs economic problems against these factors, shows how they interacted, and stays within 1912-37. **[15]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. Success: rapid economic growth, rising agricultural output after the household responsibility system, foreign investment through Special Economic Zones, hundreds of millions lifted out of extreme poverty over the following decades, and the return of Hong Kong (1997). Limits: inflation and corruption in the late 1980s; growing inequality between coast and interior; the one-child policy's social costs; the absence of political reform, shown by the Democracy Wall's suppression and the crackdown of June 1989. A strong answer sets criteria (economic growth, living standards, stability, political freedom), judges each, and reaches an overall verdict such as major economic success achieved at the cost of political liberty. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Oceania examined?**
Yes, where your school's sections include Australia, New Zealand or the Pacific. Check your teacher's list.

**Should I give casualty figures for Nanjing or the Great Leap Forward famine?**
Only if you are certain of a figure and its source. Describing the scale accurately is better than an uncertain number.
