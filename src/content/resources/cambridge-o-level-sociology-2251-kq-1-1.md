Key question 1.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *How do sociologists approach the study
of society?* It sits at the start of the compulsory Research methods question on Paper 1, and its
vocabulary is used in every other part of that question: when you criticise a source in part (a),
evaluate a methodological view in part (d) or justify a research plan in part (e), you are using the
ideas on this page. This guide goes beyond a simple positivism-versus-interpretivism table and
explains *why* each approach holds the views it does, so that you can argue with the ideas rather
than list them.

## Positivism: why treat society like nature?

Positivists start from a claim about what society *is*. For them society is a real structure that
exists outside any one person and pushes behaviour into regular shapes, in the way gravity shapes the
movement of objects. Émile Durkheim called these external forces **social facts**: laws, religious
rules, the economy, the shared values of a community. Because social facts are external, they can be
observed and measured like any natural object, and so the **scientific method** can be used:

1. Observe a pattern and form a **hypothesis** that predicts a relationship between two variables.
2. Collect **quantitative data** from a large sample, using standardised methods.
3. Identify **patterns and trends**, such as differences between groups or changes over time.
4. Test whether the variables are linked, and whether one causes the other.
5. Accept, reject or refine the hypothesis, and publish the method so others can repeat it.

Durkheim's *Suicide* (1897) is the classic illustration. Suicide looks like the most private act a
person can commit, yet Durkheim used official statistics from several European countries to show that
suicide rates were stable within societies and differed between them. Protestant areas, for example,
had higher rates than Catholic ones. He explained the difference by the level of **social
integration**: people bound tightly into a community were protected. The point for your exam is the
logic: a social fact (integration) is used to explain a pattern in quantitative data (the rate).

### Correlation is not causation

A **correlation** is a statistical link: two things rise or fall together. **Causation** means one
thing actually produces the other. Positivists look for correlations as evidence of causes, but a
correlation alone never proves causation, for three reasons:

- **A third variable** may cause both. Areas with more police officers may record more crime, but both
  could be caused by population size.
- **The direction may be reversed.** Children who read more may do better at school, or children who
  do well may choose to read more.
- **Coincidence.** With enough data, some variables will move together by chance.

Positivists answer this by controlling for other variables, for example comparing only people of the
same age and class, and by repeating the research. An exam answer that names one of these three
problems will score far more than one that says only "correlation does not prove causation".

### Objectivity and reliability

**Objectivity** means the researcher's own values do not affect the research. Positivists try to
achieve it by keeping a distance from participants and using standardised methods in which every
respondent receives the same questions in the same order. **Reliability** means that another
researcher using the same method would obtain the same results. The two ideas support each other: if a
study can be repeated with the same findings, personal bias is less likely to explain them.

## Interpretivism: why meanings come first

Interpretivists reject the starting claim. People are not objects that react to forces; they are
conscious actors who interpret situations and choose how to act. Society is built *from below*, through
countless small interactions, which is why interpretivists take a **micro view**. To explain behaviour
you must understand the **meanings and motivations** behind it, and these cannot be read off a
statistic.

Max Weber's term **verstehen** describes the goal: understanding an action from the point of view of
the person doing it. This requires **in-depth**, **qualitative** methods such as unstructured
interviews and participant observation, in which people can describe their lives in their own words.
Interpretivists accept that this involves **subjectivity** (the researcher's own interpretation) and
that small studies cannot easily be repeated, but they argue that the result is more **valid**: it
gives a true picture of social life as people experience it.

The interpretivist critique of Durkheim shows the difference. J. Maxwell Atkinson's *Discovering
Suicide* (1978) argued that a death only becomes a "suicide" in the statistics after a coroner decides
it was one, using clues such as a note, the method of death and the person's recent history. The
statistics are therefore a record of coroners' interpretations, not a neutral measure of a social
fact. To understand suicide statistics, you have to study how people construct them.

## Combining approaches

Most sociologists today use whatever fits the aim, and the syllabus names two ways of combining
methods and evidence.

**Triangulation** means approaching one research question with two or more methods or sources and
comparing the results, so that where one method is weak another can confirm or challenge it. Paul Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977) studied twelve
working-class boys in an English school by observing them in lessons, interviewing them and holding
group discussions, which let him check what the boys said against what they did.

**Longitudinal studies** collect data from the same people at intervals over a long period. The
British Millennium Cohort Study has followed children born in the United Kingdom in 2000 and 2001. Such
studies reveal change and the order in which events happen, which helps with questions of cause. Their
problems are cost, the long wait for findings, and **attrition**: participants who drop out may differ
from those who stay, so the sample becomes less representative over time.

## Judging research choices: the 1.1.4 toolkit

These seven concepts are your evaluation vocabulary. Each one asks a different question, so name the
concept and then say exactly how it affects the research in front of you.

- **Bias:** have the researcher's views shaped the questions, the sample or the interpretation?
- **Interviewer effect:** does something about the interviewer, such as age, gender, ethnicity or
  status, change the answers given?
- **Hawthorne / observer effect:** does awareness of being watched change what people do? The term
  refers to research led by Elton Mayo at the Western Electric Company's Hawthorne works near Chicago
  between the 1920s and early 1930s. Productivity appeared to improve almost regardless of which
  working conditions the researchers altered, which was put down to the attention the workers were
  getting.
- **Validity:** does the data give a true picture of what it claims to measure?
- **Reliability:** would the method give the same results if repeated?
- **Representativeness:** is the sample typical of the population being studied?
- **Generalisability:** can the findings be applied beyond the sample?

## Worked example: a short "explain" answer

*Explain the difference between correlation and causation.* (practice question)

> A correlation is a statistical relationship in which two variables change together, for example
> pupils who are absent more often tend to gain lower grades. Causation means one variable actually
> produces a change in the other. Finding a correlation does not prove causation, because a third
> factor might cause both: here, illness or caring responsibilities at home could explain both the
> absence and the lower grades. Positivists therefore try to control other variables before claiming
> that absence causes underachievement.

**Why it works:** both terms are defined, a sociological example is used, and the answer explains why
the distinction matters, linking it to positivist method.

## Annotated plan: an evaluation answer

*Evaluate the view that sociologists can be completely objective in their research.* (practice
question)

- **Introduce:** define objectivity and note that the view is a positivist one.
- **For:** standardised questionnaires and structured interviews reduce the researcher's influence;
  quantitative data can be checked by others; publishing methods allows replication, so bias can be
  detected.
- **Against:** researchers choose topics and questions that reflect their own values; closed questions
  impose the researcher's categories; interpretivists argue that understanding meanings requires
  involvement, so some subjectivity is unavoidable; interviewer effects occur even in structured
  interviews; even "objective" official statistics depend on people's decisions, as Atkinson showed.
- **Judge:** complete objectivity is unlikely, but researchers can reduce bias by triangulating and by
  being open about their methods and values.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "positivists use quantitative data" without explaining that this follows from their view of
  society as measurable social facts.
- Treating subjectivity as simply bad: interpretivists see it as the price of validity.
- Confusing reliability (repeatable) with validity (true).
- Saying a longitudinal study is "a long study"; it studies the *same people* repeatedly.
- Naming an evaluation concept without applying it to the research in the question.

## Exam technique for 2251 command words

- **Define:** one precise sentence, then an example if time allows.
- **Explain:** make the link clear; say *why* or *how*, as in the correlation answer above.
- **Describe:** give the main features, for example three features of the interpretivist approach.
- **Evaluate:** weigh strengths against limitations and reach a judgement.
- **Assess:** make an informed judgement on how far a view holds.
