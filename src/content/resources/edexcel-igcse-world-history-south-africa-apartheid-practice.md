## Question 1 — building apartheid, 1948–59

**Extract A** (invented for this exercise).

> "Before 1948 segregation had been a habit; after 1948 it became a machine. Every law fed the next,
> until there was no part of life — birth, school, marriage, home — that the state did not sort by
> colour."

**1(a)** What impression does the author give about the apartheid laws? You must use Extract A to
explain your answer. **[6]**

**1(b)** Explain **two** effects of the Bantu Education Act (1953). **[8]**

**1(c)** "Fear of black urbanisation was the main reason for the National Party's victory in 1948."
How far do you agree? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- Afrikaner nationalism
- the electoral system

You **must** also use information of your own.

## Question 2 — resistance, 1955–78

**Extract B** (invented for this exercise).

## Question 3 — Botha and de Klerk, 1978–94

**3(b)** Explain **two** effects of P.W. Botha's reforms on South Africa. **[8]**

## Answers and mark guidance

### 1(a) [6]

The author presents apartheid as a **systematic, all-embracing system**. Contrasting "habit" with
"machine" suggests a shift from informal to deliberate, organised segregation; "every law fed the
next" implies the laws were connected; the list "birth, school, marriage, home" shows how completely
it reached into private life (linking to the Population Registration, Bantu Education, Mixed
Marriages and Group Areas Acts).

### 1(b) [8]

- **Inferior education:** black schools were overcrowded and under-funded, with a curriculum aimed at
  manual work, limiting black opportunities for a generation.
- **Resistance:** many mission schools closed rather than accept state control, and anger at Bantu
  Education fed later student protest, culminating in Soweto (1976).

### 1(c) [16]

**Agree:** white fears of black workers moving to the cities in the war years; the National Party's
promise of strict segregation; the language of *swart gevaar* ("black danger").

**Other factors:** Afrikaner nationalism and resentment of British influence; the over-weighting of
rural constituencies, which let the Nationalists win more seats with fewer votes; the United Party's
weaknesses under Smuts; economic worries among Afrikaner workers and farmers.

**Judgement:** racial fear provided the appeal, but Afrikaner nationalism and the electoral system
explain how it turned into victory.

### 2(a) [6]

The author suggests Sharpeville was a **turning point that pushed the ANC from peaceful protest to
armed struggle**. "Ended an argument" implies a debate settled by events; "met with bullets" stresses
the violence; contrasting "asking" with "plan" hints at the move to sabotage through Umkhonto we
Sizwe (1961).

### 2(b) [8]

- **Spread of resistance:** protest spread to other townships, many young people fled to join the ANC
  in exile, and Black Consciousness gained support.
- **International condemnation:** pictures of the shootings increased pressure on South Africa,
  contributing to the mandatory UN arms embargo of 1977.

### 2(c) [16]

**Agree:** the Defiance Campaign (1952) and the Freedom Charter (1955) set out a non-racial vision;
Mandela's leadership; Umkhonto we Sizwe kept resistance alive after 1961; the Rivonia Trial made
Mandela a world symbol.

**Other opponents:** the PAC and Sharpeville; the women's march (1956); Biko and Black Consciousness,
which inspired the Soweto students; international opposition (Olympic exclusion, arms embargo).

**Limits of the ANC:** banned in 1960, leaders imprisoned by 1964; little activity inside South Africa
for much of the 1960s.

**Judgement:** the ANC set the goals, but in 1960–76 other groups led resistance inside the country.

### 3(b) [8]

- **Split in white politics:** hardliners left to form the Conservative Party (1982), and the AWB
  threatened violence, weakening the National Party.
- **New black resistance:** the Tricameral Parliament excluded black South Africans, prompting the UDF
  (1983) and the township unrest of 1984–86, which led to the State of Emergency.

### 3(c) [16]

**Agree:** sanctions (the US Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act, 1986), banks refusing loans after the
"Rubicon" speech (1985), sports and cultural boycotts and economic decline.

**Other factors:** township unrest, the UDF and the churches made the country ungovernable; Mandela's
leadership and willingness to negotiate; de Klerk's decisions in 1990; the end of the Cold War.

**Judgement:** international pressure raised the cost of apartheid, but internal resistance and the
choices of de Klerk and Mandela turned that pressure into a negotiated end.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing Sharpeville (1960) and Soweto (1976).
- Listing apartheid laws without explaining their effects.
- Ignoring the bullet points or failing to add own knowledge.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is each Paper 1 depth study question structured?**
Each 4HI1 Paper 1 question has three parts: (a) what impression an extract gives (6 marks), (b) explain two effects (8 marks) and (c) a 'how far do you agree' essay (16 marks), with bullet prompts and your own knowledge.

**Which two events are most often confused?**
Sharpeville (21 March 1960) and the Soweto uprising (1976).

**What do the essays ask you to judge?**
For example whether international pressure was the main reason apartheid ended, weighed against internal resistance, the economy and the decisions of de Klerk.
