## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Charge (Q) | A property of matter; measured in coulombs (C) |
| Current (I) | The rate of flow of charge; measured in amperes (A) |
| Time (t) | Measured in seconds (s) in calculations |
| Conventional current | Drawn from the positive terminal round to the negative terminal |
| Electron flow | In a metal, electrons move from negative to positive, opposite to conventional current |
| d.c. | Direct current: flows in one direction only |

## P3.1e: what makes charge flow

Both conditions are needed:

1. A source of potential difference (cell, battery or power supply).
2. A closed circuit: a complete conducting loop.

Missing either one means no current. An open switch, a break in a wire or an insulator in the loop all give no current anywhere in that loop.

**Must-know distinction:** electrons move opposite to the arrows on a circuit diagram. The arrows show conventional current.

## P3.1f: same current round a single loop

- One path, no branches: every point carries an equal current.
- An ammeter before a component and an ammeter after it read the same.
- Components transfer energy; they do not use up charge.

Method in steps for a question about ammeter readings in a loop:

1. Check there are no branches.
2. State that the current is equal everywhere in the loop.
3. Give the same value for each ammeter.

## P3.1g: Q = I t

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Charge | Q | coulomb, C |
| Current | I | ampere, A |
| Time | t | second, s |

Three forms:

- Q = I × t
- I = Q / t
- t = Q / I

**Method in steps:**

1. Write down what you know, with units.
2. Convert minutes to seconds (multiply by 60) and mA to A (divide by 1000).
3. Choose the form that has your unknown on its own.
4. Substitute, calculate, and add the unit.

### Worked reminders

**Find charge.** Current 2.5 A for 30 s.

```
Q = I t = 2.5 × 30 = 75 C
```

**Find current.** 72 C in 4.0 minutes.

```
t = 4.0 × 60 = 240 s
I = Q / t = 72 / 240 = 0.30 A
```

**Find time.** 33 C at 0.55 A.

```
t = Q / I = 33 / 0.55 = 60 s
```

**Milliamperes.** 280 mA for 50 s.

```
I = 0.280 A
Q = 0.280 × 50 = 14 C
```

## P3.2a: series against parallel

| Feature | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Layout | Components one after another in one loop | Components on separate branches between the same two points |
| Current | Same everywhere | Splits at a junction; branch currents add up to the supply current |
| Potential difference | Shared between the components | Same across every branch |
| Energy | Source energy shared between the components in the loop | Each branch takes energy from the source directly |
| If one component breaks | The whole circuit stops | Other branches keep working |

### Meters

| Meter | Measures | Connected |
|---|---|---|
| Ammeter | Current | In series, in the loop |
| Voltmeter | Potential difference | In parallel, across the component |

Reminder of why: an ammeter has to have the current pass through it. A voltmeter compares the two sides of a component.

## P3.2b: symbols and conventions

| Component | Symbol in words |
|---|---|
| Cell | Long thin line (positive) and short thick line (negative) |
| Power supply | Marked with positive and negative terminals |
| Switch | A gap with a lever; closed when the lever touches both ends |
| Filament lamp | Circle with a cross |
| Fixed resistor | Rectangle |
| Variable resistor | Rectangle crossed by a sloping arrow |
| Diode | Triangle pointing at a bar; conventional current passes the way the triangle points |
| LDR | Rectangle in a circle, with arrows pointing in |
| NTC thermistor | Rectangle with a diagonal line ending in a hook |
| Ammeter | Circle with A |
| Voltmeter | Circle with V |

Draw with a ruler, use right angles at corners and leave no gaps in the loop (unless a switch is meant to be open).

## Fast links between the ideas

- Current needs a closed loop, so a break anywhere in a single loop stops the current everywhere in it.
- The same current in a single loop (P3.1f) is why Q = I t gives the same charge through every component in that loop in the same time.
- In a parallel circuit, apply Q = I t to each branch separately. Branch charges add up to the charge from the supply in the same time.
- Series: one loop, one current, potential difference shared. Parallel: several loops, current shared, potential difference the same across each branch.

### Worked reminder: parallel branches

A supply current of 1.00 A splits into branch currents of 0.60 A and 0.40 A. In 20 s the branches carry 0.60 × 20 = 12 C and 0.40 × 20 = 8 C. The supply provides 1.00 × 20 = 20 C, and 12 + 8 = 20 C. The numbers agree.

### Worked reminder: two-step question

A lamp carries 0.50 A. How many minutes does it take for 450 C to pass?

```
t = Q / I = 450 / 0.50 = 900 s
900 / 60 = 15 minutes
```

## Writing good answers

- For "explain" questions about a series loop, use the pattern: one path, so the same current everywhere, so the readings are equal.
- For "describe how to connect" questions, give the position and the reason: ammeter in series so the same current passes through it; voltmeter in parallel so it compares the two sides of the component.
- For symbols, label the plus and minus of a cell and show the arrow of a diode in the direction of conventional current.
- Show each step of a calculation: equation, substitution, answer with unit.

## Quick self-test

1. State the two conditions needed for charge to flow round a circuit.
2. In which direction do electrons move in a wire compared with conventional current?
3. Write the equation linking charge, current and time, with units.
4. Calculate the charge that flows when a current of 1.8 A passes for 25 s.
5. A current of 0.15 A flows for 3.0 minutes. Calculate the charge.
6. 66 C of charge flows in 2.0 minutes. Calculate the current.
7. A current of 20 mA flows. How long does it take for 72 C to pass? Give the answer in minutes.
8. In a series circuit, an ammeter before a lamp reads 0.40 A. What does an ammeter after the lamp read?
9. How should an ammeter be connected, and how should a voltmeter be connected?
10. In a parallel circuit the supply current is 1.20 A. One branch carries 0.45 A. What current is in the other branch (two branches only)?
11. Describe what happens to the other lamps when one lamp is removed from a series circuit and from a parallel circuit.
12. Describe the symbol for a diode and say which way conventional current flows through it.

### Answers

1. A source of potential difference and a closed circuit.
2. They move in the opposite direction, from negative to positive.
3. Q = I t, with charge Q in coulombs (C), current I in amperes (A) and time t in seconds (s).
4. Q = 1.8 × 25 = 45 C.
5. t = 3.0 × 60 = 180 s; Q = 0.15 × 180 = 27 C.
6. t = 120 s; I = 66 / 120 = 0.55 A.
7. I = 0.020 A; t = 72 / 0.020 = 3600 s = 60 minutes.
8. 0.40 A: the current is the same everywhere in a single loop.
9. Ammeter in series with the component; voltmeter in parallel with the component.
10. 1.20 - 0.45 = 0.75 A.
11. Series: the loop is broken so the other lamps go out. Parallel: the other lamps stay lit because their branches are unaffected.
12. A triangle pointing at a bar; conventional current flows in the direction the triangle points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving only one condition for charge to flow when two are needed.
- Saying that current is "used up" or "lost" in a lamp.
- Forgetting to change minutes to seconds before using Q = I t.
- Treating 250 mA as 250 A.
- Leaving off the unit, or writing the wrong one (for example A for charge).
- Drawing arrows on a diagram from the negative terminal.
- Wiring a voltmeter into the loop, or an ammeter beside a component on its own branch.
- Describing parallel branches as sharing the potential difference, when each branch has the full supply value.
- Drawing a circuit with a gap that was not meant to be there, so no current could flow.

An examiner reading an explanation is looking for the physics idea written in a full sentence. A bare "it is a series circuit" gains little; "there is only one path, so the current is the same at each point" is the idea the question is after.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is electric current?**
Electric current is the rate of flow of charge, measured in amperes. One ampere means one coulomb of charge passing a point each second; in a metal wire, the moving charges are electrons.

**What is conventional current?**
Conventional current is drawn flowing from the positive terminal of the source, round the circuit, to the negative terminal. Electrons in a metal actually move the other way, from negative to positive.

**How do you draw a cell symbol the right way round?**
A cell is two parallel lines, one long and thin and one short and thick. The long line is the positive terminal.

**Which part of the OCR GCSE Physics A specification covers current and circuits?**
It is statements P3.1e to P3.1g and P3.2a to P3.2b in Topic P3 (Electricity) of OCR GCSE (9-1) Physics A (Gateway Science) J249. None is in bold, so all are assessed on both tiers.
