**3.2.3 Mental health** is the third topic of Paper 2 (Social Context and Behaviour) in OxfordAQA
International GCSE Psychology (9218), Version 1.1. It covers why the incidence of significant mental
health problems changes over time, the effects of those problems on individuals and society, and two
named disorders — **obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)** and **post-traumatic stress disorder
(PTSD)** — each with its ICD diagnosis, explanations, a therapy and a named study of that therapy's
effectiveness.

  Kearns's study of the effectiveness of CBT for OCD
- PTSD: use of the ICD in diagnosis; characteristics; biological factors and dual representation
  theory; prolonged exposure therapy; Eftekhari's study of the effectiveness of prolonged exposure
  therapy

## Why does the incidence of mental health problems change over time?

Good mental health is more than the absence of illness. The specification gives **characteristics of
mental health** such as **positive engagement with society** and **effective coping with
challenges**. Recorded rates of significant mental health problems rise and fall for several reasons:

- **Cultural variations in beliefs.** Whether a pattern of behaviour is seen as a mental health
  problem, a spiritual matter or a normal reaction varies between cultures and over time, which
  changes what gets reported and diagnosed.
- **Increased challenges of modern living.** Features of modern life such as **isolation** — living
  alone, moving away from family, or relying on online rather than face-to-face contact — may raise
  the real rate of problems.
- **Increased recognition and less stigma.** As people understand mental health problems better and
  feel less ashamed, more people seek help and more cases are diagnosed. Some of the rise in recorded
  incidence may therefore reflect better recognition rather than more illness.

## What effects do mental health problems have?

| On individuals | On society |
|---|---|
| Damage to relationships with family and friends | Need for more social care and health services |
| Difficulty coping with day-to-day life, such as work or study | Increased crime rates in some cases |
| Negative impact on physical wellbeing, such as sleep, diet and illness | Economic costs: lost working days, lower productivity, cost of treatment |

## How is OCD diagnosed and explained?

**Diagnosis with the ICD.** The International Classification of Diseases, published by the World
Health Organization, lists the features a clinician looks for. **Obsessions** are persistent,
unwanted and intrusive thoughts, images or urges (for example about contamination or causing harm)
that cause anxiety. **Compulsions** are repetitive behaviours or mental acts (such as washing,
checking or counting) carried out to reduce that anxiety or prevent a feared event. For a diagnosis,
the obsessions and/or compulsions are recognised as the person's own, are time-consuming or cause
significant distress, and interfere with daily functioning.

**Biological explanations.** OCD tends to run in families, and concordance is higher for identical
than non-identical twins, suggesting a **genetic** influence. **Neurochemical** explanations link OCD
to low levels of **serotonin**, supported by the fact that drugs which increase serotonin reduce
symptoms for many patients. **Neural** explanations point to unusual activity in brain circuits
involving the frontal lobes, which may make it hard to stop worrying thoughts.

**Cognitive explanations.** People with OCD may have **faulty thinking**: they overestimate the
likelihood of danger, feel excessive **responsibility** for preventing harm, and treat having a
thought as if it were as bad as acting on it. Trying to suppress intrusive thoughts makes them more
frequent, and the compulsion is kept going because it briefly relieves the anxiety.

**Therapy: cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT).** CBT helps the person identify and challenge their
irrational beliefs about threat and responsibility, and usually includes **exposure and response
prevention**: the person is gradually exposed to the feared situation (such as touching a "dirty"
object) and supported not to carry out the compulsion, so they learn the anxiety falls without it.
**Kearns's study** is the specification's named investigation of how effective CBT is for OCD; learn
its aim, procedure, findings and conclusion from your course materials and be ready to evaluate it
as evidence for the therapy.

## How is PTSD diagnosed and explained?

**Diagnosis with the ICD.** PTSD can develop after exposure to an extremely threatening or horrific
event, such as war, assault or a serious accident. Characteristics include **re-experiencing** the
event (intrusive memories, flashbacks or nightmares), **avoidance** of thoughts, places or people
that are reminders of it, and a persistent sense of **current threat** (hypervigilance and an
exaggerated startle response). The symptoms last for at least several weeks and cause significant
impairment.

**Biological factors.** Some people may have a **genetic vulnerability**. Brain research links PTSD
to an over-active **amygdala** (fear responses) and to the **hippocampus**, which places memories in
their time and context. Stress hormones released during trauma may affect how the memory is stored.

**Dual representation theory** (Brewin and colleagues) proposes that traumatic events are stored in
two memory systems. A **verbal, contextual** memory can be deliberately recalled and put into words.
A **sensory, situational** memory holds the raw sights, sounds and feelings, and is triggered
automatically by reminders. In PTSD the sensory memory is very strong and poorly linked to the
contextual one, so it intrudes as a flashback that feels as though it is happening now.

**Therapy: prolonged exposure therapy.** Over a series of sessions the therapist explains how PTSD is
maintained, then uses **imaginal exposure** (repeatedly recounting the trauma in detail) and
**in-vivo exposure** (gradually approaching safe situations the person has been avoiding). Avoidance
stops, the memory is processed, and anxiety reduces. **Eftekhari's study** (Eftekhari and colleagues,
2013) examined prolonged exposure delivered by therapists trained in a national programme for United
States military veterans, and found significant reductions in PTSD and depression symptoms after
treatment — evidence that the therapy works outside tightly controlled trials.

## Common mistakes

- Defining obsessions and compulsions the wrong way round: obsessions are thoughts, compulsions are
  actions.
- Saying rising incidence proves mental illness is increasing, without considering better recognition
  and reduced stigma.
- Describing a therapy without linking it to the explanation it targets (CBT challenges faulty
  thinking; prolonged exposure ends avoidance).
- Forgetting that each disorder has both a biological and a psychological explanation.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between an obsession and a compulsion?** An obsession is an unwanted,
intrusive thought, image or urge that causes anxiety. A compulsion is a repetitive behaviour or mental
act carried out to reduce that anxiety.

**What does dual representation theory say about flashbacks?** That trauma creates a strong sensory
memory that is poorly linked to a verbal, contextual memory. Reminders trigger the sensory memory
automatically, so it is re-experienced as if happening now.

**Why might recorded rates of mental health problems rise?** Partly because of real pressures such as
isolation, and partly because better recognition and less stigma mean more people seek a diagnosis.

**Which therapies does the specification name?** Cognitive behaviour therapy for OCD and prolonged
exposure therapy for PTSD.
