## Section A

**1.** Which case held that a local authority cannot sue for defamation? A: Lingens v Austria. B: Derbyshire County Council v Times Newspapers. C: Handyside v United Kingdom. D: Cream Holdings v Banerjee. **[1]**

**2.** Which of the following is expressly permitted by the final sentence of Article 10.1? A: licensing of broadcasting, television or cinema enterprises. B: censorship of newspapers. C: banning political parties. D: restricting expression to protect reputation. **[1]**

**3.** State the two elements of freedom of expression named in the AQA specification under Article 10.1. **[2]**

**4.** State the principle in Handyside v United Kingdom (1976) about the kinds of ideas Article 10 protects. **[2]**

**5.** State what section 12(3) of the Human Rights Act 1998 requires, and how Cream Holdings v Banerjee (2004) interpreted it. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain why political speech receives stronger protection under Article 10 than some other forms of expression, referring to one case. **[3]**

**7.** Explain the decision in Sunday Times v United Kingdom (1979) and what it shows about the right to receive information. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the extent to which Article 10 gives a right of access to information held by the state. **[5]**

**9.** Explain why the European Court found a breach of Article 10 in Jersild v Denmark (1994). **[3]**

**10.** *A broadcaster refuses, under a statutory rule, to show a paid television advertisement from a campaign group urging people to lobby for a change in the law on animal testing.* Suggest why the United Kingdom is unlikely to be found in breach of Article 10. **[4]**

**11.** *Leon, a reporter on a local newspaper, has a document leaked to him showing a hospital trust hid poor infection-control results. Before publication, the trust applies for an interim injunction to stop the story and for an order that Leon disclose who gave him the document.* Advise Leon on how Article 10 and section 12 of the Human Rights Act 1998 apply. **[7]**

**12.** Discuss whether the European Court's different levels of protection for different kinds of expression are justified. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B: Derbyshire County Council v Times Newspapers. **[1]**

**2.** A: licensing of broadcasting, television or cinema enterprises. **[1]**

**3.** The right to **receive** information and ideas [1] and the right to **communicate** (impart) information and ideas [1].

**4.** Article 10 protects not only ideas that are favourably received or inoffensive [1] but also those that **offend, shock or disturb** the state or any sector of the population [1].

**5.** A court must not grant relief restraining publication before trial [1] unless the applicant is **likely to establish that publication should not be allowed** [1]. In Cream Holdings the House of Lords held that "likely" normally means **more likely than not** [1].

**6.** Free debate on public affairs is essential to a **democratic society**, so restrictions on political speech need strong justification [1]. In **Lingens v Austria (1986)** [1] the Court held that the limits of acceptable criticism are **wider for a politician** than for a private individual, and the journalist's defamation conviction breached Article 10 [1].

**7.** An injunction for **contempt of court** stopped the Sunday Times publishing an article about the **thalidomide litigation** [1]. The European Court held the injunction was **not necessary in a democratic society** and breached Article 10 [1]. The Court stressed that the **public has a right to receive** information on matters of public concern [1], so a restraint on the publisher is also an interference with the audience's right [1].

**8.** The right to receive information mainly prevents the state from **blocking information others are willing to give** [1]. In **Leander v Sweden (1987)** there was no right of access to a secret police register [1]. In **Magyar Helsinki Bizottság v Hungary (2016)** the Grand Chamber accepted that a right of access **can arise in some circumstances** [1], especially where access is instrumental to the work of a **public watchdog** such as the press or an NGO [1]. In **Kennedy v Charity Commission (2014)** the Supreme Court held there is no general Article 10 right of access and that claims must go through **statute, such as the Freedom of Information Act 2000, and the common law** [1].

**9.** The journalist was convicted for broadcasting an interview in which young men made **racist remarks** [1]. The Court held that, viewed as a whole, the programme was a **serious report on racism** and did not aim to spread racist views [1]. Punishing a journalist for helping to disseminate others' statements would **seriously hamper the press** in its role on matters of public interest [1].

**10.** The facts resemble **Animal Defenders International v UK (2013)** [1], where the Grand Chamber upheld the UK ban on **paid political advertising** on television and radio [1]. The Court accepted the ban protected the democratic process from **distortion by wealthy groups** buying broadcast time [1], and the group could still campaign through **other media** such as print, online and public meetings, so the restriction was proportionate [1].

**11.** Leon's article is **communication of information** under Article 10.1, and the public has a right to **receive** it [1]. It concerns **public health and the conduct of a public body**, which attracts strong protection [1]. The trust is a public body, but the **court's own duty** under section 6 also applies [1]. Under **section 12** the court must have **particular regard to the importance of freedom of expression** [1], and under **section 12(3)** it cannot grant an interim injunction unless the trust is **more likely than not** to succeed at trial (Cream Holdings) [1]. On the source order, **Goodwin v UK (1996)** shows that compelling a journalist to reveal a source needs an **overriding requirement in the public interest** [1]. Conclusion: an injunction and source order are both unlikely to be granted, subject to the trust showing a strong case under Article 10.2 [1].

**12.** One mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- Political speech is central to **democracy**, so strong protection for it has a clear rationale (Lingens) [1].
- The **press watchdog** role justifies protection for reporting and sources (Goodwin; Jersild) [1].
- Wider margins for **artistic expression on morals or religion** respect differences between states (Müller; Otto-Preminger-Institut) [1].
- But the line between political and artistic expression is **unclear**; art often makes political points, so the categories can be arbitrary [1].
- **Handyside** promises protection for ideas that offend, yet the outcomes in Otto-Preminger and Müller show offence often wins, which critics see as inconsistent [1].
- **Animal Defenders** shows that even political speech can be restricted where the medium is broadcasting, which some see as undermining the hierarchy [1].
- A tiered approach gives **predictability** and lets the Court calibrate the margin of appreciation [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that tiers are broadly justified but need clearer definitions [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying Handyside found a breach of Article 10.
- Ignoring the right to receive information when the facts concern a publisher.
- Leaving out section 12 HRA 1998 in an injunction scenario.
- Treating Article 10 as a general right to demand information from public bodies.
- Concluding on breach without noting that justification is tested under Article 10.2.
