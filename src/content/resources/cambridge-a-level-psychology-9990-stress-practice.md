**This practice set covers topic 3.4, Stress, in Specialist Option 3 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All people and data are fictional.

Related: [Stress study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-stress/) · [Stress revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-stress-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline **two** characteristics of Type A personality. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why Holmes and Rahe included positive events such as marriage in their life events scale. **[2]**

**3.** Outline research into work as a source of stress. **[3]**

**4.** Describe the three phases of stress inoculation training. **[3]**

**5.** Stress can be managed in different ways.

**(a)** Describe the study by Bridge et al. (1988). **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate the study by Bridge et al. (1988), including a discussion of ethics. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** Tomás, a nurse, has been working long night shifts for a year. He feels exhausted and has caught three colds this winter.

**(a)** Using the General Adaptation Syndrome, explain his health problems. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest **one** biological measure of Tomás's stress and explain why it suits this situation. **[3]**

**7.** A researcher measures salivary cortisol in office workers in an open-plan and a private office.

**(a)** Explain why all samples should be taken at the same time of day. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify **one** other extraneous variable she should control, and explain how. **[2]**

**8.** A university counsellor wants to help students who get tension headaches before exams.

**(a)** Suggest how biofeedback could be used. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** practical weakness of biofeedback in this setting. **[2]**

**9.** A psychologist gives 50 adults the SRRS and records how often they visit their doctor in the next year (fictional data). She finds a positive correlation of +0.42 between LCU scores and doctor visits.

**(a)** Explain what this correlation shows. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why it does not show that life events cause illness. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** weakness of using the SRRS as a self-report measure. **[2]**

**10.** Wang et al. (2005) used fMRI during a mental arithmetic task. Explain **one** strength and **one** weakness of using fMRI to measure stress. **[4]**

**11.** Compare the sympathomedullary pathway and the HPA axis. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each for any two: competitiveness and drive to achieve (1); time urgency and impatience (1); hostility and anger (1).

**2.** Up to 2 marks. The scale measures the amount of change and readjustment an event demands (1); positive events such as marriage still require adjustment and so can be stressful (1).

**3.** Up to 3 marks. Chandola et al. (2008) used data from the Whitehall II study of British civil servants (1). Work stress was associated with coronary heart disease (1), with a stronger link in younger workers, partly through health behaviours and biological changes (1).

**4.** One mark per phase. Conceptualisation: client and therapist explore the stressors and how the client thinks about them (1). Skill acquisition and rehearsal: coping skills such as relaxation and positive self-talk are learned and practised (1). Application and follow-through: skills are used in increasingly stressful real situations, with follow-up (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Randomised controlled trial lasting six weeks (1) with 154 women receiving radiotherapy for early breast cancer (1). Groups: control, encouraged to talk about themselves; relaxation, focusing on muscle groups; relaxation plus imagery of a peaceful scene of their choice (1). Intervention groups practised with tapes for at least 15 minutes a day (1). At six weeks, total mood disturbance was lower in both intervention groups, with the combined group most relaxed, while the control group worsened (1). Women aged 55 and over benefited most; there was no difference on separate depression and anxiety scales (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named issue, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Ethics*: patients with cancer are vulnerable, so consent, confidentiality and the right to withdraw were essential; the control group received less help, though they did receive attention; the study risks raising hopes, so debriefing and continued care matter; the benefit to future patients justifies the research.
- *Design*: random allocation and a control group support cause and effect.
- *Validity*: mood measured by self-report questionnaires; possible demand characteristics because women knew which treatment they had.
- *Generalisation*: one group of patients (women with early breast cancer).
- *Application*: cheap, safe, home-based; useful in hospitals.
- *Attrition*: 15 dropped out, which could bias results.
- *Conclusion*: good evidence for imagery and relaxation as a supportive treatment, within a narrow sample.

**6.**

**(a)** Up to 3 marks. Prolonged stress from night shifts has taken Tomás past the resistance stage, in which cortisol stays raised (1), into the exhaustion stage, where the body's resources are depleted (1). Raised cortisol suppresses the immune system, so he is more likely to catch infections such as colds (1).

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. Salivary cortisol (1). It is non-invasive and can be collected during shifts (1), and it reflects long-term HPA activity, which fits a long-term stressor (1). Credit heart rate monitoring with an appropriate justification.

**7.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Cortisol levels vary naturally across the day, highest in the morning (1), so sampling at different times could produce differences not caused by the office type (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Caffeine or food intake before sampling (1): ask all participants not to eat or drink for a set time beforehand (1). Credit exercise, smoking or workload with a suitable control.

**8.**

**(a)** Up to 3 marks. Attach electrodes to measure forehead muscle tension and show it as a sound or display (1). The student practises relaxing so the signal falls, which is rewarding (1). With practice they learn to relax the muscles without the device before exams (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. It needs specialist equipment and trained staff (1), so it is expensive and only a few students could use it at once (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. A moderate positive correlation (1): people with higher LCU scores tended to visit the doctor more often (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Correlation does not show cause (1); a third variable, such as anxiety or poor health already present, could increase both reported life events and doctor visits (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. Participants may not remember all events in the past year accurately (1), so scores may be inaccurate and reduce validity (1). Credit that the same event, e.g. moving house, may be more stressful for some people than others.

**10.** Strength, up to 2: it gives objective data on brain activity (1) that does not depend on what the participant reports (1). Weakness, up to 2: the scanner is noisy and confining, so it may itself cause stress (1), and mental arithmetic in a scanner is not like real-life stressors, lowering ecological validity (1).

**11.** Up to 3 marks. Both are activated by the hypothalamus in response to a stressor (1). The sympathomedullary pathway is fast, releasing adrenaline from the adrenal medulla (1), whereas the HPA axis is slower, using ACTH from the pituitary to release cortisol from the adrenal cortex (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 4, naming the phases without saying what happens in each.
- In question 6(a), listing the GAS stages without linking exhaustion to Tomás's colds.
- In question 9(b), writing "correlation is not causation" without a third variable.
