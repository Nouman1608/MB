This guide covers section **3.4.3 Motivating employees** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 4, Human resources. The specification asks you to understand the **benefits of a motivated workforce**, such as **staff retention and high productivity**; the **financial methods** of motivation, including the main methods of payment (**salary, wage, commission and profit sharing**); and the **non-financial methods**, including **styles of management, training, greater responsibility and fringe benefits**. Specific motivation theories, such as Maslow's, will not be examined. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Human Resources study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-human-resources/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-motivating-employees-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-motivating-employees-practice/).

## What is motivation?

**Motivation** is the desire or willingness of a person to do a job well. A motivated employee puts effort into the work, takes pride in it and wants to stay with the business. A demotivated employee does the minimum, makes more mistakes, takes more time off and may leave.

### Benefits of a motivated workforce

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| High productivity | Motivated workers produce more per hour, so unit costs fall |
| Staff retention | Fewer leave, so the business saves the cost of recruiting and training replacements |
| Better quality and customer service | Staff who care take more trouble and treat customers well |
| Lower absence | Staff are less likely to take unnecessary time off |
| Better reputation as an employer | Easier to recruit good staff |
| Better teamwork | A positive atmosphere helps cooperation and new ideas |

**Worked example 1: the value of higher productivity.** A workshop's staff each make 50 units a day. After a motivation scheme, they make 56.

```
Increase = (56 - 50) / 50 x 100 = 12%
```

With 25 workers, daily output rises from 25 x 50 = 1,250 to 25 x 56 = 1,400 units, which is 150 extra units a day, produced by the same workforce with the same wage bill.

## Financial methods of motivation

Financial methods use money to encourage employees. The specification names four methods of payment.

### Wages

A **wage** is a payment made for hours worked, usually calculated at an hourly rate and paid weekly. Extra hours may be paid at a higher overtime rate.

```
Weekly wage = 38 hours x $12.50 = $475
```

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Easy to understand and fair for the time worked | Pay rises with hours, not with effort or quality |
| Employees can increase income by working overtime | Costs rise with extra hours |
| Cheap to run | Does not encourage staff to work faster |

### Salary

A **salary** is a fixed annual amount, usually paid monthly, however many hours are worked.

```
Annual salary = $2,800 a month x 12 = $33,600
```

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Staff know what they will earn, which gives security | Does not reward extra effort or results |
| Easy to budget for | Some staff may do the minimum |
| Suits jobs where hours vary or results are hard to measure | May lack incentive to increase productivity |

### Commission

**Commission** is extra pay based on the sales a person makes, usually a percentage of sales. It is common for sales staff. It may be paid on top of a basic salary or instead of one.

**Worked example 2: commission.** A car salesperson earns a basic $1,200 a month plus 4% commission on sales. In a month with sales of $60,000:

```
Commission = 4% x $60,000 = $2,400
Total pay  = $1,200 + $2,400 = $3,600
```

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Strong incentive to sell more | Income is uncertain, which causes stress when sales are low |
| Costs rise only when sales rise | Staff may pressure customers and harm service |
| Rewards the best performers | Staff in support roles, who do not sell, cannot earn it |

### Profit sharing

In **profit sharing**, a proportion of the business's profit is shared among employees, often as a bonus once a year.

**Worked example 3: profit sharing.** A company with a profit of $150,000 sets aside 10% for staff. It has 60 employees.

```
Profit share pool = 10% x $150,000 = $15,000
Each employee     = $15,000 / 60  = $250
```

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Encourages everyone to work to make the business profitable | If profit is low, staff get nothing, and may be disappointed |
| Builds loyalty and teamwork | Individual effort has little effect on the total, so some may feel their effort is not rewarded |
| Costs less when the business does poorly | Paid only once a year, so the incentive can feel distant |

Financial rewards can also include bonuses and performance-related pay, though the specification emphasises the four above.

### Limits of money as a motivator

Money matters, but it does not always motivate for long. A pay rise is soon taken for granted. If workers dislike the work, the manager or the conditions, extra pay may not stop them from leaving.

## Non-financial methods of motivation

Non-financial methods improve the experience of work without extra pay.

### Style of management

The way managers treat their staff affects motivation.

| Style | Description | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Autocratic | Manager makes decisions and tells staff what to do | Quick decisions; staff may feel undervalued and not take initiative |
| Democratic | Manager consults staff and takes their views into account | Staff feel valued and involved; decisions take longer |
| Laissez-faire | Manager leaves staff to make their own decisions | Suits skilled, self-driven staff; others may lack direction |

A democratic style often raises motivation, but an autocratic style can be suitable in an emergency or with inexperienced staff.

### Training and development

Training shows that the business invests in its staff. It helps them do their job better, builds confidence and opens the way to promotion. (See the separate guide on training.)

### Greater responsibility

Giving staff more responsibility, for example **delegating** decisions, leading a project or training new colleagues, makes work more interesting and shows trust. This is sometimes called job enrichment.

### Fringe benefits

**Fringe benefits** are extras given on top of pay, such as:

- staff discounts on the firm's products
- a company car or phone
- free or subsidised meals
- health insurance
- paid holiday beyond the minimum
- childcare support or gym membership.

They make the job more attractive and can encourage staff to stay. They cost the business money, and some employees would rather have extra pay.

### Other non-financial methods

Job rotation (moving between tasks), flexible hours, recognition schemes such as "employee of the month" and a pleasant working environment can also help.

## Choosing a method

Different groups respond to different rewards.

| Situation | Likely best method | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sales team | Commission | Pay is linked to results |
| Factory workers with fixed hours | Wage with a bonus, plus fringe benefits | Incentive to work well, with security |
| Highly skilled professionals | Salary, responsibility and training | Value independence and development |
| Staff needing team spirit | Profit sharing and democratic management | Encourage cooperation |

**Worked example 4: the cost of a wage rise.** A garage employs 20 mechanics on $12 an hour for a 40-hour week. A 5% rise would make the rate $12.60.

```
New weekly pay        = $12.60 x 40 = $504 (was $480)
Extra per mechanic    = $504 - $480 = $24 a week
Extra for 20 mechanics over 52 weeks = $24 x 20 x 52 = $24,960 a year
```

The garage must decide whether $24,960 is worth the gain in motivation, or whether training or flexible hours would motivate staff more cheaply.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that money is the only way to motivate staff. Non-financial methods are equally important.
- Confusing a wage (paid by the hour) with a salary (fixed annual amount).
- Describing the method without saying how it affects effort, productivity or retention.
- Giving only advantages of a method. Each has drawbacks.
- Writing about theories such as Maslow's. The specification says they will not be examined.
- Recommending the same method for every business. Match the method to the staff and the job.

## Exam technique for this section

For **state** and **identify** questions, name the method. For **explain** questions, state the method and its effect on the employee, then the effect on the business: "commission encourages sales staff to sell more, which raises revenue". For **calculate** questions, show each part of pay. For **evaluate** questions, weigh the cost to the business against the likely gain in productivity and retention, consider the type of employee, and give a reasoned recommendation.
