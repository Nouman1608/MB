## Globalisation and why it has happened

Globalisation is the increasing connection between the world's economies, as goods, services, money, people and ideas move more freely between countries. Reasons for it:

- **Cheaper, faster transport**: container shipping and air freight lower the cost of moving goods.
- **Better communications**: the internet lets businesses sell, buy and manage operations across borders.
- **Fewer trade barriers**: many governments have reduced tariffs and quotas, often through trade agreements.
- **Growth of multinationals**, which spread production and sales across many countries.
- **Rising incomes** in many economies, creating new markets.

## Opportunities and threats: think about the type of business

Globalisation does not affect every business in the same way. Ask whether the business **sells abroad**, **buys from abroad**, or **competes at home with imports**.

| Type of business | Main opportunities | Main threats |
|---|---|---|
| Exporter | Bigger markets, more sales, spreading risk | Foreign rivals in those markets; exchange rate changes |
| Business buying inputs from abroad | Cheaper or better materials and components | Supply disruption; costs rise if the currency falls |
| Local business facing imports | Can learn from or partner with foreign firms | Cheaper imports take its customers |
| Business setting up abroad | Lower costs; closer to new customers | Unfamiliar laws, culture and political risk |

Exam answers gain when they name which of these the business in the case is.

## Why governments introduce tariffs and quotas

An **import tariff** is a tax on imported goods. An **import quota** is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. Governments may introduce them to:

- **protect local businesses and jobs** from cheaper imports
- **protect new industries** while they grow large enough to compete
- **reduce spending on imports** when a country buys much more from abroad than it sells
- **raise revenue** (tariffs only, since a quota collects no tax).

### Reasoning chain: who wins and who loses from a tariff

A tariff raises the price of imports → local producers face less price competition → their sales and jobs are protected. **But** consumers pay higher prices and have less choice → local businesses that **use** imported materials face higher costs → other countries may respond with tariffs on this country's exports, hurting its exporters.

So in a case study, check whether the business makes goods that compete with imports (it gains) or buys imported inputs (it loses).

## Multinational companies

A multinational company (MNC) has operations, such as factories, offices or shops, in more than one country.

### Benefits to a business of becoming a multinational

- **Access to new markets** and more customers.
- **Lower costs** of labour, materials or transport by producing near resources or customers.
- **Avoiding trade barriers**, since goods made inside a country are not imports there.
- **Spreading risk** across several economies.
- **Economies of scale** from producing and buying in larger quantities.

### Impact on the MNC's stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Possible impact |
|---|---|
| Shareholders | Higher profits and dividends if expansion succeeds; risk of losses if it fails |
| Employees in the home country | New career opportunities, but jobs may move abroad if production is relocated |
| Customers | More choice and possibly lower prices |
| Suppliers in the home country | May lose orders if the MNC buys locally abroad |
| Managers | More complex organisation to control across countries |

### For the host country

| Potential benefits | Potential drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Jobs and training for local workers | Local businesses lose sales to the MNC |
| Exports, earning foreign currency | Profits sent back to the home country (repatriation) |
| Increased choice for consumers | Senior jobs may go to staff from the home country |
| Investment in factories and infrastructure | The MNC may leave if costs rise elsewhere |
| New technology and skills | Possible environmental damage or use of local resources |

## Worked example: annotated "explain" answer

**Case.** Corvalis Foods, a large MNC, opens a processing plant in a small country, Teraland. It buys tomatoes from local farmers and employs 400 local workers, but sends most of its profit to its head office abroad.

**Explain one benefit and one drawback to Teraland of the Corvalis plant.**

*Model answer, with notes.*

Benefit: the plant creates 400 jobs for local people, *(point from the case)* so those workers earn incomes they spend in local shops, *(development)* which raises sales for other Teraland businesses and increases the government's tax revenue. *(effect on the country)*

Drawback: Corvalis sends most of its profit abroad, *(point)* so this money is not reinvested in Teraland or spent there, *(development)* which means the country gains less from the plant than the size of the business suggests. *(effect)*

Each point follows the same pattern: case fact, reason, consequence.

## Exchange rates: appreciation and depreciation

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.

- **Appreciation**: the home currency rises in value, so it buys **more** foreign currency.
- **Depreciation**: the home currency falls in value, so it buys **less** foreign currency.

You will not be asked to calculate exchange rate conversions, but you must explain the effects.

### Reasoning chains

**Depreciation and an exporter.** The exporter's goods become cheaper for foreign buyers (if it keeps its home-currency price) → it becomes more price competitive abroad → sales may rise → profit may rise. Alternatively it can keep the foreign price the same and earn more home currency on each sale.

**Depreciation and an importer.** Imported goods and materials cost more in home currency → costs rise → the business either raises prices (and may lose customers) or accepts lower profit margins.

**Appreciation** reverses both chains: exporters become less competitive abroad, while importers' costs fall.

**Businesses that do both** need extra care. A furniture maker that imports timber and exports chairs faces higher timber costs after a depreciation, but its chairs are more competitive abroad. The overall effect on profit depends on which effect is larger, and on how much customers respond to price changes.

## Exam technique for the 7115 command words

- **Define / State**: precise meaning, for example "a quota limits the quantity of imports".
- **Identify**: pick the opportunity, threat or stakeholder from the case.
- **Explain**: finish every chain with an effect on prices, competitiveness, sales or profit.
- **Consider**: weigh both sides for this business or this country, then judge.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a tariff limits quantity, or that a quota raises tax revenue.
- Assuming globalisation helps or harms all businesses equally.
- Confusing appreciation (rise in value) with depreciation (fall).
- Saying a depreciation is "good for business" without saying whether it exports or imports.
- Treating an MNC as wholly good or wholly bad for a host country.
- Stopping an explanation before the effect on the business.
