## 3.3.15 at a glance (all A-level only)

- Scientists use a **range of analytical techniques** together to confirm the structure of a new
  compound: mass spectrometry, infrared and NMR.
- NMR gives information about the **position of ¹³C or ¹H atoms** in a molecule.
- **¹³C NMR gives simpler spectra than ¹H NMR.**
- Chemical shift is recorded on the **δ scale** and **depends on the molecular environment**.
- **Integrated** ¹H spectra give the relative numbers of ¹H atoms in each environment.
- ¹H samples are dissolved in **deuterated solvents or CCl₄**.
- **TMS** is the standard.
- Splitting follows the **n+1 rule**, limited to doublets, triplets and quartets in aliphatic compounds.
- No required practical is listed for this section.

## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Environment | The set of atoms around a nucleus. Atoms in the same environment give one signal |
| Equivalent atoms | Atoms made interchangeable by the molecule's symmetry |
| Chemical shift, δ | Position of a signal in ppm, measured relative to TMS at δ = 0 |
| Integration | Relative area under a ¹H peak, proportional to the number of H in that environment |
| Spin–spin splitting | Splitting of a ¹H signal by non-equivalent H on adjacent carbon atoms |
| TMS | Tetramethylsilane, Si(CH₃)₄, the standard |

## The δ scale

Larger δ means the nucleus is near an electronegative atom (such as O) or a C=O group. The specification
expects you to read shifts from the Chemistry Data Booklet. The approximate ranges below are written for
these notes and are **not copied from the AQA booklet**. Always work from the AQA booklet itself.

| ¹H (approximate) | δ / ppm |
|---|---|
| Alkyl CH₃, CH₂, CH | 0.7–1.7 |
| H–C next to C=O | 2.0–2.7 |
| H–C next to O | 3.3–4.3 |
| Alkene H | 4.5–6.0 |
| Aldehyde –CHO | 9.0–10.0 |
| Acid –COOH | 10.0–12.0 |

| ¹³C (approximate) | δ / ppm |
|---|---|
| Alkyl C–C | 5–50 |
| C–O | 50–90 |
| C=C | 100–160 |
| C=O in acids and esters | 160–185 |
| C=O in aldehydes and ketones | 190–220 |

## TMS: four reasons it is suitable

1. **12 equivalent H atoms** (and 4 equivalent C atoms), so it gives **one strong single peak**.
2. Its peak is at **lower δ than almost all organic protons**, so it does not overlap the sample's peaks.
3. It is **inert** and does not react with the sample.
4. It is **volatile**, so it is easily removed afterwards.

## Solvents

- **CDCl₃** and other deuterated solvents: deuterium (²H) gives **no signal** in a ¹H spectrum, so the
  solvent does not swamp the sample's peaks.
- **CCl₄**: contains **no H atoms** at all.
- CHCl₃ would be unsuitable: its ¹H would give a large peak.

## ¹³C spectra

- Number of peaks = number of **carbon environments**.
- Each peak's δ tells you the type of carbon.
- No splitting, wide δ range, little overlap, so the spectrum is simpler than a ¹H spectrum.
- Don't try to read numbers of carbons from ¹³C peak heights.

**Worked reminder.** Methyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₃, has three carbon environments: the C=O (160–185), the
O–CH₃ (50–90) and the CH₃ on the C=O (5–50). That gives **3 peaks**.

## ¹H spectra: method in steps

1. Count the peaks (groups of lines): that is the number of **H environments**.
2. Convert integration heights to the **simplest whole-number ratio**, then scale to the H count in the
   molecular formula.
3. Read each **δ** from the data to say what each set of H is near.
4. Use the **n+1 rule** to find how many H are on adjacent carbons.
5. Assemble fragments, then check the structure gives every peak, ratio and splitting.

**Worked reminder.** A compound C₄H₁₀O gives two ¹H peaks with integration heights 15 mm and 10 mm.
Ratio = 15 : 10 = 3 : 2. The formula has 10 H and 3 + 2 = 5, so scale by 2: **6 H and 4 H**. Two
equivalent ethyl groups are likely, which fits ethoxyethane, CH₃CH₂OCH₂CH₃.

## Combining techniques

| Technique | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| Mass spectrometry | Molecular ion, so Mr and (with precise masses) the molecular formula |
| Infrared | Bonds present, e.g. C=O, O–H, so the functional group |
| ¹³C NMR | Number and type of carbon environments |
| ¹H NMR | Number of H environments, their ratio, their neighbours |

Use them in that order: formula, functional group, carbon skeleton, then the H detail.

## The n+1 rule

A signal is split into **n + 1** lines by **n** H atoms on **adjacent** carbon atoms.

| n | Pattern | You can say |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | singlet | no H on neighbouring carbons (e.g. CH₃ next to C=O or O) |
| 1 | doublet | next to a CH |
| 2 | triplet | next to a CH₂ |
| 3 | quartet | next to a CH₃ |

- **Equivalent H do not split each other**: ClCH₂CH₂Cl gives one singlet.
- An **O–H** proton usually shows as a singlet in the spectra you meet.
- **Quartet (2H) + triplet (3H)** = ethyl group, CH₃CH₂–.
- **Singlet (9H)** = (CH₃)₃C–.

**Worked reminder.** Chloroethane, CH₃CH₂Cl: the CH₂ (2H) has 3 neighbouring H, so it is a **quartet**.
The CH₃ (3H) has 2 neighbouring H, so it is a **triplet**. Ratio 2 : 3.

## Must-know distinctions

- **¹³C vs ¹H**: ¹³C counts carbon environments and shows no splitting. ¹H counts hydrogen
  environments and gives integration and splitting.
- **Peak position vs splitting**: δ tells you what the H is near. Splitting tells you how many H are on
  the next carbons.
- **Ratio vs actual number**: integration gives a ratio. You need the molecular formula for actual
  numbers.
- **CH₃ next to C=O vs CH₃ next to O**: both are singlets. δ separates them (about 2.0–2.7 vs 3.3–4.3).
- **Aldehyde vs ketone**: both show C=O near 190–220 in ¹³C. Only an aldehyde has a ¹H peak at δ 9–10.
- **Carboxylic acid vs ester**: only the acid has a ¹H peak at δ 10–12.

## Quick self-test

1. How many peaks are in the ¹³C spectrum of propanone, CH₃COCH₃?
2. How many peaks are in the ¹H spectrum of propanone, and what is their splitting?
3. How many peaks are in the ¹³C spectrum of 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH₃)₃COH?
4. Integration heights are 18 mm, 12 mm and 6 mm. Give the ratio.
5. A ¹H signal is a quartet. How many H atoms are on the adjacent carbon atoms?
6. Give two reasons why TMS is used as the standard.
7. Why is CDCl₃ used instead of CHCl₃ as the solvent?
8. A ¹H peak appears at δ 9.7. What does it suggest?
9. A ¹³C peak appears at δ 205. What type of carbon is it?
10. Predict the ¹H spectrum of methoxymethane, CH₃OCH₃.
11. Why does ClCH₂CH₂Cl give a singlet, not a triplet?
12. State one way in which ¹³C spectra are simpler than ¹H spectra.

### Answers

1. **2** (the two equivalent CH₃ carbons, and the C=O).
2. **1 peak**, a **singlet**: all 6 H are equivalent, and the neighbouring carbon (C=O) has no H.
3. **2** (three equivalent CH₃ carbons, and the C–O carbon).
4. Divide by 6: **3 : 2 : 1**.
5. **3**.
6. Any two: a single strong peak from 12 equivalent H; peak away from (lower δ than) other peaks;
   inert; volatile, so easily removed.
7. CDCl₃ gives **no signal** in the ¹H spectrum. CHCl₃ would give a large peak from its H.
8. An **aldehyde** H, –CHO (δ 9.0–10.0).
9. A **C=O in an aldehyde or ketone** (190–220).
10. **One singlet**: all 6 H are equivalent, and the adjacent atom is O, which carries no H.
11. All four H are **equivalent**, and equivalent protons do not split each other.
12. Either: no splitting, so one line per environment; or the wider δ range means peaks rarely
    overlap.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting atoms instead of **environments**, especially missing symmetry in molecules such as
  (CH₃)₃C– groups or symmetrical ketones.
- Applying n+1 to the H in the group itself rather than the H on **adjacent** carbons.
- Splitting a CH₃ next to a C=O or O. It has no H neighbours, so it is a **singlet**.
- Leaving an integration ratio unscaled when the molecular formula is given.
- Saying TMS "has a peak at zero" without the reason: one strong peak, away from other peaks.
- Saying deuterated solvents are chosen because they are unreactive, rather than because they give
  **no ¹H signal**.
- Proposing a structure that fits the splitting but not the δ values (for example, mixing up which
  ester group is bonded to O).
- Forgetting to check the final structure against **every** peak, ratio and splitting pattern.
