This guide goes deeper into **3.3.2.3 Combustion of alkanes**, part of section 3.3.2 Alkanes in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 (Organic 1 and Physical 1) and available again in the synoptic Unit 5. Questions mix balancing, mole and gas-volume calculations with explanations of where pollutants come from and how they are removed.

## What the specification asks for

- Alkanes are used as fuels.
- Combustion of alkanes and other organic compounds can be complete or incomplete.
- The internal combustion engine produces a number of pollutants, including NOₓ, CO, carbon and unburned hydrocarbons.
- These gaseous pollutants from internal combustion engines can be removed using catalytic converters.
- Combustion of hydrocarbons containing sulfur leads to sulfur dioxide, which causes air pollution.
- Explain why sulfur dioxide can be removed from flue gases using calcium oxide or calcium carbonate.

## Complete and incomplete combustion

| | Complete | Incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen supply | plenty | limited |
| Carbon ends up as | CO₂ | CO and/or C (soot), possibly some CO₂ |
| Hydrogen ends up as | H₂O | H₂O |
| Energy released per mole of fuel | more | less |

Alkanes are good fuels because their combustion is highly exothermic and the products of complete combustion are simple molecules. Incomplete combustion wastes energy and makes two hazards: **carbon monoxide**, a toxic gas, and **carbon particles**. Carbon monoxide is toxic because it replaces the oxygen that is co-ordinately bonded to iron(II) in haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried in the blood (a link to 3.2.5.2).

### Balancing method for any organic compound

1. Balance **carbon** by putting the right number in front of CO₂ (or CO, or C).
2. Balance **hydrogen** by putting the right number in front of H₂O.
3. Count the **oxygen atoms** in the products. Subtract any oxygen atoms already in the fuel. Halve the result to get the number of O₂ molecules (fractions such as ½ are allowed).

### Worked example 1: four equations

```
(a) Complete combustion of heptane
    C₇H₁₆ → 7CO₂ + 8H₂O        O atoms: 14 + 8 = 22 → 11O₂
    C₇H₁₆ + 11O₂ → 7CO₂ + 8H₂O

(b) Incomplete combustion of heptane to carbon and water
    C₇H₁₆ → 7C + 8H₂O          O atoms: 8 → 4O₂
    C₇H₁₆ + 4O₂ → 7C + 8H₂O

(c) Incomplete combustion of octane to CO and water
    C₈H₁₈ → 8CO + 9H₂O         O atoms: 8 + 9 = 17 → 8½O₂
    C₈H₁₈ + 8½O₂ → 8CO + 9H₂O

(d) Complete combustion of ethanol (an "other organic compound")
    C₂H₅OH → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O       O atoms: 4 + 3 = 7; fuel supplies 1 → 6 → 3O₂
    C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O
```

Compare (a), (b) and (c): the less oxygen available per carbon, the more of the carbon ends as CO or C.

## Calculations with combustion

### Worked example 2: masses

*Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide and of water formed when 5.00 g of heptane burns completely. (Ar: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, O = 16.0)*

```
Mr(C₇H₁₆) = 100.0       n(C₇H₁₆) = 5.00 ÷ 100.0 = 0.0500 mol
n(CO₂) = 7 × 0.0500 = 0.350 mol    mass = 0.350 × 44.0 = 15.4 g
n(H₂O) = 8 × 0.0500 = 0.400 mol    mass = 0.400 × 18.0 = 7.20 g
```

### Worked example 3: gas volumes

At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of moles, so gas volumes react in the ratio of the coefficients.

*20 cm³ of propane is mixed with 150 cm³ of oxygen and ignited. After cooling to room temperature (water is now liquid), what volume of gas remains, and what is its composition? Then the gas is passed through aqueous sodium hydroxide, which absorbs CO₂. What is left?*

```
C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O(l)
O₂ used    = 5 × 20 = 100 cm³    O₂ left = 150 − 100 = 50 cm³
CO₂ formed = 3 × 20 = 60 cm³
Gas after cooling: 60 cm³ CO₂ + 50 cm³ O₂ = 110 cm³
After NaOH(aq): 50 cm³ of oxygen remains
```

Liquid water has a negligible volume, which is why "after cooling" matters.

## Pollutants from the internal combustion engine

| Pollutant | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| NOₓ (NO and NO₂) | N₂ and O₂ **from the air** combine at the very high temperature in the engine: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO; then 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ |
| CO | Incomplete combustion where oxygen is limited |
| Carbon (particles) | Incomplete combustion where oxygen is very limited |
| Unburned hydrocarbons | Fuel that passes through the engine without burning |

The nitrogen in NOₓ comes from the air, not from the fuel; petrol is a mixture of hydrocarbons.

### Catalytic converters

A catalytic converter is fitted in the exhaust. It contains a ceramic honeycomb support coated with a thin layer of catalyst metals such as platinum and rhodium, giving a large surface area at low cost. The hot exhaust gases react on the catalyst surface (heterogeneous catalysis, as in 3.2.5.6):

```
2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂
C₈H₁₈ + 25NO → 8CO₂ + 9H₂O + 12½N₂
2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂
```

CO and unburned hydrocarbons are **oxidised**; NO is **reduced** to N₂. The products, CO₂, N₂ and H₂O, are not toxic. The converter removes the **gaseous** pollutants; carbon particles are solids and are not converted in this way.

### Worked example 4: balancing a converter equation

*Hexane is converted by NO into CO₂, H₂O and N₂ only. Write the equation.*

```
C₆H₁₄ + ?NO → 6CO₂ + 7H₂O + ?N₂
O atoms needed in products: 12 + 7 = 19, all from NO → 19NO
N atoms: 19 → 9½N₂
C₆H₁₄ + 19NO → 6CO₂ + 7H₂O + 9½N₂
```

## Sulfur dioxide

Some fossil fuels contain sulfur compounds. When they burn, the sulfur is oxidised to **sulfur dioxide**, which causes air pollution:

```
S + O₂ → SO₂
```

### Removing SO₂ from flue gases

Flue gases are the waste gases from power stations and factories. SO₂ is an **acidic oxide**. Calcium oxide is a **basic oxide**, and calcium carbonate is a base that reacts with acids. So both neutralise SO₂ and turn it into a solid, calcium sulfite, which is removed:

```
CaO + SO₂ → CaSO₃
CaCO₃ + SO₂ → CaSO₃ + CO₂
```

### Worked example 5: how much calcium carbonate?

*A power station burns 1000 tonnes of coal containing 2.0 % sulfur by mass. Calculate the mass of SO₂ formed and the mass of CaCO₃ needed to remove it. (Ar: S = 32.1, O = 16.0, Ca = 40.1, C = 12.0)*

```
mass of S       = 2.0 % of 1000 t = 20 t
n ratio S : SO₂ = 1 : 1, so mass of SO₂ = 20 × 64.1 ÷ 32.1 = 39.9 t
n ratio SO₂ : CaCO₃ = 1 : 1
mass of CaCO₃   = 39.9 × 100.1 ÷ 64.1 = 62.4 t
```

Working with tonnes throughout is fine, because the same mass unit cancels in each ratio.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to subtract the oxygen already in an alcohol or other oxygen-containing fuel.
- Writing H₂ as a product of incomplete combustion. Hydrogen always ends up as water.
- Saying NOₓ comes from nitrogen in the fuel.
- Saying the catalytic converter "filters" or "absorbs" pollutants; they react on the catalyst surface.
- Claiming that catalytic converters remove carbon dioxide or carbon particles.
- Describing CaO as "absorbing" SO₂ without the acid–base reasoning.
- Counting the volume of liquid water in gas-volume problems.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Write an equation**: balance C, then H, then O; state symbols if asked.
- **Explain** (NOₓ): nitrogen and oxygen from air, high temperature, equation.
- **Explain** (SO₂ removal): SO₂ is acidic, CaO or CaCO₃ is basic, neutralisation, equation.
- **Calculate**: moles and mole ratios, or volume ratios for gases at the same temperature and pressure.
