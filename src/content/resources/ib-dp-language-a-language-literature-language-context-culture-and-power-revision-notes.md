## The unit in one sentence

A text offers **a version** of the world, from a position, to an audience -- and different readers,
in different times and places, may accept it, bend it or reject it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Context of production | Who made the text, when, where, for whom, why, and through which institution |
| Context of reception | Who is reading it now, where, and with what beliefs and stake |
| Representation | How a text presents people, groups, places or events through its choices |
| Stereotype | A fixed, simplified image of a group, repeated until it seems natural |
| Othering | Presenting a group as different from, and less than, an assumed "normal" reader |
| Perspective | The position a text is written from; every text has one |
| Stance | The writer's attitude to the subject, shown through language |
| Bias | One-sided treatment presented as fair; a claim you must prove |
| Agency | Who is shown acting, and who is only acted upon |
| Agentless passive | A passive verb with no "by..." phrase, so the actor is hidden |
| Nominalisation | An action turned into a noun ("the cuts"), which can hide who acted |
| Euphemism | A mild word used in place of a harsh one ("let go" for "sacked") |
| Generic masculine | "He" or "man" used for everyone ("each pupil... his") |
| Marked term | An extra word that signals someone is unusual for a role ("female pilot") |
| Code-switching | Moving between languages or varieties, often to signal identity or belonging |
| Preferred reading | The meaning the producer intends the audience to accept |
| Oppositional reading | A reading that rejects the intended meaning |

## Features to look for, and what to ask

| Feature | Ask | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Naming and labels | What is each group called? By whom? | Builds sympathy or suspicion before any argument |
| Verb choice and agency | Who does what to whom? Is anyone hidden? | Assigns or removes responsibility |
| Quotation and sources | Whose words appear? Whose are missing? | Gives some groups authority and silences others |
| Modality | "Must", "will" or "may", "might"? | Makes claims sound certain or open |
| Pronouns | Who is "we", "you", "they"? | Builds an in-group and an out-group |
| Register and jargon | Who can follow this language? | Includes experts, excludes others |
| Descriptors of people | Appearance, family, age -- or skill, action? | Reveals unequal treatment, often by gender |
| Omission | What would another source include? | Shows the version is one of several |

## Method in steps

> **Analysing a text for context, culture and power**
>
> 1. **Place it.** Production: who, when, where, for whom, why. Reception: who is reading now?
> 2. **Map the people.** List every person or group and the name each is given.
> 3. **Track agency.** For each main verb, note who acts and who is acted upon.
> 4. **Find the voices.** Who is quoted, summarised or silent?
> 5. **Name the stance.** Pick two or three words that reveal the writer's attitude.
> 6. **Read from two places.** Give the preferred reading, then the reading of a reader with a
>    different stake or culture.
> 7. **Link to meaning.** Say what version of the world the text offers, and who it serves.

## Small worked reminders

*Original texts written for Marlbridge; places and people invented.*

**Agency in headlines.** Three versions of one event:

- "Police clear protest camp" -- the police act; responsibility is clear.
- "Protest camp cleared" -- agentless passive; no one is responsible.
- "Protesters removed after week-long camp" -- the protesters are the subject but still acted upon.

**Generic masculine.** "Every applicant must send his portfolio by Friday." The notice assumes the
applicant is male. "Every applicant must send their portfolio" or "Applicants must send their
portfolios" includes everyone.

**Euphemism and power.** "Following a review, some roles will not continue into the new year." The
agentless verb "will not continue" and the noun "roles" hide two facts: someone decided, and people lose
jobs. The institution speaks; the workers are reduced to "roles".

**Reception across place.** A school notice says: "Families are welcome to bring a dish to share at
our end-of-term party -- please avoid pork." A reader who shares the dietary rule may read it as
welcome and respect. A reader unused to the rule may read it as a restriction. The meaning shifts with
the reader's culture.

**Power and literacy (public domain).** In Frederick Douglass's *Narrative* (1845), Chapter VI, his
enslaver bans his reading lessons, and Douglass concludes: "From that moment, I understood the pathway
from slavery to freedom." Remember the pattern: the powerful control access to language, and the
writer takes power back by writing. The study guide analyses the full extract.

**Gender asymmetry.** "Tom Reyes, 34, leads the new research team. Dr Ana Vidal, a keen baker and
mother of three, joins him." One scientist is defined by his role; the other by hobbies and family,
and "joins him" makes her secondary.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Perspective vs bias.** Every text has a perspective. Bias is a stronger claim that needs
  evidence of one-sided language.
- **Representation vs reality.** Analyse how the text builds its version; do not argue about what
  "really" happened.
- **Production vs reception.** Production is where the text came from; reception is where it is
  read. Say which one you mean.
- **Active vs agentless passive.** "The council closed the library" names the actor; "The library
  was closed" hides it.
- **Marked vs unmarked.** "Nurse" is unmarked; "male nurse" is marked. Marking shows an assumption
  about who normally fills the role.
- **Preferred vs oppositional reading.** Name the reader. The same words can serve one audience and
  offend another.
- **Self-naming vs being named.** A group that names itself holds power over its identity; a group
  named by others does not.

## Linking to assessment

- **Paper 1** (35% at both levels): unseen non-literary passages "from different text types". Use
  the features table on any news report, advert or brochure.
- **Paper 2** (35% SL, 25% HL): comparative essay on two literary works. Gender, identity and power
  make strong points of comparison.
- **Individual oral** (30% SL, 20% HL): a literary work and a non-literary body of work on "a common
  global issue". Power and identity supply many global issues.
- **HL essay** (HL only, 20%): 1,200--1,500 words. A line of inquiry on representation works well.

## Quick self-test

1. Define *representation* in one sentence.
2. What is the difference between *perspective* and *bias*?
3. Rewrite "The council closed three youth clubs" as an agentless passive.
4. What does the agentless passive in question 3 hide?
5. Identify the marked term: "The lady doctor saw twelve patients."
6. What is the generic masculine in "Each member should bring his own racket"?
7. Give the euphemism in "The company is rightsizing its workforce" and say what it hides.
8. In "We must protect our borders from them", which pronouns build an in-group and an out-group?
9. What is nominalisation? Give the noun in "The cuts hit hardest in the north."
10. Name the three reading positions described by Stuart Hall.
11. A brochure calls a village "unspoilt by progress". Give one preferred and one oppositional reading.
12. Which of the brief's assessment objectives names "contexts in which texts are written and/or
    received"?

### Answers

1. The way a text presents people, groups, places or events through its choices of language, selection
   and framing.
2. Perspective is the position every text is written from; bias is a one-sided treatment presented as
   fair, which you must prove with evidence.
3. "Three youth clubs were closed."
4. Who closed them: the council is no longer named, so responsibility disappears.
5. "Lady" -- it marks the doctor as unusual for the role because she is a woman.
6. "His" -- it assumes every member is male.
7. "Rightsizing" -- it hides that people are losing their jobs, and makes the cuts sound correct.
8. "We" and "our" build the in-group; "them" builds the out-group.
9. Turning an action into a noun; "cuts" -- it hides who is cutting.
10. Dominant (preferred), negotiated and oppositional.
11. Preferred: a visitor reads peace and charm. Oppositional: a resident reads a claim that the village
    is backward or should stay poor for tourists.
12. Assessment objective 1 (know, understand and interpret).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the text is biased" with no quotation and no effect.
- Treating one of two texts as neutral instead of showing that both select.
- Giving context as a paragraph of history with no link to a word in the text.
- Arguing for or against the issue instead of analysing the language.
- Spotting a passive verb without saying **who** it hides and **why** that matters.
- Naming a feature ("pronouns") without the specific words ("we", "them").
- Describing "the reader" as if every reader responds the same way.
- Discussing gender only in texts about women, and missing how men are represented too.
- Forgetting to say which version of the world the text offers, and who it serves.
