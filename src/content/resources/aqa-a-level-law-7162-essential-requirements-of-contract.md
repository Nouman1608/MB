This guide covers the section 3.4 row **Essential requirements of contract** in AQA A-level Law (7162). The specification names three statements: "Offer and acceptance", "Consideration (including privity of contract)" and "Intention to create legal relations". Together they decide whether a contract exists at all. The [Law of contract study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-law-of-contract-study-guide/) gives the headline cases; this page goes further into the rules that problem questions test most often: whether a statement is an offer, how offers end, when acceptance takes effect, what counts as consideration, who can enforce a contract, and when agreements are binding.

## Offer

An offer is a statement of the terms on which the offeror is willing to be bound, made with the intention that it will become binding as soon as it is accepted.

**Offer or something less?** Words matter. In *Gibson v Manchester City Council* (1979) a council letter saying it "may be prepared to sell" a house was not an offer, so no contract arose when the tenant replied. In *Harvey v Facey* (1893) a telegram stating the lowest price for which the defendant would sell was a supply of information, not an offer.

**Invitations to treat** include goods on display, most advertisements and, by analogy, website listings. Auctions follow the same pattern: the auctioneer's request for bids is an invitation to treat, each bid is an offer, and the fall of the hammer is acceptance (*Payne v Cave*, 1789, where a bidder could withdraw before the hammer fell).

**Communication.** An offer must be communicated to the offeree. A person who performs the act requested in a reward offer without knowing of the offer is generally treated as not having accepted it.

## How an offer ends

| Method | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Revocation | Effective only when communicated to the offeree; posting a revocation is not enough | *Byrne v Van Tienhoven* (1880) |
| Revocation through a third party | Valid if the offeree learns of it from a reliable source | *Dickinson v Dodds* (1876) |
| Rejection or counter-offer | Ends the original offer | *Hyde v Wrench* (1840) |
| Request for information | Does not end the offer | *Stevenson v McLean* (1880) |
| Lapse of time | Offer lapses after a stated or reasonable time | *Ramsgate Victoria Hotel v Montefiore* (1866) |
| Death | May end the offer, depending on the contract | General rule |

**Revoking a unilateral offer.** Once the offeree has started performing, the offeror cannot revoke. In *Errington v Errington* (1952) a father promised his son and daughter-in-law that the house would be theirs if they paid off the mortgage; while they continued paying, the promise could not be withdrawn.

## Acceptance

Acceptance is a final and unqualified agreement to all the terms of the offer.

**Mirror image.** Acceptance must match the offer exactly; new terms make it a counter-offer. In business, competing standard forms create a "battle of the forms". In *Butler Machine Tool v Ex-Cell-O* (1979) the buyer's order on its own terms was a counter-offer, and the seller's return of the buyer's tear-off acknowledgment slip accepted it, so the buyer's terms governed.

**Conduct.** Acceptance can be by conduct. In *Brogden v Metropolitan Railway* (1877) the parties had never signed a draft agreement, but by ordering and supplying coal on its terms they had accepted it.

**Communication.** Acceptance must generally be communicated; silence is not acceptance (*Felthouse v Bindley*, 1862). Exceptions and timing:

| Method | When effective | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Post (where reasonable to use) | When posted | *Adams v Lindsell* (1818); *Henthorn v Fraser* (1892) |
| Instant methods | When received | *Entores v Miles Far East* (1955) |
| Telex or similar to a business | When received, or when it would reasonably be read in office hours | *The Brimnes* (1975); *Brinkibon* (1983) |
| Unilateral contract | By performance; no need to communicate | *Carlill* (1893) |

## Consideration

**Definition.** "Some right, interest, profit or benefit accruing to the one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss or responsibility given, suffered or undertaken by the other" (*Currie v Misa*, 1875). Each side must give something in return for the other's promise.

**Rules and authorities.**

- **Must move from the promisee:** only a person who has given consideration can enforce the promise (*Tweddle v Atkinson*, 1861).
- **Sufficient but need not be adequate:** a rent of £1 a year was good consideration in *Thomas v Thomas* (1842), and chocolate wrappers in *Chappell v Nestlé* (1960).
- **Must be real and of some value:** in *White v Bluett* (1853) a son's promise to stop complaining was not consideration.
- **Must not be past:** work already done before a promise to pay is not consideration (*Re McArdle*, 1951). But if the act was done at the promisor's request, both parties understood it would be paid for, and payment would have been legally enforceable, it can be (*Lampleigh v Braithwait*, 1615; *Pao On v Lau Yiu Long*, 1980).
- **Existing public duty:** doing only what the law already requires is not consideration (*Collins v Godefroy*, 1831), but going beyond it is (*Glasbrook Bros v Glamorgan CC*, 1925, extra police protection at a mine).
- **Existing contractual duty:** not consideration (*Stilk v Myrick*, 1809), unless the promisee does more than the contract requires (*Hartley v Ponsonby*, 1857, where so many crew had deserted that the remaining sailors' duties changed) or the promisor obtains a practical benefit without duress (*Williams v Roffey Bros*, 1991).
- **Part payment of a debt:** not consideration for a promise to forgive the rest (*Pinnel's Case*, 1602; *Foakes v Beer*, 1884), unless something different is given, such as early payment or a different item. **Promissory estoppel** may prevent the creditor going back on the promise where the debtor relied on it: *Central London Property Trust v High Trees House* (1947), where wartime rent reductions were binding for the war years.

## Privity of contract

Only the parties to a contract can enforce it or be bound by it (*Dunlop v Selfridge*, 1915, where a manufacturer could not enforce a price agreement between a wholesaler and a retailer).

**Exceptions and reform.**
- **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999, s1:** a third party may enforce a term if the contract expressly provides that they may, or the term purports to confer a benefit on them, unless it appears the parties did not intend the term to be enforceable by them. The third party must be identified by name, class or description (s1(3)).
- **Agency:** a principal can enforce a contract made by an agent on their behalf.
- **Contracts for the benefit of others:** in *Jackson v Horizon Holidays* (1975) a father recovered damages for his family's spoiled holiday as well as his own.
- **Beswick v Beswick** (1968): the widow could not sue personally, but succeeded as administratrix of her husband's estate.

## Intention to create legal relations

The courts use presumptions, which can be rebutted by evidence.

**Social and domestic agreements: presumed not binding.**
- *Balfour v Balfour* (1919): a husband's promise of an allowance to his wife was not binding.
- *Merritt v Merritt* (1970): rebutted where a separated couple put their agreement in writing.
- *Jones v Padavatton* (1969): a mother's arrangement to support her daughter studying for the Bar was not binding.
- *Simpkins v Pays* (1955): rebutted where three people in a house shared competition entries and agreed to share winnings.

**Commercial agreements: presumed binding.**
- *Edwards v Skyways* (1964): an "ex gratia" redundancy payment promised to a pilot was binding; the words did not rebut the presumption.
- *Rose & Frank v Crompton* (1925): rebutted by an "honour clause" stating the agreement was not a formal legal agreement.
- *Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining Corporation* (1989): a "letter of comfort" stating a company's policy was a statement of present fact, not a contractual promise.

## Worked problem

*On Monday Rhys emails Bea offering to sell his drum kit for £500, "offer open until Friday". On Tuesday Bea replies, "Would you take £400?" Rhys does not answer. On Wednesday Bea's friend tells her Rhys has sold the kit to Cal. On Thursday Bea emails, "I accept your £500 offer."*

```
Offer:        Rhys's email - definite terms, intention to be bound. Offer.
Tuesday:      "Would you take £400?" - a request or a counter-offer? It is
              phrased as an inquiry, so arguably a request for information
              (Stevenson v McLean); but if read as a counter-offer it ends
              the original offer (Hyde v Wrench).
Wednesday:    Revocation communicated via a reliable third party
              (Dickinson v Dodds). A promise to keep the offer open until
              Friday is not binding without consideration.
Thursday:     Acceptance comes after revocation, so it is too late.
Conclusion:   No contract between Rhys and Bea, whichever view is taken of
              Tuesday's email.
```

## Common mistakes

- Treating a price statement or "may be prepared to sell" as an offer.
- Applying the postal rule to revocation (*Byrne v Van Tienhoven*).
- Saying any promise to keep an offer open is binding: without consideration it is not.
- Confusing adequacy (not required) with sufficiency (required).
- Forgetting the 1999 Act when a third party is named in the contract.

## Exam technique

Work through formation in strict time order: identify each communication, classify it (offer, invitation, counter-offer, inquiry, revocation, acceptance) with an authority, and decide when it took effect. For consideration and intention, state the general rule, then whether an exception applies.
