This guide covers subtopic **11.3 Fuels** of Topic 11, Organic chemistry, in the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. Every learning outcome in 11.3 is **Core**, so it can appear on all four theory and multiple-choice papers, and fractional distillation is also one of the separation techniques named for the practical papers. For a shorter recap use the [fuels revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-fuels-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [fuels practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-fuels-practice/). The wider picture, including cracking and alkene chemistry, is in [Petroleum, Alkanes and Alkenes](/resources/petroleum-alkanes-and-alkenes/).

## What 11.3 asks you to do

The seven outcomes fall into three groups:

1. **Recall facts about fossil fuels** – name coal, natural gas and petroleum; name methane as the main constituent of natural gas; state that hydrocarbons contain hydrogen and carbon only; state that petroleum is a mixture of hydrocarbons.
2. **Describe a process** – the separation of petroleum into useful fractions by fractional distillation.
3. **Describe trends and uses** – how chain length, volatility, boiling point and viscosity change from the bottom to the top of the column, and the use of each of the eight named fractions.

Most marks in this subtopic come from precise wording. "Hydrocarbon" questions are lost by saying "contains carbon and hydrogen" without the word **only**; fractional distillation answers are lost by saying the oil is "boiled at the top" or that fractions are "pure substances".

## Fossil fuels and hydrocarbons

The three fossil fuels named in the syllabus are **coal**, **natural gas** and **petroleum** (crude oil). Natural gas is mainly **methane**, CH₄, the simplest alkane.

A **hydrocarbon** is a compound containing **hydrogen and carbon only**. The word "only" matters: ethanol, C₂H₅OH, contains carbon and hydrogen but also oxygen, so it is not a hydrocarbon. Methane, octane (C₈H₁₈) and ethene (C₂H₄) all are.

**Petroleum is a mixture** of hydrocarbons, most of them alkanes, with molecules of many different chain lengths. Because it is a mixture, not a compound, it has no single boiling point. Each component keeps its own boiling point, and that is exactly what fractional distillation exploits.

## Why chain length controls boiling point

Hydrocarbon molecules are simple covalent molecules. Boiling one means separating molecules from each other, which requires overcoming the weak **intermolecular forces** between them; no covalent bonds break. Longer molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, so:

- a longer chain needs more energy to separate the molecules, giving a **higher boiling point**;
- a longer chain makes the liquid **more viscous** (thicker, flows less easily);
- a longer chain makes the liquid **less volatile** (it evaporates less readily).

Every property trend in outcome 6 follows from this one idea, so learn the reason once and apply it to each property.

## Fractional distillation of petroleum

Describe the process as a sequence:

1. Petroleum is **heated** until most of it **vaporises**.
2. The vapour enters the **fractionating column** near the bottom.
3. The column has a **temperature gradient**: it is **hottest at the bottom** and **coolest at the top**.
4. Vapours **rise** up the column. As they rise they cool.
5. Each substance **condenses** when it reaches a level where the temperature is **below its boiling point**; the liquid is collected at that level.
6. Molecules with low boiling points (short chains) stay as vapour longest and leave **near the top**; those with high boiling points (long chains) condense **low down**. The very longest chains do not vaporise at all and are drawn off at the bottom as a residue.

Each **fraction** is still a **mixture**, but of hydrocarbons with **similar boiling points** and similar chain lengths. That is why fractions are useful: their molecules behave alike, so a fraction can be matched to a job.

## Property trends from bottom to top

The syllabus limits outcome 6 to four trends. Moving **from the bottom to the top** of the column:

| Property | Change going up the column | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Chain length | **decreases** | short molecules have the lowest boiling points, so they condense highest |
| Volatility | **higher** | weaker intermolecular forces, so molecules escape into the vapour more easily |
| Boiling point | **lower** | less energy needed to separate the molecules |
| Viscosity | **lower** | shorter molecules flow past each other more easily |

Read the direction in the question carefully. If it asks about moving **down** the column, every trend reverses: longer chains, lower volatility, higher boiling points, higher viscosity.

## The eight fractions and their uses

These are the fractions and uses the syllabus names, listed from the top of the column (refinery gas) to the bottom (bitumen).

| Fraction | Use named in the syllabus |
|---|---|
| Refinery gas | gas used in heating and cooking |
| Gasoline / petrol | fuel used in cars |
| Naphtha | chemical feedstock |
| Kerosene / paraffin | jet fuel |
| Diesel oil / gas oil | fuel used in diesel engines |
| Fuel oil | fuel used in ships and home heating systems |
| Lubricating oil | lubricants, waxes and polishes |
| Bitumen | making roads |

Fuel oil and lubricating oil have overlapping boiling ranges, and textbook diagrams do not all draw them in the same order. Secure the clear pattern instead: gases and car fuel come off at the top, the thick, sticky fractions used for lubrication and roads come off at the bottom.

**Naphtha** is the one fraction not described as a fuel. A **chemical feedstock** is a raw material used to make other chemicals, for example plastics and other products made via cracking. If a question asks which fraction is used to make other chemicals, the answer is naphtha, not "petrol".

**Bitumen** is used for roads because it is very viscous and does not flow at road temperatures; **lubricating oil** is viscous enough to stay on moving parts. Linking the use to the property is a good way to remember the bottom of the column.

## Worked example 1: applying the trend

*Three fractions, X, Y and Z, are drawn off a fractionating column. X has the lowest boiling range, Z the highest. Which fraction is collected highest in the column, which is the most viscous, and which evaporates most readily at room temperature? Explain one of your choices.*

- **Collected highest:** X. A low boiling point means it stays as vapour until it reaches the coolest part of the column, at the top.
- **Most viscous:** Z. The highest boiling range means the longest chains, and longer chains flow past each other less easily.
- **Evaporates most readily:** X, the most volatile fraction.
- **Explanation (for Z):** Z has the longest molecules, so the intermolecular forces between them are strongest; more energy is needed to separate them, giving the highest boiling point and the greatest viscosity.

The method is the point: decide where each fraction sits from its boiling range, then apply the four trends. You never need to memorise data to answer this kind of question.

## Worked example 2: an annotated model answer

**Question:** Describe how petroleum is separated into fractions. [4]

**Model answer:** Petroleum is heated so that it vaporises [1]. The vapour passes into a fractionating column which is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top [1]. As the vapours rise they cool, and each fraction condenses at the level where the temperature falls below its boiling point [1]. Fractions with lower boiling points are collected higher up the column [1].

*Why it scores:* each sentence gives one separate, creditable step. The answer names the temperature gradient, uses "condenses" rather than "evaporates" for what happens at each level, and links the collection height to boiling point.

*A weaker answer* such as "the oil is heated and the different fractions come out at different levels" scores at most one mark: it gives no gradient, no condensing and no reason.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing "hydrocarbons contain hydrogen and carbon"** without "only".
- **Calling petroleum a compound**, or calling each fraction a pure substance. Petroleum is a mixture; each fraction is a smaller mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points.
- **Reversing the temperature gradient.** The column is hottest at the bottom.
- **Saying fractions "evaporate" at their level.** They condense there; vaporising happens before the column.
- **Describing volatility the wrong way round.** Short-chain fractions are *more* volatile, not less.
- **Giving natural gas as "methane and ethane"** when asked for the main constituent. The required answer is methane.
- **Naming petrol as the feedstock fraction.** Naphtha is the chemical feedstock.

## Exam technique for command words

- **Name / State:** one word or short phrase. "Name the main constituent of natural gas": *methane*. No explanation is needed and none earns extra credit.
- **Describe** (fractional distillation): give the steps in order, with the key terms *heated*, *vapour*, *temperature gradient*, *condense*, *boiling point*. Count the marks and give at least that many distinct points.
- **Describe** (trends): state the direction of each property, tied to the direction of travel stated in the question ("from bottom to top, the boiling point decreases").
- **Explain:** add the reason, usually chain length and intermolecular forces.
- **Suggest:** apply the trends to unfamiliar data, such as a fraction described only by its boiling range.
