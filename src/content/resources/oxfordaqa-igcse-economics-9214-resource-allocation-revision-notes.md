Quick recall for section 3.1.2 Resource allocation (3.1.2.1 Markets and allocation of resources, 3.1.2.2 Economic sectors, 3.1.2.3 Specialisation, division of labour, and exchange) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Economics (9214) specification, for exams from May/June 2025 onwards. Worked explanations are in the [resource allocation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-9214-resource-allocation/); questions are in the [resource allocation practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-9214-resource-allocation-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market | "An opportunity for buyers and sellers to interact in order to establish price" (specification wording) |
| Resource allocation | Deciding which uses scarce resources go to |
| Product market | Where goods and services are bought, mainly by households from firms |
| Factor market | Where land, labour, capital and enterprise are bought, mainly by firms from households |
| Primary sector | Extracting natural resources |
| Secondary sector | Manufacturing, processing, construction |
| Tertiary sector | Providing services |
| Good / service | Tangible and storable / intangible and consumed as provided |
| Specialisation | Concentrating on a limited range of tasks, products or services |
| Division of labour | Splitting production into tasks, each done by a different worker |
| Exchange | Trading what you produce for what others produce, usually using money |

## 3.1.2.1 How price allocates resources

Three roles of price, in order:

1. **Signal**: a rising price shows buyers want more.
2. **Incentive**: higher profit draws land, labour and capital into that product.
3. **Rationing**: buyers who value the product less drop out, so limited supply goes to those willing and able to pay.

The reverse also works: falling demand → lower price → lower profit → resources leave.

Mini example: the price of a product rises from 40 to 46. Rise = 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%. Firms producing substitutes may switch their resources to it.

**Factor versus product markets**

| | Product market | Factor market |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer | Households | Firms |
| Seller | Firms | Households |
| Price | Price of the good or service | Rent, wages, interest |

One firm is in both: it rents premises and hires workers (factor markets) and sells its output (product market). Income earned in factor markets is spent in product markets.

## 3.1.2.2 Sectors

- Chain of production: grower (primary) → processor (secondary) → transport and retail (tertiary).
- Sector size can be measured by **employment** or by **output**.
- A sector can **grow in output while its share falls**, if other sectors grow faster.
- Reasons for growth of the tertiary sector: rising incomes raise spending on services; machinery reduces farm jobs; cheaper imported manufactured goods.
- Mixed products: classify by the main activity (a restaurant is tertiary).

Formulas:

- Share (%) = sector value ÷ total × 100
- Change in share = new share − old share (in **percentage points**)
- Percentage change in output = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

## 3.1.2.3 Specialisation and exchange

**Types**: individuals, firms, regions, countries.

**Why producers specialise**: skill from repetition; no time lost switching tasks; specialised machinery; quicker training; concentrating on what they do best.

| | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Worker | Skill, speed, possibly higher pay | Boredom, lower motivation, narrow skills if the job disappears |
| Firm | Higher output per worker, lower unit cost, quicker training | More mistakes from bored workers, one stoppage halts the line, less flexibility |
| Country (narrow range) | High output of what it is best at, export earnings, large-scale production | Exposure to falls in world demand or price, weather, disease, resources running out |

**Why specialisation leads to exchange**: a specialist produces far more of one thing than it needs and none of others, so it must trade. Mini example: if one village specialises in fish and another in baskets, total output of both rises, but each must exchange to get the other good.

## Analysis chains to reuse

- Demand rises → price rises → profit rises → firms move resources in → output rises.
- Division of labour → repetition → faster work → output per worker rises → unit cost falls.
- Narrow export range → price of main export falls → export earnings fall sharply → less to pay for imports.

## Exam traps

- A market need not be a physical place: online platforms count.
- In factor markets households sell and firms buy.
- "Share fell" does not mean "output fell".
- Specialisation is the wider idea; division of labour is one form of it.
- Give costs **and** benefits when asked about specialisation, and to whom (worker, firm or country).
- Exchange is a consequence of specialisation; say why.

## Self-test

1. Give the specification's definition of a market.
2. State the three roles of price in allocating resources.
3. In the labour market, who is the buyer?
4. What is the reward paid in the market for land?
5. A mine and a steelworks: which sectors are they in?
6. Is a hairdresser providing a good or a service?
7. Total output is 400 and tertiary output is 260. Calculate the tertiary share.
8. Secondary output rose from 80 to 120, but its share fell from 40% to 30%. State the change in share.
9. Give two reasons why individuals specialise.
10. Give one cost of the division of labour to a firm.
11. Why does specialisation lead to exchange?
12. Give one risk to a country of specialising in one export.

### Answers

1. An opportunity for buyers and sellers to interact in order to establish price.
2. Signal, incentive, rationing.
3. Firms (employers).
4. Rent.
5. Mine: primary; steelworks: secondary.
6. A service.
7. 260 ÷ 400 × 100 = 65%.
8. A fall of 10 percentage points (output still rose by 50%).
9. Any two: skill from repetition, no time lost switching tasks, quicker training, doing what they are best at.
10. One stoppage halts the line, or bored workers make more mistakes, or less flexibility.
11. Specialists produce more of one thing than they need and none of other things, so they must trade.
12. A fall in world demand or price for that export cuts earnings sharply (or bad weather, disease, resources running out).
