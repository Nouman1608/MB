These notes condense **4.2.2.8 Metals as conductors** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification (for
teaching from September 2016, version 1.2). It is Paper 1 content for both tiers, with no Higher-only
statements and no required practical. For full explanations, see the
[metals as conductors study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metals-as-conductors/); then practise with
the [metals as conductors practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-metals-as-conductors-practice/).

## Core facts

- Metals are **good conductors of electricity** because the **delocalised electrons** in the metal **carry
  electrical charge** through the metal.
- Metals are **good conductors of thermal energy** because energy is **transferred by the delocalised
  electrons**.

## The structure behind it

- Positive metal ions sit in a regular arrangement.
- Outer electrons are **delocalised**: they do not belong to one atom and are free to move through the structure.
- The ions stay in place. The electrons carry the charge and the energy.

```
  +   +   +   +
    e-  e-  e-
  +   +   +   +      (+ = positive ion, e- = delocalised electron)
```

## Conduction compared

| Substance | What can move | Conducts electricity? |
|---|---|---|
| Solid metal | Delocalised electrons | Yes |
| Molten metal | Delocalised electrons | Yes |
| Solid ionic compound | Nothing (ions fixed) | No |
| Molten or dissolved ionic compound | Ions | Yes |
| Small molecules | Nothing | No |
| Diamond, silicon dioxide | Nothing | No |
| Graphite | Delocalised electrons | Yes |

## Model answers

**Electrical conduction:** The metal has delocalised electrons. They are free to move through the structure.
They carry the electrical charge.

**Thermal conduction:** The delocalised electrons gain energy at the hot end, move through the metal and transfer
the energy to cooler parts.

## Everyday uses

| Object | Property used |
|---|---|
| Copper wire | Electrical conductor; can be drawn into wires |
| Aluminium or copper pans | Thermal conductor |
| Radiator | Thermal conductor |
| Plastic handle on a pan | Poor thermal conductor, so it stays cool to touch |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Delocalised electrons | Outer electrons that are free to move through the whole metal structure |
| Electrical conductor | A material that allows charge to flow through it |
| Thermal conductor | A material that transfers thermal energy quickly |
| Metallic bonding | Attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons |
| Current | A flow of charge |
| Insulator | A material that does not conduct |

## Reading data

| Test result | Probable type |
|---|---|
| Conducts as a solid, high melting point | Metal |
| Does not conduct as a solid, conducts when molten, high melting point | Ionic |
| Does not conduct at all, low melting point | Small molecules |
| Does not conduct at all, very high melting point | Giant covalent |

Graphite is a giant covalent structure that conducts because it has delocalised electrons.

## Worked examples in brief

**Example 1.** "Explain why a copper wire conducts." Delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge.

**Example 2.** "Explain why the handle of a metal spoon gets hot in hot soup." Energy is transferred from the soup to
the spoon, and the delocalised electrons carry it along the metal to the handle.

**Example 3.** "Why does solid sodium chloride not conduct but solid sodium does?" Sodium chloride has fixed ions
and no free electrons. Sodium has delocalised electrons.

## Testing for conduction

1. Build a series circuit with a cell, a bulb (or ammeter) and a gap.
2. Place the sample in the gap.
3. If the bulb lights, the solid conducts.
4. Record "conducts" or "does not conduct" in a table, and repeat for each sample.

## Why non-metals usually do not conduct

| Material | Reason |
|---|---|
| Plastic, wood | No ions or free electrons |
| Sulfur | Small molecules, no overall charge |
| Diamond | All outer electrons used in covalent bonds |
| Solid ionic compound | Ions fixed in position |

Graphite is the non-metal exception, because it has delocalised electrons.

## Six-mark answer plan

1. Positive ions in a regular arrangement.
2. Outer electrons delocalised, forming a sea.
3. Metallic bonding holds the ions in place.
4. Electrons free to move.
5. Electrons carry charge: electrical conduction.
6. Electrons transfer energy: thermal conduction.

## Exam traps

- **"Free ions in metals."** The charge carriers are delocalised electrons.
- **"The ions move in a solid metal."** Positive ions stay in position.
- **"The electrons are shared between two atoms."** In a metal they are delocalised through the whole structure.
- **"Electricity just flows."** Say what carries the charge.
- **Missing the thermal energy idea.** The electrons transfer energy as well as charge.
- **Treating all conductors as metals.** Graphite also conducts.

## Quick self-test

1. Why are metals good conductors of electricity?
2. What are delocalised electrons?
3. Which particles stay in fixed positions in a solid metal?
4. Why are metals good conductors of thermal energy?
5. Does solid sodium chloride conduct electricity? Why or why not?
6. Does a metal conduct when it is molten?
7. Name a non-metallic element that conducts electricity.
8. Why are pan handles often made of plastic?
9. A solid conducts electricity and has a melting point of 660 °C. What type of substance is it likely to be?
10. Give the word that describes electrons that are not attached to one atom.

### Answers

1. The delocalised electrons carry electrical charge through the metal.
2. Outer electrons that are free to move through the whole structure and do not belong to one atom.
3. The positive ions.
4. Energy is transferred by the delocalised electrons.
5. No. The ions are fixed in the lattice and there are no free electrons.
6. Yes.
7. Carbon, as graphite.
8. Plastic is a poor thermal conductor, so the handle stays cool.
9. A metal.
10. Delocalised.
