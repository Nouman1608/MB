## Condensed recall

### Forcings

- **Forcing**: a factor that changes the balance of incoming and outgoing energy. Positive = warming.
- **Natural**:
  - Milankovitch cycles: eccentricity (about 100 000 years), obliquity (about 41 000 years), precession (roughly 20 000 years); paced ice ages, amplified by CO₂ and ice feedbacks.
  - Solar output: small variations, including an 11-year cycle.
  - Volcanoes: stratospheric sulfate aerosols reflect sunlight; cooling for a year or two (Mount Pinatubo, 1991).
- **Human**: greenhouse gases (largest, positive); aerosols (mostly negative, masking some warming); land-use change (albedo and carbon).

### Evidence of human cause

| Evidence | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Timing | Warming over about 150 years tracks CO₂; orbital cycles are far too slow |
| Solar measurements | No rise in solar output since the mid-twentieth century |
| Cooling stratosphere | Fits greenhouse trapping, not a brighter Sun |
| Carbon isotopes | Added CO₂ has a fossil-fuel signature |
| Models | Reproduce warming only with human forcings |

- **IPCC**: set up 1988 by the WMO and UNEP; Sixth Assessment Report: human influence on warming is unequivocal.

### Carbon sinks

- Roughly half of human CO₂ emissions taken up each year by oceans and land; the rest accumulates.
- Sinks weaken: warmer water dissolves less CO₂; drought and fire turn forests into sources.

### Rate of rise

- Rate = change in concentration ÷ years (ppm per year).
- Short reminder: 340 → 370 ppm over 15 years = **2.0 ppm per year**.

### Lifetimes

- **Methane**: broken down mostly within about a decade → quick benefit from cuts.
- **CO₂**: no single lifetime; part remains for thousands of years → warming depends on cumulative emissions.
- **N₂O, many halocarbons**: around a century or more.
- GWP depends on the time horizon; methane looks more powerful over 20 years than over 100.

### Comparing emitters

- Per person (t) = emissions (Mt) ÷ population (millions).
- Total: highlights today's largest emitters.
- Per person and cumulative: highlight wealthy, early-industrialising countries.

### Tipping elements

- Thresholds beyond which change becomes self-sustaining and hard to reverse.
- Examples: Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, Amazon rainforest, permafrost, Atlantic overturning circulation, warm-water coral reefs.
- Can cascade (Greenland melt → weaker Atlantic circulation → tropical rainfall changes).

### Ocean acidification

```
CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
```

- More H⁺ → lower pH; H⁺ also binds carbonate, so less is available for shells and skeletons.
- Caused by dissolved CO₂, not by warming.

### Vulnerability

- **Exposure** × **sensitivity**, reduced by **adaptive capacity**.
- Small island states: sea-level rise, storm surge, little high ground.
- Arctic: thawing permafrost, sea-ice loss.
- Drylands: drought, shifting rainy seasons.
- Ecosystems: timing mismatches between species.
- Health: heat stress, spread of disease vectors.
- Lowest emitters per person often most vulnerable → climate justice.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Climate forcing | A factor that changes Earth's energy balance |
| Milankovitch cycles | Long-term changes in Earth's orbit and axis |
| Carbon sink | A store that takes up more carbon than it releases |
| Cumulative emissions | Total emissions added over time |
| Per-capita emissions | Emissions divided by population |
| Atmospheric lifetime | Typical time a gas remains in the atmosphere |
| Tipping element | Part of the Earth system that can cross a threshold to a new state |
| Ocean acidification | Fall in seawater pH caused by dissolved CO₂ |
| Exposure | Presence of people or ecosystems in places affected by hazards |
| Sensitivity | Degree to which a system is affected by a change |
| Adaptive capacity | Ability to adjust to and cope with change |
| Climate justice | Fairness in sharing the burdens and responsibilities of climate change |

## Exam traps

- Natural forcings exist, but cannot explain the timing and pattern of recent warming.
- The sinks are not fixed; say how warming weakens them.
- Acidification is a CO₂ effect, not a temperature effect.
- Total emissions alone do not settle questions of responsibility.
- A tipping point is a threshold, not any large change.
- Impacts need named groups or places and reasons for their vulnerability.

## Self-test

1. Define climate forcing.
2. Name the three Milankovitch cycles.
3. Why did the 1991 Pinatubo eruption cool the climate only briefly?
4. Give two lines of evidence that recent warming is not caused by the Sun.
5. What fraction of human CO₂ emissions is taken up by sinks each year, roughly?
6. Give one reason the ocean sink may weaken.
7. CO₂ rose from 325 to 400 ppm over 40 years. Calculate the mean rate of rise.
8. Why does cutting methane slow warming faster than cutting the same CO₂e of CO₂?
9. A country emits 600 Mt CO₂ a year and has 48 million people. Calculate emissions per person.
10. Name three tipping elements.
11. Explain how ocean acidification affects corals.
12. Why are many small island states especially vulnerable?

### Answers

1. A factor that changes the balance between energy entering and leaving the Earth system.
2. Eccentricity, obliquity (tilt) and precession (wobble).
3. Its sulfate aerosols settled out of the stratosphere within a year or two.
4. Any two: solar output has not risen since the mid-twentieth century; the stratosphere is cooling while the troposphere warms; warming tracks CO₂ timing.
5. Roughly half.
6. Warmer water dissolves less CO₂ (or stronger stratification reduces mixing of CO₂ into deep water).
7. 75 ÷ 40 = **1.9 ppm per year** (1.875).
8. Methane is broken down within about a decade, so its warming effect falls quickly when emissions stop; CO₂ persists for centuries or longer.
9. 600 ÷ 48 = **12.5 t per person**.
10. Any three: Greenland ice sheet, West Antarctic ice sheet, Amazon rainforest, permafrost, Atlantic overturning circulation, coral reefs.
11. Extra hydrogen ions reduce the carbonate available, so corals build skeletons more slowly and weakened reefs erode.
12. They are low-lying, so sea-level rise and storm surges threaten land and fresh water, and they have little high ground or money to adapt.
