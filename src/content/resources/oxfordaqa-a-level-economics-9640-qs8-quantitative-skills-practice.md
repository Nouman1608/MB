These questions practise quantitative skill QS8, calculating elasticities and interpreting the result, from section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification. Every firm and figure is invented. Work percentage changes from the starting values. Revise first with the [QS8 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs8-quantitative-skills/) and the [QS8 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-economics-9640-qs8-quantitative-skills-revision-notes/), and use the [course hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/economics/) for other topics.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Define income elasticity of demand and state what a negative value shows. **[2]**

**2.** A cinema raises its ticket price from 8.00 to 8.80. Weekly admissions fall from 2,500 to 2,350. Calculate the price elasticity of demand and state whether demand is elastic or inelastic. **[3]**

**3.** A firm estimates that the price elasticity of demand for its product is -1.8. It cuts its price by 5%. Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded and the percentage change in total revenue. **[3]**

**4.** During a recession average incomes fall by 3%. The income elasticity of demand is +2.5 for restaurant meals and -0.4 for own-brand pasta. Calculate the change in demand for each product. **[2]**

**5.** The price of good B rises from 4.00 to 4.60. Demand for good A falls from 1,200 to 1,110 units. Calculate the cross elasticity of demand and state the relationship between the goods. **[3]**

**6.** Which statement describes a price elasticity of supply of zero? **[1]**

A: The supply curve is horizontal.

B: Quantity supplied does not change when price changes.

C: Supply is perfectly elastic.

D: Firms supply nothing at any price.

### Section B

**7.** A government plans a tax that will raise the price of sugary drinks by 20%. The price elasticity of demand is estimated at -0.6. Current sales are 50 million litres a year.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded and the new level of sales. **[2]**

**(b)** The government wants consumption to fall by 20%. Calculate the price rise needed, using the same estimate. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the tax may raise substantial revenue but fall short of the government's health objective. **[3]**

**8.** A hotel sells 400 room-nights a month to business travellers at 150 and 600 to leisure travellers at 90. It estimates PED at -0.3 for business travellers and -2.0 for leisure travellers. It plans to raise the business rate by 10% and cut the leisure rate by 10%.

**(a)** Calculate the forecast room-nights for each group. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate total revenue before and after the change. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain one reason why the forecast might prove wrong. **[2]**

**9.** The price of new homes in Region H rises by 15%. Builders complete 20,000 homes a year. PES is estimated at 0.3 in the short run and 1.2 in the long run.

**(a)** Calculate the forecast number of completions in the short run and in the long run. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain two reasons why the supply of new homes is price inelastic in the short run. **[4]**

**10.** A snack's price rises from 2.00 to 2.20 and its sales rise from 10,000 to 10,300 a week. Over the same period average incomes rise by 8%. The income elasticity of demand for the snack is estimated at +1.25.

**(a)** A student calculates PED as +0.3. Show how this figure was obtained and explain why it is misleading. **[3]**

**(b)** Use the YED estimate to find the change in sales caused by income alone, and hence give a rough estimate of PED. **[3]**

**11.** A music-streaming service estimates its cross elasticity of demand at +0.4 with a rival service's price and at -0.2 with the price of home broadband. The rival raises its price by 15% and broadband prices rise by 10%.

**(a)** Calculate the effect of each change on demand for the streaming service and the approximate combined effect. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain what the two values tell the firm about its market. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that a firm facing price-inelastic demand should always raise its price. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** YED is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income [1]. A negative value shows an inferior good, for which demand falls as income rises [1]. **[2]**

**2.** %change in price = 0.80 / 8.00 = +10% [1]. %change in admissions = -150 / 2,500 = -6% [1]. PED = -6 / 10 = **-0.6**, so demand is **inelastic** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus sign and say what the value means; "inelastic" without a reason is weaker than "admissions fall proportionately less than the price rises".

**3.** %change in quantity = -1.8 x -5 = **+9%** [1]. Revenue factor = 0.95 x 1.09 = 1.0355 [1], so revenue rises by **3.55%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The approximation 9 - 5 = 4% is close but not exact; show the multiplication.

**4.** Restaurant meals: 2.5 x -3 = **-7.5%** [1]. Own-brand pasta: -0.4 x -3 = **+1.2%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The inferior good gains in a recession; the double negative is where marks slip.

**5.** %change in price of B = 0.60 / 4.00 = +15% [1]. %change in demand for A = -90 / 1,200 = -7.5% [1]. XED = -7.5 / 15 = **-0.5**, so the goods are **complements** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** **B** [1]. A PES of zero is a vertical supply curve: quantity supplied is fixed whatever the price. **[1]**

**7. (a)** -0.6 x 20 = **-12%** [1]. New sales = 50 x 0.88 = **44 million litres** [1].

**(b)** -20 / -0.6 = **+33.3%** [1]. A price rise of about a third is needed [1].

**(c)** Demand is inelastic, so sales fall only 12% and most of the tax base remains [1]. Revenue is therefore large and fairly predictable [1], but consumption falls well short of the 20% target unless the tax is larger or combined with other measures such as information campaigns [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the low PED both to the revenue and to the weak behavioural effect; they are two sides of the same fact.

**8. (a)** Business: -0.3 x 10 = -3%, so 400 x 0.97 = **388** [1]. Leisure: -2.0 x -10 = +20%, so 600 x 1.2 = **720** [1].

**(b)** Before: 400 x 150 + 600 x 90 = 60,000 + 54,000 = **114,000** [1]. After: 388 x 165 = 64,020 and 720 x 81 = 58,320 [1], total **122,340**, a rise of 8,340 [1].

**(c)** The estimates may not hold for a 10% change [1], or rival hotels may cut their own leisure rates, so the hotel gains fewer extra guests than forecast [1]. Other answers: capacity limits on extra rooms, business travellers switching to leisure rates. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Charging different groups according to their PED is the key idea; the calculation shows why it raises revenue.

**9. (a)** Short run: 0.3 x 15 = 4.5%, so **20,900** [1]. Long run: 1.2 x 15 = 18%, so **23,600** [1].

**(b)** Building takes a long time from planning to completion [1], so output cannot respond quickly to a price rise [1]. Land with permission to build is limited [1], and skilled labour and materials may be in short supply when builders are already busy [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs its link to the speed or size of the supply response.

**10. (a)** %change in price = +10% and %change in sales = +3% [1], so 3 / 10 = +0.3 [1]. It is misleading because incomes also rose, so the rise in sales is not a response to price alone; other things were not held equal [1].

**(b)** Income effect = 1.25 x 8 = **+10%** [1]. Sales rose only 3%, so the price rise reduced sales by roughly 10 - 3 = 7 percentage points [1]. Rough PED = -7 / 10 = **-0.7** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Label this as an approximation; it adds percentage effects, which is only roughly right.

**11. (a)** Rival price effect: 0.4 x 15 = **+6%** [1]. Broadband effect: -0.2 x 10 = **-2%** [1]. Combined, roughly **+4%** [1].

**(b)** The positive XED shows the rival is a substitute, but at 0.4 a weak one, so the service has some loyal users [1]. The negative XED shows broadband is a complement [1], but the small value means broadband prices have only a minor effect on demand [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Interpret sign and size separately for each value.

**12.** With inelastic demand, a price rise raises total revenue [1], and because fewer units are sold, total cost falls, so profit should rise [1]. For example, with PED of -0.5 a 10% rise cuts sales by only 5%, giving revenue up by about 4.5% [1]. However, PED is an estimate and may be wrong or out of date [1]. Demand usually becomes more elastic over time as buyers find substitutes, so a firm can lose customers in the long run [1]. Rivals may not follow the rise, making the firm's own demand more elastic than the market's [1]. Regulators or public opinion may react to high prices, and the firm may value market share or growth [1]. Judgement: a price rise is likely to raise short-run profit, but "always" is too strong; it depends on the reliability of the estimate, the time period and rivals' responses [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use a calculation to support the first half, then challenge the word "always".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing price change by quantity change.
- Losing the sign in forecasts.
- Treating a positive but small YED as inferior.
- Saying a revenue rise proves a profit rise without mentioning costs.
- Ignoring other changes in the data when calculating PED.
- Interpreting PES without a time period.
