## Equations

| Equation | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| ρ = m/V | Density | kg m⁻³ |
| F = kΔL | Hooke's law (up to the limit of proportionality) | N |
| tensile stress = F/A | Force per unit cross-sectional area | Pa |
| tensile strain = ΔL/L | Extension per unit original length | none |
| E = stress/strain = FL/(AΔL) | Young modulus | Pa |
| energy stored = ½FΔL = ½kΔL² | Elastic strain energy (Hooke's-law region) | J |
| A = πd²/4 | Cross-sectional area of a wire of diameter d | m² |

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Stiffness (spring constant) k | Force per unit extension, F/ΔL |
| Limit of proportionality | Point beyond which F is no longer proportional to ΔL |
| Elastic limit | Maximum force (or stress) after which the material still returns to its original length |
| Elastic deformation | Material returns to its original shape when the load is removed |
| Plastic deformation | Permanent change of shape remains after unloading |
| Breaking stress | Maximum stress a material can withstand before breaking |
| Brittle | Breaks with little or no plastic deformation |
| Ductile | Can undergo large plastic deformation (be drawn into wires) |
| Elastic strain energy | Energy stored in a deformed material; area under the F–ΔL graph |
| Young modulus | Ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the elastic, proportional region |

## Reading the graphs

| Feature | Force–extension graph | Stress–strain graph |
|---|---|---|
| Gradient of straight section | Stiffness k | Young modulus E |
| Area under graph | Work done / energy stored | (not required) |
| End point | Breaking force | Breaking stress |

- **Ductile metal** (copper): straight section, then a large plastic region before fracture.
- **Brittle material** (glass): straight line to fracture, no plastic region.
- **Loading past the elastic limit, then unloading**: the unloading line runs parallel to the original straight section and leaves a permanent extension.

## Energy

**Method for a spring-launch problem**
1. Stored energy = ½kΔL² (ΔL in metres).
2. Set it equal to ½mv² for launch speed, or mgh for maximum height (if no losses).
3. State any assumption, such as no air resistance or a light spring.

Worked reminder: a spring with k = 25 N m⁻¹ stretched by 12 cm stores ½ × 25 × 0.12² = **0.18 J**.

**Energy to deform**: past the elastic limit, some work done goes into permanent deformation, so less energy is returned on unloading. Crumple zones use this to absorb kinetic energy in a collision.

## Young modulus method in steps

1. Convert d to metres; A = πd²/4.
2. Stress = F/A.
3. Strain = ΔL/L (both lengths in the same unit).
4. E = stress ÷ strain.

Worked reminder: a wire 1.8 m long and 0.50 mm in diameter extends 0.90 mm under 20 N. A = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m²; stress = 1.02 × 10⁸ Pa; strain = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴; **E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa**, a typical value for steel.

**Required practical 4**: long thin wire; metre rule for L; micrometer for d at several points; equal load steps; extension against a fixed reference; plot stress against strain and take the gradient of the straight part. Largest uncertainty: diameter (doubled in A). Eye protection.

## Exam traps

- Using diameter as radius in πr².
- Squaring mm before converting to m.
- Measuring ΔL as the new length rather than the extension.
- Giving strain a unit, or quoting E in N m⁻¹.
- Using ½FΔL on a curved section of the graph.
- Treating the elastic limit as the limit of proportionality.
- Saying a thicker wire has a larger Young modulus.
- Forgetting g when a load is given as a mass.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Stiffness vs Young modulus**: k depends on the object's dimensions; E is a property of the material.
- **Elastic vs plastic**: returns to original length vs permanent deformation.
- **Brittle vs ductile**: fractures with no plastic region vs large plastic region before fracture.

9. What does the gradient of a stress–strain graph represent?

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does a wire's diameter dominate the uncertainty in the Young modulus?**
The diameter is small, so its percentage uncertainty is large, and the area depends on its square, which doubles that percentage uncertainty.

**Is the energy stored always ½FΔL?**
Only where the force–extension graph is a straight line through the origin. Beyond the limit of proportionality the energy is still the area under the graph, but that area is not a simple triangle.
