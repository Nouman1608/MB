Revise first with the [business forms study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-different-business-forms-aqa-alevel-business/) and the [business forms revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-different-business-forms-aqa-alevel-business-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A company has 5 million shares in issue, trading at £1.80 each. What is its market capitalisation? **[1]**

A: £0.9 million

B: £1.8 million

C: £5 million

D: £9 million

**2.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to cause a company's share price to rise? **[1]**

A: The company warns that profits will be lower than forecast

B: Interest rates rise sharply

C: Reports that a rival is preparing a takeover bid

D: The company cuts its dividend

**3.** Define the term "ordinary share capital". **[2]**

**4.** Explain why limited liability makes it easier for a company to raise finance. **[3]**

**5.** State **two** rights usually held by ordinary shareholders. **[2]**

**6.** Elmhurst plc has 12 million shares in issue. Its share price rises from £4.10 to £4.60.

**(a)** Calculate the change in Elmhurst's market capitalisation. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why this rise does not give Elmhurst any extra cash. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Jaya runs a catering business as a sole trader. Revenue has doubled in two years. She wants to buy a £120,000 commercial kitchen, and her brother and a friend have each offered £50,000 in return for shares. Analyse **two** benefits to Jaya of turning the business into a private limited company. **[6]**

**8.** Harrowgate Outdoor Ltd has been owned by the same family for three generations. Its mission is "kit built to last a lifetime". The directors need £30 million to expand overseas and are considering becoming a public limited company. Assess whether Harrowgate Outdoor should become a plc. **[9]**

**9.** Tolvane Energy plc's share price fell by 30% in a week after it lost its largest contract. Analyse the significance of this fall for Tolvane Energy. **[6]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why an investor might buy shares in a plc that does not currently pay a dividend. **[4]**

**11.** A local bus service run by a public sector organisation is sold to a private company. Explain how the change of ownership might change the service's objectives. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether a small, growing business should become a private limited company. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **D** [1]. 5,000,000 × £1.80 = £9,000,000. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A takeover bid is usually made at a premium to the current price, so buyers move in; the other three tend to lower the price. **[1]**

**3.** The money a company raises by selling ordinary shares [1], which gives shareholders part-ownership, usually with voting rights and a right to dividends, and does not have to be repaid [1]. **[2]**

**4.** With limited liability, investors can lose only the amount they put in [1], not their personal possessions [1], so they are more willing to buy shares and the company can raise capital from more people [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any two: a vote at general meetings (for example on directors) [1]; a share of any dividends declared (or a claim on what remains if the company is wound up) [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Rise per share = £0.50 [1]; 12,000,000 × £0.50 = **£6 million** increase [1].

**(b)** The shares are traded between investors on the stock market [1], so the money goes to the selling shareholders, not to Elmhurst; the company gains cash only if it issues new shares [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Benefit 1: limited liability [1]. The kitchen is a large commitment, and if the business ran up debts Jaya would lose only her investment rather than her home or savings [1], which reduces her personal risk as the business grows [1]. Benefit 2: access to finance [1]. Selling shares to her brother and friend raises £100,000 towards the £120,000 kitchen [1], so she needs to borrow only £20,000, keeping interest costs and repayment pressure low; the cost is that she gives up part of the ownership and must share future dividends [1]. **[6]**

**8.** For: selling shares to the public can raise the £30 million needed without heavy borrowing [1], and family members could sell some shares, which they cannot easily do now [1]. Being a plc may also raise the company's profile overseas [1]. Against: the family would lose control as new shareholders gain votes [1], and investors may press for faster profit growth and higher dividends [1], which could conflict with a mission based on long-lasting products that customers replace rarely [1]. Flotation is expensive and the company would be open to takeover [1]. Judgement: Harrowgate should become a plc only if the family cannot raise the money another way, for example through loans or selling shares to chosen investors as a Ltd [1]; if it does float, keeping a majority of shares in family hands would protect the mission [1]. **[9]**

**9.** The fall reduces shareholders' wealth by 30%, so they may lose confidence in the directors and vote against them [1]. A lower market capitalisation makes Tolvane cheaper to take over [1], so a rival could buy it at a modest premium and replace management [1]. It would be harder to raise finance by issuing new shares, because more shares would have to be sold to raise the same amount [1]. Lenders and suppliers may see the fall as a sign of weakness and offer worse terms [1]. However, the fall does not take cash out of the business, so its ability to operate day to day is unchanged unless the lost contract also hits its cash flow [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Reason 1: capital growth [1]. The investor expects the share price to rise as the company reinvests its profits in growth, so they can later sell the shares for more than they paid [1]. Reason 2: future dividends [1]. The company may be keeping profit to fund expansion now and pay larger dividends later, once it is established (also credit: influence over the company through voting) [1]. **[4]**

**11.** In the public sector the main objective is likely to be a service for the community, including routes that do not cover their costs [1], so that people without cars can travel [1]. A private company must make a return for its owners, so profit becomes a key objective [1]; it may cut loss-making routes or raise fares, unless the government pays it to run them or sets conditions in the contract [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: limited liability protects the owner's personal assets as debts grow [1]. Shares can be sold to chosen investors, raising capital without the interest and repayment of loans [1]. A company may appear more established, helping it win larger customers and credit from suppliers [1]. Against: the company must be registered and publish accounts, adding cost and losing privacy [1]. Selling shares means sharing control and profits [1]. Lenders to small companies often ask the owners for personal guarantees, so in practice limited liability may protect them less than it seems [1]. Factors: the size of the risks the business takes [1], how much finance it needs [1], and how much the owner values control and privacy [1]. Judgement: a small business taking on large commitments, such as premises or staff, should usually become a Ltd [1], because the protection and access to share capital outweigh the administrative cost [1]; a small, low-risk business that needs little finance may be better staying a sole trader [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The strongest evaluations name the factor the decision depends on and apply it to the business in the case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Placing a plc in the public sector.
- Assuming a rise in share price gives the company cash.
- Forgetting that ordinary shares carry votes, so selling them affects control.
- Recommending a plc without weighing loss of control and short-term pressure.
- Describing a form of ownership without linking it to objectives.
- Calculating market capitalisation without using the current share price.
