This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.3 Biochemical reactions in cells are controlled by enzymes**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

Enzyme questions reward a particular habit: separate the two reasons why a factor changes the rate. Some factors change how often enzyme and substrate collide. Others change whether the active site can bind the substrate. This guide is organised around that split. Test yourself afterwards with the [enzymes practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-enzymes-control-biochemical-reactions-practice/).

## How enzymes work

Enzymes are **proteins**, and their properties depend on the **tertiary structure** of their active sites. The active site is a small region with a specific three-dimensional shape and charge pattern, formed by the folding of the polypeptide. A substrate with a complementary shape binds there and forms an **enzyme-substrate complex**.

An enzyme is a **catalyst**. It lowers the **activation energy** of the reaction, which is the energy needed to start the reaction. It does this by holding the substrate in a position where bonds are easier to break or form, so that more collisions result in a reaction at a given temperature. The enzyme is not used up and is released unchanged.

Enzymes are **specific**. Only substrates whose shape fits the active site bind, and so one enzyme usually catalyses one reaction.

### Two models of action

- **Lock and key.** The active site has a fixed shape that matches the substrate exactly, like a key in a lock.
- **Induced fit.** The active site is not an exact match at first. When the substrate binds, the enzyme changes shape slightly, moulding the active site around the substrate and putting strain on the bonds. This explains why enzymes can bind related substrates and why binding changes the enzyme.

The specification notes that models of enzyme action have changed over time. A good statement is that the induced fit model replaced the lock and key model because new evidence, for example that enzymes change shape, did not fit the older one. Scientific models change when new evidence is found.

## Collisions: temperature, substrate and enzyme concentration

The rate of reaction is related to the number of collisions between enzyme and substrate.

**Temperature (rising).** Molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster. More collisions happen each second, and more of them have enough energy to react. The rate rises.

**Substrate concentration.** With a fixed amount of enzyme, adding substrate increases the collisions with free active sites, so the rate rises. At high concentrations all active sites are occupied at any moment, and the enzyme concentration becomes the limiting factor. The rate levels off. Adding more substrate does not help.

**Enzyme concentration.** With plenty of substrate, more enzyme means more active sites and more collisions, so the rate is proportional to enzyme concentration until the substrate becomes limiting.

## Binding: temperature, pH and inhibitors

The rate also depends on the ability of the active site to bind complementary substrate molecules.

**High temperature.** Above the optimum, the vibrations break hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that hold the tertiary structure. The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is **denatured**, and this is irreversible. The rate falls steeply, even though collisions are more frequent.

**pH.** A change in pH from the optimum alters the charges on R-groups in and near the active site. Ionic and hydrogen bonds break and the tertiary structure changes. The substrate binds less well and the rate falls. Extreme pH denatures the enzyme.

**Competitive inhibitors.** The inhibitor has a shape similar to the substrate and binds to the active site. The substrate cannot bind while the inhibitor is there. Because the inhibitor and the substrate compete, increasing the substrate concentration reduces the effect of the inhibitor. At high substrate concentration the rate approaches the rate without inhibitor.

**Non-competitive inhibitors.** The inhibitor binds at another site on the enzyme, not the active site. This changes the tertiary structure, so the active site no longer fits the substrate. Because the substrate does not compete for this site, increasing the substrate concentration does not overcome the inhibition. The maximum rate is lower.

| Feature | Competitive | Non-competitive |
|---|---|---|
| Binds to | Active site | Another site |
| Shape similar to substrate | Yes | Not necessarily |
| Effect of more substrate | Reduces the inhibition | Little or no effect |
| Maximum rate | Can reach the same rate | Lower |

## Measuring rate

Rate of reaction can be found from the amount of product formed or substrate used in a given time. Two forms are common:

- **Rate = quantity / time.** For example, 12 cm3 of oxygen in 40 s gives 12 / 40 = 0.30 cm3 per second.
- **Rate = 1 / time** when the time to reach the same end point is measured. A shorter time means a faster rate.

**Initial rate** is the gradient of the progress curve at the start, when the substrate concentration is highest and the least product has built up. A progress curve flattens over time because substrate is used up. Take the gradient of a tangent at time zero, or use the first short interval.

## Required practical 1: planning and evaluating

You must know the procedure, how to control variables, and how to evaluate it. A common design uses catalase, hydrogen peroxide and a gas syringe. To test temperature:

1. Set up water baths at a range of temperatures, for example 10 to 60 degrees Celsius.
2. Bring both the enzyme and the substrate to the temperature before mixing them. If you mix first, the reaction starts at the wrong temperature.
3. Mix, start the timer and measure the gas collected over a fixed time, or the time to a fixed volume.
4. Repeat each temperature and calculate a mean.

**Variables to control:** pH (use a buffer), enzyme concentration, substrate concentration and volume, and the method of measurement. **Hazards:** hydrogen peroxide is an irritant, so use eye protection.

**Evaluation points:** the reading at the start is hard to time as the syringe fills; gas can dissolve or leak; the temperature of the water bath may drift; the intervals between temperatures may be too wide to show the optimum.

## Worked example 1: interpreting temperature data

**Data:** the time for 10 cm3 of gas to be produced was 250 s at 10 degrees Celsius, 125 s at 20, 62 s at 30, 40 s at 40, 100 s at 50 and 500 s at 60.

1. Rate = 10 / time, in cm3 per second. The rates are 0.040, 0.080, 0.161, 0.250, 0.100 and 0.020.
2. The rate roughly doubles for each 10-degree rise from 10 to 30 degrees Celsius, because collisions are more frequent.
3. The maximum rate is at 40 degrees Celsius, so the optimum is close to this value.
4. The rate falls at 50 and 60 because the enzyme is denatured.
5. A conclusion: the optimum lies between 30 and 50 degrees Celsius, and more readings in this range would locate it better.

## Worked example 2: inhibitor data

**Data:** at a substrate concentration of 0.1 unit the rate with no inhibitor was 2.0 and with inhibitor A was 1.0. At 1.6 units the rates were 8.4 and 7.4.

1. Reduction at 0.1: (2.0 - 1.0) / 2.0 x 100 = 50 %.
2. Reduction at 1.6: (8.4 - 7.4) / 8.4 x 100 = 11.9 %.
3. The effect of the inhibitor becomes smaller as the substrate concentration rises, so the inhibitor is competitive.

## Common mistakes

- Saying high temperature "kills" the enzyme. An enzyme is not alive; it is denatured.
- Saying the active site is "denatured". The enzyme is denatured and the active site changes shape.
- Saying a low temperature denatures the enzyme. It slows it, and activity returns on warming.
- Saying a competitive inhibitor "blocks the enzyme" without saying that it binds to the active site.
- Forgetting the pre-incubation step in a temperature practical.
- Writing "the enzyme is used up" in a substrate-limited graph.
- Describing the plateau as "enzyme concentration being limiting" when substrate is the limiting factor at low concentration. State which factor is limiting at each part of the graph.

## Exam technique

- **Two-line explanations.** For each factor, state what happens to the rate and why, using collisions or the active site.
- **Quote values.** Use data from the table or graph, including units.
- **Command words.** "Explain" needs a reason; "suggest" allows reasoning from first principles; "evaluate" asks for strengths and weaknesses.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the rate not keep increasing with temperature?**
Two effects compete. Collisions increase but the enzyme's tertiary structure is damaged. Beyond the optimum, denaturation outweighs the rise in collisions.

**Is denaturation reversible?**
For the temperature changes in this course, it is treated as irreversible. A small pH change away from the optimum may be reversed if the pH returns.
