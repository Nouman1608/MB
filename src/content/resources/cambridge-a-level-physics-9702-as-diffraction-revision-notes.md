## What 8.2 asks you to do

- Explain the meaning of the term diffraction
- Show an understanding of experiments that demonstrate diffraction, including the **qualitative** effect of **gap width relative to wavelength** (for example, water waves in a ripple tank)

No diffraction calculations beyond finding a wavelength with v = fλ are needed for this subtopic.

## Definition

> **Diffraction**: the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around the edge of an obstacle.

| Quantity | After diffraction |
|---|---|
| Wavelength | Unchanged |
| Frequency | Unchanged |
| Speed | Unchanged (same medium) |
| Direction | Spreads into a wider range of directions |
| Amplitude | Smaller, as the energy spreads over a wider region |

## The key rule

- Spreading is **greatest** when the gap width is **similar to the wavelength**.
- Gap **much wider** than λ → little spreading; mostly straight wavefronts with curved ends.
- Gap **narrower** than λ → wide spreading, but little energy passes.
- It is the **ratio λ/gap** that decides the amount of spreading.
- Same gap, longer wavelength → more diffraction.

## Ripple tank experiment

**Apparatus:** straight dipper (plane waves), barriers with an adjustable gap, lamp above, screen below, stroboscope to "freeze" the pattern.

**Procedure and observations:**

1. Set a wide gap: wavefronts pass through nearly straight; shadow regions behind the barriers.
2. Narrow the gap towards λ: wavefronts emerge almost semicircular.
3. Keep the gap fixed and lower the dipper frequency: λ increases, more spreading.
4. Throughout: wavefront spacing equal on both sides of the gap.

**Example.** f = 8.0 Hz, v = 0.24 m s⁻¹ → λ = 0.24 ÷ 8.0 = **3.0 cm**. A 3.0 cm gap gives strong spreading; a 15 cm gap (5λ) gives little.

## Other demonstrations

| Wave | Typical λ | Gap needed for clear diffraction | Everyday consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sound (everyday) | about 0.1 m to a few m | Doorways, gaps between buildings | Heard round corners |
| Microwaves (lab) | a few cm | Gaps of a few cm between metal plates | Receiver detects signal off-axis |
| Visible light | 4–7 × 10⁻⁷ m | Very narrow slits (fraction of a mm) | Sharp shadows from ordinary objects |

**Light through a single narrow slit:** a laser beam spreads into a pattern on a screen; a **narrower** slit gives a **wider** pattern.

## Diffraction vs refraction

| | Diffraction | Refraction |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Gap or edge | Change of speed at a boundary between media |
| Wavelength | Unchanged | Changes |
| Medium | Same throughout | Changes |

## Drawing diffracted wavefronts

- Keep the spacing beyond the gap **equal** to the incident spacing.
- Wide gap (several λ): straight wavefronts the width of the gap, curving only at their ends.
- Gap about λ: wavefronts close to **semicircles** centred on the gap.
- Round a single edge: wavefronts curve into the region behind the edge, with smaller amplitude there.

## Why it matters next

Diffraction at each slit spreads waves so that waves from two slits (or many grating slits) **overlap** and **interfere**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to recall |
|---|---|
| Diffraction | Spreading of a wave through a gap or round an obstacle |
| Gap width | Width of the opening the wave passes through |
| Plane wavefront | Straight wavefront, as from a bar dipper |
| Circular wavefront | Curved wavefront, as from a point source or a narrow gap |
| Stroboscope | Flashing light that makes a periodic wave pattern appear stationary |

## Exam traps

- "Diffraction is bending" without saying it is spreading through a gap: may be read as refraction.
- Wavelength drawn shorter after the gap.
- "A wider gap gives more diffraction" (reversed).
- "Diffraction only happens if the gap equals λ" — it always happens; it is most noticeable then.
- "Light does not diffract" — it does, through very narrow slits.
- Describing an experiment without saying what was changed and what was seen.

## Self-test

1. Explain the meaning of diffraction.
2. What happens to the wavelength of a wave as it diffracts through a gap?
3. When is diffraction most noticeable?
4. In a ripple tank, f = 8.0 Hz and v = 0.24 m s⁻¹. Find λ, and predict the spreading through a 3.0 cm gap.
5. The dipper frequency is increased with the gap unchanged. What happens to the spreading?
6. Why does sound diffract noticeably through a doorway?
7. Why does light not diffract noticeably through a doorway?
8. A laser beam passes through a single slit, which is then made narrower. What happens to the pattern on the screen?
9. Why is the amplitude of a wave smaller after it diffracts through a gap?
10. Give one difference between diffraction and refraction.
11. Microwaves of wavelength 2.8 cm meet a 3.0 cm gap. Is significant diffraction expected?
12. Why is diffraction needed for a two-slit interference pattern?

**Answers:**
1. The spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or round the edge of an obstacle.
2. It is **unchanged**.
3. When the gap width is similar to the wavelength.
4. λ = **3.0 cm**; gap ≈ λ, so strong spreading (near-semicircular wavefronts).
5. λ decreases, so the spreading **decreases**.
6. Its wavelength (around 1 m) is comparable to the width of the doorway.
7. Its wavelength (about 5 × 10⁻⁷ m) is far smaller than the doorway.
8. The pattern becomes **wider**.
9. The same energy spreads over a wider region.
10. Diffraction leaves the wavelength unchanged; refraction changes it (because the speed changes).
11. **Yes**: the gap is similar to the wavelength.
12. It spreads the waves from each slit so that they overlap and can superpose.
