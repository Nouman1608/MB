This guide covers **3.2.2.5 Employer-employee relations** from OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725), Version 1.1, in Unit 2 (International AS). The [Human resources study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-human-resources/) defines the terms; this page traces how a dispute builds and is resolved, costs the effect of industrial action, and shows how to judge which form of involvement suits a business. ## What the specification asks for

**Employee involvement**

- Value of employee involvement in decision making.
- Advantages and disadvantages of **consultation**, **trade unions** and **employee forums**.
- Influences on the extent and methods of employee involvement.

**Employer-employee relations**

- The value of good employer-employee relations.
- Reasons for employer-employee conflict.
- Actions employees might take: **withdraw cooperation, work to rule, leave, strike, overtime ban**.
- How to resolve conflicts: **participation, bargaining, arbitration**.
- How to manage and improve employer-employee relations.

**Managing human resources**

- Impact of developments in technology (for example on recruitment and selection, the nature of work, the skills required, and ways of working such as remote working).
- The value of managing people effectively and its impact on competitiveness.
- The interrelationship between HR decisions and other functions.
- Ethical issues in human resources (for example unsafe working conditions and practices, and low pay).

## Why involve employees?

Employees who do the work often know best where time is wasted or where customers are unhappy. Involving them in decisions:

- brings in practical ideas, improving the quality of decisions;
- makes change easier to accept, because staff understand the reasons;
- meets esteem needs and supports engagement, lowering labour turnover.

The cost is time: involvement slows decisions, and staff become frustrated if their views are asked for and then ignored.

## Three methods compared

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consultation | Managers ask employees' views before deciding | Cheap; flexible; ideas from the front line | Managers still decide; staff may feel ignored |
| Trade unions | An independent organisation represents members and bargains collectively | One channel for many employees; professional negotiators; balances employer power | Can resist change; disputes may escalate to industrial action |
| Employee forums | Elected staff representatives meet managers regularly | Direct, regular two-way communication; covers non-union staff | May lack bargaining power; representatives may not speak for everyone |

**Influences on the extent and method of involvement:** management style (authoritarian or democratic), size of the business, how urgent a decision is, the skills and interest of employees, the law in the country, the culture and history of the industry, and whether a union is already recognised.

## How a dispute builds

**Scenario.** Harrow Bay Foods (invented) packs frozen fish. Managers announce a new rota requiring weekend work, with no extra pay, starting next month. Staff were not consulted.

1. **Cause**: change to working practices, plus poor communication.
2. **Early signs**: absence rises; overtime requests are refused.
3. **Union response**: the union asks for talks; managers refuse to delay the rota.
4. **Industrial action**: the union announces an overtime ban, then a work to rule.

**Common reasons for conflict:** pay and conditions, job security and redundancies, changes to working practices or hours, health and safety, and how change is communicated.

## The actions employees might take

| Action | What happens | Effect on the business |
|---|---|---|
| Withdraw cooperation | Staff stop doing goodwill tasks, such as covering for absent colleagues | Flexibility lost; service suffers |
| Work to rule | Staff follow every rule exactly, slowing work | Output falls without a formal stoppage |
| Overtime ban | Staff refuse to work beyond contracted hours | Peaks in demand cannot be met |
| Strike | Staff stop work entirely | Output halts; orders and customers lost |
| Leave | Staff resign | Turnover rises; skills and experience lost |

### Worked example: the cost of an overtime ban

Harrow Bay normally uses 600 hours of overtime a week, producing 15 packs per hour. Each pack earns a contribution (selling price minus variable cost) of $1.20.

```
Lost output per week       = 600 × 15 = 9,000 packs
Lost contribution per week = 9,000 × 1.20 = $10,800
Over a four-week ban       = 10,800 × 4 = $43,200
```

The firm also saves the overtime wages it would have paid, but there are hidden costs: late orders, penalties from supermarket customers, and damaged trust. A cost estimate like this helps managers decide how much to offer to end a dispute.

## Resolving conflict

- **Participation**: involve employees in finding the solution, for example a joint working group to design the rota. Builds commitment; takes time.
- **Bargaining**: managers and employee representatives negotiate and compromise, for example weekend work in exchange for a pay premium. Usually reaches agreement, but at a cost.
- **Arbitration**: an independent third party hears both sides and recommends or decides a settlement. Used when bargaining fails; both sides lose some control over the outcome.

At Harrow Bay, the managers could suspend the rota, set up a joint working group (participation), and negotiate a weekend premium (bargaining), leaving arbitration as a last resort.

## Managing and improving relations

- Communicate plans early and explain the reasons.
- Consult before decisions that affect staff.
- Set fair pay and clear grievance procedures.
- Train managers to handle concerns quickly.
- Recognise good work and involve staff through forums or quality circles.

**Value of good relations:** fewer disputes, lower labour turnover and absence, easier change, and a better reputation as an employer, which helps recruitment.

## Managing human resources

**Technology.**

- **Recruitment and selection**: online job boards, application tracking software and video interviews widen the pool and cut cost, but may screen out good candidates.
- **Nature of work and skills required**: automation removes routine tasks and raises demand for technical and digital skills, so retraining matters.
- **Ways of working**: remote working cuts office costs and widens recruitment, but makes supervision, teamwork and engagement harder to manage.

**Managing people effectively and competitiveness.** Engaged, skilled and well-managed staff raise productivity, quality and service. These support lower costs or a premium price, so HR affects competitiveness directly.

**Interrelationships.** HR decisions depend on and affect other functions: workforce plans follow marketing's sales forecasts; recruitment and pay depend on the finance budget; training and motivation affect operations' productivity and quality.

**Ethical issues.** Unsafe working conditions and practices, and low pay, may cut costs in the short run but risk accidents, legal action, high turnover and damage to the brand. Treating staff ethically costs money now but protects reputation and retention.

## Common mistakes

- Treating trade unions only as a source of conflict, ignoring their value as a single channel of communication.
- Confusing bargaining (negotiation between the two sides) with arbitration (a third party decides or recommends).
- Describing work to rule as a strike.
- Discussing technology in general without linking it to recruitment, skills or ways of working.
- Ignoring the effect of HR decisions on other functions.

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: name the action or method, show how it works, and give its effect in context.
- **Analyse**: trace a chain, such as no consultation → resentment → work to rule → late orders → lost customers.
- **Evaluate**: weigh the cost of conceding against the cost of the dispute, and say what the best method of resolution depends on (urgency, trust, union strength).
