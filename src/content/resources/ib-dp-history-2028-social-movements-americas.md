**HL only.** This is the eleventh study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. The study has two halves. The first half is the **African American civil rights movement** in the USA. The second half asks about **one other social movement** in the Americas. The guide suggests Hispanic, feminist, Indigenous, youth, counterculture, disability, Afro-Latino and Afro-Caribbean movements, among others. This guide uses the **Chicano movement and the United Farm Workers (UFW)** as its second movement, with notes on other movements for comparison.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: the reasons for the civil rights movement's emergence; its methods; its extent of change; and the same three for a second movement. Use **cause and consequence**, **continuity and change** and **significance**. See the [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/).

## Key chronology

- 1941: Executive Order 8802 bans discrimination in defence industries. 1948: Truman desegregates the armed forces.
- 1954: *Brown v. Board of Education*. 1955 to 1956: Montgomery Bus Boycott.
- 1957: Little Rock. 1960: Greensboro sit-ins. 1961: Freedom Rides.
- 1963: Birmingham; March on Washington. 1964: Civil Rights Act; Freedom Summer.
- 1965: Selma; Voting Rights Act; Malcolm X killed. 1966: Black Panther Party founded.
- 1968: King assassinated; Fair Housing Act.
- 1962: National Farm Workers Association formed. 1965: Delano grape strike. 1970: grape contracts.
- 1968: East Los Angeles school walkouts. 1970: Chicano Moratorium. 1975: California Agricultural Labor Relations Act.

## Emergence of the African American civil rights movement

**Political factors.** Black Americans in the South were excluded from voting by poll taxes, literacy tests and violence. Southern Democrats dominated Congress and blocked federal action. The Second World War and Cold War created pressure: the US claimed to defend freedom but practised segregation. Truman's order in 1948 desegregated the military.

**Social factors, including Jim Crow.** Laws and customs enforced segregation in schools, transport, housing and public places, based on *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) ("separate but equal"). Lynching and other violence enforced the system. The murder of 14-year-old Emmett Till in 1955 shocked the nation.

**Economic factors.** Many Black southerners were poor sharecroppers or low-paid workers. Mechanisation of cotton farming pushed people north in the Great Migration, creating Black communities with greater political freedom and urban organisation.

**Role of ideas.** The Black church was a base of community organisation and moral language. The NAACP's legal strategy, led by Thurgood Marshall, challenged segregation in courts. Gandhi's non-violence influenced leaders such as Bayard Rustin and Martin Luther King Jr. The wartime "Double V" campaign asked for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home.

## Methods used by the civil rights movement

**Non-violent protest and Martin Luther King Jr.** The Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955 to 1956), begun after Rosa Parks's arrest, lasted 381 days and led to desegregated buses. King became a national figure and led the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (1957). Student sit-ins began at Greensboro in 1960 and led to the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC). The Freedom Rides (1961) tested desegregation of interstate buses. In Birmingham (1963), protests faced police violence under Bull Connor, and King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail" defended direct action. The March on Washington (August 1963) drew some 250,000 people. At Selma (1965), the attack on marchers on "Bloody Sunday" built support for voting rights.

**Radical activism: Malcolm X, the Panthers and Black Power.** Malcolm X, a minister of the Nation of Islam, argued for Black pride and self-defence ("by any means necessary") and criticised integration, before he was killed in 1965. Stokely Carmichael called for "Black Power" in 1966. The Black Panther Party, founded in Oakland in 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale, patrolled against police abuse and ran community programmes such as free breakfasts. The FBI's COINTELPRO targeted it. Historians disagree about whether radical groups helped by pressuring the government to negotiate with moderates, or harmed the movement by alarming white opinion.

**Role of women.** Ella Baker, a veteran organiser, helped to found SNCC and promoted "group-centred" leadership by local people rather than a single leader. Fannie Lou Hamer, a sharecropper, spoke for the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party in 1964. Angela Davis became a prominent radical activist and scholar. Women often did the daily organising but received less recognition than male leaders.

**Grassroots organisations.** The NAACP, CORE, SNCC, SCLC and local churches and community groups all contributed. Freedom Summer (1964) brought volunteers to Mississippi to register voters, and three workers were murdered.

## Extent of change achieved

**Supreme Court decisions.** *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) ruled that school segregation was unconstitutional, though implementation was slow, and many southern schools remained segregated for years. In Little Rock in 1957, President Eisenhower sent federal troops to protect the Black students.

**Legislation and presidential support.** Presidents Kennedy and Johnson eventually backed legislation. The **Civil Rights Act (1964)** banned discrimination in public accommodation and employment. The **Voting Rights Act (1965)** ended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to the South. Black voter registration in Mississippi rose from under ten per cent in 1964 to over half by the late 1960s. The Fair Housing Act of 1968 followed.

**Social and cultural change.** Segregation by law ended, and Black Americans entered public life and the arts, but segregation by housing and school districts continued, especially in the North. Riots in the late 1960s (Watts, Detroit) showed frustration. The Black Arts Movement and growing Black pride shaped identity.

**Economic change.** A Black middle class grew, but poverty and unemployment remained higher than for whites, and wealth gaps persisted. King turned to poverty and the Poor People's Campaign before his death in 1968.

## A second movement: the Chicano movement and the UFW

**Reasons for emergence.** *Political*: Mexican Americans faced discrimination, limited political representation and, in some places, segregated schools. *Social*: the American GI Forum and others had fought discrimination since the 1940s, and Mexican American youth faced low school completion rates and police tension. *Economic*: farm workers, many Mexican or Filipino, were poorly paid, excluded from the protections of national labour law and exposed to pesticides. *Role of ideas*: the example of the civil rights movement, Mexican heritage and the idea of Aztlán, and the Catholic social teaching that influenced Chávez.

**Methods.** Non-violent: César Chávez and Dolores Huerta formed the National Farm Workers Association (1962), joined the Delano grape strike begun by Filipino workers in 1965, led a march to Sacramento in 1966, ran a national grape boycott and used fasts. The union became the United Farm Workers. More militant and cultural: Reies López Tijerina fought for land grants in New Mexico (the Tierra Amarilla courthouse raid, 1967), Rodolfo "Corky" Gonzales founded the Crusade for Justice and wrote the poem "I am Joaquín", and the Brown Berets formed in Los Angeles. High-school students staged walkouts in East Los Angeles (1968). The Chicano Moratorium against the Vietnam War (August 1970) ended in violence, and the journalist Rubén Salazar was killed. La Raza Unida Party, founded in Texas in 1970, ran candidates. Women, such as Huerta, played key roles, and Chicana activists also raised sexism within the movement.

**Extent of change.** *Legal*: bilingual education law (1968), the Mexican American Legal Defense and Educational Fund, and California's Agricultural Labor Relations Act (1975), which gave farm workers the right to bargain. *Political*: more Latino officials were elected. *Social and cultural*: Chicano studies programmes, murals and pride in heritage. *Economic*: UFW contracts improved wages and conditions for some, but many farm workers remained poor, and the union weakened in the 1980s. Overall, change was real but partial.

**Other possible movements.** The American Indian Movement (the Alcatraz occupation of 1969 to 1971 and Wounded Knee in 1973), second-wave feminism, and Afro-Latino and Caribbean movements are suitable alternatives.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Was the movement driven by national leaders such as King, or by local grassroots activity (the Ella Baker view)?
- Did radical groups help or harm the cause?
- How far was change real: legal change versus lasting social and economic change?
- Was federal action a response to protest, or did the federal government lead?
- The "long civil rights movement" argument, associated with historians such as Jacquelyn Dowd Hall, suggests a wider time frame and wider issues than 1954 to 1968.

## Common mistakes

- Concentrating only on King.
- Treating Black Power as only violent.
- Not choosing a second movement, or covering several with thin evidence.
- Ignoring women's roles.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the second movement have to be in the USA?**
No. It can be anywhere in the Americas, for example Indigenous movements in Canada or Mexico.
