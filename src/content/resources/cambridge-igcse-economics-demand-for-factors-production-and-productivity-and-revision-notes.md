## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Derived demand | Demand for a factor that comes from demand for the product it helps to make |
| Labour-intensive | Production that uses a lot of labour compared with capital |
| Capital-intensive | Production that uses a lot of machinery and equipment compared with labour |
| Production | Total output of goods and services |
| Productivity | Output per unit of input, such as output per worker |
| Competitive market | A market with a high number of firms selling similar products |
| Monopoly | A market with one firm, or one firm with a very large share, protected by barriers to entry |

## 3.6.1 Demand for factors: four influences

1. Demand for the product. More sales mean more factors needed.
2. Prices of different factors. Firms shift towards the cheaper factor.
3. Availability. A scarce factor is harder and dearer to hire.
4. Productivity. A more productive factor gives more output for its cost.

**Chain for any answer:** change in the product market or the factor market, then change in the factor's cost or usefulness, then how many units of the factor the firm demands.

### Method: choosing between two mixes of factors

1. Cost each mix: workers × wage + machines × machine cost.
2. Choose the cheaper one for the same output.
3. Change one price and cost both again.

Reminder: Mix A is 6 workers and 2 machines. Mix B is 2 workers and 5 machines. A worker costs 50 and a machine 80. A costs 300 + 160 = 460. B costs 100 + 400 = 500. A is cheaper. If the wage rises to 90, A costs 540 + 160 = 700 and B costs 180 + 400 = 580. B is now cheaper.

## 3.6.2 Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production

| | Labour-intensive | Capital-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Typical products | Personal services, craft goods, small or varied orders | Standard goods in large quantities |
| Reasons for choosing | Cheap and plentiful labour; low start-up cost; need for flexibility or a personal touch | Dear labour; large steady demand; need for speed or exact repeatability; finance available |
| Advantages | Flexible; can change task quickly; low set-up cost; creates jobs | Fast; consistent quality; low cost per unit at high output; less affected by wage rises |
| Disadvantages | Costs rise with output; slower; quality varies; absence and skill gaps matter | Expensive to buy and maintain; high fixed costs if sales fall; needs skilled operators; may remove jobs |

**Cost share test:** wages ÷ total cost × 100. A wage bill of 36,000 in total costs of 48,000 is 75%, so the firm is labour-intensive. A share of 15% would point to capital-intensive production.

**Worked reminder:** Method L has a fixed cost of 2,000 and costs 3 per unit. Method C has a fixed cost of 8,000 and costs 1 per unit. Set them equal: 2,000 + 3q = 8,000 + 1q, so 2q = 6,000 and q = 3,000. At 3,000 units both cost 11,000. At 5,000 units L costs 17,000 and C costs 13,000, so C is cheaper above 3,000 units.

**Output rule:** a capital-intensive method has high fixed cost and low cost per extra unit. It wins at high output and loses at low output. A labour-intensive method is the reverse.

## 3.6.3 Production and productivity

| | Production | Productivity |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | Total output | Output per input |
| Formula | Units made | Output ÷ number of workers (or machines) |
| Rises when | More inputs, or better use of inputs | Each input produces more |
| Example | A factory makes 3,600 units | 3,600 ÷ 30 workers = 120 per worker |

If the same factory makes 4,000 units with 25 workers, productivity is 4,000 ÷ 25 = 160 per worker. The change is (160 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = 33.3%.

**Influences on productivity:** training and education; better machinery and technology; better organisation of tasks; motivation, pay and working conditions; health of workers; quality of materials.

**Link to cost:** labour cost per unit = wage per hour ÷ output per hour. Higher output per hour lowers cost per unit unless pay rises by more. That can allow lower prices, higher profit or better pay.

**Warning:** production can rise while productivity falls. Always compute output per worker before you state what happened.

## 3.8.1 Competitive markets (many firms)

| Variable | Effect of a high number of firms |
|---|---|
| Price | Lower: firms compete for customers, and customers can switch |
| Quality | Higher or well maintained, because firms try to attract buyers |
| Choice | Wider: many suppliers and varieties |
| Profit | Lower: competition holds prices down, and high profit draws in new firms |

Evaluation: low prices can push firms to cut corners; small firms may miss economies of scale; weak firms may leave the market. The theory of perfect and imperfect competition and diagrams are not required.

## 3.8.2 Monopoly

**Characteristics:** one firm or a very large market share; barriers to entry (high start-up cost, patents, legal protection, control of resources); few or no close substitutes; price-setting power; potential for long-lasting high profit.

**Market share** = firm sales ÷ market sales × 100. A firm selling 4,200 units in a market of 6,000 has 4,200 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = 70%.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale lower cost per unit | Higher price, lower output |
| Profit can pay for research and development | Less choice; less pressure to improve quality |
| Can compete abroad | Less pressure to cut costs |
| Natural monopoly can be efficient (one network instead of several) | Barriers keep out rivals; may use power over suppliers and workers |

**Evaluation checklist:** are cost savings passed on to buyers? Is the position protected by law or by high start-up cost? Is it regulated? Are there substitutes? Diagrams are not required for this topic.

## Building an answer

- **State** or **define**: one precise sentence, no example needed unless asked.
- **Calculate**: write the formula, substitute, give the answer with units.
- **Explain**: make a point, then link it to a consequence with because or so.
- **Evaluate**: give both sides in context, then a reasoned judgement. Say what the result depends on, such as the size of the firm, the product or the time period.

## Must-know distinctions

- Production is a total; productivity is a ratio.
- Labour-intensive means a large share of labour in costs. It does not mean low output.
- Many firms means competition among sellers. A monopoly can still have lots of buyers.
- A derived demand starts in the product market and moves to the factor market.

## Quick self-test

1. Define derived demand.
2. Demand for a firm's product rises by 40%. Productivity stays the same. The firm employs 20 workers. How many does it need?
3. A firm makes 900 units with 15 workers. Calculate labour productivity.
4. A firm has a wage bill of 36,000 and total costs of 48,000. Is it labour-intensive? Support your answer with a calculation.
5. In the reminder above, which mix is cheaper if the worker costs 90 and the machine 80? State the cost of each.
6. Output per hour rises from 10 units to 12. Calculate the percentage change in productivity.
7. State two influences on productivity.
8. A firm sells 4,200 units in a market of 6,000. Calculate its market share.
9. State two effects of having many firms in a market.
10. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of monopoly.

### Answers

1. Demand for a factor that comes from the demand for the product it makes.
2. 20 × 1.40 = 28 workers.
3. 900 ÷ 15 = 60 units per worker.
4. 36,000 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = 75%, so wages are most of costs and it is labour-intensive.
5. Mix A costs 6 × 90 + 2 × 80 = 700. Mix B costs 2 × 90 + 5 × 80 = 580. B is cheaper.
6. (12 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = 20%.
7. Any two of: training, technology and machinery, organisation, motivation, health, quality of materials.
8. 4,200 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = 70%.
9. Any two of: lower prices, better quality, wider choice, lower profit.
10. Advantage: economies of scale. Disadvantage: higher prices or less choice.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the definition of productivity as "how much a firm makes". Say output per worker or per input.
- Stating a result without calculating it. Show the working.
- Mixing up percentage change in output and percentage change in productivity.
- Explaining demand for labour without linking it to demand for the product.
- Writing that labour-intensive firms always have lower costs. Say when this holds.
- Listing monopoly effects with no development, such as "high price" and nothing more.
- Giving only the disadvantages of monopoly in a question that asks for both sides.
- Writing "more competition means more profit". The effect is the opposite.
- Skipping a judgement in an evaluation question. State which side is stronger and why.
