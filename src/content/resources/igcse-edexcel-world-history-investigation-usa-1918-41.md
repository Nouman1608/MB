Historical Investigation A3, "The USA, 1918–41", is one of five investigation options in Section A
of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1) Paper 2. It covers the economic boom and
"Roaring Twenties", the social tensions of the 1920s, the Wall Street Crash and the Great
Depression, and Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal and the opposition it faced. The exam is
source-based: you describe features, cross-reference two sources and evaluate an interpretation.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics for A3:

1. **The Roaring Twenties** — the economic benefits of the First World War, reasons for the boom, Henry
   Ford and mass production, hire purchase, advertising, consumerism and the stock market; problems in
   farming and older industries; leisure (cinema, jazz, dancing, sport, radio, motoring); the changing
   position of women and the flappers.
2. **Increased social tensions in the 1920s** — immigration, the Palmer Raids and the Red Scare, Sacco
   and Vanzetti, attitudes to black Americans, the Ku Klux Klan, the "Monkey Trial", Prohibition and
   the gangsters.
3. **The USA in Depression, 1929–33** — causes and consequences of the Wall Street Crash, Hoover's
   response, and the impact on banking, farming, industry and people: Hoovervilles, the Bonus Marchers,
   unemployment and homelessness.
4. **Roosevelt and the New Deal, 1933–41** — aims, the Hundred Days and Alphabet Agencies including the
   TVA, the Second New Deal (WPA, welfare), the Social Security Act, the Wagner Act and the Banking Act
   of 1935, rural electrification, and the New Deal's achievements and shortcomings.
5. **Opposition to the New Deal** — the Supreme Court, Republicans, business and the Liberty League,
   Huey Long's Share Our Wealth and Father Coughlin's Social Justice campaign.

## Why did the US economy boom in the 1920s?

The **First World War** left the USA the world's leading creditor: it had supplied the Allies and taken
over markets. Republican governments under Harding and Coolidge followed **laissez-faire** policies,
cut taxes and raised tariffs to protect industry. **Henry Ford**'s moving assembly line made the Model
T cheap enough for ordinary families, and the car industry stimulated steel, rubber, glass, oil and
road building. **Hire purchase** let people buy now and pay later, **advertising** created demand for
new electrical goods, and rising share prices drew ordinary investors into the **stock market**.

Not everyone shared the boom. **Farmers** faced over-production and falling prices after European
demand fell; mechanisation increased output and debts. **Older industries** such as coal and textiles
declined. Wealth was very unequally distributed.

Leisure changed: **cinema** (the first major "talkie", *The Jazz Singer*, came in 1927), **jazz** and
dances such as the Charleston, **radio**, spectator **sport** and **motoring**. **Women** gained the vote in
1920; more worked in offices and shops, and the **flappers** challenged older ideas of behaviour,
though most women's lives changed less than the image suggests.

## Why were the 1920s a time of social tension?

Fear of foreigners and radicals produced quotas on **immigration** (1921 and 1924), which favoured
northern Europeans. After bombings in 1919 the **Red Scare** led to the **Palmer Raids** (1919–20) on
suspected radicals. Two Italian anarchists, **Sacco and Vanzetti**, were convicted of murder on weak
evidence and executed in 1927. **Black Americans** faced segregation in the South and discrimination
in Northern cities. The **Ku Klux Klan** revived, claiming millions of members by the mid-1920s, before
scandal weakened it. The **"Monkey Trial"** (1925) in Tennessee, where teacher John Scopes was prosecuted
for teaching evolution, showed the clash between rural fundamentalism and modern ideas. **Prohibition**
(from 1920) banned alcohol but led to speakeasies, bootlegging and organised crime; gangsters such
as Al Capone grew rich, and the law was repealed in 1933.

## What caused the Wall Street Crash and the Depression?

Share prices had been pushed far above real values by **speculation** and buying "on the margin" with
borrowed money. In **October 1929** confidence collapsed (Black Thursday, 24 October; Black Tuesday,
29 October). Underlying causes were **over-production**, unequal wealth that limited demand, weak and
unregulated **banks**, and falling exports as tariffs choked trade.

The **consequences** were devastating. Thousands of banks failed and savers lost everything; firms cut
production and laid off workers; farm prices collapsed. By 1933 roughly a quarter of the workforce was
unemployed. Homeless families lived in shanty towns called **Hoovervilles**. In 1932 the **Bonus
Marchers**, unemployed ex-servicemen demanding early payment of a promised bonus, were driven out of
Washington by troops.

**Hoover** believed in **voluntarism** — that business and charities, not government, should provide
relief — and that recovery was "around the corner". He did intervene more than any earlier president,
for example through the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (1932), but his response looked
inadequate and he lost the 1932 election to Roosevelt.

## What did the New Deal do?

Roosevelt aimed at **relief, recovery and reform**. In the **Hundred Days** (1933) he closed the banks
and reopened sound ones (Emergency Banking Act), explained his policies in radio "fireside chats" and
created **Alphabet Agencies**: the CCC (work for young men), the AAA (paying farmers to reduce output),
the NRA (codes on wages and prices), the PWA (public works), and the **TVA**, which built dams to
control floods and generate electricity in a poor region.

The **Second New Deal** (from 1935) went further: the **Works Progress Administration** employed millions,
the **Social Security Act** created pensions and unemployment insurance, the **Wagner Act** protected
workers' right to join unions, the **Banking Act of 1935** strengthened the Federal Reserve, and the
Rural Electrification Administration brought power to farms.

**Achievements:** restored confidence in banks, gave work and relief to millions, built lasting
infrastructure and established federal welfare. **Shortcomings:** unemployment remained high,
recovery stalled in the 1937–38 recession, black Americans and women benefited less, and full
recovery came only with rearmament for war.

## Who opposed the New Deal?

The **Supreme Court** struck down the NRA (1935) and the AAA (1936); Roosevelt's plan to add judges (1937)
failed. **Republicans** and **business** interests, including the **American Liberty League**, saw the New
Deal as socialism and costly interference. From the other side, **Huey Long**'s **Share Our Wealth**
programme demanded the redistribution of large fortunes, and **Father Coughlin** attacked Roosevelt in
radio broadcasts through his **Social Justice** campaign.

## Frequently asked questions

**Were the 1920s "roaring" for everyone?**
No — farmers, black Americans, workers in old industries and
many immigrants missed out; this makes a good interpretation question.

**Did the New Deal end the Depression?**
It brought relief and reform, but unemployment stayed high
until wartime spending.

**Why did Hoover lose in 1932?**
His belief in limited government looked inadequate, and the Bonus
Army episode damaged him badly.
