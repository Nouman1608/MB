These questions test the section 3.2 row on non-fatal offences against the person. In Section B, classify the injury first and test offences from the most serious down.

---

## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between an assault and a battery. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by "immediate" in the actus reus of assault, using **one** case. **[2]**

**3.** Define actual bodily harm, giving an authority. **[2]**

**4.** State the mens rea of s47 Offences Against the Person Act 1861, giving an authority. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the difference between a wound and grievous bodily harm. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the difference between the mens rea of s20 and s18. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Bilal stands outside Cara's flat every night for a week, staring through her window. She is terrified he will break in. Explain whether Bilal has committed an assault. **[4]**

**8.** During an argument, Dev throws a glass of water at Ellie. The glass slips from his hand and hits her face, bruising her cheek. Advise Dev on his liability for s47. **[5]**

**9.** Fay, angry with her elderly neighbour Gus, shoves him. He falls and breaks his hip, which in a fit young adult might have been a lesser injury. Advise on Fay's liability for s20. **[6]**

**10.** Hal sends Ivy months of threatening messages. She develops a severe anxiety disorder diagnosed by a psychiatrist. Explain which non-fatal offence Hal may have committed. **[5]**

**11.** Jack is stopped by a police officer who lawfully tries to arrest him. Jack slashes the officer's arm with a knife to get away, causing a deep cut. Advise on Jack's liability under s18. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the law on non-fatal offences against the person. **[12]**

---

## Answers

**1.** An assault is causing the victim to apprehend immediate unlawful force [1]; a battery is the actual application of unlawful force, which need not cause injury [1].

**2.** "Immediate" means imminent, not instantaneous, so it is enough that the victim fears force in the near future [1], as in *Smith v Chief Superintendent of Woking Police Station* (1983), where the victim feared what a man looking through her window at night might do next [1].

**3.** Any hurt or injury calculated to interfere with the health or comfort of the victim, more than transient or trifling [1] (*R v Miller*, 1954) [1].

**4.** Only the mens rea of the assault or battery, meaning intention or recklessness as to causing apprehension of force or applying force; no foresight of harm is required [1] (*R v Roberts*, 1971; *R v Savage*, 1991) [1].

**5.** A wound is a break in both layers of the skin (*JCC v Eisenhower*, 1984) [1], which may be a minor injury [1]. Grievous bodily harm means really serious harm (*DPP v Smith*, 1961), which need not involve a wound [1].

**6.** s20 requires intention or subjective recklessness [1] as to causing some harm, not necessarily serious harm (*R v Mowatt*, 1968) [1]. s18 requires a specific intention to cause GBH, or to resist or prevent lawful arrest [1]; recklessness as to GBH is not enough (*R v Belfon*, 1976) [1].

**7.** Assault requires the victim to apprehend immediate unlawful force [1]. Cara is terrified he will break in, so she apprehends force [1]. "Immediate" means imminent, and in *Smith v Woking* (1983) looking through a window at night was enough because the victim feared what he would do next [1]. Bilal intends or at least foresees that she will apprehend force, so he is likely to be guilty of assault [1].

**8.** s47 requires an assault or battery occasioning ABH [1]. Being hit by the glass is a battery, as force was applied, even indirectly [1]. Bruising is ABH, as it interferes with health or comfort (*R v Miller*, 1954) [1]. Dev intended to throw water, so he was at least reckless as to applying force to Ellie, which is the mens rea of battery [1]. He need not foresee any harm (*R v Savage*, 1991), so he is likely to be guilty under s47 [1].

**9.** A broken hip in an elderly victim is likely to be really serious harm [1], and seriousness is judged against the actual victim, taking his age and health into account (*R v Bollom*, 2003) [1]. Fay inflicted it; "inflict" does not need a direct application of force (*R v Burstow*, 1997), and here the shove caused the fall [1]. Mens rea: she must intend or foresee some harm (*R v Mowatt*, 1968) [1]; shoving an elderly man suggests she foresaw at least some harm, which the jury could find [1]. She is likely to be guilty under s20; s18 fails as there is no evidence of intention to cause GBH [1].

**10.** A diagnosed severe anxiety disorder is a recognised psychiatric illness [1]. If it is serious enough, it can be GBH (*R v Burstow*, 1997) [1], inflicted without any application of force, so s20 is possible if Hal foresaw some harm [1]. If the illness is less serious, it can still be ABH (*R v Chan-Fook*, 1994) [1]; the threatening messages could amount to an assault (*R v Ireland*; *R v Constanza*, 1997), giving s47 as the alternative [1].

**11.** A deep cut breaking both layers of skin is a wound (*JCC v Eisenhower*, 1984) [1]. s18 can be committed with intent to resist or prevent lawful arrest [1]. The arrest was lawful and Jack slashed the officer to get away, showing an intent to resist arrest [1]. For this form of s18, he must also be at least reckless as to causing some harm (*R v Morrison*, 1989) [1]; slashing with a knife clearly shows this [1]. Jack is likely to be guilty under s18, which carries a maximum of life [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Problems:** the 1861 Act uses outdated language such as "maliciously" and "grievous" [1], and inconsistently uses "inflict" in s20 and "cause" in s18 [1]. It breaches correspondence: s47 needs no foresight of harm (*R v Savage*, 1991) [1], and s20 needs only foresight of some harm (*R v Mowatt*, 1968) [1]. s47 and s20 have the same maximum sentence despite the difference in harm, raising fair labelling concerns [1]. The Act did not contemplate psychiatric injury, so courts had to stretch it in *R v Ireland* and *R v Burstow* (1997) [1]. **In its favour:** judges have adapted the Act to modern harms, such as HIV in *R v Dica* (2004) [1]; constructive liability can be justified because a person who chooses violence accepts the risk of worse harm [1]; s18 complies with correspondence [1]. **Reform:** the Law Commission's 2015 report recommended replacing the Act with offences that match fault to harm [1], but it has not been enacted [1]. A reasoned conclusion [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not classifying the injury before choosing the offence.
- Requiring foresight of harm for s47 or of serious harm for s20.
- Forgetting the victim's age and health in judging GBH.
- Missing the resisting-arrest form of s18.
