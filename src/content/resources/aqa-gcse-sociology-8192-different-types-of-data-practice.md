These questions cover the "different types of data" row of section 3.7 in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification: the usefulness of qualitative and quantitative data and of official and non-official statistics. Methods can be assessed in Paper 1 or Paper 2. All organisations, places and figures are fictional.

Revise first with the [different types of data study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-different-types-of-data/) and the [different types of data revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-different-types-of-data-revision-notes/). For questions on all of section 3.7, use the [research methods practice set](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods-practice/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of the following is an example of qualitative data?

A. The number of school exclusions in a year
B. A table of divorce rates
C. A diary kept by a young carer
D. The percentage of pupils gaining a grade 4 in English

**[1]**

**2.** Crime that is not reported to or recorded by the police is called:

A. The dark figure of crime
B. Selective use of data
C. Recorded crime
D. Triangulation

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "official statistics". **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** examples of organisations that produce non-official statistics. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** strength of quantitative data for studying changes in family size. **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** reason why some official statistics are seen as social constructs. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Study the item. The data are fictional.

> **Kelmouth (a fictional county): two measures of burglary, 2025**
> Police recorded burglaries: 1,800
> Estimated burglaries from a large survey of households asking whether they had been burgled: 3,000

**(a)** Calculate the number of burglaries not included in police recorded figures, according to the survey estimate. **[1]**
**(b)** What percentage of the estimated burglaries were recorded by the police? **[1]**
**(c)** Explain **one** reason why some burglaries do not appear in police figures. **[2]**

**8.** Read the item.

> "Screen Time Matters", a fictional pressure group calling for a ban on smartphones in schools, surveyed 500 parents who signed up to its mailing list. It reports that 82% "worry about the effect of phones on their child's learning".

Using the item, explain **two** reasons why a sociologist should be cautious about using this survey. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **one** strength and **one** limitation of qualitative data for studying experiences of bullying in schools. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why a change in the way a government defines unemployment could make official statistics less useful for showing trends. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of official statistics for sociologists studying crime. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far quantitative data are more useful than qualitative data for studying educational achievement. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** C. A diary kept by a young carer [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* every other option is numerical; personal documents are a classic source of qualitative data.

**2.** A. The dark figure of crime [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "dark figure" is in the specification's key terms; use it in every answer on crime statistics.

**3.** Numerical data produced by government and its agencies [1], such as the census, exam results or recorded crime figures [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the producer is the key part of the definition; an example secures the second mark.

**4.** One organisation correctly named, such as a university research team or a charity [1], and a second, such as a pressure group, polling company or business [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* government departments and the ONS produce official, not non-official, statistics.

**5.** Quantitative data such as the average number of children per family [1] can be collected in the same way each year [1], so changes over time can be compared and a trend, such as smaller families, can be shown clearly [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the strength must be tied to studying change; reliability and comparability are the concepts to name.

**6.** Some official statistics depend on decisions made by people rather than simply counting events [1]. For example, a crime only appears in recorded figures if a victim reports it and the police decide to record it [1]. This means the figures partly reflect the behaviour of victims, police and officials, so interactionists see them as socially constructed [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* exclusions (decided by head teachers) are an equally good example.

**7. (a)** 3,000 − 1,800 = 1,200 burglaries [1]
**(b)** 1,800 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = 60% [1]
**(c)** Victims may not report a burglary [1] because nothing valuable was taken, they lack insurance, or they believe the police could do nothing, so it never enters recorded figures [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* a police decision not to record a reported crime is also creditworthy for (c).

**8.** First, the sample was drawn from the group's own mailing list [1], so these parents are likely to share the group's concerns and are not representative of all parents [1]. Second, the producer has an interest in the result because it is campaigning for a ban [1], so the question wording or the way findings are reported may be biased towards showing worry [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each reason should name a detail from the item: the mailing list and the campaign aim.

**9.** Strength: interviews or diaries let pupils describe bullying in their own words [1], revealing how it feels and why it happens, which gives valid, detailed data [1]. Limitation: qualitative studies usually involve small samples [1], so it is hard to know how common the experiences described are across all schools [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* bullying is sensitive, so you can also mention that rapport helps victims speak.

**10.** Official statistics are only as consistent as the definitions behind them [1]. If the government changes who counts as unemployed, for example by excluding people on training schemes [1], the figures before and after the change are no longer measuring the same thing [1]. A fall could then reflect the new definition rather than a real change in the number of people without work, so the trend may mislead [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the final step, "the trend may mislead", is where the explanation mark is earned.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Official crime statistics include police recorded crime and court and prison figures [1].
- They are collected regularly, so trends over time can be tracked [1].
- They cover the whole country and are free and quick to use [1].
- They allow comparison between areas, offences and groups [1].
- Victim surveys such as the Crime Survey for England and Wales add estimates of unreported crime [1].
- Against: the dark figure of crime means many offences never appear [1].
- Victims may not report crimes, for example domestic violence or minor theft [1].
- Police decisions about what to record shape the figures [1].
- Interactionists see crime statistics as social constructs reflecting policing and labelling [1].
- Changes in counting rules or definitions can create false trends [1].
- Statistics show how many crimes are recorded but not why people offend [1].
- Judgement: they are useful for showing broad patterns and trends, especially when combined with victim surveys, but must be treated with caution and supported by qualitative research to explain crime [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* use both police records and victim surveys; showing how they differ is strong evaluation.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Quantitative data, such as exam results, show patterns by class, gender and ethnicity [1].
- Official exam statistics are national, regular and comparable over time [1].
- Halsey, Heath and Ridge's large sample showed class inequalities in staying on and university entry [1].
- Numbers allow comparison of schools and groups, and show trends such as girls outperforming boys [1].
- Quantitative data are reliable because collected in standard ways [1].
- But numbers do not explain why differences exist [1].
- Qualitative data, such as Ball's participant observation, revealed how banding and teacher expectations shape achievement [1].
- Willis's methods showed how the lads' counter culture led to rejecting school [1].
- Qualitative data have high validity but small samples, so may not be representative [1].
- Exam statistics depend on definitions and grading systems that can change [1].
- Mixed methods can combine patterns with explanations [1].
- Judgement: quantitative data are more useful for measuring how achievement differs, but qualitative data are more useful for explaining why, so neither is simply more useful [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* bring in named education studies to show you can apply methods to a substantive topic.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling official statistics "always true" or "useless".
- Saying a source is biased without naming who and why.
- Ignoring item details such as who was surveyed.
- Forgetting the dark figure of crime in crime statistics answers.
- Stating "useful" with no purpose.
