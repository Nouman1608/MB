## Question 1 — the origins of the Cold War, 1943–49

**Extract A** (invented for this exercise).

> "At Yalta the Big Three still spoke as allies; at Potsdam, five months later, they already
> listened as rivals. Little had changed in Europe. A great deal had changed at the table."

**1(a)** What impression does the author give about relations between the Allies in 1945? You must
use Extract A to explain your answer. **[6]**

**1(b)** Explain **two** effects of the Marshall Plan. **[8]**

**1(c)** "Soviet expansion in Eastern Europe was the main reason for the Cold War in the years
1945–49." How far do you agree? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- Stalin's policy in Poland and Czechoslovakia
- the Truman Doctrine

You **must** also use information of your own.

## Question 2 — the 1950s and the three crises

**Extract B** (invented for this exercise).

> "The Wall was an admission of failure dressed up as a triumph. A state that had to imprison its
> own citizens to keep them had lost the argument with the West."

**2(a)** What impression does the author give about the building of the Berlin Wall? You must use
Extract B to explain your answer. **[6]**

**2(b)** Explain **two** effects of the Soviet invasion of Hungary (1956). **[8]**

**2(c)** "The Cuban Missile Crisis was the most dangerous moment in superpower relations in the years
1956–68." How far do you agree? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- the U2 incident
- the invasion of Czechoslovakia (1968)

You **must** also use information of your own.

## Question 3 — thaw and détente, 1963–72

**3(b)** Explain **two** effects of the Cuban Missile Crisis on superpower relations. **[8]**

**3(c)** "By 1972 détente had transformed relations between the superpowers." How far do you agree?
**[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- the Test Ban Treaty (1963)
- the SALT talks

You **must** also use information of your own.

## Answers and mark guidance

### 1(a) [6]

The author suggests relations **deteriorated quickly in 1945** and that the change was in attitudes
rather than events. The contrast between "spoke as allies" and "listened as rivals" shows a shift
from cooperation to suspicion; "five months later" stresses speed; "a great deal had changed at the
table" hints at new leaders (Truman, Attlee) and the atomic bomb.

### 1(b) [8]

- **Western European recovery:** billions of dollars of aid rebuilt economies and reduced the appeal
  of communism in countries such as France and Italy.
- **Deeper division of Europe:** Stalin rejected the aid for the Eastern bloc and set up Cominform
  (1947) and Comecon (1949), so Europe split into two economic blocs.

### 1(c) [16]

**Agree:** communist takeovers through salami tactics; Poland's government under Soviet control
despite Yalta; the Czech coup (1948); the Berlin Blockade.

**Other factors:** ideological rivalry dating back to 1917; Truman's tougher attitude and the
atomic bomb; the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan, which Stalin saw as aggressive; disputes over
Germany (Bizonia, the new currency).

**Judgement:** Soviet actions created Western fears, but American policy turned rivalry into a
lasting division; strong answers show how each side reacted to the other.

### 2(a) [6]

The author presents the Wall as a **sign of weakness disguised as strength**. "Admission of failure
dressed up as a triumph" points to the gap between GDR propaganda and reality; "imprison its own
citizens" describes the Wall's purpose of stopping emigration; "lost the argument" suggests
communism had lost the contest of systems.

### 2(b) [8]

- **Soviet control reinforced:** Nagy was removed (and later executed) and Kádár installed, showing
  that no satellite would be allowed to leave the Warsaw Pact.
- **Western limits exposed:** the USA and UN condemned the invasion but did not intervene, showing
  that the West accepted the Soviet sphere in practice and that containment did not mean
  liberation.

### 2(c) [16]

**Agree:** nuclear missiles ninety miles from the USA; the US quarantine and DEFCON alert; the U2
shot down over Cuba; leaders believed war was possible within days.

**Other moments:** the Berlin Wall standoff (1961), when US and Soviet tanks faced each other at
Checkpoint Charlie; the U2 incident (1960), which wrecked the Paris Summit; Hungary (1956) and
Czechoslovakia (1968), which were serious but stayed within the Soviet sphere.

**Judgement:** Cuba was the most dangerous because it involved direct nuclear confrontation; the
others raised tension without the same immediate risk.

### 3(b) [8]

- **Direct communication:** the Hotline (1963) was set up so leaders could talk directly in a crisis.
- **Arms control:** the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963) began a series of agreements leading to the
  NPT (1968) and SALT.

### 3(c) [16]

**Agree:** a chain of treaties — Test Ban, Outer Space, NPT, SALT I and the ABM Treaty; Nixon's visit
to Moscow in 1972; reduced risk of direct confrontation.

**Disagree:** rivalry continued in Vietnam; the invasion of Czechoslovakia (1968) and the Brezhnev
Doctrine; SALT I froze rather than reduced arsenals; both sides continued developing new weapons.

**Judgement:** détente changed the way the superpowers managed their rivalry, not the rivalry
itself.

## Where marks are usually lost

- In (a), describing the event rather than the author's impression of it.
- In (b), giving one effect in two ways.
- In (c), narrating crises in order without comparing them.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is each Paper 1 depth study question structured?**
Each 4HI1 Paper 1 question has three parts: (a) what impression an extract gives (6 marks), (b) explain two effects (8 marks) and (c) a 'how far do you agree' essay (16 marks), with bullet prompts and your own knowledge.

**Which crises are covered?**
The origins of the Cold War 1943–49, Berlin, Hungary and Cuba, and the thaw and détente to 1972.

**What does part (a) reward?**
Explaining the author's impression from the extract's language, not describing the event itself.
