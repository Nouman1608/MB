## 4.8.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inflation | A sustained increase in the general price level |
| Deflation | A sustained decrease in the general price level |
| Rate of inflation | How fast the price level is changing, as a percentage per year |
| Purchasing power | What a unit of money can buy; it falls in inflation and rises in deflation |
| Nominal value | Measured in money at the prices of the time |
| Real value | Nominal value adjusted for price changes |

Must-know distinction: **falling inflation is not deflation.** If the rate drops from 5% to 2%, prices are still rising. Deflation means the rate is below zero.

## 4.8.2 Measuring with the Consumer Prices Index (CPI)

The CPI follows the cost of a basket of goods and services that typical households buy. Each category carries a weight for its place in the household budget. The base year is 100.

| Formula | Use |
|---|---|
| Price relative = new price / base price x 100 | Index for one item |
| CPI = sum of (weight x category index) / total of weights | Weighted average |
| Inflation rate = (new CPI - old CPI) / old CPI x 100 | Percentage change |
| New value = old value x (1 + rate/100) | Applying a rate |

Method in steps:
1. Write each category index and its weight.
2. Multiply each pair, then add.
3. Divide by the total of the weights (100 if they are percentages).
4. Compare two years with the inflation formula and keep the sign.

Worked reminder: a household has three categories (illustrative) with weights 40, 35 and 25 and indices 110, 120 and 90. Weighted sum: 44 + 42 + 22.5 = **108.5**. A price that was 5.50 and is now 6.60 has an index of 6.60 / 5.50 x 100 = **120**.

Worked reminder: the CPI moves from 104 to 110.24. Rate = 6.24 / 104 x 100 = **6.0%**.

Worked reminder: the CPI moves from 120 to 115.2. Rate = -4.8 / 120 x 100 = **-4.0%**, which is deflation.

Limits of the CPI: it is an average; weights go out of date; quality changes and new goods are hard to include; different households have different baskets.

## 4.8.3 Causes

| Type | What changes | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| Demand-pull inflation | Total demand rises | More consumer spending, investment, government spending or export demand; faster growth of the money supply; economy near full capacity |
| Cost-push inflation | Total supply falls (costs rise) | Higher wages without higher productivity; dearer raw materials or energy; weaker exchange rate; higher indirect taxes |
| Demand-side deflation | Total demand falls | Weak confidence; high interest rates; lower exports; cuts in government spending |
| Supply-side deflation | Total supply rises (costs fall) | New technology; higher productivity; cheaper inputs or imports |

Chains of reasoning to practise:
- Demand-pull: more spending, so excess demand at capacity, so firms raise prices, so the price level rises.
- Cost-push: higher wages or fuel costs, so unit costs rise, so firms pass on the rise, so the price level rises.
- Demand-side deflation: lower confidence, so households save, so demand falls, so firms cut prices and output.
- Supply-side deflation: better technology, so unit costs fall, so firms cut prices and output rises.

Cost share calculation: if wages are 50% of costs and rise by 8%, total costs rise by 50% x 8% = **4%**.

### Effects on prices and output (explain in words)

The syllabus says aggregate demand and aggregate supply are not required (in its note on fiscal policy), so explain each cause by saying whether total demand or total supply changes.

| Event | What changes | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Demand-pull inflation | Total demand rises | Higher price level, higher output (little output rise near capacity) |
| Cost-push inflation | Total supply falls | Higher price level, lower output |
| Demand-side deflation | Total demand falls | Lower price level, lower output |
| Supply-side deflation | Total supply rises | Lower price level, higher output |

State the cause, the change in total demand or supply, then the effect on prices and on output.

### Must-know distinctions

- Demand-pull comes from a rise in total demand; cost-push comes from a fall in total supply.
- Inflation (prices rising) is not the same as a high price level. A country can have a high price level and low inflation.
- Nominal is in money terms; real allows for price changes.
- Demand-side deflation lowers output and jobs; supply-side deflation raises output.
- Fiscal policy uses taxes and government spending; monetary policy uses interest rates and credit.

## 4.8.4 Consequences

| Group | Inflation | Deflation |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Purchasing power falls, especially with fixed incomes | Money buys more, but spending may be delayed |
| Workers | Real wage falls if pay rises more slowly than prices | Wage cuts or job losses possible |
| Savers | Lose if interest rate is below inflation | Gain in real terms |
| Lenders | Lose on fixed-rate loans | Gain |
| Borrowers | Gain: the real debt shrinks | Lose: the real debt grows |
| Firms | Higher costs, uncertainty, loss of competitiveness | Lower revenue and profit, harder debt repayment, less investment |
| Economy | Weaker exports, possible wage-price spiral | Falling demand, output and jobs; lower tax revenue |

Real calculations:
- Real wage change is approximately the nominal pay rise minus inflation: 6% pay rise, 2.5% inflation, so **+3.5%**.
- Real interest rate is approximately the nominal interest rate minus inflation: 2% interest, 4.5% inflation, so **-2.5%**.
- Applying inflation to a price: 80 rising by 12.5% gives 80 x 1.125 = **90**.
- Compounding: inflation of 3.5% for two years takes an index of 100 to 100 x 1.035 x 1.035 = **107.1** (to 1 decimal place).

Mild, steady inflation can be consistent with growth. Fast or unpredictable inflation and any deflation tied to falling demand are more damaging.

## 4.8.5 Policies and effectiveness

| Policy | Against inflation | Against deflation | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fiscal | Raise taxes, cut government spending | Cut taxes, raise government spending | Slow to organise; unpopular; may raise debt |
| Monetary | Raise interest rates, restrict credit | Cut interest rates, expand credit | Time lags; weak if confidence is low |
| Supply-side | Improve education, training, infrastructure, competition | Rarely needed for supply-side deflation | Takes years; costly |
| Exchange rate | Stronger currency lowers import prices | Weaker currency raises import prices | Hurts exporters or importers |
| Direct controls | Limits on wages or prices | Not usual | Shortages; hard to enforce |

Evaluation checklist: (1) policy matches the cause; (2) time lags; (3) cost to growth and jobs; (4) confidence and expectations; (5) the economy's size and openness; (6) a final judgement with a reason.

## Quick self-test

1. Define deflation.
2. The CPI rises from 250 to 262.5. Calculate the rate of inflation.
3. Explain why a fall in the rate of inflation from 4% to 1% is not deflation.
4. State two possible causes of cost-push inflation.
5. State one cause of supply-side deflation and say what happens to output.
6. A worker's pay rises 6% while inflation is 2.5%. Approximate the change in the real wage.
7. A saver earns 2% when inflation is 4.5%. Approximate the real interest rate.
8. Explain why a lender with a fixed-rate loan loses in inflation.
9. Give one reason why raising interest rates might fail to control cost-push inflation.
10. Name one fiscal policy to deal with deflation and one drawback.

### Answers

1. A continuing decrease in the general price level.
2. (262.5 - 250) / 250 x 100 = **5.0%**.
3. Prices are still rising, only more slowly. Deflation needs a negative rate.
4. Any two of: higher wages not matched by productivity, dearer raw materials or energy, a weaker currency, higher indirect taxes.
5. For example new technology lowers costs. Output rises while prices fall.
6. About 6 - 2.5 = **+3.5%**.
7. About 2 - 4.5 = **-2.5%**.
8. Repayments are made in money that buys less than when it was lent.
9. Higher rates reduce spending, yet the source of cost-push inflation is on the supply side. Output and employment may fall while prices keep rising.
10. Cut taxes or raise government spending; the drawback is higher government borrowing (or a time lag).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "prices rise" without "general" and "sustained" in a definition.
- Treating lower inflation as deflation.
- Forgetting to divide by the old value, not the new one, in a percentage change.
- Dropping the negative sign on a deflation rate or a negative real interest rate.
- Giving a list of effects without saying who gains and who loses.
- Giving "raise interest rates" with no chain of reasoning to prices.
- Using demand-side policies for cost-push inflation without noting the limit.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement.
