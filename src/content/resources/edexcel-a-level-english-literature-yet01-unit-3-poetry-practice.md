## The poems

> **Inheritance** *(original)*
>
> My grandmother's sewing box  
> still smells of camphor and decisions.  
> Needles in a felt heart, threaded  
> with colours she never finished choosing.
>
> I do not sew. I open it  
> the way you open a letter  
> addressed to someone else —  
> carefully, and with a little shame.
>
> Today I took one pin  
> and fixed a hem that didn't need it.

> **Sonnet for a Closing Factory** *(original)*
>
> They rang the bell at five for forty years  
> and men came out like water from a lock,  
> their faces grey, their voices loud with beers  
> not drunk yet, and the town would set its clock  
> by them. The bell is in a skip today.  
> I watched them lift it, two men and a crane,  
> as careful as a nurse. It didn't weigh  
> as much as I'd imagined. In the rain  
> the yard looked smaller. Someone took a photo.  
> Someone else said nothing, which was right.  
> But who will tell the town when it can go?  
> Who rings the hour now? Who decides the night?  
> The bell is silent. Still, at five, I hear  
> the town hold its breath, as if it were near.

> **Field Notes, Late October** *(original)*
>
> The geese have gone. The sky keeps their shape for a while, a grey arrow pointing south, and then
> forgets it. In the stubble field a single hare sits upright, listening to something none of us can
> hear. My daughter asks where everything goes. I tell her south, or underground, or into the soup. She
> laughs. The light goes too, while we are laughing, without asking anyone.

## Section A: short questions on "Inheritance"

**1.** Explain the effect of the zeugma "camphor and decisions" in line 2. **[2]**

**2.** Analyse the image "colours she never finished choosing". **[2]**

**3.** Explain the effect of the simile in the second stanza. **[3]**

**4.** Comment on the effect of the dash at the end of line 7. **[2]**

**5.** How does the final couplet change the poem's mood? **[3]**

**6.** Identify the poem's form and explain how its final stanza departs from the pattern. **[2]**

**7.** What attitude to the past does the poem express? Support your answer. **[3]**

**8.** Write a thesis statement (one or two sentences) for the question: *Explore the poet's presentation
of inheritance.* **[2]**

## Section B: essay questions

Each essay is marked out of 20 (AO1, AO2). In your answer you should consider the poet's use of
language, form and structure.

**9.** Explore the poet's presentation of the relationship between past and present in "Inheritance".
**[20]**

**10.** Explore the poet's presentation of change and loss in "Sonnet for a Closing Factory". **[20]**

**11.** Explore the poet's presentation of time and loss in "Field Notes, Late October". **[20]**

**12.** For "Sonnet for a Closing Factory", write a full plan: a thesis and four paragraph topic

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Pairing a concrete smell with an abstract noun links physical memory to the grandmother's
character (1), suggesting the box holds not only objects but traces of a life of choices (1).

**2.** The colours stand for possibilities left open (1); "never finished choosing" suggests a life
interrupted, perhaps by death, so the unused threads carry quiet loss (1).

**3.** The speaker compares opening the box to opening "a letter / addressed to someone else" (1); this
suggests the box is private and the speaker feels like an intruder (1); "a little shame" adds guilt,
showing she feels unworthy of, or disconnected from, her inheritance (1).

**4.** The dash creates a pause before the qualification (1), slowing the reader and emphasising the
hesitant, careful way she approaches the past (1).

**5.** The poem moves from passive observation to action ("I took one pin") (1). Fixing "a hem that
didn't need it" is a gesture of connection rather than necessity (1), so the mood shifts from shame to
tender, slightly comic continuity: she finds a small way to inherit the skill (1).

**6.** Free verse in unrhymed quatrains (1), ending with a two-line stanza that breaks the pattern,
which isolates the closing action and gives it the weight of a resolution (1).

**7.** The past is valued but distant (1): the box is treated with reverence ("carefully") (1) and guilt
("a little shame"), and the final action suggests a wish to stay connected to it even without the same
skills (1).

**8.** One mark for a clear claim on the focus (1); one for referring to how structure or form carries
it (1). Example: *"The poem presents inheritance as something felt before it is understood, moving from
the speaker's guilty distance from her grandmother's sewing box to a small, unnecessary act that lets
her share in it."*

### Section B: how to mark questions 9 to 11

Marked holistically on AO1 and AO2. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not
Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 17–20 | Perceptive, personal reading sustained throughout; precise, discriminating analysis of form, structure and language; ambiguity explored |
| 13–16 | Clear, developed argument; detailed analysis with apt terminology |
| 9–12 | Relevant reading; explanation of several methods; some generalisation |
| 5–8 | Some comment on methods; tendency to paraphrase |
| 1–4 | Mainly paraphrase |

**9. Indicative content ("Inheritance").** Smell as memory; the zeugma in line 2; unfinished colours as
an interrupted life; the letter simile and shame; the dash; tense shift to past ("Today I took") in the
final couplet; form (quatrains, then a couplet); the paradox of a repair that is not needed, as a ritual
of remembrance.

**10. Indicative content ("Sonnet for a Closing Factory").**
- Form: fourteen lines of mostly iambic pentameter with alternating rhyme and a couplet, so a sonnet
  in the English pattern, a traditionally lyrical form given to an industrial subject.
- Turn: the rhetorical questions in lines 11 and 12 ("But who will tell the town...?") mark the shift
  from description to protest or grief.
- Imagery: men "like water from a lock" suggests a natural, regular flow; the bell lifted "as careful as
  a nurse" makes the removal a death; "the yard looked smaller" shows diminishment.
- Rhythm and syntax: enjambment and mid-line full stops ("The bell is in a skip today.") break the
  regular rhythm, as the routine is broken.
- Ending: the paradox of hearing a silent bell; the town personified as holding its breath; the
  closing couplet's rhyme of "hear" and "near" gives the conventional sonnet resolution, yet what it
  resolves on is an absence, so the close feels haunted. The slant rhyme of "photo" and "go" earlier
  suggests the routine already coming apart.

**11. Indicative content ("Field Notes, Late October").**
- Form: a prose poem; the absence of line breaks gives a sense of continuous time slipping away.
- Imagery: the sky that "keeps their shape... and then forgets it" personifies memory fading; the hare
  "listening to something none of us can hear" suggests mystery and attention to change.
- Voice: the conversational parent and child; the list "south, or underground, or into the soup" moves
  from migration to death to comedy, deflecting the child's question.
- Ending: "The light goes too, while we are laughing, without asking anyone" brings loss into the human
  moment; the personification of light as indifferent makes time impersonal; tone mixes tenderness and
  melancholy.

**12.** One mark for a thesis on change and loss (1); one mark for each of four topic sentences that
names a specific method and links it to the focus (4); one mark for a logical order that follows the
poem's movement (1). Total 6. Example topic sentences: the sonnet form and its regular rhythm as the
lost routine; the industrial imagery and the nurse simile; the turn into rhetorical questions; the
paradoxical ending and slant rhyme.
