These notes condense outcomes 1.1.11 and 1.1.12 of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11) specification, Issue 2: capital expenditure and revenue expenditure, and their correct accounting treatment with reference to relevant accounting concepts. The full explanation is in the [capital and revenue expenditure study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-capital-and-revenue-expenditure/), and exam-style questions are in the [capital and revenue expenditure practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-capital-and-revenue-expenditure-practice/). Amounts are in dollars; businesses are invented.

## Core recall

| | Capital expenditure | Revenue expenditure |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Acquire or improve a non-current asset | Run the business; maintain existing assets |
| Benefit | Several periods | Current period |
| Debit | Non-current asset account | Expense account |
| Where shown | Statement of financial position, then depreciated | Statement of profit or loss in full |

The test is **purpose, not size**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Non-current asset | A resource held for use in the business for more than one year |
| Cost of an asset | All costs to bring it to the location and condition for its intended use |
| Trade discount | Reduction in list price; deducted before recording cost |
| Improvement | Work that extends life, raises capacity, improves output quality or cuts running costs significantly |
| Repair or maintenance | Work that restores or keeps an asset in its existing condition |
| Capitalise | Debit spending to a non-current asset account |
| Capitalisation threshold | A minimum value below which long-lasting items are expensed (materiality) |

## Part of the asset's cost?

| Capital | Revenue |
|---|---|
| Price after trade discount | Training staff |
| Import duty | Advertising |
| Delivery | Fuel, power, insurance |
| Site preparation | Routine servicing, repairs |
| Installation, assembly | Administration |
| Testing | Idle-period costs once ready |
| Legal fees on buying property | |
| Own wages and materials used to build the asset | |

## Mini examples

| Item | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Extension adding two classrooms to a school building | Capital | Increases capacity |
| Replacing broken windows with identical ones | Revenue | Restores condition |
| Rewiring a factory with a system that halves energy use | Capital | Significant cut in running costs |
| Annual software licence fee | Revenue | Benefit for this year only |
| Legal fees on purchasing a shop | Capital | Part of the cost of the property |
| Repainting a delivery van in company colours when bought | Capital | Getting it ready for intended use |
| Repainting the same van five years later | Revenue | Maintenance |

## Misclassification effects (straight line depreciation, 20% of cost, full year in year of purchase)

**Repair of $6,000 capitalised in Year 1**

```
Year 1: profit overstated by 6,000 − 1,200 = 4,800; assets overstated by 4,800
Years 2–5: profit understated by 1,200 each year
```

**Machine of $15,000 expensed in Year 1**

```
Year 1: profit understated by 15,000 − 3,000 = 12,000; assets understated by 12,000
Years 2–5: profit overstated by 3,000 each year
```

Always give both effects: profit and the statement of financial position.

## Correcting journals

Capital item charged to an expense:

```
Dr Non-current asset      Cr Expense account
Dr Depreciation expense   Cr Provision for depreciation   (for the year's charge)
```

Revenue item capitalised:

```
Dr Expense account        Cr Non-current asset
Dr Provision for depreciation   Cr Depreciation expense   (reverse any charge made on it)
```

Own work on an asset:

```
Dr Non-current asset      Cr Wages / Purchases
```

## Concepts to quote

- **Accruals**: spread benefit over periods via depreciation.
- **Prudence**: doubtful future benefit → expense.
- **Materiality**: small items expensed.
- **Consistency**: same capitalisation policy every year.
- **Going concern**: spreading cost assumes future use.

## Exam traps

- Classifying by size.
- List price instead of price after trade discount.
- Training or advertising capitalised.
- A large repair treated as capital.
- Profit effect without the asset effect.
- Forgetting depreciation in later-year effects.
- Own wages left in expenses when they built an asset.

## Self-test

1. Define capital expenditure.
2. Define revenue expenditure.
3. A machine has a list price of $20,000 with 5% trade discount, delivery of $400 and staff training of $600. Find its cost.
4. Is the cost of a test run of a new machine capital or revenue?
5. Give two tests that show spending on an existing asset is an improvement.
6. A $7,000 overhaul restores a machine to its normal condition. Classify it.
7. Which concept explains expensing a $20 kettle?
8. Which concept explains spreading the cost of a van over its life?
9. A $10,000 machine was charged to repairs. Depreciation is 10% of cost a year. State the effect on Year 1 profit.
10. A $2,000 repair was capitalised and depreciated at 25% of cost. State the effect on Year 1 profit and on non-current assets.
11. Give the journal to transfer $3,500 of the business's own wages used to build a storeroom.
12. An invoice of $8,000 covers $3,000 of repairs and a $5,000 extension. How is it recorded?

### Answers

1. Spending to acquire a non-current asset or improve one beyond its original condition.
2. Spending on running the business, including maintaining assets in their existing condition.
3. 20,000 × 0.95 = 19,000 + 400 = $19,400; training is revenue.
4. Capital: it is part of bringing the machine into working condition.
5. Any two: extends useful life; increases capacity; improves output quality; significantly reduces running costs.
6. Revenue expenditure.
7. Materiality.
8. Accruals (matching).
9. Understated by 10,000 − 1,000 = $9,000.
10. Profit overstated by 2,000 − 500 = $1,500; non-current assets overstated by $1,500.
11. Dr Premises (or Buildings) $3,500, Cr Wages $3,500.
12. Split it: $3,000 to repairs (revenue) and $5,000 to premises (capital).
