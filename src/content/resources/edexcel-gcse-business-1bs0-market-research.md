This guide goes deeper into content item 1.2.2, Market research, in Topic 1.2 Spotting a business opportunity of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022, part of Theme 1 (Paper 1, 1BS0/01). ## What 1.2.2 asks you to know

| Strand | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| The purpose of market research | to identify and understand customer needs; to identify gaps in the market; to reduce risk; to inform business decisions |
| Methods of market research | primary research: survey, questionnaire, focus group, observation; secondary research: internet, market reports, government reports |
| The use of data in market research | qualitative and quantitative data; the role of social media in collecting market research data; the importance of the reliability of market research data |

## Part 1: the four purposes as a sequence

The purposes are easier to use in answers if you see them as steps an entrepreneur works through.

1. **Identify and understand customer needs:** who might buy, and what do they want (price, quality, choice, convenience)?
2. **Identify gaps in the market:** which of those needs are not met well by existing businesses?
3. **Reduce risk:** evidence of demand before money is spent lowers the chance of financial loss. It never removes the risk.
4. **Inform business decisions:** the findings shape the product, the price, the location and the promotion.

## Part 2: choosing the right method

There is no single best method. The right choice depends on what the business needs to know, how much money and time it has, and how up to date and specific the information must be.

| Situation | Sensible method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A start-up needs to know how many people live in an area | Secondary: government reports | Already collected, cheap or free, covers the whole population |
| An owner wants to know how many local people would buy a new product | Primary: survey or questionnaire | Specific to the idea; gives numbers |
| An owner wants to know why customers dislike a new design | Primary: focus group | Gives reasons and detailed opinions |
| A shop wants to know which displays customers actually stop at | Primary: observation | Shows real behaviour, not what people say they do |
| An owner wants a quick sense of trends in a whole industry | Secondary: market reports or the internet | Wide coverage without collecting data |

A small business with little money often starts with free secondary research, then carries out cheap primary research to fill the gaps that secondary data cannot answer, such as opinions about its own idea.

### Survey or questionnaire?
The specification lists both. A **survey** is the process of gathering answers from a sample of people, which might be done face to face, by phone or online. A **questionnaire** is the written list of questions used to collect the answers. A survey often uses a questionnaire.

## Part 3: writing questions that produce useful data

The way a question is written decides the kind of data it produces.

- **Closed questions** offer fixed answers (yes/no, tick boxes, a rating from 1 to 5). They produce **quantitative data** that is easy to count and compare.
- **Open questions** let people answer in their own words ("What would make you visit more often?"). They produce **qualitative data** that explains reasons.

Common faults to avoid:
- **Leading questions** that push towards an answer: "Wouldn't you love a new café here?"
- **Vague questions:** "Do you eat out often?" (What counts as often?)
- **Two questions in one:** "Is our service fast and friendly?"

## Worked example: Lantern Yard Games Café

Joss plans a board-game café in a market town. He uses three kinds of research.

**Secondary (government report).** Population figures show many residents aged 16 to 34, the age group most likely to visit. This is quick and free but does not say whether those people want a games café.

**Primary (questionnaire).** He asks 150 people in the town centre: "What is the most you would pay for a two-hour table?"

| Price (£) | Number of people |
|---|---|
| 4 | 30 |
| 5 | 55 |
| 6 | 45 |
| 7 | 20 |
| Total | 150 |

- Mean price = (4 × 30 + 5 × 55 + 6 × 45 + 7 × 20) ÷ 150 = 805 ÷ 150 = **£5.37** (to the nearest penny)
- Share willing to pay £6 or more = (45 + 20) ÷ 150 × 100 = **43.3%** (to one decimal place)

So if Joss charges £6, fewer than half of those asked would pay. A price of £5 would suit 120 of the 150 (everyone except the 30 who would pay at most £4).

**Social media.** He posts a poll for his followers: "Would you visit a games café in town?" Of 420 votes, 70% say yes, which is 294 people.

**Judging reliability.**
- The questionnaire asked 150 people in the town centre on one weekday afternoon. People at work or college were missed, and they may be the 16 to 34 age group he most wants.
- The poll's 70% is probably too high: followers already like his idea, anyone can vote, and voting yes costs nothing.
- The questions asked about willingness to pay, not actual spending, and people often overstate what they will do.

**Decision informed by research.** Joss sets a £5 price for weekdays and tests a £6 price on weekend evenings, while repeating his questionnaire at a college and on a Saturday to get a more representative sample.

## Part 4: qualitative and quantitative data together

Quantitative data shows **how many** or **how much**; qualitative data shows **why**. The two work best together. If 60% of survey replies rate a product 2 out of 5, the number shows there is a problem; the comments explain whether it is price, taste or packaging, so the owner knows what to fix.

## Part 5: the role of social media

Social media lets a small business collect research quickly and cheaply: polls, comments, reviews, direct messages and the number of reactions to different posts. It can reach people who would not stop for a street questionnaire and can show results within hours.

Its weaknesses follow from who takes part. Followers are not a random sample of the market; some respondents may not be real customers; people may react to a post without any intention of buying. Treat social media findings as a useful signal to be checked, not as proof.

## Part 6: why reliability matters

Market research only reduces risk if it can be trusted. Unreliable data can be worse than none, because the owner acts confidently on a wrong picture: ordering too much stock, choosing the wrong price, or opening in the wrong place. When judging reliability, check sample size, whether the sample matches the target market, bias in the questions or the place of asking, how old the data is and who collected it.

## Common mistakes

- Saying market research "removes" or "eliminates" risk.
- Calling a market report primary research because the business paid for it. It is secondary: someone else collected it.
- Mixing up observation (watching behaviour) with a focus group (a discussion).
- Treating a large social media poll as reliable because it has many votes.
- Calculating a mean by averaging the price categories instead of weighting them by the number of people.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate:** percentages and means from survey tables; show each step.
- **Explain:** one method or purpose, then two developments, such as cost and reliability.
- **Analyse:** apply the method to the business's situation and budget.
- **Justify:** recommend primary or secondary, or one method over another, using what the owner needs to know.
- **Evaluate:** judge how reliable the case's research is and whether it supports the owner's decision.
