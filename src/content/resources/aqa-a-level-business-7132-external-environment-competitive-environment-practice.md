These questions cover section 3.7.7 the competitive environment of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-external-environment-competitive-environment/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-external-environment-competitive-environment-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a barrier to entry? **[1]**

A: Many small customers

B: A patent held by an existing firm

C: Slow industry growth

D: A wide range of suppliers

**2.** Which **one** of the following would increase supplier power? **[1]**

A: Many alternative suppliers

B: Standardised inputs

C: A single supplier of a unique ingredient

D: Low switching costs

**3.** Define "substitute product". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why buyer power is high when a business sells most of its output to one customer. **[3]**

**5.** Dunstan Coffee spends £200,000 a year on beans and makes an operating profit of £80,000. The bean supplier raises its prices by 10%. Calculate the new operating profit and the percentage fall, if no other changes occur. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Marl Street Cafe is one of six cafes in a small town. Slow growth in town centre footfall means the cafes compete mainly on price. Analyse how rivalry affects its profit. **[6]**

**7.** A start-up plans to launch an online food delivery service in a city that already has two large rivals. Explain **two** barriers to entry that the start-up may face. **[4]**

**8.** A supermarket chain buys 40% of the output of Penhallow Jams. The chain demands a 5% price cut or it will switch to own-label jam. Penhallow's annual revenue is £6m and operating profit £600,000.

**(a)** Calculate the revenue lost if Penhallow accepts the price cut. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the new operating profit, and the percentage fall, if costs do not change. **[2]**

**(c)** Analyse **one** way in which Penhallow could reduce the power of this buyer. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how a falling cost of setting up an online business might change the strength of the five forces in the retail industry. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse how the threat of substitutes might affect the pricing decisions of a rail company. **[6]**

**11.** Quillon Brewery has the following situation: few suppliers of hops, many small pubs as customers, moderate rivalry from craft breweries, high start-up costs for new breweries, and a growing trend for non-alcoholic drinks. Assess which force is likely to be the biggest threat to its profit. **[9]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of Porter's five forces to a business deciding whether to enter a new market. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Patents legally restrict others from copying a product, so new firms cannot enter easily. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A single supplier of a unique ingredient can set its price because buyers cannot go elsewhere. **[1]**

**3.** A product that meets the same need or want as another [1] in a different way, so customers can switch to it, for example video calls instead of business travel [1]. **[2]**

**4.** If one customer takes most of the output, losing it would be very damaging [1], so the customer can threaten to switch to another supplier [1] and demand lower prices or better terms, reducing the supplier's profit [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Extra cost = 10% × £200,000 = £20,000 [1]; new profit = £80,000 − £20,000 = **£60,000** [1]; fall = 20,000 ÷ 80,000 = **25%** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Rivalry is high because there are six similar cafes and customer numbers are not growing [1]. To keep customers, they must compete on price [1], which reduces the profit margin on each sale [1]. Cafes may also spend more on promotion or loyalty cards [1], raising costs [1]. With high fixed costs such as rent, a drop in sales hits profit hard, so price cuts are tempting even though they hurt everyone [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Barrier 1: scale advantages of the existing rivals [1]. They can spread costs of delivery and advertising across many orders, so the start-up's costs per order are higher [1]. Barrier 2: brand loyalty and switching [1]. Customers already use the apps they know and may be reluctant to switch without strong incentives, so the start-up must spend heavily on promotion [1]. (Credit also: high start-up costs; the chance of retaliatory price cuts.) **[4]**

**8. (a)** 40% × £6m = £2.4m; 5% × £2.4m = **£120,000** [1]. (Award [1] for £2.4m.) **(b)** New profit = £600,000 − £120,000 = **£480,000** [1]; fall = 120,000 ÷ 600,000 = **20%** [1]. **(c)** Penhallow could widen its customer base [1], by selling to independent shops, cafes or overseas [1], so that no single customer is as important, and the supermarket's threat to leave loses force [1]. (Credit also: differentiation through a premium brand.) **[7]**

**9.** Lower set-up costs weaken barriers to entry [1], so the threat of new entrants rises [1]. Existing retailers face more rivalry as more online sellers compete [1]. Buyer power also rises, since customers can easily compare prices and switch [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Substitutes include cars, coaches and video conferencing [1]. If these are cheaper or more convenient, passengers will switch if rail fares are too high [1]. This puts a ceiling on fares [1], so the rail company cannot raise prices without losing customers [1]. It may need to offer discounts or advance fares to fill trains [1], or improve quality, such as Wi-Fi and punctuality, to justify higher prices [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Supplier power: few hop suppliers could raise prices [1], which would increase costs, though hops may be a small part of total costs [1]. Buyer power: many small pubs have low individual power [1], though pub chains could be stronger. Rivalry: craft breweries give moderate rivalry [1] but a growing market may soften it. Entry threat: high start-up costs protect the brewery [1]. Substitutes: the rise of non-alcoholic drinks is a substitute threat and a long-term trend [1], which could cut demand for beer overall [1] and so affect every brewery's sales. Judgement: the substitute threat is probably the biggest, because it is rising and the brewery cannot easily control it [1]. The brewery could respond by launching low-alcohol beers [1]. **[9]**

**12.** For: the model gives a structured way to judge how profitable the market is [1], by looking at all forces, not only current rivals [1]. A market with high entry barriers, weak buyers and suppliers and few substitutes is more attractive [1]. It shows what the firm will have to overcome, such as entry barriers or supplier power [1], and which strategy might work [1]. Against: it is a snapshot, and the market may change quickly [1]. It is hard to define the market and judge the strength of the forces precisely [1]. It does not consider the firm's own strengths and resources [1], or the role of government, complementary products and cooperation [1]. Factors: the quality of the data available [1], how fast the market changes, and whether the model is used with other tools such as SWOT and financial forecasts. Judgement: the model is a useful first filter, but not enough on its own [1]; the decision should also depend on the firm's own capabilities and finances [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* High marks go to answers that rank the forces for the case, use evidence from the stimulus, and say what the firm should do.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing buyers and suppliers.
- Describing all five forces for a question about only one.
- Treating a competitor's product as a substitute rather than a different product meeting the same need.
- Giving a list of barriers with no application to the business.
- Not saying what the forces mean for profit or strategy.
