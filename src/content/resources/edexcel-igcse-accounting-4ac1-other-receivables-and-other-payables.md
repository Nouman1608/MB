## What section 2.7 asks you to do

| Point | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| 2.7 (a) | Explain why it is necessary to account for other receivables and payables |
| 2.7 (b) | Distinguish between other receivables and payables |
| 2.7 (c) | Calculate and record other receivables and payables in the books of account |

The glossary links the names: other receivables were previously called prepayments (and accrued income); other payables were previously called accruals (and income received in advance).

## Why they must be accounted for

Cash is often paid or received in a different period from the one it relates to. The accruals concept says the income statement must show the income earned and the expenses incurred in the period, not the cash movements. Accounting for other receivables and payables is necessary because:

- **Profit is measured correctly.** Without the adjustments, an expense owed would be missing and an expense paid in advance would be overcharged.
- **The statement of financial position is complete.** Amounts owed by the business are liabilities and amounts paid ahead are assets; leaving them out misstates both.
- **Years can be compared.** If one year carries thirteen months of rent and the next eleven, the change in profit says nothing about trading.

## Distinguishing the two

| | Other receivables (current assets) | Other payables (current liabilities) |
|---|---|---|
| Expense | Prepaid: paid now for a later period | Accrued: used now, paid later |
| Income | Accrued income: earned now, received later | Received in advance: received now, earned later |
| Balance carried down in the account | Debit | Credit |

"Other" separates these from trade receivables and trade payables, which arise from buying and selling goods on credit.

## A reliable method for any account

1. Work out the **correct charge or income for the period** from the time covered: months × monthly amount.
2. Enter the opening balance, the cash paid or received, and the transfer to the income statement.
3. The balancing figure is the closing other receivable or other payable. Check that it makes sense in time: which months does it cover?

## Worked example 1: opening accrual, closing prepayment

Ashby Print rents its unit for 800 a month. Its year end is 31 December. At 1 January 2027 the December 2026 rent of 800 was still owed. During 2027 it paid 11,200 in rent.

Charge for 2027: 12 × 800 = **9,600**.

```
Rent payable account (2027)
Debit                                  Credit
Bank                     11,200        1 Jan  Balance b/d (owed)    800
                                       31 Dec Income statement    9,600
                                       31 Dec Balance c/d (prepaid) 800
                         11,200                                  11,200
2028
1 Jan  Balance b/d (prepaid)  800
```

The 11,200 paid covered the 800 owed from 2026, the 9,600 for 2027 and 800 for January 2028. The opening accrual is a credit balance (owed); the closing prepayment is a debit balance carried into 2028 and shown as an other receivable.

## Worked example 2: income received in advance

Kingsmead Studio sublets a room for 450 a month. At 1 January 2027 the tenant owed 450 for December 2026. During 2027 it received 6,300.

Income for 2027: 12 × 450 = **5,400**.

```
Rent receivable account (2027)
Debit                                  Credit
1 Jan  Balance b/d (owed to us)  450   Bank                     6,300
31 Dec Income statement        5,400
31 Dec Balance c/d (in advance)  450
                               6,300                            6,300
                                       2028
                                       1 Jan  Balance b/d (in advance) 450
```

Receipts of 6,300 = 450 for 2026 + 5,400 for 2027 + 450 for January 2028. The tenant has paid ahead, so the business owes a month of use: a credit balance shown as an other payable. Only 5,400 is income for 2027, not the 6,300 received.

## Worked example 3: four adjustments to a draft profit

Penrose Florists has a draft profit of 26,400 for the year. None of these has been recorded.

| Item | Effect on profit | Other receivable | Other payable |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Wages owed at the year end, 780 | − 780 | | 780 |
| Insurance paid for next year, 510 | + 510 | 510 | |
| Rent receivable owed by a tenant, 300 | + 300 | 300 | |
| Commission received in advance, 240 | − 240 | | 240 |
| **Totals** | **− 210** | **810** | **1,020** |

Adjusted profit = 26,400 − 780 + 510 + 300 − 240 = **26,190**.

The pattern to remember: items that make the business owe something (accrued expense, income in advance) reduce profit; items that make the business owed something (prepaid expense, accrued income) increase profit.

## Time apportionment

Many questions give one payment covering a period that straddles the year end. Split it by months.

Example: a 12-month maintenance contract of 2,160 is paid on 1 September; the year end is 31 December. Monthly cost = 2,160 ÷ 12 = 180. September to December is 4 months, so the charge is 4 × 180 = **720** and the other receivable is 8 × 180 = **1,440**.

## Statement of financial position presentation

```
Current assets
  Inventory
  Trade receivables (less provision)
  Other receivables            prepaid expenses + accrued income
  Bank / cash
Current liabilities
  Trade payables
  Other payables               accrued expenses + income received in advance
```

Show the totals by heading; the question may ask for the separate items to be listed.

## Exam technique

- **Explain why**: link the accruals concept to profit and to the statement of financial position, with a consequence.
- **Distinguish**: give the asset/liability classification and an example of each, in one comparative sentence.
- **Calculate**: start from the period's correct figure, not the cash. Show months × rate.
- **Record**: label balances "owed" or "prepaid" (or "in advance") and bring them down on the correct side.

## Common errors

- Using cash paid as the expense for the year.
- Carrying down an accrued expense on the credit side of the account. It is written on the debit side so that it is brought down as a credit balance.
- Treating rent received in advance as income of this year.
- Showing a prepayment as a liability or an accrual as an asset.
- Ignoring the opening balance in the account.
- Confusing other receivables with trade receivables.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is income received in advance an other receivable or an other payable?**
An other payable. The business has been paid for something it has not yet provided, so it owes the customer that service.

**Why does the opening balance matter in an expense account?**
An opening accrual was a cost of last year, so cash paid to settle it is not this year's expense. An opening prepayment is a cost of this year paid last year, so it is added.

**What concept requires other receivables and payables?**
The accruals concept: income and expenses are matched to the period in which they are earned or incurred, not when cash moves.
