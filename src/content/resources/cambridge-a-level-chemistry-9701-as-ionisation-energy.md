This guide covers subtopic **1.4, Ionisation energy**, from Topic 1 (Atomic structure) of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content; every atom or ion is in the ground state, and only hydrogen to krypton is assessed. The broader [Atomic Structure: Orbitals and Ionisation Energy guide](/resources/as-atomic-structure-orbitals-and-ionisation-energy/) gives the definition, the trends and the magnesium example. This page goes further: the four factors applied to real Period 3 data, the pattern in *second* ionisation energies, and a step-by-step method for reading successive data to find configuration and position in the Periodic Table.

## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. define and use the term first ionisation energy;
2. construct equations for first, second and subsequent ionisation energies;
3. identify and explain trends in ionisation energies across a period and down a group;
4. identify and explain the variation in successive ionisation energies of an element;
5. understand that ionisation energies are due to the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron;
6. explain the factors influencing ionisation energies in terms of nuclear charge, atomic or ionic radius, shielding by inner shells and sub-shells, and spin-pair repulsion;
7. deduce electronic configurations using successive ionisation energy data;
8. deduce the position of an element in the Periodic Table using successive ionisation energy data.

## Definition and equations

The **first ionisation energy** is the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions. Units: kJ mol⁻¹.

Every ionisation equation has three features: one electron removed, gaseous species on both sides, and the charge going up by one.

| Ionisation | Equation for calcium |
|---|---|
| First | Ca(g) → Ca⁺(g) + e⁻ |
| Second | Ca⁺(g) → Ca²⁺(g) + e⁻ |
| Third | Ca²⁺(g) → Ca³⁺(g) + e⁻ |

The equation Ca(g) → Ca²⁺(g) + 2e⁻ is **not** the second ionisation energy. Its energy change is the *sum* of the first and second ionisation energies.

## The four factors

Ionisation energy measures how strongly the nucleus attracts the electron being removed. Four factors control that attraction:

| Factor | Effect on ionisation energy |
|---|---|
| Nuclear charge | More protons, stronger attraction, higher IE |
| Atomic or ionic radius | Electron further from nucleus, weaker attraction, lower IE |
| Shielding by inner shells and sub-shells | More electrons between nucleus and outer electron, weaker attraction, lower IE |
| Spin-pair repulsion | Electron sharing an orbital is repelled by its partner, so it is easier to remove, lower IE |

A good explanation names which factor changes and which stays roughly the same.

## Period 3: the data explained

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First IE / kJ mol⁻¹ | 496 | 738 | 578 | 787 | 1012 | 1000 | 1251 | 1521 |
| Electron removed | 3s¹ | 3s² | 3p¹ | 3p² | 3p³ | 3p⁴ | 3p⁵ | 3p⁶ |

(Values rounded; data books differ slightly.)

**General rise.** From sodium to argon nuclear charge increases from 11 to 18. The electron removed is always in the third shell, shielded by the same inner shells (1s²2s²2p⁶), so shielding is roughly constant and radius decreases. Attraction increases.

**Dip from Mg to Al (738 to 578).** Aluminium's electron is removed from 3p, not 3s. A 3p electron is higher in energy and slightly further from the nucleus, and it is also shielded by the 3s² electrons. Both effects outweigh the extra proton.

**Dip from P to S (1012 to 1000).** In phosphorus the three 3p electrons are in separate orbitals. In sulfur the fourth 3p electron must share an orbital, and **spin-pair repulsion** between the paired electrons makes one of them easier to remove.

**Down a group.** For Group 1, Li 520, Na 496, K 419 kJ mol⁻¹. Each element has an extra shell, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded. These effects outweigh the larger nuclear charge.

## Second ionisation energies: the pattern shifts by one

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Second IE / kJ mol⁻¹ | 4562 | 1451 | 1817 | 1577 | 1907 | 2252 | 2298 | 2666 |
| Ion losing the electron | Na⁺ (2p⁶) | Mg⁺ (3s¹) | Al⁺ (3s²) | Si⁺ (3p¹) | P⁺ (3p²) | S⁺ (3p³) | Cl⁺ (3p⁴) | Ar⁺ (3p⁵) |

Second ionisation removes an electron from a 1+ ion, so each element behaves like the one before it did for the first IE.

- **Sodium is highest** because Na⁺ has the configuration of neon: the second electron comes from the 2p sub-shell, much closer to the nucleus with far less shielding.
- **Al⁺ is higher than Si⁺** (1817 against 1577): Al⁺ loses a 3s electron, while Si⁺ loses a 3p electron, which is higher in energy and shielded by 3s². This mirrors the Mg/Al dip in first ionisation energies.
- The spin-pair dip in this row appears between S⁺ (3p³) and Cl⁺ (3p⁴): the rise from 2252 to 2298 is unusually small.

Seeing that the pattern is the first-IE pattern moved one element to the right is a quick check on any explanation.

## Successive ionisation energies

Successive ionisation energies of one element always increase, because each electron is removed from an ion with a greater positive charge: the remaining electrons are attracted more strongly and the ion is smaller.

A **large jump** shows that the next electron comes from a shell closer to the nucleus. Smaller jumps can show a change of sub-shell within the same shell.

### Worked example: deducing a group and a configuration

An element in Period 3 has these first six ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹): 1012, 1907, 2914, 4964, 6274, 21 267.

1. Compare each value with the previous one: ratios 1.88, 1.53, 1.70, 1.26, 3.39.
2. The largest ratio is between the 5th and 6th values. Five electrons are in the outer shell, so the element is in **Group 15**.
3. In Period 3, Group 15 is **phosphorus**: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p³.
4. Look again within the first five: the rise from the 3rd to the 4th value (ratio 1.70) is larger than its neighbours. The first three electrons come from 3p and the 4th from 3s, which is lower in energy. The data therefore also show the 3s and 3p sub-shells.

### Worked example: from data to shells

Aluminium's first five ionisation energies are 578, 1817, 2745, 11 577 and 14 842 kJ mol⁻¹.

- Ratios: 3.14, 1.51, 4.22, 1.28.
- The big jump after the 3rd electron places aluminium in **Group 13**.
- The ratio of 3.14 between the 1st and 2nd values reflects the change from 3p (first electron) to 3s (second and third).
- If all 13 values were given, they would fall into groups of 3, 8 and 2 separated by two large jumps: the shell arrangement 2, 8, 3.

### Using a log graph

Successive values span a huge range, so they are often plotted as log₁₀(IE) against electron number. On this graph, each shell appears as a cluster of points with a steep step between clusters. Count the points in each cluster from the right-hand end (innermost shell) to the left to read the shell occupancy.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the second IE equation as X(g) → X²⁺(g) + 2e⁻.
- Leaving out state symbols, or using (s) for a metal.
- Explaining the rise across a period with "more shielding". Shielding is roughly constant.
- Explaining the Mg/Al dip with spin-pair repulsion. That explains P/S; Mg/Al is about the new 3p sub-shell.
- Saying "stability of a half-filled shell" for P/S without naming repulsion between paired electrons.
- Counting the large jump in the wrong place: a jump between the 2nd and 3rd value means Group 2, not Group 3.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: include *one mole*, *gaseous atoms* and *1+ ions*.
- **Construct an equation**: one electron, state symbols on both sides.
- **Explain a trend or anomaly**: name the electron's sub-shell or orbital and use at least two of the four factors.
- **Deduce** from successive data: show the ratios or differences you used and state the group before naming the element.
