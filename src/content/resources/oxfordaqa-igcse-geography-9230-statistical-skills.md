This guide covers section 3.3.1.4, Statistical skills, within Section A (Geographical skills) of Unit 3 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The specification says that students should be able to:

- use appropriate measures of **central tendency, spread and cumulative frequency** (median, mean, range, quartiles and inter-quartile range, mode and modal class);
- calculate **percentage increase or decrease** and understand the use of **percentiles**;
- describe relationships in **bivariate data**: sketch trend lines through scatter plots, draw estimated lines of best fit, make predictions, and interpolate and extrapolate trends;
- **identify weaknesses** in selective statistical presentation of data.

The specification adds that geographical skills "will be assessed in all three written exams", so statistics can appear in Paper 1, 2 or 3. Paper 3 (1 hour 15 minutes, 60 marks) has the most skills content. For the wider skills picture, see the [geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/). This page goes deeper on the statistics, with worked examples. Every number below has been calculated and checked.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mean | The sum of all values divided by the number of values |
| Median | The middle value when the data are in order |
| Mode | The most common value |
| Modal class | The class interval with the highest frequency in grouped data |
| Range | The largest value minus the smallest |
| Quartiles | Values that divide ordered data into four equal parts |
| Inter-quartile range (IQR) | Upper quartile minus lower quartile; the spread of the middle half of the data |
| Cumulative frequency | A running total of frequencies |
| Percentile | The value below which a given percentage of the data lie |
| Bivariate data | Data on two variables for each item, such as distance and land value |
| Anomaly (outlier) | A value that is far from the others |

## Measures of central tendency

### A worked data set

A student measures the long axis of 12 pebbles on a beach, in millimetres:

12, 15, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 25, 25, 31, 34, 45

The values are already in order.

- **Mean:** the total is 287. Divide by 12 to get 23.9 mm (to one decimal place).
- **Median:** with 12 values, the median lies between the 6th and 7th values (22 and 25). So the median is (22 + 25) ÷ 2 = 23.5 mm.
- **Mode:** 25 mm, which appears three times.
- **Range:** 45 - 12 = 33 mm.

### Choosing the right average

| Average | Best when | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | Data are fairly even and you want to use every value | Affected by extreme values |
| Median | There are outliers or the data are skewed | Ignores the size of most values |
| Mode | You want the most common category or value | May not be unique; not useful for small samples |

In this example, the largest pebble (45 mm) pulls the mean above the median. When the mean is higher than the median, the data are skewed to the right.

## Measures of spread: quartiles and IQR

The range uses only the highest and lowest values, so one outlier distorts it. Quartiles solve this.

To find quartiles for an even number of values, split the ordered data into a lower half and an upper half.

- Lower half: 12, 15, 15, 18, 20, 22. The median of this half is (15 + 18) ÷ 2 = **16.5**, the **lower quartile (LQ)**.
- Upper half: 25, 25, 25, 31, 34, 45. The median is (25 + 31) ÷ 2 = **28**, the **upper quartile (UQ)**.
- **IQR** = 28 - 16.5 = **11.5 mm**.

The IQR shows the spread of the middle half of the pebbles. A smaller IQR means more consistent sizes. If a second beach had an IQR of 25 mm, its pebbles would be much more variable.

## Grouped data and cumulative frequency

Fieldwork data are often grouped. Suppose 50 pebbles are counted in size classes.

| Size (mm) | Frequency | Cumulative frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to under 10 | 4 | 4 |
| 10 to under 20 | 10 | 14 |
| 20 to under 30 | 18 | 32 |
| 30 to under 40 | 12 | 44 |
| 40 to under 50 | 6 | 50 |

- **Modal class:** 20 to under 30 mm, as it has the highest frequency (18).
- **Estimated mean:** use class midpoints (5, 15, 25, 35, 45). The sum of midpoint × frequency is 20 + 150 + 450 + 420 + 270 = 1 310. Divide by 50 to get **26.2 mm**.

### Cumulative frequency graph

Plot each cumulative frequency against the **upper limit** of its class: (10, 4), (20, 14), (30, 32), (40, 44), (50, 50). Join the points with a smooth curve.

- **Median:** read across at 25 (half of 50). Between the points (20, 14) and (30, 32), the estimated value is 20 + (25 - 14) ÷ (32 - 14) × 10 = **26.1 mm**.
- **Lower quartile:** read across at 12.5 (a quarter of 50). Between (10, 4) and (20, 14): 10 + (12.5 - 4) ÷ 10 × 10 = **18.5 mm**.
- **Upper quartile:** read across at 37.5. Between (30, 32) and (40, 44): 30 + (37.5 - 32) ÷ 12 × 10 = **34.6 mm**.
- **IQR:** 34.6 - 18.5 = **16.1 mm**.

(When you read values off a drawn graph, accept small differences from these calculated values.)

## Percentiles

A percentile tells you where a value sits in a data set. The 90th percentile is the value below which 90% of the data fall. For 50 pebbles, 90% is 45 pebbles. Between (40, 44) and (50, 50), the estimated 90th percentile is 40 + (45 - 44) ÷ 6 × 10 = about **41.7 mm**. Quartiles are the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles.

## Percentage change

**Percentage change = (new value - old value) ÷ old value × 100.**

- Visitors to a national park rise from 48 000 to 66 000: (66 000 - 48 000) ÷ 48 000 × 100 = **37.5% increase**.
- Rainfall at a station falls from 1 600 mm to 1 250 mm: (1 250 - 1 600) ÷ 1 600 × 100 = **-21.9%**, so a **21.9% decrease** (to one decimal place).

Always divide by the **old** value, not the new one. A common error is to give the percentage point difference between two percentages, which is a different thing.

## Scatter graphs, trend lines and predictions

A scatter graph shows two variables. This invented fieldwork data set shows land value index (higher means more expensive) at 8 sites at increasing distance from a city centre.

| Distance from centre (km) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Land value index | 48 | 44 | 41 | 37 | 34 | 29 | 27 | 22 |

- **Describe the relationship:** a strong negative relationship. As distance increases, land value falls steadily.
- **Draw a line of best fit by eye:** a straight line with about the same number of points on each side, passing near the centre of the data. Do not force it through the origin, and do not join the dots.
- **Equation (calculated):** the best-fit line is about value = 51.6 - 3.6 × distance.
- **Interpolation** means estimating a value within the range of your data. At 5.5 km, the line gives about 32 (accept 30 to 33 when reading from a graph).
- **Extrapolation** means extending the line beyond the data. At 10 km, the line gives about 15, but this is less reliable because the pattern may change (land value might level off rather than keep falling).

A strong relationship does not prove one thing causes another. Other factors, such as transport links or zoning, may be involved.

## Identifying weaknesses in selective presentation

The specification asks you to spot misleading uses of data.

| Weakness | Example |
|---|---|
| Truncated axis | A bar chart starting at 90 instead of 0 makes a small difference look huge |
| Cherry-picked time period | Starting a graph in a year of unusually low values to show rapid growth |
| Only the mean | A mean hides a wide spread or outliers |
| Small or biased sample | 10 respondents from one street does not represent a town |
| Percentages of small numbers | "Up 100%" from 2 to 4 incidents |
| Leaving out anomalies | Dropping inconvenient results without saying so |
| Distorting symbols | Making a symbol twice as tall and wide so it appears four times as big |
| Unequal scales or intervals | Class intervals of different widths without adjusting the chart |

When you evaluate a source, say what is shown, what is missing, and how it might mislead.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** A student says, "The mean pebble size is 23.9 mm, so the beach has small pebbles." Suggest **two** reasons why this statement may not be reliable. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "The mean is affected by extreme values, such as the 45 mm pebble, and it does not show the spread. The median was 23.5 mm and the IQR was 11.5 mm, which shows quite a lot of variation. Also, only 12 pebbles were measured, which is a small sample, so it may not represent the whole beach. A larger random sample would be more reliable."

## Skewed data and the choice of average

A histogram or cumulative frequency curve that rises steeply and then flattens suggests many small values and a few large ones: the data are positively skewed. River pebble size, city size and household income are often skewed in this way. For such data the median is a fairer summary than the mean. Where the data are fairly symmetrical, such as heights of adults in a sample, the mean and median are close together and either can be used. Always compare the two before you decide, and say why you chose the one you report.

## Common mistakes

- **Not ordering the data before finding the median.**
- **Dividing by the wrong value in percentage change.** Use the old value.
- **Plotting cumulative frequency at the class midpoint.** Use the upper limit.
- **Joining scatter points instead of drawing a trend line.**
- **Extrapolating far beyond the data without warning.**
- **Confusing correlation with cause.**
- **Rounding too early.** Round only the final answer.
