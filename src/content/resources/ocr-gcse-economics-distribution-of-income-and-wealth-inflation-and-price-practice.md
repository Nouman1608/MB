## Questions

**1.** A worker in Country C earns a gross salary of £3,200 per month. Deductions are £520 income tax, £160 pension contribution and £20 union fee.

**(a)** Calculate her net pay. **[2]**
**(b)** State two types of income, other than wages and salaries. **[2]**

**2.** A household owns a house worth £150,000, savings of £8,000 and a car worth £5,000. It owes £90,000 on a mortgage and £4,000 on a personal loan.

**(a)** Calculate the household's net wealth. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain the difference between income and wealth. **[3]**

**3.** Country C has 8 million households, 1.6 million in each fifth, and total household income of £192 billion. The poorest fifth receives 5% of income and the richest fifth 48%. After taxes and benefits the shares are 7% and 41%.

**(a)** Calculate the income per household of the richest fifth before taxes and benefits. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the ratio of the richest fifth's income per household to the poorest fifth's, before taxes and benefits. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the same ratio after taxes and benefits and state what the change shows. **[2]**

**4.** Explain two causes of differences in the distribution of income in an economy. **[4]**

**5.** Evaluate the consequences of a large gap between rich and poor households for an economy. **[8]**

**6.** A worker's monthly pay rises from £1,800 to £1,854. During those months the general price index climbs from 100 to 106.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in nominal pay. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate real pay, in base-year prices, to the nearest pound. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the worker's purchasing power has changed. **[2]**

**7.** A CPI basket has four categories. Price indices this year (last year = 100) and weights are: food (weight 0.35, index 110), housing (0.30, 104), transport (0.20, 102) and other items (0.15, 98).

**(a)** Calculate the CPI for this year. **[3]**
**(b)** Next year the CPI is 107.9. Calculate the inflation rate for next year to one decimal place. **[2]**

**8.** Country D's inflation rates (illustrative) were 1.2%, 1.9%, 4.5%, 8.1%, 5.0% and 2.2% in Years 1 to 6.

**(a)** Identify the year with the highest inflation and describe the pattern of the figures. **[2]**
**(b)** An item cost £20 at the start of Year 1. Calculate its price at the end of Year 3, to the nearest penny. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why prices were still rising in Year 5. **[1]**

**9.** A saver deposits £5,000 for one year at 1.5% interest. Prices rise by 4% over the year.

**(a)** Calculate the interest earned. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the end-of-year balance in start-of-year purchasing power, to the nearest pound. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a person with a fixed-rate loan might gain from this inflation. **[1]**

**10.** Explain the difference between demand-pull and cost-push inflation, giving one cause of each. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the consequences of a period of high inflation for consumers, producers, savers and the government. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Net pay = 3,200 − 520 − 160 − 20 [1] = **£2,500** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Any two from interest on savings, rent from property, profit or dividends, benefits or pensions, one mark each [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtract every deduction listed. Candidates who stop after the tax figure give a number that is neither gross nor net.

**2. (a)** Assets = 150,000 + 8,000 + 5,000 = £163,000 [1]. Liabilities = 90,000 + 4,000 = £94,000 [1]. Net wealth = **£69,000** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Income is money received over a period of time, such as a month, so it is a flow [1]. Wealth is the value of assets owned less debts at one moment, so it is a stock [1]. For example, a salary is income, whereas a house is wealth [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A clear contrast of flow and stock, backed by an example, shows more understanding than two separate definitions.

**3. (a)** 48% of £192bn = £92.16bn [1]. Divide by 1.6 million = **£57,600** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Poorest fifth: 5% of £192bn = £9.6bn, so £6,000 per household. Ratio = 57,600 ÷ 6,000 = **9.6** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** After: 41 ÷ 7 = 8,400 and 49,200, so ratio = **5.9** [1]. The gap has narrowed because taxes and benefits redistribute income, though the richest fifth still has far more [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the households in one fifth, not all 8 million. State what the numbers show, not only the numbers.

**4.** Cause 1: differences in education and skills [1]. Higher skills raise productivity, so employers pay more and incomes rise [1]. Cause 2: ownership of assets such as property or shares [1]. These earn rent, interest or dividends on top of wages, so asset owners receive higher incomes [1]. (Other valid causes include unemployment, occupation, inheritance, discrimination or taxes and benefits.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each cause needs a stated link to income. A bare list of causes earns less than two developed ones.

**5.** Consequence 1: poverty at the bottom, with lower living standards, health and education [1], which can reduce skills and future earnings, a waste of potential [1]. Consequence 2: government spends more on benefits and collects less tax [1], with an opportunity cost for other services [1]. Consequence 3: poorer households spend most of any extra income, so a very uneven distribution can weaken consumer spending [1]. Counterpoint: some inequality rewards skills and effort and encourages enterprise, and redistributing too much might weaken incentives [1]. Evaluation: the effect depends on the size of the gap, whether people can move up, and what policies exist [1]. A reasoned judgement on which consequence matters most, with a reason [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Reserve space for evaluation. A judgement that names the factor it depends on, and then chooses, is stronger than "it depends".

**6. (a)** (1,854 − 1,800) ÷ 1,800 × 100 = **3.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Real pay = 1,854 ÷ 106 × 100 [1] = **£1,749** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Pay went up 3% in money terms, yet the price index rose 6% [1]. Real pay therefore dropped about 2.8%, so she can afford fewer goods [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the pay rise with the price rise before drawing a conclusion. Nominal growth alone does not show a gain.

**7. (a)** Weight × index: 0.35 × 110 = 38.5, 0.30 × 104 = 31.2, 0.20 × 102 = 20.4, 0.15 × 98 = 14.7 [2]. Total = **104.8** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** (107.9 − 104.8) ÷ 104.8 × 100 [1] = **3.0%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each weight times index. A single unexplained total gives little to credit if the sum slips.

**8. (a)** Year 4 had the highest inflation at 8.1% [1]. Inflation was low, rose sharply to the peak, then fell back in Years 5 and 6 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Price = 20 × 1.012 × 1.019 × 1.045 [1] = **£21.55** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Inflation was still positive, so the price level kept rising, only more slowly than in Year 4 [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the table in your description. "Inflation went up and down" is too vague.

**9. (a)** 5,000 × 1.5% = **£75** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Balance = £5,075 [1]. Real value = 5,075 ÷ 1.04 = **£4,880** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The loan is repaid in money that has lost value, so the real burden of the debt falls [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert the final balance into start-of-year prices before judging the saver. The nominal figure alone suggests a gain that does not exist.

**10.** Demand-pull inflation comes from total spending rising faster than the economy can supply [1]; one cause is a cut in interest rates that raises borrowing and spending [1]. Cost-push inflation comes from firms' costs rising and being passed on in higher prices [1]; one cause is higher energy or raw material prices [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The difference is the starting point, spending or costs. Naming the cause alone, without the type of inflation it leads to, does not answer the question.

**11.** Consumers: purchasing power falls if pay rises slower than prices [1], hurting those on fixed incomes most [1]. Producers: costs rise and prices change often, creating uncertainty that may deter investment [1], although firms that can raise prices faster than costs may protect profit [1]. Savers: savings lose real value if the interest rate is below inflation [1]. Government: tax revenue rises but so does spending, and the real value of its debt falls [1]. Judgement: the impact depends on how high, how long and how expected inflation is, and on whether pay keeps pace [1]. A supported conclusion on which group is hurt most and why [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover all four named groups, with at least one reason each. Then finish with a decision, because the same inflation can help borrowers while hurting savers.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating debts as assets, or ignoring them, in net wealth.
- Dividing a fifth's income by all households, not the households in that fifth.
- Quoting a ratio without saying what it shows.
- Adding inflation rates across years instead of multiplying.
- Using the wrong index when finding real values.
- Describing falling inflation as falling prices.
- Weights missing or not summing to 1 in a CPI calculation.
- Listing consequences with no chain of reasoning.
- Evaluating without a judgement, or giving a judgement without a reason.
