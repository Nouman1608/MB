## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between the intrinsic value and the instrumental value of a species. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the role of CITES in conserving biodiversity. **[2]**

**3.** State two limitations of NGOs as conservation organisations. **[2]**

**4.** Outline why, according to the theory of island biogeography, a large island near a mainland holds more species than a small, remote island. **[4]**

**5.** Define *land sparing*. **[1]**

**6.** Identify the three zones of a UNESCO biosphere reserve and state the main use of each. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) A conservation group must choose between two reserve designs for the same 50.3 km² area of forest. Edge effects reach 0.8 km inside every boundary.

- Design R: a rectangle 12.575 km long and 4 km wide.
- Design O: a circle of radius 4 km.

**(a)** Calculate the core area of each design. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of each design that is core. **[2]**
**(c)** Justify which design better conserves species that need interior habitat. **[2]**

**8.** (Data response) Bird surveys on five fictional islands in the same ocean give the following data.

| Island | Area / km² | Distance from mainland / km | Bird species |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 400 | 20 | 58 |
| B | 400 | 300 | 31 |
| C | 25 | 20 | 24 |
| D | 25 | 300 | 11 |
| E | 1600 | 300 | 47 |

**(a)** Using islands A and B, describe the effect of distance on species number. **[1]**
**(b)** Using islands B, D and E, describe the effect of area on species number. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the theory of island biogeography accounts for these patterns. **[4]**
**(d)** Suggest one implication of these data for the design of protected areas. **[1]**

**9.** Compare and contrast land sharing and land sparing as strategies for conserving biodiversity in farmed regions. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why community-based conservation may succeed where strict protection that excludes local people fails. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the reintroduction of a top predator as a method of rewilding an ecosystem. **[6]**

**12.** "Conservation should be based on the intrinsic value of species, not on their usefulness to people." Discuss this statement with reference to different environmental value systems. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Intrinsic value is the worth of a species in itself, regardless of human use [1]; instrumental value is its worth because of what it provides to people, such as food, medicine or ecosystem services [1]. **[2]**

**2.** CITES regulates international trade in listed endangered species and their products [1] through a system of permits, with the most threatened species protected from almost all commercial trade [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two: no power to make or enforce laws [1]; dependent on donations, so funding is uncertain [1]; may focus on charismatic species or single issues; may lack the trust of local communities or governments. **[2]**

**4.** A large island has larger populations, so the extinction rate is lower [1], and more habitat types, so more niches for species [1]. An island near the mainland receives colonists more often [1], so its immigration rate is higher [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Both mechanisms, extinction and immigration, need naming; "bigger islands have more space" earns one mark at most.

**5.** Farming a smaller area intensively for high yields so that the land saved can be protected as natural habitat [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Core: strictly protected for conservation [1]. Buffer zone: research, education and low-impact uses [1]. Transition zone: people live and work, using resources sustainably [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** R: core = (12.575 − 1.6) × (4 − 1.6) = 10.975 × 2.4 [1] = **26.34 km²** [1]. O: core radius = 4 − 0.8 = 3.2 km; core = π × 3.2² [1] = **32.17 km²** [1].
**(b)** R: 26.34 ÷ 50.3 × 100 = **52.4%** [1]; O: 32.17 ÷ 50.27 × 100 = **64.0%** [1] (accept 50.3 as the total for both).
**(c)** Design O has the shorter boundary for the same area, so less of it is affected by edge effects such as light, wind, drying and invasive species [1]; its larger core supports larger populations of interior species, which are less likely to die out [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* For the rectangle, the edge depth comes off both ends of each side (2 × 0.8 = 1.6 km).

**8. (a)** At the same area (400 km²), the near island A has 58 species and the remote island B only 31, so species number falls with distance [1].
**(b)** At the same distance (300 km), species number rises with area: 11 species at 25 km², 31 at 400 km² and 47 at 1600 km² [1]; the increase slows as area grows (a 16-fold increase from D to B adds 20 species, a further 4-fold increase adds 16) [1].
**(c)** Species number reflects a balance between immigration and extinction [1]. Near islands receive more colonists, so immigration is higher [1]. Large islands hold larger populations and more habitat types [1], so extinction rates are lower [1].
**(d)** Reserves should be as large as possible and close to other habitat or linked by corridors, so species can recolonise [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Both aim to conserve biodiversity while producing food [1]. Land sparing farms less land intensively and protects separate habitat, whereas land sharing spreads lower-intensity, wildlife-friendly farming over more land [1]. Sparing suits species needing large undisturbed areas, whereas sharing suits species that can live in farmland and keeps ecosystem services on farms [1]. Sparing risks pollution and further clearance if intensive farming is profitable, whereas sharing needs more land for the same yield [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Strict protection that excludes local people can remove their access to land, food and income [1], so they may resent the reserve and continue hunting or clearing illegally [1]. Community-based conservation gives local people a share of benefits, such as tourism income or sustainable harvests [1], and uses their knowledge in management, so they have a reason to protect the area and help enforce rules [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative points, one mark each:
- Predators can restore top-down control of herbivores, reducing overgrazing [1].
- This may allow vegetation, and the species that depend on it, to recover (a trophic cascade) [1].
- Example: grey wolves reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995 have been linked to changes in elk behaviour and numbers [1].
- Limitation: the size of such cascades is debated, because other factors such as climate and other predators also changed [1].
- Limitation: conflict with farmers over livestock losses, which needs compensation or protection measures [1].
- Judgement: effective where habitat is large and connected and local support exists, but not a guaranteed or quick solution [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so use these points as a guide:
- Ecocentric value systems hold that species have intrinsic value and a right to exist [1].
- Intrinsic arguments protect species with no known use, which purely economic arguments would neglect [1].
- Technocentric or anthropocentric value systems stress instrumental value: medicines, crop genes, ecosystem services [1].
- Instrumental arguments, such as valuing pollination or flood control, can persuade governments and businesses to fund conservation [1].
- But economic arguments may fail when another land use pays more, so protection becomes conditional [1].
- Many species' uses are unknown, so a use-based approach risks losing future value [1].
- Cultural and spiritual values of indigenous peoples combine intrinsic and relational value [1].
- Non-use values such as existence and bequest value bridge the two positions [1].
- Judgement: intrinsic value gives the strongest ethical basis, but instrumental arguments are often needed in practice to secure funding and support [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the edge depth off only one side of a rectangle, or twice from a radius.
- Describing island data without quoting areas, distances and species numbers.
- Explaining island biogeography without the words immigration and extinction.
- Evaluating rewilding with benefits only.
- Discussing values without naming the value systems.
