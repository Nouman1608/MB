## Section A

**1.** Explain why rocks and minerals are described as finite resources. **[2]**

**2.** State the difference between reusing and recycling a material. **[2]**

**3.** Name the **three** conditions that the syllabus links to successful recycling. **[3]**

**4.** Give **one** example of an alternative material that can replace a scarce mineral. Name the material that is replaced and the material that replaces it. **[2]**

**5.** Define sustainable management of resources. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows household metal waste in three towns in one year. The data are invented for practice.

| Town | Metal waste (tonnes) | Metal recycled (tonnes) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 12,000 | 4,800 | Collection from every home; sorting guide sent to every household |
| B | 9,000 | 2,700 | Collection points in each district |
| C | 15,000 | 1,500 | One collection point, 12 km from the town centre; no publicity |

**(a)** Calculate the recycling rate for Town B. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the notes, suggest **two** reasons why Town C has the lowest recycling rate. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **one** further strategy Town C could use to increase recycling. **[1]**

**7.** A country has a known reserve of 900 million tonnes of an ore and currently extracts 30 million tonnes a year.

**(a)** Calculate how many years the reserve will last at the current rate. **[1]**

**(b)** Reduction, reuse and recycling cut annual extraction by 40%. Calculate the new lifetime of the reserve. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the reserve may in fact run out sooner than your answer to (b). **[2]**

**8.** Describe how increased efficiency of extraction helps to conserve a mineral, and state **one** limitation of this strategy. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why legislation on mineral resources is only effective when it is combined with enforcement. Use an example in your answer. **[4]**

**10.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of using alternative materials to conserve rocks, ores and minerals. **[4]**

**11.** A government wants to reduce the amount of newly quarried rock used to build roads. Suggest and explain **three** strategies it could use. **[6]**

**12.** "Recycling on its own can make the use of metals sustainable." Discuss this statement. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** They are used up at a faster rate than they are replaced [1]; they form through the rock cycle over millions of years, whereas people extract them within decades [1].

**2.** Reusing means using the object again, for the same or another purpose, without processing it [1]; recycling means processing the waste so that it becomes a raw material for new products [1].

**3.** Accessibility [1]; ease [1]; education [1].

**4.** One correct pair, for example: copper cable [1] replaced by fibre-optic (glass) cable [1]; or copper pipes [1] replaced by plastic pipes [1]; or newly quarried rock [1] replaced by recycled crushed concrete [1].

**5.** The use of strategies that ensure the needs of the present are met [1] without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs [1].

**6. (a)** 2,700 ÷ 9,000 × 100 [1] = 30% [1].

**(b)** Any two: the single collection point is far from the town centre, so recycling is not accessible [1]; there is no publicity, so people do not know what or why to recycle (lack of education) [1]; there is no collection from homes, so recycling is not easy [1].

**(c)** Any one: introduce collection from homes [1]; set up more collection points in each district [1]; run an education campaign or send a sorting guide [1]; pass a law requiring metal to be separated from other waste [1].

**7. (a)** 900 ÷ 30 = 30 years [1].

**(b)** New extraction = 30 × 0.6 = 18 million tonnes a year [1]; 900 ÷ 18 = 50 years [1].

**(c)** Any two: demand may rise with population growth or industrial development [1]; part of the reserve may be too costly or difficult to extract [1]; the strategies may not achieve the full 40% cut, for example if recycling rates fall [1].

**8.** Better technology recovers more of the mineral from each tonne of ore, or recovers mineral from old tailings and spoil [1]; this means less ore needs to be mined for the same output, so the deposit lasts longer [1]. Limitation, any one: the technology is expensive; the mineral is still finite and is still being used up [1].

**9.** Legislation sets rules, for example requiring mining companies to restore land or limiting how much may be extracted [1]. Without enforcement, companies may ignore the law to save money [1]. Inspections and monitoring identify those who break the law [1], and fines or the loss of a licence deter others from doing the same [1].

**10.** Any four, with at least one benefit and one limitation:
- Benefit: reduces demand for the scarce mineral, so less needs to be mined [1].
- Benefit: the alternative may perform better, for example fibre-optic cable carries more data than copper [1].
- Benefit: recycled alternatives, such as crushed concrete, reduce quarrying and waste at the same time [1].
- Limitation: the alternative may itself be finite or harmful, for example plastics are made from petroleum and add to plastic pollution [1].
- Limitation: alternatives may be more expensive, or need new equipment and skills [1].

**11.** Three strategies, each identified and explained, 2 marks each:
- **Use of alternative materials**: use recycled crushed concrete or waste rock from demolition [1], which replaces some newly quarried rock [1].
- **Legislation**: require a set percentage of recycled material in new roads, or tax newly quarried rock [1], making recycled material the cheaper choice [1].
- **Reduce**: design roads that need less material, or repair existing roads rather than rebuilding them [1], so less rock is needed in total [1].
- **Enforcement**: inspect road projects and fine contractors who do not meet the rules [1], so the law is followed [1].
- **Increased efficiency of extraction**: use more of the rock from each quarry instead of discarding it [1], so fewer new quarries are opened [1].

**12.** Levels-based. Credit up to 6 from:
- **For**: recycling reduces demand for new ore, so less mining and habitat loss [1]; metals can be recycled many times, so a stock already extracted can keep meeting needs [1]; recycling usually needs less energy than processing new ore [1].
- **Against**: recycling can only supply as much metal as is thrown away, so it cannot meet a rising demand [1]; collection depends on accessibility, ease and education, so rates are often low [1]; some metal is lost in mixed products and landfill and cannot be recovered [1]; collection and processing still use energy and money [1].
- **Judgement**: recycling helps but must be combined with reduce and reuse, efficient extraction, legislation and enforcement to be sustainable, so the statement is not fully correct [1].

Maximum 6; a one-sided answer scores no more than 4.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1**: "they will run out" without the idea of rates. Use the syllabus definition.
- **Question 2**: describing reuse as "recycling at home". Reuse involves no processing.
- **Question 6(b)**: copying the notes without naming the condition (accessibility, ease or education) they show.
- **Question 7(b)**: dividing by 40% of the extraction rather than the 60% that remains.
- **Question 12**: agreeing or disagreeing without weighing both sides and giving a reasoned judgement.
