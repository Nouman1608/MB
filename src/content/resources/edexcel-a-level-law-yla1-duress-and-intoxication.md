This guide covers content areas **2.2.8** and **2.2.9** of Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Law, Issue 4, listed under "General defences": "Duress of threats and duress of circumstances" and "Voluntary and involuntary intoxication". The [criminal offender study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-law-yla1-the-criminal-offender/) states the *Graham* test, the exclusion of murder and the *Majewski* rule in one paragraph each. This page builds each defence into a step-by-step test with the cases on every step, so that you can apply it to a scenario in Paper 2.

## How general defences work

A general defence can apply to many offences. The defendant must raise **evidence** of it; the **prosecution** must then disprove it **beyond reasonable doubt**. Duress is a **complete defence**: if it succeeds, the defendant is acquitted. Intoxication is not really a defence at all: it is a way of arguing that the defendant lacked the *mens rea*.

## Duress of threats

Duress applies where the defendant commits a crime because someone threatens them with death or serious injury unless they do so. Lord Bingham set out the limits in *R v Hasan* [2005]. Work through these steps in order:

**1. A threat of death or serious injury.**
- Threats to expose secrets or damage property are not enough. In *R v Valderrama-Vega* [1985] threats to reveal the defendant's sexuality and financial pressure were present, but only the threat of death or serious injury counted; the jury may take the threats together provided that threat was one of the causes.
- *R v Quayle* [2005]: pain from illness is not a threat of death or serious injury, so taking cannabis to relieve it could not be excused.

**2. Against the defendant, their immediate family, or someone for whom they reasonably feel responsible** (*Hasan*).

**3. The threat must be directed at the commission of a particular crime.** In *R v Cole* [1994] moneylenders threatened the defendant, who robbed two building societies to repay them. They had not told him to commit robbery, so duress failed.

**4. Immediacy and no chance to escape.** The defendant must reasonably believe that the threat will be carried out **immediately or almost immediately**, and there must be no evasive action they could reasonably take, such as going to the police (*Hasan*). *Hasan* disapproved the more generous approach in *R v Hudson and Taylor* [1971], where two young witnesses lied in court after threats, even though the threatener was in the public gallery and could have been reported.

**5. The *Graham* test** (*R v Graham* [1982], approved in *R v Howe* [1987]):
- **Subjective:** was the defendant compelled to act as they did because they **reasonably believed** they faced death or serious injury?
- **Objective:** would a **sober person of reasonable firmness**, sharing the defendant's characteristics, have responded in the same way?

Which characteristics count? In *R v Bowen* [1996] the Court of Appeal said relevant characteristics include **age, sex, pregnancy, serious physical disability and recognised psychiatric illness**, but not a low IQ that falls short of mental impairment, nor being unusually timid or vulnerable.

**6. Not available after voluntarily associating with criminals.** If the defendant voluntarily joined a criminal gang or associated with people they knew or **ought reasonably to have known** might pressure them with violence, duress is unavailable (*Hasan*). Earlier cases show the development: in *R v Sharp* [1987] a member of a violent robbery gang could not use duress; in *R v Shepherd* [1987] a member of a non-violent shoplifting gang could, because he could not have foreseen violence.

**7. Not available for murder or attempted murder.** *R v Howe* [1987] (murder, whether as principal or accessory); *R v Gotts* [1992] (attempted murder); and in *R v Wilson* [2007] a 13-year-old who helped his father kill his mother under threats could not rely on duress either.

## Duress of circumstances

The same test applies where the threat comes from the **situation** rather than from a person's demand that the defendant commit a particular crime.

- *R v Willer* (1986): a driver mounted the pavement to escape a gang threatening him; the defence should have been left to the jury.
- *R v Conway* [1988]: a driver sped away from two men in plain clothes he feared were attackers (they were police officers).
- *R v Martin* [1989]: a disqualified driver drove his stepson to work because his wife threatened suicide if he did not.
- *DPP v Bell* [1992]: a drunk man drove off to escape an attack; the defence was available because he drove only as far as necessary.
- *R v Pommell* [1995]: a man took a gun from someone intending to shoot another and kept it overnight; the court held the defence could be left to the jury, but the defendant must stop committing the offence as soon as the danger has passed.
- *R v Abdul-Hussain* [1999]: Iraqi men who hijacked a plane to escape feared execution; the trial judge was wrong to withdraw the defence.

## Intoxication

### The key distinction: specific and basic intent

| | Specific intent offences | Basic intent offences |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Murder; s 18 OAPA 1861; theft; robbery; s 9(1)(a) burglary with intent to steal | Manslaughter; s 20 and s 47 OAPA 1861; assault; criminal damage |
| Voluntary intoxication | Can be a defence **if** D was so intoxicated that they did not form the intent | **No defence** (*DPP v Majewski* [1977]) |

**Specific intent.** In *R v Sheehan and Moore* [1975] two drunk men poured petrol on a homeless man and set him alight. The question was whether, drunk or not, they had formed the intent. **A drunken intent is still an intent.** If D was too intoxicated to form it, they are acquitted of the specific intent offence but usually convicted of a basic intent fallback, such as manslaughter instead of murder.

**Basic intent.** In *DPP v Majewski* [1977] the House of Lords held that becoming voluntarily intoxicated is itself a reckless course of conduct, which supplies the *mens rea* for basic intent offences. In *R v Lipman* [1970] the defendant killed his girlfriend while hallucinating on LSD; he was guilty of manslaughter. In *R v Heard* [2007] the Court of Appeal treated sexual assault as a basic intent offence for this purpose.

**Dutch courage.** If D forms the intent while sober and drinks to gain the courage to act, intoxication is no defence even to a specific intent offence (*Attorney General for Northern Ireland v Gallagher* [1963]).

### Involuntary intoxication

Intoxication is involuntary where D's drink is **spiked**, or where D takes a drug in accordance with a prescription or a **non-dangerous** drug that has an **unexpected** effect.

- **No intent formed:** D has a defence to **both** specific and basic intent offences, because there was no reckless choice to become intoxicated.
- **Intent formed:** no defence. In *R v Kingston* [1994] the defendant's coffee was drugged and he sexually assaulted a boy; he had formed the intent, and "a drugged intent is still an intent".
- **Sedatives:** in *R v Hardie* [1985] the defendant took Valium, generally a calming drug, and set fire to a wardrobe; the jury should have considered whether taking it was reckless.
- **Knowing it is alcohol is enough to make it voluntary:** in *R v Allen* [1988] a man who did not realise how strong the wine was had still chosen to drink alcohol, so his intoxication was voluntary.

### Intoxicated mistakes

A defendant who, because of voluntary intoxication, mistakenly believes they need to defend themselves **cannot** rely on that mistake (*R v O'Grady* [1987]; *R v Hatton* [2005]). The exception is criminal damage, where s 5(2) of the Criminal Damage Act 1971 allows an honest belief, even a drunken one (*Jaggard v Dickinson* [1981]).

## Worked example

*Ravi owes money to a gang. They tell him to set fire to a rival's shop that night or they will break his legs; they wait outside his flat. Before going, he drinks heavily "for courage". At the shop he punches a passer-by he wrongly thinks is about to attack him.*

```
Arson (criminal damage by fire)
  Duress   threat of serious injury; nominated crime (Cole satisfied);
           immediacy and no escape? The gang is outside: arguable.
  BUT      he owed money to a gang; if he knew or ought to have known
           they might use violence, voluntary association (Hasan) removes
           the defence.
  Drink    Dutch courage: no defence (Gallagher); in any case criminal
           damage is basic intent (Majewski).
Assault on the passer-by
  Mistake  drunken belief in the need for self-defence: not available
           (O'Grady; Hatton).
```

## Common mistakes

- Allowing duress for murder or attempted murder.
- Skipping the immediacy and escape step, or applying the old *Hudson and Taylor* approach after *Hasan*.
- Using a characteristic *Bowen* excluded, such as low IQ, in the objective test.
- Treating intoxication as a defence to a basic intent offence.
- Saying involuntary intoxication is always a defence: not if intent was formed (*Kingston*).

## Exam technique

In a scenario, take **one defence at a time** after establishing the offence: state each element, apply the facts, cite the case and conclude. For **evaluate** questions, discuss whether excluding murder from duress (*Howe*) and the *Majewski* rule are justified, weighing protection of the public against fairness to individual defendants.
