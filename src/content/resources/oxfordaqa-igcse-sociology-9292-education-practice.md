## Section A: short questions

**1.** Identify **two** levels of education. **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by **formal** education? **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** types of school, other than state schools. **[2]**

**4.** What is meant by the **halo effect** in schools? **[2]**

**5.** Study **Item A** and answer the questions that follow.

> **Item A** — Adult literacy rate (%) by sex in four countries

**(a)** Using Item A, identify the country with the largest gap between male and female literacy, and state the size of the gap. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**6.** Describe what functionalists mean by **role allocation**. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **one** way in which Bourdieu's idea of cultural capital helps to explain class differences in attainment. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which pupil subcultures may form in schools. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which poverty can affect children's education in different parts of the world. **[6]**

## Answers: Section A

**A1** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: nursery or pre-school; primary; secondary; tertiary.

**A2** [2 marks]

Education that takes place in **institutions such as schools, colleges or universities** [1], with a **planned curriculum**, trained teachers and usually qualifications [1].

**A3** [2 marks — 1 each]

Any two: private; selective; non-selective; faith or religious; international; boarding or residential; progressive; vocational.

**A4** [2 marks]

When a teacher's **positive impression of one feature** of a pupil, such as good behaviour or neat work [1], **shapes their judgement of the pupil in general**, so the pupil is seen as able across the board [1].

**A5 (a)** [2 marks]

**Country J** [1]; the gap is **21 percentage points** (79 − 58) [1].

**A5 (b)** [3 marks — 1 for the reason, 2 for development]

e.g. **Gendered expectations** [1]: in some societies girls are expected to do domestic work or marry early [1], so they leave school sooner and fewer women learn to read and write [1]. Or: **poverty** [1]: when families cannot afford fees for every child, they may prioritise sons [1], so girls miss out on schooling [1]. Or: **safety** concerns about travel to school [1] [1].

## Answers: Section B

**A6** [4 marks]

Davis and Moore argued that education **sorts and selects** pupils by ability [1], through examinations and qualifications [1]. This ensures that the **most able** are allocated to the **most important jobs** in society [1], so society runs efficiently and rewards are based on merit [1].

**A7** [4 marks]

**Cultural capital** is the knowledge, language, tastes and attitudes valued by schools [1]. Middle-class children are more likely to gain it at home, through books, museum visits and educated conversation [1]. Schools reward these qualities, so these children find schoolwork easier and are seen as able [1]. Working-class children lack this advantage, so class inequality is **reproduced** while appearing to result from ability [1].

**A8** [6 marks — up to 3 per way]

Two from: **labelling and setting** [1] — pupils placed in low sets and labelled as failures [1] may form an anti-school subculture that rejects the school's values to gain status from peers [1]; **class background** [1] — Willis's lads saw school as irrelevant to the manual work they expected [1], so they valued "having a laff" over achievement [1]; **positive labelling** [1] — pupils rewarded by the school [1] form pro-school subcultures that value achievement [1]; **racism or sexism** [1] — pupils who feel unfairly treated [1] may form groups that resist the school [1].

**A9** [6 marks — up to 3 per way]

Two from: **child labour** [1] — poor families may need children to work [1], so they miss school or drop out early [1]; **costs** [1] — fees, uniforms and materials [1] can be unaffordable, particularly for large families [1]; **underfunded schools** [1] — poor areas may have large classes, few trained teachers and poor buildings [1], lowering the quality of learning [1]; **digital inequality** [1] — without devices or internet [1], pupils could not continue learning when schools closed during the COVID-19 pandemic [1].

**A10** [12 marks — levels]

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Detailed knowledge; well applied to a named society; balanced discussion with a clear, justified conclusion |
| 3 | 7–9 | Good knowledge; some application; discussion with a conclusion |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some relevant knowledge; limited application or one-sided |
| 1 | 1–3 | Basic, mainly descriptive points |

Indicative content:

- **Equal opportunity**: functionalist view of meritocracy and role allocation (Davis and Moore); free, compulsory state education; anti-discrimination policies; scholarships.
- **Unequal opportunity**: Bowles and Gintis's myth of meritocracy; Bourdieu's cultural capital; private and selective schools buying advantage; material deprivation; labelling and the ideal pupil (Becker); ethnocentric curricula; gender barriers in some societies.
- **Application**: a specific society the candidate knows, such as fee-paying international schools alongside underfunded state schools.
- **Judgement**: for example, schools offer formal equality of access, but outcomes still differ by class, gender and ethnicity.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating the literacy gap from the wrong columns, or giving a country with no figure.
- Describing cultural capital as money.
- Naming Willis without explaining why the lads rejected school.
- Treating poverty as a single point rather than explaining how it affects education.
- An extended answer with no example from a named society.
