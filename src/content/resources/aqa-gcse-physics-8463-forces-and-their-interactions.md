## 4.5.1.1 Scalars and vectors

A **scalar** has magnitude (size) only. A **vector** has magnitude **and** an associated direction.

| Scalars | Vectors |
|---|---|
| distance, speed, mass, time, energy, temperature | displacement, velocity, force, acceleration, momentum, weight |

A vector is drawn as an **arrow**. The arrow's length represents the magnitude, using a stated scale, and the
arrow points in the vector's direction. With a scale of 1 cm = 5 N, a 35 N force is a 7.0 cm arrow.

Test any quantity by asking: "Does it make sense to say which way?" "A speed of 12 m/s north" mixes ideas; that
is a velocity.

## 4.5.1.2 Contact and non-contact forces

A **force** is a push or pull that acts on an object because of its **interaction with another object**.
Every force between objects is one of two kinds.

| Contact forces (objects touching) | Non-contact forces (objects separated) |
|---|---|
| friction, air resistance, tension, normal contact force | gravitational force, electrostatic force, magnetic force |

**Worked example 1.** A steel paper clip hangs on a thread below a magnet but does not touch it. The thread is
pulled tight. Name every force on the clip and classify it.

```
weight (gravitational, Earth on clip)     non-contact, downwards
magnetic force (magnet on clip)            non-contact, upwards
tension (thread on clip)                   contact, along the thread
```

### Interaction pairs

An interaction always produces **a force on each of the two objects**. The two forces in a pair:

- are the **same type** of force;
- have **equal size**;
- act in **opposite directions**;
- act on **different objects**.

The Earth pulls a falling apple down with a gravitational force; the apple pulls the Earth up with an equal
gravitational force. Because each force acts on a different object, the two never cancel each other. A book
resting on a table pushes down on the table with a normal contact force, and the table pushes up on the book
with an equal normal contact force. Draw each force as an arrow starting on the object it acts on.

A common trap: the book's **weight** and the table's **upward push** on the book are equal and opposite, but
they are **not** an interaction pair. They are different types of force acting on the same object.

## 4.5.1.3 Gravity

**Weight** is the force acting on an object due to gravity. Close to the Earth, it is caused by the Earth's
gravitational field. Weight depends on the **gravitational field strength** where the object is.

```
W = m g      W in newtons (N), m in kilograms (kg), g in newtons per kilogram (N/kg)
```

You must recall this equation. In every calculation the value of g is given.

- Weight acts at a single point called the object's **centre of mass**.
- Weight and mass are **directly proportional**: W ∝ m. Double the mass, double the weight.
- Weight is measured with a calibrated **spring-balance (newtonmeter)**.

**Worked example 2.** A rover has a mass of 180 kg. Find its weight on Mars, where g = 3.7 N/kg, and the mass
of a rock that weighs 26 N there.

```
W = m g = 180 × 3.7 = 666 N  (670 N to 2 s.f.)
m = W / g = 26 / 3.7 = 7.0 kg
```

The rover's mass is 180 kg on Earth, on Mars and in space. Only its weight changes.

| Mass | Weight |
|---|---|
| Amount of matter; a scalar | A force; a vector, acting downwards towards the planet |
| Kilograms (kg) | Newtons (N) |
| Same everywhere | Depends on g |

## 4.5.1.4 Resultant forces

The **resultant force** is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting on an object
together.

For forces along one line, choose a positive direction, add forces that way and subtract forces the other way.

**Worked example 3.** A boat is pushed forwards by an engine force of 1800 N. Water resistance is 950 N and a
headwind pushes back with 350 N. Find the resultant force.

```
taking forwards as positive:  1800 − 950 − 350 = +500 N
resultant = 500 N forwards
```

If the resultant is zero, the forces are **balanced**.

### Free body diagrams (Higher tier only)

A **free body diagram** shows one isolated object as a dot or box, with an arrow for **every** force acting on
it and nothing else. For a crate pulled at a steady speed across a floor by a horizontal rope:

- weight, downwards, from the centre;
- normal contact force from the floor, upwards, equal in size to the weight;
- tension, forwards, along the rope;
- friction, backwards, equal in size to the tension.

The arrows pair up and cancel, so the resultant is zero. Use the diagram to describe qualitatively what
happens if one force changes: if the rope tension increases, the forwards force is now larger than friction
and there is a resultant force forwards.

### Resolving a force (Higher tier only)

A single force can be **resolved** into two **components at right angles**. Together, the two components
have the same effect as the original force. The specification asks for **scale drawings only**.

**Worked example 4.** A child pulls a sledge with a force of 60 N along a rope at 40° above the horizontal.
Use a scale drawing to find the horizontal and vertical components.

```
1  choose a scale: 1 cm = 10 N, so draw the 60 N force as a 6.0 cm line at 40° to the horizontal
2  from the start, draw a horizontal line; from the arrow tip, draw a vertical line down to meet it
3  measure: horizontal line ≈ 4.6 cm, vertical line ≈ 3.9 cm
4  convert: horizontal component ≈ 46 N, vertical component ≈ 39 N
```

The horizontal component moves the sledge forwards; the vertical component slightly lifts it, reducing the
force between sledge and snow.

### Resultant of two forces at an angle (Higher tier only)

Draw the forces **tip to tail** to scale, then draw the resultant from the start of the first arrow to the
tip of the last. Measure its length for the magnitude and its angle for the direction.

**Worked example 5.** Two tugs pull a ship: 30 kN due east and 40 kN due north.

```
scale 1 cm = 10 kN:  draw 3.0 cm east, then 4.0 cm north from its tip
resultant line measures 5.0 cm  →  50 kN
angle measured with a protractor ≈ 53° north of east
```

### Equilibrium (Higher tier only)

If the force arrows drawn tip to tail form a **closed shape**, the resultant is zero and the object is in
equilibrium. A picture hanging from two strings is in equilibrium when the two tensions and its weight, drawn
tip to tail, make a closed triangle. If a gap remains, the arrow needed to close it is the force that would
restore equilibrium; the resultant is equal in size to it but in the opposite direction.

## Common mistakes

- Calling weight a scalar, or measuring it in kilograms.
- Saying the weight of a book and the table's push on it are an interaction pair.
- Using g = 10 N/kg when the question gives 9.8 N/kg.
- Adding all forces without signs, so 1800 N forwards and 950 N backwards gives 2750 N.
- Forgetting the direction when stating a resultant force.
- Drawing a vector diagram with no scale, or measuring angles from the wrong line.
- Including forces the object exerts on other things in a free body diagram.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **give**: a short answer, such as one contact force.
- **Describe** an interaction: name both objects, the type of force, equal size and opposite directions.
- **Calculate**: write the equation, substitute with units, give the answer with a unit and, for a resultant,
  a direction.
- **Determine** using a scale drawing: state your scale, draw with a sharp pencil and ruler, and show the
  measured length before converting.
