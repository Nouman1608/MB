This topic is less about learning one more set of events and more about learning how historians argue about events you already know. The same evidence has produced very different accounts of why the First World War began, how the Holocaust came about and why the Cold War started. Your task is to explain those differences, judge which arguments are better supported and write your own reasoned conclusion.

## Where this fits in the specification

Historical interpretations is one of the A Level topics in Cambridge 9489, with three themes: the origins of the First World War, the Holocaust, and the origins and development of the Cold War. It draws on knowledge from the wider course, but the skill being tested is different. It is examined on **Paper 3** (1 hour 15 minutes, 40 marks, 20% of the A Level): one unattributed extract of no more than 600 words from a historian is set on each topic, and you answer the single 40-mark question on your topic: "What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it?" Marks are split equally between historical knowledge (AO1) and historical interpretations (AO4). You are not asked to name the author.

## What an interpretation is

An interpretation is not just an opinion. It is an argument about the past that rests on selected evidence and is shaped by the author's question, sources, method and context. Three things are worth asking of any extract.

1. **What is the argument?** State it in one sentence, including the cause or factor emphasised.
2. **What is it built on?** Identify the evidence the author appears to rely on and what they leave out.
3. **Why might it look like this?** Consider when the historian wrote, which archives were available and what questions mattered at the time.

Distinguish a **difference of emphasis** (two historians agree on the causes but rank them differently) from a **difference of kind** (they disagree about who was responsible or what happened).

## The origins of the First World War

**Responsibility for the war.** Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles (1919) placed responsibility for the war on Germany and its allies, a position that shaped early writing. In the 1920s and 1930s many historians moved away from single-country blame; David Lloyd George's remark that the powers had "slithered" into war captures the idea of shared drift.

**The Fischer thesis.** In 1961 Fritz Fischer published *Griff nach der Weltmacht* ("Grasp for World Power"). He argued that Germany's leaders pursued expansionist aims and took a calculated risk in July 1914, backed by what has been called the "September Programme" of war aims. The argument reopened debate on German responsibility and linked policy before 1914 to policy after it. Critics asked whether war aims developed during the war can be read back into 1914.

**Structural and system arguments.** Other historians stress alliances, arms races, nationalism and the diplomatic system, arguing that no state alone created the situation.

**Recent work.** Christopher Clark's *The Sleepwalkers* (2012) emphasises the many decision-makers in several capitals and presents the outbreak as the result of choices made under uncertainty, not of one nation's plan. Critics respond that it reduces the weight of German decisions in July 1914.

## The Holocaust

The central debate is how the persecution of Jewish people moved towards mass murder.

- **Intentionalist interpretations** argue that Hitler's antisemitic ideology and plans drove events from early on.
- **Functionalist (structuralist) interpretations** emphasise the improvised, competing and radicalising nature of Nazi government. Mommsen and Broszat are associated with the view that policy escalated through rivalry and local initiative, often described as cumulative radicalisation.
- **Syntheses** combine ideology with circumstance: war, particularly the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, created conditions in which decisions were taken, and the Wannsee Conference of January 1942 co-ordinated an existing programme.

Two further debates concern perpetrators. Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992) studied Reserve Police Battalion 101 and argued that conformity, peer pressure and career concerns were important alongside ideology. Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* (1996) argued that an eliminationist antisemitism was widespread in German society, a thesis that was widely criticised by other historians. Raul Hilberg's early work on the machinery of destruction shaped later study of how bureaucracy contributed.

When you use these writers, be careful to represent their arguments accurately and avoid turning disagreement into caricature.

## The origins and development of the Cold War

Interpretations are often grouped into three schools.

- **Orthodox (traditionalist).** Writing in the West soon after the war, this school blamed Soviet expansion in Eastern Europe and Stalin's ideology. It reads Western policy such as the Truman Doctrine (1947) and the Marshall Plan (1947) as defensive.
- **Revisionist.** From the 1960s historians such as William Appleman Williams and Gabriel Kolko stressed American economic aims and the pursuit of an open world economy, presenting Soviet behaviour as largely defensive.
- **Post-revisionist.** Historians such as John Lewis Gaddis, in early work, combined factors, including misperception and security fears on both sides. Later work drawing on Soviet archives, such as Gaddis's *We Now Know* (1997), gave more weight to Stalin's role and ideology.

Documents often used in this debate include Kennan's Long Telegram (1946), the Novikov telegram of the same year, the Berlin blockade and airlift (1948-49) and the creation of NATO (1949). Notice how the interpretation changes depending on which documents are given most weight, and on when archives opened.

## Worked example: planning a Paper 3 answer

**Task:** an unattributed extract argues that Germany's leaders accepted, and in part sought, a general war in July 1914 to break out of "encirclement" and to achieve European dominance. Question: "What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it?"

1. **Main interpretation (whole extract).** Germany bore the main responsibility: its leaders took a calculated risk of general war for expansionist aims. State this in one sentence before anything else.
2. **Approach.** The historian focuses on German decision-making and war aims, using German government documents; the questions asked are about intention and responsibility, in the tradition associated with Fritz Fischer.
3. **Test with knowledge (support).** The "blank cheque" to Austria-Hungary (5–6 July 1914), the Schlieffen Plan's dependence on attacking France through Belgium, and the September Programme of 1914.
4. **Test with knowledge (limits).** Decisions in Vienna and St Petersburg (the ultimatum to Serbia, Russian general mobilisation on 30 July) suggest shared responsibility, the view of historians such as Christopher Clark.
5. **Conclusion.** Explain what the extract shows about the historian's claims and approach as a whole, rather than listing sub-messages.

**Timing check.** Paper 3 gives 75 minutes for one answer. A 10-minute plan leaves 65 minutes to write: for example 5 minutes for an opening that states the interpretation, about 50 minutes for four or five paragraphs testing it with knowledge, and 10 minutes for a conclusion and a final check: 10 + 5 + 50 + 10 = 75.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a historian as proof rather than as an argument to be tested.
- Summarising the extract instead of evaluating it.
- Using names and book titles without explaining what the argument is.
- Judging an interpretation only by its date ("it is old so it is wrong").
- Writing a general account of events with no reference to the interpretations.

## Exam technique

Paper 3 always uses the command phrase "What can you learn...". State the extract's overall interpretation early, then explain the historian's approach and support your reading with brief quotation or close paraphrase from the extract. Use your own knowledge to test the interpretation, not just to add information. Keep an eye on timing and end with a reasoned conclusion.

## Check your understanding

1. What did Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles do?
   *It stated the responsibility of Germany and its allies for the war.*
2. What was Fritz Fischer's main argument?
   *That Germany's leaders pursued expansionist aims and deliberately risked war in 1914.*
3. Distinguish intentionalist from functionalist interpretations of the Holocaust.
   *Intentionalists stress planned ideological goals; functionalists stress improvised, escalating policy within a competitive system.*
4. What did Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 suggest?
   *That ordinary men took part in killing through factors such as conformity and peer pressure as well as ideology.*
5. Name the three main schools of Cold War interpretation.
   *Orthodox, revisionist and post-revisionist.*
6. Why might a historian writing after the Soviet archives opened reach different conclusions?
   *They can use Soviet evidence about decision-making, which earlier writers lacked.*
7. How long is Paper 3, and how many questions do you answer?
   *1 hour 15 minutes; one 40-mark question on one extract.*
8. Why is it weak to dismiss an interpretation just because it is old?
   *Its quality depends on the evidence and reasoning, and older work may still raise valid points.*

## Frequently asked questions

**How is Paper 3 structured?**
One unattributed historian's extract of up to 600 words on each topic and one 40-mark question on your topic, in 1 hour 15 minutes; it is worth 20% of the A Level.

**Do I need to name the historian?**
No. The author is not identified and you are not asked to identify them; explain the interpretation and approach instead.

**What are the three Cold War schools?**
Orthodox (blaming Soviet expansion), revisionist (stressing US economic and political aims) and post-revisionist (stressing mutual suspicion and misperception).
