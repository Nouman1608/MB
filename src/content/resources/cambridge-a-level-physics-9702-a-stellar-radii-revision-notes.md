Subtopic 25.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers Wien's displacement law, the Stefan-Boltzmann law and using both to estimate the radius of a star. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Stellar radii study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-stellar-radii/); to apply the ideas, try the [Stellar radii practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-stellar-radii-practice/).

## Equations

```
Wien:              λ_max ∝ 1/T      λ_max T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K (given in questions)
Stefan-Boltzmann:  L = 4πσr²T⁴      σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ (data sheet)
radius:            r = √( L / (4πσT⁴) )      r ∝ √L / T²
luminosity from measurement:  L = 4πd²F
```

## What each law tells you

| Law | Links | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Wien | Peak wavelength and surface temperature | Hotter star → peak at shorter wavelength (bluer) |
| Stefan-Boltzmann | Luminosity, radius and temperature | Power = surface area 4πr² × power per unit area σT⁴ |

Stars are treated as **black bodies** (ideal emitters with a spectrum set only by temperature), so results are **estimates**.

## Proportionality toolkit

| Change | Effect |
|---|---|
| T doubled | λ_max halved |
| r doubled, T same | L × 4 |
| T doubled, r same | L × 16 |
| L × 100, T same | r × 10 |
| T doubled, L same | r ÷ 4 |

## Method for a radius

1. T from λ_max (convert nm to m; T in K).
2. L given, or L = 4πd²F from distance and measured flux.
3. r = √(L/(4πσT⁴)); work out T⁴ on its own line.
4. Sense-check: the Sun's radius is about 7 × 10⁸ m.

## Reference values (calculated with λ_max T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K)

| λ_max | T | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| 970 nm | 2990 K | Red, cool |
| 725 nm | 4000 K | Orange-red |
| 500 nm | 5800 K | Yellow-white (Sun-like) |
| 414 nm | 7000 K | White |
| 290 nm | 10 000 K | Blue-white, hot |

**Worked results:**

- Sun-like: λ_max = 500 nm, L = 3.8 × 10²⁶ W → r = 6.9 × 10⁸ m.
- Red giant: λ_max = 970 nm, L = 1.5 × 10²⁹ W → r = 5.1 × 10¹⁰ m.
- White dwarf: λ_max = 290 nm, L = 1.0 × 10²⁵ W → r = 3.7 × 10⁷ m.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Black body | Ideal object that absorbs all incident radiation and emits a spectrum depending only on its temperature |
| λ_max | Wavelength at which a black body emits maximum power per unit wavelength |
| Surface temperature | Temperature of the star's radiating surface, in kelvin |
| Luminosity | Total power of radiation emitted by a star |
| Stefan-Boltzmann constant σ | 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ |
| Red giant | Large, cool, luminous star |
| White dwarf | Small, hot, faint star |

## Exam traps

- Temperature in °C instead of K.
- λ_max left in nm.
- T² instead of T⁴; forgetting √ for r.
- Using the distance d where the radius r belongs.
- "Red stars are hottest": red stars are the coolest.
- "Brightest star must be hottest": luminosity also depends on r².
- Including σ and 4π in a ratio question when they cancel; more steps, more errors.

## Self-test

1. State Wien's displacement law.
2. A star's spectrum peaks at 725 nm. Calculate its surface temperature.
3. State the Stefan-Boltzmann law in words.
4. Why can a star be treated as a black body, and what does this imply about results?
5. Star P has twice the surface temperature of star Q. Compare their peak wavelengths.
6. Two stars have the same temperature; one is 100 times as luminous. Compare their radii.
7. Calculate the luminosity of a star of radius 2.1 × 10⁹ m and surface temperature 4500 K.
8. A star has T = 4000 K and L = 2.0 × 10²⁷ W. Calculate its radius.
9. Explain how a cool star can have a very large luminosity.
10. Star X: T = 12 000 K, r = 1.5 × 10⁹ m. Star Y: T = 6000 K, r = 6.0 × 10⁹ m. Compare their luminosities.
11. What are the three steps to find a star's radius from its distance, flux and spectrum?
12. At what wavelength does a 3500 K star emit most strongly?

**Answers:** 1. The wavelength at which maximum power is emitted is inversely proportional to the surface (thermodynamic) temperature. 2. T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ / 725 × 10⁻⁹ = 4000 K. 3. The luminosity of a star equals its surface area multiplied by σ and by the fourth power of its surface temperature. 4. Its spectrum is close to a black-body spectrum; temperatures and radii found are estimates. 5. P's peak wavelength is half Q's. 6. The luminous star has 10 times the radius. 7. L = 4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (2.1 × 10⁹)² × 4500⁴ = 1.3 × 10²⁷ W. 8. r = √(2.0 × 10²⁷ / (4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 4000⁴)) = 3.3 × 10⁹ m. 9. L ∝ r², so a very large surface area compensates for the low power per unit area. 10. Equal: X has (12 000/6000)⁴ = 16 times more per unit area but (1.5/6.0)² = 1/16 of the area. 11. L = 4πd²F; T from Wien's law; r from L = 4πσr²T⁴. 12. λ_max = 2.9 × 10⁻³ / 3500 = 8.3 × 10⁻⁷ m (830 nm).
