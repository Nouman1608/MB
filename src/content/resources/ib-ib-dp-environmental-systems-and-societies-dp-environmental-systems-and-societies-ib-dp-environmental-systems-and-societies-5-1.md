## How soil is built

### Weathering

Weathering breaks down rock in place, supplying the mineral fraction of soil:

- **Physical weathering** breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its chemistry, for example by freeze-thaw (water expanding as it freezes in cracks) or by repeated heating and cooling.
- **Chemical weathering** changes the minerals, for example when slightly acidic rainwater dissolves limestone or alters feldspar to clay minerals. It is fastest where it is warm and wet.
- **Biological weathering** is caused by organisms: roots widen cracks, and lichens and microorganisms release acids.

### Processes within the profile

Once weathered material and organic matter are present, several processes create horizons:

- **Humification**: decomposers turn dead organic matter into dark, stable **humus**.
- **Leaching**: water percolating downwards carries dissolved nutrients and minerals out of upper horizons.
- **Eluviation and illuviation**: fine particles and dissolved substances are washed out of one horizon (eluviation, usually the upper part) and deposited in another (illuviation, usually the B horizon).
- **Mixing** by earthworms and other burrowing animals carries organic matter down and mineral matter up.

Where rainfall greatly exceeds evaporation, leaching dominates and soils become acidic and nutrient-poor. Where evaporation exceeds rainfall, water moves upwards by capillary action, and salts or calcium carbonate can build up near the surface.

### Soil organisms

Soil is a living system. Key groups and their roles:

| Organism | Role in the soil system |
|---|---|
| Bacteria and fungi | Decompose organic matter and release nutrients (transformations) |
| Mycorrhizal fungi | Form partnerships with plant roots, extending them to take up water and phosphorus in exchange for sugars |
| Nitrogen-fixing bacteria | Convert nitrogen gas to ammonium, some living in root nodules of legumes such as beans and clover |
| Earthworms | Mix horizons, create channels for air and water, and bind particles into aggregates |
| Arthropods (mites, springtails) | Shred litter, speeding decomposition |

Practices that kill or disturb these organisms, such as heavy ploughing or some pesticides, slow nutrient cycling and weaken soil structure.

## Reading a texture triangle

A **soil texture triangle** has three axes, for the percentages of sand, silt and clay, which must add up to 100%. To classify a sample:

1. Find the clay percentage on its axis and follow the line parallel to the base of the triangle.
2. Find the silt percentage and follow its guideline across.
3. Where the lines meet (the sand line will pass through the same point) is the texture class.

### Worked example: classifying three samples

Using the widely used USDA texture triangle:

| Sample | Sand / % | Silt / % | Clay / % | Check total | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 40 | 40 | 20 | 100 | Loam |
| Q | 70 | 15 | 15 | 100 | Sandy loam |
| R | 20 | 20 | 60 | 100 | Clay |

**Interpretation.** Sample P, with a balance of particle sizes, will usually support the highest productivity. Sample Q will drain quickly and lose nutrients by leaching, so it suits crops that tolerate dry conditions or needs organic matter added. Sample R will hold nutrients well but may waterlog and be hard to work when wet. Always check that the three percentages total 100 before reading the triangle.

## Texture versus structure

**Texture** is fixed: it is the proportion of particle sizes inherited from the parent material. **Structure** is the way particles are grouped into aggregates (crumbs, blocks, plates). Good crumb structure, built by humus, fungal threads and earthworm activity, creates pores between aggregates, so even a clay soil can drain and breathe. Structure can be damaged quickly by compaction, by working wet soil and by loss of organic matter, and rebuilt slowly by adding organic matter and reducing tillage. This is why management focuses on structure: farmers cannot change texture, but they can change structure.

## pH and nutrient holding

Clay particles and humus carry negative charges on their surfaces, so they hold positively charged nutrient ions such as calcium, magnesium, potassium and ammonium. This is why clay and humus-rich soils are more fertile than sandy, low-humus soils, where nutrients are leached away.

Soil **pH** controls which nutrients are available. Most crops grow best in slightly acidic to neutral soil. In strongly acidic soil, nutrients such as phosphorus become less available and aluminium can reach toxic levels. Acidity increases with heavy leaching, with some nitrogen fertilisers and with acid deposition. Lime (calcium carbonate) raises pH.

## Soil as a carbon store

Soils hold more carbon than the atmosphere and all vegetation combined, mostly as soil organic matter. Peat soils, formed where waterlogging slows decomposition, are especially carbon-rich. When soils are ploughed, drained or eroded, organic matter is exposed to oxygen and decomposes faster, releasing CO₂. Practices that add organic matter and reduce disturbance can store carbon in soil, linking 5.1 to climate mitigation in Topic 6.

## Fieldwork: measuring infiltration

A simple **infiltration** test uses a ring (such as a metal cylinder) pushed into the ground. Water is poured in and the fall in water level is timed.

### Worked example: comparing two sites

In a fictional study, a ring was pushed into the soil at two sites and filled to the same depth.

| Site | Fall in water level / mm | Time / minutes |
|---|---|---|
| Woodland | 60 | 10 |
| Path through woodland | 9 | 10 |

```
woodland rate = 60 mm ÷ 10 min = 6.0 mm per minute
              = 6.0 × 60       = 360 mm per hour
path rate     = 9 mm ÷ 10 min  = 0.9 mm per minute = 54 mm per hour
ratio         = 6.0 ÷ 0.9      = 6.7 times faster in the woodland
```

**Interpretation.** Trampling has compacted the path, reducing pore space, so water enters about seven times more slowly. In heavy rain, more water would run off the path, carrying soil with it. For a fair test, repeat the measurement several times at each site, keep the ring depth and water depth the same and avoid testing just after rain, when soils are already wet.

## Case study: the Dust Bowl

In the 1930s, the southern Great Plains of the United States suffered the **Dust Bowl**. Native prairie grasses, whose deep roots held the soil, had been ploughed up to grow wheat. When severe drought struck, the bare, dry topsoil was blown away in huge dust storms, and many farming families had to leave the region. The United States responded by setting up the Soil Conservation Service in 1935, which promoted contour ploughing, shelter belts of trees and the return of some land to grass. The case links four ideas: removal of vegetation cover, ploughing that broke soil structure, a climatic trigger and the need for conservation by many landowners at once.

## Model answer: explain

**Question.** Explain how earthworms improve soil fertility. **[3]**

**Answer.** Earthworms pull dead leaves down into the soil and break them into smaller pieces, speeding decomposition and the release of nutrients [1]. Their burrows create channels that improve drainage and aeration, so roots and decomposers get the oxygen they need [1]. Their casts bind mineral particles and organic matter into crumbs, improving structure and water-holding capacity [1].

## Common mistakes

- Confusing texture (particle sizes) with structure (how particles are grouped).
- Calling leaching "erosion". Leaching removes dissolved substances downwards; erosion removes the soil itself.
- Reading the texture triangle along the wrong guideline; check the direction of each axis.
- Saying sandy soil is infertile "because it is sandy" without the mechanism of leaching and low nutrient holding.
- Describing the Dust Bowl as caused by drought alone; ploughing of the grassland was the underlying cause.
- Reporting infiltration without units or without saying how the test was kept fair.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a soil profile from a diagram: name horizons, colour, organic content and depth.
- **Explain** fertility differences: link particle size, organic matter and pH to nutrient holding and drainage.
- **Calculate** and **interpret** infiltration or percentage data, giving units.
- **Evaluate** a conservation method: how it works, cost, suitability, judgement.
