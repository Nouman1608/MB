Knowledge tells you what a feature is called. Skills turn that knowledge into marks. The Paper 1 Reading section of
the Cambridge International AS & A Level English Language (9093) syllabus lists fourteen skills and techniques. This
guide groups them into four families, shows where each is tested, and models the hardest ones: integrating evidence
into an argument, and writing in a style that is not your own.

## Where the skills are tested

Paper 1 has two sections, each worth 25 marks, and each based on one unseen text of about 550-750 words.

| Task | What you do | Assessment objectives |
|---|---|---|
| Question 1(a), 10 marks | Write a directed response of 150-200 words for a set form, purpose and audience | AO1, AO2 |
| Question 1(b), 15 marks | Compare the form, structure and language of your 1(a) response with the original text | AO1, AO3 |
| Question 2, 25 marks | Comment on the form, structure and language of a second text | AO1, AO3 |

AO1 is reading and understanding a wide variety of texts; AO2 is writing effectively and appropriately for audiences
and purposes; AO3 is analysing how writers' choices of form, structure and language produce meaning and style.

## Family 1: reading the unseen text

**Skills:** reading a range of unseen texts; recognising different non-fiction forms and their conventions.

Use a two-pass reading. On the first pass, read for **gist and situation**: what form is this, who wrote it, for whom,
and why? On the second pass, read with a pen, marking **shifts** (in tone, time, focus or sentence pattern), because
shifts are where a writer's choices are most visible. Do not start annotating features until you can state the
text's form, audience and purpose in one sentence. Everything you later say about language must connect to that
sentence.

## Family 2: analysing and writing about effects

**Skills:** analysing how linguistic elements come together to create meaning; writing analytically about the effects
of linguistic elements and literary features; recognising and commenting on overall style, exemplifying it through
specific instances.

The key phrase is **come together**. Strong analysis shows features working in combination, not in isolation. If a
writer uses second-person address, present tense and short imperatives in the same paragraph, the point is not three
separate features but one effect: urgency directed at the reader.

**Overall style** is a summary judgement you can defend: "conversational but authoritative", "detached and clinical",
"warm, nostalgic and gently comic". State it early, then prove it with specific instances. A style judgement without
examples is assertion; examples without a style judgement are a list.

## Family 3: evidence and argument

**Skills:** selecting and interpreting words and phrases with care and precision; using quotations and evidence with
judgement; using appropriate language to link quotations with explanatory comments; integrating quotations into a
cohesive argument; integrating individual points into broader overall responses.

These five skills describe one process.

1. **Select** the smallest piece of text that carries the effect. Often this is one word or a short phrase.
2. **Embed** it in your own sentence so your sentence stays grammatical.
3. **Link** it with precise analytical verbs: *suggests, implies, foregrounds, undercuts, positions, intensifies,
   softens, distances*. Avoid "shows" every time; it tells the examiner nothing about the type of effect.
4. **Interpret**: say what the choice does for this reader in this context.
5. **Integrate**: connect the point back to your overall reading of the text, so each paragraph advances one argument.

Compare two treatments of the same evidence.

> **Weak:** The writer says "the council has once again failed residents". This shows the writer is angry.
>
> **Strong:** The adverbial "once again" turns a single decision into a pattern, so the council's "failure" reads as
> characteristic rather than accidental; this supports the article's wider strategy of presenting residents as
> long-suffering and officials as indifferent.

The strong version selects two words, names the feature, explains the effect precisely and links it to the argument
of the whole answer.

## Family 4: directed writing and comparison

**Skills:** writing short, directed pieces in response to a text; writing in the same style as another text; writing in
a different style while reusing the content; comparing the style and linguistic elements of your own writing with
those of a given text.

### Writing in the same style

Imitating a style means reproducing its **features**, not its words. Before you write, list the source's stylistic
fingerprints: sentence length and type, register, person, tense, characteristic devices. Then produce new sentences
that share them. If the source uses long cumulative sentences with sensory detail and one short sentence per paragraph
for emphasis, so should yours.

### Writing in a different style

Here you keep the **content** and change the **style** to fit a new form, purpose and audience. The test is
transformation: a reader of your text should receive the relevant information in a form that suits them, not a
paraphrase of the original.

### Comparing your writing with the source

Question 1(b) carries more marks than 1(a). It rewards analysing both texts with the same terminology, point by point.
Plan it while you write 1(a): each deliberate choice you make (a heading, a shift to second person, a simpler sentence
structure) becomes a point of comparison you can later explain.

## Worked example: same content, different style

**Source extract** (original, written for this guide, in the style of a reflective magazine column):

> *There is a particular silence that settles over a library in the last hour before closing. The radiators tick. A
> trolley squeaks somewhere among the shelves. And the few readers who remain seem to read more slowly, as though
> reluctant to let the day end.*

**Brief:** Using the content, write the opening of a short notice for the library's website informing users of new
evening opening hours.

**Directed response (extract):**

> **Late opening starts Monday**
> From 3 March, the library will stay open until 9 pm on weekdays. The final hour is our quietest time, ideal for
> focused study. Please return trolleys and books to the desk by 8.50 pm.

**Comparison paragraph for 1(b):**

> The source is a reflective column whose purpose is to evoke a mood, so it uses sensory detail ("the radiators
> tick", "a trolley squeaks") and personification of the silence that "settles". My notice reuses the same content,
> the quiet final hour and the trolleys, but its informative purpose and a general audience of library users require
> a different structure: a headline that states the news, then dates and times placed first so readers can act on
> them. Where the source's sentences lengthen into a cumulative final clause ("as though reluctant to let the day
> end"), mine are short declaratives and one polite imperative ("Please return"), because a notice is scanned, not
> savoured. Both texts present the quietness positively, but the source makes it an experience to feel, while my text
> converts it into a practical benefit: "ideal for focused study".

The paragraph compares by point (purpose, structure, sentence style, attitude), quotes both texts, and relates each
difference to audience and purpose. That is the skill the question names.

## Common mistakes

- Annotating features before establishing form, audience and purpose.
- Quoting long stretches instead of selecting the exact words that carry the effect.
- Using "shows" as the only linking verb.
- Writing paragraphs that do not connect to an overall reading of the text.
- In "same style" tasks, reusing the source's phrases instead of its features.
- In "different style" tasks, paraphrasing the source with a few changes of word.
- Describing your 1(a) response in 1(b) without comparing it with the source.

## Exam technique: the command words

The syllabus defines **analyse** as examining in detail to show meaning, identifying elements and the relationship
between them. "Relationship between them" is Family 2's skill of showing features working together. **Compare** means
commenting on similarities and differences, so 1(b) needs both: do not only list differences. **Discuss** means
writing in depth in a structured way, which is why integration and a cohesive argument matter.

Allocate planning time to Question 1(b) and Question 2 in line with their marks, and read each brief twice: once for
form, once for audience and purpose.
