**These notes condense how to answer the whole-text question in Paper 1 Section A of OxfordAQA
International GCSE English Language (9270).** The specification says the text "will be divided into
sections with questions. At least one question will relate to the whole text." For full explanation
and a model answer, use the
[whole-text question study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-whole-text-question/);
for the section as a whole, see the
[Section A literary non-fiction revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-a-literary-non-fiction-revision-notes/).

## Section question or whole-text question?

| | Section question | Whole-text question |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence | One section | Beginning, middle and end |
| Focus | Effects of particular words | How impressions or attitudes develop |
| Main skills | R1, R4 | R2, R3 and R4 together |
| Shape of answer | Close analysis | Overview, stages, judgement |

## Four common question shapes

1. **Presentation across the text**: how is a person, place or feeling presented?
2. **Structure**: how has the writer structured the text to interest the reader?
3. **Change**: how do the writer's feelings change?
4. **Statement**: "A student said…". To what extent do you agree?

## The margin-word method

- First read: mark the **opening**, a **turning point**, the **ending**.
- While answering section questions: write **one word** per section for the attitude or impression.
- At the whole-text question: read the words **in order**. That is the text's journey.

```
Section 1: afraid      Section 2: steadied      Section 3: inherited
=> overview: fear becomes understanding, and the writer becomes like him
```

## Answer frame

```
OVERVIEW   one sentence naming the movement across the text
STAGE 1    how it begins: point + short quotation + method + link
STAGE 2    where and how it shifts: point + quotation + method + link
STAGE 3    how it ends: point + quotation + method + link
JUDGEMENT  (if "to what extent") which reading the whole text supports, and why
```

## Synthesis inside one text (R2)

Gather a repeated detail from different sections into one point:
*"Across the text, the writer links him with silence: … in Section 1, … in Section 2, … in
Section 3, so silence becomes the thread she uses to rethink him."*

## Structural vocabulary

| Term | Use it when |
|---|---|
| Opening | Discussing what the first lines set up |
| Shift | Focus, time, tone or place changes |
| Turning point | The attitude or situation changes decisively |
| Contrast | Two sections set different impressions side by side |
| Echo | A later detail repeats an earlier one |
| Cyclical structure | The ending returns to the opening |
| Build-up | Tension or feeling increases across sections |
| Final reflection | The writer comments on the experience at the end |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Overview | A sentence that sums up the whole text's movement |
| Synthesis | Combining details into one developed point |
| Sustained interpretation | Developing one reading across the answer |
| Evaluate | Judge how effectively the writer achieves an effect |
| Perspective | The writer's viewpoint and how it develops |
| Hindsight | Understanding gained later, often shown at the end |

## Statement questions: weighing the view

- Agree with evidence from at least two sections.
- Qualify with evidence that complicates the view.
- Conclude: "Mostly, because…", or "Only partly, because…".
- Avoid "I totally agree" with no counter-point.

## Weak and strong openings compared

- **Weak:** "In Section 1 the writer talks about her grandfather and the bees." This retells and has
  no argument.
- **Strong:** "The writer's view of her grandfather moves from fear to understanding, and the ending
  shows that she has become like him." This names the movement and sets up three stages.

## Timing check

Before starting Section A, note which question relates to the whole text and protect time for it.
Plan it in about a minute using your margin words; do not reread the whole text from scratch.

## Exam traps

- Retelling the text in order with no argument about change.
- Writing about one vivid section only.
- Copying points from your section answers instead of building a pattern.
- Ignoring the ending.
- No judgement on a "to what extent" question.
- Leaving too little time, since the answer needs evidence from every section.

## Self-test

1. What does the specification say about the whole text in Section A?
2. Which three reading objectives come together in the whole-text question?
3. Name four common shapes of whole-text question.
4. What is the margin-word method?
5. What should the first sentence of your answer do?
6. How many stages are usually enough?
7. What does synthesis mean when there is only one text?
8. What is a cyclical structure?
9. What is the difference between a shift and a turning point?
10. What must a "to what extent" answer include?
11. Why should you not organise the answer by section numbers alone?
12. Why is the ending especially important?

## Answers

1. The text will be divided into sections with questions, and at least one question will relate to
   the whole text.
2. R2, R3 and R4.
3. Presentation across the text; structure; change; a statement to evaluate.
4. Writing one word per section for the attitude or impression while answering section questions,
   then reading the words in order to see the journey.
5. Name the overall movement or argument across the text.
6. Three: beginning, shift or turning point, ending.
7. Gathering details from different sections into one developed point.
8. An ending that returns to the opening.
9. A shift is any change of focus, time or tone; a turning point is a decisive change in attitude or
   situation.
10. A judgement, with evidence that supports and evidence that qualifies the view.
11. Because the question asks how ideas develop; stages organised around change show the movement
    better than a list of sections.
12. It often contains the writer's final perspective or reflection.
