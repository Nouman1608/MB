## Functions of prices (3.1.8.1)

| Function | What it does |
|---|---|
| Rationing | Allocates scarce goods to those willing and able to pay the most |
| Signalling | Tells buyers and sellers about changing scarcity and preferences |
| Incentive | Rewards producers for moving resources to where profit is higher |

- **Advantages of the price mechanism:** decentralised, responds quickly, rewards efficiency, needs no planner.
- **Disadvantages:** impersonal (allocates by ability to pay, not need); ignores externalities; may give
  unequal outcomes; some goods are not provided at all.
- Extending markets into new areas can change the activity itself (the specification's example: a market
  for blood alters the motives for giving).

## Market failure (3.1.8.2)

- **Definition:** a market leads to a misallocation of resources.
- **Complete failure:** a missing market (pure public goods).
- **Partial failure:** a market exists but gives the wrong quantity or price.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Non-rival | One person's consumption does not reduce the amount available to others |
| Non-excludable | People who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting |
| Quasi-public good | Has some public good features, but is partly rival or excludable, such as a busy road |
| Free-rider problem | People benefit without paying, so a private firm cannot charge for the good |
| Tragedy of the commons | An unowned shared resource is overused because no user bears the full cost |
| Externality | Divergence between private and social costs or benefits |
| Merit good | Under-consumed in a free market; benefits undervalued (a value judgement) |
| Demerit good | Over-consumed in a free market; harms underestimated (a value judgement) |
| Asymmetric information | One party to a transaction knows more than the other |
| Regulatory capture | A regulator comes to act in the interests of the firms it regulates |
| Government failure | Intervention leads to a misallocation of resources |

## Externalities in formulae

- **MSC = MPC + marginal external cost**
- **MSB = MPB + marginal external benefit**
- Negative externality: market output where MPB = MPC is **above** the optimum where MSB = MSC.
- Positive externality: market output is **below** the optimum.
- **Welfare loss** = ½ × (difference between market and optimal output) × (gap between MSC and MSB at
  market output), for straight-line curves.
- Absence of **property rights** is a root cause: nobody can charge for the harm or benefit.
- Technology can change goods: broadcasting became excludable through encryption and subscriptions.

## Market imperfections (3.1.8.6)

- **Imperfect or asymmetric information:** buyers misjudge quality or long-term effects.
- **Monopoly power:** output restricted, price above marginal cost.
- **Factor immobility:** geographical (cost of moving, housing) and occupational (lack of skills).

## Intervention tools (3.1.8.9)

| Tool | Best suited to | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Negative externalities, demerit goods | Hard to set at the external cost; regressive |
| Subsidy | Positive externalities, merit goods | Opportunity cost; may go to firms not consumers |
| Maximum price | Making essentials affordable | Shortages, black markets |
| Minimum price | Reducing consumption of demerit goods; raising producer income | Surpluses; higher prices for poor households |
| State provision | Public goods, merit goods | Weak incentives for efficiency; funded by taxes |
| Regulation | Pollution limits, safety, bans | Enforcement cost; may be evaded |
| Property rights | Shared resources | Hard to define for air or oceans |
| Pollution permits | Emissions | Setting the cap; volatile permit prices |

## Competition and ownership (3.1.8.7–3.1.8.8)

- **Competition policy:** prevents abuse of market power, for example through scrutiny of mergers and
  agreements between firms; costs include investigation time and possible loss of economies of scale.
- **Public ownership:** can serve social objectives and natural monopolies; risks weak efficiency incentives.
- **Privatisation:** profit motive may raise efficiency and reduce the burden on public finances; risks private
  monopoly and loss of social objectives.
- **Regulation:** controls prices or quality in uncompetitive markets; risks capture and administrative cost.
- **Deregulation:** removes barriers to entry, raising competition; risks loss of standards.

## Government failure (3.1.8.10)

- Sources named by the specification: **inadequate information**, **conflicting objectives**,
  **administrative costs**.
- Interventions can **create distortions** and have **unintended consequences**.
- So market failure alone does not prove that intervention will raise welfare.

## Worked reminders (invented)

- MPB: P = 60 − Q; MPC: P = 10 + Q; external cost 10. Market: 60 − Q = 10 + Q, **Q = 25**. Optimum:
  60 − Q = 20 + Q, **Q = 20**. Welfare loss = ½ × 5 × 10 = **25**.
- Qd = 200 − 4P, Qs = 6P − 50: equilibrium **P = 25**, Q = 100. Maximum price 20: Qd = 120, Qs = 70,
  **shortage 50**.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three functions of prices.
2. Distinguish complete from partial market failure.
3. Why will a private firm not supply a pure public good?
4. Give an example of a quasi-public good and say why it is not pure.
5. Why do negative externalities lead to over-production?
6. Why does classifying a good as a merit good involve a value judgement?
7. Explain how immobility of labour can cause market failure.
8. Which intervention is best suited to a public good, and why?
9. State one advantage of pollution permits over a uniform emissions rule.
10. What is regulatory capture?
11. Give the three sources of government failure the specification names.
12. In the first worked reminder, what tax per unit would achieve the optimum?

### Answers

1. Rationing, signalling and incentive.
2. Complete: no market exists (a missing market). Partial: a market exists but misallocates resources.
3. It is non-excludable, so people can free ride and the firm cannot charge enough to cover costs.
4. A road: non-excludable at most times, but rival when congested, and excludable with tolls.
5. Producers base output on private cost, ignoring the external cost, so the market produces where MPB = MPC,
   beyond the point where MSB = MSC.
6. Saying people "should" consume more is a judgement about what is good for them, not a testable fact.
7. Workers cannot move to regions or occupations with vacancies, so labour is unemployed in one place while
   shortages persist elsewhere.
8. State provision financed by taxation, because free riding prevents a market from supplying it.
9. The cut in emissions is made by the firms that can reduce pollution most cheaply, so the target is met at
   lower total cost.
10. When a regulator comes to act in the interests of the firms it regulates rather than consumers.
11. Inadequate information, conflicting objectives and administrative costs.
12. **10 per unit**, equal to the marginal external cost.
