Subtopic 2.2 lists eight statutory offences; for each you need the actus reus, mens rea and maximum sentence. The [Topic 2 overview](/resources/a-level-cambridge-law-criminal-law/) lists them; this guide breaks each into elements with interpreting cases, building on [elements of a crime](/resources/cambridge-a-level-law-9084-elements-of-a-crime/).

## 2.2.1 Theft – s1 Theft Act 1968

Theft is the **dishonest appropriation of property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving** the other of it.

| Element | Section | Key rules and cases |
|---|---|---|
| Appropriation (AR) | s3 | Any assumption of the rights of an owner; one right is enough (*R v Morris* (1984), swapping price labels). Consent of the owner is irrelevant (*R v Gomez* (1993)), and even a valid gift can be appropriated (*R v Hinks* (2000)) |
| Property (AR) | s4 | Money and all other property, including things in action and intangible property. Land generally cannot be stolen (s4(2)); picking wild plants is not theft unless for reward or sale (s4(3)). Confidential information is not property (*Oxford v Moss* (1979)) |
| Belonging to another (AR) | s5 | Anyone with possession, control or a proprietary interest – an owner can steal his own car from a garage in possession (*R v Turner (No 2)* (1971)). Also property received under an obligation to deal with it in a particular way (s5(3)) and property got by mistake that must be restored (s5(4); *Attorney-General's Reference (No 1 of 1983)*, overpaid wages) |
| Dishonesty (MR) | s2 | Not dishonest if the defendant believes in a legal right, the owner's consent, or that the owner cannot be found by reasonable steps (s2(1)); willingness to pay does not prevent dishonesty (s2(2)). Otherwise *Ivey v Genting Casinos* (2017), applied in *R v Barton and Booth* (2020): what did the defendant actually believe, and was the conduct dishonest by the standards of ordinary decent people? |
| Intention to permanently deprive (MR) | s6 | Treating the property as one's own to dispose of regardless of the owner's rights. Borrowing counts only if the "goodness, virtue and practical value" has gone (*R v Lloyd* (1985)); taking money intending to repay with different notes still counts (*R v Velumyl* (1989)) |

**Sentencing (s7):** maximum seven years' imprisonment.

## 2.2.2 Robbery – s8 Theft Act 1968

Robbery is **stealing** and, immediately before or at the time and in order to do so, **using force on any person** or putting or seeking to put any person in fear of force then and there.

- **AR:** a completed theft – no theft, no robbery (*R v Robinson* (1977), belief in a legal right). Appropriation is a continuing act, so force while making off can be "at the time" (*R v Hale* (1979)). "Force" is for the jury: a nudge can suffice (*R v Dawson and James* (1976)), and wrenching a bag from a hand is force on the person (*R v Clouden* (1987)), but snatching a cigarette without touching the victim was not (*P v DPP* (2012)).
- **MR:** the mens rea of theft, plus intention to use force or put in fear in order to steal.
- **Sentencing (s8(2)):** maximum life imprisonment.

## 2.2.3 Burglary – s9 and s10 Theft Act 1968

| | s9(1)(a) | s9(1)(b) |
|---|---|---|
| AR | Entering a building or part of a building as a trespasser | Having entered as a trespasser, stealing or attempting to steal, or inflicting or attempting to inflict grievous bodily harm |
| MR | Knowing or reckless as to trespass; intention **at entry** to steal, inflict grievous bodily harm or do unlawful damage (s9(2)) | Knowing or reckless as to trespass; the mens rea of the further offence |
| Completed when | On entry | When the further offence is committed |

- **Entry:** "effective" (*R v Brown* (1985)); a man stuck halfway through a window had entered (*R v Ryan* (1996)).
- **Trespasser:** entering without permission or beyond it – a son taking his father's televisions (*R v Jones and Smith* (1976)).
- **Part of a building:** the area behind a shop counter (*R v Walkington* (1979)).
- **Building (s9(4)):** includes an inhabited vehicle or vessel, whether or not anyone is there; a long-standing storage container was a building (*B and S v Leathley* (1979)).
- **Sentencing (s9(3)):** maximum 14 years for a dwelling, 10 years for any other building.

**Aggravated burglary (s10):** committing burglary while having with one a **firearm or imitation firearm, weapon of offence or explosive**. The time is the moment of entry for s9(1)(a) and the moment of the further offence for s9(1)(b), so picking up a knife inside and using it to steal is enough (*R v O'Leary* (1986)). Maximum: life.

## 2.2.4 Blackmail – s21 Theft Act 1968

- **AR:** making a **demand with menaces**. A posted demand is made on posting (*Treacy v DPP* (1971)). "Menaces" are threats that would influence an ordinary person of normal stability (*R v Clear* (1968)), or that the defendant knows will affect a timid victim (*R v Garwood* (1987)).
- **MR:** a **view to gain** for oneself or another, or **intent to cause loss**, in money or other property. The demand is **unwarranted** unless the defendant believes both that there are reasonable grounds for making it and that the menaces are a proper way of reinforcing it. A threat to commit a serious crime cannot be believed proper (*R v Harvey* (1981)).
- **Sentencing:** maximum 14 years.

## 2.2.5 Handling stolen goods – s22 Theft Act 1968

- **AR:** otherwise than in the course of the stealing, **receiving** stolen goods, or **undertaking or assisting in their retention, removal, disposal or realisation** by or for the benefit of another, or arranging to do so. Lying to police to conceal stolen goods assisted in their retention (*R v Kanwar* (1982)); a buyer is not "another" for whose benefit the defendant acts (*R v Bloxham* (1983)).
- **MR:** **knowing or believing** the goods to be stolen, and dishonesty. Suspicion, even strong suspicion, is not enough (*R v Moys* (1984)); belief means the defendant felt there could be no other reasonable conclusion (*R v Hall* (1985)).
- **Sentencing:** maximum 14 years.

## 2.2.6 Making off without payment – s3 Theft Act 1978

- **AR:** **making off** from the spot where payment is required or expected, without paying, for goods supplied or a service done. The spot can be a taxi's destination (*R v Aziz* (1993)); if the service was not completed, payment was not legally required (*Troughton v Metropolitan Police* (1987)).
- **MR:** **knowing** that payment on the spot is required or expected, **dishonesty**, and **intent to avoid payment permanently** – intending to pay later is not enough (*R v Allen* (1985)).
- **Sentencing (s4):** maximum two years.

## 2.2.7 Criminal damage – Criminal Damage Act 1971

**s1(1) destroying or damaging property.** AR: destroying or damaging property belonging to another. Damage includes temporary impairment of value or usefulness, such as water-soluble paint on a pavement (*Hardman v Chief Constable of Avon and Somerset* (1986)), but not spit easily wiped off a raincoat (*A (a juvenile) v R* (1978)). MR: intention or recklessness (*R v G* (2003)) as to damaging property belonging to another; an honest belief that the property is one's own negates mens rea (*R v Smith* (1974)). **s1(2)** is the aggravated form, where the defendant intends or is reckless as to endangering life; **s1(3)** covers damage by fire (arson).

**s2 threats.** AR: making a threat to another to destroy or damage property. MR: intending the other to fear it would be carried out.

**s3 possession.** AR: having anything in one's custody or control. MR: intending it to be used to destroy or damage property.

**s4 sentencing:** maximum life for arson and the s1(2) offence; ten years otherwise.

**s5 "without lawful excuse".** Lawful excuse exists if the defendant believed the owner **had or would have consented** (s5(2)(a); even a drunken belief, *Jaggard v Dickinson* (1981)), or acted to **protect property** believed to be in immediate need of protection by reasonable means (s5(2)(b)). An honest belief need not be justified (s5(3)), but whether the act was done to protect property is judged objectively (*R v Hill and Hall* (1989)). The s5 defence does not apply to offences involving danger to life.

## 2.2.8 Fraud – Fraud Act 2006

All three forms carry a maximum of ten years and share the mens rea of **dishonesty** and **intention to make a gain or cause a loss** or risk of loss (s5: temporary or permanent, in money or property). No gain or loss need occur.

| Section | Actus reus | Additional mens rea |
|---|---|---|
| s2 false representation | Making a representation, express or implied, as to fact, law or state of mind, that is untrue or misleading; it can be made to a machine (s2(5)) | Knowing the representation is or might be untrue or misleading |
| s3 failing to disclose | Failing to disclose information one is under a legal duty to disclose | – |
| s4 abuse of position | Occupying a position in which one is expected to safeguard another's financial interests, and abusing it, by act or omission | – |

Older cases show implied representations: ordering a meal implies an intention to pay (*DPP v Ray* (1974)).

**s11 obtaining services dishonestly.** AR: obtaining services by a dishonest **act** (not an omission), where the services are made available on the basis that payment will be made, without paying in full. MR: knowing that the services are available on that basis or might be, and intending not to pay in full. Example: climbing a fence into a paid concert. Maximum: five years.

## Worked example

*Mo enters a jewellery shop during opening hours, intending to steal. He slips a ring into his pocket, then pushes the assistant aside as he runs out. Advise on Mo's liability.*

1. **Theft:** pocketing the ring appropriates (s3) property (s4) belonging to the shop (s5), with intent to keep it (s6), dishonestly (*Ivey*).
2. **Burglary s9(1)(a):** he had permission to enter as a customer, but entering with intent to steal exceeds it (*Jones and Smith*), so he entered as a trespasser with intent to steal.
3. **Robbery:** under *Hale* appropriation continues while he makes off, so the push is force "at the time" of the theft and robbery is likely.

## Common mistakes

- Finding robbery where force came after the theft was over, or there was no theft.
- Forgetting that s9(1)(a) is complete on entry, while s9(1)(b) needs a further offence.
