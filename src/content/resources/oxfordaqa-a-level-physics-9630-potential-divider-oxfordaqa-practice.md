These questions cover section 3.4.5, Potential divider, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), shared International AS and A-level content. Learn the content first with the [potential divider study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-potential-divider-oxfordaqa/) and the [potential divider revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-potential-divider-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/). Supplies have negligible internal resistance; assume no current is drawn from an output unless a load is described.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain why two fixed resistors in series across a supply give an output pd that is a fixed fraction of the supply pd. **[2]**

**2.** A 12 V supply is connected across a 1.2 kΩ resistor in series with a 3.3 kΩ resistor. Calculate the pd across the 3.3 kΩ resistor. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why a slider (potentiometer) arrangement, rather than a variable resistor in series, is used to supply a pd that can be varied from zero. **[2]**

**4.** State how the resistance of a light dependent resistor changes as the light intensity on it increases. **[1]**

**5.** A resistor of 5.6 kΩ and an unknown resistor R are in series across a 6.0 V supply. The pd across the 5.6 kΩ resistor is 4.2 V. Calculate R. **[2]**

**6.** An ntc thermistor is in series with a fixed resistor, and the output is taken across the fixed resistor. Explain what happens to the output pd when the thermistor is warmed. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Two 5.0 kΩ resistors are connected in series across a 10 V supply.

**(a)** State the pd across the lower resistor. **[1]**

**(b)** A 5.0 kΩ load is connected across the lower resistor. Calculate the new pd across it. **[2]**

**(c)** The load is replaced by a voltmeter of resistance 500 kΩ. Calculate the reading. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the results of (b) and (c) differ. **[1]**

**8.** An ntc thermistor has resistance 10 kΩ at 25 °C and 33 kΩ at 0 °C. It is in series with a 10 kΩ resistor across a 5.0 V supply. The output is taken across the thermistor.

**(a)** Calculate the output at 25 °C. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the output at 0 °C. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest a use for this circuit, explaining how the output behaves. **[1]**

**9.** An LDR has resistance 1.0 kΩ in bright light and 100 kΩ in darkness. It is in series with a 47 kΩ resistor across a 12 V supply, and the output is taken across the LDR.

**(a)** Calculate the output in bright light. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the output in darkness. **[1]**

**(c)** A switch connected to the output operates when the output reaches 6.0 V. Calculate the resistance of the LDR at this point. **[2]**

**(d)** State how the fixed resistor should be changed so that the switch operates only when it is darker. **[1]**

**10.** A 9.0 V supply is connected across an ntc thermistor in series with a fixed resistor R. The thermistor's resistance is 6.0 kΩ at 40 °C. The output, taken across R, must be 4.5 V at 40 °C.

**(a)** Calculate R. **[2]**

**(b)** State and explain how the output changes as the temperature rises above 40 °C. **[2]**

**11.** A 6.0 V supply is connected across a 1.0 kΩ resistance track. The output is taken between the bottom of the track and a slider placed one quarter of the way up.

**(a)** Calculate the output pd with nothing connected. **[1]**

**(b)** A 250 Ω load is connected across the output. Calculate the new output pd. **[3]**

**12.** A student wants a circuit whose output pd increases as the temperature increases and whose switching temperature can be adjusted. Describe a suitable potential divider circuit and explain how it works. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The same current passes through both resistors, so the pd across each is proportional to its resistance (V = IR) [1]; the pds add to the supply pd, so each is a fixed fraction R/(R₁ + R₂) of it [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Mention the common current; it is the reason the ratio of pds equals the ratio of resistances.

**2.** V = 12 × 3.3 / (1.2 + 3.3) [1] = 8.8 V [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Check that the larger resistor has the larger pd: 8.8 V out of 12 V is sensible.

**3.** In the slider arrangement the output can be taken across zero resistance at one end of the track, giving 0 V [1]; with a variable resistor in series there is always some pd across the component, so it cannot reach zero [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "It is more accurate" earns nothing; the point is the range of output.

**4.** It decreases [1] **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* The same direction as an ntc thermistor with temperature; both have falling resistance as the condition increases.

**5.** pd across R = 6.0 − 4.2 = 1.8 V [1]; R = 5.6 × 1.8 / 4.2 = 2.4 kΩ [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Ratios of pds equal ratios of resistances, so the current is not needed.

**6.** The thermistor's resistance falls when warmed [1]; it takes a smaller share of the supply pd, so the pd across the fixed resistor, the output, rises [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* State the direction of the sensor's resistance change first, then the share of the pd.

**7. (a)** 5.0 V [1]

**(b)** 5.0 kΩ in parallel with 5.0 kΩ = 2.5 kΩ; V = 10 × 2.5 / 7.5 [1] = 3.3 V [1]

**(c)** 5.0 kΩ in parallel with 500 kΩ = 4.95 kΩ; V = 10 × 4.95 / 9.95 = 5.0 V (4.98 V) [1]

**(d)** The 5.0 kΩ load greatly reduces the resistance of the lower part, but the 500 kΩ voltmeter is much larger than 5.0 kΩ, so it hardly changes it [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* A load must be much larger than the resistor it is across to leave the output unchanged.

**8. (a)** Equal resistances, so 2.5 V [1]

**(b)** V = 5.0 × 33 / (33 + 10) [1] = 3.8 V [1]

**(c)** A frost or low-temperature warning: the output rises as the temperature falls, so it could switch on an alarm or heater when it passes a set value [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* A suggestion needs a link to the direction of change in the output.

**9. (a)** V = 12 × 1.0 / 48 = 0.25 V [1]

**(b)** V = 12 × 100 / 147 = 8.2 V [1]

**(c)** 6.0 V is half the supply, so the LDR's resistance equals the fixed resistance [1]: 47 kΩ [1]

**(d)** Increase the fixed resistance, so the LDR must reach a higher resistance (darker) before it takes half the pd [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* For (d), reason from the switching condition: the LDR must equal the fixed resistance.

**10. (a)** pd across thermistor = 9.0 − 4.5 = 4.5 V, so R equals the thermistor resistance [1]: 6.0 kΩ [1]

**(b)** The output rises [1]: the thermistor's resistance falls, so it takes a smaller share and R takes a larger share of the 9.0 V [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Equal pds mean equal resistances; no current calculation is needed.

**11. (a)** Lower part 250 Ω out of 1000 Ω, so 6.0 × 0.25 = 1.5 V [1]

**(b)** 250 Ω in parallel with 250 Ω = 125 Ω [1]; V = 6.0 × 125 / (125 + 750) [1] = 0.86 V [1] **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* The upper part of the track (750 Ω) is unchanged; only the lower part is in parallel with the load.

**12.** An ntc thermistor in series with a variable resistor across the supply [1]; output taken across the variable resistor, so as temperature rises the thermistor's resistance falls and the output rises [1]; adjusting the variable resistor changes the temperature at which the output reaches the switching pd [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Say which component the output is across; the same circuit with the output across the thermistor would behave the opposite way.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Resistances swapped in the divider fraction.
- Predicting the output for the wrong component.
- Ignoring the load, or putting it in parallel with the whole divider.
- Treating the upper part of a slider track as part of the load combination.
- Not explaining the direction of a sensor's resistance change.
- Mixing kΩ and Ω.
