This guide goes deeper into **3.3.10.1 Bonding**, the opening subtopic of section 3.3.10 Aromatic chemistry in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5. The specification deliberately keeps the bonding model simple, so marks come from using a small number of ideas precisely: planar ring, intermediate bond length, delocalised p electrons, extra stability measured by hydrogenation, and the link from stability to substitution.

## What the specification asks for

The subtopic has two content statements and two skills:

- The nature of the bonding in a benzene ring, **limited to planar structure and bond length intermediate between single and double**.
- **Delocalisation of p electrons** makes benzene more stable than the theoretical molecule **cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene**.
- Use **thermochemical evidence from enthalpies of hydrogenation** to account for this extra stability.
- Explain why **substitution reactions occur in preference to addition reactions**.

Orbital hybridisation and molecular orbital theory are not required. A clear description of p orbitals overlapping is enough.

## Two models of C₆H₆

| Feature | Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene (theoretical) | Benzene (real) |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon–carbon bonds | Three C=C and three C–C, alternating | Six identical bonds |
| Bond lengths | Two values, about 134 pm and 154 pm | One value, about 139 pm |
| Shape | Planar, but an irregular hexagon | Planar regular hexagon, all angles 120° |
| π electrons | Six, in three localised pairs | Six, delocalised round the whole ring |
| Predicted reactions with electrophiles | Addition, like an alkene | Substitution |

Benzene is drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside. The circle represents the six delocalised electrons; drawing three double bonds represents the triene, not benzene.

## Where the delocalised electrons come from

Each carbon atom in the ring forms three bonds: two to neighbouring carbons and one to hydrogen. Three bonding pairs around each carbon repel to 120°, so all twelve atoms lie in **one plane**. That uses three of each carbon's four outer electrons.

The fourth electron on each carbon occupies a **p orbital** at right angles to the plane of the ring. Each p orbital overlaps sideways with the p orbitals on **both** neighbouring carbons, not just one. The overlap forms a continuous ring of electron density above and below the plane, holding six electrons that are shared by all six carbons. Because every carbon–carbon bond gets the same share of this density, all six bonds are the same length, between a single and a double bond.

A useful way to write this in an exam: "each carbon has one electron in a p orbital; the p orbitals overlap above and below the plane of the ring to form a delocalised system of six electrons."

## Thermochemical evidence

Hydrogenation adds H₂ across C=C bonds. Each reaction below ends at cyclohexane, so the enthalpy changes compare the energies of the starting molecules.

### Worked example 1: measured data

Data: cyclohexene + H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹. Benzene + 3H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −208 kJ mol⁻¹. Deduce the extra stability of benzene.

1. Cyclohexene has one C=C, so −120 kJ mol⁻¹ is released per C=C hydrogenated.
2. Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene would have three C=C: predicted ΔH = 3 × (−120) = −360 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. Benzene releases only 208 kJ mol⁻¹, which is 360 − 208 = **152 kJ mol⁻¹** less.
4. Same product, less energy released, so benzene starts **152 kJ mol⁻¹ lower** in energy: it is more stable than the triene.
5. The extra stability is due to delocalisation of the p electrons.

On an energy level diagram, draw cyclohexane at the bottom, benzene + 3H₂ 208 kJ mol⁻¹ above it, and cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene + 3H₂ 360 kJ mol⁻¹ above it. Label the 152 kJ mol⁻¹ gap.

### Worked example 2: mean bond enthalpies

Use mean bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹: C=C 612, C–C 348, H–H 436, C–H 412) to predict ΔH for hydrogenating cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene, and compare it with the measured value for benzene, −208 kJ mol⁻¹.

1. For one C=C: bonds broken = C=C + H–H = 612 + 436 = 1048 kJ mol⁻¹.
2. Bonds made = C–C + 2 C–H = 348 + 824 = 1172 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. ΔH per C=C = 1048 − 1172 = −124 kJ mol⁻¹; for three, −372 kJ mol⁻¹.
4. Benzene is 372 − 208 = **164 kJ mol⁻¹** more stable than this prediction.

The two methods give different numbers (152 and 164 kJ mol⁻¹) because mean bond enthalpies are averages taken over many compounds. Both lead to the same conclusion. If a question gives data, use exactly those data and do not quote a value you have memorised.

## Why substitution, not addition

The extra stability is the key to benzene's chemistry. In an addition reaction at least one carbon would leave the delocalised system, so the continuous ring would be broken and the stabilisation lost. In a substitution, a hydrogen is replaced and the ring of six delocalised electrons is restored in the product.

### Worked example 3: is the first addition step favourable?

Data: cyclohexa-1,3-diene + 2H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −232 kJ mol⁻¹; benzene + 3H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −208 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔH for benzene + H₂ → cyclohexa-1,3-diene and comment.

1. Use Hess's law, with cyclohexane as the common product: ΔH = −208 − (−232) = **+24 kJ mol⁻¹**.
2. Adding H₂ across one bond of benzene is **endothermic**, whereas adding H₂ to an isolated C=C (cyclohexene) releases about 120 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. Comment: the first addition step costs energy because it destroys the delocalised ring, which is why benzene does not undergo addition easily.

A second reason concerns the electrophile. In an alkene, two π electrons are concentrated between two carbons, which strongly attracts electrophiles such as bromine. In benzene, six electrons are spread over six carbons, so the electron density at any one bond is lower. Benzene therefore does not decolourise bromine water at room temperature, and its substitution reactions need a strong electrophile, usually a positive ion generated with a catalyst.

## Common mistakes

- Saying benzene "has three double bonds" or drawing the triene when benzene is asked for.
- Describing the bond length as "the average" or giving no comparison; say "between that of C–C and C=C".
- Saying benzene is more stable because it "releases more energy" on hydrogenation. It releases **less**.
- Quoting the stabilisation energy without saying which molecule is more stable.
- Explaining stability as "strong bonds" without mentioning delocalisation of p electrons.
- Saying the delocalised electrons are "in the plane of the ring"; they are above and below it.
- Saying addition "does not happen because benzene is unreactive" without the reason: addition would lose the delocalisation stability.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the bonding: planar, p orbitals overlap above and below the ring, six delocalised electrons, equal bond lengths.
- **Use the data** or **explain using the data**: show the predicted value for the triene, the difference, and a sentence saying which compound is lower in energy and why.
- **Explain** why substitution occurs: addition would disrupt the delocalised system; substitution retains it.
- **Draw an energy level diagram**: horizontal levels, labelled species and labelled enthalpy changes with arrows pointing downwards for exothermic steps.
