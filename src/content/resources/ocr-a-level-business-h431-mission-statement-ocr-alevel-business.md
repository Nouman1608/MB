## Mission, vision, aims and objectives

Students often blur these terms. The specification does not define them, so the forms below are the standard ones used in business studies.

| Term | What it states | Time frame | Example for Calderbrook Vets, a group of six animal clinics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mission statement | Why the business exists and what it does, for whom | Open-ended, revised rarely | "To give every animal in our valleys skilled, unhurried care, and every owner honest advice." |
| Vision | The future position it hopes to reach | Long term | To be the practice local farmers and pet owners trust first |
| Aim | A broad intention that follows from the mission | Several years | To grow without losing the personal service |
| Objective | A measurable, time-bound target | Usually one to three years | Open a seventh clinic and keep client satisfaction above 90% by 2029 |

The chain runs downwards. The mission gives purpose; aims turn it into intentions; objectives make those intentions measurable; strategy and tactics deliver them.

## The parts of a mission statement

Most effective statements combine some of four elements:

1. **Purpose.** What the business is for. Calderbrook's purpose is animal care, not "being a successful company".
2. **Scope.** The products, customers or places it serves. "In our valleys" sets a geographical scope that would make a national chain of clinics a break from the mission.
3. **Values.** What it believes in, such as honesty, craft, sustainability or fairness. "Honest advice" is a value that tells staff not to sell unnecessary treatments.
4. **Standards of behaviour.** How staff are expected to act. "Unhurried care" implies longer appointment slots, which has a cost in clinic capacity.

A statement need not contain all four, but one with values and no scope will rule nothing out.

### What makes a statement useful

Test any draft against five questions:

- **Is it specific to this business?** Could a rival use it word for word? If so, it says little.
- **Can staff remember it?** One or two sentences is typical.
- **Does it rule something out?** A useful mission makes some options inconsistent.
- **Does it match what the business actually does?** If behaviour contradicts the words, the statement becomes a liability.
- **Will it last?** It should survive a change in next year's objectives.

### How the type of organisation changes the wording

A public limited company may stress long-term value for shareholders alongside service. A social enterprise puts its social purpose first and may say how profits are used. A sole trader rarely needs a formal statement, because the owner makes decisions directly.

## Worked example 1: recommending a mission statement

**Case.** Ferrand Optical runs nine opticians' shops in market towns. It offers eye tests, glasses and contact lenses, and it is known for home visits to elderly customers who cannot travel. The founder is retiring and the new managing director wants a written mission statement before opening three more shops. Three drafts are on the table.

| Draft | Wording | Specific? | Rules anything out? | Matches behaviour? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | "To be the leading opticians in the UK." | No: any rival could say it | No | No: Ferrand is regional |
| B | "To raise sales by 15% and open three shops by 2028." | Yes, but it is an objective | Only in the short term | Partly |
| C | "To look after the eyesight of people in our market towns, in our shops or in their homes, with time and care." | Yes | Yes: low-cost, high-speed testing would conflict | Yes |

**Recommendation.** Draft C is the most suitable. It names the purpose (eyesight care), the scope (market towns, shops and homes) and a standard of behaviour (time and care). It fits the home-visit service, which is Ferrand's distinctive feature, so it supports the brand and tells new staff in the three new shops what matters. It would help screen decisions: a proposal to cut appointment times to raise throughput would clearly clash with "time and care".

**Weighing the alternatives.** Draft A is aspirational but unrealistic and gives no guidance. Draft B confuses a mission with an objective; once three shops open, it is out of date.

**Limitation.** "Time and care" is not measurable, so the business must set objectives such as average appointment length or customer satisfaction scores to make it checkable. The statement may also limit Ferrand if it later wants to open in cities. A strong answer ends by saying under what condition the recommendation would change: if the new managing director plans rapid national growth, the scope "our market towns" would need wider wording.

## When a mission statement matters most

Listing advantages and disadvantages earns limited credit. Evaluation asks when the advantages outweigh the disadvantages. The balance depends on several factors.

- **Size and spread.** In a business with many sites or remote teams, managers cannot supervise every decision, so a shared statement of purpose does more work. In a five-person firm the owner's daily example matters more than any written words.
- **Stage of growth.** During rapid expansion, new staff arrive faster than the culture can pass on informally. A clear mission helps them act consistently. A stable, mature business may gain less.
- **Whether actions match words.** The main disadvantage, cynicism, arises only when behaviour contradicts the statement. A firm that promises "honest advice" but sets staff sales targets for extras will lose trust faster than one with no statement at all.
- **Market conditions.** In a fast-changing market a narrowly worded mission can hold a business back, for example a mission tied to one technology. Broader wording keeps options open but guides less.
- **Stakeholder expectations.** Where customers choose on values, such as ethical sourcing, a credible mission is part of the product. Where customers buy on price alone, the mission adds little revenue.

A judgement might read: "For Ferrand, the advantages outweigh the costs because it is about to add three shops and its home-visit service is the main reason customers choose it; the statement is cheap to write, and the risk of cynicism is low if appointment-time targets are set to match it."

## Worked example 2: the impact of changing a mission statement

**Case.** Wrexley Coaches operates rural bus and coach routes. Its mission has been "To keep our villages connected, every day of the year." It has been bought by a larger transport group, which proposes a new mission: "To run the fastest, best-value intercity coach network in the region." The new owners plan to cut 14 loss-making village routes and add four express routes between cities.

An annotated answer to "Evaluate the impact of changing the mission statement on Wrexley Coaches and its stakeholders":

1. **Identify the size of the change.** This is a change of purpose and scope, not wording. The old statement put service to villages first; the new one puts speed and value on intercity routes first. *(Shows the change is fundamental, so the impact will be large.)*
2. **Impact on the business.** The new mission fits the new owners' strategy and may raise profit if express routes attract more passengers. Costs include rebranding, new timetables and possibly new vehicles. Its reputation as a community operator may be lost. *(Links mission to strategy, as the specification expects.)*
3. **Impact on stakeholders, weighed by power and effect.**
   - Village passengers lose services; some depend on them to reach work or hospital. Their power is low individually, but local councils that fund or license routes may respond.
   - Employees on rural routes face redeployment or redundancy; drivers who valued the community purpose may feel less committed.
   - Shareholders of the group gain if the express routes earn more than the routes cut.
   - Intercity passengers gain faster, cheaper journeys.
4. **Judgement.** The change is likely to raise profitability, which is why the owners want it, but the impact on village communities is severe and could damage the group's reputation and its relations with councils. The impact would be smaller if the change were phased and explained, for example by keeping a reduced village service for essential journeys. The most important factor is whether the express routes attract enough passengers: if they do not, the business will have lost its old identity without gaining a new market.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a numerical target and calling it a mission statement.
- Recommending a statement without saying why it suits this business rather than any business.
- Treating a mission statement as a guarantee of good behaviour.
- Assuming that changing a mission statement only means changing words; a real change alters strategy, objectives and stakeholder relationships.

## Exam technique

"Explain" needs a point plus its effect. "Recommend and justify" needs a choice, reasons from the case, a rejected alternative and a condition. "Evaluate" needs both sides weighed and a judgement that says what it depends on.
