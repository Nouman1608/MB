## Core recall

- Five structures: sole trader, partnership, private limited company (ltd), public limited company (plc), not-for-profit organisation.
- Compare them on the specification's four issues: management and control, sources of finance, liability, distribution of profits.
- Sole traders and partnerships have unlimited liability; ltd and plc companies have limited liability.
- In a partnership, any partner can be pursued for the whole business's debts.
- Limited liability means shareholders can lose only what they paid for their shares.
- Selling new shares raises finance but dilutes the existing owners' share of control.
- Ltd shares are sold privately; plc shares can be sold to the public, usually on a stock exchange.
- A plc is in the private sector; "public" refers to who may buy its shares.
- Not-for-profit organisations reinvest any surplus in their cause.
- A structure suits a stage of growth; businesses change structure as their needs change.
- You do not need to know the legal process of incorporation.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Legal structure | The form of ownership a business adopts |
| Sole trader | A business owned by one person, who may employ others |
| Partnership | A business owned by two or more people who share decisions, profits and liability |
| Partnership agreement | A written document setting out how partners share profit and decisions |
| Private limited company (ltd) | A company owned by shareholders whose shares are not offered to the public |
| Public limited company (plc) | A company whose shares can be bought by the public |
| Shareholder | An owner of part of a company |
| Director | A person elected by shareholders to run a company |
| Dividend | A share of company profit paid to shareholders |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business for investment |
| Unlimited liability | Owners are personally responsible for all business debts |
| Limited liability | Owners' losses are limited to the money they invested |
| Dilution | A fall in an existing owner's percentage of shares when new shares are issued |
| Takeover | Gaining control of a company by buying enough of its shares |

## Comparison grid

| | Sole trader | Partnership | Ltd | Plc | Not-for-profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Control | Owner alone | Shared by partners | Directors and shareholders; founders can keep a majority | Directors answer to many shareholders | Trustees or volunteers, guided by the cause |
| Finance | Savings, loans, retained profit | Partners' capital, loans | Private share sales, loans | Public share sales, loans | Donations, grants, trading |
| Liability | Unlimited | Unlimited | Limited | Limited | Depends on set-up |
| Profits | All to owner | Shared by agreement | Dividends or retained | Dividends or retained | Reinvested in the cause |

## Calculation methods

```
Ownership share (%)  = shares held ÷ total shares × 100
Finance raised       = new shares × price per share
Dividend per share   = total dividend ÷ number of shares
Retained profit      = profit after tax − dividends
Partner's share      = profit × (partner's ratio part ÷ total parts)
```

## Recommending a structure

1. What does the business need most: finance, control, protection or simplicity?
2. Which structure meets that need?
3. What does it cost (control, privacy, paperwork, risk)?
4. Decide, and say what would change your decision.

## Exam traps

- "Limited liability means limited debts" is wrong; the owners' loss is limited.
- Partners do not owe only their half of the debts.
- A plc is not government-owned.
- A plc is rarely right for a small start-up.
- Issuing shares does not leave control unchanged.
- Always compare on the four issues, using case figures.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four issues the specification uses to compare legal structures.
2. Which structures have limited liability?
3. Explain why a partner may have to pay more than half the debts of a failed partnership.
4. A shareholder paid £8,000 for shares in a company that fails £50,000 in debt. What is the most she can lose?
5. Two partners share £45,000 profit in the ratio 3:2. How much does each receive?
6. A founder owns 700 of a company's 1,000 shares. The company issues 400 new shares to an investor. What is the founder's new percentage?
7. Has the founder in question 6 kept a majority? Explain.
8. A company pays £90,000 in dividends on 300,000 shares. Calculate the dividend per share.
9. What does "public" mean in "public limited company"?
10. Give one drawback of becoming a plc for the founders.
11. What happens to the surplus of a not-for-profit organisation?
12. Recommend a structure for a family firm borrowing £200,000 that wants to keep control, and give one reason.

### Answers

1. Management and control; sources of finance; liability; distribution of profits.
2. Private limited companies and public limited companies.
3. Each partner can be pursued for the whole debt, so if the other partner cannot pay, the one with assets may pay it all.
4. **£8,000**, the amount she invested.
5. £45,000 ÷ 5 = £9,000 per part; **£27,000** and **£18,000**.
6. 700 ÷ 1,400 × 100 = **50%**.
7. No. 50% is not more than half, so she can no longer outvote everyone else on her own.
8. £90,000 ÷ 300,000 = **£0.30 (30p)**.
9. Members of the public can buy its shares, usually on a stock exchange; it is still a private-sector business.
10. Any one: loss of control; risk of takeover; more public scrutiny; higher costs.
11. It is reinvested in the organisation's purpose.
12. A private limited company: limited liability protects family members' personal assets from the large loan, and shares can be kept within the family.
