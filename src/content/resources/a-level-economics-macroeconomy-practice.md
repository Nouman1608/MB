## Questions

**1.** A consumer price index rises from 120 to 126 over one year. Calculate the rate of inflation. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between *deflation* and *disinflation*. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **two** limitations of the consumer price index (CPI) as a measure of inflation. **[2]**

**4.** State the four components of aggregate demand and give one example of spending in each. **[4]**

**5.** In the fictional country of Zeeland, nominal GDP grows by 8% in a year in which the inflation rate is 5%. Estimate the growth of real GDP, then calculate it exactly to one decimal place. **[3]**

**6.** Zeeland's nominal GDP is 240 billion zeds in Year 1 and 264 billion zeds in Year 2. The price index is 100 in Year 1 and 110 in Year 2. Calculate real GDP in Year 2 (in Year 1 prices) and the growth in real output. Explain what your result shows about the rise in nominal GDP. **[4]**

**7.** A fictional consumer price index uses three categories. Food has a weight of 40 and its prices rise by 10%. Housing has a weight of 35 and its prices rise by 4%. Transport has a weight of 25 and its prices fall by 2%. Calculate the weighted rate of inflation. **[4]**

**8.** Explain, with an example of each, the difference between demand-pull inflation and cost-push inflation. **[4]**

**9.** In an economy, consumption is 520 bn, investment 150 bn, government spending 200 bn, exports 180 bn and imports 210 bn (all in the same currency units).
(a) Calculate aggregate demand. **[2]**
(b) Investment then falls to 120 bn and exports rise to 200 bn. Calculate the new level of aggregate demand and the percentage change, to one decimal place. **[2]**
(c) Explain one way in which a fall in aggregate demand could affect employment. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse **two** consequences of a high rate of inflation for an economy. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss whether an increase in government spending will raise real GDP. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss whether deflation is always harmful to an economy. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Inflation = (126 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 [1] = **5%** [1].

*Examiner insight:* always divide the change by the starting index, not the end index. Dividing by 126 gives 4.8% and loses the second mark.

**2.** **Deflation** is a sustained fall in the general price level (a negative inflation rate) [1]. **Disinflation** is a fall in the *rate* of inflation: prices are still rising, but more slowly [1].

*Examiner insight:* a falling inflation rate that stays above zero is disinflation. Add the phrase "prices are still rising" to show you know the difference.

**3.** Two valid limitations, each explained: the basket and weights reflect an **average household** and may not match a particular household's spending [1]; it is slow to reflect **changes in quality** and **new products**, or switching to cheaper substitutes [1]. Other valid limitations may be credited. Maximum 2 marks.

*Examiner insight:* name the limitation and say why it makes the figure inaccurate. "The CPI is not accurate" alone gains nothing.

**4.** Consumption (household spending), for example on food or clothing [1]; investment (spending by firms on capital goods), for example a new machine [1]; government spending, for example on road building [1]; net exports (exports minus imports), for example sales of goods abroad [1]. **AD = C + I + G + (X − M)**. Mark each component with its example, but do not award a mark for an example alone.

*Examiner insight:* "net exports" or "X − M" is needed. Exports on their own are not the fourth component.

**5.** Estimate: 8% − 5% = **3%** [1]. Exact: 1.08 ÷ 1.05 = 1.02857 [1], so real GDP grows by about **2.9%** [1].

*Examiner insight:* the subtraction is only an approximation and the exam may ask for the exact figure. State which method you used.

**6.** Real GDP in Year 2 = 264 ÷ 110 × 100 [1] = **240 billion zeds** [1]. Real growth = 0% [1]. The 10% rise in nominal GDP came entirely from higher prices, so there was no increase in the volume of output [1].

*Examiner insight:* the final comment carries a mark. Say what the result means for output, not only what the number is.

**7.** Food: 0.40 × 10 = 4.0 [1]. Housing: 0.35 × 4 = 1.4 [1]. Transport: 0.25 × (−2) = −0.5 [1]. Total = **4.9%** [1].

*Examiner insight:* weights of 40, 35 and 25 add to 100, so multiply by the weight as a fraction. Forgetting the negative sign for transport is a common slip.

**8.** Demand-pull: aggregate demand rises faster than the economy's productive capacity, pulling up the price level [1]; for example, higher government spending when the economy is near full capacity [1]. Cost-push: a rise in firms' costs of production shifts aggregate supply to the left, raising prices [1]; for example, a rise in the world price of oil or a rise in wages [1].

*Examiner insight:* the cause must be linked to the right curve. Demand-pull is a shift of AD, and cost-push is a shift of AS.

**9.** (a) AD = 520 + 150 + 200 + (180 − 210) [1] = **840 bn** [1].
(b) New AD = 520 + 120 + 200 + (200 − 210) = **830 bn** [1]. Change = −10 ÷ 840 × 100 = **−1.2%** [1].
(c) Lower AD means firms sell less [1], so they may cut output and reduce the number of workers they employ [1].

*Examiner insight:* in part (b) the two changes partly offset each other. Recalculate the whole sum rather than guessing the net effect.

**10.** Any two consequences, each with up to 3 marks (point, development, link to the economy):
Falling real value of money and savings [1]: if interest rates are below inflation, savers lose purchasing power [1], which can reduce saving and so funds for investment [1].
Loss of international competitiveness [1]: if domestic prices rise faster than those of trading partners, exports become dearer and imports cheaper [1], which can worsen the trade balance and reduce AD [1].
Other valid answers include uncertainty reducing investment, menu costs, and redistribution from lenders to borrowers.

*Examiner insight:* each consequence needs a short chain of reasoning. A list of effects with no explanation stays in the lowest band.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response. A model plan with indicative points:
- Government spending (G) is a component of AD, so a rise shifts AD to the right [1].
- If there is spare capacity, output and real GDP rise with little change in prices [1].
- Higher output creates jobs and income, which raises consumption further [1].
- If the economy is near full capacity, real GDP rises little and the price level rises instead [1].
- Spending financed by borrowing may raise interest rates and reduce private investment [1].
- Imports may rise as incomes rise, so some of the effect leaks abroad [1].
- Time lags and the type of spending (for example capital or current) affect the size of the effect [1].
- Judgement: the outcome depends mainly on how much spare capacity exists [1].

*Examiner insight:* the evaluation mark depends on a clear condition, such as spare capacity. "It depends" with no reason does not gain credit.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response. A model plan with indicative points:
- Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level [1].
- Harm: consumers may delay purchases expecting lower prices, reducing AD [1].
- Falling prices raise the real value of debt, so borrowers cut spending [1].
- Falling revenues may lead firms to cut output and jobs, raising unemployment [1].
- Real wages may rise if money wages are sticky, increasing firms' costs [1].
- Not harmful: deflation caused by falling costs (higher productivity) raises real incomes [1].
- Lower prices can improve export competitiveness [1].
- Central banks may struggle to cut interest rates below zero to respond [1].
- Savers and those on fixed incomes gain purchasing power [1].
- Judgement: the cause and the speed of the fall matter. Deflation from higher supply with growing output is benign, while deflation from falling demand is damaging [2].
- Reference to a developed example or a fictional case, used to support one argument [1].

*Examiner insight:* separate deflation caused by supply from deflation caused by weak demand. This is the distinction that earns the top-band judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing deflation (falling prices) with disinflation (prices rising more slowly).
- Stating CPI limitations without explaining why they matter.
- Treating a rise in nominal GDP as growth in output.
- Dividing a change by the new value instead of the original value when finding a percentage.
- Leaving out net exports, or listing only exports, as a component of AD.
- Forgetting a negative sign when weighting price changes in an index.
- Linking demand-pull and cost-push inflation to the wrong curve on the AD/AS diagram.
- Listing consequences of inflation with no chain of reasoning.
- Ending an evaluation with "it depends" and no condition that decides the answer.
