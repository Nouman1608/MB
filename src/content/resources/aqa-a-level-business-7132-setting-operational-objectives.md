This guide goes deeper into section **3.4.1 Setting operational objectives** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification asks you to know:

- **the value of setting operational objectives**
- the main operational objectives: **costs, quality, speed of response, flexibility, environmental objectives and added value**.

Throughout section 3.4 you should consider technology (for example online businesses, better links with customers and suppliers, inventory control, automation and robotics), ethical and environmental influences, market conditions and competition, how operations decisions improve competitiveness, and links with other functions. The wider [operational management study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-operational-management/) covers the whole topic. This page looks at one question: what should operations be trying to achieve, and how does a business choose between competing aims? Companion pages: [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-operational-objectives-revision-notes/) and [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-operational-objectives-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## What operations does

**Operations management** is the function that turns inputs (materials, labour, equipment, information) into outputs (goods and services) that customers value. Every business has operations, including a hairdresser, a hospital or a software firm. An **operational objective** is a specific target for how well the operations function performs, set within the corporate objectives of the whole business.

## The value of setting operational objectives

Objectives are valuable because they:

1. **Link operations to the business's strategy.** If the corporate aim is to be the low-price supplier, operations needs cost objectives. If the aim is to be a premium brand, quality comes first.
2. **Give clear targets** that managers and workers can understand and be judged against.
3. **Allow performance to be measured and improved** over time, for example unit cost falling or delivery times shortening.
4. **Coordinate decisions**: they guide choices on capacity, technology, suppliers and staffing.
5. **Improve competitiveness**: a business that is better than rivals on the factors customers care about can win and keep customers.
6. **Help other functions**: marketing can promise only what operations can deliver, finance can budget around cost targets, and HR can plan training.

Limits: objectives can conflict; they may be set without enough information; a narrow focus on one target (such as cost) can harm another (such as quality); and the market may change faster than the objectives.

## The main operational objectives

### Costs

A **cost objective** aims to reduce or control the cost of producing each unit (**unit cost**), or total costs. Lower costs allow either lower prices or higher profit margins. Methods include economies of scale, lower waste, efficient use of capacity and negotiating with suppliers. A cost objective suits a business competing on price. The risk is cutting corners: if cost cuts reduce quality or staff morale, long-term losses may exceed the savings.

### Quality

A **quality objective** aims to meet or exceed customers' expectations. Quality is judged by the customer. For a restaurant it might be taste, service and cleanliness; for a phone maker, reliability and few defects. Measures include customer satisfaction scores, the rate of defects or returns and the number of complaints. High quality supports a premium price, loyalty and a good reputation, but it costs money in training, materials and checking.

### Speed of response

**Speed of response** is how quickly the business can meet customers' orders or changing needs, for example delivery times, time to serve a customer or time to bring a new product to market. It is vital where customers want fast service (online delivery, emergency repairs) or where fashions change quickly. Speed may need spare capacity, high inventory or premium delivery, all of which cost money.

### Flexibility

**Flexibility** is the ability to change the output quickly: to adjust volume (more or fewer units), to change the product mix, or to make customised products. It helps a business to meet fluctuating demand, to serve different segments and to react to rivals. Flexible production may need multi-skilled workers and adaptable machinery, which can reduce the economies of scale from very long, standard production runs.

### Environmental objectives

**Environmental objectives** aim to reduce the harm caused by the business's activities: lowering energy use and emissions, cutting waste, using recycled or sustainable materials, and reducing packaging and transport miles. Reasons include legal duties, pressure from customers and investors, the chance to cut costs (less waste and energy), and the business's own values and reputation. They may increase costs in the short run, but can reduce long-run costs and support a brand's image.

### Added value

**Added value** is the difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials and services used to make it:

> added value = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs

Added value can also be shown as a percentage of the selling price:

> added value (%) = (selling price − cost of bought-in inputs) ÷ selling price × 100

A business can increase added value by raising the price customers will pay (through branding, design or service) or by cutting the cost of its inputs. Added value is not the same as profit: it must still cover wages, rent and other overheads before anything is left as profit.

## Worked example: Alderbank Furniture

Alderbank makes three products. Operations records the following.

| Product | Selling price | Bought-in materials per unit | Added value | Added value % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chair | £120 | £48 | £72 | 60% |
| Table | £300 | £150 | £150 | 50% |
| Shelf unit | £60 | £15 | £45 | 75% |

**Calculations.**

- Chair: £120 − £48 = **£72**; 72 ÷ 120 × 100 = **60%**
- Table: £300 − £150 = **£150**; 150 ÷ 300 × 100 = **50%**
- Shelf unit: £60 − £15 = **£45**; 45 ÷ 60 × 100 = **75%**

The table earns the highest added value in pounds, but the shelf unit adds the highest proportion of its price. An operational objective might be to raise the chair's added value to 65% by negotiating cheaper wood or by redesigning it so that customers will pay £130 (then (130 − 48) ÷ 130 × 100 = 63.1%).

Alderbank also sets two other objectives:

- **Unit cost:** cut the average unit cost of the chair from £86 to £80, a reduction of £6, which is 6 ÷ 86 × 100 = **7.0%** (to one decimal place).
- **Environmental:** wood and fabric purchases are £400,000 a year, and 12% is wasted. Reducing waste to 8% cuts waste from £48,000 to £32,000, saving **£16,000** a year.

**Evaluation.** The cost and environmental objectives support each other because less waste lowers cost. But a cost objective may clash with quality if cheaper wood is weaker, and with speed if the business stops carrying spare stock to save money.

## Influences on the choice of objectives

- **Corporate objectives and strategy**, as noted above.
- **Market conditions and competition**: if rivals compete on delivery speed, a business may have to match them.
- **Customers' needs**: what do target customers value most: price, quality, speed or choice?
- **Type of product**: a bespoke product needs flexibility; a mass product needs low unit cost.
- **Resources**: finance, skills and technology.
- **Technology**: automation and robotics can reduce costs and raise consistency; online ordering raises expectations about speed and flexibility.
- **Ethical and environmental pressures**: laws, public opinion and supply chain responsibilities.

## Trade-offs between objectives

Operations rarely achieves all the objectives at once. Common conflicts:

| Conflict | Reason |
|---|---|
| Cost against quality | Better materials and more checks cost money |
| Cost against speed | Fast delivery needs spare capacity or express transport |
| Cost against flexibility | Short, varied production runs cost more per unit than long standard ones |
| Cost against environmental | Cleaner methods may cost more at first |
| Speed against quality | Rushing work can cause errors |

A good business decides which objectives are **order winners** (the reasons customers choose it) and which are **order qualifiers** (the minimum customers expect). For a budget airline, low cost is an order winner and punctuality is a qualifier. For a luxury hotel, quality is an order winner and a reasonable price is a qualifier.

## Operational objectives and other functions

- **Marketing:** a promise of next-day delivery needs a speed objective in operations.
- **Finance:** cost and added value targets affect profit and the budget.
- **HR:** quality and flexibility need trained, multi-skilled staff.
- **Corporate objectives:** operational objectives should help achieve growth, profit or social aims.

## Technique for exam questions

- **Define** the objective precisely, and give the formula for added value if relevant.
- **Analyse** by linking the objective to its effect on customers, costs and competitiveness.
- **Evaluate** by considering conflicts between objectives and what the business's customers value most.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing added value with profit.
- Listing the six objectives without saying which fits the business in the case.
- Saying that a cost objective always means low quality.
- Forgetting that flexibility can reduce economies of scale.
- Writing "improve quality" as an objective without any measure.
