**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 11, "Revolution, reform and foreign relations in the Middle East (circa 1945 to 2020)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two 15-mark essays from different regional studies, each evaluating perspectives on an argument. The six inquiry topics are: the reasons for the 1979 revolution in Iran; the impact of post-revolution Iran; the impact of the Iran-Iraq War; the impact of Gamal Abdel Nasser's rule in Egypt; the reasons for the 2011 Egyptian revolution; and the impact of the Lebanese Civil War on regional politics.

## 1. Reasons for the 1979 Revolution in Iran

- **Mosaddeq and the 1953 coup.** Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddeq nationalised the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company in 1951. Britain organised a boycott, and in August 1953 the UK and USA helped to remove him and restore the Shah's power. Many Iranians later saw this as proof that the Shah depended on foreign powers.
- **The Shah and the White Revolution.** Mohammad-Reza Shah launched the White Revolution (1963), with land reform, votes for women, literacy campaigns and the sale of state factories. The programme created resentment among landowners, clergy and peasants who gained little. The Shah relied on the secret police (SAVAK) and a one-party state in the 1970s.
- **External influence, including the role of the UK and USA.** The Shah was a Cold War ally, bought large amounts of US arms, and allowed immunity for US personnel. Critics called him a puppet.
- **Religious factors and Ayatollah Khomeini.** Khomeini condemned the White Revolution and was exiled in 1964. His teaching on the "guardianship of the jurist" (*velayat-e faqih*) offered an alternative to monarchy. His sermons spread on cassettes. Mosques and the bazaar merchants gave organisation to protest.

The crisis in 1978 grew from the economic strain after the oil boom (inflation, inequality, corruption) and protest cycles, with strikes, including in the oil sector. The Shah left on 16 January 1979, and Khomeini returned on 1 February. A referendum created the Islamic Republic in spring 1979.

**Argument to weigh:** "The revolution was caused mainly by religion." Support: Khomeini's leadership and the role of the clergy. Challenge: a broad coalition of liberals, leftists, merchants and workers joined; economic and political grievances were widely shared.

## 2. Impact of post-revolution Iran

- **Political and economic developments.** The 1979 constitution created a Supreme Leader above elected bodies, a Guardian Council and a Majlis. Liberal and leftist allies were sidelined, and President Bani-Sadr was removed in 1981. The Revolutionary Guards became a major force. Oil income remained central to the economy, which was hurt by war, mismanagement and sanctions. Later presidents (Rafsanjani, Khatami, Ahmadinejad, Rouhani) differed on reform and the economy.
- **Foreign relations.** The slogan "neither East nor West" rejected both superpowers. The US embassy hostage crisis (November 1979 to January 1981) broke relations with the USA. Iran supported groups such as Hezbollah in Lebanon. Disputes over the nuclear programme led to sanctions, the 2015 agreement (JCPOA) and the US withdrawal in 2018.
- **Experiences of women.** Dress rules and legal changes reduced women's rights in some areas. Yet literacy and university education for women grew, and women took part in politics and work, especially during reformist periods.
- **Marginalised groups.** The Baha'i community was persecuted. Kurds and Baluch faced state suppression. Recognised religious minorities such as Christians and Jews had limited representation.

## 3. Impact of the Iran-Iraq War (1980 to 1988)

Iraq under Saddam Hussein invaded Iran in September 1980, aiming to take the Shatt al-Arab waterway and oil-rich Khuzestan, and fearing the spread of Shi'a revolution. Iran drove back the invaders by 1982 and then attacked into Iraq in costly offensives.

- **Economic impact.** Both economies were damaged. Iraq ended the war with large debts to Gulf states and western creditors, which contributed to its invasion of Kuwait in 1990. Iran's reconstruction was slow.
- **Social impact.** Hundreds of thousands were killed, and estimates vary widely. Cities were bombed in the "war of the cities", and Iran mobilised volunteers, including young men. The war reinforced the revolutionary regime's authority at home.
- **Regional and global involvement.** Gulf states and many Western and Soviet-bloc suppliers aided Iraq; Iran received arms from sources such as Syria and Libya, and secretly from the USA in the Iran-Contra affair. The "tanker war" drew in US naval forces, and in 1988 a US warship shot down an Iranian civilian airliner.
- **Technology, including chemical weapons.** Iraq used mustard gas and nerve agents against Iranian forces and against Kurdish civilians, as at Halabja in March 1988. The world response was weak. A UN resolution (598) led to a ceasefire in August 1988.

## 4. Impact of Gamal Abdel Nasser's rule in Egypt (from 1954)

Nasser was one of the Free Officers who overthrew King Farouk in 1952, and he became the leader of the country by 1954 and president in 1956.

- **Domestic politics, including authoritarianism.** Parties were banned, the Muslim Brotherhood repressed, and a single national organisation created. The press and security services were controlled by the state.
- **Economic transformation and modernisation.** Land reform in 1952 reduced the holdings of large landowners, and the state nationalised major industries from 1961, in "Arab socialism". The Aswan High Dam, built with Soviet help, increased electricity and farmland.
- **Social and cultural change.** Free education expanded, and universities opened to many more students. Women gained the vote in 1956. Radio, especially the *Voice of the Arabs*, carried Nasser's message.
- **Regional politics.** Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal in July 1956. The Anglo-French-Israeli attack failed politically, and made Nasser a hero in the Arab world. He led Pan-Arabism, forming the United Arab Republic with Syria (1958 to 1961), but the union collapsed. Egyptian intervention in Yemen was costly. The defeat in the Six-Day War of 1967 damaged his prestige. He died in 1970.

**Argument to weigh:** "Nasser's rule was more successful abroad than at home." Support: Suez and Pan-Arab leadership. Challenge: his social reforms had lasting effects, while the 1967 defeat and the failure of the UAR undermined his foreign policy.

## 5. Reasons for the 2011 Egyptian Revolution

- **Presidency and assassination of Anwar Sadat.** Sadat changed course with the *infitah* (economic opening), made peace with Israel (1979) and moved from Soviet to US support. He was assassinated in October 1981 by Islamist soldiers.
- **Hosni Mubarak's presidency.** The emergency law stayed in force for decades, the ruling party dominated elections (including the badly manipulated vote of late 2010), and police brutality was widespread. The killing of Khaled Said in 2010 became a rallying point.
- **Economic and demographic challenges.** Many young, educated people faced unemployment. Food prices and inequality rose, while privatisation was seen as benefiting well-connected businessmen.
- **External factors.** The Tunisian revolution (January 2011) showed that a ruler could fall. Satellite television and social media spread information. The USA gave Egypt large military aid and supported the regime, while Egypt kept the peace treaty with Israel.

Protests began on 25 January 2011, centred on Tahrir Square. After 18 days, with the army refusing to crush the protests, Mubarak resigned. Later events, including the election of Mohamed Morsi (2012) and his removal in 2013, show the difficulty of lasting change.

## 6. Impact of the Lebanese Civil War (1975 to 1990) on regional politics

- **Initial involvement of the PLO.** After its expulsion from Jordan in 1970, the PLO relocated to Lebanon. The country's delicate sectarian balance, based on the 1943 National Pact, was strained by the Palestinian presence and by inequality between communities. The war began in April 1975.
- **Deepening of conflict.** Syria intervened in 1976, at first against the Palestinians and their Lebanese allies, and later changed sides. Israel invaded in 1978 and then in 1982, reaching Beirut and forcing the PLO to leave. The Sabra and Shatila massacres (September 1982) were carried out by Lebanese Christian militia in an area controlled by Israel.
- **Foreign and UN involvement.** UNIFIL was created in 1978. A US and French force came in 1982, and bombings of their barracks in October 1983 killed hundreds and led to their withdrawal.
- **Establishment of Hezbollah.** Iran's Revolutionary Guards helped form Hezbollah in the early 1980s, among the Shi'a community. It resisted the Israeli occupation of the south, which ended in 2000, and became a political party and a major regional force. The Taif Agreement (1989) rebalanced political power between Christians and Muslims, and the war ended in 1990 under Syrian dominance.

## Worked example: a Paper 3 plan

*Question:* "The Iranian Revolution of 1979 was caused mainly by the policies of the Shah." Evaluate this claim.

1. Define "policies": political repression, the White Revolution, economic policy, foreign alignment.
2. For: SAVAK, closure of political space, inflation after the oil boom, closeness to the US.
3. Against: Khomeini's leadership and religious networks, the 1953 coup's memory, the role of the bazaar, global oil prices, and the failure of the army to act.
4. Perspectives: some historians stress modernisation and its tensions; others stress religion and culture; others emphasise contingent events in 1978.
5. Judgement: the Shah's policies created conditions, but the revolution needed leadership and organisation from religious and other groups.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the revolution as inevitable.
- Confusing Sunni and Shi'a roles in Lebanon and Iran.
- Writing about Egypt in 2011 without covering Sadat and Mubarak.
- Ignoring the Cold War context.
