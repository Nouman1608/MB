## 3.3.1 Production processes

Operations covers how a business converts its inputs into the goods or services it sells. The specification names four methods and expects you to know when each is appropriate.

| Method | What happens | Suits | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | One item made at a time to a customer's own order | Unique, high-value, made-to-measure products | Slow and costly per item; skilled labour needed |
| Batch | Identical items made in a group, then a changeover to the next group | Products sold in set quantities or varieties, such as a bakery making one recipe after another | Machines idle between batches; stock may build up |
| Flow | Continuous production of a standard product along a line | Very large demand for identical items | Costly equipment; hard to change the product |
| Cell | Production split into small self-contained teams, each making a complete part or product | Medium volumes where teamwork and variety matter | Needs trained, flexible staff |

Examples of fit. Savernake Joinery builds fitted wardrobes to each customer's room measurements, so it uses job production. Gleneagle Bakery bakes 400 seeded loaves, then 400 white loaves, then 400 rolls, so it uses batch production. Pendragon Cycles assembles one standard model of children's bike at high volume on a moving line, so flow production fits. Ormskirk Print Works splits its factory into teams, each handling a customer's brochure from design to binding, which is cell production.

### Efficiency in production

Efficiency means producing more with the resources used, or producing the same with fewer resources, so that cost per unit falls. The specification points you to two techniques.

- Just in time (JIT): materials arrive when they are needed, so the business holds little stock. Section 3.3.2 covers it in detail.
- Continuous improvement (Kaizen): all employees look for small, regular improvements, such as a quicker way to set up a machine or a layout that cuts walking time. Many small gains add up, and staff who suggest changes may feel more involved.

When you evaluate Kaizen, consider the benefits (lower waste, lower costs, better ideas from the people who do the job) against the costs (time in meetings, training, and the risk that staff stop suggesting ideas if nothing is acted on).

## 3.3.2 The role of procurement

Procurement is buying the materials, components and services a business needs.

### Managing stock with JIT

Under JIT the business orders small amounts often, timed to arrive just before use. The benefits are lower costs: less cash tied up in stock, smaller storage needs and less risk of waste or damage. The specification says these savings must be set against two costs: paying for deliveries more often, and losing purchasing economies of scale (the bulk-buying discounts that come with large orders). JIT also depends on reliable suppliers, because a late delivery can stop production.

Buffer stock is spare stock kept to satisfy demand if sales jump or a delivery is late. Its benefit is that orders can still be filled. Its cost is the cost of holding it: storage, insurance, possible damage or going out of date, and cash tied up.

**Worked example.** Gleneagle Bakery buys 9,600 bags of flour a year. Now it takes 12 deliveries at 2.40 a bag and holds a buffer of 600 bags. Holding costs are 15% of stock value a year. A supplier offers JIT: 52 deliveries a year, but each delivery costs 45 and the bag price rises to 2.55.

- Buffer holding cost now: 600 x 2.40 x 0.15 = 216 a year.
- Extra delivery cost under JIT: (52 - 12) x 45 = 1,800.
- Lost discount: 9,600 x (2.55 - 2.40) = 1,440.
- Net extra cost of JIT: 1,800 + 1,440 - 216 = **3,024 a year**.

On these figures alone JIT costs more. The bakery would only switch if the saving from freed space, less waste and less cash tied up is worth more than 3,024, or if it can negotiate cheaper deliveries. A good evaluation says this and gives a judgement.

### Factors affecting choice of supplier

For a given business you should analyse price, quality and reliability.

- Price: the lowest quoted price is not always the lowest cost once delivery, discounts and credit terms are counted.
- Quality: poor materials lead to faulty products, returns and a poor reputation.
- Reliability: delivering the right goods on time, every time. This matters most for a business using JIT.

**Worked example.** Sundridge Fabrics needs cloth. Supplier A charges 4.00 a metre but 5% arrives unusable. Supplier B charges 4.20 and only 1% is unusable. Cost per usable metre: A = 4.00 / 0.95 = 4.21; B = 4.20 / 0.99 = 4.24. A is cheaper by about 0.03 per usable metre. If A also delivers late one time in five, and Sundridge runs JIT, the cheaper price may not be worth it. State the numbers, then make the judgement for this business.

## 3.3.3 The concept of quality

Quality means meeting customer expectations. For goods this is usually durability, safety, performance and finish. For services it is usually accuracy, speed, courtesy and a consistent experience. Expectations depend on price and market: customers expect more from a premium product than from a budget one.

### Identifying and measuring quality problems

A business can identify problems through inspection, customer complaints, returns and refunds, customer reviews and surveys, and staff reports. It can measure quality with the percentage of items that are defective, the return rate, the number of complaints, repeat-purchase rate and customer satisfaction scores.

### Consequences of poor quality

Faulty goods lead to refunds, replacements and repair costs. Wider consequences include lost sales, a damaged reputation, lower customer loyalty, falling staff morale and possible legal problems if goods are unsafe.

### Methods of maintaining consistent quality: TQM

Total quality management (TQM) treats quality as the job of every employee in every department, so that faults are prevented at each stage rather than found only by final inspection. Advantages for a business: fewer defects and less waste, lower costs of rework and returns, happier and more loyal customers, a reputation that may support higher prices, and staff who feel involved. Costs include training, time to change habits, and the need for management commitment.

### Costs and benefits of maintaining quality

Costs: better materials, training, equipment and checking. Benefits: fewer returns, stronger reputation, loyalty and often higher prices. A business should compare them.

**Worked example.** Tolpuddle Toys sold 20,000 games last year. Three per cent were returned, each return costing 12 to handle and replace, so returns cost 600 x 12 = 7,200. A quality programme costing 5,000 a year should cut returns to 1%: 200 x 12 = 2,400. The saving is 7,200 - 2,400 = 4,800, which is 200 less than the cost. On direct costs alone the programme does not pay for itself in year one. A fuller answer adds the harder-to-measure gains, such as reputation and repeat sales, and may recommend going ahead.

### Quality as a business grows

Growth can put quality at risk. Managers see less of the daily work, new staff need training, and demand may outrun the business's ability to check. Two routes carry particular risks.

- Outsourcing: another firm does part of the work. The business has less direct control, and the supplier may cut corners; it still carries the blame if customers are unhappy.
- Franchising: independent owners run outlets under the brand name. A weak franchisee can damage the brand everywhere, so the franchisor needs clear standards, training and inspections.

## 3.3.4 Good customer services

Customer service is the help and attention a business gives before, during and after a sale.

### Methods of good service

- Product knowledge: staff who understand what they sell can advise accurately, match the product to the need and answer questions.
- Customer engagement: creating a positive experience, for example friendly greetings, listening, personal recommendations and quick handling of problems.
- Post-sales services: help after purchase, such as user training, help lines and servicing.

### Benefits and dangers

Benefits of good service include greater customer satisfaction, loyalty and repeat custom, good word of mouth and the chance to charge more. Costs of poor service include dissatisfied customers, a poor reputation and lost sales. Good service itself costs money in training, staff time and systems, so businesses weigh it against the return.

**Worked example.** Ormskirk Print Works spends 6,000 a year on a help line. Without it, it expects to lose 5% of its 800 regular customers, which is 40 customers. Each is worth 180 a year in sales contribution. Value kept: 40 x 180 = 7,200, which exceeds the 6,000 cost by 1,200. The help line looks worthwhile, though the figure of 5% is an estimate.

### How ICT has changed customer service

- Websites: 24-hour information, frequently asked questions, order tracking and live chat.
- E-commerce: customers buy online at any time, with fast payment and delivery tracking; it widens the market but needs reliable delivery and returns.
- Social media: quick, public two-way contact. Businesses can answer queries and gather feedback, but a poor reply is visible to many people.

## Common errors

- Describing a production method without saying why it fits the business in the stem.
- Treating JIT as only a benefit. The specification wants costs of frequent deliveries and lost bulk discounts weighed too.
- Choosing the cheapest supplier without checking quality and reliability.
- Writing "TQM means checking quality at the end". It means every employee builds in quality throughout.
- Listing customer service ideas without linking them to loyalty, reputation or sales.
