## What 2.2.3 contains

The specification has two bullet points:

- appropriate promotion strategies for different market segments: advertising, sponsorship, product trials, special offers, branding
- the use of technology in promotion: targeted advertising online, viral advertising via social media, e-newsletters.

The word **appropriate** is the key. A question rarely asks you only to describe advertising; it asks which method suits a particular segment, or whether a method is worth the money for a particular business.

## The five promotion strategies

### Advertising

Paid messages in media such as television, radio, newspapers, magazines, posters, cinema and websites. Advertising reaches many people, but the business pays whether or not they buy. The choice of medium should follow the segment: a local radio station reaches local listeners of particular ages, a specialist magazine reaches people with a particular hobby, and a poster at a railway station reaches commuters.

### Sponsorship

A business pays, or provides goods, to support an event, team, programme or organisation in return for its name being displayed. Sponsorship builds awareness and links the brand with something the target segment already likes. A sports drink sponsoring a local half marathon reaches runners directly; a building firm sponsoring a youth football team reaches local parents and builds goodwill. Its weakness is that it is hard to measure how many sales it brings.

### Product trials

Customers try the product free or at low cost: free samples, tasting stands, free trial periods for software or a first class free at a gym. Trials remove the risk of trying something new, so they suit new products and segments that are cautious about switching. They cost the business the product given away, and some customers take the free trial with no intention of buying.

### Special offers

Temporary incentives such as discounts, "buy one get one free", loyalty points, free gifts or competitions. They create urgency and suit price-sensitive segments. Used too often, they cut profit and teach customers to wait for the next offer.

### Branding

A brand is the name, logo, design and image that make a business's products recognisable and distinct. Strong branding builds recognition and trust, supports a higher price and encourages repeat purchases. It takes time and consistent spending to build, and one bad story can damage it.

## Matching strategies to segments

| Segment | Likely appropriate strategies | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Teenagers | Viral social media, influencer posts, special offers | Heavy social media users; limited incomes |
| Busy professionals | Targeted online adverts, e-newsletters, branding | Short of time; respond to convenience and trust |
| Retired people | Local press, radio, product trials | Often read local papers; may be cautious about new products |
| Families | Special offers, sponsorship of local children's activities | Price-sensitive; value community links |
| Luxury buyers | Branding, selective magazine advertising | Image matters more than price; offers can cheapen the brand |

These are typical tendencies, not rules. Use the details in the case: if it says the target customers are students who use a particular app, the right answer follows from that.

## Technology in promotion

**Targeted advertising online.** Search engines and social media platforms show adverts to people chosen by age, location, interests or recent searches. The business can set a small budget, pay mainly when people click or view, and measure results closely. Customers may find the adverts intrusive, and many people use ad blockers or scroll past.

**Viral advertising via social media.** Content that people choose to share with their own contacts spreads rapidly at little cost to the business. It can reach huge audiences, but the business cannot control whether content goes viral or how people react; a joke that misfires spreads just as fast.

**E-newsletters.** Regular emails to people who have signed up. They are cheap to send, reach customers who have already shown interest and can include offers and links to buy. They need a list of subscribers built over time, and many emails go unopened or are unsubscribed from.

## Judging whether promotion is worth it

### Worked example 1: cost per new customer at Harlow Pet Grooming

Harlow Pet Grooming tried two methods last spring.

| | Local radio advert | Targeted social media adverts |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | £3,000 | £1,200 |
| New customers | 60 | 40 |
| Cost per new customer | £3,000 ÷ 60 = **£50** | £1,200 ÷ 40 = **£30** |

Each new customer brings about £35 of contribution in their first year. The radio advert cost more per customer than one year's contribution, while the social media adverts cost less. The targeted adverts look better value, but radio reached more customers in total, and a groomed dog's owner may return for several years, which changes the calculation.

### Worked example 2: does a special offer pay?

Fernlea Fudge sells 1,000 bags a week at £4. Each costs £1.60 to make, so weekly contribution is £2.40 × 1,000 = £2,400. A 20% discount cuts the price to £3.20 and sales rise to 1,400 bags.

New contribution = (£3.20 − £1.60) × 1,400 = **£2,240**, which is £160 a week lower.

The offer increases sales by 40% but lowers contribution, because each bag now earns £1.60 instead of £2.40. It could still be worthwhile if it brings new customers who keep buying at the full price after the offer ends.

### Worked example 3: an e-newsletter funnel

Larkspur Books sends a newsletter to 4,000 subscribers. 30% open it (1,200), 10% of those click a link (120), and 25% of clickers buy (30). With an average order of £40, the newsletter brings **£1,200** of sales. If it costs £150 to produce and send, it is good value, but the figures show how few readers actually buy.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a method without saying why it suits the segment in the question.
- Assuming that more sales from a special offer means more profit.
- Treating viral advertising as guaranteed; the business cannot make content go viral.
- Confusing branding with advertising. A brand is the identity; advertising is one way to communicate it.
- Ignoring cost: a television advert is rarely appropriate for a small local business.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** cost per customer, the effect of a discount on revenue or contribution, or a percentage from a newsletter or advert.
- **Outline:** two linked points in context, such as why sponsorship suits Harlow's dog-owning customers.
- **Explain:** a general statement with two expansions, for example why e-newsletters cost little to reach interested customers.
- **Analyse:** take one strategy and follow its effect on awareness, sales and costs for the business in the case.
- **Justify:** choose between two promotion options using the figures and the target segment.
- **Evaluate:** weigh cost, reach and suitability for the segment, and conclude on what the decision depends.
