These questions cover section 1.2, Accounting concepts, in Topic 1 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. All businesses and figures are invented. Amounts are in dollars, written without a currency sign. Teaching is in the [accounting concepts study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-accounting-concepts/) and the [accounting concepts revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-accounting-concepts-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Name the accounting concept that applies to each statement.

**(a)** The owner's private holiday paid from the business bank account is recorded as drawings. **[1]**
**(b)** A loyal customer base is not shown in the statement of financial position. **[1]**
**(c)** Inventory is valued in the same way as last year. **[1]**
**(d)** Commission earned in December but received in January is included in this year's income. **[1]**

**2.** State the significance of the money measurement concept for a user of the statement of financial position. **[2]**

**3.** The owner of a shop pays the shop's rent of 800 from her personal bank account. Give the double entry. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why prudence does not mean deliberately understating profit. **[2]**

**5.** A business with a year end of 31 December pays insurance of 3,600 on 1 October for twelve months. Calculate the insurance expense for the year and the amount carried forward at 31 December. Name the concept applied. **[3]**

**6.** A business treats any item costing under 250 as an expense. It buys a calculator for 35 and a laptop for 900. State how each is recorded and name the concept. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Rushmere Traders had revenue of 120,000 in each of two years. In Year 1, cost of sales was 72,000, including carriage inwards of 3,000. In Year 2 the figures were identical, but the bookkeeper included the 3,000 carriage inwards with expenses instead.

**(a)** Calculate gross profit, and gross profit as a percentage of revenue, for each year as reported. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain the significance of the consistency concept in this case. **[3]**

**8.** During the year, Leila, owner of Leila's Florist:

- took 2,500 cash from the business for private use;
- paid her home internet bill of 60 from the business bank account;
- paid a business supplier 1,200 from her personal bank account;
- brought her own van, valued at 8,000, into the business for permanent use.

**(a)** Give the double entry for each transaction. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how the business entity concept affects the profit and capital shown. **[2]**

**9.** At the year end, trade receivables are 18,500. A customer owing 700 cannot pay and the debt is to be written off. A provision for irrecoverable debts of 5% of the remaining receivables is to be created; there was no provision before.

**(a)** Calculate the provision, the receivables shown in the statement of financial position, and the total reduction in profit. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain the significance of prudence in this case. **[2]**

**10.** Explain the significance of the accruals concept, using an example of income. **[4]**

**11.** The owner of a business wants to change from the straight line to the reducing balance method of depreciation, saying it is "more prudent". Discuss the conflict between consistency and prudence in this situation. **[4]**

**12.** The owner of Bracken Hill Pottery says: "Our statement of financial position undervalues us. It leaves out our brand, which everyone knows, and my house, which I used as security for the business loan." Explain why both items are left out, naming the concept in each case. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Business entity [1]. (b) Money measurement [1]. (c) Consistency [1]. (d) Accruals [1]. **[4]**

**2.** Only items that can be measured in money are recorded, so things such as staff skill or reputation are left out [1]; users should not treat the statement as a full valuation of the business [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Debit rent (expense) 800 [1]; credit capital 800 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The rent is a business expense, even though the money came from the owner; her payment increases her capital.

**4.** Prudence means profit and assets should not be **overstated**, with expected losses recognised promptly [1]; deliberately understating would also make the statements unreliable, so it is not allowed [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Expense 3,600 × 3/12 = **900** [1]. Carried forward (other receivable) 3,600 − 900 = **2,700** [1]. Accruals [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Calculator: expense; laptop: non-current asset [1]. Materiality [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Year 1: gross profit 120,000 − 72,000 = **48,000** [1], **40%** [1]. Year 2: cost of sales 69,000, gross profit **51,000** [1], **42.5%** [1].
**(b)** Trading was the same in both years, but gross profit appears 3,000 higher in Year 2 only because the classification changed [1]. Users comparing years would wrongly think buying had become more efficient [1]. Consistency requires the same classification each year so that comparisons are fair; profit for the year is unaffected, but the gross margin is distorted [1]. **[7]**

**8. (a)** Debit drawings 2,500, credit cash 2,500 [1]. Debit drawings 60, credit bank 60 [1]. Debit trade payables 1,200, credit capital 1,200 [1]. Debit motor vehicles 8,000, credit capital 8,000 [1].
**(b)** Private items such as the internet bill go to drawings, not expenses, so profit is not reduced by them [1]; resources the owner puts in increase capital and resources she takes out reduce it, so capital shows her true investment [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Remaining receivables 18,500 − 700 = 17,800; provision 17,800 × 5% = **890** [1]. Receivables shown 17,800 − 890 = **16,910** [1]. Profit reduced by 700 + 890 [1] = **1,590** [1].
**(b)** Prudence stops assets and profit being overstated [1]: the business expects not to collect these amounts, so showing the full 18,500 would mislead the owner and lenders [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Accruals means income and expenses are recorded in the period in which they are earned or incurred, not when cash is received or paid [1]. Example: rent receivable for December of 500, received in January, is included in this year's income [1] and shown as an other receivable [1]. This makes profit reflect the year's activity rather than the timing of payments, so years can be compared fairly [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Consistency says the same depreciation method should be used each year so that profit can be compared [1]. Prudence may favour a method that charges more depreciation early, so the asset value is not overstated [1]. Changing method would alter profit even though nothing about the asset's use has changed, which could mislead users [1]. A change is justified only if reducing balance better reflects how the asset loses value, not simply to adjust profit [1]. **[4]**

**12.** The brand is left out because of money measurement [1]: it has no reliable money value from a transaction, so it cannot be recorded [1]. The house is left out because of business entity [1]: it belongs to the owner, not the business, even if it secures the loan [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the concept but not its effect on profit, assets or capital.
- Recording an owner's payment of a business bill as drawings instead of capital.
- Describing prudence as deliberate understatement.
- Recording insurance paid as the full year's expense regardless of the period covered.
- Forgetting that a change of classification also breaks consistency.
