## Questions

**1.** Define aridity using the UNEP aridity index, and state the index range for a semi-arid area. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how a cold ocean current can make a west-coast desert more arid. **[4]**

**3.** The table shows mean monthly data for Tamberet, a fictional semi-arid weather station at about 14 degrees north.

| | J | F | M | A | M | J | J | A | S | O | N | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall (mm) | 0 | 0 | 1 | 3 | 12 | 38 | 96 | 128 | 54 | 9 | 1 | 0 |
| Mean temperature (°C) | 23 | 25 | 29 | 32 | 33 | 31 | 28 | 27 | 28 | 29 | 26 | 23 |

**(a)** Calculate the annual rainfall and the percentage that falls in July, August and September. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the seasonal pattern of rainfall and temperature shown. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest why mean temperature falls in July and August even though the sun is high in the sky. **[2]**

**4.** At a fictional site in a hot desert, a rock surface measured 12 °C at 06:00 and 58 °C at 14:00.

**(a)** Calculate the range between these two readings. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how daily changes like this may lead to rock breakdown, and why moisture may also be needed. **[4]**

**5.** Distinguish between saltation, traction and suspension as ways that wind transports sediment. **[3]**

**6.** With the help of a labelled sketch, explain the formation of zeugen. **[5]**

**7.** Explain how episodic rainfall leads to flash floods, and how flash floods help form alluvial fans. **[6]**

**8.** Distinguish between physical and physiological drought, and explain two ways plants in hot arid areas are adapted to drought. **[5]**

**9.** The table shows changes on the fictional Qasr Nadir irrigation scheme after canal irrigation began without field drainage.

| Years since irrigation began | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Topsoil salinity (EC, dS/m) | 1.2 | 2.6 | 4.1 | 5.8 |
| Wheat yield (tonnes per hectare) | 3.0 | 2.8 | 2.3 | 1.7 |

**(a)** Describe the changes shown, using data. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how irrigation without drainage can cause these changes. **[4]**

**10.** Assess the relative importance of wind and water in shaping the landforms of hot arid environments. **[12]**

**11.** To what extent is desertification in semi-arid areas the result of human activity rather than natural factors? **[12]**

**12.** With reference to a case study of a hot arid or semi-arid environment, evaluate the success of attempts to manage it sustainably. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Aridity is a long-term water deficit, measured by the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration (P/PET) [1]. A semi-arid area has an index of 0.20 to 0.50 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition based on rainfall alone, such as "under 250 mm", is weaker because it ignores evaporation.

**2.** Cold currents (such as the Benguela) chill the air above the sea [1]. Cool air beneath warmer air forms a temperature inversion [1]. The inversion stops air rising, so convection clouds and rain cannot form [1]. Moisture condenses as fog or low stratus rather than rain [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The inversion is the step most answers miss; without it the chain from cold water to no rain is incomplete.

**3. (a)** 0+0+1+3+12+38+96+128+54+9+1+0 = 342 mm [1]. July to September = 278 mm, which is 278/342 x 100 = 81.3% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Rainfall is concentrated in one wet season, peaking in August at 128 mm [1]. Seven months receive under 10 mm, giving a long dry season [1]. Temperature peaks before the rains, at 33 °C in May, with a range of 10 °C between the warmest and coolest months [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Thick cloud during the wet season reduces incoming solar radiation [1]. Rain and moist soils use energy for evaporation, which cools the surface [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Using data" means quoting figures with units; a description without numbers is held at the lowest mark.

**4. (a)** 58 - 12 = 46 °C [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Rock heats and expands by day and cools and contracts at night [1]. Dark and light minerals expand by different amounts, so stress builds between grains (granular disintegration) [1]. The outer layer changes temperature more than the interior, so shells may peel off (exfoliation) [1]. Laboratory work suggests dry rock rarely breaks from heating alone, so dew or rain probably weakens it through chemical or salt processes [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the 46 °C figure in your explanation to show the fictional data has been applied, not ignored.

**5.** Saltation: sand grains are lifted and bounce along in short hops, mostly near the ground [1]. Traction (surface creep): larger grains are rolled or pushed along the surface, often by the impact of saltating grains [1]. Suspension: fine dust is held up in the air and can travel long distances [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs a clear difference for each term, so state particle size or movement type every time.

**6.** Zeugen form where horizontal bands of hard rock lie over softer rock [1]. Vertical joints in the hard cap are widened by weathering and abrasion [1]. Wind-blown sand abrades most strongly near the ground, where saltation is concentrated [1]. The softer rock beneath is undercut faster, leaving tabular ridges with a hard cap [1]. A sketch labelling the cap rock, soft rock, undercut base and wind direction [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A sketch earns credit only if its labels explain processes, not just name parts.

**7.** Rainfall in deserts comes in rare, intense storms [1]. Bare, crusted surfaces have low infiltration, so overland flow begins quickly [1]. Water concentrates in channels and rises rapidly as a flash flood with a high sediment load [1]. Where the channel leaves the mountain front, it loses confinement and spreads out [1]. Gradient and velocity fall and water is lost by seepage, so the load is deposited [1]. Coarse material is dropped near the apex and finer material towards the edge, building a cone-shaped fan [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain why energy falls at the mountain front; naming the fan without this sequence limits the mark.

**8.** Physical drought is an actual shortage of water in the soil [1]. Physiological drought occurs when water is present but plants cannot take it up, for example because salty soil water reverses osmosis [1]. Succulents store water in stems or leaves [1] and open their stomata at night to reduce water loss [1]. Phreatophytes grow very deep roots to reach groundwater [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Each adaptation needs both the feature and how it saves or gains water.

**9. (a)** Salinity rises steadily from 1.2 to 5.8 dS/m, nearly five times higher [1]. Wheat yield falls from 3.0 to 1.7 tonnes per hectare, a fall of 43.3% [1]. The yield decline speeds up after year 5 as salinity rises further [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Extra water from canals raises the water table [1]. Close to the surface, water is drawn up by capillary action [1]. High evaporation leaves dissolved salts behind in the topsoil [1]. Salty soil water causes physiological drought, so crops take up less water and yields fall [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the two variables; describing salinity and yield separately misses the relationship the question invites.

**10.** Indicative content (the real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point):
- Define aeolian (wind) and fluvial (water) processes [1].
- Strong winds and bare surfaces make deserts high wind energy environments [1].
- Wind forms dunes, such as barchans where wind is from one direction [1].
- Abrasion and deflation form yardangs, zeugen and deflation hollows [1].
- Episodic storms on bare ground produce flash floods [1].
- Water cuts wadis and arroyos and builds alluvial fans and bahadas [1].
- Sheet floods help form pediments; playas collect water in enclosed basins [1].
- One flood can move more sediment than years of wind [1].
- Many fluvial landforms are inherited from pluvials, e.g. Lake Bonneville shorelines [1].
- Weathering (salt, thermal) prepares material for both agents [1].
- Balance varies: sand seas are wind-dominated; mountain fronts are water-dominated [1].
- Reasoned conclusion answering "relative importance" [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* High-level answers judge the balance by place and landform rather than listing wind features then water features.

**11.** Indicative content (marked by levels of response in the real exam):
- Define desertification as land degradation in drylands from climatic variations and human activities [1].
- Natural factor: long droughts, such as those in the Sahel in the 1970s and 1980s [1].
- Natural factor: high rainfall variability reduces plant cover [1].
- Charney's albedo idea: less vegetation may reduce rainfall further [1].
- Human factor: overgrazing, especially around boreholes [1].
- Human factor: overcultivation and shorter fallows reduce fertility [1].
- Human factor: cutting trees for fuelwood [1].
- Human factor: poorly drained irrigation causes salinisation [1].
- Underlying causes: population growth and settled herders concentrate pressure [1].
- Counter-evidence: regreening as rainfall partly returned shows climate matters [1].
- Interaction: drought starts degradation; land use decides whether it lasts [1].
- Judgement on "to what extent" with a supported conclusion [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided answer, all human or all natural, cannot reach the top level on a "to what extent" question.

**12.** Indicative content using the Sahel (levels of response apply in the real exam):
- Locate the case: semi-arid Sahel, Niger and Burkina Faso [1].
- Problem: low, variable rainfall and a short wet season [1].
- Problem: population growth raises demand for food and fuelwood [1].
- Problem: crusted soils and runoff; shorter fallows [1].
- FMNR began in Maradi in 1983 [1].
- About five million hectares by 2004 [1].
- FMNR is cheap and uses local knowledge [1].
- Zaï pits and stone lines in Yatenga trap water and soil [1].
- Zaï need heavy labour and scarce manure [1].
- Great Green Wall: AU, 2007, target 100 million hectares by 2030 [1].
- A 2020 UN status report found about 4% of the target reached [1].
- Funding gaps and conflict limit progress [1].
- Bottom-up schemes worked better where farmers gained directly [1].
- Sustainability judged on environmental, economic and social grounds [1].
- Supported overall judgement [1]. **[15]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate the success" needs criteria for success; without them, the answer stays descriptive.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting no figures when the question says "using data".
- Explaining aridity with "it is hot" rather than pressure, currents or rain shadow.
- Leaving out the temperature inversion when explaining cold-current deserts.
- Confusing saltation and traction.
- Drawing sketches with labels that name features but do not explain processes.
- Ignoring water in desert landform essays.
- Treating desertification as wholly human or wholly natural.
- Describing a case study without judging success against clear criteria.
