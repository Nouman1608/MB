Key development 3.1.4.1 of the 9236 specification, Earlier Mughal emperors (c1526-1658), includes
"social and cultural developments up to 1658, including religion, literature, education, music and
architecture". This guide takes each of those five areas in turn, adding painting, which runs through
several of them. It belongs to the optional topic The Mughal Empire (c1526-c1850s), examined in Paper
1 Section C. For the political story of the emperors, use the
[Mughal Empire study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-mughal-empire/).

## Why Mughal culture was distinctive

Mughal culture brought together three traditions: the **Persian** culture of the court, the
**Central Asian Turkic** heritage of Babur's family, and the **Indian** traditions of the lands they
ruled. Wealth from a large, stable empire paid for buildings, books and artists, and emperors used
culture to display power and legitimacy. When you explain a cultural development, ask what it showed
about the emperor's aims.

## Religion

**Babur and Humayun** were Sunni Muslims. Humayun's long exile in Safavid Persia (1540s-1555) brought
him into close contact with Persian Shia culture and artists, whom he later brought to India.

**Akbar (1556-1605)** developed the most distinctive religious policy.

- He abolished the tax on Hindu pilgrims (1563) and the **jizya** (1564).
- He married Rajput princesses, beginning with a princess of Amber in 1562, and gave Rajput nobles
  high rank.
- In 1575 he built the **Ibadat Khana** (House of Worship) at Fatehpur Sikri, where Muslim scholars
  debated, later joined by Hindus, Jains, Zoroastrians and Jesuit priests from Goa.
- The **Mahzar** (1579) declared his right to decide between the opinions of religious scholars.
- From 1582 a small circle followed the **Din-i-Ilahi**, a court discipline of loyalty and ethics
  centred on the emperor, which attracted very few followers.
- The principle behind these policies is often called **sulh-i-kul**, "peace with all".

Orthodox scholars criticised Akbar's policies; the reformer **Ahmad Sirhindi** argued for a return
to the Sharia.

**Jahangir (1605-27)** largely continued toleration, but he executed the Sikh Guru Arjan (1606) after
the Guru was accused of supporting a rebellion by Jahangir's son Khusrau. **Shah Jahan (1628-58)**
was more orthodox in his public religion, while his eldest son **Dara Shikoh** studied Sufism and
Hindu philosophy, writing *Majma-ul-Bahrain* (*The Mingling of the Two Oceans*) and sponsoring a
Persian translation of the Upanishads.

## Literature

**Persian** was the language of the court, government and most literature.

- **Memoirs.** Babur wrote his memoir, the *Baburnama*, in Chagatai Turkish; it was translated into
  Persian under Akbar. Gulbadan Begum, Babur's daughter, wrote the *Humayun-nama*. Jahangir wrote his
  own memoir, the *Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri*.
- **Histories.** Abul Fazl wrote the *Akbarnama* and the *Ain-i-Akbari*, which describes the
  empire's government, revenue and customs. Badauni wrote a history critical of Akbar's religious
  policy. Under Shah Jahan, Abdul Hamid Lahori wrote the official *Padshahnama*.
- **Translation.** Akbar's translation workshop put Sanskrit works into Persian, including the
  *Mahabharata* (as the *Razmnama*) and the *Ramayana*.
- **Poetry.** Faizi, Abul Fazl's brother, was Akbar's court poet. Hindi poetry also flourished: the
  noble Abdur Rahim Khan-i-Khanan wrote Hindi verse, and Tulsidas, author of the *Ramcharitmanas*,
  lived in Akbar's reign.

## Education

- **Maktabs**, often attached to mosques, taught reading, writing and the Qur'an; **madrasas**
  offered higher study of religion, law, logic and literature.
- The *Ain-i-Akbari* records Akbar's wish that students should also learn ethics, arithmetic,
  agriculture, geometry, astronomy, medicine and history, and that Hindus study Sanskrit learning.
- Emperors built large **libraries**. Humayun died in 1556 after falling on the stairs of his library
  in Delhi. Akbar, who did not learn to read, had books read aloud to him daily.
- Royal women were often highly educated: Gulbadan Begum wrote history; Nur Jahan was a poet;
  Jahanara, Shah Jahan's daughter, wrote on Sufism.

## Music

Akbar was a great patron of music. The singer **Tansen**, who came to Akbar's court in 1562, is
remembered as one of the greatest musicians in the subcontinent's history and is associated with the
*dhrupad* style. Persian and Indian musical traditions mixed at court. Jahangir and Shah Jahan
continued to employ court musicians, and music accompanied festivals such as Nauroz.

## Painting

- **Humayun** brought the Persian painters Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad to India.
- **Akbar's** workshop combined Persian and Indian painters, such as Daswanth and Basawan, and
  produced the huge illustrated *Hamzanama* as well as illustrated copies of the *Akbarnama*.
- **Jahangir** preferred close, naturalistic studies; Ustad Mansur painted birds and animals, and
  Abu'l Hasan painted portraits and court scenes.
- **Shah Jahan's** painters illustrated the *Padshahnama* with formal court ceremonies.

## Society and court life

The specification asks about social as well as cultural developments.

- **The emperor in public.** Akbar began the practice of *jharokha darshan*, appearing each morning at
  a palace window so that subjects could see him; Jahangir and Shah Jahan continued it. Jahangir
  describes in his memoir a "chain of justice" with bells, which petitioners could ring to reach him.
- **Festivals.** The Persian new year, **Nauroz**, was celebrated at court, and Akbar also took part in
  Hindu festivals, a sign of his inclusive policy.
- **Women at court.** **Nur Jahan**, Jahangir's wife, held real political influence; coins were issued
  in her name. Royal women commissioned buildings, gardens and books.
- **Travel and trade.** Roads and *sarais* (rest houses) linked cities, helping merchants, pilgrims and
  officials, and spreading court fashions in dress, food and language.

## Architecture

| Reign | Buildings | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Babur | Gardens at Agra | The *charbagh*, a garden divided into four by water channels |
| Humayun (built under Akbar) | Humayun's Tomb, Delhi | Garden tomb; red sandstone with white marble; double dome |
| Akbar | Agra Fort; Fatehpur Sikri with the Buland Darwaza and Jama Masjid; Lahore Fort rebuilt | Red sandstone; Persian and Indian forms combined |
| Jahangir | Akbar's tomb at Sikandra completed; Itimad-ud-Daulah's tomb, Agra, built by Nur Jahan | White marble with *pietra dura* inlay of coloured stones |
| Jahangir (built after his death) | Jahangir's tomb, Shahdara, Lahore | Garden tomb with minarets |
| Shah Jahan | Taj Mahal, Agra; Red Fort and Jama Masjid, Delhi; Shalimar Gardens and Sheesh Mahal, Lahore; Wazir Khan Mosque, Lahore; Shah Jahan Mosque, Thatta | White marble, symmetry, inlay, tile work; the high point of Mughal architecture |

Shah Jahan began the **Taj Mahal** in 1632 as the tomb of his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died in 1631.
The buildings in Lahore and Thatta matter for Pakistan Studies because they show that Mughal culture
shaped the cities of present-day Pakistan.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain why Akbar's religious policies were important. **[4]**

```
Point 1   abolished jizya (1564) and married Rajput princesses
Explain   won the loyalty of Hindu subjects and Rajput nobles, so the
          empire was more stable and could recruit able commanders

Point 2   Ibadat Khana debates and the Mahzar (1579)
Explain   gave Akbar authority over religious disputes but provoked
          orthodox opposition, such as Ahmad Sirhindi's later reforms
```

## Common mistakes

- **Saying Akbar made everyone convert to Din-i-Ilahi.** It had very few followers.
- **Attributing the Badshahi Mosque to Shah Jahan.** It was built by Aurangzeb after 1658, so it falls
  outside this content point.
- **Calling the Taj Mahal a palace.** It is a tomb.
- **Listing buildings without explaining what they show** about wealth, power or cultural blending.
