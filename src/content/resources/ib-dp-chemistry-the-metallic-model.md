## The metallic bond (2.3.1)

Metal atoms hold their outer electrons loosely. In a solid metal, each atom releases its valence electrons. These electrons no longer belong to any one atom. They spread through the whole structure and are described as **delocalized**.

What is left behind is a positive ion, a **cation**. The cations are packed closely in a regular, repeating **lattice**.

> **Metallic bonding** is the electrostatic attraction that holds a lattice of cations to the delocalized electrons spread around them.

Three features of this model explain almost everything else in the unit.

- The attraction acts **in all directions**. No electron pair links one particular atom to another, so the bonding is **non-directional**.
- The delocalized electrons are **mobile**. They can drift through the lattice.
- The bond is a **collective** attraction. Every cation is attracted to the electron cloud around it, not to a single partner.

A sample of metal contains no molecules and no fixed ratio of ions to electrons that you write as a formula. You write it with its element symbol, such as Ca or Rb.

### Worked example 1: describing the bonding in calcium

Describe the structure and bonding in solid calcium. Calcium is in group 2.

```
Electron configuration: [Ar] 4s²
Each atom releases its two 4s electrons into the lattice
Particles left in the lattice: Ca²⁺ cations, closely packed in a regular arrangement
Delocalized electrons: two per atom, free to move through the whole structure
Bond: electrostatic attraction between the Ca²⁺ lattice and the delocalized electrons
```

Notice the two parts of a full answer: what the particles are, and what attracts what.

## Explaining metal properties (2.3.1)

### Electrical conductivity

An electric current is a flow of charge. A metal conducts because its delocalized electrons can move. When a potential difference is applied across a piece of metal, the electrons drift towards the positive terminal, and electrons enter from the negative terminal to replace them. The cations do **not** move. They stay at their lattice sites.

This works in the solid and in the liquid. Mercury, a metal that is liquid at 298 K, still conducts, because melting does not remove the delocalized electrons.

### Thermal conductivity

When one end of a metal is heated, the delocalized electrons there gain kinetic energy. Because they are mobile, they carry that energy quickly to cooler parts of the metal and pass it on through collisions. Vibrations of the closely packed cations also pass energy along. The mobile electrons are the reason metals conduct heat far better than most non-metals.

### Malleability

A **malleable** material can be hammered or pressed into a new shape without breaking. A related property, **ductility**, is the ability to be drawn into wire.

When a force is applied to a metal, layers of cations slide over one another. The delocalized electrons move with them, so after the shift each cation is still surrounded by the electron cloud and still attracted to it. Because the bonding is non-directional, no particular bond has to break, and the metal changes shape instead of shattering.

Compare this with an ionic lattice. If one layer of ions slides, ions of the same charge end up side by side. They repel, and the crystal splits. That is why ionic solids are brittle and metals are not.

### Relating properties to uses

| Use | Property that matters | Link to the bonding |
|---|---|---|
| Copper wiring in buildings | High electrical conductivity; ductility | Mobile delocalized electrons; layers slide so copper can be drawn into wire |
| Aluminium cooking foil | Malleability; thermal conductivity; low density | Non-directional bonding lets it be rolled thin; mobile electrons carry heat |
| Copper base on a saucepan | High thermal conductivity | Delocalized electrons transfer energy across the base quickly |
| Thin gold layer on electrical contacts | Conducts electricity; does not corrode | Delocalized electrons carry charge; gold reacts very little with air |

When you link a property to a use, give three things: the use, the property, and the feature of metallic bonding that explains it. Density and resistance to corrosion often matter as well, even though they are not explained by the bond model alone.

## Strength of the metallic bond (2.3.2)

The strength of the attraction depends on two factors.

- **Charge of the cation.** A cation with a higher charge attracts the electron cloud more strongly. A metal that forms 2+ ions also releases two electrons per atom, so the cloud has more negative charge in the same space. This is a higher **electron density**, the term the guide uses in its guidance.
- **Radius of the metal ion.** A smaller cation lets the delocalized electrons come closer to its nucleus, so the attraction is stronger. A larger cation gives weaker bonding.

To melt a metal, you must loosen the lattice so the cations can move past each other. A stronger metallic bond needs more energy, so it gives a **higher melting point**.

### Trend down a group

Going down group 1, the cation charge stays at 1+, but the ion radius increases because each element has an extra occupied shell. The attraction between cations and delocalized electrons weakens, so melting points fall.

| Metal | Melting point / K |
|---|---|
| K | 337 |
| Rb | 312 |
| Cs | 302 |

Caesium melts just above room temperature. Its large ions hold the electron cloud weakly.

### Trend across a period

Across a period, from group 1 to group 2, the cation charge rises from 1+ to 2+, the number of delocalized electrons per atom doubles, and the ion becomes smaller because the nuclear charge is greater. All three changes strengthen the metallic bond. Potassium melts at 337 K, while calcium, next to it in period 4, melts at 1115 K.

### Worked example 2: comparing electron density

Use these values to compare the number of delocalized electrons in 1.00 cm³ of potassium and of calcium. Take the Avogadro constant as 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

| Metal | Density / g cm⁻³ | Molar mass / g mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| K | 0.89 | 39.10 |
| Ca | 1.55 | 40.08 |

```
Mass of 1.00 cm³: K = 0.89 g        Ca = 1.55 g

Amount: n = m / M
  K:  0.89 / 39.10 = 0.0228 mol
  Ca: 1.55 / 40.08 = 0.0387 mol

Atoms: n × 6.02 × 10²³
  K:  1.37 × 10²² atoms
  Ca: 2.33 × 10²² atoms

Delocalized electrons: atoms × electrons released per atom
  K:  1.37 × 10²² × 1 = 1.37 × 10²²
  Ca: 2.33 × 10²² × 2 = 4.66 × 10²²

Ratio Ca : K = 4.66 / 1.37 ≈ 3.4
```

Calcium has more than three times the electron density of potassium, and each of its cations carries double the charge. This is the simple picture behind its much higher melting point.

### A note on p-block metals

The same ideas apply to metals in the p-block, such as aluminium (Al³⁺) and tin. Treat the trend as a guide, not a law. Real metals pack their atoms in different arrangements, and some p-block metals do not fit the pattern well: gallium, in group 13, melts at only 303 K. If a question gives you data that do not fit, describe what the data show and say the simple model does not fully account for it.

## Transition elements (2.3.3, HL only)

Transition elements have electrons in a partly filled d sublevel as well as in the outer s sublevel. In the metallic lattice, **both the 4s and the 3d electrons can become delocalized**. A period 4 transition metal can therefore release more electrons per atom than potassium or calcium.

This has two consequences.

- **High melting points.** With more delocalized electrons, the electron density is higher and each cation left behind carries a larger positive charge. The cations are also small, because the nuclear charge is high. The metallic bond is strong, so a lot of energy is needed to disrupt the lattice.
- **Electrical conductivity.** The delocalized d-electrons add to the mobile charge carriers, so transition elements are good conductors of electricity.

### Worked example 3: period 4 melting points (HL only)

Use these melting points to explain the trend from potassium to vanadium.

| Element | K | Ca | Sc | Ti | V |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Melting point / K | 337 | 1115 | 1814 | 1941 | 2183 |
| Valence configuration | 4s¹ | 4s² | 3d¹ 4s² | 3d² 4s² | 3d³ 4s² |

```
K to Ca: 4s electrons only; delocalized electrons per atom rise from 1 to 2
         Cation charge rises, ion radius falls -> big rise in melting point (778 K)

Ca to V: 3d electrons can now join the 4s electrons in the delocalized cloud
         More delocalized electrons per atom -> higher electron density
         Stronger attraction to the cations -> melting points keep rising

Answer: the number of electrons that can be delocalized rises across
the series, which strengthens the metallic bond and raises the melting point.
```

The rise is not smooth across the whole d-block. Zinc, with a full 3d sublevel, melts at only 693 K, so its d-electrons take little part in the bonding. The chemical properties of transition elements, such as variable oxidation states and coloured complexes, belong to Reactivity 3.4.

## Common errors

- Calling the electrons "free" without saying they are delocalized through the whole lattice, or describing the lattice as made of atoms rather than cations.
- Saying the cations move when a metal conducts. Only the delocalized electrons carry the current.
- Explaining malleability by "bonds breaking and reforming" between particular atoms. The point is that the bonding is non-directional and the electron cloud moves with the layers.
- Mixing up metals and ionic compounds: a solid ionic compound does not conduct, but a solid metal does.
- Using only ion size when comparing metals from different groups. The charge and the number of delocalized electrons change too.
- Writing melting points in degrees Celsius with a "K" label. If data are in kelvin, subtract 273 to get degrees Celsius.
- (HL only) Saying only the 4s electrons are delocalized in a transition metal.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is metallic bonding in IB DP Chemistry?**
Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of cations and delocalized electrons. The attraction acts in all directions and is collective, not between particular pairs of atoms.

**Why do solid metals conduct electricity but solid ionic compounds do not?**
A solid metal has mobile delocalized electrons that carry the current. In a solid ionic compound the ions are fixed in place and there are no mobile charge carriers.

**How does melting point change down a group of metals?**
Down a group the cation charge stays the same but the ion radius increases, so the delocalized electrons are further from each nucleus and the metallic bond gets weaker.

**Which part of Structure 2.3 is HL only?**
Structure 2.3.3 on transition elements is HL only. It explains their high melting points and good electrical conductivity through delocalized d-electrons.
