## 1. The three accounts

The balance of payments records all transactions between residents of one country and the rest of the world over a period.

- **Current account:** trade in goods and services, plus income flows.
- **Capital account:** small items such as capital transfers (debt forgiveness, for example) and the buying or selling of non-produced assets such as rights to natural resources.
- **Financial account:** flows of investment and lending: foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, bank loans and deposits, and changes in official reserves.

Because every transaction has two sides, the accounts together, including a small errors and omissions item, sum to zero. In the accounting sense the whole balance of payments cannot be in deficit. A reference to a "deficit" nearly always means the current account alone.

## 2. The current account in detail

| Component | What it records | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Trade in goods | Exports minus imports of physical goods | Machinery, food, fuel |
| Trade in services | Exports minus imports of services | Tourism, banking, transport, insurance |
| Primary income | Earnings from assets and labour abroad, less payments to foreign owners | Profits, dividends and interest on foreign assets; wages of cross-border workers |
| Secondary income | One-way transfers where nothing is supplied in exchange | Workers' remittances, overseas aid, payments to international bodies |

Current account balance = goods balance + services balance + primary income balance + secondary income balance. A negative total is a **deficit** (more is paid out than is earned); a positive total is a **surplus**.

**Worked example 1.** Country A has the following figures (invented, cu bn, a year labelled illustrative). GDP is 1,540.

| Item | Credits (earned) | Debits (paid) | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods | 420 | 510 | −90 |
| Services | 260 | 190 | +70 |
| Primary income | 85 | 110 | −25 |
| Secondary income | 20 | 52 | −32 |

Current account = −90 + 70 − 25 − 32 = **−77**. The trade balance (goods plus services) is only −20, so most of the deficit comes from income flows. As a share of GDP: 77 ÷ 1,540 = **5.0%**.

Suppose the capital account shows +3 and errors and omissions +2. For the identity to hold, the financial account must show a net inflow of 72, since −77 + 3 + 72 + 2 = 0. (Presentations vary; here a positive figure means net funds flowing in.) The deficit is financed by foreigners buying Country A's assets or lending to it.

## 3. What influences the current account balance

**Productivity and costs.** Unit labour cost = labour cost per hour ÷ output per hour.

**Worked example 2.** Country A pays 24 per hour and produces 12 units per hour, so unit labour cost is 2.00. Country B pays 30 and produces 20 units per hour: 1.50. Next year A's wages rise 5% and productivity 10%: 24 × 1.05 ÷ (12 × 1.10) = 1.909, a fall of 4.5%. B's wages rise 3% and productivity 2%: 1.515, a rise of 1.0%. A's cost gap narrows, which supports exports and import substitution over time, although A remains the dearer producer.

**Inflation.** What matters is inflation relative to trading partners.

**Worked example 3.** A's prices rise 6% and its partners' 2%, with the exchange rate unchanged. A's goods become 1.06 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = **3.9%** dearer relative to rivals. To restore the old price relationship, A's currency would have to fall by 1 − 1.02 ÷ 1.06 = 3.8% (say from 0.500 to 0.481 of a B-unit).

**The exchange rate.** A fall (depreciation) lowers export prices abroad and raises import prices at home.

**Worked example 4.** The rate moves from 2.00 to 2.50 A-units per B-unit. An export priced at 600 A-units costs 300 B-units before and 240 after (−20%). An import priced at 40 B-units costs 80 A-units before and 100 after (+25%). Notice that A's currency has lost 20% of its value in B-units (0.50 to 0.40), yet import prices rise 25%.

**Elasticities and time.** A depreciation improves the current account only if demand is responsive enough: the export and import price elasticities of demand must together exceed 1 (the Marshall-Lerner condition; the specification does not name it, but it is a standard evaluation tool).

**Worked example 5.** In Country D, exports are 400 and imports 500 (balance −100). The currency falls and import prices rise 10%; export prices abroad fall 10%. Assume export prices at home are unchanged.

- Elastic case: export elasticity 1.2, import elasticity 0.8 (sum 2.0). Export volume +12%: 400 × 1.12 = 448. Import volume −8%: 500 × 1.10 × 0.92 = 506. Balance = **−58**, an improvement of 42.
- Inelastic case: export elasticity 0.3, import elasticity 0.2 (sum 0.5). Exports 400 × 1.03 = 412; imports 500 × 1.10 × 0.98 = 539. Balance = **−127**, a worsening of 27.

Elasticities are low in the short run, so the balance may worsen first and improve later (the J-curve pattern).

**Other influences:** relative growth in incomes (fast domestic growth pulls in imports), non-price competitiveness (quality, design, reliability), commodity prices and protectionism abroad.

## 4. Investment flows

| | Foreign direct investment (FDI) | Portfolio investment |
|---|---|---|
| What | Lasting interest and control: building a factory, buying a controlling stake | Buying shares or bonds without control |
| Motive | Long-term production, market access, lower costs | Return, diversification |
| Stability | Hard to reverse quickly | Liquid; can leave fast ("hot money") |

**Consequences of inflows:** they finance a current account deficit; FDI can bring capital, technology and jobs; the currency may appreciate, hurting competitiveness. **Later costs:** profits and dividends flow out as primary income debits, widening the deficit; portfolio inflows can reverse suddenly, forcing the currency down and interest rates up. **Outflows** do the opposite and build overseas assets that earn income later.

## 5. Policies to correct an imbalance

**Expenditure-reducing policies** cut total demand so that fewer imports are bought: raising taxes, trimming public spending or lifting interest rates.

**Worked example 6.** In Country A, of each extra unit of income 0.1 is saved, 0.2 is taxed and 0.2 goes on imports. Multiplier = 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.2 + 0.2) = 2. Government spending is cut by 30. Income falls 30 × 2 = 60. Imports fall 0.2 × 60 = **12**. A 60 fall in output buys only a 12 improvement in the deficit of 77: the policy is blunt and costly.

**Expenditure-switching policies** move demand from imports to home output: depreciation or devaluation (for example via lower interest rates), tariffs, quotas and export subsidies, or measures that raise competitiveness. They work without cutting total demand, but they depend on elasticities, invite retaliation and may breach trade rules.

**Supply-side policies** (education, infrastructure, innovation incentives) raise productivity and non-price competitiveness. They address structural causes but act slowly.

**Correcting a surplus** reverses these: expand demand, allow the currency to rise, and reduce barriers to imports. A surplus country faces less pressure to adjust.

## 6. Effects on other objectives

| Policy | Current account | Growth | Unemployment | Inflation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spending cuts or tax rises | Improves through lower imports | Falls | Rises | Falls |
| Depreciation | Improves if elasticities are high enough | AD rises, so growth up | Falls | Rises (dearer imports, demand pull) |
| Tariffs and quotas | Improves, but partners may retaliate | Mixed | Protects some jobs | Rises (dearer goods) |
| Supply-side policies | Improves slowly | Rises | Falls | Eases |

Chain for evaluation: depreciation raises import prices, firms using imported inputs face higher costs, workers seek higher wages, and the competitiveness gain fades. Chain for reducing policies: lower spending cuts income, firms lay off workers, unemployment rises and the government gains less tax, which may worsen its budget.

## 7. How much does an imbalance matter?

A deficit matters more when it is large relative to GDP (Country A's 5.0% would draw attention, though no fixed line separates safe from risky), persistent, financed by volatile portfolio flows or borrowing rather than FDI, and caused by weak competitiveness rather than an investment boom. Persistent deficits build foreign liabilities and future income outflows. A deficit may be harmless, even healthy, if it comes from imports of capital goods that raise future output, or if the country can borrow in its own currency.

A surplus can reflect strong competitiveness but also weak domestic demand and low living standards. It can build reserves yet cause currency appreciation, inflation and tension with trading partners. Judge by size, cause, financing, time frame and the exchange rate system.

## 8. The global picture

Every deficit has a matching surplus somewhere. If a major deficit economy corrects by cutting demand while surplus economies do nothing, world demand falls.

**Worked example 7.** Country A's spending cut (example 6) lowers its imports from Country S by 12. S saves 0.15, pays 0.2 in tax and spends 0.25 on imports out of each extra unit of income, so its multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.6 = 1.67. S's income falls 12 × 1.67 = 20. Total income lost across the two: 60 + 20 = 80, for a 12 improvement in A's balance. If S instead expanded demand, adjustment would be shared. Other effects: depreciation by a large economy pushes partners' currencies up and invites retaliation; capital flows may reverse; interest rate changes spread worldwide.

## Common errors

- Calling the whole balance of payments in deficit; it always sums to zero.
- Counting a deficit on goods as the whole current account.
- Treating a falling currency as automatically improving the balance.
- Describing FDI as "just money moving"; it brings control and production.
- Forgetting that expenditure-reducing policies hurt growth and employment.
- Giving evaluation without judging size, cause or financing.
