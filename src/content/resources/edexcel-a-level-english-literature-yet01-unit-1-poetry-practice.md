In the exam, Section A is one 25-mark comparative essay (AO1, AO2, AO4) on a named poem and a second
poem of your choice from the prescribed list. Section A of this page practises comparison skills on two
original poems; Section B gives full essays, two on the original poems and two in the exam's format for
your anthology.

## The poems

> **Night Shift** *(original)*
>
> She leaves at nine. The door's soft click  
> is how I know the house is mine,  
> the kettle cooling, and the tick  
> of something counting down the line.
>
> I keep the hall light on for her.  
> I tell myself it's not for me.  
> At six the key, the coat, the blur  
> of cold air. Then she's home. Then tea.

> **Shift Pattern** *(original)*
>
> Four nights on, three off,  
> and the children grow in the gaps  
> like grass between paving.
>
> I learn their week from notes on the fridge:  
> a spelling test, a lost shoe, *Mum can you sign*.  
> I sign. I am a signature.
>
> On my nights off I stand at their doors  
> and listen to them breathing,  
> two clocks I have no hand in setting.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Identify the rhyme scheme of "Night Shift" and explain one effect of it. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how "Shift Pattern" uses form differently from "Night Shift", and one effect of that
difference. **[3]**

**3.** Compare the speakers of the two poems: who speaks, and from what perspective? **[2]**

**4.** In "Night Shift", explain the effect of "I tell myself it's not for me." **[2]**

**5.** Analyse the simile "like grass between paving" in "Shift Pattern". **[3]**

**6.** Explain the effect of the short sentences in "I sign. I am a signature." **[2]**

**7.** Compare the endings of the two poems. **[4]**

**8.** Write a comparative thesis statement (one or two sentences) answering: *Compare the ways in which
the poets present absence within families.* **[2]**

## Section B: essays

**9.** Compare the ways in which the poets of "Night Shift" and "Shift Pattern" present the relationship
between parents and children. **[25]**

**10.** Compare the ways in which the poets of "Night Shift" and "Shift Pattern" use form and structure
to present time. **[25]**

**11.** Compare the ways in which a parent is presented in *Effects* and **one** other poem of your
choice from the prescribed list. **[25]**

**12.** Compare the ways in which a sense of place or belonging is presented in *Look We Have Coming to
Dover* and **one** other poem of your choice from the prescribed list. **[25]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** ABAB in each quatrain (or alternating rhyme) (1). Effect, for example: the regular rhyme creates
order and routine, matching the child's attempt to keep control while alone (1).

**2.** "Shift Pattern" is free verse in unrhymed tercets with uneven line lengths (1), whereas "Night
Shift" uses rhymed, regular quatrains (1). Effect, for example: the looser form suits the adult's
fragmented, interrupted week, while the regular form suits the child's need for routine (1).

**3.** "Night Shift" is spoken by a child left at home overnight (1); "Shift Pattern" is spoken by the
working parent, so the poems present the same situation from opposite sides (1).

**4.** The line suggests the child denies their own fear (1); the reader sees through the denial, so the
line reveals vulnerability through what the speaker refuses to admit (1).

**5.** The simile suggests the children grow without being tended (1), in small, overlooked spaces of
the parent's routine (1); "paving" implies the hard structure of work, with growth happening despite
it, so the image combines guilt and quiet wonder (1).

**6.** The first sentence is a simple action; the second turns the speaker into the action itself (1),
suggesting she feels reduced to a name on a form, present only in writing (1).

**7.** Two marks for each poem's ending explained, compared: "Night Shift" ends with reunion and comfort
in short, end-stopped sentences, "Then she's home. Then tea." (1), suggesting relief and ordinary
safety (1); "Shift Pattern" ends with the parent watching sleeping children, and the metaphor of "two
clocks I have no hand in setting" (1) suggests helplessness and exclusion, so one poem ends in
connection and the other in distance (1).

**8.** One mark for naming both poems and the focus (1); one for an arguable comparative claim (1).
Example: *"Both poems present a parent's night work as an absence that shapes the family, but where
'Night Shift' shows a child filling that absence with routine, 'Shift Pattern' shows a parent who feels
the absence is her own."*

### Section B: how to mark questions 9 to 12

Marked holistically on AO1, AO2 and AO4. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not
Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 21–25 | Sophisticated comparative argument; perceptive analysis of form, structure and language in both poems; connections explored throughout |
| 16–20 | Clear, developed comparison; detailed analysis; connections in most paragraphs |
| 11–15 | Some sustained comparison; explanation of methods with apt references |
| 6–10 | Some links, often of content; uneven analysis |
| 1–5 | Little comparison; mainly paraphrase |

**9. Indicative content.**
- Perspective: child's voice versus parent's voice on the same separation.
- "Night Shift": the hall light as an act of love and of self-protection; the child's denial; rhyme and
  regular rhythm as routine; the reunion in the final line.
- "Shift Pattern": the parent's guilt in the grass simile; the notes on the fridge as indirect contact;
  "I am a signature" as reduction; watching the children breathe as tenderness without participation.
- Connection: both poems find love in small actions (a light left on, a signed note), but the child's
  poem resolves while the parent's does not.

**10. Indicative content.**
- "Night Shift": time marked by fixed points (nine, six); the "tick... counting down" suggests anxious
  waiting; the whole night passes in the line break between "not for me." and "At six", so the long
  wait is compressed into silence.
- "Shift Pattern": the opening rota ("Four nights on, three off") imposes work time on family life;
  growth happens "in the gaps"; the closing clocks image makes the children's time something the parent
  cannot control.
- Connection: in both, time is measured by the parent's work, but the child experiences it as waiting,
  the parent as loss.

**11. Approach (*Effects* and a second poem).** Strong second-poem choices include *Material* or *On Her
Blindness*, which also present a mother through a child's later perspective, or *Genetics*, which
presents parents through inheritance. Compare: the speaker's position (son, daughter, adult looking
back); how memory is structured; the role of objects or the body; the presence or absence of guilt; how
each poem ends. Keep both poems in every paragraph.

**12. Approach (*Look We Have Coming to Dover* and a second poem).** Strong second-poem choices
include *The Deliverer* (arrival in a new country through adoption) or *The Map Woman* (a self defined
by a home town). Compare: the voice and its relationship to Standard English; how arrival or departure
is presented; images of the landscape or body as territory; attitudes to belonging (hope, defiance,
escape); how the endings position the reader.
