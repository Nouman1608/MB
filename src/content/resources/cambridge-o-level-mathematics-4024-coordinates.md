This guide covers subtopic **3.1 Coordinates** in Topic 3, Coordinate geometry, of Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024, 2025 to 2027 series. Coordinates describe the position of a point on a flat plane with two numbers. Every later topic in coordinate geometry builds on them. The [Coordinate geometry revision notes](/resources/o-level-mathematics-coordinate-geometry-revision-notes/) summarise the whole topic. This page concentrates on reading, plotting and using coordinates with confidence, including finding the missing vertex of a shape.

Companion pages: the [Coordinates revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-coordinates-revision-notes/) and the [Coordinates practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-coordinates-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks

The syllabus asks you to use and interpret Cartesian coordinates in two dimensions. This is the shortest statement in the topic, but it underpins the rest: drawing graphs, finding gradients, lengths and midpoints, and writing equations of lines. It can be examined on both papers, often as the first step of a longer question about a shape or a line.

## The coordinate plane

The **Cartesian plane** is made from two number lines that cross at right angles.

- The horizontal line is the **x-axis**.
- The vertical line is the **y-axis**.
- They cross at the **origin**, O, which has coordinates (0, 0).

A point is written as an ordered pair **(x, y)**. The first number, x, tells you how far to move **across** from the origin: right if positive, left if negative. The second number, y, tells you how far to move **up** or **down**: up if positive, down if negative. The order matters, since (2, 5) and (5, 2) are different points.

A helpful memory is "along the corridor, then up the stairs": x first, then y.

### Plotting a point

To plot (-3, 2), start at the origin, move 3 units left, then 2 units up. To plot (4, -1), move 4 right, then 1 down. To plot (0, -5), do not move across, but go 5 down: the point is on the y-axis.

### Reading a point

To read a point from a graph, draw (or imagine) a vertical line to the x-axis and read the value, then a horizontal line to the y-axis and read that value. Check the scale on each axis carefully. If each square is worth 2 units, the coordinates are twice the number of squares counted.

## Quadrants

The axes divide the plane into four **quadrants**, numbered anticlockwise starting from the top right.

| Quadrant | Signs of (x, y) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1st (top right) | (+, +) | (3, 4) |
| 2nd (top left) | (-, +) | (-3, 2) |
| 3rd (bottom left) | (-, -) | (-2, -6) |
| 4th (bottom right) | (+, -) | (4, -1) |

A point on an axis is not in any quadrant. A point with y = 0 is on the x-axis, and a point with x = 0 is on the y-axis.

```
(0, -5)  lies on the y-axis, below the origin
(6, 0)   lies on the x-axis, to the right of the origin
```

## Horizontal and vertical lines

All the points on a horizontal line have the same y-value. The line through (-3, 4) and (5, 4) is the line **y = 4**. All the points on a vertical line have the same x-value. The line through (-2, 1) and (-2, 7) is the line **x = -2**. The axes themselves are the lines y = 0 (the x-axis) and x = 0 (the y-axis).

The point where two such lines cross has the x-value of the vertical line and the y-value of the horizontal line. The lines x = -2 and y = 3 cross at (-2, 3).

### Distance along a horizontal or vertical line

When two points share a y-value, the distance between them is the difference of their x-values. When they share an x-value, it is the difference of their y-values.

```
(-3, 4) to (5, 4):   5 - (-3) = 8 units
(-2, 1) to (-2, 7):  7 - 1 = 6 units
```

Subtracting a negative gives an addition, so 5 - (-3) = 8. A common mistake is to calculate 5 - 3 = 2.

## Finding missing vertices

Many questions give three corners of a shape and ask for the fourth. Use the properties of the shape: opposite sides of a rectangle or parallelogram are parallel and equal, and the sides of a rectangle meet at right angles.

**Worked example (rectangle).** A(-2, 1), B(4, 1) and C(4, -3) are three vertices of a rectangle ABCD. Find D and the area.

```
AB is horizontal at y = 1.  BC is vertical at x = 4.
D is below A, level with C:  D = (-2, -3)
width  AB = 4 - (-2) = 6
height BC = 1 - (-3) = 4
area = 6 x 4 = 24 square units
```

**Worked example (parallelogram).** A(1, 2), B(5, 2) and C(7, 5) are three vertices of a parallelogram ABCD. Find D.

```
To get from B to C: 2 right and 3 up.
D is obtained from A in the same way: (1 + 2, 2 + 3) = (3, 5).
```

Check: AD runs from (1, 2) to (3, 5), the same as BC, so AD and BC are equal and parallel. Also AB and DC are both horizontal with length 4: from (3, 5) to (7, 5) is 4.

**Worked example (square).** A square ABCD has A(0, 0) and B(5, 0), and C and D lie above the x-axis. Find C and D.

```
side = 5, so C = (5, 5) and D = (0, 5)
```

## Shapes on the grid

The coordinates of the vertices let you work out areas by using the horizontal and vertical distances.

**Worked example (triangle).** A triangle has vertices (0, 0), (6, 0) and (6, 8). Find the type of triangle and its area.

```
the base from (0, 0) to (6, 0) is horizontal with length 6
the side from (6, 0) to (6, 8) is vertical with length 8
these meet at (6, 0) at a right angle
area = 1/2 x 6 x 8 = 24 square units
```

**Worked example (kite).** A kite has vertices A(0, 4), B(3, 0), C(0, -2) and D(-3, 0). Find the lengths of the diagonals and the area.

```
AC is vertical: 4 - (-2) = 6
BD is horizontal: 3 - (-3) = 6
area of a kite = 1/2 x product of the diagonals = 1/2 x 6 x 6 = 18
```

Here the diagonals lie along the axes, so their lengths are found by subtraction without any formula for length.

**Worked example (isosceles triangle).** P(-4, 0), Q(4, 0) and R(0, 6) are the vertices of a triangle. Describe the triangle and find its area.

```
PQ lies on the x-axis with length 8.
R is on the y-axis above the midpoint of PQ, so RP = RQ.
The triangle is isosceles.
area = 1/2 x 8 x 6 = 24 square units
```

## Moving and reflecting points

### Translation

To move a point, add to or subtract from the coordinates. Moving 3 left and 4 down changes (2, 5) into (2 - 3, 5 - 4) = (-1, 1).

### Reflection in an axis

- Reflection in the **x-axis** changes the sign of the y-coordinate: (3, -2) becomes (3, 2).
- Reflection in the **y-axis** changes the sign of the x-coordinate: (3, -2) becomes (-3, -2).

## Points with unknown coordinates

A point with an unknown value is placed on an axis or line using the rule for that location.

**Worked example.** The point (2a - 6, a + 1) lies on the y-axis. Find a and the coordinates of the point.

```
On the y-axis, x = 0:   2a - 6 = 0,  so a = 3
y = a + 1 = 4
the point is (0, 4)
```

**Worked example.** The point (k, 2k - 1) lies on the y-axis. Find k.

```
x = 0, so k = 0, and the point is (0, -1)
```

## Choosing and using a scale

When you plot points for a graph, choose a scale that fits the paper and the data. Use equal steps along each axis, and label them. If the x-values go from -3 to 5 and the y-values from -2 to 11, you could use 1 cm per unit on the x-axis and 0.5 cm per unit on the y-axis. Mark the axes with x and y, number the grid lines, and plot with a sharp pencil. A consistent scale is required for gradients to look correct.

## Coordinates from a table of values

In the next subtopics you will complete tables such as the following and plot the points.

```
x:        -2   -1    0    1    2    3
y = 2x - 1: -5   -3   -1    1    3    5
```

The pairs (-2, -5), (-1, -3), (0, -1), (1, 1), (2, 3) and (3, 5) are the points. Notice that the points lie on a straight line.

## Exam technique

- Always write coordinates as (x, y) with brackets and a comma.
- Check which axis is which before plotting. Mixing them up is the most frequent error.
- Look at the scale on each axis separately, since the scales may differ.
- Use subtraction of coordinates for distances along horizontal or vertical lines.
- Sketch a quick diagram for shape problems, even if the question does not ask for one.
- Use properties of the shape to find a missing vertex.

## Common mistakes

- Swapping x and y.
- Counting squares without checking the scale.
- Mistakes with negative numbers when finding a distance.
- Placing a point on an axis in the wrong quadrant.
- Reflecting in the wrong axis, or changing both signs.
- Plotting (0, -5) on the x-axis.
