vulnerability and resilience). For the full explanation, read the

## Energy balance

- The Earth receives **incoming shortwave radiation** from the Sun and emits **outgoing longwave
  radiation**.
- When the two are in balance, global temperature is stable.
- Changes in the balance come from factors including **solar radiation variations** (including global
  dimming due to volcanic eruptions) and **terrestrial albedo changes**.

In more detail: averaged over the whole globe, roughly 340 watts per square metre of solar energy
arrives at the top of the atmosphere. About 30 per cent of it (roughly 100 W/m²) is reflected
straight back to space by clouds, aerosols and bright surfaces; this fraction is the planet's
**albedo**. The remaining energy, about 240 W/m², is absorbed by the surface and atmosphere and
must eventually be lost again as longwave radiation. Anything that changes the incoming, reflected
or outgoing part is called a **radiative forcing**.

## Greenhouse effect

Greenhouse gases absorb some of the longwave radiation the surface emits and re-emit it, warming
the lower atmosphere. This **natural greenhouse effect** keeps the planet warmer than it would
otherwise be. Human activity that raises greenhouse gas concentrations **enhances** the effect.

Without the natural effect, the average surface temperature would be well below freezing (about
minus 18°C) instead of about 15°C. The gases differ in source and strength:

| Gas | Main human sources | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | Burning coal, oil and gas; cement; deforestation | Largest contributor to the enhanced effect; long-lived |
| Methane | Livestock digestion, rice paddies, landfill, leaks from gas and coal extraction | Much stronger per molecule than carbon dioxide but shorter-lived (around a decade) |
| Nitrous oxide | Nitrogen fertilisers, some industrial processes | Long-lived and powerful |
| Halocarbons (such as CFCs) | Refrigeration and aerosols, now largely phased out | Synthetic; also damage ozone |
| Water vapour | Not directly controlled by people | Acts mainly as a feedback, rising as air warms |

Carbon dioxide concentration was about 280 parts per million before industrialisation and has
risen to above 400 ppm in recent years, as shown by continuous measurement at Mauna Loa,
Hawaii, since 1958 (the Keeling Curve).

## Natural and human factors

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Volcanic aerosols | Reflect incoming sunlight (global dimming), causing temporary cooling |
| Variations in solar radiation | Change the energy received |
| Albedo change | Lighter surfaces reflect more; darker surfaces absorb more |
| Higher greenhouse gas concentrations | More outgoing longwave radiation absorbed and re-emitted |

- **Volcanoes:** large explosive eruptions inject sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere, where it
  forms sulphate aerosols that reflect sunlight for one to two years. Mount Pinatubo (Philippines,
  1991) lowered global average temperature by roughly half a degree Celsius for about a year;
  Tambora (Indonesia, 1815) was followed by the "year without a summer" of 1816 in Europe and
  North America. The effect is short-lived because aerosols fall out of the atmosphere.
- **Solar output:** varies slightly over the roughly 11-year sunspot cycle. The Maunder Minimum
  (about 1645 to 1715), a period of very few sunspots, coincided with part of the Little Ice Age.
  Measured solar output has not risen since the mid-20th century, so it cannot explain recent
  warming.
- **Albedo:** fresh snow and ice reflect most sunlight; open ocean and dark forest absorb most of
  it. Deforestation, desertification, urbanisation and melting ice all change albedo.

## Feedback loops

- **Positive feedback** amplifies change. Example: warming melts ice, which lowers albedo, so the
  surface absorbs more energy, so warming increases further.
- A **negative feedback** works against change and moves a system back toward balance.

Further positive feedbacks to know:

- **Permafrost thaw:** warming thaws frozen Arctic soils; decomposing organic matter releases
  carbon dioxide and methane, which enhance warming.
- **Water vapour:** warmer air holds more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas.
- **Forest dieback:** heat and drought stress forests, which then store less carbon and may burn.

A negative feedback example: higher carbon dioxide can boost plant growth, so vegetation absorbs
some extra carbon. Feedbacks help explain **polar amplification**: the Arctic has warmed several
times faster than the global average because ice loss feeds back on itself.

## International variations in emissions

Emissions are linked to **economic development, globalisation and trade**:

- **Total emissions:** China has been the largest annual emitter since the mid-2000s; the United
  States has the largest **cumulative** (historical) emissions.
- **Per-capita emissions:** highest in some oil-producing Gulf states, Australia, the USA and
  Canada; India has large total emissions but low emissions per person; many low-income countries
  emit very little per person.
- **Trade:** emissions are usually counted where goods are made (**territorial** accounting).
  **Consumption-based** accounting assigns emissions to the country that consumes the goods, so
  some high-income importers' footprints rise and some manufacturing exporters' fall.
- **Development pathway:** industrialising economies often rely on coal, while some high-income
  countries have cut emissions partly by moving manufacturing abroad.

## Worked example: albedo and forcing (illustrative)

Incoming energy 340 W/m².

- Albedo 0.30: absorbed = 340 x (1 - 0.30) = 340 x 0.70 = 238 W/m².
- Albedo 0.29: absorbed = 340 x 0.71 = 241.4 W/m².
- A one-point fall in albedo adds about 3.4 W/m², similar in size to the forcing usually quoted for
  a doubling of carbon dioxide (about 3.7 W/m²). Small albedo changes therefore matter.

## Exam traps

- Confusing shortwave (incoming) with longwave (outgoing) radiation.
- Describing the greenhouse effect as harmful in itself; it is the **enhanced** effect that is the
  concern.
- Treating "positive" feedback as meaning beneficial.
- Mixing up causes (this subtopic) with responses (adaptation and mitigation).
- Linking the enhanced greenhouse effect to the ozone hole; they are different problems.
- Comparing countries' emissions without saying whether you mean total, per-capita or cumulative.

## Self-test

1. What happens to incoming shortwave radiation at equilibrium?
2. How do greenhouse gases warm the lower atmosphere?
3. Why do volcanic eruptions cause temporary cooling?
4. Explain the ice-albedo feedback loop.
5. Why is it called positive feedback?
6. Roughly what fraction of incoming solar radiation does the Earth reflect?
7. Give two human sources of methane.
8. Why is the cooling from Pinatubo described as temporary?
9. Explain the permafrost feedback.
10. Distinguish territorial from consumption-based emissions accounting.
11. Why can solar variation not explain recent warming?

**Answers:** 1. It is balanced by outgoing longwave radiation. 2. They absorb and re-emit some
outgoing longwave radiation. 3. Aerosols reflect incoming sunlight. 4. Melting ice lowers albedo, more
energy is absorbed, warming increases and melts more ice. 5. Because it amplifies the original change.
6. About 30 per cent. 7. For example livestock, rice paddies, landfill or gas leaks. 8. The sulphate
aerosols fall out of the stratosphere within one to two years. 9. Warming thaws permafrost, releasing
carbon dioxide and methane that cause further warming. 10. Territorial counts emissions where goods
are produced; consumption-based assigns them to where goods are consumed. 11. Measured solar output
