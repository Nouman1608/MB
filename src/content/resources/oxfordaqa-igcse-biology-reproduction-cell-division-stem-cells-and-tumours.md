## 3.5.1 Reproduction: sexual and asexual

**Sexual reproduction** is the joining (fusion) of a male gamete and a female gamete. The offspring receive a mixture of genetic information from two parents, so they vary. Half of their genes come from each parent.

**Asexual reproduction** involves no fusion of gametes, and one parent is enough. There is no mixing of genetic information, so there is no genetic variation in the offspring. Genetically identical individuals are called **clones**.

| Feature | Sexual | Asexual |
|---|---|---|
| Parents needed | Two | One |
| Gametes fuse | Yes | No |
| Genetic information | Mixed from both parents | All from the one parent |
| Offspring | Genetically different from each other | Clones of the parent |

Two clones can still look different if they grow in different conditions. A plant cutting given less light than its parent may grow smaller. That difference is environmental, not genetic.

## 3.5.2 a-c: Chromosomes, genes and alleles

The **nucleus** of a cell contains **chromosomes**. Chromosomes carry **genes**, and genes control the characteristics of the body. Each chromosome carries a large number of genes, so a chromosome is not the same thing as a gene.

Many genes come in different forms called **alleles**, which may produce different characteristics. For example, a mouse has a gene for fur colour. One allele may give black fur and another may give brown fur. Both alleles sit at the same place on the chromosome pair.

A normal body cell holds its chromosomes as **pairs**. One chromosome of each pair came from each parent.

| Species | Chromosomes in a body cell | Pairs | Chromosomes in a gamete |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human | 46 | 23 | 23 |
| Horse | 64 | 32 | 32 |
| Garden pea | 14 | 7 | 7 |
| Maize | 20 | 10 | 10 |

**Worked example 1.** A horse has 64 chromosomes in each body cell. State the number of pairs, the number in a sperm cell, and the number in the fertilised egg.

- Pairs = 64 ÷ 2 = **32 pairs**.
- A gamete has a single set, so a sperm has 64 ÷ 2 = **32 chromosomes**.
- Fertilisation joins a sperm (32) to an egg (32): 32 + 32 = **64 chromosomes**, the full number again.

## 3.5.2 d-e: Mitosis

Body cells divide by **mitosis** to produce additional cells during growth, or replacement cells when tissue is damaged or worn out.

When a body cell divides by mitosis:

1. Copies of the genetic material are made.
2. The cell then divides **once** to form **two genetically identical body cells**.

Each new cell ends up with the full chromosome number of the cell that divided. The specification asks for this outcome and its purpose, with no stage names.

**Worked example 2.** A fertilised horse egg divides by mitosis. Assume every cell divides at the same time each round.

(a) How many cells after 6 rounds of division? Each round doubles the number: 2⁶ = **64 cells**.

(b) How many chromosomes are there in total across those cells? Each cell has 64, so 64 × 64 = **4096 chromosomes**.

(c) How many rounds are needed for the cell number to exceed 1000? 2⁹ = 512 is too few and 2¹⁰ = 1024 is enough, so **10 rounds**.

This works because every cell receives a complete, identical copy of the genetic material.

## 3.5.2 f-h: Meiosis and gametes

Cells in reproductive organs divide to form **gametes**. In animals these organs are the testes and ovaries. A cell divides to form gametes by **meiosis**.

When a cell divides to form gametes:

1. Copies of the genetic information are made.
2. The cell then goes through **two** divisions, giving **four gametes**. Each gamete carries a **single set of chromosomes**.

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Body cells | Reproductive organs |
| Purpose | Growth, replacement | Make gametes |
| Divisions | One | Two |
| Cells made | Two | Four |
| Chromosomes per new cell | Full set, in pairs | Single set |
| Daughter cells | Genetically identical | Gametes that are not all the same |

The last row goes slightly beyond the wording of the specification, but it follows from sexual reproduction giving variety. Gametes from the same parent are not copies of each other.

**Worked example 3.** A mouse has 40 chromosomes in a body cell. Twenty-five cells in a testis start meiosis.

- Each cell makes 4 gametes, so 25 × 4 = **100 sperm**.
- Each sperm has 40 ÷ 2 = **20 chromosomes**.
- Had those cells divided by mitosis, 25 cells would make 50 cells, each with 40 chromosomes.

## 3.5.2 i: Fertilisation and development

Gametes join at **fertilisation** to form a single body cell with new pairs of chromosomes. One chromosome in each pair comes from the sperm and one from the egg, which is how each parent gives half of the genes.

This cell then divides repeatedly by mitosis to form many cells. As the organism develops, these cells **differentiate** to form different kinds of cells. Differentiation means a cell becomes specialised for a job, for example:

- a red blood cell, which carries oxygen
- a nerve cell, which carries electrical impulses
- a root hair cell, which absorbs water and mineral ions

All these cells have the same genes. They differ because different genes are active in each type.

## 3.5.2 j: Animals and plants compared

In animals, most cell types become specialised early in development, and then mostly stay that way. Once an animal is mature, dividing is largely limited to repair and replacement, for example of skin or of the gut lining.

Plants differ: many of their cells can still differentiate at any age. This is why a piece of stem or shoot cut from a plant can grow new roots and become a whole new plant, and why the new plant is a clone of the parent.

## 3.5.2 k-m: Stem cells and therapeutic cloning

**Stem cells** from human embryos and from adult bone marrow can be made to differentiate into many different types of human cell. Knowledge of the techniques used is not required.

**Therapeutic cloning** makes an embryo that carries the patient's own genes. The patient's body does not reject its stem cells, because the genes match, so they can be used in treatment.

Stem cell treatment might help with **paralysis**, for example, where nerve cells have been damaged and the body cannot replace them.

**Worked example 4.** A laboratory starts a stem cell culture with 400 cells. Each cell divides once every 18 hours and none die.

(a) After 72 hours: 72 ÷ 18 = 4 divisions, so 400 × 2⁴ = 400 × 16 = **6400 cells**.

(b) When does the culture first exceed 50 000 cells? We need 400 × 2ⁿ > 50 000, so 2ⁿ > 125. 2⁶ = 64 is too small and 2⁷ = 128 is enough, so n = 7. Time = 7 × 18 = **126 hours**, giving 400 × 128 = 51 200 cells.

### Making ethical judgements

The specification expects you to make informed judgements, when given appropriate information, about social and ethical issues in using stem cells from adult bone marrow and from embryos. Judge from the information you are given, and give at least one point on each side.

| Point | Embryo stem cells | Adult bone marrow stem cells |
|---|---|---|
| Possible benefit | May treat conditions such as paralysis | May treat conditions such as paralysis |
| Concern | An embryo is used and not allowed to develop | A donor must give the cells, and this involves a procedure |
| Who may object | People who regard an embryo as a human life | Fewer people, but some may worry about the donor |

Do not just say "it is wrong". Say who is affected and why, then reach a conclusion that follows from your points.

## 3.5.2 n-o: Tumours and their causes

A **tumour** results from the abnormal, uncontrolled growth of cells. In other words, cells divide by mitosis without the normal control.

- **Benign tumours** do not invade other tissues.
- **Malignant tumours** have cells that invade healthy tissue. Some malignant cells may enter the bloodstream and circulate to other parts of the body, forming **secondary tumours**.

Tumours can be caused by:

- **chemical carcinogens**, for example those found in tobacco smoke and in asbestos
- **ionising radiation**, for example UV and X-rays

A carcinogen is something that can cause cancer. Reducing exposure, for example by not smoking and protecting skin from UV, reduces risk.

**Worked example 5.** A scan shows a tumour of diameter 8 mm. Nine months later it is 17 mm.

- Increase = 17 − 8 = 9 mm.
- Percentage increase = 9 ÷ 8 × 100 = **112.5%**.
- Mean rate = 9 ÷ 9 = **1.0 mm per month**.

Growth alone does not show a tumour is malignant. Secondary tumours in other organs would be the key evidence.

**Worked example 6.** Illustrative values: lung cancer cases per 100 000 people per year are 12 in non-smokers and 168 in heavy smokers. The ratio is 168 ÷ 12 = **14**. Heavy smokers have 14 times the rate. This shows a strong link, and tobacco smoke contains chemical carcinogens that offer a reason why.

## Common errors

- Writing that a gene and a chromosome are the same, or that a chromosome carries one gene.
- Saying meiosis makes two cells, or that mitosis makes four.
- Saying a gamete has "half a chromosome" instead of half the number of chromosomes, one from each pair.
- Describing a benign tumour as harmless in every case. The specification only says it does not invade other tissues.
- Writing "the stem cells have the patient's DNA so they are the same as the patient's cells" without saying why this stops rejection.
- Giving only one side of an ethical question.
