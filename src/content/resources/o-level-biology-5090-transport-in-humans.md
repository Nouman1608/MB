## 11.1 The circulatory system

The **circulatory system** is a system of **blood vessels** with a **pump** (the heart) and **valves** that make sure blood flows **one way only**.

Humans have a **double circulation**: blood passes through the heart **twice** for each complete circuit of the body.

- **Pulmonary circulation**: right side of the heart → lungs → left side of the heart.
- **Systemic circulation**: left side of the heart → body tissues → right side of the heart.

**Why two circuits?** The double circulation gives a **low pressure** circulation to the **lungs** and a **high pressure** circulation to the **body tissues**. Low pressure suits the delicate lung capillaries. High pressure pushes blood quickly round the long circuit to all the body organs, so oxygen and glucose reach respiring cells fast. Because blood returns to the heart after the lungs, it can be pumped out again at high pressure.

**Worked example 1.** Trace a red blood cell from the vena cava back to the vena cava.

`vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs (collects oxygen) → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body organ (gives up oxygen) → vena cava`

It passes through the heart twice.

## 11.2 The heart

### Structures to identify (11.2.1)

| Structure | What it is and does |
|---|---|
| Muscular wall | Cardiac muscle that contracts to pump blood |
| Septum | Wall of muscle dividing the left and right sides; keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart |
| Right and left atria | Upper, thin-walled chambers that receive blood from veins |
| Right and left ventricles | Lower, thick-walled chambers that pump blood into arteries |
| Atrioventricular valves | Between each atrium and its ventricle; stop backflow into the atria |
| Semilunar valves | At the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery; stop backflow into the ventricles |
| Coronary arteries | Vessels on the outer surface of the heart, branching from the aorta, that supply the heart muscle itself with oxygen and glucose |

On a front-view diagram, the **left side of the heart is on the right of the page**.

### Wall thickness (11.2.2)

- **Left ventricle wall thicker than right ventricle wall.** The left ventricle pumps blood all round the body at high pressure; the right ventricle pumps blood only to the nearby lungs at low pressure. More muscle gives a stronger contraction.
- **Atria walls thinner than ventricle walls.** Atria only push blood a short distance, down into the ventricles. Ventricles must pump blood out of the heart into the arteries.

### One heartbeat (11.2.3)

1. **Atria contract.** Blood is pushed through the open **atrioventricular valves** into the ventricles.
2. **Ventricles contract.** Pressure in the ventricles rises above that in the atria, so the **atrioventricular valves close**, preventing backflow into the atria.
3. Pressure rises above that in the arteries, so the **semilunar valves open** and blood is pushed into the **aorta** and **pulmonary artery**.
4. **Ventricles relax.** Pressure in the ventricles falls below that in the arteries, so the **semilunar valves close**, preventing backflow into the ventricles.
5. Blood from the veins fills the atria again, and the cycle repeats.

Valves open and close because of **pressure differences**; they are not muscles. Both atria contract together, then both ventricles.

### Arteries and veins (11.2.4)

Blood is pumped **away** from the heart in **arteries** and **returns** to the heart in **veins**. This is about direction, not oxygen: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.

### Monitoring the heart (11.2.5)

The activity of the heart can be monitored by:

- an **electrocardiogram (ECG)**, a trace of the electrical activity of the heart; each repeating pattern is one heartbeat
- **pulse rate**, counted at the wrist or neck, which gives beats per minute
- **listening to the sounds of the valves closing**, using a stethoscope; the two sounds of each beat are the atrioventricular valves closing, then the semilunar valves closing.

**Worked example 2 (ECG).** An ECG is printed at 25 mm per second. The peaks of two neighbouring beats are 20 mm apart. Calculate the heart rate.

- Time for one beat = 20 ÷ 25 = **0.8 s**.
- Heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.8 = **75 beats per minute**.

### Physical activity and heart rate (11.2.6)

**Method.** Rest, then count the pulse for 15 s; repeat and take a mean. Do a fixed exercise (for example, step-ups) for a fixed time. Count the pulse immediately, then every minute until it returns to resting. Keep the person, exercise and counting time the same, and repeat.

**Worked example 3.** A student counts **19 beats in 15 s** at rest and **33 beats in 15 s** straight after exercise.

- Resting rate = 19 × (60 ÷ 15) = **76 beats per minute**.
- After exercise = 33 × 4 = **132 beats per minute**.
- Increase = 132 − 76 = 56; percentage increase = 56 ÷ 76 × 100 = **73.7%**.

**Explanation.** Contracting muscles respire faster to release more energy. They need more **oxygen** and **glucose**, and produce more **carbon dioxide**. The heart beats faster (and more strongly) so blood flows faster to the muscles, delivering oxygen and glucose and removing carbon dioxide. The rate stays high after exercise, partly to carry lactic acid to the liver (Topic 10).

### Coronary heart disease (11.2.7)

In **coronary heart disease (CHD)**, a **coronary artery** becomes **blocked**, for example by fatty deposits in its wall or a blood clot. Less blood reaches part of the heart muscle, so it receives too little **oxygen** and **glucose**. That muscle cannot respire aerobically or contract properly and may die: a heart attack.

**Possible risk factors** (the syllabus list):

| Risk factor | Notes |
|---|---|
| Diet | High in saturated (animal) fat and cholesterol, which add to fatty deposits; high in salt |
| Sedentary lifestyle | Little exercise |
| Stress | Long-term stress |
| Smoking | Chemicals in tobacco smoke damage artery walls and raise the risk of clots |
| Genetic predisposition | CHD in close relatives |
| Age | Risk rises with age |
| Gender | On average, men are affected at younger ages than women |

### Diet and exercise in reducing risk (11.2.8)

- **Diet**: eating less saturated fat and cholesterol reduces fatty deposits in the coronary arteries; less salt helps keep blood pressure down; a balanced energy intake avoids obesity, which adds to the risk.
- **Exercise**: regular exercise strengthens the heart muscle, helps control body mass and blood pressure, and helps keep blood cholesterol lower.
- **Balance**: diet and exercise reduce risk but cannot remove it; genetic predisposition, age and gender cannot be changed.

## 11.3 Blood vessels

### The main vessels (11.3.1)

| Vessel | Carries blood |
|---|---|
| Aorta | From the left ventricle to the body |
| Vena cava | From the body to the right atrium |
| Pulmonary artery | From the right ventricle to the lungs |
| Pulmonary vein | From the lungs to the left atrium |
| Hepatic artery | From the aorta to the liver |
| Hepatic portal vein | From the small intestine (gut) to the liver, carrying absorbed food |
| Hepatic vein | From the liver to the vena cava |
| Renal artery | From the aorta to a kidney |
| Renal vein | From a kidney to the vena cava |

"Hepatic" means liver; "renal" means kidney.

### Structure (11.3.2)

| Feature | Artery | Vein | Capillary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thickness of wall | Thick | Thin | One cell thick |
| Muscle and elastic tissue | Lots | Little | None |
| Diameter of lumen | Narrow | Wide | Very narrow (red cells pass in single file) |
| Valves | None (except at the heart) | Present | None |

In a photomicrograph, an artery has a thick wall and small, round lumen; a vein has a thin wall and a large, often irregular lumen.

### Structure and pressure (11.3.3)

- **Arteries** carry blood at **high pressure** in surges. The **thick wall** withstands the pressure; **elastic tissue** stretches with each surge and recoils, keeping blood moving; **muscle** helps keep the wall strong.
- **Veins** carry blood at **low pressure**. A thick wall is not needed. The **wide lumen** reduces resistance to flow, and **valves** prevent backflow.
- **Capillaries** carry blood at lower pressure still. The **wall one cell thick** gives a short diffusion distance for exchange with tissues. The narrow lumen slows blood, allowing more time for exchange.

## 11.4 Blood

### Components and functions (11.4.2 and 11.4.3)

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| Red blood cells | Oxygen transport |
| White blood cells: lymphocytes | Antibody production |
| White blood cells: phagocytes | Engulfing pathogens |
| Platelets | Clotting: soluble fibrinogen is converted to insoluble fibrin, forming a mesh that prevents blood loss and the entry of pathogens |
| Plasma | Transport of blood cells, ions, glucose, amino acids, hormones, carbon dioxide, urea, vitamins and plasma proteins |

### Identifying cells (11.4.1)

| Cell | Appearance under the light microscope |
|---|---|
| Red blood cell | Small, round, pale centre, **no nucleus**; by far the most numerous |
| Lymphocyte | Larger than a red cell; **large, round nucleus** filling most of the cell, with a thin rim of cytoplasm |
| Phagocyte | Larger still; **lobed** or kidney-shaped nucleus with plenty of cytoplasm |

Platelets appear as tiny fragments. See also [Cell structure and organisation](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation/).

**Worked example 4.** On a photomicrograph at ×500, a red blood cell measures 3.5 mm across. Calculate its actual diameter in µm.

- Convert: 3.5 mm = 3500 µm.
- Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 3500 ÷ 500 = **7 µm**.

### Exchange through tissue fluid (11.4.4)

1. Blood pressure at the arterial end of a capillary forces some **plasma** out through the capillary wall. This fluid, without the red blood cells and most plasma proteins, is **tissue fluid**, and it bathes every body cell.
2. **Oxygen, glucose and amino acids** diffuse from the blood into the tissue fluid, then from the tissue fluid into the cells.
3. **Carbon dioxide and urea** made by the cells diffuse the other way: cell → tissue fluid → blood.
4. Much of the tissue fluid passes back into the capillaries.

Tissue fluid is the go-between for blood and cells.

## Common errors

- Writing "the heart pumps blood twice" for double circulation. Blood passes through the heart twice per complete circuit.
- Saying arteries carry oxygenated blood. They carry blood away from the heart.
- Explaining the thick left ventricle by "it pumps more blood". Both ventricles pump the same volume; the left pumps at higher pressure over a longer circuit.
- Mixing up the hepatic portal vein (gut to liver) and the hepatic vein (liver to vena cava).
- Saying platelets "make fibrin" with no mention of fibrinogen, or calling fibrin soluble.
- Giving a 15 s pulse count instead of beats per minute.
