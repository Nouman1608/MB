These notes cover **section 3.2.3.3 Supply-side policies** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: the named policies ("investment in education and training, lower direct taxes, lower taxes on business profits, trade union reform and privatisation/de-regulation"), "the advantages and disadvantages of supply-side policies" and "how supply-side policies can be used to help achieve government objectives". They give each policy its own chain and its own drawback, building on the shorter [supply-side and externality policies revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-supply-side-and-externality-policies-revision-notes/). Full explanations are in the [supply-side policies study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-supply-side-policies/); test yourself with the [supply-side policies practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-supply-side-policies-practice/). Figures are invented.

## Condensed recall

- Supply-side policies raise the economy's **productive capacity**: the production possibility curve shifts outwards.
- They work on the quantity and quality of labour, the amount of capital, and the efficiency of markets.
- Demand-side (fiscal and monetary) policies change how much is **spent**; supply-side policies change how much **can be produced**.
- Because capacity grows, output can rise without excess demand pushing up prices.
- Their main weaknesses: **time lags**, **cost**, **uncertain responses** and **effects on inequality**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Productive capacity | The maximum output an economy can produce with its resources |
| Productivity | Output per worker (or per hour worked) |
| Direct tax | A tax on income or profit, such as income tax or corporation tax |
| Incentive | Something that encourages a particular choice, such as keeping more of extra earnings |
| Trade union | An organisation of workers that bargains with employers over pay and conditions |
| Privatisation | Transferring a firm from state ownership to private ownership |
| Deregulation | Removing rules that restrict competition or how firms operate |
| Barrier to entry | Anything that makes it hard for new firms to enter a market |

## Policy-by-policy summary

| Policy | What it improves | Chain to capacity | Specific drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Education and training | Quality of labour | Skills → productivity → output; workers move into growing industries | Takes years; costly; must match employers' needs |
| Lower direct taxes | Quantity of labour | Keep more of extra pay → incentive to work more or join the labour force | Response uncertain; lost revenue; biggest gains to high earners |
| Lower taxes on business profits | Capital | More retained profit → investment; attracts foreign firms | Profit may go to shareholders; lost revenue; other countries may match |
| Trade union reform | Flexibility of the labour market | Fewer strikes, flexible pay → lower costs, more investment | Weaker bargaining power; lower pay; inequality |
| Privatisation | Efficiency | Profit motive → cut waste, innovate | Private monopoly risk; job losses |
| Deregulation | Competition | Lower barriers → new entrants → lower prices, better quality | Weaker protection for consumers, workers or the environment |

## Links to objectives

- **Growth:** every policy aims to raise capacity, so real GDP can grow in the long run.
- **Employment:** training cuts structural unemployment; lower income tax encourages people to work.
- **Price stability:** lower costs ease cost-push inflation; capacity keeps up with demand.
- **Balance of payments:** more productive, lower-cost firms export more and compete with imports.

## Four evaluation questions

1. **How long** will it take to work?
2. **How much** will it cost the government, in spending or lost revenue?
3. **How certain** is the response of workers and firms?
4. **Who gains and who loses?**

## Worked calculations

**1. Productivity.** 120 workers make 4,800 units a week: **40 units** each. After new machinery, they make 5,280: **44 units** each, a rise of 4 ÷ 40 × 100 = **10%**.

**2. Take-home pay from extra hours.** 8 extra hours at £12.50 = £100. If 32% is deducted, the worker keeps **£68**; if 22% is deducted, they keep **£78**.

**3. Profit kept after corporation tax.** Profit £600,000. At 25%, tax is £150,000; at 20%, tax is £120,000. The firm keeps **£30,000** more.

**4. Lost revenue.** If firms' total profits are £500 million, each percentage point of corporation tax raises about £5 million, so a cut of 5 points costs the government about **£25 million** a year, unless profits grow as a result.

## Exam traps

- Interest-rate cuts and higher government spending to boost demand are **not** supply-side policies.
- "Lower taxes" can be either fiscal (to raise spending) or supply-side (to improve incentives). Say which effect you mean.
- Supply-side policies are long-run policies; they are not a fast cure for a recession.
- Privatisation changes **ownership**; deregulation changes **rules**.
- Give the drawback that belongs to the policy, not "it costs money" every time.

## Self-test

1. What is meant by productive capacity?
2. Give one example of a rule that deregulation might remove, and its effect.
3. Which factor of production does training improve, and how?
4. Explain how lower income tax could increase the size of the labour force.
5. Give one reason why a cut in income tax might not increase hours worked.
6. Explain how a cut in corporation tax could raise investment.
7. Give one disadvantage of trade union reform for workers.
8. Distinguish between privatisation and deregulation.
9. Why might a privatised firm need a regulator?
10. 50 workers produce 1,500 units; after training they produce 1,650. Find the percentage rise in productivity.
11. Explain how supply-side policies can reduce inflation.
12. Why are supply-side policies not a good response to a sudden recession?

### Answers

1. The maximum output an economy can produce with its existing resources.
2. For example, a licence that only one firm may hold to run a service; removing it lets new firms enter, increasing competition.
3. Labour: workers gain skills, so output per worker rises.
4. People keep more of what they earn, so working becomes more attractive than staying out of work, and more people look for jobs.
5. Some workers may reach the income they want with fewer hours, or may not respond to small changes in pay.
6. Firms keep more profit after tax, so they have more funds to invest, and the country becomes more attractive to foreign firms.
7. They lose bargaining power, so pay and conditions may worsen.
8. Privatisation sells a state-owned firm to private owners; deregulation removes rules that restrict competition.
9. If it has no competitors it may use its monopoly power to raise prices or cut quality.
10. 30 to 33 units per worker: 3 ÷ 30 × 100 = 10%.
11. Higher productivity lowers costs per unit, easing cost-push inflation, and capacity grows to meet demand, so less demand-pull pressure.
12. They take years to work, while a recession needs a fast rise in demand.
