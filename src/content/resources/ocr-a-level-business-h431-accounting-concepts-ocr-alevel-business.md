## True and fair

Accounts are prepared for users who cannot see inside the business: owners, lenders, investors, tax authorities, employees and suppliers. To rely on them, users need accounts that show a **true and fair view**. "True" means the figures are free from material error and describe things that really happened. "Fair" means the accounts are presented without bias and in a way that does not mislead, so that a reader gets an honest picture of profit, assets and debts.

Accounts that fail this test can lead lenders to lend to a business that is failing, investors to pay too much for shares, or owners to pay out dividends that the business cannot afford.

## Generally Accepted Accounting Practice (GAAP)

**GAAP** is the set of rules, standards and conventions that businesses follow when preparing accounts. The specification does not require knowledge of specific rules and regulations; you need to explain why compliance matters:

- It makes accounts **comparable** between businesses and between years, because everyone follows the same approach.
- It gives users **confidence** that the figures have been prepared properly.
- It is usually a **legal requirement** for companies, and auditors check compliance.
- It **reduces the scope for manipulation**, because managers cannot pick any method they like.

The cost is time and expense in following the rules, and the rules may not suit every business perfectly.

## The seven conventions

| Convention | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Consistency | Use the same accounting methods from year to year; if a change is needed, say so and show the effect |
| Going concern | Assume the business will continue to trade for the foreseeable future |
| Accruals (matching) | Record revenue and costs in the period they relate to, not when cash moves |
| Materiality | Only items large enough to affect users' decisions need separate treatment |
| Objectivity | Base figures on verifiable evidence, such as invoices, not opinion |
| Prudence (conservatism) | Do not overstate profit or assets; recognise likely losses early but gains only when certain |
| Realisation | Record revenue when it is earned, usually at the point of sale or delivery, not when an order is received |

## Worked example 1: accruals (matching)

Penrose Printing has these figures for the year ended 31 December.

- Sales earned: £180,000, of which £8,000 is from customers who have not yet paid.
- Wages £90,000, materials £30,000 and rent £12,000, all paid in the year.
- Electricity: three quarterly bills of £2,400 each were paid (£7,200). The bill for October to December, also £2,400, arrived in January.
- Insurance: £3,600 was paid on 1 October for twelve months.

**Cash basis (money in and out).** Cash received = £180,000 − £8,000 = £172,000. Cash paid = £90,000 + £30,000 + £12,000 + £7,200 + £3,600 = £142,800. Cash profit = **£29,200**.

**Accruals basis (matching).**

- Revenue earned: £180,000 (sales belong to this year whether or not they are paid).
- Electricity: £7,200 paid + £2,400 accrued = £9,600.
- Insurance: only 3 months (October to December) relate to this year: £3,600 × 3 ÷ 12 = £900. The remaining £2,700 is a prepayment, carried forward.
- Costs = £90,000 + £30,000 + £12,000 + £9,600 + £900 = £142,500.
- Profit = £180,000 − £142,500 = **£37,500**.

The accruals profit is £8,300 higher than the cash figure, mostly because £8,000 of sales have not yet been paid. This shows why profit and cash differ, and why accruals gives a fairer picture of the year's trading.

## Worked example 2: prudence and objectivity

Penrose holds 500 reams of specialist paper that cost £12 each (£6,000). A water leak damages the stock. It can now be sold for £10 a ream, with £1 a ream of selling cost, so the net realisable value (NRV) is £9.

- Stock is normally valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value**: 500 × £9 = **£4,500**.
- The write-down is £6,000 − £4,500 = **£1,500**, deducted from profit now.

Prudence requires the loss to be recognised as soon as it is likely, even though the paper has not yet been sold. If a profitable sale were only hoped for, it would not be recorded until it happened. Objectivity requires the £9 to be supported by evidence, such as an offer from a buyer, not the manager's guess.

Prudence also applies to receivables. Penrose is owed £80,000 in total, but one customer who owes £6,000 is in liquidation. A prudent business provides for the likely loss, valuing receivables at £74,000 and reducing profit by £6,000.

Together, these two adjustments reduce profit by £7,500. If profit before them was £120,000, it becomes £112,500, a fall of 6.25%.

## Worked example 3: consistency

Penrose buys a machine for £48,000 with a four-year life and no residual value. Two methods are available.

- Straight line: £48,000 ÷ 4 = **£12,000** a year.
- Reducing balance at 40%: year 1 = 40% × £48,000 = **£19,200**.

If in year 1 Penrose uses reducing balance, then in year 2 it should not switch back to straight line just because profit looks low. Year 2 reducing balance would be 40% × (£48,000 − £19,200) = 40% × £28,800 = **£11,520**. Changing method in year 1 would alter profit by £19,200 − £12,000 = **£7,200**, so the choice could be used to flatter results. Consistency forces the business to stick to its method so that this year's profit is comparable with last year's. If a change is justified, the business must disclose it and the effect on profit.

## Worked example 4: going concern, materiality and realisation

**Going concern.** Penrose holds stock that cost £60,000. If the business is expected to continue, the stock is valued at cost (assuming it will be sold at a profit). If the business were about to close, the stock would be valued at break-up value, perhaps £30,000, so the loss of £30,000 would be recognised. The assumption affects the figures, so a lender or investor needs to know if there is doubt about going concern.

**Materiality.** Penrose's revenue is £2,000,000. Suppose the business treats items below 0.5% of revenue (£10,000) as immaterial. A £40 stapler is charged immediately as an expense, not recorded as a non-current asset for years. A £14,000 press is above the limit, so it is recorded as an asset and depreciated. The test is whether leaving out or misstating the item would change a user's decision.

**Realisation.** In December Penrose receives an order for £50,000 of printing, delivered in January. Revenue is not recorded in December because the sale is not yet complete; it is recorded in January when the goods are delivered. Recording it early would overstate this year's profit.

## Evaluating usefulness

| Convention | Useful because | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Consistency | Allows year-to-year comparison | May block a better method |
| Going concern | Supports sensible asset values | Judgement about the future |
| Accruals | Shows true profit for a period | Profit can differ greatly from cash |
| Materiality | Avoids clutter | Judgement about what is "material" |
| Objectivity | Reliable evidence | May ignore relevant but uncertain value, such as brand |
| Prudence | Protects users from over-optimism | Can understate profit and assets |
| Realisation | Stops early revenue | Late recording may hide a good year |

## Impact and importance

For the **business**, the conventions guide managers on how to record transactions, affect reported profit (the Penrose adjustments cut profit by 6.25%, and accruals raised it by £8,300 over cash) and therefore dividends, tax and bonuses. For **stakeholders**, lenders rely on prudent, consistent accounts when deciding whether to lend; investors compare companies; the tax authority needs reliable profit figures; employees may judge job security from reported results.

The conventions matter most when external users rely on the accounts and when managers have a reason to flatter profit. They matter least in a very small business, where the owner already knows the position. They can pull in different directions: prudence may reduce profit while accruals says to match costs to revenue. A fair evaluation says that no single convention gives a complete view; the combination, applied honestly, does.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a convention without applying it to figures.
- Confusing accruals with prudence.
- Saying prudence means "always understate profit"; it means avoid overstating.
- Treating cash profit and accruals profit as the same.
- Forgetting to link conventions to the true and fair view.

## Exam technique

"Explain" needs the meaning and one example. "Apply" needs figures from the case: say which convention, what it requires and the effect on profit or asset value. "Evaluate" needs two sides, a stakeholder and a judgement.
