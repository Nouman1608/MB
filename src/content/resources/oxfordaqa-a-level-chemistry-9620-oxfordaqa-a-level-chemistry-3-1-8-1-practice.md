## Section A

**1.** Define the term *first electron affinity*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *enthalpy of atomisation*. **[2]**

**3.** Write an equation, with state symbols, for the lattice enthalpy of formation of calcium fluoride. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why the lattice enthalpy of formation of an ionic compound is always exothermic. **[1]**

**5.** Define the term *enthalpy of hydration*. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Data for calcium fluoride: ΔfH(CaF₂) −1220; ΔatH(Ca) +178; first IE(Ca) +590; second IE(Ca) +1145; F–F bond enthalpy +158; first EA(F) −328.

**(a)** Calculate the lattice enthalpy of formation of calcium fluoride. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the F–F bond enthalpy is used once, at its full value, in this cycle. **[1]**

**7.** Data for potassium oxide: ΔatH(K) +89; first IE(K) +419; ΔatH(O) +249; first EA(O) −141; second EA(O) +798; lattice enthalpy of formation of K₂O −2283. Calculate the enthalpy of formation of potassium oxide. **[3]**

**8.** Data for sodium bromide: ΔfH(NaBr) −361; ΔatH(Na) +107; first IE(Na) +496; first EA(Br) −325; lattice enthalpy of formation of NaBr −751.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy of atomisation of bromine. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why this value is not simply half of the Br–Br bond enthalpy. **[1]**

**9.** Lattice enthalpies of formation (values given for this question):

| Compound | Born–Haber cycle | Perfect ionic model |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium fluoride | −918 | −912 |
| Magnesium iodide | −2327 | −1944 |

Explain what these data show about the bonding in each compound, and why the two compounds differ. **[3]**

**10.** Data for lithium chloride: lattice enthalpy of dissociation +848; ΔhydH(Li⁺) −519; ΔhydH(Cl⁻) −364.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy of solution of lithium chloride. **[2]**

**(b)** State whether the temperature of the water rises or falls when lithium chloride dissolves. **[1]**

**11.** For potassium fluoride, ΔsolH = −18, the lattice enthalpy of dissociation = +821 and ΔhydH(K⁺) = −322. Calculate ΔhydH(F⁻). **[2]**

**12.** Explain why the enthalpy of hydration of F⁻ is more exothermic than that of Cl⁻. **[2]**

**13.** List, in order from the elements, the species present at each energy level of a Born–Haber cycle for magnesium chloride, up to the gaseous ions. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The enthalpy change when each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms gains one electron [1]; to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions [1]

**2.** The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed [1]; from the element in its standard state [1]

**3.** Ca²⁺(g) + 2F⁻(g) → CaF₂(s) [1]

**4.** Bonds (electrostatic attractions) form between oppositely charged ions, and bond formation releases energy [1]

**5.** The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions [1]; dissolves in water to form one mole of aqueous ions [1]

**6. (a)** Steps to gaseous ions = 178 + 590 + 1145 + 158 + 2(−328) [1]; = +1415 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; lattice formation = −1220 − 1415 = **−2635 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** CaF₂ needs two F atoms, and breaking one mole of F–F bonds gives exactly two moles of F atoms (equivalent to 2 × ΔatH(F)) [1]
*Examiner insight:* The electron affinity is doubled too, because two F⁻ ions form.

**7.** Steps to gaseous ions = 2(89) + 2(419) + 249 − 141 + 798 = +1922 [1]; ΔfH = +1922 + (−2283) [1]; = **−361 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**8. (a)** −361 = 107 + 496 + ΔatH(Br) + (−325) + (−751) [1]; ΔatH(Br) = **+112 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** Bromine is a liquid in its standard state, so its atomisation also includes the energy needed to turn the liquid into a gas [1]

**9.** Sodium fluoride: the values are very close, so its bonding is almost purely ionic [1]; magnesium iodide: the Born–Haber value is much more exothermic than the model, showing significant covalent character [1]; Mg²⁺ is small and doubly charged and I⁻ is large, so the cation polarises the anion's electron cloud strongly, whereas the small F⁻ is hardly polarised by Na⁺ [1]

**10. (a)** ΔsolH = +848 + (−519) + (−364) [1]; = **−35 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** It **rises** (dissolving is exothermic) [1]

**11.** −18 = +821 + (−322) + ΔhydH(F⁻) [1]; ΔhydH(F⁻) = **−517 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**12.** F⁻ is smaller than Cl⁻ (same charge), so its charge density is higher [1]; it attracts the δ+ hydrogen atoms of water molecules more strongly, releasing more energy [1]

**13.** Mg(s) + Cl₂(g) → Mg(g) + Cl₂(g) → Mg⁺(g) + e⁻ + Cl₂(g) [1]; → Mg²⁺(g) + 2e⁻ + Cl₂(g) → Mg²⁺(g) + 2e⁻ + 2Cl(g) [1]; → Mg²⁺(g) + 2Cl⁻(g) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Other orders (atomising chlorine first) are also correct, provided every level shows all the species with state symbols.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Missing "gaseous" or "standard state" in definitions.
- Not doubling the electron affinity or atomisation for two anions.
- Doubling the sodium terms but forgetting the oxygen terms in an M₂O cycle, or the reverse.
- Treating the second electron affinity as exothermic.
- Forgetting that liquid bromine and solid iodine have atomisation values that include a change of state.
