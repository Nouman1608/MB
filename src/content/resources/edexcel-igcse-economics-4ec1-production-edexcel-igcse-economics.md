## What the specification asks for

| Point | Wording |
|---|---|
| a | The factors of production: land, labour, capital, enterprise |
| b | Sectors of the economy: primary, secondary, tertiary |
| c | Changes in the importance of these sectors in terms of employment and output over time in developing and developed economies |

## The factors of production (point a)

Factors of production are the resources used to produce goods and services. Every business uses all four in some combination.

| Factor | What it includes | What it does **not** include |
|---|---|---|
| Land | All natural resources: soil, seas, rivers, minerals, oil, forests, fish stocks, the climate's sunshine and wind | Buildings put up on the land (those are capital) |
| Labour | The physical and mental effort of people in production, from a cleaner to a surgeon | The owner's risk-taking (that is enterprise) |
| Capital | Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services: machines, tools, vehicles, computers, factories, roads | Money in a bank account (money is used to *buy* capital) |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors, making decisions and taking the risk that the business fails | Routine management carried out for a wage (that is labour) |

### Worked classification: a fishing business

*Invented case.* Islanders Ltd catches and freezes fish on a small island.

| Resource | Factor | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| The fish stocks in the surrounding sea | Land | A natural resource |
| The crew of six who sail and fish | Labour | Human effort |
| Two boats, nets and a freezer unit | Capital | Man-made goods used in production |
| The owner, who borrowed money to buy the boats and decides where to fish | Enterprise | Organises the factors and takes the risk |
| A 40 000 bank loan | None directly | Money is used to buy capital but is not itself a factor in this classification |

Two classifications cause most errors: **money is not capital** in this sense, and **a salaried manager supplies labour**, whereas the person who bears the risk of the venture supplies enterprise.

### The factors work together

No factor produces on its own. Fish in the sea (land) are worth nothing to the market until a crew (labour) with boats (capital) catches them, organised by someone willing to risk their money (enterprise). This is why an exam answer about a shortage of one factor should explain how it limits the use of the others.

## The three sectors (point b)

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting or growing natural resources | Farming, fishing, mining, forestry, oil and gas extraction |
| Secondary | Turning raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods; building | Manufacturing, food processing, oil refining, construction |
| Tertiary | Providing services | Retail, transport, banking, insurance, tourism, education, healthcare |

### Following one product through the sectors

A bar of chocolate passes through all three sectors:

1. **Primary:** farmers grow and harvest cocoa beans.
2. **Secondary:** a factory roasts the beans, mixes the chocolate and moulds the bars.
3. **Tertiary:** a lorry firm transports the bars, a supermarket sells them and a bank provides the supermarket's payment system.

Classify by the **activity**, not the product. Extracting crude oil is primary; refining it into petrol is secondary; selling petrol at a filling station is tertiary. Construction is secondary because it turns materials into a building, and transport is tertiary because it is a service.

## How the sectors change over time (point c)

The specification asks about two measures, **employment** (the share of workers in each sector) and **output** (the share of national output each sector produces), in two kinds of economy.

### The usual pattern

| Stage | Primary | Secondary | Tertiary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early developing economy | Largest share of employment and output | Small | Small |
| Industrialising economy | Falling share | Rising share as factories grow | Rising |
| Developed economy | Very small share | Falling share | Largest share of employment and output |

### Why the pattern happens

- **Rising incomes shift demand.** As people become richer, they spend a smaller share of income on food and a larger share on manufactured goods and then services such as travel, entertainment and healthcare. Firms follow demand, so resources move towards the tertiary sector.
- **Machinery in farming.** Tractors, fertiliser and irrigation let fewer farm workers produce as much or more food, releasing labour to work in factories and services.
- **Industrialisation.** Developing economies build factories, often to export manufactured goods, so the secondary sector grows.
- **Manufacturing moves.** In many developed economies, some manufacturing moves to countries with lower costs, and factories that remain use more machines and fewer workers, so the secondary share of employment falls.

### Employment share is not the same as output share

A sector's share of employment can fall while its output rises. If farms mechanise, output per farm worker rises, so food production can grow even as the number of farm workers falls.

### Worked example: comparing two economies

*Invented data, shares of employment.*

| Sector | Country D (developing), Year 1 | Country D, Year 20 | Country E (developed), Year 1 | Country E, Year 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | 55% | 40% | 4% | 2% |
| Secondary | 20% | 28% | 26% | 18% |
| Tertiary | 25% | 32% | 70% | 80% |

- In Country D, the primary share fell by 55 − 40 = **15 percentage points**, while the secondary share rose by 8 points: a sign of industrialisation.
- In Country E, the secondary share fell by 26 − 18 = **8 percentage points** and the tertiary share rose by 10 points: services are growing while manufacturing shrinks.

Now suppose Country D's total employment rose from 30 million to 45 million. Primary workers changed from 0.55 × 30 = 16.5 million to 0.40 × 45 = 18.0 million: the **number** rose by 1.5 million, or (18.0 − 16.5) ÷ 16.5 × 100 = **9.1%**, even though the **share** fell. Always check whether a question is about shares or numbers.

### Exceptions to the pattern

Use "tends to" in answers. A country rich in oil or minerals may keep a large primary share of output for a long time, even when it is high-income; some economies move straight from primary to services, for example through tourism. A good evaluation names the exception and explains it.

## Common mistakes

- Calling money "capital" or a building "land".
- Treating any manager as enterprise; only the risk-taker supplies enterprise.
- Classifying by what is sold rather than the activity carried out.
- Confusing percentage points (change in a share) with per cent (change in a number).
- Saying the primary sector "disappears" in developed economies. It shrinks as a share but still produces.
- Stating the pattern as a rule with no exceptions.

## Exam technique

- **Define / What is meant by:** two parts, such as "natural resources" and "used in production" for land.
- **Identify / State:** name the factor or sector precisely.
- **Calculate:** show the arithmetic; label percentage points.
- **Explain:** a cause of structural change plus two developments, such as rising incomes → demand for services rises → firms and jobs move to the tertiary sector.
- **Assess:** weigh the pattern against exceptions using the data given.
