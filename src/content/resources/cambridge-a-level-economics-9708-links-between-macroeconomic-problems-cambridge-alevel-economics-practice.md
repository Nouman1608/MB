## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between the internal value and the external value of money. **[2]**

**2.** According to the expectations-augmented Phillips curve, in the long run:

A: there is a stable trade-off between inflation and unemployment

B: unemployment returns to the natural rate whatever the rate of inflation

C: inflation always returns to zero

D: unemployment falls whenever inflation rises **[1]**

**3.** A country's price index rises from 120 to 132. Calculate the percentage fall in the internal value of money. **[2]**

**4.** National income rises by 60 billion and the marginal propensity to import is 0.15. Calculate the rise in imports and state its effect on the current account if exports do not change. **[2]**

**5.** Which combination describes stagflation?

A: rising inflation and falling unemployment

B: falling inflation and rising unemployment

C: rising inflation and rising unemployment

D: falling inflation and falling unemployment **[1]**

**6.** Explain why export-led growth may improve the current account of the balance of payments. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** In a year, prices in Country K rise by 7% and prices in its trading partners rise by 3%. Country K's currency depreciates by 2%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the price of Country K's goods, in foreign currency, relative to its partners' goods. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the depreciation that would have left Country K's competitiveness unchanged. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how the depreciation could itself add to inflation in Country K. **[2]**

**8.** In an invented model, inflation = expected inflation − 0.5 × (unemployment − 5). Expected inflation each year equals the previous year's actual inflation. Inflation and expected inflation are both 6% and unemployment is 5%. The central bank tightens policy and holds unemployment at 7% for three years.

**(a)** Calculate inflation in each of the three years. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why inflation falls gradually rather than immediately. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how a credible announcement by the central bank could reduce the rise in unemployment needed. **[2]**

**9.** Using AD/AS analysis, explain why economic growth may be accompanied by inflation in one economy but not in another. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a persistent current account deficit can lead to inflation. **[3]**

**11.** Assess whether a government can achieve both low inflation and low unemployment at the same time. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The internal value of money is what a unit of currency buys at home, its purchasing power over domestic goods, which falls as the price level rises [1]; the external value is what it buys abroad, shown by the exchange rate [1].

**2.** **B** [1]. The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate.

**3.** Money now buys 120 ÷ 132 = 0.909 of what it did [1], a fall of about **9.1%** [1].

**4.** Imports rise by 0.15 × 60 = **9 billion** [1], so the current account worsens by 9 billion [1].

**5.** **C** [1]. Stagflation is high or rising inflation with high or rising unemployment, often after a supply shock.

**6.** Growth driven by rising exports adds credits to the current account [1]; as long as the extra imports that come with higher income are smaller than the rise in exports, the balance improves [1].

**7.** **(a)** 1.07 × 0.98 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 0.018 [1], so Country K's goods are about **1.8% dearer** relative to partners' goods [1].

**(b)** 1 − 1.03 ÷ 1.07 = 0.037, a depreciation of about **3.7%** [1].

**(c)** The depreciation raises the home-currency price of imports [1], which raises firms' costs and the price of imported consumer goods, adding to cost-push inflation and so to the inflation gap that weakened the currency [1].

**8.** **(a)** Each year unemployment is 2 points above the natural rate, which subtracts 0.5 × 2 = 1 point. Year 1: 6 − 1 = **5%**; year 2: 5 − 1 = **4%** [1]; year 3: 4 − 1 = **3%** [1].

**(b)** Wages and prices are set using expected inflation, which equals last year's inflation [1], so expectations fall only one step at a time and each year the economy moves to a lower short-run Phillips curve, not straight to the target [1].

**(c)** If people believe the central bank will bring inflation down, expected inflation falls immediately rather than following past inflation [1], so the short-run Phillips curve shifts down at once and inflation falls with a smaller or shorter rise in unemployment [1].

**9.** If an economy has spare capacity, a rise in AD moves along the elastic part of AS, so real output rises with little rise in the price level [1]. If it is near full capacity, AD rises along the steep part of AS, so most of the effect is on the price level, giving demand-pull inflation [1]. If growth comes from a rightward shift of LRAS and SRAS, through higher productivity or investment [1], output can rise while the price level is stable or falls [1].

**10.** A persistent deficit means more of the currency is supplied to buy imports than is demanded for exports [1], so under a floating exchange rate the currency tends to depreciate [1]. Import prices rise in home currency, raising firms' costs and consumer prices, so inflation rises [1].

**11.** For: in the long run, the expectations-augmented Phillips curve shows that unemployment returns to the natural rate at any inflation rate, so low, stable inflation is compatible with unemployment at its natural rate [1]; supply-side policies that reduce frictional and structural unemployment lower the natural rate, shifting the LRPC left [1]; credible policy keeps expected inflation low, so low inflation can be held without high unemployment [1].

Against: in the short run, reducing inflation by cutting AD moves the economy down the SRPC, raising unemployment [1]; pushing unemployment below the natural rate through higher AD causes inflation to accelerate [1]; supply shocks can raise inflation and unemployment together, forcing a choice [1]; supply-side policies take a long time to lower the natural rate [1].

Judgement: both can be achieved together only if unemployment is at a low natural rate and expectations are anchored; in the short run, and after supply shocks, governments usually face a trade-off [1].
