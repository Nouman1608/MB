## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic growth | A rise in the output of goods and services in an economy over time |
| GDP | The total value of goods and services produced within a country in a period, usually a year |
| Nominal GDP | GDP at current prices; includes the effect of price changes |
| Real GDP | GDP adjusted for price changes; the measure of growth |
| GDP per head | GDP divided by population |
| Productive potential | The maximum output the economy can produce; an outward shift of the frontier |
| Economic cycle | The pattern of faster and slower growth around a long-run trend |

## Formulas and methods

| Task | Method |
|---|---|
| Growth rate | (new - old) / old × 100 |
| Real GDP at base-year prices | nominal GDP / price index × 100 |
| GDP per head | GDP / population |
| Growth in GDP per head | Compare the per-head figures; do not subtract the percentages without checking |
| Employment change | (1 + output growth) / (1 + output per worker growth) - 1 |
| Total emissions | (1 + output growth) × (1 + change in emissions per unit) - 1 |

### Method in steps: real growth

1. Divide year 2 nominal GDP by the year 2 price index, then multiply by 100.
2. Compare with year 1 (base year, index 100).
3. Work out the percentage change and state it to 1 or 2 decimal places.

Worked reminder: nominal GDP 600 to 645, index 100 to 104. Real = 645 / 1.04 = 620.19. Real growth = 3.37%. Nominal growth = 7.5%.

## Limitations of GDP

- Nominal figures include inflation.
- Population growth shares output between more people.
- Unrecorded and unpaid work is missed, so GDP may understate output.
- Averages hide inequality.
- Pollution and resource depletion are not deducted.
- Quality of life, leisure and health are not shown.
- What is produced is not shown, only its value.
- Data may be estimated and revised.

## The economic cycle

Sketch: a wave of real GDP against time, rising and falling around an upward trend line. Label the peak as boom, the falling section as downturn, the bottom as recession and the rising section as recovery.

| Stage | Growth | Inflation | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | High | Rising | Low |
| Downturn | Slowing but positive | Rising more slowly, then easing | Starting to rise |
| Recession | Negative | Low or falling | High, rising |
| Recovery | Positive, speeding up | Low, then rising | Falling, but still high |

Unemployment lags output. In a recovery, firms wait before hiring.

## Impact of growth: pros and cons

| Area | Possible benefit | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|
| Employment | More output needs more workers | Output may rise through productivity, with no extra jobs |
| Standards of living | More goods and services per head; more tax for health and education | Averages hide inequality; longer hours |
| Poverty | Jobs and tax revenue for benefits | Gains may go mainly to the better off |
| Productive potential | Frontier shifts out; more can be made in future | Growth from idle resources does not raise potential |
| Inflation | Little if capacity grows as fast as spending | Spending rising faster than capacity pushes prices up |
| Environment | Richer countries can afford cleaner methods | More resource use, pollution and waste |

## Why each stage behaves as it does

- **Boom:** high spending, firms near full capacity. Shortages of workers and materials let firms raise prices, so inflation rises. Firms hire, so unemployment is low.
- **Downturn:** spending growth slows and stock builds up. Firms cut back on hiring and investment, so growth slows while output still rises.
- **Recession:** falling spending means falling output. Firms cut jobs, so unemployment rises. Weak demand holds prices down, so inflation is low.
- **Recovery:** spending picks up, output rises again. Firms use spare capacity before hiring, so unemployment falls slowly and inflation stays low for a while.

## Worked reminders

**Per head.** Real GDP 1,260 billion, population 40.8 million. Per head = 1,260 billion / 40.8 million = 30,882. If last year was 30,000, growth per head = 882 / 30,000 = 2.94%, compared with 5% growth in total GDP and 2% growth in population.

**Falling output over two years.** Growth of -1.3% then -0.6%: 0.987 × 0.994 = 0.98108, so output fell 1.89% in total.

**Reading a table.** Year A: growth 4.8%, inflation 4.6%, unemployment 3.9% is a boom. Year B: growth 2.1%, inflation 3.4%, unemployment 4.1% is a downturn. Year C: growth -1.3%, unemployment 5.8% is a recession. Year D: growth 1.9%, unemployment 7.0% (down from 7.2%) is a recovery.

**Averages and shares.** Household incomes 15, 25, 40, 50, 120 (mean 50) become 15, 25, 43, 55, 152 (mean 58). The mean rose 16%, two households gained nothing, and the richest household's share of the total rose from 48% to 52.4%.

**Jobs.** Output +4% and output per worker +1.5%: 1.04 / 1.015 = 1.0246, so employment rises 2.46%. If output per worker also rose 4%, employment would not change.

## Productive potential and the frontier

| Situation | Where the economy is | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployed or idle resources are put to work | Moves from inside the frontier towards it | Actual output rises; the frontier stays put |
| More or better resources, or new technology | The frontier moves outward | Productive potential rises |
| Resources are lost or damaged | The frontier moves inward | Potential falls |

Example: a frontier with maxima of 80 consumer goods and 40 capital goods that becomes 88 and 44 has risen by 10% on both axes.

## Evaluation framework for growth questions

1. Say what growth does in the context given.
2. Give at least one benefit and one cost, linked by reasons.
3. Compare: real or nominal, total or per head, average or spread.
4. Short run or long run: jobs and income now, resources and environment later.
5. Judge, then say what the judgement depends on, such as how the gains are shared.

## Must-know distinctions

- Nominal versus real GDP.
- GDP versus GDP per head.
- Slower growth (downturn) versus falling output (recession).
- Growth from using idle resources versus an outward shift of the frontier.
- Short run versus long run in evaluation.

## Quick self-test

1. Define economic growth.
2. Nominal GDP rises from 800 to 856 and the price index from 100 to 107. Calculate real growth.
3. Name the stage with falling output and rising unemployment.
4. State two limitations of GDP.
5. Real GDP rises 5% and population 2%. Is GDP per head up by 3%, more than 3% or less than 3%?
6. During which stage does inflation usually reach its highest level?
7. Output grows 3% and output per worker 3%. What happens to employment?
8. State one way growth can reduce poverty.
9. How does an outward shift of the frontier differ from using idle resources?
10. Output rises 6% and emissions per unit fall 3%. By how much do total emissions change?
11. Real GDP falls from 500 to 485. Calculate the percentage change.
12. Why does unemployment usually peak after the trough in output?

### Answers

1. A rise in output of goods and services over time, shown by an increase in real GDP.
2. 856 / 1.07 = 800.0, so real growth is **0%**: all the rise is inflation.
3. **Recession.**
4. Any two: inflation, population, unrecorded activity, inequality, environmental damage, quality of life.
5. 1.05 / 1.02 - 1 = 2.94%, so **less than 3%**.
6. **Boom**, when demand outruns supply.
7. Employment is unchanged: 1.03 / 1.03 - 1 = **0%**.
8. Growth creates jobs and gives government more tax revenue to fund benefits.
9. The shift raises the maximum possible output; using idle resources moves the economy towards the existing frontier.
10. 1.06 × 0.97 - 1 = 2.82%, a **rise of 2.8%**.
11. (485 - 500) / 500 × 100 = **-3.0%**.
12. Firms wait until demand has recovered for good before hiring, and may use spare capacity or existing workers first.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "GDP" with no word such as real or per head when the question is about growth.
- Using nominal figures, so inflation is counted as output.
- Mixing up a downturn with a recession.
- Giving a stage's effect on growth but leaving out inflation or unemployment when the question asks for all three.
- Listing limitations without explaining the distortion.
- Giving one-sided answers to "evaluate" questions with no judgement.
- Forgetting that unemployment lags the cycle.
- Leaving out units or workings in a calculation.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between nominal and real GDP?**
Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it includes the effect of price changes. Real GDP is adjusted for price changes and is the measure used for economic growth.

**What are the four stages of the economic cycle?**
The stages are boom, downturn, recession and recovery. In a boom growth is high and unemployment low, in a downturn growth slows but stays positive, in a recession output falls and unemployment rises, and in a recovery growth picks up while unemployment is still high.

**What is the difference between a downturn and a recession?**
In a downturn growth slows but output is still rising. In a recession output actually falls. Mixing up the two is a common way to lose marks.

**Why does unemployment lag behind output in the economic cycle?**
In a recovery, firms first use spare capacity before hiring new workers, so unemployment falls slowly even when output is rising. This is why unemployment usually peaks after the low point in output.

**What are the limitations of GDP as a measure?**
GDP figures may include inflation, ignore population growth, miss unrecorded and unpaid work, hide inequality, and leave out pollution, resource depletion, leisure and quality of life. Data may also be estimated and later revised.
