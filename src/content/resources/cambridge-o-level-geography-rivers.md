## What 2.2 asks you to do

The syllabus sets five learning outcomes:

1. Explain the main **hydrological characteristics and processes** in rivers
   and drainage basins: width, depth, speed of flow and discharge; watershed,
   tributary and confluence; interception, infiltration, throughflow,
   groundwater flow, evaporation and overland flow.
2. Demonstrate an understanding of the work of a river in **eroding,
   transporting and depositing**.
3. Describe and explain the formation of the **landforms**: forms of river
   valleys (long profile and cross-section shape), waterfalls, potholes,
   meanders, oxbow lakes, deltas, levées and flood plains.
4. Demonstrate an understanding that rivers present **hazards** (including
   flooding and river erosion) and offer **opportunities** for people living on
   a flood plain, a delta or near a river.
5. Explain what can be done to **manage the impacts of river flooding**.

The required case study covers **the opportunities presented by a river or
rivers, the associated hazards and their management**.

## The drainage basin as a system

A **drainage basin** is the area of land drained by a river and its
tributaries. Its boundary is the **watershed**, usually a ridge of high land.
A **tributary** is a smaller river that joins the main river, and the point
where they meet is a **confluence**. The **source** is where the river begins
and the **mouth** is where it enters the sea or a lake.

Water moves through the basin as inputs, stores, flows and outputs:

- **Input:** precipitation.
- **Stores:** interception by vegetation, surface water, soil water,
  groundwater and the river channel.
- **Flows:** **infiltration** (water soaking into the soil), **throughflow**
  (water moving sideways through the soil), **overland flow** (water running
  across the surface), percolation into rock, and **groundwater flow**
  through rock.
- **Outputs:** **evaporation**, transpiration from plants, and river
  discharge into the sea.

**Interception** slows water down; overland flow is the fastest route to the
river. Anything that increases overland flow (saturated or frozen ground,
impermeable rock, steep slopes, concrete, deforestation) raises flood risk.

## River characteristics downstream

| Characteristic | Change from source to mouth |
|---|---|
| Width | Increases |
| Depth | Increases |
| Speed of flow (velocity) | Usually increases, because a wider, deeper, smoother channel loses less energy to friction |
| Discharge | Increases, as tributaries add water |
| Gradient | Decreases |
| Load | Particles become smaller and more rounded |

Many students expect velocity to fall downstream because the gradient is
gentler. In fact, the channel becomes more efficient, so average velocity
usually rises.

**Discharge** is the volume of water passing a point per second:

```
discharge (m³/s) = cross-sectional area (m²) × velocity (m/s)
cross-sectional area = width × mean depth
```

## Erosion, transport and deposition

**Erosion processes**

- **Hydraulic action:** the force of water breaks rock from the bed and banks.
- **Abrasion:** the load scrapes and wears away the bed and banks.
- **Attrition:** pieces of load knock against each other and become smaller
  and rounder.
- **Solution:** water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone.

**Transport processes**

- **Traction:** large boulders roll along the bed.
- **Saltation:** pebbles bounce along the bed.
- **Suspension:** fine particles are carried within the water.
- **Solution:** dissolved minerals are carried in the water.

**Deposition** happens when the river loses energy: on the inside of meander
bends, where a river enters a lake or the sea, when water spreads over the
flood plain during a flood, or when discharge falls in dry periods.

## How the landforms form

**V-shaped valley.** In the upper course the river cuts downwards by vertical
erosion. Weathering and mass movement of the valley sides form the steep V
shape, and the river winds between **interlocking spurs**.

**Waterfall.** A band of hard, resistant rock lies over softer rock. The soft
rock is eroded more quickly, undercutting the hard rock to leave an overhang.
Hydraulic action and abrasion deepen a **plunge pool** at the base. The
overhang eventually collapses, and the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a
steep-sided **gorge**.

**Potholes.** Pebbles caught in swirling eddies are spun round on a rocky
river bed, drilling circular hollows by abrasion.

**Meander.** The fastest current swings to the outside of a bend, so erosion
there forms a steep **river cliff**. On the inside the water is slower and
shallower, so deposition forms a gently sloping **slip-off slope** (point
bar). The bend becomes more pronounced over time.

**Oxbow lake.** Erosion on the outer banks narrows the neck of a meander.
During a flood the river cuts through the neck, taking the shorter, straighter
route. Deposition seals off the old bend, leaving a horseshoe-shaped oxbow
lake.

**Flood plain.** Meanders migrate across and down the valley, widening it by
lateral erosion. Each flood deposits a layer of fine **alluvium**, building a
wide, flat valley floor.

**Levées.** When the river floods, the water spreads out of the channel and
loses energy immediately. The heaviest, coarsest material is deposited first,
on the banks; finer silt is carried further across the flood plain. Repeated
floods build raised banks called levées.

**Delta.** Where a river enters the sea or a lake, its speed falls sharply and
it deposits its load faster than tides and currents can remove it. In salt
water, fine clay particles also stick together and sink. The river splits into
**distributaries** across the growing deposit. The Nile delta has a curved,
fan-like (arcuate) shape; the Mississippi delta has a bird's-foot shape.

## Hazards and opportunities

**Hazards**

- **Flooding:** physical causes include heavy or prolonged rain, snowmelt,
  saturated ground and steep, impermeable catchments; human causes include
  deforestation, urbanisation (impermeable surfaces and drains) and building
  on flood plains.
- **River erosion:** riverbanks collapse, destroying farmland, roads and
  homes, especially on large rivers with shifting channels.

**Opportunities** of living on a flood plain, delta or near a river:

- Fertile alluvial soils for farming.
- Flat land for building and transport routes.
- Water for drinking, irrigation and industry.
- Fishing, river transport and trade.
- Hydro-electric power and tourism.

## Managing river flooding

| Hard engineering | Soft engineering and other measures |
|---|---|
| **Dams and reservoirs** store water and release it slowly | **Afforestation** increases interception and slows runoff |
| **Embankments and raised levées** increase channel capacity | **Flood plain zoning** keeps homes and key buildings off the highest-risk land |
| **Channel straightening and dredging** move water away faster | **Wetland restoration** stores floodwater |
| **Flood relief channels** divert excess water | **Warning systems**, raised homes and flood shelters |

Hard engineering is usually expensive and can move the problem downstream; soft
approaches are cheaper and more sustainable but give less protection against
the largest floods.

## Worked example 1: calculating discharge

*At a site, a river is 8 m wide, has a mean depth of 0.5 m and a velocity of
0.6 m/s. Calculate the discharge.* [2]

Cross-sectional area = 8 × 0.5 = 4 m² [1]. Discharge = 4 × 0.6 =
**2.4 m³/s** [1].

Always use the **mean** depth, not the deepest reading, and give the units.

## Worked example 2: case-study plan

*For a named river, describe the opportunities it offers and explain how its
hazards are managed.* [7]

A plan for **the River Nile in Egypt**:

- **Opportunities:** water for irrigation in a desert country; fertile
  alluvial soils on the flood plain and delta; transport and tourism.
- **Hazard:** before the Aswan High Dam, the annual flood was unreliable, too
  high in some years (destroying crops and homes) and too low in others.
- **Management:** the Aswan High Dam, completed in 1970, created Lake Nasser,
  controls the flood, allows year-round irrigation and generates
  hydro-electric power.
- **Evaluation:** silt is now trapped behind the dam, so farmers rely on
  artificial fertilisers and the delta coastline is no longer replenished.

The Ganges–Brahmaputra delta in Bangladesh, with its embankments, flood
shelters and warning systems, is an equally good alternative.

## Common mistakes

- Saying velocity always decreases downstream.
- Describing a meander without explaining **where** erosion and deposition
  happen and **why**.
- Forgetting the flood that cuts through the neck of an oxbow lake.
- Mixing up levées (natural or artificial embankments) with flood plains.
- Listing management methods without saying whether they are hard or soft
  engineering, or how effective they are.

## Exam technique for 2.2

- **Explain the formation of** a landform: write a sequence of stages and
  name the processes (hydraulic action, abrasion, deposition).
- **Describe** a river from a map or photograph: width, channel shape,
  valley shape, landforms seen; quote grid references or distances on Paper 2
  map questions.
- **Calculate**: show working and units.
- **Suggest** reasons for flooding: use clues in the resource such as land
  use, slope and rainfall.
