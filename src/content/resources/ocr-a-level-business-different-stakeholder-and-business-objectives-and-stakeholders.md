## 1.1 Aims and the hierarchy of objectives

Objectives only work when each level supports the one above it. This chain is the **hierarchy of objectives**. These are the standard definitions, which OCR's text does not spell out, so use them consistently.

- **Organisational aim:** the broad, long-term purpose or ambition. It is rarely a number.
- **Corporate (business) objective:** a measurable target for the whole business, such as sales revenue, profit or market share.
- **Strategic objective:** a medium to long-term target set by senior managers that shows how the corporate objective will be reached, such as entering a new market.
- **Tactical objective:** a shorter-term target for a department or team that carries out the strategy.
- **Operational objective:** a day-to-day target for a person, shift or process.

**Hollowbeck Cheese Co.** is a private-sector maker of farmhouse cheese. Its aim is to be the best-known independent cheese producer in northern England. Its corporate objective is to raise sales revenue from £3.2 million to £4.0 million within three years, a rise of £0.8 million or 25%. A strategic objective is to win regional listings with supermarket chains by the end of year two. A tactical objective is to run tasting events in 40 stores over the next six months. An operational objective is to keep rejected packs below 1.5% of weekly output, which is 36 kg on 2,400 kg.

Each level answers the question "so that we can do what?" from the one below. If an operational target does not connect to any higher level, question why it exists.

### SMART objectives

SMART (the standard form, not spelt out in the specification) stands for specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound (some courses use "agreed" or "attainable"; follow your teacher's wording, and name each letter). A vague objective such as "be more popular" cannot be checked. A SMART version is "raise the online customer rating from 3.9 to 4.3 out of 5 by 30 June next year".

For the business, SMART objectives give a clear target, allow progress to be monitored and make the success of a decision measurable. For stakeholders, they show what to expect: staff know what is asked of them, lenders and shareholders can judge progress, and customers can see what is being promised. The weakness is that a narrow numerical target can encourage short-term behaviour, such as cutting quality to hit a cost target, and a figure set too early can be overtaken by events.

### Impact and importance of setting aims and objectives

For **evaluate** questions, weigh these.

- **For:** direction and focus; a basis for decisions and for measuring performance; motivation when staff share goals; confidence for lenders and investors.
- **Against:** time and cost to set and review; rigid targets in fast-changing markets; poor objectives can push managers towards the wrong behaviour; conflicting objectives (growth against profit) can confuse.
- **Judgement:** objectives help most when they are few, linked to each other and reviewed regularly.

### Sector and objectives

The specification separates **private**, **public** and **third sector** organisations. Sector shapes objectives because the owner and the funding differ.

- A **private sector** firm, owned by individuals or shareholders, usually emphasises profit, return to owners, growth and survival, though it may also pursue ethical or environmental goals.
- A **public sector** organisation, funded by government, tends to have objectives about service quality, access, value for money and meeting budgets rather than profit.
- A **third sector** organisation, such as a charity or social enterprise, normally pursues a social purpose, with surpluses reinvested.

Contrast **Hollowbeck**, which stresses revenue and profit, with a public leisure centre run for a council, which would stress affordable access and participation. Remember that sector sets a tendency, not a rule: a private firm may have a strong social aim, and a public body may still need a surplus.

### Communicating objectives

Objectives only work when the people who must deliver them understand them. Methods include team briefings, meetings, emails and intranet pages, noticeboards, appraisals and one-to-one reviews, written plans and reports to shareholders, and public statements or websites for external stakeholders. Face-to-face methods allow questions and feedback but take time. Written methods reach many people cheaply but may be skimmed or misread.

**Mis-communication** can mean staff working towards different ends, wasted resources, duplicated effort, low morale and a loss of trust. Investors or lenders may lose confidence, and customers may receive a message that the business cannot deliver.

To **improve communication**, a business can use more than one channel, use plain language, link each objective to the person's own role, check understanding through feedback, repeat the message at set times and make sure managers explain the reasons as well as the figures. Evaluate by cost, size of the workforce, how complex the message is and whether staff are in one location.

### Why objectives change

Objectives change because the business or its environment has moved on. Typical triggers are a recession or a change in demand, new competitors or technology, growth or a change in ownership, a failure to meet earlier targets, a shift in law or public attitudes, or a crisis that makes survival the first priority. A start-up may begin with survival, then move to growth, and later to profit or a wider social role.

### Recommend and justify

When asked to **recommend and justify** aims and objectives, follow four steps. State the objective in SMART form. Link it to the stage the business has reached and its sector. Say what evidence supports it from the case. Then say how change would be carried out: explain the reason, communicate in several ways, involve staff, phase the change, and review progress. Weigh one risk, such as resistance, and give a reason for your choice.

## 1.2 Stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is any person or group that has a stake in what a business does or is touched by its actions. Ownership is not required.

- **Internal stakeholders** work in or own the business: owners and shareholders, directors and managers, employees.
- **External stakeholders** sit outside it: customers, suppliers, lenders, competitors, the local community, government and local authorities, pressure groups, and the media.

Some people belong in two groups. A shareholder who is also a customer has two interests.

### Stakeholder objectives (analyse)

| Stakeholder | Likely objective |
|---|---|
| Shareholders and owners | return on investment: dividends and a rising share value |
| Directors and managers | business success, pay, status, achievement of targets |
| Employees | fair pay, secure employment, safe conditions, training |
| Customers | quality, value, reliability, service |
| Suppliers | prompt payment, regular orders |
| Lenders | repayment with interest, low risk |
| Local community | jobs, limited noise and traffic, a clean environment |
| Government | tax revenue, compliance, employment |
| Pressure groups | change in business behaviour on a specific issue |

To **analyse**, add "because" to each point and link it to the case: a lender wants low risk because it needs repayment, so it will resist an expensive expansion.

### Conflict between stakeholders

Conflict arises mainly because stakeholders have different goals and one pool of resources. A higher wage bill cuts profit. A lower price benefits customers but cuts the margin. Faster delivery can mean more lorries on local roads. Short-term and long-term interests also clash, and stakeholders differ in power and information.

A business needs to **manage** conflict because unresolved disputes can lead to strikes, lost customers, withdrawn finance, bad publicity, legal action or planning refusal. Poor management also wastes managers' time and can damage the reputation that supports every other objective.

**Impact of conflict** (evaluate): short-term, delays, cost and tension; long-term, a stronger decision if the debate surfaces risks, or damaged relationships if one group is always overruled. The impact depends on how much power the unhappy group holds.

### Dealing with conflict (recommend and justify)

Options are to prioritise the stakeholders with the most power or interest, compromise, communicate and consult early, offer compensation or phased change, or change the decision. A power and interest grid is a standard way to sort stakeholders, though the specification does not name it. Justify by saying which stakeholders matter most to the objectives in the case and why, and accept the cost to the others.

### Influence of stakeholders (evaluate)

Stakeholders affect a business's **aims and objectives**, **decision making**, **behaviour** and **performance**. Shareholders may set profit targets. Lenders can add conditions to loans. Employees influence performance through effort and turnover. Customers influence behaviour through what they buy. Government sets rules. Pressure groups shape behaviour through publicity. Influence rests on power: control of resources, ability to withdraw, legal rights, numbers and public attention.

### Impact of business decisions on stakeholders (evaluate)

Take one decision and trace winners and losers.

**Worked example: Fellmoor Plastics Ltd.** The directors plan to move production to a cheaper site 40 km away. Unit cost falls from £6.50 to £5.80 on 120,000 units a year, a saving of 0.70 × 120,000 = £84,000 a year. The move costs £150,000, so the cost is recovered in 150,000 ÷ 84,000 = 1.79 years, about 21 months. Of the 60 employees, 22 say they cannot move, which is 37% of the workforce.

- Shareholders gain: higher profit after the payback period.
- Employees lose: 22 jobs are at risk, and staff who stay face a longer journey.
- Local community: loses an employer, but may gain less traffic.
- Customers: may gain if savings are passed on, but may suffer disruption during the move.
- Suppliers: orders may shift to different firms.

A good judgement says that the saving is real but the benefit goes to a few while the cost falls on many. The move is sensible only if the business keeps skilled staff, perhaps through transport help or a phased transfer, and communicates early.

## Common errors

- Writing "make money" as an aim, then confusing it with a corporate objective.
- Treating a tactical action as a strategic objective.
- Naming stakeholders without saying what each wants.
- Listing conflicts without explaining why they arise.
- Stating that stakeholders are "all equally important".
- Giving a recommendation that ignores the sector or the size of the business.
