**What these questions cover.** Section 2 of the 2059 Paper 1 content, Key Questions 6 to 10: the early Pakistan Movement and constitutional reform, the Khilafat Movement, the years 1927 to 1939, the search for a solution from 1940 to 1947, and the leaders of the Movement. Section B of the paper uses a 4-mark knowledge part, a 7-mark explanation and a 14-mark evaluation; the questions are grouped by those types.

## Section A: knowledge (4 marks each)

**1.** Describe the Partition of Bengal of 1905. **[4]**

**2.** Describe the Morley–Minto Reforms of 1909. **[4]**

**3.** Describe the Amritsar Massacre of 1919. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the Delhi Proposals of 1927. **[4]**

**5.** Describe the Lahore (Pakistan) Resolution of 1940. **[4]**

## Section B: explanation (7 marks each)

**6.** Explain why the Partition of Bengal was reversed in 1911. **[7]**

**7.** Explain why the Muslim League and Congress agreed the Lucknow Pact in 1916. **[7]**

**8.** Explain why the Muslim League did badly in the elections of 1937. **[7]**

**9.** Explain why the talks between Gandhi and Jinnah in 1944 failed. **[7]**

**10.** Explain why the Simla Conference of 1945 failed. **[7]**

## Section C: evaluation (14 marks each)

**11.** "The Khilafat Movement achieved nothing for the Muslims of the subcontinent." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**12.** "The Government of India Act of 1935 was a success for the Muslims of the subcontinent." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**13.** "Congress rule (1937–39) did more than any other development between 1927 and 1939 to advance the Pakistan Movement." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

## Answers

Knowledge answers earn 1 mark per relevant point or supporting detail, to a maximum of 4. The 7- and 14-mark answers are marked by levels of response (tables after the answers). The content listed is indicative; credit any other accurate, relevant point.

**1.** The Viceroy, Lord Curzon, divided the large province of Bengal in 1905, saying it was too big to govern well [1]. A new province of Eastern Bengal and Assam was created with Dhaka as its capital and a Muslim majority [1]. Muslims welcomed it because it gave them more opportunities in jobs, education and trade [1]. Hindus opposed it strongly, organising the Swadeshi campaign to boycott British goods, and there were violent protests [1]. **[4]**

**2.** They were introduced by the Indian Councils Act of 1909, named after the Secretary of State, Lord Morley, and the Viceroy, Lord Minto [1]. The central and provincial legislative councils were enlarged, with more elected Indian members [1]. Muslims were given separate electorates, so they elected their own representatives [1]. An Indian member was appointed to the Viceroy's Executive Council for the first time [1]. **[4]**

**3.** It took place on 13 April 1919 at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar [1]. A large, unarmed crowd had gathered in the enclosed garden, partly in protest at the arrest of local leaders under the Rowlatt Act [1]. General Dyer ordered his troops to fire into the crowd without warning, blocking the exit [1]. The official figure was 379 killed, with many more wounded, and martial law followed in the Punjab; Dyer was later criticised by the Hunter Committee and removed from command [1]. **[4]**

**4.** In March 1927 a meeting of Muslim leaders in Delhi, led by Jinnah, put forward proposals to Congress [1]. Muslims would give up separate electorates in favour of joint electorates with reserved seats [1], if they were given one-third of the seats in the central legislature [1], Sindh was separated from Bombay as a new province, and reforms were introduced in the North-West Frontier and Balochistan [1]. **[4]**

**5.** It was moved on 23 March 1940 at the Muslim League's session in Lahore [1], by A. K. Fazlul Haq [1]. It stated that the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and east of India should form independent states [1]. Although the word "Pakistan" did not appear in it, it became known as the Pakistan Resolution and made a separate homeland the League's official aim [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Hindu opposition was intense and continuous: mass meetings, petitions and the Swadeshi boycott of British goods hurt British trade.
- Some opponents turned to violence, including bomb attacks and assassinations, and the British wanted to restore order.
- Congress made the reversal one of its main demands, and the British wanted to win back Hindu support.
- The British believed that reversing partition would reduce unrest across India.
- The change was announced during King George V's visit, at the Delhi Durbar of 1911, along with moving the capital from Calcutta to Delhi, as a gesture to reconcile Indian opinion.
- The reversal convinced many Muslims that the British would give way to Hindu pressure, strengthening the case for their own political organisation. **[7]**

**7.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Muslims had lost confidence in the British after the reversal of the Partition of Bengal in 1911.
- The British refused to give Aligarh full university status on the terms Muslims wanted, and the Kanpur mosque incident of 1913 caused anger.
- Britain was at war with Turkey, the seat of the Caliphate, during the First World War, which upset Muslim opinion.
- The League had changed its aim in 1913 to self-government suitable for India, bringing it closer to Congress.
- Jinnah, a member of both parties, worked for Hindu–Muslim unity and helped to bring them together.
- Both parties believed a joint demand would put more pressure on the British for constitutional reform during the war.
- Congress accepted separate electorates and one-third of the seats in the central legislature for Muslims, which made the agreement attractive to the League. **[7]**

**8.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The League had only recently been reorganised by Jinnah and had a weak organisation in the provinces.
- It had little money and few party workers to campaign among ordinary voters.
- In the Muslim-majority provinces, regional parties were stronger: the Unionist Party in the Punjab and the Krishak Praja Party in Bengal.
- The League had not yet built a mass following; it was seen as a party of the educated and the landed elite.
- Congress was well organised and well funded, with a mass membership built up over many years. **[7]**

**9.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Gandhi said he was speaking only as an individual, not for Congress, so any agreement might not have been accepted by Congress.
- Gandhi's proposals, based on the Rajagopalachari formula, offered only a vote on separation in Muslim-majority areas after the British had left; Jinnah wanted Pakistan agreed before independence.
- Gandhi wanted defence, foreign affairs and communications to be shared by a central authority, whereas Jinnah wanted a fully sovereign Pakistan.
- Gandhi did not accept the Two-Nation Theory, regarding Indian Muslims as part of one nation.
- Jinnah wanted the whole of the Muslim-majority provinces, while the proposals would only have included districts with Muslim majorities, leaving out parts of Punjab and Bengal. **[7]**

**10.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- Lord Wavell proposed a new Executive Council made up of Indians, except the Viceroy and the Commander-in-Chief, with equal numbers of caste Hindus and Muslims.
- Jinnah insisted that the League alone should nominate all the Muslim members, as it was the only party representing Muslims.
- Congress claimed to represent all Indians and wanted to nominate a Muslim member itself; its president at the time, Maulana Azad, was a Muslim.
- The Unionist Party of the Punjab also wanted to nominate a Muslim member.
- Jinnah feared that Muslims would be outnumbered in the Council if non-League Muslims and other minorities sided with Congress.
- Wavell could not find a compromise and ended the conference, which led to fresh elections to test who represented the Muslims. **[7]**

**11.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* it failed in its aims: the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) divided the Ottoman lands, and the delegation to London led by Muhammad Ali Jauhar was rejected; the Hijrat of 1920 left many migrants ruined; the Moplah uprising of 1921 and the violence at Chauri Chaura in 1922, after which Gandhi called off the Non-Cooperation Movement, damaged the campaign; Mustafa Kemal abolished the Caliphate in 1924, ending the cause; Hindu–Muslim relations worsened afterwards, with the Shuddhi and Sangathan movements and communal riots.
- *Disagree:* it was the first mass movement of Indian Muslims and gave them political experience; leaders such as the Ali brothers became prominent; it showed that Muslims could organise across the subcontinent; it brought a period of Hindu–Muslim cooperation; its failure and the actions of Hindu organisations afterwards convinced many Muslims that they needed to protect their own interests separately.
- *Judgement:* for example, it failed in its own aims, but it raised Muslim political awareness that later helped the Pakistan Movement. **[14]**

**12.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Success:* provincial autonomy gave elected ministers control of most government in the provinces, so Muslims could govern where they were the majority; separate electorates were kept; Sindh became a separate province in 1936, adding a Muslim-majority province; the number of voters increased.
- *Not a success:* governors kept special powers to overrule ministers; the federal part of the Act, which would have joined British India and the princely states, gave Hindus a strong majority at the centre, and Muslims feared it; the federation never came into force; Jinnah and the League criticised the Act; Congress won the 1937 elections held under it and formed governments in most provinces, leading to Congress rule that many Muslims resented.
- *Judgement:* for example, the Act gave Muslims real power in their own provinces, but it did not protect them at the centre or in the Hindu-majority provinces. **[14]**

**13.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* Congress refused to share power with the League; policies such as singing *Bande Mataram*, the Wardha education scheme and flying the Congress flag on public buildings were seen as attacks on Muslim culture; the League's Pirpur Report recorded Muslim complaints; Muslims turned to the League in growing numbers, and Jinnah called for a Day of Deliverance when the Congress ministries resigned in 1939.
- *Disagree, other developments:* the Nehru Report (1928) showed Congress would not accept Muslim demands, and Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929) set out the Muslim position; Allama Iqbal's Allahabad address (1930) proposed a separate Muslim state in the north-west; Rahmat Ali's pamphlet of 1933 gave the idea the name Pakistan; the Round Table Conferences and the Communal Award kept separate electorates; the 1935 Act created new Muslim-majority provinces.
- *Judgement:* for example, the ideas came from Iqbal and Rahmat Ali, but Congress rule turned them into a cause that ordinary Muslims supported. **[14]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing the Simla Deputation (1906) with the Simla Conference (1945) in Question 10.
- Mixing up the Morley–Minto Reforms (1909) with the Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms (1919).
- In Question 4, forgetting that the Delhi Proposals offered to give up separate electorates: that was what made them significant.
- In Question 9, describing the talks without explaining why each disagreement prevented a settlement.
- In Question 13, writing only about Congress rule: the question asks you to compare it with other developments.
