These questions cover section 3.12.2, Mass and energy, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), which is International A-level only content. Learn the content first with the [mass and energy study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-mass-and-energy/) and the [mass and energy revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-mass-and-energy-revision-notes/). Use: proton mass 1.007276 u, neutron mass 1.008665 u, electron mass 0.000549 u, 1 u = 931.5 MeV = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the binding energy of a nucleus. **[2]**

**2.** Convert a mass of 0.0250 u into energy in MeV and in joules. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the mass, in kg, that is equivalent to 1.0 MeV. **[2]**

**4.** E = mc² applies to chemical reactions as well as nuclear ones. Explain why mass changes are not noticed in chemical reactions. **[2]**

**5.** Nucleus X has binding energy 492 MeV and nucleon number 56. Nucleus Y has a binding energy per nucleon of 7.6 MeV. Calculate the binding energy per nucleon of X and state which nucleus is more stable. **[2]**

**6.** Describe the regions of the graph of binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number in which energy can be released by fission and by fusion. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** The atomic mass of lithium-7 (3 protons, 4 neutrons) is 7.016004 u.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of the lithium-7 nucleus. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass defect of the nucleus. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the binding energy and the binding energy per nucleon, in MeV. **[2]**

**8.** Radium-226 decays by alpha emission: ²²⁶Ra → ²²²Rn + ⁴He. Atomic masses: radium-226 226.025410 u, radon-222 222.017578 u, helium-4 4.002603 u.

**(a)** Explain why atomic masses can be used in this calculation without correction. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the energy released in the decay, in MeV. **[2]**

**(c)** The radium nucleus is at rest before the decay. Calculate the kinetic energy of the alpha particle. **[2]**

**9.** A copper block of mass 2.0 kg (specific heat capacity 385 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) is heated through 500 K.

**(a)** Calculate the increase in the mass of the block. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the fractional increase in its mass. **[1]**

**(c)** In D–T fusion the mass decreases by 0.018883 u from a starting mass of 5.030 u. Compare the fractional mass change with your answer to (b), and explain why mass changes are measurable in nuclear reactions. **[2]**

**10.** In D–T fusion, ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n. Binding energies: hydrogen-2 2.22 MeV, hydrogen-3 8.48 MeV, helium-4 28.30 MeV.

**(a)** State why the neutron is given zero binding energy. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the energy released. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the binding energy per nucleon of each of the three nuclei. **[2]**

**(d)** Use your answers to (c) to explain why energy is released. **[1]**

**11.** A uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two fragments with nucleon numbers 95 and 139, releasing two neutrons. From the binding energy curve, the binding energy per nucleon is 7.6 MeV for the uranium-236 formed, 8.6 MeV for the fragment of A = 95 and 8.4 MeV for the fragment of A = 139.

**(a)** Estimate the energy released in this fission. **[3]**

**(b)** Estimate the energy released by the complete fission of 1.0 kg of uranium-235 in this way. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the answer to (a) is only an estimate. **[1]**

**12.** A free neutron decays: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄.

**(a)** Calculate the energy released, taking the antineutrino mass as negligible. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why a free proton cannot decay into a neutron in a similar way. **[1]**

**(c)** State where the energy released appears. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The minimum energy needed to separate a nucleus into its individual (free) protons and neutrons [1]; it is the energy equivalent of the mass defect [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "Minimum" and "separate into nucleons" both matter; "energy holding the nucleus together" is too vague.

**2.** 0.0250 × 931.5 = 23.3 MeV [1]; 23.3 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 3.73 × 10⁻¹² J [1] **[2]**

**3.** E = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J [1]; m = E/c² = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³/(3.00 × 10⁸)² = 1.8 × 10⁻³⁰ kg [1] **[2]**

**4.** The energy released per reaction is very small, a few eV rather than MeV [1], so the mass change is a tiny fraction of the total mass and far too small to measure [1] **[2]**

**5.** X: 492/56 = 8.8 MeV per nucleon [1]; X is more stable because it has the higher binding energy per nucleon [1] **[2]**

**6.** Fusion: light nuclei on the steep rising part of the curve, nucleon numbers below the peak near 56 [1]; fission: heavy nuclei beyond the peak, on the slowly falling part [1] **[2]**

**7. (a)** 7.016004 − 3 × 0.000549 = 7.014357 u [1]

**(b)** Free nucleons = 3 × 1.007276 + 4 × 1.008665 = 7.056488 u [1]; mass defect = 7.056488 − 7.014357 = 0.042131 u [1]

**(c)** Binding energy = 0.042131 × 931.5 = 39.2 MeV [1]; per nucleon = 39.2/7 = 5.61 MeV [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Without the electron correction the binding energy comes out 1.5 MeV too low.

**8. (a)** There are 88 electrons on each side (86 in radon plus 2 in helium), so the electron masses cancel [1]

**(b)** Mass difference = 226.025410 − (222.017578 + 4.002603) = 0.005229 u [1]; energy = 0.005229 × 931.5 = 4.87 MeV [1]

**(c)** Momentum is conserved, so kinetic energy is shared in inverse proportion to mass; the alpha particle takes 222/226 of the energy [1]; 4.87 × 222/226 = 4.78 MeV [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Giving the alpha particle all 4.87 MeV ignores the recoil of the daughter nucleus.

**9. (a)** ΔE = mcΔθ = 2.0 × 385 × 500 = 3.85 × 10⁵ J [1]; Δm = 3.85 × 10⁵/(3.00 × 10⁸)² = 4.3 × 10⁻¹² kg [1]

**(b)** 4.3 × 10⁻¹²/2.0 = 2.1 × 10⁻¹² [1]

**(c)** Fusion: 0.018883/5.030 = 3.8 × 10⁻³, about 10⁹ times larger than in (b) [1]; nuclear energy changes per particle are millions of times larger than chemical or thermal ones, so the mass change is a measurable fraction of the total [1] **[5]**

**10. (a)** It is a single free nucleon, so there is nothing to separate it from [1]

**(b)** Energy released = 28.30 − (2.22 + 8.48) [1] = 17.6 MeV [1]

**(c)** Hydrogen-2: 2.22/2 = 1.11 MeV; hydrogen-3: 8.48/3 = 2.83 MeV [1]; helium-4: 28.30/4 = 7.08 MeV [1]

**(d)** The product has a much higher binding energy per nucleon, so the nucleons are more tightly bound, the total mass falls and the difference is released as energy [1] **[6]**

**11. (a)** Binding energy after = 95 × 8.6 + 139 × 8.4 = 817 + 1168 = 1985 MeV [1]; before = 236 × 7.6 = 1794 MeV [1]; energy released ≈ 1.9 × 10² MeV [1]

**(b)** Energy per fission = 191 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 3.06 × 10⁻¹¹ J; nuclei in 1.0 kg = 1.0/(235 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷) = 2.56 × 10²⁴ [1]; energy = 7.8 × 10¹³ J [1]

**(c)** Values read from a graph are only accurate to about two significant figures, and the difference between two large totals magnifies that uncertainty [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* The free neutrons contribute no binding energy, so they do not appear in either total.

**12. (a)** Mass difference = 1.008665 − (1.007276 + 0.000549) = 0.000840 u [1]; energy = 0.000840 × 931.5 = 0.782 MeV [1]

**(b)** The proton has less mass than a neutron, so the decay would need energy to be supplied rather than release it [1]

**(c)** As kinetic energy of the decay products, shared between the electron, the antineutrino and the recoiling proton [1] **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Subtracting masses the wrong way round.
- Forgetting the electron correction when finding binding energy from atomic masses.
- Rounding masses before taking a difference.
- Giving all the decay energy to the alpha particle.
- Describing binding energy as energy released when a nucleus is split.
