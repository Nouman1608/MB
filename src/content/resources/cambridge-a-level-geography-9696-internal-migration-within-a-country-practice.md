Revise first with the [5.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-internal-migration-within-a-country/) and the [5.2 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-internal-migration-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "counterurbanisation". **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by stepped migration? **[2]**

**3.** Explain why rural-urban migration may continue even when unemployment in the city is high. **[3]**

**4.** Outline **two** causes of intra-urban movement in a city in a high income country. **[4]**

**5.** The table shows the age structure of the fictional village of Korvi and of Meran, the city that receives most of its migrants.

| Age group | 0-14 | 15-29 | 30-44 | 45-64 | 65+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Korvi (% of population) | 30 | 14 | 12 | 24 | 20 |
| Meran (% of population) | 24 | 34 | 22 | 14 | 6 |

**(a)** Calculate the dependency ratio for Korvi and for Meran, using (0-14 + 65+) ÷ (15-64) × 100. Show your working. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest how migration between Korvi and Meran helps to explain the differences in their age structures. **[4]**

**6.** In many cities in low and middle income countries, new arrivals first rent rooms near the centre and later move to build homes on the edge of the city. Explain why this sequence of intra-urban moves happens. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **two** ways in which rural-urban migrants keep links with their source areas. **[4]**

## Section B

**8.** Explain the impacts of rural-urban migration on destination areas. **[6]**

**9.** Suggest why stepped migration may be becoming less common in some countries. **[4]**

**10.** Compare the causes of rural-urban migration in low income countries with the causes of urban-rural migration in high income countries. **[6]**

**11.** "The impacts of rural-urban migration on source areas are mainly negative." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** The net movement of people from large urban areas to smaller towns, villages and rural areas [1], so that the population moves down the urban hierarchy and rural areas grow [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Migration up the settlement hierarchy in stages [1], for example from a village to a small town, then to a regional city and finally to the capital [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Migrants compare expected urban income with rural income [1]. Expected income is the urban wage multiplied by the probability of finding work [1]. If urban wages are much higher, the expected gain stays above rural income even when the chance of a job is low, so migration continues; credit reference to the informal sector providing some income [1]. **[3]**

**4.** A change in life-cycle stage [1], such as a couple with children moving to the suburbs for a larger home, a garden and schools [1]. Gentrification [1], where rising rents and house prices displace lower-income tenants from inner areas [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit a change in income, or urban renewal and clearance that rehouses residents, each developed, in place of either cause above.

**5. (a)** Korvi: (30 + 20) ÷ (14 + 12 + 24) × 100 = 50 ÷ 50 × 100 = 100 [1]. Meran: (24 + 6) ÷ (34 + 22 + 14) × 100 = 30 ÷ 70 × 100 = 42.9 [1]. Correct method shown for both [1].

**(b)** Young adults leave Korvi for jobs or education in Meran, so Korvi has only 14% aged 15-29 against 34% in Meran [1]. Korvi's population is therefore older, with 20% aged 65+ [1]. Meran's large 0-14 share may partly reflect migrants having children after arrival [1]. Korvi's high 0-14 share may reflect children left with grandparents, or higher rural fertility, giving a dependency ratio of 100 compared with 42.9 [1]. **[7]**

**6.** New arrivals have little money and need to be close to work, often in the informal sector near the centre [1], so they rent cheap, crowded rooms in inner areas (bridgeheaders) [1]. Once they have savings, a steadier income and contacts, they value owning a home more than closeness to work [1]. Land on the periphery is cheaper or can be squatted, so they move out and gradually build and improve their own housing (consolidators) [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Remittances [1]: money sent home pays for school fees, housing or farm inputs [1]. Circular migration [1]: many migrants move back and forth between city and village as work allows, keeping family ties and land rights [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit return visits at festivals or harvest, or helping relatives follow through chain migration, each developed, in place of either way above.

**8.** A large supply of young labour supports manufacturing, construction and services, helping economic growth [1]. Many migrants enter the informal sector, with low, irregular pay [1]. Demand for housing exceeds supply, so informal settlements grow, often on hazardous land [1]. Water, sanitation, schools and transport are overloaded [1]. The city's population becomes younger, with a bulge at 20-39 and later at 0-9 [1]. Cultural diversity increases, but tension between groups can develop [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Better roads and cheaper bus travel make direct journeys to the largest city easier [1]. Mobile phones and social media give migrants information about the capital before they leave [1]. Chain migration networks offer housing and jobs in the largest city, so intermediate steps are not needed [1]. Jobs and investment may be concentrated in the primate city, so smaller towns offer fewer opportunities [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Similarity: both are driven by a mix of economic and quality-of-life factors [1]. Difference: rural-urban migration in LICs is mainly driven by rural poverty and land shortage [1] and the pull of urban wage jobs and services [1]. Urban-rural migration in HICs is mainly driven by a desire for space, cheaper or larger housing and a perceived better environment [1], made possible by car ownership and remote working, so movers usually keep urban jobs [1]. Rural-urban migrants are mostly young adults, while urban-rural migrants are often families and retirees [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* a comparison needs linking words such as "whereas" and "both"; two separate paragraphs cap the answer.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* source areas are the rural districts that migrants leave [1]; impacts can be economic, demographic, social and environmental [1].

*Negative impacts:* loss of young adults leaves an ageing population and a higher dependency ratio [1]. Labour shortages at planting and harvest can reduce farm output [1]. Schools and shops lose users and close, which encourages further out-migration [1]. Families are split, and children may be left with grandparents [1].

*Positive impacts:* remittances raise household incomes and pay for education and health care [1]. Pressure on land is reduced, so remaining farmers may have larger plots [1]. Returning migrants bring savings, skills and ideas [1]. Remittances may fund new houses, wells or small businesses [1].

*Variation:* impacts depend on whether migrants are men or women, and whether they return [1]. Households with migrants gain more than those without, widening inequality within the village [1].

*Use of examples:* a named example of rural-urban migration, such as movement from Bihar to Indian cities, with specific detail [1].

*Judgement:* in the short term remittances may outweigh the costs, but in the long term the loss of young people can make decline self-reinforcing [1]. A reasoned final view on "mainly" [1]. **[15]**

*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers distinguish between groups and time scales instead of giving one verdict for the whole village.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not saying whether an impact is on the source or the destination.
- Calculating the dependency ratio with the working-age group as the numerator.
- Describing age data without quoting percentages.
- Treating counterurbanisation as any movement into the countryside, rather than a net shift down the urban hierarchy.
- Comparisons written as two separate descriptions.
- Essays that list impacts without weighing positive against negative.
