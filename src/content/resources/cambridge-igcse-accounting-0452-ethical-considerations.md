All people, businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars.

## The need for an ethical framework

An ethical framework is an agreed set of principles that guides how accountants behave. Accounting needs one for four reasons.

1. **Users rely on figures they cannot check.** A bank, an investor or a tax authority usually sees only the finished statements. It has to trust that they are honest.
2. **Accountants face pressure.** Owners, managers or clients may want profit to look higher (to borrow) or lower (to pay less tax). A framework gives the accountant a standard to point to.
3. **Accounting needs judgement.** Estimates such as depreciation rates, inventory values and allowances for irrecoverable debts can be bent in either direction. Principles guide those judgements.
4. **Accountants handle private information** about pay, customers, suppliers and plans, which could be misused.

Because the principles are set in advance, they apply whoever is asking and however senior they are.

## The five fundamental principles in depth

| Principle | What it requires | Typical threat | Example of a breach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Integrity | Being straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships | Being asked to sign or prepare figures known to be false | Recording a sale that never happened |
| Objectivity | Not allowing bias, conflict of interest or undue influence to override judgement | Personal gain, family links, pressure from a powerful person | Choosing a low allowance for irrecoverable debts to earn a profit-based bonus |
| Professional competence and due care | Having the knowledge and skill needed, keeping it up to date, and working carefully and on time | Taking on work beyond your training; rushing | Preparing a partnership appropriation account without knowing how, and not checking it |
| Confidentiality | Not disclosing information gained through work without proper authority, and not using it for personal advantage | Friends, rivals or a new employer asking for information | Telling a friend which customers owe the business money |
| Professional behaviour | Complying with laws and regulations and avoiding anything that discredits the profession | Being asked to break the law or mislead | Helping an owner hide cash sales from the tax authority |

Points examiners reward:

- **Integrity is wider than not lying.** Producing figures that are technically true but deliberately misleading, or leaving out information so the picture is false, also breaches integrity.
- **Objectivity is about influence, not honesty.** An accountant can believe a figure is honest yet still have judged it while influenced by a bonus or a relative.
- **Competence has two parts.** Knowing how to do the work, and taking care when doing it. A skilled accountant who rushes and makes mistakes breaches due care.
- **Confidentiality has limits.** Information may be disclosed with proper authority, such as the client's permission or where the law requires it. It also covers *using* information, for example buying a client's rival's goods because of what you learned.
- **Professional behaviour links to the law and reputation.** Even legal behaviour can breach it if it would discredit the profession, such as exaggerating your qualifications.

## A method for scenario questions

1. **Identify the facts** that create a problem: who is asking, for what, and why.
2. **Name the principle(s)** threatened, choosing the one that fits each fact.
3. **Explain the consequence** for the business, its stakeholders or society, using any figures given.
4. **Recommend an action**: refuse, explain the correct treatment, ask for help or training, or remove yourself from the decision.

### Worked example: the damaged inventory

Rafiq is the book-keeper at Ellwood Builders' Merchants. The owner, Mr Ellwood, is selling the business. Closing inventory cost $12,000, but goods that cost $6,000 are damaged and will sell for only $3,500. Mr Ellwood tells Rafiq to value all inventory at cost "so the buyer sees a better profit", and promises him a bonus if the sale goes through.

| Step | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Facts | Inventory should be 12,000 − (6,000 − 3,500) = 9,500. Valuing it at 12,000 overstates inventory and profit by 2,500. Rafiq is offered a personal reward. |
| Principles | **Integrity**: he would knowingly produce a misleading profit. **Objectivity**: the bonus is a self-interest threat to his judgement. **Professional behaviour**: misleading a buyer could be unlawful and would discredit the profession. |
| Consequences | The buyer pays for a business whose profit and assets are overstated by 2,500; the buyer's lender relies on false figures; prudence is broken. |
| Action | Rafiq should value the damaged goods at net realisable value, explain the prudence concept to Mr Ellwood, and decline the bonus. |

### Worked example: competence and confidentiality

Mei has just joined Kerrow and Co as a trainee. Her manager asks her to prepare the year-end accounts of a limited company before tomorrow's meeting. She has prepared only sole trader accounts before. Later, her cousin, who works for a rival firm, asks her which clients Kerrow has recently gained.

- Preparing the company accounts alone, untrained and in a rush, threatens **professional competence and due care**. She should tell her manager, ask for supervision or more time, and have the work checked.
- Naming clients to her cousin would breach **confidentiality**, and the family link creates pressure. She should politely refuse.

## Significance to stakeholders and society

Applying an ethical framework matters beyond the individual accountant.

| Stakeholder | Why ethical accounting matters to them |
|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Decisions about investing more, withdrawing or selling are based on true figures |
| Lenders and banks | Loans are made to businesses that can genuinely repay |
| Suppliers | Credit is given on reliable information about liquidity |
| Employees | Pay and job security decisions rest on the business's real position |
| Customers | They can rely on the business surviving to supply and support them |
| Tax authorities | The correct tax is collected |
| Buyers of a business | They pay a fair price |

For **society**, honest accounting:

- keeps **trust** in financial information, so people are willing to lend and invest
- directs money towards businesses that really perform well
- protects **tax revenue** that pays for public services
- reduces sudden business failures caused by hidden problems, which cost jobs and harm suppliers

When the framework is ignored, the damage often reaches people who never saw the accounts: employees who lose jobs and suppliers who are never paid.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a principle with no link to the facts.
- Giving one explanation for two principles. Each needs its own reason.
- Confusing integrity (honesty) with objectivity (freedom from influence).
- Saying confidentiality means information can never be shared; it can be shared with proper authority.
- Answering "significance to society" with effects on the owner only.
- Recommending no action. A scenario answer is stronger with a sensible response.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** a principle: one name per situation.
- **Explain** why a principle applies: the principle, what it requires, and the fact in the case that threatens it.
- **Advise** or **Suggest** what the accountant should do: a clear action plus the reason.
- **Discuss** the significance of the framework: effects on several stakeholders, then society, with a conclusion.
