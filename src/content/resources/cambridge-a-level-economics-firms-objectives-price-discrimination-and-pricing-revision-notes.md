## 7.8.1 and 7.8.2 Objectives at a glance

| Objective | Rule or aim | Output compared with profit maximum | Typical reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit maximisation | MR = MC (MC cutting MR from below) | Benchmark | Owners run the firm |
| Survival | Cover AVC now, all costs later | Often higher output, lower price | New firm, price war, recession |
| Profit satisficing | A target profit, not the maximum | Usually lower | Managers differ from owners |
| Sales maximisation | Highest output with AR = AC (no loss) | Higher, with lower price | Market share, economies of scale |
| Revenue maximisation | MR = 0, so PED = 1 | Higher, with lower price | Managers' pay or status tied to turnover |

Key points to remember.
- At the profit maximum MR = MC, not MR = 0 and not AR = AC.
- Revenue maximisation always sits at a higher output than profit maximisation when MC is positive, because MR is zero there but MC is above zero.
- Sales maximisation is limited by the break-even condition, so profit is normal (zero economic profit), not negative.
- Satisficing and the principal-agent problem go together: owners and managers want different things.

### Worked reminder: three objectives

A firm faces P = 100 - 4Q and has TC = 200 + 24Q (illustrative, mu per week).
1. MR = 100 - 8Q. MC = 24. Profit maximum: 100 - 8Q = 24, Q = 9.5, P = 62, TR = 589, TC = 428, profit 161.
2. Revenue maximum: MR = 0, Q = 12.5, P = 50, TR = 625, TC = 500, profit 125.
3. Profit given up for the extra revenue = 161 - 125 = 36.

## 7.8.3 Price discrimination

| Degree | Basis for the price | Example form | Consumer surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Each customer's own maximum willingness to pay | Individual negotiation | Taken completely |
| Second | Quantity bought or option chosen | Block tariffs, bulk discounts | Partly taken |
| Third | Group with different PED | Peak and off-peak, student and adult | Reduced for the high-price group |

**Conditions (all three needed):** price-setting power; ability to separate customers or markets and stop resale; different PEDs.

**Method for third degree**
1. Write each market's demand and its MR.
2. Set MR = MC separately in each market to find Q and P.
3. Check the higher price is in the less elastic market (PED = slope inverse × P/Q).
4. Add profits. Compare with the single-price case.

**Consequences, in two columns**

| For the firm and some consumers | Against |
|---|---|
| More profit, which may fund investment | Group charged more loses surplus |
| Output can rise when low-price groups are served | Costs of separating markets and policing resale |
| Cross-subsidy can keep a service running | Seen as unfair; may draw regulatory attention |
| Often lower price for price-sensitive groups | With linear demand output may not change, so welfare can fall |

### Worked reminder: block pricing

First 20 units at 5 mu and every unit above 20 at 3 mu. A customer buying 35 pays 20 × 5 + 15 × 3 = 100 + 45 = 145 mu. Average price = 145/35 = 4.14 mu. This is second degree, because the price depends on quantity.

### Worked reminder: third degree

Market 1 has P = 50 - Q and market 2 has P = 30 - Q. MC is 10 mu.
1. Market 1: MR = 50 - 2Q = 10, so Q = 20, P = 30. Market 2: MR = 30 - 2Q = 10, so Q = 10, P = 20.
2. PED = P/Q here (slope 1): market 1 = 30/20 = 1.5 and market 2 = 20/10 = 2.0. The less elastic market 1 pays more.
3. Profit = 20 × 20 + 10 × 10 = 400 + 100 = 500 mu.
4. A single price: total demand Q = 80 - 2P, so P = 40 - Q/2 and MR = 40 - Q = 10, giving Q = 30 and P = 25. Profit = 30 × 15 = 450 mu. Discrimination adds 50 mu.

## 7.8.4 Other pricing policies

| Policy | What the firm does | Needs | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limit pricing | Sets price just below a potential entrant's lowest average cost | Cost advantage, credible threat | Gives up profit now; entry may still happen |
| Predatory pricing | Prices below cost to force a rival out, then raises price | Deep pockets, barriers to re-entry | Losses; legal challenge |
| Price leadership | One firm moves price, others follow | Few firms, similar products | Tacit collusion concern; followers may defect |

**Method for deciding if limit pricing is worth it:** compare profit at the limit price with the profit the firm expects if entry happens. If the first is larger, deterrence pays.

### Worked reminder: limit pricing

Demand is P = 60 - Q and the incumbent's MC = AC = 10 mu. Monopoly: MR = 60 - 2Q = 10, so Q = 25, P = 35 and profit = 25 × 25 = 625 mu. A rival's lowest AC is 25 mu, so the incumbent sets 24 mu: Q = 36 and profit = 14 × 36 = 504 mu. Deterrence costs 121 mu a period and is worthwhile only if entry would cost the incumbent more.

**Types of price leader:** dominant firm (largest) and barometric (best at reading market conditions).

## 7.8.5 PED and revenue

| PED | Price rise | Price fall | MR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Above 1 (elastic) | TR falls | TR rises | Positive |
| Equal to 1 | No change | No change | Zero |
| Below 1 (inelastic) | TR rises | TR falls | Negative |

**Straight-line demand:** the upper half is elastic, PED = 1 at the midpoint and the lower half is inelastic. TR peaks at the midpoint.

**Kinked demand (oligopoly)**
- Upper section: elastic, because rivals do not follow a price rise.
- Lower section: inelastic, because rivals follow a price cut.
- Either price change lowers TR, so price is stable.
- MR is discontinuous: a vertical gap at the kink. MC can shift within the gap with no change in price or output.
- Weakness: it does not explain how the price was reached; firms do change prices.

### Worked reminder: PED and revenue

Price falls from 80 to 72 (a 10% fall) and quantity rises from 500 to 560 (a 12% rise). PED = 12/10 = 1.2, so demand is elastic. TR rises from 80 × 500 = 40,000 to 72 × 560 = 40,320.

## Must-know distinctions

- Sales maximisation (most units without loss) versus revenue maximisation (highest TR).
- Satisficing (a target) versus survival (staying in business).
- First degree (each person) versus second degree (quantity) versus third degree (group).
- Limit pricing (stays above cost, deters) versus predatory pricing (below cost, eliminates).
- Price leadership (open following) versus a cartel (formal agreement).

## Quick self-test

1. State the rule for profit maximisation.
2. A firm sells 50 units for TR 500 at TC 420. Selling 51 gives TR 508 and TC 429. Should it produce the 51st?
3. Which objective maximises output subject to no loss?
4. At what value of PED is TR at a maximum on a straight-line demand curve?
5. Price rises 5% and quantity falls 8%. Find PED and say what happens to TR.
6. Name the three conditions for effective price discrimination.
7. A customer buys 30 units on a tariff of 4 mu for each of the first 10 units and 2 mu for the rest. What is the total paid?
8. Is demand elastic or inelastic above the kink?
9. What is predatory pricing?
10. Name two types of price leader.

### Answers

1. Produce where MR = MC, with MC cutting MR from below.
2. MR = 508 - 500 = 8 and MC = 429 - 420 = 9. MR is below MC, so no.
3. Sales maximisation, at AR = AC.
4. PED = 1 (MR = 0).
5. PED = 8/5 = 1.6, elastic. TR falls.
6. Price-setting power; ability to separate the markets and stop resale; different PEDs.
7. 10 × 4 + 20 × 2 = 80 mu.
8. Elastic.
9. Pricing below cost to force out a rival or deter entry, then raising price later.
10. Dominant firm and barometric.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "profit is highest where revenue is highest".
- Naming the three degrees but not saying what differs (the basis of the price).
- Giving conditions for price discrimination without the idea of preventing resale.
- Stating that the higher price goes to the more elastic market.
- Describing limit pricing as pricing below cost.
- Giving a list of consequences with no judgement about who gains, who loses and by how much.
- Drawing kinked demand with no explanation of why rivals respond differently to rises and cuts.
- Leaving out the formula or working in a PED and revenue calculation.
