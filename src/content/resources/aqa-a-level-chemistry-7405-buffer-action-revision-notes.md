These notes condense section **3.1.12.6 Buffer action** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 3. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Buffer action study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-buffer-action/), and exam-style questions are in the [Buffer action practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-buffer-action-practice/). The wider topic is in the [Acids and bases revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases-revision-notes/).

## What you must be able to do

- Define a **buffer** and name the components of **acidic** and **basic** buffers.
- Explain **qualitatively** how acidic and basic buffers work.
- **Calculate the pH** of an acidic buffer.
- Give **applications** of buffers.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Buffer solution | Keeps pH approximately constant on dilution or addition of small amounts of acid or base |
| Acidic buffer | Weak acid plus a salt of that weak acid |
| Basic buffer | Weak base plus a salt of that weak base |
| Conjugate base | The anion of the salt; removes added H⁺ |
| Buffer capacity | How much acid or alkali a buffer can absorb before the pH changes sharply |

## Components

| Type | Example | Species that remove H⁺ | Species that remove OH⁻ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acidic | CH₃COOH and CH₃COONa | CH₃COO⁻ | CH₃COOH |
| Basic | NH₃ and NH₄Cl | NH₃ | NH₄⁺ |

The salt is fully dissociated; the weak acid or base is only slightly dissociated. So both members of the conjugate pair are present in large amounts.

## How an acidic buffer works

```
CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺
```

- **Add H⁺:** it reacts with CH₃COO⁻ to form CH₃COOH. The equilibrium shifts left. [H⁺] rises very little.
- **Add OH⁻:** it reacts with H⁺ (and with CH₃COOH): CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O. More acid dissociates. [H⁺] falls very little.
- **Dilute:** both concentrations fall by the same factor, so the ratio and the pH are almost unchanged.

Key point: pH depends on the **ratio** [HA] ÷ [A⁻], which changes only slightly.

## How a basic buffer works

```
NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
```

- **Add H⁺:** NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ (removed by NH₃).
- **Add OH⁻:** NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O (removed by NH₄⁺).

## pH calculation (acidic buffers)

```
Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]     so     [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] / [A⁻]     pH = –log[H⁺]
```

Approximations: [HA] = concentration of acid added; [A⁻] = concentration of salt added.

| [HA] : [A⁻] | pH |
|---|---|
| 1 : 1 | pH = pKa |
| 10 : 1 | pH = pKa – 1 |
| 1 : 10 | pH = pKa + 1 |

Examples (Ka of ethanoic acid = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³):

```
0.100 mol dm⁻³ acid, 0.100 mol dm⁻³ salt:  [H⁺] = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵, pH = 4.76
0.200 mol dm⁻³ acid, 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ salt: [H⁺] = 7.00 × 10⁻⁵, pH = 4.15
```

## Making a buffer by partial neutralisation

Mix excess weak acid with a smaller amount of strong base.

```
n(HA) left = n(HA) initial – n(OH⁻)
n(A⁻)      = n(OH⁻)
[H⁺] = Ka × n(HA) left ÷ n(A⁻)       (volume cancels)
```

Example: 50.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ CH₃COOH with 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.
n(CH₃COOH) = 1.00 × 10⁻² mol, n(NaOH) = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol. Left: 7.50 × 10⁻³ mol acid, 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol salt.
[H⁺] = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ × 7.50 ÷ 2.50 = 5.25 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³; pH = 4.28.

## Adding acid or alkali to a buffer

Buffer with 0.200 mol each of CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻ (pH 4.76).

| Addition | New amounts | New pH |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0100 mol HCl | acid 0.210 mol, salt 0.190 mol | 4.71 |
| 0.0100 mol NaOH | acid 0.190 mol, salt 0.210 mol | 4.80 |

Added H⁺ converts salt into acid; added OH⁻ converts acid into salt. Compare: 0.0100 mol of HCl in 1.00 dm³ of water gives pH 2.00.

## Choosing a buffer

- Choose an acid with pKa close to the required pH.
- Adjust the [HA] ÷ [A⁻] ratio to set the exact value.
- More concentrated components give a larger capacity.

## Applications

- **Blood:** H₂CO₃ and HCO₃⁻ keep the pH at about 7.4.
- **Foods and drinks:** control acidity and limit spoilage.
- **Shampoos and skin products:** keep pH close to that of skin.
- **Laboratory and medical solutions:** enzymes need a stable pH.

## Exam traps

- "Buffers keep pH constant": say approximately constant.
- Forgetting one of the two reactions (with acid or with alkali).
- Not writing the equilibrium.
- Upside-down ratio in [H⁺].
- Adding added alkali to the acid amount instead of subtracting.
- Using strong acid plus its salt as a "buffer".
- Using the volume of one solution rather than the total, when working with concentrations.
- Assuming a buffer is pH 7.

## Self-test

1. Define a buffer solution.
2. Name the components of an acidic buffer.
3. Give the components of a basic buffer.
4. Write an equation for the reaction when H⁺ is added to an ethanoic acid/ethanoate buffer.
5. Write an equation for the reaction when OH⁻ is added to the same buffer.
6. Calculate the pH of a buffer with [CH₃COOH] = [CH₃COO⁻].
7. Why does dilution change the pH of a buffer very little?
8. A buffer has 0.300 mol dm⁻³ acid and 0.100 mol dm⁻³ salt. Is its pH above or below pKa?
9. Give one use of buffers in the body.
10. Why is a solution of HCl and NaCl not a buffer?
11. What happens to a buffer if too much acid is added?
12. What does a buffer with [HA] = 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ and [A⁻] = 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ have as its pH?

### Answers

1. A solution that maintains an approximately constant pH when small amounts of acid or base are added or on dilution.
2. A weak acid and a salt of that weak acid.
3. A weak base and a salt of that weak base (for example NH₃ and NH₄Cl).
4. CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH.
5. CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O.
6. pH = pKa (4.76 for ethanoic acid).
7. The ratio of acid to salt stays the same.
8. Below, because [HA] ÷ [A⁻] = 3 is greater than 1.
9. Blood is kept at about pH 7.4 by H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻.
10. HCl is fully dissociated, so there is no reserve of undissociated acid to release H⁺.
11. The conjugate base is used up and the pH falls sharply.
12. pH = pKa of the acid.
