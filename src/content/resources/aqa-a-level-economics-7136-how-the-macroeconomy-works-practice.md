## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Distinguish between an injection and a withdrawal in the circular flow of income. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why real national income is a better indicator of economic performance than nominal national
income. **[2]**

**3.** The marginal propensity to consume is 0.6. Calculate the multiplier. **[1]**

**4.** Distinguish between saving and investment. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a fall in the price level causes a movement along the aggregate demand curve rather than a shift.
**[2]**

**6.** State **two** factors that would shift the short-run aggregate supply curve to the left. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** In Country D, injections are 300 billion at every level of income. Planned withdrawals are 200 billion when
national income is 800 billion, 250 billion at 1,000 billion and 300 billion at 1,200 billion.

**(a)** State the equilibrium level of national income and explain why. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what would happen to national income if it were 1,000 billion. **[2]**

**8.** Country E's government raises spending on school buildings by 15 billion. The MPC is 0.8.

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier and the final change in national income. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in income in each of the first three rounds of spending. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain **two** reasons why the actual rise in real national income may be smaller than your answer to (a).
**[4]**

**9.** Explain how the accelerator process can cause investment to fall even though consumer demand is still
rising. **[4]**

**10.** Using an AD/AS diagram, explain the effect of a large rise in world energy prices on the price level and real
output of an energy-importing economy. **[4]**

**11.** Using the Keynesian AS curve, explain why the effect of a rise in AD depends on the amount of spare capacity
in the economy. **[6]**

**12.** Country F's main trading partners enter a recession. Evaluate the likely effects on Country F's economy.
**[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An injection is **spending that enters the flow** from outside the household–firm loop: investment,
government spending or exports [1]. A withdrawal is **income that leaves the flow** without being spent on domestic
output: saving, taxation or imports [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Nominal income can rise simply because **prices rise**, with no extra output [1]. Real income is **adjusted
for inflation**, so it shows changes in the volume of goods and services produced [1]. **[2]**

**3.** k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 1 ÷ 0.4 = **2.5** [1].

**4.** Saving is **income not spent on consumption**, mainly by households [1]. Investment is **spending on capital
goods** such as machinery and buildings, mainly by firms [1]. **[2]**

**5.** AD is drawn against the price level, so a change in the price level is shown **on the axis** as a movement
along the curve [1]. A shift happens only when a component of AD changes for some other reason, such as a change
in confidence [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two [2]: higher money wage rates; higher raw material or energy prices; higher business taxes; a fall in
productivity.

**7. (a)** **1,200 billion** [1], because at that level planned withdrawals (300) equal planned injections (300)
[1].

**(b)** At 1,000 billion injections (300) exceed withdrawals (250) [1], so spending exceeds output; firms raise
production and national income **rises** towards 1,200 billion [1].

**8. (a)** k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = **5** [1]; change in income = 15 × 5 = **75 billion** [1].

**(b)** **15, 12 and 9.6 billion** [1].

**(c)** Any two, each explained [2] [2]: if the economy is near **full capacity**, part of the rise in AD raises the
price level instead of real output; the effects take **time** to work through, so the full rise is not reached in
the short run; higher government borrowing may raise **interest rates** and reduce private investment
(crowding out); falling **confidence** may lower the MPC.

**9.** Investment depends on the **rate of change** of demand, not its level [1]. When demand is growing fast, firms
need extra machines each year to keep up, so investment is high [1]. If demand keeps rising but **more slowly**,
fewer extra machines are needed [1], so investment falls even though demand is still higher than before [1].
**[4]**

**10.** Higher energy prices raise firms' **costs of production** [1], shifting **SRAS to the left** [1]. On the
diagram the price level rises from P₁ to P₂ [1] and real output falls from Y₁ to Y₂ [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The Keynesian AS curve is **horizontal** when there is large spare capacity [1]: a rise in AD increases real
output and employment with little effect on prices, because idle resources can be brought into use [1]. In the
**rising section**, bottlenecks appear in some industries [1], so a rise in AD raises both output and the price
level [1]. On the **vertical section** the economy is at full capacity [1], so a rise in AD only raises the price
level [1]. **[6]**

**12.** *Effects:* lower incomes abroad reduce demand for Country F's **exports** [1], so AD shifts left [1]; through
the **multiplier**, national income falls by more than the initial fall in exports [1]; lower demand and confidence
reduce **investment** through the accelerator [1]; **cyclical unemployment** rises and inflationary pressure
falls [1]. *Evaluation:* the size depends on how much of Country F's AD comes from exports to those partners [1];
if the exchange rate falls, exports become cheaper and part of the loss is offset [1]; policy responses such as
lower interest rates or higher government spending can support AD [1]. *Judgement:* the economy is likely to
slow, with the severity depending mainly on its openness and on the policy response [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using 1 ÷ MPC as the multiplier.
- Identifying equilibrium where injections are largest rather than where injections equal withdrawals.
- Calling a rise in energy prices a demand-side shock.
- Shifting AD for a change in the price level.
- Assuming the full multiplier effect happens at once and raises only real output.
- Listing effects of a shock without judging their size.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to calculate the multiplier from withdrawals for AQA?**
No. AQA requires the multiplier to be calculated from the marginal propensity to consume only, using
k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).

**Do I need to calculate the accelerator?**
No. You need to explain the basic accelerator process, but calculations are not required.
