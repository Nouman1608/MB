Condensed recall for **5.4 Managing tourism and sport for the future** (IB DP Geography, Option E: Leisure, tourism and sport). See the [Leisure, tourism and sport study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-leisure-tourism-sport-study-guide/).

## Core concept

**Sustainability:** meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Sustainable tourism balances **economic, social and environmental** goals. It is not only environmental.

| Pillar | What a sustainable destination aims for |
|---|---|
| Economic | Year-round, decently paid local jobs; low leakage; diverse income |
| Social and cultural | Residents involved in decisions; culture respected rather than commodified; housing kept affordable |
| Environmental | Visitor numbers within ecological limits; low waste, water use and emissions |

**Ecotourism** is responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, supports the well-being of local people and includes education. **Greenwashing** is when operators use the label without meeting these standards, which is why certification and enforcement matter.

## Approaches

| Approach | Example |
|---|---|
| Ecotourism, community-based tourism | Keeps income local and protects nature |
| Visitor management | Zoning, quotas, pricing, timed entry, education |
| Planning controls | Limits on development; carrying capacity assessments |
| Certification and codes | Responsible operator standards |
| Sport | Sustainable event design, reuse of existing venues, planned legacy |

**Rejuvenation strategies** for a stagnating destination include diversifying products (events, conferences, heritage, wellness), upgrading accommodation, improving the public realm and targeting new markets. They correspond to the rejuvenation branch of Butler's model.

## Case studies

### Galapagos Islands, Ecuador

The islands are a national park and marine reserve. Visitors pay a park entry fee, most visitor sites can be reached only with licensed naturalist guides, boats follow approved itineraries, and strict rules control the movement of plants and animals between islands to stop invasive species. Evaluation: the controls protect a unique ecosystem and fund conservation, but growing land-based tourism and the population of the inhabited islands have increased pressure.

### Venice, Italy

Venice is a widely cited example of **overtourism**: large numbers of day visitors crowd a small historic centre while the resident population has fallen. Responses include stopping large cruise ships from sailing past the historic centre in 2021 and trialling an entry fee for day-trippers on peak days in 2024. Evaluation: these measures signal intent, but critics argue they do little to tackle short-term rentals that drive residents out, so the Doxey-style antagonism of residents continues.

### Bhutan

Bhutan follows a "high value, low impact" approach, charging international visitors a daily sustainable development fee that funds public services and conservation. Evaluation: it limits mass tourism and protects culture, but it also limits who can afford to visit and how much income tourism brings, and the fee level has been adjusted in response to visitor numbers.

### Community-based conservation in Namibia

Communal conservancies allow rural communities to manage wildlife on their land and earn income from tourism and hunting concessions, giving local people a direct stake in conservation. Evaluation: it is often praised for linking wildlife recovery with rural incomes, but benefits can be uneven within communities and depend on stable visitor numbers.

## Sport and sustainability

Recent Games organisers have tried to reduce cost and waste by relying mainly on existing and temporary venues, as Paris did in 2024, rather than building new stadiums that may be underused. Sustainable event design also includes public transport access, renewable energy and planned after-use of every new facility.

## Stakeholders

Tourists, residents, businesses, governments, conservation bodies, international organisations. They disagree: businesses may want growth while residents want less crowding.

## Evaluating

Criteria: **who benefits**, **long-term viability**, **enforcement**. Link back to leakage and carrying capacity.

## Looking ahead

- Technology and changing travel habits
- Climate impacts on destinations such as ski resorts and beaches
- Overtourism and its effects on residents
- Scenarios: managed, community-led growth versus unmanaged growth that erodes what visitors came to see

**Ski resorts:** warming raises the snowline, so lower-altitude resorts in ranges such as the Alps face shorter, less reliable seasons. Responses include artificial snowmaking (which uses water and energy), moving lifts higher and diversifying into year-round activities such as mountain biking and hiking. **Beach resorts** face sea level rise, coastal erosion and coral bleaching that reduces the attraction of reef destinations.

## Worked check

If capacity is fixed and arrivals rise 25 per cent, the destination may approach its carrying capacity, one sign Butler's consolidation or stagnation stage may be near.

## Model paragraph

*Evaluate the effectiveness of visitor management in a fragile destination.* "In the Galapagos, licensed guides, set itineraries and entry fees have kept visitors on defined paths and funded conservation, so visitor management has protected the most sensitive sites. However, it has not controlled the growth of land-based tourism and the resident population, which increase demand for water, energy and imported goods. Visitor management is therefore effective at site level but must be combined with wider planning controls if a destination is to stay within its carrying capacity."

## Exam traps

- Treating sustainability as only environmental.
- Praising ecotourism without asking about enforcement or leakage.
- Naming schemes without saying who gains.
- Giving visitor-cap numbers for named places from memory.
- Assuming high entry fees are fair: they can exclude lower-income visitors and residents.
- Ignoring that adaptation such as snowmaking can create new environmental costs.
