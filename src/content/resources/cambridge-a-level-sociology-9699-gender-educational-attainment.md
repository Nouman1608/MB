Section 6.4 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks why boys and girls achieve differently. The syllabus lists four strands: the relationship between gender socialisation and educational attainment; wider social changes and gendered educational achievement, including changing female expectations and the crisis of masculinity; gender and subcultures; and teacher expectations and gendered behaviour in the classroom. It sits in topic 6, Education and inequality, and is examined on Paper 3 – Education.

The [ethnicity, gender and educational attainment overview](/resources/a-level-sociology-9699-ethnicity-gender-and-educational-attainment/) introduces Oakley, Francis, Skelton and Francis, Epstein, Willis, Jackson, Archer and colleagues, French and Spender. This page extends each strand with further evidence, sets out the competing feminist interpretations, and shows how to build arguments that go beyond "girls work harder".

## Frame the question: which gap?

"Gender and attainment" covers several different patterns, and answers improve when they specify which one they mean.

- **Overall attainment:** in many countries girls now outperform boys on average in school examinations, a reversal of earlier patterns.
- **Subject choice:** routes still divide by gender, with boys more likely to choose subjects such as physics and computing and girls more likely to choose subjects such as languages and the social sciences.
- **Access:** in some parts of the world, girls remain less likely than boys to complete schooling, so in those societies the gap runs the other way.

Because the overall gap has changed within a generation, explanations based on fixed biological differences struggle. The sociological task is to explain both the change and the persistence of gendered subject choice.

## Gender socialisation and attainment

Socialisation explanations argue that the different ways boys and girls are raised produce different orientations to school.

- Girls are often encouraged towards quieter, more cooperative behaviour and reading, which fits classroom expectations.
- Boys are more often encouraged towards physical activity and competition; where peer cultures define studiousness as unmasculine, effort can look like a threat to identity.

**Diane Reay** (2001), in "Spice Girls, nice girls, girlies and tomboys", studied a primary class and found several distinct femininities among the girls, alongside boys' dominant masculinities. Girls were not one group; some gained status by being "nice", others by rejecting conventional femininity.

**Evaluation.** Socialisation accounts are useful but can be static. They struggle to explain *why* outcomes changed, unless socialisation itself has changed, which points to wider social change.

## Wider social changes and changing female expectations

**Sue Sharpe**'s *Just Like a Girl* is valuable because she studied girls at two points. In the early 1970s (published 1976), working-class girls in London ranked love, marriage, husbands and children as their priorities. When she repeated the research in the early 1990s (revised edition 1994), the priorities had shifted towards jobs, careers and being able to support themselves. The study provides direct evidence of changing expectations.

**Helen Wilkinson**, in *No Turning Back* (1994), described a **"genderquake"**: a fundamental shift in young women's values towards independence and paid work.

Further changes that sociologists link to girls' improvement:

- equal opportunities legislation and policy;
- growth in service-sector and professional jobs open to women;
- rising divorce and later marriage, which make economic independence more important;
- feminist ideas reaching schools, families and the media.

**Evaluation.** Liberal feminists read these changes as real progress. Radical feminists point out that higher attainment has not removed patriarchy: gendered subject routes persist, and women remain under-represented at the top of many occupations.

## The crisis of masculinity

The crisis of masculinity argument links boys' under-achievement to economic change. As traditional manual jobs declined, the breadwinner identity that once required few qualifications lost its foundation. Some boys, especially working-class boys, may see little point in schooling if they cannot picture a future it leads to.

**Máirtín Mac an Ghaill**, in *The Making of Men* (1994), identified different masculinities among boys in one school, including the **macho lads** (anti-school, valuing toughness), the **academic achievers**, the **new enterprisers** (focused on newer vocational routes such as business and technology) and the **real Englishmen** (middle-class boys who appeared effortless). The crisis affected these groups differently; the macho lads matched it most closely.

**Evaluation.** The argument risks becoming a moral panic. Middle-class boys still achieve highly, and the overall improvement in girls' results may matter more than any decline among boys. Feminists worry that concern about "failing boys" diverts resources from girls who still face disadvantage.

## Gender and subcultures

Beyond laddish boys and "ladette" girls, the strand covers how peer groups police gender.

- **Sue Lees**, in *Sugar and Spice* (1993), showed how labels about girls' sexual reputation were used to control girls' behaviour, dress and friendships. Gendered subcultures can therefore limit girls' confidence and participation even when their attainment is high.
- Mac an Ghaill's typology shows boys' subcultures varying by class and aspiration, not a single "boy culture".

**Analytical point.** Subcultures connect structure and agency: they are responses to class and economic prospects (structure) made through choices about identity (agency).

## Teacher expectations and gendered behaviour in the classroom

**Michelle Stanworth**, in *Gender and Schooling* (1983), interviewed A Level students and teachers in a further education college. Teachers found it easier to remember and describe boys; they tended to underestimate girls' abilities, and girls tended to underestimate themselves relative to boys of similar attainment.

Combining Stanworth with evidence of boys receiving more attention and dominating classroom talk gives a paradox to analyse: if classrooms favour boys' visibility, why do girls now achieve more? Possible answers:

- attention may often be **disciplinary**, which does not raise attainment;
- girls' strategies of quiet, consistent work suit assessment, whatever the classroom dynamics;
- expectations may have changed since earlier studies, so dates matter in your evaluation.

## Worked example: Question 2 style

*Explain two ways in which wider social changes may have improved girls' educational achievement.*

> **Way 1: changing expectations.** Sharpe found that girls' priorities shifted between the 1970s and 1990s from marriage and children to careers and independence. If a girl expects a long working life, qualifications become essential, so she has a reason to invest effort in school.
>
> **Way 2: changes in employment and the family.** The growth of service and professional jobs open to women, and the rise in divorce, make economic independence both possible and necessary. Wilkinson's "genderquake" describes young women responding to these conditions with stronger commitment to paid work, which encourages higher attainment.

## Worked essay plan

*"Changes in wider society explain why girls now achieve more than boys." Evaluate this view.*

1. Clarify the pattern: overall attainment, subject choice, global variation.
2. For: Sharpe's repeated study; Wilkinson's genderquake; employment and family change; equal opportunities.
3. The boys' side: crisis of masculinity; Mac an Ghaill's masculinities.
4. Alternative in-school explanations: teacher expectations (Stanworth), classroom behaviour, subcultures, assessment styles.
5. Feminist debate: liberal progress versus radical continuity; Lees on peer control.
6. Evaluate: class and ethnicity cut across gender; moral panic concerns.
7. Judgement: wider change explains *why* the gap reversed; in-school factors explain how it is maintained and varies.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "boys are failing" when most boys' results have also risen.
- Treating the crisis of masculinity as proven.
- Ignoring subject choice, where gendered patterns remain strong.
- Using biological explanations, which are not sociological.

## Exam technique

- In **Explain** questions, state the change, link it to attainment, and support with evidence.
- In **Give** (arguments against), class and ethnicity differences within each gender make a strong counter-argument.
- In **Evaluate** essays, date your evidence: the timing of a study affects what it can show about change.
