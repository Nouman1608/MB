This study guide teaches section **4.2.2.4 Coronary heart disease: a non-communicable disease** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification, for teaching from September 2016 and exams from 2018 onwards. It is Paper 1 content at Foundation and Higher Tier, and nothing in 4.2.2.4 is marked (HT only).

The [coronary heart disease revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-coronary-heart-disease-revision-notes/) tabulate every treatment's advantages and disadvantages. This guide teaches the reasoning behind those tables: why narrowing an artery damages the heart, what a faulty valve does to blood flow, how to choose a treatment for a particular patient, and how to read treatment data. Then use the [coronary heart disease practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-coronary-heart-disease-practice/). Course hub: [AQA GCSE Biology](/boards/aqa/gcse/biology/).

## What the specification asks for

The first sentence of 4.2.2.4 sets the main skill: students should be able to "evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of treating cardiovascular diseases by drugs, mechanical devices or transplant." The rest gives the content:

- fatty material builds up in the coronary arteries, narrowing them, reducing blood flow and causing "a lack of oxygen for the heart muscle";
- stents keep the coronary arteries open; statins reduce blood cholesterol, slowing the deposit of fatty material;
- heart valves may become faulty, not opening fully or leaking, and students "should understand the consequences of faulty valves"; they can be replaced with biological or mechanical valves;
- in heart failure, a donor heart, or heart and lungs, can be transplanted; artificial hearts are "occasionally used to keep patients alive whilst waiting for a heart transplant, or to allow the heart to rest as an aid to recovery."

The skills column names WS 1.3 and WS 1.4: evaluate methods of treatment "bearing in mind the benefits and risks".

## Why a narrowed coronary artery is so serious

Heart muscle contracts continuously, so its cells respire continuously and need a constant supply of oxygen and glucose. That supply comes only through the **coronary arteries**, which branch from the aorta over the surface of the heart. The heart cannot use the blood inside its own chambers for this.

Trace the effect up the levels of organisation:

1. **Artery**: fatty deposits narrow the lumen, so less blood flows through.
2. **Cells**: heart muscle cells beyond the narrowing get less oxygen, so they carry out less aerobic respiration and release less energy.
3. **Tissue**: the muscle tissue contracts less strongly; if the supply is cut off, cells can be damaged or die.
4. **Organ**: the heart pumps less effectively; a sudden blockage can cause a heart attack.
5. **Organism**: the person may have chest pain or breathlessness, especially during exercise, when heart muscle needs more oxygen.

CHD is **non-communicable** because it is not caused by a pathogen and cannot be passed between people. Its risk factors, such as diet, smoking and lack of exercise, are covered in 4.2.2.6.

## The consequences of faulty valves

Valves keep blood flowing one way through the heart. The specification names two faults.

- **A valve that does not open fully.** The opening is narrower, so less blood passes through with each contraction. The chamber behind it must contract harder to push blood through.
- **A valve that leaks.** When the valve should be closed, some blood flows backwards into the chamber it has just left. Less blood moves forward with each beat, so the heart must pump that blood again.

Either way the heart works harder, and less oxygenated blood reaches the body per minute. Cells respire less, so the person may feel tired and short of breath. Faulty valves can be replaced with **biological** valves (from animals or human donors) or **mechanical** (man-made) valves.

## Three kinds of treatment

Group the treatments the way the specification does, then compare them with the same criteria each time.

| Category | Treatments in 4.2.2.4 | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Drugs | Statins | Lower blood cholesterol, so fatty material is deposited more slowly |
| Mechanical devices | Stents, mechanical valves, artificial hearts | Physically hold an artery open, replace a valve, or take over pumping |
| Transplant | Donor heart, or heart and lungs; biological valves | Replace a failing organ or part with living tissue |

**Criteria for comparison**

- **Invasiveness**: tablets, a minor procedure, or major surgery?
- **Speed**: immediate effect or slow?
- **Duration**: does the benefit last, or does it need repeating or continuing for life?
- **Risks and side effects**: infection, clots, rejection, side effects of drugs.
- **Availability**: is it ready now, or does it depend on a donor?

## Choosing a treatment for a particular patient

The best answer always depends on the patient described. Three cases show the reasoning.

- **Patient A has one coronary artery that is badly narrowed and causes chest pain.** A stent restores blood flow straight away through a fairly minor procedure. Statins alone act too slowly to relieve a narrowing that is already there.
- **Patient B has high blood cholesterol but no narrowing yet.** Statins are suitable: no procedure is needed and they slow future deposits. The drug must be taken long term, and side effects are possible.
- **Patient C has heart failure and is on a waiting list for a donor heart.** An artificial heart can keep the patient alive until a donor heart is available. A transplant could then give a long-term solution, but the patient will need drugs to prevent rejection, and donor hearts are in short supply.

## Worked example: reading treatment data

A trial (invented data) followed two groups of 2000 adults with high cholesterol for five years. One group took a statin; the other took a tablet with no drug in it (a placebo).

| Group | People | Heart attacks in five years |
|---|---|---|
| Statin | 2000 | 60 |
| Placebo | 2000 | 90 |

```
Statin group:  60 / 2000 x 100 = 3.0%
Placebo group: 90 / 2000 x 100 = 4.5%
Reduction in risk = (4.5 - 3.0) / 4.5 x 100 = 33% (to 2 significant figures)
```

How to evaluate the data:

- The groups are the same size and large, which makes the comparison fairer and the result more reliable.
- A placebo group shows the effect is due to the drug, not to being in a trial.
- The statin reduced heart attacks but did not prevent them all, so it lowers risk rather than curing CHD.
- The trial does not show side effects; a full evaluation would need those too.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (6 marks): A patient has a narrowed coronary artery. Evaluate the use of a stent and of statins for this patient.*

> A stent is a mesh tube placed in the narrowed coronary artery to hold it open, so blood flow to the heart muscle improves straight away (**how it works and benefit**). It avoids major surgery, but there is a risk of infection or a blood clot forming, and the artery can narrow again (**risks**). Statins lower blood cholesterol, which slows the build-up of fatty material, and no procedure is needed (**second treatment and benefit**). However, statins act slowly, must be taken for life and can cause side effects, and they do not remove the narrowing already present (**risks and limit**). For this patient, who already has a narrowed artery, a stent is needed to restore blood flow now, with statins to reduce further build-up; using both treats the cause and the effect (**justified judgement for this patient**).

## Exam technique

- **Describe** how CHD develops: give the full chain from fatty deposits to lack of oxygen in heart muscle.
- **Explain** the effect of a faulty valve: say what happens to blood flow and what that means for the heart and body.
- **Evaluate**: at least one advantage and one disadvantage of each treatment named, then a judgement that refers to the patient in the question.
- **Suggest**: apply the criteria to an unfamiliar case, such as an elderly patient unfit for surgery.

## Common errors

- Writing that CHD blocks the aorta or the heart chambers instead of the coronary arteries.
- Saying the heart muscle lacks "blood" without saying it lacks **oxygen**.
- Claiming statins remove fatty deposits; they slow further deposits.
- Calling a stent a drug.
- Forgetting that donor transplants need anti-rejection drugs.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement for the patient given.
