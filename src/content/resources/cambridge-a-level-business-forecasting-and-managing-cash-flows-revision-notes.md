## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cash flow forecast | An estimate of the cash a business expects to receive and pay out in future periods, with the expected balance at the end of each |
| Cash inflow | Cash coming in, such as cash sales, customer payments, loans, owner investment, asset sales |
| Cash outflow | Cash going out, such as materials, wages, rent, equipment, loan repayments |
| Net cash flow | Total inflows minus total outflows for the period |
| Opening balance | Cash at the start of the period |
| Closing balance | Cash at the end of the period |
| Trade receivables | Customers who owe the business for goods or services sold on credit |
| Trade payables | Suppliers the business owes for goods or services bought on credit |

The syllabus does not print the balance formulae. These are the standard forms.

| Calculation | Formula |
|---|---|
| Net cash flow | Total cash inflows − total cash outflows |
| Closing balance | Opening balance + net cash flow |
| Next month's opening balance | This month's closing balance |

## Purposes of a forecast

- Shows shortfalls early, so finance can be arranged in advance.
- Supports requests to lenders and investors.
- Helps time large purchases.
- Gives a standard to compare actual cash with.
- Helps a start-up work out how much finance it needs.

## Method: building or completing a forecast

1. List the inflows month by month. Put credit sales in the month the customer pays, not the month of sale.
2. List the outflows. Put purchases in the month they are paid.
3. Leave out non-cash items such as depreciation.
4. Total inflows and total outflows for each month.
5. Net cash flow = inflows − outflows.
6. Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.
7. Carry the closing balance to the next month as the opening balance.
8. Check each month by working back from the final closing balance.

### Worked reminder: Dormer Toys

Dormer Toys opens September with £2,500.

| £ | Sep | Oct | Nov |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 6,800 | 7,400 | 9,900 |
| Total outflows | 7,300 | 8,900 | 8,200 |
| Net cash flow | −500 | −1,500 | 1,700 |
| Opening balance | 2,500 | 2,000 | 500 |
| Closing balance | 2,000 | 500 | 2,200 |

Reading it: the lowest balance is £500 at the end of October. Two months of negative net cash flow have used up most of the cash. The recovery in November depends on inflows of £9,900 actually arriving.

## Interpreting a forecast

Look at four things.

1. Lowest balance and when it happens.
2. Cause of any deficit: one-off item, seasonal pattern or a weak trend.
3. Whether the balance is big enough to cover normal outflows.
4. How reliable the assumptions are.

A negative closing balance means the business expects to be overdrawn or without cash unless it finds finance. A very large positive balance may mean cash is not being used well.

## Amending a forecast

- Change only the lines affected, then recalculate totals.
- Carry the new closing balance forward. Every later opening balance changes.
- Delays in customer payment move cash into a later month. They do not remove it.
- State the effect on the lowest balance and the action needed.

Example: if Dormer's October inflows fall by £1,000 because a customer pays in November, October closing becomes −£500 and November closing is £2,200 only if November also gains the £1,000. The total over the quarter is unchanged, but the business is overdrawn in between.

## Limitations of forecasts

- Based on sales estimates, which are uncertain, especially for a new business.
- Costs and supplier terms can change.
- Customers may pay late or default.
- Managers can be over-optimistic.
- Events outside the business, such as a new competitor, can outdate it.
- Needs regular updating to stay useful.

## Methods of improving cash flow

| Aim | Methods |
|---|---|
| Bring cash in sooner | Reduce credit period for customers; early-payment discount; debt factoring; promotions; bank overdraft; bank loan; owner investment |
| Raise cash from assets | Sell unwanted assets; sale and leaseback of non-current assets |
| Delay cash going out | Longer credit from suppliers; leasing or hire purchase instead of buying; delay capital spending |
| Reduce cash going out | Cut costs; lower inventory |

### Drawbacks to quote

| Method | Drawback |
|---|---|
| Overdraft | Interest; can be recalled; not for long-term need |
| Loan | Interest and repayments |
| Sell assets | One-off; may lose useful assets |
| Sale and leaseback | Ongoing rent; ownership lost |
| Debt factoring | Fee; customers deal with the factor |
| Shorter credit to customers | Customers may go elsewhere |
| Early-payment discount | Revenue reduced |
| Longer supplier credit | Supplier relations; lost discounts |
| Lower inventory | Stock-outs and lost sales |
| Leasing or hire purchase | Higher total cost |
| Cut costs | Quality or growth may suffer |

## Must-know distinctions

- Cash and profit: profit counts a sale when it is made, cash counts it when money moves. A profitable business can run out of cash.
- Cash flow and working capital: working capital (standard form: current assets less current liabilities) is a position at a point in time. Cash flow is the movement of cash over a period.
- Short-term and long-term fixes: overdrafts and supplier credit suit short gaps. Loans and owner investment suit longer ones.
- One-off and repeating effects: selling an asset or cutting stock helps once. Faster collection of payments helps again and again.
- Analysis and evaluation: analysis links a cause to a result. Evaluation weighs options against the case and reaches a judgement.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a cash flow forecast.
2. Write the formula for a closing balance.
3. A business has an opening balance of £1,200, inflows of £5,400 and outflows of £6,100. What is the closing balance?
4. A closing balance in March is −£300 and April net cash flow is £900. What is the April closing balance?
5. Why is a credit sale not a cash inflow in the month of sale?
6. State two purposes of a cash flow forecast.
7. Half of March sales of £8,000 are paid for in April. What April cash receipt does this give?
8. State two methods that bring cash in sooner.
9. State one risk of delaying payments to suppliers.
10. Opening balance £3,500, closing balance £2,000. What was the net cash flow?
11. A factor advances 80% of £10,000 of invoices and charges a 2% fee on the invoice value. How much is the advance, the fee and the balance later paid to the business?
12. Why does depreciation not appear in a cash flow forecast?

### Answers

1. An estimate of expected cash inflows and outflows for future periods, showing the expected cash balance at the end of each.
2. Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.
3. 1,200 + 5,400 − 6,100 = £500.
4. −300 + 900 = £600.
5. The customer has not paid yet, so no cash has moved.
6. Any two: spot shortfalls early; support a finance request; plan the timing of spending; compare actual cash with the plan.
7. 50% of £8,000 = £4,000.
8. Any two: shorter customer credit; early-payment discount; debt factoring; overdraft; sale and leaseback.
9. Suppliers may withdraw credit, charge more or refuse future orders.
10. 2,000 − 3,500 = −£1,500.
11. Advance £8,000; fee £200; balance 10,000 − 8,000 − 200 = £1,800.
12. It is an accounting entry that spreads the cost of an asset over time. No cash leaves the business when depreciation is recorded.

## Using this in the exam

The syllabus says Paper 1 Section A has four compulsory five-mark questions, and Paper 2 has two data response questions of six parts each, with evaluation expected in the last part. Both papers test AS content, and Papers 3 and 4 assume it.

- Short questions: define, then develop with a consequence.
- Calculations: show every line and carry balances forward.
- Longer answers: apply each point to the business in the stem, weigh the options, and end with a justified judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting credit sales in the month of sale instead of the month of payment.
- Including depreciation or profit as if they were cash movements.
- Failing to carry the closing balance forward as the next opening balance.
- Getting the sign wrong on a negative balance.
- Stopping at "cash flow improves" without saying why or how.
- Recommending a long-term fix for a short gap, or the reverse.
- Listing methods without applying them to the business in the case.
- Ignoring the drawbacks, so an evaluation is one-sided.
- Mixing up cash flow with profit or with working capital.
