This guide covers **Theme 3 Economic Development**, the third and
final theme in Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series
(Version 3, exams available in the November series only). This O
Level has an identical three-theme, 19-subtopic structure to Cambridge
IGCSE Geography (0460).

## Where this fits in 2217

Theme 1 covered the human side of the syllabus (population and
settlement) and Theme 2 covered physical geography (the natural
environment). Theme 3 brings the two together: it examines how people
use natural resources and physical environments to produce goods,
services, energy and food, and the economic, social and environmental
consequences of doing so. It is the most case-study-heavy theme in
the syllabus -- seven of its seven subtopics each require a named,
specific real-world example, more than either Theme 1 or Theme 2.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL GEOGRAPHY (2217) — THEME 3 ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT**

- 3.1 Development — indicators used to measure development (GNP per
  capita, literacy, life expectancy, composite indices such as HDI),
  inequalities between and within countries, the sectors of
  production (primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary) and how their
  share of employment changes with development, and globalisation and
  its impacts. Case study required: a transnational corporation and
  its global links
- 3.2 Food Production — the features of an agricultural system
  (inputs, processes, outputs), farming types (commercial/subsistence;
  arable/pastoral/mixed; intensive/extensive), and the causes,
  effects and possible solutions to food shortages. Case studies
  required: a farm or agricultural system, and a country or region
  suffering from food shortages
- 3.3 Industry — the features of an industrial system (inputs,
  processes, outputs including waste), industry types (manufacturing,
  processing, assembly, high technology), and the factors influencing
  the location and distribution of factories and industrial zones.
  Case study required: an industrial zone or factory
- 3.4 Tourism — the growth of tourism in relation to physical and
  human attractions, the benefits and disadvantages of tourism to
  receiving areas, and sustainable management of tourism. Case study
  required: an area where tourism is important
- 3.5 Energy — the importance of non-renewable fossil fuels, renewable
  energy, nuclear power and fuelwood at different levels of
  development, and the benefits and disadvantages of nuclear and
  renewable sources. Case study required: energy supply in a country
  or area
- 3.6 Water — methods of water supply and how the balance between
  agricultural, domestic and industrial use varies with development,
  and the causes of water shortages and the need for careful
  management. Case study required: water supply in a country or area
- 3.7 Environmental Risks of Economic Development — how economic
  activities threaten the natural environment and people, locally and
  globally, the need for sustainable development and management, and
  the importance of resource conservation. Case study required: an
  area where economic development is causing environmental risk

## Development indicators and sectors (3.1)

Candidates need to be able to use development indicators -- not just
name them -- to compare countries. GNP per capita is an economic
measure; literacy rate and life expectancy are social measures;
composite indices such as the Human Development Index combine several
measures into one score, which avoids the distortion of relying on a
single indicator (a country can have high income but low life
expectancy, for example). The production sectors -- primary
(extraction of raw materials), secondary (manufacturing), tertiary
(services) and quaternary (research and information-based industries)
-- shift in relative size as a country develops: employment moves from
being dominated by primary activity towards secondary and then
tertiary/quaternary activity, a pattern the syllabus expects you to
describe and explain, not just recall as a diagram. Globalisation is
examined through the role of technology and transnational
corporations, the economic factors driving it, and its impacts at
local, national and global scale -- which can be both positive
(investment, jobs) and negative (loss of local control, environmental
cost), and a strong answer weighs both.

## Food production and industry (3.2, 3.3)

Both subtopics use the same systems approach: inputs, processes and
outputs. For farming, inputs split into natural (relief, climate,
soil) and human (economic and social) factors, and the syllabus
expects you to explain how these combine to determine the scale,
organisation and output of a farming system -- commercial versus
subsistence, arable versus pastoral versus mixed, intensive versus
extensive. Food shortages have natural causes (drought, floods,
tropical storms, pests) and economic/political causes (low capital
investment, poor transport and distribution, conflict), and candidates
should be able to describe both the negative effects of shortages and
the measures used to increase output or provide relief. Industry
follows the same inputs-processes-outputs structure, but the outputs
include waste as well as products, and the location factors examined
are land, labour, raw materials, fuel and power, transport, markets
and political factors -- a specific named industrial zone or factory
must be used to illustrate how these combine in practice.

## Tourism, energy and water (3.4, 3.5, 3.6)

Tourism is examined through growth (why an area attracts visitors,
combining physical and human attractions), impact (benefits such as
income and employment weighed against disadvantages such as
environmental damage or cultural disruption), and management (what
makes tourism in an area sustainable rather than damaging over time).
Energy content requires knowing the main supply types -- non-renewable
fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas), renewable sources (geothermal,
wind, hydroelectric power, wave and tidal, solar, biofuels), nuclear
power and fuelwood -- and being able to evaluate the benefits and
drawbacks of nuclear and renewable sources specifically, since these
are the two the syllabus singles out for evaluation rather than
description alone. Water content covers supply methods (reservoirs
and dams, wells and boreholes, desalination), the proportion of water
used by agriculture, domestic and industrial users at different
development levels, and the causes of water shortages together with
the case for careful management to protect future supply.

## Environmental risks of economic development (3.7)

This closing subtopic draws the whole theme together: economic
activities across food production, industry, tourism, energy and
water all create environmental threats, named in the syllabus as soil
erosion, desertification, enhanced global warming, and pollution
(water, air, noise and visual). Candidates must be able to describe
these threats locally and globally, and demonstrate understanding of
why sustainable development and resource conservation matter as a
response -- this is the subtopic most likely to be examined by linking
back to a specific process or industry covered earlier in Theme 3.

## How to approach it

Because six of Theme 3's seven subtopics require a specific named case
study, the single biggest risk is having only a vague, generic example
in mind rather than real, place-specific detail you can write under
exam conditions -- a case study can be reused across subtopics where
it genuinely fits (for example, the same transnational corporation
could partly support both 3.1 and 3.3), but each one still needs
concrete facts, not just a country name. Learn each subtopic through
its systems structure (inputs, processes, outputs) rather than as a
list of facts, since Paper 1 questions are built around explaining how
and why these systems work the way they do, not simply naming their
components. Where a subtopic asks you to *evaluate* (energy, tourism),
practise weighing benefits against disadvantages explicitly rather
than describing only one side.
