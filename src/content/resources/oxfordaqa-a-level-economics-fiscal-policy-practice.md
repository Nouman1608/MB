## Questions

**1.** Define fiscal policy. **[2]**

**2.** State whether each tax is direct or indirect: (a) income tax; (b) a 15% sales tax; (c) corporation tax; (d) an excise duty on petrol. **[4]**

**3.** In Country G nothing is paid on the first 15,000 of income, and 30% is paid on income above that.

**(a)** A person earns 30,000. Find the tax they pay and their average tax rate. **[3]**
**(b)** A person earning 90,000 has an average rate of 25%. Using this and your answer to (a), identify the type of tax and give a reason. **[2]**

**4.** In Country H government spending is 760, tax revenue is 700 and GDP is 4,000. Opening national debt is 1,600.

**(a)** Find the budget balance and express it as a share of GDP. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the national debt at the end of the year. **[1]**
**(c)** GDP at the end of the year is 4,200. Calculate the debt as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**

**5.** In Country J potential GDP is 3,000 and actual GDP is 2,850. Tax revenue is 30% of GDP. At potential output government spending would be 930. At actual output it is 25 higher because of extra benefit payments.

**(a)** Find the output gap, as a percentage of potential GDP. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the actual budget deficit. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the structural deficit and the cyclical deficit. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why a return to potential output would not remove the deficit. **[2]**

**6.** In Country K the marginal propensity to save is 0.15, to tax 0.25 and to import 0.10. There is spare capacity.

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier. **[2]**
**(b)** Government spending rises by 40. Calculate the maximum change in national income. **[1]**
**(c)** Instead, taxes are cut by 40 and households spend 75% of it in the first round. Calculate the maximum change in national income. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why your answers to (b) and (c) differ. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how fiscal policy could be used to influence aggregate demand in a recession. **[4]**

**8.** Household L has income of 24,000 and spends 20,000. Household M has income of 100,000 and spends 45,000. All their spending bears a 25% sales tax that is included in the price.

**(a)** Calculate the sales tax each household pays, and show it as a percentage of income. **[4]**
**(b)** State the type of tax this shows, and explain why. **[2]**

**9.** In a market for a good with an external cost, demand is Qd = 180 - 15P and supply is Qs = 30 + 15P, where P is the price and Q is the quantity.

**(a)** Find the market equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** A tax of 2 per unit is placed on sellers. Find the price buyers now pay and the new quantity. **[3]**
**(c)** Work out the tax revenue and how much of it buyers and sellers each bear. **[3]**
**(d)** The external cost is 2 per unit. Explain how the tax addresses the market failure. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse how government spending on vocational training could affect aggregate supply, and give one limit to the effect. **[6]**

**11.** A government faces high unemployment and a high national debt. Evaluate whether it should increase spending on public works, financed by borrowing. **[10]**

**12.** Government spending is 800 in year 1 and 840 in year 2. Prices, measured by an index, are 100 in year 1 and 102 in year 2.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in nominal spending. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate year 2 spending in year 1 prices. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in real spending. **[1]**
**(d)** Comment on the difference between (a) and (c). **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The use of government spending and taxation (and the budget balance) [1] to influence aggregate demand or economic objectives [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition naming just one instrument, such as taxation, is incomplete. Cover both sides of the budget and the purpose.

**2.** (a) Direct [1]; (b) indirect [1]; (c) direct [1]; (d) indirect [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The test is whether the tax falls on income or wealth, or on spending. Write one word per item and avoid hedging.

**3. (a)** Taxable income = 30,000 - 15,000 = 15,000 [1]; tax = 30% x 15,000 = **4,500** [1]; average rate = 4,500 / 30,000 x 100 = **15%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The average rate rises from 15% to 25% as income rises [1], so the tax is **progressive** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Support the type of tax with the average rates. Saying more tax is paid by the richer person does not show it is progressive, since a regressive tax can also raise more from them.

**4. (a)** Balance = 700 - 760 = **-60, a deficit of 60** [1]; 60 / 4,000 x 100 = **1.5% of GDP** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 1,600 + 60 = **1,660** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 1,660 / 4,200 x 100 [1] = **39.5%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State whether the balance is a deficit or a surplus, not just the number. Debt as a percentage of GDP should use the end-of-year GDP when the question gives it.

**5. (a)** (2,850 - 3,000) / 3,000 x 100 [1] = **-5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Revenue = 30% x 2,850 = 855 [1]; spending = 930 + 25 = 955 [1]; deficit = 955 - 855 = **100** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** At potential output revenue = 30% x 3,000 = 900, so the structural deficit = 930 - 900 = **30** [1]; cyclical deficit = 100 - 30 = **70** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The 30 structural deficit exists even at potential output [1], so it needs a tax rise or spending cut to remove it [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Build the answer in order: gap, actual balance, then the split. Check that the structural and cyclical parts add back to the actual deficit.

**6. (a)** MPW = 0.15 + 0.25 + 0.10 = 0.5 [1]; multiplier = 1 / 0.5 = **2** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 40 x 2 = **80** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** First-round injection = 40 x 0.75 = 30 [1]; 30 x 2 = **60** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Part of the tax cut is saved, so the first-round injection is smaller than 40 [1]; all government spending is injected in the first round, so the final effect is larger [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), apply the multiplier to the amount spent in the first round, not to the whole tax cut. State that spare capacity is assumed.

**7.** Government raises spending or cuts taxes [1]; spending is a direct part of AD, and a tax cut raises disposable income and so consumption [1]; AD shifts right [1]; output and employment rise by more than the first change, through the multiplier, because there is spare capacity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers link each step in a chain rather than listing instruments. A tax cut and a spending rise reach AD by different routes, so say which one you are using.

**8. (a)** Tax is 0.25 / 1.25 = 20% of spending [1]. L: 20% x 20,000 = 4,000, which is 4,000 / 24,000 x 100 = **16.7%** of income [1]. M: 20% x 45,000 = 9,000 [1], which is 9,000 / 100,000 x 100 = **9.0%** of income [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** It is **regressive** [1] because the share of income taken falls as income rises, since the lower-income household spends a higher proportion of its income [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Because the tax is included in the price, one fifth of spending is tax, not a quarter. Using 25% of spending is a common slip.

**9. (a)** 180 - 15P = 30 + 15P gives 30P = 150, so **P = 5** [1]; Q = 180 - 75 = **105** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Supply becomes Qs = 30 + 15(P - 2) [1]; 180 - 15P = 15P gives **P = 6** [1]; Q = 180 - 90 = **90** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Revenue = 2 x 90 = **180** [1]; buyers pay 1 more per unit, so 1 x 90 = **90** [1]; sellers receive 4 instead of 5, so they bear **90** [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** The tax raises the cost of the good to cover the external cost of 2 [1], so output falls from 105 towards the socially efficient level [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the new price in both equations, and link the tax to the externality in words.

**10.** Spending on training raises workers' skills [1]; labour productivity rises [1]; unit costs fall, so short-run aggregate supply shifts right [1]; the quantity and quality of labour rise, so potential output grows and long-run aggregate supply shifts right [1]. Limit: it takes years before trained workers affect output [1], so the gain is delayed and the money has an opportunity cost [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the route from spending to productivity to costs to the AS curve. A limit scores only if you say why it weakens the effect.

**11.** Extra spending raises G and shifts AD right [1]; the multiplier magnifies the initial rise [1]; with high unemployment there is spare capacity, so output and jobs rise with little inflation [1]; higher incomes raise tax revenue and cut benefits, so part of the extra deficit is recovered [1]; if the works are infrastructure, LRAS rises as well [1]. Against: borrowing adds to the debt [1]; the debt ratio may still fall if GDP grows faster than debt [1]; interest payments are an opportunity cost, and lenders may lose confidence [1]; lags may mean the economy recovers first [1]. Judgement: the case is strong when the output gap is large and the structural deficit is small, since most of the borrowing is then cyclical [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach a judgement and say what it depends on. A list of costs and benefits with no conclusion leaves the evaluation unfinished.

**12. (a)** (840 - 800) / 800 x 100 = **5.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 840 / 102 x 100 = **823.5** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** (823.5 - 800) / 800 x 100 = **2.9%** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Real growth is lower than nominal growth because prices rose, so the extra spending buys fewer goods and services than the nominal rise suggests [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Deflate by dividing by the index and multiplying by 100, then compare with the base year. State which prices the result is in.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a fiscal policy definition that covers taxation or spending but not both.
- Calling a tax progressive without checking that the average rate rises.
- Giving a deficit without its sign, or without the unit when asked for a percentage of GDP.
- Mixing up deficit and debt.
- Multiplying the full tax cut, not only the share spent in the first round.
- Treating the multiplier as certain, without saying spare capacity is assumed.
- Dividing by the wrong total when finding tax included in a price.
- Ignoring the starting position of the economy when judging a stimulus.
- Ending an evaluation without a conclusion.
