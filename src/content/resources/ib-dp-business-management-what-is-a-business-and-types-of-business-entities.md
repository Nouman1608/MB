## 1.1 The nature of business

A business is an organization that combines resources (land, labor, capital and enterprise) to produce goods or services that satisfy the needs and wants of customers. This is the standard form of the definition. Three ideas sit underneath it.

- **Needs and wants.** A need is something required to live or function, such as food or shelter. A want is something desired beyond that, such as a particular brand of trainers.
- **Goods and services.** Goods are tangible products. Services are intangible activities, such as repair, advice or transport. Many businesses sell both.
- **Adding value.** Value added is the difference between what a business pays for its inputs and what customers pay for the output. A business that cannot add value does not survive for long.

Businesses may aim at profit, at a social purpose, or at both. Section 1.2 returns to this.

### Primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors

Economic activity is grouped by what stage of production it covers. The guide names four sectors.

| Sector | What it does | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Takes or grows output directly from nature | Farming, fishing, mining, forestry |
| Secondary | Makes raw materials into finished or part-finished goods | Manufacturing, construction, food processing |
| Tertiary | Provides services | Shops, banking, transport, teaching, healthcare |
| Quaternary | Knowledge-based activity | Research and development, information technology, consultancy |

Some textbooks place quaternary activity inside the tertiary sector. The guide lists it separately, so treat it as its own sector in an answer.

**Worked example 1.** Ardenholt Oat Mill is invented for this guide. It buys oats from farms, mills them into flour and flakes, sells packs through its own cafe, and runs a small team that studies new low-sugar oat products. Of its sales revenue of £600,000, £420,000 comes from milling and £180,000 from the cafe.

- Growing the oats is primary-sector work, but the mill does not do it. The farms are primary; the mill is a customer of the primary sector.
- Milling is secondary: raw material becomes a finished good.
- The cafe is tertiary: a retail service.
- The product research team is quaternary: knowledge work.
- Milling contributes 420,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 70% of revenue, so the business is mostly secondary-sector.

The point to notice is that one business can operate in several sectors. Classify the activity, not the business name.

Sectors also change as economies develop. This is general knowledge, not a point the guide makes. Many economies see employment move from primary toward secondary and then tertiary, and some toward quaternary. For a business, this shapes where demand and skilled workers are found.

## Entrepreneurship

An entrepreneur is a person who identifies a business opportunity, takes the risk of organizing resources to exploit it, and bears the possible loss. Entrepreneurship is the process of doing this. Typical qualities are willingness to take risks, creativity, determination, and the ability to spot unmet needs.

The guide also refers to social entrepreneurs. A social entrepreneur applies the same drive to a social or environmental need, with that need as the main purpose rather than maximum profit. Paper 3, which only HL students sit, is about a social enterprise, so this idea is worth knowing even at SL, where one Paper 2 stimulus option may also be a social enterprise.

An entrepreneur is not the same as a manager. The entrepreneur starts and risks the venture; a manager runs an existing one for owners. In small firms one person is often both.

## Challenges and opportunities for starting up a business

The guide lists this as AO2, so you need to analyse the factors in a context. Use the table, then link each point to the case.

| Opportunities | Challenges |
|---|---|
| Being your own boss and keeping the profit | Raising start-up finance with no trading record |
| Meeting a gap in the market | Cash flow in the first months, with costs before sales |
| Flexibility to change quickly | Competition from established firms with brand loyalty and scale |
| Personal satisfaction and creative control | Heavy workload and personal financial risk |
| Chance of growth, or of a later sale | Finding customers, staff and a suitable location |
| Support such as grants, mentors or incubators (where they exist) | Meeting regulations and tax duties |

**Worked example 2.** Fernquist Cycle Hire is invented. Ravi plans to rent bicycles to tourists at a lakeside town. Start-up costs are:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| 12 bicycles at £650 each | £7,800 |
| Trailer and racks | £2,700 |
| Deposit on a small unit | £4,000 |
| Insurance and licences | £1,500 |
| Opening marketing | £1,000 |
| **Total** | **£17,000** |

Ravi has £11,000 of savings, so he must find £17,000 − £11,000 = £6,000 from elsewhere. That is the finance challenge in numbers.

- Opportunity: tourists want to hire bikes, there is no hire shop in the town, and low overheads let him price competitively.
- Challenge: income is seasonal, so cash flow in winter is weak while the unit rent continues.
- Challenge: a lender may hesitate to fund a firm with no record.

A good answer would judge which factor matters most. Here the seasonal cash-flow gap is likely to decide whether Ravi survives his first year, so he should plan a cash reserve before opening.

## 1.2 Types of business entities

### The private and the public sectors

This is a different split from the four sectors above, so do not mix them up.

- The **private sector** is made up of businesses owned by individuals or groups of private owners. They are usually run to make a profit for the owners, though some have a social purpose.
- The **public sector** is made up of organizations owned and controlled by the government (national or local). Their aim is often to provide a service, such as public transport or state schools, rather than to maximize profit.

Money, ownership and accountability differ. Private firms answer to owners and customers. Public organizations answer to government, and in turn to taxpayers and voters.

### Main features of the four organization types

Legal rules differ between countries, so describe features in general terms and say so in your answer if the case gives no country.

| Feature | Sole trader | Partnership | Privately held company | Publicly held company |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Owners | One person | Two or more people | Usually a small group of shareholders | Many shareholders, often thousands |
| Liability | Unlimited in general: personal assets at risk | Unlimited in general for partners | Limited to the money invested | Limited to the money invested |
| Control | The owner decides alone | Shared, often by agreement | Owners and directors | Directors, accountable to shareholders |
| Raising finance | Savings, loans, profit kept in the firm | Partners' capital, loans, retained profit | Existing owners, loans, new private investors | Selling shares to the public, loans |
| Selling ownership | Sell the whole business | Usually needs the partners' agreement | Shares usually cannot be sold freely to the public | Shares are traded on a stock exchange |
| Disclosure | Little | Little | Moderate | High, because published reports are required |
| Continuity | Ends when the owner stops | May end if a partner leaves, unless agreed otherwise | Continues if owners change | Continues if owners change |

In general, a **sole trader** keeps all profit and decisions but carries all risk. A **partnership** shares workload, skills and capital but can suffer disagreement, and each partner may be answerable for the others' decisions. A **privately held company** is a separate legal entity, which gives limited liability, but it has less access to capital than a public one. A **publicly held company** can raise very large sums by selling shares, but ownership can be diluted, managers face short-term pressure from shareholders, and disclosure is costly.

**Worked example 3.** Strathovane Joinery is invented. Two joiners, Maeve and Dilip, want to open a workshop together. They can put in £40,000 between them but need £60,000.

- As a partnership they would raise the money from their own capital plus a loan, and keep decisions simple. The risk is that each could be held responsible for the other's debts.
- As a privately held company they would gain limited liability, but the set-up and reporting duties are heavier.
- A publicly held company is not realistic at this size.

A reasoned recommendation might be a privately held company, because the £20,000 gap to be borrowed makes limited liability valuable. A weaker answer lists features without choosing.

### For-profit social enterprises

A social enterprise (standard form) is an organization that trades to meet a social or environmental aim. The guide splits the type into for-profit and non-profit. Its for-profit list has three items.

- **Private sector companies.** Privately owned firms that make profit while also pursuing a stated social purpose, for example by employing people who find it hard to get work, or by reinvesting part of profit in a cause.
- **Public sector companies.** Government-owned organizations run as businesses. They charge for goods or services and aim for surplus, but exist to serve the public interest, such as a state-owned water or rail operator.
- **Cooperatives.** Businesses owned and run by members, who may be workers, customers or producers. Typically each member has an equal vote, and surplus is shared among members or reinvested.

### Non-profit social enterprise: NGOs

A **non-governmental organization (NGO)** is independent of government and does not aim to make profit for owners. It exists for a social, humanitarian or environmental purpose. Funding often comes from donations, grants and fundraising, and any surplus is kept for its mission. Its stakeholders include donors, volunteers and the people it helps. A common challenge is dependence on funding that may not be renewed.

## Common errors

- Writing that a sole trader has "no" capital problems. Raising finance is usually its hardest challenge.
- Saying limited liability means the owners can lose nothing. They can lose what they invested.
- Using "public limited" and "public sector" as if they meant the same. Publicly held means shares are open to the public; public sector means government-owned.
- Treating a cooperative as non-profit by definition. The guide places it among for-profit social enterprises.
- Listing features with no link to the case.
