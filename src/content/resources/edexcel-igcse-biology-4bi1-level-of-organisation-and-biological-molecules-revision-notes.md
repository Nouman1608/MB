## 2.1 Levels of organisation

**organelle → cell → tissue → organ → system → organism**

| Level | Definition to learn | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Organelle | Structure inside a cell with a specific function | Nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast, ribosome |
| Cell | Basic structural and functional unit of life | Red blood cell, root hair cell, palisade cell |
| Tissue | Group of **similar** cells working together for one function | Muscle tissue, epithelium, palisade mesophyll |
| Organ | Group of **different** tissues working together | Heart, stomach, brain, leaf, root |
| System | Group of organs working together | Digestive, circulatory, nervous system |

### Method in steps: classifying a named structure

1. Is it inside a cell? → **organelle**.
2. Is it one cell? → **cell**.
3. Is it many cells of one kind? → **tissue**.
4. Does it contain more than one kind of tissue? → **organ**.
5. Is it several organs that share a job? → **system**.

### Worked reminder: one organ, many levels

A leaf is an **organ**. It contains palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, epidermis, xylem and phloem: several different **tissues**. The palisade mesophyll is made of many similar palisade **cells**, and each palisade cell contains many **chloroplasts** (organelles). The leaf works with the stem and other organs above ground, which together form the shoot **system**. Being able to walk down the levels like this, from organ to organelle, is a quick way to check any classification.

## 2.7 Elements

| Molecule | C | H | O | N |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Protein | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (often S too) |
| Lipid | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |

- Nitrogen present → protein.
- Lipids have far less oxygen relative to carbon and hydrogen than carbohydrates.

## 2.8 Building units

| Large molecule | Smaller units | Stored or used by |
|---|---|---|
| Starch | Glucose (simple sugar) | Plants (storage) |
| Glycogen | Glucose (simple sugar) | Animals and fungi (storage) |
| Protein | Amino acids | All organisms (enzymes, structure) |
| Lipid | Fatty acids + glycerol | Fats (animals), oils (plants) |

- Starch and glycogen have the **same** unit (glucose), different organisms.
- Protein shape depends on the **order** of amino acids.
- A lipid = **glycerol + fatty acids** (usually three).

## 2.9 Food tests

| Test for | Reagent | Heat? | Positive result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Benedict's solution | Yes, water bath | Blue → green / yellow / orange / brick-red |
| Starch | Iodine solution | No | Orange-brown → blue-black |
| Protein | Biuret (sodium hydroxide + copper sulfate) | No | Blue → purple |
| Fat | Ethanol, then pour into water | No | Clear → cloudy white emulsion |

### Method in steps: any food test answer

1. Prepare the sample: grind solid food with water.
2. Name the reagent and quantity (a few drops, or an equal volume).
3. State heating if needed (Benedict's only, in a water bath).
4. State the starting colour and the positive colour.
5. Include a control (water instead of food) if asked how to make it valid.

Small reminders:

- Benedict's colour order tracks glucose amount: green (little) → brick-red (lots).
- Sucrose gives a **negative** Benedict's result.
- Emulsion test: the ethanol must go into **water** to show the cloudiness.

### Worked reminder: reading a Benedict's row

Three glucose solutions give green, orange and brick-red results after the same heating time. Order of glucose concentration, lowest first: green → orange → brick-red. The test only ranks the samples; to compare fairly, use the same volume of sample and Benedict's solution and the same heating time for each.

## 2.10 Enzymes as catalysts

- **Catalyst**: speeds up a reaction, not used up.
- **Enzyme**: a protein that acts as a biological catalyst in metabolic reactions.
- Catalyse **breakdown** (starch → sugars) and **synthesis** (glucose → glycogen; amino acids → protein).
- **Active site**: region with a specific shape; substrate has a complementary shape.
- Substrate binds → enzyme-substrate complex → products released → enzyme reused.
- **Specificity**: one enzyme, one type of substrate, because of active site shape.

## 2.11 and 2.13 Temperature and pH

| Condition | What happens | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|
| Low temperature | Less kinetic energy, fewer collisions, slow rate | Yes |
| Optimum temperature | Most successful collisions; fastest rate | -- |
| High temperature | Bonds break, active site changes shape, substrate no longer fits: **denatured** | No |
| pH away from optimum | Active site altered; fewer enzyme-substrate complexes | Small shifts: often yes. Extreme pH: denatured |

Typical optima: many human enzymes about 37 °C; stomach protease about pH 2; salivary amylase about pH 7.

## 2.12 Temperature practical (and 2.14B pH practical, Paper 2 only)

### Method in steps

1. Pre-warm amylase and starch separately in a water bath at the test temperature.
2. Place iodine drops in a spotting tile.
3. Mix, start the timer, and test one drop every 30 s.
4. Record the time when iodine stays orange-brown.
5. Repeat three times; calculate a mean, leaving out anomalous results.
6. **Rate = 1 / time.**

| Variable | Temperature practical (2.12) | pH practical (2.14B) |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | Temperature | pH (buffer solutions) |
| Dependent | Time for starch to disappear | Time for starch to disappear |
| Control | pH (buffer), volumes, concentrations, sampling interval | Temperature (one water bath), volumes, concentrations, sampling interval |

### Small worked reminder

The iodine stays orange-brown after 80 s. Rate = 1/80 = 0.0125 s⁻¹. A time twice as long means half the rate.

### Method in steps: describing and explaining an enzyme graph

1. **Describe** first, with data: "rate rises from 10 °C to a peak at 40 °C, then falls to zero by 60 °C."
2. **Explain the rise**: more kinetic energy, more frequent successful collisions, more enzyme-substrate complexes.
3. **State the optimum** as read from the graph, with its unit.
4. **Explain the fall**: bonds break, active site changes shape, substrate no longer fits, enzyme denatured.
5. For a pH graph, use the same steps but explain both sides of the peak by the active site being altered.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Tissue vs organ**: similar cells vs different tissues.
- **Starch vs glycogen**: same unit, plant vs animal store.
- **Benedict's vs iodine**: glucose (heat, brick-red) vs starch (no heat, blue-black).
- **Slowed vs denatured**: cold slows an enzyme reversibly; heat above the optimum changes the active site permanently.
- **Time vs rate**: longer time = slower rate.
- **2.12 vs 2.14B**: temperature practical is on both papers; pH practical is Paper 2 only.

## Quick self-test

1. Put in order, smallest first: organ, organelle, system, tissue, cell.
2. Is the heart a tissue or an organ? Give a reason.
3. Which element is in proteins but not in carbohydrates?
4. Name the units that make up glycogen.
5. Name the two kinds of unit that make up a lipid.
6. What colour is a positive biuret test?
7. A sucrose solution is tested with Benedict's. What is the result?
8. Define the term *enzyme*.
9. Why does an enzyme stop working at very high temperature?
10. The starch disappears after 125 s. Calculate the rate.
11. Three repeat times are 62 s, 58 s and 60 s. Calculate the mean.
12. In the pH practical, how is temperature controlled, and which paper examines this practical?

### Answers

1. Organelle, cell, tissue, organ, system.
2. Organ: it contains several different tissues (such as muscle and nervous tissue) working together.
3. Nitrogen.
4. Glucose (simple sugar) units.
5. Fatty acids and glycerol.
6. Purple (lilac).
7. Negative: stays blue. Sucrose is not detected by Benedict's.
8. A protein that acts as a biological catalyst, speeding up a metabolic reaction without being used up.
9. Bonds holding its shape break, the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits; it is denatured.
10. 1/125 = 0.008 s⁻¹.
11. (62 + 58 + 60) ÷ 3 = 60 s.
12. All tubes kept in the same water bath at one temperature; Paper 2 only (2.14B).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "organ = a group of cells" instead of "a group of different tissues".
- Giving a cell type (for example, a sperm cell) when asked for a tissue.
- Putting nitrogen in lipids, or leaving it out of proteins.
- Saying proteins are made of "amino acids and glycerol" -- mixing up the units.
- Leaving out the water bath in the Benedict's test, or heating the biuret test.
- Describing a positive iodine test as "black" or "purple" rather than blue-black.
- "Enzymes are killed by heat" -- enzymes are denatured, and you must say the active site changes shape.
- Saying the substrate "no longer fits" without stating that the active site has changed shape first.
- Quoting the time as the rate; rate = 1/time, and the shortest time is the fastest rate.
- Not pre-warming the enzyme and substrate separately, so the reaction starts at the wrong temperature.
