These questions cover topic 12, Environmental management (sections 12.1 to 12.4), of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The topic is an Advanced Human Geography Option, assessed in Paper 4 with a structured question and a choice of essays. All countries, towns, rivers and data below are fictional.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term "energy security". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between renewable and non-renewable energy resources, giving one example of each. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows electricity generation in Kestria, a fictional middle-income country.

| Source | 2005 (TWh) | 2025 (TWh) |
|---|---|---|
| Coal | 48 | 62 |
| Natural gas | 20 | 38 |
| Oil | 6 | 2 |
| Nuclear | 0 | 9 |
| HEP | 14 | 18 |
| Wind | 0.5 | 16 |
| Solar | 0 | 12 |
| Biomass | 1.5 | 3 |
| **Total** | **90** | **160** |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of Kestria's electricity generated from renewable sources in 2025. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the changes in Kestria's electricity generation between 2005 and 2025. **[4]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why coal generation increased while coal's share of the total fell. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how resource endowment and climate can influence a country's energy mix. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two environmental impacts of transporting energy, one at the local scale and one at the global scale. **[4]**

**6.** The table shows water quality at five sites along the fictional River Tamsin, which flows through the fictional town of Harwick.

| Site | Location | Dissolved oxygen (mg/l) | Nitrate (mg/l) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Upland pasture, 12 km above Harwick | 9.6 | 4.1 |
| B | Arable farmland, 4 km above Harwick | 8.7 | 14.8 |
| C | Outfall from Harwick industrial estate | 5.2 | 16.2 |
| D | Sewage works outfall, 2 km below Harwick | 3.1 | 21.5 |
| E | 8 km below Harwick, after a reed-bed wetland | 6.8 | 12.0 |

**(a)** Identify the site with the lowest dissolved oxygen and calculate the percentage fall from site A. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest reasons for the changes in nitrate and dissolved oxygen between sites A and D. **[5]**
**(c)** Suggest why water quality has improved at site E. **[2]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why demand for water usually increases as a country develops. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how poor agricultural practices can lead to the degradation of rural environments. **[5]**

**9.** Lake Orvane is a fictional wetland on the edge of Dalmouth, a fictional city growing by 4% a year. Parts of the wetland are being drained for housing, and rubbish is dumped along its shore. It is a stopover for migrating birds and supplies fish to local families. Suggest the needs, possible measures and likely outcomes of a scheme to protect Lake Orvane. **[6]**

**10.** Evaluate the success of one country's overall electrical energy strategy. **[10]**

**11.** "The benefits of a large electricity scheme go to distant users, while the costs fall on local people." How far do you agree? Refer to one named located scheme. **[12]**

**12.** With reference to one degraded environment you have studied, assess the extent to which attempts to improve it have been successful. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A reliable [1] supply of enough affordable energy for a country's needs, without serious risk of disruption [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "having energy" earns little; the marks come from reliability and affordability.

**2.** Renewable resources are replenished naturally on a human time scale [1]; non-renewable resources form over geological time and are finite, so they run out [1]; one correct example of each, such as wind and coal [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* both examples are needed for the third mark; a single list of sources is not a distinction.

**3. (a)** 18 + 16 + 12 + 3 = 49 TWh renewable [1]; 49 ÷ 160 × 100 = 30.6% [1].
**(b)** Total generation rose from 90 to 160 TWh, about 78% [1]. Renewables' share rose from 17.8% to 30.6%, mostly from wind (0.5 to 16 TWh) and solar (0 to 12 TWh) [1]. Coal rose from 48 to 62 TWh but its share fell from 53.3% to 38.8% [1]. Nuclear appeared (9 TWh) and oil fell from 6 to 2 TWh [1].
**(c)** Demand grew fast, perhaps from industrialisation and rising incomes [1], so every source had to expand, including coal [1]. Kestria may have its own coal and existing coal plants, making coal cheap to expand [1], while policy or falling costs made wind and solar grow faster, so coal's share fell [1].
*Examiner insight:* in "describe" parts, quote figures from the table with units; in "suggest" parts, link each reason to the data rather than writing a general essay.

**4.** Resource endowment: countries tend to use the fuels they have [1], for example a coal-rich country building coal power stations because the fuel is cheap and secure [1]. Climate: affects demand, as cold winters raise heating needs [1], and supply potential, as reliable winds, sunshine or high rainfall in uplands favour wind, solar or HEP [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each factor needs an explained link to the mix, not just a description of the factor.

**5.** Local: an oil tanker or pipeline spill [1] contaminates coastline or soil and harms wildlife [1]. Global: methane leaking from gas pipelines [1] adds a powerful greenhouse gas to the atmosphere, contributing to climate change [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the question asks for transport, so impacts of mining or burning fuel score nothing here.

**6. (a)** Site D [1]; fall = 9.6 − 3.1 = 6.5 mg/l [1]; 6.5 ÷ 9.6 × 100 = 67.7% [1].
**(b)** Nitrate rises sharply from A to B (4.1 to 14.8 mg/l) as fertiliser runs off arable fields, a diffuse source [1]. Industrial effluent at C adds pollutants and may be warm, and warm water holds less oxygen [1]. Sewage at D adds organic matter and more nutrients, taking nitrate to 21.5 mg/l [1]. Bacteria decomposing sewage use up oxygen, so dissolved oxygen falls to 3.1 mg/l [1]. Nutrients can also cause algal growth whose decay lowers oxygen further (eutrophication) [1].
**(c)** The reed-bed wetland absorbs nutrients and filters pollutants [1], and distance downstream allows dilution and re-oxygenation, so dissolved oxygen recovers by 3.7 mg/l [1].
*Examiner insight:* use the site locations given -- farmland, industry, sewage -- to tie each change to a source.

**7.** Rising incomes increase domestic use through piped supply, flush toilets and appliances [1], so use per person goes up [1]. Industrialisation and irrigated commercial farming need large volumes of water [1], adding to demand alongside urban population growth [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "explain two reasons" needs two developed points; four undeveloped ones score less.

**8.** Overgrazing removes vegetation cover [1], exposing soil to wind and water erosion [1]. Overcultivation without fallow exhausts soil nutrients and structure [1]. Ploughing up and down slopes creates channels for runoff, causing gullying [1]. Irrigation without drainage raises water tables and leaves salts in the soil, causing salinisation [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* name the practice and the process it triggers; "bad farming damages land" earns nothing.

**9.** Needs: the wetland is a stopover for migrating birds [1] and supplies fish to local families, so livelihoods depend on it [1]. Measures: legal designation that bans drainage and building [1], plus waste collection and fines for dumping, with clean-up days [1]. Outcomes: if enforced, habitat area and bird numbers could be kept up [1], but with Dalmouth growing by 4% a year, housing pressure may continue and enforcement may be weak [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* answer all three parts -- needs, measures, outcomes -- and use the scenario's details in each.

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these points show what a strong answer would contain.

Germany, the *Energiewende*. The Renewable Energy Sources Act of 2000 guaranteed payments for renewable electricity [1]. Nuclear power was phased out, with the last reactors closing on 15 April 2023 [1], and coal is to end by 2038 at the latest [1]. Production moved from large central stations to northern and offshore wind and dispersed solar [1]. Success: renewables met 54% of demand in 2024 [1], and 2023 emissions were the lowest since the 1950s [1]. Limits: part of that fall came from weaker industry [1]; the SuedLink north-south line slipped from 2022 to 2028 after protests [1]; prices have been high [1]. Judgement: a qualified success -- the mix has changed greatly, but more slowly and expensively than planned [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* the top marks need a clear judgement that weighs evidence of success against named problems.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these points show what a strong answer would contain.

Scheme: Three Gorges Dam, on the Yangtze at Sandouping, Hubei [1]; 22,500 MW, fully running in 2012 [1]. Distant benefits: long high-voltage lines carry power to central, eastern and southern China [1]; output reached nearly 112 TWh in 2020 [1]; low-carbon electricity helps national emissions targets [1]. Local costs: about 1.13 to 1.4 million people displaced [1]; landslides on reservoir slopes, 97 significant ones in early 2010 [1]; altered river ecosystems and fish migration [1]. Counter-argument: flood control protects people downstream along the Yangtze [1], and the ship lift improves navigation for the region [1]. Sediment trapping affects the lower river and delta, so costs are not only local [1]. Judgement: largely agree, but benefits and costs both cross scales, so the statement is too simple [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* "how far" demands both sides and a judgement on the quotation itself, not just a list of impacts.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these points show what a strong answer would contain.

Case study: the Aral Sea, between Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan [1]. Cause: the Amu Darya and Syr Darya were diverted from the early 1960s to irrigate cotton [1]. Problems: area down 60% and volume 80% between 1960 and 1998 [1]; salinity rose to about 30 g/L by 1990 [1]; Muynak is now 30-90 km from the shore [1]. Attempt: Dike Kokaral, completed in 2005 with World Bank loans [1]. Success: the North Aral rose 12 m to 42 m by 2008 [1], about 24 freshwater species returned and fish were exported again [1]. Failure: the South Aral's eastern basin dried out in 2014 [1]. Constraints: the Amu Darya is shared by several countries [1], and cotton irrigation supports jobs and exports [1]. Judgement: successful in the north, where one affordable project worked, but unsuccessful overall [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* separate the degree of success by area or group; one overall verdict without that split reads as description.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out working in "calculate" parts, so a wrong final figure scores nothing.
- Describing data without quoting figures and units from the table.
- Explaining production impacts when the question asks only about transport or usage.
- Using a general definition instead of the scenario's details in "suggest" questions.
- Answering a "named located scheme" question with a country-wide strategy.
- Missing one of needs, measures or outcomes in protection questions.
- Writing essays that describe a case study well but never reach a judgement.
- Giving invented or uncertain statistics; accurate qualitative detail is safer.
