## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a method of secondary research named in the specification? **[1]**

A: Focus group

B: Observation

C: Government reports

D: Questionnaire

**2.** Which **one** of the following produces qualitative data? **[1]**

A: Counting customers entering a shop each hour

B: Asking customers to rate a product from 1 to 5

C: Asking customers why they chose a rival shop

D: Recording the number of items sold each day

**3.** Define the term primary research. **[1]**

**4.** Give **two** purposes of market research named in the specification. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** disadvantage of using secondary research for a new small business. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** reason why the reliability of market research data is important. **[3]**

### Section B: case-study questions

**7.** *Rookery Smoothies* plans to open a smoothie bar near a college. The owner, Callum, asked 240 people outside the college whether they would buy a smoothie at least once a week. 96 said yes. He then asked 200 people to rate a sample smoothie from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent).

| Rating | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of people | 10 | 20 | 60 | 70 | 40 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of people who said they would buy a smoothie at least once a week. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the mean rating of the sample smoothie. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage of people who gave a rating of 4 or 5. **[2]**

**(d)** Callum estimates there are 5,000 students and staff at the college. Using your answer to part (a), estimate how many might buy a smoothie at least once a week. **[1]**

**(e)** Outline **one** reason why Callum's estimate in part (d) may not be reliable. **[2]**

**8.** Callum also posted a poll on the Rookery Smoothies social media page asking followers which of two flavours to launch first. Outline **one** advantage to Rookery Smoothies of using social media to collect market research data. **[2]**

**9.** *Wickham Dog Wash* is a new self-service dog-washing business. Its owner, Farah, has £600 to spend on market research before choosing between two sites. She is deciding between:

Option 1: buying a market report on the pet care industry in the UK.

Option 2: carrying out her own questionnaire of dog owners in local parks near each site.

Justify which option Farah should choose. **[9]**

**10.** *Thistle and Fern* is a florist. Its owner used a questionnaire of 300 shoppers on a Saturday morning, which found that 40% would use a flower subscription service. Analyse how this research could reduce the risk of launching the subscription service. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** C: Government reports [1]. **[1]**

**2.** C: Asking customers why they chose a rival shop [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The other options produce numbers, so they are quantitative.

**3.** New information collected first-hand by, or for, a business for its own purpose [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two from: to identify and understand customer needs [1]; to identify gaps in the market [1]; to reduce risk; to inform business decisions. **[2]**

**5.** Secondary data was collected by someone else for a different purpose [1], so it may not cover the small business's local area or its particular product [1]. The owner may therefore base decisions on information that does not reflect their own customers, leading to wrong choices about price or stock [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "It may be out of date" is another valid starting point, developed in the same way.

**6.** Businesses use research data to make decisions such as how much stock to order [1]. If the data does not reflect the real market, for example because the sample was biased, the decision may be wrong [1], so the business could waste money and the research would fail to reduce risk [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 96 ÷ 240 × 100 [1] = **40%** [1].

**(b)** (1 × 10) + (2 × 20) + (3 × 60) + (4 × 70) + (5 × 40) = 710 [1]. 710 ÷ 200 = **3.55** [1].

**(c)** (70 + 40) ÷ 200 × 100 [1] = **55%** [1].

**(d)** 40% × 5,000 = **2,000 people** [1].

**(e)** The people asked were only those outside the college at one time, so they may not represent all students and staff [1]. People also often say they will buy more often than they actually do, so the true number could be well below 2,000 [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Social media polls are quick and free to set up [1], so Callum can find out which flavour followers prefer before he buys stock, and the poll may also build interest in the launch among likely customers [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Option 1: a market report covers the whole pet care industry [1], so it would show whether demand for dog grooming services is growing [1]. However, it is secondary data about the UK as a whole and cannot tell Farah which of her two sites has more local dog owners [1], and it may use up much of her £600 budget [1].

Option 2: a questionnaire of dog owners in parks near each site is primary data specific to her decision [1]. She can ask how often they wash their dogs, what they would pay and how far they would travel [1], which would let her compare the two sites directly [1]. It costs mainly her time, so it fits within £600 [1]. The weakness is that people in parks at one time of day may not represent all local dog owners.

Judgement: Farah should choose Option 2, because her decision is between two particular sites and only primary research near each site answers that question [1]. **[9]**

**10.** The questionnaire shows that 40% of the shoppers asked would use a subscription [1], which is evidence of demand before the florist spends money on the service [1]. This helps the owner judge how many flowers to buy and how many staff hours to plan, reducing the chance of waste [1]. The research could also inform the price, if shoppers were asked how much they would pay [1]. However, the sample was taken only on a Saturday morning, so it may miss weekday customers and overstate interest [1]. The research therefore reduces the risk of launching a service nobody wants, but it does not remove it, so the owner could start with a small trial [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a bought market report primary research.
- Dividing by the wrong total in percentage questions.
- Finding a mean by averaging 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 rather than weighting by the number of people.
- Treating a scaled-up estimate as certain.
- Saying research "eliminates" risk.
- Recommending a method without linking it to the decision the owner must make.
