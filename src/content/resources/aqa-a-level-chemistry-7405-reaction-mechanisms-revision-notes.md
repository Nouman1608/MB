## What you must be able to do

- Write **balanced equations** for the steps of a **free-radical** mechanism.
- Use **curly arrows** correctly: from a lone pair or bond to show a bond forming, from a bond to show a bond breaking.
- **Outline mechanisms** by drawing structures and curly arrows for electron-pair movement.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | Step-by-step account of how bonds break and form |
| Radical | Species with an unpaired electron (shown with a dot) |
| Homolytic fission | Each atom takes one electron; forms two radicals |
| Heterolytic fission | One atom takes both electrons; forms ions |
| Nucleophile | Electron-pair donor (OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃) |
| Electrophile | Electron-pair acceptor (H⁺, δ+ H) |
| Carbocation | Positively charged carbon intermediate |

## Bond fission

```
Homolytic:    Cl–Cl → Cl• + Cl•              (UV light)
Heterolytic:  CH₃CH₂–Br → CH₃CH₂⁺ + Br⁻       (polar bond)
```

## Free-radical mechanism: three stages

| Stage | Pattern | Radicals |
|---|---|---|
| Initiation | X–X → 2X• | Two made |
| Propagation | X• + RH → R• + HX; R• + X₂ → RX + X• | One used, one made |
| Termination | X• + X•, R• + X•, R• + R• | Two used, none left |

Worked example, ethane and chlorine:

```
Initiation:    Cl₂ → 2Cl•
Propagation:   Cl• + C₂H₆ → •C₂H₅ + HCl
               •C₂H₅ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + Cl•
Termination:   2Cl• → Cl₂   •C₂H₅ + Cl• → C₂H₅Cl   2•C₂H₅ → C₄H₁₀
```

- Conditions: ultraviolet light.
- Chain reaction: the radical used in propagation 1 is regenerated in propagation 2.
- Curly arrows are not required for radical mechanisms.

## Curly arrow rules

1. An arrow shows movement of an **electron pair**.
2. **Forming a bond:** starts at a **lone pair** (or a bond such as C=C) and ends at the atom that will be bonded.
3. **Breaking a bond:** starts at the **middle of the bond** and ends at the atom taking both electrons.
4. Never start at a positive charge.
5. Draw lone pairs and partial charges (δ+, δ–) where they matter.
6. Balance charge across each step.

## Mechanism types

| Mechanism | Attacking species | Key arrows |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleophilic substitution (halogenoalkane + OH⁻) | Nucleophile | Lone pair on O to δ+ C; C–Br bond to Br |
| Electrophilic addition (alkene + HBr) | Electrophile | C=C to H; H–Br bond to Br; Br⁻ lone pair to C⁺ |
| Elimination (halogenoalkane + OH⁻ in ethanol) | Base | Lone pair on O to H; C–H bond to form C=C; C–Br bond to Br |
| Free-radical substitution (alkane + Cl₂) | Radical | Equations only |

### Nucleophilic substitution, step by step

1. C–Br is polar: C is δ+, Br is δ–.
2. Arrow 1: from the lone pair on the oxygen of OH⁻ to the carbon.
3. Arrow 2: from the C–Br bond to the bromine.
4. Products: alcohol and Br⁻.

### Electrophilic addition, step by step

1. Arrow 1: from the C=C to the hydrogen of H–Br (the hydrogen is δ+).
2. Arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to the bromine.
3. Intermediate: carbocation, CH₃CH₂⁺.
4. Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the carbon with the positive charge.
5. Product: bromoethane.

## Why bonds are polar and where attack occurs

| Bond | Polarity | Attacked by |
|---|---|---|
| C–Br, C–Cl | Carbon δ+ | Nucleophiles (lone pair donors) |
| C=C | Electron-rich region | Electrophiles (electron-pair acceptors) |
| H–Br | Hydrogen δ+ | Acts as an electrophile towards C=C |
| C–H in an alkane | Almost non-polar | Radicals only |

Polarity arises from the difference in electronegativity between the atoms. The more electronegative atom takes the larger share of the bonding pair and carries δ–.

## Writing a mechanism: checklist

1. Draw the full structure of each species in the step.
2. Mark any lone pairs on the attacking species.
3. Mark partial charges on polar bonds.
4. Draw the arrows, starting at lone pairs or bonds.
5. Draw the products of the step, including any charge.
6. Check that charge and atoms balance.
7. Repeat for the next step if there is an intermediate.

## Choosing between substitution, addition and elimination

- Substitution: one group is replaced by another and the carbon skeleton stays saturated.
- Addition: two groups add across a C=C, which becomes a single bond.
- Elimination: a small molecule (such as HBr or H₂O) is removed, creating a C=C.

## Exam traps

- Arrows starting at an atom, not at a lone pair or bond.
- Arrow pointing at the wrong atom (to hydrogen instead of to the δ+ carbon).
- Lone pair missing on OH⁻, CN⁻, Br⁻.
- Missing dot on a radical.
- Propagation steps that do not regenerate a radical.
- Termination step with a radical still on the right.
- Half-headed arrows in polar mechanisms.
- Homolytic fission said to form ions.

## Self-test

1. What is a radical?
2. What type of fission forms radicals?
3. What is needed to start a radical reaction of an alkane with chlorine?
4. Write the initiation step for chlorine.
5. Write a propagation step for methane with chlorine.
6. Why is a termination step not a propagation step?
7. Where does a curly arrow start when a bond forms?
8. Where does a curly arrow start when a bond breaks?
9. Is OH⁻ a nucleophile or an electrophile?
10. What is the intermediate in the addition of HBr to ethene?
11. Why is the carbon in C–Br δ+?
12. Do radical mechanisms require curly arrows in this specification?

### Answers

1. A species with an unpaired electron.
2. Homolytic fission.
3. Ultraviolet light.
4. Cl₂ → 2Cl•.
5. Cl• + CH₄ → •CH₃ + HCl (or •CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•).
6. In termination, two radicals combine and no radical is left.
7. At a lone pair (or another covalent bond).
8. At the middle of the bond.
9. Nucleophile.
10. A carbocation, CH₃CH₂⁺.
11. Bromine is more electronegative than carbon, so it attracts the bonding pair.
12. No.
