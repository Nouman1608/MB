This guide covers **Unit 1 Language and Context**, one of two units
forming the International AS in OxfordAQA International AS and
A-level English Language (9670). The full International A-level adds
Unit 3 and Unit 4 (written) or Unit 4b (a non-exam-assessed language
investigation, the alternative to written Unit 4a).

## Where this fits in 9670

Unit 1 pairs close textual analysis with directed writing, developing
the analytical vocabulary and writing craft that later units --
Language and Society, Language Variation -- build on when applying
language study to social groups and international contexts.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL A-LEVEL ENGLISH LANGUAGE (9670) — UNIT 1

- Section A: Understanding Texts — analysing how writers use language

## How to approach it

Section A rewards precise use of linguistic terminology -- practise
applying a consistent analytical framework (lexis, grammar, phonology,
discourse) to unfamiliar texts rather than describing content in
general terms, since the unit tests the ability to explain how
language choices create meaning, not just identify them. Section B is
strongest when students clearly adapt their register and structure to
the specified audience, purpose and context given in the task, so
practise reading task briefs carefully and planning before writing.
Because Unit 1 establishes the analytical vocabulary used throughout
the rest of the qualification, treat it as a foundation to keep secure
rather than a topic to revise once early on. Reading a wide range of
text types -- articles, speeches, reviews, personal writing -- and
practising identifying their audience, purpose and context before
looking at the language itself builds the habit of thinking about
context first, which is exactly the analytical approach Unit 1 rewards.

## Language and context

The central claim of this unit is that language is never neutral: every text is shaped by, and helps shape, the situation in which it is produced. Analysis therefore always begins with context.

**Context of production** — who wrote it, when, why, for whom, under what constraints. **Context of reception** — who reads it, in what circumstances, with what expectations and prior knowledge. The same words function differently in a private letter and a published memoir.

Three variables organise most analysis:

- **Field** — the subject matter and the specialist lexis it brings.
- **Tenor** — the relationship between participants, which governs formality, politeness and directness.
- **Mode** — spoken, written, or one of the hybrid digital forms that borrow from both.

## The language levels

Analysis must be conducted through frameworks, with accurate terminology:

| Level | Focus |
|---|---|
| Lexis and semantics | Word choice, semantic fields, connotation, formality |
| Grammar and syntax | Word classes, sentence types and length, voice, mood, modality |
| Phonology | Sound patterning, and features of accent in transcripts |
| Graphology | Layout, typography, images, use of space |
| Pragmatics | Implicature, presupposition, politeness, deixis |
| Discourse | Structure, cohesion, openings, closings, turn-taking |

**Modality** repays particular attention: modal verbs (must, might, should) encode certainty and obligation, and are one of the clearest markers of a writer's stance and power position.

## Spoken language

Transcripts require their own toolkit: **adjacency pairs**, turn-taking, interruption and overlap, fillers and hesitation, false starts, repair, discourse markers, back-channelling, and non-fluency features.

Crucially, non-fluency is normal in speech and should not be treated as error. Analyse what it does — hesitation may signal uncertainty, politeness or careful thought.

**Power in interaction** is visible in who controls the topic, who interrupts, who asks and who answers, and who uses imperatives.

## Representation and attitudes

**Representation** is how language constructs a version of people, groups or events. Interrogate naming choices, modifiers, and whether agency is expressed or obscured — a passive construction can delete the actor entirely.

Attitudes to language variation are examinable in themselves: **prescriptivism** treats change and non-standard forms as decline; **descriptivism** records variation without judgement. Standard English carries **overt prestige**; non-standard varieties can carry **covert prestige** within a group.

## Worked example

Analyse: *"Mistakes were made, and lessons have been learned."*

```
Passive voice x2   "were made", "have been learned" -- no agent in either
                   clause; nobody is identified as responsible
Nominalisation     "mistakes", "lessons" turn actions into abstract things,
                   further distancing them from any actor
Present perfect    "have been learned" implies completion, closing the
                   matter without evidence
Parallel structure balanced clauses give a formal, rehearsed quality --
                   marking it as institutional rather than personal speech

Effect: accountability is acknowledged in form while being grammatically
avoided in substance -- characteristic of institutional apology.
```

## Common mistakes

Listing features without explaining effect. Treating non-fluency in speech as error. Analysing content rather than language. Ignoring context of production or reception. Confusing accent (pronunciation) with dialect (grammar and lexis). Presenting prescriptivism as correct rather than as one attitude among others.

## Quick revision checklist

- Analyse context of production and reception, and identify field, tenor and mode.
- Apply all six language levels with accurate terminology.
- Analyse modality as a marker of stance and power.
- Handle spoken transcripts using the appropriate features, without treating them as errors.
- Explain representation through naming, modification and agency.
- Distinguish prescriptivism from descriptivism, and overt from covert prestige.
