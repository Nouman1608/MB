**This guide covers two statements in 3.2.2 Social influence of OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology
(9218), Version 1.1: "Obedience as investigated by Milgram" and "Milgram's Agency theory of social
factors affecting obedience: agency, authority, culture and proximity."** The
[Conformity and Obedience study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-and-obedience/)
gives Milgram's headline result and lists the four elements. This page goes further: the procedure in
detail, the agentic state, each of the four elements with the evidence for it, and evaluation.

## Milgram's obedience study in detail

**Aim.** To find out how far ordinary people would obey an authority figure who told them to harm
another person.

**Participants.** 40 men aged 20 to 50 from the New Haven area of the United States, recruited by a
newspaper advertisement for a study of memory and paid for taking part.

**Procedure.**
1. At Yale University, each participant met an experimenter in a grey lab coat and another
   "participant", Mr Wallace, who was in fact a confederate.
2. A fixed draw made the real participant the **teacher** and Mr Wallace the **learner**. The learner
   was strapped into a chair in the next room with electrodes on his arm.
3. The teacher read word pairs and tested the learner. For each wrong answer, the teacher had to give
   an electric shock, rising by 15 volts each time, on a generator labelled from 15 volts ("slight
   shock") to 450 volts ("XXX").
4. No real shocks were given. The learner's answers and protests followed a script: at 300 volts he
   banged on the wall, and after 315 volts he went silent.
5. If the teacher hesitated, the experimenter used four standard **prods**, in order, from "Please
   continue" to "You have no other choice, you must go on."

**Results.**
- **65%** of participants (26 of 40) continued to the maximum 450 volts.
- **All** participants went to at least 300 volts.
- Many showed extreme tension: sweating, trembling, groaning and nervous laughter; a few had seizures.
- Before the study, people asked to predict the result expected very few to go to the end.

**Conclusion.** Ordinary people will obey orders to harm another person when the order comes from a
legitimate authority, even against their conscience. Milgram developed Agency theory to explain why.

## Agency: the agentic state

Milgram proposed that people can be in one of two states:

| State | What the person feels |
|---|---|
| **Autonomous state** | They act on their own judgement and feel responsible for their actions |
| **Agentic state** | They see themselves as an agent carrying out another person's wishes, so responsibility belongs to that person |

The move from autonomous to agentic is the **agentic shift**. It happens when a person accepts that
someone else is in a position of authority over them. Milgram argued that humans have evolved to work
in **hierarchies**, and that obedience is learned from early childhood at home and at school.

Participants often experienced **moral strain**: anxiety caused by knowing that what they were doing
was wrong while feeling unable to stop. They reduced it with **binding factors**, such as telling
themselves the learner had agreed to take part, or focusing on reading the words correctly instead of
on the learner's distress. Many participants asked "Who is responsible if he is hurt?" and continued
once the experimenter said that he was.

## Authority

People obey when they see the person giving orders as a **legitimate authority**: someone with a
recognised right to give instructions because of their position in a social hierarchy. Signs of
legitimacy include a uniform, a title and an impressive setting.

**Evidence from Milgram's variations**
- **Location.** When the study was moved from Yale to a run-down office building in Bridgeport,
  obedience fell to **47.5%**. The less prestigious setting reduced the authority's legitimacy.
- **Ordinary person giving orders.** When the experimenter was called away and an apparently ordinary
  member of the public took over the orders, obedience fell to **20%**.

**Real-world support.** Hofling and colleagues (1966) arranged for nurses in a hospital to receive a
telephone call from an unknown "doctor" instructing them to give a patient twice the maximum dose of
an unfamiliar drug. 21 of the 22 nurses were prepared to do so before being stopped, showing the power
of a perceived legitimate authority in a real workplace.

## Proximity

**Proximity** means physical or psychological closeness. Two kinds matter.

**Proximity of the victim.** The closer and more visible the learner, the harder it was to ignore his
suffering and the less people obeyed.

| Variation | Obedience to 450 volts |
|---|---|
| Learner in another room (baseline, voice feedback) | 65% |
| Learner in the same room as the teacher | 40% |
| Teacher had to press the learner's hand onto a shock plate | 30% |

**Proximity of the authority.** When the experimenter gave instructions by **telephone** from another
room, obedience fell to **20.5%**, and some participants pretended to give shocks or gave lower ones.
A close authority keeps people in the agentic state; a distant one lets them return to the autonomous
state.

## Culture

**Culture** affects how strongly people are socialised to obey. Societies differ in how much they
value respect for authority and hierarchy, and children learn these values through family, school
and religion. Where obedience to authority is strongly valued, people may enter the agentic state
more readily.

Replications of Milgram's procedure in other countries have produced different obedience rates: for
example, a German replication by Mantell (1971) found a higher rate than Milgram's, and an Australian
replication by Kilham and Mann (1974) a lower one. Procedures differed between replications, so the
differences cannot be put down to culture alone, but they show that obedience levels are not fixed
across societies.

## Worked example: applying Agency theory

**Question.** A new factory worker, Leila, is told by her supervisor to skip a safety check to speed up
production. She feels uncomfortable but does it. When the supervisor is away for a week, she carries
out the checks again. Use Milgram's Agency theory to explain Leila's behaviour. [4]

> Leila sees the supervisor as a **legitimate authority** because of their position in the factory,
> so she shifts into the **agentic state**, seeing herself as carrying out the supervisor's wishes
> **[1]**. She passes **responsibility** for skipping the check to the supervisor, which is why she
> obeys despite feeling uncomfortable (**moral strain**) **[1]**. When the supervisor is away, the
> **proximity of the authority** is reduced **[1]**, so she returns to the **autonomous state** and acts
> on her own judgement, carrying out the checks **[1]**.

## Evaluating Agency theory

**Strengths**
- **Explains the variations.** Changes in proximity and authority produced the changes in obedience the
  theory predicts.
- **Real-world support.** Hofling's nurses obeyed an authority figure in a real hospital setting.
- **Matches participants' words.** Many participants continued once told the experimenter was
  responsible, which fits the agentic state.

**Limitations**
- **Individual differences.** About a third of participants in the baseline study refused to continue,
  although they were in the same situation. A dispositional explanation, such as
  [Adorno's Authoritarian Personality](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-psychology-9218-authoritarian-personality/),
  is needed to explain who disobeys.
- **Description rather than explanation.** It is hard to measure the agentic state independently of
  obedience itself, so the theory can seem circular: people obey because they are agentic, and we know
  they are agentic because they obey.
- **Strain shows participants did not fully hand over responsibility.** Their distress suggests they
  still felt responsible, which does not fit a complete agentic shift.
- **Ethics of the evidence.** Milgram's participants were deceived and some were extremely distressed,
  raising concerns about protection from harm, though they were debriefed afterwards.
- **Sample.** The baseline sample was American men, so generalising to women and other cultures needs
  the replication evidence.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying participants gave real shocks.** No real shocks were given.
- **Mixing up the figures.** 65% is the baseline; 40% same room; 30% touch; 20.5% telephone orders;
  47.5% run-down office.
- **Calling Agency theory dispositional.** It is a social explanation.
- **Listing the four elements without the evidence.** Each element is strongest with a variation or
  study linked to it.
- **Treating proximity as only about the victim.** The authority's proximity matters too.

## Exam technique

- **Describe Milgram's study:** aim, participants, procedure, results, conclusion.
- **Explain an element:** say what it is, how it affects obedience and give the variation that shows it.
- **Apply:** link agentic state, authority and proximity to the details of the scenario.
- **Evaluate:** support from variations and Hofling; limitations from individual differences and
  circularity.
