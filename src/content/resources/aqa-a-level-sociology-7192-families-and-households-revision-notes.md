These notes condense Families and Households for AQA A-level Sociology (7192), from the AQA AS and A-level Sociology (7191, 7192) specification, Version 1.2 (14 October 2021). The topic is section 4.2.2 of the A-level content (also listed as 3.2.2 in the specification-at-a-glance). It is examined in Paper 2: Topics in Sociology, Section A, as one of the four option 1 topics; the specification prints Section A as extended writing, 40 marks.

Test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-families-and-households-practice/). For the full course, see the [AQA A-level Sociology hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/sociology/) and the [7192 checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/sociology/).

## The five content points (4.2.2)

1. Family, social structure and social change, especially the economy and state policies.
2. Marriage, cohabitation, separation, divorce, childbearing and the life course, including the sociology of personal life and family diversity.
3. Gender roles, domestic labour and power relationships.
4. The nature of childhood and the changing status of children.
5. UK demographic trends since 1900: birth rates, death rates, family size, life expectancy, ageing population, migration and globalisation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nuclear family | Two generations: parents and their dependent children |
| Extended family | Nuclear family plus other kin, such as grandparents |
| Beanpole family | Many generations but few relatives in each (Brannen) |
| Neo-conventional family | Nuclear family in which both partners work (Chester) |
| Reconstituted family | Family where at least one partner has a child from a previous relationship |
| Functional fit | Family structure adapts to the needs of the economy (Parsons) |
| Joint / segregated conjugal roles | Shared tasks and leisure versus separate, gendered tasks (Bott) |
| Dual burden / triple shift | Paid work plus housework; plus emotion work (Duncombe and Marsden) |
| Pure relationship | Relationship kept only while both partners are satisfied (Giddens) |
| Personal life | Approach that starts from the relationships people see as meaningful (Smart) |
| Age patriarchy | Adult domination of children (Gittins) |
| Total fertility rate | Average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime |
| Dependency ratio | Dependants compared with the working-age population |
| Global care chain | Linked care work across countries, often by migrant women (Ehrenreich and Hochschild) |

## Studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Murdock (1949) | 250 societies | Nuclear family universal; four functions | Ignores diversity and conflict inside families |
| Parsons (1955) | Functional fit | Nuclear family suits industrial economy | Laslett found nuclear households before industrialisation |
| Engels (1884) | Marxist history | Monogamy protects inheritance of property | Little direct evidence; neglects gender as a separate inequality |
| Zaretsky (1976) | Marxist | Family a refuge from capitalism, but an illusion | Assumes all families experience capitalism alike |
| Donzelot | State and family | Professionals carry out surveillance, especially of poor families | Underplays benefits of support for families |
| Young and Willmott (1973) | Symmetrical family | Roles becoming more equal | Weak measure of "helping" (Oakley) |
| Oakley (1974) | 40 London housewives | Little symmetry; housework is women's work | Small sample from one city |
| Gershuny (1994) | Lagged adaptation | Men's share rises slowly as women work more | Change may stall at a lower level |
| Crompton and Lyonette (2008) | Cultural vs material | Two competing explanations of unequal housework | Both may operate together |
| Edgell (1980) | Middle-class couples | Husbands had final say on very important decisions | Dated; may differ today |
| Pahl (1989) | Money management | Allowance system versus pooling | Pooling may hide unequal control |
| Dunne (1999) | Lesbian couples | More equal division of labour | Small, specific sample |
| Dobash and Dobash (1979) | Violence against wives | Reflects patriarchal power | Wilkinson (1996) points to stress from inequality |
| Rapoport and Rapoport (1982) | Diversity | Five types: organisational, cultural, class, life-stage, cohort | Chester says diversity is overstated |
| Smart (2007) | Personal life | Meaningful ties beyond blood and marriage | May underplay structural limits |
| Ariès (1960) | Paintings and diaries | Childhood a modern idea | Pollock (1983) found earlier evidence of childhood |
| Postman (1982) | Television | Childhood disappearing | Opie (1993): children's culture survives |
| Pilcher (1995) | Separateness | Childhood a distinct, protected status | Not all children are protected equally |
| McKeown (1976) | Death rate | Better nutrition more than medicine | Public health and medicine also mattered |
| Phillipson (1982) | Ageing | Older people marginalised under capitalism | Ignores active, high-income older people |

| Date | Event | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|

| 1964 | Peak of the post-war baby boom | Large cohort now approaching pension age |

| 1972 | School leaving age raised to 16 | Longer, more separate childhood |

| 2013 | Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act | Legal recognition of same-sex marriage |

| 2022 | No-fault divorce in effect from 6 April | Divorce without proving fault |

ONS figures worth knowing: 80,057 divorces in England and Wales in 2022, the lowest since 1971; UK life expectancy at birth of 78.6 years for males and 82.6 years for females in 2020 to 2022 (national life tables).

## Linking to the core themes

- **Socialisation, culture and identity:** primary socialisation (Parsons); gender scripts in domestic labour; childhood as a constructed identity; migrant families keeping cultural traditions across borders.
- **Social differentiation, power and stratification:** patriarchal power (feminists); class and the family (Marxists); age patriarchy (Gittins); unequal care burdens in an ageing population.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Household vs family.** A household is people living at one address; a family is people related by kinship, marriage or adoption.
- **Birth rate vs total fertility rate.** The birth rate depends on the age structure; the TFR measures women's fertility directly.
- **Separation vs divorce.** Separation is not recorded in divorce statistics, so divorce figures understate breakdown.
- **Cultural vs material explanations** of domestic labour (Crompton and Lyonette).
- **March of progress vs conflict views** of childhood (and of domestic roles).
- **Structure vs action.** Functionalism and Marxism explain families from the structure; personal life and life course approaches start from meaning and agency.
- **Modernist vs postmodern views of diversity.** Chester sees a dominant pattern; Stacey and Giddens see choice and fluidity.

## Mini essay plan

*Evaluate sociological explanations of the changing status of children since 1900.*

1. **Introduction.** Define childhood as socially constructed; set out the march-of-progress view against the conflict view.
2. **March of progress.** Laws (Education Act 1944, school leaving age of 16 from 1972, Children Act 1989, UN Convention on the Rights of the Child 1989); lower infant mortality; child-centredness. Use Pilcher on separateness.
3. **Evaluate.** Gittins on age patriarchy; Firestone on protection as control; inequalities of class, gender and ethnicity.
4. **Is childhood disappearing?** Postman and Palmer versus Opie. Link to the media and to consumption.
5. **Comparative view.** Benedict and Punch show childhood varies across cultures; challenges a single Western model.
6. **Judgement.** Children's legal status has risen, but control and inequality remain; weigh which view the evidence supports most.

## Quick self-test

1. Name Murdock's four functions of the family.
2. What are Parsons' two irreducible functions?
3. Which act made divorce possible on the grounds of irretrievable breakdown, in force from 1971?
4. When did no-fault divorce come into effect in England and Wales?
5. List the Rapoports' five types of family diversity.
6. What is the triple shift, and who identified it?
7. What did Oakley criticise about Young and Willmott's measure of symmetry?
8. What is the difference between allowance and pooling systems (Pahl)?
9. What evidence did Ariès use, and who challenged him?
10. Give two reasons for the fall in the birth rate since 1900.
11. What does Cohen mean by "denizens"?
12. What is a global care chain?

### Answers

1. Sexual, reproductive, economic and educational.
2. Primary socialisation of children and stabilisation of adult personalities.
3. The Divorce Reform Act 1969.
4. 6 April 2022, under the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020.
5. Organisational, cultural, social class, life-stage and cohort.
6. Paid work, domestic work and emotion work; Duncombe and Marsden (1995).
7. A husband counted as helping if he did a task at least once a week, which overstated equality.
8. Allowance: the earner gives the partner a sum for household costs. Pooling: both can access income and share spending.
9. Paintings and diaries; Pollock (1983) used diaries to argue childhood existed earlier.
10. Any two: changing position of women; lower infant mortality; children became an economic cost; child-centredness.
11. Privileged foreign nationals welcomed into a country who are not citizens.
12. A chain of care work across countries, as migrant women care for others' families while relatives care for their own children.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a study without saying what it found or how it was done.
- Using "the family" as if there were only one type, ignoring class, ethnic and life-stage diversity.
- Mixing up Murdock's four functions with Parsons' two.
- Giving divorce statistics without explaining them, or explaining them only through law.
- Treating time-use figures as the whole story on domestic labour, missing decision-making and money.
- Answering a childhood question with only historical evidence and nothing on today.
- Leaving out migration and globalisation from demography answers, even though the specification names them.
- Giving one side on contested debates about welfare, family policy or migration.
- Ending with a summary rather than a judgement that weighs the evidence.
