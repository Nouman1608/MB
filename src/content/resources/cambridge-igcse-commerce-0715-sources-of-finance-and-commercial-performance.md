Every business needs money to start, to run day to day and to grow. This money is called **finance**. Section 5.4 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus has three parts: **ways in which entrepreneurs and commercial enterprises raise finance** (5.4.1), **commercial calculations** (5.4.2) and **improving commercial performance** (5.4.3). This guide covers all three, with two worked examples and an annotated answer.

## What is entrepreneurship, and what financial terms must you know? (5.4.1)

An **entrepreneur** is a person who sets up a business, takes the risks and organises the resources. **Entrepreneurship** is the process of doing this: spotting an opportunity, raising money and starting an enterprise.

The syllabus also lists financial terms you must be able to use and explain.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sales** | Goods or services sold to customers |
| **Revenue** | Money received from sales (also called turnover) |
| **Profit** | What is left after costs have been taken from revenue |
| **Working capital** | The money available for day-to-day running: current assets minus current liabilities |
| **Assets** | Things the business owns, such as cash, stock, buildings and vehicles |
| **Liabilities** | Money the business owes, such as loans and bills |
| **Interest** | The cost of borrowing money, or the reward for lending it |
| **Capital** | Money invested in the business by owners or lenders |
| **Cash discount** | A reduction in price for paying quickly or in cash |
| **Trade discount** | A reduction in price given by a seller to another business, often for buying in bulk |

## What are the sources of short-term finance?

**Short-term finance** is needed for less than about a year, to pay everyday costs such as wages, stock and bills.

| Source | What it is | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade credit** | Buying goods now and paying later | No interest if paid on time; helps cash flow | Supplier may refuse future credit if late; may lose cash discount |
| **Debt factoring** | Selling unpaid invoices to a factor for cash now | Quick cash; less time chasing debts | The factor takes a fee, so the business gets less than the invoice value |
| **Hire purchase** | Deposit plus instalments, owning the item at the end | Spreads cost; use item at once | Total cost is higher than cash price; item may be taken back if payments stop |
| **Leasing** | Renting equipment for regular payments | Low start-up cost; maintenance often included | Never owned; payments continue; can cost more over time |
| **Sale and leaseback** | Selling an asset, such as a building, and renting it back | Releases cash while keeping use of the asset | Loses ownership and pays rent; may lose future rise in value |
| **Overdraft** | Spending more than the balance, up to a limit | Flexible; interest only on the amount used | Interest is high; bank can withdraw it |
| **Selling assets** | Selling unused equipment or vehicles | No interest; quick cash | Can only be done once; may sell below value |
| **Owner's savings** | The owner puts in personal money | No interest or repayment | Limited amount; owner risks losing it |
| **Retained profit** | Profit kept in the business, not paid out | No cost or interest; no outside control | Only available once profit is made; owners get less income |

## What are the sources of long-term finance?

**Long-term finance** is needed for more than a year, for buying premises, machinery or for expansion.

| Source | What it is | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary shares** | Money from shareholders who own part of the company and receive dividends that vary | No repayment; no fixed payment | Owners share control; dividends reduce profit available |
| **Preference shares** | Shares with a fixed dividend paid before ordinary shareholders | Fixed, predictable payment; usually no voting rights, so control is kept | Dividend must be paid first, even in a poor year |
| **Loans** | Fixed sum from a bank repaid with interest over a set time | Owners keep control; interest can be planned | Interest must be paid even if profits are low; security may be needed |
| **Debentures** | Long-term loans to a company, repaid on a set date with fixed interest | Fixed cost; owners keep control | Interest must be paid; the company must repay the amount |
| **Venture capital** | Investment by specialist firms in risky or new businesses in return for a share | Large sums; expert advice | Investors take a share of ownership and profit |
| **Crowdfunding** | Many people give small amounts, usually through a website | Raises money without a bank; tests interest in the idea | May not reach the target; time spent on promotion; may have to give rewards |

## How do you choose the right source of finance?

The syllabus asks for the **appropriateness** of the choice in given situations. Think about:

- **How long** is the money needed? Use short-term sources for short needs and long-term sources for long needs.
- **How much** is needed?
- **How quickly** is it needed?
- **Cost**: interest, fees or loss of profit share.
- **Control**: will owners have to share decisions?
- **Risk and security**: can the business meet repayments? Is there anything to offer as security?
- **Type of business**: a new, risky business may find it hard to borrow, but might attract venture capital or crowdfunding.

| Situation | Suitable source | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A shop needs $500 to buy stock before payday | Overdraft or trade credit | Short, small, flexible |
| A firm needs a new van | Hire purchase, leasing or a loan | Medium term, spread cost |
| A start-up with an unusual idea and no assets | Crowdfunding or venture capital | Banks may refuse a loan |
| A company wants to build a factory | Debentures, ordinary shares or a long-term loan | Large sum over many years |
| A firm waiting for large customer invoices to be paid | Debt factoring | Releases cash tied up in debtors |
| A profitable firm that wants no debt | Retained profit | No interest and no outside control |

## What are the commercial calculations? (5.4.2)

The syllabus gives six formulae.

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Selling price per unit x number of units sold |
| Gross profit | Revenue - cost of sales |
| Gross profit margin (%) | Gross profit / Revenue x 100 |
| Net profit (profit for the year) | Gross profit - expenses |
| Working capital | Current assets - current liabilities |
| Mark-up percentage | (Average selling price - unit cost) / unit cost, then x 100 for a percentage |

**Cost of sales** is the cost of buying the stock that was sold. **Expenses** are the running costs such as rent, wages and advertising. If the answer to net profit is negative, it is a **net loss**.

Margin and mark-up are different. **Margin** compares profit with the **selling price** (revenue). **Mark-up** compares profit per unit with the **cost**. Mark-up is always the larger figure for the same product.

## Worked example 1: the six calculations (invented figures)

*A shop sells 1,800 phone cases at $25 each. Cost of sales is $27,000. Expenses are $9,450. Current assets are $14,200 and current liabilities are $9,600.*

1. Revenue: 25 x 1,800 = **$45,000**.
2. Gross profit: 45,000 - 27,000 = **$18,000**.
3. Gross profit margin: 18,000 / 45,000 x 100 = **40%**.
4. Net profit: 18,000 - 9,450 = **$8,550**.
5. Working capital: 14,200 - 9,600 = **$4,600**.
6. Unit cost: 27,000 / 1,800 = $15. Mark-up: (25 - 15) / 15 x 100 = **66.7%** (to one decimal place).

**Judgement:** a 40% margin and positive working capital are healthy. The mark-up of 66.7% looks bigger than the margin because it is measured against the lower cost figure, not the selling price.

## How can commercial performance be improved? (5.4.3)

The syllabus lists methods to improve **sales turnover, profit, mark-up and working capital**, for example **reducing expenses, increasing mark-up, changing prices, improving advertising and introducing new products**.

| Method | Effect | Possible drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce expenses | Raises net profit | Cutting staff or advertising may reduce sales or service quality |
| Increase mark-up | Raises gross profit per unit | Customers may buy elsewhere |
| Change prices | Higher prices raise profit per unit; lower prices may raise sales | Demand may fall, or profit per unit may fall |
| Improve advertising | Raises awareness and turnover | Costs money, and the effect is uncertain |
| Introduce new products | Attracts new customers | Costs to develop and stock; may not sell |
| Negotiate better trade discounts | Lowers cost of sales | Suppliers may refuse |
| Hold less stock or collect debts faster | Frees cash, which improves working capital | Risk of stock shortages or upsetting customers |

## Worked example 2: changing the price (invented figures)

*Using the phone-case shop from Worked example 1, the owner raises the price from $25 to $27 and expects to sell the same 1,800 cases. Cost of sales and expenses stay the same.*

1. New revenue: 27 x 1,800 = **$48,600**.
2. New gross profit: 48,600 - 27,000 = **$21,600**.
3. New gross profit margin: 21,600 / 48,600 x 100 = **44.4%**.
4. New net profit: 21,600 - 9,450 = **$12,150**.
5. New mark-up: (27 - 15) / 15 x 100 = **80%**.

**Judgement:** net profit rises by $3,600 (12,150 - 8,550). The result depends on customers still buying 1,800 cases. If demand falls a lot, the higher price could reduce total profit.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: A new online craft business has no assets and no profit history. Explain one source of finance that it could use and one that it should not use.* **[4]**

> **Crowdfunding** could suit it [1], because many people give small sums through a website, so the business does not need assets or a profit record, and it tests whether customers like its products [1]. It should not use a **bank loan** [1], because lenders usually want security and a record of earnings, which the business does not yet have, so it would probably be refused or pay high interest [1].

Why it scores: each source is named and linked to the facts about the business.

## What are the common mistakes in 5.4?

- Giving the same source for every situation without matching the **time**, **amount** and **risk**.
- Mixing up **gross profit** (before expenses) and **net profit** (after expenses).
- Using **cost price** instead of **selling price** as the base for gross profit margin.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100 for a percentage.
- Calling working capital "money in the bank". It is current assets minus current liabilities.
- Listing ways to improve performance without saying which measure they improve.

## Exam technique

- **State** a source: name it.
- **Explain** a source: say what it is and why it suits the case.
- **Calculate**: write the formula, substitute, and give the answer with the unit.
- **Justify** a choice: give a decision linked to time, amount, cost or control.
- **Evaluate** a method of improvement: weigh the benefit against a possible drawback.
