**This guide covers content area 3.1.2.8 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Islamiat (9237), Festivals and Commemorations in Islam: "The origins and importance of Eid-Al-Adha, Eid-Ul-Fitr and Ashura and how they are observed."** It sets out where each occasion comes from, what Muslims do and why it matters. It also shows how to use the prescribed passage, a hadith in Sunan Ibn Majah about the two Eids.

## The Islamic calendar

Islamic dates follow the lunar calendar, which began with the Hijrah. A month starts when the new moon is sighted, and the year is shorter than the solar year by about ten or eleven days. This is why the three occasions move earlier through the seasons from year to year. Muharram is the first month, Ramadhan the ninth, Shawwal the tenth and Dhul Hijjah the twelfth.

## Two festivals and one commemoration

Islam has two festivals (*Eids*), and the Prophet (pbuh) is reported to have said that these two days replaced the earlier celebrations of Madinah. Ashura is different. It is a day of remembrance, and it is not marked as a feast.

| Occasion | Date | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Eid-ul-Fitr | 1 Shawwal | Festival that ends Ramadhan |
| Eid-ul-Adha | 10 Dhul Hijjah | Festival of sacrifice, during Hajj |
| Ashura | 10 Muharram | Commemoration |

## Eid-ul-Fitr

### Origins

*Fitr* means "breaking the fast". The festival began in Madinah after the Hijrah, in the same period that fasting in Ramadhan was made a duty (2:183-185). It marks the end of a month of fasting, prayer and Qur'an recitation.

### How it is observed

- **Sighting the moon.** The end of Ramadhan is announced when the new moon is sighted, or when thirty days are complete.
- **Zakah al-Fitr.** Before the Eid prayer each Muslim with enough means gives a set amount of food or its value for the poor, so that nobody is left hungry on Eid. It is separate from the yearly Zakah.
- **Preparation.** Muslims wash, wear their best clothes, use perfume and eat something sweet, often dates, before going to pray. This is the Sunnah. They say the *takbir* ("Allahu Akbar") on the way.
- **The Eid prayer.** Muslims gather in a large mosque or open ground for a short congregational prayer of two rak'ahs. The sermon comes after the prayer. This differs from Friday prayer, where the sermon comes first. The passage says the same: "He started with the prayer before the sermon."
- **Celebration.** Families visit one another, exchange greetings of "Eid Mubarak", share meals and give gifts and money to children. Many make a point of visiting the sick or lonely and forgiving past quarrels.

### Importance

- **Thanks to Allah** for the strength to complete Ramadhan.
- **Joy and reward.** Muslims believe fasting is rewarded by Allah.
- **Care for the poor.** Zakah al-Fitr makes sure the festival belongs to everyone.
- **Unity.** The community prays together as equals.

## Eid-ul-Adha

### Origins

*Adha* relates to sacrifice. The festival remembers the Prophet Ibrahim (AS), who was commanded by Allah in a dream to sacrifice his son Ismail (AS). Both showed complete obedience. As Ibrahim was about to carry out the command, Allah called to him and replaced the sacrifice with a ram (37:102-107). The lesson is submission to Allah and trust that He is merciful. Eid-ul-Adha is held on the day after the Day of Arafat during Hajj, and Muslims throughout the world share in the joy of the pilgrims.

### How it is observed

- **Eid prayer.** The same two-rak'ah congregational prayer in the morning, followed by a sermon, with the takbir recited.
- **Qurbani (sacrifice).** Muslims with the means sacrifice an animal such as a sheep, goat, cow or camel. Large animals can be shared by up to seven people. The animal must be healthy and the sacrifice done in the name of Allah. Many arrange for it to be done on their behalf.
- **Sharing the meat.** The meat is commonly divided into three parts: one for the family, one for relatives and friends, and one for the poor. This turns the festival into a means of feeding the community.
- **Celebration.** Families dress up, visit one another and share meals over the days that follow, which are known as the days of Tashriq.

### Importance

- **Obedience and sacrifice.** Muslims remember that they must put Allah first, even before what they love.
- **Generosity.** Meat reaches the poor who may rarely eat it.
- **Global unity.** The Eid is celebrated everywhere while pilgrims complete Hajj.
- **Taqwa.** The Qur'an says that it is not the flesh or blood of the animal that reaches Allah, but the piety of the giver (22:37).

## Ashura

### What it is

Ashura falls on the 10th of Muharram, the first month of the Islamic year. It is a day of remembrance, and Muslims mark it in different ways.

### Sunni observance

- **Origin: Musa (AS).** According to a hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari, the Prophet (pbuh) arrived in Madinah and found the Jews of the city fasting on this day because Allah saved Musa (AS) and the Israelites from Pharaoh. The Prophet (pbuh) said that Muslims had more right to Musa than they did, and he fasted and told his companions to fast.
- **How it is observed.** Muslims fast on the 10th, often adding the 9th or the 11th so as to be different from the others. The fast is recommended, not compulsory, and is believed to forgive the sins of the previous year.
- **Importance.** It reminds Muslims of Allah's help to prophets and of gratitude.

### Shia observance

- **Origin: Karbala.** For Shia Muslims, Ashura is the day on which Hazrat Husayn (RA), the grandson of the Prophet (pbuh), was killed at Karbala in 61 AH (680 CE) with his family and companions. He refused to give his allegiance to a ruler he considered unjust.
- **How it is observed.** The first ten days of Muharram are a time of mourning. Communities hold gatherings (*majalis*) at which the story of Karbala is recited, with processions in many places, and charity and food are given out. Many wear black.
- **Importance.** The day teaches standing up for justice and truth even at great cost, and loyalty to the family of the Prophet (pbuh).

Both communities treat the day with respect and sorrow. For an exam answer, give the origin, one way of observing it and its importance for each tradition, and describe both with respect.

## The prescribed passage: Sunan Ibn Majah 1722

The hadith reports that Umar bin al-Khattab (RA) said, "The Messenger of Allah forbade fasting on these two days, the Day of Fitr and the Day of Adha. As for the Day of Fitr, it is the day when you break your fast, and on the Day of Adha you eat the meat of your sacrifices."

- **Fasting is forbidden on both days.** They are days for celebrating, not fasting.
- **Eid-ul-Fitr** is when "you break your fast", the end of Ramadhan.
- **Eid-ul-Adha** is when "you eat the meat of your sacrifices", linking it to Qurbani.
- **The prayer comes "before the sermon"**, showing how Eid is observed.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain **two** ways in which Muslims observe Eid-ul-Adha. **[4]**

> Muslims offer an animal as Qurbani [1], remembering Ibrahim's readiness to obey Allah, and this shows submission to Allah [1]. They share the meat with family, friends and the poor [1], which spreads generosity so that those with little can celebrate too [1].

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up the two Eids.** Fitr ends Ramadhan; Adha remembers Ibrahim's sacrifice.
- **Saying Ashura is a happy festival.** It is a day of remembrance, and Shia Muslims mourn.
- **Describing only one tradition for Ashura.** Be ready to describe the Sunni fast and the Shia mourning.
- **Forgetting the origin.** The specification asks for origins and for importance, as well as how they are observed.
- **Writing that Muslims fast on Eid.** It is forbidden, as the passage states.

## Exam technique

For **explain** questions, give an action, then the reason. In a 6-mark question add a source of authority, such as 2:183-185 for Fitr, 37:102-107 for Adha or the Ibn Majah hadith for the rule against fasting. For the 12-mark question, use the words "break your fast" and "eat the meat of your sacrifices" to link the passage to the meaning of each festival, and weigh whether the celebrations or the acts of worship matter more.
