## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between structural (macro) and social action (micro) theories, naming an example of each. **[4]**

**2.** Distinguish between positivism and interpretivism. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain how a positivist and an interpretivist would each approach the study of suicide, referring to Durkheim and to Douglas or Atkinson. **[8]**

**4.** Explain the practical, ethical and theoretical factors influencing a sociologist's choice of research method. **[9]**

**5.** Explain **three** criticisms of functionalism. **[6]**

**6.** Evaluate the view that sociology can and should be a science. **[12]**

**7.** Distinguish intergenerational from intragenerational social mobility, and explain the cycle of deprivation. **[6]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by the "dual burden" and "triple shift" in relation to gender inequality, and outline the postmodernist view that class identity has fragmented. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. Structural (macro)** theories examine **society as a whole and see individuals as shaped by social structures**, e.g. functionalism or Marxism [1] [1]. **Social action (micro)** theories start from the **individual and the meanings people attach to their actions**, seeing society as constructed through interaction, e.g. symbolic interactionism [1] [1].

**2. Positivism** holds that society can be studied using the **methods of the natural sciences**, seeking objective, quantitative data and general laws of cause and effect [1] [1]. **Interpretivism** holds that people are **conscious beings who act on meanings**, so research must uncover **verstehen — subjective understanding — through qualitative methods** [1] [1].

**3. Positivist (Durkheim):** treated suicide as a **social fact with social causes** [1]; used **official statistics from different countries and groups**, looking for correlations [1]; found suicide rates varied with **levels of social integration and moral regulation**, producing four types and thus a general law relating suicide to society rather than individual psychology [1] [1].
**Interpretivist (Douglas / Atkinson):** argued the **statistics are not objective facts but the outcome of decisions made by coroners** [1]. **Douglas** held that suicide has different **meanings in different cultures**, so cases must be studied qualitatively through case studies and personal documents to recover the meaning of the act [1] [1]. **Atkinson** went further, arguing that suicide statistics tell us only about the **taken-for-granted assumptions coroners use to classify a death** — so Durkheim was studying coroners, not suicides [1].

**4. Practical factors:** the **time and funding available**, the **size and accessibility of the sample**, and the **researcher's own skills** — a lone researcher with a small grant cannot run a national survey [1] [1] [1].
**Ethical factors:** the need for **informed consent, protection from harm, confidentiality and the right to withdraw** [1]; covert methods raise particular problems, and research on vulnerable groups may require extra safeguards [1] [1].
**Theoretical factors:** a **positivist will prefer methods generating reliable, quantitative, representative data** such as questionnaires and official statistics [1]; an **interpretivist will prefer methods generating valid, qualitative data** such as unstructured interviews and participant observation [1]; the researcher's theoretical position therefore largely determines which methods appear appropriate at all [1].

**5.** Any three, 2 marks each: it is **too deterministic**, presenting individuals as puppets shaped entirely by society with no free will or capacity to resist [1] [1]. It is **teleological** — it explains institutions by their effects, arguing the family exists because society needs it, which is not a genuine causal explanation [1] [1]. It **cannot adequately explain conflict and change**, presenting society as harmonious and consensual when it is marked by inequality and struggle [1] [1]. It is **ideologically conservative**, justifying the existing order by presenting inequality as functional and inevitable [1] [1].

**6. For sociology as a science:** **positivists** argue society consists of **observable social facts external to the individual**, which can be measured and correlated [1] [1]; Durkheim's work on suicide demonstrated that **statistical patterns and general laws can be established** [1]; sociology uses **systematic, replicable methods, hypotheses and evidence**, distinguishing it from common sense [1]; **Popper** argued sociology can be scientific provided its statements are **falsifiable and open to testing** [1].
**Against:** **interpretivists** argue that people have **consciousness and act on meanings**, unlike the objects of natural science, so behaviour cannot be reduced to cause and effect [1] [1]; **laboratory experiments are impossible** for practical and ethical reasons, so the key scientific method is unavailable [1]; the sociologist is **part of the society being studied**, so complete objectivity is unattainable and values inevitably influence the choice of topic and interpretation [1]; **Kuhn** argued sociology lacks a single agreed **paradigm**, having many competing perspectives, and is therefore pre-scientific [1].
**Judgement:** much depends on **what is meant by "science"** [1]. If science requires laboratory experiment and universal laws, sociology cannot qualify [1]. If it means **systematic, evidence-based, self-critical enquiry open to falsification**, sociology can meet the standard [1]. Whether it *should* aspire to science is a separate question — some argue the attempt sacrifices the understanding of meaning that is sociology's distinctive contribution [1].

**7. Intergenerational** mobility compares an individual's social position with that of their parents [1]; **intragenerational** mobility compares stages within a person's own career, such as being promoted over time [1]. The **cycle of deprivation** describes how disadvantage reproduces across generations: poor housing and diet lead to poor health and educational outcomes, which lead to low-paid, insecure work, which reproduces poverty in the next generation, restarting the cycle [1] [1] [1] [1].

**8.** The **dual burden** refers to women who take on paid employment while remaining primarily responsible for domestic labour and childcare, effectively working two jobs [1] [1]. The **triple shift** extends this further to include a third form of unpaid work — **emotion work**, managing the family's emotional wellbeing — alongside paid work and domestic labour [1] [1]. **Postmodernists** argue that class, gender and national identity have **fragmented**: identity is increasingly **chosen and fluid** rather than fixed by structural position, and grand theories built around a single structural factor such as class no longer adequately explain identity in a media-saturated, consumer-driven society [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating positivism and interpretivism as methods rather than epistemologies.
- Describing Durkheim's study without the concept of social facts.
- Giving practical factors when theoretical ones are asked for.
- Not defining "science" before judging whether sociology qualifies.
- Discussing social mobility or the cycle of deprivation without naming the specific mechanism linking one stage to the next.
- Treating postmodernism's fragmentation thesis as simply "class no longer matters" rather than as a specific claim about identity becoming chosen and fluid.
