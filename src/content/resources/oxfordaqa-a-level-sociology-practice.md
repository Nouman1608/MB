## Section A: Families (40 marks)

**Question 1** [4 marks]

Identify and briefly describe **two** types of family structure found in
contemporary societies, other than the conventional nuclear family.

**Question 2** [6 marks]

Outline **one** difference between a functionalist perspective and a
Marxist perspective on the role of the family in society.

**Question 3** [8 marks]

Explain **two** demographic changes that have affected family life over
the last 50 years.

**Question 4** [10 marks]

Explain, using concepts from the specification, **two** ways in which
family diversity has increased in recent decades. Your answer should
refer to named sociologists or named concepts where relevant.

**Question 5** [12 marks]

"The family is losing its traditional functions to other institutions."
Evaluate this view, drawing on at least two sociological perspectives.

---
## Answers: Section A

**A1** [4 marks — 2 marks per type identified and briefly described]

Award 2 marks per named type with a brief accurate description, up to a
maximum of 4. Acceptable types include: lone-parent families; reconstituted
(step-) families; same-sex families; extended families (including
multi-generational households); single-person households; cohabiting-couple
families. A bare name with no description scores 1 mark for that type.

**A2** [6 marks — up to 3 marks per perspective]

Functionalist (e.g. Parsons): the family performs necessary functions for
society and its members — primary socialisation and the "stabilisation of
adult personalities" — and industrialisation produced a nuclear family
suited to a geographically and socially mobile workforce. Marxist (e.g.
Engels/Zaretsky): the family serves capitalism's interests rather than
society's as a whole — reproducing labour power, absorbing workers'
frustration with capitalism ("a unit of consumption"), and transmitting
private property through inheritance. Award full marks only where the
difference is explicitly drawn out, not just two descriptions placed
side by side.

**A3** [8 marks — up to 4 marks per change, explained not just named]

Two from: rising divorce rates (linked to changes in the law and reduced
stigma); increased life expectancy (more multi-generational and "beanpole"
families); declining birth rates and rising average age at first birth;
increased cohabitation and delayed or declining marriage rates; increased
lone-parent households. Marks for explanation of the change's effect on
family life, not just the statistical trend itself.

---
**A4** [10 marks — up to 5 marks per way, using named concepts]

Two from: **individualisation** (Smart) — family and personal life
increasingly shaped by individual choice and negotiation rather than
fixed roles; **transnational families** — members maintaining close ties
and obligations across national borders, often sustained by migration and
communications technology; the **"triple shift"** — women in dual-earner
households managing paid work, domestic work and emotion work; **"boomerang
families"** — adult children returning to live with parents, often for
economic reasons. Full marks require the named concept applied to explain
the *increase* in diversity, not just defined in isolation.

**A5** [12 marks — levelled response]

Indicative content: functionalists (Parsons, Fletcher) argue the family
retains its essential functions — primary socialisation and stabilisation
of adult personalities — even as some functions (education, welfare,
production) have been taken on by other institutions ("functional
fitness"). Marxist and feminist critiques (Engels, Oakley) argue the
family has not "lost" functions so much as taken on ideological ones that
serve capitalism and patriarchy — reproducing labour power and absorbing
emotional strain that would otherwise fall on the state or employers.
Postmodern approaches (e.g. Stacey's "divorce-extended family") suggest
the question of "losing functions" assumes a fixed, singular family form
that no longer describes lived reality. A strong answer weighs these
perspectives against each other and reaches a supported judgement rather
than simply listing them. Levels-based mark scheme:

- Level 1 (1–4): Undeveloped, descriptive, limited range.
- Level 2 (5–8): Explained points from more than one perspective; limited
  evaluation.
- Level 3 (9–12): Sustained analysis and evaluation across perspectives
  with a supported conclusion.

## Section B: Research methods (20 marks)

**Question 6** [6 marks: 2 + 2 + 2]

(a) Define the term "unstructured interview". [2]

(b) State **one** strength of using official statistics to research family
life. [2]

(c) State **one** weakness of using covert participant observation to
research family life. [2]

**Question 7** [14 marks]

**Item C**

*A sociologist wants to investigate the rise of multi-generational
households, in which grandparents, parents and adult children share one
home. National survey data show the proportion of households containing
three or more adult generations has grown steadily over the last fifteen
years. The sociologist is considering how best to research the reasons
families give for living this way, and the effects it has on relationships
within the household.*

(a) Using **Item C**, identify **one** trend in multi-generational
household formation. [2]

(b) Suggest **two** reasons, drawing on material from this unit, why
multi-generational households might have become more common. [4]

(c) Using material from **Item C** and elsewhere, assess **one** claim
that could be made about the effects of multi-generational living on
family relationships. [4]

(d) Evaluate **one** methodological issue the sociologist would face in
researching the reasons families give for living in a multi-generational
household. [4]

---
## Answers: Section B

**A6**

(a) [2 marks] An interview with no fixed set of questions or wording,
where the interviewer explores topics conversationally and can follow up
on the respondent's answers in an order not decided in advance. (1 mark
for a partial/vague definition; 2 for a clear, complete one.)

(b) [2 marks] Any one accurate strength, e.g. official statistics cover
very large, often whole-population samples, so findings on trends such as
divorce or household composition are highly representative and allow
comparison over time (1 mark for a bare assertion, 2 for a developed
point).

(c) [2 marks] Any one accurate weakness, e.g. covert observation raises
ethical problems because participants cannot give informed consent to
being studied, which can also make it difficult to record data openly
without breaking cover (1 mark for a bare assertion, 2 for a developed
point).

**A7**

(a) [2 marks] One trend correctly identified from Item C, e.g. "the
proportion of households with three or more adult generations has grown
steadily over the last fifteen years." Award 2 marks for a trend stated
in the item's own terms; 1 mark for a vaguer restatement.

---
(b) [4 marks — 2 marks per reason] Two from: rising housing costs making
independent living harder for young adults; increased life expectancy
meaning more grandparents are alive and able to co-reside; economic
pressures on families following job loss or the cost of living; cultural
or religious traditions in some communities that favour multi-generational
living; the need for informal childcare or eldercare within the family.
Award 2 marks for each reason that is explained, 1 for a reason merely
named.

(c) [4 marks] Assessment of one plausible claim (e.g. that multi-generational
living increases informal support and reduces isolation, or alternatively
that it increases tension over privacy and authority within the household),
using the item's data as a starting point and bringing in material from
elsewhere in the unit (e.g. functionalist ideas about mutual aid, or
feminist concerns about unequal caring loads falling on women within the
household). Award up to 4 marks for a developed, evidenced assessment;
2–3 for a claim that is stated and partly supported; 1 for an
unsupported assertion.

(d) [4 marks] One methodological issue evaluated in the context of this
specific research question, e.g. social desirability bias if family
members are interviewed together about tensions in the household, and how
this might be addressed (separate interviews, use of vignettes); or
practical/ethical difficulties of gaining access to and consent from every
generation within one household. Award up to 4 marks for an issue that is
both explained and evaluated in context; 2–3 for an issue explained but
not evaluated; 1 for a bare assertion.

*(6 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 20 marks for Section B; 40 + 20 = 60 marks total,
matching Unit 1's real mark allocation.)*

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Naming without applying.** In Question 4, students often name a
  concept such as "individualisation" or "the triple shift" correctly but
  never connect it back to the question of *increasing diversity* — the
  connection, not just the definition, carries the marks.
- **Treating Item-based questions as generic.** In Question 7, answers
  that could have been written without reading Item C at all score lower
  than ones that engage with the specific scenario (multi-generational
  households, the reasons families give, the effects on relationships).
- **One-sided essays.** Question 5 rewards weighing perspectives against
  each other and reaching a conclusion, not describing functionalist,
  Marxist and feminist views in three separate, unconnected paragraphs.
- **Confusing strengths/weaknesses of a method in general with its
  suitability for this particular topic.** Question 6(b) and 6(c) and
  Question 7(d) all score higher when the point is tied to researching
  families specifically, not method pros and cons recited from memory.
