**This practice set covers the learning approach in Cambridge International AS & A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A tests learning terms and details of the three core studies. Section B asks for evaluation, comparison and links to issues and debates. Mark-scheme answers follow.

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Outline what is meant by the *blank slate* assumption of the learning approach. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the mild aggression arousal phase in the study by Bandura et al. **[3]**

**3.** Bandura et al. used two observers to record the children's behaviour in the test room. Explain why. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** of the independent variables in the study by Bandura et al. **[2]**

**5.** Using the study by Fagen et al., explain what is meant by *behavioural chaining*. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the bridge signal in Fagen et al. is described as a secondary reinforcer. **[2]**

**7.** Outline how the boy in the study by Saavedra and Silverman developed his phobia, using the idea of classical conditioning. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why Saavedra and Silverman added an evaluative learning phase to the boy's treatment. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**9.** Evaluate the study by Bandura et al. in terms of **two** strengths and **two** weaknesses. At least **one** of your points must be about the use of children. **[10]**

**10.** Compare the research methods used by Fagen et al. and Saavedra and Silverman. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **one** way in which the study by Fagen et al. meets the ethical guidelines for research with animals. **[2]**

**12.** Explain how the findings of Bandura et al. relate to the individual versus situational explanations debate. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 2 marks. At birth we have no inborn behaviours or knowledge (1); all behaviour is shaped by experience and interaction with the environment, and these changes are directly observable (1).

**2.** Up to 3 marks. After watching the model, each child was taken to a different room containing very attractive toys (1). Shortly after starting to play, the child was told that these toys were reserved for other children (1). The aim was to frustrate the child mildly so that aggression would be more likely in the test phase, whatever model condition they had been in (1).

**3.** Up to 2 marks. To check inter-observer reliability (1): if two observers independently record similar scores for the same child, the behavioural categories are being applied consistently and the data are more reliable (1).

**4.** 1 mark each for any two: whether the model was aggressive or non-aggressive (or no model, for the control group); the sex of the model; the sex of the child (accept "same-sex or opposite-sex model").

**5.** Up to 2 marks. Behavioural chaining links separately learned behaviours into a sequence, where each step leads to the next (1). The elephants learned to raise the trunk, hold it up while liquid was introduced and then lower it, and these steps were joined into the full trunk wash (1).

**6.** Up to 2 marks. The signal has no value in itself; it becomes reinforcing because it is repeatedly paired with food, a primary reinforcer (1). It can then mark the exact moment of a correct response, even before the food arrives (1).

**7.** Up to 3 marks. Before the incident, buttons were a neutral stimulus (1). At about age five, buttons fell on him at school, an event that caused distress, an unconditioned response (1). Because buttons were paired with this distress, they became a conditioned stimulus that produced a conditioned fear and disgust response, which generalised to buttons on clothing (1).

**8.** Up to 2 marks. Exposure with reinforcement reduced his distress only a little, because his reaction involved disgust as well as fear (1). Evaluative learning targets how much a stimulus is liked or disliked, so disgust imagery was used to change his negative evaluation of buttons, which then produced a larger reduction in distress (1).

**9.** Level-based: 9–10 marks for four developed points applied to the study, including one on children, and a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Strength, controls*: matching the children on prior aggression and using the same scripted model behaviour for every child mean group differences are likely to be due to the model condition.
- *Strength, quantitative data and reliability*: recording behaviour every five seconds in defined categories, with two observers, gives objective, comparable data.
- *Weakness, validity*: a Bobo doll is designed to be hit and bounce back, so children may have seen hitting it as play rather than aggression; the model was a stranger, unlike real-life role models.
- *Weakness, use of children*: young children could not give informed consent, were deliberately frustrated and were taught new aggressive acts that might carry over into later behaviour.
- *Weakness, generalisability*: children came from one university nursery, which may not represent other backgrounds or cultures.
- *Conclusion*: a well-controlled demonstration that aggression can be imitated, but it may not show how aggression develops in everyday life, and it raises serious ethical concerns.

**10.** Up to 4 marks: 2 marks for each comparison developed with both studies. Similarity: both used observation to collect data on behaviour (1), Fagen et al. with a behavioural checklist of training steps and Saavedra and Silverman by watching the boy approach buttons (1). Difference: Fagen et al. studied a small group of animals in an observation (1), whereas Saavedra and Silverman used a case study of one child, combining interviews, rating scales and observation (1).

**11.** Up to 2 marks. Minimising harm or the guideline on reward, deprivation and aversive stimuli (1): the elephants were trained with food rewards instead of the punishment and dominance used in traditional training, so the procedure reduced pain and distress (1).

**12.** Up to 4 marks. The model's behaviour is a situational factor (1): children who saw an aggressive model showed more aggression than those who did not, so the situation shaped behaviour (1). The sex of the child is an individual factor (1): boys showed more imitative physical aggression than girls, suggesting individual differences also played a part (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know Bandura's exact age range?**
Knowing that the children were roughly three to five years old and came from a university nursery school is precise enough.

**Can I say Saavedra and Silverman "cured" the phobia?**
Describe the result carefully: distress fell markedly after the evaluative learning phase and the improvement was maintained at follow-up.
