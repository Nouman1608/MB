## A4.1.1 Getting the definition exactly right

Evolution is **change in the heritable characteristics of a population over generations**. Three words carry the marks:

- **heritable**: the change is genetic, so it can be passed on;
- **population**: individuals do not evolve, their alleles stay the same for life;
- **generations**: change is measured between generations, not within a lifetime.

A body-builder's muscles, a sun-tan or a learned skill are acquired characteristics and are not evolution. The idea that acquired characteristics are inherited is Lamarckism, which the definition rules out.

## Three lines of evidence and what each shows

| Evidence | What is observed | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Sequences (A4.1.2) | Closely related species have fewer differences in DNA, RNA or protein sequences | Common ancestry, and the order in which lineages split |
| Selective breeding (A4.1.3) | Large heritable change in a few thousand years | Populations can change substantially through selection |
| Homologous structures (A4.1.4) | Same basic structure used for different functions | Descent from a common ancestor, with modification |

### Worked example 1: reading sequence data

A 24-base section of the same gene in three invented species:

| Species | Sequence |
|---|---|
| A | ATGCCGTTAGCAAGTCTGGAACTA |
| B | ATGCCGTTGGCAAGTCTGGAACTA |
| C | ATGCAGTTGGCTAGTCTCGAAATA |

1. Count differences position by position: A–B differ at 1 position (4.2%), A–C at 5 (20.8%), B–C at 4 (16.7%).
2. A and B have the fewest differences, so they share the most recent common ancestor.
3. C is more distant from both, so its lineage split off earlier.
4. The conclusion assumes differences accumulate at a roughly steady rate; state this when asked to evaluate.

**Why sequences beat appearance.** Similar appearance can arise by convergent evolution, but a long, matching base sequence is very unlikely to arise twice by chance. Sequence comparisons are therefore stronger evidence of relationship.

**Selective breeding in practice.** Humans chose which individuals bred, generation after generation. Maize was developed from the wild grass teosinte, with far larger cobs and more kernels, and domestic dog breeds, varying hugely in size and shape, all descend from wolves. Because the changes are heritable and have happened in a short time, they show that selection can change a population markedly. Natural selection is the same process with the environment doing the choosing.

## A4.1.4 and A4.1.5 Homology, analogy and the tricky cases

**Homologous structures** share an underlying structure inherited from a common ancestor. The pentadactyl limb of tetrapods has one upper bone (humerus or femur), two lower bones (radius and ulna, or tibia and fibula), a group of wrist or ankle bones, and five digits, modified in proportion for different uses.

**Analogous structures** do a similar job but have different underlying structures and origins. They arise by **convergent evolution** when unrelated lineages face similar selection pressures.

### Worked example 2: annotated classification

| Pair | Classification | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Human arm and bat wing | Homologous | Same pentadactyl bone pattern; different function |
| Bat wing and butterfly wing | Analogous | Same function (flight); bone and skin versus cuticle |
| Shark fin and dolphin flipper | Analogous | A dolphin flipper contains pentadactyl limb bones; a shark fin has none |
| Penguin flipper and dolphin flipper | Homologous as forelimbs, analogous as flippers | Both are modified tetrapod forelimbs, but the flipper shape evolved separately in birds and mammals |

The last row is the kind of case that separates strong answers. Homology is about the *underlying structure* inherited from an ancestor; analogy is about a *shared function or form* evolved independently. One pair of structures can show both at different levels.

## A4.1.6 and A4.1.7 How one species becomes two

Speciation is the formation of a new species by the **splitting** of an existing one. Gradual change within a single lineage, with no split, does not increase the number of species.

The sequence of events:

1. **Reproductive isolation**: gene flow between two parts of a population stops, often because a geographical barrier separates them.
2. **Variation**: each part has its own mutations and allele frequencies.
3. **Differential selection**: environments differ, so different alleles are favoured on each side.
4. **Divergence**: over many generations the gene pools become so different that, even if the barrier disappeared, the populations could not interbreed to produce fertile offspring.

**Example from the guide.** Bonobos and common chimpanzees live on opposite sides of the Congo River, which their ancestors could not cross. Isolation followed by differential selection led to two species.

## A4.1.8 to A4.1.11 (HL only) Modes and mechanisms

**Allopatric versus sympatric (A4.1.8).**

| | Allopatric | Sympatric |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Populations in different areas | Populations in the same area |
| What stops gene flow | Geographical barrier | Behavioural or temporal isolation |
| Example of isolation | River, mountain range, sea | Different courtship signals; breeding at different times |

**Adaptive radiation (A4.1.9).** One ancestral species gives rise to many species, each adapted to a different niche. It is most likely where many niches are unoccupied, for example on a newly colonised island group. The finches of the Galápagos Islands, with beaks suited to different foods, are a widely used example. Because each species uses different resources, the radiation raises biodiversity without the species competing directly.

**Barriers and sterile hybrids (A4.1.10).** Barriers keep gene pools separate:

- *before fertilisation*: different courtship behaviour, different breeding times, different habitats;
- *after fertilisation*: hybrids that do not survive, or survive but are sterile.

A sterile hybrid cannot pass alleles on, so the parent species remain separate even if hybrids form.

**Abrupt speciation by polyploidy (A4.1.11).** In plants, a new species can arise in one or two generations. The guide's example group is the knotweeds and smartweeds of the genus *Persicaria*.

### Worked example 3 (HL): following the chromosomes

Invented species F (2n = 20) and G (2n = 16) cross.

1. Gametes: F gives n = 10, G gives n = 8. The hybrid has 18 chromosomes.
2. The 10 F chromosomes and 8 G chromosomes are not homologous, so they cannot pair in meiosis. The hybrid is sterile.
3. If the chromosome number doubles to 36, every chromosome has an identical partner, pairing succeeds and the plant is fertile.
4. Its gametes have 18 chromosomes. Crossed with F (gametes of 10), offspring have 28 chromosomes that cannot all pair, so they are sterile.
5. The 36-chromosome plant is therefore reproductively isolated from both parents: a new species has formed abruptly.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that individuals evolve or adapt in their lifetime.
- Calling any two structures with the same function homologous.
- Treating change within one lineage as speciation.
- Explaining speciation with isolation but no differential selection.
- Saying hybrids are sterile "because they are a mix", without unpaired chromosomes.

## Exam technique for IB command terms

- **Define**: use heritable, population and generations.
- **Distinguish**: homologous versus analogous, allopatric versus sympatric, each with the deciding feature.
- **Explain**: isolation, variation, differential selection, divergence, in order.
- **Deduce**: relatedness from a sequence table.
- **Outline** (HL): the chromosome steps in polyploid speciation.
