Condensed notes for the section 3.3 row **Liability in negligence for physical injury to people and damage to property**: duty of care ("the 'neighbour' principle; the Caparo three-part test"), breach ("the objective standard of care") and damage ("factual causation and legal causation (remoteness of damage)"). For explanation and a worked problem, use the [negligence for physical injury and property damage study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-liability-in-negligence-for-physical-injury-to-people-and-damage-to-property/).

## The structure

```
DUTY  ->  BREACH  ->  CAUSATION (but for)  ->  REMOTENESS (foreseeable type)
                                    |
                     intervening act may break the chain
Burden: claimant, balance of probabilities
```

## Duty of care

- **Neighbour principle:** *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932), Lord Atkin. Persons so closely and directly affected that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.
- **Caparo test** (1990): foreseeability; proximity; fair, just and reasonable.
- **Robinson** (2018): apply established duties directly; *Caparo* reasoning only for novel cases, developed incrementally by analogy.

| Established duty | Example |
|---|---|
| Road users to each other | *Nettleship v Weston* (1971) |
| Manufacturer to consumer | *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) |
| Doctor to patient | *Bolam* (1957) |
| Ambulance service once call accepted | *Kent v Griffiths* (2000) |
| Police for positive acts | *Robinson* (2018) |

**No duty:** claimant not foreseeable (*Bourhill v Young*, 1943); not fair, just and reasonable (*Hill*, 1989).

## Breach of duty

**Standard:** the reasonable person (*Blyth*, 1856). Objective: personal inexperience is irrelevant.

| Defendant | Standard | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Learner | Competent practitioner | *Nettleship v Weston* (1971) |
| Child | Reasonable child of that age | *Mullin v Richards* (1998); *Orchard v Lee* (2009) |
| Amateur doing DIY | Reasonable amateur | *Wells v Cooper* (1958) |
| Professional | Responsible body of opinion, logically defensible | *Bolam* (1957); *Bolitho* (1997) |
| Anyone | Knowledge at the time | *Roe v Minister of Health* (1954) |

**Risk factors:**

| Factor | Raises or lowers standard | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Low probability | Lowers | *Bolton v Stone* (1951) |
| Foreseeable vulnerable claimants | Raises | *Haley v LEB* (1964); *Paris v Stepney* (1951) |
| High cost / impracticable precaution | Lowers | *Latimer v AEC* (1953) |
| Social utility | Lowers | *Watt v Herts CC* (1954); *Daborn v Bath Tramways* (1946) |

## Damage

**Factual causation:** "but for" test.
- *Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington HMC* (1969): patient would have died anyway.
- *McWilliams v Sir William Arrol* (1962): worker would not have worn a harness anyway.

**Intervening act:** *Knightley v Johns* (1982): inspector's negligent order broke the chain.

**Remoteness:** type of damage must be reasonably foreseeable (*The Wagon Mound (No 1)*, 1961).

| Rule | Case |
|---|---|
| Manner unforeseeable, type foreseeable: liable | *Hughes v Lord Advocate* (1963) |
| Foreseeable type includes children's meddling injuries | *Jolley v Sutton LBC* (2000) |
| Different kind of risk: not liable | *Doughty v Turner* (1964) |
| Foreseeable category (cold injury): liable for frostbite | *Bradford v Robinson Rentals* (1967) |
| Extent unforeseeable: thin skull rule | *Smith v Leech Brain* (1962) |

**Property damage:** same stages; *The Wagon Mound* was a wharf-damage claim.

## Evaluation points

- **Duty:** *Robinson* brought certainty by ending repeated use of *Caparo*, but the incremental approach still leaves novel cases hard to predict.
- **Breach:** the objective standard protects claimants equally but labels honest learners as careless; insurance explains the result (*Nettleship*).
- **Causation:** "but for" struggles where the claimant would have acted differently anyway, as *McWilliams* shows: the employer escaped despite a clear breach.
- **Remoteness:** the line between a different "type" of harm (*Doughty*) and a different "manner" (*Hughes*) is fine and can seem arbitrary.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Duty of care | A legal obligation to take reasonable care not to harm the claimant |
| Proximity | A sufficiently close relationship between the parties |
| Incremental approach | Developing new duties by analogy with existing ones |
| Objective standard | Judged against the reasonable person, not the defendant's own ability |
| Factual causation | The breach must be a "but for" cause of the harm |
| Novus actus interveniens | A new intervening act that breaks the chain of causation |
| Remoteness | Limit on liability to damage of a foreseeable type |
| Thin skull rule | Defendant takes the claimant as found once some harm is foreseeable |

## Exam traps

- Running *Caparo* on an established duty: cite *Robinson* and move on.
- "She was only a learner" is never a defence to breach.
- Confusing *Hughes* (liable: type foreseeable) with *Doughty* (not liable: different risk).
- Using the thin skull rule to establish liability; it only extends the damages once liability exists.
- Forgetting a property claim hidden in the facts.

## Self-test

1. Which case gave the neighbour principle? *Donoghue v Stevenson (1932).*
2. What are the three parts of the Caparo test? *Foreseeability, proximity, fair just and reasonable.*
3. What did *Robinson* (2018) decide about *Caparo*? *It is not applied where a duty is established; novel cases develop incrementally.*
4. Why did the claimant fail in *Bourhill v Young*? *She was not a foreseeable claimant.*
5. What standard applies to a DIY householder? *A reasonably competent amateur doing that job (Wells v Cooper).*
6. What did *Bolitho* add to *Bolam*? *The body of professional opinion must be logically defensible.*
7. Why was there no breach in *Roe v Minister of Health*? *The risk was unknown at the time.*
8. What made the precautions inadequate in *Haley v LEB*? *Blind pedestrians were foreseeable and the guard would not protect them.*
9. Why did the claim fail in *McWilliams*? *The worker would not have used a harness, so the breach was not a but-for cause.*
10. What broke the chain in *Knightley v Johns*? *The inspector's negligent order to ride against the traffic.*
11. Distinguish *Hughes* and *Doughty*. *In Hughes the type of harm (burns) was foreseeable; in Doughty an eruption was a different kind of risk from a splash.*
12. What does the thin skull rule mean? *The defendant is liable for the full extent of harm even if worsened by the claimant's condition.*
