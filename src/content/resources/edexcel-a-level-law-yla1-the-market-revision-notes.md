## Formation (2.1.1)

| Element | Rule | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Offer v invitation to treat | Shelf and window displays, most adverts, are invitations | *Boots* [1953]; *Fisher v Bell* [1961]; *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] |
| Unilateral offer | An advert can be an offer to the world | *Carlill* [1893] |
| Counter-offer | Destroys the original offer | *Hyde v Wrench* [1840] |
| Request for information | Does not destroy the offer | *Stevenson v McLean* [1880] |
| Revocation | Before acceptance, must be communicated | *Routledge v Grant* [1828]; *Byrne v Van Tienhoven* [1880]; *Dickinson v Dodds* [1876] |
| Acceptance | Silence is not acceptance; postal rule; instantaneous on receipt | *Felthouse* [1862]; *Adams v Lindsell* [1818]; *Entores* [1955] |
| Consideration | Sufficient not adequate; not past; practical benefit | *Chappell v Nestlé* [1960]; *Re McArdle* [1951]; *Williams v Roffey* [1990] |
| Intention | Domestic presumed not binding, rebuttable | *Balfour* [1919]; *Merritt* [1970] |
| Capacity | Minors liable for necessaries | *Nash v Inman* [1908] |

**Formation points that often decide a problem question:**

- **Ending an offer**: rejection, counter-offer, revocation communicated before acceptance (by the offeror or a reliable third party, as in *Dickinson v Dodds*), or **lapse of time**: an offer lasts only a reasonable time if no deadline is set (*Ramsgate Victoria Hotel v Montefiore* [1866]).
- **Acceptance**: must be unconditional and match the offer exactly ("mirror image"); a unilateral offer is accepted by performing the act. The **postal rule** (acceptance complete on posting) does not apply if the offer requires actual receipt (*Holwell Securities v Hughes* [1974]); instantaneous methods take effect on receipt (*Entores*).
- **Consideration**: must move from the promisee. Performing an existing contractual duty is not usually good consideration (*Stilk v Myrick* [1809]), unless it gives the other party a **practical benefit** (*Williams v Roffey*) or goes beyond the duty (*Hartley v Ponsonby* [1857]).
- **Intention**: commercial agreements are presumed binding (*Edwards v Skyways* [1964]); domestic ones are presumed not, unless evidence such as a separated couple's written agreement rebuts it (*Merritt*).

## Terms (2.1.2)

- **Condition** (root of contract): *Poussard v Spiers* [1876]. **Warranty** (minor): *Bettini v Gye* [1876].
- **Incorporation**: signature (*L'Estrange v Graucob* [1934]); notice before contract (*Olley* [1949]; *Thornton* [1971]); course of dealing.
- **UCTA 1977**: s 2(1) no exclusion for negligent death or personal injury; s 2(2) and s 3 reasonableness; s 11 the test.
- **CRA 2015**: s 31 (goods) and s 57 (services) terms cannot be excluded; s 62 unfair terms not binding on consumer; s 65 no exclusion for negligent death or personal injury.
- **Innominate terms**: where a term is neither clearly a condition nor a warranty, the remedy depends on how serious the consequences of the breach are (*Hong Kong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki* [1962]).
- **Terms implied by the courts**: to give the contract business efficacy (*The Moorcock* [1889]) or because the term is so obvious that it goes without saying (the "officious bystander" test, *Shirlaw v Southern Foundries* [1939]).
- **Exclusion clause checklist**: (1) incorporated? (signature, reasonable notice before the contract, course of dealing); (2) does its wording cover the loss? (ambiguity is read against the party relying on it); (3) is it valid under UCTA (business to business) or the CRA (trader to consumer)?

## Consumer Rights Act 2015 (2.1.3)

| Goods term | Section | Services term | Section |
|---|---|---|---|
| Satisfactory quality | 9 | Reasonable care and skill | 49 |
| Fit for particular purpose | 10 | Reasonable time | 52 |
| Matches description | 11 | | |

Goods remedies: **s 20** short-term reject (generally 30 days) → **s 23** repair or replace → **s 24** price reduction or final reject. Services remedies: **s 55** repeat performance → **s 56** price reduction.

- **Satisfactory quality (s 9)**: judged by what a reasonable person would consider satisfactory, taking account of the description, price and other circumstances. Aspects include fitness for the usual purposes, appearance and finish, freedom from minor defects, safety and durability.
- **Particular purpose (s 10)**: applies where the consumer makes known a specific purpose, such as asking for walking boots suitable for winter mountain hiking.
- **Burden of proof**: a fault appearing within **six months** of delivery is presumed to have been present at delivery unless the trader proves otherwise; after that, the consumer must show it.
- **Services**: s 50 makes information given by the trader binding in some circumstances, and s 51 implies a reasonable price where none is agreed.
- **Order of remedies**: the short-term right to reject comes first; if the consumer instead asks for repair or replacement and it fails, is impossible or is not done within a reasonable time, the consumer can move to a price reduction or the final right to reject.

## Validity, discharge, privity (2.1.4, 2.1.5)

- **Misrepresentation**: false statement of fact inducing contract (not opinion: *Bisset v Wilkinson* [1927]); fraudulent (*Derry v Peek* [1889]), negligent (Misrepresentation Act 1967 s 2(1)), innocent. Rescission and/or damages.
- **Misrepresentation detail**: silence is generally not a misrepresentation, but a **half-truth** can be (*Dimmock v Hallett* [1866]), and so can failing to correct a statement that becomes false before the contract (*With v O'Flanagan* [1936]). Under s 2(1) of the 1967 Act the **representor** must prove they had reasonable grounds to believe the statement was true (*Howard Marine v Ogden* [1978]). Rescission is lost by affirmation, delay, third-party rights or where restoring the parties is impossible.
- **Mistake**: narrow; non-existent subject matter (*Couturier v Hastie* [1856]).
- **Damages for breach of contract**: recoverable losses are those arising naturally from the breach, or those in both parties' contemplation when the contract was made (*Hadley v Baxendale* [1854]); the claimant must mitigate the loss.
- **Discharge**: performance (*Cutter v Powell* [1795]; *Hoenig v Isaacs* [1952]), agreement, breach (*Hochster v De La Tour* [1853]), frustration (*Taylor v Caldwell* [1863]; *Krell v Henry* [1903]; not mere expense, *Davis Contractors* [1956]); 1943 Act deals with money paid and expenses.
- **Privity**: *Tweddle v Atkinson* [1861]; *Dunlop v Selfridge* [1915]. **1999 Act**: third party can enforce if expressly allowed or a benefit is conferred, and identified by name, class or description.

## Negligence as an alternative (2.1.6 to 2.1.8)

Use when there is no contract: *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932].

1. **Duty**: *Caparo* [1990]; *Robinson* [2018].
2. **Breach**: reasonable person (*Blyth* [1856]); likelihood (*Bolton v Stone* [1951]), seriousness (*Paris* [1951]), cost (*Latimer* [1953]).
3. **Damage**: but for (*Barnett* [1969]); remoteness (*Wagon Mound* [1961]); *res ipsa loquitur* (*Scott* [1865]).
4. **Defences**: *volenti* (complete; *ICI v Shatwell* [1965]); contributory negligence (reduces; *Froom v Butcher* [1976]; 1945 Act).
5. **Remedies**: damages with mitigation of loss; injunctions.

**Breach standards to apply:** a learner driver is judged by the standard of a reasonably competent driver (*Nettleship v Weston* [1971]); a professional by the standard of a responsible body of professional opinion (*Bolam* [1957]); a child by the standard of a reasonable child of the same age (*Mullin v Richards* [1998]).

**Remoteness detail:** only damage of a reasonably foreseeable **type** is recoverable (*Wagon Mound (No 1)*), but the precise way it happens need not be foreseen (*Hughes v Lord Advocate* [1963]), and D takes the claimant as they find them (thin skull: *Smith v Leech Brain* [1962]).

**Contract or negligence?** A consumer who bought a faulty product can sue the seller in contract under the CRA without proving fault. A person injured by the product who did not buy it has no contract (privity), so negligence against the manufacturer (*Donoghue v Stevenson*) is the alternative, but fault must be proved.

## Worked problem example

*Asha buys a kettle from a shop. Two months later it leaks and scalds her friend Ben, who was using it at her house.*

1. **Asha v shop (contract)**: the kettle is not of satisfactory quality (s 9) because it is unsafe and not durable. The fault appeared within six months, so it is presumed present at delivery. The 30-day short-term right has passed, so she can ask for repair or replacement (s 23) and, if that fails, a price reduction or final rejection (s 24).
2. **Ben (no contract)**: privity prevents Ben relying on Asha's contract, and the 1999 Act does not help because the contract did not purport to benefit him. Ben should sue the manufacturer in negligence: a duty is owed to the ultimate consumer (*Donoghue*), a leaking kettle falls below the standard of a reasonable manufacturer, and scalding is a foreseeable type of harm.
3. **Remedy**: damages for Ben's injury, subject to mitigation; any contributory negligence by Ben would reduce them.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Calling a shop display or most adverts an offer: they are invitations to treat.
- Applying the postal rule to email or phone acceptances.
- Using UCTA for a consumer contract: the CRA governs trader-to-consumer contracts.
- Forgetting the six-month presumption when advising a consumer on faulty goods.
- Treating extra expense as frustration (*Davis Contractors*).
- Running negligence without stating all three elements, or forgetting remoteness.
- Using privity to deny any remedy without considering the 1999 Act and negligence.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Invitation to treat | An invitation to make offers |
| Unilateral contract | Offer accepted by performing the act requested |
| Consideration | Something of value given in return for a promise |
| Frustration | An unforeseen event ends the contract automatically |
| Privity | Only parties can enforce a contract |
| Mitigation | The claimant's duty to take reasonable steps to limit loss |
| Innominate term | A term whose breach gives a remedy according to its consequences |
| Anticipatory breach | Making clear before performance is due that the contract will not be performed |
| Rescission | Setting a contract aside and restoring the parties' original positions |
| Remoteness | The limit on recoverable loss, based on foreseeability |
| Res ipsa loquitur | "The thing speaks for itself": an inference of negligence from the facts |
