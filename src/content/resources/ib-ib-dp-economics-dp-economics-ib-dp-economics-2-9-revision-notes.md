These notes condense **2.9 Market failure: public goods** from the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. For full explanations read the [2.9 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-9/); then test yourself with the [2.9 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-9-practice/). Places and figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rivalrous | One person's consumption reduces the amount available to others |
| Non-rivalrous | One more consumer does not reduce what others can consume |
| Excludable | Non-payers can be prevented from consuming |
| Non-excludable | Non-payers cannot be prevented from consuming |
| Public good | A good that is both non-rivalrous and non-excludable |
| Private good | A good that is both rivalrous and excludable |
| Free rider | Someone who benefits from a good without paying for it |
| Free rider problem | Users' incentive not to pay for a non-excludable good, so firms cannot earn revenue |
| Missing market | A situation where a good society values is not provided by the market at all |
| Direct provision | Government produces the good and funds it from taxation |
| Contracting out | Government funds the good but pays a private firm to produce it |

## Classification grid

| | Excludable | Non-excludable |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalrous | Private good (a meal) | Common pool resource (open fishery) |
| Non-rivalrous | Charged but uncrowded (streaming service) | Public good (flood barrier) |

Checks: apply **both** tests; state the conditions (an empty road at night versus a jammed road at rush hour); a fee or gate can make a good excludable.

## The free rider chain

1. The good is non-excludable.
2. Each user can benefit whether or not they pay.
3. Users understate their valuation or refuse to pay.
4. Firms cannot cover costs.
5. The market under-provides or does not provide at all, even when total benefit exceeds cost.

**Worked reminder.** Four shops on a high street value a shared security camera at 120, 90, 80 and 60 kels a year (total 350). It costs 300. Total benefit exceeds cost by 50, so it is worth providing, but no single shop values it at 300 or more, and each can benefit if the others pay. Without collective action it is not installed.

**Remember:** for a public good, add the valuations of all users, because they all consume the same unit.

## Government responses compared

| | Direct provision | Contracting out |
|---|---|---|
| Who pays | Government (taxes) | Government (taxes) |
| Who produces | Government | Private firm under contract |
| Solves free riding? | Yes | Yes |
| Main strength | Can supply goods no firm would; decisions based on social benefit | Competitive bidding can cut costs; specialist skills |
| Main limit | Opportunity cost of taxes; weak cost incentives; unknown valuations | Quality cutting; monitoring costs; few bidders |

**Method: comparing costs.** Contracting out cost = contract price + monitoring cost. Saving = in-house cost - contracting out cost. Express as a percentage of the in-house cost.

**Worked reminder.** In-house park maintenance costs 2.5 million kels; a bid is 2.1 million and monitoring 0.15 million. Contracting out costs 2.25 million, a saving of 0.25 million, or 0.25 / 2.5 × 100 = **10%**.

## Evaluation prompts

- How easily can quality be measured and written into a contract?
- How many firms will bid?
- What is the opportunity cost of the tax revenue?
- Does the government know how much people value the good?
- Who carries the risk if the provider fails?

## Exam traps

- "Public good = provided by government" is wrong; state-provided education and health care are merit goods, which are rivalrous and excludable.
- Giving only one characteristic in a definition.
- Calling free riding dishonesty instead of a rational response to non-excludability.
- Treating contracting out as privatisation: the state still pays and decides.
- Claiming that direct provision is "free".

## Quick self-test

1. Define a public good.
2. Explain what non-excludability means, with an example.
3. Is a toll motorway at midnight rivalrous? Is it excludable?
4. Why does a common pool resource differ from a public good?
5. Explain the free rider problem in four steps.
6. Five neighbours value a shared footpath light at 40, 35, 30, 25 and 20 kels; it costs 120. Is it worth providing? Will the market provide it?
7. Why do we add individual valuations for a public good?
8. What is the difference between direct provision and contracting out?
9. In-house cost 6.0 million; bid 5.2 million; monitoring 0.4 million. Calculate the saving and the percentage saving.
10. Give two limitations of contracting out.
11. Give one reason governments find it hard to decide how much of a public good to provide.
12. Why is a state school place not a public good?

### Answers

1. A good that is both non-rivalrous and non-excludable.
2. Non-payers cannot be prevented from consuming; for example, anyone in the area is protected by a flood barrier.
3. Not rivalrous when empty (one more car slows no one), but excludable, because the toll can be charged.
4. It is rivalrous: one user's catch or water use leaves less for others, although it is non-excludable.
5. Non-excludable; users can benefit without paying; they understate value or refuse to pay; firms cannot cover costs, so the good is under-provided or missing.
6. Total value = 150, above the cost of 120, so it is worth providing. No neighbour values it at 120, and each can free ride, so the market is unlikely to provide it.
7. All users consume the same unit at once, so its total benefit is the sum of what each user gains.
8. Under direct provision the government produces the good; under contracting out a private firm produces it, but the government still pays.
9. Contracting out cost = 5.6 million; saving = 0.4 million; 0.4 / 6.0 × 100 ≈ **6.7%**.
10. Any two: quality may be cut; monitoring is costly; few bidders limit competition; failure of the contractor.
11. People have no incentive to reveal their true valuations.
12. It is rivalrous (class places are limited) and excludable (entry can be controlled); it is a merit good.
