These questions cover inflation, deflation and disinflation, demand-pull and cost-push, MV = PQ, expectations, consequences and external influences for AQA A-level Economics (7136), specification version 1.3, section 4.2.3.3 (3.2.3.3 on the AQA website). Every country and figure is invented and illustrative. Read the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-inflation-and-deflation/) and [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-inflation-and-deflation-revision-notes/) first. ## Questions

**1.** In Country D the price index was 96.0 last year and is 98.4 this year. Inflation the year before was 4.0%.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of inflation this year. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain whether this is deflation or disinflation. **[2]**

**2.** In Country E the money supply is 600, velocity of circulation is 3 and real output is 900.

**(a)** Calculate the price level using MV = PQ. **[2]**
**(b)** The money supply rises to 660, with V and Q unchanged. Calculate the percentage change in the price level. **[2]**
**(c)** In another year, the money supply grows 5%, V is constant and real output grows 2%. Calculate, to 1 decimal place, the percentage change in the price level. **[2]**

**3.** Workers in Country A win a pay rise of 7% while prices rise 3.5%.

**(a)** Find the percentage rise in real pay, giving your answer to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how workers' expectations of inflation can make inflation rise. **[2]**

**4.** A household holds 50,000 in savings that earn no interest. Inflation is 3% a year for 4 years.

**(a)** Calculate the purchasing power of the savings after 4 years in today's prices, to the nearest whole unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two ways in which inflation might affect individuals other than savers. **[4]**

**5.** In Country G fuel has a weight of 5% in the price index. World fuel prices rise 15% in dollars.

**(a)** Calculate the direct contribution to inflation. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why the total effect on inflation may differ from your answer. **[3]**

**6.** Explain, using AD/AS analysis, how demand-pull inflation differs from cost-push inflation in its effect on real output. **[6]**

**7.** A borrower in Country H owes a fixed 20,000. The price level falls 3% a year for 2 years.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in the goods needed to repay the debt, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why falling prices might reduce consumer spending. **[4]**

**8.** Imported goods have a weight of 25% in Country J's price index. Import prices rise 10% because its trading partner has an inflation spike.

**(a)** Calculate the direct effect on inflation. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how faster growth in a major trading partner could affect Country J's inflation. **[3]**

**9.** Country K's government cuts spending and raises taxes. Inflation falls from 7% to 5% to 3% over three years from a starting index of 100.

**(a)** Calculate the index at the end of year 3, to 2 decimal places. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain whether the policy has caused deflation. **[2]**

**10.** Evaluate the view that the Quantity Theory of Money shows that inflation is caused only by excess growth in the money supply. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss whether deflation is always harmful to an economy. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Change = 98.4 − 96.0 = 2.4 [1]. 2.4 / 96.0 × 100 = **2.5%** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Inflation fell from 4.0% to 2.5% [1], but the index still rises, so prices rise more slowly: **disinflation, not deflation** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-word label with no reference to the figures is rarely enough; quote both inflation rates and say that prices are still rising.

**2. (a)** MV = 600 × 3 = 1,800 [1]. P = 1,800 / 900 = **2.0** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** New MV = 660 × 3 = 1,980, so P = 1,980 / 900 = 2.2 [1]. (2.2 − 2.0) / 2.0 = **+10%** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** %ΔP: 1.05 / 1.02 = 1.0294 [1], so about **2.9%** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the new value of MV before dividing; a bare percentage cannot show which step went wrong. Say which variables you are holding constant.

**3. (a)** Real pay factor = 1.07 / 1.035 [1] = 1.0338, so a rise of **3.4%** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** If workers expect inflation to be high they bargain for larger pay rises [1], raising firms' costs, so firms raise prices and the expectation is confirmed [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Explanations score for a chain of reasons; naming "expectations" without linking pay claims to costs and prices is a statement, not an explanation.

**4. (a)** Real value = 50,000 / 1.03⁴ [1] = 44,424.35, so **44,424** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Two chains of 2 each: fixed-income recipients, such as pensioners, lose because their money buys less [1] as their income is unchanged [1]; borrowers with fixed-rate debt gain because they repay in money worth less [1] so the real burden falls [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name who is affected and the direction, then give the reason. Consequences stated as bare lists score less than two developed points.

**5. (a)** 5% × 15% [1] = **0.75 percentage points** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Firms using fuel face higher costs and pass some on, so indirect effects add to inflation [1]; the exchange rate may rise or fall, changing the domestic price of dollar-priced fuel [1]; firms may absorb the rise in margins, or the rise may be temporary [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A direct-effect calculation is a starting point; the explanation needs reasons that raise or lower it, not a repetition of the calculation.

**6.** Demand-pull follows a rise in AD [1]; the AD curve shifts right along SRAS, so the price level rises [1] and real output rises [1]. Cost-push follows a rise in costs [1], so SRAS shifts left, the price level rises and real output falls [1]. Conclusion: the key difference is that output rises in one case and falls in the other [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the curve that shifts and the direction, and say what happens to real output. Describing both as "prices go up" shows no difference.

**7. (a)** Price level after 2 years = 0.97² = 0.9409 [1]. 1 / 0.9409 − 1 = 6.28%, so **6.3%** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Consumers expect lower prices, so delay purchases [1] which reduces AD [1]; the real value of debt rises, so debtors cut spending to repay [1]; falling profits lead to lower pay or job losses, reducing incomes [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each reason to AD. Describing debt without saying it leads to lower spending leaves the chain unfinished.

**8. (a)** 25% × 10% [1] = **2.5 percentage points** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Faster growth abroad raises demand for exports, so AD rises [1]; with little spare capacity, firms raise prices (demand-pull) [1]; the exchange rate may rise, which lowers import prices and partly offsets this [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-way argument limits the response. A counter-effect such as the exchange rate gains credit when it is linked to the outcome.

**9. (a)** 100 × 1.07 × 1.05 × 1.03 [1] = **115.72** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** The index rises each year [1], so the policy reduced AD and caused disinflation, not deflation [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures: an index above 100 and still rising shows prices have not fallen.

**10.** Definition: MV = PQ with V stable and Q fixed, so P changes with M [1]. Support: if Q is at full employment and V is constant, extra money raises P in proportion, as with M 400 to 440 giving P 2.0 to 2.2 [1] with the rise in P matching the rise in M [1]. Evaluation: V can change in a recession [1]; Q can rise in the short run when money rises [1]; cost-push shocks, such as dearer commodities, raise prices without money growth first [1]; money may respond to prices rather than cause them [1]. Judgement: the theory is most relevant over the long run and for sustained, high inflation, while short-run shocks need other explanations [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers use the equation to build the argument, then test the assumptions. Reciting the equation without evaluating V and Q stays at description.

**11.** Harmful case: delayed spending cuts AD [1]; real debt rises and bad debts follow [1]; falling profits reduce investment and employment [1]; real interest rates rise as nominal rates cannot fall far below zero [1]. Not harmful: if caused by productivity gains that shift LRAS right, prices fall while output grows (benign deflation) [1] with no loss of output; savers gain purchasing power [1]. Judgement: depends on the cause, size and duration, and on debt levels [1]; deflation from falling AD is the damaging kind [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided answer leaves the judgement unsupported. Separate benign from malign deflation and make your judgement depend on the cause.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "deflation" when the index is still rising.
- Giving a percentage change without showing MV or the new price level.
- Failing to state that V and Q are held constant when applying the Quantity Theory.
- Using the nominal figure when real pay or real value is asked.
- Stating consequences without saying who is affected and why.
- Leaving out the exchange rate when a commodity is priced in a foreign currency.
- Showing a cost-push shift as a change in AD.
- Ending an evaluation with a summary and no judgement.
