Section A concentrates on case study 2, Mexico and Peru. Section B is a one-hour source paper on case study 1, the final stages of Muslim rule in Spain, with the Paper 1 tariffs of 3, 2, 4, 6 and 9 marks.

**Source A** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a letter written by a Castilian nobleman serving in the royal army, to his wife, 1487.

> The great guns have battered the walls for many days and the Moors inside are eating their horses. Their own princes do nothing to help them, for the old king's brother and the young king hate each other more than they hate us. Our lord the King has sworn that no one shall leave until the city yields, and every week more men and powder come up from Seville.

**Source B** -- *Invented for practice:* description of a modern painting in a museum. A dignified Muslim ruler, on horseback and with bowed head, holds out the keys of a city to a king and queen mounted before a crowd of knights and churchmen. Behind the ruler, the towers of a red palace rise against the mountains. A priest in the crowd raises a cross.

**Source C** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a petition addressed to the monarchs by Muslim notables of Granada, about 1500.

> When we gave up our city, Your Highnesses swore that we would keep our faith, our mosques and our customs forever. Now men are baptised in crowds without instruction, our books are burned in the squares, and those who protest are imprisoned. We ask only that the agreement made in your name be kept.

**Source D** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a modern historian's study of the Catholic Monarchs, published in 2015.

> The war for Granada was a crusade in its language and its financing, but it was also an instrument of government. It gave a restless nobility employment under royal command and made the crown, not the great lords, the leader of Castile. The generous terms of 1491 were a tactic for ending the war quickly; the monarchs' later willingness to break them shows that religious unity had become a goal of state.

## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** roles played by Malinche (Doña Marina) in the conquest of Mexico. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the Tlaxcalans allied with Cortés. **[3]**

**3.** Outline the events of 1520 that led to the *Noche Triste*. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **two** ways in which the Inca civil war helped Pizarro. **[4]**

**5.** Define *encomienda* and explain why the New Laws of 1542 caused conflict in Peru. **[3]**

**6.** Outline the arguments of the two sides at the Valladolid debate (1550-1551). **[4]**

**7.** Explain why Cortés's letters to Charles V must be used with caution as evidence of the conquest of Mexico. **[3]**

## Section B

**8.** What, according to Source A, were the reasons why the besieged city was likely to fall? **[3]**

**9.** What is the message conveyed by Source B? **[2]**

**10.** With reference to its origin, purpose and content, analyse the value and limitations of Source C for a historian studying the treatment of the Muslims of Granada after 1492. **[4]**

**11.** Compare and contrast what Sources C and D reveal about the terms agreed in 1491 and what happened to them. **[6]**

**12.** Using the sources and your own knowledge, examine the reasons for the fall of the Nasrid kingdom of Granada between 1482 and 1492. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: interpreter between Nahuatl and Maya, working with Aguilar who translated Maya into Spanish (1); adviser on local politics and customs (1); intermediary in negotiations with the Mexica and potential allies (1); a source of intelligence, for example warnings before the Cholula massacre (1). **[2]**

**2.** The Tlaxcalans had never been conquered by the Mexica and were their long-standing enemies (1). After fighting the Spanish and failing to defeat them, they judged them useful allies against Tenochtitlan (1). An alliance offered the chance of revenge and of a privileged position after victory, which Tlaxcala later claimed from the crown (1). **[3]**

**3.** Cortés left the city to defeat an expedition sent by the governor of Cuba under Narváez (1). In his absence Pedro de Alvarado massacred Mexica nobles at a religious festival, and the city rose against the Spanish (1). After Moctezuma was killed during the fighting, the Spanish tried to escape at night and lost many men and much treasure on the causeways (1). **[3]**

**4.** 2 marks for each developed way: the war divided Inca elites, so Pizarro could present himself as an ally to Huáscar's supporters against Atahualpa (2); Atahualpa, just victorious and travelling with his army, underestimated the small Spanish force and met it at Cajamarca, where he was captured (2); the war had killed or scattered many leaders and weakened the empire's ability to respond after Atahualpa's capture (2). **[4]**

**5.** *Encomienda*: a grant to a Spaniard of the labour and tribute of an indigenous community, in return for protection and Christian instruction (1). The New Laws restricted new grants and ended inheritance of *encomiendas*, threatening the wealth of the conquerors (1). In Peru the viceroy's attempt to enforce them provoked Gonzalo Pizarro's revolt, in which the viceroy was killed, before royal forces restored control in 1548 (1). **[3]**

**6.** Las Casas: the indigenous peoples were rational beings with their own lords and laws (1); they should be converted peacefully, so war against them was unjust (1). Sepúlveda: drawing on Aristotle, some peoples were "natural slaves" who could rightly be ruled by others (1); war was justified to end practices such as human sacrifice and to open the way for conversion (1). **[4]**

**7.** Cortés had sailed against the governor's orders, so the letters were written to justify his actions and win royal support (1). He therefore stresses his own leadership and loyalty and plays down the role of allies and luck (1). They are still valuable as an eyewitness account of events and of how a conqueror wished to be seen (1). **[3]**

**8.** 1 mark per point, up to 3: artillery had been battering the walls for many days (1); the defenders were starving (1); the rival Nasrid princes would not help the city (1); the king was determined to continue the siege (1); reinforcements and gunpowder kept arriving (1). **[3]**

**9.** 1 mark per point, up to 2: the surrender of the city was peaceful and dignified (1); it marked the triumph of the Christian monarchs and the Church (1); the defeated ruler is shown with respect rather than humiliation (1). **[2]**

**10.** Markbands (summarised from the guide's Question 2 descriptors): **3-4 marks**, explicit discussion of both value and limitations, linked to origin, purpose and content; **1-2 marks**, limited, one-sided, or not linked to the topic.
- *Value:* origin, Muslim notables writing from Granada around 1500, during the forced conversions; purpose, to persuade the monarchs to honour the terms; content records mass baptisms, the burning of books and imprisonment, and the Muslims' own understanding of the treaty.
- *Limitations:* a petition seeks redress, so it emphasises grievances and may generalise from particular incidents; it gives no view from the Church or crown and cannot show how widely the policies were applied. **[4]**

**11.** Markbands: **5-6 marks**, detailed comparison and contrast with explicit links throughout; **3-4 marks**, some similarities and differences, possibly unbalanced; **1-2 marks**, superficial or sources described separately.
- *Comparisons:* both agree that the terms of 1491 were generous towards Muslims and that the monarchs later broke them; both link the change to religion.
- *Contrasts:* C presents the terms as a sworn promise "forever", D as a tactic for ending the war quickly; C attributes the change to the actions of officials it does not name, D to a deliberate goal of state; C focuses on the experience of Muslims, D on royal power and the nobility. **[6]**

**12.** Markbands: **7-9 marks**, focused response using the sources and accurate own knowledge, with effective synthesis; **4-6 marks**, mostly focused, uses sources and some own knowledge; **1-3 marks**, limited focus, sources described or own knowledge only.
- *From the sources:* A (artillery, starvation, Nasrid division, royal determination, supplies); B (surrender as Christian triumph); D (crusade financing and royal use of the nobility).
- *Own knowledge:* union of Castile and Aragon (1469, 1479); papal crusade taxes; Boabdil's revolt and his capture at Lucena (1483); the campaigns of Ronda (1485), Loja (1486), Málaga (1487) and Baza (1489); al-Zaghal's surrender; the camp at Santa Fe; negotiated surrender (November 1491).
- *Other factors:* lack of effective aid from North Africa, the Mamluks or the Ottomans; Granada's dependence on fortresses that artillery could now break.
- *Judgement:* for example, Christian unity and resources made victory possible, but Nasrid division decided how quickly it came. **[9]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Section B set on Granada rather than Mexico and Peru?**
Paper 1 sources are set on one case study. Section A covers the Americas; ask your teacher for a source set on that case study too.

**Does a painting made centuries later count as a valid source?**
Yes. A later painting is evidence of how an event was remembered, which is what a message question asks about.
