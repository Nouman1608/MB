## Nature and purpose of contingency planning

A **contingency plan** is a set of actions prepared in advance for an unexpected event that could disrupt the business. The event might be a fire, a supplier failure, a cyber-attack, a key person leaving suddenly, a product fault or a sharp rise in costs. The plan is written before anything goes wrong, so that managers are not inventing a response under pressure.

The nature of the plan:

- It is **prepared in advance** and written down.
- It covers **events that are possible but not expected**. A normal forecast covers what the business expects to happen. A contingency plan covers what it hopes will not.
- It sets out **who does what**, what resources are needed and how stakeholders are told.
- It is **reviewed** from time to time, because the business, its risks and its people change.

The purposes:

- To **reduce the damage** from an event, in cost, time and reputation.
- To **speed up recovery** and so protect sales revenue and customers.
- To **protect stakeholders**, such as employees' jobs, customers' orders and suppliers' payments.
- To **give confidence** to owners, lenders and insurers that risk is under control.
- To make people **calmer and clearer** when the event happens.

Contingency planning is linked to risk. A business cannot remove every risk, so it uses a plan to deal with the ones that remain. The specification also lists contingency and crisis among the types of change in the Change area, which is why this topic connects to change management.

### Building a plan (a standard approach)

1. **Identify** the events that could seriously disrupt the business.
2. **Assess** each one by how likely it is and how much damage it would do.
3. **Choose a response**: prevent the event, reduce its effect, transfer the cost (for example by insurance) or accept the risk.
4. **Assign** roles, resources and a way of deciding when to activate the plan.
5. **Plan communication** with employees, customers, suppliers and others.
6. **Test and review** the plan, so that it still works.

### Worked example 1: is a plan worth its cost?

Gladwyn Cold Chain delivers chilled food to supermarkets. Its depot refrigeration could fail. Management estimates a 4% chance of a serious failure in any year. Without a plan, a failure would cost £240,000 (spoiled stock, penalties and lost contracts). With a plan (a standby unit hire agreement and an emergency delivery rota) the loss would fall to £60,000. The plan costs £9,000 a year to maintain.

A standard way to compare is **expected loss = probability x loss**.

- Expected annual loss without a plan: 0.04 x £240,000 = **£9,600**.
- Expected annual loss with a plan: 0.04 x £60,000 = **£2,400**.
- Expected saving: £9,600 - £2,400 = **£7,200**.
- Net of the plan's cost: £7,200 - £9,000 = **-£1,800**.

On these figures alone the plan costs more than it saves. The plan breaks even if the probability is 5% or higher, because £9,000 divided by the £180,000 reduction in loss is 0.05.

A good evaluation then asks what the numbers leave out. A supermarket might drop a supplier after one failed delivery, and that loss of trust is hard to put a figure on. The 4% is only an estimate, so the result is uncertain. The judgement could still be to adopt a cheaper version of the plan.

## Advantages and disadvantages of contingency planning

**Advantages to the business**

- Faster, calmer response, so less sales revenue is lost.
- Lower cost of the event overall, if the plan works.
- Better chance of keeping customers and contracts.
- Can lower insurance costs or satisfy customers who require evidence of resilience.
- Forces managers to think about weak points, which can reveal avoidable risks.

**Disadvantages to the business**

- Costs money and management time, even if the event never happens.
- Cannot cover every event, and the actual event may differ from the one planned for.
- Plans can become **out of date** or stay untested, so they fail when needed.
- May create false confidence, or make managers follow the plan when they should improvise.
- Resources used on planning could have been used elsewhere (an opportunity cost).

**Stakeholders**

| Stakeholder | Possible advantage | Possible disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Protects the value of the business | Costs reduce profit and dividends |
| Employees | Jobs and pay more secure; clear instructions | Extra training, drills or changed duties |
| Customers | Orders and service continue | Higher prices if costs are passed on |
| Suppliers | Payment and orders more stable | May be asked to meet new standards or hold stock |
| Lenders | Lower chance of default | Little direct effect |
| Local community | Less disruption from incidents | Little direct effect |

## Impact and importance

**Impact** is the effect a plan actually has. **Importance** is how much it matters to the business and stakeholders. Both depend on context. Use these questions in an evaluation.

- **How likely and how severe** is the event? A rare, small event hardly justifies a detailed plan. A likely, severe one does.
- **How dependent** is the business on one site, supplier, system or person? The more dependent, the more important the plan.
- **Size and resources.** A large firm can afford specialist planning. A small firm may find even a simple plan a real cost, though it may have less cushion if it is hit.
- **Type of business.** A hospital, airline or food producer has stakeholders who could be harmed. A plan matters more where safety or perishable goods are involved.
- **Quality of the plan.** A tested, communicated plan has more impact than one in a drawer.
- **Time frame.** Benefits come when the event happens, which could be never. Costs are paid every year.

## Recommending and justifying a plan

For a "recommend and justify" question, do not describe planning in general. Take the business in the case and:

1. Pick the **most serious realistic risk** from the information given.
2. Recommend **specific actions** that fit its size and resources.
3. **Justify** with evidence from the case: figures, dependence, customer expectations, cost against likely loss.
4. Say what you would **not** do and why, or what could go wrong.
5. Reach a **judgement** about whether the plan is worthwhile.

Weak answers list "have insurance, have a backup" for any business. Strong answers say which backup, how it would work, who would trigger it and why that is better than the alternatives for this business.

## Nature and purpose of crisis management

A **crisis** is a serious, often sudden event that threatens the survival, reputation or normal running of a business and needs urgent decisions. **Crisis management** is how a business responds while the crisis is happening and afterwards. These are standard definitions, not quoted from the specification.

The difference from contingency planning is timing. Contingency planning happens **before** an event. Crisis management happens **during and after** it. A good contingency plan makes crisis management easier, but even a business with a plan must still make judgements on the day, because real events never match the plan exactly.

Typical elements of crisis management:

- **Protect people first**, then assets and operations.
- **Take control**: name one decision-maker and one spokesperson.
- **Communicate** quickly, honestly and consistently with employees, customers, suppliers, the media and others.
- **Contain** the problem, for example by stopping production or recalling products.
- **Restore** normal operations and rebuild confidence.
- **Review** what happened and update plans.

The purposes are to limit harm, protect the reputation, keep stakeholder trust, return to normal operations and learn from the event.

## Evaluating a business' crisis management

You judge the response against what was possible. Useful criteria:

- **Speed**: did the business act when it first knew?
- **Honesty and clarity** of communication, and whether stakeholders heard from the business first.
- **Priorities**: were people and safety put first?
- **Control**: were responsibilities clear?
- **Cost** of the response against the harm avoided.
- **Recovery**: how long to return to normal, and what happened to customers and sales revenue?
- **Learning**: did the business change its plans afterwards?

Be careful with hindsight. An outcome can be poor even when the decisions were sound, and the other way round.

### Worked example 2: judging a crisis response

Orrinbeck Seafoods found bacteria in a batch of 18,000 packs of fish. Of these, 11,500 had been shipped. The managing director announced a recall within six hours, set up a refund helpline (though it took two days to get enough staff on the phones) and kept customers informed daily.

The recall cost 11,500 x £3.80 = £43,700 for refunds, plus £6,500 for communications, a total of **£50,200**. Weekly sales revenue is normally £120,000. It fell by 30% for three weeks, so lost sales revenue was 0.30 x £120,000 x 3 = **£108,000**. The total cost was **£158,200**.

Judgement: the speed, the public announcement and the focus on customer safety are strong points. The helpline delay shows missing preparation, which links back to contingency planning. The cost is large, but harm to customers and a possible legal and reputational problem from a slower recall could have cost more. The response was effective overall, and a pre-planned helpline would have made it better.

## Common errors

- Describing a contingency plan as something done after the event.
- Writing about planning in general terms instead of the business in the case.
- Treating the plan's cost as a reason to reject it without comparing it with the loss it prevents.
- Listing stakeholder groups without saying how each is affected.
- Judging crisis management only by the final outcome.
