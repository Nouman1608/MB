This guide covers the second strand of geographical enquiry in section 3.3.2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3: "2 Selecting, measuring and recording data appropriate to the chosen enquiry". The strand lists "Difference between primary and secondary data", "Identification and selection of appropriate physical and human data", "Measuring and recording data using different sampling methods" and "Description and justification of data collection methods". It links to a numerical skill in section 3.3.1.3: "design fieldwork data collection sheets and collect data with an understanding of accuracy, sample size and procedures, control groups and reliability". For the whole unit, start with the [Geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/).

## Types of data

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Collected first-hand by you or your group | Measurements, counts, questionnaires, field sketches |
| Secondary | Collected by someone else | Census data, maps, published river-flow records, GIS layers |
| Quantitative | Numerical | River width, pedestrian count, temperature |
| Qualitative | Descriptive | Interview comments, photographs, field sketches |

The specification also accepts alternative methods where practical fieldwork is not possible, but they "must go beyond the students being presented with pre-processed data that is ready for analysis": for example, selecting data from internet sources, working with raw data provided by the teacher, or interviewing people in school or at home.

## Physical data: common methods

| Variable | Equipment | Method |
|---|---|---|
| River width | Tape measure | Measure across the water surface from bank to bank, at right angles to the flow |
| River depth | Metre rule | Measure at equal intervals across the channel, such as ten points, and calculate the mean |
| Velocity | Float, stopwatch, tape | Time a float over a measured distance, such as 10 m, three times; or use a flow meter |
| Sediment size | Ruler or callipers | Measure the longest axis of a sample of pebbles at each site |
| Sediment roundness | Roundness chart | Compare each pebble with a chart of standard shapes and record its category |
| Beach profile | Ranging poles, clinometer, tape | Measure the angle between poles placed at each change of slope, from the sea to the back of the beach |
| Vegetation cover | Quadrat | Estimate the percentage of the quadrat covered by plants, or count species |
| Microclimate | Thermometer, anemometer | Record temperature and wind speed at the same height at each site |

**Using the numbers.** Width and mean depth give the channel's cross-sectional area (width × mean depth). Multiplied by velocity, this gives discharge, the volume of water passing a point each second. This is the kind of quantitative relationship the specification's numerical skills cover.

## Human data: common methods

| Variable | Method |
|---|---|
| Pedestrian flow | Tally the number of people passing a point in a fixed time, such as five minutes, at each site and at the same time of day |
| Traffic flow | Tally vehicles by type in a fixed time |
| Land use | Record the use of each ground-floor building along a route, using a set of categories such as shops, offices, housing and open space |
| Environmental quality | Score each site on a scale for criteria such as litter, noise, greenery and building condition, for example from -2 (poor) to +2 (good) |
| Questionnaire | Ask the same set of questions to a sample of people, mostly closed questions with fixed answers for easy comparison, plus one or two open questions |
| Interview | A longer conversation with a few people, such as a shopkeeper or resident, for qualitative views |
| Photographs and field sketches | Record what each site looks like, as qualitative evidence |

Choose methods that measure the variables named in your hypothesis. If the hypothesis is about pedestrian numbers, a questionnaire about shopping habits is useful context, but the pedestrian count is the core data.

## Sampling

You cannot measure everything, so you take a **sample**. The specification asks for "different sampling methods".

**Three ways to sample a place:**
- **Point sampling:** measurements at specific points, such as sites along a river.
- **Line sampling:** measurements along a line, or transect, such as across a sand dune system.
- **Area sampling:** measurements within areas, such as quadrats on a field.

**Three ways to choose the sample:**
- **Random sampling:** every point has an equal chance of selection, for example using random numbers to choose grid coordinates. It avoids your bias, but may miss parts of the area by chance.
- **Systematic sampling:** sites at regular intervals, such as every 500 m along a river or every tenth person who passes. It gives even coverage and is easy to repeat, but may miss features between sites.
- **Stratified sampling:** the area or population is divided into groups, and each group is sampled in proportion. For example, if a park is 70% grass and 30% woodland, seven of ten quadrats go on grass and three in woodland. It represents every group, but needs information about the groups in advance.

**Sample size.** A larger sample is usually more reliable, because one unusual reading has less effect on the results. But time and safety limit what is possible. A sensible plan states the number of sites, the number of readings at each, and why.

## Designing a data collection sheet

A good recording sheet makes data collection quick, accurate and consistent. It should include:

- the title of the enquiry, date, time and weather;
- site number and location, such as a grid reference or distance;
- a column for each variable, with **units**;
- space for **repeat readings** and their mean;
- a notes column for anything unusual.

**Example: river study recording sheet.**

| Site | Distance from source (km) | Width (m) | Depth readings (cm) | Mean depth (cm) | Float time over 10 m (s): 1, 2, 3 | Mean time (s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.5 | | | | | | |
| 2 | 1.5 | | | | | | |

## Accuracy, reliability, procedures and control groups

| Term | Meaning | How to improve it |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | How close a measurement is to the true value | Use precise equipment; read scales carefully; keep tapes taut |
| Reliability | Whether repeating the method gives similar results | Repeat readings; take the mean; collect on more than one day |
| Procedures | The exact steps followed | Use the same method, equipment, time and person at every site |
| Sample size | How many measurements are taken | Take enough readings to reduce the effect of unusual ones |
| Control group | A comparison where the factor being tested is absent | For example, measure air temperature over grass as well as tarmac, to test whether tarmac is warmer |

Results can be reliable without being accurate: a faulty thermometer that always reads 2 °C too high gives consistent but wrong readings.

## Describing and justifying methods

The specification asks for "description and justification of data collection methods". In an exam, a **description** says exactly what was done, so that someone else could repeat it. A **justification** says why it was a good choice for the enquiry.

**Worked example.**

**Question:** Describe and justify one method you used to collect primary data. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "To test whether river velocity increased downstream, I timed an orange float over a measured 10 m stretch in the centre of the channel at each of six sites, three times per site, and calculated the mean. I used a float because it was cheap and quick, so we could measure all six sites in one day. Repeating the reading three times reduced the effect of the float catching on a rock, which made the results more reliable. Measuring in the centre of the channel at every site kept the procedure the same, so the sites could be fairly compared."

The answer describes equipment, distance, position, repeats and sites, then gives three justifications linked to the hypothesis, time, reliability and fairness.

## Common mistakes

- **Vague methods**: "We measured the river" without equipment, units or where.
- **Methods that do not match the hypothesis.**
- **Naming a sampling method without saying how it was carried out**.
- **Confusing accuracy and reliability.**
- **Recording sheets without units** or space for repeats.
- **Justifications that only say "it was easy"**, without linking to the enquiry.
- **Pre-processed data presented as primary data.**
