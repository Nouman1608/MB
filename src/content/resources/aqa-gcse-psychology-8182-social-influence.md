This guide goes deeper on **3.2.1 Social influence**, the first of the four topics in
**Paper 2: Social context and behaviour** of AQA GCSE Psychology (8182), first teaching 2017.
Paper 2 is a 1 hour 45 minute written paper worth 100 marks and 50% of the GCSE. For an overview
of all four Paper 2 topics, start with the
[Social Context and Behaviour study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-social-context-and-behaviour/);
this page assumes you know the basic definitions and concentrates on how the studies were done,
how to evaluate them and how to apply the factors to a new scenario.

## What 3.2.1 covers

The specification organises social influence into four behaviours. For each one you need to know
**social factors** (features of the situation) and **dispositional factors** (features of the
person), plus the named study or theory.

| Behaviour | Social factors | Dispositional factors | Named study or theory |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conformity to majority influence | Group size, anonymity, task difficulty | Personality, expertise | Asch (1951) |
| Obedience | Agency, authority, culture, proximity (Milgram's Agency Theory) | Authoritarian Personality | Milgram; Adorno |
| Bystander behaviour | Presence of others, cost of helping | Similarity to victim, expertise | Piliavin's subway study (1969) |
| Prosocial and antisocial behaviour in crowds | Social loafing, deindividuation, culture | Personality, morality | -- |

The single most useful habit for this topic is to ask, for any behaviour in a question, "is this
about the **situation** or about the **person**?" Almost every application question rewards that
distinction.

## Conformity: Asch in more detail

Asch tested male American college students in groups where every other member was a
**confederate** (an actor working for the researcher). On each trial the group said aloud which of
three comparison lines matched a standard line. The real participant answered last or next to last.
On most trials the confederates gave the correct answer, but on the **critical trials** they all gave
the same wrong answer. The task was easy: when people judged the lines alone, they made almost no
errors. Even so, participants conformed on roughly a third of critical trials, about three-quarters
conformed at least once, and about a quarter never conformed.

Asch then varied the situation, which is where the social factors come from:

- **Group size.** With only one confederate, conformity was very low. It rose sharply with two and
  three confederates, then levelled off, so adding more people beyond a small majority made little
  extra difference.
- **Anonymity.** When the participant could write the answer privately instead of saying it aloud,
  conformity fell considerably. Public answers create pressure to fit in.
- **Task difficulty.** When the lines were made more similar in length, conformity increased,
  because people are less sure of their own judgement and look to others for the right answer.

**Dispositional factors** explain why, in the same situation, some people conformed and others
did not. **Personality** matters: someone with a strong need for social approval, or low
confidence, is more likely to go along with the group, whereas someone who believes they control
their own behaviour is more likely to resist. **Expertise** matters too: a person who knows a lot
about the task (for example, an architect judging lengths) has less reason to doubt their own
answer.

**Evaluating Asch.** Strengths: the procedure was standardised and the conditions were tightly
controlled, so the effect of each factor can be isolated. Weaknesses: the task was artificial, so
the study may lack **ecological validity**; the sample was only male American students in the
1950s, a period when conformity may have been more valued, so the findings may not generalise; and
participants were **deceived** about the purpose of the study.

## Obedience: Milgram's Agency Theory

In Milgram's best-known study (1963), 40 male volunteers acted as "teacher" and were told to give
increasing electric shocks to a "learner" (a confederate) each time he made a mistake, in 15-volt
steps up to 450 volts. No real shocks were given. When the teacher hesitated, the experimenter in a
grey lab coat used verbal prods such as "the experiment requires that you continue". All 40
participants went to at least 300 volts, and 26 of the 40 (65%) went to the maximum 450 volts,
though many showed obvious distress.

**Agency Theory** explains this. People in a social hierarchy have two states: the **autonomous
state**, where they act on their own conscience and feel responsible, and the **agentic state**,
where they see themselves as carrying out someone else's wishes and pass responsibility upward.
The specification lists four social factors:

- **Agency** -- the shift into the agentic state; many participants carried on once the experimenter
  said that he, not they, was responsible for what happened.
- **Authority** -- the legitimacy of the person giving orders. When Milgram moved the study from
  Yale University to a run-down office building, obedience fell.
- **Culture** -- societies differ in how much they value deference to authority, and replications in
  different countries have found different levels of obedience.
- **Proximity** -- obedience fell when the teacher was in the same room as the learner, fell further
  when the teacher had to press the learner's hand onto a shock plate, and fell to around a fifth of
  participants when the experimenter gave orders by telephone.

**Adorno's Authoritarian Personality** is the dispositional explanation. Adorno proposed that
children raised with harsh, strict discipline and conditional love develop hostility they cannot
express towards their parents. As adults they show rigid, black-and-white thinking, respect for
people with higher status and contempt for those seen as lower. Adorno measured this with the
**F-scale** questionnaire. Supporting evidence is that participants who were fully obedient in
Milgram-type research tended to score higher on the F-scale. A weakness is that the theory cannot
explain why whole populations, with very different upbringings, obeyed in the same historical
situation; a social explanation fits that better.

## Bystander behaviour: Piliavin's subway study

Piliavin and colleagues ran a **field experiment** on New York subway trains. A confederate
collapsed during a journey between stations, carrying either a cane (appearing ill) or a bottle and
smelling of alcohol (appearing drunk). Observers recorded who helped and how quickly. The cane
victim received spontaneous help on the great majority of trials, the drunk victim on about half,
and most first helpers were men. Unlike laboratory research, the study did **not** find that more
bystanders led to less helping.

Piliavin explained the findings with a **cost-reward** idea: bystanders weigh the cost of helping
(effort, danger, embarrassment, disgust) against the cost of not helping (guilt, blame). Helping a
drunk person seems more costly, so people helped less.

- **Presence of others** -- in laboratory studies by Latané and Darley, people helped less when they
  believed others were present, through **diffusion of responsibility**.
- **Cost of helping** -- the higher the perceived cost, the less likely people are to help.
- **Similarity to victim** -- people are more likely to help someone they see as like themselves;
  Piliavin found some tendency towards same-race helping in the drunk condition.
- **Expertise** -- people with relevant skills, such as first-aid training, are more likely to help
  because helping is less risky for them.

**Evaluating Piliavin.** The natural setting gives high ecological validity, and the large number
of trials made the findings reliable. However, passengers could not give consent or be debriefed,
and the researchers could not control who was in each carriage.

## Crowds: prosocial and antisocial behaviour

- **Social loafing** -- people put in less individual effort in a group because their contribution
  is less identifiable. In research by Latané, Williams and Harkins (1979), people clapped and
  shouted less loudly per person in a group than alone.
- **Deindividuation** -- loss of personal identity and self-restraint in an anonymous crowd. In a
  study of children trick-or-treating at Halloween (Diener and colleagues, 1976), anonymous children
  in groups were more likely to take more sweets than they were allowed.
- **Culture** -- social loafing appears to be less common in collectivist cultures, where group
  goals are valued over individual ones.
- **Personality** and **morality** -- people with strong moral principles, or high self-confidence,
  are more likely to stick to their own standards in a crowd, and may lead prosocial behaviour, such
  as helping during an emergency.

## Worked example: applying factors

*Scenario: At a school fire drill, Jamal sees a younger pupil fall on the stairs. Dozens of pupils
walk past. Jamal, who has just done a first-aid course, stops to help. Explain Jamal's behaviour. [4]*

A strong answer: "Most pupils may not have helped because of the **presence of others** -- with
dozens of people around, each pupil assumes someone else will act, so responsibility is diffused.
Jamal helped because of a **dispositional factor**, **expertise**: his first-aid training means he
knows what to do, which lowers the perceived **cost of helping** (he is less likely to make things
worse or feel embarrassed)." Each point names a factor and links it to the scenario.

## Common mistakes

- Using Asch to explain obedience, or Milgram to explain conformity.
- Calling Adorno's theory a social factor. It is the **dispositional** explanation of obedience.
- Claiming Piliavin showed diffusion of responsibility. It did not; that comes from laboratory work.
- Describing deindividuation as "being in a crowd". It is the **loss of identity and restraint**
  that anonymity can produce.
- Writing "the experiment lacked validity" without saying why. Name the type of validity and the
  feature of the study that causes the problem.

## Exam technique

- **Identify / name / state**: one precise term, no explanation needed.
- **Describe / outline**: say what happened (procedure and findings) with accurate detail.
- **Explain**: give a reason and link it with "because" or "so".
- **Evaluate / discuss**: give strengths and weaknesses, each explained, and end with a judgement.
- **Application** questions: quote or paraphrase the scenario in every point. A correct factor
  that is not linked to the scenario usually gains only part of the marks.
