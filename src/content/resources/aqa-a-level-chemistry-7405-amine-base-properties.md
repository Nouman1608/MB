## What a base is, and why amines are bases

A **Brønsted–Lowry base** is a proton (H⁺) acceptor. To accept a proton, a species needs a lone pair of electrons to form a dative covalent bond to H⁺. The nitrogen atom in an amine has a lone pair, as it does in ammonia.

```
CH₃NH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃NH₃⁺
```

In water an amine accepts a proton from water, forming hydroxide ions:

```
CH₃NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻
```

The equilibrium lies mostly to the left, so only a small fraction of the amine molecules are protonated. That is the meaning of a **weak** base. The solution contains some OH⁻, so it is alkaline, with a pH above 7 but lower than for a strong base of the same concentration.

## Reaction with acids: salt formation

Amines react with acids to form ammonium salts, which are ionic and usually soluble in water.

```
CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻           (ethylammonium chloride)
2CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂SO₄ → (CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺)₂SO₄²⁻   (ethylammonium sulfate)
C₆H₅NH₂ + HCl → C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻                (phenylammonium chloride)
```

Adding aqueous sodium hydroxide reverses the reaction by removing the proton, which releases the free amine:

```
CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂O
```

This is useful in practice. An amine that is insoluble in water, such as phenylamine, dissolves in dilute hydrochloric acid because it forms the soluble salt. Adding alkali makes it separate again.

## The order of base strength

Increasing base strength:

```
phenylamine  <  ammonia  <  primary aliphatic amine (such as ethylamine)
weakest                                  strongest
```

| Compound | Group on nitrogen | Effect on the lone pair | Relative base strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂ | Phenyl (benzene ring) | Lone pair delocalised into the ring | Weakest |
| Ammonia, NH₃ | None (three H) | Neutral reference | Intermediate |
| Ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂ | Alkyl | Lone pair made more available | Strongest |

## Explaining the order: the lone pair

The strength of a base depends on how readily the lone pair on nitrogen can bond to H⁺. The more available the lone pair, the stronger the base.

### Primary aliphatic amines are stronger than ammonia

An alkyl group is **electron-donating**: it pushes electron density towards the nitrogen atom (the positive inductive effect). This increases the electron density on N, so the lone pair is more available to accept a proton than in ammonia, which has only hydrogen atoms attached. Ethylamine is therefore a stronger base than ammonia.

### Phenylamine is weaker than ammonia

In phenylamine the nitrogen atom is bonded directly to the benzene ring. The lone pair on nitrogen overlaps with the delocalised pi electrons of the ring and becomes **partly delocalised into the ring**. The electron density on the nitrogen falls, so the lone pair is less available for bonding to a proton. Phenylamine is therefore a weaker base than ammonia.

### A model answer

"The lone pair on nitrogen accepts a proton. In ethylamine the alkyl group donates electron density to nitrogen, so the lone pair is more available and ethylamine is a stronger base than ammonia. In phenylamine the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less available and phenylamine is a weaker base than ammonia."

The key words are **lone pair**, **availability**, **electron-donating** (alkyl), and **delocalised** (phenylamine).

## Worked example 1: ranking three compounds

**Question.** Put ammonia, methylamine and phenylamine in order of increasing base strength and explain.

Increasing base strength: **phenylamine < ammonia < methylamine**. The methyl group in methylamine donates electron density to nitrogen, making its lone pair most available. In ammonia there is no donating group. In phenylamine the lone pair is delocalised into the ring and is least available.

## Worked example 2: equations

**Question.** Write equations for (a) propylamine with water and (b) propylamine with hydrochloric acid.

```
(a) CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻
(b) CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻
```

Use a reversible arrow in (a), because the amine is a weak base. The reaction with a strong acid in (b) goes to completion.

## Worked example 3: neutralising an amine

25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ methylamine is neutralised by hydrochloric acid of concentration 0.125 mol dm⁻³. Calculate the volume of acid required and the mass of methylammonium chloride (Mᵣ = 67.5) formed.

```
CH₃NH₂ + HCl → CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻           (1 : 1)
n(CH₃NH₂)   = 0.0250 × 0.100 = 0.00250 mol
n(HCl)      = 0.00250 mol
V(HCl)      = 0.00250 ÷ 0.125 = 0.0200 dm³ = 20.0 cm³
mass of salt = 0.00250 × 67.5 = 0.169 g
```

The weak base reacts fully with a strong acid, because the reaction removes the protons as they form.

## Worked example 4: separating phenylamine from a neutral compound

**Question.** A mixture of phenylamine and a hydrocarbon in an organic solvent is shaken with dilute hydrochloric acid. Describe what happens and how the phenylamine can be recovered.

Phenylamine reacts with the acid to form phenylammonium chloride, an ionic salt that dissolves in the aqueous layer. The hydrocarbon stays in the organic layer. Separate the layers, then add aqueous sodium hydroxide to the aqueous layer. The phenylammonium ion loses its proton, so free phenylamine is released and separates from the water.

## Why is the strength order important elsewhere?

The same lone pair controls the **nucleophilic** reactions of amines. A more available lone pair makes both a better base and a better nucleophile. This links to the Nucleophilic properties sub-topic.

## Comparing amines with other weak bases

| Feature | Ammonia | Primary aliphatic amine | Phenylamine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle accepting H⁺ | N lone pair | N lone pair | N lone pair |
| Product on protonation | NH₄⁺ | RNH₃⁺ | C₆H₅NH₃⁺ |
| Group on nitrogen | Hydrogen only | Alkyl, electron-donating | Phenyl, accepts delocalisation |
| Product with HCl | Ammonium chloride | Alkylammonium chloride | Phenylammonium chloride |

All three behave in the same way chemically. The only difference is the extent to which the lone pair is available, which is what the strength order measures.

## Checking your answer on strength

Before you finish, check three things. Is the lone pair on nitrogen named as the species that accepts the proton? Has the effect on the lone pair been stated for each compound, as donating in the alkylamine and delocalised in phenylamine? Is the comparison made with ammonia, which acts as the reference point? An answer with all three is complete.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a base as a hydroxide-releasing species only. Amines are proton acceptors.
- Writing "the lone pair is donated to the ring" instead of "delocalised into the ring".
- Saying that phenylamine is weaker because the ring is "electron-withdrawing" without mentioning the lone pair and delocalisation.
- Explaining the strength of ethylamine by its larger size.
- Using a one-way arrow for the reaction with water.
- Drawing the salt as RNH₂Cl, with no charges.
- Forgetting that sodium hydroxide regenerates the free amine from its salt.
- Mixing up the order, putting ammonia as the strongest.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the order**: say "availability of the lone pair on nitrogen" in every answer, then give the reason for each compound.
- **Write equations**: show charges on ions, and use a reversible arrow for reactions with water.
- **Name salts**: alkylammonium chloride, for example ethylammonium chloride.
- **Compare**: always compare with ammonia, which is the reference.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is an amine a stronger base than a hydroxide?**
No. Amines are weak bases and are only partially protonated in water. Hydroxide ions are a strong base.

**Why does phenylamine dissolve in dilute acid but not in water?**
In water, the molecule is largely non-polar because of the benzene ring. In acid, it forms an ionic salt that is soluble.

**Which atom accepts the proton?**
The nitrogen, using its lone pair.
