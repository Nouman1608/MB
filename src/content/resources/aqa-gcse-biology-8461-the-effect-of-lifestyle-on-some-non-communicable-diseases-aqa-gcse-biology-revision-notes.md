These are condensed recall notes for **4.2.2.6 The effect of lifestyle on some non-communicable diseases** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification, Version 1.0, for teaching from September 2016 and exams from 2018 onwards. It is Paper 1 content at both tiers; nothing here is marked (HT only). Worked explanations are in the [effect of lifestyle study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-the-effect-of-lifestyle-on-some-non-communicable-diseases-aqa-gcse-biology/); questions are in the [effect of lifestyle practice set](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-the-effect-of-lifestyle-on-some-non-communicable-diseases-aqa-gcse-biology-practice/). All figures below are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Recall |
|---|---|
| Risk factor | Something "linked to an increased rate of a disease" (specification wording) |
| Lifestyle risk factor | A habit or behaviour: smoking, diet, alcohol, lack of exercise |
| Body or environment risk factor | A substance: high cholesterol, carcinogens, ionising radiation |
| Causal mechanism | An explanation of how a factor causes a disease |
| Carcinogen | A substance or agent that can cause cancer |
| Ionising radiation | Radiation that can damage DNA, a named carcinogen |
| Incidence | Number of new cases in a population over a stated time |
| Correlation | Two variables changing together; not proof of cause |
| Dose-response | More exposure to a factor gives a higher rate of disease |
| Non-communicable disease | Not caused by a pathogen; not passed between people |

## The six named effects

| Factor | Disease or effect | How (GCSE depth) |
|---|---|---|
| Diet, smoking, exercise | Cardiovascular disease | Saturated fat raises cholesterol → fatty deposits; smoke damages artery linings; exercise lowers risk |
| Obesity | Type 2 diabetes | Body cells stop responding properly to insulin |
| Alcohol | Liver and brain function | Liver cells damaged by long-term heavy drinking; brain function and reactions impaired |
| Smoking | Lung disease and lung cancer | Tar damages airways and alveoli; carcinogens in tar |
| Smoking and alcohol in pregnancy | Harm to unborn babies | Carbon monoxide reduces oxygen to the fetus (low birth weight, premature birth); alcohol harms development |
| Carcinogens, including ionising radiation | Cancer | DNA damaged → uncontrolled cell division |

## Key statements to quote

- A causal mechanism has been proven for some risk factors, but not in others.
- Many diseases are caused by the interaction of a number of factors.
- A risk factor raises the chance of disease; it does not make it certain.

## Evidence that a factor causes a disease

1. A correlation in large, representative, random samples.
2. A dose-response pattern.
3. A biological mechanism.
4. Consistent results from independent studies.

## Incidence at three levels

- Local: more smokers in an area → more lung cancer there.
- National: rising obesity → rising Type 2 diabetes.
- Global: changing diets, alcohol and smoking → more non-communicable disease in more countries.

## Costs to discuss

| Level | Human cost | Financial cost |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Pain, poorer quality of life, shorter life | Lost earnings, care costs |
| Local community | Family carers; strain on local services | Local health and care spending |
| Nation | Fewer healthy workers | Health service spending; lost productivity |
| Global | Many early deaths | Health spending worldwide; harder for poorer countries |

## Data methods

```
rate per 100 000 = cases / people x 100 000
expected cases = population / 100 000 x rate
times more likely = rate in exposed group / rate in unexposed group
```

Mini example: 36 cases among 9000 drinkers, 12 among 12 000 non-drinkers. Rates: 400 and 100 per 100 000, so drinkers in this sample were 4 times as likely to develop the condition.

Scatter diagrams: give direction and strength, then say "linked, not proven to cause".

## Exam traps

- Correlation is not causation: say what extra evidence would be needed.
- Compare **rates**, not raw case numbers.
- Obesity → **Type 2** diabetes.
- Name **carcinogens** or **tar** for smoking and cancer.
- In pregnancy, smoking reduces **oxygen** to the fetus.
- "Discuss costs" needs human **and** financial, at two or more levels.
- A person with a risk factor may never get the disease; another without it may.

## Self-test

1. Give the specification's description of a risk factor.
2. Give the two kinds of risk factor, with one example of each.
3. What is a causal mechanism?
4. Name the disease the specification links with obesity.
5. Name two organs whose function is affected by alcohol.
6. How does smoking in pregnancy affect the fetus?
7. Name a carcinogen listed in the specification.
8. Give one human cost and one financial cost of a non-communicable disease to a nation.
9. 24 cases in 6000 people. Calculate the rate per 100 000.
10. Rates are 900 per 100 000 in smokers and 150 per 100 000 in non-smokers. How many times more likely were smokers to develop the disease?
11. What is a dose-response pattern?
12. Why might a lifelong smoker never develop lung cancer?

### Answers

1. Something linked to an increased rate of a disease.
2. Lifestyle (such as smoking) and substances in the body or environment (such as ionising radiation).
3. An explanation of how a factor actually causes the disease.
4. Type 2 diabetes.
5. Liver and brain.
6. Carbon monoxide reduces the oxygen carried in the mother's blood, so less reaches the fetus; linked to low birth weight and premature birth.
7. Ionising radiation (or tar in tobacco smoke).
8. Human: fewer healthy people, early deaths. Financial: health service spending or lost productivity.
9. 24 ÷ 6000 × 100 000 = 400 per 100 000.
10. 900 ÷ 150 = 6 times.
11. The greater the exposure to a factor, the higher the rate of disease.
12. Diseases result from the interaction of several factors, including genes, so a risk factor raises the chance but does not make disease certain.
