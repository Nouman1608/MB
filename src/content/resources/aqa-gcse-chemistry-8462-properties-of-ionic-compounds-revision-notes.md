These notes condense **4.2.2.3 Properties of ionic compounds** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification
(for teaching from September 2016, version 1.2). It is Paper 1 content for both tiers, with no Higher-only
statements and no required practical. For full explanations, see the
[properties of ionic compounds study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-ionic-compounds/);
then practise with the
[properties of ionic compounds practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-ionic-compounds-practice/).

## Core facts

- Ionic compounds have **regular structures**: **giant ionic lattices**.
- **Strong electrostatic forces of attraction** act **in all directions** between oppositely charged ions.
- Ionic compounds have **high melting points and boiling points**, because a **large amount of energy** is
  needed to break the **many strong bonds**.
- When **melted or dissolved in water** they **conduct electricity**, because the **ions are free to move** and
  so **charge can flow**.
- You do not need to know the structure of any ionic compound other than **sodium chloride**.

## Conduction at a glance

| State | Ions free to move? | Conducts? |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | No, fixed in the lattice | No |
| Molten | Yes | Yes |
| In aqueous solution | Yes | Yes |

The particles that carry the charge are **ions**. In metals the particles that carry the charge are
**electrons**.

## Sodium chloride model

- Sodium ions (Na⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻) alternate in a regular 3D pattern.
- Every ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge.
- The formula NaCl gives the **ratio** 1 : 1. There are no separate NaCl molecules.
- It is a solid at room temperature; melting point 801 °C.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ion | An atom or group of atoms with an electric charge |
| Giant ionic lattice | A regular 3D arrangement of oppositely charged ions |
| Electrostatic force | The force of attraction between oppositely charged particles |
| Molten | Melted to a liquid |
| Aqueous | Dissolved in water |
| Electrolyte | A molten or dissolved ionic compound that conducts electricity |

## Model answers

**Why is the melting point high?**
Giant ionic lattice; strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions in all directions; many strong
bonds; a large amount of energy is needed to break them.

**Why does the solid not conduct?**
The ions are in fixed positions and cannot move, so charge cannot flow.

**Why does it conduct when molten or dissolved?**
The ions are free to move, so they carry charge through the liquid.

## Reading data

An ionic compound usually shows **all** of these:

1. a high melting point (hundreds of degrees Celsius or higher);
2. no conduction as a solid;
3. conduction when molten or dissolved.

If a substance conducts as a solid, it is probably a metal. If it has a very high melting point and never
conducts, it is probably a giant covalent structure. If it has a low melting point and never conducts, it is
probably made of small molecules.

## Worked examples in brief

**Example 1: interpret a table.** A solid melts at 801 °C. It does not conduct as a solid. Its melt conducts.
Conclusion: ionic. The high melting point shows many strong bonds. The change in conduction shows the ions move
only once the lattice has broken down.

**Example 2: explain a two-state conduction question.** "Explain why potassium chloride conducts when dissolved but
not when solid." Solid: ions fixed in the lattice, cannot move. Dissolved: lattice separated, ions free to move,
charge can flow.

**Example 3: compare two substances.** "Explain why sodium chloride melts at a much higher temperature than
methane." Sodium chloride: strong electrostatic forces between ions, many bonds, large amount of energy.
Methane: weak forces between molecules, little energy. Always name the particle and the
force.

## Compare with other structure types

| Property | Ionic | Small molecules | Giant covalent | Metals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Particles | Ions | Molecules | Atoms | Atoms with delocalised electrons |
| Melting point | High | Low | Very high | Usually high |
| Conducts as a solid? | No | No | Usually no | Yes |
| Conducts when molten? | Yes | No | Usually no | Yes |
| Conducts in solution? | Yes | No | Not applicable | Not applicable |

Use this table to sort a set of unknown substances. Start with conduction as a solid: yes points to a metal.
Then look at the melting point: low points to small molecules; very high with no conduction points to giant
covalent; high with conduction only when molten or dissolved points to ionic.

## Link to later topics

Because a molten or dissolved ionic compound contains free ions, it can be broken down by passing an electric
current through it. This is **electrolysis**, and the molten or dissolved compound is the **electrolyte**. The
same idea explains why a sodium chloride solution lights a bulb in a simple circuit.

## Exam traps

- **"Molecules of sodium chloride."** There are ions in a lattice, not molecules.
- **"Ionic compounds conduct because they contain ions."** A solid contains ions and does not conduct.
- **"Electrons carry the charge."** In ionic compounds, ions do.
- **"Weak forces between the molecules."** That is a small-molecule answer, not ionic.
- **"The bonds are strong" with no mention of many bonds and a large amount of energy.**
- **"Dissolved in water" meaning the solid is wet.** Dissolved means the lattice has separated into free ions.

## Quick self-test

1. What is the name for the regular structure of an ionic compound?
2. What type of force holds the lattice together?
3. Why do ionic compounds have high melting points?
4. Does solid sodium chloride conduct electricity? Explain.
5. Give two ways to make an ionic compound conduct electricity.
6. What carries the charge in molten sodium chloride?
7. A solid melts at 770 °C and does not conduct, but its solution does. What type of substance is it?
8. A solid melts at 1085 °C and conducts both as a solid and when molten. What type of substance is it?
9. State what the formula NaCl tells you about the lattice.
10. Name one ionic compound whose structure you are expected to know.

### Answers

1. A giant ionic lattice.
2. Strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions.
3. A large amount of energy is needed to break the many strong bonds in the lattice.
4. No. The ions are fixed in position and cannot move, so charge cannot flow.
5. Melt it, or dissolve it in water.
6. The ions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻).
7. An ionic compound.
8. A metal.
9. There is one sodium ion for every chloride ion (a 1 : 1 ratio).
10. Sodium chloride.
