This guide goes deeper on point **3.1.9.1 Electrode potentials and cells** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. The specification covers the "IUPAC convention for writing half-equations for electrode reactions", "the conventional representation of cells", measuring electrode potentials "by reference to the standard hydrogen electrode", "the importance of the conditions when measuring the electrode potential" (Nernst equation not required), standard conditions of 298 K, 100 kPa and 1.00 mol dm⁻³, and the electrochemical series. Students should be able to predict the direction of simple redox reactions, calculate the EMF of a cell, and write and apply the conventional representation; required practical 6 is to measure the EMF of a cell. It is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3.

Values used on this page (V): Mg²⁺/Mg −2.37; Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76; Fe²⁺/Fe −0.44; Ni²⁺/Ni −0.25; H⁺/H₂ 0.00; Cu²⁺/Cu +0.34; I₂/I⁻ +0.54; Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ +0.77; Ag⁺/Ag +0.80; Br₂/Br⁻ +1.09; Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36; MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺ +1.51. In an exam, always use the values provided.

## Why potentials are measured against a reference

A metal dipping into a solution of its ions sets up an equilibrium, for example Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn(s), which leaves a potential difference between metal and solution. That single potential cannot be measured: connecting a voltmeter means adding a second electrode, which has its own potential. So every electrode potential is measured **relative to a reference half-cell**, the **standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)**, which is assigned 0.00 V.

The SHE: hydrogen gas at **100 kPa** bubbled over a **platinum** electrode dipping into a solution with **[H⁺] = 1.00 mol dm⁻³**, at **298 K**. Platinum is used because it is inert and conducts, and it provides a surface for the H⁺/H₂ equilibrium.

**Standard electrode potential, E⦵**: the EMF of a cell made from the half-cell under standard conditions and the SHE. Its sign is the polarity of the half-cell's electrode relative to the SHE.

## IUPAC half-equations

All electrode half-equations are written as **reductions**, with electrons on the left:

```
Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s)                          +0.80 V
MnO₄⁻(aq) + 8H⁺(aq) + 5e⁻ ⇌ Mn²⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l)  +1.51 V
```

A **more positive** E⦵ means the species on the left is more easily reduced: it is a stronger **oxidising agent**. A **more negative** E⦵ means the species on the right is more easily oxidised: it is a stronger **reducing agent**.

## Writing the cell and calculating the EMF

**Rules:**

- The **more negative** half-cell is the **negative electrode**, written on the **left**; oxidation happens there.
- The **more positive** half-cell is the **positive electrode**, on the **right**; reduction happens there.
- `|` separates phases; `||` is the salt bridge; electrodes go on the outside.
- Left side: reduced form, then oxidised form. Right side: oxidised form, then reduced form.
- **EMF = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left)**, positive for the cell written this way.

Electrons flow through the external wire from the negative electrode to the positive electrode.

### Worked example 1: zinc and copper

1. Zn²⁺/Zn (−0.76 V) is more negative, so zinc is the negative electrode.
2. Cell: **Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)**
3. EMF = +0.34 − (−0.76) = **+1.10 V**
4. Left (oxidation): Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. Right (reduction): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.
5. Overall: **Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu**. Electrons flow from zinc to copper through the wire.

### Worked example 2: finding an unknown electrode potential

A standard nickel half-cell is connected to a standard silver half-cell. The EMF is 1.05 V and the silver electrode is positive. Find E⦵(Ni²⁺/Ni).

```
EMF = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative)
1.05 = +0.80 − E⦵(Ni²⁺/Ni)
E⦵(Ni²⁺/Ni) = 0.80 − 1.05 = −0.25 V
```

The polarity tells you which half-cell is the right-hand one; the arithmetic then follows.

### Worked example 3: a cell with no metal in the reaction

Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ (+0.77 V) is combined with I₂/I⁻ (+0.54 V).

1. Neither half-cell has a metal, so both electrodes are platinum.
2. I₂/I⁻ is less positive: negative electrode, on the left; I⁻ is oxidised.
3. Cell: **Pt(s) | I⁻(aq), I₂(aq) || Fe³⁺(aq), Fe²⁺(aq) | Pt(s)**
4. EMF = 0.77 − 0.54 = **+0.23 V**
5. Overall: 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻ and Fe³⁺ + e⁻ → Fe²⁺ (×2), so **2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂**.

A comma separates species in the **same** phase; both ions are in solution, so they share one compartment.

## Predicting the direction of redox reactions

A reaction is feasible under standard conditions if the half-equation of the **oxidising agent** has the **more positive** E⦵. That half-equation runs forwards (reduction); the other runs backwards (oxidation).

### Worked example 4: three predictions from one table

**(a) Will acidified manganate(VII) oxidise chloride ions?**
E⦵(MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺) = +1.51 V is more positive than E⦵(Cl₂/Cl⁻) = +1.36 V. **Yes.** EMF = +0.15 V.

```
2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 10Cl⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O + 5Cl₂
```

This is why hydrochloric acid is not used to acidify manganate(VII) in titrations: it would be oxidised, using up extra manganate(VII).

**(b) Will bromine oxidise iron(II) ions?**
+1.09 V (Br₂/Br⁻) is more positive than +0.77 V (Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺). **Yes**: 2Fe²⁺ + Br₂ → 2Fe³⁺ + 2Br⁻.

**(c) Will iodine oxidise iron(II) ions?**
+0.54 V (I₂/I⁻) is **less** positive than +0.77 V. **No.** The reverse reaction, Fe³⁺ oxidising I⁻, is the feasible one (worked example 3).

When balancing, multiply the half-equations so the electrons cancel. **Never** multiply the E⦵ values.

## Why conditions matter

Each electrode is an equilibrium, so changing concentration, temperature or gas pressure moves it and changes E. Only the direction is needed (the Nernst equation is not required).

**Example.** In the zinc–copper cell, the Cu²⁺ solution is diluted. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu shifts left, so the copper electrode becomes **less positive**. The positive electrode's potential falls, so the **EMF decreases** below 1.10 V.

This is why E⦵ values compare fairly only under standard conditions, and why predictions with a very small EMF (a few hundredths of a volt) may fail in practice.

## Required practical 6: getting a reliable EMF

- Clean metal electrodes with emery paper to remove oxide.
- Use 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions at about 298 K.
- Use a salt bridge of filter paper soaked in a salt, such as potassium nitrate, that does not react with either solution.
- Use a high-resistance voltmeter, so almost no current flows and the reading is the EMF.

## Common mistakes

- Writing an electrode half-equation as an oxidation.
- Putting the more positive half-cell on the left, giving a negative EMF.
- Multiplying E⦵ values when balancing.
- Forgetting Pt(s) when neither species is a metal, or the comma between ions in the same solution.
- Taking the sign of an unknown E⦵ from the voltmeter reading instead of the polarity.
- Saying electrons flow through the salt bridge; ions move there.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate the EMF**: state which is positive, then E(right) − E(left), with sign and unit.
- **Write the conventional representation**: negative electrode on the left, state symbols, Pt where needed.
- **Predict** whether a reaction occurs: compare the two E⦵ values, say which species is the oxidising agent, give the EMF and the balanced equation.
- **Explain** the effect of a concentration change: name the equilibrium, its shift, the change in E for that electrode, then the effect on the EMF.
