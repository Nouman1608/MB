These notes condense **1.1.4 Evaluation** in OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432), Version 3.1 (May 2026). The five learning outcomes are (a) evaluating results and drawing conclusions (HSW6); (b) identifying anomalies; (c) limitations in experimental procedures; (d) precision and accuracy, including margins of error, percentage errors and uncertainties in apparatus (M1.3); and (e) refining a design by suggesting improvements to procedures and apparatus (HSW3). Section 1.1 is assessed in the written papers.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Accurate | Close to the true or accepted value |
| Precise | Repeated results close to one another |
| Uncertainty | The range, ± a value, within which the true value is expected to lie |
| Margin of error | The absolute uncertainty of a result, giving its range |
| Percentage uncertainty | Absolute uncertainty ÷ measured value × 100 |
| Percentage error | (Experimental − accepted) ÷ accepted × 100 |
| Random error | Unpredictable scatter either side of the true value; reduced by repeats |
| Systematic error | A shift in the same direction every time; not reduced by repeats |
| Anomaly | A result that does not fit the pattern of the others |
| Limitation | A specific weakness of the procedure that affects the result |
| Constant mass | Heating, cooling and weighing repeated until two masses agree |

## 1.1.4(a): evaluating a result in four steps

1. Calculate the **percentage error** against the accepted value.
2. Calculate the **total percentage uncertainty** from the apparatus.
3. **Compare** them: error within uncertainty means the result agrees within its margin of error; error larger than uncertainty points to a systematic error.
4. Name the **limitation** that explains the direction of the error, and an **improvement** that fixes it.

## 1.1.4(b): anomalies

- Spot it: a repeat far from the others, or a point well off a graph's trend.
- Leave it out of the mean and the best-fit line; say that you have.
- Repeat the measurement if possible.
- A cause you suggest must fit the direction: a time that is too long needs a reason the reaction was slower or the timing longer.

## 1.1.4(d): uncertainties

**Per reading.**

- Use the value given for the apparatus if one is stated.
- Otherwise ± half the smallest division for an analogue scale, or as stated for a digital display.

**Combining.**

```
quantity from two readings (titre, ΔT, mass by difference): uncertainty × 2
result from × or ÷ of measurements: add the percentage uncertainties
```

**Reducing percentage uncertainty.**

- Measure a larger quantity: a bigger titre, mass or temperature change.
- Use apparatus with a smaller uncertainty: a burette or pipette instead of a measuring cylinder, a three-decimal-place balance.

## 1.1.4(c) and (e): limitations with improvements

| Limitation | Direction of effect | Improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Heat lost in calorimetry | ΔH less exothermic | Insulate and lid; extrapolate a temperature–time graph |
| Gas escapes before sealing | Volume too low | Keep reactants apart until the flask is sealed |
| Gas dissolves in water | Volume too low | Gas syringe |
| Subjective end point (cross, colour) | Random scatter | Light sensor or colorimeter |
| Hydrated salt not fully dehydrated | x too low | Heat to constant mass |
| Anhydrous salt decomposes | x too high | Heat gently |
| Titration end point overshot | Titre too high | Add dropwise near the end point |
| Small reading (titre, ΔT, mass) | Large percentage uncertainty | Use larger quantities |

**Rules for improvements.** Each must fix a named limitation, be specific about the apparatus or step, and not simply say "repeat" (repeats reduce random error but cannot remove a systematic one).

## Exam traps

- **Precise is not accurate.** Close repeats can all be wrong in the same direction.
- **Two readings, double uncertainty.** Titres, temperature changes and masses by difference.
- **Add percentages, not absolute values,** when combining measurements multiplied or divided.
- **Wrong direction.** Heat loss makes ΔH less exothermic; it cannot explain a value more exothermic than data.
- **Vague improvements**: "use more accurate equipment" without naming it.
- **"Repeat it" for systematic error.**
- **Anomalies silently included** in the mean.

## Quick self-test

1. Explain the difference between precise and accurate results.
2. A thermometer is marked every 0.5 °C. State the uncertainty in one reading and in a temperature change.
3. A titre is 15.20 cm³ from two burette readings each ±0.05 cm³. Calculate the percentage uncertainty.
4. Give two ways to reduce the percentage uncertainty in the titre in question 3.
5. An experimental ΔH is −52.1 kJ mol⁻¹; the accepted value is −57.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the percentage error.
6. The total percentage uncertainty in question 5 was 2.5%. What does this suggest?
7. Repeats give 4.52, 4.49, 4.95 and 4.50 g. Identify the anomaly and give the mean.
8. A result is 1.25 ± 0.03 mol dm⁻³; the accepted value is 1.27 mol dm⁻³. Does the result agree within its margin of error?
9. Magnesium ribbon is dropped into acid, then the bung is replaced quickly and hydrogen collected. Give the limitation, its effect and an improvement.
10. Why does repeating an experiment not remove a systematic error?
11. A hydrated salt is heated very strongly and the anhydrous salt begins to decompose. What happens to the calculated value of x?
12. A volume has a 0.5% uncertainty and a mass has a 0.8% uncertainty. What is the percentage uncertainty in a concentration in g dm⁻³ calculated from them?

### Answers

1. **Precise**: repeats are close to each other. **Accurate**: the result is close to the true or accepted value.
2. One reading ±0.25 °C; temperature change **±0.5 °C** (two readings).
3. 0.10 ÷ 15.20 × 100 = **0.66%**.
4. Use a more dilute titrant (larger titre), or titrate a larger pipetted volume; either gives a larger titre for the same ±0.10 cm³.
5. Difference = 57.0 − 52.1 = 4.9 kJ mol⁻¹; 4.9 ÷ 57.0 × 100 = **8.6%**, with the experimental value less exothermic than accepted.
6. 8.6% is larger than 2.5%, so the apparatus cannot explain the difference: there is a **systematic error**, such as heat loss.
7. **4.95 g** is anomalous; mean of 4.52, 4.49 and 4.50 = **4.50 g**.
8. **Yes**: the range is 1.22 to 1.28 mol dm⁻³, which includes 1.27.
9. Hydrogen **escapes** before the bung is replaced, so the volume is **too low**. Keep the magnesium apart from the acid (for example in a small tube inside the flask) until the flask is sealed.
10. A systematic error shifts every result in the **same direction**, so averaging repeats gives the same wrong value.
11. Extra mass is lost, so the "water lost" is too large and **x is too high**.
12. **1.3%** (0.5% + 0.8%).
