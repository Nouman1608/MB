These notes condense **A4.2 Conservation of biodiversity** from the IB DP *Biology guide*, first assessment 2025. Statements A4.2.1 to A4.2.8 apply to both SL and HL. The [A4.2 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-biology-dp-biology-ib-dp-biology-1-a4-2/) has the reasoning and model answers, and the [A4.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-biology-dp-biology-ib-dp-biology-1-a4-2-practice/) test them. Evolution and speciation, the first half of A4, is in the [A4.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-biology-dp-biology-ib-dp-biology-1-a4-1-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Biodiversity | Variety of life at ecosystem, species and genetic levels |
| Species richness | Number of species in an area |
| Species evenness | How similar the abundances of the species are |
| Mass extinction | Loss of a large proportion of species in a geologically short time |
| Anthropogenic | Caused by humans |
| Overexploitation | Harvesting faster than a population can replace itself |
| Invasive alien species | Species introduced outside its range that spreads and causes harm |
| In situ conservation | Protecting species in their natural habitat |
| Ex situ conservation | Protecting species away from their natural habitat |
| Rewilding | Restoring natural processes and species to an area |
| Germ plasm | Living tissue, such as seeds or sperm, stored for future breeding |
| EDGE | Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered |

## A4.2.1: levels of biodiversity

- **Ecosystem**: variety of habitats and communities.
- **Species**: richness and evenness.
- **Genetic**: variety of alleles within a species.

Losses cascade downwards: lost ecosystem → lost species → small populations → lost alleles.

## A4.2.2: now and the past

- Biodiversity has generally increased over geological time.
- Interrupted by five recognised mass extinctions, the latest at the end of the Cretaceous.
- Past mass extinctions: natural causes (volcanic activity, asteroid impact).
- Today: extinction rates raised far above the background rate by humans.
- Species counts are uncertain because classification varies ("splitters" versus "lumpers").

## A4.2.3: extinction case studies

| Species | Cause | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| North Island giant moa | Hunting by first settlers; forest burning | Extinct within about two centuries of human arrival |
| Caribbean monk seal | Killed for oil and meat; overfishing of food | Last seen 1952; declared extinct 2008 |
| Your own choice (a plant or local species) | Name the human cause | State the outcome and date if known |

Answer structure: species → vulnerability → human cause → outcome.

## A4.2.4: ecosystem loss

- **Mixed dipterocarp forest, Southeast Asia**: logging, oil palm plantations, fire.
- Learn one more case from your own region.

## A4.2.5: evidence for a crisis

- Repeated, reliable surveys across many habitats.
- International assessments: IPBES (2019) estimated around one million species at risk of extinction.
- Look for change in richness and evenness over time.

**Quality checklist**

- Same method, area and effort each time?
- Large, widespread sample?
- Professional or citizen science (coverage versus consistency)?
- Peer-reviewed?

## A4.2.6: causes

Overarching: **human population growth**.

| Cause | Example of effect |
|---|---|
| Overexploitation | Overfishing reduces fish stocks |
| Urbanisation and land clearance | Habitat destroyed or fragmented |
| Agriculture and monocultures | Many species replaced by one crop |
| Pollution | Toxins or excess nutrients harm species |
| Invasive species, pests, diseases | Spread by global travel; outcompete or kill natives |

## A4.2.7: approaches

| In situ | Ex situ |
|---|---|
| Nature reserves, rewilding, habitat restoration | Zoos, botanic gardens, captive breeding, seed and tissue banks |
| Keeps the ecosystem and natural behaviour | Back-up when wild population or habitat is failing |
| Needs large protected areas | Small populations lose genetic diversity |

Rule: combine approaches; one method rarely suits all species.

## A4.2.8: EDGE

- **Evolutionarily distinct**: few close relatives; much unique evolutionary history.
- **Globally endangered**: high risk of extinction.
- High on both means priority.
- Choices also involve cost, success chance, cultural and economic values, so they are debated.

## Worked reminders

**Percentage decline.** 48 species to 36: (36 − 48) ÷ 48 × 100 = **−25%**.

**Effort check.** A second survey using half the hours cannot be compared directly; standardise effort first.

**Evenness.** Four species with 25, 25, 25, 25 individuals are more even than 85, 5, 5, 5, though richness is the same.

## Exam traps

- Biodiversity defined as species number only.
- Case study given with no cause or outcome.
- Survey decline accepted without checking method.
- One approach recommended for every species.
- EDGE described as "most endangered" only.
- Natural mass extinctions confused with the present human-driven losses.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three levels of biodiversity.
2. Distinguish species richness from species evenness.
3. How many mass extinctions are usually recognised, and which was the most recent?
4. Why are counts of species uncertain?
5. Give the human causes of extinction of the North Island giant moa.
6. When was the Caribbean monk seal last confirmed alive?
7. Give two causes of loss of mixed dipterocarp forest.
8. Give two checks you would make before accepting a survey showing decline.
9. What is the overarching cause of the current biodiversity crisis?
10. Distinguish in situ from ex situ conservation, with an example of each.
11. Why do small captive populations cause problems for conservation?
12. What two criteria does EDGE combine?

### Answers

1. Ecosystem, species and genetic diversity.
2. Richness is the number of species; evenness is how similar their abundances are.
3. Five; the end-Cretaceous.
4. Classification varies, so the same organisms can be split into more species or lumped into fewer.
5. Hunting by the first human settlers and forest clearance by burning.
6. 1952.
7. Logging, clearance for oil palm plantations, fire (any two).
8. Same method, area and effort; large and widespread sample; peer-reviewed (any two).
9. Human population growth.
10. In situ protects species in their habitat (nature reserve); ex situ protects them elsewhere (zoo or seed bank).
11. They lose genetic diversity, and animals may not survive release.
12. Evolutionary distinctiveness and global endangerment.
