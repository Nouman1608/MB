These questions practise key question 5.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What are the roles
and functions of education?* Set A follows the six-part (a)–(f) structure the syllabus describes for
each Paper 2 question; Set B gives extra practice on individual concepts from all three views.

## Set A — Roles and functions of education

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *value consensus* **[2]**
- (ii) *cultural capital* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** roles of education identified by functionalists. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways education may help the economy, according to functionalists. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways education may perpetuate gender inequality, according to feminists. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the view that education is an agent of ideological control. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that education provides equal opportunities for all. **[14]**

## Set B — Extra practice

**(g)** Explain the correspondence principle. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** ways standardised testing supports meritocracy, according to functionalists. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** ways material factors may reproduce class inequality in education. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** criticisms of the Marxist view of education. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** ways role models in school may affect girls' aspirations. **[4]**

**(l)** Discuss the role of setting by ability in education. **[8]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Broad agreement among members of a society about shared norms and values (1), which
functionalists say education helps to create (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** The knowledge, language, tastes and attitudes valued by schools (1), mostly passed on
in middle-class families, which give children an advantage (1) (Bourdieu).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: socialisation; social control; economic; selective (role
allocation).

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each (point plus development):
- **Skills**: literacy, numeracy and specialist knowledge for a modern workforce (Durkheim).
- **Vocationalism**: courses and apprenticeships prepare pupils for particular jobs.
- **Role allocation**: the most able are sorted into the most important jobs (Davis and Moore, 1945).
- **Work discipline**: punctuality and following instructions prepare pupils for employment.
- **Qualifications** signal to employers what a person can do, making recruitment efficient.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Gendered subject choice**: girls steered away from science and technology (Kelly, 1987).
- **Teacher expectations**: teachers expect less of girls (Stanworth, 1983) or give boys more attention
  (Spender, 1982).
- **Role models**: senior posts and science teaching often held by men.
- **Access**: in some societies girls are kept out of secondary school by poverty, early marriage or
  law.
- **Peer control**: girls judged by reputation or mocked for ambition.
- **Gender hierarchy**: male power in school leadership teaches that authority is male.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, both sides with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic.
- **Marxist view:** Althusser (1971) called education an ideological state apparatus; the hidden
  curriculum teaches obedience and acceptance of hierarchy (Bowles and Gintis, 1976); the myth of
  meritocracy makes inequality look fair; the official curriculum reflects ruling-class knowledge.
- **Counter-arguments:** functionalists see the same processes as socialisation into shared values,
  not ideology; Willis (1977) shows pupils resisting; schools also teach critical thinking and
  citizenship.
- **Evaluation:** credit a judgement, for example that schools do transmit dominant values, but pupils
  do not simply absorb them.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the view:*
- Functionalists: free, compulsory schooling and universalistic standards (Parsons).
- Standardised tests apply the same measure to all.
- Equal-opportunity laws and policies against discrimination.
- Many people from poorer backgrounds achieve social mobility through qualifications.
- Girls' rising achievement in many countries suggests opportunities have widened.

*Against the view:*
- Marxists: cultural capital (Bourdieu) and the myth of meritocracy (Bowles and Gintis).
- Material factors: digital access, tuition, private schools.
- Setting by ability places working-class pupils in lower sets.
- Feminists: gendered subject choice and teacher expectations; girls denied access in some societies.
- Achievement gaps by class, ethnicity and gender persist.

*Conclusion:* formal equality of opportunity exists in many systems, but real opportunities are
unequal. Credit a reasoned judgement.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Bowles and Gintis's idea (1976) that the organisation of school mirrors the workplace (2).
For example, the hierarchy of head teacher, teachers and pupils mirrors managers and workers, and
working for grades mirrors working for wages, preparing pupils to accept work under capitalism (2).

**(h) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: the same test for everyone applies universalistic standards, so
results reflect ability; results let employers and universities select fairly; tests motivate effort
by rewarding it; results sort pupils into appropriate courses (role allocation).

**(i) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: poorer pupils may lack digital devices or internet; less space to
study at home; wealthier families pay for private tuition or private schools; costs of trips,
uniforms and books; poorer pupils may need to work part-time.

**(j) [4]** Two criticisms, 2 marks each: pupils are not passive and may resist (Willis, 1977); many
working-class pupils succeed; schools teach critical thinking as well as obedience; it ignores gender
and ethnic inequality; it may exaggerate how closely schools mirror workplaces today.

**(k) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: few women science teachers may suggest science is not for girls;
male head teachers suggest leadership is male; female teachers concentrated in teaching young children
may suggest caring work suits women; successful women role models can raise girls' ambitions.

**(l) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *Functionalist:* matches teaching to ability; stretches the most able; efficient route to role
  allocation.
- *Marxist:* working-class pupils over-represented in lower sets; lower-level examinations limit
  progress; labelling and lower expectations.
- *Feminist and others:* sets may reflect gendered expectations; low-set pupils may form anti-school
  subcultures.
- *Evaluation:* setting may help some pupils but can reproduce inequality; credit a judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name Durkheim and Parsons separately?** It helps: Durkheim is linked to social
solidarity and Parsons to meritocracy and universalistic standards.

**Is Willis a Marxist?** Willis wrote from a Marxist position but criticised Bowles and Gintis for
treating pupils as passive, so he can be used for and against the Marxist view.

**How should I evaluate the functionalist view?** Use Marxist and feminist criticisms and evidence
about persistent achievement gaps, then give a judgement.
