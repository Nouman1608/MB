## Questions

**1.** Define each of these terms for a wave.

**(a)** Wavelength **[1]**
**(b)** Frequency **[1]**
**(c)** Amplitude **[1]**

**2.** On a displacement-distance graph of a wave on a rope, the highest and lowest points are 9.0 cm apart vertically. Three complete waves occupy 1.5 m of rope.

**(a)** State the amplitude of the wave. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the wavelength. **[2]**

**3.** A wave on a spring has frequency 15 Hz and wavelength 0.20 m.

**(a)** Calculate the speed of the wave. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate its period, to 2 significant figures. **[2]**

**4.** A loudspeaker emits a note of frequency 680 Hz. In air, sound travels at 340 m/s.

**(a)** Calculate the wavelength. **[3]**
**(b)** A second note has wavelength 0.40 m in the same air. Calculate its frequency. **[2]**

**5.** Four waves are listed: ripples on a pond, sound from a speaker, a wave on a skipping rope, and a pulse made by pushing a slinky in and out along its length.

**(a)** State which two are transverse. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the direction of vibration compares with the direction of travel in a transverse wave and in a longitudinal wave. **[2]**

**6.** Describe how you would use a slinky spring to model a transverse pulse and a longitudinal pulse, and what you would see in each case. **[5]**

**7.** Describe evidence that it is the wave that travels, and not the medium, for:

**(a)** ripples on water **[3]**
**(b)** sound waves in air **[3]**

**8.** A pupil stands 68.0 m from a large wall and claps once. A digital timer records 0.40 s until the echo is heard.

**(a)** Calculate the speed of sound. **[3]**
**(b)** The pupil repeats the experiment with a hand-held stopwatch and gets times between 0.30 s and 0.55 s. Explain why, and suggest one improvement. **[2]**

**9.** A ripple tank dipper is set to different frequencies. The wavelength is measured on the screen each time.

| Frequency (Hz) | 4.0 | 6.0 | 8.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wavelength (cm) | 6.0 | 4.0 | 3.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the wave speed in m/s for the frequency of 4.0 Hz. **[2]**
**(b)** Show that the data for 6.0 Hz gives the same speed. **[2]**
**(c)** Predict the wavelength in cm at 12.0 Hz. **[2]**
**(d)** State what happens to the wavelength as the frequency increases at constant speed. **[1]**

**10.** An oscilloscope shows a trace from a microphone. Nine complete waves occupy 18 ms.

**(a)** Calculate the period. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the frequency. **[1]**
**(c)** The sound travels at 340 m/s in air. Calculate the wavelength. **[2]**

**11.** A starter pistol is fired. An observer 400 m away starts a stopwatch on seeing the flash and stops it on hearing the bang, 1.17 s later.

**(a)** Explain why the time taken by light can be ignored. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the speed of sound, to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(c)** The bang contains a component of frequency 1.2 kHz. Calculate its wavelength using your answer to (b). **[3]**
**(d)** Suggest two ways to make the result more reliable. **[2]**

**12.** A dipper in a ripple tank makes ripples at 9.0 Hz. A photograph with a ruler shows that 7 wavelengths cover 0.126 m.

**(a)** Explain why the length of 7 wavelengths is measured, not one. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the wavelength. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the speed of the ripples, to 2 significant figures. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why ripples are a suitable model for a transverse wave. **[2]**
**(e)** State what is transferred from the dipper to the edge of the tank by the ripples. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The length of one complete wave, such as the gap between neighbouring crests [1]
**(b)** The number of waves passing a point each second (or vibrations per second) [1]
**(c)** The maximum displacement from the rest (middle) position [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition needs the idea of per second for frequency and of the middle line for amplitude. Saying "how fast the wave goes" describes speed.

**2. (a)** Amplitude is measured from the middle line, so half the vertical gap [1]; 9.0 ÷ 2 = **4.5 cm** [1]
**(b)** 1.5 ÷ 3 [1] = **0.50 m** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving 9.0 cm for the amplitude is the usual slip, because the full gap is crest to trough.

**3. (a)** v = f × λ [1]; = 15 × 0.20 [1] = **3.0 m/s** [1]
**(b)** T = 1 ÷ f = 1 ÷ 15 [1] = **0.067 s** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the equation, the substitution and the answer with its unit, so that each step of your reasoning is visible.

**4. (a)** λ = v ÷ f [1]; = 340 ÷ 680 [1] = **0.50 m** [1]
**(b)** f = 340 ÷ 0.40 [1] = **850 Hz** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Rearranging from v = f λ in the wrong direction (multiplying 340 by 680) is the most common way to lose these marks.

**5. (a)** Ripples on a pond [1]; wave on a skipping rope [1]
**(b)** Transverse: the vibration is perpendicular to the way the wave moves [1]; longitudinal: the vibration is along the way the wave moves [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both directions relative to the travel direction. Saying "up and down" alone does not describe the relationship.

**6.** Stretch the slinky along a bench or floor [1]. Transverse: move one end sideways, so a pulse travels along the spring while the coils move from side to side [1]. The coils move perpendicular to the way the pulse travels [1]. Longitudinal: push the end in and out along the spring, so bunched coils (compressions) and stretched coils (rarefactions) travel along it [1]. The coils move back and forth parallel to the travel direction [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A full description states the action, what you see, and the direction of coil movement. Marks go to the observation, not just the setup.

**7. (a)** Float a cork (or small piece of polystyrene) on the water [1]. As ripples pass, it moves up and down about the same place [1]. It is not carried to the edge, so the water does not travel with the ripples; the wave does [1]
**(b)** Sound is heard across a room with no steady wind from the source [1]. Smoke or light dust near a loudspeaker shakes to and fro about its position and does not drift away [1]. So the air vibrates about fixed positions and passes on the disturbance; the air is not carried to the listener [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State the observation and then the conclusion. An answer that says only "the air does not move" gives no evidence.

**8. (a)** Distance travelled = 2 × 68.0 = 136 m [1]; v = 136 ÷ 0.40 [1] = **340 m/s** [1]
**(b)** Hand timing has reaction-time error that is large compared with 0.40 s [1]. Use a longer distance (a longer time), electronic timing, or repeat and average [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Using 68.0 m as the distance is the typical error. The sound goes to the wall and back.

**9. (a)** λ = 6.0 cm = 0.060 m [1]; v = 4.0 × 0.060 = **0.24 m/s** [1]
**(b)** 4.0 cm = 0.040 m [1]; 6.0 × 0.040 = **0.24 m/s**, the same [1]
**(c)** λ = v ÷ f = 0.24 ÷ 12.0 [1] = 0.020 m = **2.0 cm** [1]
**(d)** The wavelength **decreases** (it halves when the frequency doubles) [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert centimetres to metres before you use m/s. For "show that", write both the working and the comparison.

**10. (a)** 18 ms ÷ 9 [1] = **2.0 ms** (0.0020 s) [1]
**(b)** f = 1 ÷ 0.0020 = **500 Hz** [1]
**(c)** λ = 340 ÷ 500 [1] = **0.68 m** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Take the time for one wave from the whole trace. A single gap between peaks on a time axis is a period, not a wavelength.

**11. (a)** Light travels much faster than sound, so the time it takes over 400 m is negligible [1]
**(b)** v = 400 ÷ 1.17 [1] = **342 m/s** [1]
**(c)** f = 1200 Hz [1]; λ = 342 ÷ 1200 [1] = **0.285 m** [1]
**(d)** Any two: repeat and find the mean; use a greater distance; use electronic timing; use several observers [2] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert kHz to Hz first. Reliability points need a method (repeat and average), not just "be careful".

**12. (a)** It reduces the percentage error from reading the ruler [1]
**(b)** 0.126 ÷ 7 [1] = **0.018 m** [1]
**(c)** v = 9.0 × 0.018 [1] = 0.162, so **0.16 m/s** [1]
**(d)** The water surface moves up and down [1], at right angles to the direction the ripples travel [1]
**(e)** **Energy** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (e) is lost by answering "water". The water vibrates in place; energy is what travels.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the full crest-to-trough height as the amplitude.
- Leaving cm, mm, kHz or ms unconverted before using v = f λ.
- Rearranging the equation the wrong way round, so that speed is divided by frequency to give a speed.
- Reading a period from a displacement-distance graph or a wavelength from a displacement-time graph.
- Using the distance to the wall rather than twice that distance in echo methods.
- Describing transverse and longitudinal waves without comparing vibration with the direction of travel.
- Saying the water or air "moves with" the wave, instead of vibrating about fixed positions.
- Describing a speed measurement without stating what is measured, how, and what is calculated.
