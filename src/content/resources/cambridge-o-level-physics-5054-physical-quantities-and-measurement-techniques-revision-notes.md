## What subtopic 1.1 asks you to do

- Measure lengths with tapes, rulers and micrometers, choosing appropriate precision.
- Measure liquid volume with a measuring cylinder and solid volume by displacement.
- Measure time intervals with clocks and digital timers.
- Find small distances and short times by measuring multiples, including a pendulum's period.
- Know which quantities are scalars and which are vectors.
- Find the resultant of two vectors at right angles, by calculation or scale drawing.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Precision** | How finely an instrument can measure: its smallest scale division |
| **Parallax error** | Error from reading a scale at an angle instead of straight on |
| **Meniscus** | Curved liquid surface in a cylinder; read the bottom of it for water |
| **Displacement (volume)** | Rise in liquid level when a sinking solid is submerged, equal to its volume |
| **Oscillation** | One complete swing, out and back to the starting position |
| **Period** | Time for one complete oscillation |
| **Fiducial marker** | Fixed reference mark at the centre of a swing, used to start and stop timing |
| **Scalar** | Quantity with magnitude only |
| **Vector** | Quantity with magnitude and direction |
| **Resultant** | Single vector with the same effect as two or more vectors combined |

## Instruments at a glance

| Instrument | Typical precision | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Tape measure | 1 cm or 1 mm | Long distances |
| Metre rule | 1 mm | Everyday lengths, pendulum length, spring extension |
| Micrometer | 0.01 mm | Wire diameter, thickness of a coin or glass |
| Measuring cylinder | depends on size; smaller cylinder = finer scale | Liquid volume, displacement |
| Stopwatch | reads 0.01 s, but limited by reaction time | Intervals of seconds or more |
| Digital timer with light gates | very fine; no reaction time | Short intervals |

## Micrometer in three steps

1. Read the sleeve: whole millimetres, plus 0.5 mm if the half mark is showing.
2. Read the thimble division on the datum line and multiply by 0.01 mm.
3. Add them. Example: sleeve 4.5 mm, thimble 23 → 4.5 + 0.23 = **4.73 mm**.

Close with the ratchet; check zero with jaws closed; average readings at several points.

## Measuring multiples

- **Method:** measure N items or N oscillations together, then divide the total by N.
- **Why:** the reading or reaction-time uncertainty stays about the same, so dividing by N makes
  it N times smaller per item.
- **Paper:** 300 sheets = 28.5 mm → 28.5 ÷ 300 = **0.095 mm** per sheet.
- **Pendulum:** time 20 oscillations from a central marker, repeat, average, divide by 20.
  Example: mean of 20 oscillations = 36.0 s → period = **1.80 s**.

## Scalars and vectors (syllabus lists)

- **Scalars:** distance, speed, time, mass, energy, temperature.
- **Vectors:** displacement, force, weight, velocity, acceleration, momentum, electric field
  strength, gravitational field strength.

## Resultant of two perpendicular vectors

```
magnitude:  R = √(a² + b²)
direction:  tan θ = opposite / adjacent   (state the reference direction)
```

**Example:** 6.0 m/s east and 2.5 m/s north → R = √(36 + 6.25) = √42.25 = **6.5 m/s**;
tan θ = 2.5 ÷ 6.0 → θ = **22.6° north of east**.

**Scale drawing:** choose and state a scale → draw first arrow → draw second from its tip at 90° →
join tail of first to tip of second → measure length and angle.

## Exam traps

- The thimble scale counts hundredths of a millimetre, not tenths.
- The volume of a solid is the **difference** between the two cylinder readings, not the final
  reading.
- Never time a single short oscillation by hand.
- Perpendicular vectors combine by Pythagoras, not by adding: 3 N and 4 N give 5 N.
- A vector answer needs a direction, such as "north of east" or an angle to a stated line.
- Weight is a vector (a force); mass is a scalar.
- Stopwatch readings to 0.01 s do not make a hand-timed single swing precise.

## Self-test

1. State the precision of a micrometer.
2. A micrometer shows 3.0 mm on the sleeve and 41 on the thimble. Give the reading.
3. Why is the bottom of the meniscus read?
4. A measuring cylinder reads 42.0 cm³; a stone is lowered in and it reads 49.5 cm³. Find the
   stone's volume.
5. Why should a pendulum be timed from the centre of its swing?
6. Twenty oscillations take 24.8 s. Find the period.
7. A coil of 40 turns of wire spans 22.0 mm. Find the wire's diameter.
8. Name three vectors from the syllabus list.
9. Is temperature a scalar or a vector?
10. Find the resultant of 7.0 N and 24.0 N at right angles.
11. When is a digital timer better than a stopwatch?
12. In a scale drawing, which points does the resultant join?

### Answers

1. 0.01 mm.
2. 3.0 + 0.41 = **3.41 mm**.
3. The water surface curves up at the walls; the bottom of the meniscus gives a consistent level.
   Read at eye level.
4. 49.5 − 42.0 = **7.5 cm³**.
5. The bob moves fastest there, so the moment it passes the marker is easiest to judge.
6. 24.8 ÷ 20 = **1.24 s**.
7. 22.0 ÷ 40 = **0.55 mm**.
8. Any three: displacement, force, weight, velocity, acceleration, momentum, electric field
   strength, gravitational field strength.
9. Scalar.
10. √(49 + 576) = √625 = **25.0 N**.
11. For short intervals, because it is started and stopped electronically with no reaction time.
12. The tail of the first vector and the tip of the second.
