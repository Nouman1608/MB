**HL only.** This is the fourth study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. This study runs from the 1840s, through the sectional crisis and the war itself (1861 to 1865), to the end of Reconstruction in 1877. The questions often turn on **cause and consequence**, **significance** and **continuity and change**, and historians still disagree about the causes of the war and the success of Reconstruction.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: the impact of slavery on the USA; long-term causes of the war; short-term causes; the reasons for the outcome; the impact of the war; and the successes and failures of Reconstruction. The [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/) shows how to use the concepts in an essay.

## Key chronology

- 1832 to 1833: Nullification Crisis.
- 1846 to 1848: war with Mexico; the Wilmot Proviso debate.
- 1850: Compromise of 1850, including the Fugitive Slave Act.
- 1854: Kansas-Nebraska Act; Republican Party formed.
- 1857: *Dred Scott* decision. 1859: John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry.
- November 1860: Lincoln elected. December 1860: South Carolina secedes.
- April 1861: Fort Sumter. September 1862: Antietam. 1 January 1863: Emancipation Proclamation.
- July 1863: Gettysburg and Vicksburg. April 1865: Lee surrenders at Appomattox; Lincoln assassinated.
- 1865 to 1870: 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments. 1877: Compromise of 1877 and the end of Reconstruction.

## Impact of the system of slavery on the USA

**The cotton economy.** After the cotton gin (1793) and the opening of land in the Deep South, cotton became the leading US export, accounting for more than half of exports by the 1850s. Textile mills in Britain and New England depended on it. The enslaved population grew to nearly four million by 1860. Slave owners were a small minority of white southerners, but they held great wealth and political power.

**Conditions.** Enslaved people worked in gangs under overseers, were bought and sold (the internal slave trade broke up families), and faced punishment and sexual abuse. Slave codes limited movement and learning to read.

**Adaptation and resistance.** Enslaved people built families, churches and communities. Day-to-day resistance included slowing work and escape. The Underground Railroad and individuals such as Harriet Tubman helped some reach the North. Rebellions, such as Nat Turner's in 1831, were rare and brutally put down, and increased southern fear.

**Political impacts.** The debate over slavery shaped national politics. William Lloyd Garrison called for immediate abolition in *The Liberator* (1831). Frederick Douglass, a formerly enslaved man, became a leading speaker. In Congress, "gag rules" (1836 to 1844) blocked petitions on slavery. Many northerners opposed the expansion of slavery without calling for abolition, which is an important distinction. Southerners defended slavery as a "positive good".

## Long-term causes of the Civil War

**The Nullification Crisis (1832 to 1833).** South Carolina, led by John C. Calhoun, claimed a state could nullify a federal tariff. President Jackson threatened force, and a compromise tariff ended the crisis. It set out a states' rights argument that the South later used for secession.

**States' rights.** Southern leaders argued that the Constitution was a compact among states. Historians debate how far this was a principled belief and how far a means to protect slavery. It is worth noting that southern politicians supported federal power when it served slavery, as in the Fugitive Slave Act.

**Sectionalism.** The North developed industry, towns, railways and immigrant labour. The South remained rural and dependent on cotton and slavery. These differences shaped politics and economic interests such as the tariff and internal improvements. A common error is to claim that economic difference alone caused the war. The central difference was that one section depended on slavery and the other increasingly did not.

**Westward expansion.** Each new territory raised the question of whether slavery would spread. The Missouri Compromise (1820) set a line. After the war with Mexico (1846 to 1848), new land reopened the question.

## Short-term causes

- **Compromise of 1850**: California entered as a free state, the territories of Utah and New Mexico used popular sovereignty, the slave trade was ended in the District of Columbia, and a tougher Fugitive Slave Act was passed. The act angered northerners.
- **Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854)**: Stephen Douglas's bill allowed popular sovereignty in the territories, repealing the Missouri Compromise line. Violence followed in Kansas ("Bleeding Kansas"). The act helped form the Republican Party, which opposed slavery's expansion.
- ***Dred Scott v. Sandford* (1857)**: the Supreme Court ruled that Black Americans could not be citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories. This outraged northerners.
- **Harpers Ferry (1859)**: John Brown's raid, intended to arm enslaved people, shocked the South, which feared further attacks.
- **The election of 1860**: the Democratic Party split, and Lincoln won with about 40 per cent of the popular vote, with no southern electoral votes. Seven states seceded before he took office, and four more after Fort Sumter.

## Reasons for the outcome of the war

**Strengths and weaknesses.** The Union had about 22 million people against about 9 million in the Confederacy (including about 3.5 million enslaved people). It held most of the industry, railways and shipping. The Confederacy had fine officers, strong motivation to defend its territory and the advantage of fighting on interior lines, but lacked industry and finance, and its states' rights principles made central direction hard. A naval blockade cut its trade.

**Leaders.** Lincoln held the Union together, balancing radical and moderate Republicans and border states. He found generals who would fight: Grant in the west and then overall command, and Sherman. Jefferson Davis struggled with quarrels among governors and generals, though Robert E. Lee was a skilled field commander.

**Emancipation and African American participation.** The Emancipation Proclamation (effective 1 January 1863) freed enslaved people in areas in rebellion. It made the war one against slavery as well as for the Union, and prevented European powers from supporting the Confederacy. About 180,000 Black men served in the Union army, and many more worked for it. Their service weakened the Confederacy's labour force and added to Union strength.

**Foreign relations.** The Confederacy hoped that "King Cotton" would draw Britain and France in. It did not, because Britain had alternative supplies, public opinion opposed slavery and the Union's victories at Antietam and Gettysburg showed the strength of the Union. The Trent Affair (1861) nearly led to a break, but was settled.

## Impact of the Civil War

**Political.** Federal power grew: conscription, income tax, the suspension of habeas corpus and the end of slavery. The Republican Party dominated national politics. The question of secession was ended.

**Economic.** Northern industry expanded under wartime demand and legislation: the Homestead Act, Morrill Land-Grant Act, Pacific Railway Act (1862) and the National Banking Acts. The South was devastated: farms, railways and towns destroyed, and its slave-based wealth gone.

**Social and cultural.** The war killed at least 620,000 soldiers, and some recent estimates exceed 700,000. Emancipation freed nearly four million people. Memory of the war shaped culture, including "Lost Cause" myths in the South, which minimised slavery as a cause.

**Women.** Northern and southern women ran farms and businesses, worked in factories, and served as nurses, with Clara Barton as an example. They organised aid through bodies such as the US Sanitary Commission. Black women, including Tubman, served as scouts and nurses. Women's wartime roles did not bring political rights but led to new activism.

## Successes and failures of Reconstruction

**Presidential and Congressional Reconstruction.** Lincoln proposed a lenient plan in 1863. President Andrew Johnson, after Lincoln's death, pardoned many ex-Confederates. Radical Republicans in Congress, angered by Black Codes in the South, passed the Civil Rights Act of 1866 over Johnson's veto, and the Reconstruction Acts (1867), dividing the South into military districts. Congress impeached Johnson in 1868 but he survived by one vote. The **13th Amendment** (1865) abolished slavery, the **14th** (1868) defined citizenship and equal protection, and the **15th** (1870) barred race-based denial of the vote.

**Southern resistance.** Southern governments passed **Black Codes** to control Black labour. The **Ku Klux Klan**, founded in 1865 or 1866, used terror against Black voters and Republicans. Federal Enforcement Acts (1870 to 1871) reduced the Klan's activity for a while, but violence continued.

**Actions by formerly enslaved people.** Freed people sought land, education, family reunification and political voice. They built churches and schools, voted in large numbers, and elected representatives, including Hiram Revels to the Senate. The Freedmen's Bureau helped, but land redistribution mostly failed, and many became sharecroppers.

**Compromise of 1877.** After the disputed election of 1876, Republican Rutherford Hayes took office, and federal troops withdrew from the remaining southern states. Reconstruction ended, and southern Democrats ("Redeemers") took control.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- **Causes**: was slavery the central cause (as James McPherson and Eric Foner argue), or did a "blundering generation" of politicians fail (an older view)? Was economic difference decisive (Charles Beard's early argument)?
- **Outcome**: was Union victory inevitable because of resources, or contingent on leadership and key battles?
- **Reconstruction**: the older Dunning School view called it a failure caused by incompetent Black rule. W. E. B. Du Bois in *Black Reconstruction* (1935) rejected that. Foner called it an "unfinished revolution". Whether it was a success or failure depends on the criteria.

## Common mistakes

- Treating slavery and economic difference as the same cause.
- Giving a list of battles instead of reasons for the outcome.
- Stopping at 1865 and forgetting Reconstruction.
- Using the Dunning School view as fact.
