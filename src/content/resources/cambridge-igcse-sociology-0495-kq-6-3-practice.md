## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** An aim of punishment that removes an offender's ability to commit further crime (1), for example through imprisonment or a driving ban (1).

**2. [2]** Discouraging the wider public from committing crime (1) by making an example of offenders through visible punishment (1).

**3. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: electronic tagging; curfews; restraining orders; community sentences; probation; fines.

**4. [2]** Excluding a person from a group or community as punishment (1) while they continue to live in or near it, for example shunning (1).

**5. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: CCTV linked to facial recognition; automatic number plate recognition; analysis of mobile phone data; monitoring of social media; electronic tagging with location tracking.

**6. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: better street lighting; locks and alarms (target hardening); CCTV; designing buildings and estates to increase natural surveillance; removing cash from tills or vehicles.

### Section B

**7. [6]** Three aims, 1 for identifying and 1 for developing each:
- **Protection of citizens**: keeping the public safe from dangerous offenders, for example through restraining orders or prison.
- **Deterrence**: discouraging the offender (individual) and others (general) through fear of punishment.
- **Retribution**: making the offender pay for the crime with punishment in proportion to its seriousness.
- **Incapacitation**: making further offending impossible for a period, as with prison.
- **Rehabilitation**: changing the offender's behaviour through education, therapy or training.

**8. [6]** Three limitations, 1 + 1 each:
- **Unequal impact**: a fixed fine is a heavy burden for a poor person but trivial for a rich one, so deterrence is uneven.
- **Non-payment**: offenders may not pay, leading to further sanctions, possibly prison.
- **No rehabilitation**: a fine does not address the causes of offending, such as addiction.
- **Affects families**: fines can push the offender's family further into poverty.
- **Seen as buying the right to offend**: wealthy offenders or companies may treat fines as a cost of doing business, as in some corporate crime.

**9. [6]** Three points, 1 + 1 each, combining a way and a problem:
- **Naming and shaming online**: posting images or names of suspected offenders, which can lead to harassment of people not proven guilty.
- **Vigilante groups tracking suspects**: groups confront suspected offenders, which may expose wrongdoing but bypasses due process and risks violence.
- **Public pressure on authorities**: viral posts can push police to act on ignored crimes, but may also create moral panics.
- **Wrong targets**: mistaken identity can ruin innocent people's lives.
- **Undermining fair trials**: publicity can prejudice later court cases.

**10. [8]** Levels: 7–8 good knowledge, three developed points with evidence, terms used well; 4–6 some knowledge, most points supported, some undeveloped; 1–3 limited, descriptive.
Indicative content:
- **Strengths**: builds trust and cooperation, so residents report crime and share information; officers understand local problems and can solve them early; reduces conflict associated with heavy-handed targeting; fits informal social control by working with families, schools and religious groups.
- **Limitations**: slower to respond to serious organised or violent crime; depends on resources and stable staffing; trust may already be low in communities with experience of discrimination; harder to measure results.
- **Comparison**: military style policing may control disorder quickly but can alienate communities.
- Judgement on how far community policing is effective, and for which crimes.

**11. [8]** Levels as Question 10. Indicative content:
- **Capital punishment** is retained in some countries and some US states but abolished in many others, including all EU member states.
- **Corporal punishment**, such as judicial caning in Singapore, is used in some societies and rejected in most as a human rights violation.
- **Prison regimes** differ: Norway is often cited for rehabilitation-centred prisons, while other systems stress incapacitation and retribution.
- **Informal punishments** such as ostracism or shunning are important in some close-knit communities.
- **Historical change**: Durkheim's shift from retributive to restitutive justice; Foucault's shift from public, bodily punishment to disciplinary power; Britain's transportation of convicts to Australia (1788–1868) as exile.
- Evaluation: differences reflect values, religion, politics and levels of inequality.

**12. [14]** Levels: 12–14 excellent knowledge, three developed and evidenced points on each side, balanced, with a conclusion; 8–11 good knowledge, developed points on both sides but less balance; 4–7 some knowledge, simple two-sided or one-sided answer; 1–3 limited and descriptive. A one-sided answer cannot score more than 6.
*For the view:*
- Rehabilitation addresses causes such as addiction, lack of skills or poor education, so it can reduce reoffending.
- Retributive punishment, especially prison, often leads to reoffending and can act as a school for crime.
- Community-based rehabilitation keeps family and job ties, which Hirschi's social bond theory suggests protect against crime.
- It is often cheaper than long prison sentences.
- Labelling theory: harsh punishment can confirm a criminal identity, while rehabilitation offers a non-criminal one.
*Against the view:*
- Retribution satisfies victims and the public sense of justice; rehabilitation may be seen as soft.
- Some offenders are unwilling to change, so programmes fail.
- Dangerous offenders require incapacitation to protect citizens, whatever the rehabilitation offered.
- Rehabilitation needs funding and skilled staff, which are often lacking.
- Retributive punishment may also deter others (general deterrence).
*Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that rehabilitation is more effective at reducing reoffending for many offenders, while retribution and incapacitation remain necessary for the most serious crimes.
