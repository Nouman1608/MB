## 6.4.1 Components and calculations

| Section | Meaning | Typical items |
|---|---|---|
| Trade in goods | Buying and selling of physical products | Exports and imports of grain, machines, cars |
| Trade in services | Buying and selling of intangible products | Tourism, banking, insurance, transport, consultancy |
| Primary income | Income from assets and work abroad | Profits, dividends and interest; wages of cross-border workers |
| Secondary income | Transfers with no good or service in return | Remittances, aid, gifts, payments to international bodies |

Definition: the current account of the balance of payments records a country's trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income with the rest of the world over a period.

### Formulas

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Balance on a section | inflows - outflows |
| Balance on goods and services | goods balance + services balance |
| Current account balance | goods + services + primary income + secondary income |
| Deficit or surplus as % of GDP | balance / GDP x 100 |

A negative balance is a deficit. A positive balance is a surplus. Show the minus sign.

### Method in steps

1. Write the inflows and outflows for each section in a table.
2. Subtract outflows from inflows for each section.
3. Add the four balances, keeping signs.
4. State the answer as a deficit or surplus, with units.
5. If asked for a percentage of GDP, divide by GDP and multiply by 100.

### Worked reminder

Country C (illustrative, millions of units): goods inflows 300 and outflows 340; services 110 and 60; primary income 25 and 45; secondary income 30 and 10.

Goods: 300 - 340 = -40. Services: 110 - 60 = +50. Primary income: 25 - 45 = -20. Secondary income: 30 - 10 = +20. Current account = -40 + 50 - 20 + 20 = **+10**, a surplus. The balance on goods and services is +10 as well. GDP is 800, so the surplus is 10 / 800 x 100 = **1.25%** of GDP.

Reminder: Country C has a goods deficit but an overall surplus. Always report the section figures and the total.

## 6.4.2 Causes of deficit and surplus

| Cause of deficit | Cause of surplus |
|---|---|
| High spending on imports (fast income growth, taste for foreign goods) | Strong, competitive exports |
| Uncompetitive exports (high costs, low productivity, low quality) | Low costs, high productivity, good quality |
| Home inflation above that of trading partners | Low inflation relative to partners |
| Overvalued or rising currency | Currency that keeps export prices low |
| Recession or slow growth among trading partners | Fast growth among trading partners |
| Fall in price of a main export | Rise in price of a main export |
| Heavy interest and profit payments abroad | Large income from investments abroad |
| Aid or remittances sent out | Aid or remittances received |
| Collapse in tourism inflows | Strong tourism inflows |

Link the cause to the section it affects. For example, high debt interest appears under primary income, and a tourism fall appears under services.

## 6.4.3 Consequences

Net exports = exports - imports, and they are part of aggregate demand.

| Variable | Deficit | Surplus |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | Lower net exports reduce demand and growth | Higher net exports raise demand and growth |
| Employment | Job losses in exporting and import-competing firms | More jobs in export industries |
| Inflation | Import prices rise if the currency falls; weak demand eases price pressure | Demand-pull pressure if the economy is near full capacity; a rising currency lowers import prices |
| Exchange rate | Pressure on the currency to fall | Pressure on the currency to rise |

Further points:
- A deficit must be financed, by borrowing, selling assets or using foreign currency savings. Borrowing leads to more interest payments later.
- A small deficit used for investment may be sensible. A large one used for consumer goods is harder to defend.
- A surplus can reflect low living standards if people consume too little, and it can cause trade disputes with partners.

Chain example: income falls abroad, so demand for exports falls, so export earnings fall, so the goods balance worsens, so less foreign currency is earned, so demand for the home currency falls, so the exchange rate falls.

### Exchange rate and PED reminder

A depreciation lowers the foreign price of exports and raises the home price of imports. Whether the balance improves depends on PED.

| Case | Likely effect on the balance |
|---|---|
| Export and import demand both elastic | Improves |
| Export demand elastic, import demand inelastic | Likely improves (export gain larger) |
| Both inelastic | May worsen (little change in quantities, higher import bill) |

Worked reminder: a foreign good costs 40 foreign units. The home currency falls from 0.50 to 0.40 foreign units. Home price rises from 40 / 0.50 = 80 to 40 / 0.40 = 100, a 25% rise. With import PED 0.4, quantity falls by 0.4 x 25% = 10%.

## 6.4.4 Policies and effectiveness

| Policy | How it works | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Supply-side (education, infrastructure, research) | Raises productivity and cuts costs, so exports are more competitive | Slow and costly; results uncertain |
| Contractionary fiscal or monetary policy | Lowers incomes, so lowers import spending | Lower growth, more unemployment |
| Tariffs and quotas | Make imports dearer or limit them | Retaliation; dearer inputs; weak if demand is inelastic |
| Subsidies to exporters or import substitutes | Lower prices of domestic goods | Cost to the government; may be challenged by partners |
| Embargo | Bans trade with a country | Extreme, harms consumers and firms |
| Lower exchange rate (devaluation or allowed fall) | Cheaper exports, dearer imports | Depends on PED; raises import prices and inflation |
| Export promotion and trade agreements | Opens new markets | Takes time; depends on partners |

Worked reminder (tariff): imports of 500, tariff 10%. Import PED 0.8, so quantity falls by 8%, to 500 x 0.92 = 460, saving 40. If PED were 2.0, quantity falls by 20% to 400, saving 100.

### Linking policy to cause

| If the deficit comes from | A fitting policy | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Weak competitiveness | Supply-side measures | Tackles costs and quality directly |
| Booming spending on imports | Contractionary policy | Cuts the demand that draws imports in |
| Overvalued currency | Lower exchange rate | Corrects relative prices |
| Dependence on one export | Diversification and export promotion | Spreads the risk of a price fall |

Worked reminder (contractionary policy): a tax rise cuts household income by 200. If households spend 0.25 of each unit of income on imports, imports fall by 200 x 0.25 = 50. The cost is lower demand for domestic goods as well, so output and jobs may fall.

Evaluation checklist: match the policy to the cause; consider PED, time lag, retaliation, side effects (inflation, unemployment, growth) and who gains or loses.

## Must-know distinctions

- Primary income is earned from assets or labour. Secondary income is a transfer.
- A trade deficit in goods is not the same as a current account deficit.
- A deficit means outflows exceed inflows. A surplus means inflows exceed outflows.
- Expenditure-reducing policy lowers demand. Expenditure-switching policy moves demand from imports to home goods.

## Quick self-test

1. State the four sections of the current account.
2. In which section is each item recorded: (a) a foreign tourist's hotel bill, (b) wages earned by a resident working for a foreign firm abroad, (c) profits paid to a foreign owner, (d) aid given?
3. Country D has goods inflows 410 and outflows 365. Calculate the goods balance.
4. Country E has balances of goods +90, services +35, primary income -45 and secondary income -20. Calculate the current account balance.
5. Country A has a current account deficit of 36 and GDP of 900. Express the deficit as a percentage of GDP.
6. Give two causes of a current account deficit.
7. Explain one effect of a deficit on the exchange rate.
8. Imports are 300 and a tariff of 20% is imposed. Import PED is 0.5. Calculate the new quantity of imports (as a value at unchanged prices).
9. Explain why a fall in the exchange rate may not improve the current account.
10. Give one drawback of raising interest rates to cut a deficit.

### Answers

1. Trade in goods, trade in services, primary income, secondary income.
2. (a) Services; (b) primary income; (c) primary income; (d) secondary income.
3. 410 - 365 = **+45**.
4. 90 + 35 - 45 - 20 = **+60**, a surplus.
5. 36 / 900 x 100 = **4.0%**.
6. Any two of: uncompetitive exports, high import demand, recession abroad, fall in price of main export, high interest payments abroad.
7. Fewer foreign currency inflows mean lower demand for the home currency, so the exchange rate tends to fall.
8. Price rise 20%, quantity falls 0.5 x 20% = 10%: 300 x 0.90 = **270**.
9. If demand for exports and imports is inelastic, the volumes barely change and the import bill rises.
10. It reduces growth, investment and jobs, and raises the cost of borrowing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out the minus sign or the words deficit or surplus.
- Giving the goods balance when the question asks for the current account balance.
- Placing remittances or aid in primary income instead of secondary income.
- Describing a policy without saying how it changes the balance.
- Listing causes without linking them to the section they affect.
- Claiming a depreciation always works, without mentioning PED.
- Giving one-sided answers when the question says assess or evaluate.
- Forgetting units, or percentages of GDP when asked.
