This guide covers content item 2.1.4 Ethics, the environment and business, the last item in Topic 2.1 Growing the business of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It is assessed in Theme 2, Building a business (1BS0/02). The shared page on [globalisation, ethics and the environment](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-globalisation-ethics-and-the-environment/) gives a first overview; this guide focuses on how to argue a trade-off, which is what most 2.1.4 questions reward. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification says

Under the heading "The impact of ethical and environmental considerations on businesses", 2.1.4 has three bullet points:

- how ethical considerations influence business activity: possible trade-offs between ethics and profit
- how environmental considerations influence business activity: possible trade-offs between the environment, sustainability and profit
- the potential impact of pressure group activity on the marketing mix.

The key word in the first two bullets is **trade-off**. A trade-off means gaining one thing by giving up some of another. The specification says "possible" trade-offs: acting ethically or sustainably sometimes costs profit, but sometimes it does not, and occasionally it raises profit. Your job in an answer is to show which applies to the business in front of you.

## Part 1: what makes a decision ethical?

Ethics are moral principles about right and wrong. An ethical business decision is one that is fair and honest to the people it affects, often going further than the law requires. The law sets the minimum; ethics is about what a business chooses to do above it. Common areas in GCSE questions:

| Area | Ethical choice | Less ethical choice |
|---|---|---|
| Employees | Paying above the minimum the business must pay; safe, secure conditions | Paying the least possible; insecure hours |
| Suppliers | Fair prices and prompt payment; checking conditions in overseas factories | Squeezing prices; ignoring how goods are made |
| Customers | Honest advertising and labelling | Exaggerated claims; hidden charges |
| Animals | Avoiding cruel testing or farming methods | Choosing the cheapest source regardless |

## Part 2: the ethics–profit trade-off

Ethical choices usually raise costs in the short run. They can also bring benefits that eventually cover some or all of those costs.

**Costs:** higher wages, dearer materials from checked suppliers, staff time spent on checks, and sometimes higher prices that lose price-sensitive customers.

**Benefits:** customers who care about ethics may choose the business or pay more; staff are more motivated and less likely to leave; recruitment is easier; and the business avoids the damage that a scandal brings.

### Worked example 1: Brookfield Bakery raises pay

Brookfield Bakery employs six staff, each working 1,600 hours a year. The owner decides to pay £1.10 an hour more than she has to. Profit last year was £48,000.

1. Extra wage cost = £1.10 × 1,600 × 6 = **£10,560** a year
2. Profit if nothing else changes = £48,000 − £10,560 = £37,440, a fall of 22%

Last year four staff left, and replacing each one cost about £1,800 in advertising, interviews and training time. The owner expects only one to leave next year.

3. Recruitment saving = (4 − 1) × £1,800 = £5,400
4. Net cost = £10,560 − £5,400 = £5,160, so profit becomes **£42,840**, a fall of 10.75%

The trade-off is real but smaller than it first looks. A strong answer would add benefits that are hard to measure: experienced staff bake more consistently and serve customers better, which could raise sales. It would also note the risk: the fall in staff turnover is only an expectation.

## Part 3: the environment, sustainability and profit

Environmental considerations are the effects a business has on the natural world: pollution, waste, energy use, packaging and the resources it uses up. **Sustainability** means meeting today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs, for example by using renewable materials or reducing waste.

The trade-off works in the same way as ethics:

| Possible cost to profit | Possible gain to profit |
|---|---|
| Recycled or biodegradable materials can be dearer | Lower energy and waste bills over time |
| New equipment, such as electric vehicles or solar panels, needs investment | Attracts customers who prefer greener businesses |
| Some products may have to be redesigned or withdrawn | Avoids future costs if rules on pollution tighten |
| Time spent measuring and reporting impact | Better reputation with customers, staff and investors |

### Worked example 2: Thornbury Florist goes greener

Thornbury Florist makes 9,000 bouquets a year and earns £31,000 profit.

- An electric delivery van costs £2,400 a year more to lease than its diesel van, but fuel of £3,100 a year is replaced by charging costs of £1,600, saving £1,500. Net extra cost = £2,400 − £1,500 = **£900**.
- Paper wrapping in place of plastic costs 8p more per bouquet: £0.08 × 9,000 = **£720**.

Total extra cost = £1,620, which is about 5.2% of profit. If the greener image wins just a few wedding orders, the changes could pay for themselves. Thornbury also reduces plastic waste and exhaust emissions, which matter for sustainability whether or not profit rises.

Notice the timescale: the environmental gain begins straight away, while any sales benefit builds slowly. Examiners credit answers that separate the short run from the long run.

## Part 4: pressure groups and the marketing mix

A pressure group is an organised group of people who campaign to change the behaviour of businesses or governments. Methods include petitions, social media campaigns, boycotts, protests and gaining media coverage. The specification links pressure groups specifically to the **marketing mix**, so answers should name the element affected.

Suppose a campaign group criticises Harbour Bites, an invented takeaway chain, because its single-use packaging litters a local beach.

| Element | Possible impact |
|---|---|
| Product | Switch to compostable packaging or offer a discount for customers' own containers |
| Price | Dearer packaging raises unit costs, so prices may rise or profit per meal falls |
| Promotion | Advertising must repair the brand image and show the changes made; bad publicity may cut sales in the meantime |
| Place | A council or event organiser may refuse Harbour Bites a beachfront pitch until it changes |

The size of the impact depends on how much publicity the group gains, whether customers care, and how easily they can switch to a rival. A small group with little media reach may have almost no effect; a viral campaign can change sales within days.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming ethical always means lower profit. The specification says "possible" trade-offs.
- Confusing ethics with the law. Obeying the law is required; ethics is a choice beyond it.
- Using "environmental" and "ethical" as if they meant the same thing. Ethics concerns fairness to people and animals; environmental considerations concern the natural world.
- Writing about pressure groups without naming an element of the marketing mix.
- Giving only the cost of an ethical choice, with no benefit, or only the benefit, with no cost.

## Exam technique

- **Define:** for example, sustainability or pressure group, in one precise sentence.
- **Calculate:** extra costs per year or per unit, and the percentage change in profit. Show every step.
- **Outline:** two linked points tied to the case: "Paying suppliers more raises Brookfield's costs, so its profit falls unless it raises prices."
- **Explain:** a statement plus two expansions; no context is given, so keep it general but linked.
- **Analyse:** follow the chain from the ethical or environmental decision to costs, customers and profit for this business.
- **Justify or Evaluate:** weigh the short-run cost against the long-run benefit. Finish with what the decision depends on, such as how much the business's customers care about the issue or how much the change costs compared with profit.
