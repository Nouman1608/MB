## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between product orientation and market orientation. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the three named methods of market segmentation. **[3]**

**3.** State one advantage and one disadvantage of primary research compared with secondary
research. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** A long-established car manufacturer is planning to launch a genuinely new type of vehicle with
no direct competitors yet on the market.

**(a)** Recommend a pricing method for the launch, and justify your recommendation. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how the Boston Matrix might help the manufacturer decide how to allocate marketing
resources across its wider vehicle range once the new model has been launched. **[4]**

**5.** A small business wants to find out what proportion of local residents would be interested in a
new product before investing in it.

**(a)** Identify a primary research method the business could use, and justify your choice. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why sampling, rather than surveying the entire local population, might be necessary,
and state one limitation of sampling. **[3]**

**6.** A company selling a well-established, mass-market snack food is considering demographic
segmentation of its customer base.

**(a)** Explain what is meant by demographic segmentation, with an example relevant to this product. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest one advantage this approach offers over treating the whole market as a single, mass
market. **[2]**

**7.** A business selling a niche, specialist product decides to target only serious enthusiasts of
that product rather than the wider general public. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of
this niche marketing approach compared with mass marketing. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Product orientation means a business starts from what it is able to make and then tries to sell
it [1]; market (customer) orientation means a business starts from identifying what customers want
and then produces to meet that need [1].

**2.** Geographic, demographic, psychographic [3].

**3.** Advantage of primary research: data is collected specifically for the business's own purpose,
so it is directly relevant and current [1]. Disadvantage: it is typically more time-consuming and
expensive to collect than secondary research, which already exists [1].

**4. (a)** Price skimming is the most suitable recommendation [1], because the vehicle is genuinely
novel with no direct competitors yet, allowing the manufacturer to set a high initial price to
maximise revenue from customers willing to pay for the new technology before competitors enter the
market [2]. As competition eventually emerges, the price can then be lowered to broaden the customer
base [1].
**(b)** The Boston Matrix classifies products by market share and market growth (as "stars," "cash
cows," "question marks," or "dogs") [1–2], allowing the manufacturer to identify which existing models
in its range are generating cash to reinvest (cash cows) and which are underperforming and might need
to be discontinued or redesigned (dogs), helping to decide where the new model's expected marketing
investment should be balanced against the rest of the portfolio [2].

**5. (a)** A questionnaire or survey of local residents would be an appropriate primary research
method [1], because it directly asks the specific target population about their interest in the new
product, generating data tailored exactly to the business's question rather than relying on
pre-existing secondary sources [2].
**(b)** Surveying the entire local population would be too costly and time-consuming for a small
business to carry out [1–2]; sampling instead selects a smaller, representative group to give a
reasonably accurate picture at a manageable cost [1]. Limitation: a sample may not perfectly represent
the whole population, introducing a risk of sampling error or bias if the sample is not genuinely
representative [1].

**6. (a)** Demographic segmentation divides a market based on measurable population characteristics
such as age, income, gender, or family size [1–2]; for a snack food, this might mean segmenting
between, for example, children, young adults, and older consumers, who may have different preferences
for flavour, packaging size, or price point [1].
**(b)** Segmenting demographically allows the company to tailor its marketing mix (for example, product
variations, pricing, or promotional messaging) to each specific group's preferences, which is likely
to be more effective than a single mass-marketing approach that does not account for these differences
between groups [2].

**7.** Advantage: niche marketing allows the business to tailor its product and marketing mix
precisely to the specific needs of a well-understood, dedicated customer group, potentially building
strong customer loyalty and allowing for premium pricing since enthusiasts are likely to value the
specialism highly [2]. Disadvantage: the potential customer base is much smaller than a mass market,
which limits the maximum scale of sales and revenue the business can achieve compared with targeting
the whole market [2].

## A note on exam technique for this topic

Question 4 illustrates the connection the study guide highlights between 3.1's concepts, 3.2's
research methods, and 3.3's marketing-mix tools: a strong pricing recommendation is justified by
referring back to the specific market condition described (a genuinely novel product with no
competitors yet), not stated as a generic definition of price skimming. The same applies to Boston
Matrix and product life cycle questions — connecting the two frameworks, rather than treating them as
separate, unrelated tools, is exactly the kind of applied reasoning this topic rewards at AS and A
Level.
