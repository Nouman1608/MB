## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Recombinant DNA | DNA combining fragments from two different sources |
| Transgenic organism | An organism containing DNA transferred from another organism or species |
| Reverse transcriptase | Enzyme that makes cDNA using mRNA as a template |
| Restriction endonuclease | Enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific recognition sequence |
| Sticky ends | Short single-stranded overhangs left by a staggered cut |
| DNA ligase | Enzyme that joins sugar-phosphate backbones by forming phosphodiester bonds |
| Vector | Something that carries DNA into a host cell, such as a plasmid or a virus |
| Promoter | Sequence where RNA polymerase binds to start transcription |
| Terminator | Sequence that stops transcription |
| Marker gene | Gene used to identify cells that have taken up the vector |
| DNA probe | Short, labelled, single-stranded DNA complementary to a target allele |
| Hybridisation | Binding of a probe to a complementary single-stranded DNA sequence |
| VNTR | Variable number tandem repeat: a short sequence repeated a varying number of times |

## Why it works

The genetic code is universal, and so are transcription and translation. A transferred gene is read the same way in the recipient.

## Three ways to get the fragment

| Method | Key point | Introns |
|---|---|---|
| mRNA → cDNA with reverse transcriptase | Use cells that make a lot of the protein, so the mRNA is plentiful | None |
| Cut from DNA with restriction enzymes | Gives sticky ends | Present in eukaryotic genes |
| Gene machine | Built from a known sequence | None |

Bacteria cannot remove introns, so for a eukaryotic protein made in bacteria, use cDNA or a gene machine.

## Amplifying: in vitro and in vivo

**PCR (in vitro).** Template DNA, two primers, free nucleotides, heat-stable DNA polymerase. Denature (about 95 °C), anneal primers (about 50 to 65 °C), extend (about 72 °C). Copies double each cycle: copies = starting number × 2ⁿ.

Worked reminder: 20 molecules after 10 cycles = 20 × 2¹⁰ = 20 × 1,024 = **20,480** molecules.

**Transformed host cells (in vivo).**

1. Add a promoter and terminator to the fragment.
2. Cut the plasmid with the same restriction endonuclease, so sticky ends are complementary.
3. Mix; ligase joins the fragment into the plasmid.
4. Transform host cells with the recombinant plasmid.
5. Use marker genes to identify transformed cells.
6. Culture them: the plasmid is copied each division, and the gene can be expressed to make the protein.

| Choose PCR when | Choose host cells when |
|---|---|
| Speed matters | The protein is wanted, not just DNA |
| The sample is tiny (forensics) | Large amounts are needed over time |
| No living cells are wanted | Long fragments need copying |

## Marker genes in one paragraph

Only some cells take up a plasmid. A marker gene (for example, one giving antibiotic resistance or fluorescence) shows which did. If the wanted gene is inserted inside a marker gene, that marker stops working, so cells with a recombinant plasmid can be told apart from cells with an empty plasmid. You will not be asked to recall specific marker genes.

## Gene therapy

- Uses recombinant DNA tools: a functional allele with a promoter, carried by a vector (a modified virus or a liposome) into the patient's cells.
- Suited to conditions caused by a recessive allele: the added dominant allele lets the cell make the working protein.
- **Somatic**: body cells; not inherited; may need repeating.
- **Germ line**: eggs, sperm or embryos; inherited; much more ethically controversial.

## Evaluation checklist for recombinant DNA

- **Agriculture:** higher yield, pest resistance or added nutrients against gene flow to wild plants, harm to other species and loss of diversity.
- **Industry:** cheap production of enzymes and other proteins against patents and cost.
- **Medicine:** human proteins such as insulin, and gene therapy, against cost, unequal access and unknown long-term effects.
- **Humanitarian vs opposition:** feeding people and treating disease against environmental risk and corporate ownership of seed.
- Finish with a judgement and what it depends on.

## Probes, screening and their uses

1. Extract DNA and separate it into single strands.
2. Add the labelled probe (radioactive or fluorescent).
3. Wash away unbound probe.
4. A signal means the target allele is present.

Uses: screening for heritable conditions, drug responses and health risks → **genetic counselling** (explaining risk and options) and **personalised medicine** (choosing drugs and doses that suit a person's alleles).

Evaluate screening: early treatment, informed choices and better drug choice against anxiety, results that show a risk not a certainty, cost, privacy and possible discrimination.

## Genetic fingerprinting

1. Extract DNA.
2. Amplify VNTR regions by PCR.
3. Separate fragments by gel electrophoresis: DNA is negatively charged and moves towards the positive electrode; shorter fragments move further.
4. Show bands with a stain, fluorescent label or probe.

The probability of two individuals having the same VNTRs is very low. Uses: forensic science, medical diagnosis, animal and plant breeding (parentage, avoiding inbreeding, measuring genetic variability).

Reading a gel: every band in a child must come from the mother or the father. More different band patterns in a population means more genetic variability.

## Exam traps

- cDNA is made from mRNA, not from DNA.
- Restriction enzymes cut; ligase joins.
- The plasmid and gene are cut with the same enzyme.
- A probe is complementary to the allele, not identical to it.
- PCR copies DNA but makes no protein.
- In electrophoresis, small fragments travel furthest.
- Somatic gene therapy is not passed to offspring.
- Evaluation answers need both sides and a conclusion.

## Quick self-test

1. Why can a human gene be expressed in a bacterium?
2. Name the enzyme that makes cDNA.
3. Why is cDNA preferred to genomic DNA for making a human protein in bacteria?
4. Why are the plasmid and gene cut with the same restriction endonuclease?
5. What does DNA ligase do?
6. Why are a promoter and terminator added to the fragment?
7. Why is a heat-stable DNA polymerase used in PCR?
8. How many copies are made from 5 molecules after 8 cycles of PCR?
9. Why is a marker gene needed?
10. Give **one** difference between somatic and germ line gene therapy.
11. Why does a probe bind only to one allele?
12. In a gel, which fragments travel furthest, and why does DNA move towards the positive electrode?

### Answers

1. The genetic code is universal, as are transcription and translation.
2. Reverse transcriptase.
3. cDNA has no introns, and bacteria cannot remove introns.
4. To produce complementary sticky ends that can pair.
5. Forms phosphodiester bonds, joining the fragment into the vector.
6. So RNA polymerase can bind and start transcription, and transcription stops at the end of the gene.
7. It is not denatured at the high temperature used to separate the strands.
8. 5 × 2⁸ = 5 × 256 = 1,280.
9. To identify the few cells that have taken up the vector or recombinant plasmid.
10. Somatic therapy changes body cells and is not inherited; germ line therapy changes gametes or embryos and is inherited.
11. Its base sequence is complementary only to that allele, so hydrogen bonds form only there.
12. The shortest fragments; DNA is negatively charged because of its phosphate groups.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know named marker genes for AQA A-level Biology?**
No. The specification says you will not be required to recall specific marker genes in a written paper, but you should understand how marker genes identify transformed cells.
