This guide covers topic 2.4 **Inflation** in Economic policy objectives, Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). The specification asks you to explain inflation, deflation, disinflation and hyperinflation; the policy objective of low and stable inflation; real and nominal values; and measurement using the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) and the Retail Prices Index (RPI). You must also calculate the rate of inflation using index numbers and evaluate the causes and consequences of inflation and deflation. All figures in the worked examples are invented.

Condensed recall is in the [inflation revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-inflation-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/), and you can test yourself with the [inflation practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-inflation-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/). For the wider A Level treatment, see [inflation, deflation and index numbers](/resources/ocr-a-level-economics-inflation-deflation-and-index-numbers/).

## 1. Four terms that are easily confused

- **Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level. It is measured as a rate: the percentage change in a price index over a period, usually twelve months.
- **Disinflation** is a fall in the rate of inflation while it stays positive. Prices are still rising, only more slowly.
- **Deflation** is a sustained fall in the general price level, so the measured inflation rate is negative.
- **Hyperinflation** is extremely rapid and usually accelerating inflation, at which money stops working well as a store of value and even as a medium of exchange. One benchmark, from the economist Phillip Cagan, is prices rising by more than 50% a month. Germany in 1923 and Zimbabwe in 2008 are the examples most often cited.

The key test is the sign and direction of the rate. If inflation goes from 6% to 3%, that is disinflation. It becomes deflation only if the rate goes below zero.

## 2. Why low and stable inflation is a policy objective

The objective has two parts. **Low** inflation keeps the cost of rising prices down. **Stable** inflation makes the future predictable, which matters as much as the level itself. In the UK the government sets the Bank of England a target of 2% for CPI inflation. The target is symmetric: inflation well below 2% is treated as a miss, just as inflation well above it is.

Why not aim for zero? Three reasons are usually given. A small positive rate leaves a margin above deflation. It also lets real wages fall in struggling industries without cuts in money wages, which workers resist. Finally, the measured index may overstate true inflation slightly, because it is slow to capture quality improvements and new products.

## 3. How a price index is built

A price index measures the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services relative to a base period, in which the index is set to 100. Building one takes four steps.

1. **Choose the basket.** A household spending survey identifies what a typical household buys.
2. **Weight the items.** Each category gets a weight equal to its share of household spending, so a 10% rise in housing costs matters more than a 10% rise in the price of books.
3. **Collect prices** for the same items each month.
4. **Combine** the price changes into one weighted average.

**Worked example 1: a weighted index (invented data).** Base year index = 100 for every category.

| Category | Weight | Price index, year 2 | Weight x index |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 20 | 104 | 2,080 |
| Housing and energy | 30 | 110 | 3,300 |
| Transport | 15 | 97 | 1,455 |
| Recreation | 25 | 102 | 2,550 |
| Other | 10 | 103 | 1,030 |
| Total | 100 | | 10,415 |

Weighted index = 10,415 / 100 = **104.15**, so inflation is **4.15%**.

The table also shows where the inflation comes from. Housing and energy contributes 30 x 10 / 100 = **3.0 percentage points**, about 72% of the total, even though its weight is under a third. Transport prices fell, taking 0.45 points off. A household that spends more than the average share on energy faces a personal inflation rate above 4.15%.

## 4. Calculating inflation from index numbers

Once an index exists, the inflation rate between two dates is:

Inflation (%) = (index this period - index last period) / index last period x 100

**Worked example 2.** A CPI series (base year = 100) reads 118.4 in one year and 126.9 a year later. Inflation = (126.9 - 118.4) / 118.4 x 100 = **7.18%**.

Two errors are common: calling 8.5 *index points* a percentage, and measuring from the base year: 126.9 means prices are 26.9% higher than in the base year, not that inflation this year is 26.9%.

## 5. CPI and RPI compared

| Feature | CPI | RPI |
|---|---|---|
| Use in UK policy | The measure targeted by the Bank of England | Older measure, still used in some contracts and index-linked bonds |
| Housing costs | Excludes mortgage interest payments and council tax | Includes mortgage interest payments and council tax |
| Coverage | All private households, plus some institutional and foreign visitor spending | Excludes the very highest-income households and some pensioner households |
| Averaging formula | Mostly a geometric mean of price changes | Mostly an arithmetic mean of price changes |
| International comparison | Built on a harmonised European method, so comparable across countries | UK-specific |

Because RPI uses an arithmetic mean for individual item prices, it tends to read higher than CPI even with identical price data. This is the "formula effect". Because RPI contains mortgage interest, it rises when the Bank of England raises interest rates. A rise in Bank Rate intended to *reduce* inflation can therefore push RPI *up* in the short term. For that reason RPI is a poor guide to whether monetary policy is working.

## 6. Real and nominal values

A **nominal** value is measured in the prices of the time. A **real** value removes the effect of price changes, so it measures purchasing power.

Real value = nominal value / (price index / 100)

**Worked example 3: real wages.** A worker earns £27,000 when the price index is 112 and £28,350 a year later when the index is 119.84.

- Nominal pay rises by 5.0%.
- Inflation is (119.84 - 112) / 112 = 7.0%.
- Real pay in base-year prices: 27,000 / 1.12 = £24,107, then 28,350 / 1.1984 = £23,657.
- Real pay falls by about **1.9%**. As a check, 1.05 / 1.07 - 1 = -1.87%.

The same idea gives the **real interest rate**: roughly the nominal rate minus inflation. A savings account paying 4% when inflation is 6% gives a real return of about -2% (exactly 1.04 / 1.06 - 1 = -1.89%). Savers lose purchasing power even though their balance grows.

## 7. Causes of inflation

- **Demand-pull inflation.** Aggregate demand grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce. On an AD/AS diagram, AD shifts right along an upward-sloping AS curve, raising the price level and output. It is more likely when the economy is close to full capacity, unemployment is low and consumer and business confidence is high.
- **Cost-push inflation.** Firms' costs rise, for example from higher energy or import prices, a fall in the exchange rate or wages rising faster than productivity. SRAS shifts left, so prices rise while output falls.
- **Growth of the money supply.** If the quantity of money grows much faster than output, more money chases the same goods. Very rapid money creation, often to finance government spending, lies behind most hyperinflations.

Causes interact. A cost shock can start a **wage-price spiral**: workers bargain for higher wages to restore their real pay, firms pass the wage costs on, and inflation persists. Whether this happens depends on **expectations**. If people believe the target will be met, one-off price rises fade.

## 8. Consequences of inflation

When evaluating, separate **anticipated** inflation, which people can plan for, from **unanticipated** inflation, which causes most of the harm.

- **Redistribution.** Unanticipated inflation moves purchasing power from savers and lenders to borrowers, and hurts people on fixed incomes.
- **Uncertainty.** Volatile inflation makes long-term contracts harder to price and can discourage investment, slowing long-run growth.
- **Competitiveness.** If UK inflation exceeds that of trading partners at a fixed exchange rate, exports become dearer and imports cheaper, worsening the current account.
- **Menu and shoe-leather costs.** Firms must reprice; households and firms spend time managing cash. These are small at low rates but large under hyperinflation.
- **Fiscal drag.** If tax thresholds are frozen while incomes rise with prices, more income falls into higher bands, so the tax burden rises without any change in tax rates.

Not every effect is harmful. Moderate inflation reduces the real value of debts, including government debt, and may come with strong growth if it is demand-pull.

## 9. Deflation: is it always bad?

Deflation caused by **rising productivity** or cheaper technology (an outward shift of AS) can coexist with growth and rising real incomes. Deflation caused by **falling demand** is far more damaging:

- Households may delay purchases, expecting lower prices, which cuts AD further.
- The real burden of debt rises. A fixed debt of £10,000 is worth about £10,628 in today's money after two years of prices falling 3% a year, so borrowers cut spending.
- Real interest rates rise even if nominal rates are near zero, which limits monetary policy.
- Firms facing falling prices and sticky money wages cut output and jobs.

## Common mistakes

- Calling disinflation "falling prices".
- Reading an index level (126.9) as an inflation rate.
- Saying RPI is "more accurate" than CPI without saying what for.
- Forgetting that weights matter: a large price rise for a small item adds little to the index.
- Listing consequences of inflation without asking whether it was anticipated.
- Treating all deflation as harmful.

## Exam technique for OCR command words

- **Calculate.** Show the formula, the substitution and the answer with units: % for rates, index points for differences in levels.
- **Explain.** Build a chain: cause, mechanism, effect on the price level, and why it matters. "Energy prices rise, so firms' costs rise, SRAS shifts left, and the price level rises while output falls."
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram.** Draw AD/AS for demand-pull or cost-push, label the axes "price level" and "real output", and refer to the diagram in your writing.
- **Evaluate.** Weigh the size and type of the inflation, whether it was anticipated, who gains and who loses, and the starting position of the economy. Conclude with a judgement on how serious the problem is.
