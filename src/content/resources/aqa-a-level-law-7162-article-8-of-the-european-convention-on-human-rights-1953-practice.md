## Section A

**1.** Which of these is **not** one of the interests named in Article 8.1? A: home. B: correspondence. C: reputation. D: family life. **[1]**

**2.** In which case did the Grand Chamber hold that night flights at Heathrow did not breach Article 8? A: López Ostra v Spain. B: Hatton v United Kingdom. C: Halford v United Kingdom. D: Peck v United Kingdom. **[1]**

**3.** State what was decided in Wainwright v Home Office (2003) about privacy in English law. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** aspects of "private life" recognised by the European Court, giving a case for each. **[4]**

**5.** State the two stages of a claim for misuse of private information. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain what is meant by a "positive obligation" under Article 8, using one case. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how the European Court decides whether "family life" exists, referring to Marckx v Belgium (1979). **[3]**

**8.** Explain the decisions in Malone v United Kingdom (1984) and Halford v United Kingdom (1997) and what they show about the protection of correspondence. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how Article 8 can be relied on against a newspaper, even though a newspaper is not a public authority. **[4]**

**10.** *Ellie is filmed by a council CCTV camera sitting crying in a shopping centre after receiving bad news from her doctor. The council gives the footage, unedited, to a television programme about the uses of CCTV.* Suggest why Ellie's Article 8.1 rights have been interfered with. **[4]**

**11.** *Sam's local police force keeps the fingerprints and DNA profile of every person ever arrested, permanently, whatever the outcome. Sam was arrested five years ago and released without charge. The force also opens letters sent to him by his solicitor while he was briefly in custody.* Advise Sam on which interests in Article 8.1 are engaged and whether there has been an interference. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss whether the broad reading of "private life" by the European Court has made Article 8 too wide. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C: reputation. **[1]**

**2.** B: Hatton v United Kingdom. **[1]**

**3.** The House of Lords held that there is **no general common law tort of invasion of privacy** [1]; protection had to come from existing causes of action or from Parliament [1].

**4.** Any two, with a case for each: physical and psychological integrity (X and Y v Netherlands, 1985); sexual life (Dudgeon v UK, 1981); personal identity (Goodwin v UK, 2002); personal data (S and Marper v UK, 2008); image (Peck v UK, 2003, or Von Hannover v Germany, 2004); medical information (Campbell v MGN, 2004). One mark for each aspect [1] [1] and one for each correct case [1] [1].

**5.** Did the claimant have a **reasonable expectation of privacy** in the information [1]? If so, is that expectation **outweighed by the publisher's right to freedom of expression** under Article 10 [1]?

**6.** A positive obligation is a duty on the state to **take active steps to secure the right**, not merely to refrain from interfering [1]. In **X and Y v Netherlands (1985)** [1] the state breached Article 8 because its criminal law gave **no way to prosecute a sexual assault** on a girl with a mental disability, so it failed to protect her physical and psychological integrity [1].

**7.** Family life is a **question of fact** [1], based on **real and close personal ties** rather than formal legal status [1]. In **Marckx v Belgium** family life existed between an **unmarried mother and her child**, and rules that treated the child less favourably breached Article 8 with Article 14 [1].

**8.** In **Malone** the police tapped telephone calls [1] and the Court held this breached Article 8 because there was **no clear legal basis**, so it was not in accordance with the law [1]; Parliament passed the **Interception of Communications Act 1985** [1]. In **Halford** a senior officer's calls from her office telephone were intercepted; she had a **reasonable expectation of privacy** and the 1985 Act did not regulate internal networks, so there was a breach [1]. Together they show correspondence includes telephone calls and that surveillance must have a **clear and specific legal basis** [1].

**9.** Under **section 6 of the Human Rights Act 1998** a **court is a public authority** [1], so it must act compatibly with Article 8 when deciding a case between private parties [1]. English courts have done this by developing breach of confidence into the tort of **misuse of private information** (Campbell v MGN, 2004) [1], confirmed as a tort in **Google Inc v Vidal-Hall (2015)** [1].

**10.** Private life covers **a person's image and distressing personal moments** [1]. As in **Peck v UK (2003)**, being in a **public place** does not remove the protection of private life [1]. The council is a **public authority** under section 6 [1], and releasing unedited footage to the media goes well beyond normal monitoring and so is an **interference** with Ellie's private life [1].

**11.** **Private life** is engaged by the retention of fingerprints and DNA, which are **personal data** [1]. Under **S and Marper v UK (2008)** blanket, indefinite retention for people not convicted is an interference [1] which the Court found could not be justified [1]. **Correspondence** is engaged by opening letters from his solicitor [1]. In **Golder v UK (1975)** interference with a prisoner's correspondence with a solicitor breached Article 8 [1]. The police are a public authority, so both acts are interferences requiring justification under 8.2 [1].

**12.** One mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- The Court treats the Convention as a **living instrument**, so reading private life broadly keeps it relevant to new technology such as DNA databases (S and Marper) [1].
- Broad reading protects **vulnerable people**: X and Y and Peck show protection that a narrow reading would miss [1].
- Breadth is controlled by **8.2**: many cases accept engagement but find interference justified, as in Pretty [1].
- Breadth is also controlled by the **margin of appreciation**, wide in Hatton [1].
- Critics argue breadth gives judges, not Parliament, power over **social policy**, as in end-of-life cases such as Purdy [1].
- Breadth creates **uncertainty** for public bodies and the press, who cannot easily predict what is private [1].
- In England, broad Article 8 rights have driven a **judge-made privacy tort** (Campbell, Vidal-Hall), which some see as bypassing Parliament after Wainwright [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion** on whether breadth plus the 8.2 filter strikes an acceptable balance [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "privacy" without naming the specific interest in Article 8.1.
- Assuming events in public are outside private life.
- Treating the newspaper as a public authority instead of relying on the court's duty under section 6.
- Inventing a general English privacy tort.
- Concluding "breach" without moving to 8.2 once interference is shown.
