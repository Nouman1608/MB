## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain what sustainable tourism and sport mean and how they can be judged.
- Evaluate strategies for managing tourism and sport at different scales.
- Explain how future trends, including climate change, may affect destinations and events.
- Weigh the interests of different stakeholders.

## Measuring sustainability

A claim that a destination is sustainable needs evidence. Useful indicators, one or more for each pillar:

| Pillar | Example indicator | What a worsening value suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Share of tourism jobs that are year-round | Seasonal, insecure work |
| Economic | Share of spending retained locally | Rising leakage |
| Social | Visitors per resident in peak month | Crowding and resident hostility |
| Social | Share of housing used as short-term rentals | Displacement of residents |
| Environmental | Water use per visitor-night | Pressure on supply |
| Environmental | Condition of key habitats, such as reef cover or path erosion | Ecological capacity exceeded |

### Worked example: tourism intensity

An invented historic town has 25,000 residents. In August it receives 600,000 visitor-days (one visitor present for one day counts as one visitor-day).

1. August has 31 days, so the average number of visitors present each day = 600,000 / 31 = **19,355** (to the nearest whole number).
2. Visitors per resident on an average August day = 19,355 / 25,000 = **0.77**.
3. If day visits rise by a third to 800,000 visitor-days: 800,000 / 31 = 25,806 visitors per day, so 25,806 / 25,000 = **1.03**, more visitors than residents.

Crossing a ratio of 1 is not a scientific limit, but it signals that the town's streets, transport and services are now used more by visitors than by residents, which is when Doxey-style antagonism tends to rise. Indicators like this make an evaluation precise.

## Judging visitor management tools

Each tool has a distinct strength and a distinct weakness. Name both.

| Tool | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Entry fees and pricing | Raises money for conservation; reduces demand | Excludes lower-income visitors and can be seen as unfair to residents |
| Quotas and permits | Directly caps numbers at fragile sites | Needs enforcement; may push demand to unprotected sites |
| Timed entry | Spreads visitors through the day | Does not reduce total numbers |
| Zoning | Keeps heavy use away from the most sensitive areas | Concentrates impact in the permitted zones |
| Education and codes of conduct | Cheap; builds understanding | Voluntary, so depends on behaviour |
| Planning controls on building | Prevents overdevelopment | Opposed by businesses; long-term enforcement can weaken |

### Zoning a protected area

Many protected areas, including UNESCO biosphere reserves, use three zones:

- **Core zone:** strict protection; little or no tourism, often research only.
- **Buffer zone:** limited, low-impact uses such as guided walking or education.
- **Transition zone:** settlements, accommodation and most visitor services, where local people earn a living from tourism.

The logic is to accept heavy use somewhere in order to protect the core. When evaluating, ask whether the transition zone's growth is itself controlled.

## Tourism and climate change: both directions

### Tourism as a cause

Most of tourism's carbon footprint comes from transport, and above all from long-haul flights. Approaches include:

- encouraging rail and coach travel for shorter journeys;
- longer stays with fewer flights, so each trip yields more local income per tonne of emissions;
- carbon offsetting, which pays for emission reductions elsewhere but is criticised because offsets can be hard to verify and do not reduce the flight's own emissions.

### Tourism as a victim

Climate change alters the primary resources tourism depends on. Snow-reliant resorts at lower altitudes, coral reef destinations and low-lying beach resorts face the greatest risk. Some places may gain: cooler summer destinations may become more attractive during hotter summers elsewhere. **Last-chance tourism**, travel to see glaciers or reefs before they decline, can raise awareness but adds emissions and pressure to fragile places, a paradox worth discussing.

## Sport for the future

Sporting events face their own sustainability issues:

- **Venue strategy.** The International Olympic Committee's Olympic Agenda 2020 reforms, adopted in 2014, encouraged hosts to use existing and temporary venues and reduced the cost of bidding.
- **Heat.** Rising temperatures affect athletes and spectators. The 2022 FIFA World Cup in Qatar was played in November and December rather than the usual summer months, and the Tokyo 2020 Olympic marathons were moved north to Sapporo to avoid the heat.
- **Participation legacy.** A sustainable event leaves facilities and programmes that raise everyday sport participation, not just elite venues.
- **Travel.** Spectator travel is a large share of an event's emissions, so public transport access and compact venue plans matter.

## Stakeholder conflict: a quick method

For a management question, build a short matrix.

1. List stakeholders: residents, local businesses, international operators, visitors, government, conservation groups.
2. For each, state what they want: income, quiet, access, protection.
3. Identify the main conflict, such as operators wanting more cruise calls while residents want fewer crowds.
4. Judge which stakeholder the chosen strategy favours, and whether that is fair.

This prevents the common error of presenting a strategy as good for everyone.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the use of entry fees to manage tourism in a fragile destination.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Entry fees can reduce demand and fund conservation, as Bhutan's daily fee helps pay for public services **[strength with example]**. They are also easy to adjust as visitor numbers change **[flexibility]**. However, fees exclude lower-income visitors, raising questions of fairness, and on their own they do not stop crowding at the most popular sites **[limitations]**. Fees work best combined with quotas or zoning, and if the money raised is visibly spent on the destination's residents and environment **[conditional judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Declaring a destination sustainable without any indicator or evidence.
- Evaluating a tool's strengths but not its weaknesses, or the reverse.
- Treating climate change only as a threat to tourism, not something tourism contributes to.
- Discussing sport only as tourism and ignoring events, participation and venues.
- Presenting management as benefiting all stakeholders equally.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** a strategy with criteria (effectiveness, equity, cost, enforcement, long-term viability) and a judgement.
- **To what extent** questions on sustainability need a clear position on whether a place is moving towards or away from sustainability.
- **Suggest** management for a place in a resource by linking each measure to a problem shown.
- Use indicators and ratios to give evaluation precision.
