Section 3.2.3.4 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification is titled "Policies to correct positive and negative externalities". Its content point is "Externalities", and the additional guidance says you should understand "government policies designed to influence positive and negative externalities". This guide starts from the numbers that explain why a market gets the quantity wrong, then shows how each policy changes the decision a buyer or producer faces, and finishes with a method for choosing between policies in an evaluation question.

## The three costs and three benefits

- **Private cost:** the cost paid by the producer or consumer making the decision.
- **External cost:** a cost falling on a third party, not paid by the decision-maker.
- **Social cost** = private cost + external cost.

The same pattern applies to benefits: **social benefit** = private benefit + external benefit.

An externality exists whenever private and social values differ. The market responds only to private values, because they are what buyers and sellers actually pay and receive.

## Why the market gets the quantity wrong: two worked examples

### A negative externality (invented)

A chemicals firm sells each tonne of output for £36. Its own cost is £30 a tonne, but each tonne releases fumes that cause £12 of harm to local residents.

- Private view: £36 − £30 = £6 profit a tonne, so the firm keeps producing.
- Social view: social cost = 30 + 12 = **£42**, more than the £36 the output is worth to buyers. Each tonne makes society £6 worse off.

The firm produces more than is best for society: **over-production**.

### A positive externality (invented)

A flu jab costs £20. A person values the protection to themselves at £15, but being vaccinated also protects other people they would otherwise infect, worth £10 to them.

- Private view: £15 benefit < £20 price, so the person does not buy it.
- Social view: social benefit = 15 + 10 = **£25** > £20. Society would gain from the jab.

People buy less than is best for society: **under-consumption**.

These two examples give you the logic every policy follows: **make the decision-maker face the social value instead of the private one**.

## Policies for negative externalities

| Policy | How it changes the decision | Real UK example |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Adds the external cost to the price, so less is bought and produced. In the chemicals case, a £12 tax per tonne lifts the firm's cost to £42, above the £36 price, so it cuts output | The Plastic Packaging Tax, from April 2022, on packaging with less than 30% recycled plastic |
| Regulation and legislation | Sets legal limits, standards or bans, backed by fines | Emission limits for vehicles and factories |
| Charges | Make users pay each time they cause the harm | London's Ultra Low Emission Zone charge for older, more polluting vehicles |
| Minimum prices | Raise the cheapest price at which a harmful good can be sold | Minimum unit pricing for alcohol in Scotland, introduced in 2018 |
| Provision of information | Shows people the harm so they choose differently | Warnings on alcohol and tobacco, anti-litter campaigns |

## Policies for positive externalities

| Policy | How it changes the decision |
|---|---|
| Subsidy | Lowers the price to consumers or the cost to producers. In the flu jab case, a £10 subsidy cuts the price to £10, below the £15 private benefit, so the person now buys it |
| State provision | The government provides the good free at the point of use and pays from taxes, so price is no barrier at all, as with free NHS flu jabs for groups at higher risk |
| Regulation | Makes consumption compulsory or sets minimum standards, such as compulsory schooling or minimum insulation standards in new buildings |
| Provision of information | Encourages take-up by showing people the benefit, such as health campaigns |

## Choosing the right policy

Ask five questions about the case:

1. **How serious is the harm?** If it is severe, such as a dangerous chemical, a ban gives certainty. If some use is acceptable, a tax lets people choose while reducing the quantity.
2. **Can the external cost or benefit be measured?** A tax or subsidy should equal the external value; if it cannot be measured, the policy may be set too high or too low.
3. **How price elastic is demand?** For goods with price-inelastic demand, such as tobacco, a tax raises revenue but cuts the quantity only a little, so regulation or information may be needed too.
4. **Who pays?** Indirect taxes take a larger share of poorer households' income. Subsidies cost taxpayers and may mostly help people who would have bought anyway.
5. **Can it be enforced?** Regulations need inspectors; charges need monitoring systems; both cost money to run.

## Unintended consequences

Policies can create new problems:

- A tax on waste sent to landfill may encourage illegal dumping (fly-tipping), shifting the harm rather than removing it.
- High taxes on a good can encourage smuggling or illegal sales.
- A subsidy can make producers dependent on it, and it is hard to remove later.
- A regulation set at the wrong level may cost firms more than the harm it prevents.

Pointing out a likely side effect of the policy in the case, and how it could be limited, is a strong way to evaluate.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain how a subsidy could correct the under-consumption of home insulation. **[4]**

*Model answer:* Insulation has a positive externality because lower energy use reduces pollution that affects other people, not just the household [identifies the externality]. The household considers only its own lower bills, so it buys less insulation than is best for society [explains the under-consumption]. A subsidy lowers the price the household pays [states the mechanism], so more households find that the private benefit now exceeds the price, and consumption rises towards the socially best level [links to correction].

## Common mistakes

- Saying a polluting factory "has a cost to the firm". An externality falls on third parties.
- Suggesting a tax for a positive externality or a subsidy for a negative one.
- Claiming a tax "stops" pollution. It reduces the activity; only a ban stops it.
- Forgetting that the policy must change price, quantity or information, and saying how.
- Ignoring the drawbacks: measurement, enforcement, cost and fairness.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: name the externality, say why the market over- or under-provides, then show how the policy changes the price or the rules.
- **Evaluate** questions: compare two policies using the five questions above, and reach a judgement that fits the case, for example whether the harm is severe and whether demand is price elastic.
