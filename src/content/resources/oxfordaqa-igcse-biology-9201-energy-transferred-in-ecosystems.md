This guide goes deeper into section **3.3.1 Energy transferred in ecosystems** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE
Biology (9201) specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered and
any part of the specification may be assessed on either paper. Statement d, on the efficiency of food production, is
marked in the specification as Biology-only content.

The existing [Energy transfer, biomass pyramids and adaptations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-energy-transfer-biomass-pyramids-adaptations-and/)
covers 3.3.1 and 3.3.2 together. This page concentrates on energy transfer only and adds new evidence: an energy
budget for a grazing animal, biomass data from a woodland, building a scaled pyramid, and a comparison of two ways
of feeding people. Afterwards, use the
[Energy transferred in ecosystems revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-energy-transferred-in-ecosystems-revision-notes/)
and the [Energy transferred in ecosystems practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-energy-transferred-in-ecosystems-practice/).

## What 3.3.1 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Radiation from the Sun is the energy source for most communities; plants and algae transfer about 1 % of the incident light energy, which is stored in the substances in their cells |
| b | Only about 10 % of the biomass from each trophic level is transferred to the next, because of waste materials and energy transferred in respiration |
| c | Biomass at each stage can be drawn to scale as a pyramid of biomass; you must interpret and construct them |
| d | Efficiency of food production can be improved by reducing the number of stages in a food chain (Biology only) |

The specification states that constructing food webs and chains and pyramids of numbers is not required, and that
an understanding of pyramids of numbers is not required. Learn **pyramids of biomass** only.

## Energy from the Sun

Radiation from the Sun is the source of energy for most communities of living organisms. Plants and algae transfer
only about **1 %** of the incident energy from light for photosynthesis. Most of the light is reflected, passes
through the leaf or is not absorbed by the leaf. The energy that is transferred is **stored in the substances that
make up the cells of the plants** (for example, carbohydrates).

### Worked example 1: energy stored by producers

Invented data. A meadow receives 4.5 × 10⁶ kJ of light energy per m² each year.

```
energy stored by producers = 1 % of 4.5 × 10⁶ = 0.01 × 4 500 000 = 45 000 kJ per m² per year
to primary consumers  (10 %) = 4 500 kJ
to secondary consumers (10 %) = 450 kJ
to tertiary consumers (10 %) = 45 kJ
```

The figures fall by a factor of 10 at each step, so there is very little energy left by the fourth level. This is
the main reason why food chains seldom have more than four or five stages.

## Why only about 10 % passes on

Only approximately **10 % of the biomass** from each trophic level is transferred to the level above. The
specification gives two reasons.

1. **Some materials and energy are always lost in the organisms' waste materials**, such as faeces and urine.
2. **Respiration supplies all the energy needs for living processes, including movement.** Much of this energy is
   eventually transferred to the surroundings.

Other points that are consistent with this: not all of an organism is eaten (bones, roots or hair are left), and
the energy transferred to the surroundings by respiration cannot be reused by the next consumer.

### Worked example 2: an energy budget for a grazing animal

Invented data. A sheep eats grass containing 1 000 kJ of energy.

| Fate of the energy | kJ |
|---|---|
| Lost in faeces and urine (waste materials) | 350 |
| Transferred by respiration (mostly to the surroundings) | 520 |
| Stored in the sheep's new body material | 130 |

```
check: 350 + 520 + 130 = 1 000 kJ
percentage stored = 130 ÷ 1 000 × 100 = 13 %
percentage lost in waste = 350 ÷ 1 000 × 100 = 35 %
percentage in respiration = 520 ÷ 1 000 × 100 = 52 %
```

The 13 % that is stored is the part that is available to the next consumer, so it is close to the "about 10 %"
figure. Both of the specification's reasons appear here: waste materials (35 %) and respiration (52 %).

### Worked example 3: testing the 10 % rule with biomass data

Invented dry mass data per hectare from a woodland.

| Stage | Dry mass of biomass (kg per hectare) |
|---|---|
| Oak leaves | 9 600 |
| Caterpillars | 1 150 |
| Blue tits | 105 |

```
transfer, leaves → caterpillars = 1 150 ÷ 9 600 × 100 = 12.0 %
transfer, caterpillars → blue tits = 105 ÷ 1 150 × 100 = 9.1 %
```

Both values are close to 10 %. Real ecosystems vary, so "approximately 10 %" is the specification's wording, and
you should not expect exactly 10 % from every data set. Using dry mass avoids the variable amount of water in
organisms.

## Pyramids of biomass

A **pyramid of biomass** shows the biomass at each stage of a food chain, drawn to scale. Each stage is a horizontal
bar. The bar for producers is at the bottom, and each consumer level sits above.

### Constructing a pyramid

1. Choose a scale that lets the largest bar fit on the page. For data of 4 000 kg, 480 kg and 52 kg, a scale of
   1 cm per 500 kg is suitable.
2. Work out the bar lengths: length = biomass ÷ scale.
3. Draw the bars as rectangles of the same height, centred on a vertical line, with producers at the base.
4. Label each bar with the organism or level and write the scale.

### Worked example 4: working out bar lengths

Invented data: producers 4 000 kg, primary consumers 480 kg, secondary consumers 52 kg (dry mass per hectare).

```
scale: 1 cm = 500 kg
producers:           4 000 ÷ 500 = 8.0 cm
primary consumers:     480 ÷ 500 = 0.96 cm
secondary consumers:    52 ÷ 500 = 0.104 cm (about 1 mm)
transfers: 480 ÷ 4 000 × 100 = 12 %, then 52 ÷ 480 × 100 = 10.8 %
```

The bar for the top level is only about a millimetre long, so a scale that works for the largest bar leaves the
smallest barely visible. Say so on your drawing. This is typical of real data and illustrates the rapid fall in
biomass.

### Interpreting a pyramid

- A pyramid that narrows quickly shows a large loss of biomass at each stage.
- The bar for any level is smaller than the one below because of waste and respiration.
- Do not describe an energy pyramid as "numbers of organisms" or use it to say how many animals there are. It shows
  mass, not counts.

## Efficiency of food production (Biology only)

The efficiency of food production can be improved by **reducing the number of stages in a food chain**. Fewer stages
mean fewer opportunities for energy to be lost in waste and respiration.

### Worked example 5: crops or beef?

Invented data. One hectare of crop stores 5.0 × 10⁷ kJ of edible energy per year. A person needs 1.0 × 10⁴ kJ per
day.

```
energy needed per person per year = 1.0 × 10⁴ × 365 = 3.65 × 10⁶ kJ
people fed by eating the crop directly = 5.0 × 10⁷ ÷ 3.65 × 10⁶ = 13.7  → about 14 people
if the crop is fed to cattle first, 10 % reaches the beef: 5.0 × 10⁶ kJ
people fed by eating the beef = 5.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 3.65 × 10⁶ = 1.37 → about 1 person
```

Eating the crop directly supports about ten times as many people from the same land, because there is one fewer
stage and the losses at that stage are avoided. Real farms add other factors, such as the nutrients in meat, the type
of land and cost, but the energy argument is the one the specification asks for.

Another way to improve efficiency is to reduce the energy an animal loses. Keeping farm animals indoors where they
are warm and restricted in movement means less energy goes to respiration for movement and for keeping warm, so more
is stored in growth. You may be asked to evaluate this idea: the benefit is faster growth, and a possible
disadvantage is animal welfare.

## Common mistakes

- Saying energy is "used up" or "destroyed". It is transferred, mostly to the surroundings.
- Giving "heat lost" with no mention of respiration, or "faeces" with no mention of waste materials. Include both.
- Using 10 % for the transfer from the Sun to the producer. That is 1 %.
- Confusing biomass with number of organisms, or drawing pyramids of numbers.
- Drawing a pyramid with unequal bar heights or without a scale.
- Saying that a shorter food chain creates extra energy. It only has less loss between the producer and the final
  consumer.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** with the figures 1 % and 10 %, showing each step and the unit.
- **Explain** the loss of biomass with two reasons: waste materials and respiration (movement, transfer to the
  surroundings).
- For a pyramid, give the scale, calculate each bar length and label every bar.
- **Evaluate** farming methods by naming one benefit for efficiency and one possible drawback.
