## Questions

**1.** Define "demand". **[2]**

**2.** In Country A, monthly scooter rentals at different prices are shown below.

| Price (£) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rentals | 900 | 750 | 600 | 450 |

Bus fares then rise, and rentals at £4 become 750.

**(a)** State whether this is a movement along the demand curve or a shift. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why it happened. **[2]**
**(c)** Assuming a parallel shift, state rentals at £3 after the change. **[1]**

**3.** A shop raises the price of a gadget from £15.00 to £16.50. Weekly sales fall from 800 to 728.

**(a)** Calculate price elasticity of demand. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out how total revenue changes and what this shows. **[2]**

**4.** Incomes in Country B rise by 5%. Demand for frozen ready meals rises 2%, demand for budget noodles falls 3%, and demand for holiday packages rises 9%.

**(a)** Calculate income elasticity of demand for each good. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify the inferior good and the income-elastic good, giving a reason for each. **[2]**

**5.** A supermarket raises the price of Brand P cereal from £3.20 to £3.52. Weekly sales of Brand Q rise from 1,500 to 1,800 packs.

**(a)** Calculate cross elasticity of demand. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the value shows about the two brands. **[2]**
**(c)** When the price of tennis rackets rises 5%, demand for tennis balls falls 2%. Calculate the cross elasticity and state the relationship. **[2]**

**6.** The only bus route in a small town raises its fares. Explain two factors that affect the price elasticity of demand for the service. **[4]**

**7.** A firm sells 5,000 units a month at £4. Its estimated PED is −0.6, and variable cost is £2.50 per unit. It plans a 10% price rise.

**(a)** Predict the percentage and absolute change in quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new total revenue and its percentage change. **[2]**
**(c)** Compare contribution (revenue minus variable cost) before and after. **[2]**

**8.** Incomes rise by 8%. Demand for a loose-leaf tea is 2,400 units, and its income elasticity is −1.25. Calculate the change in demand, give the new level, and state what the sign shows. **[4]**

**9.** A café estimates PED for its lattes at −1.5. Evaluate the view that it should cut the price to raise its revenue. **[8]**

**10.** Demand for a product is Qd = 1,000 − 100P.

**(a)** Calculate quantity and total revenue at £3, £5 and £7. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate PED for a rise from £3 to £4. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate PED for a rise from £7 to £8 and explain what the two results show. **[2]**

**11.** A coach firm carries 50,000 passengers a month. Rail fares rise 10% (cross elasticity of coach demand with respect to rail price: +0.8). Incomes fall 3% (income elasticity of coach demand: −0.6).

**(a)** Calculate the effect of the rail fare rise and name the relationship. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the effect of the income fall and name the type of good. **[2]**
**(c)** Estimate passengers if the two effects simply add. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The amount of a good consumers wish to buy and can afford [1], at each price over a stated period with other influences constant [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition with only "willing" to buy is incomplete; "able" (purchasing power) is the part often left out.

**2. (a)** A shift (rightward), because price did not change [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Bus travel is a substitute for scooters [1]; dearer bus fares raise demand for scooters at every price [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 750 + 150 = **900** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* State the cause and the direction of the shift. Naming "a substitute" without saying its price rose does not explain a rightward move.

**3. (a)** %ΔP = 1.50 ÷ 15 = +10% [1]; %ΔQ = −72 ÷ 800 = −9%, so PED = **−0.9** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Revenue before 15 × 800 = £12,000; after 16.50 × 728 = £12,012, a rise of **£12** [1]. Demand is inelastic, so the price effect slightly outweighs the quantity effect [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A PED near −1 means revenue barely changes; calculate it rather than assuming a large change.

**4. (a)** Frozen meals 2 ÷ 5 = **+0.4** [1]; noodles −3 ÷ 5 = **−0.6** [1]; holidays 9 ÷ 5 = **+1.8** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Noodles are inferior because YED is negative [1]. Holidays are income elastic because YED exceeds 1, so demand grows faster than income [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the sign or size of the figure to the classification; a label with no reasoning rarely earns the mark.

**5. (a)** %ΔP = 0.32 ÷ 3.20 = +10% [1]; %ΔQ = 300 ÷ 1,500 = +20%, so XED = **+2.0** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The positive sign shows substitutes [1]; the value of 2.0 shows they are close, as a 10% rise in P's price lifts Q's sales by 20% [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** XED = −2 ÷ 5 = **−0.4** [1]; negative, so complements, with a weak link [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Put the quantity of the good being studied in the numerator and the other good's price in the denominator; reversing them gives a different value.

**6.** First factor, for example few or no close substitutes [1]: passengers with no alternative route keep travelling, so demand is inelastic [1]. Second factor, for example the proportion of income spent or the time period [1]: over the long run people find other ways to travel or move closer to work, so demand becomes more elastic [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs an explained link to responsiveness in this context, not just a named list.

**7. (a)** −0.6 × 10 = **−6%** [1]; 5,000 × 0.06 = 300, so **4,700 units** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before 5,000 × £4 = £20,000 [1]; after 4,700 × £4.40 = **£20,680**, a rise of **3.4%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Before: (4.00 − 2.50) × 5,000 = £7,500 [1]. After: (4.40 − 2.50) × 4,700 = £8,930, so contribution rises by £1,430 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the unit figures separately; a correct comparison needs both before and after values for revenue and for cost.

**8.** %Δ demand = −1.25 × 8 = **−10%** [1]; fall = 2,400 × 0.10 = 240 [1]; new demand **2,160** [1]. The negative sign shows an inferior good, as demand falls when income rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Multiply the elasticity by the percentage change in income, then apply it to the original quantity.

**9.** Demand is elastic, so a price cut raises quantity by a larger percentage than it cuts price, and revenue rises [1]. Illustration: a 10% cut gives a 15% rise in quantity, so revenue is 0.9 × 1.15 = 1.035 of the old figure, up 3.5% [2]. However, revenue is not profit: extra lattes bring extra ingredient and labour costs, so profit may fall [1]. The −1.5 figure is only an estimate and PED changes along the curve, so a larger cut may not repeat the result [1]. Rival cafés may match the cut, cancelling the gain in sales [1]. In the long run, customers may become used to lower prices, and a lower price may signal lower quality [1]. Judgement: the cut is likely to raise revenue but only worth doing if the margin on extra sales covers costs [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer applies the number to the café, gives a calculated illustration, and then tests it with costs, rivals and time before reaching a judgement.

**10. (a)** £3: Q = 700, revenue £2,100 [1]; £5: Q = 500, revenue £2,500 [1]; £7: Q = 300, revenue £2,100 [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** %ΔP = +33.3% [1]; %ΔQ = −100 ÷ 700 = −14.3%, so PED = **−0.43** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** %ΔP = +14.3%, %ΔQ = −100 ÷ 300 = −33.3%, so PED = **−2.33** [1]. Elasticity differs along a straight demand curve, being inelastic at low prices and elastic at high prices, though the slope is constant [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both results are needed to make the point; one value on its own cannot show how elasticity varies.

**11. (a)** +0.8 × 10 = **+8%** [1]; positive cross elasticity shows rail and coach are substitutes [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** −0.6 × −3 = **+1.8%** [1]; negative income elasticity shows an inferior good [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 8% + 1.8% = 9.8% [1]; 50,000 × 1.098 = **54,900 passengers** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep track of signs when both the elasticity and the change are negative; the product is positive.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the end value as the base rather than the starting value.
- Using absolute changes instead of percentage changes.
- Giving an elasticity with no sign, no label or no meaning.
- Treating "inferior" as meaning poor quality.
- Drawing a shift when only the good's own price changed.
- Swapping the good in the numerator and denominator of cross elasticity.
- Saying revenue rises after a price rise without checking whether demand is inelastic.
- Treating revenue as profit in evaluation answers.
- Listing factors affecting elasticity without applying them to the context.
