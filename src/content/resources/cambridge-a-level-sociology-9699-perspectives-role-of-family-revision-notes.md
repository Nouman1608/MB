Condensed notes for section 3.1, *Perspectives on the role of the family*, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 2. The [3.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-perspectives-role-of-family/) gives fuller explanations and a worked evaluation; test yourself with the [3.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-perspectives-role-of-family-practice/).

## Syllabus checklist

- Functionalist accounts of how the family benefits members and society, how its functions have changed, and the loss of functions debate.
- Marxist accounts of how the family benefits capitalism: ideological control, reproduction of labour, consumption.
- Feminist responses to functionalist and Marxist accounts.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Function | The contribution an institution makes to meeting society's needs |
| Nuclear family | Two generations: parents and their dependent children |
| Extended family | Three or more generations, or relatives beyond the nuclear family |
| Primary socialisation | Early learning of norms and values, mainly in the family |
| Stabilisation of adult personalities | Parsons: the family gives adults emotional security |
| Instrumental role | Parsons: breadwinning, linking the family to work |
| Expressive role | Parsons: care, emotional support, socialisation |
| Functional fit | The idea that a family type suits the needs of a particular kind of society |
| Loss of functions | The view that specialist institutions have taken over family functions |
| Reproduction of labour power | Producing and maintaining workers for capitalism |
| Ideological state apparatus | Althusser's term for institutions spreading ruling-class ideas |
| Patriarchy | A system of male dominance |
| Domestic labour | Unpaid housework and care |

## Functionalism

- **Murdock (1949)**, comparing 250 societies: four universal functions, sexual, reproductive, economic and educational.
- **Parsons**: two basic and irreducible functions, primary socialisation and stabilisation of adult personalities; instrumental and expressive roles.
- **Functional fit**: isolated nuclear family suits industrial society's need for geographical and social mobility; extended family suited pre-industrial society.
- **Loss of functions**: Parsons says the family lost production, education, health and welfare functions to other institutions and specialised; **Fletcher (1966)** says functions have become more important, with the state supporting rather than replacing families.

**Criticisms**: Laslett found small nuclear households common before industrialisation; Anderson (1971), using the 1851 census of Preston, found extended households helped families cope with industrialisation; Young and Willmott (1957) found strong extended kin networks in Bethnal Green; the "dark side" of family life; gender roles treated as natural; family diversity ignored.

## Marxism

| Idea | Argument |
|---|---|
| Inheritance | Engels (1884): monogamy arose so men could pass property to legitimate heirs |
| Ideological control | Children learn to accept hierarchy and authority; family absorbs workers' frustration |
| Reproduction of labour | The family produces and maintains workers at no direct cost to employers |
| Consumption | Families buy goods that create profit; advertising targets parents and children |
| Personal life | Zaretsky (1976): the family seems a refuge from alienating work, but cannot meet all emotional needs |

**Criticisms**: ignores diversity; ignores gender inequality; ignores benefits members gain; treats people as passive.

## Feminist responses

| Strand | Response |
|---|---|
| To functionalism | Expressive role naturalises women's unpaid labour; Oakley (*Housewife*, 1974): the housewife role is socially constructed |
| Marxist feminist | Benston (1969): women's unpaid labour reproduces labour power cheaply; Ansley (1972): wives absorb men's work frustrations |
| Radical feminist | Delphy and Leonard (1992): the family is a patriarchal economic system benefiting men |
| Liberal feminist | Inequality exists but is declining through law, education and changing attitudes |

**Criticisms of feminism**: may underestimate women's choice and change; strands disagree on how much has changed.

## Comparing the three

| | Functionalist | Marxist | Feminist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who benefits? | Society and all members | Capitalism and ruling class | Men (and capitalism, for Marxist feminists) |
| Consensus or conflict? | Consensus | Conflict | Conflict |
| View of change | Family adapts to fit society | Family changes as capitalism changes | Change depends on women's struggle and law |
| Main blind spot | Conflict and abuse | Gender | Agency and diversity among women |

## Exam traps

- **Leaving out the loss of functions debate**: the syllabus names it.
- **Marxism equals feminism**: they disagree about who benefits.
- **Unattributed criticism**: say which perspective or study makes the point.
- **Murdock's universality**: some societies raise children through wider kin, so the claim is contested.
- **Assuming historical change**: Laslett and Anderson complicate the fit thesis.
- **One-sided evaluation of a theory**: give strengths as well as limitations.

## Self-test

1. Name Murdock's four functions of the family.
2. What are Parsons' two irreducible functions?
3. Distinguish instrumental and expressive roles.
4. Why does Parsons argue the nuclear family fits industrial society?
5. State the loss of functions argument.
6. How did Fletcher respond to it?
7. What did Laslett's research show?
8. What did Engels link the monogamous family to?
9. Explain the family as a unit of consumption.
10. What did Benston argue about domestic labour?
11. How do radical feminists differ from Marxist feminists?
12. Give one criticism of the Marxist view of the family.

### Answers

1. Sexual, reproductive, economic, educational.
2. Primary socialisation of children and stabilisation of adult personalities.
3. Instrumental: breadwinning and links to work. Expressive: emotional care and socialisation.
4. It is small and mobile, so workers can move for jobs and achieve status at work rather than through kin.
5. Industrialisation transferred functions such as production, education and welfare to specialist institutions, leaving the family with fewer, specialised functions.
6. Families' functions have become more important, with the state supporting rather than replacing them.
7. Small nuclear households were common in pre-industrial England.
8. Private property and inheritance: men needed to know their heirs.
9. Families buy goods and services that generate profit; advertising and children's demands increase spending.
10. Women's unpaid domestic labour reproduces labour power cheaply for capitalism.
11. Radical feminists see patriarchy, not capitalism, as the root of inequality; Marxist feminists link women's oppression to capitalism.
12. Any one: ignores family diversity; ignores gender; ignores benefits to members; treats people as passive.
