Section A of Unit 3, *Language variation*, in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level English Language
specification (9670) is specification section **3.3.1 Learning language**. The specification defines the topic broadly:
it includes **early language acquisition in young children** and **later language development in teenagers**. Each
question gives you data as a starting point, which could be **speech, writing or multimodal texts** produced by
**native speakers or speakers of English as an additional language (EAL)**. This guide covers the parts of that range
students most often under-prepare: development after early childhood, writing, EAL learners and multimodal data.

## What the section requires

Unit 3 is a 2-hour exam worth 50 marks and 30% of the International A-level. Section A is worth 25 marks, and you
answer **one question from a choice of two**. Across the unit, the specification asks you to show three things:

- knowledge of the academic topic area, including different theories and approaches;
- skills in data analysis;
- skills in academic argument and evaluation of ideas.

The choice matters. Read both questions and their data before deciding: one may suit your strengths in spoken data,
the other in writing or EAL.

## The span of development

Think of learning language as a continuum, not a set of toddler stages.

| Period | Typical focus in data |
|---|---|
| Early childhood (0-5) | Phonological simplification, first words, telegraphic speech, overgeneralisation, caregiver interaction |
| Primary years (5-11) | Literacy, spelling development, longer narratives, subordinate clauses, growing pragmatic control |
| Adolescence (11-18) | Complex syntax, abstract and academic vocabulary, register control, peer-group slang, identity |

Early stages are covered in the [Unit 3 Language Variation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-unit-3-language-variation-study-guide/);
the sections below concentrate on later development, writing and EAL.

## Later development in children and teenagers

**Grammar.** Development continues long after the telegraphic stage. Watch for:

- **passive constructions** ("the window was broken by the ball"), which children often misinterpret, especially when either noun could be the agent, well into the primary years;
- **embedded and relative clauses** ("the girl who won the race");
- **nominalisation** in academic writing ("the reduction of waste" rather than "we reduced waste");
- **cohesive devices** beyond "and then": "however", "as a result", "on the other hand".

**Vocabulary.** Growth shifts from everyday to **abstract and subject-specific** lexis, much of it learned through
reading and schooling rather than conversation. Teenagers also acquire **polysemy and figurative meaning**: idioms,
sarcasm and irony.

**Pragmatics.** Older children and teenagers learn to **adjust register** for different audiences, use **politeness
strategies** such as indirect requests, and manage **conversational repair** and topic shifts. Understanding sarcasm
develops through middle childhood.

**Adolescent language and identity.** Teenage speech is a resource for identity. Slang, discourse markers ("like",
"innit") and new pronunciations often spread among adolescents first. Penelope Eckert's work in a Detroit-area high
school (1989) linked language variation to adolescent social categories. Research on Multicultural London English by
Jenny Cheshire, Paul Kerswill and colleagues showed young people in multilingual areas developing a new variety drawing
on several languages. In analysis, treat teenage variation as systematic and social, not as deterioration.

## Writing development

Children's writing has its own developmental path.

**Barry Kroll's model (1981)** proposes four stages:

- **Preparation**: mastering letter formation and basic spelling.
- **Consolidation**: writing much as they speak, with informal tone and "and then" chains.
- **Differentiation**: recognising that writing differs from speech, and using different genres and audiences.
- **Integration**: a personal voice controlled across forms and purposes.

**Spelling development** is often described in stages, for example by J. Richard Gentry (1982): pre-communicative
(random letters), semi-phonetic (some letters for sounds, "dg" for "dog"), phonetic (every sound represented,
"sed" for "said"), transitional (spelling patterns appear, "tode" for "toad") and correct. Phonetic spellings are
evidence of a child's phonological awareness, not carelessness.

**What to analyse in writing data**: spelling patterns, punctuation, sentence structure and length, cohesion, genre
conventions, awareness of audience, and the influence of teaching (a story that starts "Once upon a time" shows genre
learning from reading).

## EAL learners

Data may come from speakers learning English as an additional language. Concepts:

- **Interlanguage** (Larry Selinker, 1972): the learner's developing system, with its own rules, between the first
  language and the target.
- **Transfer**: features of the first language carried into English, such as article use, word order, or pronunciation
  of particular sounds. Transfer can help (shared vocabulary) as well as produce non-target forms.
- **Overgeneralisation** occurs in EAL learning too: "He goed home" may appear in an adult learner's speech.
- **Comprehensible input**: Stephen Krashen argued that acquisition depends on input that is understandable but slightly
  beyond the learner's current level. A **silent period**, when a learner listens more than speaks, is common.
- **BICS and CALP**: Jim Cummins distinguished basic interpersonal communicative skills, often developed within a couple
  of years, from cognitive academic language proficiency, which takes considerably longer. A fluent conversationalist
  may still find academic writing difficult.
- **Code-switching** between languages is a skilled resource, often used for identity, emphasis or a word that fits
  better.

Avoid a deficit framing. Describe what the learner can do, what system they are using, and what is developing.

## Multimodal data

Data may include text messages, social media posts or presentations. Here look at how young people combine writing
with speech-like features: abbreviations, emoji, non-standard spelling for effect ("sooo"), and punctuation used for
tone. David Crystal's *Txtng: The Gr8 Db8* (2008) argued that texting has not damaged literacy, and research by Clare
Wood and colleagues found no evidence that children's use of textisms harmed their spelling. Deliberate non-standard
forms require knowledge of the standard forms they play with.

## Worked example

**Data** (original, invented): a 14-year-old EAL learner, two years in an English-medium school, writes in a science
report: *"The results was showing that plant growth more fast in light. Therefore, we can conclude light is a factor
which affecting growth."*

**Analysis:**

> The writer controls features of academic genre: the discourse marker "Therefore", the formulaic "we can conclude"
> and the relative clause "which..." show emerging CALP in Cummins's terms. Non-target forms are systematic rather
> than random. Subject-verb agreement ("results was") and the progressive used for a completed finding ("was
> showing", "affecting") suggest an interlanguage in which tense and aspect marking are still developing, possibly
> influenced by a first language that marks these differently. "More fast" overgeneralises the analytic comparative
> rule. The data support Selinker's view of a rule-governed learner system, and they show academic register being
> acquired alongside, not after, grammatical accuracy.

## Common mistakes

- Revising only toddler stages when the specification includes teenagers.
- Calling EAL or teenage forms "mistakes" without explaining the system behind them.
- Ignoring writing and multimodal data in revision.
- Applying a stage model as a rigid rule.
- Listing theories without testing them against the data.
- Choosing a question without reading both sets of data.

## Exam technique

Open by describing the data's context: speaker or writer, age, language background, purpose. Organise the body by
language level or theme, combining data, concept and evaluation in each paragraph. Weigh theories rather than
reciting them, and finish with a judgement about what the data most clearly show about how language is learned.
