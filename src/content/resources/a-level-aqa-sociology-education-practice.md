**1.** Identify **two** functions of education according to
functionalists. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term **correspondence principle**, identifying the
sociologists associated with it. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** sociological explanations for differential
achievement by social class. **[2]**

**4.** Outline and explain the Marxist view of the role of education
in reproducing class inequality. **[8]**

**5.** Outline and explain **two** cultural explanations for
class-based differences in educational achievement. **[8]**

**6.** Outline and explain **two** explanations for the improved
educational performance of girls relative to boys. **[8]**

**7.** Outline and explain **two** in-school explanations for ethnic
differences in educational achievement. **[8]**

**8.** Applying material from the item and your knowledge, evaluate
the view that the education system operates primarily to benefit
wider society rather than the individual. **[30]**

**9.** Applying material from the item and your knowledge, evaluate
sociological explanations for differential educational achievement by
social class. **[30]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: **secondary socialisation** into shared norms and
values [1]; **social solidarity**, creating a sense of belonging to
wider society [1]; **role allocation**, sorting individuals into jobs
suited to their abilities via a meritocratic system of qualifications
[1]; **skills provision** for a modern economy [1].

**2.** The correspondence principle is Bowles and Gintis's concept
that the social relationships and structure of schooling (hierarchy,
discipline, extrinsic reward) directly mirror those of the capitalist
workplace [1], preparing students for their future roles as compliant
workers [1].

**3.** Any two: **material deprivation** — lack of physical or
financial resources such as books or a quiet study space [1];
**cultural capital** (Bourdieu) — the knowledge, tastes and ways of
speaking that higher-class families pass on, advantaging children in
a system that implicitly values these traits [1]; **restricted and
elaborated speech codes** [1].

**4.** Marxists argue education **reproduces existing class
inequality** rather than operating meritocratically as it appears to
[1] [1]. Through the **correspondence principle** (Bowles and
Gintis), the hierarchy, discipline and extrinsic reward structure of
schooling mirrors the workplace, producing a compliant future
workforce accustomed to accepting authority [1] [1]. Working-class
students are **systematically disadvantaged** by the system —
through unequal access to cultural capital, material resources, and
school processes such as setting — so that qualifications, while
appearing to reward ability, in practice track social class
background [1] [1]. This creates a **myth of meritocracy** that
legitimises inequality, since those who fail can be persuaded their
failure reflects their own lack of ability rather than the system's
structure [1] [1].

**5.** Any two, 4 marks each: **Bourdieu's cultural capital** — the
knowledge, tastes and behaviours that higher-class families transmit
to their children give them an advantage in a system that implicitly
rewards these traits, for example familiarity with the vocabulary and
references teachers expect [1] [1] [1] [1]. **Restricted and
elaborated speech codes** (Bernstein) — schools operate using an
elaborated code that middle-class pupils already possess from their
home environment, while working-class pupils more often use a
restricted code, putting them at a linguistic disadvantage in
lessons and assessment [1] [1] [1] [1]. (Also accept: differing
parental attitudes to education as a cultural, rather than purely
material, explanation.)

**6.** Any two, 4 marks each: **changing aspirations linked to
feminism** — the availability of a wider range of careers has
increased girls' motivation to achieve qualifications that were
previously seen as less relevant to their expected future roles [1]
[1] [1] [1]. **Coursework and modular assessment styles** — argued to
favour girls' typically more consistent, organised study habits
compared with a single terminal exam format [1] [1] [1] [1]. (Also
accept: equal-opportunity policies removing barriers to girls'
subject choices; teacher expectations favouring girls' classroom
behaviour.)

**7.** Any two, 4 marks each: **teacher labelling** — teachers may
form expectations of pupils partly shaped by ethnicity and cultural
background, which can produce a self-fulfilling prophecy as pupils
live up or down to the label [1] [1] [1] [1]. **Setting and
streaming** — placement into lower sets disproportionately affecting
some ethnic groups restricts access to the full curriculum and to
higher-tier examination entry [1] [1] [1] [1]. (Also accept:
ethnocentric curriculum content that reflects the culture and history
of the ethnic majority.)

**8.** Indicative content — award up to 30 marks using the levels
descriptors in the specification; look for a developed, evaluative
argument rather than a checklist of points. **For "wider society"**:
functionalists argue education provides secondary socialisation and
social solidarity that benefit society as a whole, while role
allocation matches individuals to jobs the economy needs [1] [1];
Marxists similarly see education as serving a wider structural
interest — reproducing a compliant workforce for capitalism via the
correspondence principle — though this benefits a ruling class rather
than society as a whole, a distinction candidates should draw
explicitly [1] [1]. **For "the individual"**: education also provides
individual credentials, social mobility opportunities, and personal
development, which candidates should weigh against the
society-benefiting functions above [1] [1]. **Evaluation**: strong
answers note that functionalism assumes a value consensus and fair
meritocracy that Marxists and others dispute, and that the same
institutional feature (e.g. qualifications) can be read as serving
either the individual or wider society depending on the theoretical
lens applied, meaning the "primarily" framing of the question invites
direct comparison rather than a one-sided answer [1] [1] [1] [1].

**9.** Indicative content — award up to 30 marks using the levels
descriptors in the specification. **Material/economic explanations**:
lack of resources such as books, internet access, or a quiet study
space, and pressure on some working-class pupils to take paid work,
directly limiting study time and opportunity [1] [1]. **Cultural
explanations**: Bourdieu's cultural capital and Bernstein's speech
codes, disadvantaging working-class pupils in a system that
implicitly rewards middle-class cultural traits and language [1] [1].
**In-school explanations**: labelling, setting and streaming, and
anti-school subcultures that can compound disadvantage that
originates outside school [1] [1]. **Evaluation**: candidates should
weigh whether material or cultural explanations carry more
explanatory weight, note that in-school and out-of-school
explanations likely interact rather than operate independently, and
consider critiques such as the risk of stereotyping working-class
culture as uniformly deficient [1] [1] [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

Describing only one sociological perspective when a question's
command word (compare, evaluate) explicitly requires weighing more
than one. Naming a general idea, such as "cultural differences,"
without the specific theorist or concept (cultural capital,
correspondence principle, labelling) — named theorists appear in
AQA's mark schemes as indicative content, so citing one strengthens
an answer even though the published levels descriptors don't require
it for full marks. Conflating material and cultural explanations for
class-based achievement as a single undifferentiated category. In
30-mark essays, describing several perspectives back to back and
saving all evaluation for a final paragraph, rather than building
evaluation into each point as it is made. Failing to link an
explanation for one
social variable (gender, ethnicity) explicitly back to that variable,
which risks the answer reading as generic rather than targeted at the
question asked.
