| 3.02b | Use a calculator to perform calculations with numbers in standard form. | First (both tiers) |

Section 3.02b cross-refers to the laws of indices (3.01c), which do most of the work in non-calculator calculations.

## The form and what it tells you

A number is in standard form when it is written as

```
A × 10ⁿ,   where 1 ≤ A < 10 and n is an integer
```

The index n tells you the size at a glance. A positive n means the number is at least 10; n = 0 means it is between 1 and 10; a negative n means it is less than 1. So 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ is 0.00036, a small positive number, and 3.6 × 10⁴ is 36 000.

## Tidying "nearly standard form" numbers

Calculations often produce answers such as 42 × 10⁻⁵ or 0.4 × 10⁵. Fix A, then compensate in the index.

```
340 × 10⁵   = 3.4 × 10² × 10⁵   = 3.4 × 10⁷
0.45 × 10⁻³ = 4.5 × 10⁻¹ × 10⁻³ = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴
0.06 × 10⁸  = 6 × 10⁻² × 10⁸    = 6 × 10⁶
```

A rule that always works: if A gets **smaller** by a factor of 10ᵏ, the index goes **up** by k, and the other way round. The value of the number does not change.

## Ordering mixed lists

**Worked example 1.** Write 4.2 × 10⁻³, 0.0039, 41 × 10⁻⁴ and 4.15 × 10⁻³ in order, smallest first.

```
put every value into standard form first:
0.0039      = 3.9  × 10⁻³
41 × 10⁻⁴   = 4.1  × 10⁻³
4.15 × 10⁻³ and 4.2 × 10⁻³ are already in standard form

all have 10⁻³, so compare A: 3.9 < 4.1 < 4.15 < 4.2
order: 0.0039, 41 × 10⁻⁴, 4.15 × 10⁻³, 4.2 × 10⁻³
```

Comparing 41 × 10⁻⁴ with 4.2 × 10⁻³ by looking at 41 and 4.2 would put them the wrong way round. Convert first.

## Multiplying and dividing without a calculator

Multiply (or divide) the A values, use the laws of indices on the powers of 10, then tidy.

**Worked example 2.**

```
(6 × 10⁴) × (7 × 10⁻⁹)   = 42 × 10⁻⁵   = 4.2 × 10⁻⁴
(2.4 × 10³) ÷ (6 × 10⁻²) = 0.4 × 10⁵   = 4 × 10⁴
```

In the division, 10³ ÷ 10⁻² = 10³⁻⁽⁻²⁾ = 10⁵. Subtracting a negative index is the step most often slipped.

**Powers of standard form numbers.** Raise both parts to the power.

```
(3 × 10⁴)²  = 3² × 10⁸   = 9 × 10⁸
(2 × 10⁻³)³ = 2³ × 10⁻⁹  = 8 × 10⁻⁹
(5 × 10⁶)²  = 25 × 10¹²  = 2.5 × 10¹³
```

## Adding and subtracting without a calculator

Rewrite one number so that both have the **same power of 10**, combine the A values, then tidy. The powers are not added.

**Worked example 3.**

```
6.1 × 10⁻³ − 4 × 10⁻⁴
= 6.1 × 10⁻³ − 0.4 × 10⁻³
= 5.7 × 10⁻³

9.8 × 10⁵ + 4.5 × 10⁵
= 14.3 × 10⁵
= 1.43 × 10⁶
```

An alternative is to convert both to ordinary numbers, combine, and convert back: 0.0061 − 0.0004 = 0.0057 = 5.7 × 10⁻³. For very large or very small indices this is slower and riskier.

## Using a calculator

Use the ×10ˣ key (labelled EXP or ×10ˣ on most scientific calculators) so that each number is entered as one value. Use brackets round a whole numerator or denominator.

**Worked example 4.** Work out (5.6 × 10⁸ × 3.2 × 10⁻³)/(7 × 10²). Then work out (4.7 × 10⁶)/(2.9 × 10⁻²) to 3 significant figures.

```
numerator:   5.6 × 10⁸ × 3.2 × 10⁻³ = 1.792 × 10⁶
divide by 7 × 10²:  2 560 = 2.56 × 10³

4.7 × 10⁶ ÷ 2.9 × 10⁻² = 162 068 965.5...
                        = 1.62 × 10⁸ (3 s.f.)
```

If the question asks for standard form, convert the display. A display such as 1.620689655E+08 is not a written answer; write 1.62 × 10⁸.

## Context problems

**Worked example 5.** A bacterium is 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m long. How many would fit end to end along 1 cm?

```
1 cm = 0.01 m = 1 × 10⁻²  m
(1 × 10⁻²) ÷ (2.5 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.4 × 10⁴ = 4 × 10³
```

About **4 000** bacteria. Units must match before dividing.

**Worked example 6.** A planet has mass 6 × 10²⁴ kg and one of its moons has mass 7.5 × 10²² kg (invented values). How many times heavier is the planet?

```
(6 × 10²⁴) ÷ (7.5 × 10²²) = 0.8 × 10² = 80
```

"How many times" questions are divisions, and the answer is often an ordinary number.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving A outside 1 ≤ A < 10, such as 14.3 × 10⁵ or 0.4 × 10⁵.
- Moving the index the wrong way when tidying.
- Adding the indices when adding two numbers in standard form.
- Working out 10³ ÷ 10⁻² as 10¹ instead of 10⁵.
- Squaring only the A part, or only the power of 10, of a number in brackets.
- Copying a calculator display instead of writing × 10ⁿ.
- Comparing A values when the powers of 10 differ.

## Exam technique

- **"Give your answer in standard form"**: the final line must be A × 10ⁿ with A between 1 and 10.
- **Non-calculator papers**: show the A calculation and the index calculation separately, then the tidy step.
- **"How many times greater"**: divide, then answer with a number, rounded sensibly if needed.
- **Calculator papers**: write the full display before rounding, so the accuracy mark is safe.
