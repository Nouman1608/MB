## Counting particles and specific charge

For a nuclide ᴬ_Z X: protons = Z, neutrons = A − Z, and electrons = Z in a neutral atom. An ion with charge n+ has lost n electrons; an ion with charge n− has gained n.

**Worked example 1: counting.** The ion ²³₁₁Na⁺ has 11 protons, 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons and 11 − 1 = 10 electrons.

**Specific charge** is charge ÷ mass (C kg⁻¹). For a nucleus, use the charge of its protons and the mass of all its nucleons; electron mass is negligible.

**Worked example 2: comparing nuclei.**

```
proton:            1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷        = 9.58 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹
alpha (⁴₂He):      3.20 × 10⁻¹⁹ / (4 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)  = 4.79 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹
carbon-14 nucleus: 9.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ / (14 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = 4.11 × 10⁷ C kg⁻¹
```

The alpha particle has twice the charge but four times the mass of a proton, so half its specific charge. Ratios like this can be found without a calculator: specific charge ∝ Z/A.

Isotopes have the same Z, so the same charge, but different A. Two isotope nuclei of the same element therefore have different specific charges, with the lighter one higher.

## Stability and the strong nuclear force

Protons repel each other electrostatically. The **strong nuclear force** acts between nucleons and keeps the nucleus together. Its key features for explanation questions:

- it is **attractive** from about 0.5 fm to about 3 fm;
- it is **repulsive** below about 0.5 fm, which stops nucleons being pushed into each other;
- it has **no significant effect** beyond about 3 fm, so it acts only between neighbouring nucleons, while the electrostatic repulsion acts across the whole nucleus.

**Alpha decay** happens in very large nuclei: ²⁴¹₉₅Am → ²³⁷₉₃Np + ⁴₂α. **β⁻ decay** happens in neutron-rich nuclei: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁β + ν̄ₑ. Check every equation: the top numbers balance (241 = 237 + 4) and the bottom numbers balance (95 = 93 + 2).

**Why the neutrino was proposed.** β⁻ particles from one nuclide are emitted with a continuous range of kinetic energies up to a maximum. If only the β particle and the daughter nucleus were produced, each β particle would have the same energy. The missing energy is carried by a third particle, the (anti)neutrino, which is very hard to detect.

## Antiparticles, annihilation and pair production

An antiparticle has the same mass and rest energy as its particle and the opposite charge (and opposite baryon, lepton and strangeness numbers).

**Pair production** needs a photon with at least the total rest energy of the pair. For a proton and antiproton:

```
minimum energy = 2 × 938.3 MeV = 1876.6 MeV = 3.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ J
f = E/h = 3.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 4.53 × 10²³ Hz
λ = c/f = 6.62 × 10⁻¹⁶ m
```

Any energy above the minimum becomes kinetic energy of the pair. Pair production usually happens near a nucleus, which takes up some momentum.

**Annihilation** of a slow particle with its antiparticle gives **two** photons travelling in opposite directions, so total momentum stays zero. Each photon carries the rest energy of one particle (plus a share of any kinetic energy).

## Interactions and exchange particles

| Interaction | Acts on | Exchange particle |
|---|---|---|
| Strong | Hadrons | Pion (between nucleons) |
| Weak | All particles | W⁺ or W⁻ |
| Electromagnetic | Charged particles | Virtual photon |
| Gravity | Particles with mass | Not tested |

In a **Feynman-style diagram**, time runs upwards; particle lines meet at vertices, and a wavy line joins vertices as the exchange particle. At each vertex, charge must balance. In **electron capture**, p + e⁻ → n + νₑ, the proton becomes a neutron and emits a W⁺, which meets the electron and turns it into an electron neutrino. Charge check at the proton vertex: +1 → 0 + (+1).

Only the weak interaction changes one quark flavour into another, and only the weak interaction involves neutrinos.

## Classification

Use a decision tree:

1. Does it feel the strong interaction? **No**: a lepton (e, μ, νₑ, ν_μ and their antiparticles). **Yes**: a hadron.
2. For a hadron, is it made of three quarks (or three antiquarks)? **Yes**: a baryon or antibaryon. **No** (a quark–antiquark pair): a meson.

| Number | Baryons | Antibaryons | Mesons | Leptons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baryon number B | +1 | −1 | 0 | 0 |
| Lepton number | 0 | 0 | 0 | +1 (particles), −1 (antiparticles) |

The muon is a heavier version of the electron and decays into one: μ⁻ → e⁻ + ν̄ₑ + ν_μ. Kaons can decay into pions.

## Quarks: building and reading particles

| Quark | Q | B | S |
|---|---|---|---|
| u | +2/3 | +1/3 | 0 |
| d | −1/3 | +1/3 | 0 |
| s | −1/3 | +1/3 | −1 |

Add the properties of the quarks to get the hadron's. A K⁰ is ds̄: charge −1/3 + 1/3 = 0, B = 1/3 − 1/3 = 0, S = 0 + 1 = +1.

**Worked example 3: an unfamiliar hadron.** A particle has quark content uus. Charge = 2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3 = +1; B = +1, so it is a baryon; S = −1, so it is strange. It is not one of the particles whose quark content you must recall, but the method is the same.

## Deciding whether an interaction can happen

Check, in order: **charge**, **baryon number**, **lepton number (Lₑ and L_μ separately)**, then **strangeness**. Charge, B and L must always be conserved. Strangeness is conserved in strong interactions but can change by 0 or ±1 in weak ones.

**Worked example 4.** Which of these are possible?

| Interaction | Q | B | Lₑ | S | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| p + ν̄ₑ → n + e⁺ | +1 → +1 | 1 → 1 | −1 → −1 | 0 → 0 | Possible (weak: it involves a neutrino) |
| n → p + e⁻ + νₑ | 0 → 0 | 1 → 1 | 0 → +2 | 0 → 0 | Not possible: Lₑ not conserved |
| p → e⁺ + π⁰ | +1 → +1 | 1 → 0 | 0 → −1 | 0 → 0 | Not possible: B and Lₑ not conserved |
| K⁺ → π⁺ + π⁰ | +1 → +1 | 0 → 0 | 0 → 0 | +1 → 0 | Possible by the weak interaction only |

In β⁻ decay a d quark becomes a u quark (udd → uud); in β⁺ decay a u quark becomes a d quark.

## Common mistakes

- Using A instead of Z for the charge when finding specific charge, or including electron mass.
- Writing a neutrino instead of an antineutrino in β⁻ decay.
- Saying the strong force acts over the whole nucleus.
- Giving one photon in annihilation.
- Forgetting to check Lₑ and L_μ separately.
- Applying strangeness conservation to a weak decay.
- Classifying a muon as a meson: it is a lepton.

## Exam technique

"State" questions want a fact, such as the exchange particle. "Explain" questions want a chain of reasoning: for the neutrino, link the range of β energies to energy conservation. "Deduce" or "show" whether an interaction is possible means checking each quantity in turn and naming the one that fails. Write a short table, as in Worked example 4, so the examiner can follow each check.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does an alpha particle have half the specific charge of a proton?**
An alpha particle has twice the charge of a proton but about four times the mass, so its charge-to-mass ratio is half as large.

**How can you tell an interaction is weak?**
It is weak if it involves a neutrino or antineutrino, changes quark flavour, or changes strangeness. These things cannot happen in strong or electromagnetic interactions.

**What is the minimum photon energy for pair production?**
The total rest energy of the particle and antiparticle. For an electron and positron that is 2 × 0.511 MeV = 1.022 MeV.
