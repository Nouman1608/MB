These questions cover sub-topic 2.9, Market failure: public goods, from the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. Places and figures are invented, and the [1] points in the answers are a revision aid, not IB marks. Revise first with the [2.9 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-9/) or the [2.9 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-9-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define *non-rivalry*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a public good and a private good. **[2]**

**3.** For each good, state whether it is rivalrous and whether it is excludable. **[4]**

- A ticketed concert in a stadium.
- A national early-warning system for earthquakes.

**4.** Explain why a lighthouse beam is often given as an example of a public good. **[2]**

**5.** Define *free rider*. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why state-funded hospital treatment is not a public good. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The fictional town of Brisk has four households that would all benefit from a sea wall. The sea wall costs 2,000 brisks a year. The households value it at 800, 650, 500 and 350 brisks a year.

**(a)** Calculate the total benefit of the sea wall and the net benefit to the town. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why a private firm is unlikely to build the sea wall even though it is worth providing. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the free rider problem leads to market failure. **[4]**

**9.** The government of Brisk spends 8.0 million brisks a year running its own service to clean the public beaches. A private firm bids 6.8 million to do the same work, and monitoring the contract would cost 0.5 million.

**(a)** Calculate the annual saving from contracting out and express it as a percentage of the current cost. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **two** reasons why the saving may not be achieved in practice. **[4]**

**10.** Explain the difference between direct provision and contracting out as responses to the under-provision of public goods. **[3]**

**11.** A road is congested at rush hour but empty at night. Explain how this affects whether the road is a public good. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate direct provision by the government as a response to the market failure caused by public goods. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Consumption of the good by one person [1] does not reduce the amount available for others to consume [1].

**2.** A public good is non-rivalrous and non-excludable [1]; a private good is rivalrous and excludable [1].

**3.** Concert: rivalrous, since seats are limited [1], and excludable, since tickets are checked [1]. Earthquake warning system: non-rivalrous, since one more person warned does not reduce the warning for others [1], and non-excludable, since no one in the area can be prevented from receiving it [1].

**4.** One more ship using the beam does not reduce its availability to others (non-rival) [1], and ships that do not pay cannot be stopped from seeing it (non-excludable) [1].

**5.** A person who benefits from a good [1] without paying for it [1].

**6.** It is rivalrous, because a bed or a doctor's time used by one patient cannot be used by another at the same moment [1], and it is excludable, because access can be controlled; it is a merit good funded by the state, not a public good [1].

**7. (a)** Total benefit = 800 + 650 + 500 + 350 = **2,300 brisks** [1]. Net benefit = 2,300 - 2,000 = **300 brisks** [1].

**(b)** No single household values the wall at 2,000 or more, so none will pay for it alone [1]. The wall is non-excludable, so each household can benefit without paying and has an incentive to understate its value [1]. The firm cannot collect enough revenue to cover costs, so the wall is not built [1].

**8.** Public goods are non-excludable, so people can consume them without paying [1]. Rational consumers therefore wait for others to pay or hide their true valuation [1]. Firms cannot earn enough revenue to cover costs [1], so the good is under-provided or not provided at all although its social benefit exceeds its cost: resources are misallocated [1].

**9. (a)** Contracting out cost = 6.8 + 0.5 = 7.3 million; saving = 8.0 - 7.3 = **0.7 million brisks** [1]; 0.7 / 8.0 × 100 = **8.75%** [1].

**(b)** The firm may cut quality to raise profit, for example cleaning less often, if the contract does not specify standards precisely [1]; the government would then face extra costs or a worse service [1]. Monitoring may cost more than expected, or the contract may need renegotiating [1], so the saving shrinks or disappears [1].

**10.** Under both methods the government pays from taxation, which removes the free rider problem [1]. With direct provision the government also produces the good itself [1]; with contracting out a private firm produces it under contract, usually chosen by competitive bidding [1].

**11.** At night one more car does not slow anyone, so the road is effectively non-rivalrous [1]. At rush hour each extra car adds to congestion, so use becomes rivalrous [1]. The road is therefore close to a public good only when uncongested; at peak times it is not a pure public good [1].

**12.** Strengths: taxation means everyone pays, which removes the free rider problem [1]; the government can provide goods such as defence or flood barriers that no firm would supply [1]; decisions can reflect social benefit, including for future generations [1]. Limitations: tax revenue has an opportunity cost, such as health or education spending forgone [1]; the government does not know how much people value the good, so it may provide too much or too little [1]; state producers may face weak incentives to control costs [1]; contracting out may achieve the same output more cheaply where quality is easy to measure [1]. Judgement: direct provision is justified where quality is hard to specify or the risk of failure is unacceptable, such as national defence, but contracting out may be better for routine services, so the answer depends on the good [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a public good with one characteristic only.
- Confusing public goods with merit goods.
- Calculating a saving without including monitoring costs.
- Evaluating "government provision" without naming a specific good or reaching a judgement.
