## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | The government's use of taxation and government spending to influence the economy and meet its macroeconomic objectives |
| Government revenue | Money received by the government, mainly from taxes |
| Direct tax | A tax on income or profit, paid straight to the government by whoever is taxed |
| Indirect tax | A tax on spending, collected by sellers and included in the price |
| Government expenditure | Government spending on public services, transfers and infrastructure |
| Fiscal deficit | Government spending greater than revenue in a year |
| Fiscal surplus | Government revenue greater than spending in a year |
| Expansionary fiscal policy | Higher spending and/or lower taxes, to raise demand |
| Contractionary fiscal policy | Lower spending and/or higher taxes, to reduce demand |

## Revenue and spending at a glance

| Revenue | Examples |
|---|---|
| Direct taxes | Income tax, corporation tax |
| Indirect taxes | Sales tax or VAT, excise duties |
| Main spending areas | Healthcare, education, social protection (benefit payments), defence and public order, infrastructure, debt interest |

## Formulas

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Net pay | Gross pay − income tax |
| Average tax rate | Tax paid ÷ gross pay × 100 |
| Tax in a price | Pre-tax price × tax rate |
| Pre-tax price from a tax-inclusive price | Tax-inclusive price ÷ (1 + rate) |
| Fiscal deficit | Spending − revenue (spending larger) |
| Fiscal surplus | Revenue − spending (revenue larger) |
| Deficit as a share of GDP | Deficit ÷ GDP × 100 |
| Share of spending | Item ÷ total spending × 100 |
| Percentage change | (New − old) ÷ old × 100 |

## Method boxes

**Banded income tax (steps)**

1. Split gross pay into the bands.
2. Multiply the income in each band by its rate.
3. Add the amounts to get total tax.
4. Subtract from gross pay for net pay.
5. Divide tax by gross pay for the average rate.

**Worked reminder.** Gross pay 36,000. Bands: 0% to 8,000; 15% from 8,001 to 24,000; 35% above 24,000.

- 16,000 × 0.15 = 2,400
- 12,000 × 0.35 = 4,200
- Tax = 6,600; net pay = **29,400**; average rate = 6,600 ÷ 36,000 × 100 = **18.3%**

**Finding the tax inside a price.** A tax-inclusive price of 138 with a 15% tax: pre-tax price = 138 ÷ 1.15 = 120. Tax = 138 − 120 = **18**.

**Deficit questions (steps)**

1. Total revenue and total spending.
2. Subtract the smaller from the larger.
3. Label it deficit or surplus.
4. If asked, divide by GDP and multiply by 100.

**Worked reminder.** Revenue 640, spending 700, GDP 3,000. Deficit = 60; as a share of GDP = 60 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = **2.0%**. If revenue were 910 and spending 880, there would be a **surplus of 30**.

## Effect chains on the macroeconomic objectives

| Objective | Expansionary policy | Contractionary policy |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | Demand rises, so output and GDP rise | Demand falls, so growth slows |
| Unemployment | More output needs more workers, so it falls | Firms cut output and jobs, so it may rise |
| Inflation | Demand outruns capacity, so prices may rise | Demand eases, so price rises slow |
| Current account | Higher incomes pull in imports, so it may worsen | Lower import spending, so it may improve |
| Redistribution | Higher benefits and progressive taxes shift income to the poor | Cuts in benefits or higher indirect taxes can hit low incomes |
| Environment | Spending on clean energy and transport; taxes on pollution | Cuts to green spending may reduce protection |

Tax cuts: a cut in income tax raises net pay. A tax cut of 2% on gross pay of 30,000 adds 600 to net pay. Part is saved and part goes on imports, so the boost to domestic demand is smaller than the cut.

## Deficit and surplus: impacts

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Deficit | Pays for infrastructure and services; supports demand in a downturn | Debt interest rises; possible crowding out of private investment; higher future taxes |
| Surplus | Pays off debt; builds a reserve | Takes demand out of the economy; may mean cuts to services |

Deficit is a yearly flow. National debt is the total stock of past borrowing.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| Direct and indirect tax | Direct: on income or profit, paid by the taxpayer. Indirect: on spending, built into the price |
| Deficit and national debt | Deficit: one year's gap. Debt: the total stock of borrowing |
| Expansionary and contractionary | Expansionary raises demand. Contractionary reduces it |
| Gross and net pay | Net pay is what remains after income tax |
| Revenue and borrowing | Borrowing is how a deficit is financed. It is not revenue |

## Worked reminder: a tax cut with leakages

Country E (illustrative) has GDP of 40,000 (millions). A tax cut gives 500,000 workers 1,200 each, which is 600 million.

- Households spend 70%: 600 × 0.7 = 420 million; they save 180 million.
- Imports take 25% of the new spending: 420 × 0.25 = 105 million.
- Domestic demand rises by 420 − 105 = 315 million.
- As a share of GDP: 315 ÷ 40,000 × 100 = **0.79%**.

The lesson for evaluation: the bigger the share saved or spent on imports, the weaker the boost to growth, jobs and prices at home.

## Which policy for which problem?

| Problem | Fiscal response | Typical risk |
|---|---|---|
| Slow growth or high unemployment | Expansionary: more spending or lower taxes | Bigger deficit; higher inflation if capacity is short |
| Rising prices from strong demand | Contractionary: less spending or higher taxes | Slower growth and higher unemployment |
| Large gap between rich and poor | Progressive income tax and benefits | Taxpayers may have less reason to work or invest |
| Pollution | Tax on polluting goods; spending on clean alternatives | Higher prices for consumers |
| Weak transport and power networks | Spending on infrastructure | Costly to fund; slow to deliver |

## Evaluation toolkit

- Size of the effect: savings and imports leak out.
- Time lags: projects take time to start and finish.
- Spare capacity: expansion works best when there are idle resources.
- Cost: borrowing now means debt interest later.
- Winners and losers: who pays and who gains?

A short evaluation structure: make a point, add its limit, then give a judgement that says which factor matters most in the case described.

## Quick self-test

1. State two direct taxes.
2. State two indirect taxes.
3. A government spends 700 and collects 640. State the size and type of the budget balance.
4. A sales tax of 20% is added to a pre-tax price of 80. Find the shelf price.
5. Gross pay is 36,000 and total income tax is 6,600. Find the average tax rate to one decimal place.
6. A government spends 900 in total, 180 of it on healthcare. What percentage is that?
7. Debt interest rises from 40 to 55. Find the percentage increase.
8. Name two main areas of government expenditure.
9. A government raises spending by 30 in an economy with GDP of 1,500. Find the direct effect as a percentage of GDP.
10. Explain one way a fiscal surplus could harm growth.

### Answers

1. Income tax and corporation tax.
2. Sales tax (or VAT) and excise duty.
3. Spending exceeds revenue, so a **deficit of 60**.
4. 80 × 1.2 = **96**.
5. 6,600 ÷ 36,000 × 100 = **18.3%**.
6. 180 ÷ 900 × 100 = **20%**.
7. (55 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = **37.5%**.
8. Any two of healthcare, education, social protection, defence, infrastructure, debt interest.
9. 30 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **2.0%** of GDP.
10. A surplus is reached by higher taxes or lower spending. This reduces total demand, so firms may cut output and growth may slow.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a definition with only one part, such as "taxes" with no mention of spending or the purpose.
- Mixing up direct and indirect taxes, especially calling a sales tax direct.
- Mixing up a deficit (one year, a flow) with the national debt (the total owed).
- Calling every deficit bad and every surplus good, with no consequences stated.
- Stopping a chain after one step, for example "spending rises so growth rises".
- Leaving out units or workings on a calculation, or rounding too early.
- Using a general point that is not set in the context of the question.
- Giving a conclusion in an evaluation answer that does not follow from the points made.
- Ignoring the objective named in the question, such as the current account.
