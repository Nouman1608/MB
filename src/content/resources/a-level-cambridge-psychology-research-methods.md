Paper 2 – Research Methods is worth 60 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes, and
is one of the two compulsory AS Level papers in Cambridge
International AS & A Level Psychology (9990), alongside [Paper 1,
Approaches, Issues and Debates](/resources/a-level-psychology-approaches-issues-and-debates/).
Where Paper 1 tests the four core approaches through 12 named core
studies, Paper 2 tests the research methodology that runs underneath
all of them -- the methods, concepts and planning skills psychologists
use to design and evaluate studies, both in the abstract and applied
to the core studies from Paper 1.

## Where this fits in 9990

Everything in this guide is AS Level content, but it is also assumed
knowledge for the A Level: Paper 4 (Specialist Options: Application
and Research Methods) builds directly on it, requiring candidates to
plan studies to a higher standard of detail than Paper 2 expects. A
secure grasp of this content at AS Level therefore pays off twice.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE A-LEVEL PSYCHOLOGY (9990) — PAPER 2 RESEARCH METHODS**

Six named research methods:

- Experiments (laboratory and field), including experimental designs
  (independent measures, matched pairs, repeated measures) and design
  concepts (counterbalancing, random allocation, order effects)
- Self-reports (questionnaires and interviews), including technique
  and question format
- Case studies — a single participant or unit studied in detail
- Observations (overt/covert, participant/non-participant,
  structured/unstructured, naturalistic/controlled)
- Correlations — positive and negative, strength, and co-variables
- Longitudinal studies, including experiments with a longitudinal design

Methodological concepts that apply across methods:

- Aims and hypotheses (null and alternative, directional and non-directional)
- Variables (independent, dependent, operational definitions)
- Controlling of variables (controls, standardisation, uncontrolled/participant/situational variables)
- Types of data (quantitative/qualitative, subjective/objective)
- Sampling of participants (opportunity, random, volunteer/self-selecting)
- Ethics, for both human participants and animals
- Validity (including ecological validity, demand characteristics, generalisability)
- Reliability and replicability (inter-rater, inter-observer, test-retest)
- Data analysis (measures of central tendency, measures of spread, tables and graphs)

## The six research methods

For each method, candidates need to be able to **describe** its main
features, **evaluate** it (typically in terms of reliability, validity
and ethics), and **apply** knowledge of it to a novel research
scenario -- this three-part pattern (describe, evaluate, apply) recurs
across every method and is a reliable structure for revision. Some
distinctions are frequently tested directly: laboratory versus field
experiments; the three experimental designs and the specific problems
each one addresses (counterbalancing addresses order effects in
repeated measures, for example); the different question formats and
techniques within self-reports; and the four descriptive pairs used
for observations (overt/covert, participant/non-participant,
structured/unstructured, naturalistic/controlled).

## Methodological concepts

These concepts are not tied to a single method and are examined both
in the abstract and in relation to specific core studies from Paper 1.
**Variables** requires being able to identify and operationalise an
independent and dependent variable in a given scenario, not just
define the terms. **Sampling** covers three named techniques
(opportunity, random, volunteer/self-selecting) and their implications
for generalisability. **Ethics** is examined separately for human
participants (minimising harm, valid/informed consent, right to
withdraw, deception, confidentiality, privacy, debriefing) and for
animals (minimising harm, replacement, species, numbers, and specific
procedural concerns such as pain, suffering, distress, housing, and
reward/deprivation/aversive stimuli) -- candidates should be ready to
apply the correct set depending on which is relevant to the scenario.
**Validity** and **reliability** are distinct concepts frequently
confused with each other: validity concerns whether a study measures
what it claims to (ecological validity, demand characteristics,
subjectivity/objectivity, generalisability), while reliability
concerns consistency (inter-rater, inter-observer and test-retest
reliability specifically). **Data analysis** is limited in scope by
design: candidates must recognise and interpret mode, median and mean,
range and standard deviation, and read bar charts, histograms and
scatter graphs, but the syllabus explicitly states no calculations are
required and no statistical tests beyond what is specified need to be
understood.

## Section A and Section B

Paper 2 has two sections. **Section A** contains short-answer
questions based on general research methods and on research methods
relating directly to a specific Paper 1 core study, plus three
short-answer scenario-based questions -- so revision of Paper 1's core
studies should include noting which research method(s) each one used.
**Section B** is a single planning question, where candidates plan a
study and evaluate some aspects of that plan.

## The Paper 2 planning question

For the planning question, some aspects of an investigation are given
and candidates plan the remaining aspects, then evaluate the plan.
Planning must include general features -- the procedure of the study,
how the plan makes the study valid, how it makes the study reliable,
and the types of data collected -- plus features specific to whichever
research method the question specifies (for example, for a field
experiment: independent variable, dependent variable, controls, choice
of experimental design and location; for an observational study:
overt/covert, participant/non-participant, naturalistic/controlled,
and the behavioural-category system used). Two things are explicitly
**not** required at Paper 2 level, and knowing this saves time under
exam conditions: candidates are not expected to include aims or
hypotheses in the plan, and are not expected to evaluate issues
relating to sample/generalisability or ethics of the plan. This is a
deliberate contrast with Paper 4's planning question at A Level, which
does require aims, hypotheses, sampling technique and ethics as part
of the plan -- so candidates should not import Paper 4's fuller
requirements into a Paper 2 answer.

## How to approach it

Learn the six methods and the block of methodological concepts as two
separate but connected layers: the methods are what a study *is*, the
concepts are what any study can be judged *by* (valid, reliable,
ethical, and so on). When revising Paper 1's core studies, always note
which method each one used, since Section A's scenario questions
frequently draw a link between a familiar study and the method that
produced it. For the planning question, work from the syllabus's own
checklist of general features before adding the method-specific
detail, and be precise about what Paper 2 does and does not require --
adding unnecessary aims, hypotheses or ethical evaluation costs time
without earning credit at this paper.
