These notes condense section 3.4 Education (3.4.1 to 3.4.4) of the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, Version 1.2 (June 2026), for GCSE exams 2019 onwards. Education is assessed in Paper 1: The sociology of families and education (1 hour 45 minutes, 100 marks, 50% of the GCSE). The specification asks you to describe the key ideas of Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Halsey, Ball and Willis, and to compare functionalist, Marxist, feminist and (for 3.4.4) interactionist views.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Social cohesion | People feeling bound together by shared norms and values |
| Social mobility | Movement up or down the class structure |
| Meritocracy | A system where rewards depend on ability and effort, not background |
| Achieved status | Status earned through your own effort, such as qualifications |
| Ascribed status | Status fixed at birth, such as a title or family position |
| Particularistic standards | Rules that apply to one person because of who they are, as in the family |
| Universalistic standards | The same rules for everyone, as in school and wider society |
| Value consensus | Broad agreement on society's main values |
| Hidden curriculum | What pupils learn from how school is organised, not from lessons |
| Correspondence principle | Bowles and Gintis's idea that school relationships mirror workplace relationships |
| Marketisation of education | Running schools like a market, with parental choice and league tables |
| Cultural capital | Middle-class knowledge, tastes and confidence that schools reward |
| Ethnocentric curriculum | A curriculum centred on one culture's history, language and values |
| Streaming | One ability group for all subjects |
| Setting | Ability groups subject by subject |
| Mixed ability teaching | Pupils of all abilities taught together |
| Labelling | Attaching a definition to a pupil, such as "bright" or "lazy" |
| Self-fulfilling prophecy | A label changes treatment and self-image until the prediction comes true |
| Counter school subculture | A pupil group whose values oppose the school's |
| De-schooling | Replacing formal schooling altogether (Illich) |
| Home schooling | Parents educating their child at home instead of at school |

## Writers at a glance

| Who | Perspective | Key idea | Finding or claim | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Durkheim (1925) | Functionalist | Education transmits norms and values | Welds individuals into a united whole; history links child to society; strict rules teach self-discipline | Marxists: the values taught may be ruling-class values |
| Parsons | Functionalist | School is a bridge between family and society | Shift from particularistic to universalistic standards; status achieved on merit; selection for adult roles | Equality of opportunity may be an illusion where wealth and privilege matter more |
| Bowles and Gintis (1976) | Marxist | Correspondence principle | School reproduces labour power: a docile, obedient, divided workforce; meritocracy is rejected | Willis: pupils resist, so socialisation is not that successful |
| Halsey, Heath and Ridge (1980) | Class inequality | Origins and destinations | Service-class boys 4x as likely to be at school at 16, 8x at 17, 10x at 18, 11x at university, compared with working-class boys | Sample of over 8,000 males only; excluding females might change the findings |
| Ball, Bowe and Gewirtz (1994; the specification prints "Gerwitz") | Parental choice | Market forces in education | Fifteen schools; choice and league tables pushed schools to reintroduce streaming and setting and focus on the more able | Their related 1995 book argued middle-class parents are better placed to use choice, so choice may widen inequality |
| Ball (1981) | Interactionist | Teacher expectations | Beachside: middle-class pupils more likely to be placed in band 1, with higher expectations; band 2 most likely to become anti-school; a second cohort taught in mixed ability classes | One school studied, so findings may not be representative |
| Willis (1977) | Marxist | Counter school culture | Twelve working-class "lads" rejected school, felt superior to "ear'oles", ended up in manual work | Small all-male sample; such jobs are now much scarcer |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Durkheim versus Parsons.** Durkheim stresses **social cohesion** through shared norms and values. Parsons stresses **meritocracy**, universalistic standards and **role selection**.
- **Bowles and Gintis versus Willis.** Both are Marxist. Bowles and Gintis see a **direct correspondence** and successful socialisation. Willis sees **resistance** and unintended consequences, though the outcome (manual work) still suits capitalism.
- **Ball's two studies.** *Beachside Comprehensive* (1981) is about **teacher expectations** inside one school (3.4.4). The 1994 study is about **parental choice and competition** between schools (3.4.3).
- **Streaming versus setting.** Streaming groups pupils for every subject; setting groups them subject by subject.
- **Labelling versus self-fulfilling prophecy.** Labelling is the act of defining a pupil. The self-fulfilling prophecy is the result when the pupil comes to match the label.
- **Home schooling versus de-schooling.** Home schooling moves education out of school. De-schooling rejects formal schooling as an institution.
- **State versus private schools.** State schools are funded by taxation and free to attend. Private (independent, fee-paying) schools are funded mainly by fees.
- **Internal versus external factors.** Setting, labelling and subcultures happen inside school. Material deprivation and cultural capital come from outside it.

## Perspectives in one line each

For the perspectives in general, see [The sociological approach](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-the-sociological-approach/).

- **Functionalist:** education serves the economy, allows social mobility and builds cohesion; differences in achievement reflect merit.
- **Marxist:** education reproduces class inequality and legitimises it with the myth of meritocracy.
- **Feminist:** education has reproduced gender roles through a gendered curriculum and hidden curriculum; girls' rising results have not removed inequality in subject choice or work.
- **Interactionist (3.4.4):** achievement is shaped by classroom interaction, labels and pupils' responses to them.

## Mini essay plan

**Question type:** "Discuss how far sociologists would agree that the main function of education is to foster social cohesion."

1. **Introduction (two sentences).** Define social cohesion. State that functionalists see cohesion as central, while Marxists and feminists ask whose values are shared.
2. **For: Durkheim.** Education transmits norms and values, welding individuals into a united whole. History lessons link the child to society; strict rules teach self-discipline. Apply: a school assembly or citizenship lesson.
3. **For, with a twist: Parsons.** Shared values (value consensus) matter, but Parsons also stresses meritocracy and selection, so cohesion is not the only function.
4. **Against: Marxist.** Bowles and Gintis argue the main role is reproducing labour power through the correspondence principle. "Cohesion" is acceptance of inequality.
5. **Against: Willis.** Not all pupils accept the school's values; the lads formed a counter culture. Shared values are not always transmitted.
6. **Against: feminist.** The values passed on have reflected a gendered curriculum, so cohesion may mean accepting patriarchy.
7. **Evidence of division.** Halsey's class gaps and Ball's findings on parental choice suggest schools also divide people by class.
8. **Judgement.** Weigh the views: education may promote some shared values, but the evidence of class and gender inequality suggests cohesion is not clearly its main function. Explain your reason.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three functions of education the specification lists.
2. What did Durkheim say the teaching of history does?
3. What is the difference between particularistic and universalistic standards?
4. State two features of the workforce that Bowles and Gintis say school produces.
5. How many times greater was the chance of a service-class boy attending university than a working-class boy, according to Halsey, Heath and Ridge?
6. What was one limitation of Halsey's sample that the specification highlights?
7. What two pressures did Ball and colleagues find parental choice and league tables put on schools?
8. What method did Ball use at Beachside, and for how long?
9. What did Willis's lads call conformist pupils?
10. Give one difference between home schooling and de-schooling.
11. Which perspective is added to the list for 3.4.4 that is not in 3.4.1 to 3.4.3?

### Answers

1. Serving the needs of the economy, facilitating social mobility and fostering social cohesion.
2. It links the individual to society, so children see they are part of something larger and develop commitment to the group.
3. Particularistic standards apply to a child as a particular person (in the family); universalistic standards apply the same rules to everyone (in school and society).
4. Any two: hardworking, docile, obedient, highly motivated, too divided to challenge management.
5. Eleven times.
6. It excluded females, which might have made a significant difference to the findings.
7. Pressure to reintroduce streaming and setting, and a tendency to focus on the more able.
8. Participant observation, over three years of fieldwork.
9. "Ear'oles".
10. Home schooling moves education to the home; de-schooling rejects formal schooling as an institution.
11. Interactionist.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a writer but giving another writer's idea, such as crediting Parsons with the correspondence principle.
- Describing Durkheim only as "socialisation" without the specific ideas: norms and values, history, cooperation with non-kin, strict rules.
- Quoting Halsey's figures without saying they compare service-class with working-class boys.
- Forgetting that the specification itself flags the all-male sample as Halsey's limitation.
- Writing about "Ball" without making clear which of his two studies you mean.
- Calling setting "streaming" or treating them as the same.
- Explaining a self-fulfilling prophecy as a single event rather than a process from label to treatment to self-image to outcome.
- In "discuss how far" answers, giving only one perspective or never reaching a judgement.
- Treating feminist views as one sentence on "girls do better" without explaining the gendered curriculum or subject choice.
