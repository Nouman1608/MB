These questions cover section 3.3.1 Production processes of AQA GCSE Business (8132). Revise first with the [Production processes study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-production-processes-aqa-gcse-business/) and the [Production processes revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-production-processes-aqa-gcse-business-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *flow production*. **[1]**

**2.** State **two** features of job production. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** types of waste that lean production aims to reduce. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why job production usually has a high cost per unit. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** disadvantage of flow production for a business whose customers' tastes change quickly. **[3]**

**6.** A workshop has 8 workers who produce 1,040 units a week.

**(a)** Calculate the productivity per worker. **[1]**
**(b)** After introducing continuous improvement, output rises to 1,144 units with the same workers. Calculate the percentage increase in productivity. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and case-study questions

**7.** Thistle Cakes makes celebration cakes designed for each customer, such as wedding and birthday cakes. Analyse why job production is appropriate for Thistle Cakes. **[6]**

**8.** Eastgate Snacks makes one type of crisp in large quantities for supermarkets. Analyse **one** benefit and **one** drawback of flow production for Eastgate Snacks. **[6]**

**9.** Explain how continuous improvement (kaizen) can make production more efficient. **[4]**

**10.** Corran Components makes parts for car manufacturers. It plans to introduce just in time (JIT) production.

**(a)** Explain **one** benefit of JIT for Corran Components. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **one** risk of JIT for Corran Components. **[3]**

**11.** Harwood Bikes makes 3,000 bicycles a year by hand at a unit cost of £420. It is considering a production line that would raise output to 12,000 bicycles a year at a unit cost of £260.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in unit cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate total production costs at each level of output. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** reason why Harwood Bikes should not move to flow production unless it is confident about demand. **[3]**

**12.** Lindale Leather makes handbags. Half its sales are standard designs sold through department stores, and half are personalised bags made to customers' orders. A consultant suggests it should switch entirely to flow production. Evaluate whether Lindale Leather should follow this advice. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The continuous production of large quantities of identical (standardised) products, usually along a production line [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Any two of: one-off products made to individual orders [1]; highly skilled workers [1]; general-purpose tools and equipment; production starts when an order is received. **[2]**

**3.** Any two of: overproduction [1]; waiting time [1]; unnecessary movement; defects; excess stock. **[2]**

**4.** Each product is different, so skilled workers spend a long time making it [1]. Their time is expensive and is spread over only one item [1], and materials are bought in small amounts, so no bulk-buying discounts are gained [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Flow production uses specialised machinery set up to make one standard product [1]. If customers' tastes change, the business has to stop the line and re-equip it [1], which is costly and slow, so it may be left with stock of products customers no longer want [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** 1,040 ÷ 8 = 130 units per worker per week [1].
**(b)** New productivity = 1,144 ÷ 8 = 143 [1]. Increase = (143 − 130) ÷ 130 × 100 = 10% [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Each customer wants a cake designed for their event, with their own colours, decoration and message [1], so every product is different and cannot be made in advance on a line [1]. Job production allows Thistle to produce exactly what each customer asks for [1], and because the cakes are personalised, customers are willing to pay a premium price [1], which covers the higher cost of skilled bakers' time [1]. Demand for celebration cakes is also irregular and linked to customers' events, so investing in production-line machinery would not be worthwhile [1]. **[6]**

**8.** **Benefit:** Eastgate makes one standard product in large quantities [1], so its machinery's fixed costs are spread over a huge output, giving a low unit cost [1]. This lets it offer the low prices supermarkets demand and still make a profit [1]. **Drawback:** the line relies on continuous operation [1], so if one machine breaks down the whole line may stop [1], meaning Eastgate cannot fulfil supermarket orders on time and could lose contracts [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Workers are encouraged to suggest small improvements to the tasks they do every day [1], because they know best where time or materials are wasted [1]. Each change might save only a little, but many changes made over time add up to large savings [1], so output per worker rises and costs fall, making production more efficient [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Corran holds very little stock of materials or finished parts [1], so it spends less on warehouse space and less money is tied up in stock [1], which improves its cash flow and lowers its costs [1].
**(b)** Corran depends on its suppliers delivering on time [1]. If a delivery is late, Corran has no spare stock to use [1], so production stops and it may fail to deliver parts to car manufacturers on time, damaging its reputation [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** £420 − £260 = £160 [1].
**(b)** By hand: 3,000 × £420 = £1,260,000 [1]. Production line: 12,000 × £260 = £3,120,000 [1].
**(c)** The unit cost of £260 is only reached if Harwood actually produces and sells 12,000 bicycles [1]. If demand is lower, the machinery's high fixed cost is spread over fewer bikes, so the unit cost rises [1], and Harwood may be left with unsold stock and a large investment that it struggles to pay for [1]. **[6]**

**12.** **For flow production:** half of Lindale's sales are standard designs [1], which could be made more cheaply on a production line, giving a lower unit cost [1] and allowing Lindale to offer department stores more competitive prices or earn a higher margin [1]. **Against:** the other half of sales are personalised bags made to order [1], which cannot be standardised, so switching entirely to flow production would mean losing these customers [1]. Personalised bags probably sell at a premium price [1], so Lindale could lose its most profitable work. Flow production also requires expensive machinery [1]. **Judgement:** Lindale should not switch entirely [1]. It should consider using flow production for its standard designs only, if demand from department stores is high and steady enough to justify the investment, while keeping job production for personalised orders [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing production methods with stock management methods.
- Saying flow production is always better without considering demand and flexibility.
- Calculating a percentage change using the new figure as the base.
- Describing lean production only as JIT.
- Forgetting the effect of production choices on staff motivation.
- Recommending one method without considering a mixed approach when the case supports it.
