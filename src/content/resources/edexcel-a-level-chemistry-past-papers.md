This page is a guide to Pearson Edexcel International A Level Chemistry (YCH11) past papers. It covers one series so far, June 2025, and for that series it gives a short account of what the examiners saw in Units 4, 5 and 6, written by us from Pearson's examiner reports. Pearson's grade boundaries for the series are not reproduced here because we could not retrieve them from a Pearson document, so no threshold is given. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report is on Pearson's own site, linked below.

## Where to get the official papers

- [Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Chemistry (2018) qualification page](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/qualifications/edexcel-international-advanced-levels/chemistry-2018.html), which links to the specification
- [Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Chemistry (2018) course materials page](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/qualifications/edexcel-international-advanced-levels/chemistry-2018.coursematerials.html), where question papers, mark schemes and examiner reports are listed
- [Pearson grade boundaries page](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/support/support-topics/results-certification/grade-boundaries.html), which is where Pearson publishes boundaries for all of its qualifications

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which shows what each mark needs. Then read the examiner report for that paper: it explains why answers that looked reasonable still lost marks. The notes for the series below are a quick way into those reports.

Check which specification a paper was set on. The qualification is a modular one with six externally assessed units: Units 1 to 3 make up the International AS and Units 4 to 6 the A2 part. The papers are coded WCH11 to WCH16, each with the suffix /01. Unit 1 (WCH11) and Unit 2 (WCH12) are 90-minute papers of 80 marks, Unit 4 (WCH14) and Unit 5 (WCH15) are 105-minute papers of 90 marks, and the practical-skills Units 3 (WCH13) and 6 (WCH16) are 80-minute papers of 50 marks. The specification (Issue 1, September 2017) was first examined in January 2019, so papers from 2019 onwards share this structure. Papers set on the earlier International A Level specification have different codes and content, so check the front cover before relying on a question.

## June 2025

The June 2025 papers tested equilibria and acid-base chemistry, organic and spectroscopic analysis, entropy and lattice-type energetics, electrochemistry, transition metal complexes, arenes and organic nitrogen chemistry, and written practical skills. Across all three reports the same message came through: factual recall and routine calculations were handled well, while questions in unfamiliar contexts and questions needing a complete, specific explanation separated the strong scripts from the weak ones. Better scripts followed every instruction in the question, showed working and kept a sensible number of significant figures. Weaker scripts restated the question, gave answers that were true but incomplete, or lost marks on charges, state symbols and units.

**Where marks were lost**

- WCH14 (Unit 4), equilibrium and acid-base: explanations of a shift in equilibrium position that stated what would be seen but not why; Kc values given to too many significant figures; answers to "explain" questions that simply restated the data. Stronger scripts linked the effect of a substituent to the stability of the anion. Weaker ones compared acid strengths without a reason.
- WCH14, pH and indicators: buffer calculations that confused moles with concentrations, and bubble-bath colour changes explained by neutralisation instead of by dilution and the balance of the two coloured forms.
- WCH14, spectroscopy and organic analysis: proton NMR splitting patterns were the hardest part of the six-mark question, and fragment ions in mass spectra were given without a formula and charge. Ester names and carboxylic acid reactions were generally better known.
- WCH14, kinetics and energetics: a catalyst removed from a sample was often not recognised as the reason a reaction stopped. Orders of reaction were deduced, but fewer candidates linked the order to the number of molecules in the slow step. In entropy and enthalpy calculations, unit conversions between joules and kilojoules and an impossible final temperature were common slips. Definitions of an enthalpy change were incomplete.
- WCH15 (Unit 5), electrochemistry: cell diagrams missed state symbols for an inert electrode or put the species in the wrong order, and descriptions of what happens in a cell said that quantities "change" without saying whether they rise or fall.
- WCH15, transition metals: the shape of a square planar complex was well known, but drawings of a trans isomer lost marks through unclear dative bonds or mixed wedge and dashed bonds. Ligand-exchange explanations and the formula of a copper ammine ion were weak spots.
- WCH15, arenes and spectroscopy: the nature of the delocalised ring bonding was poorly explained, candidates mixed up physical and chemical properties of benzene, and gas chromatography, including what is plotted on the horizontal axis and its link to mass spectrometry, was often not known. Infrared answers lost marks when wavenumbers were quoted without naming the bond responsible.
- WCH15, organic synthesis: reagents and conditions for diazotisation and for oxidising an alkene were misremembered, mechanism arrows and intermediates were drawn loosely, and names of aromatic acids were misspelt.
- WCH16 (Unit 6), qualitative analysis and equations: unfamiliar tests and the colours of transition metal ions caused trouble, and many ionic equations were unbalanced or kept spectator ions. Candidates who gave several answers risked cancelling a correct one.
- WCH16, titration and rates practical: indicator choice and end-point colours, how a clock reaction works, and why total volume is kept constant were weak. Calculations and table completion were strong, though precision was sometimes inconsistent and units were careless.
- WCH16, organic preparation: reasons for individual steps (washing, recrystallisation, drying) were vague, hazards were given generally rather than for a named substance, and few candidates used the melting-point data supplied. Diagrams of melting-point apparatus were better than expected.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find Edexcel International A Level Chemistry past papers?**
Pearson lists question papers, mark schemes and examiner reports on the course materials page for the qualification. Grade boundaries are on Pearson's separate grade boundaries page.

**Which papers make up the qualification?**
There are six units, WCH11 to WCH16. Units 1 to 3 form the International AS and Units 4 to 6 the A2 part. Units 3 and 6 are written practical-skills papers.

**Does this page give the grade thresholds?**
Not for June 2025. We could not retrieve the boundaries from a Pearson document, so we left them out rather than estimate. Use Pearson's grade boundaries page for the official figures.

**What do the June 2025 reports say candidates should practise?**
Across Units 4 to 6 the reports ask for careful reading of instructions, working shown with sensible significant figures, accurate equations and cell diagrams, and detailed reasons for practical steps.

**Can thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Boundaries change every series and should never be used to predict future ones.
