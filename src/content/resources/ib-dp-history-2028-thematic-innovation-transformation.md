The thematic study **Innovation and transformation (from 750 CE onwards)** is one of four options in IB DP History (first assessment 2028). A class studies one thematic study, and it is assessed in paper 2. The guide sets four inquiry questions, each with four lines of inquiry, and **prescribes no examples**. You and your teacher choose examples, and each line of inquiry must be explored with at least two examples from more than one region. This guide explains the framework, offers a bank of illustrative examples, and shows how to build paper 2 answers. It is the same for SL and HL.

## What the guide means by innovation and transformation

The guide defines an **innovation** as the introduction of something new in a specific context, such as an original idea, method or technology. For an innovation to be **transformative**, it should bring about a major change to the form or function of aspects of a society. The guide lists technological innovations (industrial revolutions, mass production and capitalism, the digital revolution), intellectual innovations (the Scientific Revolution, the Golden Age of Islam, the Enlightenment) and innovations in culture, society, religion and the arts. Note the wording "in a specific context": something may be new in one society and old in another.

## How the study is assessed

Paper 2 lasts 1 hour 45 minutes and carries 25 marks. Section A (6 marks) asks you to analyse a specified concept using an example from your thematic study. Section B has two questions on each thematic study; you answer one, with part (a), a short response (4 marks), and part (b), an essay (15 marks) using examples from at least two regions. The four regions are Africa and the Middle East, the Americas, Asia and Oceania, and Europe. The essay's top markband needs consistent analysis, a reasoned judgement and well-developed connections between examples.

## Inquiry question 1: Why did new innovations emerge?

**Lines of inquiry:** social factors, economic factors, political factors, environmental factors.

- **Social.** In the Abbasid Caliphate, the culture of translation and scholarship in Baghdad, supported by caliphs such as al-Ma'mun, produced new knowledge in mathematics, medicine and astronomy. Print in Europe was encouraged by growing literacy among merchants and clergy.
- **Economic.** Britain had expanding trade, available capital, coal and growing markets for cotton goods, which made new machines profitable. Song China's commercial economy created demand for paper money.
- **Political.** Governments can promote innovation. The Meiji government built railways and model factories to catch up with Western powers. The Abbasid state patronised scholars and used paper in its administration.
- **Environmental.** In the Green Revolution, population growth and the fear of famine in South Asia led to new high-yield wheat and rice varieties. Coal's availability in Britain mattered for steam power.

## Inquiry question 2: How did the innovations transform societies?

**Lines of inquiry:** economic change, political change, environmental change, cultural change.

- **Economic.** British factory production changed where and how people worked, and shifted wealth towards industrial towns. The Green Revolution raised yields, while it also increased dependence on fertiliser and irrigation.
- **Political.** Print allowed the faster spread of religious and political ideas, as in the Reformation from 1517. Industrialisation created new classes and eventually pressures for political reform.
- **Environmental.** Coal-burning industry polluted cities and, over time, contributed to climate change. Intensive irrigation in the Green Revolution lowered water tables in some regions.
- **Cultural.** Paper and the copying of books supported libraries and learning in the Islamic world; printing standardised languages and spread literacy in Europe.

## Inquiry question 3: How were the innovations resisted?

**Lines of inquiry:** resistance from established authorities, forms of popular resistance, resilience of traditional ideas, competing innovations.

- **Established authorities.** Religious and political authorities sometimes sought to control print, for example through licensing and censorship, and by placing books on a list of forbidden works. The Ottoman case is often cited for resisting Arabic-script printing until the eighteenth century; historians debate how strong and how general the ban was, so state this carefully.
- **Popular resistance.** The Luddites, between 1811 and 1816, destroyed machines in the textile districts of England to protect wages and conditions. The government responded with troops and made machine-breaking a capital crime.
- **Resilience of traditional ideas.** Many communities kept customs, skills and beliefs despite new methods, and some critics of industrialisation praised rural life and craft skills.
- **Competing innovations.** Innovations may compete with each other: for example, canals and then railways, or traditional farming methods and new seeds.

## Inquiry question 4: How did the innovations affect people's lives?

**Lines of inquiry:** experiences of innovators, experiences of social elites, experiences of women, experiences of marginalised groups.

- **Innovators.** Some, such as James Watt and Matthew Boulton, profited, while others struggled with debt or lack of recognition. In the Abbasid translation movement, scholars of different backgrounds worked together.
- **Social elites.** Landowners and merchants often adapted and gained, though some old elites lost status. In Meiji Japan, the samurai class lost its privileges.
- **Women.** Women formed a large share of the workers in early cotton mills, such as at Lowell in Massachusetts, and worked long hours for low pay. In the Green Revolution, women often did much of the labour but received less of the benefit.
- **Marginalised groups.** The cotton industry in Britain depended on cotton grown by enslaved people in the Americas, which links innovation to exploitation. Child workers in factories were a major concern of reformers.

## Making connections between examples

- **Pattern:** innovations often arise where capital, demand and patronage coincide.
- **Difference:** state-led innovation (Meiji Japan) compared with market-led (Britain).
- **Exception:** an innovation that spread more slowly or was resisted, such as Arabic-script printing in the Ottoman world, compared with the quick adoption of print in Europe.
- **Same theme in different places:** how women's experience of work varied by context.

## Worked example: a paragraph for part (b)

Question: "To what extent were innovations resisted successfully?"

"Resistance rarely stopped innovation, but it could shape how innovation developed. The Luddites of 1811 to 1816 attacked machines in the English textile districts, but the government sent troops and made machine-breaking a capital crime, so the machines spread. In the Ottoman world, the authorities restricted printing in Arabic script for a long period, though historians disagree about how strict and how long the restrictions were, and the first Ottoman Muslim press, run by Ibrahim Muteferrika, was approved in the 1720s. In both cases, resistance slowed or redirected change rather than preventing it, and the contrast between a violent popular protest and an elite policy shows that resistance took different forms."

The paragraph connects Europe and the Middle East, compares the forms of resistance and is careful about a disputed claim.

## Writing part (a)

Part (a) is a 4-mark short response on one example. Choose the example you know best for the line of inquiry named, identify it, give two or three accurate details and explain how they answer the question. For instance, to explain how an innovation affected women: in early cotton mills such as those in Lowell, young women made up much of the workforce, worked long hours for wages lower than men's, and lived in company boarding houses, which gave them earnings and independence but also strict supervision.

## Common mistakes

- Describing an invention without saying how it transformed society.
- Treating innovation as having only positive effects.
- Using two European examples in the essay.
- Overstating claims about the Ottoman ban on printing.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use a contemporary example such as the internet?** Yes, but treat it historically; the course defines contemporary as roughly the past two decades, and consequences cannot yet be judged historically.

**Is an innovation always technology?** No. The guide includes intellectual, cultural, social and religious innovations.
