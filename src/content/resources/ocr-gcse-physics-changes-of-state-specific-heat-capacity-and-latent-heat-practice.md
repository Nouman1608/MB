## Questions

**1.** A strong sealed flask contains iodine crystals. The flask and contents have a mass of 215.4 g. The flask is warmed gently and the crystals change directly into a purple gas, with no liquid forming.

**(a)** Name this change of state. **[1]**
**(b)** State the mass of the flask and contents after the change and explain your answer. **[2]**

**2.** Classify each change as physical or chemical and give a reason for each.

**(a)** Molten candle wax sets hard again. **[1]**
**(b)** The wax of a lit candle burns. **[1]**
**(c)** An iron nail left in damp air becomes covered in rust. **[1]**
**(d)** Solid carbon dioxide changes into carbon dioxide gas. **[1]**

**3.** A solid is heated steadily until it melts.

**(a)** Describe what happens to the energy supplied while the solid is warming below its melting point. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe what happens to the energy supplied while the solid is melting, and state what happens to its temperature. **[2]**

**4.**

**(a)** Define specific heat capacity. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how specific latent heat of vaporisation differs from specific heat capacity. **[2]**

**5.** A sandbox contains 4.5 kg of dry sand. The sun warms the sand from 12 °C to 37 °C. The specific heat capacity of sand is 830 J/kg °C. Calculate the energy transferred to the sand. **[3]**

**6.** A student heats a 0.45 kg metal block with an electrical heater. A joulemeter shows 16 200 J has been supplied. The block warms from 20.0 °C to 44.0 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal. **[3]**
**(b)** The accepted value for this metal is lower than the student's result. Suggest a cause and explain how it affects the result. **[2]**

**7.** A tank holds 1.6 kg of oil at 22 °C. The oil absorbs 96 000 J. Its specific heat capacity is 2000 J/kg °C. Calculate the final temperature. **[3]**

**8.** For water, the specific latent heat of vaporisation is taken as 2 260 000 J/kg.

**(a)** Calculate the energy needed to turn 0.28 kg of water at 100 °C into steam at 100 °C. **[2]**
**(b)** It takes 148 500 J to melt 0.45 kg of a metal that is already at its melting point. Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion of the metal. **[2]**

**9.** A 0.20 kg sample of a solid is heated and the energy supplied is measured with a joulemeter.

| Energy supplied (kJ) | 0 | 6 | 12 | 32 | 52 | 58 | 64 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 25 | 40 | 55 | 55 | 55 | 67.5 | 80 |

**(a)** State the melting point of the substance. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the temperature stays constant between 12 kJ and 52 kJ although energy is still being supplied. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the solid. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion. **[2]**
**(e)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of the liquid. **[1]**

**10.** A sample of steam has a mass of 40 g at 100 °C. It condenses to water at 100 °C and the water then cools to 60 °C. Calculate the total energy released. For water, use L = 2 260 000 J/kg (vaporisation) and c = 4200 J/kg °C. **[5]**

**11.** A 250 g piece of ice at −12 °C is heated in an insulated container until it has all become water at 30 °C. The specific heat capacity of ice is 2100 J/kg °C, the specific latent heat of fusion of ice is 334 000 J/kg and water has c = 4200 J/kg °C. Calculate the total energy transferred to the sample. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[8]**

**12.** Two identical beakers each hold 1.5 kg of liquid at the same starting temperature. Each is given 63 000 J. One liquid is water (c = 4200 J/kg °C) and the other is oil (c = 2100 J/kg °C).

**(a)** Calculate the temperature rise of the water. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the temperature rise of the oil. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain, using specific heat capacity, why the two rises differ. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Sublimation [1]
**(b)** 215.4 g [1]. The gas is still inside the sealed flask, so no particles have left the system and mass is conserved [1]
*Examiner insight:* "The mass is the same" earns little without a reason; link it to the particles still being in the sealed container.

**2. (a)** Physical: the wax is recovered with the same properties [1]
**(b)** Chemical: new substances (carbon dioxide and water vapour) are made and the wax cannot be recovered by reversing it [1]
**(c)** Chemical: rust is a new substance, not iron [1]
**(d)** Physical: the same substance changes state and can be condensed or frozen back with its original properties [1] 
*Examiner insight:* A bare "physical" or "chemical" is not enough when a reason is requested. Use the test from the specification: are the original properties recovered if it is reversed?

**3. (a)** The particles gain kinetic energy [1], so the temperature rises [1]
**(b)** The energy works against the attraction between particles, so their potential energy rises [1]; the temperature stays constant [1] 
*Examiner insight:* Say what the energy is doing in each case. "It melts" does not explain where the energy goes.

**4. (a)** The energy that must be transferred to 1 kg of a substance to warm it by 1 °C [1]
**(b)** Specific latent heat of vaporisation is the energy per kilogram to change liquid to gas [1] with no change in temperature, whereas specific heat capacity involves a temperature change [1] 
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer states the three quantities in each definition: energy, 1 kg, and what happens to temperature. Leaving out "1 kg" or "per kilogram" is a common loss.

**5.** Δθ = 37 − 12 = 25 °C [1]; E = 4.5 × 830 × 25 [1]; E = 93 375 J ≈ **93 400 J** [1] 
*Examiner insight:* Write the substitution before the answer so that your method is visible, and give the unit.

**6. (a)** Δθ = 44.0 − 20.0 = 24.0 °C [1]; c = E / (mΔθ) = 16 200 / (0.45 × 24.0) [1]; c = **1500 J/kg °C** [1]
**(b)** Energy escapes from the block to the air around it (poor lagging) [1], so the block warms less than expected for the energy measured and c is calculated too high [1] 
*Examiner insight:* When asked how an error affects the result, give the direction (too high or too low) with a reason, not just the source of the error.

**7.** Δθ = E / (m c) = 96 000 / (1.6 × 2000) [1]; Δθ = 30 °C [1]; final temperature = 22 + 30 = **52 °C** [1] 
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is giving the temperature rise (30 °C) as the final temperature. Read the last line of the question.

**8. (a)** E = 0.28 × 2 260 000 [1]; **632 800 J** (about 633 000 J) [1]
**(b)** L = E / m = 148 500 / 0.45 [1]; **330 000 J/kg** [1] 
*Examiner insight:* No temperature change appears in E = mL. Using a temperature change here is a sign the wrong equation was chosen.

**9. (a)** 55 °C [1]
**(b)** The supplied energy separates the particles, raising their potential energy, instead of raising their average kinetic energy [1]; the solid is melting, so the temperature is constant until it has all melted [1]
**(c)** Solid warms from 25 to 55 °C with 12 kJ: Δθ = 30 °C [1]; c = 12 000 / (0.20 × 30) = **2000 J/kg °C** [1]
**(d)** Energy for melting = 52 − 12 = 40 kJ = 40 000 J [1]; L = 40 000 / 0.20 = **200 000 J/kg** [1]
**(e)** Liquid: 12 kJ for 25 °C rise: c = 12 000 / (0.20 × 25) = **2400 J/kg °C** [1] 
*Examiner insight:* In table questions, take differences between readings. Using the total energy from zero for each stage is a typical error.

**10.** m = 40 g = 0.040 kg [1]; condensing: E₁ = 0.040 × 2 260 000 = 90 400 J [1]; cooling: Δθ = 100 − 60 = 40 °C [1]; E₂ = 0.040 × 4200 × 40 = 6720 J [1]; total = 90 400 + 6720 = 97 120 J ≈ **97 100 J** [1] 
*Examiner insight:* Compare the size of the two stages as a check: the state change dominates. Forgetting the unit conversion changes the answer by a factor of 1000.

**11.** m = 250 g = 0.250 kg [1]; ice warms: Δθ = 12 °C, E₁ = 0.250 × 2100 × 12 = 6300 J [2]; melting: E₂ = 0.250 × 334 000 = 83 500 J [2]; water warms: E₃ = 0.250 × 4200 × 30 = 31 500 J [2]; total = 6300 + 83 500 + 31 500 = 121 300 J = **121 000 J** (3 s.f.) [1] 
*Examiner insight:* Three labelled stages, each on its own line with units, make the working easy to follow. Missing the ice-warming stage is the usual loss of marks.

**12. (a)** Δθ = E / (m c) = 63 000 / (1.5 × 4200) [1]; **10 °C** [1]
**(b)** 63 000 / (1.5 × 2100) = **20 °C** [1]
**(c)** Oil has the lower specific heat capacity, so less energy is needed per kilogram for each degree and the temperature rises further [1] 
*Examiner insight:* Link the answer back to the definition of c, not just the numbers.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving mass in grams in E = mcΔθ or E = mL.
- Using E = mcΔθ across a flat section of a graph.
- Using the wrong latent heat for melting or boiling.
- Missing a stage in a multi-stage problem.
- Giving temperature change when the final temperature was asked for.
- Saying energy "stops" being supplied at a flat section of a heating graph.
- Not explaining a conservation of mass answer in terms of the particles remaining in the system.
- Omitting the unit, or rounding too early in a multi-step calculation.
