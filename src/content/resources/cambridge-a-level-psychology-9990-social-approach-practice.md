**This practice set covers the social approach in Cambridge International AS & A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A has short-answer questions on the assumptions and the three core studies. Section B has longer evaluation and comparison questions. Mark-scheme answers follow.

Related: [Social approach study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-social-approach/) · [Social approach revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-social-approach-revision-notes/)

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Outline the assumption of the social approach about the presence of others, using **one** core study as an example. **[2]**

**2.** Describe how Milgram made sure that every participant was the "teacher". **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why Milgram used standard prods. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the difference between the dispositional and situational hypotheses for obedience. **[2]**

**5.** Perry et al. used a double-blind procedure. Explain what this means and why it was used. **[3]**

**6.** Describe the results of Perry et al. for participants high in empathy and participants low in empathy. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Identify **two** independent variables in the study by Piliavin et al. **[2]**

**7. (b)** Identify **one** way the observers in Piliavin et al. measured helping. **[1]**

**8.** Explain why the findings of Piliavin et al. did not support diffusion of responsibility. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**9.** Evaluate the study by Milgram in terms of ethics, including the use of deception. **[10]**

**10.** Compare the studies by Milgram and Piliavin et al. in terms of their location and the type of data collected. **[4]**

**11.** Using the arousal: cost–reward model, explain why the cane victim was helped more often than the drunk victim. **[4]**

**12.** Suggest **one** way the study by Perry et al. could be changed to increase its ecological validity, and explain why this would help. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 2 marks. Behaviour, cognitions and emotions are influenced by the actual, implied or imagined presence of others (1). For example, in Piliavin et al. the actual presence of other passengers could have affected whether someone helped (1). Credit Milgram (presence of the experimenter) or Perry et al. (imagined approaching figure).

**2.** Up to 2 marks. The participant and the confederate drew slips of paper to decide roles (1), but the draw was rigged so that both slips said "teacher" and the confederate claimed to have drawn "learner" (1).

**3.** Up to 2 marks. To standardise the experimenter's behaviour (1), so that differences in how far participants went were due to the participants and not to some receiving more pressure than others, making the study replicable (1).

**4.** Up to 2 marks. The dispositional hypothesis says obedience is caused by something about the person, such as an obedient personality (1). The situational hypothesis says obedience is caused by features of the setting, such as the presence of an authority figure (1).

**5.** Up to 3 marks. Neither the participants nor the researchers testing them knew whether the spray contained oxytocin or the placebo (1). This stops participants' expectations from affecting their distance choices (1) and stops researchers giving unconscious cues or interpreting responses differently (1).

**6.** Up to 2 marks. Under oxytocin, participants high in empathy preferred to be closer to another person than under placebo (1). Participants low in empathy preferred to be further away under oxytocin (1).

**7. (a)** 1 mark each for any two: type of victim (cane or drunk); race of victim (white or black); whether a model helped and when; size of the group of passengers (a naturally varying variable).

**7. (b)** 1 mark for any one: how long it took for the first person to help; the number of people who helped; whether help was given spontaneously before the model acted; the race and sex of helpers.

**8.** Up to 2 marks. Diffusion of responsibility predicts that larger groups help less (1), but in Piliavin et al. helping did not fall as the number of passengers rose; the cane victim was helped on almost every trial, perhaps because the victim was clearly visible and passengers could not leave (1).

**9.** Level-based: 9–10 marks for a developed, balanced discussion with several guidelines applied to specific features of the study and a judgement. Indicative content:

- *Deception*: participants were deceived about the purpose (told it was about punishment and learning), the rigged draw and the reality of the shocks; but deception was needed because knowing the real aim would have destroyed the measure of obedience.
- *Harm*: many participants showed extreme tension and three had seizures, which breaks the guideline on minimising harm; Milgram argued the distress was short-lived and checked participants later.
- *Right to withdraw*: participants were told the money was theirs whatever happened, but the prods ("You have no other choice, you must go on") made leaving feel impossible.
- *Debriefing*: participants met the unharmed learner and were reassured; a later questionnaire found most were glad to have taken part, which partly justifies the procedure.
- *Benefit*: the findings increased understanding of destructive obedience, which can be argued to outweigh the costs.
- *Conclusion*: the study breaks several guidelines that would make it hard to repeat today, though the debrief and the importance of the findings provide some justification.

**10.** Up to 4 marks: 2 per comparison. Location: Milgram's study took place in a laboratory at Yale University (1), whereas Piliavin et al. carried out a field experiment on a real subway train, so their setting had higher ecological validity (1). Data: both collected quantitative data, Milgram the maximum voltage and Piliavin et al. the frequency and speed of help (1); both also recorded qualitative data, such as participants' signs of tension in Milgram and passengers' comments in Piliavin et al. (1).

**11.** Up to 4 marks. Seeing someone collapse causes unpleasant arousal that bystanders want to reduce (1). They choose the response with the lowest net cost (1). Helping the cane victim costs little and not helping would bring guilt or blame, so helping is the cheapest response (1). Helping the drunk victim has higher costs, such as disgust or possible harm, and not helping carries less blame because he seems responsible for his state, so fewer people helped (1).

**12.** Up to 3 marks. For example: measure how close participants actually stand to a real confederate in a waiting room after the nasal spray (1), instead of using a computerised scale or pictures (1). This is a behavioural measure in a more natural situation, so the findings are more likely to show how oxytocin affects real personal space (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Which numbers from Milgram must I learn?**
40 participants, the range 15 to 450 volts, 26 (65%) to 450 volts and all to 300 volts are the most useful.

**Should I describe Milgram's later variations?**
No. The syllabus specifies the 1963 study, in which the teacher did not hear voice feedback from the learner.
