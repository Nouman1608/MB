Condensed notes for **section 5.3 Monetary policy** (AS Level, Cambridge 9708, 2026–2028). For full explanations and worked examples, use the [monetary policy study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-monetary-policy-as-cambridge-alevel-economics/). Figures in the examples are invented.

## The four syllabus statements

| Statement | What to recall |
|---|---|
| 5.3.1 Definition | Central bank or government changes interest rates, the money supply or credit rules to influence AD and meet macroeconomic objectives |
| 5.3.2 Tools | Interest rates, money supply, credit regulations |
| 5.3.3 Stance | Expansionary shifts AD right; contractionary shifts AD left |
| 5.3.4 AD/AS | Effect on equilibrium national income, real output, price level and employment depends on spare capacity |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Policy (base) interest rate | The rate the central bank sets, which guides commercial banks' lending and deposit rates |
| Money supply | The total quantity of money in the economy |
| Bond purchases | The central bank buys government bonds, paying with new money, so bank reserves and lending rise |
| Quantitative easing | Large-scale bond purchases, used when interest rates are already very low |
| Credit regulation | A rule on the quantity or terms of lending, such as a loan-to-value or loan-to-income limit |
| Reserve requirement | The share of deposits a bank must hold rather than lend |
| Appreciation | A rise in the value of the currency against others |
| Time lag | The delay between a policy change and its full effect on spending |

## Transmission routes (rise in rates)

```
Cost of borrowing up   -> less credit spending      -> C falls
Reward for saving up   -> more saving               -> C falls
Fewer projects pay     -> less investment           -> I falls
Money flows in         -> currency appreciates      -> X falls, M rises
                                         all four -> AD shifts left
```

A cut in rates reverses every arrow.

## Money supply and interest rates

- More money supply, given demand for money: lower interest rate.
- Less money supply: higher interest rate.
- The central bank chooses one; the other follows. It cannot set both independently.

## Credit rules: quick calculations

- Maximum loan = income × loan-to-income cap. Income 30,000 with cap 5 gives 150,000; cap 4 gives 120,000.
- Lendable funds = deposits × (1 − reserve requirement). Deposits 200 million at 5% gives 190 million; at 8% it gives 184 million.

## Stance: when to use which

| Situation | Stance | Main tools |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation above target, AD outgrowing capacity | Contractionary | Raise rates, sell bonds, tighten credit |
| Weak growth, rising unemployment, low inflation | Expansionary | Cut rates, buy bonds, relax credit |
| House prices and mortgage debt rising fast, rest of economy weak | Targeted credit rule | Tighter loan-to-value or loan-to-income limits |

Judge stance by the **direction of change**, not the level. A cut from 8% to 7% is expansionary.

## AD/AS effects by section of AS

| Section of AS | Expansionary policy | Contractionary policy |
|---|---|---|
| Highly elastic (recession) | Output and jobs up, prices barely change | Output and jobs fall, prices barely change |
| Upward sloping | Output, jobs and prices all up | Output, jobs and prices (or inflation) all down |
| Vertical (full capacity) | Prices up, output unchanged in the long run | Inflationary pressure falls with little loss of capacity |

## Why monetary policy may not work as planned

- **Time lags:** fixed-rate loans and long-term plans respond slowly.
- **Confidence:** in a slump, cheap credit may not persuade firms to invest or banks to lend.
- **Household debt:** high debt makes rate rises powerful but rate cuts weak if households want to repay.
- **Cause of inflation:** rate rises tackle demand-pull inflation, not cost-push.
- **Exchange-rate side effects:** a rate rise can harm exporters through appreciation.
- **Distribution:** rate cuts help borrowers and hurt savers.

## Exam traps

- Saying lower rates encourage saving.
- Shifting AS instead of AD.
- Defining monetary policy with one tool only.
- Getting the exchange-rate direction wrong: higher rates usually mean appreciation.
- Ending the chain at "people spend more" without reaching AD and then the variable in the question.
- Assuming expansionary policy always raises output, even at full capacity.

## Self-test

1. Define monetary policy.
2. Name the three tools listed in the syllabus.
3. Explain how a rise in interest rates affects net exports.
4. Does a central bank buying government bonds expand or contract the money supply?
5. Why can a central bank not set both the money supply and the interest rate independently?
6. A household earns 50,000 and the loan-to-income cap falls from 4.5 to 4. By how much does its maximum loan fall?
7. A bank holds deposits of 300 million. The reserve requirement rises from 6% to 9%. How much less can it lend?
8. The central bank cuts its rate from 6% to 5.5%. Is this expansionary or contractionary?
9. What happens to the price level and real output when AD shifts right in the highly elastic section of AS?
10. Give one reason why a rate cut may fail to raise investment.
11. Why is a rate rise less suited to cost-push inflation?
12. Name one group that loses when interest rates are cut.
