## The big idea: bonding changes across the period

Every trend here comes from one change. Sodium and magnesium have low electronegativity, so their
oxides and chlorides are **ionic**. Silicon, phosphorus and sulfur are closer in electronegativity to
oxygen and chlorine, so their compounds are **covalent**. Aluminium sits at the crossover: its small,
highly charged ion distorts nearby anions, giving strong covalent character.

## Sodium and magnesium with water

Sodium reacts fast with cold water: it floats, melts into a sphere and fizzes, leaving a colourless
alkaline solution.

`2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)`

By contrast, magnesium barely reacts with cold water; a few hydrogen bubbles appear slowly on the surface. The
magnesium hydroxide formed dissolves only slightly, so the solution is weakly alkaline.

`Mg(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Mg(OH)₂(s) + H₂(g)`

Heated magnesium burns in steam with a brilliant white light, forming the oxide.

`Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g)`

Sodium is the more reactive element because each atom loses one electron to form Na⁺. Each magnesium atom
must lose two electrons, and removing the second one from an already positive ion costs extra energy.

## Reactions of the elements with oxygen

| Element | What you see | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Na | orange flame, white solid | `4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O` |
| Mg | intense white flame, white solid | `2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO` |
| Al | powder burns with white sparks | `4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃` |
| Si | burns only on strong heating | `Si + O₂ → SiO₂` |
| P | white phosphorus catches fire, white solid forms | `P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀` |
| S | pale blue flame, choking gas | `S + O₂ → SO₂` |

Burning sulfur gives SO₂. Making SO₃ needs heat and a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst: `2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃`.
The oxidation state in the highest oxide climbs from +1 (Na₂O) to +6 (SO₃), matching the number of
outer-shell electrons.

## Reactions of the elements with chlorine

| Element | What you see | Equation | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na | bright orange flame | `2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl` | white ionic solid |
| Mg | intense white flame | `Mg + Cl₂ → MgCl₂` | white ionic solid |
| Al | heated metal burns in a stream of dry chlorine | `2Al + 3Cl₂ → Al₂Cl₆` | very pale yellow solid that sublimes |
| Si | reacts on heating | `Si + 2Cl₂ → SiCl₄` | colourless liquid |
| P | catches fire in chlorine; excess chlorine gives the pentachloride | `P₄ + 10Cl₂ → 4PCl₅` | off-white solid |

The specification limits the chlorides to these five, so no sulfur chloride is required. The oxidation
state rises from +1 in NaCl to +5 in PCl₅.

### Why aluminium chloride is Al₂Cl₆

In an AlCl₃ unit the aluminium atom has only six electrons in its outer shell. A chlorine atom on a
second AlCl₃ unit donates a lone pair into this electron-deficient aluminium, forming a **co-ordinate
bond**. Two such bonds join the units into an Al₂Cl₆ molecule with two bridging chlorine atoms, and each
aluminium is then surrounded by four bonding pairs.

```
  Cl        Cl        Cl
     \    /    ↘    /
      Al          Al
     /    ↖    /    \
  Cl        Cl        Cl
(arrows: co-ordinate bonds from a bridging Cl to Al)
```

### Worked example 1

**Calculate the mass of Al₂Cl₆ formed when 0.540 g of aluminium reacts completely with chlorine.
(Ar: Al = 27.0, Cl = 35.5)**

```
2Al + 3Cl₂ → Al₂Cl₆
n(Al)     = 0.540 / 27.0 = 0.0200 mol
n(Al₂Cl₆) = 0.0200 / 2   = 0.0100 mol
Mr(Al₂Cl₆) = 2(27.0) + 6(35.5) = 267.0
mass       = 0.0100 × 267.0 = 2.67 g
```

## Melting points of the oxides and chlorides

**Oxides.** From highest to lowest the order is MgO > Al₂O₃ > SiO₂ > Na₂O > P₄O₁₀ > SO₃, with SO₂ the
lowest of all.

- Na₂O and MgO are giant ionic lattices. Mg²⁺ carries twice the charge of Na⁺ and is smaller, so the
  electrostatic attraction to O²⁻ is stronger and MgO melts higher.
- Al₂O₃ is still a giant lattice with high melting point, but Al³⁺ polarises the oxide ions so the
  bonding is partly covalent, and it melts below MgO.
- SiO₂ is macromolecular. Melting means breaking many strong Si–O covalent bonds.
- P₄O₁₀, SO₃ and SO₂ are simple molecules. Melting only overcomes van der Waals forces between molecules.
  The large P₄O₁₀ molecule has more electrons, so its van der Waals forces are the strongest of the three.

**Chlorides.** Typical values (°C):

| Chloride | Melting behaviour | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| NaCl | melts at 801 | giant ionic |
| MgCl₂ | melts at 714 | giant ionic, some covalent character |
| aluminium chloride | sublimes at about 180 | Al₂Cl₆ molecules in the vapour |
| SiCl₄ | melts at −69 | simple molecular |
| PCl₅ | turns to vapour at about 160 to 170 | treated as simple molecular |

Overcoming the ionic attraction in NaCl and MgCl₂ needs a lot of energy. MgCl₂ melts a little lower
because the small Mg²⁺ ion polarises the large Cl⁻ ions, adding covalent character. Aluminium chloride
sublimes at a low temperature: its bonding is largely covalent, and the vapour is Al₂Cl₆ molecules held
together only by weak intermolecular forces.
SiCl₄ is a liquid at room temperature: only van der Waals forces hold its molecules together. PCl₅ has
more electrons per formula unit (100, against 82 in SiCl₄), so stronger van der Waals forces and a higher
melting point. (In the solid PCl₅ actually exists as [PCl₄]⁺[PCl₆]⁻ ions; you are not asked about this.)

### Worked example 2

**Explain why magnesium chloride is a solid with a high melting point while silicon tetrachloride is a
liquid at room temperature.**

1. MgCl₂ is a giant ionic lattice with strong attraction between Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
2. SiCl₄ is simple molecular. Only weak van der Waals forces between its molecules must be overcome;
   the Si–Cl bonds stay intact.
3. Far less energy is needed, so SiCl₄ melts at a much lower temperature.

## Oxides with water

| Oxide | Type | pH (approx.) | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na₂O | basic | 14 | `Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH` |
| MgO | basic | 9 | `MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂` |
| Al₂O₃ | amphoteric | 7 | insoluble, no reaction |
| SiO₂ | acidic | 7 | insoluble, no reaction |
| P₄O₁₀ | acidic | 0 | `P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄` |
| SO₂ | acidic | 3 | `SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃` |
| SO₃ | acidic | 0 | `SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄` |

Ionic oxides release oxide ions, which accept protons from water (`O²⁻ + H₂O → 2OH⁻`). NaOH is very
soluble, but Mg(OH)₂ is not, so MgO gives the lower pH. Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ are giant structures that do
not dissolve, so the pH stays at 7.
Simple molecular oxides react with water to make acids. H₂SO₃ is a weak acid, only partly ionised, so
it is less acidic than H₂SO₄ of equal concentration.

## Chlorides with water

| Chloride | What happens | Equation | pH (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaCl | dissolves | `NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)` | 7 |
| MgCl₂ | dissolves | `MgCl₂(s) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq)` | 6 |
| AlCl₃ | hisses; fumes of HCl with a little water | `AlCl₃ + 6H₂O → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + 3Cl⁻` | 3 |
| SiCl₄ | violent reaction, white solid and steamy fumes | `SiCl₄ + 2H₂O → SiO₂ + 4HCl` | 1 |
| PCl₅ | violent reaction, steamy fumes | `PCl₅ + 4H₂O → H₃PO₄ + 5HCl` | 1 |

**Explaining the trend.** NaCl is ionic; its ions are simply hydrated, so the solution is neutral.
MgCl₂ is ionic too, but the small Mg²⁺ ion pulls slightly on electron density in its water molecules,
releasing a few H⁺ ions. The Al³⁺ ion is so small and highly charged that it strongly polarises the O–H
bonds of its six water ligands:

`[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H⁺`

With very little water, aluminium chloride is fully hydrolysed instead: `AlCl₃ + 3H₂O → Al(OH)₃ + 3HCl`.

SiCl₄ and PCl₅ are covalent. Water attacks the central atom, the Si–Cl or P–Cl bonds break and
hydrogen chloride forms, so the solutions are strongly acidic. With only a little cold water, PCl₅ stops at
an intermediate: `PCl₅ + H₂O → POCl₃ + 2HCl`.

### Worked example 3

**A 3.40 g sample of SiCl₄ reacts fully with water, and the acidic mixture is diluted to 500 cm³. Find
the HCl concentration, the pH and the mass of SiO₂ produced. (Ar: Si = 28.1, Cl = 35.5, O = 16.0)**

```
Mr(SiCl₄) = 28.1 + 4(35.5) = 170.1
n(SiCl₄)  = 3.40 / 170.1 = 0.0200 mol
n(HCl)    = 4 × 0.0200   = 0.0800 mol
[HCl]     = 0.0800 / 0.500 = 0.160 mol dm⁻³
pH        = −log(0.160) = 0.80
mass SiO₂ = 0.0200 × 60.1 = 1.20 g
```

## Structures of the acids and anions

- **H₃PO₄** (from P₄O₁₀ or PCl₅): phosphorus at the centre with one P=O and three P–OH groups; tetrahedral.
  Losing protons gives H₂PO₄⁻, HPO₄²⁻ and finally **PO₄³⁻**, with the negative charges on single-bonded O.
- **H₂SO₃** (from SO₂): sulfur with one S=O, two S–OH and a lone pair; pyramidal. Anions: HSO₃⁻ and
  **SO₃²⁻**, both keeping the lone pair.
- **H₂SO₄** (from SO₃): sulfur with two S=O and two S–OH; tetrahedral. Anions: HSO₄⁻ and **SO₄²⁻**.
- **HCl** (from SiCl₄ and PCl₅): a single H–Cl bond; in water it gives H⁺ and **Cl⁻**.

```
HPO₄²⁻ (one O–H left)           HSO₃⁻ (lone pair on S)
        O                               O
        ‖                               ‖
  ⁻O –  P  – O⁻                   H–O – S – O⁻
        |                               ..
        O–H
```

## Reactions with acids and bases

**Basic oxides with acids:** `Na₂O + 2HNO₃ → 2NaNO₃ + H₂O` and `MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O`.

**Amphoteric Al₂O₃:** with acid, `Al₂O₃ + 6HNO₃ → 2Al(NO₃)₃ + 3H₂O`; with alkali,
`Al₂O₃ + 2KOH + 3H₂O → 2KAl(OH)₄`.

**Acidic oxides with bases:** `SiO₂ + Na₂O → Na₂SiO₃` (on heating), `P₄O₁₀ + 12KOH → 4K₃PO₄ + 6H₂O`,
`SO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaSO₃ + H₂O` and `SO₃ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + H₂O`.

**Chlorides with alkali.** The ionic chloride of magnesium gives a precipitate:
`MgCl₂ + 2NaOH → Mg(OH)₂ + 2NaCl`. Aluminium chloride first gives white Al(OH)₃, which dissolves in
excess alkali: `AlCl₃ + 4NaOH → NaAl(OH)₄ + 3NaCl`. The covalent chlorides are neutralised completely:
`SiCl₄ + 6NaOH → Na₂SiO₃ + 4NaCl + 3H₂O` and `PCl₅ + 8NaOH → Na₃PO₄ + 5NaCl + 4H₂O`.

## Common errors

- Writing AlCl₃ as the product of aluminium and chlorine. The specification asks for Al₂Cl₆.
- Saying SiCl₄ or PCl₅ "dissolves". They react with water; the products are an acid and HCl.
- Giving pH 7 for MgCl₂ or AlCl₃. Only NaCl gives a neutral solution.
- Claiming that the low melting point of SiCl₄ comes from weak Si–Cl bonds. The bonds stay intact.
- Counting 3 mol of NaOH per mole of PCl₅. The five HCl molecules also need neutralising, giving 8.
