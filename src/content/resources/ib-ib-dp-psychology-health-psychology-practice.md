IB DP Psychology Paper 2 (first assessment 2019) sets three 22-mark essays on the health option. The seven questions below cover every content point; the model plans use stress as the health problem, but each plan notes how to adapt it to addiction or obesity. Allow about an hour per essay.

## Questions

**1.** Evaluate the biopsychosocial model of health and well-being. **[22]**

**2.** Discuss how dispositional factors and health beliefs influence health. **[22]**

**3.** Evaluate research on risk and protective factors in health. **[22]**

**4.** Contrast two explanations of one health problem. **[22]**

**5.** Discuss factors that influence the prevalence of one health problem. **[22]**

**6.** Evaluate one health promotion programme. **[22]**

**7.** Discuss how the effectiveness of health promotion programmes can be assessed. **[22]**

## Model plans

**1. Biopsychosocial model.**
- *B:* Engel's (1977) critique of the biomedical model; health as the interaction of biological, psychological and social factors.
- *C:* use stress research at each level -- Kiecolt-Glaser et al. (1984) immune effects of exam stress (biological), Lazarus's appraisal (psychological), Whitehall II and job control (social).
- *D:* holistic and useful for treatment planning; but vague about how factors interact and hard to falsify; weighting of factors differs across health problems.

**2. Dispositional factors and health beliefs.**
- *B:* optimism, hardiness, Type A and hostility; the health belief model and the theory of planned behaviour.
- *C:* Western Collaborative Group Study (Type A and coronary heart disease); Kobasa's (1979) hardiness research with executives; studies applying the HBM to screening or vaccination.
- *D:* correlational, self-report personality measures; later reanalysis pointed to hostility rather than Type A as a whole; health beliefs predict intentions better than behaviour.
- *Adapting:* for addiction, outcome expectancies and self-efficacy.

**3. Risk and protective factors.**
- *B:* risk factors increase probability of ill health; protective factors reduce it.
- *C:* Cohen et al. (1997) social network diversity and resistance to colds; Marmot's Whitehall research on job control.
- *D:* Cohen's study used quarantine and a deliberate virus exposure -- strong control but ethical issues; Whitehall's sample of civil servants limits generalisation; risk and protective factors interact.

**4. Two explanations of stress (contrast).**
- *B/C:* biological -- Selye's GAS and HPA axis, Kiecolt-Glaser et al. (1995) wound healing in carers; cognitive -- Lazarus's transactional model, appraisal research.
- *D:* the biological account explains how stress damages the body but not why people react differently to the same stressor; the cognitive account explains individual differences but relies on self-report; an integrated model combines both.
- *Adapting:* for obesity, contrast genetic explanations with sociocultural ones (food environment).

**5. Prevalence.**
- *B:* prevalence is the proportion of a population affected; influences include gender, age, income, culture and measurement.
- *C:* national stress surveys such as the APA's *Stress in America*; social gradient findings from Whitehall II.
- *D:* self-report prevalence varies with definitions and cultural willingness to report; cross-sectional data cannot show causes.

**6. One health promotion programme.**
- *B:* MBSR -- an eight-week programme of mindfulness meditation and body awareness.
- *C:* Shapiro et al. (1998): randomised trial with medical and premedical students showing reduced anxiety and depression compared with a wait-list control.
- *D:* small, motivated student sample; short follow-up; participants not blind to condition; self-report outcomes; cultural acceptability of meditation.
- *Adapting:* for addiction, the US "truth" anti-smoking campaign (Farrelly et al., 2005).

**7. Assessing effectiveness.**
- *B:* outcome measures (behaviour, physiological markers, self-report), comparison groups, follow-up, cost-effectiveness.
- *C:* contrast a randomised trial (Shapiro et al., 1998) with a population campaign evaluated by trends over time (Farrelly et al., 2005).
- *D:* trials give causal evidence but small samples; population evaluations have reach but many confounding variables; long-term maintenance is rarely measured.

## Worked paragraph: linking research to the question (Question 4)

> Lazarus's transactional model explains a weakness in purely biological accounts of stress. Selye's
> General Adaptation Syndrome describes the same physiological sequence for any stressor, but it cannot
> explain why two students facing the same exam show different stress responses. Lazarus argued that
> stress depends on primary appraisal, whether a situation is judged a threat, and secondary appraisal,
> whether one has the resources to cope. This difference means the cognitive explanation can account for
> individual differences, but it depends heavily on self-reported appraisals, which are open to social
> desirability bias. The two explanations are therefore better treated as complementary.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I switch health problems between questions?**
Yes, but it is safer to know one health problem thoroughly and adapt it.

**Do I need statistics from studies?**
Specific findings help criterion C; quote them only if you are sure they are accurate.

**What does "contrast" require?**
An account of the differences between the two explanations, referring to both throughout.
