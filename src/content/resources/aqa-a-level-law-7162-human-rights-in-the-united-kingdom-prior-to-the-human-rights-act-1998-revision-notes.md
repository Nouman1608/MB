Condensed notes on the status of the Convention before the Human Rights Act 1998 and the impact of
Strasbourg decisions. For full explanation and an annotated plan, use the
[Human Rights before the HRA 1998 study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-human-rights-in-the-united-kingdom-prior-to-the-human-rights-act-1998/).

## The position in one line

**Ratified 1951** → binding in **international** law → **not incorporated** (dualism) → courts use it
only for **ambiguity** → claimants go to **Strasbourg** (from 1966) → adverse rulings → **UK changes
law** (sometimes) → pressure for incorporation → **HRA 1998**, in force 2 October 2000.

## Status

- **Dualism**: treaties made by the government under the **royal prerogative** do not change domestic
  law unless Parliament incorporates them.
- No claim for breach of a Convention right in English courts.
- **Parliamentary sovereignty**: a clear statute prevailed over the Convention.
- **Presumption**: Parliament does not intend to breach international obligations, so the Convention
  could resolve **genuine ambiguity** in a statute.

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| Ex parte Brind (1991) | Broadcasting ban; ministers need not exercise a broad statutory discretion in line with Article 10; otherwise incorporation "by the back door" |
| Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers (1993) | Court of Appeal cited Article 10; House of Lords decided on common law alone: a council cannot sue for defamation |
| Malone v MPC (1979) | Phone tapping lawful as no law forbade it: residual liberty |
| R v Khan (1997) | Evidence from an unregulated listening device admissible |
| Ex parte Smith (1996) | Ban on gay people in the armed forces not irrational under Wednesbury |

**Strasbourg follow-ups**: Khan v UK (2000): Article 8 breached, surveillance not in accordance with law.
Smith and Grady v UK (1999): Article 8 breached, and Article 13 breached because the irrationality test
gave no effective remedy.

## Impact of Strasbourg decisions

| Decision | Article | UK response |
|---|---|---|
| Golder v UK (1975) | 6 and 8: prisoner refused access to a solicitor | Prison rules changed |
| Tyrer v UK (1978) | 3: judicial birching degrading | Birching ended in practice |
| Sunday Times v UK (1979) | 10: thalidomide injunction | Contempt of Court Act 1981 |
| Dudgeon v UK (1981) | 8: criminalising private homosexual acts in Northern Ireland | Homosexual Offences (NI) Order 1982 |
| Campbell and Cosans v UK (1982) | Protocol 1, Article 2: school corporal punishment | Education (No 2) Act 1986 |
| Malone v UK (1984) | 8: phone tapping | Interception of Communications Act 1985 |
| Brogan v UK (1988) | 5: four days without a judge | Derogation under Article 15 |
| Halford v UK (1997) | 8: office phone interception | Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 |

**Article 46**: the state must abide by final judgments against it.

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| External check on government and Parliament | Slow and costly; domestic remedies first |
| Real legislative change across many areas | British cases decided abroad, not by UK judges |
| Common law sometimes reached similar results (Derbyshire) | Liberties, not rights; gaps such as Malone |
| | Minimal responses possible, such as the Brogan derogation |
| | Judicial review threshold too high (Smith and Grady) |

**Rights Brought Home** (1997 white paper): rights should be enforceable in UK courts, saving delay and
cost, with British judges shaping Convention law.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dualism | Treaties need incorporation by statute to have effect in domestic law |
| Royal prerogative | Executive powers, including making treaties, exercised without Parliament |
| Incorporation | Making a treaty part of domestic law by Act of Parliament |
| Unincorporated treaty | Binds the state internationally but gives no domestic rights |
| Individual petition | An individual's right to complain to Strasbourg |
| Wednesbury irrationality | Decision so unreasonable no reasonable authority could make it |
| Derogation | Formal suspension of certain obligations under Article 15 |

## Exam traps

- Dating UK membership from 1998; it ratified in 1951.
- Saying courts could apply the Convention directly before 2000.
- Treating ex parte Brind as applying Article 10; it refused to require compliance.
- Listing cases without the UK's response.
- Forgetting that the UK sometimes responded by derogation, not reform.
- Confusing the date of the Act (1998) with the date it came into force (2000).

## Self-test

1. When did the UK ratify the Convention?
2. Why was the Convention not part of English law before 2000?
3. In what situation could courts refer to the Convention before the HRA?
4. What did the House of Lords decide in ex parte Brind?
5. How did the House of Lords decide Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers?
6. Why did the challenge fail in ex parte Smith, and what did Strasbourg later decide?
7. What was the UK's response to Sunday Times v UK?
8. What was the UK's response to Malone v UK?
9. How did the UK respond to Brogan v UK?
10. Which Strasbourg decision led to the Education (No 2) Act 1986?
11. What did the European Court decide in Khan v UK?
12. What were the main arguments in Rights Brought Home?

**Answers:** 1. 1951. 2. The UK is dualist: a treaty made under the prerogative needs incorporation by
Parliament. 3. To resolve genuine ambiguity in a statute, presuming Parliament did not intend to breach
international obligations. 4. Ministers need not exercise a broad statutory discretion in line with
Article 10. 5. On common law grounds alone: a council cannot sue in defamation. 6. The ban was not
irrational under Wednesbury; Strasbourg found breaches of Articles 8 and 13 in Smith and Grady v UK
(1999). 7. The Contempt of Court Act 1981. 8. The Interception of Communications Act 1985. 9. By
derogating under Article 15. 10. Campbell and Cosans v UK (1982). 11. Surveillance by an unregulated
listening device was not in accordance with the law, breaching Article 8. 12. People should enforce their
rights in UK courts without the cost and delay of Strasbourg, and British judges should help develop
Convention law.
