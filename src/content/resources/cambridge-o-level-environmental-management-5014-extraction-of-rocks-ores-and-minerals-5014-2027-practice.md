## Section A

**1.** Define an ore. **[1]**

**2.** Name **two** methods of subsurface extraction. **[2]**

**3.** State what is meant by overburden. **[1]**

**4.** Identify the extraction method that uses bacteria. **[1]**

**5.** State **three** types of pollution that a quarry can cause. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe how phytomining is used to recover metal from mine waste. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why strip mining is suitable for a coal seam lying 20 m below flat farmland, but shaft
mining is needed for a seam 800 m deep. **[4]**

**8.** A company has found a tin deposit in a remote, mountainous area with very heavy seasonal rainfall.
Explain how **three** factors in the syllabus could affect its decision to extract the tin. **[6]**

**9.** *Invented data.* The table shows two copper deposits.

| | Deposit X | Deposit Y |
|---|---|---|
| Mass of ore (tonnes) | 400 000 | 1 500 000 |
| Ore grade (% copper) | 2.0 | 0.5 |

(a) Calculate the mass of copper in each deposit.

(b) Calculate the mass of waste rock left after the copper is removed from Deposit Y.

(c) Explain why Deposit Y would cause greater problems with waste management. **[6]**

**10.** A large open-pit mine opens next to a rural village. Describe and explain **two** economic and
**two** environmental impacts on the village. **[8]**

**11.** Describe how bioremediation and tree planting can help restore land damaged by mining. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of using a worked-out quarry as a landfill site. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Rock containing minerals and metals [1].

**2.** Deep mining [1]; shaft mining [1].

**3.** The soil and rock lying above a deposit [1].

**4.** Bioleaching [1].

**5.** Any three: air [1]; land [1]; noise [1]; visual [1]; water [1].

**6.** Any four:

- Plants that absorb metal compounds through their roots are grown on the mine waste [1].
- The metal compounds build up in the plants as they grow [1].
- The plants are harvested [1].
- They are burned [1].
- The metal is extracted from the ash [1].

**7.** Any four:

- At 20 m the overburden is thin, so it is cheap to remove [1].
- The flat, near-horizontal seam can be uncovered in long strips [1].
- Surface extraction is cheaper and safer for workers and recovers more coal [1].
- At 800 m there is far too much overburden to remove economically [1].
- A vertical shaft must be sunk and tunnels dug to reach the seam [1].

**8.** Three factors, each named [1] and explained in this context [1], for example:

- **Accessibility and terrain**: steep mountains and no roads [1] mean roads must be built and machinery
  transported, raising costs [1].
- **Climate**: heavy rainfall [1] can flood pits and stop work for part of the year, cutting income [1].
- **Quantity and quality (ore grade)**: if the deposit is large and high-grade [1], it may still make a
  profit despite the high costs [1].
- **Cost and profit**: high costs of access and flood control [1] mean extraction only goes ahead if the
  tin price is high enough [1].
- **Environmental impact assessment**: steep slopes and heavy rain increase the risk of landslides and
  polluted run-off [1], so permission could be refused or conditions set [1].

**9.** (a) X: 2.0 / 100 x 400 000 = **8000 tonnes** [1]. Y: 0.5 / 100 x 1 500 000 = **7500 tonnes** [1].
(b) 1 500 000 - 7500 = **1 492 500 tonnes** [1].
(c) Any three:

- Y has a lower ore grade, so much more rock must be mined for each tonne of copper [1].
- Y leaves far more waste rock: 1 492 500 tonnes against 392 000 tonnes for X [1].
- More land is needed for spoil heaps, destroying more habitat [1].
- More waste means more rain washing through it, increasing the risk of water pollution [1].

**10.** Two economic and two environmental impacts, each described [1] and explained [1]:

*Economic*

- Jobs are created at the mine [1], so incomes in the village rise [1].
- Local shops and services gain customers [1] because miners spend their wages locally [1].
- The village may come to depend on the mine [1], so jobs are lost when it closes [1].

*Environmental*

- Land is cleared for the pit and spoil heaps [1], so habitats are destroyed and biodiversity falls [1].
- Blasting and lorries create dust [1], which settles on crops and causes breathing problems [1].
- Run-off from spoil heaps carries chemicals into streams [1], killing fish and polluting drinking
  water [1].

Maximum 4 marks for economic and 4 marks for environmental impacts.

**11.** Bioremediation, any two:

- Organisms such as plants or microorganisms are added to the polluted ground [1].
- They remove or break down pollutants, cleaning the soil without digging it out [1].
- This makes the land safe for vegetation to grow again [1].

Tree planting, any two:

- Tree roots bind the soil and reduce erosion [1].
- Trees provide habitat, increasing biodiversity [1].
- Trees take in carbon dioxide and store carbon [1].
- Trees screen the site, reducing visual pollution [1].

**12.** Benefits, any three:

- The hole is filled, so the land surface is restored [1].
- The quarry operator earns income from waste disposal [1].
- It provides a place for a town's waste close to where it is produced [1].
- Once full, the site can be covered with soil and planted [1].

Limitations, any three:

- Leachate can drain from the waste and pollute ground water and rivers [1].
- Rotting waste releases methane, a greenhouse gas [1].
- Lorries, smell and litter affect nearby residents [1].
- The land can only be used for limited purposes afterwards, because it may settle and is unsuitable for
  building [1].

Maximum 6 marks; at least one benefit and one limitation are needed for full marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing bioleaching (bacteria) with phytomining (plants).
- In 9(c), giving only the calculation without explaining what extra waste does.
- Naming factors without linking them to the scenario.
- Writing "it causes pollution" without naming the type and cause.
- Answering 12 with benefits only.
