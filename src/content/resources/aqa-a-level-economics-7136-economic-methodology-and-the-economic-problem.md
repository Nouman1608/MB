This guide covers **3.1.1 Economic methodology and the economic problem**, the opening sub-section of 3.1
Individuals, firms, markets and market failure in AQA A-level Economics (7136). Its five parts are 3.1.1.1
Economic methodology, 3.1.1.2 The nature and purpose of economic activity, 3.1.1.3 Economic resources,
3.1.1.4 Scarcity, choice and the allocation of resources, and 3.1.1.5 Production possibility diagrams.

The site's [economic methodology, scarcity and production possibility study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-economic-methodology-scarcity-resources-and-production/)
teaches each part in order. This page is about **using** the sub-section: classifying difficult statements,
showing how value judgements enter real policy choices, reading opportunity cost from data, and drawing
production possibility diagrams that answer the question set. Every country and figure below is invented for

## Why this sub-section matters across the whole course

The specification says students "should understand how thinking as an economist may differ from other forms of
scientific enquiry". That idea returns throughout A-level Economics:

- **Models and assumptions.** Demand and supply, perfect competition and AD/AS are all simplifications.
  Evaluation in later topics often means asking whether an assumption holds in the case in front of you.
- **Rationality.** Traditional theory assumes rational agents; 3.1.2 then introduces behavioural economics,
  which questions that assumption. Knowing that rationality is a modelling assumption, not a fact, prepares
  you for that debate.
- **Testing with messy data.** Economists usually cannot run controlled experiments on a whole economy, so
  they compare places or periods where a policy changed. Many other influences change at the same time, which
  is why economists often disagree about how large an effect is even when they agree about its direction.
- **Values.** Almost every policy question in 3.1.8 and 3.2 contains a positive part (what will happen) and a
  normative part (whether it is worth it). Separating the two is a core evaluation skill.

## Positive and normative: the hard cases

Easy cases contain "should" or "unfair". Examination statements are often less obvious. Use one test: **could
evidence, in principle, settle this claim?** If yes, it is positive, even if it is false. If it depends on what
someone thinks is good, it is normative.

| Statement | Type | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| A higher minimum wage in Country P reduced employment of 16 to 17 year olds. | Positive | Testable against employment data, though the evidence may be disputed |
| The minimum wage in Country P is too low to live on. | Normative | "Too low to live on" depends on a judgement about an acceptable standard of living |
| If the aim is to cut car journeys, a congestion charge works faster than new bus lanes. | Positive | Given the stated aim, the speed of each policy's effect can be compared with evidence |
| The government's priority should be lower inflation, not lower unemployment. | Normative | Ranks objectives by value |
| Most economists believe that rent controls reduce the supply of rented housing. | Positive | A claim about what economists believe, which a survey could check |
| Rent controls are a sensible policy because they protect tenants. | Normative | "Sensible" weighs protection of tenants against other effects |

Three points earn credit in short answers. First, a positive statement can be **wrong**. Second, a statement
about what people believe is positive even if the belief itself is normative. Third, a **conditional**
statement ("if the aim is X, then Y works better") can be positive because the value judgement has been
placed in the condition.

## Value judgements in decision making and policy

The specification says people's views on the best option "are influenced by the positive consequences of
different decisions and by moral and political judgements". In an answer, show both layers.

**Illustration: free university tuition in Country R (invented).**

- *Positive questions:* How many more students would enrol? How much would the scheme cost? Would graduates'
  later tax payments recover part of the cost? Who gains most?
- *Normative questions:* Is it fair for non-graduates, who on average earn less, to pay taxes that fund
  graduates? Is education a right? Should the state or the individual bear the cost?

Two people can accept the same estimate of costs and enrolment and still disagree because they weigh fairness
differently. An evaluative conclusion should say which value judgement it rests on, for example: "If equal
access is given more weight than the burden on taxpayers, the scheme is justified."

## The economic problem in three questions

The central purpose of economic activity is producing goods and services to satisfy needs and wants. Every
society answers **what to produce**, **how to produce** and **who is to benefit**. Make the questions concrete
in answers:

- **What:** Country R's government chooses between more hospital places and more road building.
- **How:** a car plant chooses between robotic assembly lines and more workers; the answer depends on the
  relative costs and productivity of the factors.
- **Who benefits:** whether new housing goes to the highest bidders or is allocated partly by need.

## Resources and the environment

The four factors of production are **land**, **labour**, **capital** and **enterprise**. The specification adds
that "the environment is a scarce resource". This is worth developing, because it links forward to market
failure. A fishery, an aquifer or the atmosphere's capacity to absorb pollution can be used up. Non-renewable
resources, such as coal, are depleted by use. Renewable resources, such as fish stocks, can be exhausted if they
are used faster than they regenerate. Because nobody pays for using them, these resources are often overused,
which is the **tragedy of the commons** studied in 3.1.8.

## Opportunity cost from data

Opportunity cost is the **next best alternative forgone**. With data, calculate it as the quantity of one good
given up for each extra unit of the other.

**Worked example (invented).** Country N's production possibilities:

| Point | Food (million tonnes) | Machines (thousand) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 50 |
| B | 10 | 48 |
| C | 20 | 44 |
| D | 30 | 37 |
| E | 40 | 27 |
| F | 50 | 0 |

Moving from A to B costs 2 thousand machines for 10 million tonnes of food: **0.2 thousand machines per million
tonnes**. From B to C it is 0.4, C to D 0.7, D to E 1.0 and E to F 2.7. The opportunity cost of food **rises**
as more food is produced, because resources are not equally suited to both goods. The first land switched to
food is the most fertile; the last is workers and capital that were far better at making machines. That is
why the boundary is drawn **concave to the origin**. A straight-line boundary would mean a constant opportunity
cost, which happens only when resources are equally productive in both uses.

**Inside the boundary.** If Country N produces 20 million tonnes of food and 30 thousand machines, resources
are unemployed. Moving to point C would add 14 thousand machines with **no** loss of food: there is no
opportunity cost to using idle resources.

## Production possibility diagrams that answer the question

The specification lists four features: resource allocation, opportunity cost and trade-offs, unemployment of
resources and economic growth. Pick the movement that matches the question.

| Event | What to draw |
|---|---|
| Choosing more of one good | A movement **along** the boundary, with the forgone quantity labelled |
| A recession | A point moving **inside** the boundary; the boundary itself does not move |
| Better technology in machine making only | The boundary pivots **outward along the machine axis**; the food intercept stays put |
| Net investment or a larger labour force | The whole boundary shifts **outward** |
| A natural disaster destroying capital | The boundary shifts **inward** |

Label axes with goods and units, label the original and new boundaries, and mark points. A diagram that is
not referred to in the text gains less credit than one your explanation uses.

### Efficiency on the boundary

All points **on** the boundary are **productively efficient**: no more of one good can be made without making
less of the other. Not all are **allocatively efficient**, because allocative efficiency requires the mix of
output that best matches what society wants. Point F in Country N (all food, no machines) is productively
efficient but almost certainly not allocatively efficient.

## Annotated model answer

**Question.** Explain, using a production possibility diagram, why an economy that devotes more resources to
capital goods today may be able to consume more in the future.

**Answer.** Draw a boundary with consumer goods and capital goods on the axes. Moving along the boundary
towards capital goods means **fewer consumer goods now**: that is the opportunity cost, the consumption given
up. *(Shows the trade-off.)* The extra capital goods add to the economy's stock of machinery and
infrastructure, which increases its productive capacity. *(Links the choice to a cause of growth.)* In later
periods the boundary shifts outward, so the economy can reach points with **more** consumer goods than it could
produce at the start. *(Uses the diagram to complete the argument.)* The gain depends on whether the
investment is productive and on whether it exceeds depreciation. *(A short qualification.)*

## Common errors

- Treating "positive" as "true" or "good".
- Calling every statement that mentions a policy normative.
- Calculating opportunity cost as the **total** of all alternatives, or as the quantity gained rather than the
  quantity given up.
- Drawing an outward shift for a fall in unemployment; that is a movement from inside to the boundary.
- Claiming every point on the boundary is allocatively efficient.

## Command words in AQA questions

- **Define / State:** precise meaning or fact, no development.
- **Explain:** reasons linked in a chain, using the diagram or data where asked.
- **Calculate:** show the working and give units.
- **Analyse:** develop the logical steps, often with a diagram.
- **Evaluate / Assess / Discuss / To what extent:** weigh arguments, separate positive from normative, and reach
  a supported judgement.
