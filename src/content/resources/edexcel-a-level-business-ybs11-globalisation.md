This guide covers section **4.3.1 Globalisation** in Unit 4 (Global business) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. The Unit 4 description notes that the impact of globalisation and global markets must be understood in relation to businesses, so every point below finishes with what it means for a firm. All businesses, countries and figures used in examples are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Global business study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-global-business/). Condensed notes are in the [Globalisation revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-globalisation-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Globalisation practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-globalisation-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Growing economies:** characteristics of developed, developing and emerging economies; the growing economic power of countries in Asia, Africa and elsewhere; implications of economic growth for individuals and businesses (trade opportunities, employment patterns); indicators of growth (GDP and GDP per capita, human development index).
2. **International trade and business growth:** exports and imports; the implications of increasing specialisation by countries and businesses; foreign direct investment (FDI) and its link to business growth.
3. **Factors contributing to increased globalisation:** trade liberalisation, lower trade barriers and the role of the World Trade Organization (WTO); political change; lower transport and communication costs; the growing importance of multinational corporations (MNCs); investment flows (FDI); migration; growth of the global labour force; structural change; the impact on businesses.
4. **Protectionism:** reasons, tariffs, import quotas, other barriers (government legislation, domestic subsidies) and the impact on business.
5. **Trading blocs:** expansion of blocs (the EU and single market, ASEAN, NAFTA) and their impact on business.

## Developed, developing and emerging economies

| Type | Typical features | Business implications |
|---|---|---|
| Developed | High income per head, large service sector, advanced infrastructure, ageing population, mature markets | Large but saturated markets; high wages; strong consumer protection |
| Developing | Low income per head, reliance on agriculture and primary products, weaker infrastructure | Low costs, but small and poorer markets; higher risk |
| Emerging | Rapid growth, rising incomes, expanding industry and middle class, improving infrastructure | Growing consumer markets and production bases; some political and currency risk |

Economic power has been moving towards parts of Asia, Africa and other regions. As the middle class grows, demand for cars, mobile phones, financial services and branded goods grows with it. Employment patterns change as workers move from farms to factories and then to services.

### Indicators of growth

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year. **GDP per capita** divides GDP by the population and indicates average income. The **human development index (HDI)** combines life expectancy, years of education and income per head into a score between 0 and 1, so it captures living standards better than GDP alone.

**Worked example 1.** Country A has a GDP of $540bn and 90 million people; Country B has a GDP of $1,200bn and 20 million people.

- Country A: 540bn / 90m = **$6,000** per head.
- Country B: 1,200bn / 20m = **$60,000** per head.

B's GDP is 2.2 times as large as A's, but its GDP per head is **10 times** as high. A is the larger market in numbers of people, but B has far more spending power per customer. If A's GDP grows by 6% and its population by 1.5%, GDP per capita rises by 1.06 / 1.015 - 1 = **4.4%**. Growth is therefore worth less than it appears when population is rising. Businesses use these indicators to decide where to sell: A's large, fast-growing market may suit low-priced goods, while B may suit premium products.

**Implications for individuals and businesses:** growth brings higher incomes and more jobs and consumer choice, but may also bring inequality, pollution, migration to cities and pressure on public services. For businesses it opens trade opportunities (new markets for exports, lower-cost production) but also raises competition, as firms from emerging economies expand abroad.

## International trade and specialisation

**Exports** are goods and services sold to other countries; **imports** are those bought from other countries. A country's trade balance is exports minus imports. With exports of $120bn and imports of $150bn, the balance is -$30bn (a deficit).

Countries and businesses **specialise** in the products they make most efficiently, then trade. Benefits: lower costs, greater output, economies of scale and more choice. Drawbacks: over-dependence on a few products or markets, vulnerability to price falls or disruption in supply chains, and the loss of jobs in industries that cannot compete.

**Foreign direct investment (FDI)** is when a business based in one country invests in productive assets in another, for example by building a factory, opening offices or buying a local firm. For the investing business, FDI gives access to new markets, lower costs, avoidance of trade barriers and growth. For the host country, it brings jobs, capital and technology, although profits may flow abroad and local firms may struggle against larger rivals.

## Factors contributing to globalisation

**Globalisation** is the increasing integration of national economies through trade, investment, migration and information. Its causes include:

- **Trade liberalisation:** the cutting of tariffs and quotas. The **WTO** sets rules for trade, runs negotiations to reduce barriers and settles disputes between members.
- **Political change:** the opening of formerly closed economies (such as the move from central planning to markets in several countries) added new markets and workers to the global economy.
- **Reduced cost of transport and communication:** container shipping, cheaper air freight, the internet and mobile phones make it cheaper to coordinate distant production and sales.
- **Growth of MNCs:** large firms operate in many countries, spread brands and set up global supply chains.
- **Increased investment flows (FDI):** capital moves more freely to wherever returns are highest.
- **Migration:** workers move between countries, filling skill shortages, sending money home and creating demand for familiar goods.
- **Growth of the global labour force:** more workers in Asia, Africa and elsewhere have joined the world economy, increasing the supply of labour.
- **Structural change:** economies shift from agriculture to manufacturing and services, which changes what they export and import.

**Impact on businesses of increased globalisation:** larger markets and chances to grow; access to cheaper inputs and labour; the ability to locate each stage of production where it is most efficient; and wider choice of suppliers. Against that, there is more intense competition, greater exposure to exchange rate and supply chain risks, pressure to cut costs, and responsibility for ethical and environmental standards across a longer supply chain.

## Protectionism

**Protectionism** means protecting domestic producers from foreign competition. **Reasons:** to protect infant industries until they are competitive; to protect jobs; to save strategic industries such as food, energy or defence; to prevent dumping (selling abroad below cost); to correct a trade deficit; and to raise revenue.

| Barrier | How it works | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Tariff | A tax on imports | Raises the price of imports; raises revenue for government |
| Import quota | A limit on the quantity imported | Restricts supply and raises price |
| Government legislation | Technical, safety or labelling rules that are costly for foreign firms to meet | Makes imports harder to sell |
| Domestic subsidies | Payments to local producers | Lower their costs and allow lower prices than imports |

**Worked example 2.** A foreign firm sells washing machines for $200. The local manufacturer charges $230. The government imposes a 20% tariff.

- Tariff per machine = 20% x 200 = $40, so the import price rises to **$240**.
- Before the tariff, the import was $30 cheaper than the local machine. After it, the local machine is **$10 cheaper**.
- If 50,000 machines are still imported, the government raises 50,000 x 40 = **$2,000,000**.

**Impact on businesses.** Local producers gain sales, but may become less efficient without competition. Importers and consumers pay more. Exporters may face retaliation: other countries may impose their own tariffs. Firms that use imported materials face higher costs. Businesses may respond by setting up production inside the protected market (FDI), finding another market or lobbying for change.

## Trading blocs

A **trading bloc** is a group of countries that agree to reduce or remove trade barriers among themselves. The forms include free trade areas, customs unions and single markets. The specification names three examples.

- **The EU and the single market:** a large group of European countries with free movement of goods, services, capital and (within the EU) people, common external tariffs and shared rules.
- **ASEAN:** the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, a group of Southeast Asian economies that reduces trade barriers and co-operates economically.
- **NAFTA:** the North American Free Trade Agreement between Canada, Mexico and the United States. Note that this agreement was replaced by the USMCA in 2020, but the specification names NAFTA, so you should know it as a case of a free trade area.

**Impact on businesses.** Firms inside a bloc gain larger markets, economies of scale, easier supply chains and more investment. Firms outside it face barriers (a common tariff) and may lose sales or have to invest inside the bloc. Disadvantages include greater competition, possible diversion of trade from cheaper non-members to less efficient members, and the need to follow common regulations.

## Common mistakes

- Treating GDP as a measure of living standards. It does not show how income is shared or how long people live. HDI is broader.
- Mixing up exports (sales abroad) and FDI (investment in assets abroad).
- Saying globalisation is good or bad for all businesses; it depends on size, sector and location.
- Describing tariffs without noting who gains (local producers, government) and who loses (importers, consumers).
- Calling a trading bloc a protectionist measure without noting that it reduces barriers inside and often keeps them outside.
- Confusing a developing and an emerging economy: emerging economies are growing faster and industrialising.

## Exam technique

For **explain** questions, make one point about the effect on a business and add evidence from the source, for example a tariff rate or GDP per head. For **analyse**, trace the chain: "falling transport costs, cheaper imports of components, lower production cost, higher margin". For **discuss** and **assess**, weigh benefits and risks for the particular firm, such as an exporter's reliance on one market, and give a judgement that depends on the firm's size, flexibility and position in the supply chain.
