> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs. Answers use Britain as the model country for the war's impact.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 6 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). Revise first with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-europe-first-world-war-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-europe-first-world-war/).

## Questions

**1.** State two features of the alliance system created by Bismarck. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the naval race mattered to Anglo-German relations. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "We do not seek war, but we cannot let Serbia insult our empire and live. If Russia moves, Berlin will stand with us; they have said so, and the Kaiser does not break his word." (Constructed for practice, in the voice of an Austro-Hungarian official in July 1914.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about the role of alliances in the July Crisis. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one limitation of the extract as evidence of why war broke out. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two reasons for the failure of the Schlieffen Plan. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the reasons for the defeat of Germany in 1918. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the experiences of women in one European country during the war. **[6]**

**7.** "German foreign policy under Wilhelm II was the main cause of the First World War." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Compare the aims of Wilson and Clemenceau at the Paris Peace Conference. **[4]**

**9.** Discuss how historians disagree about responsibility for the outbreak of war. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: the alliances were defensive; they aimed to keep France isolated; the Dual Alliance (1879) joined Germany and Austria-Hungary; Italy joined in 1882 to make the Triple Alliance. One mark each. **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4. Germany's Navy Laws (1898, 1900) and Tirpitz's building programme threatened Britain's naval supremacy, on which its security and empire depended (2). Britain responded by building more ships, such as dreadnoughts, and by moving closer to France and Russia in the Entente, which hardened the blocs (2). **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: Austria-Hungary expects German support, so the alliance emboldens it (1); the Kaiser's backing, the "blank cheque", is mentioned as the guarantee (1); the mention of Russia shows the chain by which a local conflict could widen (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: it is one official's view and cannot show the decisions of leaders in other capitals (1); the extract is constructed, but even a real statement would be written to justify action, so it may not show true motives (1); it says nothing about German aims or the role of arms and plans, so it cannot explain the war as a whole (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4, two reasons explained. Belgian resistance and the arrival of the British army delayed the advance, and Russia mobilised more quickly than expected (2). French and British forces halted the German advance at the Marne in September 1914, and the plan depended on speed with little margin for error (2). **[4]**

**5.** Up to 6 using a markband approach. Two-front war and failure to win quickly (1); the blockade caused shortages and weakened morale (1); American entry brought money, supplies and fresh troops (1); the Spring Offensive of 1918 failed and exhausted German reserves (1); the Allied Hundred Days offensive used tanks, artillery and combined tactics from August 1918 (1); the collapse of allies and internal unrest, followed by the Kaiser's abdication, with a judgement on which mattered most (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 6. For Britain: women took jobs in munitions, transport and agriculture (1); nursing and auxiliary services, such as the VADs (1); some women gained the vote in 1918, those over thirty meeting property conditions (1); many returned to domestic work after the war, which limited change (1); women also faced bereavement and food shortages (1); a judgement on how far the war changed women's lives (1). Accept equivalent points for another country. **[6]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each, 15 in all. Understanding of the claim: Weltpolitik, Wilhelm II's dismissal of Bismarck, the naval race and the blank cheque (3). Support: Germany's alliance with Austria, the Schlieffen Plan requiring war on France, and support for Austria in July 1914 (3). Other causes: Balkan nationalism, Russia's mobilisation, French revenge, British commitment, imperial rivalry and the alliance blocs (3). Perspectives: Fischer's view of German aims compared with Clark's argument for shared responsibility (3). Counter-argument and judgement: for example that Germany carried a large share of responsibility through the blank cheque and the plan, but that other powers' decisions mattered too (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 4. Wilson wanted a just peace based on self-determination, the Fourteen Points and a League of Nations (1); Clemenceau wanted security against Germany through reparations, territory and military limits (1); both wanted to weaken Germany in some ways, but Wilson was more idealistic and Clemenceau more concerned with French safety (1); the compromise left neither satisfied (1). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 6: one view, associated with Fischer, stresses German aims and the blank cheque (2); another, associated with Clark, stresses shared responsibility and the decisions of leaders in several capitals (2); explains why they differ, for example the evidence used and the time frame chosen (1); a reasoned judgement (1). **[6]**
