## Group 2 at a glance

| Element | Z | Outer electrons | Atomic radius trend | First IE (kJ mol⁻¹, approx.) | Melting point (°C, approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mg | 12 | 3s² | smallest | 738 | 650 |
| Ca | 20 | 4s² | ↓ increases | 590 | 842 |
| Sr | 38 | 5s² | ↓ | 550 | 777 |
| Ba | 56 | 6s² | largest | 503 | 727 |

Every element has two outer electrons in an s sub-shell, so Group 2 is in the s block. In compounds the
metals form 2+ ions and have oxidation state +2.

## Atomic radius

**Trend:** atomic radius increases down the group.

**Explanation:** each element down the group has one more occupied shell. The outer electrons are further
from the nucleus. The extra protons are offset by extra shielding from the additional inner shell.

## First ionisation energy

The first ionisation energy of calcium is the energy for:

```
Ca(g) → Ca⁺(g) + e⁻
```

**Trend:** first ionisation energy decreases down the group.

**Explanation, in three linked points:**

1. The outer electron is in a shell further from the nucleus (larger atomic radius).
2. There is more shielding from inner shells.
3. So the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker, and less energy is needed to
   remove it. The increase in nuclear charge is outweighed by the extra distance and shielding.

### Worked example 1: comparing two ionisation energies

Explain why barium has a lower first ionisation energy than calcium.

- Barium's outer electron is in the sixth shell; calcium's is in the fourth. Barium's outer electron is
  further from the nucleus.
- Barium has more inner shells, so more shielding.
- The outer electron in barium is attracted less strongly, so less energy is needed to remove it.

Add that distance and shielding outweigh barium's larger nuclear charge.

## Melting point

All four elements are giant metallic lattices. Metallic bonding is the attraction between the positive
ions and the delocalised electrons.

**Trend:** melting point generally decreases from Ca to Ba.

**Explanation:**

- Each element forms 2+ ions and gives two delocalised electrons per atom, so the charge stays the same.
- The ions get larger down the group.
- The delocalised electrons are further from the nucleus of each ion, so the attraction between the ions
  and the delocalised electrons is weaker.
- Less energy is needed to overcome the metallic bonding.

Magnesium does not fit: its melting point is lower than calcium's. Magnesium has a different metallic
crystal structure (hexagonal close-packed; calcium is face-centred cubic), so the simple charge and size
argument does not apply to it. Use the argument for Ca, Sr and Ba, and treat Mg as the exception.

## Reactions with water

Reactivity with water **increases** down the group. The reason links back to ionisation energy: each metal
loses its two outer electrons when it reacts, and these are lost more easily further down the group.

| Metal | Reaction |
|---|---|
| Mg | reacts very slowly with cold water; reacts with steam |
| Ca | reacts steadily with cold water: fizzing, the metal dissolves, a white suspension may form |
| Sr | reacts faster than calcium |
| Ba | reacts most vigorously |

**Cold water (Ca, Sr, Ba, and very slowly Mg)** gives the hydroxide and hydrogen:

```
Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
```

The general form is M + 2H₂O → M(OH)₂ + H₂. The solution formed is alkaline. With calcium some calcium
hydroxide may come out as a white solid, because it is only slightly soluble.

**Steam (Mg)** gives the oxide, not the hydroxide:

```
Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g)
```

Magnesium burns in steam with a bright white light, leaving white MgO.

Both reactions are redox. The metal is oxidised from 0 to +2; hydrogen is reduced from +1 to 0.

## The extraction of titanium using magnesium

Titanium is extracted from titanium(IV) chloride by reduction with magnesium:

```
TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂
```

Magnesium is the **reducing agent**: it is oxidised from 0 to +2, and titanium is reduced from +4 to 0.

### Worked example 2: masses in the titanium extraction

Calculate the mass of magnesium needed to produce 75.0 kg of titanium, and the mass of TiCl₄ used.
(Aᵣ: Ti = 47.9, Mg = 24.3, Cl = 35.5)

```
n(Ti)     = 75.0 × 10³ g ÷ 47.9           = 1566 mol (1565.8)
n(Mg)     = 2 × n(Ti)                       = 3132 mol
mass Mg   = 3131.6 × 24.3 = 76.1 × 10³ g    = 76.1 kg
Mr(TiCl₄) = 47.9 + (4 × 35.5)               = 189.9
mass TiCl₄ = 1565.8 × 189.9 = 297 × 10³ g   = 297 kg
```

Keep unrounded values in your calculator until the last step.

## The hydroxides: solubility and uses

**Trend:** the solubility of the hydroxides **increases** down the group.

```
Mg(OH)₂  sparingly soluble    (least soluble)
Ca(OH)₂  slightly soluble
Sr(OH)₂  more soluble
Ba(OH)₂  most soluble
```

Because more hydroxide dissolves further down the group, a saturated solution of Ba(OH)₂ contains a higher
concentration of OH⁻ ions and so has a higher pH than a saturated solution of Mg(OH)₂.

**Mg(OH)₂ in medicine.** Magnesium hydroxide is used as an antacid to neutralise excess stomach acid
(hydrochloric acid):

```
Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
```

Because it is only sparingly soluble, the concentration of hydroxide ions it releases stays low.

**Ca(OH)₂ in agriculture.** Calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) is spread on acidic soil to raise the soil pH,
which is needed for good crop growth:

```
Ca(OH)₂ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + 2H₂O
```

## Removing SO₂ from flue gases

Burning fossil fuels that contain sulfur releases sulfur dioxide, an acidic gas. Calcium oxide and calcium
carbonate are bases, so they react with SO₂ and remove it from flue gases:

```
CaO(s)   + SO₂(g) → CaSO₃(s)
CaCO₃(s) + SO₂(g) → CaSO₃(s) + CO₂(g)
```

Note that the calcium carbonate route releases carbon dioxide.

### Worked example 3: how much base?

A power station must remove 1.00 tonne of SO₂. Calculate the mass of CaCO₃ needed, and the mass of CaO
that would do the same job. (Aᵣ: Ca = 40.1, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, S = 32.1)

```
Mr(SO₂)   = 32.1 + 32.0 = 64.1
n(SO₂)    = 1.00 × 10⁶ g ÷ 64.1 = 1.560 × 10⁴ mol
Mr(CaCO₃) = 100.1   mass CaCO₃ = 1.560 × 10⁴ × 100.1 = 1.56 × 10⁶ g = 1.56 tonnes
Mr(CaO)   = 56.1    mass CaO   = 1.560 × 10⁴ × 56.1  = 8.75 × 10⁵ g = 0.875 tonnes
```

## The sulfates: solubility, the sulfate test and BaSO₄ in medicine

**Trend:** the solubility of the sulfates **decreases** down the group, the opposite of the hydroxides.

```
MgSO₄  soluble        (most soluble)
CaSO₄  slightly soluble
SrSO₄  less soluble
BaSO₄  insoluble      (least soluble)
```

### Testing for sulfate ions

Add dilute hydrochloric acid (or dilute nitric acid) to the sample, then add barium chloride solution.
A **white precipitate** of barium sulfate shows that sulfate ions are present:

```
Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
```

**Why BaCl₂?** Barium sulfate is insoluble, so if sulfate ions are present a precipitate forms even at low
concentrations.

**Why acidify?** Carbonate ions would also give a white precipitate with barium ions (BaCO₃), and so would
sulfite ions. The acid removes them first:

```
2H⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)
```

Do not acidify with sulfuric acid: it adds sulfate ions, so you would always see a precipitate.

### BaSO₄ in medicine

Barium sulfate is used in a "barium meal". The patient swallows a suspension of BaSO₄, which is opaque to
X-rays, so the outline of the digestive system shows up on an X-ray image. Soluble barium compounds are
toxic, but barium sulfate is insoluble, so barium ions are not absorbed into the blood.

## Required practical 4: identifying Group 2 cations

The specification lists test-tube reactions to identify Group 2 cations and the anions OH⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻
and the halide ions. For Group 2, the two solubility trends give you two tests. Use solutions of similar
concentration so the comparison is fair.

| Add to the Group 2 ion solution | Mg²⁺ | Ca²⁺ | Sr²⁺ | Ba²⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sodium hydroxide solution | white precipitate | less precipitate | little | least or none |
| dilute sulfuric acid | no precipitate | slight | more | white precipitate |

Read each row from the trend: hydroxides get more soluble down the group, so the precipitate gets smaller;
sulfates get less soluble, so it gets larger.

## Common errors

- Saying ionisation energy decreases "because there is more nuclear charge". Nuclear charge increases; the
  deciding factors are distance and shielding.
- Writing that Mg reacts with steam to form Mg(OH)₂. With steam the product is MgO.
- Mixing up the two solubility trends. Hydroxides: more soluble down the group. Sulfates: less soluble.
- Describing Mg(OH)₂ as "insoluble". The specification calls it sparingly soluble; BaSO₄ is the one it calls
  insoluble.
- Saying the acid in the sulfate test "reacts with the sulfate". It removes carbonate (and sulfite) ions.
- Explaining the safety of a barium meal by saying "barium is not toxic". Barium ions are toxic; BaSO₄ is
  safe because it is insoluble.
- Calling magnesium the oxidising agent in the titanium extraction. Magnesium is oxidised, so it is the
  reducing agent.
