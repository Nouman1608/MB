These notes focus on the research design row of section 3.7 Sociological research methods in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification. The specification asks you to "describe and explain the processes involved in research design: the establishment of appropriate aims and relevant hypotheses, the use of pilot studies, the selection of appropriate sampling methods and the analysis of data". Methods are assessed in both Paper 1 and Paper 2, always "for a specific area of research", so expect design questions set in families, education, crime or stratification.

For the whole of section 3.7, read the [research methods study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods/) and the [research methods revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods-revision-notes/). Then test yourself with the [research design practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-sociological-research-methods-practice/).

## The research process in order

1. **Choose a topic**, often because of a social issue, a theory or funding.
2. **Review existing research**: secondary sources show what is already known.
3. **Set an aim or hypothesis.**
4. **Choose a method** that fits the aim, the group and the resources.
5. **Pilot** the method on a few people.
6. **Select a sample** from a sampling frame, or build one.
7. **Collect the data**, dealing with practical and ethical issues.
8. **Analyse the data.**
9. **Report findings** and their limitations.

**Memory hook:** *Topic, Theory, Target, Tool, Trial, Take a sample, Talk to people, Tally, Tell.*

## Aims and hypotheses

| | Aim | Hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A general statement of what the study wants to find out | A testable prediction about a relationship between two things |
| Example | To explore how parents choose a secondary school | Middle-class parents visit more schools before choosing than working-class parents |
| Usually used in | Qualitative research | Quantitative research |
| Why | Keeps the study open to unexpected findings | Gives a clear statement that the data can support or reject |

**Making a hypothesis testable.** Key ideas must be **operationalised**: turned into something that can be measured. "Middle-class parents" might be measured by occupation; "visit more schools" by the number of open evenings attended. A hypothesis that cannot be measured cannot be tested.

**A hypothesis is never "proved".** Data either support it or do not.

## Pilot studies

A **pilot study** is a small trial run of the research on a few people similar to the sample.

**What it checks:**
- Are questions clear, or do respondents misread them?
- Are any questions upsetting, intrusive or leading?
- How long does it take?
- Will people agree to take part, and is access workable?
- Do the answer options cover all likely responses?

**Why it matters:** problems found early save time and money and improve validity and reliability. **Limitation:** it adds time and cost, and a pilot group may not reveal every problem.

## Sampling

- **Population:** the whole group the researcher wants to study.
- **Sample:** the smaller group actually studied.
- **Sampling frame:** a list of the population from which the sample is drawn, such as a school roll or a list of members.
- **Representative sample:** shares the population's key characteristics, so findings can be **generalised**.
- **Unrepresentative sample:** differs from the population, so findings apply only to those studied.

| Method | How it works | Best for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random | Everyone in the sampling frame has an equal chance | Large populations with a full list | Needs a sampling frame; a small sample can still be unrepresentative |
| Systematic | Every *n*th name from the list | Quick selection from a long list | A list ordered in a pattern can create bias |
| Quota | Researcher fills set numbers for each group to match the population | Street surveys; no sampling frame needed | Researcher chooses who to ask, so bias can creep in |
| Snowball | Each participant introduces the next | Hidden or hard-to-reach groups | Contacts tend to be similar, so rarely representative |

**Calculating a systematic interval:** population size ÷ sample size. From a list of 900, a sample of 60 needs every 15th name (900 ÷ 60 = 15).

**Calculating quotas:** if 40% of a population are aged under 30, a sample of 250 needs 100 people under 30 (250 × 0.4 = 100).

**Sample size:** larger samples reduce the effect of chance but cost more. A large sample is not automatically representative: Halsey, Heath and Ridge studied over 8,000 males but no females.

## Analysis of data

- **Quantitative data** (numbers): counted, turned into percentages, averages and tables or charts, then examined for **patterns**, **trends** and differences between groups.
- **Qualitative data** (words, observations): transcribed, read closely, sorted into **themes**, and illustrated with quotations.
- **Good practice:** report data that do not fit, not only supporting evidence. **Selective use of data** is bias.
- **Triangulation:** checking findings with a second method or source.

## Designing for a specific area: worked plan

*Area: why some pupils truant from school.*

- **Aim:** to explore pupils' reasons for truanting.
- **Method:** unstructured interviews, because reasons are personal and may need trust to discuss.
- **Pilot:** two interviews to check that questions are understood and not upsetting.
- **Sample:** snowball sampling through youth workers, because truants are hard to reach and there is no list of them.
- **Analysis:** themes, such as boredom, bullying or caring responsibilities, with quotations.
- **Issues:** access needs gatekeepers; under-18s need parental consent; confidentiality matters if pupils admit to rule-breaking.

## Exam traps

- **Trap:** "a random sample is representative". **Fix:** it gives everyone an equal chance; it can still be unrepresentative.
- **Trap:** writing a hypothesis as a question. **Fix:** a hypothesis is a statement.
- **Trap:** "the hypothesis was proved". **Fix:** "the data supported the hypothesis".
- **Trap:** describing a pilot study as "a small study". **Fix:** it is a trial run to test the method before the main study.
- **Trap:** choosing a sampling method with no reason. **Fix:** justify it from the group and the topic.
- **Trap:** forgetting analysis. **Fix:** say how the data would be organised and examined.

## Quick self-test

1. What is the difference between an aim and a hypothesis?
2. Turn this aim into a hypothesis: "to investigate whether housework is shared in dual-earner couples".
3. What does it mean to operationalise a concept?
4. Give three things a pilot study can check.
5. What is a sampling frame? Give an example.
6. A researcher has a list of 1,500 households and wants 75. What is the systematic interval?
7. Which sampling method needs no sampling frame and fills set numbers for each group?
8. Why might a snowball sample be unrepresentative?
9. Why is a large sample not always representative?
10. How is qualitative data usually analysed?
11. What is selective use of data?
12. Why might a researcher review existing research first?

### Answers

1. An aim is a general statement of what to find out; a hypothesis is a testable prediction about a relationship.
2. For example: "In dual-earner couples, women do more hours of housework than men."
3. To turn an idea into something that can be measured.
4. Any three of: clarity of questions, whether questions are upsetting or leading, time taken, willingness to take part, whether answer options cover all responses.
5. A list of the population from which a sample is drawn, such as a school roll.
6. Every 20th household (1,500 ÷ 75 = 20).
7. Quota sampling.
8. Each person introduces contacts who tend to be similar to them.
9. It may still leave out whole groups, as Halsey, Heath and Ridge's all-male sample did.
10. Transcribed, sorted into themes and illustrated with quotations.
11. Choosing only the evidence that supports an argument, which is a form of bias.
12. To see what is already known, avoid repeating work and help form a hypothesis.
