## Questions

**1.** Describe how atomic radius changes from Mg to Ba, and give a reason. **[2]**

**2.** Strontium's first ionisation energy is greater than barium's. Explain why. **[3]**

**3.** **(a)** Explain why calcium has a higher melting point than strontium. **[3]**
**(b)** Name the element from Mg to Ba whose melting point does not fit the general trend. **[1]**

**4.** **(a)** Give a balanced equation, including state symbols, for strontium reacting with water. **[2]**
**(b)** A small piece of calcium and a similar piece of barium are each added to cold water. Give two
differences you would observe. **[2]**
**(c)** Give the equation for magnesium reacting with steam. **[1]**

**5.** Titanium is made by reducing titanium(IV) chloride with magnesium.

**(a)** Write the equation and identify the reducing agent, using oxidation states. **[2]**
**(b)** A reactor is charged with 0.950 tonnes of TiCl₄. Calculate the minimum mass of magnesium needed and
the mass of magnesium chloride formed, both in kg. **[3]**

**6.** A student adds 0.687 g of barium to water. When the reaction stops, the solution is made up to
250 cm³ with distilled water. Portions of 25.0 cm³ are then titrated against 0.100 mol dm⁻³
hydrochloric acid.

**(a)** Give the equation for barium reacting with water. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the expected titre. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest why the same method, using the same mass of calcium, would be less reliable. **[1]**

**7.** **(a)** List the hydroxides of Mg, Ca, Sr and Ba in order of increasing solubility. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why magnesium hydroxide, rather than barium hydroxide, is used to treat indigestion. **[2]**
**(c)** A farmer spreads 185 kg of calcium hydroxide on an acidic field. Calculate the amount of H⁺ ions, in
mol, that it can neutralise. **[2]**

**8.** A power station uses 6.00 tonnes of powdered limestone per day to remove sulfur dioxide from its flue
gases. The limestone is 95.0% CaCO₃ by mass.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction of calcium carbonate with sulfur dioxide. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of SO₂ removed per day, in tonnes. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide released per day, in tonnes. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest one reason for using calcium oxide instead of limestone. **[1]**

**9.** A solution contains both sodium carbonate and sodium sulfate.

**(a)** Describe how you would show that sulfate ions are present, and give the positive result. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why barium chloride is the reagent chosen. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the acid is needed for this solution. Include an ionic equation. **[2]**

**10.** Excess acidified barium chloride is added to a 500 cm³ sample of spring water. The dried
precipitate has a mass of 0.0817 g. Calculate the concentration of sulfate ions in the water, in mg dm⁻³.
(Mr SO₄²⁻ = 96.1) **[4]**

**11.** Four unlabelled colourless solutions are magnesium nitrate, barium nitrate, sodium sulfate and sodium
carbonate. Using only dilute hydrochloric acid, sodium hydroxide solution and the four solutions
themselves, describe how you would identify each one. Give ionic equations for the reactions you use.
**[6]**

**12.** **(a)** Explain why the reactions of the Group 2 metals with water become more vigorous from Mg
to Ba. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why barium sulfate can be swallowed safely before an X-ray, even though barium ions are
toxic. **[2]**
**(c)** Barium carbonate is insoluble in water. Suggest, with an equation, why it must not be used in place
of barium sulfate. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Atomic radius increases down the group [1], because each element has one more occupied shell, so its
outer electrons are further from the nucleus [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A trend with no reason earns only half the credit; "more shells" is the reason expected.

**2.** Barium's outer electron is in the 6s sub-shell, further from the nucleus than strontium's 5s
electron [1]. Barium has more inner shells, so there is more shielding [1]. The attraction for the outer
electron is weaker, despite barium's larger nuclear charge, so removing it takes less energy [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that say nuclear charge "decreases" contradict the chemistry; acknowledge that
it increases and is outweighed.

**3. (a)** Both are giant metallic lattices held by attraction between positive ions and delocalised
electrons [1]. Both form 2+ ions, but Ca²⁺ is smaller than Sr²⁺ [1]. The delocalised electrons are closer to
the calcium ions, so the attraction is stronger and overcoming it takes more energy [1].
**(b)** **Magnesium** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing about molecules or intermolecular forces in a metal answer signals the wrong
model and costs credit.

**4. (a)** Sr(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Sr(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g): balanced equation [1]; correct state symbols [1].
**(b)** Barium fizzes faster [1]. The barium disappears more quickly (or: calcium leaves a cloudier white
mixture) [1].
**(c)** **Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Hydrogen is produced" is a deduction, not an observation; describe what you would see.

**5. (a)** TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂ [1]. Magnesium is the reducing agent: it changes from 0 to +2 while
titanium changes from +4 to 0 [1].
**(b)** n(TiCl₄) = 0.950 × 10⁶ ÷ 189.9 = 5003 mol [1].
Mass of Mg = 2 × 5003 × 24.3 = 2.43 × 10⁵ g = **243 kg** [1].
Mass of MgCl₂ = 2 × 5003 × 95.3 = 9.54 × 10⁵ g = **954 kg** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the tonnes-to-grams conversion on its own line; a slip there spoils every later
value.

**6. (a)** **Ba + 2H₂O → Ba(OH)₂ + H₂** [1].
**(b)** n(Ba) = 0.687 ÷ 137.3 = 5.004 × 10⁻³ mol, so n(Ba(OH)₂) in 25.0 cm³ = 5.004 × 10⁻⁴ mol [1].
n(HCl) = 2 × 5.004 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.001 × 10⁻³ mol [1].
Titre = 1.001 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.100 = 1.00 × 10⁻² dm³ = **10.0 cm³** [1].
**(c)** Calcium hydroxide is only slightly soluble, so some could stay undissolved and the solution would
not contain all the hydroxide formed [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The 1 : 10 scaling from 250 cm³ to 25.0 cm³ is easy to miss; write it as a separate
step.

**7. (a)** **Mg(OH)₂ < Ca(OH)₂ < Sr(OH)₂ < Ba(OH)₂** [1].
**(b)** Mg(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble, so it neutralises stomach acid without producing a strongly alkaline
solution [1]. Ba(OH)₂ is much more soluble, giving a strongly alkaline solution, and it releases toxic Ba²⁺
ions [1].
**(c)** n(Ca(OH)₂) = 185 × 10³ ÷ 74.1 = 2497 mol [1]. Each mole neutralises 2 mol of H⁺, so
n(H⁺) = **4.99 × 10³ mol** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), one point about each compound is expected; describing only magnesium
hydroxide leaves the comparison unfinished.

**8. (a)** **CaCO₃ + SO₂ → CaSO₃ + CO₂** [1].
**(b)** Mass of CaCO₃ = 0.950 × 6.00 = 5.70 tonnes [1]. n(CaCO₃) = 5.70 × 10⁶ ÷ 100.1 = 5.694 × 10⁴ mol =
n(SO₂) [1]. Mass of SO₂ = 5.694 × 10⁴ × 64.1 = 3.65 × 10⁶ g = **3.65 tonnes** [1].
**(c)** n(CO₂) = 5.694 × 10⁴ mol; mass = 5.694 × 10⁴ × 44.0 = **2.51 tonnes** [1].
**(d)** Calcium oxide does not release carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the 95.0% before converting to moles; using the full 6.00 tonnes overstates
every answer.

**9. (a)** Acidify a sample with dilute HCl and add a few drops of BaCl₂(aq) [1]. Positive result: a white
precipitate of BaSO₄ [1].
**(b)** Barium sulfate is insoluble, so barium ions precipitate the sulfate ions [1].
**(c)** Carbonate ions would also form a white precipitate (BaCO₃), giving a false result; the acid removes
them [1]. 2H⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c) the interfering ion must be named; "to stop other ions reacting" is too vague.

**10.** Mr(BaSO₄) = 233.4; n(BaSO₄) = 0.0817 ÷ 233.4 = 3.500 × 10⁻⁴ mol [1].
n(SO₄²⁻) = n(BaSO₄), so concentration = 3.500 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 0.500 = 7.001 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ [1].
Mass concentration = 7.001 × 10⁻⁴ × 96.1 = 0.06728 g dm⁻³ [1] = **67.3 mg dm⁻³** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by 0.500 dm³, not by 500, before converting grams to milligrams.

**11.**
- Add dilute HCl to a portion of each: only sodium carbonate fizzes [1].
  2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CO₂ + H₂O [1].
- Add NaOH(aq) to fresh portions of the other three: only magnesium nitrate gives a white precipitate [1].
  Mg²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Mg(OH)₂ [1].
- Add the identified sodium carbonate solution to the last two: barium nitrate gives a white precipitate;
  sodium sulfate gives none [1]. Ba²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → BaCO₃ [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Each identification needs both the test and the observation; "add NaOH to find
magnesium" without the white precipitate is incomplete.

**12. (a)** Atoms get larger down the group, so the outer electrons sit further from the nucleus, with
more shielding [1]. Ionisation energies decrease [1]. The two outer electrons are lost more easily, so the metal
is oxidised to M²⁺ more readily [1].
**(b)** BaSO₄ absorbs X-rays, so the digestive tract shows up on the image [1]. It is insoluble, so barium
ions are not released and absorbed into the blood [1].
**(c)** Stomach acid would dissolve it, forming soluble barium chloride and releasing toxic Ba²⁺ [1].
BaCO₃ + 2HCl → BaCl₂ + CO₂ + H₂O [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* For (c) the link from acid to a soluble barium compound is the point; "it is toxic"
alone does not explain the difference from BaSO₄.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving shielding out of ionisation energy answers, or claiming nuclear charge falls.
- Describing metals with words such as "molecules" or "intermolecular forces".
- Giving Mg(OH)₂ instead of MgO for magnesium with steam.
- Forgetting that one Ba(OH)₂ needs two HCl, or skipping the 250 cm³ to 25.0 cm³ scaling.
- Ignoring the purity of limestone, or the 1 : 2 ratio of TiCl₄ to Mg.
- Not naming carbonate as the ion the acid removes in the sulfate test.
- Identifying an ion without stating the observation that proves it.
- Explaining the safety of BaSO₄ as "barium is harmless" rather than "BaSO₄ does not dissolve".
