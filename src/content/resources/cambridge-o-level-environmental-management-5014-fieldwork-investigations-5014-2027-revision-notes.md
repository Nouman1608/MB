> **Syllabus edition note.** These notes follow the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014
> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029, sub-topic 5.4 and the Fieldwork requirements section.

Sub-topic 5.4 is about doing science outdoors. You need the nine steps of the scientific method, two
sampling strategies, seven sampling techniques, and the rules for recording and presenting data. Most
questions give you a method or a results table, so practise applying these notes rather than reciting them.

Full explanations: [fieldwork investigations study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-5014-fieldwork-investigations-5014-2027/).
Test yourself: [fieldwork investigations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-5014-fieldwork-investigations-5014-2027-practice/).

## The investigation cycle (5.4.1)

**Aim → hypothesis → plan → variables → collect (with repeats and replicates) → record → analyse →
anomalies → conclusion → evaluate.**

| Step | One-line recall |
|---|---|
| Aim | What you want to find out |
| Hypothesis | A testable prediction linking two variables |
| Plan | Sampling strategy + technique + apparatus; questionnaire or survey if people are involved |
| Pilot study | Small trial to test and improve the method |
| Independent variable | What you change or select (x-axis) |
| Dependent variable | What you measure (y-axis) |
| Control variables | Kept the same so they do not affect the result |
| Control | A comparison sample without the factor being tested |
| Repeats | The same point measured again |
| Replicates | Extra samples at the same level of the independent variable |
| Anomaly | A result that does not fit the pattern; give a reason, exclude or repeat |
| Conclusion | Answers the aim with data; says if the hypothesis is supported |

## Sampling strategies (5.4.2-5.4.3)

| | Random | Systematic |
|---|---|---|
| How | Random numbers as co-ordinates on a grid of two tapes | Regular intervals, usually along a transect |
| Use when | Habitat looks uniform; comparing two areas | Studying change along a gradient |
| Benefit | No investigator bias | Covers the full gradient; shows change |
| Limitation | May miss rare species or parts of the site | Misses what lies between points; may match a pattern in the habitat |

## Sampling techniques (5.4.4-5.4.5)

| Technique | Organisms | Key method detail | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pitfall trap | Ground invertebrates | Rim level with soil; raised lid; check regularly | Predation in the trap; depends on activity |
| Pooter | Very small insects | Suck through mouthpiece tube with gauze | Only tiny animals |
| Sweep net | Insects in tall vegetation | Set number of sweeps, same height | Hard to standardise; misses ground animals |
| Quadrat | Plants, slow animals | Open frame for counts; grid for % cover | Cover is an estimate; small area |
| Transect | Change across an area | Tape or string; sample at intervals | Time-consuming; one line |
| Aerial photography and drones | Vegetation, large animals | Images from above | Cost; cannot see under canopy |
| Automated sampling | Shy or night-active animals; continuous abiotic data | Camera traps, data loggers | Cost; failure; data handling |

**Apparatus list to recognise:** hand lens, humidity/moisture meter, light meter, metre ruler, pH meter,
pitfall trap, pooter, quadrat (open frame and grid), 30 cm ruler, sweep net, transect tape or string, tray
for hand-sorting.

## Data rules from the syllabus

- Table headings: quantity / unit, for example "distance / m". No units in the cells.
- Same number of decimal places or significant figures in a column.
- Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis.
- Scales use more than half the grid and sensible steps (1, 2 or 5 units per 2 cm).
- Plot points as crosses or encircled dots; one thin best-fit line or curve; ignore clear anomalies.
- Only draw a best-fit line if intermediate values can be predicted.

## Calculations

- **Mean** = total / number of readings (exclude anomalies).
- **Range** = highest - lowest.
- **Density** = mean count per quadrat / quadrat area (m²).
- **Population estimate** = density x total area.
- **Percentage cover (grid quadrat)** = squares at least half covered / total squares x 100.

*Invented example:* a 100-square grid quadrat has 42 squares at least half covered by moss: cover = 42%.
Five quadrats give 42, 38, 47, 40 and 43%: mean = 210 / 5 = 42%; range = 47 - 38 = 9 percentage points.

## Safety and ethics

Risk assessment first. Work in groups, tell someone where you are, take care near water and on slopes,
wear gloves when handling soil, wash hands, and return animals to where they were found.

## Exam traps

- "Repeat it" without the reason (to calculate a mean and check reliability).
- Mixing up control variables (kept constant) with a control (comparison sample).
- Choosing random sampling for a gradient question.
- Quadrat area in cm² when the question wants m²: 50 cm x 50 cm = 0.25 m², not 2 500 m².
- Saying a correlation proves cause.
- Using a pooter for large beetles or a pitfall trap for flying insects.

## Self-test

1. Write a testable hypothesis for an investigation of soil pH and plant species number.
2. State the difference between an aim and a hypothesis.
3. Give two control variables for a pitfall-trap comparison of two habitats.
4. What is the difference between a repeat and a replicate?
5. Which sampling strategy avoids investigator bias?
6. Give one limitation of systematic sampling.
7. Which technique would you use to sample insects living on tall grass?
8. Why must a pitfall trap be checked regularly?
9. Give one benefit of automated sampling over a person counting.
10. A 0.25 m² quadrat has a mean of 5 plants. The site is 400 m². Estimate the population.
11. Grid-quadrat cover readings are 25, 30, 80, 28 and 27%. Identify the anomaly and calculate the mean
    without it.
12. Which apparatus measures soil water?

### Answers

1. For example: "The number of plant species increases as soil pH increases" (must be testable and link
   two variables).
2. An aim says what you will find out; a hypothesis is a testable prediction of the result.
3. Any two: number of traps; trap size; time left out; time of day emptied; weather; bait or no bait.
4. A repeat measures the same point again; a replicate is an extra sample at the same level of the
   independent variable.
5. Random sampling.
6. It may miss organisms between sample points, or the interval may match a pattern in the habitat.
7. A sweep net.
8. Trapped animals may eat each other, escape or die, which would change the results.
9. It records continuously, including at night, and does not disturb animals.
10. 5 / 0.25 = 20 per m²; 20 x 400 = 8 000 plants.
11. 80% is the anomaly; mean = (25 + 30 + 28 + 27) / 4 = 110 / 4 = 27.5%.
12. A humidity/moisture meter.
