These questions cover one part of content area 1.2.3: "Judicial interpretation of statutes – why there is a need for it" and the literal, golden and mischief rules and purposive approach, "together with specific relevant case law examples".

---

## Section A

**1.** State **two** reasons why judges need to interpret statutes. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the literal rule of statutory interpretation, using **one** decided case. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between the narrow and broad approaches to the golden rule, using **one** case for each. **[4]**

**4.** Identify the **four** questions a judge asks under the mischief rule. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** difference between the mischief rule and the purposive approach. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A (fictional) Food Safety Act provides: "It is an offence to sell any food in a public place without a licence." Kofi gives away free samples of bread from a stall in a market square, inviting people to place orders for later delivery.

Explain how the literal rule and the mischief rule might be applied to decide whether Kofi has committed the offence. **[6]**

**7.** A (fictional) Act provides: "Any person who causes damage to a vehicle shall be liable to a fine." Marta, a mechanic, removes the engine from a customer's car as part of an agreed repair.

Explain how the golden rule would deal with Marta's situation. **[4]**

**8.** Explain, using **two** decided cases, why the literal rule can produce unjust results. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how the purposive approach allows judges to deal with new technology, using **one** decided case. **[4]**

**10.** A (fictional) Act provides: "No dogs, cats or other animals may be kept in a rented flat without the landlord's consent." Jin keeps a goldfish in a bowl in her rented flat.

Explain how the court might interpret "other animals", using **two** different approaches. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** advantages of the mischief rule. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that judges should always apply the purposive approach when interpreting statutes. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two [1] each: words may be **broad** or general; words may be **ambiguous**; **drafting errors**; **new developments** such as technology that Parliament did not foresee; **changes in the meaning** of language over time.

**2.** Under the literal rule, judges give words their **plain, ordinary, dictionary meaning** [1], even if the result is **absurd or unjust** [1]. Example: in *Whiteley v Chappell* (1868) impersonating a dead voter was not impersonating a person "entitled to vote"; or *Fisher v Bell* [1961]; or *LNER v Berriman* [1946] [1].

**3.** The **narrow approach** applies where a word has **more than one meaning**; the court chooses the meaning that avoids absurdity [1], as in *R v Allen* (1872), where "marry" was read as "go through a ceremony of marriage", or *Adler v George* [1964] [1]. The **broad approach** applies where the word has **only one meaning** but applying it would be repugnant, so the court modifies it [1], as in *Re Sigsworth* [1935], where a son who murdered his mother could not inherit [1].

**4.** One mark each: what was the **common law** before the Act [1]; what was the **mischief and defect** for which the common law did not provide [1]; what **remedy** Parliament had resolved to provide [1]; the **true reason** for the remedy [1] (*Heydon's Case*, 1584).

**5.** The mischief rule looks at the **defect in the law before the Act** that Parliament meant to cure [1]; the purposive approach looks at the **purpose of the Act as a whole** and may read words in to achieve it [1].

**6.** **Literal rule**: "sell" in its ordinary meaning means transferring food for a price [1]. Kofi gives samples away free, so on a literal reading he is **not selling** and commits no offence [1]; orders for later delivery are not sales in the public place [1]. **Mischief rule**: the court asks what defect the Act was meant to cure (*Heydon's Case*) [1]; if the mischief was unlicensed traders putting unsafe food into the hands of the public, Kofi's samples create exactly that risk [1], so, as in *Smith v Hughes* [1960], the court might hold that he falls within the Act [1].

**7.** The golden rule starts with the **literal meaning** [1]: removing an engine could literally be "causing damage" [1]. But convicting a mechanic for carrying out an agreed repair would be **absurd**, a result Parliament cannot have intended [1]. Using the **narrow approach**, as in *Adler v George*, the court would read "damage" as unauthorised or harmful damage, so Marta is not liable [1].

**8.** The literal rule applies words even where the result is unjust [1]. In *LNER v Berriman* [1946] a railway worker killed while oiling points was not "relaying or repairing" the track [1], so his widow received no compensation, although the Act was meant to protect workers on the line [1]. In *Whiteley v Chappell* (1868) a man who impersonated a dead voter escaped liability [1] because a dead person was not "entitled to vote", defeating the purpose of preventing electoral fraud [1].

**9.** The purposive approach interprets words to **achieve the overall purpose** of the Act [1], so it can extend old words to new situations Parliament did not foresee [1]. In *R (Quintavalle) v Secretary of State for Health* [2003] the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990 assumed embryos were created by fertilisation [1]; the House of Lords held that cloned embryos were covered, because Parliament's purpose was to regulate embryos created outside the body [1].

**10.** Any two approaches, three marks each (approach identified and explained [1], applied [1], conclusion [1]):

- ***Ejusdem generis***: general words after a list take their meaning from the list (*Powell v Kempton Park* [1899]) [1]; "dogs, cats" suggests household pets that roam, scratch or make noise [1]; a goldfish is arguably not of the same kind, so Jin is not in breach [1].
- **Literal rule**: "other animals" in its ordinary meaning includes any animal [1]; a goldfish is an animal [1]; Jin is in breach [1].
- **Mischief or purposive**: the defect was likely damage, noise or hygiene problems from pets [1]; a goldfish in a bowl causes none of these [1]; Jin is probably not in breach [1].

**11.** For each of two advantages: advantage identified [1] and explained with an example [1].

- **Fulfils Parliament's intention** [1]: in *Smith v Hughes* the Act achieved its aim of clearing the streets of soliciting [1].
- **Avoids absurdity and injustice** [1]: unlike *Berriman*, it would not leave a remedy-giving Act ineffective on a technicality [1].
- **Flexibility** [1]: in *Royal College of Nursing v DHSS* it allowed the Act to cover newer medical methods [1].
- **Supported by the Law Commission** [1], which in 1969 favoured an approach based on the purpose or mischief over strict literalism [1].

**12.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: the purposive approach and how it differs from the literal, golden and mischief rules; cases such as *Jones v Tower Boot*, *R (Quintavalle)*, *Pepper v Hart*; the influence of the Human Rights Act 1998, s 3.
- **For the view**: sensible and just outcomes; handles new technology; consistent with modern drafting and Convention rights; Parliament can still legislate to reverse an interpretation.
- **Against the view**: judges are unelected, so finding "purpose" can become judicial law making (Lord Simonds in *Magor and St Mellons*); less certainty for citizens and lawyers; the purpose may be unclear or disputed, as in the split decision in *Royal College of Nursing*; where words are clear, departing from them undermines parliamentary supremacy.
- **Evaluation**: weigh justice against certainty and supremacy, and reach a conclusion, for example that the purposive approach should be the starting point where words are unclear, but clear words should be given effect.

Top-level answers use cases accurately throughout, address both sides and reach a justified conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a rule with no case.
- Confusing the mischief rule with the purposive approach.
- Applying only one rule when the question asks for two.
- Forgetting that the golden rule starts from the literal meaning.
- In the evaluation, listing points without reaching a conclusion.
