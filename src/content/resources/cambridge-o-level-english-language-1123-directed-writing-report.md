This guide covers the **report**, one of the five forms named for **Section A, Directed Writing**, of Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 Paper 2. The syllabus says candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, email, **report**, letter or article", writing "about 250–350 words", and the report also appears among the text types the syllabus says learning should focus on. The [directed writing study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing/) lists its features in one row: "title, purpose or terms of reference; headings or clearly marked sections, findings, recommendation; impersonal, objective". This page explains how to build each of those parts, how a report weighs evidence differently from a speech or letter, and how to write in an impersonal register without sounding lifeless.

## What a report is for

A report gives a decision-maker the information and judgement they need to decide. It is written for a named reader, such as a head teacher, a committee or a manager, who has asked for it or will act on it. That shapes everything:

- **Organised for reference.** The reader may look for one section; headings help them find it.
- **Objective in tone.** The reader wants a fair account, not a passionate appeal.
- **Ends in action.** A report without a recommendation leaves the reader's question unanswered.

Reports are naturally **discursive**: they set out both sides of an issue before reaching a reasoned conclusion. If the task asks you to recommend one option, the report still shows that you considered the others.

## The parts of a report

| Part | What it contains | Example wording (original) |
|---|---|---|
| Title | the subject and, if useful, the reader | "Report for the Head Teacher: Replacing Textbooks with Tablets" |
| Introduction or purpose | why the report was written and what it covers | "This report considers the proposal and makes a recommendation." |
| Findings (two or three sections) | the evidence, grouped by theme, each under a heading | "Benefits", "Concerns", "Costs" |
| Evaluation | how reliable or complete the evidence is | "The supplier's figures have not been independently checked." |
| Conclusion | the overall judgement | "On balance, the benefits are real but uncertain." |
| Recommendations | what should happen next, specifically | "A one-term trial with Year 9." |

**Headings** should be short and informative. "Concerns raised by teachers" says more than "Part 2".

**Recommendations** can be written in sentences or as a short numbered list, but each must be specific and must follow from the findings. "Further research is needed" is weak; "Survey parents about home internet access before deciding" is strong.

## Writing the findings

The findings are where most reading marks are earned. Group the source material by theme, not by source. A section headed "Costs" might combine a figure from one text with a concern from another. In each section:

1. **State** the finding in your own words.
2. **Attribute** it: who says so? "According to the supplier...", "Teachers reported..."
3. **Develop** it: what would it mean for the school, the students or the reader?
4. **Evaluate** it: is the evidence a fact, an estimate or an opinion? Is the source neutral?

Attribution is a natural form of evaluation in reports. Saying "the company selling the tablets claims" signals a possible bias without any need for loaded language.

## Impersonal register

**W4**, "use register appropriate to context", for a report usually means an impersonal, objective style:

| Personal and emotional | Impersonal and objective |
|---|---|
| "I think tablets are amazing." | "The evidence suggests that tablets offer several benefits." |
| "Teachers are totally against it." | "Most of the teachers consulted expressed concerns." |
| "This would be a disaster for poor families." | "Families without home internet access could be disadvantaged." |
| "We must do this now!" | "It is recommended that a trial begin next term." |

Useful features: the third person, some passive constructions ("it was found that"), cautious verbs ("suggests", "indicates", "appears"), and precise nouns. A report may use "I" occasionally if the writer gathered information ("I spoke to four teachers"), but the judgement should rest on evidence, not on feelings.

**Impersonal does not mean dull.** Varied sentence structures and precise vocabulary (W3) still matter. A sentence such as "The savings are real but slow: the tablets would pay for themselves only after four years" is objective and lively at the same time.

## Worked example

Sources (original, shortened):

> **Text 1, from a supplier's leaflet:** Our tablets replace heavy textbooks with a single device.
> Schools using them report higher student engagement. Digital textbooks are updated automatically,
> and over five years the tablets cost less than buying new textbooks.
>
> **Text 2, from notes of a staff meeting:** Several teachers worried that students would be
> distracted by games and messages. The science department said digital simulations would help in
> lessons. Some families do not have internet at home. The school's Wi-Fi already struggles when two
> classes use the computer rooms.

**Task:** *Write a report for the head teacher on the proposal to replace textbooks with tablets, with a recommendation.*

**Model report (shortened):**

> **Report for the Head Teacher: Replacing Textbooks with Tablets**
>
> **Purpose**
> This report considers the proposal to replace printed textbooks with tablets and makes a
> recommendation.
>
> **Possible benefits**
> According to the supplier, tablets would lighten students' bags, keep textbooks up to date and cost
> less than new books over five years. The science department added that digital simulations could
> improve practical lessons. However, the claims of "higher engagement" and lower costs come from the
> company selling the devices and are not supported by independent figures.
>
> **Concerns**
> Teachers raised two main concerns: students may be distracted by games and messages, and some
> families lack home internet, so homework on tablets could disadvantage them. A further practical
> problem is that the school's Wi-Fi already struggles with two computer rooms in use; several hundred
> tablets could overwhelm it.
>
> **Conclusion and recommendations**
> The proposal has real advantages, but the evidence for them is incomplete and the network is not
> ready. It is recommended that the school:
>
> 1. upgrade the Wi-Fi before any wider introduction;
> 2. run a one-term trial with one year group, measuring engagement and results;
> 3. survey families about home internet access and lend devices or provide offline materials where needed.

**Commentary.** The title, purpose, headings and recommendations follow report conventions. Findings are grouped by theme (benefits, concerns), not retold source by source. Attribution ("according to the supplier", "teachers raised") and an explicit evaluation ("not supported by independent figures") earn reading marks for R3. The Wi-Fi point is **developed** from a single sentence in Text 2 into a decisive practical concern. The recommendations are specific and follow from the findings. A full answer would be a little longer, around 300 words.

## Common mistakes

- **No headings**, so the report reads as an essay.
- **Organising by source** ("Text 1 says... Text 2 says...") rather than by theme.
- **Emotional or persuasive language** in place of objective evaluation.
- **No recommendation**, or a vague one.
- **Recommendations that do not follow** from the findings.
- **Too many headings** with one sentence under each, leaving no room for development.
- **Forgetting the reader** named in the task.

## Exam technique

Plan your headings before you write: purpose, two or three findings sections, conclusion and recommendations. Under each findings heading, note which source points belong there and one development or evaluation for each. Write the recommendations in your plan early so that every section leads towards them. Keep to about 300 words and check spelling, punctuation and grammar at the end.
