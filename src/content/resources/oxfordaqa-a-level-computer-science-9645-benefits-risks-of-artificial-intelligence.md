This guide covers sub-topic **3.16.3 Benefits and risks of artificial intelligence** of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Computer Science (9645), Version 1.1, in topic 3.16 Artificial intelligence. It is International A-level content, examined in Unit 4, the written paper, which includes short-answer and extended-answer questions. The specification asks you to understand the **benefits** associated with the use of artificial intelligence and the **risks** associated with its use. The benefits include improved and more consistent decision making, the ability to analyse very large data sets quickly, continuous availability, lower cost of operation, and availability to more people and in areas where human expertise is not readily available. The risks to consider are elimination of jobs and its social impact, bias in decision making, false information from incorrect training data, plagiarism, use in surveillance systems, and the risk to human existence from superintelligent systems.

The topic-level [artificial intelligence study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-computer-science-artificial-intelligence/) covers all of topic 3.16 in one pass. This page gives benefits and risks their own space, with a worked example for each and a method for writing balanced extended answers. ## Benefits

### Improved and more consistent decision making

A person's decisions vary with tiredness, mood and distraction. An AI system applies the same learned rules to every case, so it is **consistent**, and where it is well trained it can be **more accurate**. For example, more accurate analysis of medical data.

**Worked example 1.** Suppose human specialists correctly classify 92% of 500 scans and an AI system classifies 96%. The specialists are right on 460 scans, the AI on 480, so the AI is right on 20 more. A consistent system also gives the same answer for the same scan on a Friday afternoon as on a Monday morning.

### Ability to analyse very large data sets quickly

**Worked example 2.** A reviewer can check 40 records per hour. For 1,000,000 records that is 1,000,000 / 40 = 25,000 hours, which is 12.5 years of full-time work at 2,000 hours per year. A system that processes 500 records per second takes 1,000,000 / 500 = 2,000 seconds, about **33 minutes**. This makes it possible to look for patterns in data that no team of people could ever examine.

### Continuous availability

An AI system does not need breaks or sleep. A week has 168 hours, while a person on a 40-hour working week is available for 40, so the system is available 168 / 40 = **4.2 times** as long. Customers can get a response at 3 a.m., and monitoring systems can watch for problems all the time.

### Lower cost of operation

**Worked example 3.** Suppose handling a case costs £4.00 with a person and £0.05 with an AI system. For 100,000 cases the cost is £400,000 against £5,000, a saving of **£395,000**. The system costs money to develop and maintain, so the saving must be set against that, but the running cost per case is far lower.

### Available to more people and in more places

Human expertise is scarce in some areas. An AI system that can screen medical images, translate languages or tutor students can be used on a phone or a local computer, so people in remote places, or those who cannot afford a specialist, can get help they would otherwise not have.

## Risks

### Elimination of jobs and its social impact

If AI can do a task, employers may need fewer people to do it. **Worked example 4.** A call centre has 200 staff. If an AI system handles 60% of calls, the work of 200 × 0.6 = **120** staff is no longer needed, leaving 80. The social impact includes unemployment, falling incomes in affected communities, and a need to retrain. New jobs may be created in building and supervising AI, but they may need different skills and may not appear in the same places.

### Bias in decision making

An AI system trained on biased data produces biased decisions, which could be based on characteristics such as **gender or race**.

**Worked example 5.** In past hiring records, 60 of 120 male applicants were hired (50%) and 16 of 80 female applicants (20%). A system trained to copy those decisions learns that being male predicts being hired. Its selection rate for women would be 0.2 / 0.5 = 40% of the rate for men, even if the applicants were equally well qualified. The bias is hard to spot because the system appears objective.

### False information from incorrect training data

If the data used to train a system is incorrect, the system can **output false information**, and it may state it with great confidence. **Worked example 6.** If a system answers 50,000 questions a day and 2% of its answers contain errors, then 50,000 × 0.02 = **1,000** wrong answers are given every day, or 365,000 a year. Users who trust the output may spread the mistakes.

### Plagiarism

Generative AI can produce essays, code or images quickly. A person who presents that output as their own work commits **plagiarism**. The output may also closely copy the work of the people whose material was used for training, without credit. This makes it harder to judge a person's own ability, so schools, universities and employers must change how they assess work.

### Use in surveillance systems

AI can identify faces, track movements and flag behaviour across many cameras. **Worked example 7.** 2,000 cameras running for 24 hours produce 48,000 hours of footage every day. Watching it all in real time would need 48,000 / 8 = 6,000 people on 8-hour shifts, which is unrealistic, but an AI system can do it. This brings safety benefits, such as finding a missing child, but also risks to **privacy** and freedom, particularly if used to monitor people without their knowledge, and misidentifications can lead to innocent people being wrongly suspected.

### Risk to human existence from superintelligent systems

Some people warn that if AI systems become **superintelligent**, far beyond human ability, they could pursue goals that conflict with human interests and be difficult to control. Because general intelligence remains a research area, this is a possible future risk rather than a present one, and experts disagree about how likely it is. You should state it, explain it briefly, and note that it is a long-term concern.

## Writing a balanced answer

Extended-answer questions ask you to **discuss** benefits and risks. A strong answer:

1. names the application or scenario in the question;
2. gives at least two benefits, each with a reason or example;
3. gives at least two risks, each with a reason or example;
4. reaches a short **conclusion** that weighs them up, for example stating that the benefits are greatest when the system supports human decision makers and is trained on representative data.

| Weak statement | Stronger statement |
|---|---|
| "AI is faster." | "AI can analyse a million records in about half an hour, where a person would need years, so patterns can be found that people could not find." |
| "AI is biased." | "If the training data under-represents a group, the system performs worse for that group and can repeat past unfairness." |
| "AI takes jobs." | "If AI handles most calls, fewer call-centre staff are needed, so there may be unemployment unless people are retrained." |

## Exam technique

- **Use the wording of the specification**: consistent decision making, very large data sets, continuous availability, cost, access to expertise, jobs, bias, false information, plagiarism, surveillance, superintelligence.
- **Link every point to a reason.** A bare list earns little.
- **Balance** your answer unless the question asks for one side only.
- **Link risks to causes**: for example, false information arises from incorrect training data.

## Common mistakes

- Treating AI output as always correct because it is consistent.
- Describing bias as caused by the computer "having opinions" rather than by the data.
- Writing only about jobs or only about benefits.
- Saying that AI will inevitably destroy humanity. The risk is a possible future concern about superintelligent systems.
