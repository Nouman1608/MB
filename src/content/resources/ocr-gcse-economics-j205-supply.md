This guide covers section 2.3, Supply, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019, in component J205/01. The specification is not tiered. All farms, firms and figures are invented.

The [supply, price elasticity of supply and price guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-supply-price-elasticity-of-supply-and-price/) covers 2.3 together with 2.4. This page goes further on supply alone: why the curve slopes upwards, building market supply from individual producers, every shift factor with its mechanism, consequences in figures, and price elasticity of supply (PES) in the short and long run. Condense it with the [2.3 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-revision-notes/) and practise with the [2.3 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-practice/).

## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain what is meant by supply;
- draw and explain a supply curve using data, including individual and market supply;
- draw shifts of, and movements along, the supply curve;
- analyse the causes and consequences for consumers and producers of shifts of, and movements along, the supply curve;
- explain price elasticity of supply;
- draw supply curves of different elasticity;
- evaluate the importance of price elasticity of supply for consumers and producers.

## What supply means and why the curve slopes upwards

**Supply** is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at each price over a period of time.

A supply curve normally slopes **upwards** from left to right. Two reasons are worth giving together:

1. **Profit incentive:** at a higher price, each unit earns more over its cost, so existing firms want to produce more.
2. **Covering higher costs:** producing extra output may mean overtime pay or less efficient machines; a higher price makes those extra units worth producing, and attracts new firms into the market.

## Individual and market supply from data

**Worked example: strawberries in Elmvale (invented).** Daily supply in punnets from three farms:

| Price (£) | Farm X | Farm Y | Farm Z | Market supply |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 200 | 150 | 50 | 400 |
| 3 | 300 | 200 | 100 | 600 |
| 4 | 400 | 250 | 150 | 800 |
| 5 | 500 | 300 | 200 | 1,000 |
| 6 | 600 | 350 | 250 | 1,200 |

Market supply is the **sum of individual supplies at each price**: at £4, 400 + 250 + 150 = 800.

To draw it: price on the vertical axis, quantity supplied on the horizontal axis, both labelled with units; plot the five points; join them; label the line **S**. If a farm leaves the market, its quantities are removed at every price, so market supply shifts left.

## Movements along and shifts

- A change in the good's **own price** causes a **movement along** the supply curve: an extension (price rises, more supplied) or a contraction (price falls, less supplied).
- A change in a **condition of supply** shifts the whole curve: right for an increase, left for a decrease.

| Condition | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Costs of production rise (wages, materials, energy) | Shift left | Less profit at every price |
| New technology or higher productivity | Shift right | Lower cost per unit |
| Indirect tax on the product | Shift left | Acts like an extra cost per unit |
| Subsidy to producers | Shift right | Lowers firms' costs |
| More firms enter the market | Shift right | More sellers at every price |
| Bad weather or disease (farming) | Shift left | Less output can physically be produced |

Every cause in the table works through **cost or capacity**. Saying *why* the curve shifts, not only which way, is what turns a statement into an explanation.

## Consequences in figures

The Elmvale strawberry market has this demand:

| Price (£) | Demand | Supply before frost | Supply after frost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1,200 | 400 | 0 |
| 3 | 1,000 | 600 | 200 |
| 4 | 800 | 800 | 400 |
| 5 | 600 | 1,000 | 600 |
| 6 | 400 | 1,200 | 800 |

- Before: equilibrium at **£4** and **800** punnets; growers' revenue = 4 × 800 = £3,200.
- A late frost cuts supply by 400 at every price. At £4 there is now a shortage of 800 − 400 = 400.
- The price rises to **£5**, where demand and supply both equal **600**.

```
Price change    = (5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = +25%
Quantity change = (600 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = −25%
Revenue after   = 5 × 600 = £3,000
```

**Consumers** pay 25% more and buy 25% fewer punnets. **Producers** as a group earn £200 less, and individual growers whose crops were damaged lose most; growers whose fields escaped the frost gain from the higher price.

The chain to use: **cause → curve and direction → shortage or surplus at the old price → new equilibrium → who gains and who loses.**

## Price elasticity of supply

**PES** measures how responsive the quantity supplied is to a change in price.

```
PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price
```

PES is normally **positive**, because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction.

| Value | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic: quantity cannot change |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic |
| 1 | Unitary |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic |
| Infinite | Perfectly elastic |

**Worked example: a furniture maker (invented).** The price of a table rises from £200 to £220 (+10%).

```
Short run: output rises from 500 to 520 a month (+4%)
PES = 4 ÷ 10 = 0.4    (inelastic)

Long run: after hiring and training staff, output rises to 600 (+20%)
PES = 20 ÷ 10 = 2.0   (elastic)
```

The same firm has a different PES depending on how long it has to respond.

### What decides PES

- **Time:** in the short run, firms are limited by their existing workers, machines and space; given time, they can expand.
- **Spare capacity:** a factory working below its full output can produce more quickly.
- **Stocks:** goods that can be stored, such as tinned food, can be released from stock when price rises; perishable goods cannot.
- **Ease of switching factors:** if workers and machines can move easily from another product, supply is more elastic.
- **Production time:** goods that take years to produce, such as timber from new forests, are inelastic in the short run.

### Drawing supply curves of different elasticity

- **Perfectly inelastic:** vertical, such as the seats in a stadium on match day.
- **Perfectly elastic:** horizontal at one price.
- **Relatively inelastic:** steep; **relatively elastic:** shallow.
- A straight supply line drawn **through the origin** has a PES of 1 at every point. Elmvale's market supply is like this: from £4 to £5 (+25%), supply rises from 800 to 1,000 (+25%).

Draw on the same axes and scale, and mark the same price change on each.

## Why PES matters

**For producers:** a firm with elastic supply can take advantage of a rise in demand by producing more, gaining revenue and market share. A firm with inelastic supply cannot respond quickly, so rivals or importers may win the extra sales.

**For consumers:** when supply is inelastic, a rise in demand mostly raises the **price**, not the quantity, so consumers pay more; seasonal fruit and concert tickets are examples. When supply is elastic, a rise in demand is met mainly by more output, so prices stay closer to their old level.

**Evaluation points:** PES changes over time, so a short-run shortage may ease as firms expand. Firms can make supply more elastic by holding stocks or keeping spare capacity, but both cost money: stored goods tie up cash, and idle machines earn nothing. Whether that cost is worth paying depends on how often demand changes.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting the supply curve when only the good's own price changed.
- Explaining a shift with no mechanism ("supply falls because of the tax").
- Giving PES a negative sign.
- Saying perfectly inelastic supply means "no supply"; it means the quantity cannot change.
- Forgetting the time period when discussing PES.
- Analysing consumers only when the question names producers too.

## Exam technique

- **Draw:** label axes, S1 and S2, both equilibrium prices and quantities, and show the direction of the shift with an arrow.
- **Calculate PES:** show both percentage changes, divide, then classify.
- **Analyse:** follow the cause-to-consequence chain and use figures from the question.
- **Evaluate PES:** contrast short and long run, and consider the cost of holding stocks or spare capacity.
