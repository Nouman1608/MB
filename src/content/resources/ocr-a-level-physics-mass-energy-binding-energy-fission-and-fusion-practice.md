Data: c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, equivalent to 931.5 MeV; 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹; 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²; mass of an electron = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

## Questions

**1.** Define the mass defect and the binding energy of a nucleus. **[2]**

**2.** In a nuclear reaction the total mass of the products is 3.6 × 10⁻²⁹ kg less than the total mass of the reactants. Calculate the energy released, in joules and in MeV. **[3]**

**3.** A photon passes close to a nucleus and may produce an electron-positron pair.

**(a)** Calculate the energy of a photon of wavelength 1.5 × 10⁻¹² m. **[2]**
**(b)** Show that this photon cannot produce an electron-positron pair. **[2]**
**(c)** A photon of wavelength 0.80 × 10⁻¹² m does produce a pair. Assuming the nucleus takes negligible energy and the two particles share the rest equally, calculate the kinetic energy of each particle. **[3]**

**4.** The atomic mass of aluminium-27 is 26.981539 u. A hydrogen atom has mass 1.007825 u and a neutron 1.008665 u. Aluminium-27 has 13 protons. Calculate the binding energy per nucleon of aluminium-27 in MeV. **[4]**

**5.** Describe the main features of the curve of binding energy per nucleon plotted against nucleon number, and use it to explain why both fusion and fission release energy. **[5]**

**6.** A fission reaction is ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴⁰₅₅Cs + ⁹³₃₇Rb + x ¹₀n. The binding energy per nucleon is 7.59 MeV for U-235, 8.26 MeV for Cs-140 and 8.52 MeV for Rb-93.

**(a)** Find x. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy released by one fission, in MeV. **[4]**
**(c)** Express this energy in joules. **[1]**

**7.** Complete the equations.

**(a)** ²³⁹₉₂U → ?Np + ⁰₋₁e + antineutrino, giving the proton number of neptunium. **[2]**
**(b)** ²₁H + ³₂He → ⁴₂He + ? **[2]**

**8.** This question is about a thermal fission reactor.

**(a)** Describe the function of the moderator and name a suitable material. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the function of the control rods and how they are used to reduce the reaction rate. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why, in a steady reactor, each fission must lead on average to exactly one further fission. **[2]**

**9.** A reactor has a thermal power of 2.4 × 10⁹ W. Each fission releases 190 MeV.

**(a)** Calculate the energy released per fission in joules. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of fissions per second. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the mass of uranium-235 fissioned in one day (1 day = 8.64 × 10⁴ s). Take the mass of one nucleus as 235 u. **[3]**

**10.** Discuss the environmental impact of nuclear waste from fission reactors. **[5]**

**11.** Two deuterium nuclei, each of charge +e, are brought head-on to a separation of 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.

**(a)** Calculate their electric potential energy. **[2]**
**(b)** Assuming each nucleus supplies half this energy as kinetic energy, estimate the temperature at which the mean kinetic energy of a nucleus is (3/2)kT. **[3]**
**(c)** The temperature at the centre of the Sun is about 1.5 × 10⁷ K. Comment on your answer. **[1]**

**12.** Deuterium and helium-3 can fuse: ²₁H + ³₂He → ⁴₂He + ¹₁H. Atomic masses: H-2 2.014102 u, He-3 3.016029 u, He-4 4.002603 u, H-1 1.007825 u. Total binding energies: H-2 2.22 MeV, He-3 7.72 MeV, He-4 28.3 MeV.

**(a)** Show that the equation balances. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy released using the masses. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the energy released using the binding energies. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why this reaction needs a very high temperature although energy is released. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Mass defect: the total mass of the free protons and neutrons less the mass of the nucleus [1]. Binding energy: the energy required to pull the nucleus apart into free nucleons (equal to the mass defect × c²) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say "free nucleons" and "pull apart"; a vague phrase such as "energy holding the nucleus together" is easy to misread.

**2.** E = Δmc² = 3.6 × 10⁻²⁹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² [1]; = 3.24 × 10⁻¹² J [1]; ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 20 MeV (20.25) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the conversion from joules to MeV as a separate line; the unit is part of the answer.

**3. (a)** E = hc/λ [1] = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ / 1.5 × 10⁻¹² = **1.33 × 10⁻¹³ J** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Minimum energy needed = 2mc² = 2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J [1]. The photon has less than this (1.33 × 10⁻¹³ J), so it cannot create a pair [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** E = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ / 0.80 × 10⁻¹² = 2.49 × 10⁻¹³ J [1]; energy left for motion = 2.49 × 10⁻¹³ − 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ = 8.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ J [1]; each particle **4.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ J** (about 0.26 MeV) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* In a "show that" question, state the threshold value and make the comparison explicitly; a calculation without the conclusion leaves the point unmade.

**4.** Mass of constituents = 13 × 1.007825 + 14 × 1.008665 = 13.101725 + 14.12131 = 27.223035 u [1]. Mass defect = 27.223035 − 26.981539 = 0.241496 u [1]. Binding energy = 0.241496 × 931.5 = 225 MeV [1]. Per nucleon = 225 / 27 = **8.33 MeV** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair hydrogen atom masses with an atomic mass of the nuclide; mixing nuclear and atomic masses gives a wrong mass defect.

**5.** Binding energy per nucleon rises steeply for small nucleon numbers, with a local peak at helium-4 [1]. It reaches a maximum of about 8.8 MeV near A = 56 (iron) [1]. It then falls slowly for heavier nuclei [1]. In fusion, light nuclei join to give a nucleus with a higher binding energy per nucleon [1]. In fission, a heavy nucleus splits into fragments nearer the peak with a higher binding energy per nucleon; in both cases total binding energy increases, so energy is released [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the direction of movement on the curve to the release of energy; naming the peak alone does not explain the process.

**6. (a)** 235 + 1 = 140 + 93 + x, so **x = 3** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Cs-140: 140 × 8.26 = 1156.4 MeV [1]; Rb-93: 93 × 8.52 = 792.4 MeV [1]; U-235: 235 × 7.59 = 1783.7 MeV [1]; energy released = 1948.8 − 1783.7 = **165 MeV** [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** 165 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = **2.64 × 10⁻¹¹ J** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Say which total is subtracted from which; a negative energy released should prompt a check.

**7. (a)** Proton number rises by 1 [1]; neptunium has proton number **93**, ²³⁹₉₃Np [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Nucleon number 5 − 4 = 1 and proton number 3 − 2 = 1 [1]; the missing particle is a proton, **¹₁H** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both the nucleon number and proton number checks in your working.

**8. (a)** It slows fast neutrons [1] by collisions with its light nuclei, so they are more likely to cause fission; water or graphite [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** They absorb neutrons [1]; lowering them further into the core absorbs more neutrons and reduces the rate [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** If more than one, the number of fissions grows and the reaction runs away [1]; if fewer than one, the chain dies away, so exactly one keeps the power constant [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the roles separate: "slows neutrons" belongs to the moderator and "absorbs neutrons" to the control rods.

**9. (a)** 190 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = **3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ J** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Rate = power / energy per fission [1] = 2.4 × 10⁹ / 3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ = **7.9 × 10¹⁹ s⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Mass of nucleus = 235 × 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.90 × 10⁻²⁵ kg [1]; mass per second = 7.89 × 10¹⁹ × 3.90 × 10⁻²⁵ = 3.08 × 10⁻⁵ kg [1]; per day = 3.08 × 10⁻⁵ × 8.64 × 10⁴ = **2.7 kg** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Carry extra digits and round only at the end.

**10.** Spent fuel and fission products are radioactive and emit ionising radiation [1]; some nuclides have half-lives of thousands of years or more, so waste must be kept secure for a very long time [1]; management includes cooling ponds, sealing in glass and deep underground storage in stable rock [1]; a benefit is that the waste volume is small and fission releases no carbon dioxide in operation [1]; a reasoned judgement that weighs the long-term risk against these benefits [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A discussion question rewards both sides and a conclusion; a list of hazards alone is one-sided.

**11. (a)** U = (1/4πε₀) × e² / r [1] = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² / 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ = **7.7 × 10⁻¹⁴ J** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Each nucleus has 7.67 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 2 = 3.84 × 10⁻¹⁴ J [1]; (3/2)kT = 3.84 × 10⁻¹⁴ [1]; T = 2 × 3.84 × 10⁻¹⁴ / (3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³) = **1.9 × 10⁹ K** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** The estimate is far higher than the Sun's core temperature, because only the faster nuclei need this energy, so fusion occurs even though the mean energy is lower [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the two numbers and offer a reason.

**12. (a)** Nucleon number 2 + 3 = 5 = 4 + 1; proton number 1 + 2 = 3 = 2 + 1 [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Before = 2.014102 + 3.016029 = 5.030131 u [1]; after = 4.002603 + 1.007825 = 5.010428 u [1]; Δm = 0.019703 u, energy = 0.019703 × 931.5 = **18.4 MeV** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Binding energy before = 2.22 + 7.72 = 9.94 MeV; after = 28.3 MeV (a proton has none) [1]; released = 28.3 − 9.94 = **18.4 MeV**, agreeing with (b) [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Both nuclei are positively charged, so they repel [1]; they need very high kinetic energy, and so a very high temperature, to come close enough for the strong nuclear force to act [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* When two methods are asked for, state that they agree; it shows the answer has been checked.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to convert u or grams to kilograms before using ΔE = Δmc².
- Giving one photon instead of two for annihilation, or not showing the 2mc² threshold.
- Dividing binding energy by proton number instead of nucleon number.
- Mixing atomic and nuclear masses in a mass-defect sum.
- Subtracting in the wrong order, giving a negative energy released.
- Confusing the roles of moderator and control rods.
- Writing a waste answer with only hazards, or with no reason for the claim.
