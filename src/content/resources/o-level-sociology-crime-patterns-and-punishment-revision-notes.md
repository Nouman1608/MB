These notes condense key questions 6.2 and 6.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251). Recorded crime
is highest among young, working-class men and varies by ethnicity; explanations range from strain
and subculture to labelling, power and patriarchy; and societies use punishments with different aims
and policing strategies of varying effectiveness. Always ask whether patterns show offending or
policing.

Fuller explanations: [O Level patterns of crime and how societies deal with it guide](/resources/o-level-sociology-crime-patterns-and-punishment/).

## Syllabus coverage

2251 syllabus 2025–2027: **6.2** (6.2.1 patterns; 6.2.2–6.2.5 explanations by age, ethnicity,
gender, class; 6.2.6 theories; 6.2.7 evaluation) and **6.3** (6.3.1 aims; 6.3.2 punishments;
6.3.3 policing).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Recorded crime** | Crime known to and recorded by the police |
| **Victimisation** | Being the victim of a crime |
| **Anomie** | Normlessness when goals and means are out of balance (Merton) |
| **Innovation** | Using illegitimate means to reach accepted goals (Merton) |
| **Focal concerns** | Lower-class values such as toughness and excitement (Miller, 1958) |
| **Deviant career** | Progression into deviance after a label sticks (Becker) |
| **Folk devil** | Group blamed during a moral panic |
| **Crimes of the powerful** | Corporate and state crime (Box, 1983) |
| **Seductions of crime** | Thrill and emotion as motives for crime (Katz, 1988) |
| **Chivalry thesis** | Lenient treatment of women by the justice system (Pollak, 1950) |
| **Restraining order** | Court order forbidding contact with a person |
| **Probation** | Supervision in the community instead of or after prison |

## 6.2 Explanations summary

| Group | Syllabus explanations | Named evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Police targeting; self-fulfilling prophecy; stereotyping; gangs; weak control; lack of opportunity; status frustration | A. Cohen (1955) |

## 6.2.6 Theories — one line each

- **Strain (Merton, 1938):** goals–means gap → five adaptations; innovation = crime.
- **Subculture (A. Cohen, 1955):** status frustration → delinquent subculture.
- **New Right (Murray):** underclass, inadequate socialisation.
- **Labelling (Becker, 1963):** label → master status → self-fulfilling prophecy → deviant career.
- **Moral panic (S. Cohen, 1972):** media → folk devils → more control → amplification.
- **Marxism:** law protects capitalism; crimes of the powerful under-punished (Box, 1983).
- **Feminism:** patriarchal control (Heidensohn); class and gender deals (Carlen).
- **Postmodernism:** thrill, identity and consumerism (Katz, 1988); diverse, individual crime.

## 6.3 Punishment and policing

**Aims:** protection; deterrence; retribution; incapacitation; rehabilitation.

| Punishment | Main aim | Key evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Prison | Incapacitation | High reoffending |
| Rehabilitation | Rehabilitation | Halden, Norway (2010) cited for humane approach |
| Community sentence | Reparation | Cheaper, less deterrent |
| Fines | Deterrence | Unequal impact |
| Capital punishment | Retribution | Irreversible |
| Corporal punishment | Deterrence | Human rights concerns; e.g. caning in Singapore |
| Tagging, curfews, restraining orders | Control | Rely on monitoring |
| Probation, cautions | Rehabilitation | Keep offenders in work |
| Exile, ostracism, social media shaming | Protection, shame | No due process |

**Policing:** stop and search; surveillance and digital surveillance; crime prevention; community vs
military-style policing.

## Worked example: a 6-mark "explain" answer

*Explain three reasons why the chivalry thesis may not explain gender differences in crime.*
(practice)

1. Women may receive *harsher* treatment when they break gender norms — for example being judged as
   bad mothers as well as offenders (2).
2. The gender gap also appears in self-report studies, which do not depend on police or courts (2).
3. Other explanations, such as greater social control of women (Heidensohn) or the culture of
   masculinity, explain the gap without assuming leniency (2).

## Worked example: a 14-mark evaluation plan

*Evaluate the view that crime is mainly committed by young people.* (practice)

1. **For:** recorded offending peaks in the teenage years and early twenties; young people have weaker
   ties to work and family; status frustration (A. Cohen) and focal concerns such as excitement
   (Miller) apply mainly to youth; gang culture offers status.
2. **Against:** statistics reflect police targeting and stop and search of young people in public
   spaces; labelling and moral panics exaggerate youth crime (S. Cohen); white-collar and corporate
   crimes are committed mostly by older adults and are under-recorded (Box); domestic crime is
   committed across all ages.
3. **Conclusion:** young people are over-represented in *recorded* street and property crime, but the
   claim ignores hidden crimes of older and more powerful groups.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Confusing **Albert Cohen** (status frustration) and **Stanley Cohen** (moral panics).
- Presenting statistics as fact without questioning recording and policing.
- Describing punishments without saying whether they achieve their **aims**.
- Using only functionalist theories for class explanations — include Marxism and power.
- Forgetting **victimisation** patterns in 6.2.1.

## Quick self-test

1. List Merton's five adaptations.
2. What did Box argue about crimes of the powerful?
3. Explain one postmodern explanation of crime.
4. Name the five aims of punishment.
5. Give one strength and one weakness of digital surveillance.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I evaluate a theory?** Say what it explains well, what it fails to explain, and how a
competing theory fills the gap.

**Is rehabilitation more effective than prison?** Evidence such as lower reoffending in rehabilitative
systems suggests it can be, but results depend on resources and the offender.

**Which explanations fit my own society?** Apply the syllabus explanations to local patterns, and
note where data on crime may be incomplete.
