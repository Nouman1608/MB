This guide covers section 3.1.4, Stakeholders, of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017, in Topic 1, Business in the real world. The [study guide for 3.1.3 and 3.1.4](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-setting-business-aims-and-objectives-and-stakeholders/) introduces the five main stakeholder groups, their objectives and a worked example of a pay conflict. This page goes further: it separates stakeholders from shareholders, traces one business decision through every group, puts a conflict into figures, explains the specific ways each group can influence a business, and shows how to write a stakeholder evaluation.

Use it with the [stakeholders revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-stakeholders-revision-notes/) and the [stakeholders practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-stakeholders-practice/).

## What the specification asks

Students should be able to:

- understand what is meant by a stakeholder and who the main stakeholders of a business are, including **owners, employees, customers, local community and suppliers**
- understand stakeholders' main objectives, including **maximising pay for workers, minimising environmental impact on the local community and high dividend payments for owners**
- understand the impact and influence stakeholders have on businesses and their objectives, and how businesses may face **conflict between stakeholders**

## Stakeholders and shareholders

A **stakeholder** is any individual or group with an interest in a business because it affects them or they can affect it. A **shareholder** is one particular kind of stakeholder: an owner of shares in a company. Every shareholder is a stakeholder, but most stakeholders (employees, customers, suppliers, neighbours) own no shares at all. Mixing up the two words is a frequent and costly slip.

It can help to sort stakeholders into **internal** groups, who are part of the business (owners and employees), and **external** groups outside it (customers, suppliers and the local community). Internal stakeholders usually have more day-to-day influence; external ones can still have great influence when they act together.

## Tracing one decision through every group

Exam cases often describe one decision and ask how it affects stakeholders. Work through each group in turn.

**Case:** Fellbrook Foods (fictional), a sauce maker, plans to add a night shift at its town-edge factory so it can supply a supermarket chain.

| Stakeholder | Likely gain | Likely loss |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Higher sales and profit; possible higher dividends | Risk if the supermarket contract ends |
| Employees | More jobs; overtime pay for existing staff | Unsocial hours; tiredness and family strain |
| Customers | Products more widely available in supermarkets | Little direct loss |
| Suppliers | Larger, regular orders for tomatoes, jars and labels | Pressure to deliver more often, possibly at lower prices |
| Local community | Jobs and spending in local shops | Lorries and noise at night; light pollution |

Two points lift an answer. First, the same group can gain and lose at once, so say which effect is likely to be stronger for them. Second, link the impact back to the business: angry neighbours may object to the night shift, which could delay the plan.

## Putting a conflict into figures

Conflicts are easier to judge when you can measure them. The specification names three objectives that often clash: maximising pay for workers, minimising environmental impact on the local community, and high dividend payments for owners.

### Worked example: a price rise

Fellbrook Foods sells 20,000 jars a month at £5.00 each. The variable cost of each jar is £2.00. It considers raising the price to £5.50 and expects sales to fall to 18,500 jars.

```
Before: revenue = 20,000 × £5.00 = £100,000
        variable costs = 20,000 × £2.00 = £40,000
        revenue − variable costs = £60,000
After:  revenue = 18,500 × £5.50 = £101,750
        variable costs = 18,500 × £2.00 = £37,000
        revenue − variable costs = £64,750
Fixed costs do not change, so profit rises by £64,750 − £60,000 = £4,750 a month
```

**Owners** gain £4,750 a month, which could fund higher dividends. **Customers** pay 50p more per jar, and 1,500 of them a month stop buying. **Suppliers** sell fewer jars and tomatoes. **Employees** may be unaffected, or need fewer hours if output falls. The calculation shows who wins and who loses and by how much, which turns a vague statement ("customers will be unhappy") into evidence.

### Worked example: paying suppliers later

A business that changes its payment terms from 30 to 60 days keeps cash for longer, which helps it pay its own bills. Its suppliers wait a month longer for their money, which may leave a small supplier short of cash. The business gains; the supplier loses. If the supplier is the only source of a key ingredient, it may respond by raising prices or giving priority to other customers, so the business's gain can be short-lived.

## How each group exerts influence

The specification asks about the influence stakeholders have on businesses and their objectives. Name the method, not just "they can complain".

| Stakeholder | Ways of influencing the business |
|---|---|
| Owners | Vote at shareholder meetings, appoint or remove directors, decide on dividends and objectives |
| Employees | Effort and productivity, ideas, joining a trade union, taking industrial action, leaving for other jobs |
| Customers | Choosing whether to buy, switching to rivals, writing reviews, boycotting |
| Suppliers | Setting prices, quality and delivery times, offering or refusing credit |
| Local community | Objecting to planning applications, campaigning, local media coverage, using or avoiding local businesses |

**Strength of influence** depends on how much power a group has and how much it cares. A customer in a market with many rivals has power because switching is easy. A group of residents has little influence alone but more when it organises a campaign or a planning objection. A supplier of a rare ingredient has more power than one of many sellers of a common one.

## How stakeholders shape objectives

Stakeholders do not only react to decisions; they change what businesses aim for. Pressure from customers and local communities to cut waste and emissions is one reason many businesses adopt social and ethical objectives. Shareholders in a plc push directors towards shareholder value. Employees who are hard to recruit can push a business to improve pay and conditions to keep them. Link this to [setting business aims and objectives](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-setting-business-aims-and-objectives/).

## Handling conflict

Businesses usually cannot satisfy every group fully. Options include:

- **prioritising** the group with most power or most at stake for the decision
- **compromising**, such as a smaller pay rise phased over two years
- **consulting** before deciding, so groups feel heard and objections surface early
- **reducing the harm**, such as limiting lorry movements at night or paying for noise barriers

## Exam technique

- **Identify** a stakeholder: name the group, not a person ("employees", not "Jo").
- **Explain** an impact: decision → effect on the group → why it matters to the business.
- **Analyse** a conflict: show what each group wants and why the decision helps one and harms the other, using figures if given.
- **Evaluate** a decision with stakeholder views: weigh the most important groups, state who should be prioritised and why, and suggest how to reduce the harm to others.

## Common mistakes

- Using "shareholder" when you mean "stakeholder".
- Listing stakeholders without saying how the decision affects each one.
- Describing only how the business affects stakeholders, not how they influence it.
- Saying a group "will be unhappy" without a consequence for the business.
- Claiming a business can always keep everyone happy.
