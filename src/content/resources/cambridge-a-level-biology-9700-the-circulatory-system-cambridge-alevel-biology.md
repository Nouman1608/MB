## Closed and double: what each word buys

- **Closed** means blood stays inside vessels. Pressure can therefore be kept high, and flow can be directed to particular organs by widening or narrowing vessels.
- **Double** means blood passes through the heart twice in one complete circuit. Blood returning from the lungs is pumped again before it goes to the body.

Why double is an advantage: lung capillaries are delicate and need a **low** pressure, but the body needs a **high** pressure to push blood through many narrow vessels a long way from the heart. Two separate pumps (right and left sides of the heart) let each circuit run at its own pressure.

### The four named vessels as a route

Vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → **pulmonary artery** → lungs → **pulmonary vein** → left atrium → left ventricle → **aorta** → body → vena cava.

Learn the functions as "from, to, and what it carries". The pulmonary artery is the only artery in this list carrying deoxygenated blood, and the pulmonary vein the only vein carrying oxygenated blood.

## How pressure and speed change around the systemic circuit

| Vessel | Blood pressure | Pressure fluctuates with each beat? | Speed of flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic artery (aorta) | Highest | Yes, strongly | Fast |
| Muscular artery | High | Yes, less | Fast |
| Arteriole | Falls steeply | Fluctuations die away | Slowing |
| Capillary | Low | No | Slowest |
| Venule and vein | Lowest | No | Speeds up again |

Two ideas explain the table. First, every vessel resists flow, and the many narrow **arterioles** cause the biggest drop in pressure. Second, although each capillary is tiny, there are so many that their total cross-sectional area is far larger than that of the aorta, so blood moves through them slowly, giving time for exchange.

## Structure and function: the reasons behind each feature

### Elastic arteries

Elastic arteries, such as the aorta and pulmonary artery, receive blood straight from the ventricles. Their thick tunica media contains many **elastic fibres**. During ventricular systole the wall stretches, storing some of the energy; during diastole it recoils, pushing blood on. Result: pressure stays high between beats and the surge is evened out. Collagen in the outer layer stops the wall over-stretching.

### Muscular arteries and arterioles

Further from the heart, arteries carry blood to particular organs. Their tunica media has relatively more **smooth muscle**. When it contracts the lumen narrows (vasoconstriction) and less blood flows to that organ; when it relaxes the lumen widens. **Arterioles** have the same muscle, so they decide which capillary beds receive blood at a given moment, for example more to the skeletal muscles during exercise.

### Capillaries

- Wall of **endothelium only**, one cell thick: short diffusion distance.
- **Gaps between endothelial cells**: let plasma (and some white blood cells) leave to form tissue fluid.
- **Narrow lumen**, about the width of a red blood cell: cells pass in single file, close to the wall, so oxygen diffuses only a short distance.
- Huge numbers forming dense networks: large surface area, and every cell is close to a capillary.

### Veins and venules

**Venules** collect blood from capillary beds and join to form **veins**. Blood pressure in veins is too low to push blood back to the heart unaided, so:

- **Wide lumen**: little resistance to flow.
- **Thin wall** with little muscle or elastic tissue: no high pressure to withstand, and the wall can be squeezed by surrounding skeletal muscles, pushing blood along.
- **Valves**: let blood flow only towards the heart, so squeezing moves it in one direction.

## Recognising vessels and drawing plan diagrams

| Clue in an image | Artery | Vein | Capillary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overall shape in TS | Round, holds its shape | Often flattened or irregular | Tiny ring |
| Wall relative to lumen | Thick wall, small lumen | Thin wall, large lumen | Wall one cell thick |
| Endothelium | Often folded (wavy) | Smooth | Single layer |
| Seen in LS | Parallel thick walls | Valve flaps may be visible | Red blood cells in single file |

A plan diagram of an artery or vein shows the boundaries of the layers (tunica intima, tunica media, tunica externa) in the correct proportions, with no cells and no shading. In LS, draw the walls as two parallel bands and, for a vein, include any valve visible.

### Worked example 1: comparing wall to lumen

In a photomicrograph at × 40, an artery has a wall 5.2 mm thick and a lumen 8.0 mm across; a vein beside it has a wall 1.6 mm thick and a lumen 20.0 mm across.

```
artery wall : lumen = 5.2 ÷ 8.0  = 0.65
vein   wall : lumen = 1.6 ÷ 20.0 = 0.08
actual artery wall = 5.2 mm ÷ 40 = 0.13 mm = 130 µm
```

The artery's ratio is about eight times higher, matching its need to withstand high pressure. Ratios do not depend on magnification.

## Recognising and drawing blood cells

| Cell | Size compared with a red blood cell | Nucleus | Cytoplasm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red blood cell | — | None | Pale centre (biconcave disc) |
| Lymphocyte | Slightly larger | Large, round, fills most of the cell | Thin rim |
| Neutrophil | About twice the diameter | Lobed, several lobes joined by thin strands | Granular |
| Monocyte | Largest | Large, kidney-shaped | Plenty, not obviously granular |

When drawing, show nucleus shape accurately and keep relative sizes right.

### Worked example 2: identifying a cell by size

On a blood smear photograph at × 2000, a red blood cell measures 15 mm across and an unknown white cell 24 mm across with a lobed nucleus.

```
red blood cell = 15 000 µm ÷ 2000 = 7.5 µm
white cell     = 24 000 µm ÷ 2000 = 12 µm
ratio          = 12 ÷ 7.5 = 1.6
```

About one and a half to two times the diameter of a red blood cell, with a lobed nucleus: a **neutrophil**.

## Water as the transport medium

Water is the main component of both blood plasma and tissue fluid. The syllabus limits the link to two properties.

- **Solvent action**: water molecules are polar, so ions and polar molecules (glucose, amino acids, urea, hormones) dissolve and are carried in solution. Hydrogencarbonate ions carry most carbon dioxide this way.
- **High specific heat capacity**: hydrogen bonds between water molecules must be broken to raise its temperature, so a lot of energy causes only a small temperature rise. Blood temperature therefore stays stable, and blood can carry heat from active tissues such as muscle and the liver to the rest of the body.

## Tissue fluid

**Functions**: brings oxygen, glucose, amino acids and other nutrients to cells; takes away carbon dioxide and other wastes; gives cells a stable surrounding environment.

**Formation** is a balance of two effects:

- **Hydrostatic pressure** (blood pressure) pushes fluid **out** through the gaps between endothelial cells.
- **Plasma proteins** stay in the blood, so blood plasma has a **lower water potential** than tissue fluid, and water tends to move **in** by osmosis.

At the **arterial end**, hydrostatic pressure is high enough to outweigh the water potential effect, so fluid leaves. At the **venous end**, hydrostatic pressure has fallen, so the water potential effect wins and much of the water returns. Fluid that does not return enters lymph vessels.

### Worked example 3: when the balance fails

A person's diet contains too little protein, and their plasma protein concentration falls. Suggest and explain the effect on tissue fluid.

- Plasma water potential is less negative than normal (higher).
- Less water returns to the capillaries by osmosis at the venous end.
- Tissue fluid accumulates faster than lymph vessels can drain it, and the tissues swell.

## Common mistakes

- Defining arteries as vessels carrying oxygenated blood.
- Saying veins have "thick walls to withstand pressure" or arteries have valves.
- Saying red blood cells or plasma proteins leave capillaries in tissue fluid.
- Explaining specific heat capacity without saying that blood temperature stays stable.
- Omitting the venous end: the formation description needs both ends.
- Drawing individual cells in a plan diagram of a vessel.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State**: short and exact ("closed double circulation").
- **Describe** (vessel functions): from where, to where, what is carried.
- **Explain** (structure and function): feature + consequence, one pair per mark.
- **Suggest**: apply the tissue fluid balance or vessel features to a new situation.
