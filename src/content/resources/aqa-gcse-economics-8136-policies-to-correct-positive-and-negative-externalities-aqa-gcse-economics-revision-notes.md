These notes cover **section 3.2.3.4 Policies to correct positive and negative externalities** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017, where students should understand "government policies designed to influence positive and negative externalities". They focus on the private-versus-social reasoning behind each policy and on how to choose between policies, adding to the shorter [supply-side and externality policies revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-supply-side-and-externality-policies-revision-notes/). Full explanations are in the [externality policies study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-policies-to-correct-positive-and-negative-externalities-aqa-gcse-economics/); test yourself with the [externality policies practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-policies-to-correct-positive-and-negative-externalities-aqa-gcse-economics-practice/). Figures are invented.

## Condensed recall

- Social cost = private cost + external cost. Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
- Buyers and sellers act on **private** values only.
- **Negative externality:** social cost > private cost → too much is produced or consumed.
- **Positive externality:** social benefit > private benefit → too little is produced or consumed.
- Every policy tries to make the decision-maker act as if they faced the **social** value.
- Policies work in three ways: by changing the **price** (taxes, charges, subsidies, minimum prices), by changing the **rules** (regulation, bans, compulsion, state provision) or by changing **information** (campaigns, labels).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Third party | Someone outside the transaction who is affected by it |
| External cost | A cost to third parties not paid by the producer or consumer |
| External benefit | A benefit to third parties not received by the producer or consumer |
| Social cost | The full cost to society: private plus external |
| Social benefit | The full benefit to society: private plus external |
| Indirect tax | A tax on spending that raises the price of a good |
| Subsidy | A payment by the government that lowers the cost or price of a good |
| State provision | The government supplies the good, often free at the point of use |
| Regulation | Legal rules that limit, ban or require an activity |

## Policy matrix

| | Changes the price | Changes the rules | Changes information |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Negative externality** | Indirect tax; charge for use; minimum price | Ban; limits and standards; fines | Health warnings; campaigns |
| **Positive externality** | Subsidy | State provision; compulsory use; minimum standards | Campaigns that promote the benefits |

## Strengths and weaknesses at a glance

| Policy | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Tax or charge | Raises revenue; polluter pays; choice remains | External cost hard to measure; weak if demand is price inelastic; can be regressive |
| Ban or limit | Certain and clear | Needs enforcement; no revenue; may be too strict for some firms |
| Fine | Deters if large enough | Too small a fine is treated as a cost of doing business |
| Subsidy | Encourages beneficial choices | Costs taxpayers; may go to people who would have bought anyway |
| State provision | Removes price as a barrier | Expensive; funded by taxes |
| Information | Cheap; keeps choice | Slow; people may ignore it |

## Choosing a policy: five questions

1. How **serious** is the harm or benefit?
2. Can the external value be **measured**?
3. How **price elastic** is demand?
4. **Who pays**, and is it fair?
5. Can it be **enforced**, and at what cost?

## Possible side effects

- Waste taxes may lead to illegal dumping.
- High taxes may encourage smuggling.
- Subsidies can be hard to withdraw once firms depend on them.
- Rules set at the wrong level may cost more than the harm they prevent.

## Worked calculations

**1. Social cost.** Private cost £24 a unit; external cost £9. Social cost = **£33**. A tax of **£9** a unit would make the price reflect the social cost.

**2. Is buying worthwhile?** A cycle-to-work scheme: a bike costs £400 and gives the rider a private benefit of £340; the external benefit (less traffic and pollution) is £100. Private view: 340 < 400, so the rider does not buy. Social benefit = £440 > £400, so society gains. A subsidy of at least **£60** makes the price no higher than the private benefit, so the rider buys.

**3. Is a fine high enough?** A firm can pay £8,000 to clean its waste or risk a £5,000 fine. It is cheaper to pay the fine, so the fine does not change behaviour. It must be above **£8,000** (allowing for the chance of being caught, even higher).

**4. Effect of a charge.** A city charges £12.50 a day to drive older vehicles into the centre. Daily trips by those vehicles fall from 60,000 to 48,000, a fall of 12,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **20%**. Daily revenue = 12.50 × 48,000 = **£600,000**.

**5. Cost of state provision.** Providing free vaccines at £40 each to 25,000 people costs **£1,000,000**, paid from taxes.

## Exam traps

- An externality is a cost or benefit to a **third party**, not to the firm or buyer.
- A tax reduces a harmful activity; it does not normally stop it.
- Match the tool to the type: tax for negative, subsidy for positive.
- A fine below the cost of complying does not change behaviour.
- Always say how the policy changes **price**, **rules** or **information**.

## Self-test

1. Write the formula for social cost.
2. Why does a free market over-produce goods with negative externalities?
3. Give one policy that changes the price of a harmful good.
4. Give one policy that changes the rules for a beneficial good.
5. Private cost £50, external cost £15. What tax per unit would reflect the full social cost?
6. A good has a private benefit of £30, an external benefit of £20 and a price of £40. Will a person buy it? Should society want them to?
7. What subsidy would make the person in question 6 buy the good?
8. Why might a tax on a good with price-inelastic demand not cut consumption by much?
9. Give one unintended consequence of a high tax on waste sent to landfill.
10. Why might a government choose a ban rather than a tax?
11. Give one drawback of state provision.
12. Why can a subsidy be unfair?

### Answers

1. Social cost = private cost + external cost.
2. Producers and consumers ignore the external cost, so the price is lower than the full cost to society and more is bought.
3. Any one: indirect tax, charge for use, minimum price.
4. Any one: compulsory use, minimum standards, state provision.
5. £15 a unit.
6. No: the private benefit of £30 is below the £40 price. Yes: the social benefit of £50 exceeds the price.
7. At least £10, bringing the price down to £30 or less.
8. Buyers are not very responsive to price, so the quantity falls only a little; the tax mainly raises revenue.
9. Illegal dumping (fly-tipping).
10. When the harm is so serious that any use is unacceptable, a ban gives certainty that a tax cannot.
11. It is expensive and must be paid for from taxes.
12. It may mainly benefit better-off people who would have bought the good anyway, paid for by all taxpayers.
