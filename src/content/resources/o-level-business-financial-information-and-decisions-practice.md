These questions practise the finance calculations and decisions in Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115: profit margins, return on capital employed, the current ratio, cash-flow forecasts and the people who use accounts. All firms and figures are invented, and all money is in dollars. Work in the order given, write your formula before you substitute numbers, and check the marks to see how much to write.

## Questions

**1.** Last year a printing business had revenue of 420 000, profit of 84 000 and capital employed of 560 000. Work out its ROCE as a percentage, showing your method. **[2]**

**2.** A clothing shop had revenue of 250 000 and cost of sales of 160 000 last year. Calculate its gross profit margin. Show your working. **[3]**

**3.** A hairdressing business with two salons wants to improve its profit margin. Outline **two** ways it could do this. **[4]**

**4.** A toy wholesaler has current assets of 66 000 and current liabilities of 40 000.
**(a)** Calculate its current ratio.
**(b)** State what this result suggests about the wholesaler's liquidity. **[3]**

**5.** Identify **two** external users of a business's accounts and state what **each** would want to find out from them. **[4]**

**6.** A garden centre's cash-flow forecast for March shows an opening balance of 4 500, total cash inflows of 12 300 and total cash outflows of 14 100. Calculate the closing balance for March. **[2]**

**7.** Two bakeries sell similar products. Bakery A had revenue of 380 000 and profit of 57 000. Bakery B had revenue of 300 000 and profit of 33 000.
Calculate the profit margin of each bakery and comment on your results. **[5]**

**8.** A tailor's shop begins January with 3 000 in the bank. The forecast is:

| Month | Cash inflows (dollars) | Cash outflows (dollars) |
|---|---|---|
| January | 9 000 | 10 500 |
| February | 11 000 | 8 200 |
| March | 8 800 | 12 600 |

**(a)** Calculate the closing balance for each month.
**(b)** State **two** ways the owner could improve the March cash position. **[5]**

**9.** Zarmeen Textiles, a fictional firm, gave these figures.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (dollars) | 600 000 | 660 000 |
| Gross profit (dollars) | 240 000 | 244 200 |
| Profit (dollars) | 66 000 | 59 400 |
| Capital employed (dollars) | 440 000 | 495 000 |

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit margin for each year.
**(b)** Calculate ROCE for each year.
**(c)** Analyse **one** possible reason why the gross profit margin fell. **[10]**

**10.** A fictional retailer has inventories of 30 000, receivables of 22 000 and cash of 4 000. Its payables are 35 000 and its bank overdraft is 14 000.
**(a)** Calculate its current ratio.
**(b)** Explain why a ratio above 1 could still hide a liquidity problem for this retailer.
**(c)** Recommend **one** action to improve its liquidity and justify it. **[8]**

**11.** Dawood Cycles, a fictional firm, has two shops. Last year it had revenue of 520 000, profit of 78 000 and capital employed of 600 000. The owner plans a third shop costing 150 000, paid for by a bank loan at 8% a year. The cash-flow forecast shows a net cash outflow of 6 000 a month for the first four months.
Evaluate whether the owner should open the third shop. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** ROCE = (profit ÷ capital employed) × 100 = (84 000 ÷ 560 000) × 100 [1] = **15%** [1].

*Examiner insight:* Using revenue instead of profit is the usual slip, and the % sign is part of the answer.

**2.** Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales = 250 000 − 160 000 = 90 000 [1]. Gross profit margin = (gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100 = (90 000 ÷ 250 000) × 100 [1] = **36%** [1].

*Examiner insight:* Divide by revenue, not by cost of sales; dividing by 160 000 gives 56.25% and loses the last two marks.

**3.** Any two ways, each with a development. For example: **raise prices** [1] on colouring, so each sale earns more profit [1]; **reduce variable costs** [1] by buying dye in bulk from a cheaper supplier, so each service costs less [1]. (Reducing fixed costs, such as negotiating lower rent for one salon, is also creditworthy.)

*Examiner insight:* Ways to sell more do not by themselves raise a margin, and "price" and "cost" must not be swapped.

**4. (a)** Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities = 66 000 ÷ 40 000 [1] = **1.65** [1].
**(b)** Current assets exceed current liabilities, so the wholesaler should be able to pay its short-term debts [1].

*Examiner insight:* The current ratio is a number such as 1.65 or 1.65 : 1; adding a % sign is marked as an error.

**5.** Any two users, 2 marks each. For example: **banks** [1] want to know whether profit is enough to repay a loan and interest [1]; **suppliers** [1] want to check liquidity so they know the business can pay for goods bought on credit [1]. (Government, checking profit so the correct tax is paid, is also valid.)

*Examiner insight:* Shareholders and managers are not external users in this sense, so naming them earns nothing, and each user needs a reason linked to the accounts.

**6.** Closing balance = opening balance + inflows − outflows = 4 500 + 12 300 − 14 100 [1] = **2 700** [1].

*Examiner insight:* Net cash flow is −1 800, which is not the closing balance; add it to the opening balance.

**7.** Profit margin = (profit ÷ revenue) × 100. Bakery A = (57 000 ÷ 380 000) × 100 [1] = **15%** [1]. Bakery B = (33 000 ÷ 300 000) × 100 = **11%** [1]. Comment: Bakery A keeps more profit from each dollar of sales [1], so it controls its costs or sets its prices better than B, although the figures do not show which [1].

*Examiner insight:* A comment must compare the two results; stating the two percentages again earns no comment marks.

**8. (a)** January: 3 000 + 9 000 − 10 500 = **1 500** [1]. February: 1 500 + 11 000 − 8 200 = **4 300** [1]. March: 4 300 + 8 800 − 12 600 = **500** [1].
**(b)** Any two, for example: ask customers to pay sooner or offer a discount for quick payment [1]; delay payments to suppliers by negotiating longer credit [1]. (An overdraft for March or postponing an equipment purchase also earn credit.)

*Examiner insight:* Each month starts with the previous closing balance, and one slipped figure carries through every later month.

**9. (a)** Year 1 = (240 000 ÷ 600 000) × 100 = **40%** [1]. Year 2 = (244 200 ÷ 660 000) × 100 = **37%** [1]. Correct method shown for either year [1].
**(b)** ROCE = (profit ÷ capital employed) × 100. Year 1 = (66 000 ÷ 440 000) × 100 = **15%** [1]. Year 2 = (59 400 ÷ 495 000) × 100 = **12%** [1]. Correct formula or working [1].
**(c)** One reason, developed: the cost of buying the cloth rose [1], and the firm did not raise its prices by as much [1], so cost of sales took a larger share of revenue [1], leaving less gross profit per dollar of sales, which also lowered ROCE [1]. (Other valid reasons: more discounts to win customers, or a cheaper product mix.)

*Examiner insight:* Analysis needs a chain of cause and effect; naming the reason alone gains one mark out of four.

**10. (a)** Current assets = 30 000 + 22 000 + 4 000 = 56 000 [1]. Current liabilities = 35 000 + 14 000 = 49 000 [1]. Ratio = 56 000 ÷ 49 000 = **1.14** [1].
**(b)** Inventories make up over half of current assets, so they may be slow to sell or sell for less than cost [1], and cash is only 4 000 against 49 000 of debts due, so bills may be unpaid even though the ratio is above 1 [1].
**(c)** One action with justification. For example, chase receivables faster [1], because it turns 22 000 owed into cash without borrowing more [1], though pushing customers too hard may lose sales [1].

*Examiner insight:* A recommendation needs a reason and a drawback; a bare action earns only the first mark.

**11.** The real exam marks a question like this by levels of response, judging analysis and a supported conclusion, not by counting points. Indicative content, 12 marks in total:
- Profit margin is (78 000 ÷ 520 000) × 100 = 15% [1] and ROCE is (78 000 ÷ 600 000) × 100 = 13% [1], so the firm is profitable [1].
- Interest on the loan is 8% of 150 000 = 12 000 a year [1], and ROCE of 13% is above 8%, so a new shop earning the same ROCE would more than cover the interest [1].
- The forecast outflow is 4 × 6 000 = 24 000 over four months [1], so the firm needs cash in reserve or an overdraft [1], which may strain liquidity [1].
- A third shop may raise revenue and profit and spread fixed costs [1], but it may also split sales between shops and add management costs [1].
- Evaluation: a justified judgement, such as going ahead only if reserves cover the early losses and a sales forecast supports the 13% return [1], or waiting and testing demand first [1].

*Examiner insight:* The top level goes to answers that weigh the 13% return against the cash drain and then commit to a decision with a reason.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using revenue instead of profit in ROCE, or cost of sales instead of revenue in gross profit margin.
- Leaving out the % sign on margins and ROCE, or adding one to the current ratio.
- Stating a result without saying what it means for the business.
- Suggesting ways to increase sales when the question asks how to improve a margin.
- Confusing net cash flow with the closing balance, or forgetting to carry one month's balance into the next.
- Naming users such as shareholders or managers when external users are asked for.
- Giving an action in an evaluation question with no reason, drawback or final decision.
- Mixing profit with cash, and saying a profitable firm cannot run short of money.
