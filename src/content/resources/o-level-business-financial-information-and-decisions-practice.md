## Questions

**1.** Last year a printing business had revenue of $420 000, profit of $84 000 and capital employed of $560 000. Work out its ROCE as a percentage, showing your method. **[2]**

**2.** A clothing shop had revenue of $250 000 and cost of sales of $160 000 last year. Calculate its gross profit margin. Show your working. **[3]**

**3.** A hairdressing business with two salons wants to improve its profit margin. Outline **two** ways it could do this. **[4]**

**4.** A toy wholesaler has current assets of $66 000 and current liabilities of $40 000.
**(a)** Calculate its current ratio.
**(b)** State what this result suggests about the wholesaler's liquidity. **[3]**

**5.** Identify **two** external users of a business's accounts and state what **each** would want to find out from them. **[4]**

**6.** A garden centre's cash-flow forecast for March shows an opening balance of $4 500, total cash inflows of $12 300 and total cash outflows of $14 100. Calculate the closing balance for March. **[2]**

---

## Answers

**1.** ROCE = (profit ÷ capital employed) × 100 = (84 000 ÷ 560 000) × 100 [1] = **15%** [1].

*Examiner insight (June 2024):* A common mistake was dividing revenue, rather than profit, by capital employed. The mark scheme gave full marks only if the answer was shown as a percentage, and leaving out the % sign suggested weak understanding.

*Try the real question next:* Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115, June 2024, Paper 12, Question 2(b).

**2.** Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales = 250 000 − 160 000 = $90 000 [1]. Gross profit margin = (gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100 = (90 000 ÷ 250 000) × 100 [1] = **36%** [1].

*Tip:* Work out gross profit first, then divide by revenue — not by cost of sales.

**3.** Any two, each with a link to the salons: **raise prices** [1], for example charging more for haircuts or colouring treatments, so each sale earns more profit [1]; **reduce variable costs** [1], for example buying shampoo and hair dye in bulk from a cheaper supplier [1]; **reduce fixed costs** [1], for example negotiating lower rent for one of the two salons [1].

*Examiner insight (June 2024):* The most common error was giving ways to increase sales, which does not by itself raise the profit margin. Some candidates also mixed up price and cost — they are different concepts, so use the right word.

*Try the real question next:* Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115, June 2024, Paper 12, Question 2(c).

**4. (a)** Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities = 66 000 ÷ 40 000 [1] = **1.65** [1].
**(b)** The wholesaler has more current assets than current liabilities, so it should be **able to pay its short-term debts** — its liquidity looks acceptable [1].

*Tip:* The current ratio is written as a number (or as 1.65 : 1), not as a percentage.

**5.** Any two users, 2 marks each: **Banks** [1] — want to know whether the business makes enough profit to **repay a loan and its interest** before lending [1]. **Suppliers** [1] — want to check the business's **liquidity** so they know it can pay for goods bought on credit [1]. **Government** [1] — wants to check **profit so the correct amount of tax is paid** [1].

*Examiner insight (June 2024):* Credited answers explained how groups such as banks, suppliers or government use the accounts; naming shareholders was the most frequent answer that did not earn credit. Some candidates misread the question and wrote about sources of finance instead.

*Try the real question next:* Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115, June 2024, Paper 12, Question 2(e).

**6.** Closing balance = opening balance + inflows − outflows = 4 500 + 12 300 − 14 100 [1] = **$2 700** [1].

*Tip:* Net cash flow here is −$1 800 (outflows exceed inflows), but the positive opening balance keeps the closing balance above zero. Do not confuse net cash flow with the closing balance.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using revenue instead of profit in ROCE, or dividing by cost of sales in gross profit margin.
- Leaving out the % sign on ratios that are percentages, or adding one to the current ratio, which is not a percentage.
- Suggesting ways to increase sales when the question asks how to improve the profit margin.
- Writing "price" when you mean "cost", or the other way round.
- Misreading a question about users of accounts as one about sources of finance.
