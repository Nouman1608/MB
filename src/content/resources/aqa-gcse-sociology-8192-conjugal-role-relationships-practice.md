## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Which statement best describes joint conjugal roles?

A. The husband earns and the wife looks after the home
B. Partners share housework, decisions and leisure
C. Each partner keeps separate friends and hobbies
D. Both partners work full time but one does all the housework

**[1]**

**2.** Which sociologist named in the specification wrote about the idea of the conventional family?

A. Oakley
B. Zaretsky
C. Parsons
D. Willmott

**[1]**

**3.** Describe what is meant by segregated conjugal roles. **[2]**

**4.** Describe what Edgell found about decision making in middle-class couples. **[3]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** problem that dual career families may face. **[4]**

**6.** Read Item A.

> **Item A (fictional).** Priya and Tom both work full time. Tom says he "helps a lot" because he cooks twice a week. Priya fills in school forms, books dental appointments and remembers family birthdays. They have a joint bank account, but Tom chooses the car and where they go on holiday.

- **(a)** Identify **one** sociological concept shown in Item A. **[1]**
- **(b)** Explain how a feminist sociologist might interpret Priya and Tom's relationship. **[3]**

**7.** Explain **one** difference between Marxist and feminist views of the domestic division of labour. **[3]**

**8.** Describe **two** parts of Oakley's argument about the idea of the conventional family. **[4]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**9.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Brindle (a fictional town): average hours per week spent on housework and childcare by partners in dual-earner couples, from time-use diaries**

| Year | Women | Men |
|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 22 | 9 |
| 2025 | 18 | 11 |

- **(a)** Calculate the difference between women's and men's hours in 2025. **[1]**
- **(b)** Explain how far the data support the view that conjugal roles in Brindle are becoming joint. **[3]**
- **(c)** Examine **one** weakness of using time-use diaries to study the domestic division of labour. **[2]**

**10.** Identify and explain **one** way in which leisure activities may differ for women and men in families. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that men and women now share power equally within families. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that the idea of the conventional family still shapes conjugal roles today. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. A describes segregated roles; D describes a dual burden. **[1]**

**2.** A [1]. Oakley's "Conventional families" (1982) is the text the specification names. **[1]**

**3.** Roles in which partners have clearly separate tasks, usually with the husband as breadwinner and the wife as homemaker [1], and often separate friends, decisions and leisure activities [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Edgell studied middle-class couples [1] and found that husbands dominated decisions seen as very important, such as moving house and major finance [1], while wives made decisions seen as less important, such as interior decoration, food and children's clothes [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Identify: role conflict [1]. Explain: both partners face the demands of a career and of family life at the same time [1], for example when a child is ill and both are expected at work [1]. Feminists note that it is more often the woman who reduces her hours or interrupts her career to resolve this, which links to the glass ceiling [1]. *Also credit:* the cost and availability of childcare; little time for joint leisure; problems of work life balance. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Dual burden [1]. *Also credit:* triple shift or emotion work; segregated roles; unequal decision making.

**(b)** Feminists would argue that the relationship appears equal but is not [1]. Priya does the emotion work and organising, such as remembering birthdays, on top of paid work, which is a triple shift [1]. Tom's control of major decisions, despite the joint account, matches Edgell's and Pahl's findings that men often keep more power, so the relationship reflects patriarchy rather than true joint roles [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Marxists argue that women's unpaid domestic labour mainly benefits capitalism, because it reproduces and maintains workers at no cost to employers [1]. Feminists such as Delphy and Leonard argue that it is men, rather than capitalists as such, who mainly benefit [1], because men gain free services and leisure time from women's work within the family [1]. **[3]**

**8.** First, the power of the idea: the conventional family is presented as normal by government, media and advertising [1], even though many people do not live in one [1]. Second, the strains of being conventional: trying to live up to the ideal can leave housewives isolated and undervalued [1], and puts pressure on both partners to perform fixed roles [1]. *Also credit:* its origins, linked to the separation of home and paid work; its role as social control. **[4]**

**9. (a)** 18 − 11 = 7 hours per week [1].

**(b)** The data partly support the view: the gap fell from 13 hours in 2000 (22 − 9) to 7 hours in 2025 [1], and men's hours rose from 9 to 11, so roles are moving towards joint [1]. However, women still do over one and a half times as many hours as men (18 compared with 11), so roles are not yet joint, and the data show only time spent, not who has responsibility or power [1].

**(c)** Couples may change their behaviour or exaggerate their contribution because they know they are recording it [1], so the diaries may not give a valid picture of the usual division of labour [1]. *Also credit:* a diary kept for a short period may not represent a typical week. **[6]**

**10.** Identify: women often have less free time than men [1]. Explain: because women usually carry more of the housework and childcare, even when they work [1], their leisure is shorter and more often interrupted, for example watching television while supervising children [1]. Men are more likely to have uninterrupted leisure outside the home, such as sport with friends, which reflects segregated rather than joint roles [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Power within families can be measured by decision making and control of money [1].
- For: Willmott and Young's symmetrical family suggests shared decisions in home-centred families [1].
- Dual career families give women their own income and more say [1].
- Joint bank accounts and pooling are common among couples today [1].
- Against: Edgell found husbands dominated the most important decisions [1].
- Pahl found that even pooled money was often controlled more by men [1].
- Delphy and Leonard argue men exploit women's work within the family [1].
- Oakley's conventional family idea keeps the man as the main breadwinner and decision maker [1].
- Marxists argue men's power at home compensates for their lack of power at work [1].
- Domestic abuse shows extreme inequality of power in some families [1].
- Evaluation: Edgell's and Pahl's studies are not recent, and younger couples may be more egalitarian [1].
- Judgement: power is more equal than in traditional families, but evidence on money and major decisions suggests it is not yet shared equally [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Power is not the same as housework; use decision making and money management as your main evidence.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Oakley defined the conventional family as a nuclear family of a legally married couple who voluntarily chose parenthood [1].
- She argued the idea is powerful because government, media and advertising present it as normal [1].
- The ideal includes a breadwinning husband and caring wife, linked to Parsons' instrumental and expressive roles [1].
- Women in dual-earner couples still do more housework and childcare, the dual burden, which suggests the ideal still shapes roles [1].
- Mothers are more likely than fathers to reduce work hours after children arrive [1].
- The idea acts as social control, making other arrangements seem odd [1].
- Against: Oakley herself concluded official stereotypes were coming to seem "increasingly archaic" [1].
- Dual career families and the new man show roles moving away from the conventional model [1].
- Family diversity, including single sex families, means many couples do not follow the conventional pattern [1].
- Willmott and Young argued roles had become more symmetrical [1].
- Evaluation: Oakley's paper predates civil partnerships and same-sex marriage, so it may describe an earlier period [1].
- Judgement: the conventional family is less dominant as a way of living, but as an idea it still influences who takes responsibility for the home [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Separate the conventional family as a type of household from the conventional family as an idea; Oakley's argument is about the idea.

## Where marks are usually lost

- In 6(b), describing the item without naming a concept or a named study.
- In 9(b), using only one year's figures, or forgetting that time spent is not the same as responsibility.
- Confusing a dual career family with the dual burden.
- In 12-mark answers on power, writing only about housework.
