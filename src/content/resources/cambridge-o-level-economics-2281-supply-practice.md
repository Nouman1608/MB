This set tests section 2.4 **Supply** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: the definition of supply, extensions and contractions, individual and market supply, and the conditions of supply. All firms and figures are invented. Teaching is in the [supply study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-supply/) and the [supply revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-supply-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define supply. **[2]**

**2.** Which change causes a contraction of supply of coffee?

A: A rise in the wages of coffee pickers

B: A fall in the price of coffee

C: A new tax on coffee producers

D: A drought in coffee-growing areas

**[1]**

**3.** Farmers can grow either maize or soya on the same land. Which change shifts the supply curve of maize to the right?

A: A rise in the price of maize

B: A subsidy paid to maize growers

C: A rise in the price of soya

D: A rise in consumers' incomes

**[1]**

**4.** For each event, state whether there is a movement along or a shift of the supply curve of the named product, and give the direction.

**(a)** The number of boats in a country's fishing fleet doubles (fish). **[1]**

**(b)** The price of eggs falls (eggs). **[1]**

**(c)** The price of electricity used by aluminium smelters rises (aluminium). **[1]**

**(d)** Car factories install robots that cut the cost of each car (cars). **[1]**

**5.** The price of a bag of fertiliser rises from $12 to $15, and the quantity supplied rises from 400 to 520 bags a week.

**(a)** Name this change in supply. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in price. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity supplied. **[1]**

**6.** The table shows the weekly supply of three firms making plastic buckets.

| Price ($) | Firm A | Firm B | Firm C |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 30 | 0 | 10 |
| 8 | 45 | 20 | 25 |
| 10 | 60 | 40 | 40 |

**(a)** Calculate market supply at each price. **[2]**

**(b)** Firm B closes. State the new market supply at each price and identify the type of change in market supply. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A furniture workshop has this monthly supply schedule for chairs.

| Price ($) | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity supplied | 100 | 140 | 180 | 220 |

**(a)** Describe how you would draw the supply curve from this schedule. **[2]**

**(b)** The government pays a subsidy of $10 per chair. Give the new quantity supplied at each price. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in quantity supplied at a price of $50. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the subsidy shifts the supply curve rather than causing a movement along it. **[2]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why a supply curve usually slopes upwards from left to right. **[4]**

**9.** Explain, with the help of a supply diagram described in words, how a rise in wages affects the supply of hand-made clothing. **[4]**

**10.** Farmers in Country T can grow tea or coffee. Explain how a fall in the price of tea might affect the supply of coffee. **[3]**

**11.** Analyse how an improvement in technology affects the supply of mobile phones. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether a subsidy paid to farmers will increase the supply of food. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The quantity of a product that sellers are willing and able to offer for sale [1] at each price over a given period of time [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "The amount produced" misses both marks' key ideas. Willingness and ability, and the link to each price, are what make it a definition.

**2.** B [1]. A contraction is a movement down the supply curve, caused only by a fall in the product's own price. A, C and D are conditions of supply that shift the curve left. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. A subsidy lowers the cost of supplying each unit, so supply increases. A causes an extension, C moves supply of maize to the left, and D affects demand. **[1]**

**4. (a)** Shift to the right (increase in supply). [1]

**(b)** Movement down the curve (contraction of supply). [1]

**(c)** Shift to the left (decrease in supply), because costs rise. [1]

**(d)** Shift to the right (increase in supply), because cost per car falls. [1]

**5. (a)** An extension of supply. [1] **[1]**

**(b)** (15 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = 25% [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** (520 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = 30% [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** At $6: 30 + 0 + 10 = 40 [1]. At $8: 90 and at $10: 140 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** New market supply is 40, 70 and 100 [1]. This is a decrease in market supply (a shift to the left), although the fall differs at each price: 0, 20 and 40 [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Put price on the vertical axis and quantity of chairs per month on the horizontal axis [1]. Plot the four points and join them, labelling the line S [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** At $40 the workshop receives $50 in total, so it offers 140; the new schedule is 140, 180, 220 and 260 [2]. One mark for two or three correct figures. **[2]**

**(c)** (180 − 140) ÷ 140 × 100 = 28.6% [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** The subsidy is a condition of supply, not a change in the chair's own price [1]. It lowers the cost of supplying each chair, so more is offered at every price and the whole curve moves right [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Students often say "the price goes up for the firm, so it is an extension". The market price has not changed; the firm's cost per unit has.

**8.** First reason: a higher price raises the revenue from each unit, so producing more becomes profitable [1], so firms offer more [1]. Second reason: extra output usually costs more per unit, for example through overtime pay or less efficient machines [1], so firms only produce it if the price is higher [1]. (Credit also: higher prices attract new sellers into the market.) **[4]**

**9.** Wages are a cost of production, especially for hand-made goods that use a lot of labour [1]. Higher wages raise the cost of each garment, so at every price fewer garments are profitable [1]. On a diagram with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, the curve shifts from S1 to S2, to the left [1]. At any given price, such as $20, the quantity offered is now lower than before: a decrease in supply [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Tea and coffee compete for the same land and labour [1]. A fall in the price of tea makes growing tea less profitable than before [1], so some farmers switch to coffee and the supply of coffee increases, shifting the curve right [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Better technology raises output per worker or per machine [1], which lowers the cost of making each phone [1]. At every price, more phones are now profitable to supply [1], so supply increases and the curve shifts to the right; it may also let new firms enter, adding to market supply [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: a subsidy is paid per unit, so farmers receive more for each unit sold [1], which lowers their effective cost and shifts supply to the right [1]. It may also keep high-cost farms in business, adding to market supply [1]. Against: farm output depends on weather and land, so a poor harvest can cut supply whatever the subsidy [1]; and if the subsidy is small compared with other costs, such as fuel and fertiliser, the increase may be small [1]. Judgement: the subsidy is likely to increase food supply, but the size of the increase depends on the size of the subsidy and on natural conditions, and it may take a growing season or more to appear [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* A supported conclusion that says what the outcome depends on earns the final mark. Repeating "it may or may not" does not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing a contraction (price-driven) with a decrease (condition-driven).
- Moving supply for an event that affects buyers, such as a change in incomes.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when finding market supply.
- Dividing by the new figure when calculating a percentage change.
- Forgetting the time period when defining supply or labelling the quantity axis.
- Explaining a shift without naming the cost change behind it.
