IB DP Psychology Paper 2 (first assessment 2019) sets three 22-mark essays on the developmental option. The eight questions below cover every content point of the option's three topics, each with a model plan organised by the ERQ criteria. Allow about an hour per essay.

## How the essays are marked

| Criterion | Marks | Top-band expectation |
|---|---|---|
| A Focus on the question | 2 | Explains the problem or issue posed by the question |
| B Knowledge and understanding | 6 | Accurate, detailed, well-explained terminology |
| C Use of research | 6 | Relevant research, explained and linked to the question |

## Questions

**1.** Discuss the role of peers and play in cognitive and/or social development. **[22]**

**2.** Evaluate research on childhood trauma and resilience. **[22]**

**3.** Discuss the effect of poverty on development. **[22]**

**4.** Evaluate research on brain development. **[22]**

**5.** Contrast two theories of cognitive development. **[22]**

**6.** Evaluate one theory of attachment. **[22]**

**7.** Discuss how gender identity and social roles develop. **[22]**

**8.** Evaluate research on the development of empathy and theory of mind. **[22]**

## Model plans

**1. Peers and play.**
- *B:* Vygotsky's view that play creates a zone of proximal development and builds self-regulation; peers as models and as more knowledgeable others; Piaget's view that peer conflict prompts cognitive change.
- *C:* research on pretend play and self-regulation; social learning from peers (Bandura's modelling principles applied to peers).
- *D:* much play research is correlational; definitions of play vary; cultural differences in play and the role of adults.

**2. Trauma and resilience.**
- *B:* define trauma and resilience; risk and protective factors.
- *C:* Rutter et al. (ERA study) -- Romanian orphans adopted before six months showed better recovery than those adopted later; Hodges and Tizard (1989) -- later social difficulties in ex-institutional children; Werner and Smith (Kauai) -- protective factors in high-risk children.
- *D:* natural experiments cannot control which children were adopted; attrition in longitudinal studies; ethical constraints mean no experimental manipulation; resilience is a process, not a fixed trait.

**3. Poverty.**
- *B:* mechanisms -- chronic stress, poorer nutrition, less cognitive stimulation, parental stress and caregiving.
- *C:* Luby et al. (2013) -- poverty linked to smaller hippocampal and amygdala volumes, mediated by caregiving support and stressful life events; Hart and Risley (1995) -- differences in language exposure by socio-economic status.
- *D:* correlation and confounding variables; Hart and Risley's small sample and observer effects; risk of deficit framing; protective role of caregiving suggests interventions.

**4. Brain development.**
- *B:* synaptogenesis, pruning, myelination; neuroplasticity; the prefrontal cortex and executive functions.
- *C:* Chugani (1987) PET studies of glucose metabolism in infants; Gogtay et al. (2004) longitudinal MRI showing grey-matter loss progressing from back to front, with prefrontal regions maturing last.
- *D:* small samples; imaging shows structure and correlation, not behaviour directly; ethical limits on scanning children; links between brain change and behaviour are interpretive.

**5. Two theories of cognitive development (contrast).**
- *B/C:* Piaget -- universal stages, child as lone scientist, conservation tasks; Vygotsky -- social and cultural learning, ZPD, scaffolding, language as a tool of thought.
- *D:* Piaget emphasises maturation before learning, Vygotsky learning leading development; Piaget's tasks may underestimate young children; Vygotsky's ideas are harder to test but better explain cultural differences; implications for education differ (discovery learning versus guided participation).

**6. Bowlby's attachment theory.**
- *B:* innate attachment system, monotropy, critical period, internal working model.
- *C:* Harlow (1958) contact comfort; Ainsworth et al. (1978) Strange Situation; Van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988) cross-cultural meta-analysis.
- *D:* Harlow's animal research -- ethics and generalisation; the Strange Situation may be less valid in cultures with different childcare practices; multiple attachments challenge monotropy; temperament as an alternative explanation.

**7. Gender identity and social roles.**
- *B:* social learning (models and reinforcement); gender schema theory (Martin and Halverson, 1981); biological influences.
- *C:* Smith and Lloyd (1978) -- adults' toy choices depended on the infant's perceived sex; research on children's gender-schema-consistent memory.
- *D:* artificial lab settings; difficulty separating biological and social causes; changing cultural norms; interaction of explanations.

**8. Empathy and theory of mind.**
- *B:* theory of mind -- understanding others' mental states; false belief; link to empathy.
- *C:* Wimmer and Perner (1983) false-belief tasks; Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985) Sally-Anne task with autistic, Down syndrome and typically developing children.
- *D:* tasks depend on language and may underestimate younger children; small samples; cultural variation in the age of passing; theory of mind deficits are not universal among autistic people.

## Worked paragraph: criterion D in action (Question 6)

> Van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg's (1988) meta-analysis of Strange Situation studies in eight countries
> found secure attachment to be the most common type everywhere, which supports Bowlby's claim that
> attachment is a universal, evolved system. However, the proportions of insecure types varied: avoidant
> attachment was more common in Germany and resistant attachment in Japan and Israel. This variation may
> reflect cultural childcare practices rather than poorer attachment -- for example, Japanese infants
> rarely separated from their mothers may find the procedure unusually stressful. The Strange Situation
> may therefore be an imposed etic measure, so its findings support Bowlby's universality claim only in
> part.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many studies should each essay include?**
Two or three relevant studies, each described and evaluated, are usually enough for criterion C.

**Do I need numbers from studies?**
Precise findings strengthen criterion B and C, but accuracy matters more than statistics.

**Can I write about adolescence?**
Yes, where relevant -- brain development, for instance, continues into the twenties.
