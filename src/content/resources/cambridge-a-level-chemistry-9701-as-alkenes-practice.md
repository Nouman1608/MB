## Answers

**1.** Steam, H₂O(g) [1]; phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) catalyst [1].

**2.** The orange solution is decolourised (turns colourless) [1].

**3.** An electron-pair acceptor (attracted to an electron-rich site) [1].

**4.** 1,2-dichloropropane, CH₂ClCHClCH₃ [1].

**5. (a)** Addition polymerisation [1].

**(b)** –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–, with CH₃ as a side group and continuation bonds at each end [1].

**6.** As Br₂ approaches the π bond, its electrons are repelled and an induced dipole forms, Brᵟ⁺–Brᵟ⁻
[1]. A curly arrow from the C=C π bond to Brᵟ⁺, and a curly arrow from the Br–Br bond to Brᵟ⁻, so Br⁻
leaves by heterolytic fission [1]. A carbocation, CH₂BrCH₂⁺, forms [1]. A curly arrow from a lone pair on
Br⁻ to the C⁺ gives 1,2-dibromoethane, CH₂BrCH₂Br [1].

**7. (a)** H adds to the CH carbon: (CH₃)₂C⁺CH₂CH₃, a tertiary cation [1]. H adds to the C(CH₃)₂ carbon:
(CH₃)₂CHCH⁺CH₃, a secondary cation [1].

**(b)** 2-bromo-2-methylbutane, (CH₃)₂CBrCH₂CH₃ [1]. Alkyl groups are electron-donating by the inductive
effect and stabilise the positive charge [1]. The tertiary cation has three alkyl groups on C⁺ and is the
more stable, so it forms faster and gives the major product [1].

**8. (a)** CO₂ and H₂O come from a =CH₂ end, and butanone comes from an end carrying CH₃ and C₂H₅,
(CH₃)(C₂H₅)C= [1]. So P is CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ [1].

**(b)** 2-methylbut-1-ene [1].

**(c)** 2-methylbutane-1,2-diol, HOCH₂C(OH)(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ [1].

**9. (a)** Heated Al₂O₃, or heat with concentrated H₂SO₄ (or concentrated H₃PO₄) [1].

**(b)** Pent-1-ene [1]; cis-pent-2-ene [1]; trans-pent-2-ene [1].

**10.** Alkenes have a π bond whose electrons lie above and below the plane, an exposed region of high
electron density that attracts electrophiles; the π bond is also weaker than a σ bond [1]. Alkanes have
only strong, almost non-polar σ bonds with no region of high electron density [1].

**11. (a)** Phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄ [1].

**(b)** Propan-2-ol [1], because addition goes through the secondary carbocation, CH₃CH⁺CH₃, which is
more stable than the primary cation as it has two electron-donating alkyl groups [1].

**12. (a)** Mr(C₂H₄) = 28.0, so n = 2.80 / 28.0 = 0.100 mol [1].

**(b)** Mr(C₂H₄Br₂) = 2(12.0) + 4(1.0) + 2(79.9) = 187.8; mass = 0.100 × 187.8 = 18.8 g [1].

**(c)** n(H₂) = 0.480 / 24.0 = 0.0200 mol [1]; ratio H₂ : hydrocarbon = 2 : 1, so two C=C bonds per
molecule [1].

**13. (a)** But-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃ (each =CHCH₃ end gives ethanoic acid) [1].

**(b)** Rotation about the C=C is restricted because the π bond would break [1], and each C=C carbon
carries two different groups, H and CH₃ [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Starting the first curly arrow from the bromine atom instead of the π bond.
- Leaving out the induced dipole on Br₂ or the lone pair on Br⁻.
- Explaining the major product by saying "Markovnikov's rule" without the inductive effect and cation
  stability.
- Writing aqueous NaOH for elimination or H₂SO₄ as the hydration catalyst.
- Reading hot KMnO₄ products backwards: =CH₂ ends give CO₂, not methanoic acid.
- Forgetting cis and trans forms when listing "all the alkenes formed".

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I deduce an alkene from its hot KMnO₄ products?**
Work out which end each product came from: CO₂ from =CH₂, a carboxylic acid RCOOH from =CHR, and a ketone
RCOR′ from =CRR′. Then join the two ends with a C=C and check the molecular formula.

**Why is the tertiary cation more stable than the secondary one?**
It has three alkyl groups donating electron density towards the positive carbon instead of two, so the
positive charge is spread out more and the cation is stabilised more.
