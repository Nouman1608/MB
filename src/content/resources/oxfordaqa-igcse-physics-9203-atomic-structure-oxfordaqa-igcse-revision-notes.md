These notes condense section **3.7.1 Atomic structure** (statements a to f) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered. For fuller explanations and worked examples read the [atomic structure study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-atomic-structure-oxfordaqa-igcse/), then use the [atomic structure practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-atomic-structure-oxfordaqa-igcse-practice/).

## Condensed recall

**Size (a).** Atom radius about 10⁻¹⁰ m. Nucleus radius about 10⁻¹⁵ m, so the atom is about 100 000 times wider than the nucleus.

**Nuclear model (a).** Small, central, positive nucleus of protons and neutrons; electrons around it; almost all the mass in the nucleus.

**Alpha scattering (b).** Thin metal foil, alpha particles fired at it. It provides evidence for how mass is distributed in the atom.

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Most pass straight through | Atom mostly empty space |
| Some deflected slightly | Positive charge concentrated in a small region |
| Very few bounce back | Tiny nucleus holds the positive charge and nearly all the mass |

Only a massive object could turn a fast alpha particle right round, which is why the result shows where the **mass** is.

**Particles (c).**

| Particle | Relative mass | Relative charge |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | 1 | +1 |
| Neutron | 1 | 0 |
| Electron | Very small | −1 |

**Neutral atom (d).** Electrons = protons, so no overall charge.

**Ions (e).** Electrons sit at various distances from the nucleus. Lose outer electrons → positive ion. Gain electrons → negative ion. Charge = protons − electrons. Nucleus unchanged.

**Isotopes and notation (f).** Same number of protons, different number of neutrons. Symbol: mass number on top, atomic number below, as in ²³⁸₉₂U.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nucleus | Tiny, dense, positive centre of the atom, made of protons and neutrons |
| Atomic number (proton number), Z | Number of protons in the nucleus |
| Mass number, A | Total number of protons and neutrons |
| Isotopes | Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons |
| Ion | Charged particle formed when an atom loses or gains outer electrons |
| Nucleon | A proton or a neutron (a particle in the nucleus) |

## Counting method

1. Protons = Z (bottom number).
2. Neutrons = A − Z.
3. Electrons in a neutral atom = Z.
4. For an ion: electrons = Z − charge (so a +2 ion has two fewer, a −1 ion has one more).
5. Check: protons + neutrons must add up to the mass number, and protons − electrons must equal the charge.

## Must-know distinctions

| Often confused | The difference |
|---|---|
| Atom and nucleus | The atom (about 10⁻¹⁰ m) is mostly empty; the nucleus (about 10⁻¹⁵ m) is tiny and dense |
| Atomic number and mass number | Protons only, against protons plus neutrons |
| Isotope and ion | Isotopes differ in neutrons; ions differ in electrons |
| Positive and negative ion | Positive: electrons lost. Negative: electrons gained |
| Charge of nucleus and charge of atom | Nucleus positive; neutral atom has no overall charge |
| Evidence for mass and evidence for charge | Bounce-back shows concentrated mass; deflection by repulsion shows concentrated positive charge |

## Answer templates

- **Nuclear model:** "A tiny, positively charged nucleus containing protons and neutrons is surrounded by electrons. The nucleus is much smaller than the atom but holds almost all of its mass."
- **Isotopes:** "Same number of protons, different number of neutrons, so the same element with different mass numbers."
- **Ion formation:** "The atom loses (or gains) outer electrons; the number of protons does not change."
- **Scattering:** give each observation, then "this shows that..." for each one in turn.

## Exam traps

- "Mostly empty space" describes the atom, not the nucleus.
- Neutrons = A − Z, not Z.
- A negative ion has more electrons than protons.
- Ions change electrons only; isotopes change neutrons only.
- Electron mass is very small, not zero.
- The scattering experiment used **thin** foil; say so if asked to describe it.

## Quick self-test

1. What is the approximate radius of an atom?
2. Where is almost all the mass of an atom?
3. What does the bouncing back of a few alpha particles show?
4. Why must the metal foil be thin?
5. Give the relative charge of a neutron.
6. Why does an atom have no overall charge?
7. How many neutrons are in ³⁷₁₇Cl?
8. ²⁴₁₂Mg forms an ion with 10 electrons. What is the charge?
9. ¹⁶₈O gains two electrons. How many electrons does the ion have?
10. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes. What is the same and what is different?
11. Which model did the alpha scattering results replace?
12. Uranium has atomic number 92. How many neutrons are in uranium-238?

### Answers

1. About 10⁻¹⁰ m.
2. In the nucleus.
3. A tiny nucleus contains the positive charge and nearly all the mass.
4. So each alpha particle meets only a few atoms, and a large deflection comes from one close encounter with a nucleus.
5. 0.
6. It has equal numbers of protons (+1 each) and electrons (−1 each).
7. 37 − 17 = **20**.
8. 12 − 10 = **+2**.
9. 8 + 2 = **10**.
10. Same number of protons (6); different numbers of neutrons (6 and 8).
11. The plum pudding model, in which positive charge and mass were spread through the atom.
12. 238 − 92 = **146**.
