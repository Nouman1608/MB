These questions cover section 2.1, Kinetic particle model of matter (2.1.1 to 2.1.3), of the Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028.

> **Tier note:** questions 1 to 5 and 7 to 9 use Core content only, and every candidate should do them. Questions 6 and 10 to 12, marked *(Extended)*, use Supplement content (2.1.2 Supplement 6–8; 2.1.3 Supplement 3), examined on Papers 2 and 4 only. The marks shown are indicative only.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Name the state of matter (solid, liquid or gas) that:

**(a)** has a fixed volume but no fixed shape **[1]**
**(b)** is easily compressed **[1]**
**(c)** has a fixed shape. **[1]**

**2.** Name the change of state when:

**(a)** water droplets form on a cold window **[1]**
**(b)** melted candle wax hardens. **[1]**

**3.** **(a)** Convert −40 °C into kelvin. **[1]**
**(b)** Convert 400 K into °C. **[1]**
**(c)** State the value of absolute zero in kelvin. **[1]**

**4.** Describe the arrangement, separation and motion of the particles in a liquid. **[3]**

**5.** Use the particle model to explain why gases are easily compressed but solids are not. **[2]**

**6.** *(Extended)* A gas cylinder holds 0.050 m³ of gas at a pressure of 2.0 × 10⁶ Pa. All the gas is released into the atmosphere at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa, at the same temperature. Calculate its new volume. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A student observes Brownian motion using smoke in air.

**(a)** Describe the apparatus used. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe what the student sees. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what causes this motion. **[2]**
**(d)** State what this observation is evidence for. **[1]**

**8.** A fixed mass of gas is sealed in a rigid metal container and heated.

**(a)** State what happens to the average speed of the gas particles. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of particles, why the pressure of the gas increases. **[3]**
**(c)** State the temperature, in °C, at which the particles would have the least kinetic energy. **[1]**

**9.** This question is about particle diagrams.

**(a)** Describe how particle diagrams for a solid, a liquid and a gas should differ. **[3]**
**(b)** Use the particle model to explain why a liquid takes the shape of its container. **[1]**
**(c)** Use the particle model to explain why a solid keeps its shape. **[1]**

**10.** *(Extended)* This question is about the forces between particles.

**(a)** Explain how the forces and distances between particles account for the properties of solids, liquids and gases. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how gas particles create a pressure on the walls of their container, using the idea of force per unit area. **[2]**

**11.** *(Extended)* A student slowly compresses a fixed mass of air in a sealed syringe at constant temperature and records these results.

| Volume / cm³ | 40 | 32 | 25 | 20 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure / kPa | 100 | 125 | 160 | 200 | 250 |

**(a)** Show that the results obey pV = constant. **[2]**
**(b)** Predict the pressure when the volume is 10 cm³. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe the shape of a graph of p against V, and state what should be plotted to give a straight line. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain, in terms of particles, why the pressure increases as the volume decreases. **[2]**

**12.** *(Extended)* A student writes: "In the smoke cell, the smoke molecules are hit by air particles."

**(a)** Rewrite the sentence using the terms molecules and microscopic particles correctly. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the motion of the smoke is random. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** liquid [1] **(b)** gas [1] **(c)** solid [1]

**2. (a)** condensation [1] **(b)** solidification (freezing) [1]

**3. (a)** −40 + 273 = **233 K** [1] **(b)** 400 − 273 = **127 °C** [1] **(c)** **0 K** [1]

**4.** Arrangement: irregular (random) [1]; separation: close together, mostly touching [1]; motion: move around, sliding past each other [1].

**5.** Gas particles are far apart, so they can be pushed closer together [1]; solid particles are already touching (very close), so there is almost no space to push them into [1].

**6.** p₁V₁ = p₂V₂: 2.0 × 10⁶ × 0.050 = 1.0 × 10⁵ × V₂ [1]; V₂ = **1.0 m³** [1]

**7. (a)** A small glass cell containing smoke [1], lit from the side and viewed through a microscope [1].
**(b)** Small bright specks [1] moving in a random, jerky (zigzag) way [1].
**(c)** The air particles move quickly and randomly and collide with the smoke particles [1]; at any moment the collisions on different sides are unequal, so each smoke particle is pushed in changing directions [1].
**(d)** The kinetic particle model of matter (air is made of moving particles) [1].

**8. (a)** It increases [1].
**(b)** The particles move faster [1]; they collide with the walls more often [1]; each collision exerts a larger force on the wall [1].
**(c)** **−273 °C** [1]

**9. (a)** Solid: particles touching in a regular pattern [1]; liquid: particles mostly touching but arranged irregularly [1]; gas: particles far apart and randomly spaced [1]. (All particles drawn the same size.)
**(b)** The particles can move past each other, so the liquid flows to fill the bottom of the container [1].
**(c)** The particles are held in fixed positions and can only vibrate [1].

**10. (a)** In a solid the forces between particles are strong and the particles are very close [1], so they are held in fixed positions: fixed shape and volume [1]; in a liquid the particles are still close, so the volume is fixed, but they can move past each other, so it flows [1]; in a gas the particles are far apart and the forces are very weak, so the particles move freely, the gas fills its container and is easily compressed [1].
**(b)** Each particle that collides with a wall exerts a force on it [1]; the total force from all the collisions divided by the area of the wall is the pressure [1].

**11. (a)** pV for each pair: 40 × 100 = 4000; 32 × 125 = 4000; 25 × 160 = 4000; 20 × 200 = 4000; 16 × 250 = 4000 [1]; the product is the same every time, so pV = constant [1].
**(b)** 4000 ÷ 10 = **400 kPa** [1]
**(c)** A curve that falls steeply at first and then less steeply (it does not reach either axis) [1]; plot p against 1/V (a straight line through the origin) [1].
**(d)** The particles have the same speeds (constant temperature) but less space [1]; so they hit each unit area of the walls more often, giving a larger force per unit area [1].
*Examiner insight:* "The particles move faster when compressed" contradicts the constant temperature and scores nothing.

**12. (a)** The smoke **particles** (microscopic particles) [1] are hit by air **molecules** [1].
**(b)** The air molecules move randomly, so the unequal collisions on different sides change from moment to moment [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the particles get bigger, or expand, when heated.
- Explaining gas pressure by particles colliding with each other.
- Adding 273 to convert from K to °C.
- Describing what is seen in a smoke cell without the words "random" or "jerky".
- *(Extended)* Saying particles move faster when a gas is compressed at constant temperature.
