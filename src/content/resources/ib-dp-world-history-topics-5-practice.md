## Section A

**1.** Define "absolutism" and identify **one** ruler often described as absolute. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the purpose of Peter the Great's Table of Ranks (1722). **[2]**

**3.** Explain **two** ways in which the *devshirme* strengthened the Ottoman sultan. **[4]**

**4.** Identify **three** features of Akbar's *mansabdari* system. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why Peter the Great replaced the patriarchate with the Holy Synod. **[3]**

**6.** Identify **two** limits on royal power set out in the English Bill of Rights (1689). **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** military and **one** diplomatic achievement of the Kangxi Emperor. **[4]**

---

## Section B

**8.** Explain **two** reasons why Peter the Great was able to defeat Sweden in the Great Northern War. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why conflict between Charles I and Parliament led to civil war in 1642. **[6]**

**10.** Compare and contrast the religious policies of **two** Early Modern rulers, each chosen from a different region. **[6]**

**11.** "Early Modern rulers depended more on cooperation with elites than on coercion." Explain **two** arguments for and **two** against this view, using examples from at least **two** states. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the success of Peter the Great's reforms in strengthening the Russian state. **[15]**

**13.** Compare and contrast the methods used by **two** rulers, each chosen from a different region, to maintain control over a large and diverse empire. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Absolutism: a ruler's claim to sovereign power not shared with or limited by any representative assembly, often justified by divine right (1). Example: Louis XIV, Peter the Great or Frederick II (1). **[2]**

**2.** It created a hierarchy of fourteen ranks in the army, navy and civil service, so status depended on service to the state (1). Commoners who reached a certain rank gained noble status, which rewarded merit and bound nobles to state service (1). **[2]**

**3.** 2 marks for each developed way: the recruits were taken from their families and converted, so their loyalty was to the sultan rather than to any noble family or region (2); they formed the Janissaries, a disciplined standing infantry that gave the sultan military power independent of the cavalry holders of *timars* (2); trained boys filled senior offices, including grand viziers, giving the sultan an administration of servants who owed him everything (2). **[4]**

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: each noble held a numbered rank (*mansab*) (1); the rank set his status and pay (1); it set the number of cavalry he had to maintain for the emperor (1); nobles were paid with revenue assignments (*jagirs*) or cash (1); assignments were transferred regularly (1); ranks were not hereditary and depended on the emperor's favour (1). **[3]**

**5.** The patriarch was a rival source of authority, and the Church had supported traditionalist opposition to Peter's reforms (1). Leaving the patriarchate vacant and creating the Holy Synod (1721) placed the Church under a council supervised by a state official (1). This allowed Peter to use the Church's wealth and influence to support his state and reforms (1). **[3]**

**6.** Any two, 1 mark each: taxation required the consent of Parliament (1); a standing army in peacetime required Parliament's consent (1); the monarch could not suspend laws (1); elections and freedom of speech in Parliament were to be free (1); a Catholic could not succeed to the throne (1). **[2]**

**7.** Military, up to 2 marks: suppressing the Revolt of the Three Feudatories (1673-1681), which secured Qing control of southern China (2), or taking Taiwan in 1683 (2). Diplomatic, up to 2 marks: the Treaty of Nerchinsk with Russia (1689), which fixed the frontier and opened regulated trade (2). **[4]**

**8.** 2 marks for each developed reason: after the defeat at Narva (1700) Peter rebuilt and enlarged the army by conscription and created a navy, so Russia could fight a long war (2); Charles XII's invasion of Russia overstretched Swedish supply lines through a harsh winter, and his army was destroyed at Poltava (1709) (2); Russia's greater population and resources outlasted Sweden's over two decades (2). **[4]**

**9.** 3 marks for each developed reason, up to 6: Charles raised money without Parliament during the Personal Rule (1629-1640), using measures such as Ship Money, which angered taxpayers (3); his religious policies, associated with Archbishop Laud, seemed to many to move the Church towards Catholicism and provoked war with Scotland (3); the Long Parliament's demands for control of the army and ministers, especially after the Irish rebellion of 1641, and Charles's attempt to arrest five members (1642) destroyed trust (3). **[6]**

**10.** Up to 6 marks, needing both similarity and difference, for example Akbar (Asia and Oceania) and Louis XIV (Europe). Similarities (up to 2): both used religion to strengthen royal authority (1); both sought to place the ruler above religious factions (1). Differences (up to 4): Akbar abolished the *jizya* and tolerated diverse religions, while Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes (1685) and persecuted Huguenots (1); Akbar sought unity through inclusion, Louis through uniformity (1); Akbar's policy won the support of Hindu elites, while Louis's caused many skilled Protestants to emigrate (1); Akbar drew authority from beyond a single faith, Louis from his role as the "Most Christian King" (1). **[6]**

**11.** 2 marks for each developed argument. For: Akbar's *mansabdari* system and Rajput alliances rewarded elites for cooperation (2); Louis XIV kept nobles at court with honours, offices and pensions, protecting their tax privileges (2). Against: Peter the Great used compulsion, executing *streltsy* rebels and forcing nobles into lifelong service (2); the Tokugawa required regular attendance at Edo and kept daimyo families there as hostages, a coercive system (2). **[8]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. Strengths: military and naval power proven at Poltava (1709) and Nystad (1721); access to the Baltic and St Petersburg; Senate, colleges and the Table of Ranks; increased revenue from the poll tax; the Church under state control. Limits: reliance on serfdom and coercion; heavy burdens on peasants; western ways adopted mainly by the elite; corruption and weak enforcement in the provinces; a disputed succession after 1725. A strong answer defines "strengthening the state", judges each reform against it and reaches a balanced conclusion. **[15]**

**13.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands. *For example, Suleiman (Ottoman empire) and Akbar (Mughal empire); check that your two examples meet the regional requirement.* Suleiman: *devshirme* elite; *timar* cavalry; legal codes; religious communities managing their own affairs; conquest. Akbar: *mansabdari*; rotated *jagirs*; revenue reform; Rajput alliances; religious toleration. Comparison: both created service elites dependent on the ruler and accommodated religious diversity; the Ottomans relied more on slave-born servants, the Mughals on incorporating existing aristocracies. A strong answer compares in each paragraph and judges which methods were most effective. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Are the Ottoman and Mughal empires in different regions?**
The Mughal empire is in Asia and Oceania. The Ottoman empire spanned Europe and the Middle East, so it can be paired with the Mughals, but check how your teacher has framed it.

**Can I use Louis XIV in this topic?**
Yes. He is a classic example, but prepare at least one non-European state for comparative questions.
