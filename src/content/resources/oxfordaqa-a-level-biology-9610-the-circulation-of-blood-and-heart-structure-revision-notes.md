For worked traces and tissue fluid problems, use the [circulation and heart study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-the-circulation-of-blood-and-heart-structure/); then try the [circulation and heart practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-the-circulation-of-blood-and-heart-structure-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Double circulation | Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit: pulmonary (to the lungs) and systemic (to the body) |
| Artery | Vessel carrying blood away from the heart |
| Vein | Vessel carrying blood towards the heart |
| Capillary | Vessel with a wall one cell thick, where metabolic exchange occurs |
| Tissue fluid | Fluid formed from plasma that surrounds cells; contains no red blood cells or large proteins |
| Hydrostatic pressure | Pressure of the blood against the vessel wall, pushing fluid out |
| Atrioventricular valve | Valve between atrium and ventricle; prevents backflow into the atrium |
| Semilunar valve | Valve at the base of the aorta and of the pulmonary artery; prevents backflow into the ventricle |
| Stroke volume | Volume of blood pumped by one ventricle in one beat |
| Cardiac output | Volume pumped by one ventricle per minute = heart rate × stroke volume |

## Route through the heart

Vena cava → right atrium → AV valve → right ventricle → semilunar valve → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → AV valve → left ventricle → semilunar valve → aorta → body. Coronary arteries branch from the aorta to supply cardiac muscle.

## Wall thickness

- Ventricles thicker than atria: they pump blood out of the heart, not just into the next chamber.
- Left ventricle thicker than right: higher pressure needed for the whole body; same volume per beat.

## Vessels in one table

| | Artery | Vein | Capillary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure | High, pulsing | Low | Falling along its length |
| Wall | Thick: elastic tissue, muscle, collagen | Thin, little elastic or muscle | One layer of endothelial cells |
| Lumen | Narrow | Wide | About one red cell wide |
| Special features | Elastic recoil smooths flow | Valves stop backflow | Gaps between cells; large total surface area |

## Tissue fluid in four lines

1. Arteriole end: hydrostatic pressure > water potential effect, so water and small solutes forced out.
2. Large plasma proteins and red cells stay in the blood.
3. Venule end: hydrostatic pressure lower; plasma has lower water potential, so water returns by osmosis.
4. Excess fluid drains into lymph vessels and returns to the blood.

**Predicting changes:**

| Change | Effect |
|---|---|
| Higher blood pressure | More fluid out, less back: tissue fluid builds up |
| Less plasma protein | Plasma water potential less negative, less water returns: tissue fluid builds up |
| Blocked lymph vessels | Excess fluid not drained: tissue fluid builds up |

## Valves and pressure

Valves move passively: they open when pressure behind exceeds pressure in front.

| Pressure event | Valve |
|---|---|
| Ventricle > atrium | AV valve closes |
| Ventricle > artery | Semilunar valve opens |
| Ventricle < artery | Semilunar valve closes |
| Ventricle < atrium | AV valve opens |

Both valves closed: volume unchanged while ventricular pressure rises (start of contraction) or falls (start of relaxation).

## Calculation toolkit

- Stroke volume = maximum volume − minimum volume on the ventricle volume trace.
- Heart rate (beats per minute) = 60 ÷ time for one cycle (s).
- Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume (cm³ per minute).

**Worked reminder.** Cycle 0.8 s, volumes 125 cm³ and 55 cm³: heart rate = 75 beats per minute; stroke volume = 70 cm³; cardiac output = 5250 cm³ per minute.

## Exam traps

- "Valves are opened by muscles." They respond to pressure.
- "The left ventricle pumps more blood." Higher pressure, same volume.
- "Arteries carry oxygenated blood." The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
- "Plasma proteins form tissue fluid." They are too large to leave.
- "Water returns by hydrostatic pressure." It returns by osmosis.
- Forgetting "per minute" on cardiac output.
- Reading the wrong crossover: AV valve events involve atrial pressure; semilunar events involve arterial pressure.

## Quick self-test

1. Which chamber receives blood from the pulmonary vein?
2. Why is the right ventricle wall thinner than the left?
3. Name the vessels that supply cardiac muscle with blood.
4. How do elastic fibres in an artery wall help blood flow?
5. Why do veins have valves?
6. Give two features of capillaries that help metabolic exchange.
7. Why does tissue fluid leave at the arteriole end of a capillary?
8. Why does water return at the venule end?
9. Predict the effect of a fall in plasma protein concentration on tissue fluid.
10. When does the atrioventricular valve close?
11. A cycle lasts 0.6 s. Calculate the heart rate.
12. Cardiac output is 6300 cm³ per minute and heart rate 90 beats per minute. Calculate the stroke volume.

### Answers

1. The left atrium.
2. It pumps blood only to the nearby lungs, so it needs to generate a lower pressure.
3. Coronary arteries.
4. They stretch as blood is ejected and recoil, smoothing flow and maintaining pressure between beats.
5. Pressure is low, so valves prevent backflow.
6. Any two: wall one cell thick (short diffusion distance); large surface area; narrow lumen so blood flows slowly close to the wall; gaps between cells let fluid out.
7. Hydrostatic pressure of the blood is high enough to force water and small solutes out through the gaps.
8. Hydrostatic pressure is lower and plasma proteins keep the water potential of the plasma lower than the tissue fluid, so water re-enters by osmosis.
9. Less water returns by osmosis, so tissue fluid accumulates.
10. When ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure.
11. 60 ÷ 0.6 = **100 beats per minute**.
12. 6300 ÷ 90 = **70 cm³**.

## Frequently asked questions

**What makes heart valves open and close?**
Pressure differences. A valve opens when the pressure behind it is higher than in front, and closes when blood starts to flow back.

**What is the difference between stroke volume and cardiac output?**
Stroke volume is the volume pumped by one ventricle per beat. Cardiac output is the volume pumped per minute: heart rate multiplied by stroke volume.
