## 4.2.3(a)(i): Quickfit apparatus

Quickfit glassware has standard ground-glass joints, so a flask, condenser, still head and adapter fit together without corks or bungs. Clamp the flask and condenser to a stand. Use a round-bottomed or pear-shaped flask no more than about half full, with anti-bumping granules so the liquid boils smoothly. Most organic liquids are flammable, so heat with an electric heating mantle or a water bath, not a Bunsen flame.

### Heating under reflux

The condenser stands vertically on top of the flask. Cooling water enters at the bottom and leaves at the top, so the jacket stays full. Vapour rises, condenses and drips back into the flask. The top of the condenser stays open to the air.

Reflux lets you heat a slow reaction for a long time without losing volatile reactants or products.

### Distillation

The flask is fitted with a still head. The thermometer bulb sits level with the side arm, so it reads the temperature of the vapour leaving for the condenser. The condenser slopes down to a receiver, again with water in at the bottom. You collect the liquid that distils over close to the boiling point of the substance you want.

Distillation removes a volatile product as it forms, or separates liquids with different boiling points.

### Worked example 1: choosing the set-up

A primary alcohol is oxidised with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Choose the set-up for each target product.

1. **Aldehyde wanted.** Use distillation. With no O–H group, the aldehyde lacks the hydrogen bonding of the alcohol, has the lower boiling point, and escapes from the flask before more oxidation can happen.
2. **Carboxylic acid wanted.** Use heating under reflux with excess oxidising agent. Any aldehyde that vaporises condenses and falls back, so it stays in contact with the dichromate until it is oxidised to the acid.
3. Either way, the colour turns from orange to green.

### Safety

Never heat a closed system: a stoppered reflux condenser builds pressure and can shatter. A PAG5 risk assessment also covers corrosive acids, flammable liquids and eye protection.

## 4.2.3(a)(ii): Preparing and purifying an organic liquid

A haloalkane can be made from an alcohol using sodium bromide and sulfuric acid (4.2.1(e)). The crude product contains unreacted alcohol, water, acid and by-products. Purify it in this order.

1. **React.** Heat the alcohol, sodium bromide and sulfuric acid under reflux.
2. **Isolate the crude product.** Distil the haloalkane out of the reaction mixture.
3. **Separate.** Pour the distillate into a separating funnel with water. Stopper, invert and shake, opening the tap often to release pressure. Let the layers settle. The less dense liquid floats on top.
4. **Wash.** Remove leftover acid by shaking with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution. Carbon dioxide forms, so vent often. Run off the aqueous layer each time.
5. **Dry.** Run the organic layer into a dry conical flask. Add an anhydrous salt such as anhydrous magnesium sulfate or anhydrous calcium chloride and swirl. The liquid is dry when it changes from cloudy to clear and some drying agent moves freely instead of clumping.
6. **Redistil.** Decant or filter off the drying agent, then redistil. Collect only the fraction that boils within a narrow range around the product's boiling point.

### Worked example 2: which layer is which?

The haloalkane in a preparation has density 1.21 g cm⁻³ (value supplied). After shaking with water, a student is unsure which layer to keep.

1. Water has density about 1.00 g cm⁻³.
2. The haloalkane is denser, so it forms the **lower** layer.
3. Run the lower layer into a clean flask and keep it. Discard the aqueous upper layer.
4. If still unsure, add a few drops of water: the layer that grows is the aqueous one.

### Worked example 3: percentage yield of a preparation

This links to 2.1.3 in Module 2. A student heats 11.0 cm³ of 3-methylbutan-1-ol (density 0.810 g cm⁻³) under reflux with excess sodium bromide and sulfuric acid. After purification, 9.80 g of 1-bromo-3-methylbutane is collected. Take Br as 79.9, O as 16.0, C as 12.0 and H as 1.0.

The equation is: (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂OH + NaBr + H₂SO₄ → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂Br + NaHSO₄ + H₂O

1. Mass of alcohol = 11.0 × 0.810 = 8.91 g.
2. M(C₅H₁₂O) = 88.0 g mol⁻¹, so n(alcohol) = 8.91 ÷ 88.0 = 0.10125 mol.
3. One mole of alcohol gives one mole of bromoalkane, so at most 0.10125 mol can form.
4. M(C₅H₁₁Br) = 150.9 g mol⁻¹, so the theoretical mass = 0.10125 × 150.9 = 15.28 g.
5. Percentage yield = 9.80 ÷ 15.28 × 100 = **64.1%**.

Losses at each transfer, in aqueous layers and on the drying agent make yields well below 100% normal.

## 4.2.3(b): Molecules with several functional groups

You must recognise the functional groups met in Module 4 and predict how each behaves. Treat each group on its own, then check whether a reagent would attack more than one group.

| Functional group | How to spot it | Typical reactions in Module 4 |
|---|---|---|
| Alkene | C=C | Adds H₂ (Ni catalyst), halogens, hydrogen halides, steam (H₃PO₄ catalyst); decolourises bromine; addition polymerisation |
| Primary alcohol | –CH₂OH | Oxidised to aldehyde (distil) or carboxylic acid (reflux); dehydration; substitution to haloalkane |
| Secondary alcohol | >CHOH | Oxidised to ketone; dehydration; substitution |
| Tertiary alcohol | OH on a carbon bonded to three carbons | Resists oxidation; dehydration; substitution |
| Haloalkane | C–X | Hydrolysis by aqueous alkali to an alcohol; hydrolysis by water with AgNO₃ and ethanol |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | Further oxidation to carboxylic acid |
| Ketone | C=O between two carbons | No reaction with acidified dichromate |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | Acid reactions (2.1.4) |
| Alkane chain | C–C and C–H only | Combustion; radical substitution with Cl₂ or Br₂ in UV |

An O–H group allows hydrogen bonding, raising the boiling point and giving some water solubility. A C=C bond is reactive because the π-bond has a relatively low bond enthalpy. A polar C–X bond makes the carbon a target for nucleophiles.

### Worked example 4: predicting reactions

Compound A is CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH(OH)CH₂Cl, 1-chlorohex-5-en-2-ol.

**(b)(i) Identify the groups.** A has an alkene (C=C at the end of the chain), a secondary alcohol (OH on carbon 2, which is bonded to two other carbons) and a chloroalkane (C–Cl on carbon 1).

**(b)(ii) Predict reactions.**

1. **Bromine.** The C=C adds Br₂, so bromine is decolourised. Product: CH₂BrCHBrCH₂CH₂CH(OH)CH₂Cl.
2. **H₂ with a nickel catalyst.** Only the C=C reacts. Product: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH(OH)CH₂Cl.
3. **Acidified potassium dichromate(VI), heated under reflux.** The secondary alcohol is oxidised to a ketone; the colour turns from orange to green. Product: CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂COCH₂Cl.
4. **Aqueous sodium hydroxide, heated.** The C–Cl bond is hydrolysed. Product: CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH(OH)CH₂OH, a diol.
5. **Properties.** The OH group hydrogen bonds, so A is less volatile than an alkene of similar Mr and partly soluble in water.

## 4.2.3(c): Two-stage synthetic routes

The specification says you should devise two-stage routes using transformations between all the functional groups met up to this point. Exam papers may also supply extra reactions to extend your toolkit. Use them as given.

### Module 4 reaction toolkit

| From | To | Reagents and conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Alkane | Haloalkane | Cl₂ or Br₂, UV radiation (gives a mixture of products) |
| Alkene | Alkane | H₂, Ni catalyst |
| Alkene | Dihaloalkane | Br₂ or Cl₂ |
| Alkene | Haloalkane | HBr or HCl (Markownikoff's rule for unsymmetrical alkenes) |
| Alkene | Alcohol | Steam, H₃PO₄ catalyst |
| Alcohol | Alkene | H₃PO₄ or H₂SO₄ catalyst, heat |
| Alcohol | Haloalkane | NaBr and H₂SO₄ (halide ions with acid) |
| Haloalkane | Alcohol | NaOH(aq), heat under reflux |
| Primary alcohol | Aldehyde | K₂Cr₂O₇ / H₂SO₄, distil the aldehyde off as it forms |
| Primary alcohol | Carboxylic acid | Excess K₂Cr₂O₇ / H₂SO₄, reflux |
| Secondary alcohol | Ketone | K₂Cr₂O₇ / H₂SO₄, reflux |

### Method

1. Compare the starting material and the target. Which groups change? No Module 4 reaction adds carbon atoms, so the skeleton must match.
2. Work backwards: what single step makes the target, and from what?
3. Check that the intermediate can be made from the starting material in one step.
4. Write each step with reagents, conditions and the structure of the intermediate.

### Worked example 5: 1-chloropentane to pentanoic acid

1. The target is a carboxylic acid with the same five-carbon chain, which comes from a primary alcohol.
2. Step 1: NaOH(aq), heat under reflux. 1-chloropentane → **pentan-1-ol**, CH₃(CH₂)₃CH₂OH.
3. Step 2: excess K₂Cr₂O₇ with H₂SO₄, heat under reflux. Pentan-1-ol → **pentanoic acid**, CH₃(CH₂)₃COOH.

### Worked example 6: pentan-2-ol to pentane

1. Step 1: heat with an acid catalyst such as H₃PO₄. Pentan-2-ol loses water to give pent-1-ene and pent-2-ene (E and Z).
2. Step 2: H₂ with a Ni catalyst. Every one of these alkenes gives **pentane**, so the mixture from step 1 does not matter.

### Worked example 7: cyclopentane to cyclopentanol

1. An alkane has no functional group, so start with radical substitution.
2. Step 1: Br₂ in UV radiation gives bromocyclopentane. All ten hydrogens are equivalent, so only one monobromo product forms, though further substitution still occurs (4.1.2(g)).
3. Step 2: NaOH(aq), heat under reflux, gives **cyclopentanol**.

## Common errors

- Stoppering a reflux condenser, or putting the distillation thermometer bulb in the liquid.
- Giving "heat" with no mention of reflux or distillation for an alcohol oxidation.
- Trying to oxidise a tertiary alcohol in a route.
- Using a reagent that also attacks a group you wanted to keep.
