The new IB Psychology course (first assessment 2027) is built on six **concepts**: bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective and responsibility. They are not a separate unit to learn and forget. The guide says the concepts, the content (the biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches and research methodology) and the four contexts are "not meant to be taught separately". The concepts are the lens you use on everything else, and they are tested directly in Paper 1 Section C and Paper 2 Section B. This study guide covers all six at standard level (SL) and higher level (HL).

Read this page first, then use the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-concepts-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-concepts-practice/).

## What a concept is, and how it is assessed

A concept is a broad idea that shows up in many places. The guide's own example is bias. It appears in how research is organised (researcher bias), in how theories are built (biological determinism) and in behaviour itself (cognitive biases). These are different ideas, but they are all connected through the concept of bias. Being able to see that connection, and to use it to ask a good question, is the skill.

Three things matter for the examination.

- **Paper 1 Section C** (15 marks) gives you two concept-based extended response questions, each from a different context, and you answer one. The markbands reward understanding of the demands of the question, critical analysis, **links between concepts** that are explained, and a reasoned conclusion. Knowledge of the details of named studies is not assessed in Section C.
- **Paper 2 Section B** (15 marks) gives you an unseen research study and asks you to discuss it with regard to the concepts the question names. You need specific references to the study.
- **Related terms** are given for each concept in the guide. You do not have to teach or learn all of them, they are not exhaustive and they will not be used to set the concept-based questions. Any other term is acceptable if its link to the concept is "relevant, explained and clearly justified".

The guide recommends choosing three to four relevant concepts when you study each context. It also says concepts relate to each other: for example reductionism is linked to both bias and causality. A link between concepts is credited as long as you explain it.

## Bias

The guide defines a bias as a limitation in objective thinking: a tendency to see information through a "cognitive filter" of experience and preference. Bias has three main homes.

1. **In the research process.** Researcher bias, participant bias, sampling bias, confirmation bias and publication bias. The important step is not just naming the bias but explaining how it changes what was done or how the results were read. A researcher who expects a result may treat groups differently (researcher bias). A sample of volunteers may differ from the target population (sampling bias). Journals that mostly publish positive results give a distorted picture of the evidence (publication bias). Creating controls for bias is part of good research.
2. **In theories and approaches.** Biological determinism and reductionism are biases towards one perspective. Gender bias and cultural bias can affect diagnosis, prevalence statistics and treatment in clinical psychology.
3. **In everyday thinking.** Cognitive biases such as anchoring, representativeness and availability affect decisions, especially when there is not enough time, information or motivation.

Related terms in the guide: researcher, participant, sampling and confirmation bias; publication bias; gender and cultural bias; sampling techniques; determinism; cognitive biases; credibility, reflexivity and inter-rater reliability; positivism; reductionism and holism.

## Causality

Psychologists try to find cause-and-effect relationships, but behaviour is complex. Often several variables interact, so the cause is not a single direct effect. The guide separates **influence** from **interaction**: one factor may influence behaviour, but most behaviours come from several factors acting together.

The key questions are about what a method allows you to claim.

- A **true experiment** manipulates an independent variable and controls extraneous variables, so it is the method best placed to support a causal claim. Controls named in the guide include placebos, double-blind procedures and wait-listing.
- A **correlation** shows a relationship but not a cause. Two problems arise: a third variable may explain both, and there may be **bidirectional ambiguity** (A may cause B, or B may cause A).
- **Internal validity** is whether the study really shows what caused the change; **external validity** is whether the finding generalises. **Mundane realism** is how close the task is to real life.
- **Significance** tells you how likely the result is if chance alone were operating. It does not tell you the effect is large or important.

Related terms also include reductionism, complexity, agency and motivation.

## Change

Change is about how behaviour can be altered and why it is hard. The guide describes change that is gradual (development) or sudden (mood), planned or unplanned, and deliberate (for example motivation to change through education or treatment). It links to the old debate between free will and determinism.

Researchers study how to promote change, such as stress management, healthy eating or stopping a harmful habit, and they also study **barriers to change**. They judge **effectiveness of treatment** and of prevention and promotion strategies at individual, local and global levels. Designs matter here: **longitudinal** and **repeated measures** designs track change in the same people over time, while a cross-sectional design compares different people at one time.

A useful question is the guide's own: how do we know whether a behaviour is a result of natural maturation or of purposeful intervention? A control or wait-list group is the usual answer, because it shows what happens without the intervention.

Related terms: barriers to change, prevalence, longitudinal and repeated measures designs, intervention, prevention and promotion strategies, effectiveness of treatment, development and maturation, determinism, agency, motivation.

## Measurement

Behaviour is hard to observe and measure objectively. Measurement therefore depends on the context and on the theory behind it. A central skill is **operationalisation**: stating a variable in terms that can be measured. "Stress" might be operationalised as score on a questionnaire, heart rate, or cortisol level in saliva. Each choice gives a different picture, which is why **triangulation** (using more than one method) increases credibility.

The guide asks you to recognise different kinds of evidence and data.

- Direct and indirect measurement. Measuring brain activity with an MRI scanner to infer the role of a neurotransmitter is indirect.
- Anecdotal, empirical and self-reported evidence, each with strengths and limitations.
- Quantitative data (numbers), qualitative data (words, meanings) or both.
- Techniques including brain imaging, twin studies, virtual reality simulations and questionnaires.

Related terms: choice of research method, constructs and variables, operationalisation, types of data, statistical significance and type I and type II errors, brain imaging, interpretive approaches such as content analysis, prospective and retrospective approaches, longitudinal and cross-sectional designs.

## Perspective

No single perspective explains behaviour fully. The course uses three: the biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches. Each rests on assumptions about behaviour and beliefs about research. The sociocultural approach, for example, is more holistic and uses both **etic** and **emic** methods. An etic approach studies behaviour from outside, comparing cultures with the same measures. An emic approach studies a group from the inside, in its own terms.

Perspective also covers deductive and inductive reasoning, psychological theories and models, **alternative explanations**, and the **perspectives of Indigenous people**. A strong answer shows what each perspective explains well, what it leaves out, and how they can be combined.

## Responsibility

Responsibility is about how psychologists treat participants and how they use research. The guide lists informed consent, deception, anonymity, debriefing, right to withdraw, protection from harm and cost-benefit analysis. It adds the **3Rs for animal research**: reduce the number of animals, refine conditions to minimise harm, and replace animals with alternatives wherever possible. Child participants, research in public spaces (including observation-only studies) and animals are specifically mentioned.

Two points deserve emphasis because they are easy to miss.

- Ethical standards can **affect results**. Informed consent can introduce participant variables, the right to withdraw can lead to sampling bias, and anonymity can increase the validity of self-reported data.
- Responsibility continues after the study. Psychologists must protect marginalised groups from stigma, make sure the public understands the uncertainty in findings, and may advocate for policy that matches the evidence.

Related terms: ethical standards, cost-benefit analysis, socially sensitive issues, stigma, publication of findings, advocacy.

## A worked example across concepts

Take a short description (constructed for practice): "Researchers advertised a study of sleep and exam performance on a social media group for students who were already proud of their sleep habits. Participants reported their hours of sleep each night and their latest exam mark. The researchers found that more sleep was linked with higher marks and concluded that sleep improves exam performance."

- **Bias:** the sample is self-selected from one group, which is sampling bias. Self-reported sleep may involve social desirability.
- **Causality:** the study is correlational. High marks might lead to less anxiety and more sleep (bidirectional ambiguity), or conscientiousness might explain both. The conclusion goes beyond the design.
- **Measurement:** "sleep" is operationalised as self-reported hours, which is indirect and less reliable than actigraphy or a sleep lab.
- **Responsibility:** if the findings were published as advice, the public should be told about the uncertainty.

Notice how each concept raises a different question about the same study, and how the answers support each other. That is the link between concepts the markbands reward.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the concept but never applying it to a study or context.
- Listing related terms without explaining their link to the question.
- Using one concept only. Section C and Paper 2 Section B reward links between concepts.
- Treating bias as always deliberate. Bias can be implicit or explicit.
- Writing "this proves" about a correlation.
- Describing ethics as a list of rules without saying what they cost or protect in the particular study.
