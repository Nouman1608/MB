This guide covers **3.4 The human impact** in Topic 3, Rocks and weathering, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content is:

- **The impact of human activities on the stability of slopes:** increasing stability and decreasing stability.
- **Strategies to modify slopes to reduce mass movements:** pinning, netting, grading and afforestation.
- **Case study:** the impacts of human activity on slopes, showing the effect on the stability of the slope, and an evaluation of attempts to reduce mass movement.

It is examined in Paper 1, often as the final, extended part of the Rocks and weathering structured question in Section B. The [3.4 human impact revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-slopes-human-impact-revision-notes/) contain a worked case study outline of Hong Kong and a contrast with Aberfan. This guide gives a framework for explaining any human impact, examines the four named strategies in depth, and shows how to evaluate them. Practise with the [3.4 human impact practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-human-impact-3-4-practice/).

## The framework: stress and strength

A slope is stable while the **shear strength** of its material exceeds the **shear stress** acting downslope. Every human impact can be explained by asking which side of this balance it changes. This approach, built in 3.3, turns a list of activities into an explanation, which is what examiners reward.

## Human activities that decrease stability

| Activity | Effect on stress | Effect on strength |
|---|---|---|
| Cutting into the base of a slope for roads, railways or building platforms | Removes support at the toe and steepens the slope, raising stress | Exposes weaker material and joints to weathering |
| Loading the top of a slope with buildings, spoil tips, fill or reservoirs | Adds weight, raising stress | Loose fill or spoil has low strength |
| Changing drainage: leaking pipes and sewers, irrigation, soakaways, blocked drains | Water adds weight | Raises pore water pressure, lowering friction |
| Deforestation or clearing vegetation | Little change | Root cohesion lost as roots decay; less transpiration, so soils stay wetter |
| Filling reservoirs against valley sides | Water in the slope adds weight | Raises the water table and pore water pressure in the slope |
| Vibration from blasting, heavy traffic or pile driving | Short pulses of extra stress | Can loosen material and break cohesion |
| Mining and quarrying | Steep faces and removal of support | Underground workings can cause subsidence |

The **Vajont disaster** in north-east Italy in October 1963 is a stark example of how a reservoir can destabilise a slope: after the reservoir behind the new Vajont Dam was filled, a huge mass of rock slid from the side of Monte Toc into it, sending a wave over the dam that destroyed Longarone and other villages and killed about 2,000 people. The dam itself survived; the failure was of the valley side, whose weak, water-saturated layers had been made less stable by the rising water level.

## Human activities that increase stability

People also make slopes more stable, sometimes deliberately and sometimes as a side effect:

- **Drainage:** surface channels and deep horizontal drains remove water, lowering pore water pressure. Often this is the most cost-effective single measure.
- **Retaining walls and gabions** support the toe of a slope.
- **Terracing** for agriculture reduces the length and angle of each section of slope and slows runoff, as long as terrace walls are maintained. Abandoned terraces can collapse and become a hazard.
- **Planting vegetation** (see afforestation below).
- **Removing weight** from the top of a slope.

## The four named strategies, examined

**Pinning** fixes unstable material to stable rock beneath it. Steel **rock bolts** are drilled through loose blocks into sound rock, and **soil nails** are steel bars inserted into soil slopes and grouted in place. Pinning raises shear strength directly, works on steep slopes where there is no room to regrade, and takes up little land. However, it is expensive per square metre, needs skilled engineering and site investigation to know how deep stable rock lies, and steel can corrode, so bolts and nails must be inspected for decades.

**Netting** drapes steel mesh over a rock face, or anchors it in place, to hold loose fragments against the slope or to guide falling debris safely to the base; **catch fences** and barriers can stop falling blocks before they reach a road. Netting is relatively cheap and quick to install along transport routes. Its key limitation is that it **does not stop rock from detaching**; it manages the result. Debris collecting behind nets must be cleared, and nets cannot hold very large failures.

**Grading** (regrading) reduces the slope angle by removing material from the top or adding it at the foot, sometimes cutting the slope into steps (**benching**). A lower angle reduces the downslope component of weight, so shear stress falls. It is effective for slides and slumps in soil and weak rock. However, it needs a lot of land, which is a serious problem in crowded cities, and it can take a long time and disturb the area.

**Afforestation** plants trees and other vegetation on slopes. Roots bind soil and add cohesion; canopies intercept rain and reduce rainsplash; transpiration dries the soil and lowers pore water pressure. It is cheap, sustainable and improves the landscape and habitats. But trees take years to establish, roots mainly strengthen the top metre or two of soil, so they do little against deep-seated slides, and large trees add weight and can be uprooted in storms.

## Worked example: how much land does grading need?

*A fictional slope beside a road is 40 m high and stands at 45°. Engineers propose regrading it to 30°. How much extra horizontal distance will the slope occupy?*

Horizontal run = height / tan(angle).

- At 45°: 40 / tan 45° = 40 / 1 = **40 m**.
- At 30°: 40 / tan 30° = 40 / 0.577 = **69.3 m**.

Extra land needed = 69.3 - 40 = **29.3 m** along the whole length of the slope. If houses or a road stand at the foot or crest, that land may not be available, which is why pinning or netting is often chosen in dense urban areas despite their cost.

## Evaluating strategies: criteria to use

When asked to **evaluate**, judge each strategy against criteria rather than listing advantages:

| Criterion | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Does it prevent movement, or only manage the consequences? Does it work for the type and depth of movement involved? |
| Cost | What are the initial and maintenance costs? |
| Land and space | How much land does it need? |
| Time | How quickly does it become effective? |
| Environment and appearance | Is it visually intrusive? Does it support or damage habitats? |
| Sustainability | Will it last, and who will maintain it? |

A strong evaluation recognises that **combinations** usually work best: for example, drainage plus soil nailing plus surface planting on an urban cut slope.

## Planning the slope case study

Prepare one real case study, with located and dated detail, under three headings that match the syllabus wording:

1. **Human impacts on the slope:** what people did (cutting, loading, drainage changes, deforestation) and where.
2. **Effect on stability:** explain each impact with the stress-strength balance and give the type of mass movement that followed, with dates and consequences.
3. **Evaluation of attempts to reduce mass movement:** which strategies were used, how well they worked (evidence such as fewer failures or deaths), their costs and limitations, and a judgement.

The Hong Kong outline in the revision notes covers all three headings; the Vajont example above is best used as supporting evidence of a single human impact rather than as a full case study.

## Annotated model answer

*"Evaluate the effectiveness of netting and afforestation in reducing mass movement." [8, extract]*

> Netting is effective along roads below rock faces because it holds small loose fragments in place and guides falling debris to the base, so it reduces the danger from rockfalls quickly and at relatively low cost **(effectiveness with mechanism)**. However, it does not stop rock detaching, and it cannot hold large failures, so it manages rather than prevents mass movement **(limitation)**. Afforestation tackles causes rather than effects, because roots add cohesion and transpiration lowers pore water pressure **(contrast in approach)**, but it takes years to work and roots rarely reach the deep slip surfaces of large slides **(limitation)**. Netting therefore suits rock faces where the threat is small falls, and afforestation suits shallow soil slopes over the long term, so each is effective only for particular movements **(judgement)**.

## Common mistakes

- Listing human activities without explaining how each changes stress or strength.
- Forgetting the syllabus asks for activities that increase stability as well as decrease it.
- Describing netting as if it prevents rockfalls.
- Saying afforestation works immediately.
- Writing a case study with no place, date or evidence of success or failure.
- Evaluating with "it is expensive" and nothing else.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** an impact: name the activity, the change to stress or strength, and the resulting mass movement.
- **Evaluate** strategies: apply criteria, compare strategies and reach a judgement that depends on the type of movement and setting.
- **Calculate** slope geometry carefully, stating which trigonometric ratio you used.
