## The atmosphere as a dynamic system

The atmosphere is an open system with **inputs** (solar radiation; gases from volcanoes, respiration, decomposition and combustion), **storages** (gases, water vapour, aerosols, heat), **flows** (winds, convection, the hydrological cycle) and **outputs** (radiation emitted to space; gases absorbed by oceans, rocks and organisms). Its composition is held roughly steady by balances between sources and sinks, and it changes when those balances shift. That is the key idea behind 6.2: human emissions are new inputs that exceed the capacity of the sinks.

## How the atmosphere evolved

Earth's present air is very different from its early atmosphere.

1. **Early atmosphere.** Volcanic outgassing released water vapour, carbon dioxide and nitrogen. There was almost no free oxygen.
2. **Oceans form.** As the planet cooled, water vapour condensed into oceans. Carbon dioxide dissolved in the water and was locked into carbonate rocks, removing much of it from the air.
3. **Photosynthesis.** Photosynthetic microorganisms, especially cyanobacteria, released oxygen. At first it reacted with iron and other substances in rocks and oceans. Once these sinks were used up, oxygen built up in the atmosphere. This rise, about 2.4 billion years ago, is called the **Great Oxidation Event**.
4. **Ozone layer.** With free oxygen in the upper atmosphere, ultraviolet radiation could form ozone. The ozone layer screened the surface from harmful UV, which allowed life to spread onto land.
5. **Living regulation.** Since then, photosynthesis, respiration, burial of organic carbon and the weathering of rocks have kept oxygen and carbon dioxide within ranges that support life.

The lesson for exam answers: organisms have shaped the atmosphere as much as it has shaped them. Nitrogen, the most abundant gas, is mostly unreactive but is cycled slowly through fixation and denitrification.

## Variable components

Dry-air percentages hide two important variable components:

- **Water vapour** varies from almost zero in cold, dry air to a few per cent by volume in warm, humid air. It is the most abundant greenhouse gas, and its amount depends on temperature, which is why it acts as a feedback rather than a direct driver of climate change.
- **Aerosols** are tiny particles and droplets: dust, sea salt, soot, sulfate from volcanoes and pollution. They scatter or absorb sunlight and act as nuclei for cloud droplets. Most aerosols have a cooling effect by reflecting sunlight, but soot (black carbon) absorbs it and warms the air.

## Layers and the temperature profile

The atmosphere is divided by how temperature changes with height. Each change has a cause.

| Layer | Approximate height | Temperature with height | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Troposphere | Surface to about 12 km (higher at the equator, lower at the poles) | Falls | Heated from below by the ground, which absorbs sunlight; rising air expands and cools |
| Stratosphere | To about 50 km | Rises | Ozone absorbs UV and warms the air |
| Mesosphere | To about 80–85 km | Falls | Little ozone to absorb UV; air very thin |
| Thermosphere | Above the mesosphere | Rises steeply | Few molecules absorb high-energy solar radiation |

The boundaries are the tropopause, stratopause and mesopause. Because temperature rises with height in the stratosphere, warm air sits above cooler air. This stable layering limits vertical mixing, so pollutants that reach the stratosphere can stay there for years.

### Worked example: the lapse rate

In the troposphere, temperature falls on average by about 6.5 °C per kilometre. A fictional town at sea level has an air temperature of 24 °C. Estimate the temperature at the summit of a 3.0 km mountain nearby and at 11 km, near the tropopause.

```
fall at 3.0 km = 6.5 °C km⁻¹ × 3.0 km = 19.5 °C
temperature    = 24 − 19.5 = 4.5 °C

fall at 11 km  = 6.5 × 11 = 71.5 °C
temperature    = 24 − 71.5 = −47.5 °C
```

**Interpretation.** The average lapse rate explains why mountain tops are cold even in the tropics and why biomes change with altitude as they do with latitude. Actual rates vary with humidity and weather: moist rising air cools more slowly because condensation releases latent heat.

## Pressure and density

Air is compressible, so it is densest at the surface, where it is squeezed by the weight of air above. Pressure falls with height: roughly half the mass of the atmosphere lies below about 5.5 km, and about 90% lies within the lowest 16 km or so. Consequences:

- The troposphere holds most of the atmosphere's mass and nearly all its water vapour, so weather happens there.
- At high altitude, each breath contains fewer oxygen molecules, which limits where people and many organisms can live.
- The upper layers are so thin that, although the thermosphere is very hot in terms of molecular energy, it would not feel hot, because there are so few molecules to transfer heat.

## Albedo of different surfaces

Albedo is the fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected by a surface. Typical values:

| Surface | Typical albedo |
|---|---|
| Fresh snow | 0.80–0.90 |
| Sea ice | 0.50–0.70 |
| Desert sand | 0.30–0.40 |
| Grassland | 0.20–0.25 |
| Forest | 0.10–0.20 |
| Open ocean (sun high in the sky) | about 0.06 |

### Worked example: albedo of a mixed region

A fictional region is 30% snow-covered (albedo 0.85) and 70% forest (albedo 0.15). Calculate its overall albedo and the fraction of sunlight absorbed.

```
overall albedo = (0.30 × 0.85) + (0.70 × 0.15)
               = 0.255 + 0.105 = 0.36
fraction absorbed = 1 − 0.36 = 0.64 (64 %)
```

If the snow melts and the region becomes all forest, absorption rises from 64% to 85%. Changes in land cover, snow and ice therefore alter how much energy the surface absorbs, linking 6.1 to the feedbacks in 6.2.

## Why some gases absorb infrared

The surface absorbs short-wave radiation and emits long-wave infrared. Only some gases absorb that infrared. A molecule absorbs infrared if its vibrations change the way electric charge is distributed across it. Molecules made of three or more atoms, such as water vapour (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O) and ozone (O₃), can vibrate in these ways and are greenhouse gases. Molecules made of two identical atoms, nitrogen (N₂) and oxygen (O₂), cannot, so although they make up about 99% of dry air, they are almost transparent to infrared. A molecule that absorbs infrared then re-emits it in a random direction, so part of the energy returns towards the surface.

## Model answer: explain

**Question.** Explain why temperature increases with height in the stratosphere. **[2]**

**Answer.** Most atmospheric ozone is in the stratosphere, and ozone absorbs incoming ultraviolet radiation [1]. The absorbed energy heats the surrounding air, and more UV is absorbed higher up where it has not yet been filtered out, so temperature rises with height [1].

## Common mistakes

- Saying the troposphere is heated directly by sunlight. It is heated mainly from below, by the ground.
- Treating water vapour as a fixed percentage of air.
- Claiming nitrogen and oxygen are greenhouse gases because they are abundant.
- Mixing up the order of layers or their temperature trends.
- Calling albedo the "amount of heat" reflected. It is the fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected.
- Forgetting that oxygen built up only after photosynthesis and only once oxygen sinks were filled.
