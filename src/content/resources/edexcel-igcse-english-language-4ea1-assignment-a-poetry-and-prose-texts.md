Assignment A is the reading half of the coursework route in Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Language A (4EA1). Instead of a 45-minute exam essay on one text, you write a considered essay of about
1200 words on **three** Anthology Part 2 texts. This guide covers the decisions that shape a strong
assignment: choosing the trio and focus, building a line of argument across three texts, and using the
drafting stage within the rules. The
[Component 3 overview](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-language-component-3/) explains how the
component fits the qualification.

## What the specification requires

- **Three texts** from Part 2 of the Anthology, with **at least one poetry text and one prose text**.
- A **focus** for the assignment; the specification suggests setting, character(s), event(s) or
  theme(s).
- A task that allows access to **AO1 (12 marks)** and **AO2 (18 marks)**: 30 marks in total.
- An advisory length of **about 1200 words**; there is no penalty for exceeding it.
- The suggested task format: *Discuss how the writers use language and structure to present [focus] in
  [text 1], [text 2] and [text 3].* The response should discuss the writers' ideas and perspectives
  about the focus, how they use language and structure to achieve their effects, and include textual
  references.

The task may be devised by your teacher or self-generated, and students in a class may write on the
same texts.

## Choosing a trio and a focus

The best focus is **narrow enough to argue** and **broad enough to fit all three texts**. Test it with
three questions:

1. Does each text have at least two or three moments where the focus is central?
2. Do the writers present it **differently**, so there is something to discuss beyond "all three show
   it"?
3. Do the texts use **different methods** (form, viewpoint, structure), giving AO2 range?

| Weak focus | Why | Stronger version |
|---|---|---|
| "Feelings" | Too vague to argue | "Moments of private realisation" |
| "War" | Fits few texts | "Violence and its aftermath" |
| "Women" | Topic, not idea | "The pressure of other people's expectations" |

## How the marks work

The specification marks the assignment on a **best fit** basis. Key wording from the assessment grids:

| Level | AO1 (12) | AO2 (18) |
|---|---|---|
| Level 3 | "Clear and relevant selection"; "relevant interpretation" (7–9) | "Makes clear explanations of the effects of language and structure" (7–10) |
| Level 4 | "Detailed and persuasive selection"; "discriminating use of supporting quotations"; "perceptive interpretation" (10–12) | "Makes a detailed exploration of the effects" (11–14) |
| Level 5 | — (AO1 has four levels) | "Subtle and discriminating selection"; "perceptive analysis" (15–18) |

Two practical consequences:

- **AO2 is 60% of the marks**, so most of each paragraph should analyse methods.
- The specification counts a **textual reference** as either a direct quotation or a reference to a
  specific moment or event. For prose, a precise reference to a moment can be as useful as a quotation.

## Building a line of argument

A strong Assignment A is one essay, not three. Two structures work:

**Integrated (by idea):** each paragraph takes one aspect of the focus and discusses two or three texts
within it. This makes connections visible and suits trios with clear similarities and contrasts.

**Sequenced with links:** a section on each text, but each section opens by linking back to the
previous one and closes by pointing forward. This suits trios where the methods are very different.

Either way, open with a **thesis** that names the focus and a claim about how the writers present it,
and close with a judgement about what the three texts, taken together, suggest.

## Worked example

**Trio:** "The Bright Lights of Sarajevo" (poem), "Significant Cigarettes" (prose), "An Unknown Girl"
(poem). **Focus:** encounters with strangers.

**Task:** *Discuss how the writers use language and structure to present encounters with strangers in
"The Bright Lights of Sarajevo", "Significant Cigarettes" and "An Unknown Girl".*

**Plan:**

```
Thesis:  In all three texts a brief meeting with a stranger becomes a moment of
         connection that the setting threatens; the writers differ in whether
         that connection lasts.
Para 1:  Setting as threat - the besieged city at night; the long coach journey
         far from home; the crowded evening bazaar
Para 2:  How the meeting is shaped - darkness and light (Harrison); hesitant,
         awkward conversation in close third person (Tremain); present-tense
         physical closeness as the henna is applied (Alvi)
Para 3:  Identity - each stranger helps define the speaker or character
Para 4:  Endings - hope (Harrison); uncertainty (Tremain); something that will
         fade (Alvi)
Conclusion: encounters with strangers as fragile but meaningful
```

**Model paragraph (paragraph 4):**

> The writers end their encounters very differently, and these endings shape how the reader values the
> meeting. Harrison closes on the possibility of love surviving in the dark, so the stranger becomes a
> source of hope in a city defined by danger. Tremain leaves Lev's acquaintance unresolved; the story
> stops before the reader knows what the meeting will mean, which mirrors the uncertainty of arriving in
> a new country. Alvi's speaker knows the henna pattern will fade, so the encounter with the unknown girl
> is presented as beautiful because it is temporary: the short lines and present tense hold the moment
> still, as if the speaker is trying to keep what she already knows she will lose.

What earns credit: a comparative point on structure (endings) across all three texts (AO2), an
interpretation of what the encounter means in each (AO1), and analysis of form in the Alvi example
(line length, tense).

## Drafting within the rules

- Your teacher **may suggest general improvements at the draft stage**, but **you** must make the
  corrections and update the work.
- Work **must not be rewritten after it has been marked**.
- You must **work independently**, acknowledge any sources, and sign the authentication sheet; teachers
  are told to watch for essays derived from the internet or AI.

Use the draft stage for three jobs: check that every paragraph answers the focus, replace any retelling
with analysis, and make sure each text has at least one detailed, close-reading moment.

## Common mistakes

- **Three poems, or only two texts.** At least one poem and one prose text are required.
- **A focus that fits one text well and two others badly.**
- **Three mini-essays** with no connections.
- **Retelling** stories in place of analysis.
- **Quotation dumping**: long quotations with little comment.
- **Ignoring structure**: openings, turning points and endings are often the richest AO2 material.
- **Context-heavy introductions**: AO1 and AO2 are assessed, not historical background.

## Command words in the task

| Wording | What to do |
|---|---|
| Discuss how the writers use language and structure | Analyse methods in all three texts |
| Present [focus] | Keep every paragraph on the chosen focus |
| Ideas and perspectives | Show what each writer seems to think or suggest (AO1) |
| Include textual references | Short quotations or precise references to moments |
