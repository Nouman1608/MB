## Resonance and delocalization (2.2.11)

Sometimes one Lewis formula cannot describe a species, because a double bond could sit in more than one place. Each acceptable drawing is a **resonance structure**. You show them side by side with a double-headed arrow (↔) between them.

The real species does not flip between the drawings. It is one **resonance hybrid**, with π electrons spread over three or more atoms instead of held between two. This spreading is **delocalization**. It has three consequences:

- The bonds that take part are all the same length.
- That length lies between a single and a double bond of the same atoms.
- The species is more stable (lower in energy) than any single resonance structure suggests.

### Worked example 1: ozone, O₃

1. Count valence electrons: 3 × 6 = 18, so nine pairs.
2. Draw one O=O and one O–O, then complete octets: one lone pair on the central O, two on the double-bonded O, three on the single-bonded O.
3. The double bond could be on either side, so there are **two resonance structures**: O=O–O ↔ O–O=O. The two real bonds are identical.

This links to Structure 1.3. Each bond in O₃ is only partly double, so it is weaker than the O=O bond in O₂. Ozone is therefore split by lower-energy, longer-wavelength ultraviolet light than oxygen.

### Worked example 2: the methanoate ion, HCOO⁻

1. Valence electrons: 1 + 4 + 6 + 6 + 1 (charge) = 18.
2. Carbon is central, bonded to H and both O atoms. One C–O is double and one is single; the single-bonded O carries three lone pairs and the negative charge.
3. Swap which O holds the double bond and you get the second resonance structure.
4. Three shared pairs are spread over two C–O positions, so each C–O bond is worth 3/2 = **1.5 bonds**. Both bonds have the same length, shorter than the C–O in methanol and longer than C=O in methanal.

Resonance structures move electrons only, never atoms.

## Benzene (2.2.12)

Benzene, C₆H₆, is a ring of six carbon atoms, each bonded to one hydrogen. Kekulé drew it with alternating single and double bonds. Those two resonance structures combine into a hybrid, drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside.

In the delocalized model each carbon uses three of its four valence electrons in σ bonds (two to ring carbons, one to H). Each carbon's fourth electron occupies a p orbital perpendicular to the plane of the ring. These p orbitals overlap side by side around the ring, giving a ring of delocalized π electron density above and below the plane.

### The evidence

**Physical evidence**

- **Bond lengths.** X-ray diffraction shows all six C–C bonds are the same length, between a single and a double bond. Alternating bonds would give two different lengths.
- **Enthalpy of hydrogenation.** Adding hydrogen to benzene releases much less energy than expected for three separate C=C bonds (see the example below).

**Chemical evidence**

- **Only one 1,2-disubstituted isomer.** Fixed double bonds would give two forms of 1,2-dichlorobenzene (Cl atoms across a single or a double bond). Only one exists.
- **Reactions.** Benzene does not readily decolourize bromine water, unlike alkenes. It tends to react by **substitution**, which keeps the delocalized ring intact, rather than addition.

### Worked example 3: resonance energy from hydrogenation data

Use these values. Cyclohexene + H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −116 kJ mol⁻¹. Benzene + 3H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −204 kJ mol⁻¹.

1. A ring with three isolated C=C bonds should give 3 × (−116) = **−348 kJ mol⁻¹**.
2. Benzene actually gives −204 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. Difference = −204 − (−348) = **+144 kJ mol⁻¹**.
4. Both routes end at cyclohexane, so benzene is 144 kJ mol⁻¹ lower in energy than the Kekulé structure. This **resonance energy** explains its relative unreactivity and preference for substitution.

## Expanded octets: five and six electron domains (2.2.13)

Atoms from period 3 downwards are larger and can hold more than eight valence electrons, so they can carry five or six electron domains. Period 2 atoms such as C, N and O cannot.

VSEPR works as before: each bond (single or multiple) and each lone pair is one domain. Choose the electron domain geometry, then name the shape from the atoms only.

| Domains | Electron domain geometry | Bonding + lone | Molecular geometry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 5 + 0 | trigonal bipyramidal (90°, 120°) |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 4 + 1 | seesaw |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 3 + 2 | T-shaped |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 2 + 3 | linear (180°) |
| 6 | octahedral | 6 + 0 | octahedral (90°) |
| 6 | octahedral | 5 + 1 | square pyramidal |
| 6 | octahedral | 4 + 2 | square planar (90°) |

In a trigonal bipyramid, lone pairs go in the **equatorial** positions, where they have more room. In an octahedron, two lone pairs sit **opposite** each other.

### Worked example 4: SeF₄

1. Se is in group 16, so it has 6 valence electrons. Four go into Se–F bonds, leaving 2: one lone pair.
2. Domains: 4 bonding + 1 lone pair = **5**. Electron domain geometry: **trigonal bipyramidal**.
3. The lone pair takes an equatorial position, so the four F atoms make a **seesaw** shape. Its stronger repulsion closes the bond angles to below 120° and 90°.

### Worked example 5: ICl₄⁻

1. I has 7 valence electrons, plus 1 for the charge: 8. Four are used in I–Cl bonds, leaving 4: two lone pairs.
2. Domains: 4 + 2 = **6**. Electron domain geometry: **octahedral**.
3. The lone pairs sit opposite each other, so the four Cl atoms form a **square planar** ion with Cl–I–Cl angles of **90°**. Put square brackets and the charge round the Lewis formula.

## Formal charge (2.2.14)

Formal charge (FC) treats every bond as perfectly shared and compares the electrons each atom "owns" with the free atom:

**FC = V − (L + ½B)**

where V is the number of valence electrons in the free atom, L is the number of electrons in lone pairs on that atom, and B is the number of electrons in bonds to that atom. The formal charges in a species always add up to its overall charge.

To choose between possible Lewis formulas, the preferred one has:

1. formal charges as close to zero as possible on every atom;
2. any negative formal charge on the most electronegative atom.

### Worked example 6: the cyanate ion, OCN⁻

Valence electrons: 6 + 4 + 5 + 1 = 16. Carbon is central. Three Lewis formulas give every atom an octet:

| Structure | FC on O | FC on C | FC on N |
|---|---|---|---|
| [O=C=N]⁻ | 0 | 0 | −1 |
| [O≡C–N]⁻ | +1 | 0 | −2 |
| [O–C≡N]⁻ | −1 | 0 | 0 |

The second structure has the largest charges, so reject it. The first and third both have only one −1 charge. Oxygen is more electronegative than nitrogen, so **[O–C≡N]⁻** is preferred.

### Sulfate: why formal charge favours double bonds

The guide links formal charge to the structure of sulfate, SO₄²⁻. With four S–O single bonds, S has FC = 6 − (0 + 4) = +2 and each O has −1. With two S=O and two S–O bonds (an expanded octet), S has FC = 6 − (0 + 6) = **0**, the two double-bonded O atoms have 0, and the two single-bonded O atoms have −1. The total is still −2. Formal charge prefers this second structure.

### Formal charge versus oxidation state

Formal charge assumes bonding electrons are shared **equally**. Oxidation state assumes they all go to the **more electronegative** atom. So S in the preferred sulfate structure has a formal charge of 0 but an oxidation state of +6.

## Sigma and pi bonds (2.2.15)

- A **sigma (σ) bond** forms by **head-on** overlap of two orbitals (s, p or hybrid). Its electron density lies mainly on the bond axis.
- A **pi (π) bond** forms by **sideways** (lateral) overlap of two parallel p orbitals. Electron density lies on opposite sides of the bond axis.

Counting rule:

| Bond | σ | π |
|---|---|---|
| single | 1 | 0 |
| double | 1 | 1 |
| triple | 1 | 2 |

### Worked example 7: counting bonds

**Propenenitrile, CH₂=CH–C≡N (organic).** Bonds: three C–H, one C=C, one C–C and one C≡N.
σ bonds = 3 + 1 + 1 + 1 = **6**. π bonds = 1 (in C=C) + 2 (in C≡N) = **3**.

**Carbonyl fluoride, COF₂ (inorganic).** One C=O and two C–F bonds: **3** σ and **1** π.

## Hybridization (2.2.16)

**Hybridization** is the mixing of atomic orbitals on one atom to make new, equivalent hybrid orbitals for bonding. A carbon atom has one 2s and three 2p orbitals in its valence shell:

- **sp³**: s + three p mix to give four sp³ orbitals, pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron (109.5°). No p orbitals left over.
- **sp²**: s + two p give three sp² orbitals in a plane at 120°. One unhybridized p orbital is left at right angles to the plane.
- **sp**: s + one p give two sp orbitals at 180°. Two unhybridized p orbitals are left.

Hybrid orbitals form σ bonds or hold lone pairs; unhybridized p orbitals form π bonds. Only sp, sp² and sp³ are needed.

### The link you must know

| Electron domains on the atom | Hybridization | Electron domain geometry |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | sp³ | tetrahedral |
| 3 | sp² | trigonal planar |
| 2 | sp | linear |

The chain runs Lewis formula → domains → hybridization → geometry, and backwards too: an sp² atom has three domains.

### Worked example 8: but-1-en-3-yne, CH₂=CH–C≡CH

1. C1 (CH₂=): three domains (two C–H, one C=C) → **sp²**, 120°.
2. C2 (=CH–): three domains → **sp²**, 120°.
3. C3 (–C≡): two domains → **sp**, 180°.
4. C4 (≡CH): two domains → **sp**, 180°.
5. σ bonds: four C–H + C=C + C–C + C≡C = **7**. π bonds: 1 + 2 = **3**, all formed from unhybridized p orbitals.

**Inorganic examples.** O in H₃O⁺ (three bonds, one lone pair) is **sp³** and trigonal pyramidal. B in BCl₃ is **sp²**, trigonal planar. C in CO₂ is **sp**, linear.

## Common errors

- Counting a double bond as two electron domains in VSEPR or hybridization. It is one domain.
- Placing lone pairs in axial positions of a trigonal bipyramid.
- Getting B wrong in the formal charge formula: use half the bonding electrons, not all of them.
- Writing that a triple bond is three σ bonds.
- Assigning hybridization from the number of atoms bonded instead of the number of electron domains.
