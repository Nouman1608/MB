These questions cover section 3.6 Finance of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225) specification, version 3.1, for exams from May/June 2022 onwards: sources of finance, cash flow, financial terms and calculations, and analysing financial performance. You will not be given formulae in an exam, so write each one out in your working. ## Questions

**1.** State two internal sources of finance. **[2]**

**2.** Sunbird Pottery is a new business. The owner needs £6,000 for a second kiln. She has no savings left and no profit history. Her options are crowdfunding or a bank overdraft. Analyse one advantage of crowdfunding and one disadvantage of an overdraft for this business. **[4]**

**3.** Bellfounder Furniture has traded for 20 years and wants £500,000 for a new factory. It can take out a bank loan or make a new share issue. Evaluate which source is more suitable. **[6]**

**4.** Peregrine Print Studio is completing part of its cash flow forecast (in £).

| | April | May | June |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening balance | 3,000 | (a) | (c) |
| Cash inflows | 14,000 | 11,000 | 15,000 |
| Cash outflows | 12,500 | 14,500 | 13,000 |
| Net cash flow | 1,500 | (b) | 2,000 |
| Closing balance | 4,500 | 1,000 | (d) |

Calculate the values (a), (b), (c) and (d). **[4]**

**5.** Explain the difference between cash and profit. Use this example: Peregrine Print Studio sells £5,000 of posters to a shop on 60 days' credit and pays £3,000 in cash for paper and ink. **[4]**

**6.** Sunbird Pottery's forecast shows a negative closing balance in two successive months. Customers (gift shops) pay 60 days after delivery, while clay suppliers and the kiln lender are paid each month. Evaluate whether re-scheduling payments or increasing cash inflow is the better way to solve the problem. **[8]**

**7.** Foxhollow Prints sells framed prints at £8 each. Fixed costs are £15,000 and variable costs are £3 per print. Output and sales are 4,000 prints. Calculate revenue, total costs and profit. **[4]**

**8.** Thornwick Tea Co is considering a new blend. The table shows revenue and total costs at different output levels.

| Output (packs) | 0 | 1,000 | 2,000 | 2,500 | 3,000 | 4,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total revenue (£) | 0 | 20,000 | 40,000 | 50,000 | 60,000 | 80,000 |
| Total costs (£) | 30,000 | 38,000 | 46,000 | 50,000 | 54,000 | 62,000 |

**(a)** Identify the break-even output. **[1]**
**(b)** The firm plans to sell 3,200 packs. State the margin of safety. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate the value of break-even analysis to Thornwick Tea Co. **[6]**

**9.** Bellfounder Furniture buys a delivery vehicle for £54,000. Its net cash inflows are £12,000 in year 1, £18,000 in year 2, £18,000 in year 3 and £20,000 in year 4.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain one limitation of using payback to judge the investment. **[2]**

**10.** Ambleside Outdoor Gear's income statement for the current year shows revenue £400,000, cost of sales £260,000 and expenses £100,000. Last year its gross profit margin was 35% and its net profit margin was 12%. A competitor reports a gross profit margin of 38% and a net profit margin of 11%.

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit margin and net profit margin for the current year. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate Ambleside's performance from the point of view of its shareholders. **[6]**

**11.** The statement of financial position of a small firm shows: delivery vans, inventories, cash, trade payables and a bank loan. Non-current assets total £90,000, current assets £35,000, current liabilities £25,000 and non-current liabilities £50,000.

**(a)** Classify each of the five items as an asset or a liability. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate net assets. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the statement of financial position is described as a snapshot in time. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Retained profit [1]; selling unwanted assets [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A "state" question wants two distinct sources; a family loan or overdraft is external and would not fit.

**2.** Advantage of crowdfunding: many people each give a small sum, so she does not need a lender to approve her with no profit history [1], which makes the £6,000 reachable for a new business [1]. Disadvantage of an overdraft: interest is high [1] and the bank can withdraw it, which is risky for a business with no profit to repay it from [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis needs the point developed with because or so, tied to the new business, rather than a bare statement.

**3.** Loan: fixed repayments and no change in ownership [1]; a 20-year record makes lending easier [1]; but interest on £500,000 is a heavy cost and security may be needed [1]. Share issue: no repayment and large sums can be raised [1]; but ownership is shared and dividends are expected [1]. Judgement: for a long-established firm with steady profit, a loan (possibly with a smaller share issue) is more suitable if it can afford repayments, because owners keep control; a share issue is better if profit is uncertain [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation that lists both sources but never decides, or decides without a reason, leaves the top level out of reach.

**4. (a)** Opening May = closing April = 4,500 [1]. **(b)** 11,000 − 14,500 = −3,500 [1]. **(c)** Opening June = closing May = 1,000 [1]. **(d)** 1,000 + 2,000 = **3,000** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each missing value uses the forecast rule that closing equals opening plus net cash flow; show the sum so a slip still shows method.

**5.** Profit is revenue minus costs, counted when the sale is made [1]: here £5,000 − £3,000 = £2,000 profit [1]. Cash is counted only when money moves [1]: the £3,000 has gone out but the £5,000 will not arrive for 60 days, so cash is down £3,000 for now despite the profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the numbers in the stem; a general definition alone does not apply the idea to the business.

**6.** Re-scheduling payments: asking the clay supplier and lender to accept later payment costs nothing and acts quickly [1], but suppliers may refuse, add charges or stop supplying, and a lender may see it as risk [1]. Increasing cash inflow: asking shops to pay in 30 days or offering a small discount for prompt payment attacks the cause, since the 60-day delay is the problem [1]; it keeps working in later months [1]; but a discount cuts profit and shops may not agree [1]; stricter terms may lose customers [1]. Judgement: increasing cash inflow is better because it deals with the root cause, while re-scheduling is a short-term fix that can damage supplier relationships; re-scheduling could still bridge the gap while new terms start [2]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the two options against each other and against the cause given in the case; two separate evaluations with no comparison do not reach a judgement.

**7.** Revenue = 8 × 4,000 = £32,000 [1]. Variable costs = 3 × 4,000 = £12,000 [1]. Total costs = 15,000 + 12,000 = £27,000 [1]. Profit = 32,000 − 27,000 = **£5,000** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula and each stage; total costs include both fixed and variable.

**8. (a)** **2,500 packs**, where revenue and total costs are both £50,000 [1].
**(b)** Margin of safety = planned output − break-even output [1] = 3,200 − 2,500 = **700 packs** [1].
**(c)** For: it shows the output needed to avoid a loss (2,500 packs) [1]; it shows the risk, as a margin of safety of 700 is modest [1]; it can support a loan application or pricing choice [1]. Against: it assumes every pack made is sold [1] and that price and costs stay fixed, whereas a price cut or bulk discounts would move the lines [1]. Judgement: useful as a first check on a new blend, but the firm should test demand and costs because the result is only as good as its forecasts [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) asks for output, so state the unit; part (c) needs a balanced judgement tied to a new product.

**9. (a)** Cumulative inflows: £12,000, £30,000, £48,000 [1]. Still to repay after three years: 54,000 − 48,000 = £6,000 [1]. Fraction of year 4 = 6,000 ÷ 20,000 = 0.3 years, about 4 months [1]. Payback = **3 years 4 months** (approximately) [1].
**(b)** It ignores cash earned after the payback point [1], so a vehicle that earns a lot later could be rejected unfairly [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the answer in years and months, since a part-year remains; a limitation needs a consequence, not just a label.

**10. (a)** Gross profit = 400,000 − 260,000 = £140,000 [1]; gross profit margin = 140,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = **35%** [1]. Net profit = 140,000 − 100,000 = £40,000 [1]; net profit margin = 40,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = **10%** [1].
**(b)** Gross margin is unchanged at 35% on last year, so cost of sales is under control [1], but it is below the competitor's 38% [1]. Net margin has fallen from 12% to 10% [1], so expenses are taking more of each sale, and it is below the competitor's 11% on net margin too [1]. Shareholders want profit and returns: this falling margin is a concern even if revenue has grown [1]. Judgement: performance is weaker than last year and than the competitor, so shareholders may be disappointed unless expenses can be reduced [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare with last year and the competitor, and write from the shareholders' view; quoting figures without comment shows only data handling.

**11. (a)** Assets: delivery vans, inventories, cash [1]. Liabilities: trade payables, bank loan [1]. Assets are items owned or owed to the firm; liabilities are what it owes [1].
**(b)** Total assets = 90,000 + 35,000 = £125,000 and total liabilities = 25,000 + 50,000 = £75,000 [1]. Net assets = **£50,000** [1].
**(c)** It shows assets and liabilities at one date [1], which can change soon after as cash moves and inventories are sold [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Snapshot" needs the point that it is one date, not a period; do not confuse it with the income statement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing sources of finance without linking them to the size, timescale or age of the business in the stem.
- Offering advantages only when the question asks for an evaluation or judgement.
- Mixing up cash and profit in the same answer.
- Not carrying a closing balance forward as the next opening balance.
- Giving margin of safety in pounds when the chart reading is in output.
- Giving a payback period in years only, ignoring the part-year.
- Calculating margins on cost of sales rather than on revenue.
- Quoting figures from the case without saying what they mean for the stakeholder named.
- Showing a final answer with no formula or working.
