## Forces that cause rotation

A force, or a system of forces, may cause an object to rotate about a **pivot** (a fixed point).

| Example | Pivot | Force that causes the turning |
|---|---|---|
| Opening a door | Hinges | Your push on the handle |
| Tightening a nut with a spanner | Centre of the nut | Your pull on the handle |
| A seesaw | Central support | The weights of the people sitting on it |
| A wheelbarrow | Wheel axle | Your lift on the handles |
| Turning a steering wheel | Centre of the wheel | Your hands on the rim |

## The moment of a force

The turning effect of a force is called its **moment**.

```
moment of a force = force × distance
M = F d      M in newton-metres (N m),  F in newtons (N),
             d = perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force, in metres (m)
```

You must recall this equation. The key word is **perpendicular**: measure the shortest distance from the
pivot to the line along which the force acts.

**Worked example 1.** A mechanic pulls on a spanner 0.30 m from the nut with a force of 45 N at right angles
to the spanner. She then pulls with the same force at an angle, so that the perpendicular distance from
the nut to the line of the force is only 0.18 m.

```
at right angles:  M = 45 × 0.30 = 13.5 N m
at an angle:      M = 45 × 0.18 = 8.1 N m
```

Pulling at right angles to the spanner gives the largest moment, because it gives the largest perpendicular
distance. This is why door handles are fitted far from the hinges and why you push at right angles to the
door.

To get the same moment with a shorter spanner, you need a larger force: for 13.5 N m with a 0.15 m spanner,
F = 13.5 / 0.15 = 90 N.

## The principle of moments

If an object is **balanced** (not rotating), then about any pivot:

```
total clockwise moment = total anticlockwise moment
```

### Method in steps

1. Identify the pivot.
2. For each force, decide whether it turns the object clockwise or anticlockwise.
3. Multiply each force by its perpendicular distance from the pivot.
4. Add the clockwise moments; add the anticlockwise moments.
5. Set the totals equal and solve for the unknown.

**Worked example 2.** Two children sit on the left of a seesaw: one of weight 300 N at 1.5 m from the
pivot, one of weight 200 N at 0.50 m. Where must an adult of weight 500 N sit on the right to balance it?

```
anticlockwise:  300 × 1.5 + 200 × 0.50 = 450 + 100 = 550 N m
clockwise:      500 × d
balanced:       500 d = 550   →   d = 1.1 m to the right of the pivot
```

### Beams with their own weight

The weight of a **uniform** beam acts at its centre of mass, which is at its midpoint. If the pivot is not
at the midpoint, the beam's own weight has a moment too.

**Worked example 3.** A uniform plank 4.0 m long weighs 200 N. It rests on a pivot 1.0 m from its left end.
What load placed at the left end will balance it?

```
plank's centre of mass is at 2.0 m from the left end, so 1.0 m to the right of the pivot
clockwise (plank's weight):   200 × 1.0 = 200 N m
anticlockwise (load):          W × 1.0
balanced:                      W = 200 N
```

When the plank balances, the upward force from the pivot equals the total weight: 200 + 200 = 400 N. Balanced
objects have balanced moments **and** balanced forces.

## Levers

A **simple lever** transmits the rotational effect of a force. A small force applied far from the pivot
produces a large moment, and that moment can move a large load close to the pivot. The lever acts as a
**force multiplier**.

**Worked example 4: a crowbar.** A worker pushes down with 150 N at 0.90 m from the pivot. The load (a nail
or a heavy lid) is 0.10 m from the pivot on the other side.

```
moment of effort = 150 × 0.90 = 135 N m
force on load    = 135 / 0.10 = 1350 N
```

**Worked example 5: a wheelbarrow.** The load of 600 N acts 0.40 m from the wheel axle (the pivot). The
handles are 1.4 m from the axle. What upward force is needed to hold the barrow level?

```
600 × 0.40 = F × 1.4
F = 240 / 1.4 = 171 N ≈ 170 N
```

The load and effort are on the same side of the pivot here, but they turn in opposite directions, so the
principle of moments still applies.

The longer the distance between the effort and the pivot, compared with the distance between the load and
the pivot, the bigger the force on the load for the same effort.

## Gears

A **gear** is a wheel with teeth around its edge. When two gears mesh, the teeth push on each other, so a
turning effect on one gear (the **driver**) is transmitted to the other (the **driven** gear).

How gears change the moment:

- Where the teeth meet, the force on each gear is the same size.
- Each gear's moment is that force × the gear's radius.
- So a **larger** driven gear receives a **larger moment** than the driver, but it **turns more slowly**.
- A **smaller** driven gear receives a **smaller moment** but **turns faster**.
- Two meshing gears turn in **opposite directions**.

**Worked example 6.** A driver gear of radius 2.0 cm turns with a moment of 0.60 N m. It drives a gear of
radius 6.0 cm. Find the moment on the driven gear, ignoring friction.

```
force at the teeth = M / r = 0.60 / 0.020 = 30 N
moment on driven gear = 30 × 0.060 = 1.8 N m   (three times larger)
```

The driven gear has three times the radius, so it has three times the moment, but it turns at one third of
the speed. If the gears have 12 and 36 teeth, the same ratio applies: the 36-tooth gear turns once for every
three turns of the 12-tooth gear.

A bicycle in a low gear uses a small front gear driving a larger rear gear, which gives a larger moment at
the back wheel for climbing a hill.

## Common mistakes

- Using the distance along the spanner when the force is not at right angles; use the **perpendicular**
  distance.
- Leaving distances in cm, which gives N cm, not N m.
- Forgetting a person or the beam's own weight when adding moments.
- Saying a larger driven gear turns faster.
- Calling moment "the force"; a moment is force × distance and is measured in N m.
- Forgetting that a balanced object also has balanced forces, so the pivot pushes up with the total weight.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an example of rotation: object, pivot, force, direction of turning.
- **Calculate**: write "clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment", then substitute each term with its
  distance from the pivot.
- **Explain** how a lever or gear works: use the chain force → distance → moment → force on the load (or on
  the driven gear), and say what changes and what stays the same.
