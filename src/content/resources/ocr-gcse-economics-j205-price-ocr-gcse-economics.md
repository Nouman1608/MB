This guide covers section 2.4, Price, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019, in component J205/01. The specification is not tiered. All products and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain price as a reflection of worth and its role in determining an efficient distribution of resources;
- explain what is meant by equilibrium price and quantity;
- draw and analyse the interaction of demand and supply;
- explain the role of markets in the determination of price and the allocation of resources;
- analyse how the market forces of demand and supply affect equilibrium price and quantity.

## Price as a reflection of worth

In a market, the price of a good reflects how much buyers are willing to pay for it and what it costs sellers to provide. A high price usually means buyers value the good highly relative to how much is available. A low price means the good is plentiful, or buyers value it less.

This is why price differs from usefulness. Water is essential, yet tap water is cheap because it is plentiful; a rare painting is not essential, yet it can be very expensive because it is unique and some buyers value it highly. Price measures worth **at the margin**: what people will pay for one more unit, given how much already exists.

### Price and an efficient distribution of resources

Because prices reflect what buyers will pay, resources tend to move to the uses that consumers value most. If buyers will pay more for a product, producers earn more by making it, so land, labour and capital move towards it. Economists describe this as an **efficient distribution of resources**: resources are used to produce the goods people most want, at the lowest cost, without waste through large unsold surpluses or long-lasting shortages.

## Equilibrium

**Equilibrium price** is the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. **Equilibrium quantity** is the amount bought and sold at that price. The market "clears": there is no shortage and no surplus.

**Worked example: honey jars in a county (invented).** Weekly figures:

| Price (£) | Demand | Supply | Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 900 | 300 | Shortage of 600 |
| 4 | 800 | 500 | Shortage of 300 |
| 5 | 700 | 700 | **Equilibrium** |
| 6 | 600 | 900 | Surplus of 300 |
| 7 | 500 | 1,100 | Surplus of 600 |

- **Below** equilibrium, at £4: buyers want 800 but only 500 are offered. Buyers compete for jars and sellers find they can charge more, so the price rises. As it rises, demand contracts and supply extends until they meet at £5.
- **Above** equilibrium, at £6: 900 are offered but only 600 bought. Unsold stock builds up, so sellers cut prices. Demand extends and supply contracts until the market clears at £5.
- At £5, spending (and sellers' revenue) is 5 × 700 = £3,500 a week.

## The price mechanism: three jobs that price does

| Function | What happens | Honey example |
|---|---|---|
| **Signalling** | A price change sends information to buyers and sellers | A rising price tells beekeepers that buyers want more honey |
| **Incentive** | A higher price makes it more profitable to supply | Beekeepers add hives; new producers enter |
| **Rationing** | Price shares out a limited quantity among buyers | When honey is scarce, a higher price means it goes to those who value it most and can pay |

These three functions are how markets determine price and **allocate resources** without anyone being in charge. The limitation, worth stating in an evaluation, is that rationing by price favours people with higher incomes, and markets can fail to provide some goods at all (that is studied in section 3.8).

## How market forces change equilibrium

Each change is analysed in the same order: **which curve shifts, which way, what happens at the old price, the new equilibrium.**

| Change | At the old price | New price | New quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand increases (right) | Shortage | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases (left) | Surplus | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases (right) | Surplus | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases (left) | Shortage | Rises | Falls |

**Worked example 1: demand increases.** A health trend raises demand for honey by 300 jars at every price. At £5, demand is now 1,000 against supply of 700: a shortage of 300. The price rises until demand and supply meet at **£6 and 900 jars**.

```
Price change    = (6 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = +20%
Quantity change = (900 − 700) ÷ 700 × 100 = +28.6%
Revenue         = 6 × 900 = £5,400, up from £3,500
```

**Worked example 2: supply increases.** Instead, suppose demand is unchanged and new beekeepers raise supply by 300 at every price. At £5, supply is 1,000 against demand of 700: a surplus of 300. The price falls to **£4**, where demand and supply both equal **800**. Price falls by 20%; quantity rises by 14.3%.

**Worked example 3: both shift.** Now both changes happen together: demand and supply each rise by 300. At £5, demand is 1,000 and supply is 1,000. The price stays at **£5**, and quantity rises to **1,000** (+42.9%).

The lesson of example 3: when both curves shift, the direction of the change in **quantity** may be clear while the change in **price** depends on which shift is larger. In an exam, say so, and use the data to decide.

## Price and the allocation of resources: a longer chain

**Worked chain: plant-based milk (invented).** Tastes change and consumers buy more oat milk and less dairy milk.

1. Demand for oat milk shifts right; its price and quantity rise.
2. Higher prices signal profit; producers expand and new brands enter (incentive).
3. Farmers grow more oats; factories hire more workers to process them.
4. Demand for dairy milk shifts left; its price falls and some dairy farms make losses.
5. Some dairy farmers switch land and capital to other uses.

Resources have moved from a product consumers value less to one they value more, with no central plan. That is the role of markets in allocating resources. The cost falls on the dairy farmers and their workers during the change.

## Drawing and analysing the diagram

Checklist for full marks on a demand and supply diagram:

- Axes labelled **Price** and **Quantity** (with units if given).
- Original curves labelled **D1** and **S1**; equilibrium at **P1** and **Q1**, with dotted lines to both axes.
- The shifted curve labelled **D2** or **S2**, with an arrow showing the direction.
- New equilibrium **P2** and **Q2** marked.
- In the written analysis, refer to the diagram: "the shortage at P1 causes price to rise to P2".

## Common mistakes

- Writing "demand rises" when price rises: a higher price causes a contraction in demand, not a shift.
- Saying the new equilibrium has "more demand and more supply" without distinguishing shifts from movements.
- Stating a new price after a double shift without checking the data.
- Describing the price mechanism with no example.
- Forgetting that rationing by price leaves out those who cannot pay.
- Unlabelled or unmarked diagrams.

## Exam technique

- **Explain equilibrium:** define it, then explain what happens above and below it.
- **Analyse a change:** use the four-step order and quote figures from the table.
- **Explain allocation:** use signalling, incentive and rationing in a chain about a real-looking product.
- **Draw:** follow the checklist above; a diagram is analysis only if your writing uses it.
