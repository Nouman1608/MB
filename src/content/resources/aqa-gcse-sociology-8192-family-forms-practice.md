These questions cover 3.3.2 Family forms in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, assessed in Paper 1: The sociology of families and education. All towns, data and research in the items are fictional.

Revise first with the [family forms study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-family-forms/) and the [family forms revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-family-forms-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Which term describes grandparents, their adult children and their grandchildren living in one household?

A. Horizontally extended family
B. Vertically extended family
C. Reconstituted family
D. Modified extended family

**[1]**

**2.** What is polyandry?

A. One husband married to two or more wives
B. Marriage to one partner at a time
C. One wife married to two or more husbands
D. Marrying while still married to someone else

**[1]**

**3.** Describe what is meant by a modified extended family. **[2]**

**4.** Describe **one** way in which a reconstituted family differs from a nuclear family. **[3]**

**5.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Westhaven (a fictional town): families with dependent children, by family form, percentage of all such families**

| Family form | 2005 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclear (opposite-sex couple, children of both partners only) | 62 | 52 |
| Reconstituted | 10 | 13 |
| Lone parent | 24 | 26 |
| Single sex couple | 1 | 4 |
| Vertically extended | 3 | 5 |

- **(a)** Which family form fell by the most percentage points between 2005 and 2025? **[1]**
- **(b)** How many times larger was the single sex couple share in 2025 than in 2005? **[1]**
- **(c)** Explain how far the Westhaven data support the view that the nuclear family is no longer the typical family form. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** way in which class diversity, as described by the Rapoports, can affect family life. **[3]**

**7.** Identify and explain **one** reason for the increase in the number of single sex families in the UK. **[4]**

**8.** Describe **one** family form found in a global context that differs from the family forms most common in the UK. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**9.** Read Item A.

> **Item A (fictional).** A researcher wanted to find out how often adults in Britain see relatives who live in other households. She posted an online survey on a social media page for parents of primary school children. 600 people replied. Most said they saw a grandparent or sibling at least once a week, and the researcher concluded that the modified extended family is the main family form in Britain.

- **(a)** From Item A, examine **one** weakness of the research. **[2]**
- **(b)** Identify **one** other research method that could be used to study contact between relatives, and explain **one** disadvantage of using it. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why lone parent families have become more common in the UK since the 1970s. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that diversity is the most important feature of family life in the UK today. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that the nuclear family is the best family form for raising children. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. A horizontally extended family adds kin of the same generation. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. A is polygyny; D is bigamy. **[1]**

**3.** Relatives who live in separate households [1] but keep close contact and support each other, for example through regular visits, phone calls, shared childcare or lending money [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A reconstituted family includes children from a previous relationship of one or both partners [1], so it can include a step-parent and step-siblings or half-siblings, whereas a nuclear family contains only the couple and their own children [1]. Children may also move between two households and have contact with a parent who lives elsewhere [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** The nuclear family, which fell by 10 percentage points, from 62% to 52% [1].

**(b)** Four times larger: 4 ÷ 1 = 4 [1].

**(c)** The data partly support the view: the nuclear family fell from 62% to 52%, while reconstituted, lone parent, single sex and extended families all grew [1]. However, the nuclear family is still by far the largest single form in 2025, at 52%, double the next largest (lone parent, 26%), so it remains the typical form in Westhaven [1]. The data are also a snapshot of families with dependent children in one fictional town, so they do not show how many people live in a nuclear family at some stage of their lives, as Chester argued [1]. **[5]**

**6.** Class diversity refers to how a family's position in the class system affects the resources available to it [1]. A middle-class family may be able to pay for private childcare, a larger home or activities for children [1], whereas a working-class family on a low income may rely on unpaid help from grandparents and have less space, so the way childcare and family time are organised differs by class [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Identify: changes in the law [1]. Explain: the Civil Partnership Act 2004 gave same-sex couples legal recognition [1], and same-sex marriage became possible in England and Wales from 2014 [1]. This gave same-sex couples the same legal security as other couples, so more chose to form families openly and to raise children through adoption, fostering or donor conception [1]. *Also credit:* changing social attitudes and reduced stigma, making couples more willing to live openly. **[4]**

**8.** Identify: a polygynous family [1]. Describe: one husband is married to two or more wives at the same time [1]; this is legally permitted in a number of countries but is not legal in the UK, where marrying while already married is bigamy [1]. *Also credit:* polyandry; the kibbutz, where child rearing was shared by the community; extended households pooling labour on the land. **[3]**

**9. (a)** One weakness, examined. The sample came only from a social media page for parents of primary school children [1], so it leaves out people without children, older people and those not online, and the conclusion cannot be generalised to all adults in Britain [1]. *Also credit:* self-selecting volunteers may be the people with the closest family ties, which biases the results. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify: unstructured interviews [1]. Disadvantage: they take a long time to carry out and analyse [1], so only a small number of people can be interviewed [1], which makes the sample less representative and the findings harder to generalise to the wider population [1]. *Also credit:* observation of family visits, with the disadvantage that the researcher's presence may change behaviour; official statistics, which record households rather than contact between them. **[6]**

**10.** First, the rise in divorce and separation since the Divorce Reform Act 1969 [1] means more marriages end with children living mainly with one parent, usually the mother [1]. Second, changing social attitudes have reduced the stigma of having a child outside marriage [1], so more women choose to raise children alone, and women's greater economic independence makes this more possible [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- The Rapoports argued that diversity is the central feature of British family life [1].
- Organisational diversity: families divide paid work and housework in different ways [1].
- Cultural diversity: ethnic and religious groups have different values about marriage and kin [1].
- Class diversity: resources available to families differ with class position [1].
- Life course and cohort diversity: families differ by stage of life and historical period [1].
- Growth in reconstituted, lone parent and single sex families supports the view [1].
- Legal change, such as civil partnership and same-sex marriage, has widened accepted forms [1].
- Against: Chester argued most people still live in a nuclear family at some stage, now neo-conventional [1].
- Functionalists such as Parsons see the nuclear family as best suited to industrial society, so it remains the norm [1].
- Evaluation: the Rapoports' work predates the open acceptance of gay and lesbian households, so it understates today's diversity [1].
- Marxists would argue that, whatever its form, the family still performs the same role for capitalism, so diversity is less important than it seems [1].
- Judgement: diversity is a major feature, but the nuclear family remains the most common single form, so diversity is important rather than clearly the most important feature [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Each Rapoport type should come with a UK example; a list of five names scores little.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Parsons saw the nuclear family as best suited to primary socialisation and the stabilisation of adult personalities [1].
- He argued its small size and mobility fit industrial society [1].
- Two adults can share childcare and income, which may reduce the risk of poverty [1].
- Oakley's idea of the conventional family shows the powerful belief that married nuclear families are the norm [1].
- Against: feminists argue the nuclear family can be patriarchal, with women carrying most childcare [1].
- Delphy and Leonard see the family as an economic system in which men exploit women's work [1].
- Lone parent families can raise children successfully; problems often reflect low income rather than family form [1].
- Single sex families show that children can be raised well by two parents of the same sex [1].
- Extended families can offer more adults to share care, as in many societies around the world [1].
- The kibbutz shows communities can share child rearing [1].
- Evaluation: Parsons' view is criticised as idealised and middle-class, and ignores abuse within nuclear families [1].
- Judgement: what matters most for children may be stable relationships and resources, not the family form itself [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers separate family form from family circumstances such as income and conflict.

## Where marks are usually lost

- In 5(c), describing the changes without quoting figures, or never weighing the evidence both ways.
- Defining a lone parent family without mentioning dependent children.
- Using the term "polygamy" when the question asks about polygyny or polyandry.
- In 9(b), giving a disadvantage that does not relate to studying contact between relatives.
