These notes condense section 2.3, Supply, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. The worked strawberry market and the short-run and long-run PES example are in the [2.3 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-ocr-gcse-economics/); then try the [2.3 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply | The quantity producers are willing and able to sell at each price over a period |
| Individual supply | One producer's supply |
| Market supply | The total of all producers' supply at each price |
| Extension in supply | More supplied because the price rose (movement along) |
| Contraction in supply | Less supplied because the price fell (movement along) |
| Increase in supply | Curve shifts right: more supplied at every price |
| Decrease in supply | Curve shifts left: less supplied at every price |
| Condition of supply | Any factor other than price that affects supply |
| Price elasticity of supply (PES) | Responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price |
| Spare capacity | Ability to produce more with existing resources |

## The supply curve

- Price on the vertical axis, quantity supplied on the horizontal axis.
- Upward sloping: higher price → more profit per unit → firms produce more; new firms are attracted.
- Market supply: **add quantities** across producers at each price.

**Worked reminder.** At £3, two bakeries supply 120 and 80 loaves. Market supply at £3 = **200**.

## Movement or shift?

```
Own price changed?  YES → movement (extension or contraction)
                    NO  → shift (increase right, decrease left)
```

## Conditions of supply

| Change | Direction | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Higher wages, materials or energy costs | Left | Lower profit at every price |
| Better technology, higher productivity | Right | Lower cost per unit |
| Indirect tax | Left | Extra cost per unit |
| Subsidy | Right | Lower cost per unit |
| More firms | Right | More sellers |
| Bad weather, disease | Left | Less can be produced |

## Analysis chain for a supply shift

```
1. Cause (e.g. energy prices rise for bakeries)
2. Supply shifts left
3. Shortage at the old price
4. Price rises, quantity traded falls
5. Consumers pay more, buy less; producers' costs and sales change
```

## PES

```
PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price   (positive)
```

| Value | Type |
|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic (vertical) |
| 0 to 1 | Inelastic (steep) |
| 1 | Unitary (straight line through the origin) |
| Above 1 | Elastic (shallow) |
| Infinite | Perfectly elastic (horizontal) |

**Worked reminder.** Price rises from £40 to £50 (+25%); quantity supplied rises from 1,000 to 1,100 (+10%). PES = 10 ÷ 25 = **0.4**: inelastic.

## What decides PES

- Time: short run inelastic, long run more elastic.
- Spare capacity.
- Stocks: storable goods are more elastic than perishable ones.
- Ease of switching factors from other uses.
- Length of the production process.

## Why PES matters

- **Producers:** elastic supply lets a firm gain extra sales when demand rises; inelastic supply lets rivals or importers take them.
- **Consumers:** inelastic supply means a demand rise mainly raises price; elastic supply keeps prices more stable.
- **Trade-off:** stocks and spare capacity make supply more elastic but cost money.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Extension versus increase:** movement after a price rise versus shift right.
- **PES versus PED:** PES is positive; PED is normally negative.
- **Short run versus long run:** the same product can be inelastic now and elastic later.
- **Perfectly inelastic versus no supply:** a fixed quantity, not zero.

## Exam traps

- Shifting supply for an own-price change.
- No mechanism for a shift.
- Negative PES.
- Ignoring the time period.
- Unlabelled diagrams.

## Quick self-test

1. Define supply.
2. Give two reasons the supply curve slopes upwards.
3. Three firms supply 40, 25 and 35 units at £6. Find market supply at £6.
4. Wages in the clothing industry rise. What happens to the supply of clothing, and why?
5. A government gives a subsidy to bus companies. What happens to the supply of bus services?
6. The price of a product rises. Is this a shift or a movement?
7. Price rises from £10 to £12 and quantity supplied from 300 to 390. Calculate PES.
8. Is the answer to question 7 elastic or inelastic?
9. Why is the supply of fresh flowers less elastic than the supply of tinned soup?
10. Describe the shape of a perfectly inelastic supply curve and give an example.
11. Explain how spare capacity affects PES.
12. Give one reason consumers lose out when supply is inelastic.

### Answers

1. The quantity producers are willing and able to sell at each price over a period of time.
2. A higher price gives more profit per unit; it covers the higher cost of extra output and attracts new firms.
3. 40 + 25 + 35 = **100**.
4. Supply shifts left because higher costs reduce profit at every price.
5. Supply shifts right because the subsidy lowers firms' costs.
6. A movement along the supply curve (an extension).
7. Price +20%; quantity +30%; PES = 30 ÷ 20 = **1.5**.
8. Elastic.
9. Flowers are perishable and cannot be stored; tinned soup can be held in stock and released when price rises.
10. A vertical line; for example, the number of seats in a theatre for one performance.
11. With spare capacity, firms can raise output quickly without new machines or workers, so supply is more elastic.
12. A rise in demand mainly raises the price rather than the quantity available.
