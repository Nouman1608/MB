## 4.3.7 Definition of fiscal policy

**Fiscal policy** means the government changing how much it spends and how much it taxes, to steer the economy towards its macroeconomic aims.

A good definition has two parts: it is about **government spending and taxation** (not interest rates or the money supply, which are monetary policy), and it has a **purpose**, which is to move the economy towards an aim.

## 4.3.8 Fiscal policy measures

### The budget balance

The government budget compares what the government raises with what it spends in a year.

| Position | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Budget surplus | Government revenue is greater than government spending |
| Budget deficit | Government spending is greater than government revenue |
| Balanced budget | Revenue equals spending |

Budget balance = total government revenue − total government spending. A positive answer is a surplus and a negative answer is a deficit. When you state the answer, always say which one it is. A deficit is usually met by borrowing, and that borrowing is added to the national debt. A surplus can pay off some of that debt or be kept in reserve.

### The measures

| Measure | Direction | Immediate effect on the budget |
|---|---|---|
| Cut tax rates (income, sales, company tax) | Expansionary | Revenue falls, so the balance moves towards deficit |
| Raise tax rates | Contractionary | Revenue rises, so the balance moves towards surplus |
| Raise government spending (health, schools, roads, welfare, subsidies) | Expansionary | Spending rises, so the balance moves towards deficit |
| Cut government spending | Contractionary | Spending falls, so the balance moves towards surplus |

Expansionary policy aims to increase spending in the economy. Contractionary policy aims to reduce it. Governments can combine measures, for example raising spending and cutting taxes together.

Not every change in the balance is a policy decision. In a recession, tax revenue tends to fall (people earn and spend less) and welfare spending tends to rise (more people claim benefits), so the deficit grows without any new measure. Fiscal policy is the deliberate change.

### Worked example 1: finding the balance

Country A, Year 1 (illustrative, million units of an invented currency):

| Revenue | m units | Spending | m units |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | 420 | Education | 260 |
| Sales tax | 310 | Health | 240 |
| Company tax | 150 | Welfare | 250 |
| Other | 120 | Infrastructure | 180 |
| | | Defence | 120 |
| | | Other | 60 |

1. Total revenue = 420 + 310 + 150 + 120 = 1,000.
2. Total spending = 260 + 240 + 250 + 180 + 120 + 60 = 1,110.
3. Balance = 1,000 − 1,110 = −110.

Country A has a **budget deficit of 110 million units**. If GDP is 5,000 million units, the deficit is 110 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **2.2% of GDP**, a figure that lets you compare countries of different sizes.

### Worked example 2: a contractionary package

In Year 2, Country A raises the sales tax rate from 10% to 12% on taxable spending of 3,100 million units, and cuts infrastructure spending from 180 million units to 140 million units.

1. Old sales tax = 10% × 3,100 = 310. New sales tax = 12% × 3,100 = 372. Extra revenue = **62 million units**.
2. New revenue = 1,000 + 62 = 1,062. New spending = 1,110 − 40 = 1,070.
3. New balance = 1,062 − 1,070 = **−8 million units**, so the deficit falls from 110 to 8, a reduction of 102 (92.7% of the old deficit).

Check the assumption. Step 1 assumes shoppers buy just as much after the rise. If higher prices cut taxable spending by 3% to 3,007 million units, sales tax is 12% × 3,007 = 360.84, revenue is 1,050.84 and the deficit is 1,070 − 1,050.84 = **19.16 million units**. The deficit still falls, but by less than planned.

### Worked example 3: an expansionary package and a surplus turned to deficit

Country B has revenue of 800 million units and spending of 740 million units, a **surplus of 60 million units**. It raises spending by 90 million units and cuts income tax so that revenue falls by 25 million units.

1. New spending = 740 + 90 = 830. New revenue = 800 − 25 = 775.
2. New balance = 775 − 830 = **−55 million units**, a deficit.
3. The swing is 60 + 55 = 115 million units, which equals the sum of the two changes (90 + 25).

### Worked example 4: a tax cut does not always lose the full amount

Country A cuts the income tax rate from 25% to 22%. Taxable income is 1,680 million units.

1. Old revenue = 25% × 1,680 = 420 million units. New revenue at 22% = **369.6 million units**. Arithmetic loss = 50.4 million units.
2. Suppose the tax cut leaves people with more to spend and incomes rise by 5%, to 1,764. New revenue = 22% × 1,764 = 388.08 million units, so the loss is only **31.92 million units**.

The budget deficit therefore widens by less than the first sum suggests. Say "if" because the income rise is not guaranteed.

## 4.3.9 Effects on the government's macroeconomic aims

For each aim, set out a chain. Then add a limit, because judgement strengthens an answer.

### Economic growth (a rise in the economy's output)

**Expansionary chain:** government spends more on roads, schools and training, or cuts company tax, then firms and households have more to spend or more reason to invest, then firms sell more and produce more, so output rises. Spending on infrastructure and education also adds to the economy's capacity to produce in future.

**Example:** real output rises from 5,000 million units to 5,150 million units: (150 ÷ 5,000) × 100 = **3.0% growth**.

**Limits:** the effect is small if people save the tax cut; borrowing to pay for it means future interest and repayments; and projects take years to complete.

### Full employment (low unemployment)

**Chain:** government builds a hospital, so construction firms hire workers directly. Those workers spend wages in local shops, which then need more staff. A cut in income tax also raises take-home pay, so households spend more and firms hire.

**Example:** Country C has 160,000 unemployed in a labour force of 2,000,000, an unemployment rate of 8%. A programme gives jobs to 20,000 of the unemployed: (160,000 − 20,000) ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 = **7.0%**.

**Limits:** fiscal policy works poorly on structural unemployment, where workers lack the skills for the jobs on offer. There, spending on training is the better measure.

### Stable prices (low inflation)

**Chain (contractionary):** the government raises income tax and cuts spending, then households have less to spend, so firms sell less and find it harder to raise prices, and inflation slows. If the price index rises from 100 to 106 in one year (6% inflation) and then to 109.18 the next, inflation is 3.0% (109.18 ÷ 106 = 1.03), a slower rise, not a fall in prices.

**Limits:** higher indirect taxes raise prices directly, which can push the measured inflation rate up first. Cutting spending can also raise unemployment, so aims may conflict. A subsidy on a basic food lowers its price, a direct way to hold prices down at a cost to the budget.

### Balance of payments stability

**Chain (contractionary):** lower incomes mean people buy fewer imports, so spending on imports falls and the current account improves. Tax incentives or subsidies to exporting firms cut their costs and help them sell abroad.

**Opposite case:** expansion can worsen the position. If households spend 30 units of every extra 100 units on imports and incomes rise by 100 million units, imports rise by 30 million units. A deficit of 60 million units on the current account becomes 90 million units if nothing else changes.

### Redistribution of income

**Chain:** progressive taxes take a larger share from high earners, and spending on welfare, health and education helps low-income households.

**Example:** a progressive system charges 10% on income up to 20,000 units and 30% above that. A person on 12,000 units pays 1,200 units (10%). A person on 60,000 units pays 2,000 + 12,000 = 14,000 units (23.3%). Before tax the richer person earns 5 times as much. After tax they have 46,000 units against 10,800, about 4.3 times. Add a benefit of 2,000 units to the poorer person and the gap falls to 46,000 ÷ 12,800 = about 3.6 times.

### Conflicts and evaluation

Fiscal measures that help one aim can hurt another. Expansion that cuts unemployment may raise prices and imports. Contraction that helps stable prices may increase unemployment. Good evaluation also considers: how large the measure is; the time lag before it works; whether the economy has spare resources; how households and firms respond; and the cost to the budget and the national debt.

## Common errors

- Writing "deficit" when revenue is larger than spending, or leaving out the minus sign. State the word.
- Saying tax cuts always reduce revenue by the full arithmetic amount.
- Calling a fall in the inflation rate a fall in prices.
- Mixing in interest rates, which belong to monetary policy.
- Using aggregate demand or aggregate supply, which the syllabus says are not required here.
- Describing a measure without the chain that links it to the aim.
