Section **3.1.1.3 Social change and changing families** is the broadest part of the Families topic
in OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690), examined in Unit 1 Section A. It covers
demographic changes and their explanations; "the relationship between families and
industrialisation and between families and urbanisation"; family diversity, including "pivot/sandwich
generation, reconstituted/blended families, living apart together, child free couples, and beanpole
families"; explanations of diversity, including laws and social policies; individualisation and
personal life (Smart); diasporas and transnational families; friends as family; and pets or
companion animals as family members. The unit overview is in the
[Introducing Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-introducing-sociology/).

## Demographic changes

Demography is the study of population size and structure. The specification lists five changes.

| Change | What has happened in many societies | Main explanations |
|---|---|---|
| **Family size** | Fewer children per woman | Children become an economic cost rather than an asset once child labour is restricted and schooling extended; contraception; women's education and paid work |
| **Birth rate** | Births per 1,000 of the population fall | As above, plus later marriage and urbanisation |
| **Death rate** | Deaths per 1,000 fall | Better nutrition, clean water and sanitation, vaccination and medical care |
| **Infant and child mortality** | Far fewer babies and young children die | Clean water, maternal health care, immunisation; parents then need fewer births to ensure some children survive |
| **Ageing population** | Rising average age and more older people | Falling birth rates combined with longer life expectancy |

**Migration** is the fifth change, and the specification names both kinds:

- **Internal migration**, such as rural to urban movement, can split families across city and
  village. In China, millions of rural parents who moved to cities left children with
  grandparents, creating "left-behind children" and skipped-generation households.
- **International migration** creates transnational families and diasporas, discussed below.

The point for exams is to connect each change to family structure: lower fertility and longer life
produce families with more generations but fewer members in each, which is the basis of the
**beanpole family**.

## Families, industrialisation and urbanisation

Three positions are worth comparing.

1. **Functionalist fit.** Parsons argued industrialisation replaced extended families with the
   isolated nuclear family because industry needed a mobile workforce. William Goode (*World
   Revolution and Family Patterns*, 1963) argued that the conjugal family was spreading worldwide
   with industrialisation and urbanisation.
2. **Historical challenge.** Laslett's research showed nuclear households were common before
   industrialisation, and Anderson's study of Preston found extended households grew in early
   industrial towns as kin pooled resources. The relationship is not one-way.
3. **Stages of change.** Michael Young and Peter Willmott (*The Symmetrical Family*, 1973) described
   a move from the pre-industrial family as a unit of production, through an early industrial
   family with strong mother-daughter extended ties, to the home-centred, more equal **symmetrical
   family**.

Urbanisation matters separately from industry. City housing is smaller and more expensive,
distances from rural kin are greater, and urban labour markets reward individual earners; all push
towards smaller households. Yet many urban families in developing societies keep strong ties to
rural kin through visits, money and childcare, so households shrink while family networks remain
extended.

## Family diversity: the named newer types

| Type | Definition | Link to social change |
|---|---|---|
| **Pivot / sandwich generation** | Adults, often women in mid-life, caring at the same time for children and for ageing parents | Ageing populations and later childbearing |
| **Reconstituted / blended family** | A family in which at least one partner has children from a previous relationship | Divorce, separation and repartnering |
| **Living apart together (LAT)** | A committed couple who choose, or are forced, to keep separate homes | Careers in different cities, wish for independence, older couples keeping their own homes |
| **Child-free couples** | Couples who choose not to have children (distinct from involuntary childlessness) | Women's careers, cost of children, individualisation |
| **Beanpole family** | Long, thin family structure: several generations alive, few members in each | Falling fertility and rising life expectancy |

Julia Brannen's work in the early 2000s described the **beanpole family** and the middle
**pivot generation** that gives support both up and down the generations.

**Explanations of diversity** combine demographic change with **laws and social policies**. Divorce
law reform makes ending marriages easier, which increases single-parent and reconstituted
families. China's one-child policy, introduced in 1980 and later relaxed to allow two and then
three children, produced a "4-2-1" structure of four grandparents, two parents and one child.
Welfare, housing and parental leave policies shape whether young adults can leave home, whether
lone parents can work, and how care for older people is shared.

## Individualisation and personal life (Smart)

**Individualisation** theorists argue that traditional ties of class, religion and kinship have
weakened, so individuals must design their own lives. Ulrich Beck and Elisabeth Beck-Gernsheim
(*The Normal Chaos of Love*, 1995) saw relationships as more chosen and also more fragile. Anthony
Giddens described the **pure relationship**, which lasts only as long as both partners find it
satisfying.

**Carol Smart** (*Personal Life*, 2007) argued this overstates freedom. People remain embedded in
webs of relationships, memories and obligations; choices are made within histories and
constraints. She proposed **personal life** as a wider concept than "the family", covering all
significant relationships, including with friends, ex-partners, the dead and animals. This
broadening is what links the specification's final three items.

Judith Stacey (*Brave New Families*, 1990), another named sociologist, studied families in
California's Silicon Valley and found women creating new arrangements after divorce, such as the
**divorce-extended family** linking former in-laws, which she saw as evidence of a postmodern
family with no single dominant form.

## Diasporas and transnational families

A **diaspora** is a population dispersed from a homeland that keeps a sense of shared identity. A
**transnational family** keeps family life across national borders: members live in different
countries but share decisions, money and care. Rhacel Parreñas studied Filipino families in which
mothers worked abroad as domestic workers while children stayed at home, keeping contact through
calls, letters and remittances. Arlie Hochschild's idea of **global care chains** describes how a
migrant woman cares for a richer family's children while her own children are cared for by
relatives or paid help at home. Cheap video calls and money transfer apps have made
"doing family" at a distance easier but do not remove the emotional costs.

## Friends as family and pets as family

- **Friends as family**: Jeffrey Weeks and colleagues used the term **families of choice** for
  networks of friends who give the support usually expected of kin. Ray Pahl and Liz Spencer's
  *Rethinking Friendship* (2006) described **personal communities** in which friends and kin are
  blended, with some friends treated like family and some kin like friends.
- **Pets/companion animals**: Nickie Charles and Charlotte Aull Davies found that many people
  include pets as family members, describing them in kinship terms. Smart's concept of personal life
  makes room for these bonds, while critics ask whether the term "family" is being stretched too
  far.

## Worked example: annotated 20-mark plan

*Using examples from a society you know, evaluate the view that demographic changes are the main
cause of family diversity.*

- **Introduction:** define family diversity; name two or three newer types.
- **For:** ageing and low fertility explain beanpole and pivot-generation families; falling infant
  mortality allows smaller families and more child-free couples; migration explains transnational
  families (Parreñas).
- **Against:** laws and policies, such as divorce reform or China's birth limits, directly change
  structures; individualisation and changing values explain LAT and child-free choices; economic
  change and urbanisation shape household size; Smart argues personal choice is embedded in
  relationships, so no single cause works alone.
- **Application:** apply each point to a named society, for example an ageing East Asian society or
  a society with high emigration.
- **Conclusion:** demographic change is a necessary condition, but laws and cultural change decide
  which forms appear.

## Common mistakes

- Listing family types without explaining **why** they have increased.
- Confusing birth rate (per 1,000 people) with family size (children per family).
- Treating industrialisation and urbanisation as the same process.
- Forgetting **laws and social policies**, which the specification names explicitly.
- Describing Smart as an individualisation theorist: she criticises it.

## Exam technique

**Explain** questions on this section reward a chain: change, cause, effect on families. In
**evaluate** questions, set demographic explanations against cultural, legal and economic ones, and
always apply to a named society, because the specification requires it in selected extended
responses.
