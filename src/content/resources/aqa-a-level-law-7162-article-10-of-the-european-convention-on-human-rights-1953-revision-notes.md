Condensed notes on Article 10.1. For full explanation and a worked scenario, use the
[Article 10 study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-article-10-of-the-european-convention-on-human-rights-1953/).

## The text in summary

Everyone has the right to freedom of expression: to **hold opinions** and to **receive and impart
information and ideas**, without interference by public authority and regardless of frontiers. States
may **license** broadcasting, television and cinema. The right is **qualified** (restrictions in 10.2).

The specification splits 10.1 into two elements: **receive** information and ideas, and
**communicate** information and ideas.

## Scope: the Handyside principle

Article 10 protects ideas that "offend, shock or disturb the State or any sector of the population"
(Handyside v UK, 1976). Handyside itself found **no breach**: seizure of the book was justified to
protect morals.

## A rough hierarchy of protection

| Type of expression | Protection | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Political speech, criticism of politicians | Strongest; politicians must tolerate wider criticism | Lingens v Austria (1986): breach |
| Press reporting on public affairs | Strong; press is "public watchdog" | Sunday Times v UK (1979); Observer and Guardian v UK (1991) |
| Journalistic sources | Protection of sources is a basic condition of press freedom | Goodwin v UK (1996): breach |
| Reporting others' offensive views | Protected where part of serious reporting | Jersild v Denmark (1994): breach |
| Artistic expression touching morals or religion | Wider margin | Müller v Switzerland (1988); Otto-Preminger-Institut v Austria (1994): no breach |
| Paid political advertising on broadcast media | Restriction can be justified | Animal Defenders International v UK (2013): no breach |

## Communicating

- **Sunday Times v UK (1979)**: contempt injunction stopping a thalidomide article was not necessary;
  the public was entitled to the information.
- **Observer and Guardian v UK (1991)**: *Spycatcher* injunctions unjustified after the book was published
  in the United States.
- **Goodwin v UK (1996)**: order for a journalist to reveal his source breached Article 10.

## Receiving

- Mainly a right not to be prevented from receiving information **others are willing to give**.
- **Leander v Sweden (1987)**: no right of access to a secret police register.
- **Magyar Helsinki Bizottság v Hungary (2016)**: a right of access to state-held information can arise in
  some circumstances, especially for watchdogs such as the press and NGOs.
- **Kennedy v Charity Commission (2014)**: no general Article 10 right to information from a public body;
  use statute, including the Freedom of Information Act 2000, and the common law.

## English law

- **Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers (1993)**: a local authority cannot sue in defamation.
- **R v SSHD ex p Simms (2000)**: policy stopping prisoners giving oral interviews to journalists
  investigating possible wrongful convictions was unlawful.
- **HRA 1998 s12**: where relief might affect expression, the court must have particular regard to the
  importance of the right; s12(3) no pre-trial restraint unless the applicant is **likely** to succeed.
- **Cream Holdings v Banerjee (2004)**: "likely" normally means more likely than not.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Freedom of expression | The right to hold opinions and to receive and impart information and ideas |
| Impart | To communicate or pass on information and ideas |
| Prior restraint | Stopping publication before it happens, usually by interim injunction |
| Public watchdog | The Court's description of the press's democratic role |
| Chilling effect | The deterrent effect of sanctions on lawful expression |
| Margin of appreciation | Latitude given to states, wider on morals and religion than on political speech |
| Licensing | The power in 10.1 for states to license broadcasting, television and cinema |

## Exam traps

- Handyside: principle protecting offensive ideas, but **no breach** on the facts.
- Do not treat all speech the same; say where on the hierarchy the facts sit.
- The audience's right to **receive** is part of 10.1; Sunday Times relied on it.
- In any injunction question, cite **s12 HRA** and Cream Holdings.
- Article 10 is **not** a general freedom of information right (Leander; Kennedy).
- Remember 10.1 is engaged before 10.2 is considered; do not start with justification.

## Self-test

1. What three freedoms does Article 10.1 expressly include?
2. What power does the final sentence of Article 10.1 give to states?
3. State the Handyside principle and the outcome of the case.
4. Why did the Court find a breach in Lingens v Austria?
5. What did Goodwin v UK decide?
6. Why was there a breach in Jersild v Denmark?
7. Give a case where a restriction on artistic expression was upheld.
8. What did Leander v Sweden decide about the right to receive information?
9. How did Magyar Helsinki Bizottság v Hungary develop the right to receive information?
10. What did Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers decide?
11. What does section 12(3) HRA 1998 require?
12. What does "likely" mean in section 12(3), according to Cream Holdings?

**Answers:** 1. To hold opinions, to receive information and ideas, and to impart information and ideas.
2. To require the licensing of broadcasting, television or cinema enterprises. 3. Article 10 protects
ideas that offend, shock or disturb; the seizure of the book was nevertheless justified. 4. A politician
must tolerate wider criticism than a private individual, so the defamation conviction of a journalist
was not necessary. 5. Ordering a journalist to disclose his source breached Article 10; protection of
sources is a basic condition of press freedom. 6. The programme was a serious report on racism, and
punishing the journalist for spreading the interviewees' views would seriously hamper the press.
7. Müller v Switzerland (1988) or Otto-Preminger-Institut v Austria (1994). 8. It does not give a right
of access to a secret police register; it mainly stops the state blocking information others will give.
9. It accepted that a right of access to state-held information can arise in some circumstances,
especially for watchdogs. 10. A local authority cannot sue for defamation. 11. No relief restraining
publication before trial unless the applicant is likely to establish that publication should not be
allowed. 12. Normally more likely than not.
