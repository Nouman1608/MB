## Questions

**1.** Define a supply curve. **[2]**

**2.** In Country C the price of a ceramic mug rises from 16 to 20. Quantity supplied rises from 800 to 860 a week.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of supply. **[3]**
**(b)** State what your answer shows about supply. **[1]**

**3.** State whether each event causes a movement along, or a shift of, the supply curve for wheat, and give the direction. **[4]**

- (i) The price of wheat rises.
- (ii) A drought reduces the harvest.
- (iii) The government pays farmers a payment for each tonne grown.
- (iv) Fertiliser becomes cheaper.

**4.** Price elasticity of supply for a product is 1.8. Its price rises from 20 to 21 and it supplies 3,000 units at the old price. Calculate the new quantity supplied. **[3]**

**5.** A market in Country D has the following supply schedule.

| Price | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity supplied | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 |

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of supply when price rises from 6 to 8. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate it when price rises from 8 to 10, and explain why it is lower. **[3]**

**6.** Explain two factors that would make the supply of fresh cut flowers price inelastic in the short run. **[6]**

**7.** Supply in Country D (question 5) is Qs = 15P − 30. A subsidy of 2 per unit is introduced.

**(a)** Find the quantity supplied at a price of 8 after the subsidy. **[2]**
**(b)** State the size and direction of the shift in supply at any given price. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why a supply curve slopes upward. **[4]**

**9.** After a 20% price rise, the quantity supplied by a market of olive growers changes by 1% immediately, by 9% after one year and by 30% after five years.

**(a)** Calculate the elasticity for each period. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the values differ. **[3]**

**10.** Two firms supply a market. Firm A has Qs = 10P − 20 and firm B has Qs = 6P − 6.

**(a)** Find market supply at prices of 5 and 7. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the market's price elasticity of supply between these prices. **[3]**

**11.** In Country E, a 10% price rise leads to the following changes in quantity supplied: fresh strawberries +3%; plastic buckets +14%; limited-edition prints +0.5% (for a 5% price rise).

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of supply for each product. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the buckets are more elastic than the strawberries. **[3]**

**12.** In Country A, price of tomatoes grown in greenhouses rises by 15%. Quantity supplied rises by 3% in the first season and by 21% after five years. Assess whether supply is likely to be more elastic in the long run than in the short run. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A supply curve shows the relationship between price and the quantity [1] that producers are willing and able to supply, in a given period [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Include both the link between price and quantity and the idea of willingness and ability to supply. Naming only "price and quantity" is thin.

**2. (a)** Price change = (20 − 16) ÷ 16 × 100 = 25% [1]. Quantity change = (860 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 7.5% [1]. PES = 7.5 ÷ 25 = **0.3** [1].
**(b)** Supply is inelastic, since PES is below 1 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Using the old value as the base for each percentage is the standard approach, and showing both percentage changes makes your method clear to the reader.

**3.** (i) Movement along the curve (an extension of supply) [1]. (ii) Shift left [1]. (iii) Shift right [1]. (iv) Shift right [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the direction as well as the type. "Shift" on its own does not show you know the effect.

**4.** Price change = 1 ÷ 20 × 100 = 5% [1]. Quantity change = 1.8 × 5 = 9% [1]. New quantity = 3,000 × 1.09 = **3,270** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Rearrange the formula to find the percentage change in quantity before applying it to the original quantity.

**5. (a)** Price change = 2 ÷ 6 × 100 = 33.3% [1]. Quantity change = 30 ÷ 60 × 100 = 50% [1]. PES = 50 ÷ 33.3 = **1.5** [1].
**(b)** Price 8 to 10 is +25% and quantity 90 to 120 is +33.3%, so PES = **1.33** [1]. It is lower because the line meets the price axis [1]. Each rise of 2 in price adds the same 30 units, but 30 is a smaller percentage of a larger quantity (33.3% against 50%), while 2 is a smaller percentage of a larger price (25% against 33.3%), so the quantity percentage falls by more and elasticity falls towards 1 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* When asked to explain a change in elasticity along a straight line, refer to percentages. A comment on slope alone does not explain the fall.

**6.** First factor: perishability or no stocks [1]. Cut flowers wilt, so firms cannot store them to release when the price rises [1]. Therefore quantity supplied cannot rise quickly, and PES is below 1 [1]. Second factor: production period [1]. Flowers take weeks or months to grow, so growers cannot increase output quickly in response to a price rise [1]. Therefore supply is unresponsive over the short run [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs a developed link to flowers and to the short run. A list of factors with no application gains little.

**7. (a)** After the subsidy firms act as if the price were 2 higher: Qs = 15(P + 2) − 30 [1]. At P = 8 this is 15 × 10 − 30 = **120** [1].
**(b)** Before the subsidy, quantity at 8 was 90 [1]. So supply shifts right by **30 units** at every price (15 × 2), equivalent to a downward shift of 2 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say whether the shift is right or left and give its size in units. A shift can also be described as vertical, but name which description you are using.

**8.** A higher price raises profit per unit if costs stay the same [1]. This gives existing firms an incentive to expand output [1] and may attract new firms into the market [1]. Extra output often costs more per unit as capacity is stretched, so a higher price is needed to supply more [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Build a chain from price to profit to incentive to output. A bare statement that "firms want to make more" is not enough.

**9. (a)** Immediate: 1 ÷ 20 = **0.05** [1]. One year: 9 ÷ 20 = **0.45** [1]. Five years: 30 ÷ 20 = **1.5** [1].
**(b)** Olive trees take years to plant and mature [1]. In the short run growers cannot add land or trees, so output barely changes [1]. In the long run new groves can be planted and new growers can enter, so supply becomes elastic [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the pattern to time and to the product shows application. Saying "firms have more time" without saying what they can do with it earns less.

**10. (a)** At 5: 30 + 24 = 54 [1]. At 7: 50 + 36 = 86 [1].
**(b)** Price change = 2 ÷ 5 × 100 = 40% [1]. Quantity change = 32 ÷ 54 × 100 = 59.3% [1]. PES = 59.3 ÷ 40 = **1.48** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Add the quantities at each price before calculating a percentage. Adding the two firms' elasticities is a common wrong route.

**11. (a)** Strawberries: 3 ÷ 10 = **0.3** [1]. Buckets: 14 ÷ 10 = **1.4** [1]. Prints: 0.5 ÷ 5 = **0.1** [1].
**(b)** Buckets are made in factories, so firms can run extra shifts and use stocks [1]. Strawberries depend on a growing season and cannot be stored long [1]. This makes strawberry output slow to respond to the price [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The prints have a different price change, so check the percentage for each product. Compare the two products directly to answer the "why" part.

**12.** Short run: PES = 3 ÷ 15 = 0.2 [1]. Long run: PES = 21 ÷ 15 = 1.4 [1]. Supply is more elastic in the long run [1]. Reasons: greenhouses can be built, extra staff trained, and new growers can enter [1]. In the short run growers have fixed greenhouses and a growing cycle that takes weeks, so output cannot rise quickly [1]. Evaluation: the long-run response is limited if land, water or energy is scarce [1], if barriers to entry such as high set-up costs deter new growers [1], or if growers expect the price rise to be temporary [1]. Judgement: supply is more elastic in the long run, but how much depends on whether inputs and investment are available [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A good answer calculates both values, explains the cause of the difference and then weighs it. Noting that the long-run figure depends on conditions is the evaluative step, and a final judgement should follow.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the new value as the base for a percentage change.
- Stating a PES figure but not saying whether supply is elastic or inelastic.
- Describing a change in the good's own price as a shift of the supply curve.
- Shifting the curve the wrong way for a subsidy or a tax.
- Writing about factors without applying them to the product in the question.
- Adding the elasticities of two firms instead of adding their quantities.
- Explaining elasticity with slope instead of percentages.
- Ending an evaluation answer without a clear judgement.
