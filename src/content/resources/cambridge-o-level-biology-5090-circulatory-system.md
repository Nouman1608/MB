This guide covers subtopic **11.1 Circulatory system** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe the circulatory system as a system of blood vessels with a pump and valves to ensure one-way flow of blood, to describe a double circulation as a system in which blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit, and to understand that a double circulation provides a low pressure circulation to the lungs and a high pressure circulation to the body tissues.

The [Transport in humans guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-transport-in-humans/) covers the whole topic, and the [11.1 and 11.2 revision notes](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-transport-in-humans-revision-notes/) give a condensed version. This page explains why a transport system is needed, traces the route of the blood step by step and gives the reasons for a double circulation. Test yourself with the [11.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-circulatory-system-practice/).

## Why a transport system?

A human body is large, and most cells are far from the surface, so diffusion alone is too slow to supply them. The circulatory system carries substances around the body in the blood.

| Carried to the cells | Carried away from the cells |
|---|---|
| Oxygen from the lungs | Carbon dioxide to the lungs |
| Glucose and amino acids from the gut | Urea to the kidneys |
| Hormones from glands | Heat, to the skin |
| Water and ions | Waste products |

## Parts of the system

The circulatory system has three parts.

1. **Blood vessels**: tubes (arteries, veins and capillaries) that carry the blood.
2. **A pump**: the **heart**, which pushes the blood around.
3. **Valves**: flaps in the heart and in veins that stop the blood flowing the wrong way.

Together they make a **closed system**: the blood stays inside vessels. The pump supplies the pressure, the vessels form the route, and the valves make sure that the flow is in **one direction only**.

### Why one-way flow matters

If blood could flow backwards, it would not move steadily to the tissues. The valves in the heart prevent backflow between the atria and ventricles and between the ventricles and the arteries. The valves in the veins prevent blood from falling back when the pressure is low (see subtopic 11.3). Each valve opens when the pressure behind it is greater, and shuts when the pressure in front of it is greater.

## Double circulation

In humans, blood passes through the heart **twice** during each complete circuit of the body. This is a **double circulation**. There are two loops.

| Loop | Route | Blood pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Pulmonary circulation | Heart (right side) → lungs → heart (left side) | **Low** pressure |
| Systemic circulation | Heart (left side) → body tissues → heart (right side) | **High** pressure |

The right side of the heart receives deoxygenated blood and pumps it to the lungs. The left side of the heart receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it to the rest of the body.

### The route in order

Start at the right atrium and follow the blood.

1. Deoxygenated blood from the body enters the **right atrium** through the **vena cava**.
2. It passes to the **right ventricle**.
3. The right ventricle pumps it through the **pulmonary artery** to the **lungs**.
4. In the lungs, the blood picks up oxygen and loses carbon dioxide.
5. Oxygenated blood returns in the **pulmonary vein** to the **left atrium**.
6. It passes to the **left ventricle**.
7. The left ventricle pumps it through the **aorta** to the body.
8. The blood passes through the organs, and returns, deoxygenated, to the vena cava.

Note that the pulmonary artery carries *deoxygenated* blood and the pulmonary vein carries *oxygenated* blood. This is the opposite of what happens in most other arteries and veins, because arteries are defined by the *direction* of flow (away from the heart), not by the oxygen content.

### Worked example: tracing a route

*Trace the route taken by a molecule of oxygen from the air in the alveoli to a leg muscle cell.*

1. It diffuses from the alveolus into the blood in the lung capillary.
2. It travels in the **pulmonary vein** to the **left atrium**.
3. It passes to the **left ventricle**.
4. It is pumped into the **aorta**.
5. It travels through arteries to the leg, then into capillaries in the muscle.
6. It diffuses out of the red blood cell, through the capillary wall and tissue fluid, into the muscle cell.

Note that the molecule passes through the heart **once** on this part of the journey, but the blood has gone through the heart twice in a complete circuit: once on the way to the lungs and once on the way to the body.

## Why a double circulation?

The syllabus states that the double circulation provides **a low pressure circulation to the lungs and a high pressure circulation to the body tissues**. The reasons are as follows.

**Low pressure to the lungs**

- The capillaries in the lungs have very thin walls, to allow gas exchange. High pressure would damage them, and could force fluid into the alveoli.
- A lower pressure means the blood flows more slowly through the lung capillaries, giving more time for the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide.

**High pressure to the body**

- The body tissues are far from the heart. A high pressure drives the blood quickly through the long vessels to all the organs.
- Fast flow supplies oxygen and glucose quickly, which is important for organs with a high energy demand, such as the muscles and brain.

**Re-pressurising the blood**

After passing through the lung capillaries, the pressure of the blood falls. Returning it to the heart means it can be pumped again at high pressure by the left ventricle, so it reaches the body quickly. In a single circulation, the blood would arrive at the body at lower pressure and flow more slowly.

### Comparison with a single circulation

In a fish, the blood passes through the heart **once** per circuit: heart, gills, body, heart. The blood loses pressure in the gill capillaries, so it moves more slowly to the body. In humans, the heart pumps blood twice per circuit, which keeps the pressure high to the body. This is an advantage for warm-blooded animals with a high demand for oxygen.

## Heart muscle and the two pumps

The heart is really two pumps side by side: the right side pumps blood to the lungs and the left side pumps blood to the body. The wall of the left ventricle is much thicker than the right, because it must produce a higher pressure (see subtopic 11.2).

## Worked example: comparing pressures

*A table gives illustrative maximum pressures, in kilopascals (kPa), in two arteries of a resting person.*

| Artery | Maximum pressure (kPa) |
|---|---|
| Aorta (to the body) | 16.0 |
| Pulmonary artery (to the lungs) | 3.2 |

1. Which is higher? The aorta, at 16.0 kPa.
2. How many times higher? 16.0 divided by 3.2 = **5 times**.
3. Explain: the left ventricle pumps blood into the aorta and has a thick muscular wall, so it can produce a high pressure to drive blood round the whole body. The right ventricle pumps blood only to the nearby lungs, so a lower pressure is enough and avoids damaging the lung capillaries.

When an exam question gives numbers like these, first compare (higher or lower), then calculate (ratio or difference), then explain using the function of each loop.

## Time for one circuit

*A resting adult has about 5.0 dm cubed of blood, and the heart pumps about 5.0 dm cubed per minute.* Time for all the blood to pass through the heart once = 5.0 divided by 5.0 = **1 minute**. Because the blood goes through the heart twice per circuit (once for each loop), each loop takes less time than this, and a molecule of blood returns to the heart many times in an hour. During exercise the heart pumps more blood per minute, so the circulation time is shorter.

## Common mistakes

- Defining double circulation as "two hearts" or "two sets of blood". It is two loops, with the blood passing through the heart twice per circuit.
- Saying that arteries always carry oxygenated blood.
- Saying that the pulmonary vein carries deoxygenated blood.
- Forgetting the valves in a description of the circulatory system.
- Saying that blood is pumped to the lungs at high pressure.
- Writing the route in the wrong order, for example putting the left ventricle before the lungs.
- Explaining the low pressure in the lungs only as "to protect them", without mentioning the thin capillary walls or more time for exchange.

## Command words

- **Describe**: give the parts and the route of the circulation.
- **Understand** and **explain**: give reasons why a double circulation is better than a single one.
- **State**: give the name of a vessel or the pressure in a loop.
- **Compare**: give differences between the pulmonary and systemic circulations.
