Section A of Component 2 is a single essay on one poem or prose text from **Part 2 of the Pearson
Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology**. You know the ten possible texts in advance, and the
named text is printed in the exam. This guide explains how the essay is assessed, how to prepare each
text, and how to turn a question into a 45-minute answer. For the paper as a whole, see the
[Component 2 overview](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-language-component-2/).

## What Section A covers

| Detail | Section A: Poetry and Prose Texts |
|---|---|
| Task | One essay question on a poetry or prose text from Anthology Part 2 |
| Text | Made available in the examination |
| Marks | 30: AO1 12, AO2 18 |
| Advised time | About 45 minutes |
| Paper | Component 2 is 1 hour 30 minutes and 60 marks in total |

**AO1:** read and understand a variety of texts, selecting and interpreting information, ideas and
perspectives. **AO2:** understand and analyse how writers use linguistic and structural devices to
achieve their effects.

AO2 is worth 18 of the 30 marks, so analysis of the writer's methods should drive every paragraph. AO1
is shown through the ideas you select and the interpretation you build, not through retelling.

## The ten Part 2 texts

| Poetry | Prose |
|---|---|
| "Disabled", Wilfred Owen | "The Story of an Hour", Kate Chopin |
| "Out, Out–", Robert Frost | "The Necklace", Guy de Maupassant |
| "An Unknown Girl", Moniza Alvi | "Significant Cigarettes" (from *The Road Home*), Rose Tremain |
| "The Bright Lights of Sarajevo", Tony Harrison | "Whistle and I'll Come to You" (from *The Woman in Black*), Susan Hill |
| "Still I Rise", Maya Angelou | "Night", Alice Munro |

The anthology is fixed by specification issue; check the current list with your teacher before
revising.

## Preparing each text

Build one revision card per text with five headings:

1. **Situation:** who, where, what happens, in two sentences.
2. **Viewpoint and voice:** first person, third person, a speaker addressing someone.
3. **Structure:** how the text opens, where it turns, how it ends, how time moves.
4. **Three key methods** with a short reference for each.
5. **Ideas and perspectives:** what the writer seems to suggest, and one alternative reading.

Because the text is printed in the exam, you do not need to memorise long quotations. You do need to
know where the key moments are, so you can find them quickly.

## Reading a poem for AO2

- **Form and stanza shape:** regular stanzas suggest control; irregular ones can mirror disorder.
- **Line breaks and enjambment:** a sentence running over a line can create momentum or a pause before a
  key word.
- **Caesura:** a mid-line break, often a moment of shock or reflection.
- **Repetition and refrain:** emphasis, insistence or defiance.
- **Rhyme and rhythm:** a regular beat can sound confident; broken rhythm can sound hesitant.
- **Voice and address:** who speaks, and to whom; how the tone changes.

## Reading a prose text for AO2

- **Narrative viewpoint:** a first-person narrator gives intimacy and possible unreliability; third
  person gives distance or irony.
- **Pacing:** short sentences and close detail slow a key moment; summary speeds past years.
- **Structure:** a time shift, a delayed revelation, a twist ending, an open ending.
- **Dialogue:** what characters say, and what they avoid saying.
- **Setting and symbolism:** objects or places that carry meaning beyond themselves.

## Worked example: a prose question

Question (original): *Explore how the writer presents the consequences of pride in "The Necklace". In
your answer you should consider the writer's use of language and structure.*

**Plan (five minutes):**

```
Thesis:   Maupassant presents pride as a trap that costs Mathilde far more than
          the necklace was worth, and the structure makes the reader feel that cost.
Para 1:   Opening portrait - Mathilde's dreams set against her real home (contrast)
Para 2:   The ball - her moment of success; why choosing the diamond necklace matters
Para 3:   The loss and the decision not to tell Madame Forestier - pride over honesty
Para 4:   Ten years compressed into a few lines; the change in her appearance
Para 5:   The final revelation - structure reframes everything that came before
```

**Model paragraph (paragraph 4):**

> Maupassant compresses ten years of hardship into a short passage of summary, so the reader feels the
> sudden weight of time that Mathilde's pride has cost her. The narration lists her labour and the
> household economies plainly, without the dreamy detail that described her wishes at the start, and
> this shift in style mirrors her loss of illusions. By placing this summary immediately after the
> decision to replace the necklace secretly, the writer makes the consequence feel like a direct result
> of that one proud choice, preparing the reader for the irony of the ending.

What earns credit: an interpretation (AO1), structural analysis of pacing and placement, and a comment
on narrative style (AO2), all linked to the focus of the question.

## Worked example: a poetry paragraph

Question (original): *Explore how Owen presents loss in "Disabled".*

> Owen structures the poem around a movement between the man's bleak situation now and remembered
> scenes of his past, so loss is shown as a contrast rather than simply stated. The opening places him
> alone in a wheelchair, waiting for dark, while the memories are full of light, crowds and
> admiration; each return to the present therefore feels like a fresh loss. The poem closes with the
> repeated question "Why don't they come?", which ends on helplessness rather than resolution: the reader
> is left with the man's dependence on others, the final form of what the war has taken.

## Building the essay

- **Introduction:** two or three sentences giving your thesis on the focus. No biography, no
  summary.
- **Body:** three or four paragraphs, each on one method or one moment, moving through the text so that
  you cover the opening, a turning point and the ending.
- **Every paragraph:** point on the focus, short reference, analysis of the method, effect on the reader,
  link back to the question.
- **Conclusion:** a sentence or two on what the text suggests overall, perhaps with an alternative
  reading.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling the story or paraphrasing the poem.** Retelling demonstrates basic understanding only.
- **Feature-spotting** without explaining effect.
- **Ignoring structure**, especially endings, which often carry the writer's main point.
- **Writing about the whole text with equal weight**, so nothing is analysed in depth.
- **Drifting from the focus** named in the question into a general essay.
- **Overrunning:** Section B is worth the same 30 marks and needs its 45 minutes.

## Exam technique and command words

| Command | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Explore how the writer presents... | Analyse methods and their effects on the named focus, not just describe content |
| Consider the writer's use of language and structure | Cover both; structure is not optional |
| Support your answer with references | Short quotations or precise references to moments |

Read the printed text again before planning, even if you know it well. Mark three or four moments
that answer the focus, then order them by position in the text so your essay tracks its structure.
