## Section A

**1.** The N pole of a magnet is brought near the end of a second magnet. State what happens if the end of the second magnet is:

**(a)** an N pole **[1]**
**(b)** an S pole. **[1]**

**2.** From this list, name the **two** materials that are attracted by a magnet: copper, steel, aluminium, nickel, brass. **[2]**

**3.** Describe what is meant by a magnetic field. **[1]**

**4.** State how the direction of a magnetic field at a point is defined. **[1]**

**5.** State one difference between the magnetic properties of soft iron and steel. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A chain of four soft-iron nails hangs from the S pole of a bar magnet.

**(a)** Name the process that makes each nail a magnet. **[1]**
**(b)** State the pole formed at the top of the first nail, and explain why the second nail is attracted to the first. **[2]**
**(c)** The magnet is lifted away from the top nail. Describe and explain what happens to the chain. **[2]**

**7.** A student holds an unlabelled bar P near the N pole of a freely suspended magnet. The N pole is pushed away.

**(a)** State what this shows about bar P. **[1]**
**(b)** State which pole of P was nearest the magnet. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why seeing attraction instead would not have allowed the student to reach the conclusion in (a). **[2]**

**8.** This question is about the field of a bar magnet.

**(a)** Describe the pattern and direction of the magnetic field lines around a bar magnet. **[3]**
**(b)** State where the magnetic effect of a bar magnet is strongest. **[1]**

**9.** A student wants to show the magnetic field around a bar magnet.

**(a)** Describe how she plots the field lines using a small plotting compass. **[4]**
**(b)** She also uses iron filings. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of iron filings compared with a plotting compass. **[2]**

**10.** A crane in a scrap yard lifts old cars using an electromagnet.

**(a)** State whether the core of the electromagnet should be made of soft iron or steel, and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why an electromagnet is used rather than a permanent magnet. **[2]**
**(c)** Give **two** uses of permanent magnets. **[2]**

**11.** A soft-iron rod and a steel rod are each placed inside a coil carrying a current, so both become magnetised. The current is then switched off and both rods are tested with paper clips.

**(a)** State which rod still picks up paper clips. **[1]**
**(b)** State which material would be suitable for a compass needle, and give a reason. **[2]**

**12.** *(Extended)* Two bar magnets are placed in a line with their N poles facing each other, a short distance apart.

**(a)** Describe the shape of the field lines between the two N poles. **[2]**
**(b)** State what is found at the point midway between the poles. **[1]**
**(c)** State what the force between the magnets is due to. **[1]**

**13.** *(Extended)* This question is about the strength of magnetic fields.

**(a)** A U-shaped magnet has flat, parallel pole faces. Describe the field lines in the gap between the poles. **[2]**
**(b)** On a field diagram, the lines at point A are much closer together than at point B. Compare the field at A and B, and give a reason. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** They **repel** [1].
**(b)** They **attract** [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **Steel** [1] and **nickel** [1]. **[2]**

*Common mistake:* choosing aluminium or copper. They are metals but are non-magnetic.

**3.** A region in which a magnetic pole experiences a force [1]. **[1]**

**4.** The direction of the force on the **N pole** of a magnet placed at that point [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Soft iron is easily magnetised but loses its magnetism when the field is removed (temporary); steel is harder to magnetise but keeps its magnetism (permanent) [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** **Induced magnetism** (induction) [1].
**(b)** An **N pole** forms at the top of the first nail [1]; the first nail is now a magnet, and it induces magnetism in the second nail, with the opposite pole at the near end, so they attract [1].
**(c)** The nails fall apart [1], because soft iron is a temporary magnet and loses its magnetism when the magnet is removed [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** P is a **magnet** (it is magnetised) [1].
**(b)** An **N pole** [1].
**(c)** A magnet attracts unmagnetised magnetic material as well as unlike poles of another magnet [1], so attraction could mean P was a magnet or just a piece of iron; only repulsion proves P is magnetised [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Lines leave the N pole and curve round to enter the S pole [1]; arrows point from N to S [1]; lines are closest together near the poles, spread out further away, and never cross [1].
**(b)** At the **poles** (the ends) [1]. **[4]**

*Common mistake:* drawing arrows from S to N. Field direction is the direction of the force on an N pole, which is pushed away from the magnet's N pole.

**9. (a)** Place the magnet on paper and put the compass near the N pole [1]. Mark a dot at the tip of the compass needle [1]. Move the compass so the tail of the needle is over that dot, mark the new tip position, and repeat until the S pole is reached [1]. Join the dots with a smooth line and add an arrow in the direction the needle points; repeat from other starting points [1].
**(b)** Advantage: shows the whole pattern quickly [1]. Disadvantage: does not show the direction of the field [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** **Soft iron** [1]; it loses its magnetism when the current is switched off (a steel core would stay magnetised) [1].
**(b)** The magnetism can be switched off [1], so the crane can release (drop) the car at the right place [1].
**(c)** Any two: loudspeakers or headphones; electric motors; compass needles; magnetic door catches or fridge door seals [1 each]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** The **steel** rod [1].
**(b)** **Steel** [1]; it keeps its magnetism (it is a permanent magnet) [1]. **[3]**

**12. (a)** The lines from each N pole curve away from the other magnet [1]; no lines cross the gap from one pole to the other [1].
**(b)** A **neutral point**, where the fields of the two magnets cancel [1].
**(c)** The interaction between the magnetic fields of the two magnets [1]. **[4]**

**13. (a)** Parallel, straight lines [1], evenly spaced, running from the N pole to the S pole (a uniform field) [1].
**(b)** The field at A is **stronger** than at B [1], because the relative strength of the field is shown by the spacing of the lines: closer lines mean a stronger field [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating attraction as proof that something is a magnet.
- Calling copper or aluminium magnetic.
- Field-line arrows pointing from S to N, or lines that cross.
- Saying iron filings show the field direction.
- Choosing steel for an electromagnet core.
- Explaining a chain of nails without naming induced magnetism.
