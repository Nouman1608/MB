This guide covers **Aggregate demand (AD)** in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification. The specification asks you to explain aggregate demand and its components; to explain, with the aid of a diagram, the relationship between aggregate demand and the price level and shifts in the AD curve; and to evaluate the relationship between changes in income and consumption and the role of expectations. All figures are invented.

It builds on the [circular flow of income](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-circular-flow-of-income-ocr-aslevel-economics/) and leads into [aggregate supply](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-supply-as-ocr-aslevel-economics/).

## 1. What aggregate demand is

**Aggregate demand** is the total planned spending on an economy's goods and services at each possible price level, over a period of time.

**AD = C + I + G + (X - M)**

- **C, consumption:** household spending on goods and services. It is the largest component in most developed economies.
- **I, investment:** spending by firms on capital goods such as machinery, buildings and software, plus additions to stocks. It is the most volatile component.
- **G, government spending:** spending on goods, services and public sector pay. Transfer payments are excluded because they buy no output.
- **X - M, net exports:** foreign spending on domestic output minus domestic spending on foreign output. It is negative when imports exceed exports.

**Worked example 1: weight versus volatility.** Country N's AD is made up as follows (billion currency units).

| Component | Value | Share of AD |
|---|---|---|
| C | 1,300 | 65.0% |
| I | 350 | 17.5% |
| G | 450 | 22.5% |
| X | 600 | |
| M | 700 | |
| X - M | -100 | -5.0% |
| AD | 2,000 | 100% |

Suppose investment collapses by 10% (a fall of 35) while consumption rises by only 2% (a rise of 26). AD changes by 26 - 35 = -9, a fall of 0.45%. A large percentage swing in investment is almost offset by a small change in consumption, because consumption is nearly four times as large. When a question gives data, check both the size of a component and how much it changed.

## 2. What drives each component

| Component | Main influences |
|---|---|
| Consumption | Disposable income, wealth (house and share prices), interest rates, availability of credit, consumer confidence |
| Investment | Expected future demand and profits, interest rates, business confidence, technology, corporation tax and investment allowances |
| Government spending | Government decisions, political priorities, the economic cycle (spending on support rises in a downturn) |
| Exports | Incomes abroad, the exchange rate, relative inflation, quality and non-price competitiveness |
| Imports | Domestic incomes, the exchange rate, relative inflation, domestic supply bottlenecks |

## 3. The AD curve and the price level

Draw AD with the **price level** on the vertical axis and **real output** (real national income) on the horizontal axis. It slopes downwards from left to right. Three effects explain why a higher price level reduces the quantity of real output demanded.

1. **The wealth effect.** A higher price level cuts the real value of money and savings, so households feel poorer and consume less.
2. **The interest rate effect.** A higher price level means households and firms need more money for transactions. Demand for money rises, pushing up interest rates, which reduces borrowing for consumption and investment.
3. **The international trade effect.** If domestic prices rise relative to foreign prices, exports become less competitive and imports more attractive, so net exports fall.

A change in the price level causes a **movement along** the AD curve. It is wrong to say "higher prices shift AD to the left": that would count the same effect twice.

## 4. Shifts in the AD curve

AD **shifts** when any component changes for a reason other than the price level.

| Rightward shift (AD increases) | Leftward shift (AD decreases) |
|---|---|
| Lower interest rates | Higher interest rates |
| Rising house prices or share prices | A fall in asset prices |
| Rising consumer or business confidence | A collapse in confidence |
| Higher government spending or lower taxes | Fiscal consolidation |
| A depreciation of the exchange rate | An appreciation of the exchange rate |
| Faster growth in trading partners | A recession abroad |

On a diagram, show the original curve AD1 and the new curve AD2, mark the change in real output at each price level, and say which component caused the shift. The effect on actual output and prices depends on aggregate supply, which comes in the next subtopics.

## 5. Evaluating the link between income and consumption

The simplest view is that consumption depends on current income: when income rises, households spend part of the rise and save the rest. This can be written as a **consumption function**, for example:

**C = 30 + 0.8Y**

Here 30 is spending that happens even at zero income (financed by savings or borrowing), and 0.8 is the **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)**: each extra pound of income raises consumption by 80p.

**Worked example 2.**

| Income (Y) | Consumption (C) | Average propensity to consume (C / Y) |
|---|---|---|
| 200 | 190 | 0.95 |
| 400 | 350 | 0.875 |
| 600 | 510 | 0.85 |

As income rises, consumption rises by less than income, and the share of income consumed falls.

How reliable is the link? Evaluation should use these points:

- **Temporary versus permanent changes.** Households tend to smooth spending over time. A one-off bonus is largely saved; a permanent pay rise is largely spent. A temporary tax rebate may therefore raise consumption by much less than the same sum as a permanent tax cut.
- **Wealth.** Rising house prices can raise spending even when income has not changed.
- **Credit and interest rates.** Easy credit lets households spend more than their income; tight credit forces them to spend less.
- **Distribution.** Lower-income households spend a larger share of extra income than higher-income households, so who gains a rise in income matters.
- **Confidence and uncertainty.** Fear of job losses can raise precautionary saving even while income is unchanged.

So income is the main influence on consumption, but the size of its effect varies with the type of change and the circumstances.

## 6. The role of expectations

Expectations drive spending decisions today, because consumption and investment are choices about the future.

- **Consumer confidence.** Households expecting rising incomes and secure jobs borrow and spend more; those expecting unemployment save.
- **Business confidence.** Investment depends on expected future demand. Keynes called the waves of optimism and pessimism that drive it "animal spirits".
- **Expected inflation.** If households expect prices to rise, they may bring purchases forward. If they expect prices to fall, they may delay purchases.
- **Expected policy.** Expected tax rises or interest rate rises can reduce spending before the change happens.

Expectations can be **self-fulfilling**. If firms expect a recession, they cut investment and hiring, which reduces incomes and AD and helps bring the recession about. This is one reason why policymakers try to influence confidence through clear communication.

When evaluating, note that expectations are hard to measure (surveys of confidence are only a guide), can change suddenly, and can make AD move even when interest rates and incomes have not changed.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling the axes "price" and "quantity" instead of "price level" and "real output".
- Shifting AD because the price level changed.
- Including transfer payments in G.
- Assuming that a rise in income raises consumption by the full amount.
- Forgetting that imports are subtracted, so a rise in imports reduces AD.
- Calling investment "buying shares": in economics, investment means spending on capital goods.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a shift as a chain that names the component: "A cut in interest rates lowers the cost of borrowing, so firms invest more, I rises, and AD shifts right."
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** draw AD1 and AD2, label the axes and arrows, and refer to the diagram in the text.
- **Evaluate** the income-consumption link by asking whether the income change is permanent, who receives it, and what is happening to wealth, credit and confidence.
- **Evaluate** expectations by weighing their power to move AD against the difficulty of measuring or controlling them.
