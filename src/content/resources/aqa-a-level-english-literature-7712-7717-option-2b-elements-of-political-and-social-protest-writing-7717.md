**Option 2B Elements of Political and Social Protest Writing** is the second option for Paper 2
Texts and Genres in AQA A-level English Literature B (7717). The
[Paper 2 Texts and Genres overview](/resources/a-level-aqa-english-literature-b-texts-and-genres/)
covers the paper's structure and rules. This guide explains what AQA means by protest "elements",
shows how they work in each set text, and models the unseen and connecting essays.

## What makes a protest text

AQA acknowledges that "it could be claimed that all texts are political", so it defines this option
more narrowly: the set texts "have issues of power and powerlessness at their core, with political
and social protest issues central to each text's structure". They "foreground oppression and
domination", cover "both public and private settings", and "look at the cultures we live in and have
lived in over time". As with crime, the key word is **elements**: you ask which features of protest
writing each text uses.

## The set texts

You study three: **one post-2000 prose text, one poetry text and one further text, one of which must
be written pre-1900**.

| Text | Author | Requirement it can meet |
|---|---|---|
| *Harvest* | Jim Crace | Post-2000 prose |
| *Brotherless Night* | V. V. Ganeshananthan | Post-2000 prose |
| *The Kite Runner* | Khaled Hosseini | Post-2000 prose |
| *Songs of Innocence and of Experience* | William Blake | Poetry, pre-1900 |
| *Selected Poems*: "V", "National Trust", "Them and [uz]", "Divisions", "Working", "Marked with D" | Tony Harrison | Poetry |
| *Hard Times* | Charles Dickens | Further text, pre-1900 |
| *A Doll's House* (Bloomsbury Methuen Drama edition, translated by Michael Meyer) | Henrik Ibsen | Further text, pre-1900 |
| *Henry IV Part I* | William Shakespeare | Further text, pre-1900 |
| *The Handmaid's Tale* | Margaret Atwood | Further text |

AQA treats the Meyer translation of *A Doll's House* as Ibsen's own words for assessment, and that
edition must be used. The paper is open book with clean, unannotated copies.

## The elements, grouped

**The type of text.** "Post-modern novel, science fiction, satirical poetry, historical and
political drama." *The Handmaid's Tale* is speculative fiction framed by a future academic
lecture; *Henry IV Part I* is a history play; Harrison writes satire and elegy in strict verse
forms.

**Settings for power struggles**, "real and imagined", in place and time: the theocratic state of
Gilead; Coketown's factories and schoolroom; an English village facing enclosure in *Harvest*;
Kabul before and after invasion in *The Kite Runner*; Jaffna during the Sri Lankan civil war in
*Brotherless Night*; the Helmers' living room.

**The nature of the struggle.** "The behaviours of those with power and those without";
"the pursuit of power itself, rebellion against those with power, warfare"; "the workings of the
ruling political classes"; "corruption, conspiracy, control". Hotspur's rebellion against King
Henry, Gradgrind's and Bounderby's control of minds and labour, the Commanders' regime in Gilead.

**The smaller world and the larger.** "The connection of the smaller world to the larger world"
and "human organisation: domestically, in the work place, in local and national governments". *A
Doll's House* shows the politics of a marriage; Blake's "The Chimney Sweeper" poems link one child
to a society's cruelty; "Marked with D" makes a father's working life and death a question of class.

**Gender politics and social class.** Nora's discovery that she has been a doll-wife; Offred's
reduction to a reproductive function; Harrison's "Them and [uz]", where accent becomes a battle
over who is allowed to speak poetry; Hassan's position as a Hazara servant in *The Kite Runner*.

**Structure and language.** "How political tensions are heightened and perhaps resolved", and "the
way that language is used in the worlds that are created". Gilead's renamed people and places,
Gradgrind's insistence on facts, Blake's paired contrary poems, the final door in *A Doll's House*.

**Comment and audience.** Protest writing comments on society "at particular historical periods"
and invites readers to reflect "on our own world".

## The unseen passage

Section A prints an unseen extract from the protest genre: poetry, prose or drama, including
literary non-fiction. Identify who has power and who lacks it; how the setting stages the struggle;
which language and methods construct the oppression or resistance; and whether the extract
resolves the tension. Then turn the notes into one argument about significance.

**Worked example on an original prose extract.** The passage below is **original**, written for
this guide.

> The new rules went up on the mill gate on a Monday, which Mr Haldane said was a kindness, since it
> gave the women the whole week to learn them. No talking at the looms. No children in the yard.
> Wages to be paid in tokens, redeemable at the company shop. Underneath, someone had written in
> chalk, *We are not looms*, and by Tuesday it had been scrubbed off, and by Wednesday it was back,
> in three different hands.

**Model reading:** The extract presents power through the language of order and generosity. Mr
Haldane's claim that a Monday notice is "a kindness" exposes paternalism as control, and the
clipped, verbless rules ("No talking at the looms") reproduce the voice of authority without a
speaker, as if the gate itself commanded. Payment in tokens for the company shop shows economic
control reaching into private life, the smaller world of the household bound to the larger world
of the mill. Resistance appears in a different register: the chalked *We are not looms* refuses
the reduction of workers to machines, and its italic form sets a human voice against the printed
rules. The structure of the final sentence, moving from Monday to Wednesday, enacts a cycle of
repression and protest, and the "three different hands" turn one act of defiance into collective
action. The extract does not resolve the struggle; its significance is that protest survives
erasure.

## Set-text and connecting essays

**Section B** is an essay on one set text; **Section C** connects two texts you have not used in
Section B.

**Section C plan (original question shape):** "Protest writing is more interested in the powerful
than the powerless." Discuss with reference to two texts. Using *A Doll's House* and *The
Handmaid's Tale*: both are told from or centred on a woman within a system controlled by men, so
the powerless are the focus; yet Torvald and the Commander are carefully drawn, and both texts
expose how power depends on the cooperation of the powerless. Counter-view: Atwood's Historical
Notes, in which a male academic discusses Offred's account, shift attention back to institutions
and interpreters. Contexts: nineteenth-century marriage law; the late twentieth-century politics
Atwood was responding to. Judgement: both texts examine power through the experience of those
without it, but end by questioning who controls the story.

## Common mistakes

- **Reducing protest to a message**: analyse how the text constructs power, not only what it
  condemns.
- **Ignoring private settings**: AQA includes domestic and workplace power.
- **Generalised context**: tie history to specific words and scenes.
- **Unseen answers that list techniques** without a reading.
- **Reusing the Section B text in Section C.**

## Exam technique

Allow about an hour for each answer in the 3-hour paper. Use the open book for short, precise
quotations. The five assessment objectives apply: response and terminology (AO1), methods (AO2),
contexts (AO3), connections (AO4) and interpretations (AO5).
