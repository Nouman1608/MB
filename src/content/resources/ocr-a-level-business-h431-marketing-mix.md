## The marketing mix

The **marketing mix** is the combination of decisions a business makes about how to market a product. The traditional four elements are the **four Ps**:

- **Product:** what is sold, including quality, design, features, brand and packaging.
- **Price:** what the customer pays, including discounts and credit terms.
- **Place:** how the product reaches the customer, through distribution channels, stores and online sales.
- **Promotion:** how customers are informed and persuaded, through advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, public relations and digital media.

The elements work together. A luxury product with a low price and discount-store distribution gives a confusing message. A good mix is **consistent**: all four elements point to the same target customer and the same image. Services use three more Ps, covered in the [service marketing study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-service-marketing/).

## Strategy and mix

A **marketing strategy** is the long-term plan for achieving marketing objectives. It sets out which markets and segments to target, how to position the product against rivals, and which resources to commit. The **marketing mix** is the set of tools used to carry out the strategy.

| | Marketing strategy | Marketing mix |
|---|---|---|
| Question answered | Who do we target, and how will we compete? | What exactly do we offer, at what price, where and with what message? |
| Time scale | Long term (usually years) | Can be adjusted quickly (prices and promotions can change within weeks) |
| Level | Broad direction | Detailed tactics |
| Example | Position as a premium organic brand for health-conscious families | A £4.50 pack, sold in health shops and online, promoted on social media |

The mix must fit the strategy. If the strategy is premium positioning, discount pricing would undermine it.

## Three contexts

| Context | Meaning | Typical features |
|---|---|---|
| **Local** | Customers in one town or area | Few competitors known personally; word of mouth matters; personal service |
| **National** | Customers across a country | Wider distribution; mass media; brand matters; larger investment |
| **International/global** | Customers in several countries | Different cultures, languages, laws, incomes and exchange rates; transport and tariffs add cost |

A business may grow through these contexts or may be in more than one at once.

### Worked example 1: one product, three mixes

Bramble & Co makes handmade fruit preserves. The table shows how an appropriate mix could differ.

| | Local | National | International/global |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product | Fresh batches, local fruit, personal labels | Standard range with consistent quality and shelf life | Adapted flavours and labels in the local language; compliance with food rules |
| Price | Premium; cash and market-stall flexibility | Competitive against known brands; supermarket pricing | Set after counting transport, tariffs and distributor margins |
| Place | Farmers' markets, local shops, own café | Supermarkets and an online shop with national delivery | Importers and distributors; online sales to selected countries |
| Promotion | Word of mouth, local press, tastings | National advertising, social media, in-store promotions | Local-language digital advertising; trade fairs; local partners |

**What this shows.** The product has not changed, but the best mix does. At the local level Bramble can charge a premium because customers value the story; nationally it faces strong rivals and must meet supermarket expectations on price and supply; internationally it must change its product and labels to fit regulations and tastes.

## Worked example 2: price in a foreign market

A key reason the mix must be adapted internationally is the cost added between factory and shelf. Bramble sells a gift hamper that costs **£22.00** ex-factory.

**UK retail price.** The retailer adds a 40% mark-up, and VAT of 20% is added.

1. £22.00 × 1.40 = £30.80.
2. £30.80 × 1.20 = **£36.96**.

**Selling to an overseas market through a distributor.** Shipping is £3.00 per hamper and an import tariff of 8% applies to the cost plus shipping. The distributor adds 30%, the retailer 40%, and local sales tax is 20%.

1. Cost plus shipping = £22.00 + £3.00 = £25.00.
2. Tariff = 8% × £25.00 = £2.00. Landed cost = **£27.00**.
3. Distributor margin: £27.00 × 1.30 = £35.10.
4. Retailer mark-up: £35.10 × 1.40 = £49.14.
5. Sales tax: £49.14 × 1.20 = **£58.97**.

The shelf price abroad is £58.97 against £36.96 at home. That is £22.01 more, or about 60% higher (58.97 ÷ 36.96 = 1.595). Bramble gets the same £22.00 from each hamper, but the extra layers of cost make the product much more expensive for the foreign customer.

**Adapting the place element.** Suppose Bramble sells directly online instead, adding a 25% margin to the landed cost and then 20% sales tax:

1. £27.00 × 1.25 = £33.75.
2. £33.75 × 1.20 = **£40.50**.

This is £18.47 less than the distributor route (58.97 − 40.50) and only 9.6% above the UK price. The change in place has changed the price the customer pays, which shows how the elements of the mix are linked. Direct selling has its own costs, however: delivery problems, customer service in another language, and returns.

## Evaluating the choice

To evaluate an appropriate mix, ask:

- **Who is the target customer** in that context, and what do they value?
- **How much can the business afford?** National television is out of reach for a small firm.
- **What are competitors doing?** A mix that copies a rival's gives customers no reason to switch.
- **Is it consistent** with the brand and the objectives?
- **Standardise or adapt?** A standard global mix saves cost and keeps the brand image the same. An adapted mix fits local tastes and laws but raises cost and complexity.
- **Can the business control it?** Distributors and retailers in other countries may not follow the business's plan.

| Standardised mix | Adapted mix |
|---|---|
| Lower costs from large-scale production and one campaign | Fits local tastes, laws and incomes |
| Consistent global brand | Higher cost; more complex to manage |
| Risk: cultural mistakes, rules not met | Risk: diluted brand image |

A balanced judgement usually favours **standardising the core brand and adapting the details** (language, price, flavours, channel) to each market.

## Keeping the mix up to date

The mix is not fixed. Businesses revisit it as conditions change:

- **Product life cycle:** promotion is heavy at launch and lighter in maturity, and price may fall in decline.
- **Competitors:** a rival's price cut or new product may force a response in the price or product element.
- **Customers:** changing tastes, income or technology (such as growth in online shopping) alter the best channel and message.
- **Costs:** a rise in transport or material costs may force a price change or a cheaper pack size.

Because elements are linked, a change in one needs a check on the others. If Bramble raised its hamper price by 15%, it should ask whether the packaging and the promotion still support a higher price. A review each quarter, using sales data and customer feedback, helps to keep the mix consistent with the strategy.

## Common mistakes

- Listing the four Ps without applying them to the context in the case.
- Treating strategy and mix as the same thing.
- Assuming the best mix is the same for every country.
- Ignoring costs between the factory and the customer when pricing abroad.
- Judging one element in isolation, such as price, without checking that it fits the other three.

## Exam technique

Define the term, apply it to the case, and give a judgement. For "evaluate an appropriate mix", select the context, describe the mix for each of the four elements, explain why it suits that context, and state a risk.
