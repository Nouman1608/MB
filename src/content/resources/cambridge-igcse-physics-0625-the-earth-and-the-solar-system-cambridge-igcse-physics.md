This guide covers one subtopic of the Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus (for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028): **6.1 The Earth and the Solar System**, in two parts: the Earth (6.1.1) and the Solar System (6.1.2). Core content is for every candidate; content marked **Extended only** is Supplement material, examined on Papers 2 and 4. The [Space physics study guide](/resources/igcse-physics-0625-space-physics/) covers all of Topic 6 with worked examples for the Earth's orbital speed and the Sun's light; this page explains the observations in more depth and gives new calculations and data-handling practice.

## Three motions, three cycles

The key to 6.1.1 is matching each **observation** to the right **motion**:

| Motion | Time | Explains |
|---|---|---|
| Earth **rotates** on its axis | about 24 hours | Day and night; the Sun's apparent daily path |
| Earth **orbits** the Sun (axis tilted) | about 365 days | The seasons |
| Moon **orbits** the Earth | about one month | The Moon's cycle of phases |

**Apparent daily motion of the Sun.** The Earth rotates from west to east, so the Sun appears to move the other way across the sky: it **rises in the east**, climbs to its highest point around midday and **sets in the west**. The Sun is not moving round the Earth; we are turning beneath it. Day is when your side of the Earth faces the Sun; night is when it faces away.

**Seasons.** The Earth's axis is **tilted** and stays pointing in the same direction in space as the Earth orbits. For part of the year the northern hemisphere is tilted **towards** the Sun: the Sun climbs higher in the sky, days are longer than nights, and sunlight strikes the ground more directly, delivering more energy to each square metre. That is summer in the north. Half an orbit later the north is tilted away: winter. The southern hemisphere has the opposite season at the same time, which is strong evidence that distance from the Sun is **not** the cause.

**Phases of the Moon.** The Moon does not produce light; it reflects sunlight. One half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun. As the Moon orbits the Earth, we see **different amounts of the lit half**: none at new Moon (the Moon lies roughly between Earth and Sun), half the disc at first quarter, all of it at full Moon (the Earth lies roughly between Sun and Moon), then decreasing again. The full cycle takes about one month.

## Average orbital speed (Extended only)

For a roughly circular orbit, the distance travelled in one orbit is the circumference, 2πr, so:

**v = 2πr / T**, where r is the average orbital radius and T is the orbital period.

### Worked example 1 (Extended)

The Moon orbits the Earth at an average radius of 3.8 × 10⁸ m with a period of 27.3 days. Calculate its average orbital speed.

```
T = 27.3 × 24 × 3600 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s
v = 2πr / T = (2π × 3.8 × 10⁸) / 2.36 × 10⁶
v = 1.0 × 10³ m/s   (about 1 km/s)
```

The commonest error is leaving T in days, which gives an answer in metres per day. Convert first: 1 day = 86 400 s.

## Members of the Solar System

The Solar System contains:

- **one star**, the Sun;
- **eight planets**, in order from the Sun: **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune**;
- **minor planets** that orbit the Sun, including **dwarf planets** such as Pluto and **asteroids** in the asteroid belt (between Mars and Jupiter);
- **moons**, which orbit planets;
- smaller bodies, including **comets** and natural satellites.

The first four are small and **rocky**; the outer four are large and **gaseous**.

## The accretion model

The syllabus asks you to explain the rocky–gaseous difference using an accretion model with three named features:

1. **Gravity:** an interstellar cloud of gas and dust collapsed under its own gravitational attraction.
2. **Many elements:** the cloud contained many elements, not just hydrogen and helium.
3. **Rotation and an accretion disc:** the cloud was rotating, so it flattened into a spinning disc of material around the young Sun.

Near the Sun the disc was **hot**, so light gases could not remain; only rocky and metallic material came together, forming small, rocky planets. Further out it was **cold**, so gases (and ices) were plentiful; large cores formed and their strong gravity attracted huge amounts of gas, forming the gas giants.

## Gravity in the Solar System

- The gravitational field strength **at the surface of a planet depends on its mass**: more massive planets have a stronger surface field.
- The field strength **around a planet decreases as the distance from it increases**.
- The **Sun contains most of the mass** of the Solar System, which is why the planets orbit the Sun.
- The force that keeps an object in orbit around the Sun is the **gravitational attraction of the Sun**.

**Extended only.** The strength of the Sun's gravitational field **decreases with distance**, and so the **orbital speeds of the planets decrease** as their distance from the Sun increases. Outer planets move more slowly **and** have further to go, so their periods are much longer.

### Worked example 2 (Extended)

Jupiter orbits at an average radius of 7.8 × 10¹¹ m. Calculate (a) how long sunlight takes to reach Jupiter and (b) its orbital speed, given a period of 12 years. Speed of light = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

```
(a) t = d / v = 7.8 × 10¹¹ / 3.0 × 10⁸ = 2600 s  (about 43 minutes)
(b) T = 12 × 365 × 86 400 = 3.78 × 10⁸ s
    v = 2π × 7.8 × 10¹¹ / 3.78 × 10⁸ = 1.3 × 10⁴ m/s
```

Part (a) is Core (light travel time); part (b) is Extended. Compare (b) with the Earth's orbital speed of about 3 × 10⁴ m/s: the further planet is slower, as the Supplement statement predicts.

## Elliptical orbits (Extended only)

Planets, minor planets and comets have **elliptical** orbits. The Sun is **not at the centre** of an elliptical orbit, except when the orbit is approximately circular (as most planetary orbits nearly are). Comets have very stretched ellipses.

An object in an elliptical orbit travels **faster when closer to the Sun**. Explain this with **conservation of energy**: as it falls inwards towards the Sun, it loses gravitational potential energy and gains the same amount of kinetic energy, so it speeds up. As it moves away again, kinetic energy is converted back to gravitational potential energy and it slows down. Its total energy stays constant.

## Analysing planetary data (Extended only)

You may be given a table of orbital distance, orbital duration, density, surface temperature and surface gravitational field strength. Useful patterns to look for:

- Larger orbital distance → longer orbital period (and lower orbital speed).
- High density (about 4–6 g/cm³) → rocky planet; low density (around 1 g/cm³ or below) → gaseous planet.
- Surface temperature generally falls with distance from the Sun.
- Surface field strength depends on mass, so a large gas giant can have a high value even with low density.

Always **quote numbers from the table** to support each statement, and state the trend in full ("as orbital distance increases, the period increases").

## Common mistakes

- Explaining seasons by the Earth being closer to the Sun in summer.
- Confusing rotation (day and night) with orbit (seasons).
- Explaining phases by the Earth's shadow (that is an eclipse).
- Getting the planet order wrong, or calling Pluto a planet.
- Leaving days or years unconverted in v = 2πr / T.
- Saying the Sun sits at the centre of a comet's orbit.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** day and night or seasons: name the motion **and** the tilt where relevant.
- **Describe** the accretion model: use all three syllabus features (gravity, many elements, rotating disc), then hot near the Sun, cold far away.
- **Calculate:** convert to seconds and metres first; give the unit.
- **Use the data:** quote values and describe trends with both variables named.
- **Explain** speed changes in an ellipse: name both energy stores and say energy is conserved.
