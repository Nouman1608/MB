The 9675 specification lists "place as a political space" among the ideas that "can usefully be
explored" in Unit 2, Place in Literary Texts, next to "the representation of social identity and
how people are placed in society". This guide develops that one angle for the Section A prose
essay. The task, the six novels and open-book technique are covered in the
[Unit 2 Section A study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-2-section-a/);
here you learn how to read a fictional place as a site of power.

## What makes a place political

A place becomes political when the novel shows **who controls it, who belongs in it and who is kept
out**. Five questions open almost any setting:

1. **Ownership.** Who owns the land, house or building, and how was it acquired?
2. **Authority.** Which institutions are visible in the place: police, company, army, landlord,
   embassy, council?
3. **Borders and thresholds.** Where are the doors, gates, checkpoints, queues and frontiers, and who
   is allowed through?
4. **Naming and mapping.** Who gives the place its name, draws its map or writes its history?
5. **Scale.** How does a small space (a room, a street, a building) stand for a national or imperial
   order?

Political does not mean only governments and elections. In Unit 2 it includes empire, migration law,
property development, class hierarchy inside a house and the politics of race in a city.

## Methods that make place political

- **Juxtaposition of spaces.** Two places side by side expose an inequality: a mansion beside a slum,
  a European city beside a colonial station.
- **Institutional detail.** A form, a company name, a uniform or an official letter brings the state
  or the market into a private scene.
- **Movement and restriction.** Journeys, deportations, refused entry and enforced waiting show
  power acting on bodies.
- **Narrative viewpoint.** A narrator who belongs to the powerful group may not see the politics of
  the place, which gives the reader ironic distance.
- **Symbolic objects.** Maps, flags, keys and title deeds turn power into something the reader can
  see.

## The six novels as political places

**Heart of Darkness.** Marlow's journey for a trading company links a European city he describes in
terms of death and whiteness with the stations along the river, where he sees a chained gang of
workers and men dying in a grove. A map in the Company office, coloured by empire, makes the
continent a possession. Kurtz's report for a society for suppressing "savage customs" ends in a
scrawled call to exterminate, so the language of civilising is exposed as violence. Chinua Achebe's
1975 lecture "An Image of Africa" argued that the novella itself denies Africans a voice and uses
Africa as a backdrop; weighing that reading against Conrad's critique of empire is central AO1
territory.

**The Great Gatsby.** The novel's politics lie mostly in class and race rather than government.
Tom Buchanan's enthusiasm for a book warning that the "white race" will be swamped shows East Egg's
old money defending its position in political terms, and the Wilsons' garage under the eyes of the
billboard shows where the cost of that order falls.

**Americanah.** University strikes under military rule help push Ifemelu out of Lagos; in America
she discovers that she has become "black", a political identity she did not have at home, and her
blog maps race onto the places she lives. Obinze's life in London as an undocumented worker, ending
in deportation, makes Britain a place defined by papers and borders.

**Small Island.** Set mainly in 1948, the novel tests the idea of Britain as the "mother country".
Hortense finds her Jamaican teaching qualification refused; Gilbert, who served in the RAF, cannot
find work or lodging; Queenie's house in Earls Court becomes a contested space when neighbours
object to her tenants. The wartime cinema scene, in which American military police try to enforce
segregation in a British town, brings an imported colour line into a local space and ends in
violence.

**Last Man in Tower.** A developer offers the residents of Vishram Society in Mumbai far more than
their flats are worth, provided all agree to leave. Masterji's refusal turns one building into a
miniature state with factions, pressure and finally a collective murder. The novel presents property
development in a growing city as politics conducted through money rather than law.

**The Remains of the Day.** Darlington Hall is a house where international politics was conducted in
the 1920s and 1930s, including Lord Darlington's meetings with Nazi German representatives. Stevens
serves the dinners without questioning them and carries out his employer's order to dismiss two
Jewish maids. His narration presents these events as matters of professional duty, so the reader
must see the politics he will not. The villagers at Moscombe, debating democracy and dignity, offer
a different idea of who should have a say.

## Building an argument

A strong essay on this angle does three things.

- **Shows how the place encodes power**, through specific methods, rather than stating that it does.
- **Connects small and large scale**, showing how a room or building stands for a national, imperial
  or economic order.
- **Weighs readings.** For example, whether a novel critiques the power it depicts or partly shares
  its viewpoint; or whether characters resist the political order or are defeated by it.

Because Unit 2 is open book, you can check the precise wording of the two or three passages you plan
to use; choose them before the exam so that time goes on analysis.

## Worked example on an original passage

The passage is **original**, written for this guide. It is not from any set text.

> The new fence went up in a single morning. By noon the path to the river, which the children had
> used for longer than anyone could remember, ended in green mesh and a sign that said PRIVATE
> PROPERTY, with a company name nobody in the village could pronounce. Old Musa walked to the fence,
> put his hand flat against it as if testing a wall in his own house, and walked home the long way.
> That evening the children played in the road.

**Model paragraph:**

> The passage turns a familiar landscape into a political space by showing ownership arriving
> overnight. The contrast between the path the children used "for longer than anyone could remember"
> and a fence built "in a single morning" sets the slow time of custom against the speed of legal
> power. The capitalised sign and the unpronounceable company name bring an outside institution into
> the village, and its foreignness suggests that the decision was made elsewhere. Musa's gesture,
> testing the fence "as if testing a wall in his own house", shows him measuring how much of home is
> left; his silent walk "the long way" is the only resistance offered, and it is a form of
> accepting the new order. The final sentence moves the children into "the road", a more dangerous
> public space, so the cost of the change falls on those with least power. Place here is not a
> backdrop but the record of who decides.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a history essay.** Context should explain how a place is presented, not replace analysis.
- **Ignoring viewpoint.** A narrator's blindness to politics is often the key method.
- **Treating politics only as governments.** Ownership, class, race and migration law all count.
- **Losing place.** Every paragraph should be anchored in a specific location and how it is written.

## Exam technique

Questions often ask you to **explore the significance** of an aspect of place, or to debate a view.
For this angle, test any claim with a small space and a large one: for example, a room and a city,
or a house and an empire. Unit 2 lasts 2 hours for two essays, so give Section A about an hour.
