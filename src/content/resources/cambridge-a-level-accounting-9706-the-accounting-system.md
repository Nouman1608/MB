This guide covers **1.2 The accounting system**, from Topic 1, Financial accounting, for Cambridge International AS & A Level Accounting 9706, syllabus for 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content, examined in Paper 1 (multiple choice) and Paper 2 (Fundamentals of Accounting), and the double entry it teaches is assumed in every later topic. The [business entities and accounting system guide](/resources/business-entities-and-the-accounting-system/) lists the books and concepts; this guide works a full set of transactions from source to trial balance and shows each concept applied to a decision. Afterwards, use the [accounting system revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-9706-the-accounting-system-revision-notes/) and the [accounting system practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-9706-the-accounting-system-practice/). All names and amounts are invented, in dollars.

## What the syllabus asks for

Section 1.2.1 asks candidates to understand:

- the principles of the double entry system to record business transactions
- the accounting equation
- the role of books of prime entry: sales journal, sales returns journal, purchases journal, purchases returns journal, cash book, general journal
- preparation of ledger accounts
- the purpose of a trial balance
- the advantages and disadvantages of maintaining full accounting records
- the accounting concepts: business entity, historic cost, money measurement, going concern, consistency, prudence, realisation, duality, materiality, objectivity, matching/accruals and substance over form
- the use of computerised accounting systems, their advantages and disadvantages, and how the security of data can be ensured

Candidates are expected to use this understanding "to evaluate relevant information and make informed business decisions". Knowledge of specific applications or software is not required.

## Double entry and the accounting equation

Every transaction has two equal effects (**duality**), so each is recorded as a **debit** in one account and a **credit** in another, for the same amount.

| Account type | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|
| Asset | Debit | Credit |
| Expense (including purchases) | Debit | Credit |
| Drawings | Debit | Credit |
| Liability | Credit | Debit |
| Capital | Credit | Debit |
| Income (including sales) | Credit | Debit |

The **accounting equation** is

```
assets = liabilities + capital
```

Profit increases capital and drawings reduce it, so during a period:

```
assets = liabilities + opening capital + income − expenses − drawings
```

Because each double entry has equal debits and credits, the equation always stays in balance.

## From source document to trial balance

```
source documents → books of prime entry → ledger accounts → trial balance → financial statements
```

**Books of prime entry** list similar transactions together before posting, so the ledger receives totals rather than hundreds of single entries, and each entry can be traced back to a document.

| Book | Records | Source document |
|---|---|---|
| Sales journal | Credit sales of goods | Copy of sales invoice |
| Sales returns journal | Goods returned by credit customers | Copy of credit note issued |
| Purchases journal | Credit purchases of goods for resale | Purchase invoice received |
| Purchases returns journal | Goods returned to credit suppliers | Credit note received |
| Cash book | All cash and bank receipts and payments | Receipts, cheque counterfoils, bank records |
| General journal | Anything else: opening entries, credit purchases of non-current assets, corrections, year-end adjustments | Internal memos and agreements |

The cash book is both a book of prime entry and a ledger account, so entries in it count as one side of the double entry.

## Worked example: Amira's first month

Amira starts a trading business. In March:

1. Pays $10 000 of her own money into a business bank account.
2. Buys equipment for $3 000, paying from the bank.
3. Buys goods on credit from Lee for $2 400.
4. Sells goods on credit to Kim for $1 800.
5. Sells goods for $900, paid into the bank.
6. Returns goods costing $200 to Lee.
7. Pays Lee $2 200 from the bank.
8. Receives $1 000 from Kim into the bank.

**Books of prime entry.** Items 1, 2, 5, 7 and 8: cash book. Item 3: purchases journal. Item 4: sales journal. Item 6: purchases returns journal.

**Ledger accounts** (Dr = debit, Cr = credit):

```
Bank
Dr  Capital            10 000 | Cr  Equipment           3 000
Dr  Sales                 900 | Cr  Lee                 2 200
Dr  Kim                 1 000 | Cr  Balance c/d         6 700
                       11 900 |                        11 900
Dr  Balance b/d         6 700 |

Lee (payable)
Dr  Purchases returns     200 | Cr  Purchases           2 400
Dr  Bank                2 200 |
                        2 400 |                         2 400

Kim (receivable)
Dr  Sales               1 800 | Cr  Bank                1 000
                              | Cr  Balance c/d           800
                        1 800 |                         1 800
Dr  Balance b/d           800 |

Capital: Cr 10 000     Equipment: Dr 3 000     Purchases: Dr 2 400
Sales: Cr 1 800 (Kim) + 900 (bank) = 2 700     Purchases returns: Cr 200
```

**Balancing an account.** Add both sides; enter the difference on the smaller side as "balance c/d" so the totals agree; bring it down on the other side as "balance b/d". Lee's account has equal sides, so it is closed with no balance.

**Trial balance at 31 March:**

| Account | Debit $ | Credit $ |
|---|---|---|
| Bank | 6 700 | |
| Equipment | 3 000 | |
| Purchases | 2 400 | |
| Kim | 800 | |
| Capital | | 10 000 |
| Sales | | 2 700 |
| Purchases returns | | 200 |
| **Total** | **12 900** | **12 900** |

**Equation check.** Assets (6 700 + 3 000 + 800) = $10 500. Capital plus income less expenses = 10 000 + 2 700 + 200 − 2 400 = $10 500, with no liabilities left because Lee has been paid.

## The purpose of a trial balance

- It checks the arithmetic accuracy of the double entry: if debits do not equal credits, there is an error.
- It provides a summary of balances from which the financial statements are prepared.

A trial balance that agrees does not prove the records are correct. Errors that do not affect the totals, and their correction, are part of 1.4 Reconciliation and verification, covered in the [reconciliation and verification guide](/resources/accounting-reconciliation-and-verification/).

## Full accounting records: worth the cost?

**Advantages**: profit can be calculated accurately; the owner knows what customers owe and what is owed to suppliers; errors and fraud are easier to detect; reliable figures support loan applications and tax returns; managers have information for decisions such as pricing or extending credit.

**Disadvantages**: keeping full double entry takes time and skill, or the cost of a bookkeeper or software; for a very small cash business the benefit may be less than the cost.

## Accounting concepts applied

| Concept | Applied to a decision |
|---|---|
| Business entity | The owner's private car is not recorded as a business asset; business cash used privately is drawings |
| Historic cost | Land bought for $50 000 stays at $50 000 in the records under this concept, even if it is now worth more |
| Money measurement | The skill of the workforce is not recorded, because it cannot be measured reliably in money |
| Going concern | Assets are shown at cost less depreciation, not at the lower amount they would fetch if the business closed |
| Consistency | Once a depreciation method is chosen, it is used every year so profits are comparable |
| Prudence | Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value; an expected loss is recognised, an expected gain is not |
| Realisation | Revenue is recognised when goods pass to the customer and the customer accepts liability to pay, not when an order is received |
| Duality | Every transaction affects two accounts; the basis of double entry |
| Materiality | A $15 stapler is treated as an expense, not a non-current asset, because the amount is too small to affect decisions |
| Objectivity | Figures are based on verifiable evidence such as invoices, not the owner's opinion |
| Matching/accruals | Rent for December, unpaid at the year end, is still charged as an expense of that year |
| Substance over form | An asset bought on instalments is recorded as an asset now, though legal ownership passes only at the final payment |

Concepts can pull in different directions. Prudence favours caution, while the accruals concept requires expenses and income to be matched to the period. Questions often ask you to identify a concept from a situation; name it and say how it applies.

## Computerised accounting systems

Computerised systems apply the same double entry: one input posts both sides automatically, and ledgers and the trial balance are produced instantly. **Advantages** include speed, fewer arithmetic errors and up-to-date reports. **Disadvantages** include set-up, software and training costs, and the risk of data loss, system failure or unauthorised access. **Security** measures include passwords and access levels for different staff, regular backups stored separately, firewalls and anti-virus software, and audit trails showing who changed what. The A Level process of transferring to a computerised system is covered in the [computerised accounting systems guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-computerised-accounting-systems/).

## Common mistakes

- Recording cash sales in the sales journal: they go in the cash book.
- Recording a credit purchase of equipment in the purchases journal: it goes in the general journal.
- Debiting the supplier's account for a credit purchase.
- Bringing a balance down on the wrong side.
- Saying a trial balance proves the accounts are correct.
- Naming a concept without explaining how it applies.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare** ledger accounts: date or number, the name of the other account, the amount; balance off where asked.
- **Identify** a concept: name it precisely from the syllabus list.
- **Explain**: link the concept or book to the transaction.
- **Evaluate** or **advise** on keeping full records or computerising: give both sides and a justified decision for the business described.
