## 5.2.3(a): oxidising and reducing agents

An **oxidising agent** takes electrons from another species. It is reduced, so its oxidation number falls. A **reducing agent** gives electrons to another species. It is oxidised, so its oxidation number rises.

### Worked example 1

Identify the agents in 2Fe³⁺(aq) + Sn²⁺(aq) → 2Fe²⁺(aq) + Sn⁴⁺(aq).

Iron goes from +3 to +2 (reduced); tin goes from +2 to +4 (oxidised). So **Fe³⁺ is the oxidising agent and Sn²⁺ is the reducing agent**.

## 5.2.3(b): building redox equations

### Method 1: half-equations

1. Balance the changing atom, then O with H₂O, then H with H⁺.
2. Balance charge with electrons.
3. Equalise electrons, add, and cancel species on both sides.

### Worked example 2: manganate(VII) and hydrogen peroxide

Reduction: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O
Oxidation: H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻

Match ten electrons (×2 and ×5), add, and cancel 10H⁺: **2MnO₄⁻ + 6H⁺ + 5H₂O₂ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5O₂ + 8H₂O**. Check: O is 18 on each side; charge is +4 on each side.

### Method 2: oxidation numbers

Make the total increase equal the total decrease.

### Worked example 3: iodate(V) and sulfite

IO₃⁻ oxidises SO₃²⁻ to sulfate and is reduced to iodide.

- I: +5 → −1, a decrease of 6.
- S: +4 → +6, an increase of 2.
- Three S atoms balance one I: **IO₃⁻ + 3SO₃²⁻ → I⁻ + 3SO₄²⁻**.

Oxygen (12) and charge (−7) already balance.

## 5.2.3(c): interpreting and predicting electron transfer

Electrons lost by the reducing agent equal electrons gained by the oxidising agent, so you can predict an unfamiliar product.

### Worked example 4: how far does zinc reduce vanadium?

In acid, 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol of zinc reduces 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol of VO₂⁺ (vanadium +5). Assume vanadium gains every electron zinc loses.

1. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻, so electrons released = 2 × 3.00 × 10⁻³ = 6.00 × 10⁻³ mol.
2. Electrons per vanadium = 6.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2.00 × 10⁻³ = 3.
3. New oxidation number = +5 − 3 = **+2, so the product is V²⁺**.

## 5.2.3(d): redox titration techniques

### Manganate(VII) and iron(II)

- Put potassium manganate(VII) in the burette and the Fe²⁺ solution, measured by pipette, in the conical flask.
- Acidify the flask with excess dilute sulfuric acid. Do not use hydrochloric acid: manganate(VII) oxidises chloride ions (see worked example 9), so the titre would be too large.
- No indicator is added. Purple MnO₄⁻ is decolourised as it is reduced to almost colourless Mn²⁺. The end point is the **first permanent pale pink**.
- The dark solution hides the bottom of the meniscus, so read the top consistently.

### Iodine and thiosulfate

- The oxidising agent is added to **excess** potassium iodide, releasing yellow-brown iodine.
- Titrate with sodium thiosulfate: I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻.
- At pale yellow, add starch (blue-black). The end point is when the blue-black colour **just disappears**.
- Adding starch near the end gives a sharper end point than adding it at the start.

Average only concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³).

## 5.2.3(e): titration calculations

### Worked example 5 (structured, Fe²⁺/MnO₄⁻): iron tablets

Four iron tablets are dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid to give 250.0 cm³ of solution. Samples of 25.0 cm³ react with 0.00800 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Concordant titres: 20.85, 20.95 and 20.90 cm³. The ionic equation is MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O.

1. Mean titre = 20.90 cm³.
2. n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.00800 × 20.90 ÷ 1000 = 1.672 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
3. n(Fe²⁺) in 25.0 cm³ = 5 × 1.672 × 10⁻⁴ = 8.36 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
4. In 250.0 cm³: 8.36 × 10⁻³ mol, so per tablet = 2.09 × 10⁻³ mol.
5. Mass of iron per tablet = 2.09 × 10⁻³ × 55.8 = 0.117 g, about **117 mg**.

### Worked example 6 (structured, I₂/S₂O₃²⁻): standardising thiosulfate

0.535 g of potassium iodate(V), KIO₃ (M = 214.0 g mol⁻¹), is made up to 250.0 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ portion is added to excess acidified KI:

IO₃⁻ + 5I⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3I₂ + 3H₂O

The iodine needs a mean titre of 24.60 cm³ of sodium thiosulfate.

1. n(KIO₃) in 25.0 cm³ = 0.535 ÷ 214.0 ÷ 10 = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
2. IO₃⁻ gives 3I₂, which needs 6S₂O₃²⁻: ratio 1 : 6.
3. n(S₂O₃²⁻) = 6 × 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.50 × 10⁻³ mol.
4. Concentration = 1.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02460 = **0.0610 mol dm⁻³**.

### Worked example 7 (non-structured, non-familiar system)

10.0 cm³ of a hydrogen peroxide solution is diluted to 250.0 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ portion, acidified, needs a mean titre of 17.65 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Find the concentration of the original solution.

Plan first: equation (worked example 2 gives MnO₄⁻ : H₂O₂ = 2 : 5), moles, then dilution.

1. n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.0200 × 17.65 ÷ 1000 = 3.53 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
2. n(H₂O₂) in 25.0 cm³ = 3.53 × 10⁻⁴ × 5 ÷ 2 = 8.825 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
3. In 250.0 cm³, which all came from 10.0 cm³: 8.825 × 10⁻³ mol.
4. Original concentration = 8.825 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0100 = **0.883 mol dm⁻³**.

## 5.2.3(f): standard electrode potential

Only the voltage between two half-cells can be measured. The reference is the **standard hydrogen electrode**: platinum in 1 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq), with hydrogen gas at 100 kPa passed over it, at 298 K. Its potential is defined as 0.00 V.

The **standard electrode potential, E⦵**, of a half-cell is the e.m.f. measured when that half-cell is connected to the standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions: 298 K, 100 kPa and 1 mol dm⁻³ solutions. Half-equations are written as reductions, for example Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s), E⦵ = +0.80 V.

More positive E⦵: the left-hand species is a stronger oxidising agent. More negative E⦵: the right-hand species is a stronger reducing agent.

## 5.2.3(g): measuring cell potentials (PAG8)

Connect two half-cells with a salt bridge (filter paper soaked in saturated KNO₃(aq)) and a high-resistance voltmeter, so almost no current flows.

- **Metal with its ions**: clean the metal with emery paper and dip it in a 1 mol dm⁻³ solution of its ions, for example lead in Pb²⁺(aq).
- **Non-metal with its ions**: use platinum, for example chlorine at 100 kPa bubbled over Pt in 1 mol dm⁻³ Cl⁻(aq).
- **Ions of one element in two oxidation states**: inert platinum dips into a solution of both ions, for example Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺, each 1 mol dm⁻³ or equimolar.

The specification's example PAG8 activity investigates how concentration affects a cell's potential.

## 5.2.3(h): standard cell potential

E⦵cell = E⦵(positive electrode) − E⦵(negative electrode)

The half-cell with the more positive E⦵ is the positive electrode, where reduction happens.

### Worked example 8

Fe³⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺(aq), E⦵ = +0.77 V; Pb²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Pb(s), E⦵ = −0.13 V.

1. Positive electrode: Pt in the Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ half-cell.
2. E⦵cell = +0.77 − (−0.13) = **0.90 V**.
3. Fe³⁺ is reduced and lead is oxidised: **2Fe³⁺ + Pb → 2Fe²⁺ + Pb²⁺**.

## 5.2.3(i): predicting feasibility and its limits

A reaction is feasible when the oxidising agent sits in a half-equation with a more positive E⦵ than the half-equation containing the reducing agent, so E⦵cell is positive.

### Worked example 9: can acidified manganate(VII) oxidise chloride ions?

MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ ⇌ Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O, E⦵ = +1.51 V; Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻, E⦵ = +1.36 V.

E⦵cell = 1.51 − 1.36 = +0.15 V, so it is **feasible**: 2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 10Cl⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5Cl₂ + 8H₂O. This is why hydrochloric acid is not used in manganate(VII) titrations.

### Limitations

- **Kinetics**: E⦵ values predict feasibility, not rate. A feasible reaction with a high activation energy can be too slow to notice. MnO₄⁻ (+1.51 V) should oxidise water (O₂/H₂O, +1.23 V), yet acidified manganate(VII) is stable enough for titrations because this reaction is very slow.
- **Concentration**: E⦵ applies only at 1 mol dm⁻³. Raising [Fe³⁺] shifts Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺ to the right, making its potential more positive, so the cell in worked example 8 would exceed 0.90 V. When two E⦵ values are close, a concentration change can reverse the prediction.

## 5.2.3(j): storage cells

Storage cells hold their chemicals sealed inside; charging a rechargeable cell reverses the discharge reactions. You are given the half-equations and data.

### Worked example 10: nickel–cadmium cell

Use these values: Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cd + 2OH⁻, E⦵ = −0.81 V; NiO(OH) + H₂O + e⁻ ⇌ Ni(OH)₂ + OH⁻, E⦵ = +0.49 V.

1. E⦵cell = 0.49 − (−0.81) = **1.30 V**.
2. Cadmium is the negative electrode; on discharge Cd + 2OH⁻ → Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻.
3. Overall discharge: **Cd + 2NiO(OH) + 2H₂O → Cd(OH)₂ + 2Ni(OH)₂**. Charging runs this backwards.

Lithium-based cells are useful, but their materials can be toxic and damaged cells can catch fire.

## 5.2.3(k): fuel cells

A fuel cell uses the energy from a fuel reacting with oxygen to create a voltage. Fuel and oxygen are supplied continuously, so it needs no recharging.

In an acidic hydrogen fuel cell:

- Negative electrode (fuel oxidised): H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ (0.00 V)
- Positive electrode (oxygen reduced): O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O (+1.23 V)
- Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O; E⦵cell = 1.23 V

## Common errors

- Calling MnO₄⁻ the reducing agent because it "is reduced".
- Using the IO₃⁻ : I₂ ratio (1 : 3) instead of IO₃⁻ : S₂O₃²⁻ (1 : 6).
- Forgetting the ×10 for a 25.0 cm³ portion taken from 250.0 cm³.
- Doubling an E⦵ value when a half-equation is doubled.
