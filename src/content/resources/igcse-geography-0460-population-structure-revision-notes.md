## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Population structure** | The make-up of a population by age and sex |
| **Population pyramid** | A paired bar chart showing that structure in five-year age groups |
| **Dependants** | People too young or too old to work: under 15 and over 64 |
| **Economically active** | People of working age, 15 to 64 |
| **Youthful population** | A large share of children, shown by a wide base |
| **Ageing population** | A rising share of elderly people, shown by a wide top |
| **Life expectancy** | The average number of years a person can expect to live |
| **Sex ratio** | The number of males compared with females |

## What a pyramid shows

A **population pyramid** shows the number or percentage of males and females in each age group.
Males are usually on the left and females on the right. Three things to read:

- **Base** -- the birth rate and number of young people.
- **Sides** -- how quickly people die at each age (steep narrowing = high death rates).
- **Top** -- life expectancy and the number of elderly.

### How to describe a pyramid (a four-step method)

1. **Overall shape:** triangular, beehive-shaped (straight sides) or with a narrowing base.
2. **Base:** wide or narrow, and whether it is shrinking (the youngest bar shorter than the next).
3. **Top:** how many people over 64 and over 80, and whether females outnumber males.
4. **Anomalies:** any notch or bulge, with the age group and a possible reason.

Quote figures read from the axes, for example "about 8 per cent of males are aged 0 to 4".

## Pyramid shapes and the DTM

| Shape | Meaning | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Very wide base, narrowing sharply at every age | High birth and high death rates; youthful but short-lived | 1 |
| Wide base, narrowing more slowly | Birth rate high, death rate falling; the shape behind rapid growth | 2 |
| Narrow base, near-vertical sides, wide top | Low birth and death rates, long life expectancy; ageing | 4 or 5 |

Notches or bulges in a pyramid can point to events such as war, disease, a baby boom or migration.
Women usually outnumber men at the top because they tend to live longer.

**Reading anomalies:**

- A **bulge in young adults**, often more males than females, suggests in-migration of workers.
- A **notch** in one age group can show a war (men killed or born fewer), a famine, or an epidemic.
- A **narrowing base** in a stage 3 country shows the birth rate is now falling.
- A **base narrower than the bars above it** shows fewer births than in the past: a sign of a
  shrinking future population.

## Dependency ratio

```
dependency ratio = (under 15 + over 64) / (15 to 64) x 100
```

Worked example (illustrative): 30 000 under 15, 60 000 aged 15 to 64, 10 000 over 64.
(30 000 + 10 000) / 60 000 x 100 = **66.7**, about 67 dependants for every 100 workers.

A **high** ratio means a small working population supports many dependants.

**Second worked example (illustrative, using percentages):** 13% under 15, 58% aged 15 to 64, 29%
over 64. (13 + 29) / 58 x 100 = **72.4**. The ratio is high, but most dependants are elderly: this is
an ageing population. Always state **which group** makes the ratio high, because the problems differ.

**Limitations:** not everyone aged 15 to 64 works (students, unemployed people, carers), and many
people over 64 or under 15 do work, especially in farming in low-income countries. The ratio is an
estimate of the burden, not an exact measure.

## Youthful or ageing: consequences

| | Problems | Possible advantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Youthful** (wide base) | Pressure on schools and healthcare; need for jobs later; food and housing demand | Large future workforce |
| **Ageing** (wide top) | Higher pension and healthcare costs; shrinking workforce; fewer taxpayers; pressure on government spending | Experience; often a wealthier, healthier older group |

**Responses to ageing:** raise the retirement age, encourage immigration of working-age people,
pro-natalist policies.

**Responses to a youthful population:** invest in schools and training so the young become a skilled
workforce; family planning and education for women to lower the birth rate; job creation for school
leavers. If jobs are created, a falling birth rate can give a large working-age population with
fewer dependants, which can speed up development.

## Case study card: a country with a high dependent population

**Ageing example -- Japan.** Japan has one of the highest life expectancies in the world and a
birth rate well below replacement level. Its pyramid has a narrow base and a wide top, and close to
three in ten people are aged 65 or over. Its population has been falling.

- **Causes:** excellent healthcare and diet; women marrying and having children later; high costs of
  housing and education; long working hours.
- **Problems:** rising pension and healthcare spending; labour shortages in care and farming; rural
  villages with mostly elderly residents; fewer taxpayers to fund services.
- **Responses:** a later retirement age and more older people working; more childcare and parental
  leave to raise the birth rate; robots and technology in care and industry; carefully increased
  numbers of foreign workers.

**Youthful example -- Niger.** Niger, in the Sahel of West Africa, has one of the highest birth
rates in the world. Roughly half its population is under 15, giving a very wide base.

- **Causes:** early marriage, limited access to contraception, children needed for farm work and
  as support in old age, high infant mortality.
- **Problems:** too few schools and teachers; pressure on food and water in a drought-prone country;
  not enough jobs for young people as they reach working age.

Learn one in depth, with the cause, the problems and the responses linked together.

## Exam traps

- Reading a wide base as "many old people" -- it means many young.
- Forgetting that 15 and 64 are the boundaries: the working group is 15 to 64.
- Describing only the shape without linking it to birth rate, death rate or life expectancy.
- Leaving out the "x 100" in the dependency ratio.
- Giving only problems for a youthful population, when a large future workforce is also possible.
- Calculating a high dependency ratio without saying whether the young or the old cause it.

## Self-test

1. What does a wide base on a pyramid show?
2. Write the dependency ratio formula.
3. Name two problems of an ageing population.
4. Which DTM stage has a wide base that narrows slowly?
5. Give two ways a government can respond to an ageing population.
6. A pyramid has a bulge of males aged 20 to 39. Suggest a reason.
7. Calculate the dependency ratio: 40% under 15, 55% aged 15 to 64, 5% over 64.
8. Why do women usually outnumber men in the oldest age groups?
9. Give one limitation of the dependency ratio.
10. Give two reasons why Japan's population is ageing.
11. How can a youthful population become an advantage?

**Answers:** 1. A high birth rate and many young people. 2. (Under 15 + over 64) divided by (15 to 64),
multiplied by 100. 3. For example higher pension and healthcare costs, and a shrinking workforce. 4.
Stage 2. 5. For example a higher retirement age and encouraging immigration. 6. In-migration of young
male workers. 7. (40 + 5) / 55 x 100 = 81.8, a high ratio caused mainly by young dependants. 8. Women
tend to live longer than men. 9. Not everyone aged 15 to 64 works, and some dependants do work. 10. For
example long life expectancy from good healthcare and diet, and a low birth rate as people marry
later and children are costly to raise. 11. If the young are educated and find jobs, they form a
large workforce paying taxes and supporting growth.
