This guide covers sub-topic **4(c) Work and power**, statements 4.11 to 4.17 of the Pearson Edexcel International
GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. It is core content, so it can be examined on either paper. The
[Energy Resources and Energy Transfers study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-energy-resources-and-energy-transfers/)
introduces the equations; this page concentrates on applying them, especially statement 4.15, which links
gravitational potential energy, kinetic energy and work in multi-step problems.

## The four relationships

```
work done = force × distance moved              W = F × d           (4.11)
GPE = mass × gravitational field strength × height    GPE = m × g × h     (4.13)
kinetic energy = ½ × mass × speed²              KE = ½ × m × v²     (4.14)
power = work done / time taken                  P = W / t           (4.17)
```

The first three are in the specification's list of relationships you must recall (statements 4.11, 4.13 and 4.14
say "know and use"). Statement 4.17 says only "use" for the power relationship and it is not in that list, but you
will need it so often that it is worth learning. Work, GPE and KE are in joules (J); power is in watts (W).

## Work done is energy transferred (4.11, 4.12)

Work is done when a force moves something **in the direction of the force**. The work done equals the energy
transferred, so "calculate the work done" and "calculate the energy transferred" are the same calculation.

Three cases test whether you understand the "in the direction of the force" part:

- **Pushing a trolley 15 m with a steady 120 N force:** `W = 120 × 15 = 1800 J`. The energy goes into the kinetic store
  (if it speeds up) and the thermal store of the wheels and floor (friction).
- **Holding a heavy bag still:** your arm muscles tire, but the bag does not move, so no work is done on the bag.
- **Carrying a bag along a level corridor:** your upward force on the bag is at right angles to the motion, so it
  does no work on the bag. The bag's gravitational store does not change.

## Gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy (4.13, 4.14)

`GPE = m × g × h` gives the energy transferred to (or from) an object's gravitational store when its height changes by
`h`. Use the **change** in height, not the height above some arbitrary point you have not been asked about.

`KE = ½ × m × v²` depends on the square of the speed: doubling the speed multiplies the kinetic energy by four, while
doubling the mass only doubles it.

## Conservation links GPE, KE and work (4.15)

When an object falls freely, the work done by gravity transfers energy from its gravitational store to its kinetic
store. If air resistance is small enough to ignore:

```
loss in GPE = gain in KE
m × g × h = ½ × m × v²
```

The mass appears on both sides and cancels, so all objects dropped from the same height reach the same speed (if air
resistance is negligible).

**Worked example 1: a falling ball.** A ball is dropped from 5.0 m (g = 10 N/kg). Find its speed just before it hits
the ground.

```
g × h = ½ × v²
10 × 5.0 = 0.5 × v²
v² = 100        v = 10 m/s
```

**Worked example 2: a ball thrown upwards.** A ball leaves your hand upwards at 8.0 m/s. How high does it rise?

```
½ × v² = g × h
h = v² / (2 × g) = 8.0² / 20 = 64 / 20 = 3.2 m
```

**Worked example 3: when friction does work.** A roller-coaster car of mass 500 kg starts from rest at the top of a
20 m drop and reaches the bottom at 18 m/s (g = 10 N/kg). The track is 250 m long. Find the work done against
friction and the average frictional force.

```
GPE lost = m × g × h = 500 × 10 × 20 = 100 000 J
KE gained = ½ × 500 × 18² = 0.5 × 500 × 324 = 81 000 J
work done against friction = 100 000 − 81 000 = 19 000 J
average friction force = W / d = 19 000 / 250 = 76 N
```

Conservation of energy is still obeyed: the 19 000 J has been transferred to the thermal store of the car, track and
air, not destroyed.

**Worked example 4: braking.** A 1000 kg car travelling at 15 m/s brakes with a constant force of 4500 N. Find the
braking distance.

```
KE = ½ × 1000 × 15² = 112 500 J
work done by brakes = KE, so F × d = 112 500
d = 112 500 / 4500 = 25 m
```

Because KE depends on v², doubling the speed to 30 m/s would make the braking distance four times longer (100 m)
with the same force.

## Power is a rate (4.16, 4.17)

Power is the **rate** of transferring energy, or the rate of doing work: the energy transferred each second. One watt
is one joule per second. Two people who lift identical boxes onto the same shelf do the same work; the one who does it
in half the time develops twice the power.

**Worked example 5: running up stairs.** A 60 kg student runs up a flight of stairs with a vertical height of 4.5 m
in 5.4 s (g = 10 N/kg).

```
work done against gravity = m × g × h = 60 × 10 × 4.5 = 2700 J
P = W / t = 2700 / 5.4 = 500 W
```

To measure this yourself you need a bathroom scale (mass), a tape measure (the **vertical** height of the stairs, not
the length along the slope) and a stopwatch. The value found is the power used to gain GPE; the body's total power is
higher because some energy also heats the body.

**Worked example 6: a water pump.** A pump lifts 300 kg of water through a height of 8.0 m every minute.

```
work done per minute = 300 × 10 × 8.0 = 24 000 J
P = 24 000 / 60 = 400 W
```

## Choosing the right equation

| The question gives you... | Use |
|---|---|
| a force and a distance in its direction | `W = F × d` |
| a mass and a change in height | `GPE = m × g × h` |
| a mass and a speed | `KE = ½ × m × v²` |
| energy or work and a time | `P = W / t` |
| a fall or a rise, and asks for a speed or height | `m × g × h = ½ × m × v²` |
| a speed lost to braking or friction over a distance | `F × d = ½ × m × v²` |

## Common mistakes

- Using the distance along a slope in `GPE = m × g × h`; `h` is the vertical height change.
- Forgetting to square the speed, or squaring ½ × v instead of v alone.
- Taking the square root too early or forgetting it: in example 1, `v² = 100` is not the answer.
- Saying work is done holding an object still.
- Using time in minutes in `P = W / t`.
- Writing "power is energy". Power is energy **per second**.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the equation in symbols or words, substitute with units converted, and give the unit. In a
  two-step problem, label each line ("GPE lost =", "KE gained =") so an examiner can follow your method.
- **Show that:** give every step and a final value more precise than the one in the question, so you are not just
  copying it.
- **Explain** why a measured value differs from a calculated one: name the energy that was not counted, such as work
  done against friction or air resistance, transferred to the thermal store of the surroundings.
- **Describe** an experiment to measure power: list what you measure, with which instrument, and the equation you use.
