Prescribed subject 2 has two case studies: the final stages of Muslim rule in Spain (1482-1492) and the conquest of Mexico and Peru (1519-1551). Revise each under context and motives, key events and actors, and impact.

Related: [Conquest and its impact study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-world-history-dp-history-ib-dp-world-history-prescribed-subjects-2/) and [Conquest and its impact practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-world-history-dp-history-ib-dp-world-history-prescribed-subjects-2-practice/).

## Granada: condensed recall

**Why Granada fell when it did**
- *Christian unity*: Isabella and Ferdinand married in 1469; Castile and Aragon were ruled jointly after 1479.
- *Nasrid division*: Abu'l-Hasan Ali, his son Boabdil (Muhammad XII) and his brother al-Zaghal fought each other; Castile exploited this by backing Boabdil.
- *Siege warfare*: artillery reduced fortresses that had once resisted for years.
- *Isolation*: little effective help reached Granada from North Africa or the Ottomans.
- *Resources*: papal crusade taxes and the mobilisation of Castilian towns and nobles.

**Sequence**: Zahara raid (1481) → Alhama (1482) → Boabdil captured at Lucena (1483) → Ronda (1485) → Loja (1486) → Málaga (1487) → Baza; al-Zaghal surrenders (1489) → siege from Santa Fe (1491) → Capitulations (November 1491) → entry into Granada (2 January 1492).

**Impact**
- Generous terms at first; Talavera's persuasion policy.
- Cisneros's forced baptisms from 1499; Albaicín and Alpujarras revolts (1499-1501).
- 1502: Castilian Muslims must convert or leave; converts are *moriscos*.
- Alhambra Decree (March 1492) expels unconverted Jews; the Inquisition watches converts.
- Prestige for the monarchs, named the "Catholic Monarchs" by the Pope in 1496.

## Mexico and Peru: condensed recall

**Mexico, 1519-1521**: Cortés leaves Cuba (1519) → Malinche and Aguilar as interpreters → Totonac and Tlaxcalan alliances → Cholula massacre → entry to Tenochtitlan (November 1519), Moctezuma held → Alvarado's festival massacre (1520) → death of Moctezuma; *Noche Triste* → smallpox in the city → brigantines and siege → fall of Tenochtitlan (August 1521); Cuauhtémoc captured.

**Peru, 1532-1551**: Inca civil war (Huáscar against Atahualpa) → Cajamarca, capture of Atahualpa (November 1532) → ransom; execution (1533) → Cusco taken (1533) → Manco Inca's rebellion (1536); Vilcabamba → Almagro executed (1538) → Pizarro assassinated (1541) → New Laws (1542) → Gonzalo Pizarro's revolt, defeated (1548) → Valladolid debate (1550-1551).

**Factors in Spanish success**

| Factor | Mexico | Peru |
|---|---|---|
| Indigenous allies | Tlaxcalans and other enemies of the Mexica | Factions opposed to Atahualpa; later, many Andean groups |
| Disease | Smallpox during the 1520 siege | Epidemic before the Spanish arrived, linked to the civil war |
| Political division | Tribute peoples resented Mexica rule | Civil war between brothers |
| Technology | Steel, horses, brigantines on the lake | Steel and horses at Cajamarca |
| Leadership | Cortés's diplomacy and use of interpreters | Pizarro's seizure of the ruler |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nasrid | The dynasty that ruled the emirate of Granada |
| Capitulations of Granada | The 1491 surrender terms protecting Muslim religion, property and law |
| *Mudéjar* | Muslim living under Christian rule while keeping his faith |
| *Morisco* | Muslim convert to Christianity, or a descendant, in Spain |
| *Converso* | Jewish convert to Christianity |
| *Encomienda* | Grant of indigenous labour and tribute to a Spaniard in return for protection and instruction |
| New Laws (1542) | Crown laws limiting *encomiendas* and banning the enslavement of indigenous people |
| Viceroyalty | A territory ruled by a viceroy for the crown: New Spain (1535), Peru (1540s) |
| Requerimiento | A declaration read to indigenous peoples demanding submission to the crown and Church |
| Black Legend | A tradition portraying Spanish rule as uniquely cruel, drawing on Las Casas |

## Interpretations to know

- **The few conquered the many?** Matthew Restall (2003) argues that indigenous allies and disease made the conquests possible and that the "handful of Spaniards" image is a myth.
- **Las Casas against Sepúlveda.** At Valladolid, Las Casas argued that indigenous peoples were rational and free; Sepúlveda argued that war against them was just.
- **Granada as crusade or state-building.** It can be read as the last act of the Reconquista or as the monarchs' use of war to strengthen royal power.

## Source types to expect

| Source type | Example | Value | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conqueror's report | Cortés's letters to Charles V | Eyewitness; shows how conquest was justified to the crown | Self-promoting; written to excuse defying the governor |
| Veteran's memoir | Bernal Díaz del Castillo | Ordinary soldier's detail | Written decades later; answers rival accounts |
| Indigenous account | Florentine Codex | Nahua perspective on the siege and the epidemic | Compiled a generation later under a friar's direction |
| Petition or complaint | Granadan Muslims' grievances | Voice of the conquered | Written to win redress, so stresses wrongs |

## Exam traps

- **Wrong date range.** The Spanish case ends in 1492, so the 1609 expulsion lies outside it. The American case ends in 1551, so do not write about Potosí's later silver boom.
- **Single-cause explanations.** "Guns, germs and horses" ignores allies and indigenous politics.
- **One-sided impact.** Include Spanish debate and law as well as the fate of the conquered.
- **Taking a conquistador at his word.** Cortés's letters were written to win royal favour.
- **Treating later accounts as eyewitness.** Bernal Díaz wrote decades later; the Florentine Codex was compiled in the following generation.

## Self-test

1. Which marriage united the crowns of Castile and Aragon, and when?
   *Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon, 1469.*
2. Name the three Nasrid rivals whose quarrels weakened Granada.
   *Abu'l-Hasan Ali, Boabdil (Muhammad XII) and al-Zaghal.*
3. Which siege of 1487 ended with much of the population enslaved?
   *Málaga.*
4. What did the Capitulations of 1491 promise?
   *Freedom of religion, mosques, property and Islamic law for the Muslims of Granada.*
5. Which archbishop's policy of forced baptism provoked revolt in 1499?
   *Cardinal Cisneros.*
6. What did the Alhambra Decree of 1492 order?
   *The expulsion of Jews who would not convert.*
7. Who interpreted for Cortés between Nahuatl and Maya?
   *Malinche (Doña Marina).*
8. What was the *Noche Triste*?
   *The Spanish flight from Tenochtitlan in 1520, with heavy losses.*
9. When did Tenochtitlan fall?
   *August 1521.*
10. Where and when was Atahualpa captured?
    *Cajamarca, November 1532.*
11. Why did the New Laws provoke revolt in Peru?
    *They restricted encomiendas, threatening the conquerors' wealth and labour.*
12. Who debated at Valladolid in 1550-1551, and about what?
    *Las Casas and Sepúlveda, on whether war against the indigenous peoples was just.*
