Condensed recall for **4.4 Future resilience and adaptation** (IB DP Geography, Option D: Geophysical hazards). The [Geophysical hazards study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-geophysical-hazards-study-guide/) gives fuller coverage.

## Options for reducing risk

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Structural | Earthquake-resistant design, retrofitting, tsunami barriers, lava diversion, evacuation shelters |
| Non-structural | Land-use planning, building codes, hazard education, drills, insurance, early warning systems |
| Community-based | Local knowledge and social networks |
| Governance and finance | Investment in risk reduction rather than only paying for response; promoted by international frameworks |

### How structural measures work

- **Base isolation:** a building sits on flexible bearings that absorb ground movement, so less shaking reaches the structure.
- **Dampers and cross-bracing:** absorb and spread energy so frames flex without collapsing.
- **Retrofitting:** strengthening existing buildings, for example adding steel frames to schools or wrapping concrete columns.
- **Tsunami sea walls and vertical evacuation towers:** block or give refuge from waves where higher ground is far away.
- **Lava diversion:** barriers or cooling with water. In 1973 on Heimaey, Iceland, residents pumped seawater onto advancing lava, helping to slow it and protect the harbour.

### Finance and insurance

Insurance spreads the cost of recovery but is often unaffordable for the poorest. **Parametric insurance** pays out automatically when a measured trigger is reached (for example a given magnitude near a city), giving fast cash. The Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility pools risk between governments in this way.

## The international framework

The **Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015-2030**, adopted by UN member states in 2015, followed the earlier Hyogo Framework. Its four priorities are:

1. Understanding disaster risk.
2. Strengthening disaster risk governance.
3. Investing in disaster risk reduction for resilience.
4. Enhancing preparedness for effective response, and to "build back better" in recovery and reconstruction.

It shifts attention from managing disasters after they happen to reducing risk before they happen. It is voluntary, so progress depends on national action and funding.

## What may change future risk

- Urbanisation and population growth in hazardous areas
- Climate change interactions, for example intense rainfall triggering lahars or landslides
- Inequality, which shapes who can adapt

Rapidly growing cities on or near major faults, such as Istanbul, Kathmandu and Lima, concentrate exposure. Informal settlements spreading onto steep slopes raise landslide risk. Melting ice on high volcanoes can increase the water available for lahars. On the positive side, better monitoring, mobile phone alerts and building codes can reduce vulnerability if they reach everyone.

## Case studies

### Japan: living with frequent hazards

Japan has strict building standards that were strengthened after major earthquakes, widespread use of flexible high-rise design, an earthquake early warning system, and national preparedness drills every 1 September, the anniversary of the 1923 Great Kanto earthquake. Even so, the 2011 Tohoku tsunami overtopped many sea walls along the north-east coast, showing that structural defences can be exceeded and can create a false sense of security. Evacuation education was credited with saving many schoolchildren in Kamaishi. Lesson: structural and non-structural measures must work together.

### Indian Ocean tsunami warning system

In 2004 there was no regional warning system in the Indian Ocean, and the tsunami killed people in many countries hours after the earthquake. A regional system was set up afterwards, with seismic sensors, sea-level gauges and national warning centres. Evaluation: detection is now much better, but warnings still have to reach remote coastal villages through the "last mile", which depends on local communication and education.

### Turkey and Syria, 2023

A very large earthquake in February 2023 killed more than 50,000 people across Turkey and Syria. Turkey had modern building codes, but many collapsed buildings had not met them, and past construction amnesties had legalised non-compliant buildings. Syria's response was hampered by civil war. The case shows that codes reduce risk only if they are enforced, and that conflict multiplies vulnerability.

## Evaluating a strategy

Use criteria:

1. **Cost**
2. **Feasibility**
3. **Equity** (who is protected?)
4. **Speed**
5. **Sustainability**

Adaptation is a **continual process**, not a single event: plans need review as exposure and evidence change.

## Example comparison

Retrofitting existing buildings is structural, costly and slow but directly protects people; hazard education and drills are non-structural, cheap and fast but depend on sustained participation. A strong answer uses both and says who each benefits.

## Command words

- **Evaluate** a strategy: apply criteria, weigh strengths against limitations, and finish with a judgement about where and for whom it works.
- **To what extent** can risk be reduced: take a clear position (for example "largely for wealthy, well-governed places; only partly elsewhere") and support it.
- **Suggest** ways to increase resilience: offer realistic, justified measures suited to the place described in the source.

## Model paragraph

*To what extent can future earthquake risk be reduced?* "Risk can be reduced substantially where building codes are enforced, as Japan and Chile show, because collapse of buildings causes most earthquake deaths. Early warning and drills add seconds of protection and better behaviour during shaking. However, the 2023 earthquake in Turkey shows that codes fail without enforcement, and rapid informal urban growth in cities such as Kathmandu adds exposure faster than retrofitting can keep up. Risk can therefore be reduced to a large extent technically, but in practice the limit is set by governance, money and equity."

## Exam traps

- Listing measures without criteria-based evaluation.
- Treating structural measures as sufficient: governance and behaviour matter.
- Ignoring how future population growth changes exposure.
- Giving figures for a named programme from memory; stay general if unsure.
- Assuming a warning system works once sensors exist: the last mile matters.
- Forgetting that insurance helps recovery but does not reduce physical damage.
