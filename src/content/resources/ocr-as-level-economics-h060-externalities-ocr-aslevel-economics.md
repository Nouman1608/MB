| Positive consumption externality | External benefit from consuming | MSB above MPB | Too low | 4 |
| Positive production externality | External benefit from producing | MSC below MPC | Too low | 5 |

## 1. What an externality is

An **externality** is a cost or benefit that falls on a third party, someone outside the transaction, and is not reflected in the market price. Because decision makers ignore it, the market produces the wrong quantity.

Two questions classify any externality:

1. **Is it a cost or a benefit to the third party?** A cost is a negative externality; a benefit is a positive externality.
2. **Does it come from producing the good or from consuming it?** This decides which curve you move. A **production** externality separates MSC from MPC. A **consumption** externality separates MSB from MPB.

Getting the second question right is where most diagram marks are won or lost. Smoke from a factory chimney arises from production. Noise from a party arises from consumption.

## 2. Negative production externality

**Worked example 1 (invented quarry).** Q is in thousand tonnes of stone a month and prices in £.

- MPB = MSB = 100 − 4Q
- MPC = 20 + 4Q
- Dust, noise and road damage impose an MEC of £16 per tonne, so MSC = 36 + 4Q.

| | Rule | Output | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | MPB = MPC | 100 − 4Q = 20 + 4Q, so Q = **10** | £60 |
| Social optimum | MSB = MSC | 100 − 4Q = 36 + 4Q, so Q = **8** | £68 |

At the market output, MSC is £76 but MSB is only £60. Welfare loss = ½ × (10 − 8) × (76 − 60) = **£16 thousand** a month.

**Diagram:** MPB = MSB slopes down; MPC slopes up; MSC is drawn parallel above MPC by £16. Mark Q market (10) and Q optimum (8); shade the triangle between MSC and MSB from 8 to 10. The price at the optimum is higher, because the full cost of stone is now reflected.

## 3. Negative consumption externality

Here the consumer's private benefit exceeds society's benefit, because consuming harms others: car journeys into a crowded city centre add to congestion and fumes for everyone else.

**Worked example 2 (invented city-centre journeys).** MPB = 70 − 2Q; each journey imposes £10 of cost on others, so MSB = 60 − 2Q. MPC = MSC = 10 + 3Q.

- Market: 70 − 2Q = 10 + 3Q, so Q = **12** thousand journeys a day.
- Optimum: 60 − 2Q = 10 + 3Q, so Q = **10** thousand.
- At Q = 12, MSC = 46 and MSB = 36, so welfare loss = ½ × 2 × 10 = **£10 thousand** a day.

**Diagram:** draw MSB **below** MPB, with one supply curve (MPC = MSC). Overconsumption is the gap between the two outputs.

## 4. Positive consumption externality

Consumers ignore benefits to others. Vaccination is the classic case: a vaccinated person is less likely to pass on an infection, so others gain.

**Worked example 3 (invented flu vaccinations).** Q is in thousand doses, prices in £.

- MPB = 40 − 2Q
- MEB = £6 per dose, so MSB = 46 − 2Q
- MPC = MSC = 10 + Q

| | Rule | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Market | MPB = MPC | 40 − 2Q = 10 + Q, so Q = **10** |
| Social optimum | MSB = MSC | 46 − 2Q = 10 + Q, so Q = **12** |

At Q = 10, MSB is £26 and MSC is £20. Welfare loss = ½ × (12 − 10) × (26 − 20) = **£6 thousand**. This loss comes from units **not** consumed: 2 thousand doses that are worth more to society than they cost.

**Diagram:** MSB is drawn **above** MPB; the welfare-loss triangle lies between MSB and MSC from the market output up to the optimum.

Education and health care are other common contexts: a better educated workforce raises productivity for employers and colleagues, and treating one person's illness can protect others.

## 5. Positive production externality

A firm's production benefits third parties. A firm that trains its workers in general skills benefits other employers when those workers move on. A fruit grower's orchard helps a neighbouring beekeeper.

**Worked example 4 (invented training firm).** MPB = MSB = 80 − 2Q. MPC = 20 + 2Q. Each unit of output creates £8 of benefit to other firms, so MSC = MPC − 8 = 12 + 2Q.

- Market: 80 − 2Q = 20 + 2Q, so Q = **15**.
- Optimum: 80 − 2Q = 12 + 2Q, so Q = **17**.
- Welfare loss = ½ × 2 × 8 = **8** (£ thousand).

**Diagram:** MSC is drawn **below** MPC. The market underproduces.

## 6. Evaluating externalities

- **Valuation:** external effects such as illness, lost wildlife or lost time in traffic have no market price. Estimates differ, so the size of the welfare loss is uncertain.
- **Constant or changing MEC:** the examples assume a fixed £ per unit. In reality the damage from pollution may rise as output grows, which makes the welfare loss larger than a parallel-curve diagram suggests.
- **Who bears the cost:** third parties may be poorer households living near main roads or industry, so externalities raise equity issues as well as efficiency ones.
- **Time and place:** some effects build up over years or cross borders, so the market and even a single government struggle to deal with them.
- **Not every externality is significant:** a small external effect may not be worth correcting, given the cost of doing so.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Explain, with the aid of a diagram, why the free market may lead to underconsumption of vaccinations."

> *[Diagram: MPB and MSB downward sloping, MSB above MPB; MPC = MSC upward sloping; Q market where MPB = MPC, Q optimum where MSB = MSC; welfare loss shaded between Q market and Q optimum.]*
> When people decide whether to be vaccinated, they weigh their private benefit against the price and ignore the benefit to others who are less likely to catch the infection *[cause]*. MSB therefore lies above MPB by the marginal external benefit *[curve placement]*. The market settles at Q market, where MPB = MPC, below Q optimum where MSB = MSC *[diagram used]*. The units between them are worth more to society than they cost, so there is a welfare loss and underconsumption *[consequence]*.

## Common mistakes

- Moving the cost curve for a consumption externality, or the benefit curve for a production externality.
- Drawing MSC below MPC for an external cost.
- Shading the welfare loss on the wrong side of the optimum.
- Forgetting that a positive externality's welfare loss comes from units not produced.
- Describing an externality without naming the third party.

## Exam technique

- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** classify the externality (cost or benefit; production or consumption), draw the correct curve, label both outputs and shade the loss, then refer to the diagram in your words.
- **Calculate:** find market output from the private curves and the optimum from the social curves; the welfare-loss height is the gap between MSB and MSC at the market output.
- **Evaluate:** consider valuation, distribution and the significance of the effect before judging whether it needs correcting.
