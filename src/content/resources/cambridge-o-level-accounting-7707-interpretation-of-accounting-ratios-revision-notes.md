These notes condense section **6.2 Interpretation of accounting ratios** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for 2027 to 2029. The section covers comparison statements for different years, interpreting the 6.1 ratios, recommendations "for improving profitability, liquidity and working capital", the two margins as profitability indicators, what affects gross profit and profit for the year, and "the reasons for the difference between cash and profit". The two-year worked example is in the [interpretation study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interpretation-of-accounting-ratios/); test yourself with the [interpretation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interpretation-of-accounting-ratios-practice/). The formulas are in the [ratio calculation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-calculation-and-understanding-of-accounting-ratios-revision-notes/). Figures are invented and in dollars.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Comparison statement | Two or more years' figures side by side with the change in dollars and as a percentage |
| Percentage change | Change / earlier year's figure x 100 |
| Percentage point | The difference between two percentages, such as 40% to 35% (5 points) |
| Profitability | The ability to earn profit from sales and from the capital used |
| Liquidity | The ability to pay debts as they fall due |
| Working capital | Current assets less current liabilities |
| Other income | Income that is not from sales, such as rent received or commission |

## The comment formula

For each ratio: **direction and size** ("fell from 40% to 35%"), **meaning** ("$5 less gross profit on each $100 of sales"), **likely cause from the case**, **consequence**. One complete comment beats three bare statements.

## Reading the margins together

| Pattern | Look at |
|---|---|
| Gross profit margin falls, profit margin falls by about the same | The trading section: selling prices, purchase prices, inventory |
| Gross profit margin steady, profit margin falls | Expenses rising, or other income falling |
| Gross profit margin falls, profit margin steady | Expenses or other income improved enough to offset |
| Revenue and gross profit rise by the same %, margin steady | More units sold at unchanged prices and costs |

## What affects gross profit

- **Valuation of inventory**: overvalued closing inventory raises this year's gross profit and lowers next year's.
- **Sales quantity**: more units raise gross profit, not the margin.
- **Selling prices**: a rise lifts the margin if costs are unchanged.
- **Purchasing prices**: a rise cuts gross profit and the margin unless passed on.

**Profit for the year** = gross profit + other income - expenses, so any of the three changes it.

## Liquidity and efficiency readings

- A **current or acid test ratio that falls** suggests difficulty paying suppliers; below 1 : 1 on the acid test means liquid assets do not cover current liabilities.
- A **very high current ratio** may mean idle cash or too much inventory.
- **Receivables days above the credit period allowed** means customers pay late and cash is tied up.
- **Payables days rising** keeps cash longer but may lose discounts and supplier goodwill if beyond the agreed period.
- **Inventory days rising** means goods are sold more slowly, with a risk of obsolete stock.

## Why cash and profit differ

| Raises cash more than profit | Lowers cash more than profit |
|---|---|
| Depreciation and other non-cash expenses | Buying non-current assets |
| Lower inventory or receivables | Higher inventory or receivables |
| Higher payables | Lower payables |
| Loans received, capital introduced | Loan repayments, drawings, dividends |
| Expenses accrued but unpaid | Expenses prepaid |

## Recommendations to match the problem

| Problem | Suggestions |
|---|---|
| Low gross profit margin | Raise selling prices where customers will accept; seek cheaper suppliers or trade discounts |
| Low profit margin, steady gross margin | Cut unnecessary expenses; find other income |
| Low liquidity | Chase receivables, offer cash discounts, reduce drawings, replace an overdraft with a long-term loan |
| Too much working capital tied up | Hold less inventory; use the full credit period suppliers allow |

## Exam traps

- A ratio described without a cause or consequence earns little.
- Name the cause of a margin change, not just "the margin fell".
- Percentage points and percentage change are different: 40% to 35% is 5 points, a 12.5% fall.
- A recommendation must solve the problem shown, not a different one.
- Use the question's figures when explaining cash against profit.

## Self-test

1. The gross profit margin fell from 32% to 28% and the profit margin from 12% to 8%. Where would you look for the cause?
2. The gross profit margin stayed at 30% but the profit margin fell from 15% to 10%. Give two possible causes.
3. Revenue rose from $200,000 to $220,000 and gross profit from $80,000 to $88,000. What is the most likely cause?
4. Closing inventory was undervalued by $900. State the effect on gross profit this year.
5. The current ratio rose from 1.8 : 1 to 3.5 : 1 because inventory doubled. Is this an improvement? Explain.
6. Trade receivables turnover is 48 days and customers are allowed 30 days. Comment and give one recommendation.
7. Give two reasons a profitable business may have a bank overdraft.
8. Depreciation for the year is $5,000. State its effect on profit and on cash.
9. Suggest two ways to improve liquidity.
10. Expenses rose from $40,000 to $46,000. Calculate the percentage change.
11. Trade payables turnover rose from 35 days to 70 days. Suppliers allow 60 days. State one risk.
12. Gross profit rose by $6,000, other income fell by $1,000 and expenses rose by $3,500. Calculate the change in profit for the year.

### Answers

1. The trading section: lower selling prices, higher purchase prices or inventory valuation, since both margins fell by the same amount.
2. Any two: expenses rose faster than revenue; other income fell; a new expense such as loan interest was added.
3. More units sold at unchanged prices and costs: both rose by 10% and the margin stayed at 40%.
4. Gross profit is **$900 lower** (and next year's will be $900 higher).
5. Not necessarily: the extra inventory ties up cash, may become obsolete and does not help pay debts quickly; the acid test would show this.
6. Customers take 18 days longer than allowed, tying up cash and raising the risk of irrecoverable debts. Recommend chasing overdue accounts or offering a cash discount for prompt payment.
7. Any two: bought non-current assets; built up inventory or receivables; high drawings; repaid a loan.
8. It lowers profit by $5,000 but has no effect on cash.
9. Any two: collect receivables faster; reduce inventory; reduce drawings or dividends; raise long-term finance; sell unused non-current assets.
10. 6,000 / 40,000 x 100 = **15%**.
11. Paying beyond the 60 days may lose supplier goodwill, credit or discounts.
12. 6,000 - 1,000 - 3,500 = **+$1,500**.
