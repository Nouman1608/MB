This guide covers **section 10.2 Links between macroeconomic problems and their interrelatedness** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content. The syllabus lists five relationships: between "the internal value of money and the external value of money", "the balance of payments and inflation", "growth and inflation", "growth and the balance of payments", and "inflation and unemployment", the last covering the "traditional Phillips curve" and the "expectations-augmented Phillips curve (short- and long-run Phillips curve)".

The broader [macroeconomic objectives and Phillips curve study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-macroeconomic-objectives-and-their-interrelationships/) explains each link and shows how holding unemployment below its natural rate makes inflation accelerate. This page goes further: it writes every link as a chain that can run in **both directions**, combines inflation and exchange-rate changes in one competitiveness calculation, and works through the reverse Phillips curve case, the cost of bringing inflation **down**. All countries and figures are invented.

## Why "interrelatedness" matters

Macroeconomic problems rarely arrive one at a time. A single shock, such as a surge in AD, can raise inflation, worsen the current account and reduce unemployment at once. Policy aimed at one problem therefore affects the others. Exam answers gain credit for tracing a chain from one variable to another and then saying what the chain depends on.

## 10.2.1 Internal and external value of money

- The **internal value of money** is what a unit of currency buys at home. It moves inversely with the price level: if the price index rises from 100 to 125, the same money buys 100 ÷ 125 = 0.8 of what it did, a fall of 20% in its internal value.
- The **external value of money** is what it buys abroad, shown by the exchange rate.

**Chain A (internal to external):** home inflation above that of trading partners → home goods dearer abroad, imports relatively cheaper → demand for the currency falls and supply of it rises → under a floating rate, the currency depreciates.

**Chain B (external to internal):** depreciation → import prices rise in home currency → higher costs for firms and a higher price index → internal value falls.

**Worked example 1: does a depreciation restore competitiveness?** Country A's prices rise by 8% in a year and its partners' prices by 2%. Country A's currency depreciates by 4%.

- Change in the foreign-currency price of A's goods relative to partners' goods: 1.08 × 0.96 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = **+1.6%**.

Even after the depreciation, A's goods are about 1.6% less competitive than a year before. A depreciation of about 1 − 1.02 ÷ 1.08 = 5.6% would have been needed to offset the inflation gap. Chains A and B can then feed each other: the depreciation raises import prices, which adds to inflation, which puts more pressure on the currency.

## 10.2.2 The balance of payments and inflation

**Inflation to the balance of payments:** higher relative inflation reduces price competitiveness, so export volumes fall and import volumes rise; the current account worsens, by more if demand for exports and imports is price elastic.

**Balance of payments to inflation (deficit):** a deficit can weaken the currency; depreciation raises import prices and adds cost-push inflation.

**Balance of payments to inflation (surplus):** a large surplus adds to AD through net exports; near full capacity, this adds demand-pull inflation. A surplus may also push the currency up, which lowers import prices and offsets some of that pressure.

## 10.2.3 Growth and inflation

| Source of growth | Effect on inflation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| AD rising with spare capacity | Little extra inflation | Firms can raise output without bidding up costs |
| AD rising near full capacity | Demand-pull inflation | Firms compete for scarce labour and materials |
| LRAS shifting right (productivity, investment) | Lower inflationary pressure | Capacity grows with demand; unit costs fall |
| Cost shock shifting SRAS left | Inflation with falling output | Stagflation: higher prices and lower growth together |

**Reverse direction:** high and volatile inflation can reduce growth, because uncertainty discourages long-term investment and loss of competitiveness reduces exports. Low, stable inflation is compatible with steady growth.

## 10.2.4 Growth and the balance of payments

**Growth to the balance of payments:** higher income raises spending on imports. If national income rises by 40 billion and the marginal propensity to import is 0.25, imports rise by 0.25 × 40 = **10 billion**, worsening the current account unless exports rise too.

**Exceptions:** export-led growth improves the account; growth driven by rising productivity can make exports more competitive; investment-led growth may worsen the account at first, through imports of machinery, and improve it later.

**Balance of payments to growth:** net exports are a component of AD, so a rise in exports raises growth. A persistent deficit that forces contractionary policy to defend the currency can hold growth back.

## 10.2.5 Inflation and unemployment: the Phillips curves

**Traditional Phillips curve.** In 1958, A. W. Phillips published evidence from the United Kingdom showing an inverse relationship between the rate of change of money wages and unemployment. Later versions plot price inflation against unemployment. The curve implied a stable trade-off: lower unemployment at the cost of higher inflation.

**Expectations-augmented Phillips curve.** In the late 1960s, Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that the trade-off exists only in the short run. Workers and firms set wages on the basis of expected inflation. Each short-run Phillips curve (SRPC) is drawn for one expected rate. When actual inflation exceeds expected inflation, expectations rise and the SRPC shifts up. In the long run, actual and expected inflation are equal, and unemployment returns to the **natural rate**, so the long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) is vertical there.

**Worked example 2: the cost of disinflation.** In an invented model, inflation = expected inflation − 0.8 × (unemployment − 6), so the natural rate is 6%. Workers expect last year's inflation. Inflation and expected inflation are both 8%, and unemployment is 6%. The central bank tightens policy so that unemployment rises to 8.5%, which subtracts 0.8 × 2.5 = 2 points from inflation each year.

| Year | Expected inflation | Unemployment | Inflation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 | 8.5 | 8 − 2 = 6 |
| 2 | 6 | 8.5 | 4 |
| 3 | 4 | 8.5 | 2 |
| 4 | 2 | 6.0 | 2 |

Bringing inflation from 8% to 2% costs three years with unemployment 2.5 points above the natural rate. The economy moves down each SRPC and then onto a lower one as expectations fall. If the central bank's commitment were believed at once, so that expected inflation fell to 2% immediately, the same fall in inflation could be achieved with a much smaller rise in unemployment. This is why credibility matters for anti-inflation policy.

**Stagflation explained.** A supply shock, such as a sharp rise in import prices, raises inflation at every level of unemployment, shifting the SRPC up. Inflation and unemployment can then rise together, which a single stable curve cannot explain.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* Explain why the long-run Phillips curve is vertical.

> When the government raises AD to cut unemployment below the natural rate, inflation rises above what workers expected, so real wages fall and firms hire more. **[Short-run trade-off.]** Workers then revise their expectations upward and bargain for higher money wages, so the SRPC shifts up. **[Expectations adjust.]** Once actual and expected inflation are equal, real wages return to their earlier level and firms no longer have the incentive to employ the extra workers, so unemployment returns to the natural rate. **[Return to the natural rate.]** Any inflation rate is therefore consistent with the natural rate in the long run, so the LRPC is vertical at that rate. **[Conclusion.]**

## Common mistakes

- Writing each link in one direction only.
- Assuming a depreciation always restores competitiveness without comparing it with the inflation gap.
- Saying growth always worsens the current account.
- Drawing the LRPC at zero unemployment instead of at the natural rate.
- Stating that the Phillips curve shows inflation causes unemployment; it shows an association.
- Naming the Phillips curve without using it to explain the question's scenario.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Explain:** write the chain, one step per clause, and state the direction.
- **Analyse:** use an SRPC and LRPC diagram, or an AD/AS diagram, and refer to it.
- **Evaluate:** say what each link depends on, such as spare capacity, elasticities, the exchange-rate regime or how fast expectations adjust, and reach a judgement.
