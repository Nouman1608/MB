These questions cover **3.1.4.4 Bond enthalpies** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [bond enthalpies study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-4/) or the [bond enthalpies revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-4-revision-notes/) first.

Use these bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹) throughout:

| Bond | C–H | C–C | C=C | C–O | C=O | C≡O | O–H | O=O | O–O | H–H |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Value | 413 | 347 | 612 | 358 | 805 | 1077 | 463 | 498 | 146 | 436 |

| Bond | N≡N | N–N | N–H | N=O | Br–Br | C–Br |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Value | 945 | 158 | 391 | 631 | 193 | 290 |

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** The O–H value in the table is described as a *mean* bond enthalpy. Explain what this means. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate ΔH for N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g). **[2]**

**3.** Calculate ΔH for CH₂=CH₂(g) + Br₂(g) → CH₂BrCH₂Br(g). **[2]**

**4.** Explain why mean bond enthalpies should not be used directly to find the standard enthalpy of combustion of liquid ethanol. **[1]**

**5.** One mole of ethane burns: C₂H₆(g) + 3½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(g). State the number of moles of each type of bond broken. **[2]**

**6.** ΔfH for NH₃(g) is −46 kJ mol⁻¹. Use this and the N≡N and H–H values to calculate the N–H bond enthalpy. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Ethanol vapour burns: C₂H₅OH(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(g).

**(a)** List the bonds broken in one molecule of ethanol. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate ΔH for the reaction using mean bond enthalpies. **[3]**

**(c)** The standard enthalpy of combustion of ethanol is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. Give two reasons why your answer differs. **[2]**

**8.** The enthalpy change for CH₃CH=CH₂(g) + H₂(g) → CH₃CH₂CH₃(g) is −124 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Use this value, with the C–C, C–H and H–H values, to calculate the C=C bond enthalpy in propene. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why your answer is not exactly the same as the C=C value in the table. **[1]**

**9.** Hydrazine, H₂N–NH₂, reacts with oxygen: N₂H₄(g) + O₂(g) → N₂(g) + 2H₂O(g).

**(a)** Calculate ΔH for this reaction. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why it is valid to use bond enthalpies for this reaction as written. **[1]**

**10.** This question is about the definition and reliability of bond enthalpies.

**(a)** Define the term *mean bond enthalpy*. **[3]**

**(b)** A bond enthalpy estimate for H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g) agrees with the Hess's law value much more closely than the estimate for the combustion of propane does. Suggest why. **[2]**

**11.** Methanol can be made from carbon monoxide: CO(g) + 2H₂(g) → CH₃OH(g).

**(a)** Calculate ΔH using bond enthalpies. **[3]**

**(b)** ΔfH: CO(g) −111, CH₃OH(g) −201 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔH using Hess's law. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the two values differ. **[1]**

**12.** This question is about hydrogen peroxide gas, H₂O₂(g).

**(a)** Draw the displayed formula of H₂O₂. **[1]**

**(b)** Use bond enthalpies to estimate ΔfH for H₂O₂(g): H₂(g) + O₂(g) → H₂O₂(g). **[3]**

**(c)** The value from Hess's law is −136 kJ mol⁻¹. State why the bond enthalpy method gives only an approximate value. **[1]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** It is the energy needed to break one mole of O–H bonds in gaseous molecules [1]; averaged over many different compounds containing O–H bonds [1] **[2]**

**2.** Broken: 945 + 498 = 1443; formed: 2 × 631 = 1262 [1]; ΔH = 1443 − 1262 = **+181 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[2]**

**3.** Broken: C=C 612 + Br–Br 193 = 805; formed: C–C 347 + 2 × C–Br 290 = 927 [1]; ΔH = 805 − 927 = **−122 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* The four C–H bonds are unchanged, so they can be left out of both lists.

**4.** Bond enthalpies apply only to gases; the energy to vaporise the liquid ethanol (and to condense the water formed) would be ignored [1] **[1]**

**5.** 6 mol C–H and 1 mol C–C [1]; 3.5 mol O=O [1] **[2]**

**6.** ½(945) + 1½(436) − 3x = −46 [1]; 3x = 472.5 + 654 + 46 = 1172.5, so x = **391 kJ mol⁻¹** [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Five C–H [1]; one C–C, one C–O and one O–H [1]

**(b)** Broken: 5(413) + 347 + 358 + 463 + 3(498) = 4727 [1]; formed: 4(805) + 6(463) = 5998 [1]; ΔH = 4727 − 5998 = **−1271 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** Mean bond enthalpies are averages, not exact for the bonds in these molecules [1]; the standard value has liquid ethanol and liquid water, so it includes changes of state that the estimate leaves out [1] **[7]**

**8. (a)** Broken: x + 436; formed: C–C 347 + 2 × C–H 826 = 1173 [1]; x + 436 − 1173 = −124 [1]; x = **613 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** The table value is a mean over many compounds; the C=C bond in propene has its own slightly different value [1] **[4]**

**9. (a)** Broken: N–N 158 + 4 × N–H 1564 + O=O 498 = 2220 [1]; formed: N≡N 945 + 4 × O–H 1852 = 2797 [1]; ΔH = 2220 − 2797 = **−577 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** All the reactants and products are gases [1] **[4]**

**10. (a)** The enthalpy change to break one mole of a specified covalent bond [1]; in gaseous molecules [1]; averaged over a range of different compounds [1]

**(b)** H–H, Cl–Cl and H–Cl each occur in only one compound, so their values are exact, not means [1]; propane's C–H and C–C bonds and the C=O and O–H bonds use averaged values, and the standard combustion value involves liquid water [1] **[5]**

**11. (a)** Broken: C≡O 1077 + 2 × H–H 872 = 1949 [1]; formed: 3 × C–H 1239 + C–O 358 + O–H 463 = 2060 [1]; ΔH = 1949 − 2060 = **−111 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** ΔH = ΔfH(CH₃OH) − ΔfH(CO) = −201 − (−111) [1]; = **−90 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** The C–H, C–O and O–H values are means, not exact for the bonds in methanol [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* In (b), H₂ is an element, so it contributes zero; including 436 kJ mol⁻¹ for it confuses formation enthalpy with bond enthalpy.

**12. (a)** H–O–O–H, with every bond shown [1]

**(b)** Broken: H–H 436 + O=O 498 = 934 [1]; formed: O–O 146 + 2 × O–H 926 = 1072 [1]; ΔH = 934 − 1072 = **−138 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** The O–O and O–H values are means over many compounds, not exact for hydrogen peroxide [1] **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Subtracting broken from formed and getting the sign wrong.
- Missing a bond, or not multiplying by balancing numbers.
- Using C–O for C=O, or treating a double bond as two single bonds.
- Giving "heat loss" or "experimental error" as the reason for a difference from Hess's law.
- Forgetting "gaseous" in the definition.
