## Section A

**1.** In which year did the UN General Assembly adopt the Universal Declaration of Human Rights? A: 1945. B: 1948. C: 1950. D: 1953. **[1]**

**2.** Which organisation created the European Convention on Human Rights? A: the European Union. B: the United Nations. C: the Council of Europe. D: NATO. **[1]**

**3.** State two facts about the drafting or adoption of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the European Convention on Human Rights is dated 1953 although it was signed in 1950. **[2]**

**5.** Identify the two international covenants adopted by the United Nations in 1966. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain the significance of the Nuremberg trials for the development of human rights law. **[3]**

**7.** Explain the legal status of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and why it remains important. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the enforcement machinery of the European Convention on Human Rights has developed since 1953. **[5]**

**9.** Explain the difference between the Council of Europe and the European Union, and the effect of the United Kingdom's departure from the European Union on its obligations under the Convention. **[4]**

**10.** Compare the protection of rights under the Universal Declaration of Human Rights with protection under the European Convention on Human Rights. **[5]**

**11.** *A student writes: "After the war, the United Nations gave everyone in Britain the right to take the government to an international court if their human rights were breached."* Consider the accuracy of this statement. **[5]**

**12.** Discuss how far the Second World War and its aftermath explain the form taken by the European Convention on Human Rights. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B: 1948. **[1]**

**2.** C: the Council of Europe. **[1]**

**3.** Any two of: drafted by the **UN Commission on Human Rights chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt** [1]; contributors included **René Cassin** and **John Humphrey**; adopted by the **General Assembly in Paris on 10 December 1948**; the vote was **48 for, none against, eight abstentions** [1].

**4.** It was **signed in Rome on 4 November 1950** [1] but came **into force on 3 September 1953**, once the required ten states had ratified it [1].

**5.** The **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights** [1] and the **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights** [1].

**6.** The International Military Tribunal tried leading Nazis for crimes including **crimes against humanity** [1]. It established that **individuals are responsible under international law** [1], and that obeying orders or domestic law does not give a complete defence, so a state's own law is not the final measure of legality [1].

**7.** The UDHR is a **resolution of the UN General Assembly**, not a treaty [1], so it does **not bind states** as a treaty would [1]. It remains important because it set **common standards**, many argued to reflect **customary international law** [1], and it was the **model for later treaties**, including the ECHR and the 1966 Covenants [1].

**8.** In 1954 a **European Commission of Human Rights** was set up to filter applications [1], and in **1959** the **European Court of Human Rights** [1]. Originally, **individual petition** and the Court's jurisdiction were **optional**; the UK accepted both in **1966** [1]. **Protocol 11 (1998)** abolished the Commission and created a **single full-time Court** with compulsory individual petition [1]. The **Committee of Ministers** supervises the execution of judgments [1].

**9.** The Council of Europe, founded in 1949 with its seat in Strasbourg, is a **separate organisation from the European Union** [1], which has its own **Court of Justice in Luxembourg** and Charter of Fundamental Rights [1]. The ECHR is a Council of Europe treaty [1], so leaving the EU in 2020 did **not** affect the UK's obligations under the Convention, which it remains a party to [1].

**10.** The UDHR is **not binding**, whereas the ECHR is a **binding treaty** [1]. The UDHR has **no court**; the ECHR has the **European Court of Human Rights**, whose judgments bind the state concerned [1]. The UDHR covers **civil, political, economic and social** rights; the ECHR covers mainly **civil and political** rights [1]. The UDHR is **global**; the ECHR is **regional**, among Council of Europe states [1]. Conclusion: the UDHR is broader in scope, but the ECHR gives far stronger, enforceable protection [1].

**11.** The statement is inaccurate. The **UN** produced the **UDHR**, which is **not binding** and created **no court** [1]. The right to take a state to an international human rights court came from the **ECHR**, a **Council of Europe** treaty [1]. The Court was set up only in **1959** [1], and the UK accepted the **right of individual petition** only in **1966** [1]. It is accurate only in that the post-war period produced both systems [1].

**12.** Award one mark per developed point, to a maximum of 8:
- **State atrocities under domestic law** explain why the Convention binds states internationally rather than relying on national law [1].
- The **Nuremberg** emphasis on individual protection under international law influenced the idea of rights enforceable against states [1].
- The **UDHR** supplied the list of rights, and the ECHR drew heavily on its civil and political Articles [1].
- The Convention's focus on **civil and political** rights reflects the aim of preventing dictatorship and protecting democracy [1].
- **Cold War** concerns made Western European democracies keen to define shared values against communist states [1].
- The **qualified rights** model, with legitimate aims such as national security, reflects states' wish to keep some discretion [1].
- But some features came later, such as **compulsory individual petition** and the **full-time Court** under Protocol 11, so the form has developed since the war [1].
- A **reasoned conclusion**, for example that the war explains the Convention's purpose and content, while its enforcement machinery is the product of later development [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing the UN, the Council of Europe and the EU.
- Calling the UDHR binding or giving it a court.
- Mixing up the dates 1948, 1950, 1953 and 1966.
- Describing events without explaining their significance for rights.
- Leaving an essay without a conclusion.
