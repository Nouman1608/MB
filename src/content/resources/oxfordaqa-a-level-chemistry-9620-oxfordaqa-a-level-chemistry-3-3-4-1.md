## What the specification asks for

Two statements make up this subtopic:

- "Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons."
- "Bonding in alkenes involves a double covalent bond, a centre of high electron density."

The section introduction adds the consequence: "the high electron density of the carbon–carbon double bond leads to attack on these molecules by electrophiles." Your job is to explain that chain of ideas: structure → electron density → type of reagent attracted → type of reaction.

## Unsaturated hydrocarbons

A **hydrocarbon** contains carbon and hydrogen only. It is **unsaturated** if it contains at least one carbon–carbon multiple bond, so it could take up more atoms by addition. Alkanes are **saturated**: every carbon forms four single bonds.

For a chain (non-cyclic) alkene with one C=C, the general formula is **CₙH₂ₙ**: two hydrogen atoms fewer than the alkane with the same number of carbons, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

Two consequences of the formula are worth knowing:

- **Cycloalkanes share the formula CₙH₂ₙ.** Cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂, and hex-1-ene, C₆H₁₂, are structural isomers. A formula alone cannot tell you whether a compound is an alkene; a reaction such as decolourising bromine (in [3.3.4.2](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-4-2/)) can.
- **Each C=C removes two H.** Each ring also removes two H compared with the alkane. So a hydrocarbon with formula C₁₀H₁₆ is six H short of C₁₀H₂₂: it has three rings or double bonds in total.

## The double bond as a centre of high electron density

In a C=C bond, the two carbon atoms share **two pairs of electrons**: four electrons between two nuclei instead of the two in a C–C single bond. That concentration of negative charge is what the specification calls a **centre of high electron density**.

Some structural facts you already know from other sections fit with this:

- **Shape.** Each carbon of C=C has three bonding regions and no lone pair, so the arrangement around it is **trigonal planar**, with bond angles of about **120°** (see [shapes and intermolecular forces](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-shapes-polarity-and-intermolecular-forces/)). The two carbons and the four atoms attached to them lie in one plane.
- **Restricted rotation.** The double bond prevents rotation, which is why alkenes can show E/Z isomerism (see the [introduction to organic chemistry](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-introduction-to-organic-chemistry/)).
- **Bond enthalpy.** One data set gives mean bond enthalpies of 612 kJ mol⁻¹ for C=C and 348 kJ mol⁻¹ for C–C. The double bond is stronger than a single bond but **less than twice as strong** (2 × 348 = 696). Converting C=C into C–C during addition breaks only part of the double bond, which needs less energy than breaking a whole C–C bond.

## Why electrophiles, not nucleophiles

An **electrophile** is an **electron pair acceptor**. It is either positively charged or has a δ+ atom, so it is attracted to regions of high electron density. A nucleophile is the opposite: an electron pair donor, attracted to electron-deficient atoms.

| Compound type | Reactive site | Attacked by | Typical reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkane | none polar; C–C and C–H bonds | free radicals (with UV light) | substitution |
| Halogenoalkane | δ+ carbon of C–X | nucleophiles | substitution or elimination |
| Alkene | electron-rich C=C | **electrophiles** | **addition** |

A nucleophile carries its own lone pair and negative or δ− charge, so it is **repelled** by the electron-rich double bond. That is why OH⁻ or CN⁻ do not add to ethene under AS conditions, while HBr does.

The electrophiles in the 9620 alkene reactions are:

- the δ+ hydrogen of **H–Br** (bromine is more electronegative than hydrogen);
- the δ+ hydrogen of **H₂SO₄**;
- **Br₂**, which is non-polar on its own but becomes polarised as it approaches the double bond: the electron density of C=C repels the electrons in Br–Br, so the nearer bromine atom becomes δ+.

## From structure to reaction type

When an electrophile accepts a pair of electrons from the C=C bond, one of the two carbon–carbon bonds is used up and two new single bonds form, one to each carbon. Nothing is lost from the molecule, so the reaction is **addition**. Put together, alkenes undergo **electrophilic addition**. The name describes both the attacking species and what happens overall.

### Worked example 1: classifying reagents

*Which of these species could act as an electrophile towards propene: H⁺, Cl⁻, H₂O, Br₂, NH₃? Explain.*

- **H⁺**: positive, accepts an electron pair. **Electrophile.**
- **Br₂**: can be polarised by the double bond and its δ+ end accepts an electron pair. **Electrophile.**
- **Cl⁻**, **NH₃**, **H₂O**: each has a lone pair to donate and no tendency to accept one; they are nucleophiles and are repelled by the C=C electron density. (Water reacts with alkenes only when an acid catalyst supplies H⁺ first.)

### Worked example 2: how many double bonds?

*A hydrocarbon X has the formula C₁₀H₁₆ (Mr 136.0). 0.680 g of X reacts with exactly 1.60 g of bromine, Br₂ (Mr 159.8). Deduce the number of C=C bonds and the number of rings in X.*

```
n(X)   = 0.680 ÷ 136.0 = 0.00500 mol
n(Br₂) = 1.60 ÷ 159.8  = 0.0100 mol
ratio  Br₂ : X = 2 : 1  → each Br₂ adds across one C=C → 2 C=C
C₁₀H₂₂ − C₁₀H₁₆ = 6 H missing → 3 rings or double bonds in total
3 − 2 = 1 ring
```

Answer: X has **two C=C bonds and one ring**. The method combines the general formula (unsaturation) with the reaction that detects it.

## Common mistakes

- Defining unsaturated as "has a double bond" without saying **carbon–carbon**; C=O does not make a compound an unsaturated hydrocarbon.
- Saying alkenes are reactive "because the double bond is weak". The double bond is stronger than a single bond; it is reactive because it is **electron rich**.
- Calling an electrophile "an electron pair donor" (that is a nucleophile).
- Assuming every CₙH₂ₙ compound is an alkene; cycloalkanes share the formula.
- Describing Br₂ as polar. It is non-polar until the double bond induces a dipole.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** what is meant by *unsaturated*: "contains at least one carbon–carbon double (multiple) bond".
- **Explain** why alkenes react with electrophiles: four electrons in C=C → high electron density → attracts electron pair acceptors.
- **Explain** why bromine reacts even though it is non-polar: the C=C electron density repels the Br–Br bonding pair, inducing a dipole.
- **Deduce** structural information: convert masses to moles and use the 1 : 1 Br₂ : C=C ratio.
- **Compare** reactivity with alkanes: name the reactive site and the type of reagent in each case.
