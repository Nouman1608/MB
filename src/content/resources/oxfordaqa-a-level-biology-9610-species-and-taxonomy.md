This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.10 Species and taxonomy**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

The section sets up a definition, then asks you to criticise it, and then asks you to use molecular evidence to sort organisms into groups. The skills are definition, critical evaluation and data interpretation. Test yourself afterwards with the [species and taxonomy practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-species-and-taxonomy-practice/).

## The concept of a species

Species are the basic unit of biological classification. The specification gives this definition:

> A **species** is the largest group of organisms capable of interbreeding and producing fertile offspring.

Each part of the definition does a job:

- **"Interbreeding"** means that the members of a species can mate with each other.
- **"Fertile offspring"** is needed because some different species can mate and produce offspring that cannot reproduce. A mule, from a horse and a donkey, is a standard example. Horses and donkeys are separate species because their offspring are sterile.
- **"Largest group"** means that all the organisms that can interbreed are in one species, not split into smaller groups.

### Binomial naming

Each species is universally identified by a **binomial**: two Latin names, the **genus** (with a capital letter) followed by the **species** (with a lower-case letter). Binomials are printed in italics, or underlined when handwritten. For example, the human is *Homo sapiens*. The genus name alone is not enough, and a species name alone is not enough, because the same species word may be used in different genera.

The binomial system has two advantages: it is understood by scientists in every country whatever their language, and it removes confusion from common names, because one common name can refer to several species, and one species may have several common names.

### The difficulty of defining species

The specification asks you to **recognise the difficulty** of defining species and to examine the definition critically when given data. The definition fails in several kinds of case.

1. **Organisms that reproduce asexually.** Bacteria and many fungi do not interbreed, so the definition cannot be applied.
2. **Fossils and extinct organisms.** We cannot test whether two fossil forms could have interbred. Species are distinguished by their structure alone.
3. **Hybrids that are fertile.** Some closely related species interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring, for example some species of plants and of birds. By the definition they would be one species, although they look different and live separately.
4. **Geographically separated populations.** Two populations that live apart may be unable to interbreed in the wild because of distance, but could breed successfully in captivity. Whether they are the same species depends on the test used.
5. **Gradual variation.** In some cases, one population grades into another around a geographical ring (a ring species). Neighbouring populations interbreed, but the populations at the ends of the ring do not.
6. **Practical difficulties.** It is impossible to carry out breeding experiments on most organisms, so species are normally identified from appearance, behaviour and DNA.

**Worked example 1: examining the definition with data.** Two populations of frog, X and Y, live in separate ponds. In captivity, X and Y breed successfully, and the offspring are fertile. In the wild, they never meet. X frogs have a longer call than Y frogs.

1. By the definition, X and Y can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, so they are one species.
2. However, they never interbreed in the wild, so they may be evolving into different species.
3. The difference in call suggests behavioural isolation if they ever met.
4. **Conclusion:** the definition places them in one species on the evidence of captive breeding, but the situation shows the difficulty, because the result depends on whether the test is done in captivity or in the wild.

## Biological classification

**Biological classification** attempts to arrange species in groups that reflect their relationships and may reflect their evolutionary origins. It uses a **hierarchy**, in which smaller groups are placed within larger groups, and each group is a **taxon** (plural: taxa).

One hierarchy has eight taxa, from largest to smallest:

**domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.**

Each taxon contains organisms that share more features with each other than with the organisms of other taxa of the same level. A species is in a genus with other very similar species. Because the groups are nested, there is no overlap: a species belongs to one genus, a genus to one family, and so on.

**Worked example 2: a classification.** Humans can be placed as: Eukarya (domain), Animalia (kingdom), Chordata (phylum), Mammalia (class), Primates (order), Hominidae (family), *Homo* (genus), *sapiens* (species). The cat, *Felis catus*, shares the first four taxa and is in the order Carnivora. This tells you that humans are more closely related to other primates than to cats, since they share more taxa.

The traditional grouping used observable features. Modern classification uses molecular evidence, which can clarify relationships when appearance is misleading. Two unrelated organisms may look alike because they live in similar environments, a result of natural selection acting independently, and molecular data can show that they are not closely related.

## Using immunology to clarify relationships

The specification asks you to use given data to show how **immunology** and **genome sequencing** help to clarify taxonomic relationships.

**Immunology.** If a protein from species A is injected into a rabbit, the rabbit makes antibodies against it. When the antibodies are mixed with a protein from another species, the amount of precipitate indicates how similar the proteins are. A species whose proteins are more similar to those of species A reacts more strongly. A greater reaction means a closer relationship.

**Worked example 3.** Antibodies against human serum albumin were mixed with serum from four primates. The precipitate formed, as a percentage of the amount with human serum, was: species M, 97 %; species N, 85 %; species O, 75 %; species P, 60 % (illustrative data).

1. The reaction is strongest with species M, so its albumin is most similar to the human protein.
2. The proteins are similar because the amino acid sequences are similar, which reflects similar DNA.
3. The order of closeness to humans is M, N, O, P.
4. The pattern is evidence that M is the most recent relative of humans, but a single protein is a limited sample.

## Using genome sequencing to clarify relationships

DNA sequencing compares the **base sequences** of genes or whole genomes. Organisms that share a more recent common ancestor have had less time to accumulate differences, so their sequences are more alike. The same logic applies to the **amino acid sequences** of proteins coded for by those genes.

**Worked example 4.** Short sequences of the same gene from four species (20 bases):

- Species A: ATGGCTAAGCTGACCGTTAA
- Species B: ATGGCTAAGCTCACCGTAAA
- Species C: ATGGCCAGGCTCACAGTTGA
- Species D: ATGACCGAACTCTCAGAAGA

1. Compare each sequence with A, base by base.
2. A and B differ at 2 positions, so 18 of 20 bases match: 90 % similarity.
3. A and C differ at 5 positions: 75 % similarity.
4. A and D differ at 10 positions: 50 % similarity.
5. **Conclusion:** species B is the closest relative of A, then C, then D, which is the most distantly related. Short sequences are limited evidence, so longer sequences or several genes would give a more reliable conclusion.

Genome sequencing gives much more data than a single protein, and it can be used on organisms that cannot be examined in other ways. It has allowed many species to be reclassified.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a species as "a group of similar organisms". The definition needs interbreeding and fertile offspring.
- Writing binomials with a capital on the second word, or not italicising them.
- Saying that a mule shows that horses and donkeys are the same species. The mule is sterile.
- Putting the taxa in the wrong order. Check that domain is the largest and species the smallest.
- Saying that similar appearance proves a close relationship.
- Concluding that a very small difference in one protein proves a relationship, without noting the small sample.
- Describing the immunology test without linking the amount of precipitate to protein similarity.

## Exam technique

- **Quote the definition accurately**, then apply it to the data.
- **"Examine critically"** means to state what the data show under the definition and what the problem is.
- **For data on sequences**, calculate the percentages of matching bases or amino acids and order the organisms by similarity.
- **Use cautious wording**: "suggests", "is likely to be more closely related".

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to remember the taxa for particular organisms?**
No. You need to know the order of the hierarchy and be able to use classification data you are given.

**Why do scientists keep the definition if it has problems?**
It works well for most sexually reproducing animals and gives a clear, testable idea. Understanding its limits is part of the skill.
