Condensed recall for one element named in the 9675 Unit 3 content: "the victims of crime and the
inclusion of suffering". For the unit overview see the
[Unit 3 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-unit-3-elements-of-crime-and-mystery-study-guide/);
for the full explanation and a model paragraph see the
[victims and suffering study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-victims-and-suffering/).

## The idea in one box

- Crime stories pull attention to **criminals and detectives**; victims are often silent or dead.
- The **imbalance is a method**: what a text leaves out is analysable.
- Link to the specification's final element: crime stories create "suspense, repugnance,
  excitement and relief".
- Unit 3 is **closed book**, two essays on two texts, 25 marks each.

## Six questions

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Voice | Does the victim speak? Who tells their story? |
| Innocence | Blameless, compromised or guilty of something else? |
| Visibility | Shown, reported, implied or skipped? |
| Range | Families, communities, animals, nations, the criminal |
| Function | Person, clue, symbol or plot device? |
| Response | Pity, repugnance, suspense, guilty enjoyment? |

## Victims text by text

| Text | Victims | Angle worth arguing |
|---|---|---|
| *Macbeth* | Duncan (offstage), Banquo, Macduff's family, Scotland | Crime turning from political to cruel; criminals as victims of guilt |
| *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* | The albatross, the crew, the Mariner, the Wedding-Guest | Suffering as penance spreading to the listener |
| Browning | The Duchess, Porphyria, the rival in "The Laboratory", the slandered lady in "Count Gismond", Hamelin's children | Monologue silences the victim |
| *Oliver Twist* | Oliver, Nancy, and Sikes after the murder | Institutions as criminals; suffering made morally central |
| Poe | The old man, the cat and the wife, the Rue Morgue women, the Inquisition's prisoner | Victims reduced to objects or evidence; suffering from inside |
| Stevenson | The trampled child, Carew, the body on the table, the dealer | Victims as signs of hidden evil; conscience after the crime |
| *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* | Mrs Ferrars, Roger Ackroyd | Puzzle over pity; the narrator's calm made chilling |
| *Death of a Red Heroine* | Guan Hongying | Public image against private life; justice and political power |
| *When Will There Be Good News?* | A murdered family and its survivor; the young Reggie | The long aftermath of crime and trauma |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Use it to say |
|---|---|---|
| Victimology | The study of victims and their treatment | How a text values or ignores the harmed |
| Dramatic monologue | A poem spoken by one character to a silent listener | Why Browning's victims cannot answer |
| Objectification | Treating a person as a thing | How Poe and Browning reduce victims |
| Body as evidence | The corpse used to solve the mystery | When a victim becomes a clue |
| Aftermath | What follows the crime | Suffering beyond the act itself |
| Pathos | Writing that arouses pity | How a text makes suffering felt |
| Repugnance | Disgust or revulsion | The specification's term for one reader response |
| Complicity | Sharing in a wrong | Readers enjoying suspense at a victim's expense |

## Essay checklist

- A claim about how the text treats victims, not a list of them.
- Evidence from the first crime, a turning point and the ending.
- Method: point of view, onstage or offstage, description, other characters' speech.
- A counter-reading: silence as a deliberate gap the reader is meant to notice.
- Context only where it explains a choice (attitudes to women and children; state power).

## Exam traps

- Writing only about the criminal and the detective.
- Assuming victims are always innocent.
- Ignoring animals, communities or the criminal as sufferers.
- Outrage in place of analysis of how the response is created.
- Inaccurate memory quotations in a closed-book exam.

## Self-test

1. Quote the phrase from the 9675 Unit 3 content that names this element.
2. Why are victims easy to overlook in crime narratives?
3. Give the four reader responses the specification lists for crime stories.
4. Which of Duncan's and Banquo's deaths is shown on stage?
5. Who suffers in *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* besides the albatross?
6. Why can the Duchess in "My Last Duchess" not tell her story?
7. Which Browning poem gives a slandered woman a defender?
8. Name two kinds of victim in *Oliver Twist*.
9. How does "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" use the victims' injuries?
10. Who is the victim in *Death of a Red Heroine*, and what contrast does she embody?
11. Why is the narrator's calm account of the victims chilling in *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd*?
12. What is unusual about *When Will There Be Good News?* in its treatment of crime?

## Answers

1. "The victims of crime and the inclusion of suffering".
2. Attention goes to the criminal's motive and the detective's quest; victims are often dead,
   silent or reduced to evidence.
3. Suspense, repugnance, excitement and relief.
4. Banquo's death is shown on stage; Duncan's happens offstage.
5. The crew who die, the Mariner himself, and the Wedding-Guest who is changed by the tale.
6. The poem is a dramatic monologue spoken by the Duke, and she survives only as a painting he controls.
7. "Count Gismond".
8. Any two: Oliver as a child harmed by institutions; Nancy as murder victim; Sikes as a sufferer
   after his crime.
9. As evidence for Dupin's reasoning, which turns suffering into a puzzle.
10. Guan Hongying, a celebrated model worker whose public image hides a private life.
11. The narrator is revealed to be the murderer, so his calm account looks back as concealment.
12. It focuses on the long aftermath of crime, following a survivor into adult life.
