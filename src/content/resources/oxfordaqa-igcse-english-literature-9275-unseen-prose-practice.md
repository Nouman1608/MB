## Section A: short skills questions

Read Extract A, then answer Questions 1 to 6.

> **Extract A**
>
> The ferry was late, which meant that Grace had time to notice everything she had decided not to
> notice. The paint peeling from the railings. Her brother's hand, which kept going to his pocket to
> check the tickets. The gulls, who seemed to know exactly where they were going.
>
> "It's a good school," her brother said, for the third time since breakfast.
>
> "I know," she said. She did know. That was the problem.
>
> When the ferry finally came round the headland it was smaller than she had imagined, and much
> louder, and it did not wait.

**1.** Identify the narrative perspective of Extract A. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the effect of the list of things Grace notices in the first paragraph. **[4]**

**3.** What does the brother's repeated line, "for the third time since breakfast", suggest about
him? **[3]**

**4.** How does the writer use short sentences in "She did know. That was the problem." to reveal
Grace's feelings? **[4]**

**5.** Explain how the final sentence presents the ferry and what it suggests about Grace's
situation. **[4]**

**6.** The writer never says what Grace feels about leaving. Explain why this restraint can be
effective. **[2]**

**7.** A student writes: *"Grace is probably going to boarding school because her parents have
died."* Explain why this would not earn credit, and rewrite it as an analytical point. **[3]**

## Section B: exam-style questions

Read Extract B, then answer Question 8.

> **Extract B**
>
> Mrs Halloran had run the corner bakery for forty-one years, and on her last morning she arrived at
> four, as always, and turned on the ovens, as always, and tied her apron in a double knot that her
> hands made without asking her.
>
> The new owners would arrive at nine. They had been polite on the telephone. They had plans, they
> said: a coffee machine, a menu on a blackboard, little tables outside. Mrs Halloran had said that
> sounded lovely. She had said it twice.
>
> At six the first customer came in, a builder whose order she knew before he opened his mouth. She
> handed him the bag. He handed her the coins. Then he stood there a moment longer than usual, as if
> there were something else on the counter he was meant to collect.
>
> "Well," he said.
>
> "Well," she said.
>
> When he had gone she wiped the counter where his hands had been, although it was perfectly clean,
> and went to check on the bread, which was rising, as it always did, without any help from her at
> all.

**8.** How does the writer present Mrs Halloran's feelings on her last morning at the bakery? **[30]**

Read Extract C, then answer Question 9.

> **Extract C**
>
> The storm reached the valley an hour before the doctor did. By then the river had already crossed
> the road, carrying fence posts and a child's red boot, and the lights in the farmhouse had gone out
> twice and come back once.
>
> Inside, Leila held the torch while her grandfather argued with the radio. It hissed at him. He
> hissed back. Upstairs her mother had stopped calling out, and the silence was worse.
>
> When the headlights appeared at last, swinging across the yard like someone searching for a dropped
> key, Leila ran to the door before anyone could tell her not to. The rain hit her face like gravel.
> She did not notice. She was waving the torch so hard that its beam flew up into the trees and back
> down, up and back down, a small, frantic lighthouse in the dark.

**9.** Explore how the writer creates tension in Extract C. **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Third-person limited narration, close to Grace's perceptions [1].

**2.** Up to four marks. The list of sentence fragments ("The paint peeling...", "Her brother's
hand...", "The gulls...") slows the pace, mirroring the delay [1]. The details are small and
specific, showing a mind trying not to think about something larger [1]. The brother's checking hand
suggests his nervousness [1], and the gulls "who seemed to know exactly where they were going"
contrast with Grace's uncertainty [1].

**3.** Up to three marks. The repetition suggests he is trying to reassure himself as much as Grace
[1]; "for the third time" shows his anxiety or guilt [1]; it also implies that Grace has not been
convinced [1].

**4.** Up to four marks. "She did know" echoes her reply, emphasising that she understands the
argument [1]. "That was the problem" is blunt and slightly ironic [1]: knowing it is a good school
leaves her no reason to object, so her feelings have nowhere to go [1]. The short sentences show
restraint, as if she is holding back emotion [1].

**5.** Up to four marks. The ferry is "smaller than she had imagined", undercutting the build-up [1].
"And much louder" adds an overwhelming sensory detail [1]. The final clause, "and it did not wait",
personifies the ferry as indifferent [1], suggesting that events are moving on whether Grace is ready
or not [1].

**6.** Up to two marks. Leaving the feeling unstated makes the reader infer it from details [1], which
can make the emotion feel more real and powerful than a direct statement [1].

**7.** One mark for explaining that it speculates beyond the extract and is not supported by the text
[1]. Two marks for an analytical rewrite. Example: *"The writer hints that Grace is reluctant to
leave: her brother's insistence that 'It's a good school' suggests she needs persuading."* [2]

### Section B

These questions are marked holistically against level descriptors, not by adding up points. The
indicative content below shows what a high-level response might include; credit other valid
readings.

**8. Mrs Halloran.**

AO1:

- She feels sadness and loss, held in check by routine and politeness; the extract presents a quiet,
  dignified grief at the end of a long working life.

AO2:

- **Repetition of "as always"** stresses the comfort of habit and makes the final morning feel like
  any other, which deepens the poignancy.
- **"her hands made without asking her"** personifies her hands, showing that forty-one years of work
  have become part of her body.
- **Reported speech** for the new owners ("They had plans, they said") creates distance; the list of
  changes suggests a modern style that will replace hers.
- **"She had said it twice"**: the short sentence hints that her approval was forced.
- **The builder's pause**, "as if there were something else on the counter he was meant to collect",
  suggests an unspoken goodbye; the echoed "Well" shows two people unable to name the feeling.
- **Wiping a clean counter** shows a need to keep busy, or a wish to hold on to the moment.
- **The ending**: the bread rising "without any help from her at all" is gently ironic, suggesting
  both that life will continue without her and that she fears being unnecessary.
- **Structure**: time markers (four o'clock, six o'clock, and the owners due at nine) count down the
  last morning, with short dialogue lines at the emotional centre and a long, trailing final sentence.

AO3:

- A personal judgement on whether the extract is mainly sad, or also shows pride and acceptance,
  using accurate terminology.

Level guidance: a lower-level answer retells the morning with some comments on feeling; a mid-level
answer explains several methods clearly; a top-level answer explores how restraint, routine and the
ironic final image convey feelings that Mrs Halloran never speaks aloud.

**9. Tension in Extract C.**

AO1:

- The extract builds fear for the mother upstairs and a desperate hope for the doctor's arrival.

AO2:

- **The opening sentence** sets up a race between the storm and the doctor that the doctor has
  already lost.
- **Precise, unsettling details**: "a child's red boot" carried by the river is a small image of
  danger; the lights that "had gone out twice and come back once" create uncertainty.
- **Personification**: the radio "hissed", and the grandfather "hissed back", showing frustration and
  a breakdown of communication.
- **Withholding**: "her mother had stopped calling out, and the silence was worse" never explains what
  is wrong, so the reader imagines the worst.
- **The headlight simile**, "like someone searching for a dropped key", is oddly ordinary, contrasting
  with the crisis.
- **Pace**: short sentences ("She did not notice.") and repetition ("up and back down, up and back
  down") convey urgency.
- **The final metaphor**: "a small, frantic lighthouse in the dark" presents Leila as a guide and a
  signal of hope, but "small" and "frantic" keep the tension high.
- **Structure**: three paragraphs move from outside, to inside, to the threshold, and the extract ends
  before the outcome is known.

AO3:

- A personal response to the ending, for example that the unresolved close leaves the reader sharing
  Leila's suspense.

Level guidance: lower-level answers describe the storm; mid-level answers explain how several methods
create tension; top-level answers explore how withholding, pace and structure work together, and why
the writer ends before resolution.
