## What the syllabus lists

**6.1 Intelligence and educational attainment**
- The difficulties in defining intelligence.
- IQ tests and the extent to which they are influenced by social factors.
- Intelligence as an influence on educational attainment.

**6.2 Social class and educational attainment**
- The relationship between material factors and educational attainment.
- Cultural explanations, including parental attitudes, values, speech codes and cultural capital.
- In-school factors, including labelling, ability grouping and pupil subcultures.
- Compensatory education programmes.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Intelligence | A general ability to learn, reason and solve problems; no agreed definition |
| IQ | A score from a standardised test, scaled so the population average is 100 |
| g | One general ability thought to underlie performance on many tests |
| Reification | Treating an abstract idea, such as IQ, as if it were a real object |
| Cultural bias | Test items that assume knowledge more common in one group |
| Hereditarian view | Differences in intelligence, and so attainment, are mainly inherited |
| Material deprivation | Lack of money and what it buys: housing, diet, resources |
| Cultural deprivation | The claim that some homes lack the attitudes, values and language schools need |
| Immediate gratification | Seeking rewards now rather than waiting for larger ones later |
| Restricted code | Short, context-bound speech relying on shared understanding |
| Elaborated code | Explicit, detailed speech that makes sense out of context |
| Cultural capital | Knowledge, tastes and skills that schools reward as if they were ability |
| Labelling | Attaching a fixed identity to a pupil |
| Self-fulfilling prophecy | A prediction that comes true because people act on it |
| Ability grouping | Streaming, banding or setting pupils by judged ability |
| Educational triage | Focusing effort on pupils near a pass and writing off others |
| Polarisation | Pupils splitting into pro- and anti-school groups |
| Compensatory education | Extra resources to make up for disadvantage outside school |

## Theories and studies at a glance

### 6.1 Intelligence

| Who | What | Finding or claim | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binet and Simon (1905) | First intelligence test, France | Designed to identify children needing remedial help | Built for diagnosis, later used for selection |
| Spearman (1904) | Factor analysis | A general factor, g, underlies mental tests | Gould (1981) called g a reification |
| Gardner, *Frames of Mind* (1983) | Multiple intelligences | Linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial and more | Hard to test |
| Sternberg, *Beyond IQ* (1985) | Triarchic theory | Analytical, creative, practical intelligence | Shows IQ tests are narrow |
| Eleven-plus, England | Selection after the Education Act 1944 | Early items on servants and composers favoured middle-class children | Redesign as an IQ-style test did not end the class pattern |
| Flynn (1987) | IQ trends in 14 nations | Large score gains over time | Too fast for genes, so environment matters |
| Kamin (1974) | Critique of Burt's twin data | Correlations identical to three decimal places | Hearnshaw (1979) judged the post-war data unreliable or fraudulent |
| Jensen (1969) | Hereditarian | About 80% of IQ variation genetic; Head Start failed | Heritability within a group cannot explain group differences |
| Herrnstein and Murray (1994) | *The Bell Curve* | IQ predicts outcomes better than parental status | Highly controversial; ignores class effects on scores |

### 6.2 Social class

| Who | What | Finding or claim | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gewirtz, Ball and Bowe (1995) | School choice | Middle-class parents used choice to keep their advantage; working-class choice more limited | Shows money and knowledge combine |
| Douglas (1964) | Parental attitudes | Working-class parents less involved in schooling | Lack of time is not lack of interest |
| Hyman | Values | Working class valued education less, saw fewer chances | Stereotypes a varied class |
| Sugarman | Values | Fatalism, collectivism, immediate gratification, present-time orientation | May reflect real limits, not choice |
| Bernstein (1971) | Speech codes | Schools use the elaborated code | He denied the restricted code was inferior |
| Keddie (1973) | Critique | Culturally different, not deprived | Shifts blame to schools |
| Labov | Critique | Non-standard speech carries complex logic | Supports "difference, not deficit" |
| Bourdieu and Passeron | Cultural capital | Schools reward middle-class culture as ability | Hard to measure |
| Sullivan (2001) | 465 pupils, four schools | Reading linked to vocabulary and attainment | Class effect remained after cultural capital |
| Lareau (2003) | *Unequal Childhoods* | Concerted cultivation vs natural growth | Small number of families |
| Becker (1952) | Chicago teachers | "Ideal pupil" matched middle-class behaviour | Old, US-based |
| Rist (1970) | Kindergarten | Tables set by day eight, by appearance and background | One classroom |
| Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968) | Field experiment | "Bloomers" chosen at random gained most when young | Weak methods; replication problems |
| Ball (1981) | *Beachside Comprehensive* | Non-manual pupils more likely in top band | One school |
| Gillborn and Youdell (2000) | Two British secondary schools | Educational triage; setting and tiering | Shows policy pressures shape labels |
| Hargreaves (1967) | Streaming | Low-stream "failures" formed a delinquent subculture | Deterministic |
| Lacey (1970) | *Hightown Grammar* | Differentiation leads to polarisation | Pupils still have agency |
| Willis (1977) | 12 "lads" | Counter-school culture | Small sample; lads chose their path |
| Head Start (1965) | US pre-school | Early gains often fade | Perry Preschool found later-life gains |
| Plowden Report (1967) | England | Positive discrimination for deprived areas | Assumes cultural deficit |
| Sure Start (1998), Pupil Premium (2011) | UK policy | Early-years support; money per disadvantaged pupil | Small sums against wider inequality |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Intelligence vs IQ.** IQ is a test score. Intelligence is the idea the score tries to measure.
- **Hereditarian vs environmental.** Is the gap about genes, or about resources, culture and schools?
- **Material vs cultural.** Lacking money vs lacking (or not having rewarded) attitudes, language and knowledge.
- **Cultural deprivation vs cultural capital.** Deprivation blames the home; cultural capital blames the school for rewarding one culture.
- **Restricted vs elaborated code.** Context-bound and implicit vs explicit and context-free.
- **Home factors vs in-school factors.** Outside the school gate vs labelling, grouping and subcultures inside it.
- **Streaming vs setting.** Streaming groups pupils for all subjects; setting groups them subject by subject.
- **Short-term vs long-term success.** Compensatory programmes may raise later-life outcomes but not test scores.

## Mini essay plan

**Question:** Evaluate the view that cultural factors are the main cause of social class differences in educational attainment.

1. **Introduction.** Define cultural factors (attitudes, values, speech codes, cultural capital). Note the rival explanations: material, in-school and intelligence.
2. **For: cultural deprivation.** Douglas on parental involvement; Sugarman on values; Bernstein on speech codes. Link: home culture shapes readiness for school.
3. **For: cultural capital.** Bourdieu; Lareau's concerted cultivation; Sullivan's link between reading and attainment.
4. **Against: deprivation is a myth.** Keddie and Labov: difference, not deficit. Parents may lack time, not interest.
5. **Against: material factors.** Housing, diet, hidden costs, part-time work, school choice (Gewirtz, Ball and Bowe). Sullivan's class effect remained after cultural capital.
6. **Against: in-school factors.** Becker, Rist, Ball and Gillborn and Youdell show schools produce failure. Hargreaves and Willis on subcultures.
7. **Against: intelligence.** Hereditarian claims (Jensen; Herrnstein and Murray), but IQ is itself shaped by class.
8. **Conclusion.** Judge which factor carries most weight. Cultural factors matter, but they combine with material resources and school processes. Cultural capital explains how schools convert class into "ability".

## Quick self-test

1. Give two reasons why intelligence is hard to define.
2. What did Binet and Simon design their 1905 test to do?
3. What does the Flynn effect suggest about IQ?
4. What problem did Kamin find in Burt's twin data?
5. Name Lareau's two parenting styles.
6. What did Sullivan find after controlling for cultural capital?
7. Describe one difference between the restricted and elaborated codes.
8. What did Rist observe by the eighth day of kindergarten?
9. What is educational triage?
10. What did the Plowden Report recommend?
11. Give one criticism of compensatory education.

### Answers

1. Psychologists disagree on whether it is one ability (g) or several (Gardner, Sternberg); and any test measures only the version its designers chose.
2. Identify children who needed remedial help within ordinary schools.
3. Scores rose too fast for genetic change, so environment shapes IQ.
4. His correlations stayed identical to three decimal places even when new twins were added.
5. Concerted cultivation and the accomplishment of natural growth.
6. A large direct effect of social class on attainment remained.
7. The restricted code relies on shared context; the elaborated code spells meanings out.
8. The teacher had placed children at three tables, largely by appearance and background.
9. Focusing resources on pupils near a pass while writing off others.
10. A national policy of "positive discrimination" in favour of schools in deprived areas.
11. It can assume a cultural deficit in working-class homes, or it offers small resources against wide inequality.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining intelligence as "what IQ tests measure" with no comment on why that is a problem.
- Saying IQ tests are biased without giving a social factor (language, cultural content, coaching).
- Presenting Jensen or Herrnstein and Murray without any criticism.
- Claiming Bernstein said working-class speech is inferior.
- Using "cultural capital" when the point being made is about cultural deprivation.
- Describing Rosenthal and Jacobson as a study of social class. It was about teacher expectations.
- Treating labelling and subcultures as inevitable. Always note that pupils can resist.
- Writing about one factor only in an essay that asks you to weigh explanations.
- Listing compensatory programmes with no judgement on whether they worked.
