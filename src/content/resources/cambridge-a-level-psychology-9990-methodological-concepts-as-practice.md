**This practice set covers the methodological concepts in section 3.1.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A has short-answer questions, some linked to core studies. Section B applies the concepts to two fictional scenarios, one with invented data. No calculations are needed.

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Write a directional (one-tailed) hypothesis for the study by Andrade. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis. **[2]**

**3.** Milgram used a volunteer sample. Explain **one** weakness of this sampling technique for his study. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by *demand characteristics*, using an example from a core study. **[2]**

**5.** Outline **one** way in which Bandura et al. controlled participant variables. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the guideline of *replacement* could not easily be met in the study by Fagen et al. **[2]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by *test-retest reliability*. **[2]**

**8.** Outline the difference between subjective and objective data, using the study by Dement and Kleitman as an example. **[3]**

## Section B: scenario questions

**9. (a)** A psychologist tests whether students remember more words from a list when they learn it in silence or with background chatter. Identify the IV and operationalise the DV. **[3]**

**9. (b)** Identify **one** situational variable that should be controlled in this study, and explain how. **[2]**

**9. (c)** The students are the first 20 people the psychologist meets in the college library. Name this sampling technique and explain **one** reason why it may limit generalisation. **[3]**

**10. (a)** The table shows invented results from the study in Question 9 (number of words recalled out of 20).

| Condition | Scores |
|---|---|
| Silence | 9, 11, 12, 12, 13, 14, 16 |
| Chatter | 4, 6, 8, 8, 8, 11, 14 |

Find the mode and the range for each condition. **[4]**

**10. (b)** The standard deviation for the chatter condition is larger than for the silence condition. Explain what this tells you. **[2]**

**10. (c)** State which type of graph should be used to show the mean number of words recalled in each condition, and explain why. **[2]**

**11.** A researcher plans to observe pupils' behaviour in school playgrounds without their knowledge. Explain **two** ethical issues and how the researcher could address them. **[4]**

**12.** Two researchers rate the aggressiveness of the same children from video recordings and their ratings differ widely. Explain what this shows and suggest **one** way to improve it. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 2 marks. 1 mark for direction, 1 for operationalised variables. For example: "Participants who shade shapes while listening to a telephone message will recall more names and places in a surprise test than participants who do not shade shapes."

**2.** Up to 2 marks. A null hypothesis predicts no difference or no relationship between variables (1). An alternative hypothesis predicts that there will be a difference or relationship, which may be directional or non-directional (1).

**3.** Up to 2 marks. People who answer an advertisement for a study on memory and learning may be more curious, cooperative or keen to help than others (1), so they may have been more obedient than the general population, which limits generalisation of the 65% figure (1).

**4.** Up to 2 marks. Cues in a study that reveal its aim, so participants change their behaviour to fit what they think is expected (1). For example, in Bandura et al. children may have hit the Bobo doll because the toy seemed designed for it, believing this was what the adults wanted (1).

**5.** Up to 2 marks. Children were matched on their existing level of aggression (1), using ratings by an experimenter and a nursery teacher, so that groups started with similar aggression and differences after the model could not be due to prior aggressiveness (1).

**6.** Up to 2 marks. Replacement means using alternatives to animals where possible (1). The aim was to train elephants themselves to take part in a trunk wash for their own health care, so the research question could only be answered with elephants (1).

**7.** Up to 2 marks. Giving the same test to the same participants on two occasions (1); if the scores are similar both times, the test is consistent, so it has test-retest reliability (1).

**8.** Up to 3 marks. Objective data are not affected by personal opinion (1), such as the EEG recordings of REM and NREM sleep (1). Subjective data depend on personal interpretation, such as participants' descriptions of their dreams, which cannot be checked (1).

**9. (a)** Up to 3 marks. IV: whether the word list is learned in silence or with background chatter (1). DV operationalised: the number of words (1) correctly written down from a 20-word list in two minutes immediately after learning (1).

**9. (b)** Up to 2 marks. For example: time of day (1), controlled by testing all participants at the same time, such as mid-morning (1). Or: room temperature or lighting, kept the same; the length of time allowed to learn the list.

**9. (c)** Up to 3 marks. Opportunity sampling (1). People in the library may be more studious or better at memory tasks than other students (1), so the sample may not represent all students and results may not generalise (1).

**10. (a)** 1 mark for each correct value. Silence: mode 12 (1), range 16 − 9 = 7 (1). Chatter: mode 8 (1), range 14 − 4 = 10 (1).

**10. (b)** Up to 2 marks. Standard deviation shows how far scores are spread around the mean (1); a larger standard deviation in the chatter condition means that participants' scores there varied more from the mean, so people differed more in how much chatter affected them (1).

**10. (c)** Up to 2 marks. A bar chart (1), because the two conditions are separate categories rather than continuous data (1).

**11.** 2 marks for each issue with a solution. Consent: pupils do not know they are observed, and children cannot consent themselves (1), so the researcher should obtain consent from the school and parents beforehand (1). Privacy and confidentiality: pupils may be recorded in a way that identifies them (1), so the researcher should record no names, observe only public areas and store data securely (1). Accept debriefing (explain the study to pupils and parents afterwards).

**12.** Up to 3 marks. The study has low inter-rater reliability (1), because the researchers are not applying the measure consistently (1). Improvement: agree clear operational definitions of aggressive acts, such as "hits with an open or closed hand", and train both raters on practice videos before scoring (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Will I be asked to calculate a mean?**
No. The syllabus says you will not be required to carry out calculations; you must know how to find the mode, median and range and recognise and interpret the mean and standard deviation.

**Is a correlational hypothesis written differently?**
Yes. It predicts a relationship between co-variables (positive, negative or simply "a correlation") rather than a difference between conditions.
