These questions cover section 2.1 (microeconomics and macroeconomics) of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. All countries, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [microeconomics and macroeconomics study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-microeconomics-and-macroeconomics/) and the [microeconomics and macroeconomics revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-microeconomics-and-macroeconomics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which of the following is a macroeconomic issue? **[1]**

A: The wage paid to hotel cleaners

B: The price of mobile phones

C: The country's rate of inflation

D: A bakery's decision to open a second shop

**2.** Which decision maker is most closely linked to macroeconomics? **[1]**

A: A trade union representing teachers

B: A central bank

C: A household choosing a holiday

D: A farmer choosing which crop to plant

**3.** Define macroeconomics. **[2]**

**4.** Identify whether each of the following is microeconomic or macroeconomic. **[3]**

**(i)** A fall in the number of people employed in the country as a whole.

**(ii)** A government subsidy for electric buses.

**(iii)** A rise in the price of cinema tickets in one city.

**5.** State **two** decisions a firm makes that are studied in microeconomics. **[2]**

**6.** Country Sorrel (invented) has total output of 800 million. Its tourism industry produces 120 million.

**(a)** Calculate tourism's share of total output. **[1]**

**(b)** Tourism output falls by 20%. Calculate the percentage fall in total output, assuming other industries are unchanged. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Explain the difference between microeconomics and macroeconomics, using the example of employment. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why a government tax on cigarettes is usually treated as a microeconomic decision, while a rise in income tax is treated as a macroeconomic decision. **[4]**

**9.** Identify **two** microeconomic decision makers and explain one decision each makes. **[4]**

**10.** A large car factory in Country Sorrel closes, and 6,000 workers lose their jobs. Analyse how this event can be studied both as a microeconomic issue and as a macroeconomic issue. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how a rise in interest rates set by a central bank can affect microeconomic decisions by households and firms. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether it is useful to separate economics into microeconomics and macroeconomics. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. Inflation is a change in the general price level across the whole economy; the others concern one market or one firm. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A central bank sets interest rates for the whole economy; the others are individual decision makers. **[1]**

**3.** The study of the economy as a whole [1], such as total output, the general price level, total unemployment and trade with other countries [1]. **[2]**

**4.** (i) Macroeconomic [1]. (ii) Microeconomic, because it targets one market [1]. (iii) Microeconomic [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any two: what to produce [1]; how much to produce, what price to charge, which production method to use or how many workers to employ [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 120 ÷ 800 × 100 = **15%** [1].

**(b)** Fall in tourism = 120 × 0.2 = 24 million [1]; 24 ÷ 800 × 100 = **3%** fall in total output [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Microeconomics studies individual decision makers and markets [1], so for employment it looks at, for example, how many workers one firm hires or the wages in one occupation [1]. Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole [1], so it looks at total employment and the national unemployment rate [1]. **[4]**

**8.** A tax on cigarettes affects mainly one market [1]: it raises the price and reduces the quantity of one product [1]. A rise in income tax affects the incomes of most households across the economy [1], so it changes total spending, which is a macroeconomic matter; it is part of fiscal policy [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Any two decision makers with a matching decision. Households [1]: decide how to divide income between spending and saving [1]. Firms [1]: decide what price to charge for their product [1]. Other acceptable: workers choosing an occupation; trade unions negotiating wages. **[4]**

**10.** Micro: the car firm has decided to reduce output, perhaps because demand for its cars has fallen [1]; local suppliers lose orders and may cut their own workforce [1]; the 6,000 workers must look for new jobs, and wages in the local labour market may fall [1]. Macro: the job losses add to total unemployment and may raise the national unemployment rate [1]; lower incomes reduce total spending, so total output may fall [1]; if the cars were exported, the country's export earnings fall, affecting the balance of payments [1]. **[6]**

**11.** A higher interest rate raises the cost of borrowing [1], so households may delay buying goods on credit, such as cars or furniture [1]. Saving becomes more rewarding, so some households save more of their income [1]. Firms may postpone investment in new machinery because loans cost more [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Useful: it lets economists focus on one level at a time, such as one market's price or the whole economy's inflation [1]; different decision makers and policies apply at each level, for example a tax on one good versus interest rates [1]; it organises government aims, such as low unemployment, separately from firms' aims [1]. Limits: the two are linked, because macro totals are built from micro decisions [1]; a micro event such as a large factory closure can affect macro data [1]. Conclusion: the separation is a useful starting point, but good analysis often needs both, as when tracing how a change in one industry affects the whole economy [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* In "Discuss", end with a judgement that explains when the separation helps and when it does not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling every government decision macroeconomic.
- Using "small" and "large" as the definition rather than individual markets and the whole economy.
- In Question 6, dividing the fall in tourism by tourism output (20%) when the question asks about total output.
- Giving only the micro or only the macro side of an event when both are asked for.
- Naming a decision maker without the decision it makes.
