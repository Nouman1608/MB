> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the effectiveness of treatment in the
> health and well-being context (IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper
> questions. Question 13 uses the 15-mark tariff of Paper 1 section C; the others use practice
> tariffs. The trial in Question 9 is invented, with invented figures.

Related: [Assessing treatment effectiveness study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-assessing-treatment-effectiveness/),
[revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-assessing-treatment-effectiveness-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between efficacy and effectiveness. **[2]**

**2.** Define "relapse". **[1]**

**3.** Explain why a wait-list control group is used in therapy trials. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what is meant by "publication bias". **[2]**

**5.** State two reasons why symptoms of depression may improve in a group that receives no active treatment. **[2]**

**6.** State one finding of the TADS Team (2004) study. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain why it is difficult to run a double-blind trial of cognitive behavioural therapy, and suggest one way to reduce bias in such a trial. **[3]**

**8.** Using Hollon et al. (2005), explain why the time at which outcomes are measured can change conclusions about a treatment. **[4]**

**9.** Invented trial: 120 adults with depression were randomly allocated to an antidepressant (60) or a placebo (60) for eight weeks. At eight weeks, 33 in the antidepressant group and 21 in the placebo group met the criterion for response. Twelve people dropped out of the antidepressant group, mostly reporting side effects; the results above include only those who completed the trial.
(a) Calculate the response rate in each group as a percentage of those randomised.
(b) Explain why analysing only those who completed the trial could overestimate the drug's effectiveness.
(c) Suggest one further outcome that would help judge the drug's value. **[6]**

**10.** Explain one strength and one limitation of meta-analysis as a way of assessing treatment effectiveness. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why researcher allegiance should be considered when evaluating a study comparing two psychological therapies. **[2]**

**12.** A clinic manager reads that a therapy produced large improvements in a randomised controlled trial run at a university. Explain two reasons why the clinic may not see the same results. **[4]**

**13.** Evaluate research on the effectiveness of two treatments for one disorder, with reference to the concept of change. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Efficacy is whether a treatment works under ideal, controlled trial conditions **[1]**; effectiveness is whether it works in ordinary clinical practice with typical patients and therapists **[1]**.

**2.** The return of symptoms after a period of improvement or recovery **[1]**.

**3.** A wait-list group receives no treatment during the trial but is treated afterwards **[1]**, so it controls for improvement due to the passage of time, such as spontaneous remission, while still allowing everyone to be treated eventually **[1]**.

**4.** Studies with positive results are more likely to be published than those with negative or null results **[1]**, so the published literature overestimates how well a treatment works **[1]**.

**5.** Spontaneous remission over time **[1]**; regression to the mean, because people often enter trials when symptoms are at their worst **[1]**.

**6.** For example: the combination of fluoxetine and CBT produced the greatest improvement at 12 weeks **[1]**. Also creditable: fluoxetine was better than placebo; CBT alone was not clearly better than placebo at 12 weeks.

**7.** The client and the therapist both know which therapy is being delivered, so neither can be blind **[2]**. Bias can be reduced by having symptoms rated by an assessor who does not know which condition each participant was in **[1]**.

**8.** DeRubeis et al. (2005) found cognitive therapy and medication equally effective at the end of 16 weeks **[1]**. Hollon et al. (2005) followed responders after treatment stopped **[1]** and found those who had received cognitive therapy were less likely to relapse than those switched from medication to placebo **[1]**. A study measuring only at the end of treatment would conclude the treatments were equal, while follow-up favours cognitive therapy **[1]**.

**9.** (a) Antidepressant: 33 / 60 = 55% **[1]**; placebo: 21 / 60 = 35% **[1]**.
(b) The twelve who dropped out because of side effects are people for whom the drug was not acceptable **[1]**; if completers alone are analysed, the response rate becomes 33 / 48 = 68.75%, making the drug look more effective than it was for everyone allocated to it **[1]**.
(c) For example, relapse rate at a later follow-up **[1]**, because response at eight weeks does not show whether improvement lasts **[1]**.

**10.** Strength: combining many trials gives a large sample and a more reliable estimate of the average effect than a single study **[2]**. Limitation: if unpublished negative trials are missing, the combined estimate inherits publication bias; Kirsch et al. (2008) avoided this by using regulator data **[2]**.

**11.** Researchers often obtain better results for the therapy they prefer or developed, as Luborsky et al. (1999) reported **[1]**, so a comparison run by supporters of one therapy may overstate its advantage over the other **[1]**.

**12.** Trial samples often exclude people with other disorders, substance use or suicidal thoughts **[1]**, so the clinic's patients may be more complex and respond less well **[1]**. Therapists in trials are usually highly trained and closely supervised **[1]**, while clinic therapists may have less training and larger caseloads, so the therapy may be delivered less faithfully **[1]**.

**13.** Marked with the Paper 1 section C markbands (15 marks), which reward knowledge and understanding of the content and concept, analysis with explained links between concepts, a reasoned conclusion consistent with the arguments, and accurate use of psychological terminology. Indicative content:
- **Knowledge and understanding:** names the disorder and two treatments; brief outline of SSRIs and CBT; efficacy and effectiveness; response, remission and relapse; links to the concept of change (intervention and the effectiveness of treatment).
- **Use of evidence as examples:** two or three of TADS Team (2004), DeRubeis et al. (2005), Hollon et al. (2005) and Kirsch et al. (2008), used to illustrate points about effectiveness. The guide states that the details of research studies are not assessed in section C, so studies support the argument rather than being described at length.
- **Analysis:** blinding in therapy trials; time of measurement; publication bias; trial samples versus clinic patients; generalising from adolescents to adults.
- **Conclusion:** a reasoned judgement that answers the question, for example that medication may act faster while CBT may give more lasting protection against relapse, and that no single trial settles the question.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 5:** giving "placebo effect" twice in different words.
- **Question 9(a):** dividing by the number who completed rather than the number randomised.
- **Question 9(b):** saying drop-out is "unethical" instead of explaining its effect on the result.
- **Question 12:** repeating "the sample was small" without explaining how trial and clinic patients differ.
- **Question 13:** spending half the essay describing CBT techniques.
