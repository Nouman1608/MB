Sub-topic 6.4 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus (2027-2029) is the last of the four natural hazards. It has five outcomes: a definition (6.4.1), causes (6.4.2), impacts (6.4.3), strategies for managing the impacts before, during and after a drought (6.4.4), and a discussion of their benefits and limitations (6.4.5). Drought differs from the other hazards in Topic 6 because it builds up slowly over months or years and has no clear start or end, and that difference shapes how it is managed. This guide explains each outcome, works through two data examples and models a discussion answer.

For an overview of all four hazards, see the [natural hazards study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-natural-hazards-study-guide/).

## Definition (6.4.1)

Learn the syllabus wording exactly: a drought is **a period of dry weather that is longer or more severe than normal**.

Two points in the definition matter in answers. First, drought is measured against what is *normal* for that place: a month without rain is ordinary in a desert but would be a drought in a rainforest. Second, it can be *longer* than normal (the dry season lasts months beyond its usual end) or *more severe* (much less rain than usual over the same period).

## Causes (6.4.2)

The syllabus limits causes to two.

**(a) Lack of rain.** Rain fails when the weather systems that usually bring it do not arrive or are weaker than usual. For example, a seasonal rain belt may arrive late or bring less rain, or a high-pressure system may stay over an area for weeks, bringing sinking, dry air and clear skies.

**(b) Climate change: increase in global temperatures and increase in extreme weather events.**
- Higher temperatures increase evaporation from soils, rivers and reservoirs and transpiration from plants, so water is lost faster even if rainfall stays the same.
- An increase in extreme weather events means longer and more intense dry spells in some regions, as well as heavier rain in others.

Note that the syllabus does not list human water use as a cause of drought. Over-use makes the *impacts* of a drought worse, which you can mention when discussing management, but the causes in 6.4.2 are lack of rain and climate change.

## Worked example 1: is it a drought?

*The figures are invented for practice.* A farming region has a mean annual rainfall of 600 mm. Over three years it receives 540 mm, 410 mm and 330 mm.

- Year 1: 600 - 540 = 60 mm below normal, or 60 / 600 x 100 = **10%** below.
- Year 2: 600 - 410 = 190 mm below, or **31.7%** below.
- Year 3: 600 - 330 = 270 mm below, or **45%** below.

The rainfall is below normal every year and falls further each year, so the dry period is both longer and more severe than normal: it meets the definition. Questions like this reward using the definition and quoting the values.

## Impacts (6.4.3)

The syllabus lists ten impacts. They link together, so learn them as chains rather than a list.

- **Water sources dry up:** rivers, lakes, wells and reservoirs fall or dry out, leaving people and animals without water.
- **Loss of crops, livestock and habitats:** crops wilt and fail, animals die of thirst or hunger, and wetlands dry out.
- **Soil erosion:** dry soil with little plant cover is easily blown away by wind or washed away when rain returns.
- **Desertification:** if drought and soil loss continue, land in dry regions becomes desert-like and stops being productive.
- **Death of organisms:** fish die as rivers shrink and warm; plants and animals that cannot move die.
- **Famine:** when harvests fail over a wide area and food cannot be bought or brought in, people face a severe shortage of food.
- **Increased risk of wildfires:** dry vegetation burns easily and fires spread fast.
- **Decrease in air quality:** dust from bare soil and smoke from wildfires pollute the air.
- **Financial losses:** lost harvests and livestock, higher food prices, the cost of bringing in water, and less income for businesses that rely on water.
- **Human health:** dehydration, malnutrition, breathing problems from dust and smoke, and disease where people must drink unsafe water.

A strong chain: lack of rain → crops and grazing fail → livestock die and harvests fail → famine → malnutrition and disease.

## Strategies before, during and after (6.4.4)

The syllabus lists five strategies, one of which has four parts.

**(a) Monitoring and warning.** Rainfall, soil moisture, river and reservoir levels are measured over time, and satellites show the health of vegetation. Because drought builds up slowly, monitoring can give months of warning, so farmers can plant drought-tolerant crops or sell livestock early, and governments can plan food and water supplies.

**(b) Emergency water supplies.** Water is brought in by tanker, bottled or piped from elsewhere during the drought. This keeps people alive and healthy but is costly and short-term.

**(c) Water conservation.** Using less water: limits on garden watering and car washing, repairing leaking pipes, water-efficient taps and toilets, recycling waste water, and efficient irrigation such as drip irrigation that delivers water to plant roots.

**(d) Increasing water supply**, limited to:
- **Dams and reservoirs**, which store water from wet seasons for use in dry ones.
- **Use of aquifers and wells**, which draw on ground water.
- **Desalination**, which removes salt from sea water.
- **Rainwater harvesting**, which collects rain from roofs or the ground and stores it in tanks.

**(e) International aid.** Food, water, money and equipment from other countries or international organisations, especially where drought has led to famine.

### Worked example 2: how long will the reservoir last?

*The figures are invented for practice.* A town's reservoir holds 12 million m³ of water when the drought begins, and no rain is expected. The town uses 40 000 m³ per day.

- Days of supply = 12 000 000 / 40 000 = **300 days**.
- If water conservation cuts use by 25%, daily use = 40 000 x 0.75 = 30 000 m³.
- Days of supply = 12 000 000 / 30 000 = **400 days**, an extra 100 days.

The calculation shows why conservation is often the first response: it costs little and extends supply significantly. A good evaluation adds that evaporation from the reservoir in hot weather would shorten both figures.

## Benefits and limitations (6.4.5)

| Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring and warning | Months of warning; time to plan | Warnings help only if people can act on them; equipment cost |
| Emergency water supplies | Saves lives quickly | Expensive; short-term; hard to reach remote areas |
| Water conservation | Cheap; extends existing supply | Depends on people's cooperation; cannot replace water that is not there |
| Dams and reservoirs | Store large volumes for dry seasons | Costly; flood land and habitats; lose water to evaporation; empty in long droughts |
| Aquifers and wells | Ground water is less affected by evaporation | Over-pumping lowers the water table; wells can dry up; recharge is slow |
| Desalination | Unlimited sea-water source for coastal areas | Very expensive; uses a lot of energy; salty waste (brine) harms marine life |
| Rainwater harvesting | Cheap, local, simple technology | Collects little when rain fails, which is the problem in a drought |
| International aid | Food and water when local supply fails | Can be slow; may create dependence; may not reach those in need |

### Model answer outline

**Question:** Discuss the benefits and limitations of desalination and rainwater harvesting for managing the impacts of drought. **[6]**

Desalination provides a supply that does not depend on rainfall *(benefit)* and suits coastal cities *(benefit)*, but it is very expensive and uses large amounts of energy *(limitation)*, and the brine it produces can harm marine ecosystems *(limitation)*. Rainwater harvesting is cheap and can be done by households *(benefit)*, but it collects little water during a drought because the rain has failed *(limitation)*. Judgement: rainwater harvesting helps prepare before a drought by storing water from wet periods, while desalination gives reliable supply during a long drought where a country can afford it.

## Common mistakes

- Defining drought as "no rain". The syllabus definition is dry weather that is longer or more severe than normal.
- Giving human water use as a cause in a 6.4.2 question; the listed causes are lack of rain and climate change.
- Treating rainwater harvesting as a solution during a drought without noting that it depends on rain.
- Forgetting that drought impacts include wildfires and decreased air quality.
- Confusing desertification (long-term land degradation) with a drought (a period of dry weather).

## Exam technique

**Define** with the syllabus wording. **Describe and explain** impacts with a chain. **Calculate** with working and units (mm, %, m³, days). **Discuss** strategies by giving a benefit and limitation for each, then judge which suits the place in the question: coastal or inland, rich or poor, short or long drought.

## Check your understanding

1. Give the syllabus definition of drought. *A period of dry weather that is longer or more severe than normal.*
2. How do higher temperatures make drought worse? *They increase evaporation and transpiration, so water is lost faster.*
3. Name the four ways of increasing water supply in 6.4.4(d). *Dams and reservoirs; aquifers and wells; desalination; rainwater harvesting.*
4. Rainfall is 280 mm against a normal 400 mm. What percentage below normal is this? *120 / 400 x 100 = 30%.*
5. Give one limitation of desalination. *High cost and energy use, or brine harming marine life.*
