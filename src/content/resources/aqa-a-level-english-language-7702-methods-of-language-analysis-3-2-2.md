This guide covers **3.2.2 Methods of language analysis** in AQA A-level English Language (7702), the toolkit
integrated into Paper 2. The levels are the same six as in Paper 1, but the specification gives them three distinct
jobs here. The broader
[Paper 2 language discourses and writing guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-language-paper-2-language-discourses-and-writing/)
shows how Section B uses them; this page teaches the methods themselves.

## Three jobs for the methods

The specification says you use methods of language analysis to:

| Job | What it looks like | Where it pays off |
|---|---|---|
| Identify and describe features of language diversity and change | Naming a dialect form, an older spelling convention or a semantic shift precisely | Evidence in the Section A essay; analysis of older Section B texts |
| Research diversity and change | Reading dictionary entries, corpus results, surveys and research findings | Supporting claims about when and how a change happened |
| Analyse how texts present ideas about language | Examining the lexis, grammar and pragmatics a writer uses *about* language | The Section B analysis of two texts |

Note the specification's wording for lexis and semantics: "the vocabulary of English, including **social and
historical variation**". In Paper 2, variation is the point.

## Job 1: describing diversity and change precisely

**Phonetics, phonology and prosodics.** Describe accent features by the sound involved, not by spelling alone.
Useful terms: h-dropping; TH-fronting (/θ/ pronounced as /f/, so "think" sounds like "fink"); the glottal stop
replacing /t/ in "butter"; rhotic accents (pronouncing /r/ after vowels, as in many Scottish and West Country
accents) versus non-rhotic accents. If a text represents accent through spelling ("'ow", "reet"), say so: that is
**phonetic respelling**, a representation chosen by the writer. Strictly, **eye dialect** is non-standard spelling
that does not change the pronunciation at all ("wuz" for "was"), used to make a speaker seem uneducated.

**Graphology.** For older print: the long s (ſ), capitalised common nouns, italic for emphasis or names,
blackletter type in early printing, dense layout. For digital texts: emoji, capitals for volume, repeated letters
("sooo") for prosody, and platform-specific layout.

**Lexis and semantics.** Identify dialect lexis ("bairn", "ginnel"), archaisms ("wherefore", "pray"), neologisms,
borrowings, and words whose meaning has shifted. When a word looks familiar but its meaning has changed, say what
it meant in the text's period.

**Grammar, including morphology.** Use descriptive labels, not "wrong grammar":

- **multiple negation**: "I never said nothing";
- **levelled past tense of BE**: "we was", "it weren't";
- **non-standard past forms**: "I seen it", "she done it";
- **zero plural after numbers**: "ten mile";
- **second-person plural pronouns**: "youse", "y'all";
- older forms: "-eth" and "-est" verb endings, "thou/thee", absence of do-support ("I know not").

For word formation, name the morphological process: compounding, blending, affixation, clipping, conversion.

**Pragmatics.** Politeness conventions change and vary: "Pray", "Sir", "your humble servant" in older letters;
regional terms of address ("love", "duck", "pet"); differences in directness across varieties.

**Discourse.** Genre conventions change too: the structure of letters, newspaper reports or adverts in different
periods; turn-taking patterns in different communities; the conventions of online interaction.

## Worked example: describing a dialect utterance

The sentence below is **original**, constructed to show several features at once.

> *"We was sat there for ages and there weren't nobody about, so we come home."*

```
"We was"              levelled past tense of BE: "was" with a plural subject
"sat"                 past participle used for the progressive "sitting",
                      a construction common in northern and Midland English
"weren't nobody"      multiple negation, plus levelled "weren't" with a
                      singular subject
"come" (past)         non-standard past tense, identical to the base form
```

Each feature is described by its grammar, compared with Standard English, and placed as a regular pattern of a
variety, not an error. In an essay, you could then explain such features through networks, prestige or identity.

## Job 2: researching diversity and change

| Source | What it tells you | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Historical dictionary (for example the Oxford English Dictionary) | Earliest recorded uses and dated quotations showing meaning over time | First *recorded* use is not first use, especially for speech |
| Corpora (for example the British National Corpus) | Frequency, collocates and concordance lines from large text collections | A corpus reflects the text types it contains |
| Frequency tools across books (such as Google Books Ngram Viewer) | Trends in printed use over centuries | Printed books under-represent informal and spoken language |
| Dialect surveys (such as the Survey of English Dialects, 1950s) | Where forms were used and by whom | Speakers recorded may not represent the whole community |
| Research findings (tables, graphs, statistics) | Patterns across groups or periods | Check sample size, method and what is actually measured |

**Reading corpus results.** Frequencies are usually **normalised** (for example per million words) so corpora of
different sizes can be compared: frequency per million = (raw count ÷ corpus size in words) × 1,000,000. As a
hypothetical illustration, 50 occurrences in a 2-million-word corpus is 25 per million. A **concordance** shows each
occurrence in its context, which lets you check meaning; **collocates** show which words a term habitually appears
with, which can reveal semantic change or attitudes.

**Real time and apparent time.** A real-time study compares the same community at two dates. An apparent-time study
compares age groups at one date, assuming that older speakers preserve earlier usage; differences may instead be
age grading.

## Job 3: analysing how texts present ideas about language

When a text is *about* language, the methods reveal its stance:

- **Lexis:** evaluative words for language and its users ("sloppy", "mangled", "vibrant", "rich"); metaphors of
  decay, disease, invasion or growth.
- **Grammar:** modal verbs of obligation ("should", "must") signal prescriptive views; hedged claims ("may",
  "research suggests") signal descriptive ones; generic noun phrases ("young people today") generalise.
- **Pragmatics:** presuppositions ("the decline of English"), irony and humour, appeals to shared common sense.
- **Discourse:** anecdote versus evidence, the order of arguments, whose voices are quoted.
- **Graphology:** quoted "errors" in bold or capitals invite ridicule; headings frame the issue.

## Common mistakes

- Calling dialect grammar "slang" or "incorrect" rather than describing it.
- Describing accent only through spelling in a transcript that uses phonemic symbols, or ignoring respellings and
  eye dialect in a written text.
- Treating a first recorded date as the date a word was invented.
- Using corpus raw counts to compare corpora of different sizes.
- Describing a Section B text's opinions without the language features that carry them.

## Exam technique

- In the Section A essay, use precisely described examples as evidence for an argument about causes or attitudes.
- In Section B, choose the levels that reveal the writer's stance, and link each to the discourse it draws on.
- Use the specification's language level names, so your method is visible.
