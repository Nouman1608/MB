> **HL only.** These are original questions written for Marlbridge on the motivation extension of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions. The sources are constructed for practice and the data are invented. Questions 6 to 9 follow the pattern of the four Paper 3 questions (3, 6, 6 and 15 marks); the others use practice tariffs.

## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. **[3]**

**2.** Distinguish between primary and secondary motives, giving one example of each. **[4]**

**3.** Outline one way in which a primary motive can be measured. **[2]**

**4.** Outline the role of autonomy in self-determination theory. **[2]**

**5.** Explain one problem with using self-reported reasons to measure secondary motives. **[3]**

## Section B

Read the sources (constructed for practice; data are invented). They concern learning and cognition and the role of motivation.

> **Source A (experiment).** In a school, 90 students aged 12 to 13 were randomly allocated to three groups of 30 for a reading club that ran for four weeks. In Group 1 students were promised a sticker for every book finished. In Group 2 students received no reward. In Group 3 students chose their own books and were told to "read what you like". Two weeks after the club ended, the researcher recorded the mean number of minutes each student chose to read in free time per week.
>
> | | Group 1 (sticker) | Group 2 (no reward) | Group 3 (choice) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Mean minutes of free reading per week | 38 | 52 | 61 |
> | Standard deviation | 14 | 15 | 16 |
>
> **Source B (survey).** A questionnaire to 400 students in the same school scored "interest in learning" from 1 to 20. Students who reported higher interest in learning had higher marks (correlation coefficient r = +0.35).
>
> **Source C (interview extract).** A student from Group 1 said: "After the stickers stopped, I did not see the point. I only read when someone checked." A student from Group 3 said: "I had never picked my own book before. I found one I liked and read on the bus."

**6.** Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of Source B. **[3]**

**7.** Analyse the findings in Source A and state a conclusion. **[6]**

**8.** To what extent are the findings in Source C transferable to other students? **[6]**

**9.** Using Sources A, B and C and your knowledge of the role of motivation, discuss the claim that "rewards reduce students' motivation to learn". **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Intrinsic motivation comes from within the person, such as interest or enjoyment of the activity itself **[1]**. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside, such as rewards or punishments **[1]**. Extrinsic rewards can encourage behaviour but may reduce intrinsic motivation if they undermine the person's sense of choice **[1]**.

**2.** Primary motives are biological needs such as hunger, thirst or sleep **[1]**; example given, for example thirst **[1]**. Secondary motives are learned or social, such as affiliation, competition or personal goals **[1]**; example given, for example the need to belong **[1]**.

**3.** Physiological testing can be used **[1]**, for example a glucose test to study motivation to eat **[1]**.

**4.** Autonomy is the need to feel in control of and to choose one's own actions **[1]**; when supported, motivation becomes more internal and persistence and well-being improve **[1]**.

**5.** Participants may not know their real motive, especially if it is unconscious **[1]**. They may give answers that please the researcher or look socially acceptable (demand characteristics or social desirability) **[1]**, so the reasons given may not reflect the actual causes of behaviour, which reduces validity **[1]**.

**6.** Any one, three marks: the correlation is positive but not strong (r = +0.35), so it explains only a limited part of the variation in marks **[1]**; the study is correlational, so it does not show whether interest raises marks or marks raise interest (bidirectional ambiguity) **[1]**, and a third variable such as home support may explain both **[1]**. Also creditable: interest is self-reported.

**7.** Analysis, up to 4: the mean free reading time was lowest in Group 1 (38 minutes), higher in Group 2 (52) and highest in Group 3 (61) **[1]**. Group 1 read 14 minutes less than Group 2 and 23 minutes less than Group 3 **[1]**. The standard deviations (14 to 16 minutes) are fairly large relative to the 9-minute difference between Groups 2 and 3, so the groups probably overlap; no inferential test is given, so statistical significance is unknown **[1]**. Random allocation and equal group sizes support internal validity **[1]**. Conclusion, up to 2: among these students, the reward was followed by less free reading than no reward, which is consistent with a loss of intrinsic motivation, and choice was followed by the most **[1]**; the study took place in one school with a short follow-up, so the conclusion should not be generalised **[1]**. Maximum **[6]**.

**8.** Markband guide: 5 to 6 for a detailed discussion with explicit reference to the source; 3 to 4 for some reference to the source; 1 to 2 for generic points. Indicative content, up to two marks each:
- The extract is two quotations from two students, so the sample is very small and may have been chosen because the comments fit the study **[2]**.
- Qualitative research aims for transferability: the researcher should describe the school, ages and setting in enough detail for readers to judge whether it applies to other contexts **[2]**.
- The comments fit the quantitative data in Source A, which supports credibility through triangulation **[2]**.
- Students may give the answers they think the researcher expects (social desirability), and the researcher knows which group the student was in **[2]**.
- Judgement: transferability is limited to similar students in similar schools, but the findings are consistent with a wider theory of motivation (overjustification) **[2]**.
Maximum **[6]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for detailed, well-developed critical analysis that interprets at least three sources, uses theory and evaluates different points of view with a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good but less developed analysis. Indicative content, up to the markband:
- Source A supports the claim for the sticker condition: Group 1 read the least (38 minutes), below the no-reward group (52), though groups probably overlap and there is no significance test **[3]**.
- Source A also suggests that choice (autonomy) raises motivation (61 minutes), which fits self-determination theory **[2]**.
- Source B shows that interest and marks are positively related (r = +0.35), but it is correlational and does not show that rewards reduce interest **[2]**.
- Source C illustrates the mechanism from the students' view (the stickers removed the point of reading) but is anecdotal and limited in transferability **[2]**.
- Own knowledge: the overjustification effect (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett, 1973); and the counter-view that rewards can help when they give useful information or the task is dull, or when they are not seen as controlling **[3]**.
- Different points of view: the claim is too general; the effect may depend on the type of reward, the age of the child and the length of follow-up **[2]**.
- Conclusion consistent with the arguments, for example that the claim is supported for expected tangible rewards in these data but that it should be qualified **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 7:** stating a conclusion about "all students" from one school.
- **Question 8:** writing about the quality of Source A when the question is about Source C.
- **Question 9:** a claim of "proof" from a correlational source, or no use of theory.
