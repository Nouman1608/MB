This guide covers **1.4 The human impact** within Topic 1, Hydrology and fluvial geomorphology, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content has four parts:

- **Modifications** to catchment flows and stores and to channel flows by land-use changes (deforestation, afforestation, urbanisation), abstraction and water storage.
- **The causes and impacts of river floods**, and the prediction of flood risk and **recurrence intervals**.
- **The prevention and amelioration of river floods**: forecasts and warnings; hard engineering (dams, straightening, levées and diversion spillways); soft engineering (floodplain and drainage basin management, wetland and river bank conservation, and river restoration).
- A **case study of a recent river flood event**: its causes, its impacts on people and the environment, and an evaluation of attempts to reduce its impact.

Use the [1.4 human impact revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-drainage-basin-human-impact-revision-notes/), which include a worked flood case study outline, and test yourself with the [1.4 human impact practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-human-impact-1-4-practice/).

## Land-use change: tracing the effect through the system

The safest way to answer any land-use question is to follow the change through the stores and flows from 1.1, then state the effect on the hydrograph from 1.2.

| Change | Stores affected | Flows affected | Hydrograph effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deforestation | Interception store removed; soil water store shrinks as soil is compacted or eroded | Less infiltration, more overland flow, less transpiration | Shorter lag time, higher peak, more sediment in the channel |
| Afforestation | Interception store grows; deeper roots increase soil water storage | More infiltration and transpiration, less overland flow | Longer lag time, lower peak; lower total runoff once trees mature |
| Urbanisation | Surface and soil stores replaced by impermeable surfaces | Infiltration almost stops; drains carry water rapidly to the channel | Much shorter lag, much higher peak, lower baseflow |

Two refinements earn credit. First, afforestation has little effect in its first years, and drainage ditches dug before planting can briefly increase runoff. Second, urbanisation lowers **baseflow** because less water recharges groundwater, so urban rivers can be both flashier in storms and lower in dry weather.

## Abstraction and water storage

**Abstraction** is the removal of water from rivers or groundwater for public supply, irrigation or industry. Taking water from a river reduces channel flow downstream. Pumping from wells lowers the water table, which reduces baseflow and can dry up springs and small streams.

**Water storage** in reservoirs behind dams changes channel flows downstream:

- flood peaks are reduced, because the reservoir stores floodwater and releases it gradually;
- the annual regime is flattened, with lower high flows and often higher low flows;
- sediment is trapped in the reservoir, so the water released is "clear" and can erode the channel downstream;
- evaporation from the reservoir surface adds a new output.

## Causes and impacts of river floods

A river flood happens when discharge exceeds the capacity of the channel, so water spills onto land that is normally dry. Causes are best grouped:

- **Physical causes:** prolonged or intense rainfall, rapid snowmelt, saturated or frozen ground, steep impermeable basins and storm surges that block river outflow at the coast.
- **Human causes:** urbanisation and deforestation (which speed up runoff), building on floodplains (which increases exposure), channel straightening upstream (which passes floodwater to downstream areas faster) and the failure of dams or levées.

Impacts should be split into **people** (deaths, injuries, homes and businesses damaged, transport and power cut, disease from contaminated water, lost crops and livelihoods) and **the environment** (bank and slope erosion, sediment deposited on farmland, pollution from sewage and chemicals, habitat damage, but also wetland recharge and fresh silt for floodplain soils). Impacts are usually more deadly in lower-income countries, where warnings, defences and emergency services are weaker, and more costly in money terms in high-income countries, where property values are higher.

## Predicting flood risk: recurrence intervals

A **recurrence interval** (return period) is the average time between floods of a given size or greater. It is calculated from a record of the highest discharge in each year (the annual maximum series):

**T = (n + 1) / m**, where n is the number of years of record and m is the rank of the flood (1 = largest).

## Worked example: calculating recurrence intervals

*A fictional gauging station has nine years of annual maximum discharges (cumecs): 210, 145, 320, 180, 260, 150, 390, 200, 170.*

Rank them: 390 (m = 1), 320 (2), 260 (3), 210 (4), 200 (5) and so on. With n = 9, n + 1 = 10.

- 390 cumecs: T = 10 / 1 = **10 years**
- 320 cumecs: T = 10 / 2 = **5 years**
- 260 cumecs: T = 10 / 3 = **3.3 years**
- 210 cumecs: T = 10 / 4 = **2.5 years**

The annual probability of a flood is 1 / T, so a 390-cumec flood has a 10% chance of happening in any one year.

**Interpreting results:** a "1 in 100 year" flood has a 1% chance each year. It does not happen only once a century: the chance of at least one such flood in 10 years is 1 - 0.99¹⁰, about 9.6%. Short records give unreliable estimates for large floods, and land-use change or a changing climate can make past records a poor guide to future risk. Flood risk maps combine these estimates with land height, so planners can restrict building in high-risk zones.

## Prevention and amelioration

**Forecasts and warnings** use rainfall radar, river gauges and computer models to predict where and when rivers will flood, and alert people by text, sirens, broadcasts and websites. They save lives and allow property to be protected, but depend on reliable data, communication and public trust.

**Hard engineering:**

- **Dams** store floodwater in reservoirs and release it slowly. They also provide water and power but are costly, displace people, trap sediment and can fail. The Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze was built partly for flood control, and its reservoir required the relocation of more than a million people.
- **Straightening** (channelisation) shortens and deepens the channel so water moves through faster. It protects the reach treated but can pass the flood problem downstream and destroys habitats.
- **Levées** (embankments) raise the banks so the channel holds more water. They can fail or be overtopped, and they encourage building behind them.
- **Diversion spillways** carry excess water away from the main channel into an overflow channel or storage area. The Bonnet Carré Spillway diverts Mississippi floodwater into Lake Pontchartrain to protect New Orleans.

**Soft engineering:**

- **Floodplain management:** land-use zoning keeps housing out of high-risk areas and uses floodplains for parks and farming that can tolerate flooding.
- **Drainage basin management:** afforestation, leaky dams and better soil management upstream slow the flow of water to the channel.
- **Wetland and river bank conservation:** wetlands store floodwater like sponges; vegetated banks resist erosion.
- **River restoration:** returning a straightened river to a more natural, meandering course with a connected floodplain. The Kissimmee River in Florida, channelised in the 1960s, is a well-known example where parts of the old meandering course have been restored.

## Planning the flood case study

The case study must be a **recent river flood event**. Prepare it under four headings, each with located, dated detail:

1. **Causes:** separate physical and human causes, and judge which mattered most.
2. **Impacts on people:** social and economic, short and long term.
3. **Impacts on the environment:** both damage and any benefits.
4. **Evaluation of attempts to reduce the impact:** what worked, what failed and why, and for whom.

The revision notes outline the December 2015 Cumbria floods; any recent, well-documented event works if you can cover all four headings.

## Annotated model answer

*"Evaluate the use of hard engineering to reduce the impact of river floods." [10, extract]*

> Levées and dams can protect large populations: a reservoir can store a flood peak and release it over days, cutting downstream discharge **(benefit with mechanism)**. However, they are designed to a chosen standard, such as a 1 in 100 year flood, so a larger event can overtop them, as in Carlisle in 2015 **(limitation with example)**. Protection behind levées also encourages building on the floodplain, so when failure does happen the damage is greater **(unintended consequence)**. Hard engineering therefore works best for frequent, moderate floods and alongside warnings and land-use planning, rather than on its own **(judgement)**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying afforestation reduces flooding at once; the effect grows as trees mature.
- Treating a recurrence interval as a timetable ("it will not happen again for 100 years").
- Describing management methods without evaluating them.
- Writing about flood impacts with no named place or date in the case study.
- Forgetting that abstraction and storage are human impacts in the syllabus, not only land use.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** recurrence intervals step by step: rank, apply the formula, state units (years).
- **Evaluate** or **assess** strategies by weighing effectiveness, cost, environmental impact and who benefits, then reach a judgement.
- In Section B, flood case study questions usually need causes, impacts and evaluation; allocate time to each.
