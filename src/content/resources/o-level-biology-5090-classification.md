## 2.1 Concept and use of a classification system

### Grouping by shared features (2.1.1)

There are far too many kinds of organism to study one at a time. Biologists sort them into groups, and each group is defined by **features its members share**. Organisms that share many features are placed in the same small group. Organisms that share only a few features end up in the same large group but different small groups.

The features used must be ones that every member of the group has. "Lives in water" is a poor basis for a group: whales, sharks and water lilies all live in water but have very different body structures. "Has feathers" is a good basis, because every bird has feathers and no other animal does.

### Species (2.1.2)

A **species** is a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce **fertile offspring**. The word "fertile" is the part examiners look for. Offspring that are healthy but cannot themselves reproduce do not show that their parents belong to one species.

**Worked example.** A horse and a donkey can mate and produce a mule. Mules are almost always infertile. Are horses and donkeys the same species?

- Step 1: check whether they can produce offspring. They can (the mule).
- Step 2: check whether the offspring are fertile. They are not.
- Step 3: conclude. Horses and donkeys are **different species**, because the definition needs fertile offspring.

By contrast, two very different-looking dog breeds produce puppies that grow up able to breed, so all domestic dogs belong to one species.

### The binomial system (2.1.3)

The **binomial system** is an internationally agreed way of naming species. Every species has a scientific name made of **two parts**:

1. the **genus** name, written first, with a capital letter
2. the **species** name, written second, all in lower case.

The whole name is printed in *italics*. When you handwrite it, underline the two words instead. After the first use, the genus can be shortened to its initial, for example *P. leo*.

Because the system is agreed worldwide, one organism has one scientific name, whatever the local name is in Urdu, Arabic or English. Common names can be confusing: different places use the same common name for different animals.

**Worked example.** The lion is *Panthera leo*, the tiger is *Panthera tigris* and the domestic cat is *Felis catus*. Which two are most closely related?

- Step 1: compare the first word (genus). Lion and tiger share the genus *Panthera*; the cat is in *Felis*.
- Step 2: organisms in the same genus share more features than organisms in different genera.
- Step 3: conclude: the **lion and tiger** are most closely related.

### Dichotomous keys (2.1.4)

A **dichotomous key** identifies an organism by a series of steps. "Dichotomous" means dividing into two. Each step (a **couplet**) gives two contrasting statements about **one** feature. You pick the statement that fits your specimen, and it either names the organism or sends you to another numbered step.

**Rules for constructing a key**

- Use features you can see on the specimen: number of legs, presence of wings, leaf shape, veins. Avoid size and colour, which vary between individuals of the same species, and avoid features you cannot check, such as behaviour.
- Make each pair a true opposite about the same feature ("three pairs of legs" / "four pairs of legs"), not two unrelated features.
- Every organism must end at its own name, and no organism may appear twice.
- A key for *n* organisms needs **n − 1** couplets.

**Worked example: constructing a key.** Make a key to separate a housefly, a spider, a crab, a centipede, a sparrow and a frog.

Six organisms need 6 − 1 = 5 couplets. Start with the feature that splits the group most evenly: four of the six have an exoskeleton and jointed legs.

```
1 a  Jointed legs and a hard exoskeleton ............. go to 2
  b  No exoskeleton .................................. go to 5
2 a  More than four pairs of legs .................... go to 3
  b  Four pairs of legs or fewer ..................... go to 4
3 a  Two pairs of antennae ........................... crab
  b  One pair of antennae ............................ centipede
4 a  Three pairs of legs ............................. housefly
  b  Four pairs of legs .............................. spider
5 a  Body covered in feathers ........................ sparrow
  b  No feathers ..................................... frog
```

**Using it.** A specimen has jointed legs, ten legs and two pairs of antennae. Step 1: exoskeleton, go to 2. Step 2: five pairs is more than four, go to 3. Step 3: two pairs of antennae, so it is the **crab**. Always write down the route (1a → 2a → 3a) in an exam answer if the question asks how you reached the name.

## 2.2 Features of organisms

### The five kingdoms (2.2.1)

| Kingdom | Main features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Animal | Multicellular; cells have a nucleus but no cell wall and no chloroplasts; feed on organic substances made by other organisms; usually move from place to place | Insects, fish, humans |
| Plant | Multicellular; cells have a nucleus and a cellulose cell wall; many cells have chloroplasts, so they make their own food by photosynthesis | Ferns, maize, mango trees |
| Fungus | Cells have a nucleus and a cell wall not made of cellulose; no chloroplasts; usually made of thread-like hyphae; feed by secreting enzymes onto dead or living material and absorbing the products | Mushrooms, moulds; yeast is single-celled |
| Prokaryote | Single-celled; **no nucleus**: circular DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, often with plasmids; cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes | Bacteria |
| Protoctist | Mostly single-celled, with a nucleus; some have chloroplasts and are plant-like, others are animal-like | *Amoeba* (animal-like), *Euglena* (has chloroplasts) |

The key splits to remember: **nucleus or no nucleus** separates Prokaryote from the rest; **cell wall and chloroplasts** separate Plant from Animal; **non-cellulose wall, no chloroplasts, hyphae** mark out Fungus. The prokaryote structures are the same ones you met for the bacterial cell in [cell structure and organisation](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation/).

### Groups within the animal kingdom (2.2.2)

**Vertebrates** have a backbone. The syllabus lists five groups.

| Group | Main features |
|---|---|
| Mammals | Hair or fur on the skin; external ears (pinnae); mammary glands that produce milk for the young; most give birth to live young |
| Birds | Feathers; a beak; front limbs are wings; lay eggs with hard shells |
| Reptiles | Dry skin covered in scales; lay eggs with rubbery or leathery shells on land |
| Amphibians | Moist skin with no scales; lay soft eggs without shells in water; larvae (tadpoles) live in water and use gills; adults usually live on land and use lungs |
| Fish | Body covered in wet scales; fins; gills for gas exchange; a lateral line along each side |

**Arthropods** have no backbone. All arthropods have **jointed legs**, a **segmented body** and a hard **exoskeleton**. The four groups are separated mainly by counting legs, antennae and body sections.

| Group | Body sections | Legs | Antennae | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Insects | 3 (head, thorax, abdomen) | 3 pairs | 1 pair | Usually 1 or 2 pairs of wings |
| Arachnids | 2 (cephalothorax, abdomen) | 4 pairs | None | Several simple eyes |
| Crustaceans | Often 2 (cephalothorax, abdomen) | More than 4 pairs | 2 pairs | Many live in water; exoskeleton often hardened |
| Myriapods | Many similar segments | 1 or 2 pairs on each segment | 1 pair | Centipedes and millipedes |

### Groups within the plant kingdom (2.2.3)

**Ferns** have roots, stems and leaves called **fronds**, but **no flowers**. They reproduce by **spores**, which form in small brown patches on the underside of the fronds.

**Flowering plants** produce **flowers** and reproduce by **seeds**, which form inside the ovary (later the fruit). They are split into two groups.

| Feature | Monocotyledons | Dicotyledons |
|---|---|---|
| Cotyledons (seed leaves) in the seed | One | Two |
| Leaf veins | Parallel | Branching network |
| Flower parts | Usually in threes or multiples of three | Usually in fours or fives, or multiples of these |
| Examples | Maize, rice, grasses, onion | Bean, sunflower, mango |

### Classifying organisms (2.2.4)

To classify an organism, work from the largest group down and justify each step with a feature.

**Worked example.** An organism has jointed legs, a body in two sections, four pairs of legs and no antennae. Classify it.

- Kingdom: multicellular, moves, has legs, so **Animal**.
- Large group: jointed legs and exoskeleton, so **arthropod**.
- Arthropod group: two body sections, four pairs of legs, no antennae, so **arachnid**.

Each feature you quote should be one the syllabus lists for that group. "It has eight legs" earns more credit than "it looks like a spider".

### Viruses (2.2.5 and 2.2.6)

A virus is not a cell. The syllabus limits its features to two:

- a **protein coat**
- **genetic material** inside it (DNA or RNA).

A virus has no cytoplasm and none of the structures that a cell uses to make proteins. So a virus **can only replicate inside a living cell**. It enters a host cell and the host cell's materials are used to make new copies of the virus. Outside a living cell, a virus does not reproduce. This is why viruses are not placed in any of the five kingdoms.

## Common errors

- Defining a species as "organisms that can reproduce together" and leaving out **fertile offspring**.
- Writing a binomial name with two capital letters (*Panthera Leo*) or without underlining when handwritten.
- Using colour or size in a key, or putting two different features in one couplet.
- Saying prokaryotes have "no DNA". They have DNA; they have no **nucleus**.
- Calling a fungus a plant. Fungi have no chloroplasts and their cell walls are not cellulose.
- Giving "eight legs" for an insect, or "antennae" for an arachnid.
- Saying amphibians have scales, or that reptile eggs are laid in water.
- Describing a virus as a bacterium or a prokaryote. A virus has no cell structure at all.
