This guide goes deeper into **A1.1 Water**, the first sub-topic of Theme A (Unity and diversity) in the IB Diploma Programme *Biology guide*, first assessment 2025. A1.1 asks how the properties of water make it the medium for life, and it builds every explanation from one idea: hydrogen bonding between polar molecules. Statements A1.1.1 to A1.1.6 are for SL and HL; A1.1.7 and A1.1.8 are additional higher level.

## From polarity to every property

Each property in A1.1 follows from the same chain. Learn the chain once and reuse it:

1. Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so electrons in each O–H covalent bond are shared unequally.
2. The oxygen end becomes slightly negative (δ−) and each hydrogen slightly positive (δ+).
3. The molecule is bent, so the partial charges do not cancel: water is a **polar molecule**.
4. The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of another: a **hydrogen bond**.
5. Each hydrogen bond is weak, but there are vast numbers of them, so together they are strong.

A useful exam habit is to name the step in the chain that a property depends on. Cohesion depends on step 4 between water molecules. Adhesion and solvent action depend on step 3, attraction between polar water and *other* polar or charged substances.

## A1.1.1 Water as the medium for life

The first cells are thought to have originated in water, and water is still the medium in which most of their chemistry happens. Cytoplasm, blood plasma, tissue fluid, xylem sap and phloem sap are all aqueous. When asked why water is "the medium for life", link it to the solvent properties in A1.1.5: reactants must be dissolved to collide and to reach enzymes.

## A1.1.3 and A1.1.4 Cohesion and adhesion compared

| | Cohesion | Adhesion |
|---|---|---|
| Attraction between | Water and water | Water and another polar or charged material |
| Cause | Hydrogen bonds between water molecules | Hydrogen bonds or attraction to charged groups |
| Consequence named in the guide | Transport under tension in xylem; surface tension as a habitat | Capillary action in soil and in plant cell walls |

**Transport under tension.** Water is pulled, not pushed, up the xylem. As water evaporates from leaf cell walls, more is drawn from the xylem, and cohesion transmits the pull down the column to the roots. The column is under tension, which is why xylem vessels have thickened, lignified walls that resist collapsing inwards.

**Surface tension.** At the surface, water molecules are hydrogen-bonded only to molecules beside and below them, so there is a net inward pull and the surface resists breaking. Small animals such as pond skaters can stand on it, so the surface itself is a habitat.

**Capillary action.** In a narrow space, adhesion draws water along the surface of the solid, and cohesion pulls more water behind. In soil, this holds water in the small spaces between particles against gravity. In plants, water moves through the cellulose of cell walls, which has many polar hydroxyl groups.

### Worked example 1: annotated model answer

*Question (4 marks): Explain how the properties of water allow it to reach the top of a tall tree.*

> Water evaporates from the walls of leaf cells **[1: transpiration creates the pull]**, which pulls water out of the xylem so the water is under tension **[1: tension, not pressure]**. Hydrogen bonds between water molecules (cohesion) keep the column continuous as it is pulled up **[1]**. Adhesion of water to the polar cellulose and lignin of the xylem walls also helps hold the column in place **[1]**.

Each mark is a separate idea. An answer that says only "cohesion and adhesion help water move" names the properties without explaining either.

## A1.1.5 Water as a solvent

Water dissolves substances that are polar or charged, because water molecules cluster around them and are attracted to them. Such substances are **hydrophilic**. Substances that are non-polar, and so do not attract water molecules, are **hydrophobic**: they do not dissolve and tend to cluster together in water.

The guide links solvent properties to two roles:

- **Medium for metabolism.** Enzymes and their substrates are dissolved in the cytoplasm, so molecules move freely and collide. Many enzymes are themselves soluble proteins.
- **Transport.** In animals, blood plasma carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, mineral ions and urea. In plants, xylem carries mineral ions and phloem carries dissolved sucrose.

Substances with low solubility need help. Oxygen dissolves only poorly, so most oxygen in blood is carried bound to haemoglobin. Hydrophobic lipids are carried in blood combined with proteins rather than dissolved.

### Worked example 2: predicting how a substance is carried

A table gives the solubility of four substances in water at 37 °C (invented, relative values: glucose very high, sodium chloride very high, oxygen very low, a triglyceride effectively zero).

1. Glucose: polar, with many –OH groups, so it is hydrophilic and dissolves in plasma.
2. Sodium chloride: ionic, so Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are surrounded by water molecules and carried in solution.
3. Oxygen: non-polar, so it is poorly soluble; carried mainly by haemoglobin.
4. Triglyceride: hydrophobic, so it cannot travel dissolved; it is carried in combination with protein.

The pattern to state: polar or charged means dissolved; non-polar means a carrier is needed.

## A1.1.6 Physical properties and aquatic animals

The guide names four properties and asks you to relate them to animals in water, using the black-throated loon and the ringed seal as examples.

| Property | Water compared with air | Consequence for aquatic animals |
|---|---|---|
| Buoyancy | Water gives much greater upthrust | Less need for a skeleton to support body weight |
| Viscosity | Water is far more viscous | More drag, so streamlined bodies are favoured |
| Thermal conductivity | Water conducts heat much faster | Heat is lost rapidly, so insulation is needed |
| Specific heat capacity | Water needs more energy per degree | Aquatic habitats change temperature slowly and are thermally stable |

**Black-throated loon.** A diving bird that lives both on and under water. Its legs are set far back on the body for propulsion when swimming, and its bones are less air-filled than those of most flying birds, which reduces buoyancy and helps it dive. The same leg position makes walking on land awkward: an adaptation to one medium can be a cost in another.

**Ringed seal.** A mammal of Arctic waters. A thick layer of blubber reduces heat loss in water, which conducts heat away far faster than air. Its streamlined body reduces drag in viscous water, and buoyancy supports its body mass while swimming.

## A1.1.7 and A1.1.8 (HL only) Water beyond Earth

**Origin and retention (A1.1.7).** One hypothesis is that much of Earth's water was delivered by asteroids or other bodies from space. Earth retained it because its gravity is strong enough to hold an atmosphere, and its surface temperature allowed water vapour to condense into oceans rather than escape.

**Search for life (A1.1.8).** Because all known life depends on liquid water, the search for extraterrestrial life focuses on places where liquid water could exist. The "Goldilocks zone" is the range of distances from a star at which a planet's surface temperature would allow liquid water.

### Worked example 3 (HL): applying the Goldilocks idea

Three invented planets orbit a star. Their estimated mean surface temperatures are 180 °C, 15 °C and −90 °C, and each has an atmosphere at about Earth's pressure.

1. At about Earth's pressure, water is liquid between 0 °C and 100 °C.
2. Only the 15 °C planet could hold surface liquid water, so it lies in the Goldilocks zone.
3. This makes it a candidate for life, not evidence of life. Liquid water is necessary for life as we know it, but not sufficient.

## Common mistakes

- Placing hydrogen bonds inside a water molecule. The bonds within are covalent; hydrogen bonds are between molecules.
- Saying water "sticks to itself" for adhesion; that is cohesion.
- Writing that water is pushed up the xylem.
- Explaining solubility without the words polar, charged or hydrophilic.
- Confusing thermal conductivity (how fast heat moves) with specific heat capacity (energy needed per degree).

## Exam technique for IB command terms

- **State**: one property or one term, no reason.
- **Outline**: a brief account, such as the steps from polarity to a hydrogen bond.
- **Explain**: property, then cause, then consequence for the organism.
- **Compare and contrast**: give similarities and differences, for example cohesion and adhesion, using "whereas".
- **Suggest**: apply the properties to an unfamiliar animal or habitat.
- **Deduce**: reach a conclusion from data given, as in worked example 3.
