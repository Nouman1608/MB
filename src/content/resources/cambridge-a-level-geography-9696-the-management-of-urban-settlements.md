This guide covers section 6.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus requires case studies of "urban settlements showing the challenges of, and evaluating the attempted solutions in, each of the following: a shanty town (squatter settlement) in an LIC or MIC" and "providing infrastructure (either power or transport) for a city." It completes Topic 6, Settlement dynamics, in Paper 2 Core Human Geography.

The [6.4 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-management-of-urban-settlements-revision-notes/) outline Dharavi in Mumbai and TransMilenio in Bogotá. This guide works through two different case studies, the Orangi settlement in Karachi and the Metrocable in Medellín, and shows how to evaluate them. Use whichever examples your teacher has taught; the method is the same. Practise with the [6.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-urban-settlements-practice/).

## What the two case studies need

Both case studies have the same two parts: **challenges** and **evaluation of attempted solutions**. A description of what was built is not enough.

| | Shanty town | Infrastructure for a city |
|---|---|---|
| Place | A named squatter settlement in an LIC or MIC | A named city, with either power or transport |
| Challenges | Insecure tenure, overcrowding, lack of water and sanitation, hazards, poor access to jobs and services | Growing demand, congestion or power shortages, cost, environmental damage, unequal access |
| Solutions | Clearance, relocation, site and service, upgrading, self-help, tenure reform | New networks, public transport schemes, pricing, renewable generation |
| Evaluation | Who benefited, at what cost, how sustainable, how replicable | The same |

## Challenges of shanty towns

- **Insecure tenure.** Without legal title, residents risk eviction and have little incentive to invest in their homes. Governments may refuse to supply services to settlements they regard as illegal.
- **Water and sanitation.** Shared taps, water bought from vendors at high prices, open drains and few toilets spread disease.
- **Hazards.** Settlements occupy land that formal developers avoid: steep slopes prone to landslides, floodplains, and land next to railways or factories.
- **Overcrowding and fire risk** in dense, self-built housing.
- **Access.** Narrow lanes prevent vehicles, and long journeys to work cost time and money.
- **Livelihoods.** Most residents work in the informal sector with irregular incomes.

## Types of solution

Approaches range from removing settlements to improving them in place:

1. **Clearance and relocation** removes unsafe housing but often moves people far from jobs and breaks up communities.
2. **Site and service schemes** provide plots with water, drainage and roads, on which residents build their own homes.
3. **In-situ upgrading** improves the existing settlement: paving lanes, adding water, drains and lighting, and granting tenure.
4. **Self-help and community-led schemes** organise residents to plan, finance and build improvements themselves.

Since the 1970s, much expert opinion has moved from clearance towards upgrading and community-led schemes, because they are cheaper and keep residents near their work and social networks.

## Worked case study: Orangi, Karachi (Pakistan, an MIC)

**Context.** Orangi is one of the largest informal settlements in Karachi, built up from the 1960s by migrants and refugees on land at the city's north-western edge. Residents built their own houses, but the area had no sewers. Waste ran in open lanes and drains, causing disease and damaging houses.

**The challenge.** Government agencies regarded the cost of a conventional sewerage system as too high, and residents could not wait for one.

**The solution.** In 1980 the social scientist Akhtar Hameed Khan founded the Orangi Pilot Project (OPP). Its approach divided sanitation into two parts:

- **"Internal" development**, the household latrine, the lane sewer and the small collector drain, was planned, paid for and built by residents of each lane, with technical advice and training from the OPP.
- **"External" development**, the large trunk sewers and treatment, was left to the government.

Lane residents chose a lane manager, collected money from each household, and built their sewers using simplified designs. Because labour was their own and designs were simple, costs were a fraction of official estimates.

**Evaluation.**

- *Success:* most lanes in Orangi were eventually connected, built and maintained by residents, which gave them ownership and a reason to look after the system. The model was copied in other parts of Karachi and other Pakistani cities.
- *Limits:* the scheme relied on government building the trunk sewers, which was slow and incomplete, so some lane sewers emptied into natural drains. It dealt with sanitation, not tenure, jobs or flood risk.
- *Judgement:* Orangi shows that community-led upgrading can achieve at low cost what a top-down plan did not, but it works best as a partnership in which government supplies what residents cannot.

**A contrasting approach: Favela-Bairro, Rio de Janeiro (Brazil).** From 1994 the city government ran a programme to upgrade favelas in place, with loans from the Inter-American Development Bank. It paved roads, built water and sewer connections, crèches and public spaces, aiming to integrate favelas into the formal city. It reached many settlements, but critics argued that maintenance was weak after the works finished, and that improvements did not tackle insecurity and violence. Comparing Orangi (bottom-up) with Favela-Bairro (top-down, city-funded) is a good basis for evaluation.

## Challenges of providing infrastructure

**Transport:** rapid growth in car ownership causes congestion and air pollution; the poorest live on the periphery, far from jobs, and spend a large share of their income and time on travel; steep or crowded areas cannot be reached by conventional buses or rail; building costs are high.

**Power:** demand rises faster than supply, causing load shedding (planned power cuts); illegal connections are dangerous and lose revenue; fossil-fuel generation adds pollution and greenhouse gas emissions.

## Worked case study: the Metrocable, Medellín (Colombia, an MIC)

**Context.** Medellín lies in a narrow valley in the Andes. Many of its poorest neighbourhoods, built informally by migrants, cling to steep hillsides above the valley floor. The city's metro, opened in 1995, ran along the valley but did not reach these areas. Residents faced long, expensive journeys on steep, congested roads.

**The solution.** In 2004 the city opened the first Metrocable line, a cable car system connecting hillside neighbourhoods in the north-east to a metro station on the valley floor. Fares are integrated with the metro. Further lines followed. The cable cars formed part of a wider policy, known as social urbanism, in which investment in libraries, schools, public spaces and walkways was focused on the poorest districts.

**Evaluation.**

- *Benefits:* journeys to the valley floor became much shorter and safer. Stations brought public spaces, lighting and police presence to neglected areas. The scheme became an international model for cable transport in hilly cities.
- *Limits:* cable cars carry far fewer passengers per hour than a metro line, so they serve a small share of residents. Many people still depend on buses and informal transport. Some flagship buildings associated with the programme later needed major repairs.
- *Judgement:* the Metrocable was an effective, relatively low-cost way to link isolated communities to the city, and its value came as much from the wider investment around it as from the transport itself. It is not, on its own, a solution to transport demand across the whole city.

## Evaluation criteria for both case studies

- **Effectiveness:** did it solve the challenge it targeted, with evidence?
- **Equity:** who gained, who lost, and were the poorest reached?
- **Cost and finance:** who paid, and was it value for money?
- **Participation:** were residents involved in decisions?
- **Sustainability:** will it last without continuing outside funding, and is it environmentally sound?
- **Scale and replicability:** how many people did it reach, and has it been copied elsewhere?

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Evaluate the success of attempts to improve a shanty town you have studied. [10]*

> In Orangi, Karachi, residents had no sewers and waste ran through the lanes (**challenge**). From 1980 the Orangi Pilot Project helped each lane plan, finance and build its own sewer, while government was responsible for trunk sewers (**solution**). Because residents provided labour and used simple designs, costs were a fraction of official estimates (**evidence of success: cost**), and most lanes were eventually connected (**evidence: scale**). However, trunk sewers were built slowly, so some sewage still reached open drains (**limitation**). The scheme improved sanitation but not tenure or flood risk (**limited scope**). It was highly successful within its aims, and the model has been copied elsewhere in Pakistan, but it depended on a partnership that government did not fully deliver (**judgement**).

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a shanty town in an HIC. The syllabus requires an LIC or MIC.
- Writing about both power and transport thinly. Choose one and know it well.
- Describing the scheme without challenges, or the challenges without solutions.
- No dates, names or evidence of results.
- Evaluating only benefits. Every scheme has limits, costs or groups who lose out.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** challenges: be specific to the place, such as steep slopes in Medellín, rather than generic.
- **Explain** why a solution was chosen: link it to the challenge and the resources available.
- **Evaluate**: use the criteria above, give evidence for and against, and end with a clear judgement.
- In Section B, 6.4 questions may combine with 6.2 on urbanisation; explain how rapid growth created the challenges.
