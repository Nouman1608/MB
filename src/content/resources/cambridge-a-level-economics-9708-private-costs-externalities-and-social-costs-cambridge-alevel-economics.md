This guide covers **section 7.4 Private costs and benefits, externalities and social costs and benefits** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content. The syllabus asks for the "definition and calculation of social costs (SC) as the sum of private costs (PC) and external costs (EC)" and of "social benefits (SB) as the sum of private benefits (PB) and external benefits (EB)", including the marginal versions; "positive and negative externalities of both consumption and production"; "deadweight welfare losses"; "asymmetric information and moral hazard"; and the "use of costs and benefits in analysing decisions" (net present value is not required).

The broader [efficiency, market failure and externalities study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-efficiency-market-failure-and-externalities/) solves externality problems with equations. This page works from **tables**, the form data-response questions often use, sorts costs and benefits in a real decision, and compares two projects using social rather than private values. All firms, projects and figures are invented.

## 7.4.1 and 7.4.2 Private, external and social: sorting the items

- **Private costs** fall on the producer or consumer making the decision; **private benefits** go to them.
- **External costs** and **external benefits** fall on third parties, who are not part of the transaction and receive no payment or pay nothing.
- **SC = PC + EC** and **SB = PB + EB**. At the margin: **MSC = MPC + MEC** and **MSB = MPB + MEB**.

**Sorting example: a new airport runway.**

| Item | Category |
|---|---|
| Fuel, staff and landing equipment paid for by the airport and airlines | Private cost |
| Ticket revenue and passengers' benefit from travelling | Private benefit |
| Noise and air pollution for nearby residents | External cost |
| Extra trade for local hotels and shops not connected with the airport | External benefit |

The test for "external" is whether the person affected is a party to the decision. Residents did not choose the runway, so their noise is an external cost.

## 7.4.3 and 7.4.4 Four types of externality

| Type | Example | Curve relationship |
|---|---|---|
| Negative production | A dye works discharges waste into a river used by fishers | MSC above MPC |
| Positive production | A beekeeper's bees pollinate a neighbour's orchard | MSC below MPC |
| Negative consumption | Drivers in a city centre slow each other and add fumes | MSB below MPB |
| Positive consumption | A person who completes a first-aid course can help strangers in an emergency | MSB above MPB |

A production externality comes from making the good; a consumption externality comes from using it. The free market settles where **MPB = MPC**; the social optimum is where **MSB = MSC**.

## Worked example 1: a negative production externality from a table

A chemical plant. Output is in thousands of tonnes; values are per tonne.

| Output | MPB | MPC | MEC | MSC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 80 | 20 | 10 | 30 |
| 20 | 70 | 30 | 15 | 45 |
| 30 | 60 | 40 | 20 | 60 |
| 40 | 50 | 50 | 25 | 75 |
| 50 | 40 | 60 | 30 | 90 |

There is no external benefit, so MSB = MPB.

1. **Market output:** MPB = MPC at **40 thousand tonnes** (both 50).
2. **Social optimum:** MSB = MSC at **30 thousand tonnes** (both 60).
3. The market overproduces by 10 thousand tonnes.
4. **Deadweight welfare loss:** at the market output, MSC is 75 and MSB is 50, a gap of 25 per tonne. The loss is the triangle ½ × 25 × 10,000 = **125,000**.
5. A tax per tonne equal to the MEC at the optimum (20) would raise MPC to MSC at that output and move the market to 30 thousand tonnes.

## Worked example 2: a positive consumption externality from a table

Home insulation, which lowers energy use and so reduces pollution that harms others. Output is in thousands of installations; values are per installation. The marginal external benefit is a constant 12.

| Output | MPB | MEB | MSB | MPC = MSC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 50 | 12 | 62 | 26 |
| 40 | 44 | 12 | 56 | 32 |
| 60 | 38 | 12 | 50 | 38 |
| 80 | 32 | 12 | 44 | 44 |
| 100 | 26 | 12 | 38 | 50 |

1. **Market output:** MPB = MPC at **60 thousand** (both 38).
2. **Social optimum:** MSB = MSC at **80 thousand** (both 44).
3. The market under-provides by 20 thousand installations.
4. **Deadweight welfare loss:** at 60 thousand, MSB is 50 and MSC is 38, a gap of 12. The loss is ½ × 12 × 20,000 = **120,000**.

## 7.4.5 What the deadweight welfare loss means

The deadweight welfare loss is the net loss of welfare to society from producing the wrong quantity.

- **Overproduction** (negative externality): each unit between the optimum and the market output costs society more (MSC) than it is worth (MSB). The triangle adds up these excess costs.
- **Underproduction** (positive externality): each unit between the market output and the optimum is worth more to society than it costs, but it is not produced. The triangle adds up the benefits lost.

In a diagram, shade only the triangle between MSB and MSC, bounded by the two quantities, and label both quantities and both curves.

## 7.4.6 Asymmetric information and moral hazard

**Asymmetric information** exists when one party to a transaction knows more than the other. Examples: a seller of a used phone knows whether the battery is failing; a buyer of health insurance knows more about their own health than the insurer. The less-informed party may pay too much, refuse to trade, or set terms that drive good-quality sellers or low-risk buyers out of the market, so mutually beneficial trades are lost.

**Moral hazard** occurs when a party takes more risk, or less care, because someone else bears the cost of the outcome. Examples: an insured driver who parks carelessly; a bank that lends riskily because it expects to be rescued if it fails; an employee who works less hard when the employer cannot observe effort. Moral hazard arises from asymmetric information after an agreement is made, because the other party cannot see the change in behaviour.

## 7.4.7 Using costs and benefits to analyse decisions

A decision taken on private costs and benefits can differ from one taken on social costs and benefits. A social decision-maker, such as a government, should count **all** costs and benefits, private and external.

**Worked example 3: two projects for one site.** Values in millions.

| | Project A: public park | Project B: shopping centre |
|---|---|---|
| Private benefit (fees, sales revenue) | 2 | 30 |
| External benefit (health, amenity, jobs nearby) | 9 | 2 |
| Private cost | 7 | 22 |
| External cost (congestion, noise) | 1 | 9 |
| Net private benefit | 2 − 7 = −5 | 30 − 22 = +8 |
| Net social benefit | (2 + 9) − (7 + 1) = +3 | (30 + 2) − (22 + 9) = +1 |

A private developer would choose B, the only project with a private gain. On social values, A gives the greater net benefit. This gap between private and social decisions is the reason governments use cost-benefit analysis for public projects.

**Problems to evaluate:** intangible items such as health or noise are hard to value in money; future costs and benefits must be forecast; the result can change if one uncertain value changes; and the analysis may ignore who gains and who loses.

## Common mistakes

- Adding external costs to benefits, or external benefits to costs.
- Calling any cost to the firm an external cost.
- Shading the whole area between curves instead of the triangle bounded by the two quantities.
- Forgetting to multiply by the quantity scale (thousands) in a welfare-loss calculation.
- Confusing moral hazard with asymmetric information in general; moral hazard is a change in behaviour after an agreement.
- Using net present value; the syllabus says it is not required.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Calculate:** build MSC or MSB columns before finding any equilibrium.
- **Explain:** name the third party and the cost or benefit they bear.
- **Analyse:** draw MPC, MSC (or MPB, MSB), both quantities and the welfare-loss triangle.
- **Evaluate:** in cost-benefit questions, test whether the decision changes if a hard-to-value item changes.
