## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alcohol | Compound containing the –OH (hydroxyl) functional group on a saturated carbon; homologous series CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH |
| Hydrogen bond | Attraction between a δ+ H on O–H and a lone pair on O of another molecule |
| Primary alcohol | C–OH carbon bonded to one other carbon (methanol: none) |
| Secondary alcohol | C–OH carbon bonded to two other carbons |
| Tertiary alcohol | C–OH carbon bonded to three other carbons |
| [O] | Symbol for oxygen from the oxidising agent, used to balance oxidation equations |
| Elimination | Removal of a small molecule (here H₂O) from a larger one, forming a C=C bond |
| Substitution | One atom or group (here –OH) replaced by another (here a halogen) |

## 4.2.1(a)(i): Physical properties in one table

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Polarity | O is more electronegative than C and H, so O–H and C–O bonds are polar |
| Higher boiling point than an alkane of similar Mᵣ | Similar London forces, plus hydrogen bonds between –OH groups; more energy to separate molecules |
| Low volatility | Same reason: hydrogen bonds hold molecules in the liquid |
| Short-chain alcohols soluble in water | –OH forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules |
| Solubility falls with chain length | Larger non-polar chain cannot hydrogen-bond and disrupts hydrogen bonding in water |
| Boiling point rises with chain length | More electrons, stronger London forces |

Link to 2.2.2(l) for hydrogen bonding and 4.1.2(c) for London forces in alkanes.

### Method: comparing boiling points

1. Pick compounds of similar Mᵣ, so London forces are about equal.
2. Name the extra force in the alcohol: hydrogen bonding.
3. Say hydrogen bonds are stronger than London forces.
4. Conclude: more energy needed to overcome the intermolecular forces, so a higher boiling point.

## 4.2.1(a)(ii): Classification shortcut

Count the hydrogens on the C–OH carbon as a check:

| Class | H atoms on C–OH carbon | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 2 (3 in methanol) | heptan-1-ol, CH₃(CH₂)₆OH |
| Secondary | 1 | 2-methylpentan-3-ol |
| Tertiary | 0 | 2-methylhexan-2-ol |

## 4.2.1(b): Combustion

General equation: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH + (3n/2) O₂ → n CO₂ + (n + 1) H₂O

| Alcohol | Balanced equation |
|---|---|
| methanol | 2CH₃OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 4H₂O |
| propan-1-ol | 2C₃H₇OH + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 8H₂O |
| butan-2-ol | C₄H₉OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O |

A half such as 4.5O₂ still balances the equation; double everything when whole numbers are asked for. Incomplete combustion gives CO or C.

## 4.2.1(c): Oxidation with Cr₂O₇²⁻/H⁺

Reagent: potassium dichromate with sulfuric acid (K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄). Colour change when oxidation happens: **orange to green**.

| Alcohol | Conditions | Product | [O] equation (general) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Gentle heat; distil the aldehyde out as it is made | Aldehyde | RCH₂OH + [O] → RCHO + H₂O |
| Primary | Excess oxidising agent, heat under reflux | Carboxylic acid | RCH₂OH + 2[O] → RCOOH + H₂O |
| Secondary | Heat under reflux | Ketone | R₂CHOH + [O] → R₂CO + H₂O |
| Tertiary | Heat | None; the colour remains orange | No equation |

### Must-know distinctions

- **Distil vs reflux.** Distillation removes the aldehyde from the oxidising agent; reflux keeps it in the flask until it becomes the acid.
- **Aldehyde vs alcohol boiling point.** Aldehydes have no O–H, so no hydrogen bonds between their molecules. They boil lower and distil out first.
- **Ketone vs acid.** A secondary alcohol stops at the ketone. Only primary alcohols reach a carboxylic acid.
- **Why tertiary resists.** No H atom on the C–OH carbon to remove.

### PAG7 test

Warm the sample with a few drops of acidified potassium dichromate in a water bath. Green means a primary or secondary alcohol or an aldehyde (6.3.1(c)(vi)); orange remaining fits a tertiary alcohol.

### Small worked reminder

Butan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH:

- distilled as formed: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO + H₂O (butanal)
- reflux, excess oxidant: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH + H₂O (butanoic acid)

Check: count H atoms. Alcohol 10 H; aldehyde 8 H + water 2 H. Balanced.

## 4.2.1(d): Dehydration to alkenes

- Reagent: concentrated H₃PO₄ or concentrated H₂SO₄ as acid catalyst; heat.
- General: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH → CₙH₂ₙ + H₂O
- Mechanism not required.

### Method: predict the alkene(s)

1. Find the C–OH carbon.
2. Note every adjacent carbon bearing one or more H atoms.
3. Make a C=C to each different neighbour in turn; each gives a product.
4. Check each product for E/Z isomerism (4.1.3).

Example: 2-methylhexan-2-ol, (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃, gives 2-methylhex-1-ene and 2-methylhex-2-ene. The second has two CH₃ groups on one C=C carbon, so it has no E/Z isomers.

## 4.2.1(e): Substitution to haloalkanes

- Reagents: halide ions in acid, for example NaBr with H₂SO₄, heated.
- In-situ step: NaBr + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HBr
- Substitution: ROH + HBr → RBr + H₂O
- Mechanism not required.

Example: heptan-1-ol gives 1-bromoheptane, CH₃(CH₂)₆Br.

## Reaction map

| From an alcohol | Reagent | Product type |
|---|---|---|
| Any alcohol | O₂, burn | CO₂ + H₂O |
| Primary | Cr₂O₇²⁻/H⁺, distil | Aldehyde |
| Primary | excess Cr₂O₇²⁻/H⁺, reflux | Carboxylic acid |
| Secondary | Cr₂O₇²⁻/H⁺, reflux | Ketone |
| Any with H on a neighbouring carbon | H₃PO₄ or H₂SO₄, heat | Alkene |
| Any | NaBr/H₂SO₄, heat | Bromoalkane |

## Quick self-test

1. Classify each alcohol: (a) 2-methylhexan-3-ol, (b) 3-ethylhexan-3-ol, (c) 2-ethylbutan-1-ol.
2. Write a balanced equation, with whole numbers, for the complete combustion of heptan-1-ol.
3. Calculate the amount of O₂, in mol, for complete combustion of 0.200 mol of propan-1-ol.
4. Explain why ethanol mixes with water but hexane does not.
5. Name the product when 2-methylpropan-1-ol is warmed with acidified dichromate and the product is distilled off as it forms.
6. Write the [O] equation for oxidising 2-methylpropan-1-ol fully to the carboxylic acid.
7. 2-methylbutan-2-ol is warmed with acidified potassium dichromate. What is seen, and why?
8. Name every alkene that forms when pentan-3-ol is heated with concentrated H₃PO₄.
9. Give the reagents and an equation for making 2-bromobutane from butan-2-ol.
10. 3.52 g of pentan-2-ol (Mᵣ 88.0) is oxidised and 2.75 g of pentan-2-one (Mᵣ 86.0) is collected. Calculate the percentage yield.

### Answers

1. (a) Secondary: the C–OH carbon (C3) is bonded to C2 and C4. (b) Tertiary: C3 is bonded to C2, C4 and the ethyl carbon. (c) Primary: the OH is on a –CH₂OH at the end of the chain.
2. 2C₇H₁₅OH + 21O₂ → 14CO₂ + 16H₂O
3. propan-1-ol needs 4.5 mol O₂ per mole, so 0.200 × 4.5 = **0.900 mol**.
4. Ethanol's –OH forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules. Hexane is non-polar and cannot hydrogen-bond, so it cannot replace the hydrogen bonds between water molecules.
5. **2-methylpropanal**, (CH₃)₂CHCHO.
6. (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH + 2[O] → (CH₃)₂CHCOOH + H₂O
7. The mixture stays orange. It is a tertiary alcohol with no H on the C–OH carbon, so it is not oxidised.
8. Only **pent-2-ene**, as (E)- and (Z)-pent-2-ene. Both neighbours of C3 are equivalent, so removing H from C2 or C4 gives the same structure.
9. NaBr and H₂SO₄, heat. CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ + HBr → CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃ + H₂O
10. n(pentan-2-ol) = 3.52 ÷ 88.0 = 0.0400 mol; theoretical mass = 0.0400 × 86.0 = 3.44 g; yield = 2.75 ÷ 3.44 × 100 = **79.9%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing boiling points without matching Mᵣ, or naming "stronger London forces" as the reason an alcohol boils higher.
- Writing that covalent O–H bonds break when an alcohol boils.
- Drawing a hydrogen bond to the H of a C–H bond; only the H on O–H takes part.
- Classifying by the number of H atoms on the whole molecule, or by the position number in the name.
- Leaving out water as a product in [O] equations, or putting 2[O] in the ketone equation.
- Writing "reflux" for making the aldehyde.
- Saying tertiary alcohols "do not react" without the observation: the solution stays orange.
- Naming only one alkene when the C–OH carbon has two different neighbours carrying H.
- Writing "Br₂" or "bromine water" as the reagent for making a bromoalkane from an alcohol.
