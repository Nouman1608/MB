## Health and disease (B6.3a)

Health is more than not being ill. It is a state of physical, mental and social wellbeing. A person can have no infection and still be in poor health because of stress, isolation or poor sleep. Mental health counts as health, a point the specification itself flags as a common gap.

Disease is a condition that stops part of the body, or a plant, from working normally. The two ideas are linked in both directions:

- Disease lowers health. Pain, tiredness and low energy affect physical health.
- Poor health makes disease more likely. A poor diet weakens the body's defences, so infections take hold more easily.
- One disease can harm the other dimensions. A long illness can stop someone working or seeing friends (social) and can cause anxiety or low mood (mental).

So disease is one cause of poor health, not the only one. Many diseases are also interlinked, which is the subject of B6.3c.

## Types of disease (B6.3b)

The central split is communicable against non-communicable.

| | Communicable | Non-communicable |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | A pathogen: virus, bacterium, protist or fungus | No pathogen; genes, diet, lifestyle, environment, ageing |
| Passed between people? | Yes | No |
| Examples | Influenza, cholera, malaria, athlete's foot | Cystic fibrosis, scurvy, many cancers, type 2 diabetes |

Other useful groupings, all non-communicable:

- Inherited diseases, caused by faulty alleles passed from parents. Cystic fibrosis is one.
- Deficiency diseases, caused by missing nutrients. Scurvy comes from too little vitamin C.
- Diseases linked to lifestyle, such as many cardiovascular diseases.
- Mental illness, which is a disease of mental health with no pathogen.

The specification warns that learners confuse which diseases are inherited and which are caught. Cancer is a good test case. It is not caught like a cold, and it is mostly not inherited either: it usually starts with changes in the DNA of body cells during life. Treat it as non-communicable.

Plants have both types too. A plant virus is communicable. Magnesium deficiency in a plant, which turns leaves yellow, is non-communicable.

## Interactions between diseases (B6.3c)

Types of disease affect each other. The specification names two pairs.

**HIV and tuberculosis (TB).** HIV is a virus that damages the immune system. TB is a bacterial infection of the lungs. A person with HIV is less able to fight bacteria, so they are more likely to develop TB after exposure, and more likely to have a latent TB infection become active. TB then adds further strain to an already weakened body. One communicable disease makes another easier to catch and harder to survive. You do not need the detail of how HIV attacks immune cells for this statement; the cause and effect is the point.

**HPV and cervical cancer.** Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a virus. A long-lasting infection with certain types of HPV can change the cells of the cervix so that they divide out of control. Here a communicable disease is a cause of a non-communicable one. The cancer itself cannot be passed on.

Other links follow the same logic: malnutrition can weaken defences against infection, and a long infection can lead to depression. When asked to "describe an interaction", name both diseases, say which direction the effect runs, and give the mechanism in one clause.

## How communicable disease spreads (B6.3d)

A pathogen needs a route from an infected host to a new one. The four pathogen groups can each use several routes.

| Pathogen group | Animal example | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Virus | Influenza | Droplets from coughs and sneezes breathed in |
| Bacterium | Cholera | Drinking water or food contaminated with faeces |
| Protist | Malaria | Mosquito vector carries the protist in its saliva |
| Fungus | Athlete's foot | Direct contact, and shared damp floors and towels |

General routes in animals:

- Direct contact, including body fluids and mother to baby.
- Air, through droplets.
- Water and food, where hygiene or sanitation is poor.
- Vectors, organisms that carry a pathogen without being harmed.

Plants cannot cough, so routes differ. Tobacco mosaic virus spreads by contact, including on hands and tools. Fungal spores of diseases such as ash dieback travel on the wind. Water splash moves bacteria and spores between leaves, contaminated soil spreads root diseases (clubroot of cabbages is caused by a soil protist), and sap-feeding insects can carry viruses between plants. Humans spread plant disease on equipment and in imported plants.

Spread is faster when hosts are crowded, when hygiene is poor, when climate suits the pathogen or vector, and when no one has immunity. Plants grown close together pass leaf-to-leaf infections quickly.

### Working with numbers (M2c, M2d, M2g, M4a)

**Pathogen numbers.** Bacteria divide by binary fission, doubling each time. After n divisions, number = start × 2ⁿ.

*Worked example 1.* A culture starts with 40 bacteria that divide every 30 minutes. How many are there after 5 hours? How long to pass one million?

- 5 hours = 10 divisions.
- 40 × 2¹⁰ = 40 × 1024 = 40 960, or 4.1 × 10⁴ in standard form to 2 significant figures.
- 40 × 2¹⁴ = 655 360 is below a million; 40 × 2¹⁵ = 1 310 720 is above. So 15 divisions: 15 × 30 min = 7.5 hours.

Real numbers grow slower: food, space or oxygen run out, and the body kills some cells.

**Estimating cases from a sample.** Counting everyone is often impossible, so a random sample is tested and scaled up.

*Worked example 2.* A town has 12 000 people. A random sample of 300 is tested and 27 are infected.

- Proportion infected = 27 ÷ 300 = 0.09, or 9%.
- Estimated cases = 0.09 × 12 000 = 1080.

This is an estimate. A larger, random and representative sample gives a better one. Testing only people with symptoms would overestimate it.

**Incidence and percentage change.** Incidence lets you compare places of different size: new cases per 100 000 people.

*Worked example 3.* A district of 35 000 people has 84 new cases in a year.

- Incidence = 84 ÷ 35 000 × 100 000 = 240 per 100 000.
- If annual cases fall from 180 to 117, change = (180 − 117) ÷ 180 × 100 = 35% decrease.

**Scatter diagrams and correlation.** The table gives cases of a vector-borne disease in a clinic against the number of vectors trapped in a month.

| Vectors trapped | 10 | 22 | 35 | 41 | 58 | 66 | 79 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clinic cases | 3 | 6 | 10 | 9 | 16 | 19 | 22 |

Plotted, the points rise from bottom left to top right close to a straight line: strong positive correlation. As vectors increase, cases increase. Correlation alone does not prove cause; here a known biological link (the vector carries the pathogen) makes cause likely, but another factor such as rainfall could affect both.

## Reducing and preventing spread (B6.3e)

Each control method breaks one link in the route from host to host. Match the method to the route.

| Route | Control in animals |
|---|---|
| Contact | Hand washing, isolation of cases, protective clothing |
| Air | Covering coughs, ventilation, isolation |
| Water and food | Sewage treatment, clean drinking water, food hygiene |
| Vector | Mosquito nets, insecticide, draining standing water, removing breeding sites |
| Host not immune | Vaccination (covered on the immune system page) |

For plants:

- Destroy or remove infected plants so they cannot release spores.
- Quarantine and inspect imported plants.
- Clean tools, wash hands and rotate crops so pathogens do not build up in soil.
- Space plants, remove weeds that act as hosts, and control insect vectors.
- Use fungicides on fungal disease, or grow resistant varieties.

**Detection** lets control start early. The specification lists three methods:

- Visual identification: recognising characteristic symptoms such as a rash, or leaf spots and mosaic patterns. Quick and cheap, but similar symptoms can have different causes and some infected hosts show none.
- Detection of the antigen: a test finds a protein from the pathogen in a sample. It is fast and can be used on site.
- DNA testing: finds pathogen DNA, so it can identify the exact species or strain, even at low numbers.

Detecting a case lets you isolate the host, treat it and trace contacts before the pathogen spreads further.

*Worked example 4.* Fungicide trial: 100 plants each. Untreated, 64 became infected; sprayed, 21.

- Reduction = (64 − 21) ÷ 64 × 100 = 67%.
- Conclusion: the spray reduced infection by about two thirds, but did not stop it completely. A repeat on more plants would show whether the result is reliable.

## Common errors

- Writing that health means "no disease". Include mental and social wellbeing.
- Calling cancer or heart disease communicable. They are not caught from others.
- Saying HPV "is" cervical cancer. HPV is the virus; the cancer is a separate, non-communicable disease it can cause.
- Treating a vector as the pathogen. The mosquito is the carrier; the protist is the pathogen.
- Giving a control method that does not fit the route, such as mosquito nets for a water-borne disease.
- Stating that a correlation proves one thing causes another.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the relationship between health and disease?**
Disease lowers health, and poor health makes disease more likely; for example, a poor diet weakens the body's defences. Disease is one cause of poor health, but not the only one.

**Which disease interactions are named in OCR GCSE Biology A?**
The OCR GCSE Biology A (J247) specification names two interactions between types of disease: HIV and tuberculosis, and HPV and cervical cancer.

**How can the spread of disease in plants be detected?**
The specification names detecting the antigen, DNA testing and visual identification as ways to identify infections so that their spread can be reduced or prevented.

**Is this topic on both tiers?**
Yes. None of statements B6.3a to B6.3e is printed in bold, so they are assessed in both Foundation and Higher Tier papers.
