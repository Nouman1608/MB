These questions cover section 6.4 Implementation and Measurements of Unit 6: Practical Skills in Physics II, Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics (YPH11), Issue 3. Each question gives partial details of how an experiment was carried out.

## Section A

**1.** Amend each column heading: (i) T² / s; (ii) lg I / W m⁻²; (iii) 1/V / cm³; (iv) Q / C⁻¹. **[4]**

**2.** A digital thermometer reads to 0.1 °C. A student records 23.47 °C, 40 °C and 48.66 °C. Write each to the correct number of figures. **[3]**

**3.** Explain how a marker improves the timing of oscillations of a pendulum. **[2]**

**4.** A student times 5 oscillations of a spring of period about 0.60 s. Reaction-time uncertainty is 0.2 s. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the time, and the percentage uncertainty if 30 oscillations were timed instead. **[3]**

**5.** To investigate the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field, a student measures the force at currents of 0.5 A, 1.0 A and 1.5 A. The supply can give up to 5.0 A safely. Comment on the number and range of readings. **[3]**

**6.** A student records counts from a radioactive source but describes no measurement without the source. Explain why this is a weakness. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A 1.00 kg aluminium block is heated by a 36 W heater. A student records its temperature every minute.

| t / s | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| θ / °C | 20.0 | 22.4 | 24.80 | 28.5 | 29.6 | 32 |

**(a)** Identify and amend the incorrect unit. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify and correct **two** entries with incorrect significant figures, assuming the thermometer reads to 0.1 °C. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify the inconsistent reading and state the value expected. **[2]**
**(d)** Use the consistent readings to calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium, ignoring energy losses. **[3]**
**(e)** Suggest **two** improvements, using additional apparatus, and explain each. **[4]**

**8.** To find the volume of a large can, a student holds a 30 cm ruler across the end of the can, reads the diameter once as 7.4 cm, and measures the height with the same ruler.

**(a)** Describe how set squares should be used to measure the diameter. **[3]**
**(b)** Comment on the way the diameter is recorded. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the diameter should be measured at more than one orientation. **[1]**

**9.** A student records the counts in 10 s from a decaying source every 30 s. The background is 5 counts in 10 s.

| t / s | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Counts in 10 s | 405 | 325 | 261 | 238 | 169 | 136 |

**(a)** Correct the counts for background and show which reading is inconsistent. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the background correction matters more at later times. **[2]**
**(c)** The half-life is about 93 s. Comment on the range of times used. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest how the random nature of decay should be allowed for. **[2]**

**10.** A student measures how the period of a mass on a spring depends on the mass. She uses masses of 0.10 kg, 0.15 kg and 0.20 kg, and times 5 oscillations once for each, starting the stopwatch when the mass is at the top of its motion. Comment on her method and suggest improvements. **[6]**

**11.** In a collision experiment a card on a glider passes through a light gate. The student measured the card's length with a 30 cm ruler as 5 cm. His table includes the headings "velocity / m s" and "momentum / kg m s⁻²".

**(a)** Amend both headings. **[2]**
**(b)** Comment on the card measurement and suggest an improvement. **[3]**

**12.** A student pushes the plunger of a sealed syringe quickly to each new volume and reads a pressure gauge at once.

| V / cm³ | 50 | 40 | 30 | 25 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| p / kPa | 100 | 125 | 175 | 200 | 250 |

**(a)** Use pV to identify the inconsistent reading and state the value expected. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how pushing quickly and reading at once could affect the readings. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest an improvement to the method. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) T² / s² [1]. (ii) lg (I / W m⁻²) [1]. (iii) V⁻¹ / cm⁻³ [1]. (iv) Q / C [1]. **[4]**

**2.** 23.5 °C [1]; 40.0 °C [1]; 48.7 °C [1]. **[3]**

**3.** A fixed marker at the equilibrium position gives a definite point to start and stop the timer [1]; the bob passes it at its highest speed, so the moment is sharper than judging the turning point [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 5 oscillations: 0.2 ÷ 3.0 × 100% = **6.7%** [1]. 30 oscillations: total about 18 s [1], so 0.2 ÷ 18 × 100% = **1.1%** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Three readings are too few to establish a proportional relationship [1]. The range uses only up to 1.5 A of the 5.0 A available [1], so take at least six readings from about 0.5 A to 5.0 A [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Without a background count rate, counts from other sources are included in every reading [1], so the count rate from the source is overestimated, a systematic error, especially when the count is low [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** The times are in minutes, so the heading should be **t / min** (or convert the values to seconds) [1].
**(b)** 24.80 should be **24.8** [1]; 32 should be **32.0** [1].
**(c)** **28.5 °C at 3 min** [1]: the temperature rises 2.4 °C each minute, so 27.2 °C is expected [1].
**(d)** Rate of rise = 2.4 °C per minute = 2.4 ÷ 60 = 0.0400 K s⁻¹ [1]. P = mc(Δθ/Δt), so c = 36 ÷ (1.00 × 0.0400) [1] = **900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1].
**(e)** Wrap the block in insulation [1], reducing energy lost to the surroundings, which would make c too large [1]. Put a few drops of oil in the thermometer hole [1], improving thermal contact so the thermometer reads the block's true temperature [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* A joulemeter measuring the energy supplied directly is also a creditworthy improvement.

**8. (a)** Lay the can on its side against a metre rule fixed to the bench [1]. Place a set square on each side with its vertical edge just touching the can [1], and read the rule at each set square; the diameter is the difference between the readings [1].
**(b)** 7.4 cm is given to 1 mm, which a ruler can read, but a single reading taken across a curved end may be a chord, not the diameter, and should be written in mm as, for example, 74 mm with its uncertainty [1].
**(c)** The can may not be perfectly circular, so readings at several orientations give a mean diameter [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** Corrected counts: 400, 320, 256, 233, 164, 131 [1]. Each step should multiply by 0.80; 256 × 0.80 = 205 is expected at 90 s [1], so **238 (233 corrected)** is inconsistent [1].
**(b)** The source's count falls with time while the background stays the same [1], so the background becomes a larger fraction of each reading [1].
**(c)** 150 s is only about 1.6 half-lives [1]; continue to about 300 s, three or more half-lives, to test the exponential decay fully [1].
**(d)** Count for longer than 10 s at each time, or repeat [1], because random variation is a smaller fraction of a larger count [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Three masses are too few, and 0.10–0.20 kg is a narrow range [1]: use at least six masses over a wider range, for example 0.10–0.60 kg [1]. Timing 5 oscillations gives a large reaction-time uncertainty [1]: time 20 oscillations and divide [1]. Starting at the top of the motion, where the mass is momentarily still, makes the instant uncertain [1]: use a fiducial marker at the equilibrium position and repeat each timing three times [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** velocity / m s⁻¹ [1]; momentum / kg m s⁻¹ [1].
**(b)** "5 cm" is recorded to the nearest centimetre, although a ruler reads to 1 mm [1]; the card length goes straight into the velocity, so a large uncertainty here spoils every result [1]. Measure it with Vernier calipers, for example 50.0 mm, at both edges [1]. **[5]**

**12. (a)** pV is 5000 kPa cm³ for every reading except 30 cm³, which gives 5250 [1]; expected p = 5000 ÷ 30 = **167 kPa** [1].
**(b)** Rapid compression warms the air [1], so the pressure read at once is higher than at constant temperature [1].
**(c)** Move the plunger slowly and wait about a minute after each change [1], so the air returns to room temperature before p is read [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting a unit on a logarithm, or forgetting to square or invert a unit.
- Commenting on significant figures without writing the corrected value.
- Identifying an inconsistent reading with no expected value.
- Forgetting that background counts must be subtracted as rates before any pattern is checked.
- Giving improvements with no error named.
