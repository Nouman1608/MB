## Section A

**1.** State approximately when the Quaternary period began. **[1]**

**2.** Outline how ice cores provide evidence of past climate. **[2]**

**3.** Name **two** natural causes of climate change. **[2]**

**4.** Define mitigation and adaptation in the context of climate change. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain how tree rings and pollen analysis provide evidence of past climate. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why orbital changes cannot account for the warming observed since about 1850. **[2]**

**7.** Atmospheric carbon dioxide has risen from about 280 parts per million before the industrial era to about 420 parts per million. Calculate the percentage increase. Show your working. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how a large volcanic eruption can affect global climate. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how agriculture contributes to climate change. **[4]**

**10.** Describe **two** effects of climate change on people and **two** effects on the environment. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways a low-lying coastal region could adapt to rising sea levels. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate international agreements as a way of mitigating climate change. **[6]**

**13.** "Mitigation is more important than adaptation in managing climate change." Do you agree? Justify your answer. **[9]**

---

## Answers

**1.** About 2.6 million years ago [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Air bubbles trapped in layers of ice preserve samples of past atmosphere, so carbon dioxide levels can be measured [1]. The chemistry of the ice reflects the temperature when the snow fell, and layers can be dated back hundreds of thousands of years [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two: orbital changes [1]; volcanic activity [1]; solar output [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Mitigation: reducing the causes of climate change, such as cutting greenhouse gas emissions [1]. Adaptation: responding to the effects of climate change, such as changing farming or building flood defences [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Trees grow one ring a year [1]; wide rings show warm or wet years with good growth and narrow rings show cold or dry years, so the sequence records past conditions [1]. Pollen is preserved in peat and lake sediments [1]; because each plant species has distinctive pollen, the mix shows which plants grew and so what the climate was like [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Orbital cycles act over tens of thousands of years [1], so they are far too slow to cause the rapid warming seen in about 170 years [1]. **[2]**

**7.** (420 - 280) ÷ 280 × 100 [1] = **50%** [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Large eruptions send sulphur dioxide high into the atmosphere [1], where it forms tiny droplets that reflect sunlight back to space [1], cooling the Earth for a year or two, as after Mount Pinatubo in 1991 [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Livestock such as cattle release methane during digestion [1]; flooded rice fields also release methane [1]. Nitrogen fertilisers release nitrous oxide [1]. These are greenhouse gases that trap heat, so expanding agriculture to feed growing populations strengthens the greenhouse effect [1]. **[4]**

**10.** People, any two: coastal flooding and erosion of homes and farmland [1]; changing crop yields [1]; water shortages where rivers depend on glaciers [1]; heat stress or spread of insect-borne disease [1]. Environment, any two: sea-level rise [1]; loss of sea ice and glaciers [1]; coral bleaching [1]; species moving to new ranges or declining [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Any two, 2 marks each: build sea walls or flood barriers [1] to hold back higher tides and storm surges [1]; raise buildings on stilts [1] so floodwater passes underneath, reducing damage [1]; restore mangroves [1], which absorb wave energy and trap sediment [1]; move homes and services away from the most at-risk coast [1] so fewer people are exposed [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Climate change is global, so agreements allow countries to act together rather than leaving action to a few [1].
- The Paris Agreement (2015) sets a shared goal of keeping warming well below 2 °C [1].
- It lets each country set its own plan, so many countries can take part [1].
- However, targets depend on countries keeping promises, and some may not [1].
- Earlier agreements such as the Kyoto Protocol (1997) set targets only for richer countries, limiting their effect [1].
- Judgement: essential for coordinating action, but only as effective as the national policies that follow [1]. **[6]**

**13.** Levels-style indicative content:
- Agree: mitigation tackles the cause, so without it warming and its effects keep increasing [1].
- Agree: adaptation becomes harder and more expensive the more warming occurs [1].
- Agree: examples of mitigation, such as alternative energy and tree planting, benefit the whole world [1].
- Disagree: some change is already locked in, so people must adapt now whatever is done [1].
- Disagree: adaptation protects lives directly, for example sea walls or drought-resistant crops in vulnerable areas [1].
- Disagree: lower-income countries contribute little to emissions, so adaptation may matter more to them [1].
- Uses an example of each, such as the Paris Agreement and the Delta Works [1].
- Judgement: both are needed; mitigation decides how much change happens, adaptation how much harm it does [1].
- Judgement is justified with a clear reason [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting tree planting under adaptation or sea walls under mitigation.
- Saying volcanoes cause long-term warming.
- Naming evidence without explaining how it records climate.
- Dividing by the new value instead of the original in percentage calculations.
- Effects listed with no example or place.
- Extended answers that list points for both sides without a supported judgement.
