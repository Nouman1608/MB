This guide covers **Article 2 of the European Convention on Human Rights 1953**, part of section 3.5
Human Rights in AQA A-level Law (7162). The specification lists two things: "Article 2.1: right to life"
and "Article 2.2: justified exceptions". How English law gives effect to the right, through homicide
law, policing and inquests, is covered in the
[Human Rights and English law study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-human-rights-and-english-law/).
For the whole option in outline, start with the
[Human Rights study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-human-rights-study-guide/).

## Where Article 2 fits

Article 2 is a **limited** right: the only exceptions are the ones written into Article 2.2, and there is
no general balancing test. It is among the most fundamental rights in the Convention. Under Article 15,
no derogation is permitted from it, except for deaths resulting from lawful acts of war.

## Article 2.1: the right to life

Article 2.1 begins: "Everyone's right to life shall be protected by law." It then provides that no one
shall be deprived of life intentionally save in execution of a court's sentence following conviction of
a crime for which the death penalty is provided by law.

- **The death penalty exception** is now of historical interest for the UK. **Protocol 6** abolished the
  death penalty in peacetime and **Protocol 13** in all circumstances; the UK has ratified both, and
  Article 1 of Protocol 13 is a Convention right under the Human Rights Act 1998.
- **"Protected by law"** is the source of the state's **positive obligations**: it must not only
  refrain from killing but must also take steps to protect life.

### Three kinds of obligation

| Obligation | Content | Leading case |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | State agents must not take life except as Article 2.2 allows | McCann v UK (1995) |
| Positive: framework | An effective system of criminal law and regulation to deter and punish threats to life | Öneryildiz v Turkey (2004): failure to regulate a dangerous rubbish tip where a methane explosion killed nearby residents |
| Positive: operational | Reasonable steps to protect a person from a real and immediate risk to life that the authorities knew or ought to have known about | Osman v UK (1998) |
| Procedural | An effective official investigation into deaths involving the state | Jordan v UK (2001) |

### The operational duty in more detail

The **Osman test** is demanding. The authorities must have known, or ought to have known, of a **real and
immediate risk to the life of an identified individual** from the criminal acts of a third party, and
failed to take measures within their powers which, judged reasonably, might have been expected to avoid
that risk.

- In **Osman v UK (1998)** the test was not met: the police could not be said to have known of a real
  and immediate risk at any decisive stage.
- In **Edwards v UK (2002)** a prisoner was killed by a mentally ill cellmate. The authorities had
  information about the cellmate's dangerousness that was not passed on, and Article 2 was breached.
- In **Van Colle v Chief Constable of Hertfordshire (2008)** the House of Lords applied Osman strictly:
  a witness was shot by the defendant in a criminal case, but the earlier threats did not show a real and
  immediate risk to life, so there was no breach.
- In **Rabone v Pennine Care NHS Foundation Trust (2012)** the Supreme Court held that a hospital owed
  the operational duty to a voluntary psychiatric patient at real and immediate risk of suicide, and
  breached it by allowing her home leave.

### Scope: when life begins and ends

- **Unborn children**: in **Vo v France (2004)** the Court declined to decide whether an unborn child is
  a "person" under Article 2, leaving the question within the state's margin of appreciation. In
  **Evans v UK (2007)** there was no breach of Article 2 when embryos were to be destroyed after the
  father withdrew consent.
- **A right to die**: in **Pretty v UK (2002)** the Court held that Article 2 cannot be interpreted as
  conferring the opposite right, a right to die, so the ban on assisted suicide did not breach it.

## Article 2.2: justified exceptions

Article 2.2 provides that deprivation of life is not a breach when it results from the use of force which
is **no more than absolutely necessary**:

- **(a)** in defence of any person from unlawful violence;
- **(b)** in order to effect a lawful arrest or to prevent the escape of a person lawfully detained;
- **(c)** in action lawfully taken for the purpose of quelling a riot or insurrection.

### "Absolutely necessary"

The words **"absolutely necessary"** set a **stricter test** than "necessary in a democratic society" in
the qualified rights. The force must be **strictly proportionate** to achieving one of the listed aims.

Article 2.2 covers **unintentional** as well as intentional killing: it applies whenever lethal force is
used, even if death was not the aim.

### McCann v UK (1995)

Three IRA suspects believed to be planning a car bomb attack in Gibraltar were shot dead by soldiers. The
soldiers honestly believed that the suspects could detonate a bomb remotely, so their own actions were
not a breach. But the Court found a **violation** because of the **planning and control** of the
operation: the authorities failed to arrest the suspects earlier, did not allow for the possibility
that their intelligence was wrong, and the soldiers were trained to shoot to kill. Article 2 therefore
requires the state to plan operations to minimise the risk to life.

### Honest belief

In **Da Silva v UK (2016)** the Grand Chamber held that force is assessed on the basis of an **honest
belief, perceived for good reasons, to be valid at the time**, even if it turns out to be mistaken. The
test is subjective, but the belief must be genuinely held and the reasons for it are examined.

### Riots and demonstrations

- In **Stewart v UK (1984)** the European Commission found no breach where a soldier fired a plastic
  baton round during a riot in Northern Ireland and killed a boy; the force was within 2.2(c) in the
  circumstances.
- In **Güleç v Turkey (1998)** security forces used live ammunition against demonstrators and killed a
  boy. The Court found a breach: the force was not absolutely necessary, and the forces lacked
  less lethal equipment.

## The procedural duty

Wherever a person dies as a result of the use of force by the state, or in state custody, there must be
an effective official investigation. **Jordan v UK (2001)** sets out its features: it must be
**independent**, capable of establishing the facts and identifying those responsible, **prompt**, open
to sufficient **public scrutiny**, and must involve the **family** of the deceased.

## Worked example

*Facts: Police receive a tip-off that Marek has a gun and plans to rob a bank. They let him enter the
bank so they can catch him "in the act" rather than arrest him at home. An officer shoots him dead when
he raises an object later found to be a phone. Marek's family were told nothing for two weeks, and an
investigation was carried out by officers from the same force.*

```
Article 2.2:    Was the shooting for an aim in (a) or (b)? Defence of others
                from unlawful violence, or effecting a lawful arrest.
Absolutely      The officer's honest belief, for good reasons, that Marek
necessary:      was raising a gun may justify the shot itself (Da Silva).
Planning:       Choosing to let an armed suspect enter a bank instead of
                arresting him earlier may breach Article 2 through poor
                planning and control (McCann).
Procedural:     Investigation by the same force is not independent, and
                the family was not involved (Jordan): a separate breach.
Conclusion:     Likely violation of Article 2 on planning and on the
                investigation, even if the officer is not personally at fault.
```

## Common mistakes

- Applying a proportionality balancing test as if Article 2 were qualified.
- Treating "absolutely necessary" as the same as "necessary in a democratic society".
- Looking only at the person who used force and ignoring planning and control (McCann).
- Forgetting the procedural duty to investigate, which can be breached on its own.
- Saying Article 2 protects a right to die; Pretty v UK held it does not.
- Treating Osman as a case where the state was liable; the test was not met.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: set out 2.1, the obligations and 2.2, each with one case.
- **Suggest** and **Advise** questions: identify the obligation in play (negative, operational or
  procedural), apply the relevant test and conclude on breach.
- **Consider** questions: develop both sides, for example whether a risk was "real and immediate" or
  whether force was absolutely necessary.
- **Discuss** questions: evaluate how well Article 2 protects life, for example whether the Osman test is
  too demanding or whether McCann places too heavy a burden on security forces.
