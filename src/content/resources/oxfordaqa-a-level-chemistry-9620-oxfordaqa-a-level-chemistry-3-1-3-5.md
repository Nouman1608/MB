This guide covers **3.1.3.5 Shapes of simple molecules and ions** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. The specification treats bonding pairs and lone (non-bonding) pairs of electrons as **charge clouds that repel each other**, states that electron pairs arrange themselves **as far apart as possible to minimise repulsion**, and gives the order of repulsion:

**lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair**

You must be able to name and explain the shapes of, and bond angles in, simple molecules and ions with **up to six electron pairs** (including lone pairs) around the central atom.

The [shapes, polarity and intermolecular forces overview](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-shapes-polarity-and-intermolecular-forces/) summarises the theory. This page gives a reliable counting method, works through ions and five- and six-pair species, and shows how to write a full-mark explanation. Then use the [shapes revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-5-revision-notes/) and the [shapes practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-5-practice/).

## A counting method that works for molecules and ions

For a central atom joined to other atoms by single bonds:

1. Take the number of **outer electrons** on the central atom (its group number for Groups 1 to 7; 8 for xenon).
2. **Add one** electron for each atom bonded to it (each single bond contains one electron supplied by the bonded atom).
3. For a **negative ion add** one electron per negative charge; for a **positive ion subtract** one per positive charge.
4. Divide by two to get the **number of electron pairs**.
5. **Bonding pairs** = number of atoms bonded. **Lone pairs** = total pairs − bonding pairs.

The total number of pairs fixes the arrangement of the charge clouds; the number of lone pairs then fixes the shape, because **a shape is named from the positions of the atoms only**.

## Why lone pairs change bond angles

A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it sits closer to the central atom and spreads out more than a bonding pair, which is pulled out between two nuclei. It therefore repels the other pairs more strongly. Each lone pair pushes the bonding pairs closer together, and a common working rule at this level is a reduction of about 2.5° per lone pair in the four-pair family: 109.5° → 107° → 104.5°.

In ammonia and the oxonium ion, H₃O⁺, the arrangement of four pairs is tetrahedral, but one position is a lone pair. The atoms form a pyramid, and the H–X–H angle closes to about 107°.

## Five and six pairs: where do the lone pairs go?

**Five pairs.** The trigonal bipyramid has two kinds of position. Three **equatorial** positions are 120° apart in a triangle; two **axial** positions are above and below, at 90° to the triangle. Lone pairs go **equatorial**, where they have more room and fewer neighbours at 90°. This gives:

- one lone pair: **see-saw** (SF₄). The angles close from 90° and 120°; the equatorial F–S–F angle in SF₄ is about 102°.
- two lone pairs: **T-shaped** (ClF₃), with F–Cl–F angles just under 90°.
- three lone pairs: all three equatorial, leaving the two atoms axial: **linear**, exactly 180° (XeF₂, I₃⁻).

**Six pairs.** All six positions of an octahedron are equivalent, so one lone pair can go anywhere (square pyramidal, as in BrF₅). A second lone pair goes **opposite** the first, at 180°, to keep lone pair–lone pair repulsion as small as possible. The four atoms are left in a square: **square planar**, 90°.

## Worked example 1: a full explanation for an ion

**Question.** Name the shape of the NH₂⁻ ion and explain why its bond angle is 104.5°.

**Annotated answer.**

- Nitrogen has 5 outer electrons, plus 2 from the hydrogens, plus 1 for the negative charge: 8 electrons, so **4 electron pairs** [counting is shown, not assumed].
- 2 are bonding pairs and 2 are lone pairs [both numbers stated].
- The four pairs repel and arrange themselves as far apart as possible, in a tetrahedral arrangement [the principle].
- Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so the two lone pairs push the bonding pairs closer together [the reason for the reduction].
- The shape is **bent**, with a bond angle of about **104.5°** [shape and angle].

Stating "two lone pairs, so 104.5°" without the repulsion order earns only part of the credit.

## Worked example 2: an octahedral ion

**Question.** Phosphorus(V) chloride in the solid state contains PCl₄⁺ and PCl₆⁻ ions. Give the shape and bond angle of each.

- PCl₄⁺: 5 + 4 − 1 = 8 electrons, 4 pairs, all bonding: **tetrahedral, 109.5°**.
- PCl₆⁻: 5 + 6 + 1 = 12 electrons, 6 pairs, all bonding: **octahedral, 90°**.

The same element can sit at the centre of two different shapes: the shape depends on the number of electron pairs, not on the element.

## Drawing shapes

- Use **solid lines** for bonds in the plane of the paper, **wedges** for bonds towards you and **dashed wedges** for bonds away from you.
- Draw lone pairs as two dots or a lobe on the central atom. Do not add lone pairs to the outer atoms unless the question asks.
- Mark at least one bond angle with an arc and its value.
- For square planar shapes, draw the four bonds in one plane at 90°; for octahedral shapes, add one bond up and one down.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to adjust for the charge on an ion, or adjusting in the wrong direction.
- Naming the shape from all the electron pairs (calling water "tetrahedral").
- Quoting 109.5° for every species with four pairs.
- Placing a lone pair axially in a five-pair species.
- Putting two lone pairs next to each other (at 90°) in a six-pair species, which would give the wrong shape.
- Stating the repulsion order the wrong way round, or writing "lone pairs repel more" without saying what that does to the angle.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **name**: the shape name and the angle are enough.
- **Explain** or **deduce**: show the electron count, give bonding and lone pairs, state the "as far apart as possible" principle, apply the repulsion order, then give the shape and angle.
- **Draw**: show the three-dimensional arrangement, any lone pairs on the central atom and at least one labelled angle.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you work out the number of electron pairs in an ion?**
Add the outer electrons of the central atom to one electron for each bonded atom. Add one more electron for each negative charge or subtract one for each positive charge, then divide by two.

**Why is the bond angle in H₃O⁺ about 107°?**
H₃O⁺ has four electron pairs around oxygen: three bonding and one lone pair. The lone pair repels more strongly than the bonding pairs, so it pushes the O–H bonds closer together than the 109.5° of a regular tetrahedron.

**Why is XeF₂ linear?**
Xenon has five electron pairs: two bonding and three lone pairs. The three lone pairs occupy the equatorial positions, leaving the two fluorine atoms opposite each other at 180°.
