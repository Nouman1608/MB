## Questions

**1.** This question is about bases and alkalis.

**(a)** State the difference between a base and an alkali. **[2]**
**(b)** Which one of these is an alkali: magnesium oxide, potassium hydroxide, copper(II) hydroxide? **[1]**

**2.** Name the salt formed in each reaction.

**(a)** iron(II) oxide and hydrochloric acid **[1]**
**(b)** aluminium hydroxide and sulfuric acid **[1]**
**(c)** ammonia and hydrochloric acid **[1]**

**3.** Four colourless solutions, A to D, are tested with universal indicator: A yellow, B purple, C red, D green.

**(a)** Describe how the student should use universal indicator to find the approximate pH of each solution. **[2]**
**(b)** State which solution contains the highest concentration of hydrogen ions. **[1]**
**(c)** State which solution is neutral and give its pH. **[1]**

**4.** This question is about the ions in acids and alkalis.

**(a)** Name the ion that makes a solution alkaline. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the ionic equation for neutralisation. Include state symbols. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why sodium hydroxide with nitric acid and potassium hydroxide with hydrochloric acid have the same ionic equation. **[1]**

**5.** Dilute sulfuric acid is added from a burette to 25.0 cm³ of potassium hydroxide solution. The pH is recorded after each addition.

| Volume of acid added / cm³ | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 | 20 | 24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pH | 13.0 | 12.9 | 12.7 | 12.4 | 7.0 | 1.9 | 1.6 |

**(a)** State the volume of acid that exactly neutralised the alkali. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe how the pH changes as the acid is added and explain the change in terms of ions. **[3]**
**(c)** Write a balanced equation for the reaction. **[2]**
**(d)** Name the salt in the flask at 16 cm³. **[1]**

**6.** This question is about ammonia and limewater.

**(a)** Explain why a solution of ammonia in water turns universal indicator blue. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the product of ammonia with hydrochloric acid and write a balanced equation. **[2]**
**(c)** A gas is bubbled through limewater and the limewater turns cloudy. Name the solid that forms and write a balanced equation for its formation. **[3]**

**7.** A student wants to make crystals of iron(II) sulfate from iron filings.

**(a)** Name the acid she should use. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why this method would be unsuitable for making copper sulfate from copper, and also for making sodium sulfate from sodium. **[2]**
**(c)** Write a balanced equation for the reaction of iron with the acid. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the iron is added in excess, and state how she can tell that the reaction has finished. **[2]**

**8.** Magnesium nitrate can be made from magnesium oxide, an insoluble base.

**(a)** Write a balanced equation for the reaction of magnesium oxide with nitric acid. **[2]**
**(b)** A student adds 3.00 g of magnesium oxide to warm nitric acid. After filtering and drying, 0.48 g of solid is left. Calculate the mass of magnesium oxide that reacted, and explain why it matters that some solid was left. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how the student gets dry crystals of magnesium nitrate from the filtrate. **[3]**

**9.** To make ammonium sulfate, a student puts 20.0 cm³ of ammonia solution and indicator in a conical flask and runs in dilute sulfuric acid from a burette.

**(a)** Explain why an indicator is used instead of adding an excess of one reactant and filtering. **[1]**
**(b)** The volumes of acid needed in three trials were 18.10 cm³, 18.20 cm³ and 18.55 cm³. Using only the results that agree within 0.10 cm³, calculate the mean volume. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how the student then makes a solution of ammonium sulfate that contains no indicator. **[2]**
**(d)** Write a balanced equation for the reaction. **[1]**

**10.** This question is about insoluble salts.

**(a)** Name two solutions that could be mixed to make silver chloride. **[2]**
**(b)** Name two solutions that could be mixed to make barium carbonate, and write a balanced equation for the reaction. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the precipitate is washed with distilled water after filtering. **[1]**

**11.** A technician has these chemicals: zinc hydroxide (insoluble), copper metal, lead(II) nitrate solution, sodium sulfate solution, and dilute hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acids.

**(a)** Describe how to make pure, dry crystals of zinc sulfate. Include an equation. **[5]**
**(b)** Describe how to make a pure, dry sample of lead(II) sulfate, an insoluble salt. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the copper metal cannot be used to make copper(II) chloride, and name a type of copper compound that could be used instead. **[2]**

**12.** Acidic waste water from a metal-finishing works contains dissolved copper(II) ions. Calcium hydroxide is added before release.

**(a)** Give two reasons for adding calcium hydroxide. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the solid containing copper that forms. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest how this solid is removed from the water. **[1]**
**(d)** Give one example of precipitation being used to remove unwanted ions when treating water for drinking, naming the precipitate. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A base is a metal oxide or hydroxide [1]; an alkali is a base (hydroxide) that is soluble in water [1].
**(b)** **Potassium hydroxide** [1].
*Examiner insight:* "An alkali neutralises acids" does not separate it from a base; solubility earns the second mark.

**2. (a)** **Iron(II) chloride** [1]
**(b)** **Aluminium sulfate** [1]
**(c)** **Ammonium chloride** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Ammonia chloride" is not the name of a salt; the ion is ammonium, and the spelling change is usually needed for the mark.

**3. (a)** Add a few drops of universal indicator to a sample (or use indicator paper) [1]; compare the colour with the pH colour chart [1].
**(b)** **C** (red) [1].
**(c)** **D, pH 7** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A method that never mentions the colour chart is incomplete; the colour alone is not the pH.

**4. (a)** **Hydroxide ion, OH⁻** [1].
**(b)** H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l): correct ions and product [1]; correct state symbols [1].
**(c)** In both, the only reaction is between H⁺ and OH⁻ ions; the other ions do not change [1].
*Examiner insight:* When state symbols are asked for, an equation without them usually earns only one of the two marks.

**5. (a)** **16 cm³** [1].
**(b)** The pH starts at 13 and falls slowly [1], then drops sharply through 7 at 16 cm³ to below 2 [1]. H⁺ from the acid reacts with OH⁻; once all OH⁻ is used up, extra H⁺ makes the solution acidic [1].
**(c)** 2KOH + H₂SO₄ → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O: correct formulae [1]; balanced [1].
**(d)** **Potassium sulfate** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Quote values from the table (13, 7, 1.6) before giving the ionic reason; a general "it gets more acidic" scores little.

**6. (a)** Ammonia reacts with water to form hydroxide ions [1]: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, so the solution is alkaline [1].
**(b)** **Ammonium chloride** [1]; NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl [1].
**(c)** **Calcium carbonate** [1]. Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O: correct formulae [1]; balanced [1].
*Examiner insight:* Adding water as a product in part (b) makes the equation wrong; ammonia joins with the acid directly.

**7. (a)** **Sulfuric acid** [1].
**(b)** Copper is not reactive enough to react with dilute acid [1]; sodium is too reactive and would react dangerously [1].
**(c)** Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂ [1]
**(d)** So that all the acid is used up and the solution contains only the salt [1]; bubbling (fizzing) stops and iron is left over [1].
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) needs one point about copper and one about sodium; "it doesn't work" earns nothing.

**8. (a)** MgO + 2HNO₃ → Mg(NO₃)₂ + H₂O: correct formulae [1]; balanced [1].
**(b)** 3.00 − 0.48 = **2.52 g** [1]. Left-over solid shows that all the acid has reacted, so no acid contaminates the salt [1].
**(c)** Heat the filtrate to evaporate some water until crystals form on a cold glass rod [1]; leave to cool [1]; filter off the crystals and dry between filter papers [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Evaporate all the water" or "heat until dry" describes something different from crystallisation and usually loses the first mark.

**9. (a)** Both reactants are solutions, so there is no excess solid to filter off [1].
**(b)** Use 18.10 and 18.20 [1]; mean = (18.10 + 18.20) ÷ 2 = **18.15 cm³** [1].
**(c)** Repeat with 20.0 cm³ of ammonia solution and 18.15 cm³ of acid [1], but without adding indicator [1].
**(d)** 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄ [1]
*Examiner insight:* Including the 18.55 cm³ trial in the mean is the usual slip; give two decimal places, like the readings.

**10. (a)** **Silver nitrate** solution [1] and **sodium chloride** solution (or any soluble chloride) [1].
**(b)** **Barium chloride** (or barium nitrate) solution and **sodium carbonate** solution [1]. BaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → BaCO₃ + 2NaCl: correct formulae [1]; balanced [1].
**(c)** To remove the soluble salt (such as sodium chloride) left on the solid [1].
*Examiner insight:* Naming "silver" or "chlorine" in part (a) does not score; each reagent must be a named solution of a soluble compound.

**11. (a)** Add zinc hydroxide to warm dilute sulfuric acid, stirring, until some is left undissolved [1]. Filter off the excess solid [1]. Heat the filtrate until it is saturated [1], then cool so crystals form, filter and dry them [1]. Zn(OH)₂ + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + 2H₂O [1].
**(b)** Mix lead(II) nitrate solution and sodium sulfate solution [1]; filter off the precipitate [1]; wash with distilled water and dry [1].
**(c)** Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it does not react with dilute acid [1]; use an insoluble base such as copper(II) oxide [1].
*Examiner insight:* The excess, the filtering and the crystallising are separate creditworthy steps; leaving one out costs a mark.

**12. (a)** It neutralises the acid in the waste water [1]; it supplies hydroxide ions that precipitate the copper(II) ions [1].
**(b)** **Copper(II) hydroxide** [1].
**(c)** Let it settle and filter (or decant) the water off [1].
**(d)** Removing calcium ions from hard water [1] by precipitating them as calcium carbonate [1].
*Examiner insight:* In part (a), two separate points are needed for two marks; repeating "it neutralises" in different words scores once.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling an insoluble oxide or hydroxide an alkali.
- Adding water to the products of ammonia and an acid.
- Forgetting state symbols in H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) when they are asked for.
- Using universal indicator without the colour chart.
- Missing the "filter off the excess" step, or keeping the residue when the salt is in the filtrate.
- Naming an element or an insoluble solid as a reagent for an insoluble salt.
- Including a non-concordant trial in a mean volume.
