## Why conventions matter

A **text type** is a kind of text defined by its purpose, audience and form: an advert, a speech, a
news report. A **convention** is a feature readers expect in that type, such as a headline on a
news report or a call to action on an advert.

Conventions matter in analysis for three reasons.

1. **They create expectations.** "Our view" above an article tells you to expect the paper's own
   opinion.
2. **Each convention does a job.** A byline names who is responsible; a source line asks you to
   trust the numbers.
3. **Breaking a convention is a choice.** An advert with no product in it stands out because it is
   unexpected.

Say which convention you see, what it does, and why it suits this audience and purpose.

## The eight text types

### Advertisements

Purpose: sell a product, service or brand, or build a feeling about it. Conventions: brand name and
logo, a slogan or tagline, a headline, a dominant image, short body copy, a call to action ("Book
now"), contact details or a web address, and sometimes small print. Adverts often use direct address
("you"), imperatives and appeals to emotion, authority or reason. Ask: what does the image promise
that the words do not say outright?

### Editorials

An editorial is the publication's own opinion on a current issue. It is usually unsigned and speaks
as "we", the voice of the paper. A common shape is: the issue, the paper's position, reasons and
evidence, a concession to the other side, then a recommendation. Compare an **opinion column**,
which is signed and uses a personal "I". Ask: whose authority stands behind the "we"?

### Speeches

A speech is written to be heard, on a particular occasion, by a particular audience. Conventions:
an opening address ("Friends", "Members of the board"), reference to the occasion, inclusive "we",
repetition, lists of three, rhetorical questions, short sentences and clear signposts, because a
listener cannot re-read. A transcript loses tone and pauses.

### Blogs

A blog is a series of posts by one writer or a small group, shown with the newest first. Conventions:
a title and date on each post, first person, a conversational register, a recognisable persona,
headings, embedded images, links, tags and a comments section. Ask: what relationship does the
writer build with regular readers, and how?

### Infographics

An infographic presents information visually for quick reading. Conventions: a headline or headline
figure, charts, icons or pictograms, colour coding, very little running text, a clear reading path
and a source line. Every infographic selects. Ask: which number does the design make you see first, and why?

### Photographs

In analysis, treat a photograph as a set of choices, not a window. Look at **shot distance**
(close-up, medium, long), **angle** (high, eye level, low), **framing and cropping**, **focus**,
**lighting**, **colour**, **composition** (where the subject sits in the frame) and **gaze** (does
the subject look at the viewer or away?). A **caption** often fixes the meaning of an image. This is
called **anchorage**: the same picture can say different things under different captions.

### Cartoons

An editorial cartoon comments on a topical issue, usually in a single panel. Conventions:
caricature and exaggeration, visual metaphor, symbols the reader will recognise, labels on people
or objects, a caption or speech bubble, and irony. A cartoon relies on the reader knowing the
context, so explain what the reader must already know for the joke to work.

### Reports

A **news report** gives an account of recent events. Conventions: headline, standfirst (a short
summary under the headline), byline, dateline, third person, quotations from sources, and the
**inverted pyramid**: the most important facts first, detail and background lower down. A report
appears neutral, but choice of headline, quotations and order still shapes the reader's view. A
**formal report** from an organisation uses a title, a summary, headed sections, findings and
recommendations, an impersonal register and often the passive voice ("It was found that...").

## Reading images and layout

Use this vocabulary for any visual or multimodal text.

| Term | Meaning | Typical effect to discuss |
|---|---|---|
| Salience | What stands out most, through size, colour, contrast or position | Tells you what the producer wants noticed first |
| Reading path | The route the eye takes around the page | Controls the order in which ideas are met |
| Vector | A line that leads the eye: a pointing arm, a road, a gaze | Links two elements, for example a face and a product |
| Demand and offer | Subject looks at the viewer (demand) or away (offer) | Demand creates contact; offer invites us to observe |
| Anchorage | Words that fix the meaning of an image | Narrows many possible readings to one |
| Hierarchy of type | Differences in font size and weight | Signals what matters most and what can be skimmed |
| White space | Empty areas of the page | Isolates an element and makes it feel important or calm |
| Typeface | The style of lettering | Can suggest tradition, informality, urgency or luxury |

In languages written left to right, readers often start top left and finish bottom right, so a
call to action or logo is commonly placed at the end of that path. Treat this as a tendency to test
against the text, not a rule.

Describe precisely before you interpret: "a low-angle shot against a grey sky" is evidence; "a nice
picture" is not.

## Worked example 1: an editorial

> **Original text written for Marlbridge** -- editorial from an invented local newspaper
>
> **Our view: Keep the night market in the square**
>
> For eleven summers, Friday nights in Kelby have ended in the square: forty stalls, strung lights,
> a fiddler nobody booked. Next month the council will vote on moving the market to the car park
> behind the leisure centre, where, it says, there is "more room and less mess".
>
> We understand the worry about litter. We have seen the bins on a Saturday morning. But the answer
> to litter is more bins, not fewer people.
>
> The square is the only place in Kelby where the town meets itself. Move the market to a car park
> and it becomes a shopping trip. Keep it in the square and it stays an evening out.
>
> We urge councillors to vote no, and to spend the money for the move on bins and cleaners instead.

**Step 1: name the type and its conventions.** The label "Our view" and the unsigned, collective
"We" mark this as an editorial: the paper speaks as an institution. It follows the usual shape:
issue (the vote), position (keep the market), concession ("We understand the worry about litter"),
rebuttal, and a recommendation ("We urge councillors to vote no").

**Step 2: explain what each convention does.** The institutional "We" gives the view the weight of
the whole paper, not one writer. The concession makes the paper sound fair, and the rebuttal is short
and balanced: "more bins, not fewer people". Quoting the council's phrase "more room and less mess"
lets readers hear the other side in its own words, and the paper then answers it.

**Step 3: look at language inside the conventions.** The list "forty stalls, strung lights, a
fiddler nobody booked" ends with an unplanned detail, which makes the market feel alive and
unofficial. The contrast "a shopping trip" against "an evening out" states the argument in two short
phrases a reader can repeat.

**Step 4: link to audience.** Local readers know the square, so the paper appeals to shared memory
before it asks them to agree.

## Worked example 2: a photograph and two captions

> **Original text written for Marlbridge** -- description of a news photograph
>
> [Photograph: a low-angle shot of a woman in a high-visibility jacket standing on top of a new
> flood barrier. She looks out of the frame to the left. A grey sky fills the top two-thirds of the
> image. The town's rooftops are small in the bottom corner.]
>
> Caption A: "Engineer Dana Okoro checks the new barrier on its first day."
>
> Caption B: "Is this all that stands between Wexmoor and the next flood?"

**Describe.** Low angle; the figure stands above the viewer; she looks away (an offer, not a
demand); the sky dominates; the town is small and low in the frame.

**Interpret with Caption A.** The low angle makes the engineer look strong and in control. Her gaze
out of frame suggests she is watching for danger on the town's behalf. The picture reads as
reassurance.

**Interpret with Caption B.** The same grey sky now looks threatening, and the tiny rooftops look
exposed. The rhetorical question turns one person on a wall into a sign of how little protection
there is. The picture reads as a warning.

**Conclude.** The image has not changed; the caption has anchored it.

## Common errors

- **Naming the type and stopping.** "This is an infographic" earns little. Say which conventions it
  uses and what they do.
- **Confusing editorial and opinion column.** Check for a byline and for "we" or "I".
- **Treating a report as neutral.** Selection and order are choices. Look at what comes first and
  who is quoted.
- **Vague visual description.** Name the angle, shot distance, position and colour before you
  interpret.
- **Analysing the image and the words separately.** Explain how they combine.
- **Ignoring broken conventions.** When a text does something unexpected for its type, that is
  usually worth a paragraph.
