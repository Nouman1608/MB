## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Define marginal revenue product. **[1]**
**(b)** A potter sells every item at a fixed price of 7. Employing one more worker raises output by 9 items. Calculate the MRP of that worker. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the demand for carpenters is a derived demand. **[4]**

**3.** A print stall sells posters at a fixed price of 8. Its total output of posters per day is 28, 52, 72, 88 and 100 with 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 workers.
**(a)** Calculate the MPP and the MRP of each worker. **[3]**
**(b)** The wage is 120 per day. State how many workers the stall hires and justify your answer. **[2]**
**(c)** The poster price falls to 7. Calculate the new employment at a wage of 120. **[2]**
**(d)** State whether (c) is a movement along or a shift of the demand curve for labour. **[1]**

**4.** A furniture maker must cut its price to sell more. With 1, 2, 3 and 4 workers it makes 6, 11, 15 and 18 chairs and sells them at 50, 46, 43 and 41 each.
**(a)** Calculate total revenue and the MRP of each worker. **[3]**
**(b)** The wage is 100. How many workers are hired? **[2]**

**5.** Explain two factors, other than the wage, that would shift a firm's demand curve for labour. **[4]**

**6.** A firm sells its output at a fixed price and uses a fixed amount of machinery. Explain why its demand curve for labour, which is its MRP curve, slopes downward. **[3]**

**7.** Explain two non-wage factors that affect the supply of labour to an occupation. **[4]**

**8.** In an illustrative region the wage for care assistants rises by 10%, and at the same time a new rule requires two extra years of training before starting the job. Analyse the effects on the supply curve of labour to this occupation. **[4]**

**9.** In a labour market, with W in £ per hour and quantities in thousands, QD = 260 − 8W and QS = −40 + 12W.
**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium wage and employment. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the excess at a wage of 18 and say what pressure it creates. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the excess at a wage of 12. **[1]**

**10.** Using the market in question 9:
**(a)** Demand falls to QD = 220 − 8W. Calculate the new equilibrium wage and employment. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in employment compared with question 9(a), to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Supply then also changes to QS = 20 + 12W. Calculate the new equilibrium and comment on employment. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of marginal revenue product theory for explaining wages in a perfect labour market. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Marginal revenue product is the extra revenue from employing one more worker [1] **[1]**
**(b)** MRP = MPP × price [1] = 9 × 7 = **63** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that mentions revenue, one extra worker and the word "marginal" is easier to credit than one that only says "what a worker earns for the firm".

**2.** Workers are hired to produce goods and services, not wanted for themselves [1]. Demand for carpenters therefore depends on demand for what they build [1]. If demand for new homes or furniture rises, firms expect more sales and want more output [1], so they hire more carpenters. A fall in demand for those products reduces demand for carpenters [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit when they run the chain from product demand to output to labour. Simply stating "it is derived" repeats the question.

**3. (a)** MPP: 28, 24, 20, 16, 12 [1]. MRP = MPP × 8: 224, 192, 160, 128, 96 [2] **[3]**
**(b)** Hire while MRP is at or above the wage [1]. MRP of the fourth worker is 128, above 120, and the fifth's is 96, below it, so **4 workers** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** New MRP = MPP × 7: 196, 168, 140, 112, 84 [1]. The third worker's 140 exceeds 120, the fourth's 112 does not, so employment falls to **3 workers** [1] **[2]**
**(d)** A **shift** (to the left), since the product price changed, not the wage [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show the full MRP row before choosing the number hired. A bare final number cannot show that you compared MRP with the wage at the margin.

**4. (a)** Total revenue: 300, 506, 645, 738 [1]. MRP is the change in TR: 300, 206, 139, 93 [2] **[3]**
**(b)** The third worker adds 139, above 100, and the fourth adds 93, below it [1], so **3 workers** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Using price as MR here would give the wrong MRP. Show TR first so the examiner can see you have used the change in revenue.

**5.** Any two factors, each explained for two marks. For example: a rise in demand for the product raises the revenue from each worker's output [1], so MRP rises and the demand curve shifts right [1]. A rise in labour productivity, through training or better tools, raises MPP and so MRP [1], shifting the curve right [1]. Other answers: a rise in the price of capital makes labour relatively cheaper (right), or a higher cost of employing each worker besides the wage (left). **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of the shift as well as the factor. A list of factors without a direction or a reason gains little.

**6.** The machinery is fixed, so each extra worker has less capital to work with and shares it with others (diminishing marginal returns) [1]. MPP therefore falls as more workers are hired [1]. With a fixed price, MR equals price, so MRP = MPP × price also falls, giving a downward-sloping curve [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the fixed factor and say what happens to MPP before concluding that MRP falls. Answers that jump straight to the shape of the curve leave the chain incomplete.

**7.** Any two factors, each worth two marks. For example: working conditions and hours [1]; better conditions make the job more attractive, shifting supply right [1]. The length of training needed [1]; a longer period raises the cost of entry and shifts supply left [1]. Others: pay in other occupations, migration, retirement age, state benefits. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Higher wages" is a movement along the curve, not a non-wage factor, and gains no credit here.

**8.** The 10% wage rise is a wage factor, so there is an extension along the existing supply curve [1] as more workers are attracted from other jobs [1]. The training rule is a non-wage factor [1] that raises the cost and delay of entry, shifting the curve left [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the movement from the shift. The net effect on the quantity supplied depends on the size of each change, and saying so is sound.

**9. (a)** 260 − 8W = −40 + 12W [1], so 300 = 20W and **W = 15** [1]. Employment = 260 − 120 = **140 thousand** (supply: −40 + 180 = 140) [1] **[3]**
**(b)** At 18: QD = 116 and QS = 176, so **excess supply of 60 thousand** [1]. Unemployed workers compete and the wage falls toward equilibrium [1] **[2]**
**(c)** At 12: QD = 164 and QS = 104, so **excess demand of 60 thousand** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Check the answer in both equations. Equal quantities show the solution is right and are worth showing.

**10. (a)** 220 − 8W = −40 + 12W [1], so 260 = 20W and **W = 13** [1]. Employment = 220 − 104 = **116 thousand** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Change = 116 − 140 = −24 [1]. −24/140 × 100 = **−17.1%** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** 220 − 8W = 20 + 12W gives 200 = 20W, so **W = 10** and employment = 140 thousand [1]. Employment returns to its original level while the wage is lower, because the supply increase offsets the fall in demand [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* When two curves move, state the resulting wage and employment separately. Candidates often assume both must fall.

**11.** Strengths (up to 3). The theory links pay to the value of a worker's output [1]. It explains the hiring rule: employ until MRP equals the wage [1]. It also explains why demand slopes down, through diminishing returns, and why product demand changes labour demand [1]. Limits (up to 4). MRP is hard to measure when workers produce in teams or the output has no market price [1]. The model assumes a profit-maximising firm, identical workers and full information, which real markets rarely have [1]. It is shaped by supply too, and adjustment is slow, so wages may not match MRP in the short run [1]. Information is also imperfect in practice, so workers and firms may not respond to wage differences quickly [1]. Judgement [1]: the theory is a strong benchmark for a perfect market, but it should not be treated as a full account of actual pay. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that says which limit matters most and why tends to be more convincing than a second list of points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using price as marginal revenue when the firm has to lower its price to sell more.
- Showing only the final number of workers hired, without comparing MRP with the wage.
- Treating a wage change as a shift of the demand curve.
- Drawing or describing curves without naming the vertical axis as the wage rate.
- Listing labour supply factors without saying which way the curve shifts.
- Giving a higher wage as a "non-wage factor".
- Skipping diminishing returns when explaining why the labour demand curve slopes downward.
- Giving evaluation as a list with no judgement.
