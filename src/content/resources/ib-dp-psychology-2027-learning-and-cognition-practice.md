## Section B

**6.** Read the summary (constructed for practice).

> A researcher tested whether the wording of a question changes memory for a short film. Sixty students watched a one-minute film of a bicycle hitting a parked car. Thirty were asked, "How fast was the bicycle going when it smashed into the car?" and thirty were asked, "How fast was the bicycle going when it touched the car?" The groups were formed by random allocation. A week later all students were asked, "Did you see a broken mirror?" There was no broken mirror in the film.

**(a)** Identify the independent variable and the dependent variable. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why random allocation was used. **[2]**
**(c)** Predict the likely result and explain it using reconstructive memory. **[3]**
**(d)** Suggest one limitation of the study, and explain one change that would address it. **[3]**

**8.** Compare two models of one cognitive process you have studied. **[6]**

**9.** Explain one strategy that could improve a cognitive process, and evaluate the evidence for it. **[5]**

## Section C

**10.** Evaluate the contribution of research on learning and cognition to our understanding of how behaviour can be changed. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** An organised mental framework built from experience **[1]** that guides perception, memory and behaviour **[1]**.

**2.** Decisions or estimates are influenced by an initial reference value (the anchor) **[1]**, even when the anchor is irrelevant, so judgements stay close to it **[1]**.

**3.** Classical conditioning is learning by association between stimuli, with a neutral stimulus becoming a conditioned stimulus that triggers a response **[1]**. Operant conditioning is learning from consequences, where reinforcement increases and punishment decreases behaviour **[1]**. Classical conditioning is based on association before behaviour; operant conditioning acts on behaviour that is voluntary **[1]**.

**4.** Any one application, such as a token economy in a classroom **[1]**: pupils receive a token for completing homework on time **[1]**, which is positive reinforcement that makes the behaviour more likely to be repeated; tokens are later exchanged for a reward **[1]**.

**5.** The model proposes two modes of thinking **[1]**. One is fast, automatic and intuitive and uses shortcuts; the other is slow, effortful and analytical **[1]**. Fast thinking is efficient but can lead to systematic errors such as cognitive biases, while slow thinking can correct them **[1]**.

**6.** (a) Independent variable: the wording of the question (smashed into or touched) **[1]**. Dependent variable: reported speed (and the report of a broken mirror) **[1]**.

(b) Random allocation gives each student the same chance of being in either group **[1]**, so participant differences such as memory ability are spread evenly and are less likely to explain a difference between groups **[1]**.

(c) The "smashed" group will give higher speed estimates and more false reports of a broken mirror **[1]**. Reconstructive memory proposes that recall combines the original event with later information **[1]**, so the more forceful wording leads participants to incorporate a more violent crash, including details such as broken glass, into the memory **[1]**.

(d) Limitation, any one: students are not representative of the general population, or the film is not an emotional real event, so ecological validity is low **[1]**. Change: use a more varied sample or a staged live event **[1]**, and explain how this would improve generalisability or realism **[1]**.

**7.** Any one process and one cultural factor, for example perception and attention **[1]**. In Masuda and Nisbett (2001) Japanese participants paid more attention to background and relationships, American participants to the main fish **[1]**. The researchers linked this to holistic thinking in some East Asian cultures and analytic thinking in some Western cultures **[1]**. Evaluation: students may not represent whole cultures, so there is a risk of overgeneralising **[1]**.

**8.** Example: multi-store and working memory models. Similarities, any two (up to 2): both propose limited capacity short-term processing; both link short-term to long-term memory; both are tested by lab experiments. Differences, any two (up to 2): the multi-store model has one short-term store while the working memory model has several components; the multi-store model is mainly a flow of information between stores while the working memory model describes active processing. Judgement or evaluation (up to 2): the working memory model explains dual-task findings better; the multi-store model explains primacy and recency effects but oversimplifies rehearsal. Credit explicit comparison words **[6]**.

**9.** Any one strategy, for example spaced practice **[1]**: learning spread across several sessions rather than one long session **[1]**. Evidence: experiments show better long-term recall for spaced practice, supporting the view that repeated retrieval strengthens memory **[1]**. Evaluation, any two: most studies use students and word lists, so ecological validity may be low **[1]**; it requires planning and motivation, so it may not be used consistently **[1]**.

**10.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good analysis lacking development; lower bands for description. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- Operant conditioning and token economies change behaviour, but changes may fade when rewards stop **[2]**.
- Social learning theory: role models change behaviour, as in Bandura's research; applications include health and anti-bullying campaigns **[2]**.
- Dual processing and cognitive biases: knowing about biases allows decision-makers to slow down and use analytic thinking, though awareness does not always remove the bias **[2]**.
- Strategies to improve memory such as spaced and retrieval practice show change in performance **[2]**.
- Change: longitudinal evidence is needed to show lasting change rather than short-term effects in a lab **[2]**.
- Causality and measurement: experiments allow strong causal claims, but short-term tasks such as word recall may not reflect real behaviour change **[2]**.
- Bias and responsibility: student samples limit generalisation, and strategies to influence behaviour raise ethical issues of autonomy **[2]**.
- A conclusion that weighs the evidence **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 6(c):** predicting without using reconstructive memory.
- **Question 8:** describing two models separately without comparison language.
- **Question 10:** listing studies without discussing whether behaviour actually changed, or using only one concept.
