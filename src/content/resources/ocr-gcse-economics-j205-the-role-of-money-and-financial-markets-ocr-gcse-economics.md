## Money as a medium of exchange

A medium of exchange is anything people accept in return for goods and services because they know they can spend it again. The key word is **acceptance**: a payment works only if the seller trusts that others will take the money from them.

Barter has three weaknesses that money solves:

1. **Double coincidence of wants.** Each trader must want what the other offers, at the same time.
2. **Indivisibility.** A farmer cannot easily give a third of a cow for a pair of boots. Money can be split into small amounts.
3. **Too many exchange rates.** With 50 goods and no money, every good needs a rate against every other good. With money, each good has one price, so values are easy to compare.

Money works as a medium of exchange because it is widely accepted, easy to carry, hard to forge, lasts and can be divided. Today most money is not notes and coins but **bank deposits**, moved by card, phone or transfer. That is the first link to the financial sector: the payment system is run by banks.

**Why it matters for the economy.** Because exchange is cheap and quick, people can specialise in one job, sell their labour for money, and buy everything else. Specialisation raises output, so money supports the gains from specialisation you met in section 2.1.

## The financial sector and what it does

The financial sector is the set of institutions that handle money for others. For the economy it performs four jobs.

| Job | How it works | Main institution |
|---|---|---|
| Payments | Accounts, cards and transfers let wages, bills and taxes move safely | Banks |
| Linking savers and borrowers | Deposits from savers are lent to households and firms | Banks and building societies |
| Funding big purchases | Mortgages spread the cost of a home over many years | Building societies and banks |
| Sharing risk | Many people pay small premiums so that the few who suffer a loss are paid | Insurance companies |

**Banks** are companies owned by shareholders. They earn profit mainly from the gap between the rate they charge borrowers and the lower rate they pay savers.

**Building societies** are mutual: they are owned by their members, the savers and borrowers, rather than by shareholders. They concentrate on savings accounts and mortgages, and any surplus can go back to members through better rates.

**Insurance companies** pool risk. Suppose 10,000 households each pay a £150 home insurance premium, a pool of £1.5 million. If about 100 of them make a claim of £12,000 on average, the insurer pays out £1.2 million and keeps the rest for its costs and profit. No single household could afford to rebuild alone, but together they can.

## How different interest rates affect saving, borrowing and investment

The interest rate is the reward for saving and the price of borrowing. There is never one rate: savings rates are below loan rates, a mortgage secured on a house is usually cheaper than an unsecured credit card, and a fixed rate stays the same for an agreed period while a variable rate moves with the central bank's rate.

**Higher rates.** Saving pays more, so households save a larger share of income. Loans cost more, so fewer people borrow for cars, holidays or homes. Firms compare the return on a project with the cost of borrowing, so fewer projects are worth doing and investment falls.

**Lower rates.** The reverse: saving is less rewarding, borrowing is cheaper, and more projects clear the cost of finance.

How strong the effect is depends on:

- **Fixed or variable debt.** A household on a fixed-rate mortgage feels nothing until the deal ends.
- **Net saver or net borrower.** A rise helps a household with savings and no debt, but hurts one with a large mortgage.
- **Confidence.** If firms expect weak sales, cheap loans will not persuade them to invest.
- **Size of the change.** A 0.25 percentage point change matters less than a 2 point change.

## Worked example 1: the effect of a rate change on savings

Amira has £2,400 in an easy-access account paying 3.2% a year. Her bank raises the rate to 3.8%.

- Interest before: 2,400 × 0.032 = **£76.80**.
- Interest after: 2,400 × 0.038 = **£91.20**.
- Change: £14.40 more per year.
- Percentage change in interest earned: 14.40 ÷ 76.80 × 100 = **18.75%**.

The rate rose by 0.6 **percentage points**, but her interest rose by 18.75 **per cent**. Keep these two ideas apart in written answers.

## Worked example 2: net savers and net borrowers

The central bank's rate rises and banks pass on 1.5 percentage points to both savings and mortgage rates.

| | Household P (young family) | Household R (retired couple) |
|---|---|---|
| Savings | £5,000, rate 2.5% to 4.0% | £60,000, rate 2.5% to 4.0% |
| Interest earned | £125 to £200, up £75 | £1,500 to £2,400, up £900 |
| Variable-rate mortgage | £160,000, rate 4.0% to 5.5% | None |
| Interest paid | £6,400 to £8,800, up £2,400 | £0 |
| Net effect per year | Worse off by £2,325 | Better off by £900 |

Household P will probably cut spending by about £194 a month; household R may spend more. The same policy moves the two households in opposite directions, which is why the overall effect on spending depends on how much of the economy's debt is on variable rates.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the importance of the financial sector for producers.*

"Producers rely on banks for loans to buy machinery, so a bakery can invest in a new oven before it has earned the money, which raises its output and future profit. **[a clear chain from service to benefit]** Insurance means a firm whose warehouse floods is paid for the lost stock, so it can keep trading rather than close. **[a second, different service]** However, borrowing has a cost: if interest rates rise, repayments on variable loans take a larger share of revenue, and a firm with falling sales may be unable to repay. **[the other side, linked to interest rates]** Small new firms may also be refused loans because they have no record. **[limitation for a named type of producer]** Overall the sector is essential for producers who need to invest, but its value depends on loans being affordable and available, so it matters most for capital-heavy firms and least for small service firms that fund themselves from profit." **[judgement that says what it depends on and chooses]**

## Common mistakes

- Defining money as "notes and coins". Most money is bank deposits.
- Saying a building society is "a small bank". The distinguishing point is mutual ownership by members.
- Writing "interest rose by 0.6%" when the rate rose by 0.6 percentage points.
- Assuming every borrower is hit at once by a rate rise, ignoring fixed rates.
- Treating saving and investment as the same thing. In economics, investment means firms buying capital goods.
- Forgetting the government when asked about consumers, producers **and** government.

## Exam technique

Component 01 begins with 20 multiple-choice questions in Part A; Part B has three questions built on short case studies. In this subtopic:

- **State / give an example**: one precise point, such as "insurance companies pool risk".
- **Explain**: give the role and say why it matters, using the case study if there is one.
- **Calculate**: write the formula, substitute, and give units; show the change as well as the new figure if asked for "the effect".
- **Analyse**: link each step: rate rises, loan repayments rise, disposable income falls, spending falls.
- **Evaluate**: cover both sides for the group named, then judge what the outcome depends on.
