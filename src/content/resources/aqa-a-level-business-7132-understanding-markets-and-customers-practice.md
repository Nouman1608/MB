## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A company has 600 factory staff and 400 office staff. It uses a stratified sample of 50 employees for a survey. How many office staff should be in the sample? **[1]**

A: 10

B: 20

C: 25

D: 30

**2.** A product has a price elasticity of demand of −0.6. The business raises its price by 5%. Which **one** of the following is most likely? **[1]**

A: Quantity demanded falls by more than 5% and revenue falls

B: Quantity demanded falls by less than 5% and revenue rises

C: Quantity demanded is unchanged and revenue falls

D: Quantity demanded rises and revenue rises

**3.** Define the term "confidence interval". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** disadvantage of using quota sampling. **[3]**

**5.** A bakery chain surveys 500 of its 12,000 regular customers. 35% say they would use a home delivery service. The result has a margin of ±4 percentage points at 95% confidence.

**(a)** State the confidence interval for the percentage of all customers who would use the service. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the range of the number of the 12,000 customers who might use the service. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain how the interval would change if the bakery surveyed 2,000 customers. **[2]**

**6.** Carrow Garden Tools sells 2,000 spades a month at £15 each. Its price elasticity of demand is −0.5. It raises the price by 10%.

**(a)** Calculate the new monthly revenue. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why revenue changed in this way. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** value of primary research and **one** value of secondary research to a business launching a new product. **[4]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Brightwell Gyms is considering a third gym. Membership (in hundreds) was 12 in 2021 and has risen steadily by about 2 a year, reaching 20 in 2025. The managers extrapolate the trend to forecast 26 for 2028. Assess how useful this forecast is for deciding whether to open the third gym. **[9]**

**9.** Analyse **two** reasons why a start-up should use secondary research before carrying out primary research. **[6]**

**10.** A retailer sells luxury watches. The income elasticity of demand for its watches is +2.4. Analyse how this information could help the retailer to plan during an economic downturn. **[6]**

**11.** A marketing manager says: "A strong positive correlation between social media posts and sales proves that posting more will raise sales." Explain **two** reasons why the manager may be wrong. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of marketing data to managers when making decisions about a new product. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. 400 ÷ 1,000 × 50 = 20. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Demand is inelastic, so quantity falls by about 3%, less than the 5% price rise, and revenue rises. **[1]**

**3.** The range within which the true value for the whole population is likely to lie [1], at a stated level of confidence, such as 95% [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Interviewers choose which people to approach to fill each quota [1], so they may pick people who are easy or pleasant to approach [1], which introduces bias and means the sample may not represent the population, making findings less reliable [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 31% to 39% [1].

**(b)** 31% × 12,000 = 3,720 [1]; 39% × 12,000 = 4,680 [1]. So between 3,720 and 4,680 customers.

**(c)** The interval would become narrower [1], because a larger sample gives a more precise estimate of the population [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** New price = £15 × 1.10 = £16.50 [1]. Quantity falls by 5% (10% × 0.5) to 1,900 [1]. Revenue = £16.50 × 1,900 = **£31,350** [1] (up from £30,000).

**(b)** Demand is inelastic (below 1) [1], so quantity falls by a smaller percentage than the price rises, and revenue increases [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Primary: it gives data specific to the new product and its target customers, for example reactions to a prototype [1], and is up to date and owned by the business [1]. Secondary: it is cheaper and quicker, such as using existing market reports to learn the market size and growth [1], helping the business decide whether the market is worth entering before it spends on its own research [1]. **[4]**

**8.** For: the trend has been steady for four years, which gives some confidence it may continue [1]. Extrapolation is quick and cheap, and gives a figure to plan capacity and staffing [1]. A forecast of 2,600 members suggests demand for a larger or additional site [1]. Against: it assumes conditions stay the same [1]; a rival opening nearby, a recession or a change in fitness fashion could stop the trend [1]. Three years is a long way beyond the data, so the error is likely to grow [1]. It says nothing about where the new gym would be or whether members would use it [1]. A third gym is a large commitment, so the forecast should not be the only evidence [1]. Judgement: it is a useful starting point but should be backed by primary research in the planned location and a range of forecasts (high, medium, low) [1]. **[9]**

**9.** Reason 1: cost and time [1]. A start-up has little money, and secondary sources such as government statistics or trade reports are free or cheap [1], so it can learn the size and growth of the market before spending on surveys [1]. Reason 2: it shapes better primary research [1]. Secondary data shows which segments and competitors matter [1], so the questions and sample for primary research can be targeted, saving money [1]. **[6]**

**10.** A YED of +2.4 means demand is income elastic: demand falls by more than the fall in incomes [1]. In a downturn, incomes fall, so a 5% fall in incomes could cut demand by about 12% [1]. The retailer can therefore forecast a large fall in sales and cut orders of stock to avoid unsold watches [1]. It can reduce staffing hours or delay expansion [1]. It could add lower-priced lines or promotions to keep sales going [1], and use the information to plan cash flow, since revenue will fall faster than incomes [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Reason 1: correlation shows that two things move together, not that one causes the other [1]. Sales may have risen because of a seasonal peak or a promotion running at the same time [1]. Reason 2: the relationship may not continue at higher levels of posting, since extra posts may reach the same customers with no further effect [1], and the direction of cause may be reversed, for example busy periods leading staff to post more [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: data reduces risk by showing the size and growth of the market and the share of competitors [1]. Sampling and surveys can show which features customers value and what price they will pay [1]. Elasticity data helps set a price likely to maximise revenue [1]. Extrapolating past trends helps forecast sales and plan production [1]. Qualitative data explains why customers like or reject a product [1]. Against: data may be out of date, or based on small or biased samples, so results mislead [1]. What customers say they will do is not always what they do, especially for new products with no history [1]. Confidence intervals may be wide [1]. Correlations may be wrongly read as causes [1]. Collection costs time and money, and ethical rules limit how personal data can be used [1]. Factors: the cost of getting the decision wrong, the quality and age of the data, and whether there are comparable products [1]. Judgement: data is essential, but it supports rather than replaces managers' judgement, and for a very new product a trial launch in a small area may be more reliable than any survey [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong evaluation comments on the quality of the data (sample size, bias, age), not only on whether it exists.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a quota sample random.
- Treating correlation as proof of cause.
- Saying a larger sample makes the interval wider.
- Applying the wrong rule for elasticity and revenue.
- Quoting an elasticity figure without saying what it means for the business.
- Treating extrapolation as reliable over a long period.
