This guide covers the full framework of **IB Middle Years Programme
Design**, from 2014. It complements the
[subject overview](/resources/ib-myp-design-subject-guide/) and the
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-design-assessment-revision-notes/)
already on the site by setting out the design cycle that structures the
subject and the four assessment criteria used to judge student work --
the actual process and standards students are held to, not just the
course's aims.

The MYP requires at least 50 hours of teaching time for design in each
year of the programme, rising to a recommended 70 hours of guided
learning per year in MYP years 4 and 5 for students pursuing MYP
eAssessment.

## The design cycle

Design, and the technologies it produces, has driven profound changes
in how societies access and process information, adapt their
environments, communicate and solve problems -- MYP Design asks
students to engage with this directly rather than studying it only in
the abstract. Inquiry and problem-solving sit at the heart of the
subject, structured through the **design cycle** -- the methodology used to structure
inquiry, analyse problems, develop feasible solutions, create those
solutions, and then test and evaluate them. A solution in this subject
can be a model, prototype, product or system independently created and
developed by the student. This cycle is not a one-off sequence followed
once per year; it is the repeated structure every design project in the
course is built around.

## Key and related concepts

The framework is organised around key concepts of **communication,
communities, development and systems**, explored through more specific
related concepts such as adaptation, ergonomics, sustainability and
innovation. As in every MYP subject group, students explore these
concepts through the same six global contexts -- identities and
relationships, orientation in space and time, personal and cultural
expression, scientific and technical innovation, globalization and
sustainability, and fairness and development.

## The four assessment criteria

- **Criterion A: Inquiring and analysing** -- presented with a design
  situation, students identify a problem needing a solution, analyse the
  need for that solution, and conduct an inquiry into the nature of the
  problem.
- **Criterion B: Developing ideas** -- students write a detailed
  specification that drives development of a solution, and present that
  solution.
- **Criterion C: Creating the solution** -- students plan the creation
  of their chosen solution, then follow that plan to create a prototype
  sufficient for testing and evaluation.
- **Criterion D: Evaluating** -- students design tests to evaluate the
  solution, carry out those tests, and objectively evaluate its success,
  identifying areas for improvement and explaining the solution's impact
  on its client or target audience.

Each criterion maps directly onto one stage of the design cycle, so the
four criteria are, in effect, the design cycle's four stages made into
assessable outcomes -- inquiring and analysing (the problem stage),
developing ideas (the specification stage), creating the solution (the
build stage), and evaluating (the test stage). As with every MYP
subject, each criterion is scored on eight achievement levels (1-8),
grouped into four descriptor bands that teachers apply consistently
across every design project a student submits.

## MYP eAssessment: the ePortfolio

Students seeking IB-validated design course results demonstrate their
achievement by submitting an **ePortfolio** rather than sitting a
written on-screen examination, unlike several other MYP subject
groups. Presented with a design situation, students identify a
challenge or problem, research, develop and create a product or
solution, and evaluate its success. The submitted ePortfolio takes the
form of a complete design folder containing a design brief and
specification, and is formally assessed as **product design, digital
design, or combined digital and product design**.

ePortfolios are marked by students' own classroom teachers against the
published criteria for MYP year 5, and in each examination session the
IB moderates a sample of ePortfolios from every school, adjusting
grades as necessary to ensure rigorous, internationally consistent
standards -- so classroom marking is not the final word on a student's
result. This moderation process is what makes an MYP-validated design
result comparable across different schools and countries, despite the
subject's inherently open-ended, project-based nature.

## How to approach it

Because the ePortfolio is assessed as a complete design folder rather
than a single final product, documenting the process -- not just
presenting a finished prototype -- is where most of the marks actually
sit: a polished product with thin process documentation across
criteria A and B will typically score lower than a rougher prototype
backed by a genuinely thorough inquiry, specification and evaluation.
Because criterion D specifically requires objective testing and an
honest account of a solution's limitations, resist the instinct to only
showcase what worked -- design work that transparently identifies real
weaknesses and explains how the solution could be improved is exactly
what this criterion rewards, not a claim that the solution is already
perfect. MYP Design eAssessment tasks are explicitly aligned with the
skills that prepare students for the DP Design Technology course, so
building genuine fluency with the design cycle here pays forward
directly if a student continues design at Diploma level.

Design is one of the subjects the MYP certificate accepts as
satisfying its arts/practical requirement -- the certificate requires a
satisfactory level of achievement in at least one course from physical
and health education, arts or design, so for students taking design as
that elective, a genuinely well-documented ePortfolio carries weight
beyond the subject itself.
