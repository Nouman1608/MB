## Questions

**1.** Define the term payback period. **[2]**

**2.** Wilderby Gym buys fitness equipment for 135,000. It expects extra net returns of 30,000 each year. Work out the payback period, giving your answer in years and months. **[2]**

**3.** Windrow Greenhouses plans to build a new glasshouse costing 240,000. Forecast net returns over five years are 50,000, 70,000, 80,000, 90,000 and 60,000.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period, in years to two decimal places. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the average rate of return (ARR), to one decimal place. **[3]**

**4.** Cobalt Quay Couriers is considering a van costing 48,000. It expects net returns of 14,000, 16,000, 16,000 and 12,000 over four years. The owners want an ARR of at least 6%. Calculate the ARR and state whether the van meets the target. **[4]**

**5.** Explain one limitation of using payback period to appraise an investment. **[3]**

**6.** (HL only) Explain why NPV uses a discount rate. **[3]**

**7.** (HL only) Lumen Arc Studios plans to buy lighting equipment costing 90,000. It forecasts net returns of 30,000, 35,000 and 40,000 in years 1 to 3. Use the 10% factors of 0.9091 (year 1), 0.8264 (year 2) and 0.7513 (year 3) to find the net present value (NPV). **[4]**

**8.** Bluefin Kayak Hire can fund one of two options, each costing 60,000 and lasting four years.

| Year | Option P returns | Option Q returns |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20,000 | 10,000 |
| 2 | 25,000 | 15,000 |
| 3 | 25,000 | 30,000 |
| 4 | 10,000 | 45,000 |

**(a)** Find the payback period of each option, in years to one decimal place. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the ARR of each option, to one decimal place. **[3]**
**(c)** Evaluate the two options and recommend one. **[5]**

**9.** (HL only) Caldervale Foods is appraising a 55,000 project with net returns of 15,000, 20,000, 25,000 and 15,000 in years 1 to 4. Discount factors at 4% are 0.9615, 0.9246, 0.8890 and 0.8548. At 20% they are 0.8333, 0.6944, 0.5787 and 0.4823.

**(a)** Calculate the NPV at 4%. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the NPV at 20%. **[3]**
**(c)** Comment on what the two results suggest about the project. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse two non-financial factors that a business might consider when deciding whether to invest in new equipment. **[4]**

**11.** Garnet Mill is considering rooftop solar panels costing 180,000. They are expected to save 36,000 a year for eight years. The mill's policy is to accept projects with a payback of four years or less. Its bank charges 6% a year on borrowing.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the ARR. **[3]**
**(c)** Justify whether Garnet Mill should invest. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** The length of time [1] it takes for the returns (cash inflows) from an investment to repay its capital cost [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong definitions refer to both the returns and the capital cost; "how long a project takes" on its own is vague.

**2.** 135,000 ÷ 30,000 = 4.5 [1] = **4 years 6 months** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* When the question asks for years and months, converting the decimal (0.5 × 12) completes the task; leaving 4.5 is incomplete.

**3. (a)** Cumulative returns: 50,000; 120,000; 200,000 [1]. Still 40,000 needed in year 4, and 40,000 ÷ 90,000 = 0.444 [1]. Payback = **3.44 years** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Total returns = 350,000 and net gain = 110,000; 110,000 ÷ 5 = 22,000 [1]. 22,000 ÷ 240,000 [1] × 100 = **9.2%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the cumulative line and each ARR stage; an error is far simpler to locate when the working can be seen.

**4.** Total returns = 58,000 [1]. Net gain 10,000, so 2,500 a year [1]. 2,500 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = **5.2%** [1]. This is below 6%, so the van does not meet the target [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A calculation followed by a comparison with the stated target is what "state whether" asks for; the number alone is not the answer.

**5.** Payback ignores returns after the cost has been recovered [1]. A project with modest early returns but a long profitable life may look weak [1]. The business could reject a project that would add more to profits than the one it chooses [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" answers usually need the limitation, a reason and a consequence; naming the weakness alone is only the first step.

**6.** A dollar due next year is worth less than a dollar held now [1], because it could be used or invested now and future receipts are uncertain [1]. Discounting converts each return to a present value so the total can be compared with the original cost [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the discount rate to the time value of money and to the comparison with today's cost shows understanding; "it adjusts for inflation" alone is incomplete.

**7.** Present values: 30,000 × 0.9091 = 27,273 [1]; 35,000 × 0.8264 = 28,924; 40,000 × 0.7513 = 30,052 [1]. Total = 86,249 [1]. NPV = 86,249 − 90,000 = **−3,751** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two easy steps to skip are deducting the original cost and keeping the minus sign on a negative NPV; the sign changes the decision.

**8. (a)** Option P: cumulative 20,000; 45,000; 70,000, so 15,000 ÷ 25,000 = 0.6 in year 3, giving **2.6 years** [1]. Option Q: cumulative 10,000; 25,000; 55,000; 100,000, so 5,000 ÷ 45,000 = 0.11 in year 4, giving **3.1 years** [1]. Cumulative working shown [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** P: total returns 80,000, net gain 20,000, 5,000 a year, 5,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **8.3%** [1]. Q: total returns 100,000, net gain 40,000, 10,000 a year, 10,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **16.7%** [1]. Method shown [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** P pays back faster (2.6 against 3.1 years), so cash returns sooner and risk is lower [1]. Q's ARR is double P's (16.7% against 8.3%) because it earns more in total [1]. Q's biggest returns come in year 4, so they depend on forecasts furthest ahead [1]. Neither method accounts for when returns arrive, so more information on the cost of finance would help [1]. Judgement: Q is better if the forecasts are reliable and the business can wait; otherwise P [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An "evaluate" answer is stronger when both options are weighed with figures and the recommendation states what it depends on.

**9. (a)** Present values: 14,422.5; 18,492; 22,225; 12,822 [1]. Total = 67,961.5 [1]. NPV = **12,961.5** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Present values: 12,499.5; 13,888; 14,467.5; 7,234.5 [1]. Total = 48,089.5 [1]. NPV = **−6,910.5** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** The NPV is positive at 4% but negative at 20% [1], so the decision depends on the discount rate; the business needs a rate that reflects its real cost of finance [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A comment should say what changes the decision, not only that the figures differ.

**10.** Any two developed factors, for example: employee impact, such as new skills or job losses [1], which may affect morale and productivity [1]; environmental impact, such as lower or higher emissions [1], which may affect reputation and how customers see the business [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs a consequence for the business; a list of factors without development gains little.

**11. (a)** 180,000 ÷ 36,000 [1] = **5 years** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Total returns = 288,000, net gain = 108,000 [1]. 108,000 ÷ 8 = 13,500 [1]. 13,500 ÷ 180,000 × 100 = **7.5%** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Payback of 5 years is longer than the 4-year policy, so the project fails the policy [1]. ARR of 7.5% exceeds the 6% borrowing rate [1], but ARR ignores when savings arrive, so the comparison is only a rough guide [1]. Savings rely on energy prices and panel performance staying as forecast for eight years [1]. Savings after year 5 are never counted by payback [1]. Non-financial benefits, such as a lower environmental impact and a better image with stakeholders [1]. Judgement: a case to invest can be made if the policy is flexible, since the returns exceed borrowing costs and last beyond payback, but the mill should test the forecast first [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A justified decision refers to the case figures and to both sides, then commits to a position and states its conditions.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving payback as a decimal when years and months are asked for.
- Forgetting the part-year step in uneven payback, or using the wrong year's return.
- Dividing ARR by total returns instead of capital cost, or omitting the division by years.
- Giving total present value instead of NPV, so the original cost is not deducted.
- Using the wrong year's discount factor.
- Writing a limitation without a consequence, or one that does not apply to that method.
- Giving a recommendation with no counter-point, or one that ignores the figures in the case.
- Missing units, such as years, percentage or dollars.
