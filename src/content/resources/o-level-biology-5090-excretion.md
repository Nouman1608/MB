## 13.1.1 What excretion means

**Excretion** is the removal of toxic materials and the waste products of metabolism from organisms.

**Metabolism** is all the chemical reactions in a cell. Respiration and deamination are both metabolic reactions, and both make waste. The three excretory products in this topic are:

| Excretory product | Made by | Leaves the body through |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | Respiration, in every living cell | Lungs |
| Urea | Deamination, in the liver | Kidneys, in urine |
| Excess salts (ions) and excess water | Taken in with food and drink, beyond what the body needs | Kidneys, in urine |

### Excretion is not egestion

**Egestion** is the removal of undigested food as faeces, through the anus. Most of the material in faeces, such as fibre, was never absorbed into the blood and never took part in reactions inside cells. So removing it is not excretion. If a question asks which process removes urea, the answer is excretion; if it asks which process removes fibre, the answer is egestion.

## 13.1.2 Carbon dioxide and the lungs

Every living cell carries out respiration, which releases energy and makes carbon dioxide as a waste product. The route out is:

`body cell → diffuses into blood plasma → carried to the lungs → diffuses into the alveoli → breathed out`

The lungs are therefore an excretory organ as well as the gas exchange organ. The diffusion across the alveolar wall is taught in [Human gas exchange](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-human-gas-exchange/). Carbon dioxide is one of the substances the plasma transports, along with urea.

## 13.2.5 The liver and amino acids: assimilation

Proteins in food are digested to **amino acids**, which are absorbed in the small intestine. The **hepatic portal vein** carries most absorbed molecules to the liver.

**Assimilation** is the uptake and use by cells of nutrients from the blood. Liver cells assimilate amino acids by **converting them to proteins**. They build the amino acids into new protein molecules, such as plasma proteins, which are released into the blood.

## 13.1.3 and 13.2.6 Deamination and urea

The body takes in more amino acids than it needs for making proteins, and it cannot store the excess. The liver breaks the excess down.

**Deamination** is the removal of the nitrogen-containing part of amino acids, in the liver, resulting in the formation of **urea**.

```
excess amino acid (in liver cell)
      │  deamination
      ├──► nitrogen-containing part ──► converted to UREA ──► into the blood
      └──► rest of the molecule ──► can be used, for example in respiration
```

Points to remember:

- **Only the liver** deaminates amino acids and makes urea. The kidneys do not make urea; they remove it.
- Urea is a **toxic** waste product of metabolism, so its removal counts as excretion.
- Urea dissolves in water, so it is carried in the **blood plasma** from the liver to the kidneys. Water acting as a solvent is part of [Biological molecules](/resources/biological-molecules/).

**Worked example 1: following one nitrogen atom.** A nitrogen atom starts in a protein in a piece of fish. Describe its route until it leaves the body.

1. Protease enzymes digest the protein to amino acids in the stomach and small intestine.
2. The amino acids are absorbed into the blood in the small intestine.
3. The **hepatic portal vein** carries them to the **liver**.
4. If the amino acid is not needed, the liver **deaminates** it: the nitrogen-containing part is removed and turned into **urea**.
5. Urea enters the blood and leaves the liver in the **hepatic vein**, then travels via the vena cava, the heart, the lungs and the heart again to the **aorta** and the **renal artery**.
6. In the kidney, urea is **filtered** out of the blood at the glomerulus and stays in the tubule.
7. It leaves in **urine**, down the ureter to the bladder, and out through the urethra.

## 13.2.2 Why urea must be excreted

The syllabus limits this outcome to one reason: **urea is toxic**. If urea were not removed, it would be made faster than it was lost, so its concentration in the blood and tissue fluid would rise until it damaged body cells. Excretion keeps the concentration low.

**Worked example 2: interpreting data.** A person's kidneys stop working. Their blood urea concentration is 0.25 g/dm³ on day 0 and 1.5 g/dm³ on day 5. Calculate the percentage increase and explain the change.

- Increase = 1.5 − 0.25 = 1.25 g/dm³.
- Percentage increase = 1.25 ÷ 0.25 × 100 = **500%**.
- Explanation: the liver keeps deaminating excess amino acids and making urea, but the kidneys no longer remove it from the blood, so urea accumulates. Because urea is toxic, a high concentration is harmful to cells.

## 13.2.1 The urinary system

### Identifying the organs

A front view of the human urinary system looks like this:

```
   [ kidney ]          [ kidney ]      ← at the back of the abdomen, one each side
        \                  /
      ureter            ureter         ← two narrow tubes running down
          \              /
           [  bladder  ]               ← muscular bag low in the abdomen
                 |
              urethra                  ← single tube to the outside
```

On a diagram: there are **two** kidneys and **two** ureters, but only **one** bladder and **one** urethra. The ureters join the bladder; the urethra leaves it at the bottom.

### Functions

| Organ | Function |
|---|---|
| Kidney | Removes urea and excess salts and water from the blood, as urine |
| Ureter | Carries urine from the kidney to the bladder |
| Bladder | Stores urine |
| Urethra | Carries urine from the bladder out of the body |

Use the syllabus wording for the kidney: "removes urea and excess salts and water from the blood as urine". Blood reaches each kidney in the **renal artery** and leaves in the **renal vein**. Blood in the renal vein contains less urea than blood in the renal artery.

## 13.2.3 The structure of a nephron

Each kidney contains a very large number of tiny tubes called **nephrons**. Each nephron makes urine. The parts, in the order fluid passes through them:

```
 arteriole in ──►(glomerulus)──► arteriole out ──► capillaries around tubules ──► renal vein
                  inside
        ┌─[ Bowman's capsule ]
        │
        └─► first coiled tubule ─► loop of Henle ─► second coiled tubule ─► collecting duct ─► ureter
```

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Glomerulus** | A knot of blood capillaries, supplied by a branch of the renal artery |
| **Bowman's capsule** | The cup-shaped start of the nephron, wrapped around the glomerulus |
| **Tubules** | The coiled tubes that carry fluid away from the capsule and on from the loop |
| **Loop of Henle** | A U-shaped section of tubule between the two coiled parts |
| **Collecting duct** | A tube that receives fluid from many nephrons and carries urine on towards the ureter |

**Associated blood vessels.** Blood enters the glomerulus in a small arteriole (a branch of the renal artery) and leaves in another arteriole. That vessel then branches into **capillaries that wrap around the tubules**, which is where reabsorbed substances re-enter the blood. These capillaries drain into the **renal vein**.

## 13.2.4 How a nephron makes urine

The syllabus wants an outline only: the details of these processes are not required.

### (a) Filtration at the glomerulus

**Water, glucose, urea and ions** are filtered out of the blood in the glomerulus into the Bowman's capsule. The fluid in the capsule is the **filtrate**. Blood cells and large protein molecules stay in the blood.

### (b) Reabsorption along the nephron

As the filtrate flows along the tubules, useful substances are taken back into the blood in the surrounding capillaries:

- **all of the glucose**
- **some of the ions**
- **most of the water**

### (c) Urine

What is left flows down the collecting duct as **urine**, containing **urea, excess water and excess ions**. Healthy urine contains no glucose, because all of it was reabsorbed.

**Worked example 3: comparing fluids.** Concentrations (g/dm³) given for one person:

| Substance | Blood plasma | Filtrate | Urine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glucose | 1.0 | 1.0 | 0 |
| Urea | 0.30 | 0.30 | 18 |
| Protein | 70 | 0 | 0 |

(a) Explain why glucose is present in the filtrate but not in the urine.
- Glucose molecules are small, so glucose is filtered out at the glomerulus. All of it is then reabsorbed into the blood, so none is left in urine.

(b) Explain why there is no protein in the filtrate.
- Protein molecules are too large to be filtered at the glomerulus, so they stay in the blood.

(c) Calculate how many times more concentrated urea is in urine than in plasma, and explain why.
- 18 ÷ 0.30 = **60 times**.
- Urea is filtered but not reabsorbed, while most of the water is reabsorbed. The same mass of urea ends up in a much smaller volume of water.

**Worked example 4: how much water is reabsorbed?** Use these given figures: the kidneys produce filtrate at 120 cm³ per minute and urine at 1.2 cm³ per minute.

- Water reabsorbed each minute ≈ 120 − 1.2 = **118.8 cm³** (treating filtrate and urine volumes as water).
- Percentage reabsorbed = 118.8 ÷ 120 × 100 = **99.0%**.
- Filtrate per day = 120 × 60 × 24 = 172 800 cm³ = **172.8 dm³**; urine per day = **1.728 dm³**.

This is why the syllabus says **most** of the water is reabsorbed.

## Putting the topic together

| Organ | Role in excretion |
|---|---|
| All body cells | Make carbon dioxide in respiration |
| Liver | Assimilates amino acids into proteins; deaminates excess amino acids; makes urea |
| Blood plasma | Carries carbon dioxide to the lungs and urea to the kidneys |
| Lungs | Excrete carbon dioxide |
| Kidneys | Filter blood and excrete urea, excess salts and excess water as urine |

## Common errors

- Saying the kidneys make urea. The **liver** makes it; the kidneys remove it.
- Calling faeces an excretory product of digestion. Removing undigested food is **egestion**.
- Mixing up **ureter** (kidney to bladder, two of them) and **urethra** (bladder to outside, one).
- Writing that glucose is "not filtered". It **is** filtered; it is then **all** reabsorbed.
- Writing that "all the water" or "all the ions" are reabsorbed. The syllabus says **most** of the water and **some** of the ions.
- Describing deamination as "removing amino acids". It removes the **nitrogen-containing part** of each amino acid.
- Confusing **urea** (the chemical) with **urine** (the solution of urea, ions and water).
- Leaving out the reason for excreting urea: the 13.2.2 answer is that **urea is toxic**.
