This guide covers **3.2.3 Unseen prose**, assessed in Section C of Paper 2a in OxfordAQA
International GCSE English Literature (9275). It applies to **Route A only**. Route B candidates do
not sit an unseen prose question; they complete a non-exam assessment on a whole prose text instead,
covered in the
[Route B non-exam assessment study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-9275-non-exam-assessment-route-b/).
For the whole of Paper 2a, see the
[Poetry and Unseen Texts study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-english-literature-poetry-and-unseen-texts/).

## What 3.2.3 covers

The specification asks you to prepare by reading a wide range of prose, so that you can closely
analyse the variety of ways writers create meaning and influence the reader. In the exam you are
given an extract you have not seen before, printed on the paper, and answer one question on it.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Where | Paper 2a, Section C (the last section) |
| Marks | 30, one third of the 90-mark paper |
| Paper | 2 hours 15 minutes, open book, 60% of the qualification |
| Time to spend | About 45 minutes |
| Route | Route A only |

Because it comes last, unseen prose is where poor timing on the earlier sections shows. Protect your
45 minutes.

The three assessment objectives apply:

- **AO1**: understanding of the extract's ideas, shown through a clear interpretation that answers
  the question;
- **AO2**: analysis of how the writer creates meanings and effects through language, structure and
  narrative method;
- **AO3**: an informed personal response, with appropriate terminology in organised, accurate
  writing.

## How unseen prose differs from unseen poetry

The core skill is the same, close analysis of an unfamiliar text, but prose has its own methods:

- **Narrative voice and viewpoint** are central. Who tells the story, and how close are we to a
  character's thoughts?
- **Structure** works at the level of sentences and paragraphs: how the extract opens, where it
  shifts, how it ends, and how sentence length controls pace.
- **Dialogue** reveals character and relationships.
- **Description** of setting and people builds atmosphere and implies feeling.
- The extract is **part of a larger work**, but you only analyse what is printed. Do not guess what
  happens before or after.

## Key ideas: the prose toolkit

| Method | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Narrative perspective | First or third person? Whose thoughts do we see? What is withheld? |
| Free indirect style | Does the narration slip into a character's own way of thinking? |
| Sentence structure | Where are sentences short, long, fragmented? Why there? |
| Paragraphing | Where does a new paragraph shift time, place or mood? |
| Opening and ending | How does the extract begin and where does it leave the reader? |
| Imagery | Which similes, metaphors or symbols shape the reader's view? |
| Dialogue | What do characters say, avoid saying, or say indirectly? |
| Setting and atmosphere | How does place reflect or contrast with feeling? |
| Tone | Is the writing ironic, tender, tense, detached? |

## A method for the 45 minutes

1. **Read the question first** (1 minute). Underline the focus: a character, a relationship, an
   atmosphere, a moment of change.
2. **Read the extract for sense** (4 minutes). Who, where, what happens, and how does it end?
3. **Read again for method** (5 minutes). Annotate only details that relate to the question. Mark
   where the extract shifts.
4. **Plan** (5 minutes). A one-sentence interpretation and three or four points that follow the
   extract's structure from opening to ending.
5. **Write** (about 30 minutes). Introduction, developed paragraphs, and a conclusion that comments
   on how the extract ends.

## Worked example

The extract below is original, written for this guide.

> The letter had been on the hall table for three days. Anil walked past it in the mornings with his
> school bag over one shoulder, and in the evenings with his school bag over the other, and each
> time it was still there, face down, as if it were sulking.
>
> His mother had not opened it either. She dusted around it. Once, he saw her pick it up, weigh it
> in her palm the way she weighed onions at the market, and put it back exactly where it had been,
> lining its edge up with the edge of the table.
>
> On the fourth evening the letter was gone. Nobody mentioned it. At dinner his mother served him
> twice as much rice as usual and asked him about his day, and listened to every word of his answer.

**Question:** How does the writer create a sense of unease in this extract?

**Interpretation:** The writer builds unease through what the characters avoid, so the letter
becomes more threatening the less it is discussed.

**Point 1: the letter as a presence.** The opening sentence fixes a precise time, "three days",
suggesting the letter has been noticed and counted. Personification, "as if it were sulking", gives
it a mood, so it seems to resent being ignored. The repetitive, balanced sentence about the school
bag ("over one shoulder... over the other") shows days passing in routine while the letter stays the
same.

**Point 2: avoidance through action.** The mother's behaviour is reported without her thoughts, so
the reader must infer her fear. She "dusted around it", a small detail that turns cleaning into
avoidance. The simile comparing the letter to onions weighed at market is domestic and ordinary, but
it shows her measuring its importance. Lining up its edge "exactly" suggests a need for control.

**Point 3: structure and silence.** The final paragraph opens with a short sentence, "On the fourth
evening the letter was gone", which breaks the pattern of three days. "Nobody mentioned it" is even
shorter, and its flatness makes the silence feel heavy.

**Point 4: the ending.** The mother serving "twice as much rice" and listening "to every word" seems
warm, but after the build-up it reads as over-compensation. The writer never reveals what the letter
said, so the unease is left unresolved, and the reader shares Anil's uncertainty.

Notice that the answer does not guess what the letter contains. It analyses how the writer creates
the effect from what is printed.

## Common mistakes

- **Speculating about the plot.** "The letter is probably about debt" earns nothing; analyse how the
  uncertainty is created.
- **Paraphrase.** Retelling the extract paragraph by paragraph is description.
- **Ignoring narrative voice.** Many answers discuss language only and never ask who is telling the
  story.
- **Sentence-length clichés.** "Short sentences create tension" is only useful when you explain why
  that particular sentence, at that moment, has that effect.
- **Running out of time.** As the last section, unseen prose suffers most from overruns earlier in
  the paper.
- **Missing the ending.** The last lines often change the meaning; always analyse them.

## Exam technique and command words

- **How does the writer create...?** or **How does the writer present...?** Focus every paragraph on
  methods and their effects on the reader.
- **Explore** invites alternative readings; for example, the mother's kindness could be read as love,
  guilt or both.

Use short quotations and refer to the reader's experience: "the reader is left...", "this makes the
reader question...". Include at least one point about structure and one about narrative perspective.

## Preparing without a set text

- Read the opening pages of a range of novels and short stories, from different periods and places.
- After each, write two sentences: what the writer makes the reader feel, and one method that
  creates it.
- Once a fortnight, write a 45-minute answer under timed conditions, and practise it straight after a
  poetry answer to build stamina for Paper 2a.
