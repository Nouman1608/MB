## Core definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Oxidation (C3.3a) | Gain of oxygen |
| Reduction (C3.3a) | Loss of oxygen |
| Oxidation (C3.3b, Higher Tier only) | Loss of electrons |
| Reduction (C3.3b, Higher Tier only) | Gain of electrons |
| Redox | A reaction in which oxidation and reduction both happen |
| Oxidising agent | Oxidises something else; is itself reduced |
| Reducing agent | Reduces something else; is itself oxidised |
| Acid | Forms H⁺ ions when it dissolves in water |
| Alkali | A soluble base; its solution contains OH⁻ ions |
| Base | Neutralises an acid, e.g. a metal oxide or hydroxide |
| Neutralisation | Acid + alkali or base → salt + water |
| pH | Measures relative acidity and alkalinity; 7 is neutral |

## C3.3a: redox in terms of oxygen

**Method in steps**

1. Find the substance that **loses** oxygen: it is **reduced** and is the **oxidising agent**.
2. Find the substance that **gains** oxygen: it is **oxidised** and is the **reducing agent**.
3. Name whole substances (nickel(II) oxide, not nickel).

**Worked reminder.** NiO + H₂ → Ni + H₂O. Nickel(II) oxide loses oxygen: reduced, oxidising agent. Hydrogen gains oxygen to become water: oxidised, reducing agent.

## C3.3b: redox in terms of electrons (Higher Tier only)

**OIL RIG**, for electrons: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

**Method in steps**

1. Split the ionic equation into two half equations.
2. Electrons appear on the **right** of the oxidation half equation (lost).
3. Electrons appear on the **left** of the reduction half equation (gained).
4. Electrons lost must equal electrons gained.

**Worked reminder.** Mg + Zn²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Zn

- Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻: magnesium is oxidised (reducing agent).
- Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn: zinc ions are reduced (oxidising agent).

Metal + acid is also redox: Fe + 2H⁺ → Fe²⁺ + H₂. Iron atoms lose electrons; hydrogen ions gain them.

## C3.3c: ions in acids and alkalis

- Acid in water → H⁺ ions: HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻; H₂SO₄ → 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻
- Alkali solution → OH⁻ ions: LiOH → Li⁺ + OH⁻; Ca(OH)₂ → Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻
- The H⁺ ions are **free** in solution, not still attached to the acid molecule (a misconception the specification names).
- Alkalis can be just as corrosive as acids (another named misconception).

## C3.3d and C3.3e: neutralisation

| Reaction type | General equation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Acid + alkali | salt + water | LiOH + HCl → LiCl + H₂O |
| Acid + base (metal oxide) | salt + water | CuO + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O |
| Acid + base (insoluble metal hydroxide) | salt + water | Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O |

**Ionic equation for every aqueous acid–alkali neutralisation:** H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). Spectator ions (the metal ion and the acid's negative ion) do not change, so they are omitted.

**Salt names:** hydrochloric acid → chloride; nitric acid → nitrate; sulfuric acid → sulfate.

## C3.3f: carbonates and metals with acids

- acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
- acid + (some) metal → salt + hydrogen

**Method in steps for a balanced equation**

1. Name the products from the patterns above.
2. Write correct formulae (charges: Ca²⁺, Al³⁺, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻).
3. Balance metal atoms, then the acid's ion group, then H and O.
4. Count every atom on both sides.

**Worked reminders**

- CaCO₃ + 2HNO₃ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + H₂O + CO₂
- 2Al + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂

Copper, silver and gold do not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, which is why the specification says "some metals".

## PAG C7: making a pure, dry salt from an insoluble base

1. Warm the dilute acid.
2. Add the solid base or carbonate **in excess**, stirring, until no more reacts.
3. **Filter**: the unreacted solid stays in the paper.
4. Heat the filtrate to boil away part of the water, then let it **crystallise** as it cools.
5. Filter off the crystals and **dry** them on filter paper or in a warm oven.

Why excess solid? It guarantees no acid remains, and unreacted solid is simple to remove by filtering.

## C3.3g: strength and concentration (Higher Tier only)

| Pair of words | Describes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Concentrated / dilute | Amount of acid in a given volume (ratio of amount to volume) | 2 mol/dm³ is more concentrated than 0.1 mol/dm³ |
| Strong / weak | Degree of ionisation | Strong: hydrochloric, nitric, sulfuric (fully ionised). Weak: ethanoic, citric, carbonic (partly ionised) |

**Must-know distinction:** "weak" is not "dilute". A concentrated weak acid and a dilute strong acid both exist.

**Concentration as a ratio (CM3.3i).** Divide amount (mol) by volume (dm³). Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000.

## C3.3h, C3.3i and C3.3j: pH

- **C3.3h (both tiers):** pH measures relative acidity and alkalinity. Below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 alkaline.
- **C3.3i (Higher Tier only):** more H⁺ → lower pH. A neutral solution has equal H⁺ and OH⁻. Whole-number pH only.
- **C3.3j (Higher Tier only):** H⁺ concentration ×10 → pH falls by 1.

| pH change | Change in H⁺ concentration |
|---|---|
| Down 1 | 10 times more |
| Down 2 | 100 times more |
| Down 3 | 1000 times more |
| Up 1 | 10 times less |

**Worked reminder (Higher Tier only).** pH 6 → pH 3 is a fall of 3, so the H⁺ concentration is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 times greater.

**Titration curve (Higher Tier only).** Alkali added to acid: pH starts low, rises slowly, then jumps steeply through 7 near exact neutralisation, then levels off high. Acid added to alkali gives the mirror image: starts high, falls slowly, drops steeply, then levels off low.

## C3.3k and PAG C6: measuring pH

| Method | How | Strengths and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Universal indicator | Add drops or use paper; compare with colour chart | Quick and cheap; approximate whole-number pH; colour judgement is subjective; hard to use in coloured solutions |
| pH meter / probe | Calibrate in buffer solutions; rinse with distilled water between samples; read display | More precise; works in coloured solutions; can log pH continuously during a titration |

Universal indicator colours run roughly red (strongly acidic), orange, yellow, green (neutral), blue, purple (strongly alkaline). Always use the chart supplied with your indicator.

## Quick self-test

1. In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, name the substance oxidised and the oxidising agent.
2. Define reduction in terms of oxygen.
3. (Higher Tier only) Write the half equation for a calcium atom forming a calcium ion, and say whether it is oxidation or reduction.
4. (Higher Tier only) In Br₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻, is bromine oxidised or reduced?
5. Which ion do all acids form in water?
6. Write the ionic equation for neutralisation, with state symbols.
7. Name the salt formed from potassium hydroxide and sulfuric acid, and write the balanced equation.
8. Give all three products of lithium carbonate reacting with sulfuric acid.
9. (Higher Tier only) Solution C has 0.30 mol of acid in 250 cm³. Solution D has 0.090 mol in 50 cm³. Which is more concentrated?
10. (Higher Tier only) By what factor does the H⁺ concentration change from pH 2 to pH 3?
11. (Higher Tier only) Explain why ethanoic acid can be concentrated and weak at the same time.
12. Give one advantage of a pH probe over universal indicator paper.

### Answers

1. **Hydrogen** is oxidised (gains oxygen). The oxidising agent is **copper(II) oxide**.
2. Reduction is the **loss of oxygen**.
3. **Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻**; electrons are lost, so this is **oxidation**.
4. **Reduced**: bromine gains electrons.
5. **Hydrogen ions, H⁺**.
6. **H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)**
7. **Potassium sulfate**: 2KOH + H₂SO₄ → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
8. **Lithium sulfate, water and carbon dioxide**: Li₂CO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Li₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂
9. C: 0.30 ÷ 0.250 = 1.2 mol/dm³. D: 0.090 ÷ 0.050 = 1.8 mol/dm³. **D is more concentrated.**
10. pH rises by 1, so the H⁺ concentration is **10 times smaller**.
11. **Concentrated** means a large amount of acid per dm³; **weak** means only partly ionised. The two describe different things.
12. Any one: gives a **more precise** reading; not affected by the **colour** of the solution; can **record continuously**; no subjective colour matching.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the oxidising agent "oxidised"; it gets reduced.
- Giving the metal, not its oxide, when asked what is reduced.
- (Higher Tier only) Writing the electrons for an oxidation on the left-hand side.
- Leaving water out of acid + carbonate products, or forgetting carbon dioxide.
- Writing Al₂SO₄₃ or AlSO₄ instead of Al₂(SO₄)₃: brackets around a group that appears more than once.
- Writing H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O without state symbols when they are asked for.
- (Higher Tier only) Using "weak" when the question means "dilute", or the reverse.
- (Higher Tier only) Saying a pH drop of 2 means twice as much H⁺, instead of 100 times.
- Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ before dividing.
- In PAG C7, adding excess **acid** rather than excess solid, so the salt is contaminated.
