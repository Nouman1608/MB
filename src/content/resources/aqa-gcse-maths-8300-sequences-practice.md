These questions cover section **3.2.4 Sequences** (A23, A24 and A25) of the AQA GCSE Mathematics (8300) specification, for teaching from September 2015 with exams from May/June 2017 (version 1.0). Questions and parts on content from the specification's "Higher content only" column are labelled **(Higher)**; everything else is for both tiers. Each question is labelled (non-calculator), in the style of Paper 1, or (calculator), in the style of Papers 2 and 3.

Learn the content first in the [Sequences study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-sequences/) and the [Sequences revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-sequences-revision-notes/). These questions are different from the sequences items in the topic-level [Algebra practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-mathematics-algebra-practice/), so you can do both. ## Questions

**1.** (non-calculator) The nth term of a sequence is 5n − 8.

**(a)** Write down the first three terms of the sequence. **[1]**
**(b)** Work out the 40th term. **[1]**

**2.** (non-calculator) The first term of a sequence is 4. The term-to-term rule is "multiply by 3, then subtract 5". Work out the 2nd, 3rd and 4th terms. **[2]**

**3.** (non-calculator) Matchsticks are used to make a row of hexagons. Next to each other, the hexagons share a side. Pattern 1 uses 6 matchsticks, pattern 2 uses 11 and pattern 3 uses 16.

**(a)** Work out how many matchsticks are in pattern 10. **[1]**
**(b)** Find an expression for the number of matchsticks in pattern n. **[2]**
**(c)** Can a pattern use exactly 100 matchsticks? Show how you decide. **[2]**

**4.** (non-calculator) Here is a list of numbers: 3, 8, 9, 21, 25, 27, 36, 64

**(a)** Write down all the triangular numbers in the list. **[1]**
**(b)** Write down the number in the list that is both a square number and a cube number. **[1]**
**(c)** Write down the next triangular number after 36. **[1]**

**5.** (non-calculator) In a Fibonacci-type sequence, each term is the sum of the two terms before it. The 3rd term is 13 and the 6th term is 59. Work out the first two terms. **[4]**

**6.** (calculator) Here are the first four terms of a geometric progression: 2500, 1000, 400, 160, ...

**(a)** Work out the common ratio. **[1]**
**(b)** Work out the next two terms. **[1]**
**(c)** Which term is the first one less than 1? **[2]**

**7.** (non-calculator) Here are the first four terms of a linear sequence: 47, 41, 35, 29, ...

**(a)** Find an expression for the nth term. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the first term of the sequence that is less than −40. **[2]**

**8.** (non-calculator) Sequence A has nth term 4n + 9. Sequence B has nth term 7n − 12. For one value of n, the nth terms of the two sequences are equal. Work out this value of n and the term. **[3]**

**9.** (non-calculator) Here are the first five terms of a quadratic sequence: 3, 4, 7, 12, 19, ...

**(a)** Work out the next two terms. **[2]**
**(b)** (Higher) Find an expression for the nth term. **[3]**

**10.** (non-calculator, Higher) A geometric progression begins 3, 3√2, 6, ...

**(a)** Write down the common ratio. **[1]**
**(b)** Work out the 8th term. Give your answer in the form k√2. **[2]**
**(c)** Which term is equal to 96? **[2]**

**11.** (calculator) Each pattern is a square of white tiles with a border of grey tiles around it. Pattern 1 has 1 white tile and 8 grey tiles. Pattern 2 has 4 white tiles and 12 grey tiles. Pattern 3 has 9 white tiles and 16 grey tiles. The pattern continues.

**(a)** Find an expression for the number of grey tiles in pattern n. **[2]**
**(b)** Write down the number of white tiles in pattern 10. **[1]**
**(c)** Show that the total number of tiles in pattern n is (n + 2)². **[2]**
**(d)** One pattern has 140 grey tiles. Work out the number of white tiles in this pattern. **[2]**
**(e)** (Higher) Show that no pattern has the same number of white tiles as grey tiles. **[2]**

**12.** (non-calculator) A sequence has first term 2. Each term after that is found using the rule

next term = 1 ÷ (1 − previous term)

**(a)** Work out the 2nd, 3rd and 4th terms. **[2]**
**(b)** (Higher) Work out the 50th term. Explain how you know. **[2]**
**(c)** (Higher) Work out the sum of the first 50 terms. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 5 − 8, 10 − 8, 15 − 8 give **−3, 2, 7** [1]
**(b)** 5 × 40 − 8 = **192** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The mark in (a) needs all three terms correct, so a sign slip on the negative first term loses it; write each substitution so the slip is easy to spot.

**2.** 3 × 4 − 5 = 7 and 3 × 7 − 5 = 16 [1]; 3 × 16 − 5 = 43, so **7, 16, 43** [1]
*Examiner insight:* An error in the 2nd term is usually followed through for the later terms if the rule is applied correctly, so keep going rather than starting again.

**3. (a)** The sequence goes up by 5: 6, 11, 16, 21, ... Pattern 10 = 6 + 9 × 5 = **51** [1]
**(b)** Common difference 5, so start with 5n: 5, 10, 15 [1]; each term is 1 more, so **5n + 1** [1]
**(c)** 5n + 1 = 100 gives 5n = 99, n = 19.8 [1]; n is not a whole number, so **no** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), an answer of "n + 5" scores nothing, while "5n + k" with the wrong k often earns the first mark; in (c) the final mark needs the reason, not just "no".

**4. (a)** **3, 21, 36** [1]
**(b)** **64** (8² = 64 and 4³ = 64) [1]
**(c)** 36 + 9 = **45** [1]
*Examiner insight:* For a "write down all" part, including a wrong number (such as 9 or 25 in part (a)) usually loses the mark even if every correct number is there.

**5.** Let the first two terms be a and b. Then the terms are a, b, a + b, a + 2b, 2a + 3b, 3a + 5b [1]
a + b = 13 and 3a + 5b = 59 [1]
3a + 3b = 39, so subtracting gives 2b = 20, b = 10 [1]
a = 13 − 10 = 3, so the first two terms are **3 and 10** [1] (check: 3, 10, 13, 23, 36, 59)
*Examiner insight:* Trial and improvement can reach 3 and 10, but if it fails it earns nothing; correct expressions for the later terms earn method marks even when the solving goes wrong.

**6. (a)** 1000 ÷ 2500 = **0.4** [1]
**(b)** 160 × 0.4 = **64**, 64 × 0.4 = **25.6** [1]
**(c)** Continuing: 10.24, 4.096, 1.6384 (9th), 0.65536 (10th) [1]; the first term less than 1 is the **10th term** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The method mark in (c) needs the terms listed with their positions; the answer is a position, so giving "0.65536" alone answers a different question.

**7. (a)** Common difference −6, so −6n: −6, −12, −18 [1]; each term is 53 more, so **53 − 6n** [1]
**(b)** 53 − 6n < −40 gives 6n > 93, n > 15.5 [1]; the 16th term is 53 − 96 = **−43** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "6n + 53" or "53 + 6n" gets only the mark for the constant, since the sign of n is the common difference; in (b) stopping at n = 16 does not answer "work out the term".

**8.** 4n + 9 = 7n − 12 [1]
21 = 3n, so **n = 7** [1]
4 × 7 + 9 = **37** (and 7 × 7 − 12 = 37) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Listing terms of both sequences until they meet is acceptable if every term is correct, but the equation method is quicker and gets the method mark straight away.

**9. (a)** First differences 1, 3, 5, 7; second difference 2 [1]; next first differences 9 and 11, so **28** and **39** [1]
**(b)** Second difference 2, so a = 1: start with n² [1]
Term − n²: 3 − 1, 4 − 4, 7 − 9, 12 − 16, ... = 2, 0, −2, −4, which is −2n + 4 [1]
nth term **n² − 2n + 4** [1] (check n = 5: 25 − 10 + 4 = 19)
*Examiner insight:* The first mark is for the correct n² coefficient, which is half the second difference; writing 2n² usually means no further marks can be earned on the linear part.

**10. (a)** 3√2 ÷ 3 = **√2** [1]
**(b)** Terms: 3, 3√2, 6, 6√2, 12, 12√2, 24, 24√2, so 3 × (√2)⁷ [1] = 3 × 8√2 = **24√2** [1]
**(c)** Every second term doubles: 6 (3rd), 12 (5th), 24 (7th), 48 (9th), 96 [1]; 96 is the **11th term** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A decimal such as 33.9 for (b) does not meet "the form k√2", so the accuracy mark is lost even with correct method.

**11. (a)** Common difference 4, so 4n: 4, 8, 12 [1]; each term is 4 more, so **4n + 4** [1]
**(b)** White tiles are square numbers, n², so 10² = **100** [1]
**(c)** Total = n² + 4n + 4 [1] = (n + 2)(n + 2) = **(n + 2)²** [1]
**(d)** 4n + 4 = 140, so n = 34 [1]; white tiles = 34² = **1156** [1]
**(e)** n² = 4n + 4 gives n² − 4n − 4 = 0 [1]
n = (4 ± √32)/2 = 2 ± 2√2, about 4.83 or −0.83; neither is a positive whole number, so **no such pattern** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c) a "show that" needs the expansion of (n + 2)² or the sum n² + 4n + 4 shown in full; checking one pattern number only does not prove it for all n.

**12. (a)** 1 ÷ (1 − 2) = −1 and 1 ÷ (1 − (−1)) = 1/2 [1]; 1 ÷ (1 − 1/2) = 2, so **−1, 1/2, 2** [1]
**(b)** The terms repeat in a cycle of 3: 2, −1, 1/2, 2, −1, 1/2, ... [1]; 50 = 3 × 16 + 2, so the 50th term is the 2nd in the cycle: **−1** [1]
**(c)** Each cycle sums to 2 − 1 + 1/2 = 3/2 [1]
16 cycles give 16 × 3/2 = 24, then terms 49 and 50 are 2 and −1 [1]
Sum = 24 + 2 − 1 = **25** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A bare "−1" in (b) does not earn the explanation mark; you must show the repeating cycle and link 50 to its position in the cycle.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the term-to-term rule ("add 5") instead of the nth term when the question asks for an expression in n.
- Using the first term as the coefficient of n, as in "6n + 5" for 6, 11, 16, ...
- Leaving out the reason when deciding whether a number is in a sequence: show the equation and the value of n.
- Answering a "which term" question with the term's value, or a "work out the term" question with the position.
- Losing the sign on a decreasing sequence.
- Including extra numbers in a "write down all" list question.
- Using the full second difference as the n² coefficient instead of half of it (Higher).
- Giving surd answers as decimals when an exact form is asked for (Higher).
- Not showing the repeating pattern when a recursive sequence cycles (Higher).
