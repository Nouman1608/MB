## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Absolute advantage | Can produce a good using fewer resources than another country |
| Comparative advantage | Lower opportunity cost of a good than another country |
| Opportunity cost (in trade) | Amount of the alternative good sacrificed when output of this one rises by a unit |
| Specialisation | Concentrating production on the goods with the lowest opportunity cost |
| Terms of trade | Ratio of export prices to import prices, shown as an index |
| Protectionism | Government measures that make imports less competitive against home-made goods |
| Tariff | Tax on imports |
| Quota | Limit on the quantity of imports |
| Export subsidy | Payment to firms for each unit they export |
| Dumping | Selling abroad at a price below cost, or below the price at home |
| Infant industry | A new industry that needs time to build scale and skills |

## Formulas and rules

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Cost of one unit of X, measured in Y | Maximum output of Y ÷ maximum output of X |
| Comparative advantage | The country with the lower opportunity cost |
| Mutually beneficial terms | Price of a unit lies strictly between the two countries' opportunity costs |
| Terms of trade | (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100 |
| Percentage change | (new − old) ÷ old × 100 |
| Exports needed for the same imports | 100 ÷ terms of trade index |
| Tariff revenue | Tariff per unit × imports after the tariff |
| Quota rent | (Domestic price − world price) × quota |
| Ad valorem tariff | Percentage of the import price |

## Method 1: find comparative advantage

1. Write the maximum output of each good for each country.
2. Work out the opportunity cost of one unit of one good in each country (divide the other good's output by it).
3. The country with the lower figure has the comparative advantage in that good. The other country has it in the second good.
4. State the range of terms of trade where both gain.

**Reminder (invented).** In a month, Country C makes 90 chairs or 30 lamps, and Country D makes 40 chairs or 20 lamps.

- One lamp costs C 90 ÷ 30 = 3 chairs and costs D 40 ÷ 20 = 2 chairs. D has the comparative advantage in lamps.
- One chair costs C 30 ÷ 90 = 1/3 lamp and costs D 20 ÷ 40 = 1/2 lamp. C has the comparative advantage in chairs.
- C is better at both (90 > 40 and 30 > 20), but it still gains by specialising in chairs.
- Both gain if one lamp trades for between 2 and 3 chairs.

## Method 2: terms of trade

1. Put the export price index on top and the import price index below.
2. Multiply by 100.
3. Above 100 means improved, below 100 means deteriorated, and the comparison is always with the base year.

**Reminder.** Exports 132, imports 110: 132 ÷ 110 × 100 = **120**, an improvement. If the terms of trade then fall from 120 to 96, the change is (96 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = **−20%**. At 96, against a base of 100, a country needs 100 ÷ 96 = 1.042 times as many exports as in the base year for the same imports, about 4.2% more.

**Causes:** changes in commodity prices, the exchange rate, relative inflation, world demand, tariffs and other barriers.

**Effects:** an improvement lets each export buy more imports. The effect on the trade balance depends on how responsive volumes are to price, so it is never automatic.

## Method 3: tariffs, quotas and export subsidies

Use the same sequence for each:

1. Find the free-trade price (the world price for a small country), quantity demanded, quantity supplied and imports.
2. Apply the policy and find the new price and quantities.
3. Calculate changes in consumer surplus, producer surplus and government revenue.
4. Find the net welfare loss, which equals the two triangles.

| Policy | Price at home | Imports | Who gains | Who loses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tariff | Rises by the tariff | Fall | Domestic producers and government | Consumers |
| Quota | Rises until demand minus supply equals the quota | Fixed at the quota | Domestic producers and licence holders | Consumers |
| Export subsidy | Rises to world price plus subsidy | No direct change; exports rise | Producers | Consumers and taxpayers |

**Reminder.** A 12% ad valorem tariff on an import priced at 250 adds 250 × 0.12 = 30. A tariff of 3 on 40 units of imports brings revenue of 120. A subsidy of 3 per unit on 50 exported units costs 150.

## Reasons for and against protection

| Reason given | Weakness to raise |
|---|---|
| Infant industry | Hard to pick winners; protection can last for ever |
| Declining industry and jobs | Delays adjustment and raises prices for everyone |
| National or food security | Stockpiles or other policies may cost less |
| Dumping | Hard to prove; consumers gain from the low price |
| Revenue | Reduces trade, so revenue may fall short |
| Narrow economy | Other policies may do more |

## Must-know distinctions

- Absolute advantage compares **outputs**. Comparative advantage compares **opportunity costs**.
- A **tariff** raises revenue for the government. A **quota** creates a gain for licence holders unless licences are sold.
- A higher terms of trade index does not by itself mean a stronger current account.
- Free trade area: no internal barriers. Customs union adds a common external tariff. Common market adds free movement of labour and capital. Monetary union adds a single currency.

## Evaluation chains

- **Tariff**: price rises, consumers buy less, domestic firms supply more, imports fall, government gains revenue. But other countries may retaliate, firms using imported inputs face higher costs, and the long-run effect on competitiveness may be negative.
- **Infant industry**: protect now, firm gains scale and learns, costs fall, protection removed. This works only if protection is temporary and the industry could really become competitive.
- **Costs of trade**: an industry shrinks, workers lose jobs, retraining is slow, so unemployment lasts. The cost is lower if labour is mobile and the economy is diverse.
- **Comparative advantage changes**: wages rise or new skills develop, costs shift, and the pattern of trade changes. A policy based on today's advantage may look wrong later.
- **Bloc**: members trade more with each other. This can raise efficiency, but if a cheaper non-member is shut out the gain is smaller.
- **WTO**: lowers barriers and settles disputes, but relies on members accepting its decisions, and it is slow to reach agreements.

## Quick self-test

1. In a month, Country C makes 90 chairs or 30 lamps. Country D makes 40 chairs or 20 lamps. Which country has the comparative advantage in lamps?
2. State the range of terms (chairs per lamp) at which both countries gain.
3. Export prices index 132 and import prices index 110. Calculate the terms of trade.
4. Export price index 120 and terms of trade 96. Find the import price index.
5. Terms of trade fall to 85. By what percentage must export volume rise to buy the same imports? Give one decimal place.
6. A 15% ad valorem tariff is placed on an import costing 40. What is the new price?
7. A quota of 40 units lifts the home price 3 above the world price. Calculate the quota rent.
8. Which bloc type applies a shared tariff to outsiders yet still restricts the movement of workers between members?
9. State two roles of the WTO.
10. Give two reasons why a country's comparative advantage might change.
11. Define dumping.
12. A subsidy of 3 per unit is paid on 50 exported units. What does it cost the government?

### Answers

1. Country D (opportunity cost 2 chairs against 3 chairs).
2. Between 2 and 3 chairs per lamp.
3. 132 ÷ 110 × 100 = 120.
4. Import index = 120 ÷ 96 × 100 = 125.
5. 100 ÷ 85 = 1.176, so about 17.6%.
6. 40 × 1.15 = 46.
7. 3 × 40 = 120.
8. A customs union.
9. Any two of: forum for negotiating lower trade barriers, monitoring members' trade policies, settling disputes, administering agreements.
10. Any two of: investment in education or capital, new technology, discovery or exhaustion of resources, wage changes, exchange rate changes.
11. Selling in a foreign market at a price below cost, or below the price charged at home.
12. 3 × 50 = 150.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total output instead of opportunity cost to find comparative advantage.
- Giving a range of terms of trade that includes the opportunity costs themselves.
- Dividing the wrong way round when finding opportunity cost or the terms of trade.
- Stating that a tariff "makes imports cheaper" or that quotas raise government revenue.
- Leaving out the welfare triangles when asked for the net effect of a tariff.
- Listing reasons for protection without any evaluation of whether it works.
- Giving a one-sided answer on trading blocs with no mention of costs or non-members.
- Saying "terms of trade improved" without saying whether the index rose or fell against a base year.
