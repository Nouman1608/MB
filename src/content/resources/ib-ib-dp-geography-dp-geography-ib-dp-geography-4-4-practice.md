## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between pre-event and post-event hazard management. **[2]**

**2.** State the four stages that follow impact in Park's disaster-response model, in order. **[2]**

**3.** Outline why earthquake early warning systems cannot predict earthquakes. **[2]**

**4.** Outline how slope drainage reduces the risk of a landslide. **[2]**

**5.** Suggest two reasons why the economic losses from geophysical disasters are often highest in high-income countries. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the number of recorded geophysical disasters may rise even though the frequency of earthquakes does not change. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows invented data for a hillside district that suffers rainfall-triggered landslides.

| Year | Houses on slopes steeper than 30 degrees | Landslides recorded | Slopes upgraded with drainage and walls (total) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 400 | 12 | 0 |
| Year 10 | 900 | 15 | 20 |
| Year 20 | 950 | 6 | 140 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in houses on steep slopes between Year 1 and Year 10. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why landslides increased between Year 1 and Year 10. **[2]**

**(c)** Using the data, explain why landslides fell between Year 10 and Year 20 even though exposure stayed high. **[2]**

**8.** Two towns recover from a volcanic eruption. Town R's quality of life returns to its pre-eruption level after 2 years and then rises above it. Town S's quality of life is still below its pre-eruption level after 8 years.

**(a)** Using the Park model, describe the difference in the two recovery curves. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two reasons for Town S's slower recovery. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what "building back better" means, with reference to Town R. **[2]**

**9.** A city on an active fault is growing quickly. Suggest three pre-event measures it could adopt, and explain which group of residents each measure would most benefit. **[6]**

**10.** Explain why a hazard forecast may fail to reduce deaths even when the science is accurate, using one located example. **[4]**

## Section C

**11.** Evaluate the role of technology in increasing resilience to volcanic hazards. **[10]**

**12.** "Future geophysical hazard risk will be determined more by human decisions than by physical processes." To what extent do you agree? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Pre-event management reduces risk before a hazard occurs, through mitigation and preparedness such as zoning or drills [1]; post-event management responds to and recovers from an event once it has happened, such as rescue and reconstruction [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Disruption, relief, rehabilitation, reconstruction [2]; award [1] if all four are named but the order is wrong. **[2]**

**3.** They detect an earthquake after the rupture has begun [1] and send alerts faster than the slower, more damaging surface waves travel, so they give only seconds of warning rather than a forecast of when an earthquake will occur [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Drainage removes water from the slope, lowering pore-water pressure [1], which increases the friction and strength holding material in place, so heavy rain is less likely to trigger failure [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Buildings, infrastructure and property are worth more [1]; more assets are insured, so losses are fully costed and recorded [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Exposure is increasing because more people and assets are located in hazard zones, for example through urban growth on faults or volcanic slopes [1]. Vulnerability may rise through densification or informal building on unstable land, so an event of the same size causes harm that counts as a disaster [1]. Reporting and recording have also improved, so more events are counted than in the past [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Increase = 900 - 400 = 500 [1]. 500 / 400 x 100 = **125%** [1].

**(b)** More than twice as many houses were built on steep slopes, so more slopes were cut and loaded, and more exposed houses meant more slides were recorded [1]; very few slopes had been upgraded by Year 10 (20), so new construction outpaced engineering [1].

**(c)** Houses on steep slopes rose only slightly, from 900 to 950, so exposure remained high [1], but upgraded slopes rose from 20 to 140, so drainage and walls reduced the number of slope failures from 15 to 6 [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Both curves fall at impact, but Town R's curve recovers to its original level within 2 years and finishes higher [1], whereas Town S's curve stays below its original level for at least 8 years, showing a longer and incomplete reconstruction stage [1].

**(b)** Any two, for example: less government funding or insurance for rebuilding [1]; weaker institutions or slower aid delivery [1]; continued exposure, for example rebuilding inside the hazard zone [1]; loss of the main source of income, such as farmland buried by ash [1]. Maximum [2].

**(c)** Building back better means reconstructing in a way that leaves the place less vulnerable than before the event [1], for example Town R rising above its pre-eruption quality of life suggests safer housing, better services or relocation away from the hazard zone [1]. **[6]**

**9.** One mark for each suitable measure and one for a linked explanation of who benefits, up to 6:

- Enforced building codes for new construction [1], which protect future residents of new apartment blocks if inspection is effective [1].
- A retrofitting programme for schools and hospitals [1], which benefits children and patients, and keeps services open after the event [1].
- Land-use zoning that keeps housing off the fault trace and liquefiable ground [1], which benefits low-income migrants who would otherwise settle on the cheapest, most hazardous land [1].
- Earthquake drills and education [1], which benefit everyone, including informal residents whose homes do not meet codes [1].

Accept other realistic measures with a justified beneficiary. **[6]**

**10.** A forecast saves lives only if it is communicated, trusted and acted on [1]. Warnings may not reach residents in time, or may be ignored because earlier alerts proved false [1]. Officials may delay evacuation because of its cost or political risk [1]. Example: at Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia, in 1985 the lahar hazard had been mapped, but the town of Armero was not evacuated in time and more than 20,000 people died [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content; credit a balanced, evaluative answer:

- **Monitoring strengths:** seismometers detect magma movement; satellite and ground measurement detect swelling; gas sensors detect changing emissions [1]. These precursors allow forecasts, as at Mount Pinatubo in 1991, where evacuation before the main eruption saved many lives [1].
- **Mapping:** hazard maps show lava, pyroclastic flow and lahar zones, guiding land-use planning and evacuation routes [1].
- **Engineering:** lahar channels and barriers, and lava diversion, as on Heimaey in 1973 [1].
- **Limitations of forecasting:** volcanoes can show unrest for years without erupting, so false alarms erode trust and evacuations are costly [1]. Timing and scale remain uncertain [1].
- **Limits of technology alone:** Nevado del Ruiz in 1985 shows that a map without decision-making and communication fails [1].
- **Equity:** many active volcanoes in low-income countries have limited monitoring networks [1].
- **Complementary measures:** education, drills and trusted local communication turn data into action [1].
- **Judgement:** technology greatly increases resilience where it is linked to governance and public trust, but on its own it is a necessary rather than sufficient condition [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Physical processes set the hazard:** plate boundary location, magnitude and frequency of earthquakes, and eruption style do not change on human timescales [1]; some events, such as very large tsunamis, can exceed defences regardless of planning [1].
- **Climate change** may alter some physical triggers, such as heavier rain on slopes and volcanic lahars [1].
- **Human decisions shape exposure:** urban growth on faults and slopes, densification, and land-use change [1] [1].
- **Human decisions shape vulnerability:** code enforcement, poverty, governance and conflict; for example, unenforced codes and amnesties in Turkey before the 2023 earthquake [1].
- **Human decisions shape capacity:** monitoring, warning systems, insurance and reconstruction choices that build back better [1].
- **Uneven agency:** the poorest groups often have least choice over where they live, so "human decisions" are made by governments and markets as much as by individuals [1].
- **Interaction:** risk = hazard x vulnerability, so the physical process is a precondition, but the size of future losses depends on human choices [1].
- **Conclusion:** a supported judgement, for example agreeing to a large extent because the hazard is fixed while exposure and vulnerability are changing rapidly [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* both Section C questions reward located examples and a judgement that responds to the exact wording, such as "role of technology" or "more than".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling early warning a prediction.
- Describing a Park curve without stating what its depth and speed of recovery show.
- Listing pre-event measures without saying who benefits.
- Forgetting that exposure and vulnerability, not hazard frequency, drive most future change.
