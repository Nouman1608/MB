This guide covers section **2.1 The double entry system of book-keeping** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The syllabus asks you to understand the double entry system; how to prepare ledger accounts and post transactions to them; how to calculate balances and make transfers to financial statements; how to interpret ledger accounts and their balances; accounts in a three-column running balance format; the division of the ledger into the sales ledger, the purchases ledger and the nominal (general) ledger; and how ledger accounts can be kept digitally. The syllabus notes that you do not need to explain or use folio columns. If you sit an earlier series, check the syllabus for your exam year.

Use it with the [double entry revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-the-double-entry-system-of-book-keeping-revision-notes/) and the [double entry practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-the-double-entry-system-of-book-keeping-practice/). The [accounting equation study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-the-accounting-equation/) explains why every transaction has two sides. The examples follow one invented business, Tamarind Stationers, owned by Joel Mensah. Amounts are in dollars.

## 1. The double entry rule

Every transaction is recorded twice: as a **debit** in one account and a **credit** in another, for the same amount. This is the duality concept in practice, and it is why total debits equal total credits.

| Type of account | Increase recorded on the | Decrease recorded on the |
|---|---|---|
| Asset | Debit | Credit |
| Expense | Debit | Credit |
| Drawings | Debit | Credit |
| Liability | Credit | Debit |
| Capital | Credit | Debit |
| Income (sales, rent received) | Credit | Debit |

A quick check for any transaction: ask "which account receives value?" (debit) and "which account gives value?" (credit).

Goods bought and sold for resale go to the **purchases** and **sales** accounts, not to an inventory account. Goods returned go to **purchases returns** and **sales returns**.

## 2. Posting a month of transactions

Tamarind Stationers' transactions for June:

| Date | Transaction | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Joel pays 10,000 into a business bank account | Bank | Capital |
| 3 | Buys goods on credit from Kirby Limited, 2,400 | Purchases | Kirby Limited |
| 5 | Cash sales of 850, paid straight into the bank | Bank | Sales |
| 8 | Sells goods on credit to R Dutta, 1,300 | R Dutta | Sales |
| 12 | Pays rent by bank transfer, 600 | Rent | Bank |
| 15 | Returns damaged goods to Kirby Limited, 200 | Kirby Limited | Purchases returns |
| 20 | R Dutta pays 1,000 by bank transfer | Bank | R Dutta |
| 25 | Pays Kirby Limited 1,500 by bank transfer | Kirby Limited | Bank |
| 28 | Buys a shelving unit (equipment) for 1,800 by bank transfer | Equipment | Bank |

| | | **11,850** | | | **11,850** |
| Jul 1 | Balance b/d | 7,950 | | | |

**Kirby Limited account**

| Date | Details | $ | Date | Details | $ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jun 15 | Purchases returns | 200 | Jun 3 | Purchases | 2,400 |
| Jun 25 | Bank | 1,500 | | | |
| Jun 30 | Balance c/d | 700 | | | |
| | | **2,400** | | | **2,400** |
| | | | Jul 1 | Balance b/d | 700 |

**R Dutta account**

| Date | Details | $ | Date | Details | $ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jun 8 | Sales | 1,300 | Jun 20 | Bank | 1,000 |
| | | | Jun 30 | Balance c/d | 300 |
| | | **1,300** | | | **1,300** |

The other accounts have one or two entries: Capital credit 10,000; Purchases debit 2,400; Sales credit 850 and 1,300 (total 2,150); Rent debit 600; Purchases returns credit 200; Equipment debit 1,800.

**Check that the double entry works.** Debit balances: bank 7,950 + R Dutta 300 + purchases 2,400 + rent 600 + equipment 1,800 = 13,050. Credit balances: Kirby 700 + sales 2,150 + purchases returns 200 + capital 10,000 = 13,050. They agree.

## 3. Balancing an account

1. Add both sides.
2. Write the difference on the **smaller** side as "Balance c/d" (carried down), so both sides total the same.
3. Total both sides on the same line.
4. Bring the balance down on the **opposite** side, dated the first day of the next period, as "Balance b/d" (brought down).

A balance b/d on the debit side is a **debit balance**; on the credit side, a **credit balance**.

## 4. Transfers to the financial statements

At the year end, two kinds of account are treated differently.

- **Income and expense accounts** (sales, purchases, returns, rent, wages and so on) are closed by transferring their balances to the **statement of profit or loss**. They start the next year at zero.
- **Asset, liability and capital accounts** are balanced and carried down. Their balances appear in the **statement of financial position**.

Suppose Tamarind's rent for the whole year totals 7,200. The rent account is closed like this:

| Details | $ | Details | $ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bank (total payments) | 7,200 | Statement of profit or loss | 7,200 |

Because rent is an expense, the transfer is on the credit side of the rent account and the debit is in the statement of profit or loss.

## 5. Interpreting ledger accounts and their balances

You may be given an account and asked what it shows. Read each entry by asking what the other account tells you.

| Balance or entry | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Debit balance on a customer's account | The customer owes the business (trade receivable) |
| Credit balance on a supplier's account | The business owes the supplier (trade payable) |
| Debit balance on the bank account | Money in the bank |
| Credit balance on the bank account | A bank overdraft |
| "Sales returns" on the credit side of a customer's account | The customer sent goods back, reducing the debt |
| "Bank" on the debit side of a supplier's account | The business paid the supplier |

From Tamarind's accounts: R Dutta still owes 300; Tamarind owes Kirby Limited 700; it has 7,950 in the bank.

## 6. Three-column running balance format

Instead of two sides, a running balance account has debit, credit and balance columns. The balance is updated after every entry. This is the format most digital systems display.

**Bank account (running balance)**

| Date | Details | Debit $ | Credit $ | Balance $ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jun 1 | Capital | 10,000 | | 10,000 Dr |
| Jun 5 | Sales | 850 | | 10,850 Dr |
| Jun 12 | Rent | | 600 | 10,250 Dr |
| Jun 20 | R Dutta | 1,000 | | 11,250 Dr |
| Jun 25 | Kirby Limited | | 1,500 | 9,750 Dr |
| Jun 28 | Equipment | | 1,800 | 7,950 Dr |

The final balance, 7,950 Dr, is the same as in the two-sided account. Mark each balance Dr or Cr: a balance that became Cr on a bank account would show an overdraft. The advantage is that the balance can be read at any date without balancing the account.

## 7. Division of the ledger

A business with many accounts divides its ledger into three:

| Ledger | Contains | Tamarind's examples |
|---|---|---|
| Sales ledger | Personal accounts of credit customers (trade receivables) | R Dutta |
| Purchases ledger | Personal accounts of credit suppliers (trade payables) | Kirby Limited |
| Nominal (general) ledger | All other accounts: income, expenses, assets, liabilities, capital | Sales, purchases, purchases returns, rent, equipment, capital |

Dividing the ledger lets different staff work on different parts at the same time, makes accounts easier to find, and helps with control and checking.

## 8. Keeping ledger accounts digitally

Ledger accounts can be kept in a spreadsheet or an accounting program. The double entry rules are the same, but:

- one input can post the debit and the credit together, so the two sides always match
- running balances are calculated automatically
- accounts can be sorted into the three ledgers and printed or viewed at any time

Digital ledgers still depend on correct input. If a transaction is posted to the wrong account, or not entered at all, the system will not notice.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the entries for capital introduced (it is debit bank, credit capital).
- Debiting an "inventory" account for goods bought for resale instead of purchases.
- Bringing a balance down on the same side as it was carried down.
- Writing "Balance c/d" on the larger side.
- Transferring asset or liability balances to the statement of profit or loss.
- Leaving out Dr or Cr in a running balance column.

## Exam technique

- **Prepare** a ledger account: dates, details naming the other account, amounts, balance c/d and b/d, and totals on the same line.
- **Calculate** a balance: show the two side totals and the difference.
- **Explain** or **Interpret** an entry: say what the transaction was and its effect.
- **Identify** the ledger an account belongs to: sales, purchases or nominal.
