## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Patient ID | Unique number that identifies a patient and links their records |
| Patient record | Database record of a patient's personal details, history, allergies, results and treatment |
| Pharmacy record | Database of prescriptions, medicines and stock used by a pharmacy |
| Drug interaction | When two medicines taken together cause harm or stop working properly |
| 3D printer | Device that builds an object layer by layer from a 3D design |
| CAD | Computer-aided design software used to create the 3D model |
| Prosthetic | Artificial body part, such as a hand or leg |
| Tissue engineering | Printing layers of living cells to build tissue (bioprinting) |
| Access levels | Permissions so staff see only the data they need |

## Patient records

**Contents:** personal details, patient ID, next of kin, medical history, allergies, blood group, test results and scans, medicines, appointments.

**Uses:**

- full history available to any authorised doctor
- fast checks of allergies and blood group in emergencies
- results shared instantly between departments
- appointment reminders produced automatically
- data analysed to plan services

## Pharmacy records

**Contents:** patient ID and details, prescriptions (medicine, dose, frequency, duration, doctor), medicines database (doses, side effects, interactions), stock levels.

**Uses:**

- check each prescription against allergies and current medicines; warn of interactions or unusual doses
- print labels with dosage instructions
- manage repeat prescriptions
- update stock and reorder automatically

## Computerised records: for and against

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Shared instantly between departments and pharmacies | Sensitive data at risk from hacking or misuse |
| Quick access in emergencies | Data protection laws must be followed |
| No handwriting to misread | Unavailable if the system or network fails |
| Automatic allergy and interaction checks | Expensive to set up; staff training |
| Less storage space; backups | Typing errors spread to every user |

## Keeping medical data secure

- **Access levels**: each role sees only what it needs (receptionist: appointments; doctor: full record; pharmacist: prescriptions and allergies).
- **Strong passwords or smart cards**, with automatic log-out.
- **Audit trail**: records who viewed each record and when.
- **Encryption** of stored and transmitted data.
- **Backups** kept separately so records can be restored.

## 3D printing in medicine

**Process:** scan the patient, design the item in CAD, print layer by layer, test and fit.

| Use | What is printed | Key benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Prosthetics | Artificial limbs and hands shaped to the patient | Better fit; cheaper and quicker; easy to reprint as a child grows |
| Tissue engineering | Layers of living cells forming tissue | May repair damage, such as skin for burns, with less need for donors |
| Artificial blood vessels | Tubes with a structure like blood vessels, in body-compatible materials | Replace or bypass damaged vessels; supply printed tissue |
| Customised medicines | Tablets with an exact dose, shape or combination | One tablet suited to one patient; controlled release |

**For:** customised; fast; often cheaper; easy to change the design; less waste.
**Against:** printers and materials can be expensive; complex items print slowly; bioprinting is still developing; strict testing needed; skilled staff required.

## Exam traps

- Listing record contents without uses when the question says "describe the uses".
- Saying the pharmacy system is only for stock. Its main safety use is interaction and allergy checks.
- Stating that whole printed organs are in routine use.
- Leaving out the scan and CAD stages when describing printing a prosthetic.
- Writing "it is faster" without a comparison, such as faster than finding paper notes.
- Giving "hacking" as a disadvantage with no consequence. Say what is exposed: sensitive health data, breaching privacy and data protection law.
- Confusing a prosthetic (an artificial body part) with tissue engineering (printing living cells).

## Self-test

1. What field links a patient's records across departments?
2. Give four items stored in a patient record.
3. Give two uses of patient records in an emergency.
4. What does the pharmacy system check when a prescription is entered?
5. Give two other uses of pharmacy records.
6. Give two advantages of computerised patient records over paper records.
7. Give two disadvantages of computerised patient records.
8. Describe the stages in producing a 3D-printed prosthetic.
9. Why are 3D-printed prosthetics useful for children?
10. What is tissue engineering?
11. Give one benefit of a customised 3D-printed tablet.
12. Give two disadvantages of 3D printing in medicine.

### Answers

1. The unique patient ID number.
2. Any four: name; date of birth; address; next of kin; medical history; allergies; blood group; test results; medicines.
3. Checking allergies before giving medicine; checking blood group or current medicines.
4. The patient's allergies and current medicines, to warn of interactions or unusual doses.
5. Any two: print labels; manage repeat prescriptions; update stock; reorder automatically.
6. Any two: shared instantly; quicker to find; no handwriting to misread; automatic checks; less storage space.
7. Any two: risk of hacking; must follow data protection laws; unavailable if the system fails; expensive; training needed.
8. Scan the patient's limb; design the prosthetic in CAD; print it layer by layer; test and fit.
9. They can be reprinted cheaply and quickly as the child grows.
10. Printing layers of living cells to build tissue.
11. Exact dose for that patient (or several medicines combined in one tablet, or controlled release).
12. Any two: expensive equipment; slow for complex items; bioprinting still developing; strict testing needed; skilled staff required.
