## 13.1 The listed technologies

The syllabus says everything listed after "including" must be studied, so learn every row.

| Technology | One-line definition | Typical use | Plus | Minus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Artificial intelligence | Computer performing tasks needing human intelligence, including learning from data | Chatbots, fraud detection, scan analysis | Fast, consistent analysis of big data | Bias; decisions hard to explain |
| Augmented reality | Computer images overlaid on a live view of the real world | Maintenance guides, furniture previews | Information where it is needed | Needs suitable device; distraction |
| Virtual reality | Fully computer-generated, immersive 3D world, usually via headset | Flight or surgical training, tours | Safe practice of risky tasks | Cost; motion sickness; isolation |
| Robotics | Programmable machines using sensors, processor and actuators | Assembly lines, warehouses, surgery | Precise, tireless, works in danger | High cost; job losses |
| Computer-assisted translation | Software aids a human translator (translation memory, terminology database) | Manuals, legal documents | Faster, consistent terms | Translator must still check |
| Holographic imaging | 3D images viewable from different angles, from laser interference patterns | Card and banknote security, anatomy teaching | Shows depth; hard to forge | Specialist equipment |
| Holographic / 4th generation optical storage | Data stored through the depth of the medium or in many layers, beyond Blu-ray | Long-term archives | Huge capacity; whole pages read at once | Mostly in development; costly |
| 3D printing | Additive manufacturing, layer by layer, from a CAD model | Prototypes, prosthetics, spare parts | Cheap one-offs; less waste | Slow for mass production |
| Vision enhancement | Improves or restores sight: night vision, electronic magnifiers, smart glasses, implants | Low-vision users, security staff | Independence; safety at night | Cost; some need surgery |
| Wearable computing | Computing devices worn on the body with sensors | Smartwatches, fitness trackers | Continuous hands-free monitoring | Privacy; battery life |
| Blockchain | Distributed ledger of blocks, each holding the previous block's hash | Cryptocurrency, supply chains | Tamper-evident; no central control | Energy use; errors hard to reverse |
| Internet of Things | Everyday objects with sensors and network connections | Smart meters, thermostats, farm sensors | Automation; remote control | Weak security; privacy |
| Molecular data storage | Data encoded in molecules, e.g. DNA base sequences | Archives | Extreme density; long life | Slow, expensive read/write |
| Autonomous transport | Vehicles that sense surroundings and drive themselves using AI | Driverless trains, cars, drones | No tiredness; runs 24/7 | Liability; hacking; driver jobs |

### Blockchain in steps

1. Transactions are grouped into a **block**.
2. The block stores the **hash of the previous block**.
3. Nodes in the network **agree** the block is valid.
4. The block is added and **copies are updated on every node**.
5. Altering an old block changes its hash, so the next block no longer matches: tampering shows.

### 3D printing in steps

1. Create or scan a **CAD model**.
2. Software **slices** it into thin horizontal layers.
3. The printer **deposits or hardens material** one layer at a time.
4. The object may need finishing (supports removed, smoothing).

### Autonomous transport in steps

1. **Sensors** (cameras, radar, lidar, GPS) collect data about the surroundings.
2. **AI software** identifies objects such as other vehicles, people and signs.
3. The system **decides** on speed and direction.
4. **Actuators** steer, brake and accelerate.
5. The loop repeats continuously, many times a second.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| AR vs VR | AR **adds to** the real world; VR **replaces** it |
| CAT vs machine translation | CAT: the human translates with software help. Machine translation: the software translates on its own |
| Holographic imaging vs holographic storage | Imaging makes 3D **pictures**; storage keeps **data** in holograms |
| AI vs robotics | AI is the **intelligence** (software); a robot is the **machine**. A robot may or may not use AI |
| IoT vs wearable computing | Wearables are **worn on the body**; IoT covers any connected everyday object. A connected smartwatch is both |
| Molecular vs optical storage | Molecular uses **chemistry** (molecules); optical uses **light** on a disc or crystal |
| Robot vs autonomous vehicle | An autonomous vehicle is a kind of robot that **moves people or goods**; many robots stay in one place, such as on an assembly line |

## Impact grid

| Area | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Individuals | Convenience, health tracking, new leisure and learning | Skills gap, job loss, data privacy, cost |
| Organisations | Lower running costs, speed, new products, better data | Set-up cost, retraining, security risk, restructuring |
| Scientific research | AI pattern-finding, robot labs, IoT field data, 3D visualisation | Cost of equipment; results depend on data quality |

### Medicine and healthcare: learn all five named areas

- **Patient care and monitoring**: wearables and IoT send vital signs remotely, with alerts.
- **Prosthetics**: 3D printing gives custom fit, low cost, fast replacement; robotics gives powered movement.
- **Tissue engineering**: growing or bioprinting living tissue to repair damage.
- **Artificial blood vessels**: bioprinted or engineered vessels being developed for grafts.
- **Medical tools and equipment**: CAD and 3D printing for prototypes and custom guides; VR and AR training.

### The environment: learn all five named areas

- **E-waste**: short-lived wearables and IoT devices add hazardous waste.
- **Recycling**: AI-vision robots sort waste; recycled plastic used in 3D printing.
- **Power consumption**: AI data centres and some blockchains use a lot; smart IoT controls can save energy.
- **Manufacturing processes**: additive printing wastes less; local production cuts transport.
- **Energy from wireless signals**: low-power sensors harvest energy from radio signals such as Wi-Fi, reducing battery use.

## Method: answering Discuss and Evaluate questions

1. Name the **technology** and the **user** from the scenario.
2. Make a point (feature or benefit).
3. Develop it: say **so what** for that user.
4. Give a **drawback** with its consequence.
5. End with a short **judgement** that weighs the two sides.

A reminder in one line: "The hospital's IoT heart monitors send readings every minute [point], so nurses are alerted at once if a patient deteriorates [development], but if the ward Wi-Fi fails, alerts are missed [drawback]."

## Quick self-test

1. State the difference between augmented reality and virtual reality.
2. Name the two main tools in computer-assisted translation software.
3. What does each block in a blockchain store that links it to the chain?
4. Give one use of holographic imaging.
5. Why is 3D printing called additive manufacturing?
6. An object 48 mm tall is printed in layers 0.2 mm thick. How many layers are printed?
7. State one advantage and one disadvantage of molecular data storage.
8. Give two sensors an autonomous car might use.
9. Explain one way new technologies add to e-waste.
10. What is meant by "energy from wireless signals"?
11. Name one technology used in patient monitoring.

### Answers

1. AR overlays computer images on a live view of the real world; VR replaces the real world with a fully computer-generated environment.
2. Translation memory and a terminology database.
3. The hash of the previous block.
4. Security holograms on bank cards or banknotes (also accept 3D anatomy images for teaching).
5. It builds the object by adding material layer by layer, rather than cutting material away.
6. 48 ÷ 0.2 = **240 layers**.
7. Advantage: very high density (or long life). Disadvantage: slow and expensive to write and read.
8. Any two: camera, radar, lidar, GPS.
9. Cheap devices such as fitness trackers are replaced often and contain hazardous materials, so more electronic waste is thrown away.
10. Harvesting small amounts of energy from radio signals, such as Wi-Fi, to power low-power devices like sensors.
11. Wearable devices or IoT sensors that send vital signs to staff remotely.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling CAT "software that translates for you"; the human translator is central.
- Defining blockchain as "secure" without explaining hashes linking blocks and copies held on many nodes.
- Writing about holographic **imaging** when the question asks about holographic **storage**.
- Giving a generic medical point when the question names a specific area, such as tissue engineering.
- Ignoring the **power used by the new devices themselves** when claiming energy savings.
- Listing advantages only on Evaluate or Discuss questions, with no drawback or judgement.
- Answers that name the technology but not the user or task in the scenario.
- Treating AI and robotics as the same thing.
- Describing an IoT device without saying what data it collects, where it sends the data, or what action follows.
- Saying 3D printing is "cheap" without the condition: cheap for one-off, customised items, not for mass production.
- Forgetting that 4th generation optical storage is still largely in development, so cost and availability are real limits.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between augmented reality and virtual reality?**
Augmented reality overlays computer images on a live view of the real world, such as directions over a camera view. Virtual reality creates a fully computer-generated, immersive 3D world, usually viewed through a headset.

**What is the difference between computer-assisted translation and machine translation?**
In computer-assisted translation, a human translator does the translating, helped by software such as a translation memory and terminology database. In machine translation, the software produces the translation on its own.

**Which emerging technologies are in the Cambridge 9626 syllabus?**
Section 13.1 lists artificial intelligence, augmented and virtual reality, robotics, computer-assisted translation, holographic imaging and storage, 3D printing, vision enhancement, wearable computing, blockchain, the Internet of Things, molecular data storage and autonomous transport, and every listed item must be studied.

**Is 3D printing always cheap?**
No. 3D printing is cheap for one-off, customised items such as prototypes and prosthetics, but slow for mass production, so a strong answer states that condition.
