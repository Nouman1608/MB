This guide covers section **3.6.4 Analysing the financial performance of a business** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 6, Finance. The specification asks you to understand the **purpose of financial statements**; to identify the main components of the **income statement** and the **statement of financial position**; to understand the difference between **assets and liabilities** and that the statement of financial position is a **snapshot in time**; to make judgements on performance by interpreting income statements, considering **current performance, performance against previous years, performance against competitors and the perspective of a range of stakeholders**; and to **calculate gross profit margin and net profit margin**. Formulae are not given in the exam. Question papers use the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) terms, with an alternative in brackets where appropriate, for example Statement of Financial Position (Balance Sheet). All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Finance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-finance/). Questions on this subtopic are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-analysing-the-financial-performance-of-a-business-practice/), and condensed notes are in the [Finance revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-finance-revision-notes/).

## What are financial statements for?

**Financial statements** are formal summaries of a business's finances. They are produced at least once a year. They show how much the business earned, what it owns and what it owes.

| Users | What they want to know |
|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Is the business profitable? Is it worth keeping their money in it? |
| Managers | How is the business performing? Where can costs be cut? |
| Employees | Is the business secure? Can it afford pay rises? |
| Lenders and banks | Can the business repay a loan? |
| Suppliers | Will the business pay its bills? |
| Government | How much tax is owed? |
| Potential investors | Is it a good investment compared with others? |

They help in assessing performance, making business decisions, applying for loans, and attracting investors.

## The income statement

The **income statement** (in older terms, the profit and loss account) shows the revenue, costs and profit of a business over a period, usually one year. It records **flows** of money during the year.

### Main components

| Item | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Total sales for the year |
| Cost of sales | Direct cost of the goods sold (materials, goods bought for resale) |
| Gross profit | Revenue minus cost of sales |
| Expenses | Running costs not directly tied to production: wages, rent, advertising, utilities |
| Net profit (profit for the year) | Gross profit minus expenses |

```
Gross profit = revenue - cost of sales
Net profit   = gross profit - expenses
```

**Worked example 1: an income statement.**

| | Year 1 ($) | Year 2 ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 500,000 | 560,000 |
| Cost of sales | 300,000 | 364,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **200,000** | **196,000** |
| Expenses | 150,000 | 140,000 |
| **Net profit** | **50,000** | **56,000** |

Year 1: gross profit = 500,000 - 300,000 = 200,000; net profit = 200,000 - 150,000 = 50,000.
Year 2: gross profit = 560,000 - 364,000 = 196,000; net profit = 196,000 - 140,000 = 56,000.

## The statement of financial position

The **statement of financial position** (balance sheet) shows what the business owns, what it owes and what it is worth **on one date**, usually the last day of the financial year. It is a **snapshot in time**: a week later the figures will be different. This differs from the income statement, which covers a period.

### Assets

**Assets** are things the business owns that have value.

- **Non-current assets** (fixed assets) are kept for more than a year: buildings, machinery, vehicles, equipment.
- **Current assets** are expected to be turned into cash within a year: inventories (stock), receivables (money owed by customers) and cash.

### Liabilities

**Liabilities** are amounts the business owes to others.

- **Current liabilities** must be paid within a year: payables (money owed to suppliers), overdrafts, tax due.
- **Non-current liabilities** are due after more than a year: long-term bank loans, mortgages.

### Equity

**Equity** (owners' capital) is the owners' stake in the business: money they put in plus profit kept in the business (retained earnings).

```
Net assets = total assets - total liabilities
Equity     = net assets
```

**Worked example 2: a statement of financial position.**

| Item | $ |
|---|---|
| Non-current assets | 180,000 |
| Inventories | 30,000 |
| Receivables | 20,000 |
| Cash | 10,000 |
| **Total assets** | **240,000** |
| Current liabilities | 35,000 |
| Non-current liabilities (bank loan) | 85,000 |
| **Total liabilities** | **120,000** |
| **Net assets** (240,000 - 120,000) | **120,000** |
| Equity: share capital 60,000 + retained earnings 60,000 | **120,000** |

Current assets are 30,000 + 20,000 + 10,000 = 60,000, which is more than current liabilities of 35,000, so the business should be able to pay its short-term debts. The statement balances: net assets equal equity.

## Profit margins

Profit figures on their own are hard to judge. A profit of $50,000 is excellent on revenue of $200,000 but poor on revenue of $5 million. **Profit margins** express profit as a percentage of revenue, so businesses of different sizes can be compared.

```
Gross profit margin (%) = gross profit / revenue x 100
Net profit margin (%)   = net profit / revenue x 100
```

**Worked example 3: calculating margins.**

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 200,000 / 500,000 x 100 = 40% | 196,000 / 560,000 x 100 = 35% |
| Net profit margin | 50,000 / 500,000 x 100 = 10% | 56,000 / 560,000 x 100 = 10% |

Formulae are not given in the exam, so learn them.

## Interpreting performance

The specification asks you to consider performance from four angles.

### 1. Current performance

Look at the current figures: is the business profitable? Are the margins sensible? In Year 2, net profit of $56,000 and a net margin of 10% show a healthy profit.

### 2. Performance against previous years

Compare years to spot trends.

- Revenue rose from $500,000 to $560,000: (560,000 - 500,000) / 500,000 x 100 = **12%**.
- Net profit rose from $50,000 to $56,000: also **12%**, so the net margin stayed at 10%.
- Gross profit fell from $200,000 to $196,000, and gross margin fell from 40% to 35%. Cost of sales rose faster (21.3%) than revenue (12%): (364,000 - 300,000) / 300,000 x 100 = 21.3%.
- Expenses fell from $150,000 to $140,000, a drop of 6.7%.

The fall in gross margin shows that the direct cost of goods increased. The business probably paid more for materials or cut its prices. The business protected its net profit margin by cutting expenses, but this might not continue.

### 3. Performance against competitors

Suppose a competitor reports a gross margin of 38% and a net margin of 8%.

| | This business (Year 2) | Competitor |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 35% | 38% |
| Net profit margin | 10% | 8% |

This business makes less gross profit on each sale, perhaps due to lower prices or higher costs of materials, but controls its expenses better, as its net margin is higher. Comparisons are only fair for similar businesses, in the same industry and of similar size.

### 4. The perspective of different stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Likely view of the Year 2 results |
|---|---|
| Shareholders | Pleased with 12% profit growth, but worried the gross margin is falling |
| Employees | May expect a pay rise after higher profit, but cuts to expenses might mean fewer staff or lower pay |
| Lenders | Want to see that profit covers interest and that the business can repay its loan |
| Customers | Interested in prices; falling margin may mean lower prices, which they like |
| Suppliers | Want to be paid on time; a healthy profit is reassuring |

## Limits of financial statements

- They are **historic**: they show what happened, not what will happen.
- They do not show **non-financial** factors, such as staff morale or customer satisfaction.
- Figures can be **affected by one-off events**.
- **Different accounting choices** may make comparison with other businesses difficult.
- They may not show **inflation**, so a rise in revenue may be smaller in real terms.
- Margins alone do not show **cash flow**.

A full judgement uses the statements together with market information and other data.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing gross profit (revenue minus cost of sales) with net profit (after expenses).
- Dividing by cost of sales or by expenses instead of revenue when calculating a margin.
- Treating the statement of financial position as covering a year. It is a snapshot at one date.
- Mixing up assets (owned) and liabilities (owed).
- Saying a higher profit always means better performance. Check the margin and the trend.
- Failing to compare with the previous year or a competitor, and not considering the stakeholder.

## Exam technique for this section

For **identify** and **state** questions, name the item and which statement it is in. For **calculate** questions, write the formula, then the substitution, then the answer with a percentage sign. For **analyse** questions, compare two years and say what changed and a possible reason. For **evaluate** questions, such as "has the business performed well?", use the four angles (current, past, competitors, stakeholders), include figures, and make a clear overall judgement, noting what further information you would want.
