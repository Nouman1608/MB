## Section A

**1.** Define the term *ionic bonding*. **[2]**

**2.** Give the full electron configuration of (a) a sodium ion and (b) a chloride ion. **[2]**

**3.** Deduce the formula of the ionic compound formed between aluminium and sulfur. Show how you used the ion charges. **[2]**

**4.** State the number of oppositely charged ions that surround (a) each Na⁺ ion in sodium chloride and (b) each Ca²⁺ ion in calcium fluoride. **[2]**

**5.** The ions O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ all have the same number of electrons. State this number, and list the four ions in order of decreasing radius. **[2]**

**6.** Lithium forms an ion with the configuration 1s². A student states that "all ions have eight electrons in their outer shell". Use lithium to explain why this is incorrect. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Calcium fluoride is an ionic compound.

**(a)** Describe how calcium atoms and fluorine atoms form the ions in calcium fluoride. Give the electron configuration of each ion. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why the formula is CaF₂ and not CaF. **[1]**

**(c)** Describe the forces holding the ions together in the solid. **[2]**

**8.** Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C. Magnesium oxide melts at about 2850 °C. Both have the same arrangement of ions in their lattices.

**(a)** Explain the difference in melting points. **[3]**

**(b)** A student writes: "Magnesium oxide has stronger intermolecular forces than sodium chloride." Identify two errors in this statement. **[2]**

**9.** Pauling electronegativity values for some elements are: Li 1.0, Mg 1.3, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0.

**(a)** Use the values to predict whether the bonding in lithium fluoride is ionic or covalent. Explain your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** Predict, with a reason, which of the pairs Mg–O and C–O is more likely to form an ionic bond. **[1]**

**10.** Magnesium burns in air to form a mixture of magnesium oxide and magnesium nitride.

**(a)** Give the electron configuration of a nitride ion, N³⁻. **[1]**

**(b)** Deduce the formula of magnesium nitride. **[1]**

**(c)** State the total number of electrons transferred when one formula unit of magnesium nitride forms, and from which atoms to which atoms. **[2]**

**11.** Potassium sulfide, K₂S, is an ionic solid.

**(a)** Describe the bonding in potassium sulfide. Your answer should refer to the electrons transferred, the ions formed and the type of attraction. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why it is incorrect to describe solid potassium sulfide as being made of K₂S molecules. **[2]**

**12.** The ions in calcium oxide, CaO, and in potassium fluoride, KF, have similar sizes.

**(a)** Predict which compound has the stronger attraction between its ions. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain your prediction. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Electrostatic attraction [1] between oppositely charged ions (cations and anions) [1].

**2. (a)** Na⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ [1].

**(b)** Cl⁻: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ [1].

**3.** Al³⁺ and S²⁻; two Al³⁺ give +6 and three S²⁻ give −6 [1], so the formula is Al₂S₃ [1].

**4. (a)** Six Cl⁻ ions [1].

**(b)** Eight F⁻ ions [1].

**5.** Ten electrons [1]. Order of decreasing radius: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ [1].

**6.** Li⁺ has only two electrons, in the first shell (the helium configuration) [1]. Ions reach a noble-gas configuration, which is not always an octet [1].

**7. (a)** Each calcium atom loses its two 4s electrons [1], one to each of two fluorine atoms [1]. Ca²⁺ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ [1] and F⁻ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ [1].

**(b)** One Ca²⁺ (+2) needs two F⁻ (−1 each) to balance the charge [1].

**(c)** Electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged Ca²⁺ and F⁻ ions [1], acting in all directions throughout a giant lattice [1].

**8. (a)** Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have double the charge of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ [1]. Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ are smaller than Na⁺ and Cl⁻, so the ions are closer together [1]. The electrostatic attraction between the ions in MgO is therefore much stronger and needs more energy to overcome [1].

**(b)** Magnesium oxide does not contain molecules, so there are no intermolecular forces [1]; melting overcomes the electrostatic attraction between ions (ionic bonding) [1].

**9. (a)** The difference is 4.0 − 1.0 = 3.0, which is large [1], so fluorine attracts the electron almost completely and the bonding is ionic (Li⁺ and F⁻) [1].

**(b)** Mg–O: its electronegativity difference (2.2) is much larger than for C–O (1.0) [1].

**10. (a)** 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ [1].

**(b)** Mg₃N₂ [1].

**(c)** Six electrons [1], two from each of three magnesium atoms, shared out as three to each of two nitrogen atoms [1].

**11. (a)** Each of two potassium atoms loses its single 4s electron to the same sulfur atom [1]. This forms two K⁺ ions and one S²⁻ ion (each with the argon configuration) [1]. The ions are held by electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions [1].

**(b)** Solid potassium sulfide is a giant lattice in which each ion is attracted to all the oppositely charged ions around it [1]; K₂S is only the simplest ratio of ions, not a discrete particle [1].

**12. (a)** Calcium oxide [1].

**(b)** Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ carry double charges, whereas K⁺ and F⁻ carry single charges [1]. With ions of similar size, higher charges give a stronger electrostatic attraction between the ions [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining an ionic bond as "electron transfer" instead of the attraction between ions.
- Writing configurations as 2,8,8 when sub-shell notation is expected.
- Describing CaF₂ as calcium giving two electrons to one fluorine atom.
- Using "intermolecular forces" or "molecules" for ionic compounds.
- Explaining lattice strength with charge alone, without ionic radius, or without saying the attraction is between ions.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name the arrangement of ions in calcium fluoride?**
You are not asked to name lattice types for CaF₂. Knowing that the ions are in a 1 : 2 ratio, with each calcium ion surrounded by fluoride ions, is enough to describe the bonding.
