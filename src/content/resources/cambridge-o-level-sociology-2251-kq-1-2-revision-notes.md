These notes condense key question 1.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What types of data and
evidence do sociologists use?* Data is classified by who produced it (primary or secondary) and by
its form (quantitative or qualitative). Five kinds of secondary evidence are named in the syllabus,
each with typical strengths and limitations, and you must be able to read and criticise sources
containing tables, charts, graphs, diagrams and summaries of studies.

Fuller explanations: [key question 1.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-sociology-2251-kq-1-2/).

## Syllabus coverage

2251 syllabus 2025–2027, **1.2**: 1.2.1 sources of data (primary and secondary; qualitative and
quantitative; strengths and limitations); 1.2.2 secondary evidence (official statistics, historical
and personal documents, digital sources, media content); 1.2.3 analysis, interpretation and
evaluation of summaries of studies, diagrams, tables, charts and graphs.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Primary data** | Collected first-hand by the researcher for the current study |
| **Secondary data** | Already exists, produced by others for a different purpose |
| **Quantitative data** | Numerical data that can be counted and compared |
| **Qualitative data** | Words, images or sound describing meanings and experiences |
| **Official statistics** | Numerical data produced by governments and their agencies |
| **Census** | A count of a whole population, usually by the state |
| **Dark figure** | Crime or other activity that never appears in official records |
| **Social construction** | The idea that data reflects decisions made by those who record it |
| **Historical document** | Record produced in the past, such as court files or old newspapers |
| **Personal document** | Diary, letter, autobiography or blog written by an individual |
| **Digital source** | Online material such as posts, forums, websites and search data |
| **Content analysis** | Systematic counting and categorising of media content |
| **Percentage point** | The unit for the difference between two percentages |

## The two classifications

| | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Researcher's own questionnaire with closed questions | Researcher's own unstructured interviews or observation notes |
| **Secondary** | Census, school results, recorded crime | Diaries, letters, newspaper articles, social media posts |

- **Primary — strengths:** designed for the aims; current; researcher knows how it was collected.
  **Limitations:** costly and slow; access may be refused.
- **Secondary — strengths:** cheap and quick; may cover huge populations or the past.
  **Limitations:** not designed for the aims; definitions may not match; accuracy unknown.
- **Quantitative — strengths:** comparisons, patterns and trends; reliable; can be generalised from
  large samples. **Limitations:** little depth; imposes the researcher's categories.
- **Qualitative — strengths:** depth and validity; shows meanings. **Limitations:** hard to compare;
  small samples; interpretation can be subjective.

## Scott's four criteria (1990)

Use these to evaluate any document: **authenticity** (genuine?), **credibility** (sincere and
accurate?), **representativeness** (typical, and enough surviving?), **meaning** (can we understand
it?).

## Secondary evidence at a glance

| Type | One strength | One limitation | Linked study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Official statistics | National coverage; trends over time | Definitions change; dark figure; socially constructed | Durkheim, *Suicide* (1897), used them; Atkinson (1978) criticised them |
| Historical documents | Only window on the past | Survival favours the literate and powerful | Thomas and Znaniecki used migrants' letters (1918–1920) |
| Personal documents | Rich insight into feelings | Writers may exaggerate; unrepresentative | Diaries and autobiographies |
| Digital sources | Huge, current, reach hidden groups | Fake or anonymous accounts; users not representative | Forums, posts, search data |
| Media content | Shows representations; cheap | Shaped by owners and editors; says little about audiences | Glasgow University Media Group, *Bad News* (1976) |

## Reading data: the routine

1. Title, source, date.
2. Units: numbers, percentages, rates?
3. Overall pattern first.
4. Exact figures second, with units.
5. A problem tied to *this* source: sample, missing groups, definitions, self-reporting, correlation
   presented as cause.

**For summaries of studies:** underline method, sample, findings and the detail that signals a
problem (where the sample came from, how the researcher gained access, who was left out).

## Exam traps

- A researcher's own survey is **primary**, even though it is numerical.
- Stating "the data might be biased" without saying *how* earns little; name the mechanism.
- "%" versus "percentage points": 30% to 45% is a rise of 15 percentage points.
- Graphs with a cut-off vertical axis make small changes look large; check where the axis starts.
- Media content shows what was *published*, not what audiences *believe*.
- Official statistics on crime show recorded crime, not all crime.

## Worked example: a 4-mark "explain two problems" answer

*Source summary (invented for practice): a researcher studied attitudes to climate change by
analysing 1,000 comments posted under online news articles.*

- Commenters are not representative of the population: older people and those without internet
  access are under-represented, so the findings cannot be generalised (2).
- People may post deliberately extreme or insincere comments, and some accounts may be fake, so the
  data lacks credibility and validity (2).

## Self-test

1. Give one example of secondary qualitative data.
2. Why is a researcher's own closed-question survey primary rather than secondary data?
3. What is meant by the dark figure of crime?
4. Name Scott's four criteria for judging documents.
5. Give one reason why official statistics on unemployment may not be comparable over time.
6. Give one strength of historical documents.
7. Why might personal documents lack credibility?
8. Give two reasons why digital sources may be unrepresentative.
9. What is content analysis?
10. A figure rises from 20% to 30%. By how many percentage points has it risen?
11. Why should you describe the overall pattern before quoting figures?
12. Give one ethical issue in using public social media posts.

### Answers

1. A diary, a letter, a newspaper article or a social media post.
2. It was collected by the researcher for this study; being numerical makes it quantitative, not
   secondary.
3. Crime that is never reported to or recorded by the police, so it is missing from the statistics.
4. Authenticity, credibility, representativeness and meaning.
5. Governments can change the definition of who counts as unemployed.
6. It may be the only evidence about the past and allows comparison with today.
7. Writers may exaggerate, forget or present themselves favourably.
8. Internet access varies by age, income and region; people choose what to post; some accounts are
   fake or automated.
9. Systematic counting and categorising of what appears in media content.
10. 10 percentage points (a rise of 50% in relative terms).
11. Examiners credit identifying the trend; isolated figures do not show a pattern.
12. Users have not given informed consent to being studied, and quoting posts may identify them.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is official statistics a positivist or interpretivist source?** Positivists use it as objective
evidence of patterns; interpretivists study it as a social construction.

**Can secondary data be used in part (e)?** Yes. The question asks for primary methods, sampling and
secondary evidence, so name one secondary source and justify it.
