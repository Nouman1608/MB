## Section A

**1.** State **two** items that are included in cost of sales. **[2]**

**2.** A trader had inventory of 8,400 at the start of the year. During the year purchases were 52,300, carriage inwards was 1,100 and purchases returns were 1,900. Closing inventory was 9,700. Calculate cost of sales. **[3]**

**3.** Revenue was 91,000 and sales returns were 2,000. Using the cost of sales from Question 2, calculate gross profit. **[2]**

**4.** Identify where each item appears in the financial statements of a sole trader.

**(a)** Discount received **[1]**

**(b)** Carriage outwards **[1]**

**(c)** Drawings **[1]**

**(d)** Closing inventory **[1]**

**5.** Explain the difference between gross profit and profit for the year. **[2]**

## Section B

The balances below were taken from the books of Danvers Bakery, owned by Ines Danvers, at 30 June 2026. Closing inventory was 7,600. Capital at 1 July 2025 was 73,580.

Revenue 148,600; sales returns 2,300; inventory at 1 July 2025 6,800; purchases 71,400; purchases returns 1,500; carriage inwards 900; wages 29,800; rent 7,200; insurance 2,100; light and heat 3,900; general expenses 3,650; carriage outwards 1,250; discount allowed 520; discount received 640; interest received 600; shop premises 60,000; ovens at cost 22,000; provision for depreciation: ovens 8,800; trade receivables 9,100; trade payables 7,300; bank 4,800; cash 300; drawings 15,000.

**6.** Prepare the statement of profit or loss of Danvers Bakery for the year ended 30 June 2026. **[11]**

**7.** Prepare the statement of financial position of Danvers Bakery at 30 June 2026. **[10]**

**8.** A sole trader began the year with capital of 40,000. During the year she made a profit of 18,500, introduced a further 5,000 of her own money and took drawings of 12,200. Calculate her capital at the end of the year. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why drawings are not shown as an expense in the statement of profit or loss. **[2]**

**10.** Using your answer to Question 7, state the working capital of Danvers Bakery and comment on what it shows. **[4]**

**11.** Ines revalues the shop premises from 60,000 to 72,000. Explain where the gain is shown and how it affects the statement of financial position. **[3]**

**12.** Calculate, to one decimal place, gross profit as a percentage of net revenue and profit for the year as a percentage of net revenue for Danvers Bakery. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: opening inventory [1]; purchases [1]; carriage inwards [1]; less purchases returns [1]; less closing inventory [1].

**2.** Net purchases 52,300 + 1,100 − 1,900 = 51,500 [1]; goods available 8,400 + 51,500 = 59,900 [1]; less closing inventory 9,700 = **50,200** [1].

**3.** Net revenue 91,000 − 2,000 = 89,000 [1]; gross profit 89,000 − 50,200 = **38,800** [1].

**4. (a)** Statement of profit or loss, as other income (added after gross profit) [1]

**(b)** Statement of profit or loss, as an expense (a selling cost) [1]

**(c)** Statement of financial position, deducted in the capital section [1]

**(d)** Both: deducted in cost of sales and shown as a current asset [1]

**5.** Gross profit is net revenue less cost of sales, so it measures the profit on trading before running costs [1]. Profit for the year is gross profit plus other income less all expenses [1].

**8.** 40,000 + 18,500 + 5,000 [1] − 12,200 [1] = **51,300** [1].

**9.** Drawings are the owner taking money or goods out for personal use [1]; they are not a cost of running the business, so they reduce capital rather than profit [1].

**10.** Working capital is current assets 21,800 less current liabilities 7,300 = **14,500** [1]. Comment: current assets cover trade payables about three times (21,800 ÷ 7,300 = 2.99) [1], so the business can pay its short-term debts [1]; some of the current assets are inventory, which may take time to turn into cash, so the true cover is lower [1].

**11.** The gain of 12,000 (72,000 − 60,000) [1] is not profit for the year; it is shown as other comprehensive income [1] and the premises are shown at 72,000, which increases net assets and the owner's capital by 12,000 [1].

**12.** Gross profit percentage: 76,300 ÷ 146,300 × 100 [1] = **52.2%** [1]. Profit for the year percentage: 29,120 ÷ 146,300 × 100 [1] = **19.9%** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Closing inventory deducted in cost of sales but left out of the current assets (Questions 6 and 7).
- Carriage inwards put with expenses, which understates gross profit.
- Drawings deducted from profit in the statement of profit or loss (Question 9).
- Dividing by revenue instead of net revenue in percentages (Question 12).
- A statement of financial position that does not agree and is forced with a made-up figure.

## Approaching these questions

Sort every balance first: income, cost of sales, expenses, assets, liabilities and owner's interest. Build cost of sales line by line, then gross profit, then add other income and deduct expenses. For the statement of financial position, work out net book values first, then working capital, then the capital section. Check that net assets equal closing capital.
