## Definitions (2.2.2 and 2.2.3)

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Monomer | Small molecule that joins with others like it to form a polymer |
| Polymer | Large molecule made of many repeating monomer units |
| Macromolecule | Very large biological molecule (polysaccharide, protein, nucleic acid) |
| Monosaccharide | Single sugar unit, e.g. glucose, fructose |
| Disaccharide | Two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond, e.g. maltose, sucrose |
| Polysaccharide | Many monosaccharides joined by glycosidic bonds, e.g. starch, glycogen, cellulose |

**Role of covalent bonds:** they join monomers together to make polymers. Glycosidic bonds (sugars) and ester bonds (lipids) are covalent. They are strong and need hydrolysis to break.

Triglycerides are large but **not polymers** (no repeating monomer chain).

## Glucose rings (2.2.1)

- Ring = **5 C + 1 O**. Carbon 6 is the CH₂OH outside the ring, on carbon 5.
- Ring OH pattern (Haworth view): C2 below, C3 above, C4 below.
- **Carbon 1 decides the form:** OH **below** = **α**; OH **above** = **β**.
- In β-glucose, the C1 OH is on the same side of the ring as the CH₂OH.

## Reducing and non-reducing sugars (2.2.4)

| Sugar | Type | Reducing? |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Monosaccharide | Yes |
| Fructose | Monosaccharide | Yes |
| Maltose | Disaccharide (α-glucose + α-glucose) | Yes |
| Sucrose | Disaccharide (α-glucose + fructose) | **No** |

## Condensation and hydrolysis (2.2.5 and 2.2.6)

**Method in steps: forming a glycosidic bond (condensation)**

1. OH on carbon 1 of one sugar lines up with an OH on the other sugar.
2. H from one OH and the whole OH from the other leave as **H₂O**.
3. The sugars are left joined by a C–O–C **glycosidic bond**.

**Method in steps: breaking it (hydrolysis)**

1. **Water** is added across the glycosidic bond.
2. The bond breaks; each sugar gets back an OH group.
3. In cells, enzymes catalyse this; in the lab, boiling with dilute HCl does it.

| Product | Monomers | Bond |
|---|---|---|
| Maltose | α-glucose + α-glucose | α-1,4 glycosidic |
| Sucrose | α-glucose + fructose | 1,2 glycosidic |
| Amylose | α-glucose | α-1,4 |
| Amylopectin, glycogen | α-glucose | α-1,4 chains, α-1,6 branch points |
| Cellulose | β-glucose | β-1,4 |

**Counting rule:** an unbranched chain of n monomers has n − 1 bonds, so n − 1 waters released on making it and n − 1 used to fully hydrolyse it.

*Worked reminder:* an amylose chain of 300 glucose units forms by **299** condensation reactions.

**Non-reducing sugar test in steps**

1. Benedict's test on the sample: stays **blue** (no reducing sugar).
2. Fresh sample + dilute HCl, heat in a boiling water bath: hydrolyses the glycosidic bond.
3. Cool and **neutralise** with sodium hydrogencarbonate.
4. Benedict's again and heat: **orange-red / brick-red precipitate** = non-reducing sugar was present.

## Polysaccharides (2.2.7 and 2.2.8)

| | Amylose | Amylopectin | Glycogen | Cellulose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monomer | α-glucose | α-glucose | α-glucose | β-glucose |
| Branched? | No | Yes | Yes, more than amylopectin | No |
| Shape | Helix | Branched | Compact, highly branched | Straight chains |
| Role | Plant store | Plant store | Animal and fungal store | Plant cell wall |

**Storage polysaccharides: structure → function**

- Compact (coils, branches) → lots of glucose in small space.
- Insoluble → no effect on water potential; stays in the cell.
- Branches → many chain ends → enzymes release glucose quickly (glycogen fastest).
- Glycosidic bonds hydrolysed easily by enzymes when energy is needed.

**Cellulose: arrangement → cell wall function**

- β-glucose: every other unit rotated 180° → straight chain.
- Parallel chains linked by many **hydrogen bonds** → **microfibrils** → **fibres**.
- Fibres in layers in a matrix → **high tensile strength**: wall resists bursting when water enters by osmosis (cell becomes turgid).
- Gaps between fibres → wall is **freely permeable**.

## Triglycerides (2.2.9 and 2.2.10)

- **1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids**, joined by **3 ester bonds**, releasing **3 H₂O** (condensation).
- **Non-polar and hydrophobic.** Insoluble in water.
- **Saturated** fatty acid: no C=C, straight chain, formula CₙH₂ₙO₂.
- **Unsaturated** fatty acid: one or more C=C; each double bond = kink, and 2 fewer H atoms.

*Worked reminder:* tripalmitin = glycerol (Mr 92) + 3 palmitic acid (Mr 256) − 3 water: 92 + 768 − 54 = **806**.

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| Many C–H bonds, little O | More energy per gram than carbohydrate |
| Insoluble | Large stores with no osmotic effect |
| Low density | Buoyancy |
| Poor heat conductor | Insulation (fat under skin, blubber) |
| Oxidation produces water | Metabolic water |
| Cushioning layers | Protection of organs |

## Phospholipids (2.2.11)

- **Glycerol + 2 fatty acids** (ester bonds) + **phosphate group**.
- Phosphate head: charged, **polar, hydrophilic**.
- Fatty acid tails: **non-polar, hydrophobic**.
- Bilayer formation is Topic 4: [Cell membranes and transport revision notes](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-cell-membranes-and-transport-revision-notes/).

## Drawing and calculation checklists

**Drawing a glucose ring (check every box)**

- Hexagon with the O in the top right corner.
- Carbons 1 to 5 in the ring, numbered clockwise from the right-hand corner.
- CH₂OH (carbon 6) above carbon 5.
- An H and an OH on each of carbons 1 to 4, on opposite sides of the ring.
- C1 OH placed correctly for the form asked (α below, β above).

**Drawing a glycosidic bond**

- Show both rings, with only one O bridging carbon 1 of the first sugar and the correct carbon of the second.
- Show H₂O leaving (condensation) or joining (hydrolysis) with an arrow.
- Label the bond "glycosidic" and give its number, e.g. α-1,4.

**Mr method in steps**

1. Add the Mr values of all the monomers.
2. Count the bonds formed (n − 1 for an unbranched chain; 3 ester bonds for a triglyceride).
3. Subtract 18 for each bond.

*Worked reminder:* maltose = 180 + 180 − 18 = **342**.

## Must-know distinctions

| Don't confuse | Difference |
|---|---|
| α-glucose vs β-glucose | C1 OH below vs above the ring |
| Condensation vs hydrolysis | Releases water to make a bond vs uses water to break one |
| Glycosidic vs ester bond | Sugar to sugar vs fatty acid to glycerol |
| Amylose vs amylopectin | Unbranched helix, α-1,4 only vs branched, α-1,4 and α-1,6 |
| Amylopectin vs glycogen | Plants, fewer branches vs animals, more branches |
| Starch vs cellulose | α-glucose, energy store vs β-glucose, structural |
| Bonds within vs between cellulose chains | Glycosidic within; hydrogen bonds between |
| Triglyceride vs phospholipid | 3 fatty acids, wholly hydrophobic vs 2 fatty acids + phosphate, hydrophilic head |
| Saturated vs unsaturated | No C=C vs at least one C=C |

## Quick self-test

1. How many carbon atoms are in the ring of a glucose molecule?
2. Which carbon atom differs between α-glucose and β-glucose, and how?
3. Name the two monosaccharides that make up sucrose.
4. Which of these are reducing sugars: maltose, sucrose, fructose?
5. How many water molecules are released when a chain of 50 glucose units is made?
6. Name the glycosidic bonds at the branch points of glycogen.
7. Give two reasons why glycogen is a better energy store than glucose.
8. What holds neighbouring cellulose molecules together in a microfibril?
9. Why must the acid be neutralised in the non-reducing sugar test?
10. A fatty acid has the formula C₁₈H₃₂O₂. How many C=C double bonds does it have?
11. How many ester bonds are there in one phospholipid molecule, linking fatty acids to glycerol?
12. Explain why a triglyceride is described as hydrophobic.

### Answers

1. **Five** (the sixth ring atom is oxygen).
2. **Carbon 1**: OH below the ring in α, above in β.
3. **Glucose and fructose.**
4. **Maltose and fructose.** Sucrose is non-reducing.
5. **49** (n − 1).
6. **α-1,6** glycosidic bonds.
7. Any two: insoluble, so no effect on water potential; compact; does not leave the cell; branched with many ends for rapid hydrolysis.
8. **Hydrogen bonds.**
9. Benedict's solution only works in **alkaline** conditions.
10. **Two.** Saturated C₁₈ would be C₁₈H₃₆O₂; each C=C removes 2 H, and 36 − 32 = 4 H, so 2 double bonds.
11. **Two.**
12. It is **non-polar** overall: its long hydrocarbon tails cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, so it does not mix with or dissolve in water.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting six carbons in the glucose ring, or forgetting the CH₂OH on carbon 5.
- Drawing α-glucose with the C1 OH above the ring.
- Saying hydrolysis "releases water" or condensation "uses water".
- Writing "glucose" without α or β when the question asks about a specific polysaccharide.
- Saying cellulose molecules are joined to each other by glycosidic bonds.
- Writing that starch is "a branched molecule". Only amylopectin branches; amylose does not.
- Missing the neutralisation step in the non-reducing sugar test.
- Giving "fats are an energy store" without the reason (more C–H bonds per gram, more energy released per gram).
- Saying triglycerides are polar or that phospholipid tails are hydrophilic.
- Calling a triglyceride a polymer.
