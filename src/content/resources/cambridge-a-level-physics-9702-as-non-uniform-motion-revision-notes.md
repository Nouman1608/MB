Condensed for the final weeks. For the full explanation and worked examples, use the [Non-uniform motion study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-non-uniform-motion/); for exam-style questions, the [Non-uniform motion practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-non-uniform-motion-practice/). These notes cover subtopic **3.2 Non-uniform motion** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series.

## What the syllabus asks for (all qualitative)

- Friction and viscous/drag forces, including air resistance. No coefficients of friction or viscosity.
- Simple model: **drag increases as speed increases**.
- Motion in a uniform gravitational field **with** air resistance.
- Terminal (constant) velocity against a resistive force.

## Resistive forces at a glance

| Force | Where it acts | Direction | Depends on speed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Friction | Between surfaces in contact | Parallel to surfaces, opposing relative motion | Not in this model |
| Drag / viscous force | Object moving through a fluid | Opposite to velocity relative to the fluid | Yes: increases with speed |
| Air resistance | Drag in air | Opposite to velocity | Yes |

## The falling-object chain (learn the order)

1. Released from rest: drag = 0, resultant = weight, a = g.
2. Speed increases, so drag increases.
3. Resultant force (W − D) decreases, so acceleration decreases.
4. Drag rises until D = W.
5. Resultant = 0, a = 0: **terminal velocity**, constant speed.

## Velocity-time graph features

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Starts at origin, gradient g | Only weight acts at release |
| Gradient decreasing | Resultant force decreasing as drag rises |
| Horizontal line | Terminal velocity, a = 0 |
| Area under graph | Distance fallen |
| Free-fall line (no drag) | Straight, gradient g, above the curve |

## Parachute sequence

| Event | Drag vs weight | Acceleration | Velocity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Terminal velocity before opening | D = W | 0 | Constant, high |
| Parachute opens | D ≫ W | Large, **upwards** | Still downwards, decreasing |
| Slowing | D decreasing towards W | Upwards, decreasing | Decreasing |
| New terminal velocity | D = W | 0 | Constant, lower |

**Recall example:** 75 kg skydiver (W = 736 N); drag 1500 N when the parachute opens: a = (736 − 1500) ÷ 75 = −10.2 m s⁻², that is, 10.2 m s⁻² upwards.

## Thrown upwards with air resistance

| Stage | Drag direction | Acceleration |
|---|---|---|
| Rising | Down (with weight) | Greater than g |
| At the top | Zero (v = 0) | Exactly g |
| Falling | Up (against weight) | Less than g |

Consequences: lower maximum height; descent takes longer than ascent; returns at lower speed.

## Other terminal-velocity cases

- **Car or cyclist, constant driving force:** top speed when resistive force = driving force.
- **Ball-bearing in oil:** weight = upthrust + viscous drag at terminal velocity; reached quickly because viscous drag in a liquid is large.
- **Same shape, larger mass:** higher terminal velocity, since a larger drag (so a larger speed) is needed to balance the larger weight.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Resistive force | Any force opposing motion: friction, drag, air resistance |
| Viscous force | Drag in a fluid due to its resistance to flow |
| Terminal velocity | Constant velocity reached when the resistive force balances the driving force (or weight) |
| Qualitative | Described in words and trends, without a formula for the force |
| Uniform gravitational field | A field in which g has the same value everywhere, as near Earth's surface |

## Exam traps

- "Acceleration is constant until terminal velocity": it decreases continuously.
- "No forces act at terminal velocity": two forces act and balance.
- "The skydiver goes up when the parachute opens": the velocity stays downwards.
- Drawing the parachute-opening part of a v-t graph as a vertical line: the speed changes quickly but not instantly.
- Leaving out upthrust for an object falling in a liquid.
- Writing a formula for drag where only the qualitative model is expected.
- Forgetting that drag points down while a thrown ball rises.

## Self-test

1. What simple model of drag does the syllabus require?
2. What is the acceleration of a falling object at the instant it is released?
3. Why does the acceleration of a falling object decrease?
4. State the condition for terminal velocity of a falling object in air.
5. What does the gradient of a velocity-time graph represent?
6. A 60 kg skydiver experiences 300 N of drag. Find the acceleration.
7. In which direction is the acceleration just after a parachute opens?
8. Why is the new terminal velocity lower after the parachute opens?
9. A ball is thrown upwards with air resistance. Is its acceleration while rising greater or less than g?
10. Why does the ball take longer to fall than to rise?
11. A car's driving force is 3000 N. At what value of the resistive force does it reach its top speed?
12. What three forces act on a ball-bearing falling through oil?

### Answers

1. Drag increases as speed increases.
2. g (9.81 m s⁻²), because drag is zero at zero speed.
3. As speed increases, drag increases, so the resultant force (weight − drag) decreases.
4. Drag equals weight, so the resultant force is zero.
5. The acceleration.
6. W = 60 × 9.81 = 589 N; a = (589 − 300) ÷ 60 = 4.8 m s⁻² downwards.
7. Upwards (the skydiver decelerates).
8. The open parachute has a larger drag at any given speed, so drag equals weight at a lower speed.
9. Greater than g, because drag and weight both act downwards.
10. Its downward acceleration is less than g (drag opposes weight), and it reaches a lower maximum speed on the way down.
11. 3000 N.
12. Weight (down), upthrust (up), viscous drag (up).
