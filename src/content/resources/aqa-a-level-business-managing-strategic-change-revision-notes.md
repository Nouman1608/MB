## 3.10.1 Managing change

### Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Internal change | Starts inside the business (restructure, new system, new leader) |
| External change | Starts in the environment (rival, law, technology, tastes) |
| Incremental change | Small, steady steps; lower risk; may be too slow |
| Disruptive change | Sudden and large; forces a fast response |
| Driving force | Pushes for the change |
| Restraining force | Holds the change back |
| Delayering | Removing layers from the hierarchy |
| Mechanistic structure | Rigid hierarchy, narrow tasks, formal rules |
| Organic structure | Flat, flexible, wide roles, shared decisions |

### Force field analysis in steps

1. State the change clearly.
2. List the driving forces and the restraining forces.
3. Score each (for example 1 to 5).
4. Total each side and find the net score.
5. Plan: reduce the strongest restrainers, or strengthen drivers.
6. Judge: the scores are subjective, so say what the result suggests and what could change it.

Reminder: drivers 12 and restrainers 9 give a net of +3. The change is more likely to work, but a single strong restrainer (such as a key group refusing to cooperate) can still stop it.

### The flexible organisation (specification list)

| Feature | Benefit | Cost or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Restructuring | Fits structure to new strategy | Disruption; uncertainty for staff |
| Delayering | Faster communication, lower pay costs, wider spans | Overloaded managers, fewer promotion routes |
| Flexible contracts | Staffing matches demand; lower fixed costs | Lower loyalty, higher turnover |
| Organic structure | Quick response, ideas shared | Less control; roles unclear |
| Mechanistic structure | Clear control; suits stable markets | Slow, rigid in a changing market |
| Knowledge and information management | Better decisions, less duplication, know-how kept | System cost, overload, security risk |

### Kotter and Schlesinger

| Four reasons for resistance | Six ways of overcoming it |
|---|---|
| Parochial self-interest | Education and communication |
| Misunderstanding | Participation and involvement |
| Low tolerance of change | Facilitation and support |
| Different assessments of the situation | Negotiation and agreement |
| | Manipulation and co-optation |
| | Explicit and implicit coercion |

Matching guide: misunderstanding goes with education and communication; self-interest with negotiation or participation; low tolerance with facilitation and support; different assessments with participation. Coercion is fast but damages trust.

## 3.10.2 Organisational culture

**Culture** is the shared values and ways of behaving in a business. It affects motivation, decisions, service and how change is received.

| Handy | One-line description | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|
| Power | One person or small group controls; fast, leader-dependent | Small owner-led business |
| Role | Rules, procedures and defined jobs | Large, stable organisation |
| Task | Teams built around projects and expertise | Project or consultancy work |
| Person | Individuals matter most | Small professional partnership |

**Hofstede's national cultures**: compare countries on dimensions including power distance, individualism versus collectivism, masculinity versus femininity and uncertainty avoidance (later work adds long-term orientation and indulgence). Use it to explain why a management style may not transfer across borders.

**Influences on culture**: founder and leaders, history, size, structure, industry, staff, national background, reward systems, past success.

**Changing culture**
- Reasons: new strategy, merger, poor performance, new technology, mismatch with the market.
- Problems: slow and deep; resistance from long-serving staff; mixed messages; falling morale; staff leave.

## 3.10.3 Strategic implementation

- **Effective implementation**: clear objectives, resources, responsibilities, deadlines, monitoring, fit with structure and culture.
- **Leadership**: sets direction, builds commitment, backs the change visibly. Style should suit urgency and staff.
- **Communications**: clear, consistent, two-way, timely. Reduces misunderstanding and rumour.

| Structure | Grouped by | Plus | Minus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | Activity | Expertise | Departmental silos |
| Product based | Product line | Product focus | Duplication |
| Regional | Area | Local knowledge | Inconsistency |
| Matrix | Function and project | Shared skills | Two bosses |

### Network analysis in steps

1. Draw activities and their order; note durations.
2. Forward pass: earliest start (ES) is the largest earliest finish of the activities that must come before. Earliest finish (EF) is ES plus duration.
3. Project duration is the largest EF at the end.
4. Backward pass: latest finish (LF) of an activity is the smallest latest start of the activities that follow. Latest start (LS) is LF minus duration.
5. Total float = LS − ES (equivalently LF − EF). Zero float means critical.
6. The critical path is the longest route; it joins the zero-float activities.
7. Amend: change a duration or add or remove an activity, then redo steps 2 to 6. A longer non-critical activity may use up its float, or become critical.

**Small reminder.** A (4 weeks, first), B (3, first), C (5, after A), D (2, after B), E (3, after C and D). Forward pass: C finishes at week 9, D at week 5, E starts week 9 and ends week 12. Duration 12 weeks, critical path A, C, E (4 + 5 + 3). B and D each have 4 weeks of float. If D took 6 weeks it would end at week 9, so D would have no float and there would be two critical paths. The project would still take 12 weeks.

Uses: focus control on critical tasks, move resources from tasks with float, estimate the earliest finish. Limit: durations are estimates.

## 3.10.4 Problems with strategy

- **Difficulties**: uncertain information, unpredictable environment, conflicting stakeholder aims, limited resources, bias, long time lags.
- **Planned (deliberate) strategy**: set in advance by analysis; gives direction and control; may be rigid.
- **Emergent strategy**: grows from day-to-day decisions and responses; flexible, uses front-line knowledge; may lack direction.
- **Strategic drift**: strategy slowly moves out of line with the environment, often after success. Causes: complacency, strong culture, slow monitoring.
- **Ownership and control**: in a large company owners (shareholders) and managers differ. Managers may chase their own aims. **Corporate governance** (board structure, independent non-executive directors, committees, transparency, shareholder votes) tries to reduce the gap.
- **Evaluating strategic performance**: compare results with objectives, past results and rivals; use financial and non-financial measures; find the cause of any gap.
- **Strategic planning**: direction, alignment, resource guide. Costs time; can be rigid; depends on forecasts.
- **Contingency planning**: ready responses to possible shocks. Faster reaction, less panic; costs time and cannot cover everything.

## Must-know distinctions

- Internal v external change (where it starts) and incremental v disruptive (speed and size).
- Organic v mechanistic.
- Planned v emergent strategy.
- Ownership v control.
- Float v critical path: float is spare time, the critical path has none.
- Strategic planning (the main plan) v contingency planning (plan B for shocks).

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four types of change in the specification.
2. In a force field analysis, drivers score 4, 3 and 2 and restrainers score 5 and 3. What is the net score?
3. State Kotter and Schlesinger's four reasons for resistance.
4. Which method of overcoming resistance suits staff who simply lack information?
5. Name Handy's four cultures.
6. Which Handy culture fits a firm where one founder makes nearly every decision?
7. Define delayering.
8. A project has activities X (3 weeks, first), Y (6 weeks, first) and Z (2 weeks, after X and Y). Find the duration, the critical path and the float on X.
9. What is strategic drift?
10. What is the aim of corporate governance?

### Answers

1. Internal, external, incremental and disruptive.
2. Drivers 9, restrainers 8, net +1.
3. Parochial self-interest, misunderstanding, low tolerance of change, different assessments of the situation.
4. Education and communication.
5. Power, role, task and person.
6. Power culture.
7. Removing one or more layers from the hierarchy.
8. Z starts when Y ends at week 6 and finishes week 8. Duration 8 weeks, critical path Y then Z, float on X is 3 weeks (6 − 3).
9. A gradual shift of strategy away from what the changing environment needs.
10. To direct and check a company so managers act in the owners' interests, reducing the divorce between ownership and control.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming Lewin or Kotter and Schlesinger without applying them to the case.
- Scoring forces but never comparing the totals or commenting on how subjective they are.
- Recommending a method of overcoming resistance that does not match the reason for resistance shown.
- Describing delayering as only a cost saving and ignoring the workload on remaining managers.
- Treating Handy's cultures as good or bad rather than as a fit or mismatch with the strategy.
- Mixing up float and duration, or giving a critical path as a single activity.
- Forgetting to rework the network after an amendment.
- Writing about strategy generally when asked about planned versus emergent, or ownership versus control.
- Making a one-sided case when asked to evaluate, with no judgement at the end.
