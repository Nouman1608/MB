These notes condense section 3.4.6 (Homeostasis and negative feedback) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1, in Unit 4. For explanations and worked examples, use the [homeostasis study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-homeostasis-and-negative-feedback/); then try the [practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-homeostasis-and-negative-feedback-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Homeostasis | Maintenance of the internal environment within restricted limits |
| Set point | The normal level of a factor |
| Receptor | Detects a change from the set point |
| Coordinator | Processes the information and sends signals to effectors |
| Effector | Carries out the corrective response |
| Negative feedback | A response that reverses a change, restoring the original level |
| Positive feedback | A response that increases a change, giving a greater departure |
| Core temperature | The temperature deep inside the body, about 37 degrees Celsius in humans |
| Vasodilation | Widening of blood vessels near the skin surface |
| Vasoconstriction | Narrowing of blood vessels near the skin surface |

## Why a stable internal environment matters

- **Core temperature:** too high denatures enzymes (hydrogen bonds break, the active site changes); too low slows reactions because there are fewer collisions.
- **Blood pH:** a change alters the charges on R-groups, so the tertiary structure of enzymes (and of haemoglobin) changes and the active site no longer fits.
- A small change in pH matters: a fall of 0.3 pH units doubles the hydrogen ion concentration (10 to the power 0.3 is about 2).

## Negative feedback: the loop

1. Change from the set point.
2. Receptor detects it.
3. Coordinator compares it with the set point.
4. Effector produces a response.
5. The response reverses the change.
6. The receptor detects the return, and the response switches off.

**Key phrase:** "restores the system to its original level".

## Two mechanisms are better than one

| Rise above the set point | Fall below the set point |
|---|---|
| Vasodilation | Vasoconstriction |
| Sweating | Shivering |
| Hairs lie flat | Hairs raised |

**Advantage:** each mechanism reverses a change in one direction, so control is faster and finer, with less variation around the set point.

## Positive feedback

- The response increases the change.
- The system moves further from its original level, often at an increasing rate.
- Often associated with the breakdown of control, as in temperature control.
- Example in temperature: high temperature raises the rate of reactions, which releases more heat, which raises temperature further, until enzymes are denatured.
- Positive feedback can be useful if limited, for example the opening of sodium channels in an action potential.

## Reading data

| Question | What to do |
|---|---|
| Is it negative feedback? | Does the response oppose the change and return towards the set point? |
| Is it positive feedback? | Does the change increase, and the rate of change increase? |
| Rate of change | Change in value divided by time |
| Overshoot | Look for whether the value goes past the set point before returning |

**Worked data:** 37.0 (0 min), 37.6, 38.2 (20 min), then 37.8, 37.4, 37.1 and 37.0 (60 min). The rise was 1.2 degrees in 20 min (0.06 degrees per minute). The fall was 1.2 degrees in 40 min (0.03 degrees per minute). It returned to 37.0, so this is negative feedback.

**Positive feedback data:** 39.0, 40.0, 41.5, 43.5 at 30-minute intervals. The rises are 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 degrees, so the rate is increasing. This is positive feedback.

## A model five-mark answer

**Question:** Describe how negative feedback keeps the core temperature of a mammal stable. [5 marks]

**Answer:** Thermoreceptors detect a change in core temperature (1). They send impulses to the hypothalamus, which acts as the coordinator (1). If the temperature is too high, effectors cause vasodilation and sweating, so more heat is lost (1). If the temperature is too low, effectors cause vasoconstriction and shivering, so heat is conserved and generated (1). When the temperature returns to the set point, the receptors detect it and the responses are switched off, which is negative feedback (1).

## Command words

| Word | What the answer needs |
|---|---|
| Define | A precise statement, such as "maintaining the internal environment within restricted limits" |
| Explain | A reason, such as the effect on the tertiary structure of enzymes |
| Describe | The sequence from change to correction |
| Interpret | Use values and rates from the graph, and say whether the response reverses or increases the change |

## Link to other topics

Blood glucose control (section 3.4.7) is another example of negative feedback with separate mechanisms for a rise and a fall. The control of heart rate (section 3.4.8) is also a negative feedback loop.

## Exam traps

- Enzymes are denatured, they do not die.
- "Negative" refers to the direction of the response, not to a bad effect.
- Positive feedback leads to a greater departure and does not restore the original level.
- For separate mechanisms, give an advantage, not just a list.
- Give the effector and the response, for example "sweat glands secrete sweat, which evaporates".
- Quote data when asked to interpret a graph.

## Quick self-test

1. Define homeostasis.
2. Why must core temperature be kept stable?
3. Why must blood pH be kept in a narrow range?
4. Define negative feedback.
5. Name the three parts of a control loop that detect, process and respond.
6. Give two responses that lower core temperature.
7. Give two responses that raise core temperature.
8. State one advantage of having separate mechanisms for rises and falls.
9. What is positive feedback?
10. Why is positive feedback in temperature control dangerous?
11. A value moves 37.0, 37.5, 38.5, 40.0. Is this negative or positive feedback? Explain.
12. By what factor does the hydrogen ion concentration change if pH falls by 0.3 units?

### Answers

1. Maintaining the internal environment within restricted limits.
2. To keep enzymes working at their optimum, and so keep metabolic reactions at a suitable rate.
3. A change in pH alters the tertiary structure of enzymes and other proteins, so they stop working.
4. A response that reverses a change and restores a system to its original level.
5. Receptor, coordinator, effector.
6. Any two from vasodilation, sweating, hairs lying flat.
7. Any two from vasoconstriction, shivering, raising the hairs.
8. Control is faster and finer, because a change in either direction can be reversed.
9. A response that increases the change, giving a greater departure from the original level.
10. A rise in temperature increases metabolic rate and heat production, causing a further rise, until enzymes are denatured.
11. Positive feedback. The value moves away from the set point and the rise per step increases (0.5, 1.0, 1.5).
12. It roughly doubles (a factor of about 2).
