## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Limbic system | Subcortical structures, including the amygdala and hypothalamus, that process emotion |
| Amygdala | Limbic structure that assesses threat and triggers rapid emotional responses |
| Orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) | Frontal area that regulates limbic activity and impulse control |
| Serotonin | Inhibitory neurotransmitter; low levels linked to impulsive aggression |
| Testosterone | Androgen hormone linked to dominance and male aggression |
| MAOA gene | Codes for monoamine oxidase A, an enzyme that breaks down serotonin; low-activity variant linked to aggression |
| Innate releasing mechanism (IRM) | Inborn neural process triggered by a specific environmental stimulus |
| Fixed action pattern (FAP) | Universal, ballistic, single-purpose behaviour sequence released by an IRM |
| Ritualistic aggression | Displays that end in appeasement, so conflict rarely kills |
| Cuckoldry | A man unknowingly raising another man's child |
| Mate retention | Strategies to keep a partner, such as direct guarding and negative inducements |
| Displacement | Aggression redirected to a safer target than the source of frustration |
| Catharsis | The claim that expressing aggression releases it |
| Vicarious reinforcement | Learning that a behaviour pays by seeing others rewarded |
| De-individuation | Loss of personal identity and self-awareness in crowds or under anonymity |
| Importation model | Dispositional: prisoners bring aggressive traits and norms into prison |
| Deprivation model | Situational: prison conditions cause aggression |
| Desensitisation | Reduced emotional and physical response to violence after repeated exposure |
| Disinhibition | Media weaken usual restraints by making violence seem normal |
| Cognitive priming | Media activate aggressive thoughts that later cues can trigger |

## Theories and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Klüver and Bucy (1939) | Temporal lobes removed on both sides in rhesus monkeys | Monkeys became placid and lost normal fear | Animal brains may not generalise to humans |
| Gospic et al. (2011) | Ultimatum game in a scanner, drug vs placebo | Drug lowered amygdala activity and rejection of unfair offers | Drug manipulation shows cause, not just correlation |
| Raleigh et al. (1991) | Serotonin-raising or -lowering drugs in male vervet monkeys | Raised serotonin: treated males became dominant; lowered: cage mates did | Animal study; dominance is not the same as aggression |
| Dabbs et al. (1987) | Saliva testosterone in 89 male prisoners | 10 of the 11 highest had committed violent crimes | Correlational; direction of cause unclear |
| Mehta and Josephs (2010) | Dual-hormone hypothesis | Testosterone predicts dominance mainly when cortisol is low | Shows a single-hormone account is too simple |
| Coccaro et al. (1997) | Adult male twins | Genes explained about 47% of the variance in direct physical aggression | Assumes equal environments for MZ and DZ twins |
| Brunner et al. (1993) | Affected men in one Dutch family | MAOA mutation, no MAOA activity, impulsive aggression | One family; may not generalise |
| Caspi et al. (2002) | Dunedin boys, MAOA and maltreatment | Maltreated boys with high-activity MAOA less likely to become antisocial | Supports gene-environment interaction |
| Tinbergen (1951) | Stickleback models | Red underside triggered attack | Controlled, but animal-only evidence |
| Lorenz | Ethology | Aggression adaptive and often ritualistic | Gombe chimpanzee killings show lethal aggression |
| Shackelford et al. (2005) | Couples survey | Men's mate retention predicted partner violence | Self-report and correlational |
| Dollard et al. (1939) | Frustration-aggression hypothesis | Frustration always leads to aggression | Too absolute; Berkowitz (1989) revised it |
| Berkowitz and LePage (1967) | Shocks with guns vs racquets nearby | More shocks with guns present | Aggressive cues matter |
| Bushman (2002) | Hitting a punchbag while thinking of the provoker | More aggressive than doing nothing | Contradicts catharsis |
| Bandura et al. (1961) | Bobo doll | Children imitated adult aggression | Doll is not a person |
| Bandura (1965) | Model rewarded or punished | Punished model, less imitation until reward offered | Learning differs from performance |
| Zimbardo (1969) | Hooded vs named participants | Hooded gave longer shocks | Postmes and Spears (1998): weak overall support |
| Irwin and Cressey (1962) | Importation model | Prisoners bring norms in | Camp and Gaes: similar misconduct at different security levels |
| Sykes (1958) | Pains of imprisonment | Five deprivations cause frustration | Useful for prison management |
| Anderson and Dill (2000) | Violent game in the lab | More aggressive thoughts and behaviour | Lab measures lack validity |
| Carnagey et al. (2007) | Violent or non-violent game, then videos of real-life violence | Lower skin conductance after violent game | Short-term lab effect |
| Josephson (1987) | 396 boys, violent or non-violent TV, cue, floor hockey | Violent TV plus cue raised aggression in groups with more high-aggressive boys | Naturalistic observation raises validity; effect not found in all boys |

## Must-know distinctions

- **IRM vs FAP.** The IRM is the trigger mechanism; the FAP is the behaviour it releases.
- **Ethological vs evolutionary.** Ethology studies animals directly. Evolutionary explanations apply natural selection to human aggression.
- **Dispositional vs situational.** Importation is about the prisoner; deprivation is about the prison.
- **Learning vs performance.** Bandura (1965) showed children learn aggression without performing it until motivated.
- **Desensitisation vs disinhibition.** Desensitisation reduces arousal; disinhibition changes norms about what is acceptable.
- **Original vs revised frustration-aggression.** Dollard: frustration always leads to aggression. Berkowitz: frustration makes it more likely.
- **Gene vs gene-environment interaction.** MAOA-L alone predicts little; with maltreatment it predicts much more.

## Mini essay plan: discuss institutional aggression in the context of prisons [16]

1. **AO1, importation.** Irwin and Cressey: prisoners bring traits, gang links and an inmate code into prison.
2. **AO1, deprivation.** Sykes' pains of imprisonment: liberty, goods and services, heterosexual relationships, autonomy, security. Add crowding and poor management.
3. **AO3, importation support.** Camp and Gaes: random assignment, similar misconduct across security levels.
4. **AO3, deprivation value.** Practical use: improving conditions should reduce violence if the model is right.
5. **AO3, debate.** Each model is one-sided. An interactionist view combines person and situation.
6. **AO3, issues.** Nature-nurture and free will-determinism; social sensitivity when blaming individuals.
7. **Conclusion.** Judge which model has the stronger evidence, with a reason.

## Issues and debates hooks

- **Nature-nurture.** Biological, ethological and evolutionary explanations stress nature; social learning, de-individuation and media explanations stress nurture. Caspi et al. (2002) supports an interactionist view.
- **Determinism.** Gene and hormone accounts are biologically deterministic; this matters if they are used to reduce responsibility for violence.
- **Reductionism.** Explaining aggression by one neurotransmitter or hormone ignores social context.
- **Gender bias.** Much hormone and evolutionary research focuses on male aggression.

The [issues and debates study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-issues-and-debates/) explains each debate in full.

## Quick self-test

1. Name two limbic structures linked to aggression.
2. What does the OFC do in relation to aggression?
3. Which neurotransmitter does MAOA break down?
4. What did Caspi et al. (2002) find about maltreatment?
5. Give the four features of a fixed action pattern.
6. What triggered attack in Tinbergen's sticklebacks?
7. What is cuckoldry, and why is it relevant?
8. State the two claims of the original frustration-aggression hypothesis.
9. What did Bandura (1965) show about learning and performance?
10. Which prison model is dispositional?
11. Define cognitive priming.

### Answers

1. The amygdala and the hypothalamus.
2. It regulates limbic activity and impulses; poor OFC control allows reactive aggression.
3. Serotonin (among other neurotransmitters).
4. The MAOA variant mattered mainly for boys maltreated as children: maltreated boys with high-activity MAOA were less likely to become antisocial.
5. Universal, ballistic, single-purpose, triggered by a specific stimulus.
6. A red underside on the model.
7. Raising another man's child; sexual jealousy and mate retention may have evolved to prevent it.
8. Frustration always leads to aggression; aggression is always caused by frustration.
9. Children who saw a model punished still learned the aggression and performed it when offered a reward.
10. The importation model.
11. Violent media activate aggressive thoughts that later cues can trigger.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the amygdala without saying how it produces aggression.
- Writing "the warrior gene causes violence" instead of explaining the gene-environment interaction.
- Muddling IRM and FAP, or calling FAPs learned.
- Applying ethology to humans without noting the cultural and learning problem.
- Describing the Bobo doll study without linking it to human aggression.
- Giving importation and deprivation without saying which is dispositional and which is situational.
- Naming desensitisation, disinhibition and priming but not showing how each leads to aggressive behaviour.
- Treating correlational studies (Dabbs et al., survey research) as proof of cause.
- Forgetting issues and debates in 16-mark answers: determinism, reductionism, nature-nurture, gender bias.
- Using Forensic Psychology content on offending in an aggression answer.
