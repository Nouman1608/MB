It works through statements C4.2a to C4.2g, the mathematical learning outcome CM4.2i and the practical suggestions linked to PAG C5. The specification places Topic C4 in Paper 2 (Foundation Tier) and Paper 4 (Higher Tier).

The specification assumes you know cations and anions from electrolysis.

### A point about mass

The specification flags a common misconception. A precipitate does not make the products heavier than the reactants; the atoms are just rearranged into a solid. A gas-forming test in an open tube does not destroy mass either: the gas escapes. Count the gas and the total is unchanged.

## Tests for gases (C4.2a)

| Gas | What you do | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | Put a glowing splint into the gas | The splint relights |
| Hydrogen | Bring a lighted splint to the open end of the tube | A squeaky pop |
| Carbon dioxide | Pass the gas into limewater (calcium hydroxide solution) | The limewater goes milky (cloudy) |
| Chlorine | Hold damp blue litmus paper in the gas | Paper turns red, then is bleached white |

Chlorine is toxic, so test it in a fume cupboard. The litmus must be damp: chlorine reacts with the water to form the substances that redden and bleach the dye. Limewater turns milky because insoluble calcium carbonate forms.

### Worked example 1: a gas that fails two tests

A student collects a colourless gas from an unknown reaction and records:

- a glowing splint goes out
- the gas does not change limewater
- a lighted splint brought to the open end of the tube makes a squeaky pop

Which gas is it?

**Step 1.** The glowing splint did not relight, so the gas is not oxygen.
**Step 2.** Limewater stayed clear, so it is not carbon dioxide.
**Step 3.** The squeaky pop is the positive test for hydrogen. **The gas is hydrogen.**

Negative results rule gases out, but always finish with the positive test.

## Flame tests (C4.2c and C4.2e)

Some metal ions colour a flame, which identifies the metal ion in a sample.

### How to carry out a flame test (C4.2c)

1. Clean a nichrome wire loop: dip it in hydrochloric acid, then heat it in a hot, blue Bunsen flame. Repeat until the flame stays uncoloured.
2. Wet the cleaned loop with acid and touch it on the solid sample to pick up a few grains.
3. Place the grains in the edge of a blue (air hole open) Bunsen flame.
4. Record the colour of the flame.
5. Clean the loop again before the next sample, or use a fresh loop.

Wear eye protection, because the acid is corrosive. The yellow safety flame has its own colour, which would hide the result. Cleaning matters because a trace of an earlier sample, especially a sodium compound, adds its own colour.

### Worked example 2: two red flames

Two white solids are labelled "lithium chloride" and "calcium chloride", but the labels may have been swapped. Both give a reddish flame. Which is which?

**Step 1.** Compare the flames side by side: lithium is crimson, calcium is orange-red.
**Step 2.** To confirm (Higher Tier only), add sodium hydroxide solution to a solution of each. Calcium ions give a white precipitate; lithium ions give none, because lithium hydroxide is soluble.
**Conclusion.** Crimson flame, no precipitate: **lithium chloride**. Orange-red flame, white precipitate: **calcium chloride**.

## Tests for aqueous cations (C4.2b, Higher Tier only)

Add a few drops of dilute sodium hydroxide solution to the test solution, then add more until it is in excess. Many metal ions form an insoluble hydroxide precipitate.

| Cation | Precipitate with a few drops of NaOH(aq) | With excess NaOH(aq) |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | White | Stays |
| Zinc, Zn²⁺ | White | Dissolves to give a colourless solution |
| Copper, Cu²⁺ | Blue | Stays |
| Iron(II), Fe²⁺ | Green (the surface slowly turns orange-brown in air) | Stays |
| Iron(III), Fe³⁺ | Orange-brown | Stays |

Calcium and zinc both give white precipitates, so the excess step is how you tell them apart.

### Worked example 3: writing the ionic equation

Sodium hydroxide solution is added to a pale green solution, and a green solid forms. Identify the cation and write an ionic equation with state symbols.

**Step 1.** A green precipitate means iron(II) ions, Fe²⁺.
**Step 2.** Each Fe²⁺ ion needs two OH⁻ ions to make a neutral solid, Fe(OH)₂.
**Step 3.** Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)

Sodium ions and the original anion are spectators. For iron(III) the solid is Fe(OH)₃.

## Tests for aqueous anions (C4.2b, Higher Tier only)

| Anion | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| Carbonate, CO₃²⁻ | Add dilute acid; pass the gas into limewater | Bubbles of carbon dioxide, which turns limewater milky |
| Chloride, Cl⁻ | Acidify with dilute nitric acid; add dilute silver nitrate solution | A white solid forms (silver chloride) |
| Bromide, Br⁻ | Same method as chloride | A cream solid forms (silver bromide) |
| Iodide, I⁻ | Same method as chloride | A yellow solid forms (silver iodide) |
| Sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution (or dilute nitric acid, then barium nitrate solution) | White precipitate (barium sulfate) |

Why acidify first? Carbonate ions also give precipitates with silver and barium ions. The acid removes carbonate as carbon dioxide, so any precipitate comes from the ion you are testing for.

Why that acid? Hydrochloric acid would add chloride ions to the halide test, so use nitric acid. Sulfuric acid would add sulfate ions to the sulfate test, so use hydrochloric or nitric acid.

The precipitates form by simple ion pairing, for example Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq) → AgBr(s) and Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s). The carbonate reaction is CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O.

## Identifying species from results (C4.2d)

PAG C5 suggests identifying an unknown compound using cation, anion and flame tests. For a single unknown salt:

1. Flame test on the solid.
2. Dissolve some in water; add sodium hydroxide to one portion.
3. Add dilute acid to a fresh portion to check for carbonate.
4. Use fresh portions for the sulfate and halide tests.
5. Combine cation and anion into a name and formula.

### Worked example 4: an unknown white solid (uses Higher Tier only tests)

Solid Z dissolves in water to give a colourless solution. Results:

- Flame test: none of the five colours in the flame test table.
- A few drops of sodium hydroxide give a white precipitate, which dissolves when more is added.
- Dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate solution give a yellow precipitate.

**Step 1.** A white precipitate that dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide shows zinc ions, Zn²⁺. (Calcium would also be white, but it would not dissolve, and it would give an orange-red flame.)
**Step 2.** A yellow precipitate with acidified silver nitrate shows iodide ions, I⁻.
**Step 3.** Zn²⁺ needs two I⁻ to balance the charges. **Z is zinc iodide, ZnI₂.**

## Instrumental methods (C4.2f)

Instrumental methods use machines, rather than test-tube reactions, to detect and identify substances. The specification names three advantages:

- **Sensitivity**: they detect very small amounts of a substance, far below what a colour change or precipitate would show.
- **Accuracy**: they identify substances reliably and measure amounts close to the true value.
- **Speed**: they give results quickly, and many samples can be run one after another, often automatically.

### Worked example 5: choosing the method

A food laboratory must check hundreds of drinks a day for a contaminant present only in tiny traces. Explain why it uses an instrumental method rather than precipitation tests.

**Answer.** Only tiny traces are present, so high **sensitivity** is needed to detect them at all. A positive result must be right, which needs **accuracy**. With hundreds of samples a day, the **speed** of an automated instrument matters.

## Reading instrumental results (C4.2g and CM4.2i)

Statement C4.2g pairs the result with a reference set in the same form. You match the unknown to the references; you do not memorise spectra.

### Features of a mass spectroscopy chart

- The horizontal axis is **m/z**, the mass-to-charge ratio. For 1+ ions, m/z is the ion's mass.
- The vertical axis is **relative abundance**. The tallest peak, the base peak, is often set to 100.
- The **molecular ion peak** is the peak at the highest m/z value. It gives the relative molecular mass, Mr.
- The other peaks are **fragments** of the molecule. Their pattern acts like a fingerprint, so compounds with the same Mr can still be told apart.

### Worked example 6: matching a spectrum

An unknown liquid gives the chart below, shown as bar heights. Simplified reference data for three compounds are supplied (use these values).

| m/z | Unknown (relative abundance) | Bar |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | 25 | ██▌ |
| 43 | 100 | ██████████ |
| 58 | 60 | ██████ |

| Reference compound | Mr | Main peaks, m/z (relative abundance) |
|---|---|---|
| Ethanol, C₂H₅OH | 46 | 31 (100), 45 (50), 46 (20) |
| Butane, C₄H₁₀ | 58 | 43 (100), 29 (45), 58 (10) |
| Propanone, CH₃COCH₃ | 58 | 43 (100), 58 (60), 15 (25) |

**Step 1.** The highest m/z peak is at 58, so Mr = 58. This rules out ethanol (Mr 46).
**Step 2.** Butane and propanone both have Mr 58 and a base peak at 43, so compare the other peaks.
**Step 3.** The unknown has a peak at 15 and no peak at 29. Its peak at 58 is 60% of the base peak, not 10%.
**Step 4.** Every peak and relative height matches propanone. **The unknown is propanone.**

Check your Mr from the formula: propanone, C₃H₆O, is (3 × 12) + (6 × 1) + 16 = 58.

## Common errors

- Testing chlorine with dry litmus paper.
- Calling the lithium flame "orange-red". Crimson is lithium; orange-red is calcium.
- Identifying calcium from a white hydroxide without adding excess sodium hydroxide to rule out zinc.
- Acidifying the silver nitrate test with hydrochloric acid.
- Taking the tallest peak in a mass spectrum as the molecular ion. The molecular ion is the highest m/z peak.
- Saying a precipitate makes the mixture heavier, or that a gas-forming reaction "loses mass" overall.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you carry out a flame test?**
Use a clean loop dipped in acid, pick up a little of the sample and hold it in a blue flame, wearing eye protection. A blue flame is used because the yellow safety flame has its own colour, and cleaning the loop prevents traces of an earlier sample, especially sodium, giving a false colour.

**How do you tell lithium and calcium apart in a flame test?**
Lithium gives a crimson flame and calcium gives an orange-red flame. To confirm, at Higher Tier, add sodium hydroxide solution: calcium ions give a white precipitate, while lithium ions give none because lithium hydroxide is soluble.

**Why is nitric acid used before the silver nitrate test?**
Acid is added first to remove carbonate ions, which would also precipitate with silver ions. Nitric acid is used because hydrochloric acid would add chloride ions and give a false positive.

**What are the advantages of instrumental methods of analysis?**
The OCR GCSE Chemistry A (J248) specification names three: sensitivity, because they detect very small amounts; accuracy, because they identify substances reliably; and speed, because they give results quickly and can run many samples.

**How do you identify an unknown compound from a mass spectrum?**
Use the highest m/z peak to find Mr and rule out references with a different Mr, then compare the fragment peaks and their relative heights with the reference set. Compounds with the same Mr can still be told apart by their fragment pattern.
