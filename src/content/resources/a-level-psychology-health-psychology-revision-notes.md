**These notes condense Specialist Option 3, Health Psychology, of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** The five topics are the patient–practitioner relationship, adherence to medical advice, pain, stress and health promotion. Three themes run through them: how a problem is explained, how it is measured, and how it is managed or improved.

For full explanations and study details, read the [Health Psychology study guide](/resources/a-level-psychology-health-psychology/). Then test yourself with the [Health Psychology practice questions](/resources/a-level-psychology-health-psychology-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Doctor-centred (directing) style | Doctor controls the consultation and gives instructions |
| Patient-centred (sharing) style | Doctor seeks patient's views and decides together |
| False positive | Diagnosing an illness the patient does not have |
| False negative | Missing an illness the patient does have |
| Munchausen syndrome | Feigning illness to gain medical attention and the sick role |
| Malingering | Faking illness for an external gain |
| Health belief model | Action depends on perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers and cues to action |
| Rational non-adherence | A reasoned decision not to follow advice (side effects, cost, doubt) |
| Acute / chronic pain | Short-term pain from injury / pain lasting beyond normal healing |
| Gate control theory | A spinal "gate" opened by pain fibres and closed by large fibres and brain messages |
| Cognitive redefinition | Reinterpreting pain in a less threatening way |
| GAS | Alarm, resistance, exhaustion (Selye) |
| Type A | Competitive, time-pressured, hostile personality |
| Biofeedback | Real-time feedback on a body process to learn control over it |
| Stress inoculation training | Conceptualisation, skill acquisition and rehearsal, application and follow-through |
| Unrealistic optimism | Belief that bad events are less likely to happen to oneself than to others |

## Measures compared

| Measure | Type | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Semi-structured interview (adherence) | Subjective | Gives reasons for non-adherence | Patients may overstate adherence |
| Pill count / electronic dispenser | Objective | Not reliant on honesty | Opening is not proof of taking |
| Blood or urine test | Biological | Most direct evidence | Invasive, costly, shows recent use only |
| McGill pain questionnaire | Psychometric | Captures quality as well as intensity | Needs good vocabulary |
| Visual analogue scale | Rating scale | Quick, quantitative, usable repeatedly | Only intensity; hard for young children |
| UAB pain behaviour scale | Observational | Usable when self-report is not possible | Behaviour may not match felt pain |
| Salivary cortisol | Biological | Objective, non-invasive | Varies with time of day and other factors |
| Life events questionnaire | Self-report | Cheap, quick | Ignores how the person perceives each event |

## Key studies at a glance

| Study | Design and sample | Main finding | Evaluate with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Savage and Armstrong (1990) | Randomised; 359 patients of one London GP | Directing style gave higher satisfaction, especially for physical problems and prescriptions | One doctor only; low one-week response rate (58%) |
| Yokley and Glenwick (1984) | Field experiment; families of 1,133 preschool children, six conditions | Monetary incentives most effective; specific prompts most cost-effective | Large random sample; real health outcome |
| Brudvik et al. (2016) | Cross-sectional; 243 children aged 3–15 in Bergen | Physicians underestimated pain (mean 3.2 vs children's 5.5) | Real clinical setting; single snapshot |
| Bridge et al. (1988) | Randomised trial; 154 women in radiotherapy, three groups, six weeks | Relaxation reduced mood disturbance; imagery added extra benefit | Controlled design; self-report mood measures |
| Shoshani and Steinmetz (2014) | School intervention vs control, Israel | Reduced distress, increased well-being measures | Applied value; groups not individually randomised |

## Must-know comparisons

- **Specificity vs gate control**: specificity sees pain as a direct signal proportional to injury; gate control adds spinal and brain modulation, explaining phantom limbs and psychological effects.
- **Biofeedback vs stress inoculation**: biofeedback trains control over a body response; stress inoculation trains thinking and coping skills in advance.
- **Fear arousal vs information**: Janis and Feshbach (1953) found minimal fear changed behaviour most; Lewin et al. (1992) found information in a self-help manual improved adjustment.
- **Munchausen vs malingering**: internal motive (sick role) versus external motive (money, avoiding duties).

## Mini essay plan: "Evaluate ways of improving adherence" [10]

1. **Community interventions**: Yokley and Glenwick (1984) large field experiment; incentives work but are costly; prompts are cost-effective.
2. **Children**: Chaney et al. (2004) child-friendly device; ethical care and parental consent needed.
3. **Behavioural techniques**: contracts and prompts are simple; they treat adherence as behaviour and ignore beliefs (reductionist), unlike the health belief model.
4. **Measurement**: how adherence is measured (self-report vs objective) affects how effective an intervention appears.
5. **Rational non-adherence**: if a patient has good reasons, prompts alone will not help; customising treatment addresses the reason.
6. **Conclusion**: combined approaches that address both behaviour and beliefs are most useful.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Mixing up the directing and sharing styles in Savage and Armstrong: it was the **directing** style that gave higher satisfaction.
- Calling the visual analogue scale a "questionnaire"; it is a single rating line.
- Writing that Brudvik et al. found parents overestimated pain; the main finding is that **physicians underestimated** it.
- Listing GAS stages without the health consequence of exhaustion.
- Forgetting the three phases of stress inoculation training in order.
- Writing "fear appeals work" when Janis and Feshbach found **minimal** fear was most effective.

## Quick self-test

1. What is a false negative diagnosis?
2. Name the five components of the health belief model on this page.
3. What were the six conditions in Yokley and Glenwick (1984)?
4. Which gate control mechanism explains why rubbing a bump reduces pain?
5. What mean pain rating did physicians give in Brudvik et al. (2016)?
6. What did the third group in Bridge et al. (1988) do in addition to relaxation?
7. Name Seligman's three routes to happiness.
8. What reinforcer did Fox et al. (1987) use in the mines?

**Answers:** (1) missing an illness the patient has; (2) perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action; (3) general prompt, specific prompt, specific prompt plus access, specific prompt plus money, contact control, no-contact control; (4) large-fibre activity closes the gate; (5) 3.2; (6) imagined a peaceful scene of their own choice; (7) pleasant, good, meaningful life; (8) stamps (tokens) exchangeable for goods.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is stress in 9990 Health Psychology the same as stress in other courses?**
The content is set by the 9990 syllabus: GAS, life events, work, Type A, the named biological and self-report measures, biofeedback, imagery and stress inoculation. Learn these rather than topics from other specifications.

**How should I use numbers from key studies?**
Use them to support a point, for example "physicians' mean rating of 3.2 compared with children's 5.5 shows underestimation". Accurate numbers make descriptions more precise.
