Condensed notes for **5.4 Costs** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, 2026–2028). Worked examples on overhead allocation, special orders at full capacity and break-even changes are in the [Costs study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-costs-cambridge-alevel-business/); exam-style questions are in the [Costs practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-costs-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

## What 5.4 asks you to handle

- **5.4.1** Accurate cost information; fixed, variable, direct, indirect costs.
- **5.4.2** Full and contribution costing: differences, uses, limitations, when each is used.
- **5.4.3** Average, marginal, total costs; pricing; monitoring performance; special orders.
- **5.4.4** Break-even: calculation, chart, margin of safety, profit, uses, limitations.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fixed cost | Does not change with output in the short run |
| Variable cost | Changes directly with output |
| Direct cost | Traceable to one product or cost centre |
| Indirect cost (overhead) | Shared across products; cannot be traced to one |
| Semi-variable cost | Has a fixed and a variable part |
| Full costing | Allocates all costs, including overheads, to products |
| Contribution costing | Uses only variable costs; fixed costs deducted in total |
| Contribution per unit | Price − variable cost per unit |
| Marginal cost | Extra cost of one more unit |
| Average cost | Total cost ÷ output |
| Break-even output | Output where total revenue = total cost |
| Margin of safety | Actual output − break-even output |

## Formulas

- Total cost = fixed costs + (variable cost per unit × output)
- Average cost = total cost ÷ output
- Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit
- Total contribution = contribution per unit × units sold
- Profit = total contribution − fixed costs
- Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit (round **up**)
- Margin of safety = actual output − break-even output (also as % of actual, or × price for revenue)
- Output for target profit = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit

## Awkward cost cases

- Semi-variable: split into fixed and variable parts.
- Stepped fixed: jumps when capacity is added.
- Labour: salaried (fixed) or piece-rate/agency (variable).

## Full costing

- **Uses**: shows whether each product covers all costs; supports cost-plus pricing.
- **Limitations**: result depends on the allocation basis; a product can look loss-making when it makes a positive contribution; dropping it can **reduce** total profit because overheads remain.

## Contribution costing

- **Uses**: keep or drop products, special orders with spare capacity, choosing what to promote.
- **Not on its own**: long-run pricing, decisions that change fixed costs, no spare capacity.
- **Limitation**: ignores fixed costs; relies on a correct fixed/variable split.

## Contribution ≠ profit

Contribution pays fixed costs first; only what remains is profit.

## Special orders

- **Spare capacity**: accept if price > variable cost (financially).
- **No spare capacity**: compare contribution gained with contribution lost from displaced regular sales.
- **Non-financial**: regular customers may demand the low price; possible repeat business; reputation.

## Costs and performance

Track unit costs against targets and past periods. Profit = total revenue − total costs, so cost data shows whether profit changes come from price, volume or cost control.

## Break-even chart

- Fixed cost line: flat.
- Total cost line: starts at fixed costs, slope = variable cost.
- Revenue line: from zero, slope = price.
- Crossing = break-even. Right of it: profit; left: loss.

| Change | Line affected | Break-even |
|---|---|---|
| Price up | Revenue steeper | Falls |
| Price down | Revenue flatter | Rises |
| Fixed costs up | Total cost shifts up | Rises |
| Variable cost up | Total cost steeper | Rises |

## Break-even: uses and limitations

- **Uses**: loan applications, targets, "what if" tests, shows risk via margin of safety.
- **Limitations**: constant price and variable cost assumed; all output sold; best for one product; says nothing about how customers react.

## Exam traps

- Round break-even output **up**.
- Contribution is **not** profit.
- Do not drop a product with positive contribution just because full costing shows a loss.
- Special orders at full capacity have an extra cost: lost regular sales.
- Total fixed costs do not fall as output rises; fixed cost **per unit** does.
- State units: $, units, %, or $ of revenue for margin of safety.

## Self-test

1. Classify factory rent: fixed or variable? direct or indirect?
2. Give an example of a semi-variable cost.
3. Fixed costs are $12 000 and variable cost is $3 a unit. Find total and average cost at 3 000 units.
4. What is the marginal cost in question 3?
5. Price $18, variable cost $10, fixed costs $16 000. Find break-even output.
6. Sales in question 5 are 2 600. Find the margin of safety and profit.
7. How many units are needed for a $8 000 profit in question 5?
8. Why might a product showing a loss under full costing still be worth keeping?
9. A factory at full capacity is offered a special order above variable cost. What extra cost must be considered?
10. What happens to break-even output if variable cost per unit rises?
11. Give one limitation of break-even analysis.
12. When would contribution costing **not** be used on its own?

**Answers**

1. Fixed and indirect.
2. For example, an electricity bill with a standing charge plus a charge per unit used.
3. Total = 12 000 + 9 000 = $21 000; average = $7.
4. $3.
5. Contribution = $8; 16 000 ÷ 8 = 2 000 units.
6. Margin of safety = 600 units; profit = 2 600 × 8 − 16 000 = $4 800.
7. (16 000 + 8 000) ÷ 8 = 3 000 units.
8. It may have a positive contribution that helps pay overheads, which would remain if it were dropped.
9. The contribution lost from regular sales that the order would displace.
10. It rises, because contribution per unit falls.
11. For example, it assumes all output is sold at a constant price.
12. For example, when setting long-run prices that must cover all costs, or when the decision changes fixed costs.
