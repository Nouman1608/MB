This set covers the research-methods areas of section 3.1.3, which Paper 1 assesses with 10 marks of
extended writing. Section A uses short recall questions for practice; Section B uses the 10-mark
"outline and explain two" style. None of the questions needs an education context: that is the job
of the separate Methods in Context question.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *sampling frame*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a hypothesis and a research question. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** reasons for carrying out a pilot study. **[2]**

**4.** Outline **two** differences between quota sampling and stratified random sampling. **[4]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by *operationalising* a concept, using "social class" as your example.
**[3]**

**6.** Outline **two** ways in which an interviewer's characteristics might affect the data
collected in an interview. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the difference between *hard* and *soft* official statistics, giving an example of
each. **[4]**

**8.** Identify **three** principles of ethical research stressed by the British Sociological
Association. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** Outline and explain **two** reasons why some sociologists use snowball sampling despite its
limitations. **[10]**

**10.** Outline and explain **two** advantages of overt rather than covert participant observation.
**[10]**

**11.** Outline and explain **two** practical factors that may influence a sociologist's choice of
research method. **[10]**

**12.** Outline and explain **two** reasons why some sociologists combine quantitative and
qualitative methods in the same study. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The list of members of the research population [1] from which the sample is actually selected,
such as an electoral register or a school roll [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A hypothesis is a testable statement predicting a relationship between variables [1]; a
research question is an open question that guides the study without predicting the answer, letting
meanings emerge [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two: checking that respondents understand the questions as intended [1]; checking how
long the method takes or what it costs [1]; checking that answers can be coded and analysed [1];
training interviewers [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Selection: in stratified random sampling individuals are chosen at random within each group
[1], whereas in quota sampling the interviewer chooses whom to approach until each quota is filled
[1]. Frame: stratified random sampling needs a sampling frame with information on the strata [1];
quota sampling does not need a frame, which makes it quicker and cheaper but less representative [1].
**[4]**

**5.** Operationalising means turning an abstract concept into measurable indicators [1]. Social
class might be measured by occupation, for example using the NS-SEC scale [1]. A different indicator,
such as income or education, could place people in different classes and change the findings [1].
**[3]**

**6.** Any two, each identified [1] and explained [1]: **status or power** — respondents may defer to
an interviewer seen as having authority and give the answers they think are wanted [1] [1];
**gender, age or ethnicity** — respondents may be less open with an interviewer from a different
social group, as Labov's research on children's speech suggested [1] [1]; **manner** — a formal
setting can make respondents guarded, while a relaxed one encourages fuller answers [1] [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Hard statistics are fairly complete, objective counts [1], for example registered births or
deaths [1]. Soft statistics depend on decisions by the public and officials about whether to report
and record events [1], for example police recorded crime [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Any three: informed consent [1]; confidentiality [1]; anonymity [1]; avoiding harm to
participants [1]; particular care with vulnerable groups such as children [1]; avoiding deception
unless strongly justified [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Marked in levels. The top level needs two clearly distinct reasons, each explained and
developed with a consequence. Indicative content:
- **Access to hidden or stigmatised groups.** There is often no sampling frame for groups such as
  illegal drug users or undocumented workers, so random sampling is impossible. A trusted member can
  vouch for the researcher. Develop: so snowball sampling may be the only way to obtain any data,
  which outweighs the loss of representativeness.
- **Building trust and validity.** Being introduced by someone the participant knows reduces
  suspicion and encourages honesty. Develop: interpretivists value this because it produces richer,
  more valid accounts in unstructured interviews or observation.
- Also creditable: low cost and speed; suitability for exploratory research that later informs a
  larger study.
- Develop the limitation briefly: members of one network share characteristics, so findings cannot
  be generalised. **[10]**

**10.** Marked in levels; two developed advantages needed. Indicative content:
- **Ethics.** Participants know about the research and can give informed consent, avoiding the
  deception at the centre of debates about Humphreys' *Tearoom Trade*. Develop: the researcher can
  also negotiate what may be published, protecting confidentiality.
- **Recording and questioning.** An overt researcher can take notes openly and ask direct questions,
  as Whyte did with the help of his informant Doc. Develop: data are more complete and less reliant
  on memory, which raises their quality.
- Also creditable: less danger to the researcher if discovered; easier exit from the group.
- Analysis: a good answer recognises the cost, the Hawthorne effect, but argues that behaviour often
  returns to normal once the group grows used to the observer. **[10]**

**11.** Marked in levels; two developed practical factors. Indicative content:
- **Time and money.** Large-scale questionnaires are cheap per respondent; participant observation can
  take months or years. Develop: researchers with limited budgets or deadlines are pushed towards
  quantitative methods regardless of their theoretical preference.
- **Funding bodies.** Funders may set the topic and expect statistical findings they can act on.
  Develop: this links practical constraints to the values debate, since whoever pays shapes the
  research.
- **Personal characteristics and access.** A researcher's age, gender or ethnicity can open or close
  doors to a group, and gatekeepers may refuse access. Develop: this limits who can use observation
  in a given setting.
- Also creditable: subject matter (some groups can only be studied in certain ways); the
  researcher's skills; opportunity. **[10]**

**12.** Marked in levels; two developed reasons. Indicative content:
- **Triangulation.** Using a second method checks the first: if interviews support questionnaire
  findings, confidence in both rises. Develop: this addresses the positivist concern with reliability
  and the interpretivist concern with validity at the same time.
- **Methodological pluralism.** Quantitative data show patterns; qualitative data explain the
  meanings behind them. Develop: Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977) combined several methods to
  build a fuller picture than any one could give.
- Also creditable: one method can generate hypotheses for another; funders may want both numbers and
  case studies.
- Analysis: critics argue the two approaches rest on different assumptions about what society is, so
  combining them does not settle the positivist–interpretivist debate. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- In Section B, giving two reasons that are really one (for example "cheap" and "saves money").
- Describing a method without explaining why that feature matters.
- Adding education examples that use up time but earn nothing extra in this question.
- Forgetting to develop each reason with a consequence ("so...", "which means...").
