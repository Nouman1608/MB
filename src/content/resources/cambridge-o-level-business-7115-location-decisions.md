This guide goes deeper into section **4.4 Location decisions** of the Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus heading is "The main factors influencing the location and relocation decisions of a business", and it lists four things you must be able to do:

- explain factors relevant to the location of **manufacturing** businesses and **service** businesses
- explain factors a business could consider when deciding **which country** to locate operations in
- explain the **role of legal controls** on location decisions
- **recommend and justify** an appropriate location for a business in given circumstances.

The wider [quality and location study guide](/resources/o-level-business-achieving-quality-production-and-location-decisions/) lists the main factors. This page concentrates on how to decide which factor matters most in a given case, which is where most marks are won or lost. Test yourself afterwards with the [location decisions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-location-decisions-revision-notes/) and the [location decisions practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-location-decisions-practice/). All businesses and figures on this page are invented.

## Why location is a big decision

A location decision is usually long-term and expensive to reverse. Once a factory is built or a lease is signed, the business is tied to that site's costs, workforce and customers for years. A poor choice raises costs every month (rent, transport, wages) or cuts revenue every month (too few customers). That is why examiners expect a balanced answer: the cheapest site and the busiest site are rarely the same place.

## Manufacturing businesses: what drives the choice

For a manufacturer the key question is usually **where are the costs lowest for making and delivering the product?** The factors are the same list every time, but their weight changes with the product.

| If the product... | The factor that tends to dominate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| loses weight or bulk during production (timber into planks, sugar cane into sugar) | closeness to raw materials | it is cheaper to move the finished product than the heavy inputs |
| gains bulk or becomes fragile (bottled drinks, furniture) | closeness to the market | moving the finished goods is the expensive part |
| needs highly skilled workers (electronics, specialist engineering) | availability of skilled labour | a cheap site is useless if the right staff cannot be recruited |
| uses large amounts of power or water (metal smelting, paper) | reliable utilities | breakdowns in supply stop production |
| is low value and made in large volumes | cost of land and wages | small savings per unit add up across high output |

Other factors still matter. Transport links (roads, ports, rail) affect both inputs and deliveries. Room to expand avoids a costly move later. Government grants can make an area attractive, but a grant is often a one-off payment, while higher transport costs continue every year.

## Service businesses: three different situations

Services cannot be stored and are often consumed where they are produced, so the main question becomes **where can the business reach its customers?** It helps to sort services into three types.

1. **Customers come to the business** (cafes, hairdressers, clinics, shops). Footfall, visibility, parking and public transport matter most. Rent is usually high where footfall is high, so the business must judge whether the extra customers cover the extra rent.
2. **The business goes to the customers** (plumbers, delivery firms, mobile repair services). Being central to the area served matters more than visibility, so travel time and cost dominate. A cheap unit on an industrial estate may be ideal.
3. **The service is delivered at a distance** (online retailers, call centres, accounting by email). Customers never see the site, so the cost of premises, wages and the availability of suitable staff and internet connections matter most.

Competitors can work either way. Many shoe shops in one street can draw shoppers who want to compare, which helps each shop. A second petrol station on a quiet road simply splits the trade.

## Choosing which country

When a business considers operating abroad, it weighs costs against market access and risk:

- **Labour costs and skills.** Lower wages reduce costs only if productivity and quality are acceptable.
- **Closeness to a large or growing market.** Producing inside a market can cut transport costs and help the business adapt products to local tastes.
- **Trade barriers.** If a country charges import tariffs or sets import quotas, producing inside it avoids them.
- **Exchange rates.** Costs and revenues in another currency change in value when the exchange rate changes, which adds uncertainty to profits.
- **Political stability.** Unrest or sudden changes of policy can disrupt production or threaten the business's assets.
- **Infrastructure.** Ports, roads, power and communications must be reliable.
- **Culture and language.** These affect managing staff, negotiating with suppliers and marketing.
- **Government incentives and laws.** Grants or tax breaks attract firms; strict rules may deter them.

## The role of legal controls

Governments use laws to steer where business activity happens. In general terms, legal controls can:

- **prevent** some activities in some places (a noisy or polluting factory near homes, or building on protected land)
- **require permission** before land is built on or its use changes, which adds time and uncertainty
- **impose conditions**, such as limits on noise, working hours, waste or emissions, which add cost
- **encourage** location in certain areas through grants, tax relief or cheaper land, often where unemployment is high.

In answers, describe the type of control and its effect on the decision: it may rule a site out, make it more expensive or slower to open, or make another area more attractive. Do not invent the names of particular laws.

## Relocation

Relocation is moving an existing operation. The reasons are usually that the current site no longer fits: premises are too small for growth, rent has risen or a lease is ending, customers or suppliers have moved, or a new site offers cheaper costs or better access. Relocation has costs that a first location does not: moving equipment, lost production during the move, staff who will not move, and customers who may not follow. A good answer weighs these one-off costs against the ongoing benefits of the new site.

## Worked example: an annotated recommendation

**Case.** Kestrel Bakes bakes bread overnight. It delivers to 40 cafes every morning and also sells from a small counter at its premises. Its lease is ending and it must choose a new site.

| Monthly figures | Site A: town centre | Site B: edge of town, by the main road |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | $2,600 | $1,500 |
| Delivery van costs | $900 (slow traffic) | $600 |
| Contribution from counter sales | $1,500 | $300 |

Site A is next to flats. Site B has space for a second oven and is on a night bus route.

**Recommend and justify which site Kestrel Bakes should choose.**

*Model answer, with notes.*

Net monthly cost of A = 2,600 + 900 − 1,500 = $2,000. Net monthly cost of B = 1,500 + 600 − 300 = $1,800. B is $200 a month, or $2,400 a year, cheaper. *(The figures are used, not just copied.)*

I recommend Site B. Most of Kestrel's sales come from deliveries to cafes, so the main factor is quick access to customers by road rather than footfall, and B is beside the main road, which cuts van costs. *(The factor is linked to this type of business.)*

B also has space for a second oven, so Kestrel can take on more cafe customers without moving again. Night staff can reach it by bus. *(A second reason from the case.)*

Site A earns more from the counter, but that extra $1,200 a month does not cover its higher rent and van costs. Because Kestrel bakes overnight beside flats, legal limits on noise could restrict its working hours at A. *(The rejected option is weighed, and legal controls are applied.)*

The decision depends on whether counter sales grow; if they rose sharply, A could become worthwhile. *(A condition shows judgement.)*

## Exam technique for the 7115 command words

- **Identify / State**: a short, precise answer. "Closeness to raw materials" is enough for one mark.
- **Outline**: set out the main points briefly; one sentence per point that adds a reason.
- **Explain**: give the factor, say why it matters **for this business**, and state the effect (on costs, sales or profit).
- **Consider**: review the information you are given and respond to it, usually looking at more than one side.
- **Recommend and justify**: make one clear choice, support it with evidence from the case, explain why the other option is weaker, and say what the decision depends on.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the same list of factors for a factory, a hairdresser and an online shop. Sort the business into its type first.
- Treating government grants as decisive when they are one-off and transport or rent costs continue.
- Saying "cheap labour" without considering skills or productivity.
- Forgetting that relocation brings one-off costs and risks.
- Recommending a site with no figures when figures are given, or without explaining why the other site was rejected.
- Naming a particular law instead of describing the type of control and its effect.
