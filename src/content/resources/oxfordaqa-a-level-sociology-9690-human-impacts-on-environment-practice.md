## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by the Human Exemptionalism Paradigm. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two views of the relationship between people and the natural world named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by the Jevons paradox. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two ways in which agriculture and food choices affect the environment. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two reasons why the media may give more attention to some environmental problems than to others. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for the fictional country of Brenmor. "Territorial" emissions are produced inside Brenmor; "consumption-based" emissions include those released abroad to make goods Brenmor imports.

| Indicator (fictional) | 2000 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Economic output (index, 2000 = 100) | 100 | 160 |
| Territorial emissions (million tonnes of carbon dioxide) | 400 | 360 |
| Consumption-based emissions (million tonnes of carbon dioxide) | 420 | 470 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in territorial emissions and in consumption-based emissions between 2000 and 2025. Give each answer to the nearest whole number. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain how the data could be used both to support and to criticise ecological modernisation theory. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> For generations, fishing families around the fictional Lake Seren agreed their own rules: no fishing in spawning season, nets with large holes so young fish escape, and a council of elders to settle disputes. Fish stocks stayed steady. Ten years ago, the government granted licences to a fictional company, Deepnet, which brought large boats from the capital. Deepnet does not follow the local rules. Catches have fallen sharply and some families have left the lake.

**(a)** Identify two features of the local rules that helped protect fish stocks. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item and the ideas of Hardin and Ostrom, analyse why the fish stocks of Lake Seren have declined. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which inequality affects people's access to acting sustainably. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three effects of extraction and mining on people and environments. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how Beck's idea of the risk society helps explain the climate crisis as a social problem. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate green Marxist explanations of human impacts on the environment. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent can governments and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change solve the climate crisis? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** The assumption, identified by Catton and Dunlap, that culture and technology free humans from natural limits [1], so that societies can be studied without considering their dependence on ecosystems [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: exploitation for human use [1]; romanticism [1]; conservation; media representations of ideas about the environment. **[2]**

**3.** The observation by Jevons that more efficient use of a resource [1] can make it cheaper to use and so increase total consumption [1], meaning efficiency gains may be outweighed and environmental harm may rise [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Livestock farming uses large areas of land and releases methane [1], so diets high in meat add to global heating and land clearance [1]. Demand for crops such as palm oil and soya drives deforestation [1], destroying habitats and releasing stored carbon [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Media news values favour dramatic, visual events such as wildfires and floods [1], so slow processes such as soil loss attract little coverage (Nixon's slow violence) [1]. Problems with strong claims-makers, such as scientists, campaigners and institutional sponsors, gain more attention [1], as Hannigan argues, so issues without powerful sponsors stay hidden [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Territorial: (360 - 400) / 400 x 100 = -10% (a 10% fall) [1]. Consumption-based: (470 - 420) / 420 x 100 = 11.9%, so about a 12% rise [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Support: output rose by 60% while territorial emissions fell 10% [1], suggesting decoupling of growth from emissions, as ecological modernisation theory claims [1]. Criticise: consumption-based emissions rose about 12% [1], suggesting emissions were offshored to countries producing Brenmor's imports, so harm moved rather than fell [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: no fishing in spawning season [1]; large-holed nets so young fish escape [1]; a council of elders to settle disputes. **[2]**
**(b)** Ostrom showed communities can manage commons through their own rules [1], and the Lake Seren families did this for generations, keeping stocks steady [1]. Deepnet does not follow the rules and has no long-term tie to the lake [1], so it fits Hardin's tragedy of the commons: each extra catch benefits the company while the costs are shared [1]. The decline was caused by the government overriding local institutions with outside licences [1], which supports Ostrom's view that the problem is not common ownership itself but the breakdown of community governance [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Sustainable options such as solar panels, insulation or electric vehicles need money upfront [1], so poorer households pay more for energy over time and cannot cut their emissions easily [1]. Poorer areas often lack safe public transport, recycling or affordable fresh food [1], so people in them have fewer sustainable choices available, not just less concern [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Mining clears forests and land [1], destroying habitats and the livelihoods of farmers and indigenous peoples [1]. Waste from mining pollutes rivers and soil [1], harming the health of nearby communities [1]. Extraction can bring jobs and revenue [1], but profits often go to companies and elites, which can feed conflict over resources [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Beck: modern societies face manufactured risks created by science and industry [1].
- Climate change results from industrial fossil-fuel use, a manufactured risk [1].
- Risks cross borders and classes, so no group is fully protected [1].
- The public relies on experts to see invisible risks, so trust in science becomes political [1].
- This links to construction: the problem must be defined and communicated [1].
- Organised irresponsibility: many actors contribute, so blame is hard to assign [1].
- Counter: environmental justice shows risks still fall hardest on the poor [1].
- Judgement: Beck explains why climate is a new kind of problem, but understates inequality [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Green Marxism links environmental harm to capitalism's drive for profit and growth [1].
- Foster: the metabolic rift, from Marx's analysis of soil depletion [1].
- Schnaiberg: the treadmill of production requires ever-rising output [1].
- Explains why harm continues despite green policies [1].
- Application: own-society or known example of a firm expanding fossil-fuel or mining output [1].
- Links to environmental justice: costs pushed onto the powerless (Bullard) [1].
- Counter: state socialist societies caused severe damage, such as the Aral Sea [1].
- Suggests industrialism, not only capitalism, is to blame [1].
- EMT: renewable costs have fallen and some economies decoupled [1].
- Ostrom: communities can govern resources within market societies [1].
- Green Marxism offers few practical routes beyond ending capitalism [1].
- Judgement: strong on why change is slow, weaker on what can be done now [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- The IPCC assesses the science; its reports state human-caused warming is "unequivocal" [1].
- Governments negotiate agreements such as Paris (2015): well below 2°C, pursuing 1.5°C [1].
- Governments can regulate, tax carbon, fund renewables and plan cities [1].
- Own-society application: a national energy or climate policy [1].
- The IPCC gives governments shared evidence, reducing room for denial [1].
- Counter: the IPCC does not make policy and cannot enforce it [1].
- National interests such as fossil-fuel exports limit what governments agree [1].
- Giddens's paradox: governments delay because harms seem distant [1].
- Organised doubt (Oreskes and Conway) slowed action [1].
- Green Marxists: states protect capital, so policy stops short of real change [1].
- Environmental movements push governments, as with Fridays for Future [1].
- Ostrom and Martínez-Alier: communities and grassroots action also matter [1].
- Climate justice: poorer states need finance and technology from richer ones [1].
- Own-society application: a local flood, heat or pollution response [1].
- Analysis: governments are necessary but not sufficient [1].
- Judgement: governments and the IPCC can frame and coordinate action but cannot solve the crisis alone [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate what the IPCC does (assess evidence) from what governments do (make policy); merging them loses analysis marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating social construction as denial.
- Citing Hardin without Ostrom's counter-evidence.
- Misreading a data question by comparing the wrong figures.
- Discussing EMT or green Marxism with no evidence.
- Calling the IPCC a government or law-making body.
