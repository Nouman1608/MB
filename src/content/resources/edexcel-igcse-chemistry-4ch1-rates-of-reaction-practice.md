These questions cover sub-topic 3(b) Rates of reaction of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024): points 3.9 to 3.16. Point 3.14C (reaction profile diagrams) is Paper 2 only, and questions on it are marked **(Paper 2 only)**. Take the molar volume of a gas as 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure. Revise with the [rates of reaction study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-rates-of-reaction/) and the [physical chemistry revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-physical-chemistry-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by a catalyst. **[2]**

**2.** A reaction produces 36 cm³ of gas in 90 s. Calculate the mean rate in cm³ per s. **[2]**

**3.** State why a reaction slows down as it proceeds. **[1]**

**4.** State one variable, other than the one being tested, that must be kept the same when investigating the effect of surface area on the rate of marble chips with acid. **[1]**

**5.** Name the gas given off when marble chips react with dilute hydrochloric acid. **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**6.** A student reacts marble chips with excess dilute hydrochloric acid and measures the volume of gas every 20 s.

| Time / s | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 | 120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of gas / cm³ | 0 | 20 | 32 | 40 | 44 | 45 | 45 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate between 0 and 20 s and between 20 and 40 s. **[2]**
**(b)** State the time at which the reaction stopped and explain how the data show this. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the rate decreases during the reaction. **[2]**
**(d)** The experiment is repeated with the same mass of powdered marble. Describe how the results would compare in the initial rate and in the final volume of gas. **[2]**

**7.** Calcium carbonate (Mr = 100) reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂.

**(a)** Calculate the moles of calcium carbonate in 2.5 g. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of carbon dioxide made, in cm³. **[2]**
**(c)** State the effect, if any, on this volume of using more concentrated acid. **[1]**

**8.** Explain, using collision theory, why increasing the concentration of a reactant in solution increases the rate of reaction. **[3]**

**9.** Explain, using collision theory, why increasing the temperature increases the rate of reaction. **[3]**

**10.** Sodium thiosulfate reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to give a cloudy precipitate of sulfur. A student times how long a cross takes to disappear.

| Temperature / °C | 25 | 35 |
|---|---|---|
| Time / s | 60 | 28 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate (1 / time) at each temperature. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times faster the reaction is at 35 °C than at 25 °C. **[1]**
**(c)** State two things that must be kept constant for a fair comparison. **[2]**

**11.** (Paper 2 only) Draw a reaction profile for an exothermic reaction, both with and without a catalyst. Label the axes, the activation energy for each pathway and ΔH. **[4]**

**12.** Hydrogen peroxide decomposes to water and oxygen. Describe an experiment to find which of three solids, X, Y and Z, is the best catalyst, and how you could show that the best catalyst has not been used up. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** A substance that increases the rate of a reaction [1] and is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction [1].

**2.** Rate = 36 / 90 [1] = 0.40 cm³ per s [1].

**3.** The concentration of the reactants decreases as they are used up [1].

**4.** Any one from: the mass of marble chips; the volume of acid; the concentration of acid; the temperature [1].

**5.** Carbon dioxide [1].

**6. (a)** 0 to 20 s: 20 / 20 = 1.0 cm³ per s [1]. 20 to 40 s: 12 / 20 = 0.60 cm³ per s [1].
**(b)** 100 s [1]; the volume stays at 45 cm³ from 100 s onwards, so no more gas is made [1].
**(c)** The concentration of the acid decreases as it is used up (or the surface area of the marble chips falls) [1], so there are fewer collisions per second between reacting particles [1].
**(d)** The initial rate would be faster, so the line is steeper at the start [1]; the final volume would be the same, 45 cm³, because the amount of calcium carbonate is the same [1].

**7. (a)** Moles = 2.5 / 100 = 0.025 mol [1].
**(b)** Moles of CO₂ = 0.025 mol (1 : 1 ratio) [1]. Volume = 0.025 x 24,000 = 600 cm³ [1].
**(c)** None: the amount of calcium carbonate is the limiting factor, so the volume is the same [1].

**8.** More particles of the reactant in the same volume of solution [1], so the particles are closer together and collide more often [1], so there are more successful collisions per second and the rate increases [1].

**9.** The particles move faster, so they collide more often [1]. A greater proportion of collisions has energy equal to or greater than the activation energy [1], so there are more successful collisions per second and the rate increases [1].

**10. (a)** 25 °C: 1 / 60 = 0.0167 per s [1]. 35 °C: 1 / 28 = 0.0357 per s [1].
**(b)** 0.0357 / 0.0167 = 2.1 times (2.14) [1].
**(c)** Any two from: the volume of thiosulfate solution; its concentration; the volume and concentration of acid; the same cross and depth of liquid [2].

**11.** Axes labelled energy and progress of reaction [1]. Reactants level above the products level, with a peak above both [1]. A lower peak for the catalysed route, with the same start and end levels; the activation energy shown from the reactants to each peak and labelled [1]. ΔH shown as a downward arrow between the reactant and product levels, labelled negative, and the same for both routes [1].

**12.** Measure the same volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide into a flask connected to a gas syringe [1]. Add the same mass of solid X and start the stopwatch [1]. Record the volume of oxygen at regular times, or the time to collect a fixed volume [1]. Repeat for Y and Z (and with no solid as a control), keeping everything else the same; the best catalyst gives the fastest rate (steepest line or shortest time) [1]. To show it is not used up, filter off the solid, wash and dry it, and weigh it; the mass is the same as at the start [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that a higher temperature only "makes particles collide more" and omitting the activation energy.
- Saying that a higher concentration gives particles "more energy".
- Using a different mass of marble in the "fair test" and then comparing results.
- Giving the catalysed reaction profile a different ΔH.
- Stating that the final volume of gas increases when the rate increases.
- Dividing by the wrong interval when calculating mean rate.
