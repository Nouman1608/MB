This guide goes deeper into content item 1.1.1, The dynamic nature of business, in Topic 1.1 Enterprise and entrepreneurship of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. Topic 1.1 belongs to Theme 1, Investigating small business, which is assessed in Paper 1 (1BS0/01). The broader [Enterprise and entrepreneurship study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-enterprise-and-entrepreneurship/) introduces all of Topic 1.1; this page works on 1.1.1 alone, with new cases and the reasoning chains that turn a list of reasons into marks. Every business named here is invented.

Companion pages: the [1.1.1 revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-the-dynamic-nature-of-business-revision-notes/) and the [1.1.1 practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-the-dynamic-nature-of-business-practice/).

## What 1.1.1 asks you to know

| Strand | Specification wording | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| Why ideas come about | changes in technology | Explain how a new technology creates an opening for a business |
| Why ideas come about | changes in what consumers want | Explain how shifting tastes or habits create an opening |
| Why ideas come about | products and services becoming obsolete | Explain how the decline of one product leaves a need to be met |
| How ideas come about | original ideas | Weigh up offering something new |
| How ideas come about | adapting existing products/services/ideas | Weigh up changing something already on sale |

The word "dynamic" is the key to the whole item. Markets never stand still, so the opportunities open to a business today are not the ones that were open five years ago, and they will not be the same in five years' time. The same forces that create chances for new businesses also threaten established ones that fail to respond.

## Part 1: the "why" and the "how" are different questions

Students often blur the two strands, so keep them apart.

- **Why** a new idea comes about is the **trigger**: something has changed in the world outside the business. The specification gives three triggers.
- **How** a new idea comes about is the **route**: what the entrepreneur actually does with the opportunity. Either they create something original, or they adapt something that already exists.

Any real idea has one of each. A trigger with no route is just a change; a route with no trigger is a product nobody asked for. In a case study, look for both.

## Part 2: the three triggers in more depth

### Changes in technology

Technology changes can work in two ways.

1. **They make a new product possible.** Before a technology exists, a product built on it cannot be offered at all.
2. **They make an existing product cheaper, quicker or easier to supply.** A small firm may now be able to do something that once needed a large factory or a big budget.

Technology can also change how a business reaches customers. Online ordering, card readers that plug into a phone and cheap website builders have lowered the cost of starting up. That is why a technology change can lead to many new small businesses at once.

### Changes in what consumers want

Consumers' wants shift because of changes in fashion, health concerns, concern for the environment, working patterns and spending power. When a want grows faster than existing businesses can serve it, a gap opens. When a want fades, businesses relying on it lose sales.

A change in wants is often gradual, so the best evidence in a case study is a trend: "more customers are asking for", "sales of X have risen each year", "younger customers prefer".

### Products and services becoming obsolete

A product or service is obsolete when it is out of date and few people still want it, usually because something newer does the job better. Obsolescence is usually a **result** of the other two triggers: a new technology or a change in wants makes the old product unattractive. Two points matter for exam answers.

- The **need** that the old product met often still exists. People still want to listen to music, keep records or travel; they just want to do it differently.
- The business selling the obsolete product has a choice: decline with it, or move into whatever replaces it.

## Part 3: the two routes in more depth

### Original ideas

An original idea offers something customers have not been able to buy before. It can give the first business in a market a head start: it can build a name and loyal customers before rivals arrive. Against that, nobody can be sure customers will buy, and the business may have to spend heavily to explain the product.

### Adapting existing products, services or ideas

Adapting is far more common. There are several ways to adapt.

| What is changed | Invented example |
|---|---|
| The product itself | A soap maker adds a refill pouch so customers reuse the bottle |
| Who it is aimed at | A climbing wall runs sessions for under-fives |
| Where or how it is delivered | A driving instructor teaches theory by video call |
| How customers pay | A tool shop rents tools by the day instead of selling them |

An adapted idea starts with evidence that people want the basic product, which lowers the risk. The difficulty is standing out, because rivals already exist and can copy the change.

## Worked example 1: Saltmarsh Keys

Saltmarsh Keys is a one-person locksmith. Most of its income came from cutting keys and replacing door locks. Over three years, more householders fitted smart locks that open with a phone app, and key-cutting jobs fell.

Annotated analysis:

- **Trigger:** a change in technology (app-controlled smart locks).
- **Obsolescence:** traditional key-cutting is becoming out of date for some customers.
- **Need that remains:** householders still want secure doors that they can open easily.
- **Route:** the owner adapts the service, training to fit and repair smart locks and set up the app for older customers who find it confusing.
- **Why the route suits the business:** it uses skills, a van and a customer list the owner already has, so it is cheaper and less risky than starting from scratch.

## Worked example 2: Thimble Row Alterations

Thimble Row is a clothing alteration shop. Its owner notices that more customers bring clothes to be mended rather than shortened, and several mention wanting to buy fewer new clothes for environmental reasons.

- **Trigger:** a change in what consumers want (keeping clothes longer).
- **Route:** adapting an existing service. The shop launches a "repair box" service: customers post damaged clothes and receive them back mended.
- **What makes it adapted, not original:** clothing repair already exists; the change is in how it is delivered (by post) and which customers it reaches (people who live too far away to visit).

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Explain):** Explain one reason why changes in technology can lead to new business ideas. **[3]**

> New technology can allow products to be made that were not possible before (statement). This means entrepreneurs can offer a product that meets a need in a new way, such as booking services through an app (first expansion). As the first businesses to offer it, they may win customers before larger rivals react (second expansion).

Why it works: Appendix 1 of the specification says Explain needs a statement of fact with two further expansion points, and that there is no context in these questions. Each sentence builds on the one before; none repeats it.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a change in technology "obsolescence". Technology is the trigger; obsolescence is what happens to the old product.
- Saying an adapted idea is risk-free. It is lower-risk than an original one, not risk-free.
- Writing "the business can adapt" without saying what is changed: the product, the customer, the place or the method.
- Treating every new product as original. If the basic product already exists, it is an adaptation.
- Forgetting the threat side. Dynamic change can close a business as well as start one.
- Listing all three triggers when the question asks for one reason.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** questions in Paper 1 point to the case: copy the trigger or route from the text in no more than a sentence.
- **Outline** needs two linked points in the context given, such as the trigger and the opportunity it creates for that business.
- **Analyse** in a case study rewards a chain: trigger, effect on customers, opportunity or threat for this business, consequence for sales or survival.
- **Evaluate** on this item often asks whether an owner should adapt or start something new. Weigh lower risk and proven demand against the chance to stand out, then decide using a detail from the case, such as the owner's skills or savings.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three reasons new business ideas come about in Edexcel GCSE Business?**
Changes in technology, changes in what consumers want, and products and services becoming obsolete.

**Is obsolescence separate from technology change?**
It is listed separately, but it is usually caused by a technology change or a change in what consumers want. Say which one caused it.

**What is the difference between an original idea and adapting an existing idea?**
An original idea offers something not available before. Adapting changes something already on sale, such as its design, its target customers, or where and how it is supplied.
