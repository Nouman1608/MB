Education is assessed in Paper 1: The sociology of families and education, a 1 hour 45 minute written exam worth 100 marks and 50% of the GCSE. All towns, schools and data below are fictional.

## Questions

**1.** In Halsey, Heath and Ridge's study, which class group included clerical and sales workers?

A. Service class
B. Intermediate class
C. Working class
D. Upper class

**[1]**

**2.** Explain what Parsons meant by "particularistic standards". **[2]**

**3.** Identify and explain one way in which education may serve the needs of the economy. **[3]**

**4.** Describe what is meant by de-schooling and explain one way it differs from home schooling. **[3]**

**5.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Elderford (a fictional town): school leavers aged 18 entering university, by parents' occupation group, 2025**

| Occupation group | School leavers | Entered university |
|---|---|---|
| Service class | 180 | 117 |
| Intermediate class | 240 | 84 |
| Working class | 300 | 45 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of intermediate-class school leavers who entered university. **[1]**
**(b)** How many times more likely were service-class leavers to enter university than working-class leavers? Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how far the Elderford data support the findings of Halsey, Heath and Ridge. **[3]**

**6.** Read the item.

> Marston Vale (a fictional town) has two secondary schools. Ashcroft School tops the local league table. Many professional families rent or buy homes near Ashcroft so their children can get a place. Brook Lane School's results have fallen, and its new head teacher has just reintroduced setting in every subject and started a "gifted and talented" programme to attract more able pupils.

Using the item and your knowledge of Ball's work on parental choice and competition between schools, explain what is happening in Marston Vale. **[4]**

**7.** Read the item.

> At Kingsmere Academy (a fictional school), Year 8 is split into an upper band and a lower band. A teacher says: "In the upper band I can push them on to GCSE work. In the lower band I mostly keep them quiet." Most upper band pupils have parents in professional jobs.

Using the item and Ball's study of Beachside Comprehensive, explain how banding at Kingsmere could affect pupils' achievement. **[4]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which, according to feminists, the hidden curriculum may reproduce gender roles. **[4]**

**9.** Explain two ways in which Willis's study of the lads challenges Bowles and Gintis's correspondence principle. **[4]**

**10.** Read the item.

> A government leaflet in Tarnwick (a fictional country) says: "Our schools reward talent and hard work. Whoever you are, you can succeed." Critics point out that pupils from wealthy families are still far more likely to reach university.

Discuss how far sociologists would agree that schools operate on meritocratic principles. **[12]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that Bowles and Gintis's correspondence principle explains the relationship between education and capitalism today. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far processes within schools, such as setting, labelling and pupil subcultures, explain differences in achievement between girls and boys. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B. Intermediate class [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Learn the specification's own list of who is in each group; the options are built to catch students who guess from job status.

**2.** Standards that apply to a child as a particular person, because of their relationship to the family [1], so the child is not judged by rules that apply to everyone, unlike universalistic standards in school [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark comes from the contrast with universalistic standards or the family setting, not from repeating "particular".

**3.** Education teaches skills and qualifications [1], such as literacy, numeracy or technical courses [1], so that the economy has a workforce able to do specialised jobs [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs the link to the economy spelled out; a list of subjects alone scores one mark.

**4.** De-schooling is the idea that formal schooling should be replaced altogether [1], linked to Illich, who argued schools make people depend on institutions [1]. Home schooling still provides a planned education, but at home instead of at school, so it moves schooling rather than rejecting it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Students often define both terms but never state the difference; the third mark needs a clear "whereas".

**5. (a)** 84 ÷ 240 × 100 = 35% [1]
**(b)** Service class: 117 ÷ 180 = 65%; working class: 45 ÷ 300 = 15% [1]. 65 ÷ 15 = about 4.3 times as likely [1].
**(c)** The data support Halsey because entry falls steadily from service to intermediate to working class (65%, 35%, 15%) [1], showing clear class inequality in education, as Halsey found [1]. However, the gap is smaller than Halsey's eleven times for university, and the Elderford data come from one fictional town and include girls, whereas Halsey's sample was male only [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c), quote figures from the table; a general point about class with no numbers will not reach full marks.

**6.** Marston Vale shows the marketisation of education, where league tables and parental choice create competition between schools [1]. Professional families moving near Ashcroft show that middle-class parents are better placed to use choice, so the more successful school gains more advantaged pupils [1]. Brook Lane's return to setting matches Ball's finding that competition puts pressure on schools to reintroduce streaming and setting [1]. Its gifted and talented scheme shows a school focusing on the more able, who improve its league table position, which may leave lower-attaining pupils with less attention [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point should name a detail from the item (house moves, setting, the gifted programme) and tie it to Ball, or it reads as general knowledge.

**7.** Ball found that at Beachside middle-class pupils were more likely to be placed in band 1, which received higher teacher expectations [1]. At Kingsmere the upper band is pushed towards GCSE work while the lower band is mainly controlled, so lower band pupils have less chance to learn the content [1]. Pupils in the lower band may come to see themselves as less able and become anti-school, as Ball found, which can create a self-fulfilling prophecy [1]. Because the upper band is mostly from professional families, banding could widen class differences in achievement [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the teacher's quotation; it is the evidence of different expectations that turns a description of Ball into application.

**8.** First, teachers may expect different behaviour from boys and girls, for example praising girls for being quiet and neat [1], which teaches girls to be passive and helpful, matching traditional female roles [1]. Second, pupils may be steered into gendered subjects through career advice or role models, such as few female physics teachers [1], so girls and boys head towards different and unequal jobs, which feminists see as part of the glass ceiling [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate mechanisms are needed; two examples of the same idea (teacher attention) earn only half the marks.

**9.** First, Bowles and Gintis claim schools produce docile, obedient workers [1], but Willis's lads were neither docile nor obedient and openly resisted teachers [1]. Second, Bowles and Gintis see a direct link between the economy and how schools operate [1], but Willis rejects a direct link, arguing education is not a very successful agency of socialisation and can have unintended consequences, even though the lads' rejection of school still led them into manual work [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers note that Willis is also Marxist, so the challenge is about how capitalism is served, not whether it is.

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Meritocracy means rewards depend on ability and effort, not background, as the leaflet claims [1].
- Parsons argues schools operate on meritocratic principles, with status achieved through merit [1].
- Pupils are judged by universalistic standards, the same rules for all, which supports the leaflet [1].
- Functionalists see examinations as a fair way to select people for future roles [1].
- Against: Bowles and Gintis reject meritocracy, arguing class background is the main influence on attainment [1].
- The critics in the item, noting wealthy pupils reach university more often, support the Marxist view [1].
- Halsey, Heath and Ridge found service-class boys eleven times as likely to reach university as working-class boys [1].
- Gewirtz, Ball and Bowe argued that middle-class parents are better placed to use parental choice, so outcomes reflect resources [1].
- Ball's Beachside study found middle-class pupils were more likely to be placed in the top band, so selection inside school is not purely on merit [1].
- Feminists add that gendered subject choice means equal results do not lead to equal careers [1].
- Evaluation: Halsey's sample was male only and born before 1953, so its figures may overstate inequality today [1].
- Judgement: schools contain meritocratic features such as exams, but class and gender still shape outcomes, so meritocracy is only partly achieved [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Use the item: the leaflet gives you the functionalist claim and the critics give you the Marxist reply, so build both into your argument.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- The correspondence principle states that relationships at school mirror relationships at work [1].
- Bowles and Gintis argue schools reproduce labour power for capitalism [1].
- Example: pupils work for grades as workers work for wages, learning to value external rewards [1].
- The school hierarchy of head, teachers and pupils mirrors managers and workers, teaching obedience [1].
- They argue this creates a workforce too divided to challenge management [1].
- Continuing class gaps in attainment support their rejection of meritocracy [1].
- Against: Willis found the lads resisted school, so socialisation into obedience often fails [1].
- Willis argues there is no direct link between economy and school, and outcomes can be unintended [1].
- Functionalists such as Parsons accept that school prepares pupils for work, but see this as fair role selection [1].
- Feminists argue the hidden curriculum also prepares girls and boys for unequal gender roles, which the theory ignores [1].
- The study was based on 1970s American schools, and many jobs today value creativity and teamwork over obedience [1].
- Judgement: the principle still helps explain how schools reward conformity, but it overstates how passive pupils are and ignores gender, so it explains only part of the relationship [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "Today" in the question needs a point about change since the 1970s; answers that never mention it struggle to reach the top level.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Girls now outperform boys at most levels, including GCSE [1].
- Labelling: if teachers expect boys to be disruptive, boys may be treated as troublemakers [1].
- This can lead to a self-fulfilling prophecy, where boys come to match the label and underachieve [1].
- Setting: if boys are placed in lower sets more often, they may receive less demanding work, as Ball found for lower bands [1].
- Pupil subcultures: Willis's lads gained status from rejecting school, showing how a masculine counter culture can lower achievement [1].
- Interactionists would say these classroom processes explain the gap from the inside [1].
- Against: external factors also matter, such as the impact of feminism on girls' ambitions [1].
- Sharpe found girls' priorities moved from marriage towards careers between the 1970s and the 1990s [1].
- Fewer traditional manual jobs for boys may weaken their motivation, which is an economic, not school-based, factor [1].
- Feminists note that gendered subject choice continues, so girls' higher results do not remove inequality [1].
- Evaluation: Willis studied twelve working-class boys, so his findings cannot explain gender gaps across all classes [1].
- Judgement: in-school processes help explain how boys fall behind, but changes outside school explain why girls' achievement rose, so both are needed [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Willis is about class as well as masculinity; credit comes from applying his study to gender carefully, not from retelling it in full.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a correct idea with the wrong writer, such as linking Durkheim to the correspondence principle.
- In data questions, describing a pattern without quoting figures from the table.
- Getting the calculation right but forgetting to show the working when asked.
- Using Ball's Beachside study for a parental choice question, or his 1994 study for a teacher expectations question.
- On item-based questions, explaining the theory well but never using the item's details.
- Defining both terms in a "difference" question without stating the difference.
- In "discuss how far" questions, listing one side, or giving both sides with no final judgement.
- Treating Willis as a functionalist or as supporting the idea of docile workers.
