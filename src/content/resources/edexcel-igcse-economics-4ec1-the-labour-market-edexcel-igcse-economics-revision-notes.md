These notes condense section 1.2.5, The labour market, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026, examined in Paper 1 with a single tier of entry. All figures are invented. For explanations and worked cases, read the [labour market study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-labour-market-edexcel-igcse-economics/); to practise, use the [labour market practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-the-labour-market-edexcel-igcse-economics-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demand for labour | Number of workers firms are willing and able to employ at each wage |
| Supply of labour | Number of people willing and able to work at each wage |
| Derived demand | Demand for labour that comes from demand for the final product |
| Equilibrium wage | Wage at which demand for labour equals supply of labour |
| Human capital | Knowledge, skills and experience of workers |
| Geographical mobility | Ability to move to a different place for work |
| Occupational mobility | Ability to move into a different type of work |
| Female participation | Share of women in, or seeking, paid work |
| Trade union | Organisation of workers that bargains with employers for its members |
| Collective bargaining | Negotiation between a union and employers on pay and conditions |

## Demand for labour: three factors

| Factor | Shifts demand right when… | Shifts demand left when… |
|---|---|---|
| Demand for the final product (derived demand) | Demand for the product rises | Demand for the product falls |
| Substitutes, including machines | Machines become dearer or less able to do the job | Machines become cheaper or better |
| Productivity of the workforce | Workers become more productive | Productivity falls |

## Supply of labour: eight factors

| Factor | Increases supply | Decreases supply |
|---|---|---|
| Population size | Population grows | Population falls |
| Migration | Immigration | Emigration |
| Age distribution | More people of working age | More people over retirement age, or more children |
| Retirement age | Raised | Lowered |
| School-leaving age | Lowered | Raised (short run) |
| Female participation | Rises (childcare, flexible work) | Falls |
| Skills and qualifications | More people qualified for the job | Fewer qualified |
| Mobility | Workers can move place or job easily | Barriers such as housing costs or lack of retraining |

## Quantity and quality for business

- **Quantity:** enough workers to meet orders; shortages mean lost sales, overtime or higher wages.
- **Quality:** skills, health and motivation raise productivity and lower unit costs.
- **Education and training** raise human capital, so the quality of labour rises.

*Drill (invented):* wage 16 an hour; output rises from 8 to 10 units an hour after training. Labour cost per unit falls from 16 ÷ 8 = 2.00 to 16 ÷ 10 = **1.60**, a fall of **20%**.

## Diagram checklist

- Vertical axis: wage rate. Horizontal axis: quantity of labour (employment).
- D slopes down; S slopes up.
- Equilibrium wage W and employment Q where D = S.
- For a shift: draw D1 or S1, mark W1 and Q1, show the direction with an arrow.

| Shift | Wage | Employment |
|---|---|---|
| D right | Up | Up |
| D left | Down | Down |
| S right | Down | Up |
| S left | Up | Down |

**Shortage and surplus:** below equilibrium, demand exceeds supply (shortage); above it, supply exceeds demand (surplus).

## Trade unions

- Aim: improve working conditions (safety, hours, holidays, fair treatment) and increase wages.
- Method: collective bargaining, backed by the threat of industrial action.
- Possible effects: higher pay and better conditions for members; possibly fewer jobs if the wage is pushed above equilibrium; disruption from strikes.
- Deciding factor: whether productivity rises with pay, and whether firms can pass on costs.

*Drill (invented):* at the equilibrium wage of 10, firms employ 500; a union wins a wage of 12, at which firms demand 440. Jobs fall by 60, or **12%**; the 440 who stay earn 2 more an hour.

## Exam traps

- **Movement versus shift:** a wage change moves along the curves.
- **Axes:** wage on the vertical axis.
- **Derived demand:** link labour demand to demand for the product.
- **School-leaving age raised:** supply falls in the short run.
- **Unions:** effects depend on where the wage is set; avoid "always".
- **Name the factor** from the specification list, then the curve and the direction.

## Quick self-test

1. Define derived demand.
2. Give the three factors in the specification that affect the demand for labour.
3. Give four factors that affect the supply of labour.
4. What happens to the supply of labour if the retirement age is raised?
5. What is human capital?
6. Why does the quality of labour matter to a business?
7. Demand for restaurant meals rises. What happens to the wage and employment of chefs?
8. Many nurses emigrate. What happens to the wage and employment of nurses at home?
9. Name the axes on a labour market diagram.
10. State two aims of a trade union.
11. At a wage of 8, firms demand 900 workers and 700 are supplied. Is there a shortage or surplus, and of how many?
12. Why might a union's pay rise not reduce employment?

### Answers

1. Demand for labour that comes from demand for the final product it produces.
2. Demand for the final product; availability of substitutes, including machines; productivity of the workforce.
3. Any four: population size, migration, age distribution, retirement age, school-leaving age, female participation, skills and qualifications, ability to move location or type of work.
4. It increases.
5. The knowledge, skills and experience of workers.
6. Higher-quality workers produce more and make fewer errors, lowering unit costs and improving quality.
7. Demand for chefs shifts right: wage rises, employment rises.
8. Supply of nurses shifts left: wage rises, employment falls.
9. Vertical: wage rate. Horizontal: quantity of labour (employment).
10. Higher wages; better working conditions.
11. A shortage of 900 − 700 = **200** workers.
12. If productivity rises enough to cover the higher wage, or if firms can pass the cost on in prices.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting a curve for a change in the wage.
- Unlabelled or swapped axes.
- Listing a supply factor without saying which way supply shifts.
- Union answers that ignore the possible loss of jobs, or assume it is certain.
