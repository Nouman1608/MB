## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Budget deficit | Government spending exceeds revenue in a year (a flow) |
| National debt | Stock of outstanding government borrowing on one date |
| Primary deficit | Deficit before interest payments on debt |
| Debt-to-GDP ratio | Debt divided by GDP, the usual measure of the burden |
| Automatic stabiliser | Part of the budget that offsets output swings with no new decision |
| Discretionary policy | Deliberate change in government spending or taxes |
| Crowding out | Government borrowing or spending that reduces private spending |
| Crowding in | Government spending that raises private spending |
| Fiscal rule | A commitment that limits borrowing, the deficit or debt |

## Deficit and debt: must-know distinction

- Deficit = this year. Debt = everything owed. Deficit adds to debt; a surplus can repay it.
- Debt can rise in money terms while the ratio to GDP is flat or falling, if GDP grows fast enough.
- The national debt is held by domestic and overseas lenders, and may be quoted gross or net of financial assets.

## Financing a deficit

| Method | Effect | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Sell bonds and bills to the public | Adds to debt, pays interest | May push up interest rates |
| Borrow from the central bank | Money creation | Inflation if money grows faster than output |
| Sell state assets | One-off cash, no new debt | Loses future income |
| Draw on cash balances or reserves | Quick | Cannot last |

Overseas buyers: interest leaves the country. Short-term bills: refinancing risk if rates rise.

## Correcting a deficit or surplus

Methods: raise taxes, cut spending, or rely on growth. Surplus: cut taxes, raise spending, repay debt.

Evaluation chain: tighten policy, GDP falls through the multiplier, tax revenue falls, benefits rise, so the deficit shrinks by less than the cut.

**Method in steps (cut in spending).**
1. Multiplier = 1 / (marginal propensity to save + marginal tax rate + marginal propensity to import).
2. Fall in GDP = cut x multiplier.
3. Fall in tax = marginal tax rate x fall in GDP. Add any rise in benefits.
4. Net deficit reduction = cut - fall in tax - rise in benefits.

**Worked reminder.** Leakages 0.1 + 0.25 + 0.15 = 0.5, multiplier 2. Cut spending by 20. GDP falls 40. Tax falls 10. Benefits rise 2. Deficit falls 8, which is 40% of the cut.

Judge by: timing in the cycle, composition (capital cuts hurt growth, benefit cuts hit the poor, tax rises hit incentives), size of the multiplier, and the size of the deficit.

## Consequences of government debt

| Costs | Benefits or offsets |
|---|---|
| Interest payments: opportunity cost | Funds investment that raises growth and revenue |
| Higher future taxes: burden on later generations | Supports demand in a recession |
| Crowding out of private investment | Can be small if debt is domestic and in own currency |
| Loss of lender confidence, higher yields, lower rating | Manageable if growth exceeds the interest rate |
| Interest leaving the country | |
| Inflation risk if money is created | |

Judgement depends on the ratio to GDP, use of the borrowing (capital or current), who holds it, currency, and interest rate relative to growth.

**Debt rule of thumb.** New ratio = old ratio x (1 + r) / (1 + g) + primary deficit as a share of GDP. With r = 2.5%, g = 5%, ratio 0.80 and primary deficit 1.90% of GDP: 0.7810 + 0.0190 = 0.8000. With r = 4.5%: 0.8152. When r is above g, a primary surplus is needed to hold the ratio.

## Automatic stabilisers

- Boom: tax receipts rise faster than income, benefits fall, budget moves to surplus, demand cools.
- Recession: receipts fall, benefits rise, deficit widens, demand supported.
- Stronger with progressive tax and generous benefits. No time lag in decisions. They soften the cycle but do not remove it.

**Worked reminder.** Leakages without tax 0.25, multiplier 4. With tax 0.5, multiplier 2. A shock of -10 gives -40 without tax and -20 with it. If GDP falls 20, tax falls 5, benefits rise 1, so the deficit widens by 6.

## Discretionary fiscal policy

- Expansionary: more spending or lower taxes, AD shifts right, for a negative output gap or high unemployment.
- Contractionary: AD shifts left, for a positive output gap or inflation pressure.
- Capital and education spending can also raise long-run aggregate supply.
- Limits: time lags (recognition, decision, implementation, impact), uncertain multiplier, political motives, debt.

**Worked reminder.** Spending +12, multiplier 2: GDP +24, tax +6, net cost 6.

## Crowding out and crowding in

| Crowding out | Crowding in |
|---|---|
| More bonds raise interest rates, private investment falls | Higher demand and sales lift confidence |
| Resource squeeze near full capacity | Accelerator effect raises investment |
| Higher rate may raise the exchange rate and cut net exports | Public infrastructure raises returns on private projects |
| Weak with spare capacity, high saving, or low central bank rates | Needs a recovering economy and credible policy |

**Worked reminder.** Spending +12 raises the interest rate by 0.5 points; investment falls by 8 per point, so 4. Net injection 8, GDP +16 instead of +24. One third crowded out. If crowding in adds 3, the net injection is 11, so GDP +22.

## Effectiveness of fiscal policy

- Strengths: direct effect on demand, targeted, known multiplier effect.
- Weaknesses: lags, crowding out, uncertain multiplier, debt limits room, expectations of future tax rises, conflicts between objectives.
- Judge using spare capacity, the multiplier, type of spending, and the time frame.

## Fiscal rules

- Types: deficit limit, debt ceiling, golden rule (borrow only to invest).
- Influence behaviour: governments must prioritise, lenders may charge less, firms and households may be more certain of future taxes.
- Strengths: credibility, discipline, protection against short-term politics.
- Weaknesses: rigid in a recession, escape clauses, accounting tricks, capital spending easily cut, rules can conflict with each other.

**Worked reminder.** Current spending 430, capital 30, revenue 400, GDP 1,500, debt 1,200. Current deficit 30. Total deficit 60 (4.0% of GDP). Borrowing 60 is above capital spending of 30, so the golden rule fails. A 3% limit allows 45, so 15 of correction. Debt of 80% breaks a 60% ceiling.

## Quick self-test

1. State the difference between a budget deficit and the national debt.
2. A government spends 330 and raises 300, and pays 20 of that spending as debt interest. What are the deficit and the primary deficit?
3. Name two ways to finance a deficit.
4. Marginal propensity to save 0.1, marginal tax rate 0.3, marginal propensity to import 0.1. Find the multiplier.
5. Using that multiplier, spending is cut by 10. Find the fall in GDP and the fall in tax revenue.
6. Why can a spending cut leave the deficit falling by less than the cut?
7. Give one example of an automatic stabiliser and say how it works in a boom.
8. Explain crowding out in one sentence.
9. Name one condition under which crowding in is more likely.
10. Opening debt is 500, GDP 1,000 and the deficit 40. If GDP grows to 1,050, what is the closing debt ratio?
11. Give one strength and one weakness of a fiscal rule.

### Answers

1. The deficit is a flow in one year; the national debt is the accumulated stock of borrowing.
2. Deficit 30; primary deficit 10.
3. Selling bonds or bills; borrowing from the central bank; selling state assets (any two).
4. Leakages 0.5; multiplier 2.
5. GDP falls 20; tax falls 6.
6. The fall in GDP reduces tax receipts and raises benefit payments, offsetting part of the saving.
7. Progressive income tax: receipts rise faster than income in a boom, which reduces disposable income growth and cools demand.
8. Government borrowing pushes up interest rates and reduces private investment.
9. Spare capacity, rising confidence or public investment that raises private returns (any one).
10. Closing debt 540; 540 / 1,050 = 51.4%.
11. Strength: credibility with lenders. Weakness: rigidity in a recession (other answers possible).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up deficit and debt, especially in definitions.
- Calculating the effect of a spending cut without allowing for the tax and benefit response.
- Describing an automatic stabiliser as a government decision.
- Stating that crowding out always happens, without mentioning spare capacity.
- Listing consequences of debt without judging which matter most and why.
- Drawing or describing AD shifts without saying why aggregate demand moves.
- Evaluating fiscal rules with only generalities such as "they can be broken".
- Forgetting to link a conclusion to the question's context or time frame.
