These questions cover **3.1.5 The market mechanism, market failure and government intervention in markets** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3. Where a diagram is asked for, use a demand and supply diagram; MSC/MSB diagrams are not required at AS. All markets and figures are invented. Revise first with the [3.1.5 study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-market-mechanism-market-failure-and-government-intervention-aqa-aslevel-economics/) and the [3.1.5 revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-market-mechanism-market-failure-and-government-intervention-aqa-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** State the two characteristics of a pure public good. **[2]**

**2.** Which one of the following is a positive externality in production? **[1]**

A: A beekeeper's bees pollinate a neighbouring farmer's fruit trees

B: A smoker's habit harms people nearby

C: A factory's noise disturbs local residents

D: A person's vaccination protects others from infection

**3.** Explain the signalling function of prices. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why classifying a good as a merit good depends on a value judgement. **[2]**

**5.** Explain one reason why the geographical immobility of labour can lead to market failure. **[2]**

**6.** In the market for milk, Qd = 500 - 25P and Qs = -100 + 25P. The government sets a minimum price of 14.

**(a)** Calculate the free-market equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the surplus at the minimum price. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** In a market for sugary drinks, Qd = 1,200 - 40P and Qs = -300 + 60P, with quantity in thousands of crates a week and price in pounds per crate.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** The government imposes a tax of 5 per unit. Calculate the new equilibrium. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate how much of the tax per unit is paid by consumers and by producers, and the total tax revenue. **[2]**

**8.** Street lighting is usually paid for by local government.

**(a)** Explain why the free market is unlikely to provide street lighting. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain, with an example, how technological change can turn a public good into a quasi-public good. **[2]**

**9.** Explain, with the help of a demand and supply diagram, why a free market is likely to under-consume flu vaccinations. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how asymmetric information in the market for used cars can lead to market failure, and suggest one way the government could reduce the problem. **[4]**

**11.** To cut gas use, a government subsidises electric heaters. Most of the country's electricity is generated in gas-fired power stations, and the subsidy is paid through a new office that checks every claim. Explain two possible sources of government failure in this policy. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether a tax on plastic packaging is a better way to reduce plastic pollution than a ban on some types of packaging. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Non-rival: one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others [1]. Non-excludable: people who do not pay cannot be prevented from benefiting [1]. *Total: 2.*

**2.** **A** [1]. The beekeeper's production benefits a third party. B and C are negative externalities; D is a positive externality in consumption. *Total: 1.*

**3.** Changes in price give information to buyers and sellers about changes in market conditions [1]. For example, a rising price shows producers that consumers want more of the good, so resources should move towards it [1]. *Total: 2.*

**4.** A merit good is one judged to be better for people than they realise, so they under-consume it [1]. Deciding what is "better for people" is a normative opinion, not a fact that can be tested, so different people may classify the same good differently [1]. *Total: 2.*

**5.** Workers who lose jobs in a declining region may be unable to move to regions with vacancies because of housing costs or family ties [1]. Labour stays unemployed while vacancies elsewhere go unfilled, so resources are misallocated [1]. *Total: 2.*

**6. (a)** 500 - 25P = -100 + 25P gives 600 = 50P, so **P = 12** [1] and **Q = 200** [1].

**(b)** At 14: Qd = 500 - 350 = 150 and Qs = -100 + 350 = 250 [1], a **surplus of 100** [1]. *Total: 4.*

**7. (a)** 1,200 - 40P = -300 + 60P gives 1,500 = 100P, so **P = 15** [1] and **Q = 600** [1].

**(b)** Qs = -300 + 60(P - 5) = -600 + 60P [1]. Then 1,800 = 100P, so **P = 18** and **Q = 480** [1].

**(c)** Consumers pay 18 - 15 = **3** per unit and producers keep 18 - 5 = 13, so they bear **2** [1]. Revenue = 5 x 480 = **2,400** [1]. *Total: 6.*

**8. (a)** Street lighting is non-excludable: once a street is lit, people who have not paid cannot be stopped from benefiting [1]. It is non-rival: one more person walking down the street does not reduce the light available to others [1]. So people have an incentive to free ride, waiting for others to pay [1]. A private firm could not collect enough revenue to cover its costs, so the market is missing, which is complete market failure [1].

**(b)** Technology can make a good excludable [1]. For example, encrypted television signals mean broadcasters can stop people who have not subscribed from watching, so broadcasting can be sold by private firms [1]. *Total: 6.*

**9.** Vaccination gives a private benefit to the person vaccinated and an external benefit to others, who are less likely to catch flu [1]. Individuals consider only their private benefit, so the market demand curve reflects private benefits only [1]. Diagram: supply curve and private demand curve meet at the market quantity; a second demand curve, "demand including external benefits", lies to the right [1]. The quantity where this second curve meets supply is greater than the market quantity [1], so the market under-consumes the vaccine [1]. Imperfect information adds to this: people may underestimate the risk of flu, lowering private demand further [1]. *Total: 6.*

**10.** Sellers know the true condition of their cars but buyers cannot tell good cars from faulty ones [1]. Buyers are unwilling to pay high prices, so owners of good cars may withdraw them, and buyers may end up with faulty cars, so resources are misallocated [1]. Government could require sellers to give a vehicle history or test certificate [1], which reduces the information gap and lets prices reflect quality [1]. *Total: 4.*

**11.** Inadequate information: if the government did not account for gas used to generate the electricity, the subsidy may not cut total gas use and could even raise it [1], so the intervention fails to correct the problem and misallocates resources [1]. Administrative costs: the new office that checks every claim uses resources [1], which raises the total cost of the policy and may outweigh the benefit it achieves [1]. *Total: 4.*

**12.** A tax raises the cost of producing plastic packaging, shifting supply left, so price rises and quantity falls [1]. It lets firms that can switch cheaply cut use most, and raises revenue that could fund recycling [1]. But the external cost is hard to measure, so the tax may be set too low or too high [1]. If demand is price inelastic because there are few substitutes, quantity falls only a little [1]. A ban removes the most harmful types directly and is simple for consumers to understand [1]. But it gives no choice to firms that need the packaging, may raise costs sharply, and requires inspection, an administrative cost [1]. A ban also brings in no revenue and may lead firms to switch to substitutes with their own external costs, an unintended consequence [1]. Judgement: a tax is better where substitutes exist and the aim is a gradual fall; a ban is better for types whose harm is severe and for which a ready substitute is available [1]. *Total: 8.*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a public good as one the government provides.
- Showing externalities on MSC/MSB diagrams instead of the AS demand and supply diagram.
- Mixing up which side of the market bears more of a tax: the side with less elastic demand or supply bears more.
- Setting a minimum price below equilibrium and still finding a surplus.
- Evaluating intervention without any source of government failure.
