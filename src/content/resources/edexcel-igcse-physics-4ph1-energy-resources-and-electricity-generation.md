This guide covers sub-topic **4(d) Energy resources and electricity generation** of the Pearson Edexcel International
GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. Both statements are Physics-only, so they are assessed only on Paper 2:

- **4.18P** describe the energy transfers involved in generating electricity using wind, water, geothermal resources,
  solar heating systems, solar cells, fossil fuels and nuclear power;
- **4.19P** describe the advantages and disadvantages of methods of large-scale electricity production from various
  renewable and non-renewable resources.

The [Energy Resources and Energy Transfers study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-energy-resources-and-energy-transfers/)
introduces the renewable and non-renewable split. This page goes through each named resource in turn and shows how to
build a balanced, well-argued comparison.

## The common chain: turbine and generator

Most power stations share the same final stages. Something makes a **turbine** spin, the turbine turns a
**generator**, and the generator transfers energy electrically to the grid. Inside the generator, a magnet rotates
inside coils of wire (or coils rotate in a magnetic field), inducing a voltage, which is the electromagnetic induction
of Topic 6. What differs between resources is what drives the turbine:

- **thermal power stations** (fossil fuel, nuclear, geothermal, and solar heating at large scale) heat water to make
  high-pressure steam, and the steam drives the turbine;
- **wind and water** drive the turbine directly with moving air or water;
- **solar cells** are the exception: they transfer energy from light directly electrically, with no turbine or
  generator.

## The energy transfers, resource by resource (4.18P)

| Resource | Energy transfers to describe |
|---|---|
| Wind | Kinetic store of moving air → mechanically → kinetic store of the turbine blades → generator → electrically |
| Water: hydroelectric | Gravitational store of water held high in a reservoir → water falls → kinetic store of the water → turbine → generator → electrically |
| Water: tides and waves | Kinetic store of moving sea water (tides rising and falling, or wave motion) → turbine → generator → electrically |
| Geothermal | Thermal store of hot rocks underground → by heating → thermal store of water pumped down, which returns as steam → kinetic store of the turbine → generator → electrically |
| Solar heating systems | Radiation from the Sun → absorbed by dark collectors (or focused by mirrors) → thermal store of water or another fluid; at large scale this makes steam → turbine → generator → electrically |
| Solar cells | Radiation (light) from the Sun → solar cell → electrically, directly |
| Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) | Chemical store of the fuel → burning → by heating → thermal store of water and steam → kinetic store of the turbine → generator → electrically |
| Nuclear power | Nuclear store of uranium or plutonium fuel → fission in the reactor → thermal store of the coolant and then water → steam → turbine → generator → electrically |

At each stage some energy is dissipated, mainly by heating the surroundings. In thermal power stations a large part of
the energy from the fuel is wasted in the cooling water and air, which is why their efficiency is well below 100%.

## Weighing the resources (4.19P)

Strong answers compare resources on several criteria rather than repeating "renewable is good". Useful criteria:

- **Reliability:** is the output available when needed, or does it depend on the weather or time of day?
- **Control:** can the output be raised or lowered quickly to match demand?
- **Environmental effects:** carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas linked to climate change), other pollution, waste,
  effects on habitats and landscapes.
- **Costs:** building, fuel, running and, for nuclear power, decommissioning.
- **Location:** many resources only work at suitable sites.
- **Renewable or not:** will the resource run out?

| Resource | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Wind | Renewable; no fuel cost; no carbon dioxide while operating | Output depends on wind speed, so unreliable; many turbines needed for a large output; visual impact and noise |
| Hydroelectric | Renewable; reliable while the reservoir holds water; can start very quickly when demand rises | Flooding a valley destroys habitats and may displace people; high building cost; needs a suitable valley and rainfall |
| Tidal | Renewable; tides are predictable | Few suitable estuaries; barrages affect wildlife habitats; output varies through the day |
| Wave | Renewable; no fuel cost | Output varies with the weather; equipment can be damaged by storms |
| Geothermal | Renewable; available all the time; no fuel cost | Only practical in regions with hot rocks near the surface; drilling is expensive |
| Solar heating and solar cells | Renewable; no fuel cost; solar cells have no moving parts and suit remote places | No output at night and less in cloudy weather; large areas of panels needed for a large output |
| Fossil fuels | Reliable; large output; gas-fired stations can start quickly | Non-renewable; burning releases carbon dioxide; coal also releases sulfur dioxide, which causes acid rain |
| Nuclear | Reliable; very large output from a small mass of fuel; no carbon dioxide released while generating | Non-renewable fuel; radioactive waste must be stored safely for a very long time; risk of accidents; high building and decommissioning costs; slow to start up |

A key idea for evaluation: the variable renewables (wind, solar, waves) cannot be switched on when demand rises, so a
grid that uses them also needs resources that can be controlled, such as hydroelectric or gas.

## Worked example: an efficiency calculation

This sub-topic sits inside Topic 4, so a question can ask for an efficiency (statement 4.4) in a power-station context.
The figures here are invented for practice.

A coal-fired power station has a total energy output of 2500 MJ each second. Of this, 900 MJ is transferred
electrically to the grid.

```
efficiency = 900 / 2500 × 100% = 36%
energy wasted each second = 2500 − 900 = 1600 MJ, mainly by heating the cooling water and air
```

## Annotated model answer

**Question.** *A country wants to build new large-scale electricity generation. Discuss the advantages and
disadvantages of building a wind farm compared with a nuclear power station.*

> A wind farm uses a renewable resource and releases no carbon dioxide while it operates, and there is no fuel to buy.
> **(two advantages, each a separate point)** However, its output depends on the wind, so it cannot guarantee
> electricity when demand is high, and many turbines spread over a large area are needed to match one large power
> station. **(reliability and scale)** A nuclear power station gives a large, steady output from a small mass of fuel
> and also releases no carbon dioxide while generating. **(fair comparison on the same criterion)** But its fuel is
> non-renewable, it produces radioactive waste that must be stored safely for a very long time, and it is expensive
> to build and to decommission. **(three disadvantages, covering environment and cost)** If the country needs a
> reliable supply, nuclear power is better, but wind would need backup from a resource that can be controlled.
> **(a judgement linked back to the criteria)**

Notice that each sentence makes one developed point, both resources are judged on the same criteria, and the answer
ends with a conclusion. That matches the specification's meaning of "discuss": explore all aspects of the issue and
reason about them.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a resource "creates energy". Energy is transferred from a store; it is never made.
- Calling nuclear power renewable, or calling it a fossil fuel. It is non-renewable and not a fossil fuel.
- Describing solar cells with a turbine. They transfer energy electrically without any moving parts.
- Writing "it is bad for the environment" with no reason. Name the effect: carbon dioxide, habitat loss, radioactive
  waste.
- Listing only advantages of renewables and only disadvantages of fossil fuels; a discussion needs both sides.
- Forgetting the generator: the turbine alone does not produce electricity.

## Exam technique

- **Describe the energy transfers:** start at the original store, name each stage and end with "electrically". Include
  the wasted energy if the question asks for all the transfers.
- **Describe advantages and disadvantages:** give at least one of each for each resource, with a reason attached.
- **Discuss / Evaluate:** compare on the same criteria and finish with a judgement that follows from your points.
