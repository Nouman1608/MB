This guide covers **4.2 Demographic transition** in Topic 4, Population, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content is:

- **Changes in birth rate and death rate over time.**
- **A critical appreciation of the demographic transition model, Stages 1–5.**
- **Issues of youthful populations and ageing populations.**
- **Links between population and development:** changes in infant mortality rate and life expectancy over time.

It is examined in Paper 2 Core Human Geography, often with a graph of birth and death rates over time, and frequently in Section B as an extended question asking you to evaluate the model. The [4.2 demographic transition revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-demographic-transition-revision-notes/) summarise the five stages, reasons for change and illustrative countries. This guide focuses on the skills the syllabus names: **critical appreciation**, analysing change over time, and linking population to development. ## The model in one paragraph

The demographic transition model (DTM) describes how a country's birth and death rates change as it develops. In **Stage 1** both rates are high and fluctuating, so population grows slowly. In **Stage 2** the death rate falls sharply while the birth rate stays high, so natural increase is rapid. In **Stage 3** the birth rate falls, so growth slows. In **Stage 4** both rates are low, and population is broadly stable. **Stage 5**, added later, shows the birth rate falling below the death rate, giving natural decrease. The model was based on the experience of Western Europe and North America, particularly England and Wales, where the death rate began to fall in the late eighteenth century and the birth rate from about the 1870s.

## Why the rates change

The **death rate** falls first because improvements in food supply, clean water, sanitation, vaccination and medical care reduce deaths, especially among infants and children. These changes can spread quickly. The **birth rate** falls later because it depends on attitudes, family decisions and economic change: lower child mortality, urbanisation, female education, access to contraception and the rising cost of children all take time to change behaviour. The **gap in timing** between the two falls is what produces the rapid growth of Stage 2 and early Stage 3.

## Worked example: identifying stages from data

*Birth and death rates (per 1000) for fictional country T:*

| Year | 1950 | 1965 | 1980 | 1995 | 2010 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Birth rate | 45 | 44 | 36 | 24 | 15 | 9 |
| Death rate | 25 | 14 | 9 | 7 | 7 | 10 |
| Natural increase | 20 | 30 | 27 | 17 | 8 | -1 |

- **1950 to 1965:** the death rate halves (25 to 14) while the birth rate barely changes, so natural increase rises to 30 per 1000 (3.0% a year): **Stage 2**.
- **1965 to 1995:** the birth rate falls by 20 points, from 44 to 24, so growth slows: **Stage 3**.
- **2010:** both rates low: **Stage 4**.
- **2025:** the birth rate (9) is below the death rate (10), giving natural decrease: **Stage 5**.

Note that the death rate **rises** from 7 to 10 between 2010 and 2025. This is not a sign of worsening health: it reflects an ageing population, since a larger share of people are in older age groups. Country T moves through all five stages in 75 years, far faster than England and Wales did, which is itself a point for critical appreciation.

## Critical appreciation of the model

"Critical appreciation" means weighing **strengths** and **limitations**, then judging the model's usefulness.

**Strengths**

- It describes the historical experience of many HICs well.
- It is simple and allows comparison of countries at different points in the transition.
- It links population change to development, helping planners anticipate future needs, such as schools in Stage 2 or pensions in Stage 4 and 5.
- Many LICs and MICs have followed the broad sequence of falling death rates followed by falling birth rates.

**Limitations**

- **Eurocentric origins:** it is based on Western European and North American experience and may not apply elsewhere.
- **Different causes:** in many LICs, death rates fell in the twentieth century because of imported medicine, vaccination and public health programmes, not industrialisation as in Europe.
- **Different speeds:** recent transitions have been far faster, as in East Asia; South Korea's total fertility rate has fallen below 1.0 in recent years.
- **Stalled or reversed transitions:** some countries have stayed at high fertility for longer than expected; in parts of southern Africa, HIV/AIDS raised death rates in the 1990s and 2000s, reversing gains in life expectancy for a time.
- **Policy and culture:** government policies (for example, China's birth limits from about 1980) and religious or cultural norms can change rates independently of economic development.
- **Migration is ignored:** the model considers only natural change, yet migration shapes many countries' populations, including those in Stage 5.
- **No timescale or end point:** it does not predict when a country will move between stages, and Stage 5 was not part of the original model.

**Judgement:** the model is a useful **description** of the general direction of change, but a weak **predictor** of the speed, causes or timing for any individual country.

## Youthful and ageing populations: the issues

| Issue | Youthful population | Ageing population |
|---|---|---|
| Dependency | High youth dependency | High old-age dependency |
| Services | Demand for schools, child health care, maternal care | Demand for health care, residential care, pensions |
| Economy | Large future workforce; risk of youth unemployment if jobs are not created | Shrinking workforce, labour shortages, smaller tax base |
| Opportunity | A **demographic dividend** if fertility falls and the large cohort finds productive work | Older people contribute experience, savings, volunteering and family care |
| Policy responses | Family planning, education and job creation | Raising the retirement age, encouraging immigration, pro-natalist policies, automation |

Japan is a widely cited example of an ageing population, with one of the highest proportions of people aged 65 and over in the world and a population that has been falling since the late 2000s. Many sub-Saharan African countries have very youthful populations, with roughly 40% or more of people under 15.

## Links with development: infant mortality and life expectancy

As countries develop, **infant mortality falls** and **life expectancy rises**. The two are linked: in countries with high infant mortality, average life expectancy at birth is pulled down sharply by deaths in the first year of life, even though adults may live to old age. As infant deaths fall, life expectancy at birth rises quickly.

The causes of death also change, a shift known as the **epidemiological transition**: from infectious diseases, malnutrition and complications of childbirth, which dominate in early stages, towards non-communicable conditions such as heart disease, cancer and dementia in later stages. This is why HICs now focus health spending on chronic illness and care for the elderly, while many LICs still prioritise vaccination, clean water and maternal care.

Falling infant mortality also feeds back into the transition: when parents are confident their children will survive, they tend to have fewer, which helps move a country from Stage 2 into Stage 3.

## Annotated model answer

*"Assess the usefulness of the demographic transition model in explaining population change in LICs and MICs." [extract]*

> The model correctly shows that death rates fall before birth rates, and many LICs and MICs have experienced rapid Stage 2 growth as a result **(strength with evidence)**. However, their death rates fell mainly because of imported vaccines and public health campaigns rather than industrialisation, so the model's causes do not fit **(limitation)**. Transitions have also been much faster: fertility in some East Asian countries fell in a few decades, compared with about a century in England and Wales **(limitation with comparison)**. The model is therefore useful as a description of the sequence of change, but much less useful for explaining its causes or predicting its speed in these countries **(judgement)**.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the five stages without any critical comment when the syllabus demands critical appreciation.
- Interpreting a rising death rate in an ageing country as a health crisis.
- Treating Stage 1 as still common today.
- Presenting youthful and ageing populations only as problems, with no opportunities.
- Confusing life expectancy at birth with the age most adults reach.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** change over time: quote rates for named years and the size of changes.
- **Explain**: give separate reasons for falling death rates and falling birth rates, and explain the time lag.
- **Assess** or **evaluate** the model: strengths, limitations with examples, then a judgement on what it is useful for.
