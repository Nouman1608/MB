This guide covers **3.2.1.2 Operations competitiveness** from OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725), Version 1.1, in Unit 2 (International AS). It is the longest operations sub-section. The [Operational performance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-operational-performance/) lists each method with its advantages and disadvantages; this page shows how to use the ideas with numbers and in judgements: the cost logic of capacity, a make-or-buy calculation, planning for seasonal demand, and how quality and customer service turn into competitive advantage. When you have finished, condense it with the [operations competitiveness revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-competitiveness-9725-revision-notes/) and practise with the [operations competitiveness practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-competitiveness-9725-practice/).

All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

- **Capacity**: benefits of high or low capacity utilisation; making versus buying; ways of matching supply to demand (increasing or decreasing output, inventory or capacity; outsourcing; flexible labour such as temporary, part-time, agency employees and zero hours contracts); problems of not matching supply to demand.
- **Efficiency and productivity**: their importance, ways of increasing them and the difficulties.
- **Lean production**: Just in Time, Kaizen, standardised processes and teamwork; benefits and difficulties.
- **Customer service**: its value and how to improve it (understand customer needs, improve customer experience, benchmark, train, reward loyalty, monitor and act on feedback and satisfaction levels).
- **Quality**: meaning, significance and measurement; quality control, quality assurance, TQM, quality circles, benchmarking; benefits and difficulties of improving quality; costs of poor quality.
- **Importance of operations management**: technology, ethical and environmental issues, effect on competitiveness, links with other functions.

## Capacity utilisation and unit cost

Fixed costs do not change with output, so the fixed cost carried by each unit falls as utilisation rises.

**Worked example 1.** Merrow Bottling has fixed costs of $90,000 a month and capacity of 10,000 cases.

```
At 60% utilisation (6,000 cases): fixed cost per case = 90,000 ÷ 6,000 = $15
At 90% utilisation (9,000 cases): fixed cost per case = 90,000 ÷ 9,000 = $10
```

Moving from 60% to 90% cuts $5 from every case before any change in working methods. That is why low utilisation is a competitive problem: rivals with fuller plants can price lower. But high utilisation has costs too: no time for maintenance, overtime, stressed staff and no room for a sudden large order. Many firms aim for high but not full utilisation.

**Raising utilisation** can mean increasing output (selling more, taking on subcontracted work) or reducing capacity (closing a line, renting out space). Which is better depends on whether the low demand is temporary or permanent.

## Making versus buying

**Worked example 2.** Merrow needs plastic caps. Making them would add fixed costs of $30,000 a year and a variable cost of $6 per thousand caps; a supplier charges $9 per thousand.

```
At 8,000 thousand caps:  make = 30,000 + (6 × 8,000)  = $78,000; buy = 9 × 8,000  = $72,000
At 12,000 thousand caps: make = 30,000 + (6 × 12,000) = $102,000; buy = 9 × 12,000 = $108,000
Costs are equal where 30,000 = (9 − 6) × quantity → 10,000 thousand caps
```

Below 10 million caps a year, buying is cheaper; above it, making is. Cost is only part of the decision: making gives control over quality and supply and protects know-how; buying needs no capital, gives access to the supplier's expertise and lets Merrow focus on bottling, but makes it depend on the supplier's reliability.

## Matching supply to demand

Demand may be seasonal (ice cream in summer), cyclical (rising and falling with the economy) or simply unpredictable.

**Worked example 3: building inventory for a peak.** Glenmere Ices can make 10,000 tubs a quarter. Demand over a year is 6,000, 9,000, 15,000 and 6,000 tubs.

| Quarter | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand | 6,000 | 9,000 | 15,000 | 6,000 |
| Production | 10,000 | 10,000 | 10,000 | 6,000 |
| Inventory at end | 4,000 | 5,000 | 0 | 0 |

Glenmere cannot make 15,000 tubs in quarter 3, so it builds inventory of 5,000 in quarters 1 and 2 and sells it in the peak. The cost is storage and the cash tied up in frozen tubs for months. Alternatives: outsource some quarter 3 production (less control over quality), hire temporary or agency staff and run extra shifts (if machines allow), or invest in more capacity (expensive and idle for three quarters).

**Problems of not matching supply to demand.** Too little supply: lost sales, customers trying rivals, damaged reputation. Too much supply: unsold inventory, idle staff and machines, high unit costs and possible price cuts to clear stock.

## Efficiency and productivity

**Efficiency** is producing output with the fewest resources; **labour productivity** is output per employee. Both lower unit costs, which supports lower prices or higher margins.

**Worked example 4.** A packing team of 20 packs 5,000 boxes a week: 250 per employee. After a Kaizen suggestion changes the bench layout, the same 20 pack 5,600: 280 per employee, a rise of (280 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = **12%**. If wages are unchanged, labour cost per box falls.

**Ways of increasing productivity:** training, better equipment, better layout and methods, motivation and involvement. **Difficulties:** investment cost, staff resistance or fear of job losses, output that is hard to measure (for example in a design studio), and the risk that speed lowers quality.

## Lean production

Lean production means cutting waste of all kinds: excess inventory, waiting, defects, unnecessary movement.

- **Just in Time**: inputs arrive as needed, so little inventory is held.
- **Kaizen**: continuous improvement through many small changes, often suggested by the people doing the work.
- **Standardised processes**: the best-known way of doing a task is written down and followed by everyone.
- **Teamwork**: teams share responsibility for output and solving problems.

The four support one another: standard methods make problems visible, teams and Kaizen fix them, and JIT works only when processes are reliable. **Difficulties**: dependence on suppliers, little buffer when things go wrong, training time, and the culture change needed for staff to suggest improvements.

## Customer service

Good service brings repeat sales, recommendations and the ability to charge more. To improve it, a firm can:

- **Understand customer needs** through feedback and research.
- **Improve the customer experience**, such as faster checkout or clearer tracking.
- **Benchmark** against the best service providers, including those in other industries.
- **Train** staff to solve problems.
- **Reward loyalty** through schemes or priority service.
- **Monitor and act on feedback and satisfaction levels**, closing the loop by telling customers what changed.

## Quality

Quality means meeting customer needs consistently. It is measured through defect rates, returns, complaints, satisfaction scores and the quality measures in the [operations management guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-operations-management-9725/).

| Method | Approach | Key difference |
|---|---|---|
| Quality control | Inspect output to find faults | Detects faults after they happen |
| Quality assurance | Build checks into every stage | Prevents faults |
| TQM | Every employee responsible for quality all the time | A culture, not a department |
| Quality circles | Small groups of staff meet to solve quality problems | Uses shop-floor knowledge |
| Benchmarking | Compare with the best performers and copy good practice | Sets an external standard |

**Costs of poor quality: worked example 5.** A factory makes 25,000 units a month with a 4% defect rate: 1,000 faulty units. Reworking costs $8 each, so **$8,000 a month** goes on fixing faults, before counting refunds, lost customers and damage to reputation. If quality assurance costing $3,000 a month halved the defect rate, rework would fall to $4,000, a net saving of $1,000 a month plus the hidden benefits.

## Why operations matter for competitiveness

- **Technology**: online businesses, better links with customers and suppliers, and computerised inventory control speed up service and cut errors.
- **Ethics**: product safety and environmental concerns affect reputation and can bring legal costs.
- **Environment**: reducing pollution and carbon footprint may cost money but meets customer and regulatory expectations.
- **Interrelationships**: operations needs marketing's forecasts, finance's budget for investment and HR's trained staff.

## Common mistakes

- Saying full capacity utilisation is always best.
- Deciding make or buy on cost alone.
- Treating quality control and quality assurance as the same.
- Calling lean production "just JIT".
- Listing ways of improving service without linking them to the business's customers.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a benefit with a chain: method → effect on operations → effect on competitiveness.
- **Analyse** using numbers from the case: fixed cost per unit, productivity change, cost of defects.
- **Evaluate** by weighing cost against control, flexibility or quality, and stating what the decision depends on (how long demand stays high, supplier reliability, staff attitudes).
