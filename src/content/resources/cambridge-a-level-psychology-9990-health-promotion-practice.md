**This practice set covers topic 3.5, Health promotion, in Specialist Option 3 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All schools, companies and data are fictional.

Related: [Health promotion study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-health-promotion/) · [Health promotion revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-health-promotion-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Define unrealistic optimism. **[1]**

**2.** Outline the findings of Janis and Feshbach (1953). **[3]**

**3.** Outline the three focuses of positive psychology described by Seligman. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how the Food Dudes programme uses learning theory to promote healthy eating. **[4]**

**5.** Individual factors affect how people respond to health advice.

**(a)** Describe the study by Shoshani and Steinmetz (2014). **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate research into individual factors in changing health beliefs, including a discussion of cultural differences. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** A road safety charity wants young drivers to slow down. It is considering an advert showing a graphic crash.

**(a)** Using research into fear arousal, explain **one** risk of this approach. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest how the advert could be changed to make it more effective. **[2]**

**7.** Many young drivers believe they are less likely to crash than other drivers their age.

**(a)** Name this bias. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest how a campaign could reduce it. **[2]**

**8.** A factory introduces a token economy to reduce accidents. Workers earn tokens for each month without an accident.

**(a)** Explain why tokens should be given soon after the safe period. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why recorded accident rates might not be a valid measure of the programme's success. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest a way to check the validity of the accident data. **[1]**

**9.** A psychologist wants to replicate Tapper et al. (2003) in four primary schools.

**(a)** Suggest how she could measure fruit and vegetable eating objectively. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why she should take measurements several months after the programme ends. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** ethical issue in this research and how she should deal with it. **[3]**

**10.** A hospital gives every heart attack patient a self-help booklet, following Lewin et al. (1992). Explain **one** strength and **one** limitation of providing information as a health promotion strategy. **[4]**

**11.** A school measures well-being with a questionnaire before and after a positive psychology course (fictional data). Mean scores rise from 31 to 37 out of 50. Calculate the percentage increase, to one decimal place. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Believing that negative events are less likely to happen to oneself than to other people (1).

**2.** Up to 3 marks. High-school students heard a talk on dental hygiene with a strong, moderate or minimal fear appeal (1). The minimal fear appeal produced the most change in reported dental behaviour (1), and the strong fear appeal the least (1).

**3.** One mark each. The pleasant life: experiencing positive emotions (1). The good life: engagement through using one's strengths (1). The meaningful life: using strengths in the service of something larger than oneself (1).

**4.** Up to 4 marks. Modelling: children watch heroic peer characters eating fruit and vegetables (1), and imitate admired models of similar age, as social learning theory predicts (1). Positive reinforcement: children receive rewards such as stickers for eating the target foods (1), which makes eating them more likely to be repeated (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Aim: to evaluate a school-based positive psychology programme to promote adolescents' mental health and well-being (1). Carried out in middle schools in Israel (1). An intervention group received the programme in class and a control group did not (1). Students completed psychometric questionnaires of distress and well-being before and after (1). The intervention group showed reduced psychological distress symptoms, such as anxiety and depression (1), and increased self-esteem and optimism, which the control group did not show (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named issue, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Cultural differences*: positive psychology emphasises individual happiness and strengths, which may reflect individualist values; collectivist cultures may value harmony or duty more, so programmes may need adapting; Weinstein's student sample is from one culture.
- *Support and application*: Shoshani and Steinmetz show programmes can be delivered in ordinary schools; unrealistic optimism explains why people ignore advice and suggests campaign designs.
- *Method*: psychometric self-reports are open to social desirability bias; groups in Shoshani and Steinmetz were not individually randomised.
- *Individual and situational explanations*: individual beliefs matter, but situational factors such as poverty also limit health choices.
- *Idiographic versus nomothetic*: averages may hide students who did not benefit.
- *Conclusion*: individual factors matter, but the evidence is culture-bound and largely self-reported.

**6.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Janis and Feshbach (1953) found strong fear produced the least change (1); very frightening messages can lead viewers to avoid or dismiss the message to reduce anxiety (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Reduce the level of fear or pair it with clear, achievable advice (1), e.g. a specific action such as keeping to the speed limit near schools, so viewers know how to reduce the risk (1).

**7.**

**(a)** Unrealistic optimism (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Show real drivers of the same age and background who have crashed (1), so viewers cannot see themselves as different from those at risk (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Reinforcement is most effective when it closely follows the behaviour (1), so workers link the reward to safe working and the behaviour is strengthened (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Workers may stop reporting minor accidents to keep their tokens (1), so a fall in recorded accidents may not reflect a real fall in accidents (1).

**(c)** Compare with independent records such as first-aid logs or insurance claims (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Weigh or count the fruit and vegetables served and left at lunch (1), so the amount eaten is calculated without relying on children's reports (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. To see whether the effect lasts after rewards and videos stop (1), since behaviour based on reinforcement may extinguish (1).

**(c)** Up to 3 marks. Children cannot give fully informed consent (1), so parental consent must be obtained (1), and children should also be asked if they are happy to take part and allowed to stop, with no pressure to eat (1).

**10.** Strength, up to 2: it is cheap and can reach every patient (1), and Lewin et al. found improved psychological adjustment with a self-help manual (1). Limitation, up to 2: knowing what to do does not guarantee doing it (1), because barriers such as cost, habit or low motivation remain (1).

**11.** (37 − 31) ÷ 31 × 100 (1) = 19.4% (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 3, describing the pleasant life as "being happy" without positive emotions.
- In question 5(a), giving results without the control group comparison.
- In question 11, dividing by 37 instead of the original score.
