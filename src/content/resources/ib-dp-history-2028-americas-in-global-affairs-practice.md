## Questions

**1.** Explain two reasons for US expansionist foreign policy in the 1890s. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract (constructed for practice).

> "Chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilised society, may in the Western Hemisphere ultimately require intervention by some civilised nation, and the United States cannot ignore this duty." (Constructed for practice: a paraphrase in the style of a presidential statement of 1904.)

**(a)** Identify two claims made in the extract. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how a Latin American government might have viewed this policy, and one limitation of using the extract alone to judge US motives. **[4]**

**3.** Distinguish between "dollar diplomacy" and "moral diplomacy". **[3]**

**4.** Analyse the impact of the Spanish-American War on Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines. **[6]**

**5.** Explain why the US Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the extent to which the Good Neighbor Policy represented a change in US relations with Latin America. **[8]**

**7.** Discuss the domestic impact of the First World War or the Second World War on one country of the Americas other than the USA. **[8]**

**8.** To what extent did Canada become more independent in its foreign policy between 1914 and 1945? **[8]**

**9.** Paper 3 style essay question: "Economic interests were the main reason for US expansionist foreign policy between 1890 and 1914." To what extent do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Two developed points, 2 marks each. Economic: overproduction and the depression of 1893 led business and political leaders to seek overseas markets and investment [1], supporting a canal and Pacific bases to protect trade [1]. Strategic: Mahan argued for a strong navy, overseas bases and a canal [1], and the closing of the frontier in 1890 encouraged a look abroad [1]. Ideological and social: manifest destiny, Social Darwinism and missionary zeal gave a sense of duty to spread "civilisation" [1], and the yellow press stirred opinion on Cuba [1]. **[4]**

**2. (a)** That "chronic wrongdoing" or weakness in a state may require intervention [1]; that the US cannot ignore a duty to act as a civilised nation in the Western Hemisphere [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** A Latin American government could see it as a claim to interfere in its domestic affairs, and a threat to sovereignty, as when the US took over customs in the Dominican Republic [1]; some governments might accept it for protection against European intervention or debt collectors [1]. Limitation: the extract is the language of justification, which stresses duty and civilisation and does not reveal economic and strategic motives such as investments or the Panama Canal [1]; it is also one statement and shows intention rather than actual practice [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Dollar diplomacy (Taft) used loans and investment, backed by the threat of force, to stabilise countries and open markets [1]. Moral diplomacy (Wilson) said it aimed to promote democracy and the rule of law and refuse support to governments seen as illegitimate [1]. In practice both involved intervention, such as Nicaragua under Taft and Haiti and the Dominican Republic under Wilson [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Cuba: nominal independence, but the Platt Amendment (1901) allowed US intervention and a naval base at Guantánamo [2]. Puerto Rico: became a US territory, with a civil government set up by the US and no independence [1]. Philippines: ceded for $20 million, leading to the Philippine-American War from 1899 and US rule [2]. Overall: the war made the US a colonial power with an empire in the Caribbean and Pacific [1]. **[6]**

**5.** Wilson compromised at Paris and the treaty included the League covenant [1]. Republican senators led by Henry Cabot Lodge wanted reservations [1]. Article 10 seemed to commit the US to defend other members, which they feared would remove Congress's power to declare war [1]. Wilson refused to compromise and was incapacitated by a stroke in 1919 [1]. Partisan politics and fear of entanglement strengthened opposition [1]. Many Americans were tired of war [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Change: Roosevelt pledged non-intervention (1933), the US accepted the principle at Montevideo [1]; Marines left Haiti in 1934 and the Platt Amendment was repealed [2]; the US accepted Mexico's oil nationalisation in 1938 [1]. Continuity: the US kept economic influence and supported friendly dictators such as Somoza in Nicaragua and Trujillo in the Dominican Republic [2]; the aim of US leadership and security remained [1]. Judgement: a change of method more than of aims [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Choose Canada in the First World War. Economic: war orders and borrowing, Victory Bonds, income tax in 1917 [2]. Political: conscription (1917) split English and French Canada and led to the Union government [2]. Women: war work and the vote by 1918 [1]. Marginalised groups: internment of "enemy aliens" and the question of Indigenous and Black volunteers [1]. Judgement on the lasting effect [2]. (Equivalent credit for the Second World War: Munitions and Supply, the 1942 plebiscite, Japanese Canadian internment.) **[8]**

**8.** Evidence of growing independence: separate signature at Paris and League membership (1920) [1]; refusal to commit automatically in the Chanak crisis (1922) [1]; the Statute of Westminster (1931) [1]; separate declaration of war in 1939 [1]. Limits: Canada's war effort in 1914 was automatic, and in 1939 it still followed Britain [1]; close ties to Britain remained, and the Ogdensburg Agreement (1940) turned Canada towards defence cooperation with the US [1]. Judgement: independence grew but was gradual and complicated by dependence on Britain and, later, the US [2]. **[8]**

**9.** Use the Paper 3 markbands. A top answer (13 to 15 marks) shows in-depth understanding of the argument, evaluates a range of perspectives and reaches a substantiated judgement. Economic factors (about 4 marks): surplus production, markets and investment, the 1893 depression, canal and bases to protect trade, dollar diplomacy. Other factors (about 4 marks): strategic ideas (Mahan), security of the Caribbean, political ideology and manifest destiny, public opinion and the press, humanitarian arguments over Cuba. Perspectives (about 3 marks): historians who stress economic motives (such as the Open Door argument associated with William Appleman Williams) and those who stress strategic, cultural or domestic political factors. Judgement (about 4 marks): for example, that economic interests were a major long-term driver but that strategic and ideological factors shaped specific actions, so "main" is only partly right. **[15]**
