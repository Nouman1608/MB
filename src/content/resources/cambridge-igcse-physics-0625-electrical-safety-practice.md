## Section A

**1.** Name the wire in a mains circuit that is connected to the metal casing of an appliance. **[1]**

**2.** State the **two** dangers that damaged insulation on a mains cable can cause. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why using mains appliances in damp conditions is hazardous. **[1]**

**4.** State one difference between the way a fuse and a trip switch break a circuit. **[1]**

**5.** State what is meant by the rating of a fuse. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** An electrician finds that a ceiling lamp has been wired with its switch in the neutral wire.

**(a)** Explain why the lamp still goes off when the switch is opened. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why this wiring is dangerous. **[2]**

**7.** A toaster is rated at 800 W.

**(a)** Calculate the normal current in the toaster. **[2]**
**(b)** Fuses rated at 3 A, 5 A and 13 A are available. Choose the most suitable fuse and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the danger of fitting the 13 A fuse instead. **[2]**

**8.** A 2.0 kW heater, a 1.2 kW iron and a 150 W television are all plugged into one extension lead designed for a maximum current of 13 A.

**(a)** Calculate the total current drawn from the extension lead. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why this is a hazard. **[2]**

**9.** A washing machine has a metal case connected to the earth wire. A fault causes the live wire to touch the case.

**(a)** Describe how the earth wire and the fuse make the washing machine safe. **[3]**
**(b)** The earth wire has become disconnected. Explain what would happen if a person touched the case. **[2]**

**10.** A hair dryer has a plastic casing and a cable with only two wires.

**(a)** Name the two wires. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the hair dryer is safe to use without an earth wire. **[2]**
**(c)** State what the fuse protects in this appliance. **[1]**

**11.** The appliances on a kitchen circuit have a total power of 5.5 kW when all are on.

**(a)** Calculate the total current when all the appliances are on. **[2]**
**(b)** Trip switch settings of 16 A, 32 A and 45 A are available. Choose the most suitable setting and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the trip switch protects the circuit if a fault causes a much larger current. **[2]**

**12.** This question is about overheating cables.

**(a)** Explain why a cable carrying too large a current is a fire hazard. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why an extension cable on a reel should be fully unwound before it is used for a heater. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The **earth** wire [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Electric **shock** (from touching the exposed live wire) [1]; **fire** (from a short circuit or sparking) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Water conducts electricity, so current can flow through the water and a person (to earth), causing a shock [1]. **[1]**

**4.** A fuse wire **melts** and must be replaced; a trip switch **opens** automatically and can be **reset** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** The maximum current that can flow through it without it melting (it melts if this current is exceeded) [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Opening the switch breaks the circuit, so there is no current in the lamp [1].
**(b)** The lamp is still connected to the live wire, so it is at a high p.d. even when switched off [1]; anyone touching the lamp fitting (for example when changing the bulb) could get an electric shock [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** I = P / V = 800 ÷ 230 [1] = **3.5 A** (3.48 A) [1].
**(b)** **5 A** [1]; it is the smallest rating above the normal current (3 A would melt in normal use) [1].
**(c)** A fault current larger than normal but below 13 A would not melt the fuse [1], so the cable or toaster could overheat and cause a fire (or the toaster is not protected) [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Total power = 2000 + 1200 + 150 = 3350 W [1]; I = 3350 ÷ 230 [1] = **14.6 A** [1].
**(b)** 14.6 A is more than the 13 A the lead is designed for (it is overloaded) [1]; the lead's cable overheats, which could melt the insulation and cause a fire [1]. **[5]**

*Common mistake:* calculating the current for each appliance and then choosing the largest. In parallel, the currents add.

**9. (a)** A large current flows from the live wire through the case and the earth wire to the ground [1]. This current is larger than the fuse rating, so the fuse melts [1], disconnecting the live supply so the case is no longer live [1].
**(b)** The case would remain at the high mains p.d. with no current and the fuse would not melt [1]; current would flow through the person to earth, giving an electric shock [1]. **[5]**

**10. (a)** **Live** and **neutral** [1].
**(b)** The outer casing is made of a non-conducting material (it is double-insulated) [1], so even if a wire inside comes loose the casing cannot become live [1].
**(c)** The **circuit and the cabling** [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** I = P / V = 5500 ÷ 230 [1] = **23.9 A** [1].
**(b)** **32 A** [1]; it is the lowest setting above the normal current; 16 A would trip in normal use and 45 A gives less protection [1].
**(c)** When the current exceeds the setting, the trip switch opens automatically [1], disconnecting the live supply; it can be reset once the fault is fixed [1]. **[6]**

**12. (a)** A large current heats the cable [1]; the insulation can melt or burn, causing a fire (and exposing the wires) [1].
**(b)** A cable wound on a reel cannot transfer thermal energy to the surroundings easily [1], so with the large current a heater draws it gets much hotter and could overheat [1]. **[4]**

*Common mistake:* saying a fuse "lowers the current". It does nothing in normal use; it melts only when the current exceeds its rating.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining the switch position as "so it switches off" rather than "so it is disconnected from the live supply".
- Choosing a fuse equal to or below the normal current.
- Forgetting to add the currents of appliances on the same lead.
- Explaining earthing without the large current and the fuse melting.
- Saying a double-insulated appliance does not need a fuse.
