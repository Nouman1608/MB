These notes condense section 12.3, Environmental degradation, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026 (Paper 4, Topic 12 option). For full explanations and examples, see the [12.3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-environmental-degradation/); then test yourself with the [12.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-environmental-degradation-practice/).

## The six syllabus statements

1. Pollution (land, air and water): nature, causes, solutions.
2. Demand for and supply of water; issues of water quality.
3. Factors in rural degradation (e.g. overpopulation, poor agricultural practices, deforestation).
4. Factors in urban degradation (e.g. urbanisation, industrial development, inadequate waste management).
5. Constraints on improving degraded environments.
6. Protection of environments at risk at local or regional scale: needs, measures, outcomes.

## Pollution in one view

| Type | Pollutants | Sources | Solutions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air | PM2.5, NO₂, SO₂, ozone | Traffic, power stations, industry, burning | Standards, cleaner fuels, scrubbers, low-emission zones |
| Water | Sewage, nitrates, phosphates, metals, oil, heat | Sewers, farms, factories, power plants | Treatment works, permits, buffer strips |
| Land | Waste, plastics, metals, pesticides, spoil | Dumps, industry, mining, farms | Sanitary landfill, recycling, remediation |

- **Point source:** one outlet; easy to regulate.
- **Diffuse:** many small sources; hard to regulate.
- **Transboundary:** crosses borders; needs agreements.
- **Water-quality indicators:** dissolved oxygen (low = stress), BOD (high = organic pollution), nitrate and phosphate, faecal coliforms (sewage).
- **Environmental Kuznets curve:** some pollution rises then falls with income; fits SO₂ better than CO₂ or waste.

## Water in one view

- **Demand:** agriculture (largest), industry, domestic; rises with population, irrigation, urbanisation, living standards.
- **Stress thresholds (m³ per person per year):** below 1 700 stress; below 1 000 scarcity; below 500 absolute scarcity.
- **Physical scarcity:** not enough water. **Economic scarcity:** water exists but not the money or infrastructure.
- **Supply options:** dams, groundwater, inter-basin transfer, desalination, recycling, each with costs.
- **Demand management:** metering, pricing, leak repair, drip irrigation, efficient appliances.
- **Quality:** sewage, salinisation, nitrates, natural arsenic in groundwater (Bangladesh tube wells).

## Rural degradation chain

Overpopulation → shorter fallows, farming of steep or dry land, fuelwood cutting → loss of cover and nutrients → erosion, lower yields → more clearance.

- **Poor practices:** overgrazing, overcultivation, ploughing down slopes, irrigation without drainage.
- **Deforestation:** less interception and root binding → erosion, flooding, siltation, biodiversity loss.
- **Debate:** Malthus (population degrades land) versus Boserup (pressure drives innovation); Machakos, Kenya: *More People, Less Erosion* (1994).

## Urban degradation chain

Rapid urbanisation → infrastructure lags → no sewerage, drainage or waste collection → polluted water, blocked drains, floods, disease.

- **Industry:** emissions, effluent, contaminated land near poorer housing.
- **Waste:** open dumping and burning; leachate; methane; blocked drains; risks for waste pickers.
- **Traffic:** street-level air pollution.

## Constraints

Financial · institutional (weak enforcement, corruption) · economic interests (jobs and tax) · physical (slow recovery) · social (poverty, awareness) · spatial (transboundary) · lack of data.

## Protection example: Galápagos Islands, Ecuador

- **Needs:** unique species; threats from introduced species, tourism, population growth, illegal fishing.
- **Measures:** national park (1959); World Heritage Site (1978); marine reserve (1998); licensed guides; cargo inspection; goat eradication on several islands (2000s).
- **Outcomes:** vegetation recovery where goats removed; but on the List of World Heritage in Danger 2007–2010 as pressures continued.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Environmental degradation | Decline in the quality of land, water, air or ecosystems |
| Pollution | Introduction of harmful substances or energy into the environment |
| Eutrophication | Nutrient enrichment causing algal growth and oxygen loss |
| BOD | Oxygen needed by microbes to break down organic matter in water |
| Leachate | Polluted liquid draining from waste |
| Water stress | Renewable water below about 1 700 m³ per person per year |
| Salinisation | Build-up of salts in soil or water |
| Overcultivation | Cropping land too intensively for nutrients to recover |
| Overgrazing | Too many animals for the vegetation to recover |
| Remediation | Cleaning up contaminated land or water |
| Environment at risk | An area whose ecosystems or resources face serious threat |
| Transboundary pollution | Pollution that crosses political borders |

## Exam traps

- Pollutant without source or effect.
- Physical and economic scarcity confused.
- Population blamed alone for rural degradation.
- Constraints listed without a place.
- Protection described without outcomes.
- Global climate change used when the question asks for local or regional protection.

## Self-test

1. Name one air pollutant and one source.
2. Distinguish point-source from diffuse pollution.
3. What does a high BOD indicate?
4. What does the environmental Kuznets curve suggest?
5. What is the threshold for water stress?
6. Distinguish physical from economic water scarcity.
7. Give two demand-management methods for water.
8. Explain how deforestation leads to flooding.
9. What did the Machakos study suggest?
10. Give two ways poor waste management degrades a city.
11. Give three constraints on improving a degraded environment.
12. Give one measure and one outcome of protection in the Galápagos.

### Answers

1. PM2.5, NO₂ or SO₂; from vehicles, power stations, industry or burning.
2. Point: one identifiable outlet. Diffuse: many scattered sources.
3. Much organic pollution, such as sewage.
4. Some pollution rises during industrialisation and falls at higher incomes.
5. Below about 1 700 m³ per person per year.
6. Physical: not enough water. Economic: water exists but money or infrastructure is lacking.
7. Any two: metering, pricing, leak repair, drip irrigation, efficient appliances.
8. Less interception and more run-off reach rivers faster; eroded soil fills channels.
9. Population growth went with more terracing and tree planting and less erosion.
10. Any two: leachate pollutes groundwater; burning causes air pollution; rubbish blocks drains and worsens floods; disease vectors.
11. Any three: cost, weak enforcement, economic interests, slow recovery, poverty, transboundary sources, lack of data.
12. Measure: marine reserve, guides, cargo inspection or goat eradication. Outcome: vegetation recovery, or in-danger listing 2007–2010.
