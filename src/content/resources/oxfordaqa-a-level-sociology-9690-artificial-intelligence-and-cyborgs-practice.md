Revise first with the [AI and cyborgs study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-artificial-intelligence-and-cyborgs/) and the [AI and cyborgs revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-sociology-9690-artificial-intelligence-and-cyborgs-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by training data in machine learning. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two emerging technologies that merge the human and machine. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a feedback loop in algorithmic decision-making. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two differences between rule-based AI and machine learning. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which AI depends on hidden human labour or material resources. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, from a fictional audit of a fictional facial recognition system.

| Group tested (fictional) | Checks carried out | False matches |
|---|---|---|
| Lighter-skinned men | 1,000 | 3 |
| Darker-skinned women | 800 | 28 |

**(a)** Calculate the false-match rate, as a percentage of checks, for each group. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain how the use of this system by police could raise issues of power and inequality. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> The fictional Willow Court care home has bought six robotic pets for residents with dementia. The robots respond to touch and voice with sounds and movements. Staff report that some residents are calmer and talk more when holding them. The home's owners say the robots will allow them to employ two fewer care assistants. Some families welcome the robots; others say their relatives "need people, not machines".

**(a)** Identify two possible benefits of the robots for residents. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, analyse the effects of robots in care work. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why decisions made by algorithms may be harder to challenge than decisions made by people. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three effects of AI on work. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how Haraway's idea of the cyborg challenges fixed boundaries between categories. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that the effects of AI depend mainly on who owns and controls it. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent has the use of AI by people and organisations been beneficial? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** The set of examples a machine-learning system learns from [1], so any biases or gaps in those examples, such as past discrimination, are built into the system's outputs [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: pacemakers [1]; cochlear implants [1]; brain-computer interfaces; deep brain stimulation; advanced prosthetic limbs; implanted chips. **[2]**

**3.** When an algorithm's outputs produce new data [1] that are then fed back into the system [1], so the original prediction appears confirmed, as when predictive policing sends more officers to an area and more crime is recorded there [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Rule-based AI follows if-then rules written by people [1], while machine learning finds patterns in large sets of examples [1]. Rule-based AI is transparent because its rules can be read [1], while machine-learning systems, especially deep learning, are often opaque even to their designers [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Many "automated" services rely on low-paid workers who label data and check outputs (Gray and Suri's ghost work) [1], so the costs of AI fall on hidden workers, often in poorer countries [1]. AI depends on mined minerals, energy-hungry data centres and user data (Crawford) [1], so it has environmental and social costs that its image as "virtual" hides [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Lighter-skinned men: 3 / 1,000 x 100 = 0.3% [1]. Darker-skinned women: 28 / 800 x 100 = 3.5% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Darker-skinned women are falsely matched at more than ten times the rate of lighter-skinned men (3.5% against 0.3%) [1], so they are much more likely to be wrongly stopped or questioned by police [1]. This builds inequality into a tool that appears neutral, echoing Buolamwini and Gebru's findings [1], and because police hold power over those flagged, the costs of the error fall on groups already more likely to be over-policed [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: residents become calmer [1]; residents talk more [1]; comfort from touch and response. **[2]**
**(b)** Residents being calmer and talking more suggests robots can meet some emotional needs [1], supporting the view that technology can improve care [1]. The owners plan to employ two fewer care assistants, showing robots used to cut labour costs [1], which a Marxist reading would see as profit taking priority over care [1]. Families saying relatives "need people, not machines" echoes Turkle's criticism that robotic companionship substitutes for human relationships [1], so the effect depends on whether robots add to or replace human care [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Many algorithms are "black boxes", with workings hidden by commercial secrecy or complexity (Pasquale) [1], so people affected cannot see why a decision was made [1]. Algorithms appear objective and scientific [1], so their decisions are trusted more readily than a person's and challenges are dismissed, even when bias comes from training data [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Some tasks are automated, reducing demand for certain jobs [1], although task-based studies suggest fewer whole jobs disappear than Frey and Osborne's 47% estimate implies [1]. New hidden jobs appear, such as data labelling and content moderation [1], often low-paid and insecure (ghost work) [1]. Algorithms are used to manage and monitor workers [1], increasing managerial control over the pace of work [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Haraway's "A Cyborg Manifesto" (1985) uses the cyborg as a figure of mixed identity [1].
- It blurs the boundary between human and animal [1].
- It blurs the boundary between organism and machine, as with implants and prosthetics [1].
- It blurs the boundary between physical and non-physical, as with digital information [1].
- She argues feminists should reject fixed, "natural" identities [1].
- Application: pacemakers or cochlear implants make many people partly cyborg [1].
- Counter: critics say the idea is abstract and ignores unequal access to technology [1].
- Analysis: categories such as "natural" and "human" are social constructions open to change [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Marxist view: AI is owned by large corporations seeking profit [1].
- Zuboff and Crawford: AI extracts data, labour and resources [1].
- Owners decide how AI is deployed, for example to cut jobs [1].
- Pasquale: secrecy protects owners from scrutiny [1].
- Application: own-society example of AI used by banks, employers or government [1].
- Eubanks: public-sector AI also harms poorer people, so state control is no guarantee [1].
- Counter: bias comes from training data, not only ownership [1].
- Design choices by engineers matter, as with facial recognition [1].
- Users shape effects by how they use tools [1].
- Regulation and public pressure, as in England's 2020 grading case, can change outcomes [1].
- Latour: outcomes come from networks of humans and machines, not owners alone [1].
- Judgement: ownership is a major influence, but data, design, regulation and use also shape effects [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- AI is used in search, translation, health, banking, education and policing [1].
- Medical imaging can speed diagnosis [1].
- Translation widens access to information across languages [1].
- Accessibility tools help disabled users [1].
- Own-society application: AI use in a local bank, hospital or school [1].
- Organisations gain efficiency and lower costs [1].
- Counter: algorithmic bias (Buolamwini and Gebru; COMPAS) [1].
- The Netherlands childcare benefits scandal shows harm from automated profiling [1].
- Job change and insecure ghost work [1].
- Opacity prevents people challenging decisions [1].
- Environmental costs of data centres (Crawford) [1].
- Benefits are unequal: digital divides limit who gains [1].
- Own-society application: concerns about AI in exams, hiring or surveillance [1].
- Pluralist reading: diverse uses and regulation spread benefits [1].
- Analysis: benefit depends on type of AI, purpose and control [1].
- Judgement: beneficial in specific uses, harmful where it automates decisions about people without accountability [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Name specific uses of AI; "AI is good and bad" without examples cannot reach the top band.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about AI in general with no type or use.
- Treating hypothetical AGI as current.
- Quoting the 47% job estimate without criticism.
- Ignoring the hidden labour and material costs of AI.
- Using Haraway only as a definition of cyborgs, not as a challenge to boundaries.
