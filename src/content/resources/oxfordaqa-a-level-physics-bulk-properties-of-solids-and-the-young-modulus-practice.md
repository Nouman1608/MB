## Questions

**1.** A metal bar, 8.0 cm by 3.0 cm by 1.5 cm, has mass 0.2844 kg. Calculate its density. **[3]**

**2.** A spring is loaded in steps. The extension is 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 27 and 38 mm for forces of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 N.

**(a)** Show that Hooke's law is obeyed up to 8 N but not at 10 N. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the spring constant. **[2]**

**3.** A cable of diameter 6.0 mm breaks when the tension reaches 21 kN.

**(a)** Calculate the breaking stress of the material. **[2]**
**(b)** A cable of the same material has diameter 9.0 mm. Calculate the tension at which it breaks. **[2]**

**4.** A spring of stiffness 150 N m⁻¹ obeys Hooke's law. Calculate the energy stored when its extension is **(a)** 0.080 m **[2]**, and **(b)** 0.120 m, and the extra work done in stretching it from 0.080 m to 0.120 m. **[2]**

**5.** A wire is loaded so that the force rises in a straight line from 0 to 40 N as the extension increases from 0 to 2.0 mm. The force then rises in a straight line to 60 N at an extension of 6.0 mm. The force is then reduced to zero along a straight line parallel to the first line.

**(a)** Find the work done in stretching the wire from zero to 6.0 mm. **[2]**
**(b)** Determine the permanent extension. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the energy recovered on unloading, and the energy dissipated. State what happens to the dissipated energy. **[2]**

**6.** Describe the force-extension graph and the behaviour on fracture of **(a)** a glass rod **[2]**, and **(b)** a copper wire loaded beyond its elastic limit. **[2]**

**7.** A spring of stiffness 420 N m⁻¹ is compressed by 0.045 m and used to fire a 0.025 kg ball vertically upwards.

**(a)** Calculate the elastic strain energy stored. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the maximum height above the release position, assuming no energy is lost. **[2]**
**(c)** The ball actually rises 1.40 m above the release position. Calculate the energy dissipated. **[2]**

**8.** A copper wire of original length 2.20 m and diameter 0.46 mm extends by 3.4 mm under a load of 28 N. Calculate the Young modulus of copper and the energy stored in the wire. **[5]**

**9.** Wire A has length L and diameter d. Wire B is made of the same material, with length 2L and diameter 2d. The same force acts on each and neither passes its elastic limit. Give the ratio B : A for **(a)** the stress **[1]**, **(b)** the extension **[2]**, and **(c)** the energy stored. **[2]**

**10.** A student finds the Young modulus of a steel wire of original length 1.85 m. The diameter is 0.28 ± 0.01 mm.

**(a)** Describe how to measure the diameter to allow for variation along the wire. **[2]**
**(b)** The gradient of the line of best fit on a graph of extension against force is 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m N⁻¹. Calculate the Young modulus. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the cross-sectional area, and say how it affects the uncertainty in the Young modulus. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how the student can check that the wire has not been stretched beyond its elastic limit. **[1]**

**11.** A wire of length 3.0 m and diameter 1.0 mm has a stress-strain graph that is a straight line from the origin to the elastic limit at strain 0.0018 and stress 290 MPa. The graph then curves to a breaking point at a stress of 380 MPa.

**(a)** Calculate the Young modulus. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the force at the elastic limit. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the extension at the elastic limit. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the energy stored in the wire at the elastic limit. **[2]**
**(e)** Calculate the force needed to break the wire. **[1]**

**12.** A 0.40 kg mass is attached to a vertical spring of stiffness 65 N m⁻¹ and released from rest with the spring at its natural length.

**(a)** Calculate the extension when the mass is at equilibrium. **[1]**
**(b)** Use energy conservation to find the maximum extension. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the speed of the mass as it passes the equilibrium position. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** V = 8.0 × 3.0 × 1.5 = 36 cm³ = 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ m³ [1]; ρ = m/V = 0.2844 / 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ [1] = **7.9 × 10³ kg m⁻³ [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Show the cm³ to m³ conversion, so a wrong power of ten can still be traced.

**2. (a)** F/ΔL = 0.40 N mm⁻¹ for every reading up to 8 N, so force is proportional to extension [1]; at 10 N, F/ΔL = 0.37 N mm⁻¹, so it is no longer proportional [1].
**(b)** k = F/ΔL = 8 / 0.020 [1] = **400 N m⁻¹ [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Support "proportional" with a calculated ratio, and convert millimetres to metres.

**3. (a)** A = π × (3.0 × 10⁻³)² = 2.83 × 10⁻⁵ m² [1]; stress = 21 × 10³ / 2.83 × 10⁻⁵ = **7.4 × 10⁸ Pa [1]**.
**(b)** A = π × (4.5 × 10⁻³)² = 6.36 × 10⁻⁵ m² [1]; F = 7.43 × 10⁸ × 6.36 × 10⁻⁵ = **4.7 × 10⁴ N [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Breaking stress belongs to the material, so reuse the part (a) value. Using the diameter as the radius affects both parts.

**4. (a)** F = 150 × 0.080 = 12 N [1]; energy = ½ × 12 × 0.080 = **0.48 J [1]**.
**(b)** Energy at 0.120 m = ½ × 150 × 0.120² = 1.08 J [1]; extra work = 1.08 − 0.48 = **0.60 J [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* "Extra work" is a difference of two stored energies, not the energy at the new extension.

**5. (a)** Work = area = ½ × 40 × 2.0 + ½ × (40 + 60) × 4.0 [1] = 40 + 200 = 240 mJ = **0.24 J [1]**.
**(b)** The initial gradient is 40 / 2.0 = 20 N mm⁻¹, so unloading from 60 N removes 60 / 20 = 3.0 mm [1]; permanent extension = 6.0 − 3.0 = **3.0 mm [1]**.
**(c)** Recovered = ½ × 60 × 3.0 = 90 mJ [1]; dissipated = 240 − 90 = **0.15 J**, transferred to internal energy of the wire [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say where the dissipated energy goes; "lost" alone does not answer the question.

**6. (a)** Straight line up to fracture [1]; it breaks suddenly at a small extension with no plastic deformation (brittle) [1].
**(b)** After the straight section the graph curves and a large plastic extension occurs [1]; unloading leaves a permanent extension, with the line parallel to the initial gradient [1].
*Examiner insight:* Describe the graph. "Glass is brittle" alone is a label, not a description.

**7. (a)** Energy = ½ × 420 × 0.045² = **0.425 J [1]**.
**(b)** mgh = 0.425 [1]; h = 0.425 / (0.025 × 9.81) = **1.73 m [1]**.
**(c)** Gravitational potential energy gained = 0.025 × 9.81 × 1.40 = 0.343 J [1]; dissipated = 0.425 − 0.343 = **0.082 J [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Write the energy equation in words first; it shows that the height is measured from the release position.

**8.** A = π × (0.23 × 10⁻³)² = 1.66 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; stress = 28 / 1.66 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.68 × 10⁸ Pa [1]; strain = 3.4 × 10⁻³ / 2.20 = 1.55 × 10⁻³ [1]; E = 1.68 × 10⁸ / 1.55 × 10⁻³ = **1.09 × 10¹¹ Pa [1]**; energy = ½ × 28 × 3.4 × 10⁻³ = **0.048 J [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Show stress and strain separately: one long substitution leaves no partial working to credit if a number is entered wrongly.

**9. (a)** Area of B is four times that of A, so stress ratio B : A = **1 : 4 [1]**.
**(b)** ΔL = FL/(AE) [1]; B has twice the length and four times the area, so the extension ratio is 2 / 4 = **1 : 2 [1]**.
**(c)** Energy = ½FΔL [1], and F is the same, so the ratio is **1 : 2 [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Start from ΔL = FL/(AE) and compare factors; a bare ratio gives little to credit.

**10. (a)** Use a micrometer at several positions along the wire [1], and in two perpendicular directions at each position, then take the mean [1].
**(b)** A = π × (0.14 × 10⁻³)² = 6.16 × 10⁻⁸ m² [1]; E = L / (A × gradient) [1] = 1.85 / (6.16 × 10⁻⁸ × 1.5 × 10⁻⁴) = **2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa [1]**.
**(c)** Uncertainty in d = 0.01 / 0.28 = 3.6%, so in A it is 2 × 3.6 = **7.1% [1]**; this contributes the same amount to the percentage uncertainty in E, and is likely to be its largest part [1].
**(d)** Remove the load in steps and check that the reading returns to its original value (or that the graph is linear) [1].
*Examiner insight:* A calculated percentage, with the squared diameter explained, is more convincing than "the diameter has the largest error".

**11. (a)** E = stress / strain = 290 × 10⁶ / 0.0018 [1] = **1.6 × 10¹¹ Pa [1]**.
**(b)** A = π × (0.50 × 10⁻³)² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; F = 290 × 10⁶ × 7.85 × 10⁻⁷ = **228 N [1]**.
**(c)** ΔL = strain × L = 0.0018 × 3.0 = **5.4 mm [1]**.
**(d)** Energy = ½FΔL = ½ × 228 × 5.4 × 10⁻³ [1] = **0.615 J [1]**.
**(e)** F = 380 × 10⁶ × 7.85 × 10⁻⁷ = **298 N [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Reuse the area from part (b) and keep spare digits until the last line; rounded intermediates can shift the final figure.

**12. (a)** mg = kx, so x = 0.40 × 9.81 / 65 = **0.0604 m [1]**.
**(b)** At maximum extension the mass is momentarily at rest [1]; mgx = ½kx² [1], so x = 2mg/k = 2 × 0.40 × 9.81 / 65 = **0.121 m [1]**.
**(c)** ½mv² = mgx − ½kx² at x = 0.0604 m [1]; v = **0.77 m s⁻¹ [1]**.
*Examiner insight:* Say that the mass is at rest at maximum extension to justify mgx = ½kx². At the equilibrium position it is still moving, so kinetic energy must appear in the equation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the diameter as the radius in A = πd²/4, or leaving the diameter in millimetres.
- Forgetting the ½ in strain energy, or using ½kΔL² for a graph that is not straight.
- Mixing up the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit.
- Treating the gradient of a force-extension graph as E rather than k.
- Measuring the height of a spring-launched object from the wrong position.
