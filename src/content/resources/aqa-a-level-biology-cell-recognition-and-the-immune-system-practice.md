## How should you use these questions?

Write full answers, then mark them. Use the specification's terms: antigen-presenting cell, helper T cell, clonal
selection, plasma cell, memory cell, complementary.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term *antigen*. **[1]**

**2.** Give **four** types of cell or substance that the immune system can identify from the molecules on their
surfaces. **[2]**

**3.** Describe how a phagocyte destroys a bacterium. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the role of helper T cells in the immune response. **[3]**

**5.** Describe the humoral response to a bacterium entering the body for the first time. **[5]**

**6.**

**(a)** Describe the structure of an antibody. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why an antibody binds to only one type of antigen. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how antibodies lead to the destruction of bacteria. **[2]**

**7.** A person is infected with the same strain of a virus twice, two years apart. Explain why they show
symptoms after the first infection but not after the second. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why a new influenza vaccine is developed most years. **[2]**

**9.** In a vaccine trial, 12 000 volunteers received the vaccine and 12 000 received a placebo. Over one year,
18 vaccinated volunteers and 162 placebo volunteers caught the disease.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of each group that caught the disease. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage reduction in cases in the vaccinated group compared with the placebo group. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why a placebo group was used. **[1]**
**(d)** Give **two** other features of the trial that would make the result more reliable. **[2]**

**10.** A baby is protected from some diseases for a few months by antibodies from breast milk. A vaccinated
adult is protected for many years. Explain the difference. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how herd immunity protects a person who cannot be vaccinated. **[2]**

**12.** Describe how HIV replicates inside a helper T cell. **[4]**

**13.** Explain why a person with AIDS may die from a disease that a healthy person would survive. **[3]**

**14.** Explain why antibiotics cannot be used to treat HIV infection. **[2]**

**15.** Describe how a monoclonal antibody can be used to target a drug to cancer cells. **[2]**

**16.** In an ELISA test for antibodies to a virus, explain:

**(a)** why the well is washed after the patient's sample is added and again after the enzyme-linked antibody is
added; **[2]**
**(b)** why a control well containing no antigen is used. **[1]**

**17.** Discuss **two** ethical issues associated with the use of vaccines. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A (foreign) molecule, usually a protein, that triggers an immune response by lymphocytes [1].

**2.** All four for 2 marks, two or three for 1 mark: pathogens; cells from other organisms of the same species;
abnormal body cells; toxins.

**3.** The phagocyte recognises the bacterium's antigens and engulfs it [1], enclosing it in a phagosome [1]; a
lysosome fuses with the phagosome [1]; lysozymes hydrolyse / digest the bacterium [1]. (Also credit: antigens
presented on the phagocyte surface.)

**4.** A helper T cell with a complementary receptor binds to an antigen on an antigen-presenting cell and divides
by mitosis [1]. It stimulates cytotoxic T cells to kill infected cells [1], and stimulates B cells to divide and
phagocytes to engulf pathogens [1].

**5.** Any five [1 each]: a B cell with a complementary antibody receptor binds the antigen; the B cell takes in and
presents the antigen; a helper T cell activates the B cell; clonal selection; the B cell divides by mitosis;
forming plasma cells that secrete antibodies; and memory cells; antibodies bind antigens, forming
antigen-antibody complexes.

**6. (a)** Four polypeptide chains: two heavy and two light [1], joined by disulfide bridges [1]; variable regions
with antigen-binding sites and a constant region (and hinge region) [1].
**(b)** The variable region has a specific amino acid sequence, so a specific tertiary structure / shape of binding
site [1], which is complementary to only one antigen [1].
**(c)** Antibodies have two binding sites, so they cause agglutination / clump bacteria together [1]; phagocytes
then engulf and destroy the clumps [1].

**7.** First infection: primary response is slow because there are few B and T cells with complementary receptors,
so the virus replicates and causes symptoms [1]. Memory cells were made [1]. Second infection: memory cells divide
rapidly into plasma cells, making antibodies quickly and in larger amounts, so the virus is destroyed before
symptoms appear [1].

**8.** The influenza virus shows antigen variability / its antigens change (by mutation) [1], so memory cells from
the previous vaccine are not complementary to the new antigens [1].

**9. (a)** Vaccinated: 18 / 12 000 x 100 = **0.15%** [1]. Placebo: 162 / 12 000 x 100 = **1.35%** [1].
**(b)** (1.35 - 0.15) / 1.35 x 100 = **88.9%** reduction (89%) [1].
**(c)** To show whether the vaccine, not other factors (such as the injection or belief in treatment), caused the
difference / to give a comparison [1].
**(d)** Any two [1 each]: double-blind design; random allocation to groups; groups matched for age, sex, health;
volunteers from different populations / several trials; followed up for longer.

**10.** Breast milk gives passive immunity: antibodies come from the mother [1]; no memory cells are made, and the
antibodies are broken down, so protection is short-term [1]. Vaccination gives active immunity, with memory cells
produced, so protection lasts years [1].

**11.** Most people in the population are immune, so the pathogen cannot pass easily between people [1]; the
unvaccinated person is less likely to meet an infected person [1].

**12.** Attachment protein binds to a receptor on the helper T cell [1]; the capsid releases RNA (and enzymes) into
the cell [1]; reverse transcriptase makes DNA from the viral RNA [1]; the DNA is inserted into the host DNA and used
to make new viral proteins and RNA, assembled into new HIV particles [1].

**13.** HIV destroys helper T cells [1], so B cells and cytotoxic T cells are not stimulated / the immune response
is weak [1]; opportunistic infections cannot be fought off [1].

**14.** Antibiotics act on bacterial structures (such as murein cell walls) or bacterial processes, which viruses
do not have [1]; HIV uses the host cell's enzymes and ribosomes to replicate, so there is no viral target for the
antibiotic [1].

**15.** The drug is attached to a monoclonal antibody complementary to an antigen found only on cancer cells [1];
the antibody binds those cells, so the drug acts on them and fewer normal cells are harmed [1].

**16. (a)** First wash removes antibodies that have not bound to the antigen [1]; second wash removes unbound
enzyme-linked antibody, which would change the substrate's colour and give a false positive [1].
**(b)** To show that colour only develops when the antigen is present / that the antibodies do not bind to the well
itself [1].

**17.** Any two [1 each]: use of animals in development and testing; side effects / risk to volunteers in trials;
whether vaccination should be compulsory (individual choice against herd immunity); fair access and cost in poorer
countries.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "lysosomes digest the bacterium" without naming lysozymes.
- Saying T cells produce antibodies.
- Describing antigens and antibodies as the same shape instead of complementary.
- Forgetting to mention memory cells in the secondary response.
- Using the wrong denominator in a percentage reduction.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which cells produce antibodies?**
Plasma cells, which form when activated B cells divide.

**Do I need to know how monoclonal antibodies are made?**
No. AQA states that details of the commercial or scientific production of monoclonal antibodies are not required.

**How detailed should HIV structure be?**
Know RNA, reverse transcriptase, capsid, lipid envelope and attachment proteins, and how the virus replicates in
helper T cells.
