They are not reproduced exam questions, and the real Paper 3 has essay questions only. Source extracts are constructed for practice. ## Section A: short answers

**1.** Give two reasons why sugar plantations in the Americas increased the demand for enslaved Africans. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why Zanzibar became important to the East African slave trade in the nineteenth century. **[3]**

**3.** Describe two forms of day-to-day resistance by enslaved people. **[2]**

## Section B: source and argument

*Constructed for practice, not a real source:* "The chiefs along the coast did not sell their neighbours because they were deceived. They sold them because the trade brought guns, and guns were the only protection against being sold themselves." (a modern historian's summary)

**4.** Explain the argument made in this extract about African involvement in the Atlantic trade. **[3]**

**5.** Explain one way in which the argument could be challenged. **[3]**

**6.** Examine the claim that the trade in enslaved Africans ended mainly because it had become unprofitable. **[6]**

## Section C: essay questions

**7.** To what extent did African states benefit from their involvement in the slave trade? **[15]**

**8.** Evaluate the view that the abolitionist movement was the most important reason for the abolition of the slave trade. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each for two of: sugar growing was labour intensive and needed large workforces; Indigenous populations had been reduced by disease and conquest; European workers were costly and limited in number; the profit of sugar paid for more enslaved people. **[2]**

**2.** One mark for the Omani link (Sayyid Said made Zanzibar a base in the 1830s and 1840s). One mark for the clove plantations on Zanzibar and Pemba that needed enslaved workers. One mark for its role as a market and harbour linking inland caravan routes, such as those of Tippu Tip, to the Indian Ocean. **[3]**

**3.** One mark for each of two: work slowdown; damaging tools or crops; feigning illness; keeping religion, music or language; keeping family and community ties; small-scale escape and return. **[2]**

**4.** One mark for the idea that African coastal leaders chose to sell captives; one for the reason (to obtain guns); one for the further point that guns were needed for defence, which implies a cycle in which participating in the trade was driven by insecurity. **[3]**

**5.** One mark for a challenge; up to two for support. For example: the extract treats the coastal chiefs as forced by fear, but historians point out that many rulers and merchants gained wealth and power voluntarily, and that Dahomey and Oyo expanded through the trade; or European demand and the credit system encouraged the cycle. Alternatively, accept the point that guns were only one import and that cloth, alcohol and cowries mattered too. **[3]**

**6.** Up to 6 marks. Credit evidence for the claim: industrialisation, growth of palm oil and groundnut trade, the argument that free labour was more efficient, and Williams's 1944 thesis. Credit evidence against: the trade was profitable in 1807 (Drescher), the moral and religious campaign led by Clarkson, Wilberforce and others, petitions and public pressure, and resistance such as the Haitian Revolution. Level guide: 1 to 2 for description; 3 to 4 for partial examination; 5 to 6 for a balanced examination and a judgement, for example that economic change made abolition easier but the movement and the law made it happen. **[6]**

**7.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding "benefit" and defining criteria (wealth, power, security, long-term development);
- up to 4 for evidence of benefit (Dahomey, Oyo, Asante, Bonny: wealth, weapons, military strength, imports);
- up to 4 for evidence of harm or limited benefit (population loss, insecurity, dependence on imports, damage to other production, a benefit mostly to elites);
- up to 4 for evaluation and a judgement, for example distinguishing short-term elite gain from long-term damage and from variation between regions. **[15]**

**8.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding the claim and naming the movement (the 1787 Society, Clarkson, Wilberforce, Equiano, petitions);
- up to 4 for evidence for the claim (public campaigning, parliamentary pressure that led to the 1807 Act, the role of religious groups);
- up to 4 for other factors (economic change, the role of enslaved people's resistance including Haiti, political and naval interest, later European colonialism);
- up to 4 for evaluating perspectives (Williams, Drescher and others) and a supported judgement. **[15]**

## Model paragraph and feedback

Here is a model opening paragraph for Question 8, followed by points on how to improve a draft.

*Model paragraph:* "Historians have long debated why Britain ended its slave trade in 1807. Eric Williams argued that economic change, especially industrialisation, made the trade less valuable. Seymour Drescher replied that the trade was still profitable and that abolition needed a political and moral movement. This essay argues that the abolitionist movement was the most important single reason, because it turned opinion into law, but that it succeeded because economic change meant the West Indian interest was less able to defend the trade. It also argues that the actions of enslaved people, above all in Haiti, shaped the debate. The strongest case for the movement is the scale of petitions after 1787 and the parliamentary work of Wilberforce. The strongest case against is the shift to palm oil and other products."

*Feedback points:*

- The paragraph states the argument in the question, names two perspectives, and commits to a line. A weak answer would only list causes.
- Check that each later paragraph tests one factor and ends by linking it back to the claim.
- Use "about" for estimates of numbers of people. Never give a precise figure unless you are sure of it.
- Do not drift into the American Civil War or later slavery unless the question asks for it.
