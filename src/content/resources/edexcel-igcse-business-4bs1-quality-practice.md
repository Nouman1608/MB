These questions cover Topic 5 Business operations, section 5.4 Quality, in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026: the concept of quality and its importance in goods and services, quality control, TQM and competitive advantage. Revise first with the [quality study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-quality/) and the [quality revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-quality-revision-notes/). Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Calculators may be used and workings should be shown. Round percentages to 1 decimal place where needed.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term total quality management (TQM). **[2]**

**2.** Give two indicators a restaurant could use to measure the quality of its service. **[2]**

**3.** Halden Bikes makes 1,600 bicycles a month. Last month 48 were faulty, and each faulty bicycle costs 35 to repair.

**(a)** Calculate the defect rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the monthly cost of repairing faulty bicycles. **[1]**
**(c)** Halden Bikes expects to cut its defect rate to 1.25%. Calculate the monthly saving in repair costs. **[2]**

**4.** Explain one reason why quality is important to a business that provides a service. **[3]**

**5.** Mira's Bakery has three staff. At the end of each day the owner inspects every cake and throws away any that are below standard. Outline one disadvantage to Mira's Bakery of this method of quality control. **[2]**

**6.** Ravi runs a hair salon with three stylists. Outline one way Ravi could check the quality of the service his salon provides. **[2]**

**7.** Sunrise Hotel had 192 complaints from 9,600 guests in Year 1 and 176 complaints from 8,000 guests in Year 2.

**(a)** Calculate the complaint rate in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** The manager says that service quality improved because the number of complaints fell. Explain whether the manager is correct. **[2]**

**8.** Explain one way in which good quality can help a business gain a competitive advantage. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**9.** Tallis Shoes sells leather boots for 96 a pair. A rival sells similar-looking boots for 80. Better leather and hand stitching cost Tallis Shoes an extra 9 a pair. It sells 5,000 pairs a year.

**(a)** Calculate the price premium Tallis Shoes charges, as a percentage of the rival's price. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the extra profit a year that Tallis Shoes earns from its price premium after the extra cost of quality. **[2]**

**10.** Pembroke Tiles is a large manufacturer with 600 employees. Tiles pass through five production stages and are checked only at a final inspection. Each week 4% of the 50,000 tiles made are rejected at that inspection. A rejected tile has already cost 1.20 in materials and processing; if the same fault were spotted at the first stage, it would have cost only 0.30. Analyse how introducing TQM could reduce Pembroke Tiles' costs. **[6]**

**11.** Corin's Cakes employs 8 staff and makes 60,000 cakes a year for local cafés. 6% are faulty, and each faulty cake costs 3 in wasted ingredients and labour. Some faulty cakes reach cafés, and two cafés have complained. The owner is considering two options.

- Option A: employ a part-time quality inspector at 9,000 a year to check finished cakes. The defect rate would stay at 6%, but almost no faulty cakes would reach cafés.
- Option B: TQM training for all staff at a one-off cost of 8,000. The defect rate is expected to fall to 2% after a few months.

Justify which option Corin's Cakes should choose. **[8]**

**12.** Northgate Foods is a large food manufacturer that makes 2 million jars of sauce a year. It uses quality control, with inspectors at the end of the line. Its defect rate is 3%, and each faulty jar costs 0.50. Last year a fault that the inspectors missed led to a product recall costing 400,000, and sales fell for several months. Introducing TQM would cost 120,000 in training and is expected to cut the defect rate to 1%. Evaluate whether Northgate Foods should introduce TQM. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** An approach in which every employee is responsible for quality [1] and faults are prevented at source throughout the process, aiming for zero defects [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Checking quality" alone describes quality control; the definition must say everyone and prevention.

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: complaint rate [1]; repeat-customer rate [1]; average waiting time for food; online review scores; number of meals sent back. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a measurable indicator, not a vague idea such as "good food".

**3. (a)** 48 ÷ 1,600 × 100 [1] = **3%** [1].
**(b)** 48 × 35 = **1,680** [1].
**(c)** 1.25% of 1,600 = 20 bicycles, 20 × 35 = 700 [1]; saving 1,680 − 700 = **980 a month** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Carry your answer from (b) into (c); an error in (b) can still earn the method mark in (c).

**4.** A service is produced and consumed at the same time, so it cannot be inspected before the customer receives it [1]. Any mistake is experienced directly by the customer [1], so a poor experience can lead to negative reviews and lost future customers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain needs a point and two expansion steps. No context is required.

**5.** The cakes are checked only after they are made, so faulty cakes have already used ingredients and staff time [1]; this waste raises the bakery's costs and cuts its profit each day [1]. Also accept, for the same 2 marks: the owner may miss faults when checking every cake herself at the end of a long day, so some poor cakes are still sold to customers. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Outline means two linked points about this bakery, not a general list.

**6.** Ravi could ask each customer to fill in a short feedback card [1], so he can see which stylist receives complaints and give that stylist more training [1]. Other acceptable methods: a mystery customer, checking online reviews, watching stylists at work. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark comes from saying what Ravi would do with the information.

**7. (a)** Year 1: 192 ÷ 9,600 × 100 = **2.0%** [1]; Year 2: 176 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = **2.2%** [1].
**(b)** The manager is not correct [1]: the number of complaints fell only because there were fewer guests, and the complaint rate rose from 2.0% to 2.2%, so quality worsened [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing rates rather than raw numbers is the skill being tested.

**8.** Good quality means customers have fewer problems with the product [1], so they are more likely to buy again and recommend it to others [1], which raises sales compared with rivals whose products are less reliable [1]. Alternative chain: quality lets a business charge a higher price than rivals because customers think the product is worth more. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* End the chain with an effect relative to rivals; that is what makes it a competitive advantage.

**9. (a)** (96 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 [1] = **20%** [1].
**(b)** Extra profit per pair = 16 − 9 = 7 [1]; 7 × 5,000 = **35,000 a year** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The premium is measured against the rival's price, so divide by 80, not 96.

**10.** Rejected tiles each week = 4% × 50,000 = 2,000 [1], costing 2,000 × 1.20 = 2,400 a week [1]. Under TQM each worker checks their own stage, so faults are found at the first stage, where the same 2,000 faults would cost 2,000 × 0.30 = 600, a saving of 1,800 a week [1]. Workers also fix the cause of faults, so fewer faults happen at all, cutting waste further [1]. Pembroke may need fewer final inspectors, lowering labour costs [1]. However, training 600 employees costs money and time, so savings will be lower in the first months [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis uses the case figures; the calculation of 2,400 against 600 is the strongest evidence available.

**11.** Option A: defect costs stay at 6% × 60,000 = 3,600 cakes × 3 = 10,800, plus 9,000 for the inspector, so 19,800 a year [1]. It protects relationships with cafés quickly, because faulty cakes stop reaching them [1]. Option B: 2% × 60,000 = 1,200 cakes × 3 = 3,600 a year, saving 7,200 on defects [1], so the 8,000 training cost is repaid in about 13 months (8,000 ÷ 7,200 × 12) [1]. With only 8 staff, training everyone is manageable [1]. Option B's weakness is that the improvement takes a few months, so faulty cakes may still reach cafés meanwhile [1]. Recommendation: Option B [1], because from the second year it costs about 16,200 a year less than Option A (19,800 − 3,600), and the owner could check finished cakes personally for the first few months to protect the café contracts [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A justification chooses one option and explains why the other is weaker, using figures from both.

**12.** Current defect cost: 3% × 2,000,000 = 60,000 jars × 0.50 = 30,000 a year [1]. With TQM: 1% gives 20,000 jars × 0.50 = 10,000, a saving of 20,000 a year [1]. On defect savings alone, payback is 120,000 ÷ 20,000 = 6 years, which is slow [1]. However, the recall cost 400,000, more than three times the training cost, so preventing one more recall would repay TQM many times over [1]. TQM prevents faults at source rather than relying on inspectors who can miss them, which lowers the risk of another recall [1]. The fall in sales after the recall suggests reputation was damaged, and fewer faults would help win back retailers and consumers [1]. Against this, training a large workforce takes time, some staff may resist, and the 1% target may not be reached [1]. Judgement: Northgate should introduce TQM [1], because the risk of another recall matters far more than the slow payback on defect savings; success depends on staff commitment, and keeping some final checks would reduce risk while TQM settles in [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation that only calculates the 6-year payback misses the main issue in the case, the recall.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing numbers of complaints or faults instead of rates when output has changed.
- Dividing a price premium by the business's own price instead of the rival's.
- Defining TQM without saying everyone is responsible for quality.
- Analysing quality in general instead of using the case figures.
- Justifying an option without saying why the other option is weaker.
- Finishing an evaluation without a judgement or without saying what it depends on.
