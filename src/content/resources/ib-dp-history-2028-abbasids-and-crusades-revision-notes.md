**HL only.** These notes condense Regional study 1 of the Africa and the Middle East option (IB DP History, first assessment 2028): transformation in North Africa and the Middle East, 750 to 1291. Paper 3 asks for two essays of 15 marks each, from different regional studies. Each essay needs an understanding of the argument in the question, an evaluation of perspectives on it, and a supported judgement. For the fuller account, see the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-abbasids-and-crusades/).

## Six inquiry topics at a glance

| Inquiry topic | Core idea | Concept it often targets |
|---|---|---|
| Rise of the Abbasid Caliphate | Umayyad weakness plus an organised eastern movement | Cause and consequence |
| Impact of Abbasid rule | Government, trade and learning, with limits | Significance, continuity and change |
| Collapse of Abbasid rule | Slow loss of power, then the Mongol blow | Cause and consequence |
| Emergence of the Crusades | Faith, politics, wealth and land together | Cause and consequence |
| Outcome of the Crusades | Muslim unity under leaders against Western weakness | Cause and consequence, perspectives |
| Impact of the Crusades | Political, economic, social, cultural | Significance, perspectives |

## Key chronology

- 750: Battle of the Zab; Abu al-Abbas (al-Saffah) becomes first Abbasid caliph.
- 762: al-Mansur founds Baghdad.
- 786 to 809: Harun al-Rashid. 813 to 833: al-Ma'mun.
- 836: capital moved to Samarra by al-Mu'tasim.
- 909: Fatimids in Ifriqiya; 969: Fatimids take Egypt.
- 945: Buyids in Baghdad. 1055: Seljuks in Baghdad.
- 1071: Manzikert. 1095: Clermont. 1099: Jerusalem taken.
- 1144: Edessa falls to Zengi. 1187: Hattin. 1192: truce after the Third Crusade.
- 1204: Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople. 1258: Mongols sack Baghdad. 1260: Mamluks beat the Mongols at Ain Jalut.
- 1268: Baybars takes Antioch. 1291: Acre falls.

## Rise of the Abbasids

- **Umayyad weakness:** tribal rivalries, grievances of non-Arab converts (*mawali*), Shi'a hopes, revolts, civil war from 744.
- **Abbasid strength:** secret network based in Khurasan; Abu Muslim as organiser; claim of descent from the Prophet's family; black banners.
- **Result:** victory at the Zab in 750; the Umayyad family destroyed; one Umayyad survivor founded a separate state in al-Andalus.
- **Change and continuity:** Arab dominance ended and Persian influence grew, but rule stayed a caliphate under Arabic language and Islamic law.

## Abbasid rule: strengths

- Baghdad as a trading and learning centre; paper; irrigation; coinage.
- Government: caliph, vizier, bureaucracy, post and intelligence.
- Harun al-Rashid: court splendour, Barmakid ministers until 803. Al-Ma'mun: House of Wisdom and translation movement; the *mihna* from 833.
- Learning: al-Khwarizmi (algebra), al-Razi (medicine), astronomy and law.

**Limits:** heavy taxation, reliance on slave labour (the Zanj revolt of 869 to 883), regional governors with growing independence.

## Collapse of Abbasid rule

| Factor | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Political | Civil war of 811 to 813; autonomous dynasties (Tahirids, Saffarids, Tulunids) |
| Economic | Falling provincial revenue; costs of army; Zanj revolt |
| Military | Turkic soldiers; Samarra; Buyids 945; Seljuks 1055; Mongols 1258 |
| Religious | Fatimid caliphate as rival; Shi'a Buyids; pressure from Byzantines and Christians on the frontier |

**Regional impact:** power centres moved to Cairo, Syria, Iran and later Anatolia; Turks and Persians led much of the Muslim world.

## Crusades: causes

- Religious: holy war, pilgrimage, indulgence; Urban II at Clermont, 1095.
- Political: papal leadership; Byzantine request for help; divided Seljuk successors after 1092.
- Economic: land, trade, Italian city interests.
- Territorial: lordships in Syria and Palestine.

**Judgement hint:** religious motive was necessary to start the movement, but political division in Syria explains why it succeeded.

## Crusades: outcome

- **Crusader success (1099):** Muslim disunity; crusaders led by Godfrey de Bouillon and others.
- **Muslim recovery:** Zengi (Edessa 1144), Nur ad-Din (unity of Syria), Salah ad-Din (Egypt 1171, Hattin 1187), Baybars (Antioch 1268).
- **Why the Franks lasted so long:** castles, military orders, sea links with Italy, divisions among Muslim rulers after 1193.
- **Richard I:** won Acre and Arsuf in 1191 but could not take Jerusalem.

## Crusades: impact in the Middle East

- Political: Ayyubid and Mamluk regimes gain legitimacy from fighting the Franks.
- Economic: Italian trade privileges; Acre as a port.
- Social: massacres, displacement, but also daily contact.
- Cultural: memory of conflict; exchange of goods and ideas.
- Judgement hint: the Mongol invasion was more destructive for the central Islamic lands than the Crusades.

## Arguments to practise evaluating

1. "The Abbasid revolution was a break with the past." For: new dynasty, new capital, Persian influence. Against: same religion, language and fiscal system.
2. "The Golden Age was the achievement of the caliphs." For: patronage by al-Ma'mun. Against: scholars of many faiths and regions, merchants and private patrons.
3. "Military factors were the main cause of the Abbasid collapse." See the worked plan below.
4. "The Crusaders failed because Muslims united." For: Zengi to Salah ad-Din. Against: Western weakness and overstretch.

## Worked example: a plan in six lines

*Question:* "To what extent was the success of the First Crusade due to Muslim disunity?"

1. Introduction: define disunity (Seljuk successor states, Fatimid rivalry in Egypt) and state the line: disunity was the main condition, not the only cause.
2. Paragraph on disunity: Damascus, Aleppo and Egypt did not combine against the crusaders; the Fatimids even sent envoys at first.
3. Paragraph on crusader strengths: determination, leadership, supply by sea, the capture of Antioch in 1098.
4. Paragraph on the Byzantine role: guidance and the fall of Nicaea in 1097.
5. Perspectives: the Muslim chroniclers' view that the Franks exploited their rivals; modern historians who stress faith as a driving force.
6. Conclusion: disunity made success possible; faith and leadership made it actual.

## Common mistakes

- No judgement: ending with a list of causes.
- Naming a historian without using the view in the argument.
- Assigning all decline to one cause such as the Mongols.
- Mixing up Zengi, Nur ad-Din and Salah ad-Din.

## Self-test

1. Where did the Abbasid revolution begin?
2. Who founded Baghdad, and when?
3. Name two reasons the Turkic soldiers mattered to the caliphs.
4. Which battle in 1187 gave Salah ad-Din control of Jerusalem?
5. Which Mamluk sultan took Antioch in 1268?
6. Give one reason the Fourth Crusade shows the limits of religious motives.

**Answers:** 1. Khurasan. 2. Al-Mansur, in 762. 3. They became the main army and, from Samarra, commanders could remove caliphs. 4. Hattin. 5. Baybars. 6. It attacked the Christian city of Constantinople in 1204 for political and financial reasons.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much narrative do I need?** Enough accurate detail to support each paragraph. A date or name that proves a point is worth more than a paragraph of story.

**Can the question ask about "the Islamic world" as a whole?** It can, so always consider differences between Egypt, Syria, Iraq and Iran.

**What is the best command-term strategy?** "To what extent" and "Evaluate" both need a clear line of argument, a counter-view and a final judgement.
