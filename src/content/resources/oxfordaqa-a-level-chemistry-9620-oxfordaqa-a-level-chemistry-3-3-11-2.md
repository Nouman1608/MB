This guide goes deeper into **3.3.11.2 Base properties**, the second subtopic of section 3.3.11 Amines in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5. The subtopic is short, but it carries one of the most common extended explanations in organic chemistry: why base strength changes from aliphatic amines to ammonia to aromatic amines. This guide shows how to build that explanation and how amine basicity appears in calculations.

## What the specification asks for

- **Amines are weak bases.**
- **The difference in base strength between ammonia, primary aliphatic and primary aromatic amines.**
- Explain the difference in base strength **in terms of the availability of the lone pair of electrons on the N atom**.

Secondary and tertiary amines are not part of the comparison, and no Kb values are required.

## Why amines are bases

A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor. The nitrogen atom of an amine has a **lone pair**, which can form a co-ordinate (dative) bond to H⁺:

`CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺`  (butylammonium ion)

In water, the amine takes H⁺ from water molecules:

`CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻`

The equilibrium lies well to the left, so only a small proportion of amine molecules are protonated. That is what **weak base** means. The OH⁻ ions formed make the solution alkaline, so it turns red litmus blue, but its pH is lower than that of sodium hydroxide at the same concentration.

## Amines and acids: salts

With a strong acid the amine is protonated completely, forming an ammonium salt. Name the salt by changing "-amine" to "-ammonium" and adding the anion:

- `CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻`  (ethylammonium chloride)
- `C₆H₅NH₂ + HCl → C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻`  (phenylammonium chloride)
- `2CH₃NH₂ + H₂SO₄ → (CH₃NH₃⁺)₂SO₄²⁻`  (methylammonium sulfate)

These salts are ionic solids, usually soluble in water. Adding a strong base such as sodium hydroxide removes H⁺ and releases the amine again:

`CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂O`

This pair of reactions explains the last step in the tin and hydrochloric acid reduction of nitro compounds, and it is how an amine can be dissolved in acid and then recovered.

## Comparing base strength

The more available the lone pair on nitrogen, the more readily it accepts H⁺, and the stronger the base. Order:

**primary aliphatic amine > ammonia > primary aromatic amine**

| Compound | What is attached to N | Effect on the lone pair | Base strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Butylamine, C₄H₉NH₂ | An alkyl group | The alkyl group pushes electron density towards N (positive inductive effect), so the lone pair is **more** available | Strongest |
| Ammonia, NH₃ | Only H atoms | No electron-releasing group; the reference point | Middle |
| Phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂ | A benzene ring carbon | The lone pair overlaps with the ring's delocalised p electrons and is partly drawn into the ring, so it is **less** available | Weakest |

### Worked example 1: an annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why ethylamine is a stronger base than ammonia, and ammonia is a stronger base than phenylamine. [5]*

Model answer, with the marking point after each sentence:

1. "A base accepts a proton using the lone pair on its nitrogen atom; the more available the lone pair, the stronger the base." (states the basis of comparison)
2. "In ethylamine the ethyl group releases electron density towards nitrogen (positive inductive effect)." (effect in the aliphatic amine)
3. "This increases the electron density on N, so its lone pair is more available than in ammonia, which has only H atoms on N." (comparison with ammonia)
4. "In phenylamine the nitrogen lone pair overlaps with the delocalised electrons of the benzene ring and is partly delocalised into it." (effect in the aromatic amine)
5. "So the lone pair is less available to accept a proton than in ammonia, and phenylamine is the weakest base." (comparison with ammonia)

Every sentence compares or explains; none just repeats the order. The words "lone pair" and "available" appear for each compound.

### Worked example 2: a ring that is not aromatic to N

Rank 2-phenylethylamine (C₆H₅CH₂CH₂NH₂), phenylamine and ammonia in order of base strength.

1. In 2-phenylethylamine, N is bonded to a CH₂ group, not to the ring. The lone pair cannot overlap with the ring's delocalised electrons, and the alkyl chain releases electron density. It behaves as a **primary aliphatic amine**.
2. In phenylamine, N is bonded directly to the ring, so the lone pair is delocalised into it.
3. Order: **2-phenylethylamine > ammonia > phenylamine**.

The test is always the atom bonded to N, not whether a benzene ring appears somewhere in the formula.

## Base properties in calculations

Because the amine reacts completely with a strong acid in a 1 : 1 ratio (for one –NH₂ group), titration with standard hydrochloric acid measures the amount of amine.

### Worked example 3: identifying an amine by titration

A 0.590 g sample of a primary aliphatic amine, RNH₂, is dissolved in water and titrated with 0.500 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. The end point is at 20.0 cm³. Identify the amine.

1. n(HCl) = 0.500 × 20.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0100 mol.
2. RNH₂ + HCl → RNH₃⁺Cl⁻, so n(amine) = 0.0100 mol.
3. Mr = 0.590 ÷ 0.0100 = **59.0**.
4. –NH₂ contributes 16.0, so R has mass 43.0: C₃H₇. Formula C₃H₉N.
5. Two primary amines fit: **propylamine**, CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂, and **propan-2-amine**, (CH₃)₂CHNH₂.

This is a weak base titrated with a strong acid, so the equivalence point is below pH 7. Choose an indicator that changes colour in the acidic range, such as methyl orange; phenolphthalein would change too early. The pH curve reasoning is in the [9620 acids and bases guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-acids-and-bases/).

## Common mistakes

- Calling amines strong bases because they react completely with HCl. "Weak" refers to their reaction with water.
- Explaining phenylamine's weakness as "the benzene ring is bulky" or "the ring withdraws electrons" without mentioning the lone pair being delocalised into the ring.
- Writing "the alkyl group makes the lone pair more negative" without "more available".
- Placing ammonia above all amines, forgetting that aromatic amines are weaker than ammonia.
- Treating any amine with a benzene ring as aromatic.
- Writing the ammonium salt with a covalent N–Cl bond; show it as RNH₃⁺Cl⁻.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** base strength: name the lone pair on N, say whether it is more or less available than in ammonia, and give the reason for each compound.
- **Compare**: every statement should contain a comparative word (more, less, stronger, weaker).
- **Write equations** with the correct ion: RNH₃⁺, not RNH₄⁺.
- **Calculate**: use the 1 : 1 ratio with HCl and the 2 : 1 ratio (amine : acid) with H₂SO₄.
