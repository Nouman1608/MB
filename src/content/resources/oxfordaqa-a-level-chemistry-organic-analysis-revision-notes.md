## 3.3.6.1 Test tube reactions (Required practical 5)

Required practical 5: "Carry out tests for alcohols, aldehydes, alkenes and carboxylic acids". The
reactions come from other 9620 sections.

| Looking for | Reagent and condition | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkene, C=C | Shake with bromine water | Orange → colourless | Remains orange |
| 1° or 2° alcohol | Warm with K₂Cr₂O₇ and acid | Orange → green | Remains orange (3° alcohol, ketone) |
| Aldehyde | Fehling's solution, warm | Blue → red precipitate (Cu₂O) | Stays blue |
| Aldehyde | Tollens' reagent, warm | Silver mirror | No mirror |
| Carboxylic acid | Na₂CO₃(aq) | Fizzing; CO₂ turns limewater milky | No fizzing |

Stage label: the carbonate reaction appears in 3.3.9.1, which is **International A-level only**. The test
is still needed for Required practical 5.

### What changes in each test

| Test | Species before | Species after | Type of change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dichromate(VI) | Cr₂O₇²⁻ (orange) | Cr³⁺ (green) | Reduction of Cr; alcohol oxidised |
| Fehling's | Cu(II) complex (blue) | Cu₂O (red solid) | Reduction of Cu; aldehyde oxidised |
| Tollens' | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ (colourless) | Ag (mirror) | Reduction of Ag; aldehyde oxidised |
| Bromine water | Br₂ (orange) | Dibromoalkane (colourless) | Electrophilic addition |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ | CO₂ gas | Acid–carbonate reaction |

### Method in steps: sorting a set of unknowns

1. Write the functional groups present in each candidate.
2. Pick a first test that only one candidate passes. The carbonate test is often a good start.
3. Remove aldehydes with Fehling's or Tollens' before any dichromate(VI) test.
4. Use bromine water for C=C.
5. Use dichromate(VI) to split 1°/2° alcohols from everything that stays orange.
6. If a ketone and a 3° alcohol remain, no AS test tube reaction separates them: switch to IR.

### Practical points

- Heat with a water bath; most of these liquids are flammable.
- Record the starting colour and the final colour.
- Run a blank with water for comparison.

## 3.3.6.2 Mass spectrometry

**Key idea.** Two formulae can share an integer Mr yet differ in precise mass. Measuring the molecular
ion's m/z to 4 decimal places pins down which formula is right. The instrument itself (ionisation,
acceleration, drift, detection) is in the [atomic structure guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-atomic-structure/).

Precise masses used here: C = 12.0000, H = 1.0078, O = 15.9949, N = 14.0031.

### Method in steps

1. Write every candidate formula.
2. For each, add (number of atoms × precise mass) for every element.
3. Keep four decimal places.
4. Match one total to the measured m/z.
5. Show the totals for the losing candidates as well.

### Worked reminder: m/z = 90.0678

```
C4H10O2  : 4(12.0000) + 10(1.0078) + 2(15.9949)               = 90.0678   match
C3H6O3   : 3(12.0000) + 6(1.0078) + 3(15.9949)                = 90.0315
C3H10N2O : 3(12.0000) + 10(1.0078) + 15.9949 + 2(14.0031)     = 90.0791
```

The molecular formula is **C₄H₁₀O₂**. It might be a diol such as butane-1,4-diol, or many other isomers.
Only IR, test tube results or fingerprint comparison can go further.

## 3.3.6.3 Infrared spectroscopy

**Key idea.** Each type of bond absorbs IR radiation in its own wavenumber range (cm⁻¹). A spectrum shows transmittance
against wavenumber; absorptions point downwards. You use the spectrum together with the Chemistry data
booklet. In the exam, take the ranges from the booklet you are given; these are the ones used here.

| Bond | Range / cm⁻¹ | Shape clue |
|---|---|---|
| O–H (alcohols) | 3230–3550 | Broad (hydrogen bonding) |
| N–H | 3300–3500 | Narrower than alcohol O–H |
| C–H | 2850–3300 | Almost always present |
| O–H (acids) | 2500–3000 | Very broad, merges with C–H |
| C≡N | 2220–2260 | Sharp |
| C=O | 1680–1750 | Intense and narrow |
| C=C | 1620–1680 | Weaker than C=O |
| C–O | 1000–1300 | In the fingerprint region |

### Method in steps: reading a spectrum

1. Broad band at 3230–3550? Alcohol O–H.
2. Very broad band at 2500–3000? Acid O–H.
3. Strong band at 1680–1750? C=O.
4. Band at 1620–1680? C=C. Band at 2220–2260? C≡N.
5. List what is missing. Each absence rules structures out.
6. Do not use C–H as evidence for a functional group.

### Fingerprint region

Below about 1500 cm⁻¹. The pattern belongs to the whole molecule, so it is characteristic of one compound.
Match it against spectra of known compounds to identify the substance. It distinguishes isomers that share
the same bonds.

### Impurities

An absorption the expected product cannot produce signals an impurity. Typical cases:

| Expected product | Extra band seen | Likely impurity |
|---|---|---|
| Aldehyde | Very broad 2500–3000 | Carboxylic acid (over-oxidation) |
| Ketone or alkene | Broad 3230–3550 | Unreacted alcohol, or water |
| Nitrile | Broad 3230–3550 | Water or an alcohol side product |

### Worked reminder: reading clues together

A compound shows a band at 1660 cm⁻¹ and a broad band at 3310 cm⁻¹, with nothing at 1680–1750 cm⁻¹. It
turns bromine water from orange to colourless and acidified dichromate(VI) from orange to green.

- 1660 cm⁻¹: C=C. Bromine water result agrees.
- 3310 cm⁻¹, broad: alcohol O–H.
- No C=O band, so the dichromate result is not from an aldehyde.
- Conclusion: an **unsaturated primary or secondary alcohol**, such as but-3-en-1-ol.

### Global warming link

The Earth's surface emits infrared. C=O bonds in CO₂, C–H bonds in methane and O–H bonds in water vapour
absorb it. The energy is passed on or re-emitted in all directions, some back to the surface. Higher
concentrations of these gases mean more infrared absorbed, which contributes to global warming.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|
| Fehling's vs Tollens' | Blue → red precipitate vs silver mirror |
| Aldehyde vs ketone | Both C=O in IR; only the aldehyde reacts with Fehling's or Tollens' |
| 1°/2° alcohol vs 3° alcohol | Orange → green vs stays orange |
| Ketone vs 3° alcohol | No test tube difference at AS; IR shows C=O or O–H |
| Acid O–H vs alcohol O–H | Very broad at 2500–3000 vs broad at 3230–3550 |
| Molecular formula vs structure | Mass spectrometry gives the first; IR, tests and fingerprinting give the second |

## Quick self-test

1. Write the formula of the silver complex in Tollens' reagent.
2. Hex-3-ene is shaken with bromine water. Give the full observation.
3. Which one of these does **not** change the colour of warm acidified dichromate(VI): pentan-2-ol,
   2-methylbutan-2-ol, 3-methylbutan-1-ol?
4. Name the red solid formed when an aldehyde reacts with Fehling's solution.
5. A molecular ion appears at m/z = 56.0624. Is the compound C₄H₈, C₃H₄O or C₂H₄N₂? Show the sums.
6. A spectrum has a sharp absorption at 2240 cm⁻¹. Which bond is present?
7. Why is the O–H absorption of an alcohol broad?
8. A spectrum shows a very broad band from 2500 to 3000 cm⁻¹ and a strong band at 1705 cm⁻¹. Which
   functional group is present?
9. Where is the fingerprint region, and how is it used to identify a compound?
10. Which gas is released when a carboxylic acid reacts with sodium carbonate, and how do you test for it?
11. Why should aldehydes be ruled out before using dichromate(VI) to test for an alcohol?
12. Name the bond in each of carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour that absorbs infrared radiation.

### Answers

1. [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺.
2. The bromine water changes from orange to colourless.
3. 2-methylbutan-2-ol. It is a tertiary alcohol, so it is not easily oxidised and the solution stays orange.
4. Copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O.
5. C₄H₈: 4(12.0000) + 8(1.0078) = 56.0624. C₃H₄O: 3(12.0000) + 4(1.0078) + 15.9949 = 56.0261.
   C₂H₄N₂: 2(12.0000) + 4(1.0078) + 2(14.0031) = 56.0374. The compound is **C₄H₈**.
6. C≡N (2220–2260 cm⁻¹), a nitrile.
7. Hydrogen bonding between molecules gives a spread of O–H bond strengths, so absorption covers a range.
8. A carboxylic acid: acid O–H (2500–3000) plus C=O (1680–1750).
9. Below about 1500 cm⁻¹. Compare it with spectra of known compounds; an exact match identifies it.
10. Carbon dioxide; it turns limewater milky.
11. Aldehydes are oxidised by dichromate(VI) as well, so they also turn it from orange to green.
12. C=O in carbon dioxide, C–H in methane, O–H in water vapour.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing bromine water as turning "clear", or reporting just the end colour of a test.
- Swapping Fehling's and Tollens' observations, or calling the Fehling's product "copper".
- Concluding "alcohol" from a dichromate(VI) result while an aldehyde is still possible.
- Claiming a test tube reaction separates a ketone from a tertiary alcohol at AS.
- Choosing a molecular formula without showing the precise-mass total for every candidate.
- Rounding precise masses so that two candidates appear identical.
- Assigning a very broad 2500–3000 cm⁻¹ band to an alcohol.
- Using C–H absorptions as evidence for a particular functional group.
- Describing fingerprinting without saying the spectrum is compared with known spectra.
- Leaving bonds absorbing infrared out of the global warming explanation.
