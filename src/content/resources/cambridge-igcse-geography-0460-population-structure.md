This guide covers topic 1.3 Population structure in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus asks you to "identify and give reasons for and implications of different types of population structure", using age/sex pyramids of countries at different levels of economic development, with a case study of a country with a high dependent population. The [Population Structure revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-population-structure-revision-notes/) give the basic shapes and the Japan and Niger case studies; this guide adds how to explain individual bulges and notches, pyramids for places smaller than a country, worked dependency calculations and a further case study. Practise with the [Population Structure practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-geography-0460-population-structure-practice/).

## Reading the pyramid before you describe it

Check three things first:

1. **The horizontal axis.** Is it a percentage of the total population or an absolute number (thousands or millions)? Quote the unit in every figure you use.
2. **The age groups.** Most pyramids use five-year groups (cohorts), with an open-ended top group such as 80+ or 85+.
3. **Which side is which.** Males are usually on the left and females on the right, but check the labels.

Then describe in this order: the **base** (birth rate), the **sides** (how quickly numbers fall with age, showing death rate), the **top** (life expectancy), and any **irregularities** or **differences between the sexes**.

## The three main types and their reasons

| Type | Shape | Reasons | Typical level of development |
|---|---|---|---|
| Youthful (expansive) | Wide base, concave sides narrowing quickly, small top | High birth rate; relatively high death rate at all ages; short life expectancy | Low-income countries |
| Transitional | Base starting to narrow; straighter sides; wide middle | Birth rate falling; death rate low; earlier large cohorts now of working age | Emerging and middle-income countries |
| Ageing (constrictive) | Narrow base, wide middle and top | Low birth rate, often below replacement; low death rate; long life expectancy | High-income countries |

## Explaining bulges, notches and imbalances

Pyramids record history. An unusually wide or narrow bar is a group born at a particular time, and it moves up the pyramid by five years every five years.

- **Baby boom**: a bulge in the cohorts born after a war or during a period of prosperity. Many high-income countries have a bulge of people born in the late 1940s to early 1960s, now in their sixties and seventies, which is why pension costs are rising.
- **Fall in births**: a notch in a cohort born during a war, famine, economic crisis or a strict anti-natalist policy. China's one-child policy narrowed the cohorts born after 1980.
- **Deaths in conflict or epidemics**: a notch in the age groups most affected, often young adult men in wars. HIV/AIDS in southern Africa narrowed young adult cohorts of both sexes.
- **Immigration**: a bulge in young working ages, often mainly men. In some Gulf states the male bars in the 25-44 age groups are several times longer than the female bars, because of temporary labour migrants.
- **Emigration**: a notch in young adult cohorts of the origin area, sometimes with more women than men left behind.
- **Female life expectancy**: in almost every country women live longer than men, so the top bars are wider on the female side.

When a question asks you to "suggest reasons" for an irregularity, give the cause and the age group it affected: "a notch at 40-44 could be due to a fall in births 40 to 44 years ago".

## Pyramids for places smaller than a country

The same reading skills apply to towns and regions, and examiners like to test them on unfamiliar resources.

| Place | Distinctive feature | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Coastal retirement town | Wide top, especially 65+ | Older people move in after retiring |
| University town | Large bulge at 18-24 | Students move in for courses |
| Rural area with out-migration | Notch at 20-39, wide top | Young adults leave for city jobs |
| Inner-city area with recent migrants | Bulge at 20-39 and a growing base | Young migrants arrive and start families |
| New suburban estate | Bulges at 30-44 and 0-14 | Young families buy new houses |

## Dependency: the calculation and what it means

The **dependent population** is usually defined as those aged 0-14 (young dependants) and 65 and over (elderly dependants). The **working** population is aged 15-64.

**Dependency ratio = (population aged 0-14 + population aged 65+) ÷ population aged 15-64 × 100**

**Worked example (fictional countries, invented data).**

| | 0-14 | 15-64 | 65+ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Country P | 42% | 54% | 4% |
| Country Q | 14% | 60% | 26% |

- Country P: (42 + 4) ÷ 54 × 100 = **85.2**. Young dependency alone = 42 ÷ 54 × 100 = 77.8.
- Country Q: (14 + 26) ÷ 60 × 100 = **66.7**. Elderly dependency alone = 26 ÷ 60 × 100 = 43.3.

Country P has the higher overall ratio, but its dependants are mostly children; Country Q's are mostly elderly. The implications are very different, so always say which group dominates.

Remember the ratio is an approximation: many 15-year-olds are still in school, while in many low-income countries children and people over 65 work.

## Implications of the two high-dependency types

**Youthful structure**

- Large demand for schools, teachers, vaccination and maternal healthcare.
- Millions of young people reaching working age each year need jobs; if jobs are not created, unemployment and out-migration rise.
- **Population momentum**: the large cohorts of children will soon have children themselves, so the population keeps growing even if each family becomes smaller.
- Opportunity: if birth rates fall and jobs are created, a large working-age population can drive economic growth.

**Ageing structure**

- Rising spending on pensions, healthcare and care homes, paid for by a shrinking number of taxpayers.
- Labour shortages, especially in care, farming and construction; pressure to raise the retirement age or recruit migrants.
- Schools close as cohorts of children shrink.
- Opportunity: healthy older people work, volunteer and spend, supporting services and leisure industries.

## Case study: a youthful high dependent population, Uganda

Uganda, a landlocked country in East Africa, has one of the youngest populations in the world: close to half of its people are under 15. Fertility has fallen since the 1990s but remains high, because of early marriage in many rural areas, limited access to family planning and the importance of children for farm labour.

- **Implications for education**: in 1997 Uganda introduced Universal Primary Education, removing primary school fees. Enrolment rose sharply, but classes became very large and there were not enough trained teachers or classrooms.
- **Implications for jobs**: the number of young people entering the labour market each year is far greater than the number of formal jobs created, so many work in farming or the informal sector.
- **Implications for health**: high demand for maternal and child health services, and pressure on clinics in fast-growing districts.

Use Uganda alongside the ageing Japan example in your revision notes to show both kinds of high dependency, or learn whichever your teacher has covered in depth.

## Model answer: give reasons and implications

*Question: Explain the shape of a pyramid for a low-income country and the problems it may cause.*

> The pyramid has a wide base and narrows quickly (knowledge) because the birth rate is high and many people die before old age, so each older cohort is smaller (application). About half the population may be under 15, so the government must provide large numbers of school places and vaccinations (analysis). When these children reach working age, there may not be enough jobs, which can lead to unemployment and migration to cities (analysis). The problems are greatest if the birth rate stays high, but if it falls, the large working-age population could help the economy to grow (evaluation).

## Common errors

- Describing the pyramid without quoting any age groups or values.
- Treating the axis as numbers of people when it shows percentages, or the reverse.
- Saying a narrow top means a high death rate in old age; it shows low life expectancy, meaning people die before reaching old age.
- Calculating the dependency ratio with working population on top.
- Listing implications for a youthful country that belong to an ageing one, such as pension costs.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a pyramid: base, sides, top, irregularities, sexes, each with figures.
- **Compare** two pyramids: use comparative words ("wider", "whereas") with a value from each.
- **Suggest reasons**: link each feature to a cause and, for bulges or notches, to a date.
- **Explain implications**: name the service or economic effect and say why it follows from the structure.
