Condensed notes on section **3.4.4 Making operational decisions to improve performance: improving quality** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The full explanations and the Penhallow Appliances worked example are in the [improving quality study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-improving-quality/); exam-style questions are in the [improving quality practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-improving-quality-practice/).

## Specification content

- Methods of improving quality, including quality assurance against quality control.
- The benefits and difficulties of improving quality.
- The consequences of poor quality.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Quality | How well a product or service meets customers' expectations and needs |
| Quality control (QC) | Checking output to find and remove defective items |
| Quality assurance (QA) | A system that builds quality into every stage to prevent defects |
| Total quality management (TQM) | Business-wide commitment to "right first time" |
| Quality circle | Small group that meets to solve quality problems |
| Kaizen | Continuous improvement through small changes |
| Benchmarking | Comparing performance with the best in the industry |
| Defect rate | Proportion of output that is faulty |

## QC against QA

| | Quality control | Quality assurance |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Find defects | Prevent defects |
| Timing | During or after production | Throughout: design, suppliers, process |
| Who | Inspectors | Everyone |
| Costs | Inspection, rework, scrap | Training, systems, design |
| Culture | Checking | Ownership and improvement |

- **QC strengths:** catches defects before customers; simple; gives data. **Weaknesses:** finds faults after they occur; inspection adds cost but no value; sampling can miss faults.
- **QA features:** clear standards; supplier checks; training and empowerment; quality circles; kaizen; benchmarking; TQM.
- Most firms use both.

## Other methods

Better design and materials; better equipment and maintenance; customer feedback and mystery shoppers; automated inspection and sensors.

## The four costs of quality

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Prevention | Stopping defects | Training, supplier checks |
| Appraisal | Finding defects | Inspection, testing |
| Internal failure | Defects found before delivery | Rework, scrap |
| External failure | Defects found by customers | Refunds, recalls, lost reputation |

External failure is usually the most damaging.

## Worked example to remember

20,000 kettles a month.

- **Control:** 5% defective = 1,000; inspection catches 90% = 900 reworked at £6 = £5,400; 100 reach customers at £40 = £4,000; inspection £8,000; total **£17,400**.
- **Assurance:** £10,000 on prevention; defect rate 1.5% = 300; 270 reworked at £6 = £1,620; 30 reach customers at £40 = £1,200; inspection £3,000; total **£15,820**.
- Saving £1,580 a month, plus fewer lost customers and better morale. The case depends on the defect rate really falling.

## Benefits of improving quality

Fewer defects and less waste; fewer returns and claims; satisfaction and loyalty; better reputation and room for higher prices; lower total costs if prevention beats correction; competitive advantage; motivated staff; ability to win demanding contracts.

## Difficulties

Cost and time to see returns; cultural change and resistance; conflict with cost, speed or flexibility; customers may not pay more; measurement is hard in services; diminishing returns near perfection.

## Consequences of poor quality

Lost sales; damaged reputation (reviews, social media); rework, refunds, recalls; legal action and fines; forced price cuts; low staff morale; lost retailer or supplier contracts; in extreme cases, failure of the business.

## Quality in services and sampling

- In services, a fault cannot be inspected out before the customer sees it, so **assurance** (recruitment, training, standards, good processes) matters more than control. Feedback, reviews and mystery shoppers show where standards slip.
- Sampling saves cost and is essential when testing destroys the product. Defect rate = faulty items ÷ sample size × 100. Expected faulty items in a batch = batch size × defect rate.
- A sample must be random and large enough to be reliable. The acceptable defect rate should reflect the cost of failure and be far lower for safety-critical goods.

## How to write a good answer

- Use "prevent" for assurance and "detect" for control.
- Apply the method to the product or service in the case.
- Weigh the cost of improving quality against the cost of failure (rework, refunds, recalls, lost customers), then judge what the business should do.

## Exam traps

- QC **detects**; QA **prevents**.
- Quality does not mean luxury; it means meeting the customer's needs consistently.
- QA is not "more inspections".
- Evaluate: compare the cost of improving with the cost of failure.
- Link the method to the business in the case (batch sampling for factories; training for services).

## Self-test

1. Define quality assurance.
2. Define quality control.
3. Give one weakness of quality control.
4. Name two features of a quality assurance system.
5. What is a quality circle?
6. Give two benefits of improving quality.
7. Give two difficulties of improving quality.
8. Which type of quality cost is a recall of faulty products?
9. A sample of 500 items contains 15 faulty items. Calculate the defect rate.
10. Using that rate, how many faulty items would you expect in a batch of 12,000?
11. Give two consequences of poor quality.
12. Why might spending more on prevention reduce total quality costs?

### Answers

1. A system that builds quality into every stage of the process to prevent defects.
2. Checking output to find and remove items that do not meet the standard.
3. Any one: finds faults only after they occur; inspection adds cost but no value; sampling may miss faults.
4. Any two: clear standards; supplier checks; training; quality circles; kaizen; benchmarking; TQM.
5. A small group of workers that meets regularly to find solutions to quality problems.
6. Any two: fewer defects; fewer returns; customer loyalty; better reputation; competitive advantage.
7. Any two: cost; time; resistance to change; conflict with cost or speed objectives; measurement difficulty.
8. External failure.
9. 15 ÷ 500 × 100 = **3%**.
10. 12,000 × 0.03 = **360 faulty items**.
11. Any two: lost sales; damaged reputation; rework and refund costs; legal action; lower prices.
12. Prevention reduces defects, so fewer faulty items need rework, scrapping, refunds or recall, and these failure costs, especially external ones, are often larger than the prevention spending.
