## Market research

**Market research** is the collection and analysis of information about a market, its customers and competitors, so that marketing decisions are based on evidence.

- **Primary research** is new information collected for the business's own purpose. Techniques include surveys and questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation and customer feedback forms.
- **Secondary research** uses information that already exists, such as government statistics, trade reports, competitors' accounts, press articles and the business's own sales records.

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Fit with the question | Designed for the exact question | May not match the question or the target group |
| Cost and speed | Often slower and more expensive | Usually quicker and cheaper |
| Control and bias | The business controls the questions and sample, but may introduce bias | Collected by others; accuracy and age vary |
| Competitors | Private to the business | Available to rivals too |
| Quality of detail | Can explore reasons and attitudes | Often broad figures with little reason behind them |

Neither is better in every case. A small firm testing a new flavour needs primary research because no one else has asked its customers. A firm considering entry to a new country may start with secondary research on market size before paying for primary research.

**Qualitative** research (focus groups, interviews) gives opinions and reasons, and numerical **quantitative** research (surveys with many respondents) gives figures that can be counted and tested. A business needs both: numbers show what is happening, and words show why.

## Trial marketing

**Trial marketing** (test marketing) offers a product or service to a limited group of customers or one area before a full launch, in order to measure reaction. For example, a bakery sells a new product in two of its twelve shops for a month.

Benefits: it reveals problems at low cost; it measures actual buying rather than stated intentions; it allows adjustments to price or packaging. Limitations: it takes time, so competitors may copy the idea; the trial area may not represent the whole market; the trial itself costs money; and a large launch may behave differently from a small test.

## Sampling

A business cannot ask every customer, so it asks a **sample** of the **population** (everyone in the target group). The method affects how far results can be trusted.

| Method | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random | Every member has an equal chance of selection | Reduces bias | Needs a full list; may miss small groups |
| Stratified | The population is split into groups (strata) and each is sampled in proportion | Represents each group accurately | Needs prior knowledge of the groups |
| Cluster | The population is divided into areas and some whole areas are surveyed | Cheap when customers are spread out | Clusters may not represent the whole |
| Systematic | Every nth member of a list is chosen | Simple and quick | Can be biased if the list has a pattern |
| Quota | Interviewers must fill set numbers of types of respondent (for example 50 men and 50 women) | Quick, ensures a mix | Interviewers choose who to ask, so selection can be biased |
| Convenience | Whoever is easy to reach is asked | Cheapest and fastest | Likely to be unrepresentative |

**Example.** A gym has 1,200 members, 720 under 30 and 480 aged 30 or over. It wants a sample of 100. A **stratified** sample takes 100 × 720 ÷ 1,200 = **60** under-30s and 100 × 480 ÷ 1,200 = **40** older members. A **systematic** sample picks every 1,200 ÷ 100 = **12th** name on the membership list.

## Worked example 1: standard deviation and the normal distribution

Standard deviation measures how widely data is spread around the mean. A small standard deviation means results are close to the mean and consistent. A large one means they vary a lot. This guide uses the formula with n in the denominator (the spread of the whole data set shown); if a question supplies a different formula, use that one.

**Case.** Two stores of Brimfield Bikes record daily sales of helmets over eight days (units).

| | Day 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Store A | 42 | 48 | 45 | 50 | 39 | 47 | 44 | 45 |
| Store B | 30 | 62 | 45 | 58 | 25 | 55 | 40 | 45 |

**Step 1: mean.** Store A: 360 ÷ 8 = **45**. Store B: 360 ÷ 8 = **45**. The means are identical.

**Step 2: deviations from the mean.**

- A: −3, 3, 0, 5, −6, 2, −1, 0
- B: −15, 17, 0, 13, −20, 10, −5, 0

**Step 3: square each deviation and add.**

- A: 9 + 9 + 0 + 25 + 36 + 4 + 1 + 0 = **84**
- B: 225 + 289 + 0 + 169 + 400 + 100 + 25 + 0 = **1,208**

**Step 4: divide by 8 to give the variance.** A: 84 ÷ 8 = 10.5. B: 1,208 ÷ 8 = 151.

**Step 5: take the square root.** A: √10.5 = **3.24**. B: √151 = **12.29**.

**Interpretation.** Both stores sell 45 helmets a day on average, but Store B's sales vary almost four times as much. Store A can plan stock and staff confidently. Store B has days when it runs out and days when stock is left. A manager would investigate why B is so variable (for example, events nearby) before deciding on orders.

**Normal distribution.** Many sets of data, such as customer spending, form a bell-shaped curve centred on the mean. About **68%** of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean and about **95%** within two. If 2,000 customers spend a mean of £48 with a standard deviation of £6: 68% (1,360 customers) spend between £42 and £54; 95% (1,900 customers) spend between £36 and £60. Customers above £54 are roughly 16% (320 customers). A business can use this to set price bands and plan offers.

**Usefulness and limits.** Standard deviation summarises consistency in one number and helps compare stores or products. But it describes the past, assumes data are roughly normal, and can be distorted by one extreme value. It does not explain why the variation happens.

## Market analysis

**Market share** is a firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales: (firm's sales ÷ market sales) × 100. **Market growth** is the percentage change in total market sales between two periods. **Quantitative** analysis uses numbers, such as sales values and share. **Qualitative** analysis uses attitudes, brand image and customer reasons. A business needs both, because a rising share could hide falling satisfaction.

### Worked example 2: share and growth together

A regional market for energy bars was worth £50.0 million last year and £53.5 million this year. Brimfield's sales were £6.00 million last year and £6.42 million this year.

1. Market growth = (53.5 − 50.0) ÷ 50.0 × 100 = **7.0%**.
2. Share last year = 6.00 ÷ 50.0 × 100 = **12.0%**.
3. Share this year = 6.42 ÷ 53.5 × 100 = **12.0%**.
4. Brimfield's growth = (6.42 − 6.00) ÷ 6.00 × 100 = **7.0%**.

Brimfield's sales grew by 7%, but because the market grew by the same amount, its share did not change. If its sales had risen only to £6.10 million, its share would have fallen to 6.10 ÷ 53.5 = **11.4%**. Sales would have risen by 1.7% and the business would still have lost ground. This is why share is a more reliable measure of competitive performance than sales alone.

**Responses.** For positive market growth, a business can invest to keep pace (more stock, capacity and promotion) and try to gain share while customers are still choosing brands. For negative growth, it may cut costs, focus on loyal customers or look for new segments and markets. For rising share, it can protect the lead and watch for retaliation. For falling share, it should find the cause (price, quality, rival promotion) with research and respond.

## Using market data

Data helps a business to choose products, prices, and segments with less risk, and to decide whether to enter or leave a market. Ignoring it can mean launching products customers do not want, missing a fall in demand, or being overtaken by rivals. But data can also mislead if the sample is biased, the questions are leading, or the figures are old. The best decisions combine data with judgement and, where possible, a trial.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the first technique found (a survey) as the best without linking it to the case.
- Calling convenience sampling random.
- Forgetting to square the deviations, or to divide before the square root.
- Calculating share with last year's market value.
- Saying sales rose, so the business is doing well, without checking market growth.

## Exam technique

Calculation questions give marks for method. Show the mean, deviations, squares, division and root. For evaluation, weigh cost, speed, accuracy and fit with the question, and finish with a judgement about the business in the case.
