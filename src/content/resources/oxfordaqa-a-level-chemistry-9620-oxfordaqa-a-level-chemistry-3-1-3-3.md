## What the specification asks for

The specification states: metallic bonding involves attraction between delocalised electrons and positive ions arranged in a lattice.

Every word of that sentence can be examined. You need to be able to:

- explain what "delocalised" means and where the electrons come from
- describe the lattice of positive ions, with the correct charge for a given metal
- identify the attraction that is the bond
- use the model to compare the strength of metallic bonding in different metals.

How metallic bonding explains the physical properties of metals, using magnesium as the named example, is developed in subtopic 3.1.3.4.

## Building the model

In a solid metal, each atom releases its outer-shell electrons. These electrons are no longer attached to any one atom: they are **delocalised**, free to move throughout the whole structure. What remains of each atom is a **positive ion**. The positive ions are packed closely in a regular, repeating arrangement: a **lattice**.

The **metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in the lattice and the delocalised electrons**. The electrons act as a kind of "glue": each ion is attracted to the delocalised electrons around it, and the electrons are attracted to all the ions.

| Metal | Group | Outer electrons released per atom | Ion in the lattice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 1 | 1 | Na⁺ |
| Magnesium | 2 | 2 | Mg²⁺ |
| Aluminium | 13 | 3 | Al³⁺ |

Three points are worth stating clearly:

1. **The metal is still neutral overall.** The total negative charge of the delocalised electrons equals the total positive charge of the ions. A magnesium lattice has two delocalised electrons for every Mg²⁺ ion.
2. **The bonding is non-directional.** Each ion is attracted to the delocalised electrons in every direction, not to one particular electron or one neighbouring ion.
3. **There are no molecules.** The structure is a giant lattice that continues throughout the piece of metal.

### Drawing a metallic lattice

A clear diagram shows several rows of circles, each labelled with the ion's charge (for example 2+ for magnesium), with electrons (e⁻) drawn in the spaces between them. A good diagram has roughly the right ratio of electrons to ions and a label such as "sea of delocalised electrons". Labelling the circles as "atoms" or leaving out the charges loses credit.

## How metallic bonding compares with other bonds

| Bond | What attracts what |
|---|---|
| Ionic | Positive ions and negative ions |
| Covalent | Two nuclei and a shared pair of electrons between them |
| Metallic | Positive ions and delocalised electrons |

All three are strong electrostatic attractions. The difference is the identity of the negative partner: anions in ionic bonding, a localised shared pair in covalent bonding, and a mobile "sea" of electrons in metallic bonding.

## What makes metallic bonding stronger

The strength of the attraction depends on two factors:

- **The charge on the ions, which also equals the number of delocalised electrons per atom.** A higher charge and more delocalised electrons give a stronger attraction.
- **The size of the ions.** Smaller ions have their positive charge closer to the delocalised electrons, so the attraction is stronger.

Stronger metallic bonding needs more energy to overcome, so a higher melting point is a common piece of evidence for stronger bonding.

### Worked example 1: across Period 3

**Data.** Approximate melting points: sodium 98 °C, magnesium 650 °C, aluminium 660 °C.

**Question.** Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium. (3 marks)

**Model answer.** "Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ ions, which have a greater charge than Na⁺ ions ✔. Each magnesium atom releases two electrons into the delocalised sea, compared with one for sodium ✔. Mg²⁺ is also smaller than Na⁺. So the attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is stronger in magnesium, and more energy is needed to overcome it ✔."

**Why it scores.** It names the charge, the number of delocalised electrons and the resulting attraction, and links stronger attraction to more energy. Aluminium continues the trend (3+ ions, three delocalised electrons per atom), although its melting point is only slightly higher than magnesium's; melting point depends on the structure as well as the strength of bonding, so do not overclaim from small differences.

### Worked example 2: down Group 1

**Data.** Approximate melting points: lithium 181 °C, sodium 98 °C, potassium 63 °C.

**Explanation.** Each of these metals forms 1+ ions and releases one delocalised electron per atom, so the charge is the same. Down the group the ions get larger, because each has an extra shell of electrons. The delocalised electrons are therefore further from the centre of positive charge in each ion, the attraction is weaker, and less energy is needed to melt the metal.

## A brief look ahead

Because the delocalised electrons can move through the lattice, metals conduct electricity in the solid state. Because the bonding is non-directional, layers of ions can slide past one another without the bonding breaking, which makes metals malleable. These explanations belong to subtopic 3.1.3.4, but they only work if the model here is described accurately.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the lattice as "metal atoms" rather than positive ions.
- Calling metallic bonding "attraction between positive and negative ions"; the negative particles are electrons, not ions.
- Describing it as "ionic bonding between ions and electrons", or as "covalent".
- Referring to "intermolecular forces" in a metal.
- Giving magnesium ions a 1+ charge, or drawing one electron per Mg²⁺ ion.
- Saying the delocalised electrons "are the bond"; the bond is the attraction between the electrons and the ions.
- Explaining melting points by "stronger atoms" or "heavier atoms" rather than stronger attraction.
- Saying the electrons are "lost" from the metal; they remain within the structure.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Describe** the bonding: name the particles (positive ions, delocalised electrons), their arrangement (lattice) and the attraction between them.
- **Explain** a difference in melting point: compare ion charge, number of delocalised electrons and ion size, then link to the strength of attraction and the energy needed.
- **Draw** or **Sketch**: label the ions with their charge and show delocalised electrons between them.
- **Suggest**: for an unfamiliar metal, use its group to predict the ion charge and the number of delocalised electrons.
- **Compare**: say what is the same and what is different, for example "both form 2+ ions, but calcium ions are larger".
