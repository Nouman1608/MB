This guide covers **Readers, Writers and Texts**, one of three areas
of exploration for IB Diploma Programme Language A: Literature,
first assessment 2021. The [full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-syllabus-guide/) confirms
all three areas carry equal, recommended (not prescriptive) teaching
hours (50 SL / 80 HL each).
Because this course studies literary works exclusively, this component
goes deeper into literary technique specifically than the equivalent
component in Language A: Language and Literature, which must split its
attention across literary and non-literary material.

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Like the course's other two areas of exploration, Readers, Writers and
Texts is not tested as a separate, named exam section — its ideas are
woven into Paper 1's guided literary analysis, Paper 2's comparative
essay, and the individual oral alike. Revision is therefore most
effective when it builds transferable fluency in explaining
technique-to-effect relationships, rather than treating this component
as isolated content to be recalled on its own.

## Syllabus coverage

**IB DP LANGUAGE A: LITERATURE — READERS, WRITERS AND TEXTS**

The component's central idea is that reading is active: two readers
can construct genuinely different, both defensible, interpretations of
the same passage based on the meaning they build from a writer's
choices. Focus areas span **narrative voice**, **poetic form**,
**dramatic structure**, and how a writer's specific craft choices shape
what a reader constructs from the text. The course's works are
selected across the literary forms defined on the IB's prescribed
reading list, which names four: fiction, non-fiction, poetry and drama.
This component's key terms must therefore be revised separately for
each form rather than assumed to transfer directly: narrative voice and structure apply differently to a novel
than to a poem's speaker, and dramatic structure (staging, dialogue,
dramatic irony) introduces performance-specific considerations that
neither prose nor poetry share.

## How to approach it

A strong answer does not just identify a technique (for example, "the
writer uses a first-person narrator") — it explains what interpretive
possibilities that specific choice opens up or forecloses for the
reader. When practising with unseen extracts for Paper 1, resist the
temptation to simply list techniques spotted in the passage; instead,
select two or three genuinely significant ones and trace their effect
across the whole extract, since guided analysis rewards depth of
connected argument over breadth of feature-spotting. For each studied
work, prepare a short list of the writer's most distinctive techniques
(a particular narrative structure, a recurring type of imagery, a
characteristic use of dialogue) along with a clear account of how each
shapes reader interpretation — this preparation serves both Paper 2's
comparative essay, where technique comparison across two works is
directly rewarded, and the individual oral, where close attention to
how craft choices shape meaning is assessed throughout.

## Worked example: narrative voice and reader construction

A novel narrated in the first person by a character whose reliability
the text gradually undermines.

```
Technique:         first-person narration

Initial effect:    reader trusts the narrator's account by default,
                    since first-person narration typically implies
                    direct access to the "truth" of events

Later shift:       as inconsistencies emerge, the reader is forced to
                    reconstruct events from a position of doubt rather
                    than trust

Analytical point:  the writer's choice of an increasingly unreliable
                    first-person narrator actively shapes how a reader
                    constructs meaning over the course of the novel,
                    not just what information the reader receives
```

Explaining the effect of a technique on the reader's construction of
meaning, not just naming the technique, is what separates strong from
adequate answers in this component.

## Adjusting technique vocabulary across forms

Because narrative-voice vocabulary developed for a novel does not
transfer cleanly to a poem or a play, build genre-specific checklists
rather than one generic list of "literary techniques." For prose,
prioritise narrative voice, point of view, and structural pacing
across chapters. For poetry, prioritise the speaker's voice (distinct
from the poet), form and structure (stanza length, rhyme scheme,
enjambment) and how these formal choices interact with the poem's
content. For drama, prioritise staging directions, dialogue,
soundscape, and dramatic irony -- considerations that depend on a work
being performed rather than only read silently. A student who revises
only prose-style vocabulary will often struggle to say anything
specific about a poem or play beyond generic comments on imagery or
theme, precisely because the vocabulary needed is genuinely different
across the literary forms the course draws on (the prescribed reading
list defines four: fiction, non-fiction, poetry and drama). Prose
vocabulary serves both fiction and non-fiction, but with non-fiction
also consider how the writer presents real people and events.

## Common mistakes

Naming a literary technique without explaining its effect on the
reader's construction of meaning. Applying prose-analysis habits
unchanged to poetry or drama, without adjusting for each form's
distinct conventions. Treating "readers construct meaning" as a
throwaway opening line rather than the governing idea that should
shape the whole analysis. Listing many techniques spotted in an unseen
extract rather than tracing two or three significant ones in depth
across the whole passage.

## Quick revision checklist

- For each studied work, identify at least one specific technique and
  explain its effect on how a reader constructs meaning, not just what
  it is called.
- Keep separate technique vocabulary for prose (fiction and
  non-fiction), poetry and drama rather than one undifferentiated list.
- Practise explaining how meaning might shift or develop for a reader
  across a text, not just at a single fixed point.
- Practise Paper 1 guided analysis by selecting a small number of
  significant techniques and tracing their effect in depth.
- Prepare technique-and-effect notes for each studied work ready for
  use in Paper 2 and the individual oral.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Readers, writers and texts a separate exam section in IB Literature?**
No. In IB DP Language A: Literature its ideas are woven into Paper 1's guided literary analysis, Paper 2's comparative essay and the individual oral rather than tested as a named section.

**How many teaching hours does Readers, writers and texts have?**
It has 50 recommended teaching hours at SL and 80 at HL, the same as each of the other two areas of exploration.

**How many techniques should you discuss in a Paper 1 analysis?**
Select two or three significant techniques and trace their effect across the whole extract, rather than listing every feature you spot. Guided analysis rewards depth of connected argument.

**What vocabulary should you prioritise for poetry and drama?**
For poetry, prioritise the speaker's voice, form and structure such as stanza length, rhyme scheme and enjambment. For drama, prioritise stage directions, dialogue, soundscape and dramatic irony.
