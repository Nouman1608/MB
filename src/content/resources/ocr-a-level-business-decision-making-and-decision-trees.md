## Nature and purpose of decision making

A decision is a choice between alternative courses of action. Decision making is the process of identifying a problem or opportunity, gathering information, comparing the options and selecting one. Its purpose is to move the business towards its aims and objectives using limited resources, and to do so with less guesswork than an instinctive choice would involve.

### Impact and importance of effective decisions

Effective decisions use resources well, protect cash flow and keep the business pointing at its objectives. The effects reach different stakeholders in different ways.

- Owners and shareholders: a good decision can raise profit and the value of their stake. A bad one can erase both.
- Employees: expansion creates promotion and job security. A poor cost decision can mean redundancies.
- Customers: decisions on price, quality and range decide whether they keep buying.
- Suppliers and lenders: they rely on the business staying solvent and paying on time.
- Local community: a decision to relocate or close affects jobs and local spending.

To evaluate, weigh the size of the impact against its timing. A decision that is slow to take effect may matter most to long-term stakeholders, such as shareholders, while hurting short-term ones, such as staff facing change.

## Aims, objectives and decisions

Aims are the broad long-term purposes of the business. Objectives are the measurable targets set to achieve them. Decisions are the choices that turn objectives into action, so the relationship runs in both directions. Objectives set the criteria for judging options: a business with a growth objective will rate an option on how much it adds to market share, while one targeting survival will favour the option that protects cash. The outcome of a decision then shows whether the objective is being met.

A mismatch is a warning sign. A business that aims at premium quality but takes a decision purely to cut cost is working against its own objectives.

## Types of decision

### By time horizon

Short-term, medium-term and long-term decisions differ in how far ahead their effects run. The specification does not fix exact numbers of months or years, and businesses define them differently, so state the horizon you are using. As the writer's own rough guide, not specification wording, a short-term decision might concern the next few weeks or months, a medium-term decision the next one to three years, and a long-term decision several years or more. Long-term decisions usually commit more resources and are harder to reverse.

### By level and scope

| Type | Level and scope | Example (a bakery chain) |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | Senior managers or directors; the whole business and its direction; high cost and hard to reverse | Entering a new region |
| Tactical | Middle managers; how to deliver the strategy in one function or period | Planning a seasonal promotion in the new region |
| Operational | Supervisors and teams; day-to-day running | Setting tomorrow's baking schedule |

Strategic decisions set the frame, tactical decisions fit inside it, and operational decisions carry it out daily. Time horizon and level often line up (strategic with long-term, operational with short-term) but they are different ideas. A cash crisis next week may be a short-term decision that is nevertheless strategic for survival.

## Factors to take into account

- Level of risk: how much the business could lose against how much it could gain. A business with thin reserves can accept less risk than one with large reserves.
- Nature of risk: risks differ in kind. Some can be given probabilities (a machine's failure rate); others cannot be quantified (a rival's unexpected move). A tool built on probabilities handles the first kind better.
- Accuracy of forecasts: any decision using forecast sales, costs or probabilities is only as sound as the forecast. Inaccurate inputs give a precise-looking but wrong answer.
- Volatility: in fast-changing markets, today's assumptions can expire before the decision pays off, so flexible options gain value.
- Potential for bias: managers may favour a pet project, ignore evidence against it or over-trust past success. Independent challenge and clear criteria reduce bias.

## Decision-making tools

Quantitative tools use numbers: forecasts, costings and expected values. They give objective comparisons but can ignore what cannot be measured. Qualitative tools or information use judgement and non-numerical evidence: staff opinion, brand image, expert views, ethics. They capture context but are subjective. Strong decisions combine both.

### Measures of performance

Financial measures (profit, margins, cash flow, return on capital) show whether the business is meeting financial objectives. Non-financial measures (market share, customer satisfaction, quality, staff turnover, environmental impact) show things the accounts miss. Evaluate usefulness by asking: does the measure match the objective, is it reliable, is it timely and does it have a benchmark? Financial figures look backwards. Non-financial figures can warn earlier. Neither is enough alone.

### Which course of action, and how successful?

To evaluate which course of action to take, set criteria from the objectives, compare the options on quantitative and qualitative evidence, weigh risk and then recommend with a stated condition ("provided cash reserves cover the first year"). To evaluate the extent of success, compare results with objectives, over time, against competitors and from several stakeholders' views. A business can hit its profit target and still fail on staff turnover, so judge "to what extent", not "yes or no".

## Decision trees: nature and purpose

A decision tree diagrams a decision, the choices open, the possible outcomes of each and their probabilities, so the business can compare options on expected value. Its purpose is to make a choice under risk more systematic and to expose the assumptions behind it.

Drawing conventions (standard practice, not specification wording):

- Decision node: drawn as a square. The business chooses a branch.
- Chance node: drawn as a circle. Chance decides which branch happens.
- Probabilities sit on chance branches and must total 1.0 (100%) at each chance node.
- Costs are shown on the branch where they are incurred. Payoffs sit at the ends of branches.

### Probabilities

Probabilities can be written as decimals or percentages: 0.55 is 55%. If two of three branches are 55% and 30%, the third is 100 - 55 - 30 = 15% (0.15). A probability of 0.15 means the outcome is expected in about 15 of 100 comparable cases. It is a judgement, not a promise.

### Economic outcomes and expected values

Where the specification does not set a formula, this is the standard form. The economic outcome of a branch is its payoff minus the costs incurred to reach it. The expected value (EV) of a chance node is the sum of probability multiplied by payoff for each branch. The net gain of an option is its EV minus its cost.

```
EV = sum of (probability x payoff)
net gain = EV - cost of the option
```

Work from right to left, calculating EVs at chance nodes and choosing the best at decision nodes.

## Worked example 1: a single decision

Pelmoor Dairy is choosing between a new bottling line (cost £90,000) and outsourcing bottling (cost £25,000). Do nothing costs and earns nothing.

Bottling line: 55% chance of a £210,000 payoff, 30% chance of £110,000, and the remaining chance of £30,000.

Outsource: 70% chance of £80,000, 30% chance of £35,000.

1. Missing probability for the line: 100 - 55 - 30 = 15%.
2. Line EV: 0.55 x 210,000 + 0.30 x 110,000 + 0.15 x 30,000 = 115,500 + 33,000 + 4,500 = £153,000.
3. Line net gain: 153,000 - 90,000 = £63,000.
4. Outsource EV: 0.70 x 80,000 + 0.30 x 35,000 = 56,000 + 10,500 = £66,500. Net gain: 66,500 - 25,000 = £41,500.
5. Do nothing: £0.

The line has the higher net gain by £21,500. Interpret: it is the better bet on average, but the worst case is 30,000 - 90,000 = a £60,000 loss, while outsourcing's worst case is 35,000 - 25,000 = a £10,000 gain. A cautious owner may still outsource.

## Worked example 2: a two-stage tree

Brackenhall Cycles can launch an e-bike range (cost £60,000). It estimates a 40% chance of high demand and a 60% chance of low demand. Low demand brings £50,000. After high demand it can either stay as it is (£140,000) or expand for a further £30,000, which brings £300,000 with probability 0.7 or £120,000 with probability 0.3.

```
Launch (60,000) -- chance: High 0.4 -- decision: Expand (30,000) -- chance: 0.7 -> 300,000 / 0.3 -> 120,000
                                                 Stay -> 140,000
                   Low 0.6 -> 50,000
Do not launch -> 0
```

1. Right-hand chance node (expand): 0.7 x 300,000 + 0.3 x 120,000 = £246,000. Net of the £30,000: £216,000.
2. Decision node: expanding (£216,000) beats staying (£140,000), so expand.
3. Launch chance node: 0.4 x 216,000 + 0.6 x 50,000 = 86,400 + 30,000 = £116,400.
4. Net gain of launching: 116,400 - 60,000 = £56,400, against £0 for not launching.

The tree recommends launching, then expanding if demand is high.

## Evaluating a tree

Evaluate the outcomes: compare EVs, but also the range between best and worst case. Evaluate the response: should the business act on the numbers alone? It should test how the answer changes if a probability falls, check it can survive the worst case and add qualitative factors.

Assess the importance of the information: a tree is most valuable for costly, hard-to-reverse choices under risk, and it shows the stakeholders most exposed to the downside. Assess usefulness:

- Strengths: forces clear thinking, makes assumptions visible, allows comparison, handles risk and sequences of decisions.
- Limits: probabilities and payoffs are estimates, often subjective; it ignores qualitative factors; EV is an average that may never actually occur; it takes no account of the timing of cash flows; and it can give false confidence.

A tree is a decision aid, not the decision. Use it with the tools above.

## Common errors

- Forgetting to subtract the cost of the option from the EV.
- Probabilities that do not total 1.0 at a chance node.
- Rolling forward instead of working right to left in a two-stage tree.
- Treating a short-term decision as automatically operational.
- Concluding "it depends" without saying on what.
