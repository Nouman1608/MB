This guide goes deeper into section **3.10.4 Problems with strategy and why strategies fail** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.10 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **difficulties of strategic decision making and implementing strategy**
- **planned versus emergent strategy**
- **reasons for strategic drift**
- **evaluating strategic performance**
- **the value of strategic planning**
- **the value of contingency planning and crisis management**.

The broader specification also asks you to consider the difficulties in forecasting future trends, and the importance of assessing feasibility and risk when making strategic decisions. A shorter set of notes on the whole topic is in the [managing strategic change revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-managing-strategic-change-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures on this page are invented.

## Why strategic decisions are difficult

Strategic decisions are hard because of their nature:

- **Uncertainty about the future:** forecasts of demand, technology, costs and competitors' actions are often wrong, and the longer the time horizon, the greater the error.
- **Incomplete or poor information:** managers rarely have all the facts, and data may be biased or out of date.
- **High stakes and irreversibility:** large resources are committed and may be hard to get back (sunk costs).
- **Complexity:** many factors interact, and different stakeholders want different things (shareholders, employees, communities).
- **Managers' biases:** over-optimism, attachment to past decisions, "groupthink", and pursuit of personal goals.
- **Time pressure** and competing priorities.
- **Ethical and social constraints:** decisions must consider CSR and the environment.
- **Feasibility and risk:** an attractive strategy may not be possible with the firm's resources or skills, or may carry too much risk.

## Why implementation fails

A sound strategy can still fail in execution. Typical reasons:

| Cause | Example |
|---|---|
| Poor communication | Staff do not understand what the strategy is or why it matters |
| Weak or inconsistent leadership | Managers say one thing and do another |
| Resistance to change | Staff and managers fear losing jobs or status (see Kotter and Schlesinger in section 3.10.1) |
| Culture mismatch | A rule-bound culture blocks a strategy that needs initiative |
| Inadequate resources | Not enough money, time or skills |
| Unsuitable structure | Responsibility unclear; slow decisions |
| Poor planning and timing | Dependencies missed; critical activities delayed |
| Lack of monitoring | Problems found too late |
| Changes in the environment | A recession, new law or competitor undermines the plan |
| Over-ambitious targets | Goals that cannot be met with the resources |

## Planned and emergent strategy

**Planned (deliberate) strategy** is the formal process: analyse the position, set objectives, choose a strategy, implement and control. It is rational, structured and gives clear direction.

**Emergent strategy** develops over time through a stream of decisions, experiments and responses to events, often with ideas from lower in the organisation. Henry Mintzberg argued that in practice most strategies are a mix of the two.

| | Planned | Emergent |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Senior managers; formal analysis | Experience, experiments, front-line staff |
| Strengths | Clear direction; co-ordination; resource allocation | Flexible; learns from events; uses staff knowledge |
| Weaknesses | Rigid; based on forecasts that may be wrong; slow to respond | Lack of direction; hard to control; inconsistent |
| Best when | Stable, predictable environment | Fast-changing, uncertain environment |

Good practice is to plan the main direction while keeping room for adaptation as events unfold.

## Strategic drift

**Strategic drift** is when a firm's strategy gradually moves away from the needs of its environment. Gerry Johnson explained that organisations make small, incremental changes that fit their current strategy and culture. As the environment changes more quickly, a gap opens between what the firm does and what the market needs, and performance declines. Eventually a crisis forces major change.

### Reasons for drift

- **Incremental change** that keeps the strategy aligned to its history rather than to the environment
- **Strong culture and "the way we do things"** that make managers blind to change
- **Success in the past,** leading to complacency
- **Poor environmental scanning:** not noticing signals of change
- **Slow or defensive reactions** to new competitors or technologies
- **Leaders who protect their own earlier decisions**
- **Resistance** to challenging the status quo
- **Rigid structures** that prevent new ideas reaching the top

### Worked example: Ferrant Printers

A printing firm gets 70% of its income from printed brochures. Demand for brochures falls by 10% a year as customers go online. The firm makes small improvements (faster presses, cost cuts) but does not enter digital services.

- Revenue from brochures: £10m falling 10% a year gives £9.0m, £8.1m, £7.29m, £6.56m over four years
- After four years, brochures have lost 34% of their starting revenue

The firm is working harder at an activity customers want less of. Careful attention to the signals (falling orders, customers asking for digital services) would have prompted a new direction earlier. To avoid drift, managers should regularly scan the environment, challenge assumptions, listen to customers and encourage new ideas.

## Evaluating strategic performance

To judge whether a strategy is working, managers compare results with objectives and standards:

- **Financial measures:** revenue growth, profit, return on capital employed, cash flow
- **Non-financial measures:** market share, customer satisfaction, innovation, staff turnover, environmental performance
- **Against targets,** against **past results,** and against **competitors**
- **Variance analysis:** actual minus target.

### Worked example: Pellerin Motors

| Measure | Target | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market share | 14% | 11% | −3 percentage points |
| ROCE | 12% | 9.5% | −2.5 percentage points |
| Revenue growth | 8% | 3% | −5 percentage points |
| Customer satisfaction (index) | 85 | 88 | +3 |

Revenue growth is 62.5% below the target (5 ÷ 8). Market share is more than a fifth short. Customers are happy, so the product is good; the problem seems to be reaching enough of them. **Judgement.** The strategy is only partly working. Managers should find out whether the shortfall comes from weak marketing, distribution gaps, a strong competitor, or unrealistic targets, before deciding to continue, adjust or change the strategy.

**Limits of evaluation:** results show the past, not the future; short-term figures may hide long-term damage; external factors (not the strategy) may explain results; targets may be wrong; numbers do not show causes.

## The value of strategic planning

**Benefits:**

- gives direction and a shared purpose
- co-ordinates departments and uses resources efficiently
- forces managers to analyse the environment and consider risks
- provides standards for control and measuring performance
- helps with raising finance and persuading stakeholders

**Limits:**

- forecasts may be wrong and plans rigid
- takes time and cost; may delay decisions
- can discourage initiative and creativity
- planners may be distant from the front line
- a plan can create false confidence

Planning is most valuable in stable settings and for large commitments; in fast-changing markets plans must be reviewed regularly.

## Contingency planning and crisis management

**Contingency planning** prepares responses to events that could happen but are not expected ("what if?"). **Crisis management** is the way a business responds when a serious threat or incident occurs, to protect people, reputation and operations.

### Contingency planning

1. Identify possible threats (supplier failure, fire, cyber attack, loss of a key customer, new competitor).
2. Estimate the **likelihood** and **impact** of each.
3. Prioritise the largest risks.
4. Prepare plans: alternative suppliers, back-up systems, insurance, spare cash, trained response teams.
5. Test and update the plans.

#### Worked example: ranking risks

Expected loss = probability × impact.

| Risk | Probability | Impact (£m) | Expected loss (£m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loss of key customer | 15% | 8 | 1.20 |
| Supplier collapse | 10% | 6 | 0.60 |
| Cyber attack | 20% | 3 | 0.60 |
| New low-cost entrant | 30% | 2 | 0.60 |

Loss of the key customer has the highest expected loss, so it deserves the most attention, for example by widening the customer base. The other three are equal on expected loss, so the firm might rank them by their worst case (supplier collapse has the highest impact).

*Limits:* probabilities are guesses; rare events with huge impact can be underestimated; plans can become out of date.

### Crisis management

Good practice is to have a crisis team with clear roles, a communications plan (honest, quick statements to staff, customers, media and regulators), and decisions focused on safety first. Poor handling, such as denying a problem, damages trust far more than the event itself.

**Value of contingency planning and crisis management:** faster, calmer response; less damage and downtime; protects reputation; reassures stakeholders. **Limits:** cost and management time; cannot cover every event; false security; staff may not follow plans under pressure.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** pick a cause of failure (for example poor communication) and trace the chain: how it affects behaviour, how this affects results.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the causes for the case, say which is most important and why, and consider what could have prevented it.
- Use correct terms: planned and emergent strategy, strategic drift, contingency plan.

## Common mistakes

- Treating planned and emergent strategy as opposites that cannot be combined.
- Confusing strategic drift with a sudden shock.
- Giving a list of causes without choosing between them.
- Saying planning is useless because forecasts are uncertain.
- Forgetting that a good result can be due to luck, and a poor result to the environment.
