## Section A

**1.** State which method of thermal energy transfer can take place through a vacuum, and explain why. **[2]**

**2.** State the effect of surface colour and texture on the emission of infrared radiation. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** ways in which the rate of emission of infrared radiation from an object can be increased. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a liquid is a poorer conductor than a metal. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain how thermal energy is conducted along a copper rod, referring to **both** mechanisms of conduction. **[4]**

**6.** Describe an experiment to compare how well four rods of different materials conduct thermal energy. State **two** factors that must be kept the same. **[4]**

**7.** Describe an experiment to show convection currents in water, and state what you would observe. **[3]**

**8.** Describe an experiment to show which of two surfaces, dull black and shiny silver, is the better absorber of infrared radiation. **[4]**

## Section C

**9.** Two identical cans, one dull black and one shiny silver, are each filled with 250 g of water at 85 °C and left with lids on in the same room. After 15 minutes the black can is at 61 °C and the silver can is at 73 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the temperature drop of each can. **[2]**
**(b)** State what the results show about the two surfaces. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why lids were used. **[1]**

**10.** An electric heater is placed near the floor on one side of a cold room. Explain how the whole room is heated. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how an infrared thermometer can measure the temperature of a person's forehead without touching it. **[3]**

**12.** A vacuum flask keeps a drink hot. Explain how each of the following reduces thermal energy transfer: **(a)** the vacuum between the walls, **(b)** the silvered surfaces, **(c)** the stopper. **[4]**

**13.** The engine of a motorcycle has metal cooling fins painted dull black. Explain why this design helps the engine lose thermal energy. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **Radiation** [1]: infrared radiation is an electromagnetic wave and **does not need a medium**, whereas conduction and convection need particles [1].

**2.** **Dull black** surfaces are the **best emitters** [1]; **shiny white or silver** surfaces are the **poorest emitters** [1].

**3.** Increase the **surface temperature** [1]; increase the **surface area** [1].

**4.** A liquid has **no free electrons** [1], and its particles are less tightly bonded than in a solid, so energy passes slowly from particle to particle; a metal has **free electrons that carry energy rapidly** [1].

**5.** At the hot end, the copper particles **vibrate more**, and because they are bonded to their neighbours they pass this energy along the lattice [1]; this is slow [1]. Copper also has **free (delocalised) electrons** [1] that gain kinetic energy at the hot end and **move quickly** to cooler parts, transferring energy by collisions; this is the main reason copper conducts well [1].

**6.** Fix a drawing pin with wax at the far end of each rod [1]; heat the near ends **equally** (for example together in the same flame) [1]; the rod whose pin falls first is the best conductor, and the order of falling gives the ranking [1]. Any two controls for the final mark: same length, same diameter, same amount of wax, same heating, same starting temperature [1].

**7.** Place a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) (or a drop of dye) at the bottom of a beaker of cold water near one side, and **heat gently directly under it** [1]. Observation: a coloured stream **rises** above the heat [1], moves across the top, **sinks** on the far side and returns along the bottom, showing a circulating convection current [1].

**8.** Place two identical metal plates, one dull black and one shiny silver, at **equal distances** on either side of a radiant heater [1]. Fix a drawing pin to the back of each with the **same amount of wax** [1]. Switch on the heater and record which pin falls first [1]. The pin on the **dull black** plate falls first, showing it is the better absorber [1].

**9. (a)** Black: 85 − 61 = **24 °C** [1]; silver: 85 − 73 = **12 °C** [1].
**(b)** The dull black surface is the **better emitter** of infrared radiation (it lost twice the temperature) [1].
**(c)** To reduce energy loss by **evaporation and convection** from the water's surface, so the comparison depends on radiation from the cans' surfaces [1].

**10.** Air next to the heater is warmed, **expands** and becomes **less dense** [1], so it **rises** [1]. Cooler, **denser** air from the rest of the room **sinks** and flows towards the heater to replace it [1], setting up a **convection current** that circulates warm air around the whole room [1].

**11.** All objects **emit infrared radiation** [1]; the **higher the surface temperature**, the greater the rate of emission [1]. The thermometer **detects** the infrared radiation from the forehead and converts the amount into a temperature reading [1].

**12. (a)** A vacuum contains no particles, so it **stops conduction and convection** [1] between the walls [1].
**(b)** Silvered surfaces **reflect infrared radiation** back towards the drink, reducing transfer by radiation [1].
**(c)** The stopper is an insulator that reduces **conduction** and prevents **convection and evaporation** from the top [1].

**13.** Fins give a much **larger surface area**, which increases the rate of emission of infrared radiation (and the area in contact with air for convection) [1]. Dull black is the **best emitter** of infrared radiation [1]. Metal is a **good conductor**, so energy passes quickly from the engine into the fins [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "heat rises" instead of explaining density changes in the fluid.
- Describing conduction in a metal without free electrons.
- Claiming a vacuum stops radiation.
- Leaving out equal distances or identical sizes in emitter and absorber experiments.
- Saying "black absorbs heat" without naming infrared radiation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these questions from past papers?**
No. They are original practice questions for subtopic 2.3 and do not copy the exam's structure, question count or mark tariffs.

**Is the data in Question 9 real?**
It is illustrative data written for this question, in a realistic range for this experiment.

**Do I need to name potassium manganate(VII)?**
Any soluble coloured crystal or dye that shows the movement of the water is acceptable; the important part is heating beneath it and describing the circulation.
