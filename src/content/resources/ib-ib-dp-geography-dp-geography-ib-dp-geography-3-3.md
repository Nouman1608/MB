This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 3.3 of Option C, Extreme environments, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): managing extreme environments. The [3.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-managing-extreme-environments-revision-notes/) already cover traditional strategies such as nomadic pastoralism, the pressures and opportunities, desertification, the Trans-Alaska Pipeline, the Antarctic Treaty and the Great Green Wall. This page focuses on **economic activity** in extreme environments and how it is managed: mining and its water demands in the Atacama, the limits of desert farming, tourism on Everest and in Antarctica, and the cost of supplying remote communities. Practise with the [3.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-3-3-practice/); the [Extreme environments study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-study-guide/) gives the overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the opportunities extreme environments offer for economic activity and the challenges of using them.
- Evaluate the role of technology and of indigenous and local knowledge.
- Evaluate strategies for managing human activity in extreme environments, and who gains and loses.

## Mining in the Atacama: minerals versus water

The Atacama Desert in northern Chile is one of the driest places on Earth, yet it is a major mining region, producing copper and lithium.

- **Copper** mines need large volumes of water to process ore. In a desert, that water competes with towns, farming and wetlands. Some large mines now pump desalinated seawater uphill from the coast, which reduces pressure on local groundwater but uses large amounts of energy.
- **Lithium** is extracted from brine beneath salt flats such as the Salar de Atacama. Brine is pumped into large evaporation ponds where the sun concentrates it. Indigenous communities living around the salt flats have raised concerns that pumping brine and fresh water lowers water tables and harms lagoons used by flamingos and by local herders.

The case shows a typical management dilemma. Demand for lithium has grown with batteries for electric vehicles and energy storage, so a resource linked to low-carbon technology creates local environmental and social pressures. Management responses include water monitoring, agreements with local communities, and technologies that extract lithium with less water. Evaluate them by asking whether monitoring is independent, whether communities share in the benefits, and whether water use is measured against recharge.

## Desert farming: the limits of technology

Technology can make deserts productive, but at a price.

**Saudi Arabia's wheat.** From the 1980s, Saudi Arabia used generous subsidies and centre-pivot irrigation to grow wheat in the desert, drawing on deep, largely non-renewable groundwater. For a time it was not only self-sufficient but an exporter. Concern over falling aquifers led the government to announce in 2008 that it would phase out domestic wheat production, completed in 2016, and to import wheat instead.

The lesson: technology overcame aridity for a few decades, but only by mining fossil water. Importing food is a way of importing **virtual water** from wetter countries.

**Lower-impact approaches** include drip irrigation, which delivers water directly to roots, drought-tolerant crops, rainwater harvesting and the traditional underground channels (known as qanats, karez or falaj in different regions) that carry groundwater by gravity without pumping.

## Tourism in extreme environments

Tourism brings income to remote places but concentrates pressure on small, fragile areas.

**Everest and the Khumbu region, Nepal.** Mount Everest lies within Sagarmatha National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979. Expedition climbing and trekking support Sherpa communities through jobs as guides, porters and lodge owners. The pressures include waste and human waste left on the mountain, crowding on the summit routes and the deaths of climbers and workers. Since 2014 Nepal has required each expedition member to bring back at least 8 kg of waste, backed by a deposit refunded on return. Evaluation: the rule creates an incentive, but enforcement high on the mountain is hard, and the benefits of tourism are unevenly shared between foreign operators and local workers who take the greatest risks.

**Antarctica.** Tourism is managed largely through self-regulation by the **International Association of Antarctica Tour Operators** (IAATO), founded in 1991, working within the Antarctic Treaty system. Key rules: ships carrying more than 500 passengers may not land visitors, and no more than 100 visitors may be ashore at one site at a time. Visitors must also clean boots and clothing to avoid bringing in seeds or microbes. Evaluation: the rules have kept landings small and controlled, but self-regulation depends on operators choosing to join, and rising visitor numbers increase fuel use, the risk of spills and the chance of introducing non-native species.

## The cost of remoteness

Living in or supplying a remote extreme environment is expensive because every item must be transported.

**Worked example (invented figures).** A remote Arctic community needs 400 tonnes of fuel and food a year. Three options:

| Route | Cost per tonne | Available | Annual cost for 400 tonnes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summer sealift | 900 | About 3 months a year | 400 x 900 = 360,000 |
| Winter ice road | 600 | About 6 to 8 weeks a year | 400 x 600 = 240,000 |
| Air freight | 6,000 | All year, weather permitting | 400 x 6,000 = 2,400,000 |

Air freight costs ten times as much as sealift. If a short winter means the ice road can carry only half the load, the other 200 tonnes must go by sea or air. By sea the extra cost is 200 x (900 - 600) = 60,000; by air it is 200 x (6,000 - 600) = 1,080,000. This explains high living costs in remote communities, why governments subsidise freight, and why local energy sources such as wind and solar, and local food from hunting or greenhouses, are attractive.

## Self-government and indigenous management

Management is not only about technology. Where indigenous peoples have political power, they can shape how resources are used. **Nunavut**, a territory of northern Canada with an Inuit majority, was created in 1999 under a land claims agreement that gave Inuit rights to land and a share in decisions about resource development. Similar arrangements elsewhere give communities a voice in mining, energy and conservation. Evaluate them by asking whether decision-making power is real, whether revenue is shared, and whether traditional knowledge is used alongside science.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the role of technology in enabling economic activity in extreme environments.* **(sample conclusion)**

"Technology has opened extreme environments to activities that were once impossible: desalinated seawater supplies mines in the Atacama, and raised supports carry the Trans-Alaska Pipeline over permafrost **[strength with located examples]**. However, technology often overcomes limits only temporarily or at high cost; Saudi Arabia's irrigated wheat depended on fossil groundwater and was abandoned once aquifers fell **[limitation with evidence]**. Technology also tends to benefit outside companies more than local communities unless governance gives them a share **[equity criterion]**. Technology is therefore necessary for large-scale activity, but sustainable use depends equally on regulation and on local and indigenous involvement **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Describing economic activity without saying how it is managed or who benefits.
- Treating technology as a permanent fix for aridity or cold.
- Ignoring the water demands of mining in deserts.
- Describing tourism impacts without any management response.
- Forgetting the cost of transport when explaining life in remote places.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** a management strategy with criteria: effectiveness, cost, enforcement, equity and long-term sustainability.
- **Examine** opportunities and challenges together, showing how one creates the other.
- **To what extent** answers should take a position and support it with more than one type of extreme environment.
- Name stakeholders and say what each wants.
