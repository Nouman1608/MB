## What the specification asks for

- Structural isomerism and stereoisomerism.
- E–Z isomerism is a form of stereoisomerism and occurs as a result of restricted rotation about the planar carbon–carbon double bond.
- Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (CIP) priority rules.
- Define the term structural isomer; draw the structures of chain, position and functional group isomers.
- Define the term stereoisomer; draw the structural formulas of E and Z isomers; apply the CIP priority rules to E and Z isomers.

Optical isomerism, the other kind of stereoisomerism, is International A2 content and is taught in the [optical isomerism study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-optical-isomerism/).

## Definitions to learn word for word

- **Structural isomers**: compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulas.
- **Stereoisomers**: compounds with the same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space.

The key difference: structural isomers have their atoms **joined in a different order**; stereoisomers have the same connections, but a different 3D arrangement.

## The three kinds of structural isomer

| Kind | What changes | Example (same molecular formula) |
|---|---|---|
| Chain | The carbon skeleton (branching) | pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane (C₅H₁₂) |
| Position | Where the functional group is on the same skeleton | butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol (C₄H₁₀O) |
| Functional group | The functional group itself | propanal and propanone (C₃H₆O); but-1-ene and cyclobutane (C₄H₈) |

Chain isomers usually have similar chemical properties but different boiling points (more branching, fewer surface contacts, weaker van der Waals forces, lower boiling point). Functional group isomers have different chemical properties, because they belong to different homologous series.

## Finding every structural isomer

Exams often ask for "all the isomers" of a formula. Random drawing misses some and repeats others. Work in this order:

1. **Decide the possible series** from the general formula. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ can only be an alkane; CₙH₂ₙ can be an alkene or a cycloalkane; CₙH₂ₙO can be an aldehyde or a ketone (or an unsaturated alcohol or ring compound).
2. **List the skeletons**: the unbranched chain first, then one branch, then two branches.
3. **Place the functional group** in every distinct position on each skeleton.
4. **Name each structure.** Two structures with the same name are the same compound, so delete the duplicate.

### Worked example 1: every isomer of C₄H₈

C₄H₈ fits CₙH₂ₙ, so it can be an alkene or a cycloalkane.

```
Alkenes, unbranched chain:   CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃        but-1-ene
                             CH₃CH=CHCH₃         but-2-ene
Alkene, branched chain:      CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₃       2-methylpropene
Rings:                       four-membered ring   cyclobutane
                             three-membered ring with CH₃   methylcyclopropane
```

That gives **five structural isomers**. Then check each alkene for E–Z isomerism: only but-2-ene has two different groups on each C=C carbon, so it exists as (E)- and (Z)-but-2-ene. Total: **six isomers**.

Classify the relationships: but-1-ene and but-2-ene are position isomers; but-1-ene and 2-methylpropene are chain isomers; but-1-ene and cyclobutane are functional group isomers.

### Worked example 2: structural isomers of C₄H₉Cl

Skeletons with four carbons: unbranched (C–C–C–C) and branched (C–C(C)–C).

```
Unbranched:  CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl     1-chlorobutane
             CH₃CH₂CHClCH₃      2-chlorobutane
Branched:    (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Cl      1-chloro-2-methylpropane
             (CH₃)₃CCl          2-chloro-2-methylpropane
```

**Four** structural isomers. On the unbranched chain, putting Cl on C3 or C4 gives the same compounds as C2 or C1 (count from the other end). On the branched skeleton, all three CH₃ carbons are equivalent.

## E–Z isomerism

### Why it happens

The C=C double bond is made of two bonds. The second bond (the π bond) is formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals above and below the plane of the molecule. Rotating one end of the double bond would break this overlap, so **rotation about C=C is restricted**. The two carbons of the double bond, and the four atoms attached to them, lie in one plane. Groups fixed on the same side cannot swap to the other side without breaking a bond.

### The condition

E–Z isomers exist only when **each** carbon of the C=C carries **two different** atoms or groups. If either carbon carries two identical groups (for example =CH₂ or =C(CH₃)₂), there are no E–Z isomers.

## The CIP priority rules

For each carbon of the double bond separately:

1. Look at the two atoms **directly attached** to it. The atom with the **higher atomic number** has higher priority.
2. If those atoms are the same, list the atoms attached to each of them, highest atomic number first, and compare the lists one atom at a time. The first difference decides.
3. If the two higher-priority groups are on the **same side** of the double bond, the isomer is **Z** (from German *zusammen*, together). On **opposite sides**, it is **E** (*entgegen*, opposite).

Useful atomic numbers: H 1, C 6, N 7, O 8, F 9, Cl 17, Br 35, I 53.

### Worked example 3: when the first atoms tie

Assign E or Z to this isomer of 3-bromo-2-methylpent-2-en-1-ol.

```
    HOCH₂         Br
          \      /
           C == C
          /      \
       H₃C        CH₂CH₃
```

- **Left carbon (C2)**: CH₂OH and CH₃ both attach through carbon, a tie. Next atoms: CH₂OH has (O, H, H); CH₃ has (H, H, H). O (8) beats H (1), so **CH₂OH** has priority.
- **Right carbon (C3)**: Br (35) against C of CH₂CH₃ (6), so **Br** has priority.
- CH₂OH and Br are both on the upper side: **Z**.

The rule compares atoms, not group size: a single O at the first point of difference outweighs any number of H atoms.

### Worked example 4: when "cis" is not Z

2-Bromobut-2-ene is CH₃CBr=CHCH₃. In one isomer, both methyl groups are on the same side.

```
     H₃C         CH₃
        \       /
         C == C
        /       \
      Br         H
```

- C2: Br (35) beats CH₃ (6); Br is on the lower side.
- C3: CH₃ beats H; CH₃ is on the upper side.
- Higher priorities on opposite sides: **E**.

The methyl groups are "cis" to each other, but the isomer is **E**. Cis–trans language only works when the same group is on both carbons; E–Z with CIP rules always works, which is why the specification uses it.

## Common mistakes

- Defining structural isomers as "same formula, different structure" without saying **molecular** and **structural** formula.
- Drawing the same compound twice, for example 1-chlorobutane written from both ends.
- Counting a chain isomer that is really the unbranched chain drawn bent (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl drawn with a corner is not 1-chloro-2-methylpropane).
- Assigning priority by group size or mass, or by the number of atoms, rather than atomic number at the first difference.
- Assuming that "cis" means Z.
- Claiming E–Z isomers for but-1-ene or 2-methylpropene, where one carbon carries two identical groups.
- Forgetting ring isomers when the formula is CₙH₂ₙ.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Define**: the exact wording, including "molecular formula" or "structural formula".
- **Draw**: E and Z isomers with the C=C drawn flat and bonds at about 120°, so it is clear which groups are on each side.
- **Explain** (E–Z): restricted rotation about C=C, plus two different groups on each carbon.
- **Deduce** (E or Z): state the priority on each carbon with atomic numbers, then the side, then the label.
- **Name the type of isomerism**: chain, position or functional group; or E–Z stereoisomerism.
