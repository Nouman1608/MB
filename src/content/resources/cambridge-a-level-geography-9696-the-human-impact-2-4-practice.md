## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between the greenhouse effect and the enhanced greenhouse effect. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** sources of methane from human activity. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how ice cores provide evidence of past climate. **[3]**

**4.** Define the term "urban heat island". **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the urban heat island is usually strongest on calm, clear nights. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how the melting of Arctic sea ice acts as a positive feedback on global warming. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows illustrative annual mean carbon dioxide concentrations, similar to those recorded at Mauna Loa.

| Year | 1960 | 1980 | 2000 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide (ppm) | 317 | 339 | 370 | 414 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase from 1960 to 2020. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the mean annual rate of increase for 1960 to 1980 and for 2000 to 2020. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons why the rate of increase has risen. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows fictional data for a city centre station and a rural station 15 km away.

| | City centre | Rural |
|---|---|---|
| Mean night-time temperature (°C) | 12.5 | 9.0 |
| Mean relative humidity (%) | 68 | 79 |
| Mean wind speed (m/s) | 2.8 | 4.5 |
| Thunderstorm days per year | 22 | 16 |

**(a)** Calculate how much lower the mean wind speed is in the city centre, as a percentage of the rural value. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the differences in relative humidity and thunderstorm days. **[4]**

**(c)** Suggest why gusts in the city centre can be stronger than in the countryside, even though the mean wind speed is lower. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why natural factors are unlikely to explain the global warming of recent decades. **[5]**

**10.** Explain the possible impacts of global warming on the atmosphere. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate the evidence that recent global warming is caused by human activity. **[8]**

**12.** With reference to an urban area you have studied, explain why the effects of human activity on temperature are easier to identify than its effects on precipitation. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The greenhouse effect is the natural warming of the Earth's surface as gases absorb outgoing longwave radiation and re-radiate it [1]. The enhanced greenhouse effect is the additional warming caused by human activity increasing the concentration of greenhouse gases [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Two from: livestock such as cattle [1]; rice paddies [1]. Accept landfill sites, or leaks from coal mining and oil and gas extraction, in place of either. **[2]**

**3.** Snow that falls on ice sheets is compressed into ice, trapping bubbles of air [1]. Analysing the air in the bubbles shows past concentrations of greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide [1]. The chemistry of the ice itself, such as its isotopes, indicates past temperatures, and the layers can be dated to build a long record [1]. **[3]**

**4.** An urban area that is warmer than the surrounding rural area [1], with the difference usually greatest at night [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Clear skies allow rural areas to lose heat rapidly by longwave radiation [1], while urban buildings and roads release heat stored during the day and narrow streets trap longwave radiation [1]. Calm conditions mean the warm urban air is not mixed with cooler rural air [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Warming melts sea ice, exposing darker ocean water [1]. The ocean has a much lower albedo than ice, so it absorbs more solar radiation [1]. This causes further warming and more melting, amplifying the original change [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** (414 - 317) / 317 x 100 [1] = 30.6% [1].

**(b)** 1960 to 1980: 22 / 20 = 1.1 ppm per year [1]; 2000 to 2020: 44 / 20 = 2.2 ppm per year [1].

**(c)** Greater burning of fossil fuels as populations grew and economies industrialised, for example in Asia [1]; continued deforestation, which releases stored carbon and reduces uptake by forests [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** (4.5 - 2.8) / 4.5 x 100 [1] = 37.8% lower [1].

**(b)** Relative humidity is 11 percentage points lower in the centre because rain drains rapidly from impermeable surfaces and there is little vegetation, so evapotranspiration is low [1], and because higher temperatures raise the air's capacity to hold vapour [1]. There are six more thunderstorm days in the centre because extra heat strengthens convection [1], and pollution particles act as condensation nuclei, helping clouds to form [1].

**(c)** Tall buildings channel air into narrow streets between them, accelerating it (the canyon effect) [1], and air deflected down the faces of tall buildings creates strong, gusty eddies at street level [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Solar output has not increased over recent decades, while global temperatures have risen [1]. Volcanic eruptions release aerosols that cool the climate, and only for a year or two [1], so they cannot produce sustained warming [1]. Changes in the Earth's orbit operate over tens of thousands of years, far too slowly to explain a century of warming [1]. The warming pattern, with the lower atmosphere warming while the upper atmosphere cools, matches increased greenhouse gases rather than a brighter sun [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Higher mean temperatures [1], with more frequent and intense heatwaves [1]. Warmer air holds more water vapour, so heavy rainfall events become more intense [1]. Precipitation patterns may change, with some dry regions becoming drier [1]. The strongest tropical cyclones may become more intense, with heavier rainfall [1]. Wind belts, storm tracks or jet streams may shift, although these changes are less certain [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Strengths: thermometer records show global warming of about 1.1°C since the late nineteenth century [1]. Direct measurements show carbon dioxide rising every year since 1958, matching the growth of fossil fuel use [1]. Ice cores show present concentrations are far higher than in previous interglacial periods [1]. Several independent sources, including glaciers, sea ice and sea level, all point the same way [1]. Natural factors such as solar output and orbital change cannot explain the timing or pace of warming [1].

Limitations: early temperature records are sparse in some regions and over oceans [1], and some station records are affected by urban growth, so they must be adjusted [1].

Judgement: the limitations add uncertainty to precise figures, but the combined evidence makes human activity the most convincing explanation, as the IPCC concluded [1]. **[8]**

**12.** Credit located detail from a named urban area.

Temperature: the heat island can be measured directly by comparing urban and rural stations or by transects [1]. The difference is often several degrees and appears night after night in calm, clear weather [1]. Its causes, such as heat storage in buildings and waste heat, are well understood and clearly urban [1]. Located evidence, such as a measured urban-rural difference or a named cooler park, supports the explanation [1].

Precipitation: any urban increase in rainfall is small compared with natural variation between storms and seasons [1]. Storms are carried by weather systems that cross the city, so effects may appear downwind and outside the urban area [1]. Rain gauges are sparse and measuring convective storms is difficult [1].

Conclusion: temperature changes are large, regular and directly measurable, whereas precipitation changes are small, irregular and hard to separate from regional climate, so they are less certain [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing global warming with the hole in the ozone layer.
- Working out a percentage increase using the final value as the base.
- Describing city and rural data separately without comparing them.
- Listing evidence without evaluating its strengths and limitations.
- Writing about an urban area without any located evidence.
