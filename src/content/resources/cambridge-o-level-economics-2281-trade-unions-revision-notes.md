Quick recall for section 3.4 **Trade unions** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: the definition, the role of unions ("collective bargaining on wages, working hours and working conditions; protecting employment; and influencing government policy"), and the advantages and disadvantages of union activity for "workers, firms and the government", with the factors influencing union strength. Full explanations are in the [trade unions study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-trade-unions/); test yourself with the [trade unions practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-trade-unions-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Trade union | An organisation of workers formed to protect and promote members' interests, especially pay, conditions and job security |
| Collective bargaining | Negotiation between a union, for all its members, and an employer over pay and conditions |
| Industrial action | Pressure on an employer by workers, such as an overtime ban, work-to-rule, go-slow or strike |
| Strike | Workers stop work altogether |
| Work-to-rule | Workers follow every rule exactly, slowing output |
| Overtime ban | Workers refuse to work beyond normal hours |
| Money wage | The wage in currency |
| Real wage | The wage adjusted for price changes; what it can buy |
| Productivity agreement | A pay rise in return for changes that raise output per worker |
| Lobbying | Trying to influence government decisions |

## The role in three parts

1. **Collective bargaining** on wages, working hours and working conditions.
2. **Protecting employment**: resisting redundancies, winning fair redundancy terms and retraining, representing members facing discipline, pressing for safety.
3. **Influencing government policy**: minimum wage, employment law, safety rules, pensions, training.

## Bargaining sequence

Claim → offer → negotiation (often with conditions on both sides) → settlement or industrial action → settlement.

## Real versus money gains

- A 5% pay rise with 4% inflation: 1.05 ÷ 1.04 ≈ 1.0096, a real rise of about 1%.
- A 3% pay rise with 4% inflation: 1.03 ÷ 1.04 ≈ 0.990, a real cut of about 1%.
- Always compare a pay rise with price rises before calling it a gain.

## Does a strike pay?

Worker earning $600 a week strikes for 3 weeks: loses $1,800. Settlement is 2 percentage points above the offer: $12 a week extra. Recovery takes 1,800 ÷ 12 = 150 weeks. Strikes are costly to members, so the threat is often used more than the action.

## Productivity agreements

A 5% pay rise matched by a 5% rise in output keeps labour cost per unit unchanged. Example: $50,000 for 10,000 units = $5; $52,500 for 10,500 units = $5.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Viewpoint | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Workers | Higher pay; better, safer conditions; support at work; a voice in change | Fees; lost pay in strikes; possible job losses if pay rises too far |
| Firms | One body to negotiate with; communication channel; motivated staff; productivity deals | Higher costs; lost output; resistance to change; less flexibility |
| Government | A partner for consultation; orderly bargaining; less inequality | Lost output and tax revenue; possible inflation; disrupted public services; unemployment |

## What makes a union strong

- High share of the workforce as members
- Skilled workers who are hard to replace
- Action that quickly costs the employer a lot
- An employer that is profitable or can pass costs to customers
- Large union funds
- Low unemployment or a shortage of the workers concerned
- Laws that permit industrial action; public support

A union is weak when members are easy to replace, the employer is struggling, or unemployment is high.

## Chains to learn

- **Higher pay without higher productivity**: wage costs rise → labour cost per unit rises → firm raises prices, accepts lower profit or employs fewer workers.
- **Strike in an essential service**: output stops → businesses and households that rely on the service lose out → government loses tax revenue and faces pressure to settle.
- **Safety campaign succeeds**: fewer injuries → less absence → workers and firm both gain.

## Exam traps

- Equating unions with strikes.
- Ignoring inflation when judging a pay rise.
- Giving only the workers' viewpoint.
- Saying pay rises always cost jobs.
- Listing strength factors with no reason.
- Writing "unions are bad for the economy" without a chain or judgement.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a trade union.
2. Name the three areas covered by collective bargaining in the syllabus.
3. What is a work-to-rule?
4. A union wins a 6% pay rise when prices rise by 2%. Is the real wage higher or lower?
5. A worker on $500 a week strikes for 2 weeks and wins $10 a week more than the original offer. How many weeks to recover the lost pay?
6. Give one advantage of a trade union to a firm.
7. Give one disadvantage of union activity to the government.
8. Why are unions of skilled workers usually stronger?
9. Why does high unemployment weaken unions?
10. What is a productivity agreement?
11. Give two ways a union can influence government policy.

### Answers

1. An organisation of workers formed to protect and promote members' interests, especially pay, working conditions and job security.
2. Wages, working hours and working conditions.
3. Workers follow every rule exactly, which slows work while they are still paid.
4. Higher: roughly 1.06 ÷ 1.02 ≈ 1.04, a real rise of about 4%.
5. Lost pay: 2 × 500 = $1,000; 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100 weeks.
6. One body to negotiate with; a channel for explaining change; motivated staff; productivity deals (any one).
7. Lost output and tax revenue from strikes; pay rises adding to inflation; disrupted public services; unemployment (any one).
8. They are hard to replace, so industrial action costs the employer more.
9. Employers can replace workers more easily, and workers fear losing their jobs, so threats carry less weight.
10. An agreement giving a pay rise in return for changes that raise output per worker.
11. Lobbying ministers or officials; campaigning publicly on issues such as the minimum wage or safety law; responding to government consultations.
