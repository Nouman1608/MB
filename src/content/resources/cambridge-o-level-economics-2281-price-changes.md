This guide covers section 2.6 **Price changes** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus names two parts: the causes of price changes, meaning "changing market conditions as causes of price changes", and the consequences, where "demand and supply diagrams [are] to be used to illustrate these changes in market conditions and their consequences for equilibrium price and sales".

The [price changes and market system guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-causes-and-effects-of-price-changes-and-the-market/) sets out the four single-shift cases and examples with schedules. This page adds a decision routine for awkward events, chains of effects that pass from one market to a related one, solving shifts with equations, and the wider consequences of a price change for workers, related markets and the government. ## 2.6.1 Causes: a four-question routine

Every "what happens to the price of X?" question can be answered with the same four questions.

1. **Which market?** Name the product whose price you are explaining. Many errors come from analysing the wrong market.
2. **Does the event change buyers' behaviour or sellers' behaviour?** Buyers: demand shifts. Sellers: supply shifts.
3. **Which direction?** More at every price is a shift right; less at every price is a shift left.
4. **What is the new equilibrium?** Use the shortage or surplus at the old price to explain the move.

Some events look like they belong to one side but belong to the other:

| Event | Market | Curve | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| A tax on cigarettes | Cigarettes | Supply left | The tax is a cost to sellers |
| A tax on cigarettes | Lighters (complement) | Demand left | Fewer smokers want lighters |
| Higher wages for bakers | Bread | Supply left | Wages are a cost of production |
| Higher wages for all workers | Restaurant meals | Demand right | Buyers' incomes rise |
| A health report praising fish | Fish | Demand right | A change in tastes |
| A storm damages the fishing fleet | Fish | Supply left | Fewer boats can fish |
| Mobile phones become cheaper | Phone cases (complement) | Demand right | More phones are bought, so more cases are wanted |
| Rail fares fall | Long-distance bus travel (substitute) | Demand left | Travellers switch to the cheaper train |

The same event can shift supply in one market and demand in another. Wages are the clearest example: a rise in bakers' wages is a cost to bakeries, but a general rise in wages is extra income for buyers.

## 2.6.2 Consequences: solving a shift

### Worked example 1: a rise in demand, with equations

The market for bus rides in invented City R, per day: Qd = 900 − 100P and Qs = 200P − 300.

1. Original equilibrium: 900 − 100P = 200P − 300, so 1,200 = 300P, P = $4 and Q = 500 rides.
2. Petrol prices rise and car users switch to buses. Demand rises by 150 rides at every price: Qd = 1,050 − 100P.
3. At the old price of $4, demand is 650 and supply is 500, a shortage of 150.
4. New equilibrium: 1,050 − 100P = 200P − 300, so 1,350 = 300P, P = $4.50 and Q = 600.
5. Price rises by 0.50 ÷ 4 × 100 = 12.5%; rides rise by 100 ÷ 500 × 100 = 20%.
6. Spending on bus rides rises from 4 × 500 = $2,000 to 4.50 × 600 = $2,700 a day.

Notice that sales rose by less than the 150-ride shift. Part of the extra demand was choked off as the price rose (a contraction along the new demand curve), and the rest was met by an extension of supply.

### Worked example 2: the knock-on effect in a related market

The same rise in petrol prices also affects small cars, a complement to petrol. Prices are in $ thousands: Qd = 600 − 20P and Qs = 30P − 150.

1. Original equilibrium: 600 − 20P = 30P − 150, so 750 = 50P, P = 15 ($15,000) and Q = 300 cars a month.
2. Running a car is now dearer, so demand falls by 100 at every price: Qd = 500 − 20P.
3. At $15,000 there is now a surplus: demand 200, supply 300.
4. New equilibrium: 500 − 20P = 30P − 150, so 650 = 50P, P = 13 ($13,000) and Q = 240.
5. Price falls by 13.3% and sales by 20%. Sellers' revenue falls from 15 × 300 = $4.5 million to 13 × 240 = $3.12 million.

One cause, two markets, opposite results: the substitute (buses) gains and the complement (cars) loses.

### Worked example 3: both curves shift

Rice in invented Country N: Qd = 800 − 40P and Qs = 60P − 200, giving P = $10 and Q = 400 tonnes.

| Case | Change | New equilibrium | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Demand +100 and supply +100 at every price | P = $10, Q = 500 | Price unchanged, sales up |
| B | Demand +100 and supply −50 at every price | P = $11.50, Q = 440 | Price up, sales up |

In case A the two shifts are equal, so the price pressure from each cancels out. In case B both shifts push the price up, so price must rise; the sales result depends on which shift is larger, and here the demand shift wins. Learn the rule: when demand and supply move in the **same** direction, sales are certain and price is uncertain; when they move in **opposite** directions, price is certain and sales are uncertain.

## Wider consequences of a price change

The syllabus asks for consequences for equilibrium price and sales; good answers then follow the effects outwards.

- **Consumers**: a higher price means some buyers stop buying, and those who continue have less income left for other goods.
- **Producers**: a higher price and higher sales raise revenue and profit, and the profit attracts new firms. A fall does the opposite.
- **Workers**: the demand for labour is derived from demand for the product. In example 2, lower car sales mean car showrooms and factories need fewer workers.
- **Related markets**: a price rise for one product changes demand for its substitutes (up) and complements (down).
- **Government**: if a tax is charged per unit sold, lower sales mean less tax revenue; higher sales mean more.
- **Resource allocation**: rising prices pull land, labour and capital towards a product; falling prices release them to other uses.

## Common mistakes

- Analysing the wrong market, for example shifting supply of petrol when the question asks about buses.
- Shifting a curve because of the product's own price change.
- Treating all wage rises as supply-side. Check whether the wages are costs to the sellers or incomes of the buyers.
- Stating a definite price change when both curves move in the same direction and the sizes are not given.
- Assuming sales change by the full size of the shift. The adjustment along the curve that did not move absorbs part of it.
- Leaving out the shortage or surplus at the old price, which is the reason the price moves.

## Exam technique for 2.6

- **Explain** the effect of an event on price: name the curve, give the direction and the reason, identify the shortage or surplus at the old price, and state the new price and sales.
- **Analyse** with a diagram: show the original curves (D1, S1), the new curve (D2 or S2), both equilibria (P1, Q1 and P2, Q2), and describe the movement in words alongside.
- **Calculate**: state each equilibrium, then any percentage change, always dividing by the original value.
- **Discuss** whether a price will rise: two conditions often change at once, so weigh which shift is likely to be larger and say what the result depends on.
