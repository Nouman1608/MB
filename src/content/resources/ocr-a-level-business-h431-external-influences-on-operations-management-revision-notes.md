Condensed notes on the External influences on operations management content (4.17 on this site) in the area of study Operations management within the external environment, OCR A Level Business H431, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027. Full explanations and three worked examples are in the [external influences on operations management study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-external-influences-on-operations-management/). Test yourself with the [external influences on operations management practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-external-influences-on-operations-management-practice/). All figures are invented.

## What the specification lists

Evaluate the influence on operations management of: social, legal, ethical, environmental, economic, political, technological and international factors.

## Core recall

### The eight influences and how they reach operations

| Influence | Example | Effect on operations |
|---|---|---|
| Social | Ageing population; demand for flexible work; community objections | Staffing patterns, product range, planning for noise and traffic |
| Legal | Minimum wage; health and safety; product safety; waste rules | Labour cost, compliance cost, testing, methods allowed |
| Ethical | Fair treatment in the supply chain; honest labelling | Supplier choice and monitoring cost; reputation |
| Environmental | Waste, energy, emissions; weather and climate | Packaging, energy choices, recycling; disruption to transport |
| Economic | Growth, unemployment, inflation, interest and exchange rates | Demand and capacity; labour availability; input costs; borrowing cost |
| Political | Taxes, subsidies, trade policy, instability | Site choice, tariffs, supply routes and risk |
| Technological | Automation, 3D printing, analytics, digital supply chains | Cost, quality, speed, skills; risk of falling behind |
| International | Overseas suppliers, markets and competition | Long supply chains, exchange-rate and tariff risk, export standards |

### Legal against ethical

- **Legal:** required by law; penalties for breaking it.
- **Ethical:** about what is right, beyond the law; enforced by customers, media and pressure groups.

### Responses to external change

- Forecasting and scenario planning.
- Flexible capacity (shifts, temporary staff, subcontracting).
- More than one supplier; contracts at fixed prices.
- Investing in technology to raise productivity.
- Holding more or less stock.
- Changing prices or product design.
- Contingency plans for supply disruption.

### Evaluating one influence: seven questions

1. How likely is it?
2. How big is the effect (with figures)?
3. How quickly does it arrive?
4. Does it hit all rivals equally?
5. Can the business respond, and at what cost?
6. What do stakeholders gain or lose?
7. Judgement: how important is it for this business?

## Worked reminder

Hobbs Plastics (invented).

- Minimum wage rise of 6% on a £660,000 wage bill = **£39,600**, or £0.18 on each of 220,000 containers. On a £9.50 price this is 1.9%.
- Resin: €440,000 at £1 = €1.20 costs £366,667; at £1 = €1.10 costs £400,000. A rise of **£33,333** (9.1%).
- Base profit £150,000 − £39,600 − £33,333 = **£77,067**: two changes together almost halve profit.
- Waste-water rule: £45,000 plus £6,000 a year, so £75,000 over five years. Energy equipment £60,000 saving £14,000 a year: payback **4.3 years**.

## Interactions between influences

- A fall in the pound (economic) makes imported materials dearer, which may push a business towards UK suppliers (international) or technology to cut waste (technological).
- A new environmental law may raise cost but also create demand for greener products (social).
- Political tariffs can reverse the case for offshoring.
- Social pressure on ethical sourcing can come before the law changes.

## Which influence matters most? Quick guide

| Business type | Influence likely to matter most | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Importer of materials | Economic (exchange rates), political (tariffs) | Input costs move with currency and trade rules |
| Labour-intensive service | Legal (minimum wage), social (availability of staff) | Wages are the main cost |
| Food producer | Legal (safety), ethical (sourcing), environmental (packaging) | Public trust and strict rules |
| High-volume manufacturer | Technological, economic (interest rates) | Equipment and borrowing drive cost |
| Exporter | International, economic (exchange rate) | Prices abroad depend on currency and standards |

Use this only as a starting point. The case facts decide, so quote them.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| External influence | A factor outside the business that affects it and which it cannot control |
| Compliance | Meeting the requirements of laws and standards |
| Exchange rate | The price of one currency in terms of another |
| Tariff | A tax on imported goods |
| Subsidy | Government payment to help a business or industry |
| Scenario planning | Preparing for several possible futures |
| Carbon footprint | Total greenhouse gas emissions caused by an activity |

## Exam traps

- Link every influence to operations (cost, capacity, quality, staffing, suppliers), not only to sales.
- Do not confuse legal and ethical factors.
- Choose the influence that matters most for this business and say why.
- Two influences acting together can have a larger effect than either alone.
- If the effect applies to all rivals, the competitive impact is smaller.
- End with a judgement and what it depends on.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between legal and ethical influences.
2. Give one social influence and one effect on staffing.
3. A business pays 50 staff £20,000 each. A 5% wage rise is required by law. Calculate the extra annual cost.
4. Total output is 100,000 units. Calculate the extra cost per unit.
5. A business buys €300,000 of materials. Calculate the cost in pounds at £1 = €1.20 and at £1 = €1.00.
6. State the difference between those two costs.
7. Give two ways to reduce the effect of exchange-rate changes.
8. Give one example of an environmental influence on operations.
9. Why might a tariff change the choice between UK and overseas production?
10. Why is the influence of a new law on all rivals less harmful than one that affects only your business?

### Answers

1. Legal influences are required by law; ethical influences concern what is right beyond the law, and are enforced by public opinion.
2. For example, demand for flexible hours leads to part-time or annualised-hours contracts.
3. 50 × £20,000 = £1,000,000; 5% = **£50,000**.
4. £50,000 ÷ 100,000 = **£0.50 per unit**.
5. At 1.20: £250,000. At 1.00: **£300,000**.
6. **£50,000** more at the weaker pound.
7. Any two: fixed-price contracts; buying forward; using UK suppliers; holding stock when the pound is strong.
8. Any one: rules on waste; pressure to cut emissions; floods disrupting transport.
9. A tariff raises the cost of imports and so reduces the cost advantage of overseas production.
10. If all rivals face the same cost rise, none gains an advantage, so prices may rise across the market; a cost that hits only you lowers your competitiveness.
