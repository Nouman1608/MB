These questions cover section 1.2.2, Productivity and division of labour, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. There is a single tier of entry. All firms, countries and figures are invented. Read the [productivity and division of labour study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-productivity-and-division-of-labour/) and the [productivity and division of labour revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-productivity-and-division-of-labour-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** What is meant by productivity? **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following would raise the productivity of land?

A: hiring more farm workers

B: irrigating dry fields

C: raising the price of the crop

D: paying farm workers more

**[1]**

**3.** Define division of labour. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** ways in which capital can raise productivity. **[2]**

**5.** A factory with 25 workers produces 6 000 units a week. Calculate output per worker. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** A call centre has 20 staff who each work 35 hours a week and handle 14 000 calls in total. After a training programme, the same staff handle 16 800 calls a week.

**(a)** Calculate calls handled per hour before and after training. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in productivity. **[2]**

**(c)** Each member of staff is paid 14 an hour. Calculate the labour cost per call before and after training. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain **one** disadvantage to the business of paying for the training. **[2]**

**7.** A farmer has 40 hectares yielding 3.2 tonnes of rice per hectare. Drainage of waterlogged fields raises the yield by 25%.

**(a)** Calculate the new yield per hectare and the new total output. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how drainage raises the productivity of land. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** way in which migration may affect the productivity of labour in a country. **[3]**

**9.** A toy maker reorganises production so that each worker carries out only one task.

**(a)** Explain **one** advantage of this division of labour for the business. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **one** disadvantage of this division of labour for the workers. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why hiring more workers increases production but may not increase productivity. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a business should invest in new technology to raise productivity. **[6]**

**12.** Assess whether division of labour is suitable for a small furniture maker that builds each piece to order. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Output [1]; per unit of input over a period of time (for example, per worker per hour) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A raises total output but not output per hectare; C and D do not change yield.

**3.** Splitting the production process into separate tasks [1]; with each worker specialising in one task [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two: more machines or tools per worker (increased quantity) [1]; technological advances that make equipment faster or more accurate [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 6 000 ÷ 25 = **240 units per worker** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Hours = 20 × 35 = 700. Before: 14 000 ÷ 700 = **20 calls per hour** [1]; after: 16 800 ÷ 700 = **24 calls per hour** [1].

**(b)** (24 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 [1] = **20%** [1].

**(c)** Before: 14 ÷ 20 = **0.70** per call [1]; after: 14 ÷ 24 = **0.58** per call (to 2 d.p.) [1].

**(d)** Trained staff may leave for a rival call centre [1]; so the business loses the benefit of the money it spent on training [1]. (Also accept: fees and lost output while staff train raise costs in the short run.) **[8]**

**7. (a)** 3.2 × 1.25 = **4.0 tonnes per hectare** [1]; 4.0 × 40 = **160 tonnes** [1].

**(b)** Drainage removes excess water from the soil [1]; so crops can grow better, giving more output per hectare [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Skilled immigrants can fill shortages in jobs such as engineering or healthcare [1]; they bring human capital that raises output per worker [1]; so average labour productivity in the country rises [1]. (Also accept: lower-skilled immigrants may reduce average output per worker at first; emigration of skilled workers lowers productivity in the country they leave.) **[3]**

**9. (a)** Each worker repeats one task and becomes skilled and quick at it [1]; so output per worker rises [1]; which lowers the cost of each toy, so the business can cut prices or earn more profit [1].

**(b)** The work becomes repetitive [1]; so workers may become bored and less motivated [1]; and their narrow skills make it harder to find other work if their task is automated [1]. **[6]**

**10.** More workers produce more in total, so production rises [1]; but productivity is output per worker, which depends on how much each worker produces [1]; if the extra workers share the same tools and space, output per worker may stay the same or fall [1]. **[3]**

**11.** For: new technology lets each worker produce more per hour [1]; so unit costs fall [1]; so the firm can compete on price or earn higher profits [1]. Against: equipment is expensive and must be paid for [1]; staff need training, and some jobs may be lost, affecting morale [1]. Judgement: worthwhile if the cost saving is large enough and demand is high enough to sell extra output; a supported conclusion [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Advantages: workers would become quicker at their tasks [1]; possibly lowering unit costs [1]. Disadvantages: each piece is different, so tasks are not repeated often and specialisation brings little gain [1]; with few workers, an absent worker could halt production [1]; customers may value a craftsperson's skill across the whole piece [1]. Judgement: division of labour is less suitable for made-to-order work in a small firm, but could help in standard stages such as sanding and finishing [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing production with productivity.
- Ignoring total hours when calculating output per hour.
- Rounding labour cost per unit too early.
- Describing division of labour as using machines.
- Evaluations that do not refer to the type of product or market.
