## Key terms

- **Power** -- the ability of a state to get the outcomes it wants, including by influencing others.
- **Hard power** -- influence through force or payment: armed forces, sanctions, trade deals, aid.
- **Soft power** -- influence through attraction: culture, values, education, media. The term is linked to the political scientist Joseph Nye (around 1990).
- **Superpower** -- a state that can project power in every region of the world, across military, economic, political and cultural fields at once. The word is usually credited to William T. R. Fox, writing in 1944.
- **Great power** -- a state with strong regional or wide influence, but not the global reach of a superpower.
- **Hegemony** -- dominance by one state over others, often accepted rather than imposed.
- **Polarity** -- how power is spread in the world system: **unipolar** (one dominant power), **bipolar** (two), **multipolar** (several).
- **Sovereignty** -- a state's authority to govern itself.

## What makes a superpower?

No single measure decides it. A superpower scores highly on most of these indicators.

| Indicator | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Economic | Large total GDP, major trading power, a widely used currency, big companies with global reach |
| Military | Large defence budget, nuclear weapons, ability to send forces far from home (navy, overseas bases) |
| Political | Permanent seat on the UN Security Council, leading role in alliances, influence on other states' decisions |
| Cultural | Films, music, language, universities and brands used worldwide (soft power) |
| Demographic and resources | Large population, land area, energy and mineral resources |

### Superpowers in the twentieth century and today

During the **Cold War** the world was **bipolar**. The United States and the Soviet Union led rival blocs, competed in an arms race and a space race, and backed opposing sides in conflicts worldwide. When the Soviet Union broke up in December 1991, many writers described a **unipolar** world led by the United States.

Today the picture is debated. China has the world's second-largest economy and has expanded its trade, lending and navy. Some analysts call it an emerging superpower in a **multipolar** world where the European Union, India, Russia and groupings such as BRICS also hold weight. Others argue that China's power is still mainly regional and that the United States keeps advantages in alliances, military reach and soft power. Present both views, with evidence.

### Worked example -- using indicators

Two fictional states give these figures for the same year.

| State | GDP (billion dollars) | Population (million) | Defence spending (billion dollars) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arvenia | 3,000 | 120 | 90 |
| Kestria | 1,200 | 20 | 24 |

**Step 1 -- GDP per capita** (GDP ÷ population):

```
Arvenia: 3,000,000,000,000 ÷ 120,000,000 = 25,000 dollars
Kestria: 1,200,000,000,000 ÷ 20,000,000  = 60,000 dollars
```

**Step 2 -- defence spending as a percentage of GDP**:

```
Arvenia: 90 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = 3.0%
Kestria: 24 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 2.0%
```

**Step 3 -- interpret.** Arvenia has the larger economy and spends more on defence, in total and as a share of GDP, so it has more **hard power**. Kestria's people are richer on average. Total size matters most for global reach, but a judgement also needs political and cultural evidence the table does not give.

## Empires

An **empire** is a state that rules other peoples or territories, usually without their consent, from a central power (the **metropole**).

- **Formal empire** -- direct political control: colonies governed by officials from the metropole.
- **Informal empire** -- control through trade, loans or influence, without official annexation.
- **Land (contiguous) empire** -- expands into neighbouring territory, such as the Mongol Empire, the largest contiguous land empire in history.
- **Maritime (overseas) empire** -- controls distant territory by sea, such as the Spanish and British empires. At its height in the early twentieth century, the British Empire covered around a quarter of the world's land area.

### Why empires rise

Look for factors in these groups:

1. **Economic** -- wealth from trade, farming or resources pays for armies and officials.
2. **Military and technological** -- better weapons, ships or organisation, such as the Mongols' mounted archers or European steam-powered ships.
3. **Political** -- strong leadership and systems to govern distant land, such as roads, laws and tax collection.
4. **Ideological** -- beliefs that justify expansion: religion, a "civilising mission", national prestige.
5. **Opportunity** -- rivals that are weak or divided.

The Ottoman Empire shows several at once: a strong army, control of trade routes between Europe and Asia, and the capture of Constantinople in 1453.

### Why empires decline

The historian Paul Kennedy, in *The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers* (1987), argued that great powers weaken through **imperial overstretch**: their commitments abroad cost more than their economy can support. Other factors include:

- **Internal** -- economic stagnation, corruption, divided leadership, costly reform.
- **External** -- defeat in war, rival powers, pressure from other states.
- **From the governed** -- nationalist and independence movements.

**Decolonisation** after 1945 is the main example of the last point: India and Pakistan became independent from Britain in 1947, and dozens of African and Asian colonies followed in the 1950s and 1960s. The Soviet Union shows the mix: a long war in Afghanistan (1979–1989), a stagnant economy, Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms from 1985, the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989 and nationalist movements in its republics all came before its break-up in 1991.

### Worked example -- describing change over time

The fictional empire of Valdoria controlled this much territory (million km²):

| Year | 1600 | 1700 | 1800 | 1850 | 1900 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Territory | 0.8 | 2.0 | 3.5 | 3.1 | 1.4 |

Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.

```
1600–1700: (2.0 − 0.8) ÷ 0.8 × 100 = +150%
1700–1800: (3.5 − 2.0) ÷ 2.0 × 100 = +75%
1800–1900: (1.4 − 3.5) ÷ 3.5 × 100 = −60%
```

A strong description uses the numbers and the shape: growth was fastest in the 1600s, peaked around 1800, fell slowly to 1850 (−11.4%) and then fast to 1900 (−54.8%). The data shows **when** decline happened, not **why**; for that you need other evidence, such as records of wars or revolts.

## Supra-national alliances and organisations

### Two ways of working together

- **Intergovernmental** -- states cooperate but keep full sovereignty; decisions usually need every member's agreement.
- **Supranational** -- states **pool** some sovereignty, so bodies above national level can make rules that bind members.

Most organisations are intergovernmental. The European Union is the clearest example with strong supranational features.

### Why states join -- and what it costs

States join for **security** (collective defence), **economic gain** (larger markets), **influence** (a stronger voice as a group) and **shared values**. The cost is less freedom of action: members follow shared rules, pay contributions and may be drawn into others' disputes.

### Five organisations to know

| Organisation | Founded | Main purpose | How it decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| **United Nations (UN)** | 1945 (Charter in force 24 October 1945); 193 member states | Keep international peace and security; promote cooperation and human rights | General Assembly: one state, one vote. Security Council: 15 members; the 5 permanent members (China, France, Russia, UK, US) each hold a veto |
| **NATO** | 1949 (North Atlantic Treaty, 12 founding members); 32 members | Collective defence | Consensus. Article 5: an armed attack on one member is treated as an attack on all; invoked once, after the 11 September 2001 attacks |
| **European Union (EU)** | Created by the Maastricht Treaty (in force 1993); roots in the 1951 coal and steel community and the 1957 Treaty of Rome; 27 members | Single market with free movement of goods, services, capital and people; shared policies | Supranational features: European Commission proposes laws, directly elected European Parliament, Court of Justice, many decisions by majority. The UK left in 2020 |
| **ASEAN** | 1967 (Bangkok Declaration; 5 founders: Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, Thailand); ten members by 1999, 11 since Timor-Leste joined in October 2025 | Regional peace, economic growth, trade | Consultation and consensus; non-interference in members' internal affairs |
| **African Union (AU)** | 2002, replacing the Organisation of African Unity (1963); 55 member states; headquarters Addis Ababa | African unity, peace and development | Intergovernmental; its Constitutive Act allows intervention in a member state in grave cases such as genocide; it has suspended members after military coups |

A good comparison uses a **shared criterion**. On sovereignty, EU members give up more than ASEAN members: EU law can override national law, while ASEAN decides by consensus. On effectiveness, the UN Security Council can act quickly when the permanent members agree, but one veto can block action.

## Criterion D: evaluating sources on power and empire

This topic produces sources with strong viewpoints: speeches, treaties, propaganda posters, imperial maps and official statistics. Use **origin, purpose, value and limitation**, and tie each point to the research question. For the full method, see the [source-evaluation exam preparation](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-societies-source-evaluation-exam-preparation/).

> **Source X -- original text written for Marlbridge.** An imagined extract from a fictional colonial governor's annual report to his home government: "The new railway has brought order and trade to the province. Local chiefs now accept our rule, and the people are grateful."

- **Origin and purpose**: written by the official in charge, for superiors who judge his success.
- **Value**: useful for the government's official view of the railway and for what the governor wanted his superiors to believe.
- **Limitation**: he has a reason to show success, and he does not record local people's own views, so it is weak evidence of whether they were "grateful".

Notice that value and limitation both come from the same fact about origin.

## Common errors

- Calling any rich country a superpower. You need global reach across several indicators.
- Writing that NATO is part of the UN, or that the EU and the Council of Europe are one body.
- Calling every organisation "supranational". Only use it where members pool sovereignty.
- Giving one cause of decline. Show how causes interact, and rank them.
- Describing a data trend without figures, or claiming data explains *why* it happened.
- Value and limitation points that could fit any source.
