This guide covers **4.7.3 Synthetic and naturally occurring polymers (chemistry only)** of the AQA GCSE
Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016. It sits in topic 4.7, Organic chemistry, and
is assessed on Paper 2 in the separate chemistry qualification. Addition polymerisation (4.7.3.1) and DNA and
other natural polymers (4.7.3.4) are for both tiers. Condensation polymerisation (4.7.3.2) and amino acids
(4.7.3.3) are **Higher tier only**.

## 4.7.3.1 Addition polymerisation

In **addition polymerisation**, many small molecules called **monomers** join together to form a very large
molecule called a **polymer**. The monomers are **alkenes** (or molecules with a C=C bond). The double bond
opens, and each monomer links to the next.

For ethene:

n CH₂=CH₂ → ( –CH₂–CH₂– )ₙ

- The monomer is **ethene**. The polymer is **poly(ethene)**.
- The part in brackets is the **repeating unit**. The n shows that the unit repeats many times.
- In an addition polymer, the **repeating unit has the same atoms as the monomer**, because **no other
  molecule is formed** in the reaction. This is why it is called addition.
- The polymer has **no C=C bond** left. All the carbon-carbon bonds in the backbone are single.

```
  H   H                    H   H
  |   |                    |   |
  C = C      n of these -> [-C - C-]n
  |   |                    |   |
  H   H                    H   H
 ethene                  poly(ethene)
```

The double bond in each monomer opens, and the two spare bonds on each unit join it to its neighbours.

### Other examples

| Monomer | Polymer | Repeating unit |
|---|---|---|
| Ethene, CH₂=CH₂ | Poly(ethene) | –CH₂–CH₂– |
| Propene, CH₃CH=CH₂ | Poly(propene) | –CH(CH₃)–CH₂– |
| Chloroethene, CH₂=CHCl | Poly(chloroethene) (PVC) | –CHCl–CH₂– |

### Skills you need

1. **Recognise addition polymers and monomers** from diagrams. Look for a backbone of single-bonded carbon atoms
   with side groups. The monomer must contain **C=C**.
2. **Draw the formation of a polymer** from a given alkene monomer.
3. **Relate the repeating unit to the monomer.**

### Worked example 1: from monomer to repeating unit

*Draw the repeating unit for the polymer of propene.*

1. Draw propene with its double bond: the two carbons joined by C=C. One carbon carries a CH₃ group and an H;
   the other carries two Hs.
2. Open the double bond: it becomes a single bond, and each carbon has an unused bond on the outside.
3. Draw the two carbons in a chain with those bonds extended to the sides, using brackets and n.

**Repeating unit:** –CH(CH₃)–CH₂– with the CH₃ as a side group. Atoms: C₃H₆, the same as propene.

### Worked example 2: from polymer to monomer

*A section of polymer is –CH₂–CHCl–CH₂–CHCl–.*

1. Find the repeating unit: –CH₂–CHCl–.
2. Put a double bond between the two carbons and remove the outer bonds: CH₂=CHCl.
3. The monomer is **chloroethene**.

### Worked example 3: sizes

Ethene has a relative formula mass of 28 (C₂H₄: 24 + 4). A poly(ethene) molecule made from 1000 monomers has a
relative formula mass of about 1000 × 28 = **28 000**, since no atoms are lost.

## 4.7.3.2 Condensation polymerisation (Higher tier only)

In **condensation polymerisation**, the monomers have **two functional groups** each. When they react they
join together, **usually losing a small molecule such as water**. Because a small molecule is lost, it is called a
**condensation** reaction.

The simplest condensation polymers come from **two different monomers**, each with **two of the same functional
group**. For example:

- **Ethanediol**, HO–CH₂CH₂–OH, a diol with two –OH groups
- **Hexanedioic acid**, HOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOH, a dicarboxylic acid with two –COOH groups

These react to form a **polyester**. Each time an –OH and a –COOH react, they form an **ester link** and release
one molecule of **water**.

```
HO-[diol]-OH  +  HOOC-[diacid]-COOH   ->   -O-[diol]-O-CO-[diacid]-CO-  +  H2O
```

A block diagram is enough for the exam: show the two monomers as boxes with their functional groups, the ester
link where they join, and the water that is lost.

### Addition and condensation compared

| | Addition | Condensation |
|---|---|---|
| Monomer | One type, with C=C | Usually two types, each with two functional groups |
| Small molecule lost | **No** | **Yes** (usually water) |
| Repeating unit | Same atoms as monomer | Different from the monomers |
| Example | Poly(ethene) | Polyester |

## 4.7.3.3 Amino acids (Higher tier only)

**Amino acids** have **two different functional groups** in one molecule: an **amino group** (–NH₂) and a
**carboxyl group** (–COOH). They can react by condensation polymerisation to form **polypeptides**.

Glycine is **H₂N–CH₂–COOH**. When two glycine molecules react, the –COOH of one joins to the –NH₂ of the next
and **one water molecule is lost**. The link formed is a **peptide link**, –CO–NH–.

n H₂N–CH₂–COOH → ( –NH–CH₂–CO– )ₙ + n H₂O

Different amino acids can be combined in one chain to give **proteins**.

### Worked example 4: relative formula masses

*Glycine, C₂H₅NO₂, has a relative formula mass of 75. Work out the relative formula mass of the compound formed
when two glycine molecules join.*

```
2 x 75 = 150
one H2O (18) is lost
150 - 18 = 132
```

The dipeptide is C₄H₈N₂O₃. Check: C 4 × 12 = 48, H 8, N 2 × 14 = 28, O 3 × 16 = 48; total = 48 + 8 + 28 + 48 = 132.

## 4.7.3.4 DNA and other naturally occurring polymers

**DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)** is a large molecule essential for life. It **encodes the genetic instructions**
for the development and functioning of living organisms and viruses. Most DNA molecules consist of **two polymer
chains** in the form of a **double helix**. Each chain is made from **four different monomers** called
**nucleotides**.

Other naturally occurring polymers important for life, and their monomers:

| Polymer | Monomer |
|---|---|
| **DNA** | Nucleotides (four different types) |
| **Proteins** | Amino acids |
| **Starch** | Sugars (glucose) |
| **Cellulose** | Sugars (glucose) |

Starch and cellulose are both polymers of glucose, but the units are linked differently, so they have
different properties. You need only to name the monomer types.

## Exam technique

- For addition polymers, say the **C=C opens** and that **no other product** forms.
- When asked for a repeating unit, draw **brackets** with **n** and show the **extending bonds** through the
  brackets.
- To name the monomer, put the double bond back and name the alkene.
- For condensation, always mention that **water (a small molecule) is lost**.
- For natural polymers, give the monomer: nucleotides, amino acids, sugars.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving a C=C in the drawn polymer.
- Writing the repeating unit with the double bond still showing.
- Forgetting to put brackets and n around the repeating unit.
- Saying addition polymers contain different atoms from the monomer.
- Saying condensation polymers form with no water given off.
- Mixing up the monomers: proteins are made from **amino acids**, not sugars.
