## Section B: structured questions

**8.** Poly(ethene) is made from ethene.

**(a)** Describe what happens to the ethene molecules during polymerisation. **[2]**

**(b)** State **one** reason why burning waste poly(ethene) can be harmful. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why poly(ethene) waste that reaches the sea causes long-term problems. **[2]**

**9.** *(Extended)* Propene, CH₂=CHCH₃, forms an addition polymer.

**(a)** Name the polymer. **[1]**

**(b)** Draw the repeat unit of the polymer. **[2]**

**(c)** State the only product of this reaction. **[1]**

**(a)** Draw the structure of the monomer and name it. **[2]**

**(b)** State what happens to the double bond during polymerisation. **[1]**

**(a)** Name the type of polymerisation. **[1]**

**(b)** Name the other product formed. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Large molecules [1] built up from many smaller molecules called monomers [1].

**2.** Ethene [1]; addition polymerisation [1].

**3.** **B** [1]. Non-biodegradable plastics are not broken down by microorganisms.

**4.** Disposal in landfill sites (they do not decompose) [1]; accumulation in oceans [1]; formation of toxic gases when burned [1].

**5.** Ester (linkage) [1].

**6.** **B** [1]. A polyamide forms from a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid; option A gives a polyester.

**7.** Amino acids [1].

**8.** **(a)** The C=C double bond in each ethene molecule breaks / opens [1]; many ethene molecules join together to form one long chain [1].

**(b)** Toxic gases (such as carbon monoxide) can form [1].

**(c)** Poly(ethene) is non-biodegradable / not broken down by microorganisms [1], so it accumulates in the oceans, where animals can swallow it or become trapped in it [1].

**9.** **(a)** Poly(propene) [1].

**(b)** Two carbon atoms joined by a single bond in the chain, with H, H on one carbon and H, CH₃ on the other [1]; brackets with extending bonds through them (and *n*) [1]:

```
     H   H
     |   |
 —[ C — C ]—
     |   |   n
     H   CH₃
```

**(c)** Poly(propene) – the polymer is the only product [1].
*This is the defining feature of addition polymerisation: every atom of every monomer ends up in the polymer.*

**10.** **(a)** CH₃CH=CHCH₃, drawn with a C=C double bond and one H and one CH₃ on each carbon [1]; but-2-ene [1].

**(b)** It breaks / opens to become a single bond, and each carbon forms a new bond to a neighbouring unit [1].
*To work back from any addition repeat unit, take the two main-chain carbons, restore the C=C between them and remove the extending bonds; the groups above and below stay attached.*

**11.** **(a)** Condensation polymerisation [1].

**(b)** Water [1].

**(c)** Correct ester linkage, –CO–O–, shown between the acid and diol parts [1]; correct repeat unit with extending bonds at each end [1]:

```
 —[ CO—□—CO—O—■—O ]—
```

*The C=O of each ester linkage may be drawn out in full.*

**12.** **(a)** Amide (linkage) [1].

**(b)** HOOC–□–COOH (dicarboxylic acid) [1]; H₂N–■–NH₂ (diamine) [1].
*Cut through each –CO–NH– link, then add –OH to each CO end and H to each NH end.*

**13.** Any two [2]:

- addition polymerisation gives only the polymer; condensation polymerisation also gives a small molecule such as water;
- addition monomers contain a C=C double bond; condensation monomers have two functional groups each;
- addition polymers usually form from one type of monomer; condensation polymers usually from two different monomers.

*Each difference must compare both types.*

**14.** **(a)** (Natural) polyamide [1].

**(b)** H₂N–CH(R)–COOH, with the amine and carboxylic acid groups on the same carbon [1].

**(c)** Water [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a polymer as "a long chain" without mentioning monomers.
- Leaving the C=C in an addition repeat unit, or drawing it with no extending bonds.
- Putting the CH₃ side group into the main chain of poly(propene).
- Forgetting water as a product of condensation polymerisation.
- Confusing the amide linkage (nylon, proteins) with the ester linkage (PET).
- Writing "pollution" instead of naming a specific environmental challenge.
