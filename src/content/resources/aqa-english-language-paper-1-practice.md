## Section A

**1.** Name the language levels you should use as a framework when analysing any text. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by **representation** in language analysis. **[2]**

## Section B

**3.** A newspaper headline reads: *"Migrant surge overwhelms coastal towns."*

**(a)** Analyse the representation created, referring to at least **three** language features. **[6]**
**(b)** Rewrite the headline to create a sympathetic representation, and explain the changes you made. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how the following contribute to the representation of a group: modification, transitivity, semantic field. **[6]**

**5.** Explain **three** features of the language of advertising and the effect of each. **[6]**

**6.** Explain the concept of **audience positioning** and how a text can position its reader without stating a view explicitly. **[5]**

**7.** Explain how **mode** affects a text, comparing a scripted news bulletin with a live radio phone-in. **[4]**

## Section C

**8.** Give the five stages of child language acquisition, in order. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how "virtuous errors" (e.g. "goed", "sheeps") are used as evidence for Chomsky's nativist theory of language acquisition, and explain how this challenges a purely behaviourist account. **[5]**

**10.** Distinguish between idiolect and sociolect, giving an example of each. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **Lexis and semantics**; **grammar and syntax**; **phonology, graphology, pragmatics and discourse structure** [1] [1] [1].

**2.** Representation is **how a text portrays a person, group, place or idea** through its language choices [1]; every text presents a **version of reality shaped by selection and wording**, rather than a neutral reflection of it [1].

**3. (a)** The **metaphor "surge"** draws on a **semantic field of water and natural disaster**, presenting people as an **uncontrollable force rather than individuals** [1] [1]. **"Overwhelms"** is a **transitive verb placing migrants in the agent position and the towns as affected patients**, so responsibility and threat are assigned to the migrants [1] [1]. The **noun "migrant" is used as a modifier rather than a full noun phrase**, so people are reduced to a category with no individuating detail — no ages, names or circumstances [1]. **Omission of a determiner and any number** makes the group seem indefinite and limitless [1].
**(b)** For example: *"Coastal towns welcome families arriving by sea."* [1] "Families" **restores human individuality and relationship** in place of a category label [1]; "welcome" **reverses the transitivity, making the towns the willing agent** rather than the victim [1]; "arriving by sea" is **neutral and descriptive**, removing the disaster metaphor [1].

**4. Modification** — the adjectives and nouns placed before a head noun ("young offenders", "hardworking families") **attach evaluative qualities to a group before it is even named** [1] [1]. **Transitivity** — whether a group is the **agent doing the action or the patient it is done to** determines whether they appear active and responsible or passive and vulnerable [1] [1]. **Semantic field** — the **cluster of related lexis running through a text** frames how the group is understood; a field of warfare presents a debate as conflict, a field of illness presents it as something to be treated [1] [1].

**5.** Any three, 2 marks each: **imperatives** — "Discover", "Taste the difference" — create a **direct instruction that assumes compliance** [1] [1]; **second person address** — "you" **individualises a mass-market message**, so the reader feels personally addressed [1] [1]; **presupposition** — "when you upgrade" assumes the decision is already made, so the reader is not invited to question it [1] [1]; **phonological patterning** — alliteration and rhythm make a slogan **memorable and easily recalled at the point of purchase** [1] [1]; **comparative adjectives with no stated comparator** — "cleaner", "stronger" — which imply superiority without a testable claim [1] [1].

**6.** Audience positioning is the way a text **constructs a particular role and set of assumptions for its reader** [1]. It can do so without stating a view by using **presupposition**, which builds a claim into the structure of a sentence so accepting the sentence means accepting the claim [1]; by **inclusive pronouns** such as "we" and "our", which enrol the reader in a shared position [1]; by **selection and omission** — deciding which voices are quoted and which are not [1]; and by **naming choices**, since "protester" and "activist" position the reader differently towards the same person [1].

**7.** A **scripted news bulletin** is **written to be spoken**: it is **planned, edited and dense with information**, uses **complete sentences and standard grammar**, and has **no interaction or repair** [1] [1]. A **live phone-in** is **spontaneous speech**: it contains **fillers, false starts, overlaps, interruption and self-repair**, and its structure is **negotiated between participants in real time** rather than fixed in advance [1] [1].

**8.** **Babbling** [1], **holophrastic (one-word)** [1], **two-word** [1], **telegraphic** [1], **post-telegraphic** [1].

**9.** A virtuous error is a **grammatically logical mistake a child could not have heard an adult produce**, e.g. "goed" for *went*, or "sheeps" for *sheep* [1] [1] — the child has **over-applied a regular grammatical rule to an irregular word** [1]. Nativists use this as evidence for an **innate language acquisition device**, since the child cannot simply be **imitating adult speech** they have never heard [1]; this directly challenges Skinner's **behaviourist** account, which explains acquisition through **imitation and reinforcement alone**, and cannot explain an error no adult ever modelled [1].

**10. Idiolect** is an **individual's** own distinctive, personal way of using language [1] — e.g. a person's characteristic catchphrases or habitual turns of phrase [1]. **Sociolect** is the variety of language **shared by a particular social group** [1] — e.g. the shared vocabulary and grammar of a regional or occupational community [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Identifying features without linking them to representation.
- Ignoring transitivity, which is where most representation marks sit.
- Describing advertising language generically rather than analysing effect.
- Treating mode as simply "spoken or written" rather than a continuum.
