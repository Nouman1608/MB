## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Which of the following best defines scarcity? **[1]**

A: A temporary excess of demand over supply at the current price.

B: The situation in which wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them.

C: A lack of money among low-income households.

D: The absence of a good from shops.

**2.** Which of the following is most likely to be a free good? **[1]**

A: Tap water in a city flat.

B: A place at a state primary school.

C: Seawater for a sailor in mid-ocean.

D: A book borrowed from a public library.

**3.** Which of the following statements is normative? **[1]**

A: The bus fare in the city rose by 10% last year.

B: A fare rise will reduce passenger numbers.

C: Bus fares should be cut to help low-income travellers.

D: Passenger numbers fell by 4% after the fare rise.

**4.** In a bakery, which of the following is an example of capital? **[1]**

A: The baker's skill.

B: The bank loan used to buy equipment.

C: The oven.

D: The land the shop stands on.

**5.** Explain the difference between a need and a want, using an example of each. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a public park with free entry is an economic good. **[2]**

**7.** State the three questions every economy must answer and explain why scarcity makes them necessary. **[3]**

### Section B

**8.** An underground water store in Region V holds 1,200 million cubic metres. Rainfall adds 40 million cubic metres a year, and farms and towns extract 64 million cubic metres a year.

**(a)** Calculate how many years the store will last at current rates. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage cut in extraction needed to keep the store at its present size. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how this case illustrates the basic economic problem. **[3]**

**9.** A newspaper extract on a proposed congestion charge in City C contains four statements. Classify each as positive or normative and give a reason.

(i) "Average traffic speeds in the centre are 11 miles per hour."

(ii) "A charge of £8 a day would cut car journeys into the centre."

(iii) "It is unfair to make commuters pay to use roads their taxes built."

(iv) "The council ought to spend the revenue on buses." **[4]**

**10.** A fishing cooperative earns revenue of £410,000 a year. It pays £176,000 in wages, £16,000 in mooring rent and £24,000 in interest on a boat loan, and buys £90,000 of fuel and bait from other firms.

**(a)** Identify the factor of production rewarded by the wages, the mooring rent and the interest. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the cooperative's profit. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the payment for fuel and bait is not a reward to a factor of production used by the cooperative. **[1]**

**11.** Explain how households, firms and government each face the basic economic problem. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that economic growth will eventually solve the basic economic problem. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Scarcity compares wants with resources; option A describes a shortage. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Seawater in mid-ocean uses no scarce resources and taking more deprives no one; the others are free at the point of use but use resources. **[1]**

**3.** **C** [1]. "Should" expresses a value judgement; B is a testable prediction. **[1]**

**4.** **C** [1]. Capital is man-made equipment; the loan is money used to buy it. **[1]**

**5.** A need is required for survival or to avoid serious harm, such as drinking water [1]. A want is desired beyond that, such as a holiday abroad [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Land, gardeners, maintenance and lighting are scarce resources with other uses [1], so providing the park has an opportunity cost, even though users pay no price [1]. **[2]**

**7.** What to produce, how to produce it and for whom [1]. Resources are limited while wants are unlimited [1], so not every want can be met and choices must be made about which goods to make, with which resources, and who receives them [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Net fall = 64 - 40 = 24 million cubic metres a year [1]. Years = 1,200 / 24 = **50 years** [1].

**(b)** Extraction must fall to 40 million, a cut of 24 / 64 = **37.5%** [1].

**(c)** Water is a scarce resource with competing uses, farms and towns [1]. Using more today reduces what is available in future, so there is a choice between present and future use [1]. Cutting extraction reduces present output or comfort, which is the opportunity cost of conserving the store, a sustainability issue [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the 50-year figure in part (c); applying the calculation to the concept is what lifts the answer.

**9.** (i) Positive: it can be checked by measuring speeds [1]. (ii) Positive: a prediction that evidence after the charge could test [1]. (iii) Normative: "unfair" is a value judgement [1]. (iv) Normative: "ought" shows an opinion about what should happen [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A prediction is positive even though it has not yet been tested.

**10. (a)** Wages reward labour [1]. Mooring rent rewards land, and interest rewards capital [1].

**(b)** Profit = 410,000 - 90,000 - 176,000 - 16,000 - 24,000 [1] = **£104,000** [1].

**(c)** Fuel and bait are bought from other firms, so the payment rewards those firms' factors of production, not the cooperative's [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Profit is the reward to enterprise, what is left after all other costs, so subtract every payment.

**11.** Households have limited incomes and time [1], so they must choose which goods to buy and how to divide time between work and leisure [1]. Firms have limited capital, labour and finance [1], so they must choose what to produce and which methods to use [1]. Government has limited tax revenue [1], so it must choose between uses such as health, education and defence, or between spending and lower taxes [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* For each agent give both the scarce resource and the choice it forces.

**12.** Growth raises the quantity of goods and services an economy can produce [1], so more wants can be met and absolute poverty can fall [1]. Technology can create substitutes for scarce resources, for example renewable energy [1]. However, wants are unlimited and tend to grow with income [1], so the gap between wants and resources does not close [1]. Growth also uses resources and can damage the environment, making some resources scarcer, such as clean air or stable climate [1]. Even rich economies face choices, for example between health spending and tax cuts [1]. The distribution of output matters: growth may not reach everyone [1]. Judgement: growth eases the effects of scarcity but cannot remove it, because it does not change the fact that resources used for one purpose cannot be used for another [1]; how far it eases scarcity depends on whether growth is sustainable [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer accepts the case for growth before showing why "solve" is too strong, and ends with a reasoned judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing scarcity with shortage in multiple-choice options.
- Classifying goods by price rather than by resource use.
- Calling predictions normative.
- Treating money as capital.
- Leaving out fuel or other bought-in inputs when calculating profit.
- Ending a 10-mark answer without a judgement.
