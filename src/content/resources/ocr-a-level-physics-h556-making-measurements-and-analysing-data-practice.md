## Section A

**1.** State whether each of the following causes a systematic error or a random error.

**(a)** A voltmeter reads 0.02 V when it is not connected to anything. **[1]**

**(b)** A student's reaction time varies when timing a falling ball with a stopwatch. **[1]**

**(c)** The zero end of a wooden metre rule has worn away by 2 mm. **[1]**

**2.** Two students each make three measurements of a standard mass whose true value is 1.000 kg. Student A records 1.012, 1.011 and 1.012 kg. Student B records 0.990, 1.010 and 1.000 kg. Describe each set of results in terms of precision and accuracy. **[2]**

**3.** A digital caliper with a resolution of 0.01 mm reads 12.46 mm. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in this reading, taking the resolution as the absolute uncertainty. **[1]**

**4.** Five readings of a p.d. are 4.82, 4.79, 5.30, 4.85 and 4.80 V.

**(a)** Identify the anomalous reading and state how it should be treated. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mean p.d. and its absolute uncertainty. **[2]**

**5.** The length of a spring is 12.4 ± 0.1 cm before loading and 18.9 ± 0.1 cm after loading. Calculate the extension with its absolute uncertainty and its percentage uncertainty. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A metal sphere has diameter 2.50 ± 0.02 cm and mass 64.2 ± 0.1 g. Its density is ρ = 6m/(πd³). Calculate the density in g cm⁻³ with its absolute uncertainty. **[4]**

**7.** The speed of a wave on a string is v = √(T/μ). The tension is 4.0 ± 0.1 N and the mass per unit length is (1.60 ± 0.05) × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹. Calculate v with its absolute uncertainty. **[4]**

**8.** A student finds the resistivity of a wire from ρ = πd²R/(4L). The measurements are R = 2.40 ± 0.05 Ω, d = 0.38 ± 0.01 mm and L = 0.800 ± 0.002 m.

**(a)** Calculate ρ. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in ρ. **[2]**

**(c)** State which measurement contributes most to the uncertainty and suggest how its uncertainty could be reduced. **[1]**

**9.** A student times a single swing of a pendulum as 1.9 s. The uncertainty in starting and stopping the stopwatch is about 0.2 s. Explain, with a calculation, why timing 10 swings gives a better value for the period. **[3]**

**10.** A student plots a graph of p.d. V against current I for a resistor, with error bars. The best-fit line has gradient 15.2 Ω. The steepest and shallowest worst lines have gradients 15.9 Ω and 14.6 Ω.

**(a)** Calculate the resistance with its absolute uncertainty, using half the difference between the worst lines. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the resistance. **[1]**

**(c)** The best-fit line crosses the V axis at 0.10 V instead of passing through the origin. Suggest a cause. **[1]**

**11.** An experiment gives the specific heat capacity of water as 4.45 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ with a percentage uncertainty of 3 %. The accepted value is 4.18 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage difference. **[1]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether the result agrees with the accepted value. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest a cause of the difference, and explain why it makes the measured value too large. **[2]**

**12.** On an oscilloscope screen one complete wave occupies 4.0 ± 0.2 divisions. The time base is set to 5.0 ms per division, taken as exact. Calculate the frequency with its absolute uncertainty. **[3]**

**13.** A student says: "My results are accurate because they are all very close together." Comment on this statement. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Systematic** (a zero error) [1].

**(b)** **Random** [1].

**(c)** **Systematic** [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* A worn rule shifts every length measured from that end by the same amount, so it is systematic; measuring between two marks away from the end avoids it.

**2.** Student A: precise (readings agree closely) but not accurate (all about 0.012 kg too high, suggesting a systematic error) [1]. Student B: accurate (mean 1.000 kg equals the true value) but not precise (readings spread over 0.020 kg) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 0.01 ÷ 12.46 × 100 = **0.08 %** [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** **5.30 V** is anomalous; it should be left out of the mean (and checked or repeated if possible) [1].

**(b)** Mean = (4.82 + 4.79 + 4.85 + 4.80) ÷ 4 = 4.815, so **4.82 V** [1]. Uncertainty = (4.85 − 4.79) ÷ 2 = **0.03 V** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Extension = 18.9 − 12.4 = 6.5 cm, with uncertainty 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.2 cm, so **6.5 ± 0.2 cm** [1]. Percentage uncertainty = 0.2 ÷ 6.5 × 100 = **3.1 %** [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* For a difference, the absolute uncertainties add, and the small result makes the percentage uncertainty much larger than for either length alone.

**6.** ρ = 6 × 64.2 ÷ (π × 2.50³) = **7.85 g cm⁻³** [1]. % in m = 0.1 ÷ 64.2 × 100 = 0.16 % [1]. % in d³ = 3 × (0.02 ÷ 2.50 × 100) = 3 × 0.80 = 2.4 % [1]. Total = 2.56 %, so Δρ = 0.0256 × 7.85 = 0.20, giving **ρ = 7.85 ± 0.20 g cm⁻³** [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* The cubed diameter dominates; the mass uncertainty is almost negligible.

**7.** v = √(4.0 ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻³) = √2500 = **50.0 m s⁻¹** [1]. % in T = 2.5 % and % in μ = 3.1 % [1]. % in T/μ = 5.6 %, so % in v = ½ × 5.6 = 2.8 % [1]. Δv = 0.028 × 50.0 = 1.4, so **v = 50.0 ± 1.4 m s⁻¹** [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Combine the percentages for the division first, then halve for the square root.

**8. (a)** d = 0.38 × 10⁻³ m [1]. ρ = π × (0.38 × 10⁻³)² × 2.40 ÷ (4 × 0.800) = **3.4 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m** [1].

**(b)** % in R = 2.1 %, % in d² = 2 × 2.6 = 5.3 %, % in L = 0.25 % [1]. Total = **7.6 %** [1].

**(c)** The **diameter**: measure it with a micrometer at several points along the wire and in different directions, and use the mean [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* An improvement must target the largest contributor; a more precise ruler for L would hardly change the result.

**9.** One swing: 0.2 ÷ 1.9 × 100 = 11 % uncertainty in the period [1]. Ten swings take about 19 s, so 0.2 ÷ 19 × 100 = 1.1 % [1]. The same timing uncertainty is shared over ten periods, so the percentage uncertainty in the period is ten times smaller [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Resistance = gradient = 15.2 Ω [1]. Uncertainty = (15.9 − 14.6) ÷ 2 = 0.65, so **R = 15.2 ± 0.7 Ω** [1].

**(b)** 0.65 ÷ 15.2 × 100 = **4.3 %** [1].

**(c)** A zero error on the voltmeter (a systematic error that adds about 0.10 V to every reading) [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** (4.45 − 4.18) ÷ 4.18 × 100 = **6.5 %** [1].

**(b)** 6.5 % is larger than the 3 % uncertainty, so the result **does not agree** with the accepted value [1].

**(c)** Thermal energy is lost from the water and container to the surroundings [1]. The energy supplied is larger than the energy the water actually gains, so E/(mΔθ) gives a value that is too large [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* "Human error" earns nothing; name a specific systematic effect and link it to the direction of the error.

**12.** T = 4.0 × 5.0 = 20 ms = 0.020 s [1]. % uncertainty = 0.2 ÷ 4.0 × 100 = 5 %, and f = 1/T has the same percentage uncertainty [1]. f = 1 ÷ 0.020 = 50 Hz, Δf = 0.05 × 50 = 2.5 Hz, so **f = 50 ± 3 Hz** [1]. **[3]**

**13.** Readings that are close together are **precise**, not necessarily accurate [1]. They could all be shifted from the true value by a systematic error such as a zero error, so accuracy can only be judged by comparing with the true or accepted value [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing precision with accuracy, or claiming averaging removes a systematic error.
- Adding percentage uncertainties for a difference.
- Forgetting the power on a squared or cubed term, or doubling for a square root.
- Quoting more significant figures in the value than the uncertainty allows.
- Improving a measurement that is not the main source of uncertainty.
- Evaluating a result without comparing the percentage difference with the percentage uncertainty.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should an anomalous reading be included in the uncertainty?**
No. Identify it, leave it out of the mean and the half-range, and repeat the measurement if you can.

**Why does the uncertainty in 1/T have the same percentage as T?**
1/T is T raised to the power −1, and the rule multiplies the percentage uncertainty by the size of the power, which is 1.

**How many significant figures should an uncertainty have?**
One, or two if the first digit is small; then round the value to the same decimal place, as in 7.85 ± 0.20 g cm⁻³.
