> These are original practice questions written in the style of Cambridge O Level
> Geography (2217) assessment objectives. They are not taken from any past paper
> and are not endorsed by Cambridge International. Countries X and Y and the
> region of Dravana are fictional, and their data is illustrative. Where a
> question asks for a named example, use your own learned case study.

Use these questions alongside the [Water study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-water/)
and the [Water revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-water-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *aquifer*. **[1]**

**2.** State two methods of water supply, other than desalination,
that a rural village might use. **[2]**

**3.** Give one advantage and one disadvantage of desalination. **[2]**

**4.** Table 1 shows the percentage of water used by each sector in two
fictional countries.

| Country | Agriculture | Industry | Domestic |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | 88 | 4 | 8 |
| Y | 35 | 48 | 17 |

**(a)** Compare the use of water in Countries X and Y. Use figures. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest which country is more economically developed. Give a
reason. **[2]**
**(c)** Show the industry figure for Country Y as a pie chart angle. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Table 2 shows the depth of the water table below the surface at a
borehole in the fictional farming region of Dravana.

| Year | 2000 | 2008 | 2016 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Depth of water table (m) | 12 | 17 | 25 | 31 |

**(a)** Calculate the total fall in the water table between 2000 and
2024, and the average fall per year to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest two reasons for the change. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain two consequences for farmers in Dravana if the trend
continues. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why some areas suffer from water shortages even though
they receive high rainfall. **[4]**

**7.** A city's population is growing rapidly, and its reservoirs no
longer meet demand.

**(a)** Suggest two ways of increasing the city's water supply. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest two ways of reducing demand for water in the city. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why reducing demand may be more sustainable than only
increasing supply. **[2]**

**8.** Evaluate the building of a large dam and reservoir as a method of
water supply. **[6]**

**9.** For a named country or area, describe its methods of water
supply and explain how it manages water to secure future supplies. **[7]**

---

## Answers

**1.** A layer of rock that stores water and allows it to pass through,
from which groundwater can be pumped [1].

**2.** Any two of: wells; boreholes; taking water from a river or lake;
rainwater harvesting tanks; small dams or ponds [1 each].

**3.** Advantage: coastal areas have an almost unlimited source of raw
water that does not depend on rainfall [1]. Disadvantage: high cost and
energy use, or salty brine waste that can harm marine life [1].

**4. (a)** Country X uses far more of its water for agriculture (88%)
than Country Y (35%) [1]. Country Y uses a much larger share for
industry, 48% compared with 4%, twelve times more [1]. Domestic use is
also higher in Y (17%) than X (8%) [1].
**(b)** Country Y [1], because its large industrial share suggests an
industrialised economy, while X's water is dominated by agriculture,
typical of a low-income country [1].
**(c)** 48 x 3.6 = **172.8 degrees** [1].

**5. (a)** 31 - 12 = **19 m** [1]; 19 / 24 = **0.8 m per year** [1].
**(b)** Any two: groundwater pumped faster than it is recharged
(over-abstraction); more boreholes for irrigation; growing population
using more water; a series of drought years reducing recharge [1 each].
**(c)** Two consequences explained, 2 marks each, for example:
- Pumping costs rise [1] because water must be lifted from greater
  depth, so poorer farmers may be unable to afford irrigation [1].
- Shallow wells dry up [1], so crop yields fall or land is abandoned,
  reducing food supply and income [1].
- Salt water or polluted water may enter the aquifer [1], making the
  water unsuitable for crops or drinking [1].

**6.** Answers may include, up to 4 marks:
- Lack of money to build reservoirs, pipes and treatment works, so
  water is not delivered to homes (economic water scarcity) [1–2].
- Rainfall may be seasonal, so there is too much water in the wet season
  and too little in the dry season without storage [1–2].
- Rivers and groundwater may be polluted by sewage, farming or industry,
  so the water is unsafe [1–2].
- Rapid urban growth may outpace the building of supply systems [1].

**7. (a)** Any two of: build a new reservoir; build a desalination plant
(if coastal); recycle treated waste water; transfer water by pipeline
from another region; drill boreholes [1 each].
**(b)** Any two of: water meters and charges; fixing leaking pipes;
water-saving appliances and toilets; hosepipe bans or restrictions;
education campaigns [1 each].
**(c)** New supply schemes are expensive and can damage environments,
and demand may keep rising to use up the new water [1]; reducing demand
saves water, energy and money and leaves more in rivers and aquifers
for the future [1].

**8.** Levels-style marking.
- Benefits [up to 3]: stores large amounts of water for use through the
  dry season; reliable supply for cities and irrigation; can also
  generate HEP and control floods; reservoir may support fishing or
  tourism.
- Disadvantages [up to 3]: very high cost; villages and farmland
  flooded and people displaced; habitats lost; evaporation losses in hot
  climates; sediment trapped behind the dam; reduced flow for people
  downstream, which can cause disputes.
- Judgement [required for full marks]: for example, "a dam gives a large,
  reliable supply but is worthwhile only where the benefits to many
  people outweigh the cost and the harm to those displaced". No
  judgement: maximum 4.

**9.** Answers depend on the case study. Credit:
- Named country or area [1].
- Supply methods with specific detail [up to 3].
- Management to secure future supply, with explanation [up to 3].

Example using Singapore: PUB's Four National Taps of local catchment
water (including the Marina Reservoir created by the Marina Barrage in
2008), imported water from Johor under an agreement to 2061, NEWater
and desalination. Future security: NEWater and desalination reduce
reliance on imports and rainfall; pricing, a conservation tax and
campaigns reduce demand. A full answer notes the high energy and money
costs of the approach.
