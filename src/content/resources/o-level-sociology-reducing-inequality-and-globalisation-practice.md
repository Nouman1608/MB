## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *welfare state* **[2]**
- (ii) *remittances* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** ways NGOs try to improve education. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways a minimum wage may affect inequality. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways climate change may increase inequalities between societies. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the criticisms that have been made of NGOs. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that the welfare state has failed to reduce social inequality. **[14]**

### Extra practice

**(g)** Explain **two** effects of rapid urbanisation on life chances. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain the culture of poverty theory. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** ways colonialism helps explain global inequality. **[4]**

**(j)** Discuss the negative perceptions of migrants in receiving societies. **[8]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** A system in which the government provides services and payments — such as education,
healthcare, pensions and benefits — to protect citizens' welfare and life chances (2).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Money sent by migrants working abroad to their families in their home country (2).

**(b) [2]** Any two: building or running schools; training teachers; providing books or digital
equipment; scholarships; literacy classes for adults; encouraging girls' attendance.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- Raises the pay of the lowest-paid workers, narrowing the gap with higher earners.
- Reduces in-work poverty and reliance on benefits.
- May particularly help women and young people, who are concentrated in low-paid work.
- *Limitation credited as a way:* employers may cut jobs or hours, or the law may not be enforced in
  informal work, limiting its effect.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- Poorer countries often depend on agriculture, which is damaged by drought and floods.
- They have fewer resources for flood defences, relief and rebuilding (for example after the 2022
  Pakistan floods).
- Disasters destroy homes, schools and infrastructure, setting back development.
- Climate change can force people to migrate, sometimes into poverty.
- Richer countries that produced most emissions are better able to adapt.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 developed and evaluated; 4–6 some development; 1–3 basic.
- Dependence on foreign donors, whose priorities may not match local needs.
- May create **dependency** rather than self-reliance.
- Limited accountability to the people they serve.
- Small scale — cannot change structural causes such as unequal trade or land ownership.
- May weaken pressure on governments to provide services.
- *Balance:* NGOs such as the Edhi Foundation reach people the state does not; microfinance (Akhuwat)
  helps poor families build businesses.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with conclusion; 7–10 developed but less
balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the view:*
- Large gaps in wealth and income remain in societies with welfare states.
- Marxists: welfare is a safety valve that protects capitalism rather than redistributing wealth.
- New Right: welfare creates dependency and an underclass, trapping people in poverty.
- Feminists: welfare systems may assume male breadwinners and undervalue women's unpaid care.
- Many developing countries have limited welfare provision.

*Against the view:*
- Free education has increased literacy and social mobility (for example in Sri Lanka).
- Free healthcare improves life expectancy for poorer groups (for example Britain's NHS).
- Pensions and benefits reduce absolute poverty.
- Cash transfers such as the Benazir Income Support Programme reach poor women directly.

*Conclusion:* welfare has reduced poverty more than inequality; credit a reasoned judgement.

**(g) [4]** Two effects, 2 marks each: access to jobs, schools and hospitals improves for some;
informal settlements lack clean water and sanitation, harming health; overcrowding and pollution;
pressure on public services; insecure work in the informal economy.

**(h) [4]** Oscar Lewis argued that people living in long-term poverty develop distinctive values —
fatalism, living for the present, low aspirations (2) — which are passed on to children through
socialisation, keeping families poor across generations (2). Credit a criticism as development:
values may be a realistic response to poverty rather than its cause.

**(i) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: colonial powers extracted raw materials and wealth, leaving
economies underdeveloped; economies were organised around exporting a few products, creating
dependence; colonial borders and institutions left political instability; former colonies may still
trade on unequal terms with former colonial powers.

**(j) [8]** Levels as for (e).
- Migrants may be blamed for unemployment and low wages, although they often fill jobs local workers
  do not take.
- Concerns about pressure on housing, schools and health services.
- Media stereotyping and moral panics may link migrants to crime.
- Cultural differences in language, dress or religion may be seen as a threat, producing racism and
  discrimination.
- *Evaluation:* perceptions often exaggerate costs and ignore benefits such as tax paid and labour
  supplied; perceptions vary between societies and over time.

## Frequently asked questions

**What makes a strong (f) answer?** At least two developed arguments on each side, linked to
inequality, with a clear conclusion that answers "failed".

**Can I bring in 3.1 concepts such as life chances?** Yes — life chances help explain why welfare
and inequality matter.

**Should I evaluate in (c) and (d)?** Not required, but a brief qualification can strengthen the
development of a point.
