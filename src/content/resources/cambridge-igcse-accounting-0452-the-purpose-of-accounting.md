This guide covers sub-topic **1.1 The purpose of accounting**. The syllabus asks you to understand three things: "the difference between book-keeping and accounting", "the purposes of measuring business profit and loss" and "the role of accounting in providing information for monitoring progress and decision-making". Every example follows one invented business, Brightwater Kayak Hire, owned by Tamsin Okoro. Amounts are in dollars.

## 1. Where book-keeping ends and accounting begins

Every figure in the financial statements starts as one transaction, and the work done on it falls into two stages.

| Stage | What happens | Which part? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A source document is issued or received, such as an invoice or a receipt | Book-keeping |
| 2 | The transaction is entered in a book of prime entry | Book-keeping |
| 3 | Entries are posted to ledger accounts, and the accounts are balanced | Book-keeping |
| 4 | A trial balance checks the arithmetic of the double entry | Book-keeping |
| 5 | Year-end adjustments are made and financial statements are prepared | Accounting |
| 6 | The results are analysed, for example by comparison with last year | Accounting |
| 7 | The findings are explained to the owner, with advice | Accounting |

**Book-keeping** is the detailed, routine recording of financial transactions, following fixed rules. **Accounting** uses those records: it summarises them into financial statements, then analyses, interprets and communicates the results so that people can act on them. Book-keeping answers "what happened?"; accounting answers "what does it mean and what should be done?".

Two points earn marks that a bare definition misses:

- **Accounting depends on book-keeping.** If a sale is never recorded, or is posted to the wrong account, the statements and every comment based on them are wrong. Accurate records come first.
- **The difference is the task, not the person.** Tamsin keeps her own books in the quiet months and also prepares her own statements. In a larger business a book-keeper records and an accountant prepares and interprets, but a question about the difference wants the two types of work, not job titles.

**Classifying tasks.** These are six jobs at Brightwater in one week.

| Task | Book-keeping or accounting | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Entering 14 hire invoices in the sales journal | Book-keeping | Routine recording from source documents |
| Balancing the account of a hotel that hires on credit | Book-keeping | Arithmetic on recorded entries |
| Filing the counterfoils of paying-in slips | Book-keeping | Keeping the evidence for entries |
| Preparing the statement of profit or loss for the year | Accounting | Summarising the year's records |
| Working out that repairs rose from 6% to 9% of revenue | Accounting | Analysing the summarised figures |
| Advising Tamsin whether to replace six old kayaks | Accounting | Using the information for a decision |

## 2. Why profit and loss are measured

**Profit** is the amount by which the revenue earned in a period is greater than the expenses incurred in earning it. A **loss** arises when expenses are greater than revenue. Both are measured for a stated period, usually a year.

The syllabus asks for the *purposes* of this measurement. Each purpose below is a reason someone needs the figure.

1. **To judge performance.** Profit shows whether the owner's money and time have earned a return and whether the business is worth continuing.
2. **To set drawings.** An owner who takes out more than the profit reduces the capital of the business year after year.
3. **To support borrowing.** A lender asks for past profits as evidence that interest and repayments can be met.
4. **To work out tax.** Tax on a business's earnings is based on its profit.
5. **To compare.** Profit can be set against earlier periods or other businesses, which is the basis of monitoring progress (section 3).
6. **To share out the result.** In a partnership profit is divided between the partners, and in a limited company it is the source of dividends.

### Worked example 1: profit, not the bank balance

During 2027 Brightwater's bank balance rose from 3,200 to 12,700. Tamsin decides the year was excellent. The financial statements show revenue of 64,800 and expenses of 61,300. During the year the business received a bank loan of 12,000, and Tamsin took drawings of 9,000.

| Measure | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Rise in the bank balance | 12,700 - 3,200 | +9,500 |
| Bank change if the loan had not been received | 9,500 - 12,000 | -2,500 |
| Profit for the year | 64,800 - 61,300 | 3,500 |
| Drawings compared with profit | 9,000 - 3,500 | 5,500 more than profit |
| Change in owner's equity from profit and drawings | 3,500 - 9,000 | -5,500 |

The rise in the bank balance came from borrowed money that must be repaid. Profit is the measure of how the business performed, and it shows a small return on a year's work. Tamsin took 5,500 more than the business earned, so her equity in the business fell. Without the profit figure, she would have kept drawing at the same rate while the loan repayments began.

## 3. Monitoring progress

Monitoring means comparing results over time to see whether the business is improving, and noticing a problem early enough to act. Accounting supplies figures at regular intervals, for the same headings each time, so that like is compared with like.

### Worked example 2: a busier summer with less profit

| | Jul-Sep 2026 | Jul-Sep 2027 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hire revenue | 28,000 | 31,500 | +3,500 (+12.5%) |
| Wages | 9,800 | 12,600 | +2,800 (+28.6%) |
| Repairs | 1,680 | 2,835 | +1,155 (+68.8%) |
| Other expenses | 7,400 | 7,560 | +160 (+2.2%) |
| **Profit** | **9,120** | **8,505** | **-615 (-6.7%)** |

Revenue grew, so the summer felt busier, yet profit fell. The comparison shows why. Wages rose from 9,800 / 28,000 = 35% of revenue to 12,600 / 31,500 = 40%, and repairs from 1,680 / 28,000 = 6% to 2,835 / 31,500 = 9%. Other expenses barely moved. For every 100 of revenue the business kept 9,120 / 28,000 x 100 = 32.6 in 2026 but only 8,505 / 31,500 x 100 = 27.0 in 2027. Tamsin now knows which two costs to investigate, rather than simply feeling that the business is doing well.

## 4. Supporting decisions

A decision is a choice between alternatives. Accounting information lets the owner compare the alternatives in money terms before committing.

| Decision | Accounting information that helps |
|---|---|
| Setting hire prices | The cost of each hire and the profit margin |
| Repairing or replacing equipment | The repair cost trend against the cost of new equipment |
| Allowing hotels to hire on credit | Customer balances and how quickly they pay |
| Borrowing to expand | Past profits as evidence of the ability to repay |
| Employing another instructor | The effect of the extra wages on profit |

### Worked example 3: repair or replace

Brightwater's six oldest kayaks cost 1,900 to repair last year, and repairs are expected to be 2,400 this year. Six new kayaks would cost 7,200, last four years with no value at the end, and need about 300 of repairs a year. Spreading the cost evenly gives 7,200 / 4 = 1,800 a year (this is straight-line depreciation, covered in Topic 4).

| Option | Yearly cost |
|---|---|
| Keep the old kayaks | 2,400 |
| Buy new kayaks | 1,800 + 300 = 2,100 |
| Difference | 300 a year in favour of new kayaks |

On profit alone, replacing is better by 300 a year. But the decision also needs the cash information from Worked example 1: 7,200 must be paid now, while the bank balance depends on a loan. Accounting gives both figures, and Tamsin might replace three kayaks this year and three next. Customer safety, which cannot be measured in money, also favours replacement. The best answers use the figures and then weigh a factor the figures leave out.

## Common mistakes

- Defining book-keeping as "done by a book-keeper": describe the type of work.
- Treating a rise in the bank balance as a profit. Loans, capital introduced and sales of assets bring in cash without earning profit.
- Saying profit is measured "to know how much money the business has".
- Listing purposes when the question says **explain**. Each point needs a reason linked to the business.
- Forgetting that drawings are not an expense: they reduce owner's equity, not profit.

## Exam technique

The syllabus defines **state** as "express in clear terms", **explain** as "set out purposes or reasons / make the relationships between things clear / say why and/or how and support with relevant evidence", and **advise** as "write down a suggested course of action in a given situation".

**Annotated answer.** *Explain two purposes of measuring the profit of Brightwater Kayak Hire.*

> Profit shows Tamsin whether the business has earned a worthwhile return
> on her capital and time *(purpose 1)*, so she can decide whether to
> continue or change how it is run *(why it matters)*. It also tells her how
> much she can take as drawings; in 2027 she drew 9,000 against a profit of
> 3,500, reducing her equity by 5,500 *(purpose 2, supported with evidence)*.

Each purpose is stated, then developed with a reason or a figure from the case.

**For advise questions**, give a clear recommendation, support it with a calculation, state one point against it, then conclude. In Worked example 3 that means: replace the kayaks; it saves 300 a year; but 7,200 is needed now while the business relies on a loan; so replace them in two stages.
