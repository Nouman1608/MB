This guide goes deeper into **3.3.3.1 Nucleophilic substitution**, the first of the two subtopics in section 3.3.3 Halogenoalkanes of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2 (Organic 1 and Physical 1), and the synoptic Unit 5 paper can return to it. Mechanisms written here are the template for almost every later organic mechanism, so the marks you secure now pay off again at A2.

Condense it afterwards with the [nucleophilic substitution revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-3-1-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [nucleophilic substitution practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-3-1-practice/). The whole section, including elimination, is summarised in the [halogenoalkanes study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-halogenoalkanes/), and the companion subtopic is [elimination from halogenoalkanes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-3-2/).

## What the specification asks for

The specification states three facts and two skills for this subtopic:

- "Halogenoalkanes contain polar bonds."
- "Halogenoalkanes undergo substitution reactions with the nucleophiles OH⁻, CN⁻ and NH₃."
- You should be able to "outline the nucleophilic substitution mechanisms of these reactions" and "explain why the carbon–halogen bond enthalpy influences the rate of reaction".

That is the full scope. Nothing here asks you to compare primary, secondary and tertiary halogenoalkanes, and the further reaction of amines with halogenoalkanes belongs to International A2 (3.3.11.3). The curly-arrow rules you apply come from 3.3.1.2, covered in the [introduction to organic chemistry guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-introduction-to-organic-chemistry/).

## Why the carbon is attacked

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine are all more electronegative than carbon, so the bonding pair in C–X is pulled towards the halogen. The carbon becomes δ+ and the halogen δ−. Two consequences follow:

1. The δ+ carbon is **electron deficient**, so it attracts species that carry a lone pair.
2. The halogen can leave as a halide ion, X⁻, taking the bonding pair with it. A stable leaving group is what allows substitution rather than simple addition.

Alkanes have no such site: C–H bonds are almost non-polar, which is why alkanes react mainly by free-radical substitution (see the [alkanes guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-alkanes/)).

## The three nucleophiles

A **nucleophile** is an electron pair donor that attacks an electron-deficient atom. What matters in a mechanism is *which atom* carries the lone pair that attacks.

| Nucleophile | Attacking atom | Typical conditions | Organic product | Functional group made |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OH⁻ | O | Aqueous NaOH or KOH, warm (reflux) | Alcohol | –OH |
| CN⁻ | **C** | KCN in ethanol and water, heat under reflux | Nitrile | –C≡N (one extra carbon) |
| NH₃ | N | Excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube | Primary amine | –NH₂ |

Hydroxide and cyanide are negative ions; ammonia is neutral, so its first product is a positive ion that must lose H⁺. That is why the ammonia mechanism has a second stage.

## Outlining a mechanism: what earns the marks

The specification defines "outline mechanisms" as drawing the structures of the species involved and curly arrows to represent the movement of electron pairs. For a one-step substitution by OH⁻ or CN⁻, a full answer shows four things:

1. The **dipole** on C–X (δ+ on carbon, δ− on the halogen).
2. A **lone pair** on the attacking atom, and an arrow from that lone pair to the δ+ carbon.
3. An arrow from the **middle of the C–X bond** to the halogen.
4. The **products**: the organic product with the new bond, and X⁻.

### Worked example 1: cyanide and 2-bromobutane

*Outline the mechanism for the reaction of 2-bromobutane with cyanide ions. Name the organic product.*

```
              CH₃
              |
  :⁻C≡N  →   C(δ+) —— Br(δ−)        arrow 1: lone pair on C of CN⁻ → C2
              |    \                arrow 2: C–Br bond → Br
              H     CH₂CH₃
      ⟹   CH₃CH(CN)CH₂CH₃  +  :Br⁻
```

Annotated marking points:

- **Arrow 1** must start on the lone pair on the **carbon** of cyanide. An arrow from nitrogen would make an isonitrile, a different compound, and loses the mark.
- **Arrow 2** starts on the C–Br bond and ends on Br, not the other way round.
- **Product.** The nitrile carbon becomes carbon 1 of the new main chain: N≡C–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃ has a four-carbon chain with a methyl group on carbon 2, so the name is **2-methylbutanenitrile**. The reactant's chain did not lengthen at its end; the new carbon joined the middle carbon, which is why a branch appears.

Equation: CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₃ + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH(CN)CH₃ + Br⁻

### Worked example 2: ammonia and 1-bromopropane

*Outline the mechanism for the reaction of 1-bromopropane with excess ammonia.*

**Stage 1.** Arrow from the lone pair on N of NH₃ to the δ+ carbon; arrow from the C–Br bond to Br. Product: CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ and Br⁻.

**Stage 2.** A second NH₃ uses its lone pair to remove H⁺ from the N⁺ of the intermediate; arrow from the N–H bond back onto nitrogen. Products: **propylamine** (propan-1-amine), CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂, and NH₄⁺.

Overall: CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br

The positive charge in the intermediate must be drawn on nitrogen. A common lost mark is a charge left on carbon or missing altogether.

## Bond enthalpy decides the rate

Every one of these substitutions breaks the C–X bond. The halogen atoms get larger down Group 7, so the C–X bond gets longer, the shared pair is further from the halogen nucleus and the bond enthalpy falls. One set of mean bond enthalpy data gives:

| Bond | Mean bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ | Polarity |
|---|---|---|
| C–F | 467 | most polar |
| C–Cl | 346 | |
| C–Br | 290 | |
| C–I | 228 | least polar |

Data books disagree slightly, so always use the values printed in a question.

The two trends point in opposite directions. If polarity controlled the rate, fluoroalkanes would react fastest because their carbon is the most δ+. In practice iodoalkanes react fastest and fluoroalkanes hardly react at all. So the deciding factor is **bond enthalpy**: a weaker C–X bond gives a lower activation energy, a larger proportion of collisions have enough energy to react, and the rate is higher.

The order can be seen in a simple comparison that uses the halide test from Group 7 (see the [halogens guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-group-7-17-the-halogens/)). If 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane are each warmed with aqueous ethanolic silver nitrate, water slowly substitutes the halogen and the freed halide ion forms a precipitate. The yellow silver iodide precipitate appears first, then the cream silver bromide, and the white silver chloride last.

### Worked example 3: a yield calculation

*8.21 g of 2-bromobutane (Mr 136.9) is heated under reflux with excess potassium cyanide. 3.49 g of 2-methylbutanenitrile (Mr 83.0) is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield.*

```
n(2-bromobutane)            = 8.21 ÷ 136.9 = 0.0600 mol
theoretical n(nitrile)      = 0.0600 mol      (1 : 1)
actual n(nitrile)           = 3.49 ÷ 83.0  = 0.0420 mol
percentage yield            = 0.0420 ÷ 0.0600 × 100 = 70.1 %
```

(Carrying unrounded values gives 70.1 %.) More practice with moles is in the [amount of substance guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-amount-of-substance/).

## Why chemists use these reactions

Because the halogen is easy to replace, halogenoalkanes are a route into other functional groups. Substitution by cyanide is especially useful: it adds a carbon atom, so it is one of the few AS reactions that lengthens a carbon chain. Substitution by ammonia has a poor atom economy. For 1-bromopropane, the ammonium bromide by-product means only 59.0 of every 156.9 mass units of reactants end up in propylamine, an atom economy of 37.6 %.

## Common mistakes

- Starting the cyanide arrow at nitrogen instead of the carbon lone pair.
- Drawing the C–X arrow from the halogen to the bond.
- Leaving out the δ+ and δ− labels, or the lone pair on the nucleophile.
- Writing OH⁻ attacking a hydrogen; that is elimination (3.3.3.2), not substitution.
- Leaving out stage 2 of the ammonia mechanism, or showing only one NH₃ in the overall equation.
- Explaining the reactivity order by polarity or electronegativity instead of bond enthalpy.
- Naming a nitrile by counting only the original carbons.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Outline the mechanism**: structures, dipoles, lone pairs, curly arrows and products; no prose needed.
- **State** the reagent or conditions: give the reagent with its solvent (aqueous, ethanolic), because the solvent changes which reaction dominates.
- **Explain** the rate order: link weaker bond → less energy to break → lower activation energy → faster. Mention that polarity predicts the opposite order if the question invites a comparison.
- **Name** the product: number nitriles from the C≡N carbon.
- **Calculate**: show moles and the ratio as labelled steps; give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data.
