This guide covers section 3.1.1 Economic foundations of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Economics (9214) specification, for exams from May/June 2025 onwards. The specification introduces the section like this: students "look at the nature and purpose of economic activity based on the production of goods and services and the satisfaction of needs and wants", explore the factors of production, and come "to an understanding that there is always a cost attached to any economic choice."

The combined [choice, factors, markets and specialisation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-economics-needs-wants-factors-of-production-opportunity-cost/) gives the core definitions for 3.1.1 and 3.1.2 together. This page goes further on 3.1.1 alone: tricky classification cases, how the three economic groups interact, and how to weigh costs and benefits in an exam answer. All people, firms and figures are invented, and money is in unnamed currency units.

## 3.1.1.1 Needs, wants and the purpose of economic activity

The specification wants you to understand "the difference between a need and a want, and how these can change over time". Two forces move the line between them.

- **Rising incomes and living standards.** As a society becomes richer, things once seen as luxuries become part of a normal life. A refrigerator was a want for most households in many countries a few generations ago; today food safety and shopping habits make it close to a need.
- **Changes in how society is organised.** When banks, schools and governments move services online, a smartphone or a computer stops being optional for many people.

The direction can also reverse: a need in one place, such as heating in a cold climate, may be a want somewhere warm. In an answer, give the item, the reason it changed and the time or place.

The **central purpose of economic activity** is the production of goods and services to satisfy needs and wants. Production includes services, so a teacher or a bus driver is producing just as a factory worker is.

### The key economic decisions in practice

Every economy must decide **what to produce**, **how to produce** and **who is to benefit**. You can see all three in one small example. A town council has land on the edge of town.

- *What*: housing, a park or a factory?
- *How*: build with many local labourers, or with imported machinery and fewer workers?
- *Who benefits*: will the homes be sold at market prices to whoever can pay, or rented cheaply to low-income families?

Exam questions often ask which decision a situation is "mainly about". Look for the verb: choosing between products is *what*; choosing methods or resources is *how*; sharing out output or income is *who*.

## The main economic groups and their interactions

The three main groups are **consumers**, **producers** and **government**. The specification asks you to understand "the interactions between the main economic groups", so learn the flows in both directions.

| From | To | What flows |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Producers | Spending on goods and services; labour |
| Producers | Consumers | Goods and services; wages, rent, interest and profit as income |
| Consumers and producers | Government | Taxes |
| Government | Consumers | Public services, benefits and pensions |
| Government | Producers | Spending on goods and services it buys; rules and regulations |

### Worked example 1: one change, three groups

The government of Country K cuts the tax on household incomes. Follow the chain:

1. **Consumers** keep more of their income, so they spend more in shops.
2. **Producers** sell more, so they hire more workers and order more materials.
3. More people in work earn wages, so consumer spending rises again.
4. **Government** collects less income tax per person, but more people in work and higher spending bring in other tax revenue. It may still need to cut spending or borrow.

An answer that follows a change through all three groups shows the interaction the specification describes, rather than treating each group separately.

## 3.1.1.2 The nature of an economic resource

An **economic resource** is any input used to produce goods and services that is **limited in supply** and has **alternative uses**. Because it could be used in another way, using it has an opportunity cost. Sunlight is used by farmers but is not usually treated as scarce; fertile land is.

### Worked example 2: classifying difficult cases

| Item | Factor | Reward | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| A lake used to farm fish | Land | Rent | A natural resource |
| A delivery van | Capital | Interest | Made by people to help produce other things |
| Accounting software used by a firm | Capital | Interest | A man-made aid to production, even though it is not physical machinery |
| A chef employed in a restaurant | Labour | Wages | Human effort for an employer |
| The restaurant owner deciding to open a second branch and risking her savings | Enterprise | Profit | Organises the other factors and bears the risk |
| Money saved in a bank account | Not a factor | (Interest is paid on it, but it produces nothing until it buys capital) | Money is a means of paying for resources |

A self-employed plumber supplies both labour (doing the work) and enterprise (running the business and taking the risk), so their income includes both wages and profit. Saying so in an answer shows understanding, not confusion.

## 3.1.1.3 Making choices

The **basic economic problem** is that resources are limited while wants are unlimited, so every economic group must choose. The specification asks "how and why choices are made, and how costs and benefits can be weighed up".

- **Why** choices are made: scarcity means not every want can be met.
- **How** they are made: by comparing the benefits of each option with its costs, including the opportunity cost.

Consumers choose with limited income and time, producers with limited resources and finance, and governments with a limited budget.

### Worked example 3: a producer weighs up its options

Rahel has one field and three options for next year.

| Option | Revenue | Costs | Net benefit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grow vegetables | 9,000 | 5,500 | 3,500 |
| Run a summer campsite | 12,000 | 9,800 | 2,200 |
| Rent the field to a neighbour | 2,800 | 0 | 2,800 |

```
Best option on these figures: vegetables (net benefit 3,500)
Next best alternative: renting the field (2,800)
Opportunity cost of growing vegetables = 2,800, the benefit of renting
```

The campsite has the highest revenue but not the highest net benefit, which is why you subtract costs before choosing. Evaluation: the vegetable figure depends on the weather and on prices, while rent is fixed, so a cautious farmer might prefer renting. Wider costs and benefits matter too: the campsite might bring trade to the village but also traffic and litter. The specification encourages you to consider moral, ethical and sustainability issues like these.

### Opportunity cost in different contexts

- **Time.** A student who spends an evening working a shift gives up revision time, even though no money is spent.
- **Government.** Spending on defence means less for health or education with the same budget.
- **Producers.** A factory line making one model of phone cannot make another model at the same time.

Opportunity cost is always the **single next best alternative**, measured in what is given up, not in money spent.

## Exam technique

The specification assesses three objectives: AO1 knowledge and understanding of economic concepts, AO2 application to contexts, and AO3 analysis and evaluation leading to judgements and conclusions.

- **AO1**: precise definitions. "Capital is man-made goods used to produce other goods" scores; "capital is money" does not.
- **AO2**: use the case. Name the firm, use its figures and its situation.
- **AO3**: build chains with "so" and "this means", then weigh: how certain are the figures, who gains and who loses, what would change the decision?

## Common mistakes

- Giving a want that has become a need without explaining what changed.
- Calling money a factor of production.
- Forgetting that services are part of production.
- Choosing the option with the highest revenue instead of the highest net benefit.
- Adding up all the alternatives as the opportunity cost.
- Describing each economic group separately when the question asks how they interact.
