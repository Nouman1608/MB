## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term "limited liability". **[2]**

**2.** Define the term "dividend yield". **[2]**

**3.** Solway Homes plc has 18 million ordinary shares in issue, trading at 275p. It pays a total dividend of £990,000.

**(a)** Calculate its market capitalisation. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate its dividend per share. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate its dividend yield. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** difference between a worker co-operative and a consumer co-operative. **[4]**

**5.** Halden Engineering is borrowing £2 million from a bank to build a new factory. Explain how the bank, as a creditor, is affected by this decision. **[4]**

**6.** Define the term "external stakeholder". **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Twelve small dairy farmers in one valley each sell their milk separately to a large processor, which sets the price. They are considering forming a producer co-operative to process and sell their milk together. Analyse **two** benefits to the farmers of forming a producer co-operative. **[6]**

**8.** An investor is comparing two companies.

| | Brindle plc | Corvo plc |
|---|---|---|
| Share price | 180p | 640p |
| Dividend per share | 9p | 16p |
| Share price over the last three years | Fallen by 40% | Risen by 60% |

**(a)** Calculate the dividend yield of each company. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse why the investor might prefer Corvo plc despite its lower dividend yield. **[6]**

**9.** Hollis Joinery Ltd is owned entirely by its founder, who is retiring. Its 18 employees have offered to buy the business together and run it as a worker co-operative. A large rival has also offered to buy it. Assess whether the founder should sell the business to its employees as a worker co-operative. **[9]**

**10.** Varga Electronics plc plans to move its production from a town in the UK to a country with lower labour costs. Analyse the impact of this decision on **two** of its stakeholders. **[6]**

**11.** The share price of Tamar plc has fallen by 35% this year. Explain **one** reason why this fall matters to Tamar's directors. **[4]**

**12.** Kelda Supermarkets must cut its costs by £2 million a year. It has two options.

- Option A: close its two least profitable stores, with the loss of 120 jobs in towns where unemployment is already high.
- Option B: cut the prices it pays to its local farm suppliers by 4%. Many of these farms already make very small profits, and Kelda advertises itself as "proud to support local farmers".

Evaluate which option Kelda Supermarkets should choose. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Shareholders can lose only the amount they have invested in the company [1]; their personal possessions cannot be used to pay the company's debts, because the company is a separate legal entity [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The dividend per share as a percentage of the current share price [1], showing the annual return from dividends on the money needed to buy a share [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** 18,000,000 × £2.75 = **£49.5 million** [1].

**(b)** £990,000 ÷ 18,000,000 = **5.5p** [1].

**(c)** 5.5 × 100 ÷ 275 [1] = **2%** [1]. **[4]**

**4.** A worker co-operative is owned and run by its employees [1], whereas a consumer co-operative is owned by its customers [1]. In a worker co-operative the surplus is shared among staff, which can raise motivation [1]; in a consumer co-operative the surplus is returned to customer members, often in proportion to what they buy, or used to cut prices [1]. **[4]**

**5.** The bank gains interest on the £2 million loan [1], which adds to its income [1]. However, it takes on the risk that Halden cannot repay if the new factory does not generate enough sales [1], so the bank is likely to check Halden's plans carefully and may ask for the factory as security (also credit: the bank may set conditions on the loan) [1]. **[4]**

**6.** An individual or group outside the business [1] that is affected by, or has an interest in, its activities, such as customers, suppliers or the government [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Benefit 1: bargaining power [1]. At present each farmer sells alone, so the large processor can set a low price [1]; by selling together, the co-operative can negotiate a better price or sell to other buyers, raising each farmer's income [1]. Benefit 2: sharing the profit from processing [1]. If the co-operative processes the milk itself, the farmers keep the margin that the processor currently earns [1], and by sharing equipment and transport they gain economies of scale none could achieve alone, although they will need finance for the processing plant [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Brindle: 9 × 100 ÷ 180 = **5%** [1]. Corvo: 16 × 100 ÷ 640 = **2.5%** [1].

**(b)** Dividend yield measures only the income from dividends, not the change in the share price [1]. Corvo's shares have risen by 60% in three years, so an investor has gained much more from capital growth than from dividends [1]. Brindle's higher yield is partly because its share price has fallen by 40% [1], which suggests investors are worried about its future, so its dividend might be cut [1]. An investor seeking long-term growth in wealth may therefore prefer Corvo [1], although past price rises do not guarantee future ones, and an investor who needs regular income might still choose Brindle [1]. **[8]**

**9.** For: as owners, employees would share in the surplus, which could raise motivation and productivity [1]. They know the business, its customers and its methods well [1], so the business is likely to continue in a similar way, protecting the 18 jobs and the founder's legacy [1]. Against: the employees may not be able to raise as much money as the large rival can pay [1], so the founder may receive less for the business [1]. With one member one vote, decisions may be slow, and the staff may lack management experience [1]. Raising finance for growth later may also be harder [1]. Judgement: the founder should sell to the employees if their offer is close to the rival's and they can show a credible plan for management [1], because this protects jobs and the business's character; if the price gap is large and the founder needs the money, the rival's offer may be the better choice [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Employees in the UK town: they are likely to lose their jobs [1]. If the town has few similar employers, they may struggle to find new work, reducing their income and possibly forcing them to move [1]. Varga has an ethical responsibility to these staff, so it could reduce the harm through redundancy support or retraining [1]. Shareholders: lower labour costs should increase profit [1], which could raise dividends and the share price [1]. However, if customers or the media react badly to the job losses, or quality falls in the new factory, the expected gains may not appear [1]. **[6]**

**11.** A falling share price means Tamar's market capitalisation has fallen [1], making it cheaper for a rival to buy the company [1]. If a takeover happens, the directors may lose their jobs [1], so they are under pressure to improve results or explain their plans to win back investors' confidence (also credit: shareholders may vote against them; bonuses linked to share price; harder to raise new share capital) [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Option A, financial effects: closing the two weakest stores removes costs that bring in little profit [1], so it may achieve the £2 million saving with a lasting effect, and the remaining stores may win some of the closed stores' customers [1].

Option A, stakeholder effects: 120 employees lose their jobs in areas of high unemployment [1], so the impact on them and on the local communities is severe [1], and the closures could damage Kelda's reputation [1].

Option B, financial effects: the saving is achieved without closing stores, keeping revenue and staff [1].

Option B, stakeholder effects: many farms already make very small profits, so a 4% cut could force some out of business [1]. This contradicts Kelda's "proud to support local farmers" message [1], and customers who value local sourcing may switch to rivals if the cut becomes public; Kelda also risks losing reliable suppliers [1]. Ethically, it would be using its power over small suppliers to protect its own profit [1].

Judgement: the criteria are the lasting saving, the harm to stakeholders and the effect on Kelda's brand [1]. Kelda should choose Option A [1]: it fits the store network to demand and avoids contradicting its brand, while Option B's harm to farmers could undermine the very message customers buy into [1]. The decision depends on whether Kelda can reduce the harm of closures, for example by redeploying staff to other stores [1]; if not, it could look for a smaller cut to supplier prices combined with savings elsewhere [1]. **[15]**

*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs both options weighed against clear criteria, using the case details, with a judgement that says what it depends on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing pounds and pence in dividend yield calculations.
- Treating a high dividend yield as automatically better.
- Confusing worker, consumer and producer co-operatives.
- Classifying shareholders as external stakeholders.
- Naming stakeholders without analysing how the decision affects them.
- Evaluations that ignore ethical responsibilities or the business's own brand message.
