## B2.2.1 What counts as an organelle

An organelle is a **discrete** structure within a cell, with its own structure and a **specific function**. Most are bounded by membranes (nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, vesicles, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus); ribosomes are organelles without a membrane.

Three parts of a cell are usually *not* counted:

- **cell wall**: outside the plasma membrane, so not within the cell;
- **cytoskeleton**: a network spread through the cell rather than a discrete unit;
- **cytoplasm**: the whole contents, in which organelles sit.

**Nature of science: a technique opened the field.** Organelles became open to biochemical study when cell fractionation and ultracentrifugation were developed. Cells are broken open in a cold, buffered solution with the same solute concentration as the cell, and the homogenate is spun at increasing speeds. Larger, denser structures form a pellet at lower speeds.

### Worked example 1: reading fractionation data

Liver cells were homogenised and spun at increasing speeds. Each pellet was tested. Invented results:

| Pellet | Speed | DNA present | Enzyme of Krebs cycle (activity) | Lysosome enzyme (activity) | rRNA present |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Low | Yes | Low | Low | Low |
| 2 | Medium | Trace | High | Medium | Low |
| 3 | High | No | Low | Low | High |

1. Pellet 1 contains DNA and settles first: it is mainly **nuclei**, the largest organelles.
2. Pellet 2 has high Krebs cycle activity: **mitochondria** (lysosomes, similar in size, also settle here).
3. Pellet 3 is rich in rRNA and settles only at high speed: **ribosomes**, the smallest.
4. The trace of DNA in pellet 2 fits mitochondria, which contain their own DNA.

The cold solution slows enzymes that would digest organelles; the buffer prevents pH changes; equal solute concentration stops organelles gaining or losing water by osmosis.

## B2.2.2 Why the nucleus is a separate compartment

In eukaryotes, transcription happens in the nucleus and translation in the cytoplasm. Separating them means the newly made RNA can be modified, for example by removing introns, before it reaches a ribosome. In prokaryotes there is no nuclear membrane, so ribosomes can begin translating an mRNA while it is still being transcribed, leaving no opportunity for such modification first.

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

*Question (3 marks): Explain the advantage to a eukaryotic cell of having its DNA enclosed in a nucleus.*

> Transcription occurs inside the nucleus and translation occurs in the cytoplasm **[1]**. The primary mRNA can therefore be modified, for example by removal of introns, before translation begins **[1]**. In prokaryotes, ribosomes can attach to mRNA while it is still being transcribed, so this post-transcriptional modification cannot take place first **[1]**.

## B2.2.3 Compartments in the cytoplasm

Membranes create spaces where conditions differ from the rest of the cell. The advantages:

- **Concentration**: enzymes and substrates for one pathway are kept close together, so reactions are faster.
- **Different conditions**: a compartment can keep its own pH.
- **Protection**: damaging enzymes are kept away from the rest of the cell.

**Lysosomes and phagocytic vacuoles.** Lysosomes hold hydrolytic enzymes that would digest the cell's own proteins and membranes if released. When a cell engulfs a particle by endocytosis, the particle sits in a phagocytic vacuole. Lysosomes fuse with the vacuole and release enzymes into it, so digestion happens inside a membrane, and useful products pass out into the cytoplasm.

## B2.2.4 and B2.2.5 (HL only) Why small compartments matter

Both mitochondria and chloroplasts make ATP using a proton gradient across a membrane. A gradient builds faster in a small space.

### Worked example 3 (HL): volume and concentration

Suppose the same number of protons is pumped into two spaces (invented values): 6.0 × 10⁴ protons into a space of 2.0 × 10⁻³ µm³, or into a space of 2.0 × 10⁻² µm³.

1. Concentration = number ÷ volume.
2. Small space: 6.0 × 10⁴ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 3.0 × 10⁷ protons per µm³.
3. Large space: 6.0 × 10⁴ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻² = 3.0 × 10⁶ protons per µm³.
4. A space with one-tenth the volume reaches ten times the concentration for the same pumping, so a steep gradient forms quickly.

**Mitochondrion adaptations (B2.2.4)**

| Structure | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Double membrane | Inner membrane separates the matrix from a narrow intermembrane space |
| Small intermembrane space | Protons pumped into it quickly build a steep gradient |
| Cristae | Folds give a large inner membrane area for electron transport chains and ATP synthase |
| Matrix | Concentrates the enzymes and substrates of the link reaction and Krebs cycle |

**Chloroplast adaptations (B2.2.5)**

| Structure | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Thylakoid membranes | Large area for photosystems, electron carriers and ATP synthase |
| Small thylakoid space | Protons accumulate quickly, building the gradient |
| Pigments in photosystems | Arranged to absorb light and pass energy to reaction centres |
| Stroma | Concentrates the enzymes and substrates of the Calvin cycle |

## B2.2.6 to B2.2.9 (HL only) The nuclear envelope and the secretory route

**Nuclear envelope (B2.2.6).** Two membranes with pores. The pores control traffic: mRNA and ribosome subunits leave; proteins such as enzymes for transcription enter. During cell division the envelope breaks into vesicles, letting the spindle reach the chromosomes, and the vesicles later fuse to re-form the envelope.

**Ribosomes and rER (B2.2.7).** Free ribosomes in the cytosol make proteins for use within the cytoplasm. Ribosomes bound to the rough endoplasmic reticulum make proteins that enter the rER lumen, destined for secretion, for lysosomes or for membranes.

**Golgi apparatus (B2.2.8).** A stack of flattened membrane sacs (cisternae). Vesicles from the rER fuse with one face; proteins are modified, for example by adding carbohydrate, as they pass through; vesicles bud from the other face carrying them to their destination.

**Vesicles (B2.2.9).** Small membrane-bound sacs that carry materials between compartments and to the plasma membrane. The protein **clathrin** forms a cage-like coat that helps the membrane curve and pinch off into a vesicle, for example in endocytosis.

**The secretory route in order:** rER-bound ribosome → rER lumen → transport vesicle → Golgi apparatus → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane → exocytosis.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the cell wall or cytoplasm an organelle.
- Saying the nucleus "protects DNA" as the main advantage; the guide's point is separating transcription from translation.
- Describing cristae as increasing "space" rather than membrane surface area.
- Placing the Calvin cycle in the thylakoid space.
- Sending a secreted protein from the Golgi back to the rER.

## Exam technique for IB command terms

- **Identify**: name a fraction or organelle from data.
- **Explain**: structure, then the function it enables, for each adaptation.
- **Outline**: the secretory route in order.
- **Distinguish**: free versus bound ribosomes by site and destination of protein.
- **Deduce**: conclusions from fractionation or enzyme data.
