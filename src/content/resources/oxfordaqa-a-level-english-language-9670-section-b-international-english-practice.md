## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between the world Englishes and English as a lingua franca approaches to international English. **[4]**

**2.** Outline Kachru's three circles and give **two** criticisms of the model. **[5]**

**3.** Explain how Modiano's centripetal circles differ from Kachru's model. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what Jenkins's Lingua Franca Core proposes. **[3]**

**5.** Summarise the positions taken by Quirk and Kachru in their exchange about standards. **[4]**

## Section B

**Data** (original, invented). Part of a video call between engineers from three countries; none speaks English as a first language.

```
ANA:    so the delivery (.) it can arrive Monday isn't it
KENJI:  Monday (.) okay (.) you mean the first part or the whole
ANA:    the first part (.) the first part only
LUKAS:  okay so first part Monday (.) and rest of it (.) maybe one week later
KENJI:  one week later (.) okay (.) I write it in the document
```

**6.** Identify **three** features of the data that differ from standard British English, and explain why they do not prevent understanding. **[6]**

**7.** Analyse the accommodation strategies the speakers use to make the interaction succeed. **[6]**

**8.** How far does the data support the view that ELF is a successful form of communication in its own right? **[6]**

**9.** Explain how a supporter of a single international standard might interpret the same data. **[3]**

**10.** Explain, using Schneider's Dynamic Model, how attitudes to a postcolonial variety of English can change over time. **[5]**

**11.** Write a plan for an essay discussing the issue: *"English no longer belongs to its native speakers."* Include a thesis, three paragraph claims with evidence, a counter-argument and a conclusion. **[10]**

**12.** Explain why describing features of world Englishes as "errors" weakens an answer. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The **world Englishes** approach studies **national and regional varieties** with their own norms [1], tied to particular places and communities [1]. The **ELF** approach studies **communication between speakers with different first languages** [1], focusing on how interactions succeed rather than on a fixed variety [1].

**2.** **Inner circle**: English as the main first language; **outer circle**: institutional role after colonial history; **expanding circle**: foreign-language use [1] [1] [1]. Criticisms, any two: it is **based on nations rather than speakers** [1]; it keeps the **inner circle at the centre** as norm-provider [1]. Alternatively, it does not capture ELF use between expanding-circle speakers, or many countries contain speakers of every type.

**3.** Modiano places at the centre **speakers proficient in international communication** [1], **regardless of whether they are native speakers** [1], whereas Kachru's model is organised by **country and historical relationship** to English [1].

**4.** It identifies the **pronunciation features most important for intelligibility** [1] in communication **between non-native speakers** [1], suggesting that some native features, such as the "th" sounds, matter less and need not be teaching priorities [1].

**5.** **Quirk** argued for **a single standard** [1] as the basis for teaching English as a foreign language [1]. **Kachru** replied that **outer-circle varieties have their own legitimate norms** [1], so a single native standard should not be imposed [1].

**6.** Any three, two marks each. **Invariant tag "isn't it"** after "it can arrive Monday": a common ELF and world English feature; the rising question function is clear from context [1] [1]. **Article omission** ("rest of it", "first part"): meaning is recoverable because the referent is already established [1] [1]. **Present tense for future action** ("I write it in the document"): the immediate context shows the action is about to happen [1] [1]. Alternatively, "maybe one week later" as a hedge in place of a modal construction.

**7.** **Repetition** of key information ("Monday (.) okay", "one week later (.) okay") confirms understanding [1] [1]. **Clarification request** ("you mean the first part or the whole") prevents misunderstanding [1] [1]. **Summarising** ("okay so first part Monday... and rest of it") consolidates the agreement [1], and **confirmation of the action** ("I write it in the document") closes the exchange clearly [1].

**8.** Support: the speakers **reach a clear, shared decision** [1] using **accommodation strategies** rather than native norms [1] [1]. Qualifications: it is a **short, practical exchange**; more complex or emotional topics might strain shared resources [1], and the data cannot show whether **native-speaker participants** would understand as easily [1]. Judgement: the data support ELF as **effective for its purpose**, measured by mutual understanding rather than conformity to a standard [1].

**9.** They might treat the features as **deviations from a standard** that could cause problems in other contexts [1], argue that a **shared codified standard** would make communication more reliable across all situations [1], and point out that **assessment and many employers still reward standard forms** [1].

**10.** In the **exonormative stabilisation** phase, norms are taken from the external colonial standard, so local features are seen as errors [1] [1]. During **nativisation**, local vocabulary, pronunciation and grammar develop and spread [1]. In **endonormative stabilisation**, local norms become accepted as a standard in their own right, so attitudes shift from correction to acceptance [1] [1].

**11.** **Thesis**: English is now shared by its users worldwide, though native-speaker norms keep influence in some domains [1] [1]. **Claim 1**: non-native users are widely agreed to outnumber native speakers, and ELF interactions often have no native speaker present (Jenkins, Seidlhofer) [1] [1]. **Claim 2**: outer-circle varieties have their own norms (Kachru, Schneider's endonormative stabilisation) [1] [1]. **Claim 3**: Widdowson's argument that no group owns an international language [1]. **Counter-argument**: testing, publishing and much teaching still use native models; Quirk's case for a single standard [1] [1]. **Conclusion**: a judgement that ownership is shared, but power over norms remains unequal [1].

**12.** World English features are **systematic**, following the rules of a variety [1]. Calling them errors **assumes a single native standard**, which the topic's debates question [1], and so shows **a weaker understanding of concepts and issues** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating ELF data as a set of mistakes.
- Using only Kachru, with no alternative model.
- Writing a survey of the topic instead of arguing the issue.
- Ignoring the data provided with the question.
- Claiming precise speaker numbers without a source.
