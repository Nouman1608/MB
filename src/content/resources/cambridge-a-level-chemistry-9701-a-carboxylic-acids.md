This guide covers subtopic **33.1, Carboxylic acids**, from Topic 33, Carboxylic acids and derivatives, of
Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content, building on the AS
chemistry in [Carboxylic Acids and Esters](/resources/as-carboxylic-acids-and-esters/). The combined page
[Carboxylic Acids and Acyl Chlorides](/resources/a-carboxylic-acids-and-acyl-chlorides/) outlines each outcome.
This page adds what that overview leaves for you to work out: **balanced equations** for the three chlorinating
reagents and how to choose between them, **observations and equations** for the two acids that oxidise further,
a **titration calculation**, and a step-by-step method for **ranking acid strength**. Afterwards, use the
[carboxylic acids revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-carboxylic-acids-revision-notes/)
and the [carboxylic acids practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-carboxylic-acids-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

1. recall the production of **benzoic acid** from an alkylbenzene with **hot alkaline KMnO₄ and then dilute
   acid**, exemplified by methylbenzene;
2. describe the reaction of carboxylic acids with **PCl₃ and heat, PCl₅ or SOCl₂** to form acyl chlorides;
3. recognise that some carboxylic acids can be further oxidised: (a) **methanoic acid** with Fehling's reagent,
   Tollens' reagent, acidified KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, to CO₂ and H₂O; (b) **ethanedioic acid** with warm
   acidified KMnO₄, to CO₂;
4. describe and explain the relative acidities of **carboxylic acids, phenols and alcohols**;
5. describe and explain the relative acidities of **chlorine-substituted carboxylic acids**.

## Making benzoic acid

    C₆H₅CH₃ + 3[O] → C₆H₅COOH + H₂O

Under the alkaline conditions the product is present as the benzoate ion, C₆H₅COO⁻. The **dilute acid** step
protonates it, and benzoic acid, which is only slightly soluble in cold water, separates as a white solid. During
the oxidation, purple manganate(VII) is reduced to brown manganese(IV) oxide. Any alkyl side-chain is oxidised to
a single –COOH on the ring.

## Making acyl chlorides: three reagents

| Reagent | Conditions | Equation (ethanoic acid) | By-products |
|---|---|---|---|
| PCl₃ | heat | 3CH₃COOH + PCl₃ → 3CH₃COCl + H₃PO₃ | H₃PO₃ (non-volatile) |
| PCl₅ | room temperature | CH₃COOH + PCl₅ → CH₃COCl + POCl₃ + HCl | POCl₃ (liquid), HCl (gas) |
| SOCl₂ | room temperature or warm | CH₃COOH + SOCl₂ → CH₃COCl + SO₂ + HCl | SO₂ and HCl (both gases) |

In each reaction the –OH of the acid is replaced by –Cl. With PCl₅, steamy fumes of HCl are seen.

**Choosing a reagent.** SOCl₂ is often preferred because **both by-products are gases**, which leave the
mixture, so the acyl chloride is easier to separate. With PCl₅ the acyl chloride must be separated from
liquid POCl₃, usually by fractional distillation. All three reactions must be **dry**, because acyl chlorides,
PCl₃, PCl₅ and SOCl₂ all react with water.

**Balancing tip for PCl₃:** one PCl₃ supplies three Cl atoms, so it converts **three** molecules of acid.

## The two acids that oxidise further

Most carboxylic acids are not oxidised further by the usual oxidising agents. Two are exceptions, each for a
structural reason.

### Methanoic acid, HCOOH

Methanoic acid has an H atom bonded directly to the carbonyl carbon, the same feature as an aldehyde. It is
oxidised to carbon dioxide and water:

    HCOOH + [O] → CO₂ + H₂O

| Oxidising agent | Observation with methanoic acid |
|---|---|
| Tollens' reagent, warm | silver mirror (or grey-black precipitate) |
| Fehling's reagent, warm | blue solution → red-brown precipitate |
| acidified KMnO₄ | purple → colourless |
| acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, warm | orange → green |

With dichromate the full ionic equation is:

    3HCOOH + Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 8H⁺ → 3CO₂ + 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O

**Use in identification:** ethanoic acid gives none of these results, so Tollens' or Fehling's reagent
distinguishes methanoic acid from ethanoic acid.

### Ethanedioic acid, HOOC–COOH

Ethanedioic acid is oxidised by **warm** acidified KMnO₄ to carbon dioxide. The C–C bond between the two
carboxyl groups breaks.

    5HOOCCOOH + 2MnO₄⁻ + 6H⁺ → 10CO₂ + 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O

The purple colour of manganate(VII) disappears as Mn²⁺ forms, and effervescence of CO₂ is seen. Because MnO₄⁻ is
intensely coloured and Mn²⁺ is almost colourless, this reaction is used in titrations without an indicator.

## Worked example 1: a titration

*25.0 cm³ of a solution of ethanedioic acid, acidified and warmed, reacts exactly with 20.0 cm³ of
0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Calculate the concentration of the ethanedioic acid.*

    n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.0200 × 20.0 / 1000 = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol
    ratio HOOCCOOH : MnO₄⁻ = 5 : 2
    n(HOOCCOOH) = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ × 5/2 = 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol
    concentration = 1.00 × 10⁻³ / 0.0250 = 0.0400 mol dm⁻³

The end-point is the first permanent pale pink colour. The solution is warmed because the reaction is slow at
room temperature.

## Ranking acid strength: carboxylic acids, phenols and alcohols

**Order: carboxylic acid > phenol > alcohol.**

| Acid | Anion | Charge in the anion | Stability |
|---|---|---|---|
| ethanoic acid | CH₃COO⁻ | delocalised over **two O atoms** of equal electronegativity; both C–O bonds equal | most stable |
| phenol | C₆H₅O⁻ | delocalised from one O into the ring carbons, which are less electronegative | less stable |
| ethanol | CH₃CH₂O⁻ | localised on O, intensified by the electron-donating ethyl group | least stable |

Evidence: ethanoic acid gives CO₂ with sodium carbonate; phenol reacts with NaOH but not with carbonate; ethanol
reacts with neither.

## Chlorine-substituted acids

Chlorine is more electronegative than carbon. A C–Cl bond withdraws electron density through the σ bonds (an
**inductive effect**), which spreads out the negative charge of the carboxylate ion and stabilises it. A more
stable anion means a stronger acid. Two rules follow:

1. **More chlorine atoms → stronger acid:**
   CH₃COOH < ClCH₂COOH < Cl₂CHCOOH < Cl₃CCOOH
2. **Chlorine closer to –COOH → stronger acid:** the inductive effect weakens with distance along the chain.
   2-chlorobutanoic acid > 3-chlorobutanoic acid > 4-chlorobutanoic acid > butanoic acid.

## Worked example 2: ranking an unfamiliar set

*Rank in order of increasing acid strength: propanoic acid, 2-chloropropanoic acid, 2,2-dichloropropanoic acid,
3-chloropropanoic acid.*

**Step 1:** the unsubstituted acid is weakest: propanoic acid.

**Step 2:** among single-Cl acids, the closer Cl wins: 3-chloro is weaker than 2-chloro.

**Step 3:** two Cl atoms on carbon 2 have the largest effect.

**Answer:** propanoic acid < 3-chloropropanoic acid < 2-chloropropanoic acid < 2,2-dichloropropanoic acid.

If Ka or pKa data are given, a larger Ka (smaller pKa) means a stronger acid. Use the data, then explain with the
inductive effect.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting "then dilute acid"** in benzoic acid production.
- **Balancing PCl₃ 1 : 1.** It reacts with three acid molecules.
- **Saying all carboxylic acids resist oxidation.** Methanoic and ethanedioic acids are syllabus exceptions.
- **Missing "warm"** for ethanedioic acid with KMnO₄.
- **Explaining chloro-acid strength by delocalisation.** It is an inductive effect through σ bonds.
- **Explaining carboxylic acid strength without the two oxygen atoms**: the charge is shared by two
  electronegative atoms.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe** the reaction with PCl₃, PCl₅ or SOCl₂: reagent, condition, balanced equation, by-products.
- **Recognise / state** which acid can be oxidised: give the reagent, the observation and the products.
- **Explain** relative acidity: name the anion, describe where the charge is and why it is stabilised, then
  give the order.
- **Calculate**: write the ionic equation, the mole ratio and each step with units.
