These questions cover key question 2.2 of Cambridge O Level History 2147, "To what extent was the League of Nations a
success?", which was the Paper 2 prescribed topic for June 2025.

## Section A

**1.** Describe the work of the Council of the League of Nations. **[4]**

**2.** Identify **two** countries that left the League of Nations in the 1930s. **[2]**

**3.** Describe the dispute over the Aaland Islands. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the League's work for refugees and prisoners of war. **[4]**

**5.** Identify **two** areas of work of the International Labour Organisation. **[2]**

**6.** Describe the events of the Manchurian crisis, 1931–33. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Why did the League fail to settle the Corfu crisis of 1923 on its own terms? Explain your answer. **[6]**

**8.** Why did the Depression make the work of the League more difficult? Explain your answer. **[6]**

**9.** "The League of Nations was a success in the 1920s." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[10]**

**10.** "The Abyssinian crisis was more damaging to the League than the Manchurian crisis." How far do you agree?
Explain your answer. **[10]**

**11.** Study Source A, which is invented for practice.

*Source A (invented)*: from an imagined letter by a British official in Geneva to his wife, December 1935. "The
delegates here talk of nothing but the plan from Paris. For weeks we told small nations that the League would stand by
Abyssinia. Now it seems London and Paris were bargaining away half the country behind our backs. I have never been so
ashamed of a newspaper headline."

Why do you think the writer was ashamed in December 1935? Use the source and your knowledge to explain your answer.
**[6]**

**12.** Study Source B, which is invented for practice.

*Source B (invented)*: a description of an imaginary cartoon from 1932, written for this page. A Japanese soldier
stands on a map marked "Manchuria", holding a rifle. A small, elderly figure labelled "League of Nations" approaches
slowly, holding a large book titled "Report — Volume 1 of many" and a magnifying glass.

How useful is Source B as evidence about the League's response to the Manchurian crisis? Use the source and your
knowledge to explain your answer. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any four, 1 mark each: it met several times a year and in emergencies (1); it had permanent members, at first
Britain, France, Italy and Japan (1), and elected temporary members (1); it dealt with disputes between states (1); it
could recommend moral condemnation or economic sanctions (1); it could ask members to provide forces (1); each
permanent member had a veto (1). **[4]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: Japan (1933) (1); Germany (1933) (1); Italy (1937) (1). The USSR was expelled in 1939 and
may be credited if described as expelled (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any four, 1 mark each: the islands lie between Sweden and Finland (1); both countries claimed them (1); most
islanders spoke Swedish and wanted to join Sweden (1); the dispute was referred to the League (1); a commission
investigated (1); the League gave the islands to Finland (1); they were demilitarised (1); Swedish language and culture
were protected (1); Sweden accepted the decision (1). **[4]**

**4.** Any four, 1 mark each: Fridtjof Nansen led the work (1); hundreds of thousands of prisoners of war were returned
home after 1920 (1); the League organised camps and transport (1); it helped refugees from the Russian Civil War (1)
and from conflicts between Greece and Turkey (1); "Nansen passports" let stateless people travel and work (1). **[4]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: working hours (1); child labour (1); wages (1); conditions and safety for workers (1);
collecting information on labour conditions (1). **[2]**

**6.** Any four, 1 mark each: in September 1931 the Japanese army staged an explosion on the railway near Mukden (1);
Japan blamed China and occupied Manchuria (1); it set up the puppet state of Manchukuo under Puyi in 1932 (1); China
appealed to the League (1); the Lytton Commission investigated (1); it reported in 1932 that Japan was in the wrong (1);
Japan left the League in 1933 (1); no sanctions were imposed (1). **[4]**

**7.** Levels. Level 4 (6): explains two reasons. Level 3 (4–5): explains one reason; 5 for a developed explanation.
Level 2 (2–3): identifies or describes reasons. Level 1 (1): general comment. Indicative content: **Italy was a great
power and a permanent Council member**, so it could block action; **Mussolini refused to accept the League's
involvement** and pressed for the Conference of Ambassadors to decide; **Britain and France were unwilling** to
confront Italy, and the Conference made Greece apologise and pay compensation; **the murder of the Italian general**
gave Mussolini a pretext that some members accepted. **[6]**

**8.** Levels as Question 7. Indicative content: **economic sanctions became costly** when trade had already collapsed,
so members were reluctant to lose more; **aggressive governments gained support**, for example militarists in Japan,
who sought land and resources by force; **Britain and France cut spending** and would not pay for military action or
rearm quickly; **disarmament failed**, as the conference of 1932–34 collapsed and Germany left. **[6]**

**9.** Levels. Level 5 (9–10): explained arguments on both sides and a supported judgement. Level 4 (7–8): explains
both sides. Level 3 (4–6): explains one side. Level 2 (2–3): describes. Level 1 (1): general points. **Agree:** the
Aaland Islands and Bulgaria were settled peacefully; humanitarian work on refugees, health, slavery and labour helped
millions; Germany joined in 1926, a sign of growing confidence; no major war occurred. **Disagree:** Poland kept Vilna;
Mussolini got his way over Corfu, and the League was bypassed; successes involved only small states; the most
important agreements of the decade, such as Locarno (1925), were made outside the League. **Judgement:** successful
with small powers and in humanitarian work, but it never faced down a great power, which was the real test. **[10]**

**10.** Levels as Question 9. **Agree:** Abyssinia was a member, and the League openly tried sanctions and failed,
which showed sanctions did not work; the Hoare–Laval Plan showed Britain and France undermining the League; it pushed
Italy towards Hitler; it distracted from the Rhineland. **Disagree:** Manchuria came first and showed that a great
power could commit aggression unpunished, setting the precedent; Japan's departure began the collapse of membership;
the League's slow response in Manchuria encouraged Mussolini. **Judgement:** Manchuria exposed the weakness, but
Abyssinia was more damaging because it was a full test of collective security close to Europe, and it failed. **[10]**

**11.** Levels. Level 3 (5–6): explains the reason using the source and context. Level 2 (3–4): explains context or
the source's content. Level 1 (1–2): describes the source. Indicative content: the Hoare–Laval Plan, agreed by the
British and French foreign ministers in December 1935 and leaked to the press, proposed giving Italy much of Abyssinia;
the League had condemned Italy and imposed sanctions only weeks before, so the plan looked like a betrayal of a member
state; the writer, as a British official, felt his own government had undermined the League. **[6]**

**12.** Levels. Level 4 (7–8): evaluates usefulness through content, provenance and knowledge, with a judgement. Level
3 (5–6): evaluates content or provenance. Level 2 (2–4): uses content at face value. Level 1 (1): general comment.
**Useful:** it shows the League as slow and old ("Volume 1 of many") while Japan acts with force, which matches the
Lytton Commission taking about a year to report while Japan created Manchukuo; the soldier already standing on
Manchuria shows the League reacting after the event. **Limits:** it is a cartoonist's opinion, from an unknown country,
and dated 1932, before the report was adopted and Japan left in 1933; it does not show why the League was slow, such as
distance, the absence of the USA and the reluctance of the great powers. **Judgement:** useful for a critical view of
the League's slowness at the time, less so for the full response. **[8]**
