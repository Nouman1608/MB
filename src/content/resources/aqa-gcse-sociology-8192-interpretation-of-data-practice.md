These questions cover the "interpretation of data" row of section 3.7 in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification: interpreting graphs, diagrams, charts and tables to discern patterns and trends. Data items can appear in Paper 1 or Paper 2. All places and figures are fictional.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** The proportion of adults who say they attend a place of worship monthly falls from 40% to 30%. This is a fall of:

A. 10%
B. 10 percentage points
C. 25 percentage points
D. 30%

**[1]**

**2.** Which form of presentation is usually best for showing a trend over time?

A. Pie chart
B. Line graph
C. Single table of one year
D. Pictogram of one group

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "trend" in relation to data. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a sociologist comparing crime in two towns should use rates rather than numbers. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why two things rising together does not prove that one causes the other, using an example. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**6.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Northcote (a fictional borough): percentage of pupils achieving grade 5 or above in GCSE English, by sex**

| Year | Girls | Boys |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 62% | 48% |
| 2022 | 66% | 51% |
| 2025 | 68% | 55% |

**(a)** Calculate the gap between girls and boys in 2019, in percentage points. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe what happened to the gap between 2019 and 2025, using figures. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** thing these data cannot tell a sociologist about gender and achievement. **[2]**

**7.** Study the item. The data are fictional.

> Ashby district: 30,000 residents, 540 recorded thefts in 2025.
> Corley district: 12,000 residents, 300 recorded thefts in 2025.

**(a)** Calculate the recorded theft rate per 1,000 residents in each district. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Using your answers, explain which district had the bigger theft problem relative to its population. **[2]**

**8.** A survey asked 200 young people in Fenwick (a fictional town) about their main source of news: social media 92; television 48; news websites 36; printed newspapers 14; radio 10.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage who named social media. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain **one** reason why these data may not represent all young people in the UK. **[2]**

**9.** The annual incomes of seven households on one fictional street are: £14,000; £16,000; £18,000; £21,000; £24,000; £30,000; £150,000.

**(a)** Calculate the mean income. **[1]**
**(b)** State the median income. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain which average gives a better picture of a typical household on this street. **[2]**

**10.** A fictional newspaper prints a bar chart of school attendance in two schools: 94% and 96%. The vertical axis starts at 93%, so one bar looks three times as tall as the other. Explain how this chart could mislead readers. **[3]**

**11.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Westmere (a fictional city): household types as a percentage of all households**

| Household type | 2000 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Married couple with dependent children | 32% | 22% |
| Cohabiting couple with dependent children | 5% | 9% |
| Lone parent with dependent children | 8% | 11% |
| One person | 27% | 32% |
| Other households | 28% | 26% |

Using the table and your wider sociological knowledge, discuss how far these data show that family life has broken down. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far statistical data in graphs and tables can be trusted to show patterns and trends in society. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B. 10 percentage points [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* as a percentage change it would be a 25% fall (10 ÷ 40), so option A is wrong twice over.

**2.** B. Line graph [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* pie charts show shares at one time, not change.

**3.** The general direction in which data change over time [1], for example rising, falling or staying stable across several years [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "over time" is essential; without it, you have described a pattern.

**4.** Towns have different population sizes [1], so a larger town may record more crimes simply because more people live there [1]. A rate, such as crimes per 1,000 residents, takes population into account and allows a fair comparison [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* give the "per 1,000" idea explicitly for the final mark.

**5.** A third factor may cause both, or the link may be coincidence [1]. For example, ice cream sales and recorded street crime may both rise in summer [1], but warm weather, which brings more people outdoors, explains both, so ice cream does not cause crime [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the example must show why the link is not causal, not just state that it is not.

**6. (a)** 62 − 48 = 14 percentage points [1]
**(b)** The gap rose slightly from 14 points in 2019 to 15 points in 2022 (66 − 51) [1], then narrowed to 13 points in 2025 (68 − 55), so it stayed broadly similar while both groups improved [1].
**(c)** The data show the size of the gap but not why it exists [1], for example whether it is due to teacher labelling, subject choice or changes in girls' ambitions, which would need qualitative research [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* in (b) quote at least two gap figures; "it got smaller" with no numbers will not score fully.

**7. (a)** Ashby: 540 ÷ 30,000 × 1,000 = 18 per 1,000 [1]. Corley: 300 ÷ 12,000 × 1,000 = 25 per 1,000 [1].
**(b)** Ashby recorded more thefts in total, 540 compared with 300 [1], but Corley's rate of 25 per 1,000 is higher than Ashby's 18, so Corley had the bigger theft problem relative to its population, although both are recorded figures and may miss unreported thefts [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the question is built so that numbers and rates point in opposite directions; spotting that is the point.

**8. (a)** 92 ÷ 200 × 100 = 46% [1]
**(b)** The sample comes from one town [1], so young people in other areas, such as rural places with different internet access or local media, may have different news habits; 200 people is also a small sample for a national claim [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* name who might be missing and why their answers could differ.

**9. (a)** Total = £273,000; £273,000 ÷ 7 = £39,000 [1]
**(b)** £21,000 [1]
**(c)** The median gives a better picture [1], because one very high income of £150,000 pulls the mean up to £39,000, higher than six of the seven households earn [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* explain why the mean is distorted using the figures, not just "because of outliers".

**10.** Starting the axis at 93% instead of 0 exaggerates the difference between the schools [1]. The real difference is only 2 percentage points (96 − 94) [1], but the bar heights above 93% are 1 and 3 points, so one bar looks three times taller and readers may think one school's attendance is far worse [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* calculate the real gap; it shows you understand the data rather than just the chart.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Married couples with dependent children fell from 32% to 22%, a fall of 10 percentage points [1].
- Cohabiting couples with children rose from 5% to 9%, and lone-parent households from 8% to 11% [1].
- One-person households rose from 27% to 32%, the largest single group in 2025 [1].
- New Right writers might use these figures to argue that the traditional family is in decline [1].
- Murray linked rising numbers of births outside marriage to an emerging underclass [1].
- Against: cohabiting couples with children are still two-parent families, so the data show change in form, not necessarily breakdown [1].
- The rise in one-person households may reflect an ageing population and young people living alone before partnering, not family breakdown [1].
- The Rapoports' idea of family diversity suggests new forms reflect choice rather than decline [1].
- Percentages of households do not show the quality of relationships or how families function [1].
- The data cover one city at two points, so they cannot show what happened between 2000 and 2025 or across the UK [1].
- Definitions matter: "breakdown" is a value-laden interpretation of a change in household types [1].
- Judgement: the data show clear change in family forms, but not that family life has broken down; interpretation depends on perspective, and qualitative evidence would be needed [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* quote and calculate figures from the table throughout; extended answers on data items need data in them.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Graphs and tables summarise large amounts of data clearly, revealing patterns and trends [1].
- Large, regularly collected official statistics can show reliable national trends [1].
- Rates and percentages allow fair comparison between groups and years [1].
- But presentation can mislead, for example a truncated axis exaggerating small differences [1].
- Changes in definitions or recording practices can produce false trends [1].
- Small or unrepresentative samples make patterns unreliable [1].
- A correlation shown in a graph does not prove causation [1].
- Judgement: they can be trusted when the source, sample, definitions and presentation are checked, so trust depends on careful reading rather than the numbers themselves [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* good answers name the checks a sociologist should make, not just the problems.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing percentages and percentage points.
- Comparing numbers when rates are needed.
- Describing a table with no figures.
- Correct calculations with no working shown.
- Treating a correlation as a cause.
