**These notes condense the structural aspects of Option 1B:** "the structural patterning of the text
as it moves from disorder to order, incorporating rule and misrule, how competition between
characters is set up and resolved, how opposites are contrasted and reconciled", plus "the use of
complex plotting and sub-plots". The full explanation and a model paragraph are in the
[rule and misrule study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7717-rule-misrule-and-comic-resolution/);
for the option as a whole, see the
[Aspects of Comedy revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-1b-aspects-of-comedy-7717-revision-notes/).

## The comic pattern in one line

**Obstacle or broken order → period of misrule, confusion or competition → discovery → new order,
often marked by marriage.** The audience laughs at discomfort because the structure promises that
"all will end well".

## Five structural questions to ask of any comedy

1. What is the opening obstacle (a law, a parent, a disguise, a lie)?
2. Where and when is ordinary rule suspended, and who presides over the misrule?
3. Which competitions are set up, and what device resolves each one?
4. Which opposites are contrasted, and are all of them reconciled?
5. What does the final scene settle, and what does it leave open?

## Text by text

| Text | Misrule or confusion | Resolution device |
|---|---|---|
| *Twelfth Night* | Sir Toby's revels against Malvolio's rule; Viola's disguise | Sebastian's arrival turns one figure into two |
| *The Taming of the Shrew* | Disguised suitors in the Bianca plot | The final wager on obedient wives |
| *She Stoops to Conquer* | "The Mistakes of a Night": a house taken for an inn | Morning discovery; Tony learns he is of age |
| *The Importance of Being Earnest* | Bunburying and double identities | The handbag, Miss Prism, and Jack's real name |
| *Emma* | Emma's matchmaking; a secret engagement | Revelations that re-order three couples |
| "The Nun's Priest's Tale" | The fox's flattery and the chase | A counter-trick: the fox opens its mouth |
| *Small Island* | Four voices, "1948" and "Before" sections | Queenie's baby given to Hortense and Gilbert |

## Opposites worth naming

- **Town and country**: *Earnest* (Act 1 in town, Acts 2 and 3 in the country); *She Stoops to
  Conquer* (London manners in a country house).
- **Festivity and restraint**: Sir Toby and Malvolio, summed up in "cakes and ale".
- **Youth and age**: Tony and Constance against Mrs Hardcastle's plan to marry them to each other;
  Bianca's suitors against her father's rule that Katherina must marry first.
- **Melancholy and action**: Orsino and Olivia against Viola.
- **Nation, class and race**: Hortense, Gilbert, Queenie and Bernard in *Small Island*.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Misrule | A period in which normal authority is turned upside down |
| Lord of Misrule | Festive figure who presided over revels in some households and colleges |
| Festive comedy | Comedy shaped by holiday release followed by return to order |
| Comic resolution | The ending that unties the plot and settles relationships |
| Sub-plot | A second line of action, often a parallel love story or a farce |
| Parallel plot | Two plots that run side by side and comment on each other |
| Farce | Comedy of fast, improbable action and physical confusion |
| Deus ex machina | A sudden, contrived device that resolves the plot |
| Anagnorisis | Recognition or discovery; in comedy it usually brings joy |
| Symmetry | A structure in which one move is answered by a matching one |

## Useful critical idea

C. L. Barber described Shakespeare's festive comedies as moving "through release to clarification".
Use it to argue about structure, not as decoration: release (the revel, the trick) is followed by
clarification (discovery, marriage). Ask whether clarification is complete.

## Exam traps

- Retelling the confusions without explaining how they are arranged.
- Calling the ending "happy" without testing which opposites are reconciled.
- Treating sub-plots as padding rather than a comment on the main plot.
- Forgetting that comic suspense depends on the promise that all will end well.
- In Section A, not saying where the passage sits in the comic pattern.
- In Section C, describing each text's structure in turn instead of comparing patterns.

## Self-test

1. How does the direction of comic structure differ from AQA's description of tragic structure?
2. What is misrule?
3. What is Goldsmith's subtitle for *She Stoops to Conquer*, and what does it suggest about structure?
4. Which line from *Twelfth Night* sums up the conflict between festivity and restraint?
5. What contest ends *The Taming of the Shrew*?
6. Which concealed plot runs underneath the visible plot of *Emma*?
7. How is the competition in "The Nun's Priest's Tale" resolved?
8. What device resolves *The Importance of Being Earnest*?
9. How does Sebastian's arrival allow opposites to be reconciled in *Twelfth Night*?
10. How is *Small Island* structured?
11. Give Barber's phrase for the movement of festive comedy.
12. Name two questions to ask about a comic ending.

## Answers

1. Tragedy moves from order to disorder; comedy moves from disorder to order.
2. A period in which normal authority is suspended or turned upside down, borrowed from festive
   tradition.
3. "The Mistakes of a Night"; it signals a structure of temporary confusion confined to one night,
   resolved by morning.
4. Sir Toby's challenge to Malvolio about whether there shall be "no more cakes and ale".
5. A wager between husbands on whose wife will come when called.
6. The secret engagement of Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax.
7. By a counter-trick: Chauntecleer flatters the fox into opening its mouth, and escapes.
8. The handbag left at a railway station and Miss Prism's confession, which reveal Jack's identity and
   that his name is Ernest.
9. Viola's disguise has made one figure serve as two; the twin's arrival lets Olivia marry Sebastian
   and Orsino marry Viola.
10. It alternates sections set in 1948 with sections set "Before", narrated by Hortense, Gilbert,
    Queenie and Bernard.
11. "Through release to clarification".
12. Any two: what does it settle; what does it leave open; who is excluded; are all opposites
    reconciled; does fortune really smile on everyone.
