## The three outcomes

1. Explain why chemists aim for processes that **need no solvent** and use **non-hazardous starting
   materials**.
2. Explain why chemists aim for production methods with **fewer steps** and a **high percentage atom
   economy**.
3. Use reactions **in the specification** to devise a synthesis of **up to four steps**.

## Formulas

| Quantity | How to calculate it |
|---|---|
| % atom economy | (Mr of desired product ÷ sum of Mr of all reactants) × 100 |
| % yield | (actual amount of product ÷ maximum theoretical amount) × 100 |
| Overall yield of a route | Multiply the step yields as decimals, then × 100 |

## Why no solvent

- The solvent has to be made and transported, which uses energy and raw materials.
- It has to be separated from the product, often by distillation, which uses energy.
- It becomes waste unless it is recovered and recycled.
- Many organic solvents are flammable, toxic or volatile: fire risk, harm to workers, pollution.

## Why non-hazardous starting materials

- Toxic, corrosive or flammable chemicals need special storage, transport, protective equipment and
  emergency plans. All of these cost money.
- They put workers and the local area at risk. Removing the hazard at the start removes the risk.
- KCN (3.3.8) is highly toxic and can release HCN gas with acid.
- For aspirin, industry prefers ethanoic anhydride to ethanoyl chloride (3.3.9.2): it is cheaper, less
  corrosive, reacts less vigorously with water and gives ethanoic acid, not HCl gas.

## Why fewer steps and high atom economy

- Each step loses product, so overall yield falls with every extra step.
- Each step needs its own reagents, solvent, separation, energy and time.
- High atom economy means fewer reactant atoms become by-products, so less waste and better use of
  raw materials.

## Must-know distinctions

| Distinction | Key point |
|---|---|
| Atom economy vs yield | Atom economy comes from the equation. Yield comes from what you actually collect. |
| Addition vs substitution or elimination | Addition (one product): **100%** atom economy. Substitution and elimination: always below 100%. |
| Anhydride vs acyl chloride | The chloride can have the higher atom economy, but the anhydride is cheaper and safer. |
| NaBH₄ vs other reductions | NaBH₄ reduces aldehydes and ketones only. Nitriles and nitro compounds need other reducing agents. |
| Aldehyde vs acid from a primary alcohol | Distil off as it forms for the aldehyde; reflux with excess oxidant for the acid. |

## Reaction map by starting group

**Alkene**
- + HBr → bromoalkane (major product via the more stable carbocation)
- + Br₂ → dibromoalkane
- + steam, acid catalyst → alcohol
- → addition polymer

**Halogenoalkane**
- NaOH(aq), warm → alcohol
- KCN in aqueous ethanol, reflux → nitrile (+1 C)
- excess NH₃ in ethanol, sealed, heat → primary amine
- KOH in ethanol, hot, reflux → alkene
- + amine → secondary or tertiary amine, or quaternary ammonium salt

**Alcohol**
- concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄, heat → alkene
- primary: acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, distil → aldehyde; excess, reflux → carboxylic acid
- secondary: acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, reflux → ketone
- + carboxylic acid, acid catalyst → ester

**Aldehyde or ketone**
- NaBH₄(aq) → primary or secondary alcohol
- KCN then dilute acid → hydroxynitrile (+1 C)

**Carboxylic acid derivatives**
- Ester: hydrolysis in acid → acid + alcohol; in alkali → carboxylate salt + alcohol
- Acyl chloride or acid anhydride + water → acid; + alcohol → ester; + NH₃ → amide; + primary amine →
  N-substituted amide

**Nitrogen compounds and benzene**
- Nitrile → primary amine by reduction (for example LiAlH₄, or H₂ with Ni)
- Benzene + concentrated HNO₃ and H₂SO₄ → nitrobenzene
- Nitrobenzene → phenylamine by reduction (for example Sn and concentrated HCl, then NaOH)
- Benzene + acyl chloride, AlCl₃ catalyst → phenyl ketone

The specification does not name the reducing agents for nitriles or nitro compounds; those above are
standard choices.

**New C–C bonds:** only three reactions make one. KCN + halogenoalkane, KCN + aldehyde or ketone, and
Friedel–Crafts acylation.

## Method in steps

```
1  Draw start and target. List functional groups. Count carbons.
2  Carbon count changed? Pick a C–C forming reaction.
3  Work backwards one step at a time from the target.
4  Check where the group ends up (major product?).
5  For every step: reagent + conditions + intermediate name or structure.
6  Count the steps (four at most) and check each reaction is in the spec.
```

## Worked reminder: splitting the starting material

Make ethyl ethanoate using bromoethane as the only organic starting material.

```
Step 1  CH₃CH₂Br + NaOH → CH₃CH₂OH + NaBr         NaOH(aq), warm
Step 2  Oxidise part of the ethanol:
        CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O            excess acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, reflux
Step 3  CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O    conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst, heat
```

Both halves of the ester come from the same starting compound. Three steps.

## Evaluating a process: a checklist

When a question asks you to compare two routes or evaluate a process, test each route against the same
points and say which route wins on each:

- **Number of steps.** Fewer steps usually means a higher overall yield and less time and energy.
- **Atom economy.** Calculate it from the balanced equation for each route. Higher means less waste.
- **Yield.** A route with a lower atom economy can still be better if its yield is much higher.
- **Solvent.** Does the route need one? Can it be recycled?
- **Hazards.** Are any reagents toxic, corrosive or flammable (KCN, acyl chlorides, concentrated acids)?
- **By-products.** Can they be sold or used, or are they waste? A useful by-product reduces the cost of a
  low atom economy.
- **Conditions.** High temperature or pressure costs energy and equipment.

Finish with a judgement that names the route you prefer and the deciding reason. A list of points with no
judgement does not answer "evaluate".

## Quick self-test

1. Give two reasons why chemists prefer a process with no solvent.
2. Calculate the % atom economy for making ethyl ethanoate from ethanoic acid and ethanol. (Ar: H = 1.0,
   C = 12.0, O = 16.0)
3. State the % atom economy for ethanal + HCN → CH₃CH(OH)CN.
4. A three-step route has step yields of 90%, 80% and 50%. Calculate the overall yield.
5. Name the three reactions in the specification that form a new C–C bond.
6. Give the reagent and conditions to make butanoic acid from butan-1-ol.
7. Outline a two-step route from 1-bromobutane to butan-2-ol.
8. Name the organic product of ethanoyl chloride with methanol.
9. Give the reagents for a two-step synthesis of phenylamine from benzene.
10. Explain why ethanal with KCN, then dilute acid, gives an optically inactive product.
11. Give two reasons why a company might choose a route with the lower atom economy.
12. Outline a two-step route from propan-1-ol to 2-hydroxybutanenitrile.

### Answers

1. Energy is needed to remove the solvent; it becomes waste unless recycled; many are flammable or toxic.
   (Any two.)
2. Mr: ethanoic acid 60.0, ethanol 46.0, ethyl ethanoate 88.0. 88.0 ÷ 106.0 × 100 = **83.0%**.
3. **100%**: addition gives a single product.
4. 0.90 × 0.80 × 0.50 = 0.36, so **36.0%**.
5. KCN with a halogenoalkane; KCN with an aldehyde or ketone; Friedel–Crafts acylation.
6. **Excess** acidified potassium dichromate(VI), heat under **reflux**.
7. KOH in ethanol, hot, reflux → but-1-ene; then steam with an acid catalyst → butan-2-ol (major product).
8. **Methyl ethanoate** (with HCl).
9. Concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄ → nitrobenzene; then Sn and concentrated HCl, followed by NaOH.
10. The C=O group is planar. CN⁻ attacks from either side with equal probability, so equal amounts of the
    two enantiomers form: a **racemic mixture**, which is optically inactive.
11. Any two: its yield is higher; it has fewer steps; its reagents are cheaper or less hazardous; it needs no
    solvent; its by-product can be sold.
12. Acidified potassium dichromate(VI), **distil off** the propanal as it forms; then KCN followed by dilute
    acid → CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CN. The chain grows from 3 to 4 carbons in step 2.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding or averaging step yields instead of multiplying them.
- Missing a reactant or a coefficient (such as 2NH₃) from the atom economy denominator.
- Calling an addition reaction "high" atom economy instead of exactly 100%.
- Using a conversion that is not in the specification, such as alcohol straight to halogenoalkane.
- Giving a reagent with no conditions ("KCN" with no solvent or reflux; "K₂Cr₂O₇" with no acid).
- Choosing NaBH₄ to reduce a nitrile or a nitro compound.
- Ignoring the major and minor products when HBr or steam adds to an unsymmetrical alkene.
- Using ammonia when the chain must grow by one carbon: that needs KCN first.
- Answering "why no solvent?" with "it is cheaper" and no reason why.
