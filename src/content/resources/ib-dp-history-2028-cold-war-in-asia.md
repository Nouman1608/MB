This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. The Cold War in Asia (1949 to 2002) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. It covers three conflicts in turn: the Korean War, the war in Vietnam, and the conflict in Afghanistan, with the causes and the effects of each.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-cold-war-in-asia-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-cold-war-in-asia-practice/).

## Reasons for the Korean War (1950 to 1953)

**Division of Korea.** After Japan's surrender in 1945, American and Soviet forces occupied the south and north of Korea, divided at the 38th parallel. Talks on a united government failed. The USA took the issue to the United Nations, which supervised elections in the south in May 1948. The Soviet Union refused entry to the UN commission in the north. The Republic of Korea (south) was proclaimed in August 1948 and the Democratic People's Republic (north) in September. Both claimed the whole country, and border clashes were frequent.

**Leaders.** *Syngman Rhee*, a strong anti-communist nationalist, ruled in the south as an authoritarian president and spoke of reunifying Korea by force. *Kim Il Sung* in the north, backed by the Soviet Union, built a one-party state and an army with Soviet weapons and experienced fighters returned from China. Both leaders pressed their allies for support.

**The great powers.** Kim Il Sung gained Stalin's approval for an attack in 1950 (after he argued that victory would be quick) and Mao Zedong's agreement. Stalin hoped to gain from the removal of an American-aligned south, and the Soviet Union had its first atomic bomb (1949). The USA's Secretary of State Dean Acheson's speech in January 1950 did not mention Korea in the American defence line, which some read as a signal. Mao, who had just won the Chinese Civil War, saw support for fellow communists as an obligation, and the war also served China's security.

**Invasion and UN response.** North Korea invaded on 25 June 1950. President Truman saw the attack as a test of containment. The UN Security Council, which the Soviet Union was boycotting over the question of China's seat, condemned the attack and authorised a force under American command. Sixteen states sent combat troops.

## The course and impact of the Korean War (to 1963)

**The war.** North Korean forces drove south to the Pusan perimeter. General MacArthur's landing at Inchon in September 1950 reversed this, and UN forces advanced beyond the 38th parallel towards the Yalu River. China then intervened in October to November 1950, as "volunteers", and pushed the UN forces back. Truman dismissed MacArthur in April 1951 after he publicly challenged policy. The fighting settled near the 38th parallel while talks dragged on, partly over the repatriation of prisoners.

**Political impact.** The Armistice of 27 July 1953 created a **demilitarised zone** (DMZ) near the 38th parallel. No peace treaty was signed. South Korea under Rhee became more authoritarian, until protests ended his rule in 1960; General Park Chung-hee seized power in 1961. In the north Kim Il Sung removed rivals and developed the ideology of *juche* (self-reliance).

**Social impact.** Millions of Koreans died, probably over two million civilians and soldiers in total (estimates vary). Families were divided, and millions of people became refugees. Bombing flattened much of the north.

**Economic and environmental destruction.** Industry, towns and farmland were devastated, especially in the north. Reconstruction was driven by Soviet and Chinese aid in the north, and American aid in the south. The south started rapid growth in the 1960s.

**Regional effects.** China gained prestige by fighting the USA to a standstill, but suffered heavy losses and a trade embargo, and the US Seventh Fleet protected Taiwan. For Japan, the war was a boon: American purchases boosted industry, and the occupation ended in 1952 with a security treaty that kept US bases.

## Reasons for conflict in Vietnam (1954 to 1975)

**Legacy of colonialism and the Japanese occupation.** France ruled Indochina from the nineteenth century. During the Second World War Japan occupied it, and a famine in 1944 to 1945 killed hundreds of thousands. The Viet Minh under Hồ Chí Minh declared independence in September 1945. France tried to restore control, and the First Indochina War (1946 to 1954) followed.

**Điện Biên Phủ and Geneva.** The Viet Minh under General Võ Nguyên Giáp besieged and defeated the French garrison at Điện Biên Phủ in May 1954. The **Geneva Accords** (July 1954) split Vietnam temporarily at the 17th parallel, with the Viet Minh in the north and a non-communist state in the south, and promised national elections in 1956. The elections were never held.

**Leaders and ideology.** *Hồ Chí Minh*, a nationalist and communist who had spent years abroad, led the Democratic Republic in the north with a programme of land reform and unification. *Ngô Đình Diệm*, a Catholic, anti-communist and anti-French nationalist, became prime minister in the south in 1954 and founded the Republic of Vietnam in 1955 with American backing. His regime favoured Catholics and his family, repressed opponents and struggled to win the peasants. The 1963 Buddhist crisis and his overthrow, with American approval, in November 1963 left the south unstable.

**Great powers.** The USA saw Vietnam through the "domino theory" and gave aid to France, then to Diệm, and sent advisers. After the Gulf of Tonkin incident (August 1964), Congress gave President Johnson wide powers. The bombing campaign Rolling Thunder began in 1965, and American ground combat troops arrived that March, rising to around 540,000 in 1969. The Soviet Union and China supplied the north with weapons, aid and, in the case of China, engineers and labour, though their rivalry limited cooperation.

## Impact of the Vietnam conflict (to 1985)

**Tactics.** The United States used "search and destroy" missions, heavy bombing (including Linebacker raids in 1972), defoliants such as Agent Orange, and napalm. These did much damage to the countryside and civilians. The **Vietcong** and North Vietnamese used guerrilla warfare: tunnels, ambushes, booby traps and the Hồ Chí Minh Trail supply route through Laos and Cambodia, and mounted the large Tet Offensive of January 1968. Tet failed militarily but shocked the American public.

**Political impact.** Opposition to the war grew in the USA. President Nixon pursued "Vietnamization", handing the fighting to the South Vietnamese army, and signed the Paris Peace Accords in January 1973. American troops left, but the fighting continued. In April 1975 the north conquered Saigon. Vietnam was reunified as the Socialist Republic in 1976.

**Economic and environmental destruction.** Perhaps two million Vietnamese died, along with some 58,000 Americans. Cities, bridges and farmland were wrecked, and large areas were contaminated by chemicals and unexploded bombs. After 1975 the new government's collectivisation caused hardship, and hundreds of thousands fled as "boat people". Vietnam fought a short war with China in 1979 after invading Cambodia in 1978.

**Destabilisation of Cambodia.** Prince Sihanouk tried to keep Cambodia neutral, but North Vietnamese used its border areas. The USA bombed these in secret from 1969, and a coup in 1970 brought Lon Nol to power. Civil war followed, and in April 1975 the communist **Khmer Rouge** took Phnom Penh. Their rule from 1975 to 1979 killed some 1.5 to 2 million people.

## Reasons for conflict in Afghanistan (1979 to 1992)

**Tensions.** Afghanistan was a poor, mountainous and diverse society of Pashtuns, Tajiks, Uzbeks, Hazaras and others, with strong tribal and religious institutions and a weak central state. Reforms from Kabul were seen as threats to local power.

**The Saur Revolution.** In April 1978 officers loyal to the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan (PDPA) killed the president, Daoud, and took power. The PDPA was pro-Soviet and divided between rival factions (Taraki and Amin, and Karmal). They announced land reform, women's education and secular policies, and used force against opponents. Rebellions began, such as that in Herat in March 1979.

**The mujahideen.** Rebel groups, motivated by Islam and local loyalties, formed. Pakistan, under General Zia ul-Haq, hosted them, along with millions of refugees. The USA, Saudi Arabia and China provided money and weapons, including American Stinger missiles from 1986. In December 1979, after Amin killed Taraki and seemed unreliable, the Soviet Union sent troops, killed Amin, and installed Karmal.

**Great powers.** The USSR wanted to keep a friendly government on its border. The USA saw a chance to hurt the Soviet Union. Historians debate whether Soviet leaders saw the decision as defensive.

## Impact of the conflict in Afghanistan (to 2002)

**The Soviet invasion.** Soviet forces held cities and roads but could not control the countryside. They used helicopters, bombing and destruction of villages. The mujahideen fought guerrilla war from the mountains. About a million Afghans died and five to six million became refugees.

**Economic and environmental destruction.** Irrigation, farms and villages were destroyed, and landmines still cover large areas.

**Withdrawal and civil war.** Gorbachev decided to leave. The Geneva Accords (1988) led to Soviet withdrawal in February 1989. President Najibullah's government survived until 1992, then fell, and the mujahideen groups fought one another for Kabul, with ethnic and tribal divisions.

**The Taliban.** The Taliban, students of religious schools, emerged in Kandahar in 1994 under Mullah Omar, and captured Kabul in 1996. They imposed a strict version of Sharia: women were barred from school and most work, music and images were banned, and punishments were public. The Northern Alliance under Massoud resisted. After the attacks of 11 September 2001, a US-led coalition removed the Taliban from power by December 2001.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Korea: traditionalist views blame Stalin and the Communist bloc, while Bruce Cumings sees a civil war with roots in Korean conditions before 1950. Soviet and Chinese archives opened after 1991 (Kathryn Weathersby, Chen Jian) show the roles of Stalin and Mao.
- Vietnam: the orthodox view saw a necessary defence of the south; revisionists (such as Marilyn Young) criticised American policy; Fredrik Logevall stresses the choices of American leaders.
- Afghanistan: Odd Arne Westad on Soviet decision-making; Steve Coll on American involvement and its effects.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent were the actions of the great powers the main reason for the outbreak of the Korean War?"** Weigh Stalin's and Mao's approval and American signals against Korean factors: the division, Kim and Rhee, and the civil conflict. Judge that the great powers made the war possible but the Korean leaders' wish to unify the country made it likely.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the Cold War conflicts as only a US–Soviet contest and ignoring local causes.
- Confusing the dates and terms of the Geneva Accords.
- Missing Cambodia in a question on the effects of the Vietnam conflict.
