This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. The emergence of independent states in Southeast Asia (1900 to 1990) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. The guide says that every examination question will require you to refer to **one** state from Southeast Asia. This guide uses **Indonesia** as its main case, with Vietnam, Burma, the Philippines and Malaya as shorter comparisons, so you can adapt the points to your own state.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-southeast-asia-independent-states-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-southeast-asia-independent-states-practice/).

## Social and economic impact of early colonialism

**Political.** In the Dutch East Indies the Dutch ruled through local aristocrats and used an army with soldiers from particular groups. They favoured some communities against others, and relied on local rulers' customary law (*adat*), a "divide and rule" approach that kept the archipelago's many peoples apart. In Indochina the French ruled Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos as separate units with different statuses, and the British in Burma used minority groups such as the Karen in the army.

**Economic.** Colonial economies were built around export crops and minerals: sugar, coffee, rubber, tin and oil in the Indies, rubber and tin in Malaya, rice in Burma and Cochinchina. Profits flowed to European firms, and prices swung with world markets. The Dutch Cultivation System (1830 to 1870) forced villages to grow export crops. In the 1930s Depression, falling prices caused hardship.

**Social.** Societies were ranked by race: Europeans at the top, then "Foreign Orientals" such as Chinese and Indians who worked as traders and labourers, then local peoples. Plantation work moved people, and in some areas settlers and plantations took land. Mobility created a new educated class.

**Cultural.** Missionaries and colonial schools challenged traditional beliefs. Islam, Buddhism and local religions adapted, and reform movements grew in response. Printing and newspapers spread in the 1900s.

## Origins of nationalism and independence movements

Use the four categories in the guide: ideas, economics, politics and shared identity.

- **Ideas.** Liberalism and ideas of self-determination reached students educated in the colony or abroad (Sukarno studied in Bandung and Hatta in the Netherlands). Islamic reform and Marxism offered other languages of protest. Japan's defeat of Russia in 1905 showed that an Asian state could beat a European power.
- **Economic factors.** Colonial exploitation, peasant poverty and the Depression created grievances. Indigenous traders saw Chinese and European competition.
- **Political factors.** Oppressive rule, such as the arrest of leaders, press controls and refusal to allow real representation, turned moderates into opponents. The Dutch rejected concessions even in the 1930s.
- **Shared identity.** A common language (Malay, which became Indonesian), religion (Islam) and the colonial boundaries themselves helped to make people think of one nation. In the Youth Pledge (1928) Indonesian youth declared "one nation, one homeland, one language".

## Growth of nationalist movements (from 1914)

**Indonesia.** *Budi Utomo* (1908) began as a cultural society for educated Javanese. *Sarekat Islam* (1912) was the first mass movement. The Indonesian Communist Party (PKI, 1920) staged revolts in 1926 to 1927, which the Dutch crushed. The Indonesian National Party (PNI), led by **Sukarno** from 1927, called for independence by mass action. Sukarno was arrested in 1929 and later exiled. **Mohammad Hatta** worked through political education.

**External influences.** The First World War and the principle of self-determination in the peace settlement raised hopes that were not met in Southeast Asia. The League of Nations did not extend self-determination to the colonies. The Russian Revolution made communism an option. The Comintern assisted in forming parties such as the Indochinese Communist Party, founded by **Ho Chi Minh** in 1930.

**Leaders.** Strong personalities mattered. Sukarno was a gifted speaker who built Pancasila (five principles) as a unifying idea. Hatta was a cautious organiser. In Burma, Aung San rose from student politics. In Vietnam, Ho Chi Minh combined nationalism with communism.

## Impact of the Second World War

**Japanese conquest and rule.** Japan invaded the Dutch East Indies in early 1942 and defeated the colonial army within weeks. It promoted itself as the liberator of Asia from Western rule and used slogans of Asian unity. But it ran the economy for war, took rice and oil, and forced hundreds of thousands to work as *romusha* labourers, many of whom died. Violence by the military police (the *Kempeitai*) was widespread. The Japanese also continued "divide and rule" by favouring some groups, such as the Muslim leadership, and by treating the Chinese with suspicion.

**Political and economic impact.** Japan removed the Dutch from government and gave Indonesians senior jobs. It trained Indonesian youth in militias and in an auxiliary army (**PETA**, 1943), which gave the future republic military skills. It worked with Sukarno and Hatta in mass organisations, which gave them platforms to speak. The economy collapsed: shipping stopped, prices rose, and rice was short.

**Social impact.** Women and ethnic groups were affected in different ways. Some women were forced into military brothels, and the Chinese community faced pressure. Many young people were politicised.

**Leaders emerge.** In Indonesia, Sukarno and Hatta; in Burma, Aung San, who first worked with the Japanese and then turned against them in 1945; in Vietnam, the Viet Minh under Ho Chi Minh, who fought the Japanese and French.

## Post-war methods used to achieve independence

**Indonesia.** After Japan's surrender in August 1945, young activists pressed Sukarno and Hatta to act, and they proclaimed independence on 17 August 1945. The Dutch, with British help at first, tried to reoccupy the country. The struggle (the Indonesian National Revolution) combined:

- **Political factors and mass mobilisation.** The Republic had a government, a constitution and the support of the youth (*pemuda*) and many villages.
- **Conflict and resistance.** Guerrilla warfare against Dutch "police actions" in July 1947 and December 1948, when the Dutch took the republican capital, Yogyakarta, and arrested the leaders. The Republic put down a communist revolt at Madiun in 1948, which won favour in Washington.
- **External support.** The United Nations and the United States pressed the Netherlands, threatening to cut Marshall Aid. India and Australia supported Indonesia.
- **Weakness of colonial authority.** The Netherlands was exhausted after German occupation and could not afford a colonial war. The Dutch recognised Indonesian sovereignty in December 1949.

**Comparison.** Vietnam: independence declared in September 1945, followed by war with France until the Geneva Accords of 1954. Burma: negotiation led to independence in January 1948 under U Nu, after Aung San's assassination in 1947. The Philippines: independence from the United States on 4 July 1946. Malaya: independence by negotiation in 1957, during a communist insurgency, the "Emergency".

## Achievements and challenges in the first ten years

**Social developments.** The Indonesian state expanded schools, promoted the national language, Bahasa Indonesia, and gave women the vote and a role in public life, though real gender equality was limited. Health services improved slowly.

**Economic developments.** Indonesia depended on exports of rubber, oil and tin. Foreign firms, especially Dutch ones, still owned much of the economy, and the state lacked trained administrators. Policies to build Indonesian businesses had mixed results, and nationalisation of Dutch firms followed in 1957.

**Political instability.** From 1950 Indonesia was a parliamentary democracy. Cabinets changed often, and the first national election in 1955 produced four large parties (PNI, Masyumi, NU and the PKI) without a majority. Sukarno, impatient with party politics, introduced "Guided Democracy" from 1957 to 1959.

**Ethnic and religious tensions.** Regional rebellions followed: the Darul Islam movement in West Java, the South Moluccas separatists (1950), and the revolts in Sumatra and Sulawesi (1957 to 1958), partly caused by resentment of Java's dominance. Many of these show the difficulty of holding a diverse archipelago together.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Benedict Anderson's work stressed how print, schools and shared experience created an "imagined community", and also the role of youth in the revolution.
- M. C. Ricklefs and others have stressed the role of Islam, class and local conflicts.
- Debate over Japan's role: some see it as a liberator that destroyed colonial prestige, others as a brutal occupier whose help to nationalists was a by-product.
- Debate over the roles of leaders against mass movements.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent did the Second World War cause the emergence of independent states in Southeast Asia?"** Use one state. Argue that the war destroyed colonial prestige and gave nationalists military training and a stage, but earlier nationalist organisation, the post-war return of colonial rule, and external pressure explain the timing. Judge that the war was a necessary condition but that nationalist movements and international politics made independence a reality.

## Common mistakes

- Using the whole region as one example when the question requires a single state.
- Describing Japan only as a liberator or only as a conqueror.
- Ignoring the first ten years after independence.
- Writing about the Cold War in Vietnam rather than the emergence of the state.
