These questions cover section 6.3 Planning of Unit 6: Practical Skills in Physics II, Pearson Edexcel IAL Physics (YPH11), Issue 3. You plan each experiment; you do not carry it out.

## Section A

**1.** The specification asks you to identify the most appropriate apparatus, "giving details". Explain what this means, with an example. **[2]**

**2.** A student will time the discharge of a 1000 µF capacitor by hand and wants a time constant of about 50 s. Calculate a suitable resistance. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why oscillations of a mass on a spring should be timed from the equilibrium position. **[2]**

**4.** A GM tube records 330 counts in 10 minutes with no source present, and 820 counts in 60 s with a source. Calculate the corrected count rate. **[3]**

**5.** Discuss whether repeat readings are appropriate when a data logger records the p.d. across a discharging capacitor every 0.1 s. **[2]**

**6.** A student plans to find the specific latent heat of fusion of ice using an immersion heater in a funnel of crushed ice. Identify **two** hazards and how each may be dealt with. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Plan an experiment to investigate the relationship between the pressure and volume of a fixed mass of air at constant temperature. Include apparatus with details, the method, control of variables, one safety point and how the data will be used. **[8]**

**8.** Plan an experiment to determine the capacitance C of a capacitor, thought to be about 2200 µF, by timing its discharge through a 22 kΩ resistor. Include the circuit and apparatus with details, the method, whether repeats are appropriate, a safety point and how the data will be used. **[9]**

**9.** Plan an experiment to determine the specific latent heat of fusion of ice using a 50 W immersion heater, two funnels of crushed ice, beakers, a top-pan balance and a stopwatch. **[8]**

**10.** Plan how to calibrate a thermistor connected in a potential divider so that the circuit can be used as a thermometer from 0 °C to 100 °C. **[8]**

**11.** A light gate measures the speed of a trolley as a card on the trolley passes through it. Identify the apparatus details the student should give, and explain how the card should be measured. **[4]**

**12.** A student wants to find out whether the period T of a mass on a spring is related to the mass m by T = kmⁿ, without assuming the value of n. Explain how the data collected will be used. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Giving details means stating the range and resolution of an instrument and/or the relevant dimensions or values of apparatus [1], such as a stopwatch reading to 0.01 s, or masses from 0.100 kg to 0.600 kg in 0.100 kg steps [1]. **[2]**

**2.** R = time constant ÷ C = 50 ÷ (1000 × 10⁻⁶) [1] = **50 kΩ** (a 47 kΩ resistor is suitable) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The mass moves fastest at the equilibrium position [1], so the instant it passes the marker is most definite, reducing timing uncertainty [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Background rate = 330 ÷ 600 = 0.55 s⁻¹ [1]. Measured rate = 820 ÷ 60 = 13.67 s⁻¹ [1]. Corrected rate = 13.67 − 0.55 = **13.1 s⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Repeating individual readings is not needed, because the logger records many readings with no reaction-time error [1]; but repeating the whole discharge two or three times checks that the results are repeatable [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The heater becomes very hot if switched on out of the ice [1]: switch on only when it is surrounded by ice and let it cool before handling [1]. Mains-powered or low-voltage supply near melted water [1]: keep connections and the supply away from the water and dry hands [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Apparatus: a sealed syringe or tube of air connected to a pressure gauge or sensor, range about 0–300 kPa, with a volume scale reading to 1 cm³ [1]. Method: move the plunger or pump to change the volume, recording p and V for at least six volumes across the full scale [1]. Controls: wait about a minute after each change so the air returns to room temperature [1]; check the seals so the mass of air stays fixed [1]. Repeats: take readings while increasing and then while decreasing the pressure, and average [1]. Safety: wear eye protection and do not exceed the gauge's maximum pressure [1]. Data: plot p against 1/V [1]; a straight line through the origin shows p ∝ 1/V, so pV is constant, or plot lg p against lg V and expect a gradient of −1 [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Circuit: a d.c. supply of about 6 V (below the capacitor's voltage rating), a two-way switch to connect the capacitor first to the supply and then to the 22 kΩ resistor [1], with a digital voltmeter of high resistance across the capacitor [1]. Expected RC ≈ 22 × 10³ × 2200 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 48 s, so hand timing is practical [1]. Method: charge the capacitor, switch to discharge and start the stopwatch at once [1]; record V every 10 s for about 2 minutes [1]. Repeats: repeat the whole discharge twice more and average V at each time, since reading a falling meter at an exact time has random error [1]. Safety: connect the electrolytic capacitor with the correct polarity [1]. Data: plot ln V against t [1]; the gradient is −1/RC, so C = −1 ÷ (R × gradient) [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A high-resistance voltmeter matters, because a low-resistance meter would provide another discharge path and shorten the time constant.

**9.** Set up two identical funnels of crushed ice over beakers; one has the heater in it and the other is a control with an identical heater switched off [1]. When water drips steadily from both, empty the beakers and start timing [1], switching on one heater [1]. After a measured time, for example 300 s, weigh the water in each beaker on the top-pan balance reading to 0.01 g [1]. The control gives the mass melted by heat from the surroundings, so the mass melted by the heater is the difference [1]. Energy supplied E = Pt = 50 × 300 = 15 000 J, or use a joulemeter [1]. L = E ÷ (m_heater − m_control) [1]. Repeat for several time intervals or powers, and plot E against the mass melted by the heater: the gradient is L [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Place the thermistor, sealed in a waterproof sleeve, in a beaker of water with a thermometer, starting with melting ice at 0 °C [1]. Connect it in series with a fixed resistor across a constant supply and measure the output p.d. with a digital voltmeter [1]. Use a thermometer of range at least 0–110 °C, reading to 1 °C or better [1]. Heat the water in steps of about 10 °C to 100 °C [1], stirring and waiting until the readings are steady before recording V and θ [1]. Keep the supply p.d. and fixed resistor the same throughout [1]. Hazard: hot water can scald, so stand the beaker securely and do not hold it [1]. Plot V against θ as a calibration curve, so any later V can be converted to a temperature [1]. **[8]**

**11.** State the card length, for example 10.0 cm [1], and the timer resolution, for example 0.001 s [1]. Measure the card with Vernier calipers rather than a ruler [1], at its top and bottom edges, since speed = card length ÷ time and the card length uncertainty passes directly into the speed [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Take logs: lg T = lg k + n lg m [1]. Plot lg T against lg m [1]. A straight line confirms the power law [1]; its gradient gives n (expected 0.5) and its intercept gives lg k [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming apparatus with no range, resolution or values.
- Choosing a circuit time constant that is too short for hand timing.
- Forgetting the control funnel in a latent heat plan.
- Not waiting for temperature to settle in gas and thermistor experiments.
- Leaving out the graph, or plotting raw variables when a log graph is needed.
- Safety points that name no specific hazard.
