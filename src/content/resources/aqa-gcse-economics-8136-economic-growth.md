## What economic growth means

**Economic growth** is an increase in the output of goods and services produced by an economy over time. It is measured by the percentage change in **real GDP**, usually over a year or a quarter.

- **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** is the total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a period, measured at the prices of that period.
- **Real GDP** is GDP adjusted to remove the effect of inflation, so it shows changes in the quantity of output, not in prices.
- **GDP per capita** is GDP divided by the population: the average output per person.

If real GDP falls instead, the economy is shrinking. A fall in real GDP for two quarters in a row is commonly called a **recession**.

## Why growth matters: its significance

Growth is one of the government's principal objectives because so much depends on it:

- **Living standards.** More output per person means people can, on average, consume more goods and services.
- **Jobs.** Firms producing more need more workers, so unemployment tends to fall.
- **Public finances.** Higher incomes and spending bring in more income tax and VAT without raising tax rates, and fewer people need unemployment benefits.
- **Business confidence.** Rising sales encourage firms to invest, which supports further growth.

## Measurement: the calculations you need

### Percentage change

Growth (%) = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100

### From nominal to real

Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ (1 + inflation rate as a decimal)

A quick estimate is real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation, but the full method is more accurate.

### Per capita

GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population

### Worked example 1: three measures together

Country Arden's nominal GDP rises from 800 billion to 856 billion in a year when prices rise by 4%. Its population rises from 40.0 million to 40.8 million.

1. **Nominal growth** = (856 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = **7.0%**.
2. **Real GDP** in year 2 at year 1 prices = 856 ÷ 1.04 = **823.1 billion**.
3. **Real growth** = (823.1 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = **2.9%**. (The quick estimate, 7 − 4 = 3%, is close.)
4. **GDP per capita in year 1** = 800 billion ÷ 40 million = **20,000**.
5. **Real GDP per capita in year 2** = 823.1 billion ÷ 40.8 million = **20,173** (to the nearest unit).
6. **Real growth per person** = (20,173 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 × 100 = **0.9%**.

The headline nominal figure of 7% hides the fact that output per person rose by less than 1%. Most of the rise was higher prices, and much of the real rise was shared among more people.

### Worked example 2: size versus living standards

| Country | GDP (billions) | Population (millions) | GDP per capita |
|---|---|---|---|
| Belmar | 1,200 | 60 | 20,000 |
| Corrin | 150 | 5 | 30,000 |

Belmar's economy is eight times larger, but Corrin's GDP per capita is 50% higher. Total GDP shows the size of an economy; GDP per capita is the better guide to average living standards.

### Worked example 3: when population grows faster

If real GDP grows by 1.5% but the population grows by 2%, real GDP per capita **falls**, by about 0.5%. The economy is growing, yet the average person has less output to share.

### Limits of GDP per capita as a measure of living standards

- It is an **average**, so it says nothing about how income is shared.
- It ignores **unpaid work** such as caring and volunteering.
- It does not count **quality of life**: pollution, working hours or health.
- Output in the **hidden economy** goes unrecorded.

## Causes of economic growth

Growth comes from two directions.

**Using more of existing capacity (demand-side).** When total spending rises, firms use idle machinery and unemployed workers to produce more. Spending can come from:

- consumers, for example after a cut in interest rates or income tax
- firms investing in new equipment
- government spending on services and projects
- exports, when foreign demand rises

**Increasing capacity (supply-side).** The economy can produce more in the long run when:

- the **quantity** of labour rises, for example through migration or more people of working age joining the labour force
- the **quality** of labour rises through education and training, raising productivity
- firms invest in more and better **capital**, such as machinery and infrastructure
- **new technology** lets the same inputs produce more output

Demand-side growth runs out when spare capacity is used up; after that, further spending mainly raises prices. Lasting growth needs capacity to rise.

## Benefits and costs

| Group | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Households | Higher incomes, more jobs, more choice | Higher prices if demand outruns supply; congestion and housing pressure in fast-growing areas |
| Firms | Higher sales and profits, more reason to invest | Rising wages and raw material costs; harder to recruit |
| Government | More tax revenue, less benefit spending | Pressure to provide infrastructure and services |
| Environment and future generations | New technology may be cleaner | Pollution, carbon emissions, use of finite resources |

Gains are also uneven: some regions and skilled workers may gain much more than others, widening inequality.

## Government policies to achieve growth

| Policy | Example | How it helps | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monetary | Lower interest rates | Cheaper borrowing raises spending and investment | Can raise inflation; weak if confidence is low |
| Fiscal | More government spending; lower taxes | Raises total demand | Increases government borrowing |
| Supply-side | Spending on education, training and infrastructure; incentives to invest | Raises productive capacity, so growth lasts without inflation | Takes years to work; costly |

## Worked evaluation: is faster growth always good?

*"Country Arden's growth has risen to 4%. This is good news for everyone." Evaluate.*

> For: higher growth raises incomes and creates jobs, and the extra tax revenue can fund public services. Against: if growth comes from spending rising faster than capacity, inflation may rise, hurting people on fixed incomes; pollution may increase; and the gains may go mainly to some groups. **Judgement:** good news for most people if it comes from rising productivity, because that kind of growth can last without inflation, but not for everyone, and it depends on how the gains are shared.

## Common mistakes

- Judging growth from nominal GDP when prices have risen.
- Dividing by the population when the question asks for total GDP, or not dividing when it asks for per capita.
- Saying a country with higher GDP must have higher living standards.
- Listing benefits of growth with no costs.
- Forgetting units: billions and millions must be handled carefully when finding GDP per capita.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: write the formula, substitute, and give the answer with units and the correct number of decimal places.
- **Explain** questions: give a chain of reasoning, such as lower interest rates → cheaper borrowing → more investment → more output.
- **Evaluate** questions (9 marks): give points for and against, then a judgement that says what it depends on.
