## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between water withdrawal and water consumption. **[2]**

**2.** Define *grey water footprint*. **[1]**

**3.** Outline why irrigated agriculture has a much greater effect on freshwater availability than cooling water used by power stations. **[3]**

**4.** State two reasons why women and girls are often most affected by poor access to water. **[2]**

**5.** Outline the main cause of the shrinking of the Aral Sea. **[2]**

**6.** Define *environmental flow*. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) The table shows annual withdrawals in a fictional country with 80 km³ of renewable freshwater.

| Sector | Withdrawal / km³ | Share consumed / % |
|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | 36 | 70 |
| Industry (mostly cooling) | 14 | 10 |
| Domestic | 6 | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate the water-stress ratio. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total volume of water consumed. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage of total consumption due to agriculture. **[1]**
**(d)** Using your answers, explain which sector a policy to reduce water stress should target first. **[2]**

**8.** (Data response) A fictional dry country, Ardena, has 3.2 km³ of renewable freshwater a year. It imports the following foods.

| Import | Mass / thousand tonnes | Water to produce / m³ per tonne (assumed) |
|---|---|---|
| Wheat | 900 | 1300 |
| Beef | 40 | 15000 |

**(a)** Calculate the total virtual water imported in km³. **[3]**
**(b)** Express this as a percentage of Ardena's renewable freshwater. **[1]**
**(c)** Comment on the difference between the two imports in mass and in virtual water. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why a water-stress ratio for a whole country may underestimate the water insecurity of some of its people. **[3]**

**10.** Evaluate charging households for water by volume using a rising block tariff. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how the construction of a large dam on an upstream section of a shared river can lead to conflict between states. **[4]**

**12.** To what extent can the water security of a dry region be achieved through demand-side measures alone? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Withdrawal is the water taken from a river, lake or aquifer [1]; consumption is the part of that withdrawal not returned to the source, because it evaporates, is transpired or is built into products [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The volume of freshwater needed to dilute pollutants from production to meet water-quality standards [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Irrigation water is largely transpired by crops or evaporates from fields [1], so most of it is consumed and lost to the basin [1]. Power stations return most of their cooling water to the river, so although they withdraw a lot, they consume little [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Any two: they usually carry out water collection, which takes hours each day [1]; collection time reduces time for school or paid work [1]; lack of private sanitation affects their safety and dignity; they often care for family members made ill by unsafe water. **[2]**

**5.** From the 1960s, the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers were diverted to irrigate cotton [1], so inflow fell far below evaporation from the sea's surface and its volume shrank [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Water deliberately left in or released to a river to keep its ecosystem functioning [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** Withdrawals = 36 + 14 + 6 = 56 km³ [1]; 56 ÷ 80 × 100 = **70%** [1].
**(b)** Agriculture 36 × 0.70 = 25.2; industry 14 × 0.10 = 1.4; domestic 6 × 0.20 = 1.2 [1]; total = **27.8 km³** [1].
**(c)** 25.2 ÷ 27.8 × 100 = **90.6%** [1].
**(d)** Agriculture, because it causes over 90% of consumption as well as most withdrawals [1]; industry withdraws a large volume but returns most of it, so cutting its withdrawals would reduce stress on the source less than an equal cut in irrigation [1]. **[7]**

**8. (a)** Wheat: 900 000 × 1300 = 1.17 × 10⁹ m³ [1]; beef: 40 000 × 15 000 = 0.60 × 10⁹ m³ [1]; total = 1.77 × 10⁹ m³ = **1.77 km³** [1].
**(b)** 1.77 ÷ 3.2 × 100 = **55.3%** [1].
**(c)** Beef is less than 5% of the imported mass (40 of 940 thousand tonnes) [1] but about one-third of the virtual water (0.60 of 1.77 km³), because producing beef needs feed crops as well as drinking water for animals [1]. **[6]**

**9.** A national ratio averages over all regions, so a water-rich region can hide a dry one [1]. It uses annual totals, so it hides dry-season shortages [1]. It measures quantity, not access or quality, so people without piped supply or with polluted sources can be insecure even where water is plentiful (economic scarcity) [1]. **[3]**

**10.** A low-cost basic allowance protects poorer households' access to essential water [1]. Higher prices for heavier use encourage saving, for example on garden watering [1]. Revenue can pay for leak repairs and treatment [1]. However, large households may pay high prices for essential use, and meters must be installed and read, so the tariff works best with allowances based on household size [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The upstream state controls the timing and volume of releases from the reservoir [1]. Filling the reservoir, especially in dry years, reduces flow reaching downstream states [1]. Downstream states that depend on the river for irrigation and drinking water fear for their water security [1]. Without a binding agreement on releases, each side claims priority on grounds of development or historical use, leading to diplomatic tension, as over the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so use these points as a guide:
- Demand-side measures include drip irrigation, metering and pricing, leak repair, greywater recycling and less water-intensive crops [1].
- Because agriculture dominates use, irrigation efficiency can make large savings [1].
- Pricing encourages households and farms to save but can harm poor users without safeguards [1].
- Importing virtual water reduces domestic demand for water-intensive crops [1].
- Demand measures are usually cheaper and use less energy than new supply such as desalination [1].
- But if population grows quickly, savings may be outweighed by rising total demand [1].
- Efficiency gains can be lost if saved water is used to irrigate more land (a rebound effect) [1].
- Where supply is very low or polluted, new supply or treatment (desalination, aquifer recharge, wastewater reuse) is still required [1].
- Judgement: demand measures should come first and can deliver most of the gain, but in very dry, growing regions they must be combined with supply measures and fair allocation [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using thousands of tonnes as tonnes in virtual-water calculations.
- Leaving answers in m³ when km³ was asked for, or the reverse.
- Treating withdrawal and consumption as the same.
- Evaluating a tariff with benefits only.
- Describing a transboundary case without the positions of each side.
