## Names and formulae (19.1)

| Family | Group | Example | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary amine | –NH₂ on carbon | CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ | propylamine |
| Secondary amine | –NH– between two carbons | (CH₃CH₂)₂NH | diethylamine |
| Aromatic amine | –NH₂ on a ring | C₆H₅NH₂ | phenylamine |
| Primary amide | –CONH₂ | CH₃CH₂CH₂CONH₂ | butanamide |
| N-substituted amide | –CONH–R | HCONHCH₃ | N-methylmethanamide |
| 2-amino acid | H₂N–CH(R)–COOH | H₂NCH(CH₃)COOH | 2-aminopropanoic acid |

- An "N-" locant means the group is on nitrogen, not on a carbon.
- Skeletal formulae: draw every N and O, and the H atoms on them.

## Amine reactions (19.2)

Learn these for butylamine (C₄H₉NH₂) and phenylamine (C₆H₅NH₂).

| Reagent | Type | Product / observation |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Base (proton acceptor) | RNH₃⁺ + OH⁻, alkaline solution |
| HCl(aq) | Acid–base | RNH₃⁺Cl⁻ salt; reversed by NaOH |
| Halogenoalkane | Nucleophilic substitution | Secondary amine, then tertiary amine, then quaternary ammonium salt |
| Ethanoyl chloride | Forms amide (room temperature, fast) | N-substituted ethanamide + RNH₃⁺Cl⁻ |
| Cu²⁺(aq) | Base, then ligand | Butylamine: pale blue precipitate, deep blue solution in excess |

General equations to adapt:

- RNH₂ + R′Br → RNHR′ + HBr
- CH₃COCl + 2RNH₂ → CH₃CONHR + RNH₃⁺Cl⁻
- [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4RNH₂ → [Cu(RNH₂)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 4H₂O (in excess amine)

## Solubility and base strength (19.3)

- **Miscible with water:** N–H and the N lone pair hydrogen bond with water. A big hydrocarbon part (the ring in phenylamine) lowers solubility.
- **Base strength depends on lone-pair availability.**

| Base | Effect on N lone pair | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Phenylamine | Delocalised into the ring π system | Weakest |
| Ammonia | No effect | Middle |
| Butylamine | Alkyl group releases electron density | Strongest |

## Making amines (19.4, 19.5)

| Target | Start from | Reagents and conditions | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary aliphatic amine | Halogenoalkane | Excess conc. NH₃ in ethanol, heat in a sealed tube | Excess NH₃ reduces multiple substitution |
| Primary aliphatic amine | Nitrile | LiAlH₄ in dry ether (or H₂ / Ni) | Only primary amine; RCN + 4[H] → RCH₂NH₂ |
| Aromatic amine | Nitro-compound | Sn + conc. HCl, heat under reflux; then NaOH(aq) | ArNO₂ + 6[H] → ArNH₂ + 2H₂O |

### Method in steps: nitrobenzene to phenylamine

1. Reflux nitrobenzene with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid.
2. Cool, then add sodium hydroxide solution until alkaline: this frees the amine from C₆H₅NH₃⁺.
3. Separate the phenylamine (for example by steam distillation, then solvent extraction).

## Azo dyes (19.6)

1. **Diazotisation:** phenylamine + NaNO₂ + HCl, **below 10 °C** → benzenediazonium chloride, C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻.
2. **Coupling:** add to **phenol in NaOH(aq)** → yellow-orange azo dye, C₆H₅–N=N–C₆H₄OH.

Too warm, and the diazonium ion breaks down to N₂ gas and phenol.

## Amides (19.7)

Acyl chloride + NH₃ → primary amide + NH₄Cl. Acyl chloride + amine → N-substituted amide. Both are fast at room temperature.

## Polymers (19.8 to 19.10)

| Polymer | Type | Monomer(s) | Repeat unit | Lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nylon-6,6 | Condensation | H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ + HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH (or the diacyl dichloride) | –[NH(CH₂)₆NHCO(CH₂)₄CO]– | H₂O (or HCl) |
| Protein | Condensation | 2-amino acids | –[NH–CH(R)–CO]– | H₂O |
| Poly(propenamide) | Addition | CH₂=CHCONH₂ | –[CH₂–CH(CONH₂)]– | nothing |
| Poly(ethenol) | Addition (made via poly(ethenyl ethanoate)) | notional monomer CH₂=CHOH | –[CH₂–CH(OH)]– | nothing |

**Properties from hydrogen bonding**

- Polyamides: N–H···O=C hydrogen bonds between chains give strength and fairly high melting temperatures (good fibres).
- Poly(ethenol): many –OH groups hydrogen bond with water, so it dissolves. Used for soluble hospital laundry bags and for liquid-detergent capsules (liquitabs).

### Method in steps: drawing a condensation repeat unit

1. Write the two monomers with their reacting groups at the ends.
2. Remove –OH (or –Cl) from the acid end and –H from the amine end.
3. Join with –CONH–, add brackets with extension bonds, and add n.

## Amino acids (19.11)

| Condition | Form of 2-aminopropanoic acid |
|---|---|
| Solid / neutral solution | Zwitterion, H₃N⁺CH(CH₃)COO⁻ |
| In acid | H₃N⁺CH(CH₃)COOH |
| In alkali | H₂NCH(CH₃)COO⁻ |

- Zwitterions explain the high melting temperature and solubility in water.
- **Plane-polarised light:** a single enantiomer rotates the plane in a polarimeter. Glycine (no chiral carbon) and racemic mixtures do not.
- **Peptide bond:** –COOH of one + –NH₂ of the next → –CONH– + H₂O. Two different amino acids give two dipeptides.

## Worked reminders

**Titre for an amine.** What volume of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ HCl neutralises 20.0 cm³ of 0.0800 mol dm⁻³ ethylamine?

1. n(amine) = 0.0800 × 20.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.00160 mol.
2. Amine : HCl = 1 : 1, so n(HCl) = 0.00160 mol.
3. Volume = 0.00160 ÷ 0.0500 = 0.0320 dm³ = **32.0 cm³**. Use methyl orange (weak base, strong acid).

**Dipeptide mass.** Glycine (Mr 75.0) + alanine (Mr 89.0) → dipeptide + H₂O. Mr = 75.0 + 89.0 − 18.0 = **146.0**. The two orders, Gly–Ala and Ala–Gly, have the same Mr but are different compounds: in one the free –NH₂ is on the glycine end, in the other on the alanine end.

**Amide equation.** Propanoyl chloride with methylamine: CH₃CH₂COCl + 2CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃ + CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻. The product is N-methylpropanamide.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Addition vs condensation polymer:** addition opens C=C and loses nothing; condensation joins two different groups and loses a small molecule.
- **Amine vs amide:** amines are bases; the –CONH– group in amides is not usefully basic, because the C=O draws the lone pair away.
- **Diazotisation vs coupling:** the first needs cold acid; the second needs alkaline phenol.
- **Halogenoalkane vs nitrile route:** the nitrile route gives one product and an extra carbon.

## Quick self-test

1. Name CH₃CH₂CH₂CONH₂.
2. Put in order of increasing base strength: ammonia, ethylamine, phenylamine.
3. Give the reagent and conditions to turn propanenitrile into propylamine.
4. What mass of ethanoyl chloride (Mr 78.5) is needed to make 0.0400 mol of an N-substituted ethanamide?
5. Calculate the percentage by mass of oxygen in the poly(ethenol) repeat unit, C₂H₄O.
6. Why must the diazotisation mixture stay below 10 °C?
7. Write the zwitterion of 2-aminopropanoic acid.
8. Explain why a glycine solution has no effect on plane-polarised light.
9. Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in the poly(propenamide) repeat unit, C₃H₅NO.
10. Give the Mr of N-butylethanamide, C₆H₁₃NO.
11. State what you see when butylamine is added drop by drop, then in excess, to copper(II) sulfate solution.

### Answers

1. Butanamide.
2. Phenylamine < ammonia < ethylamine.
3. LiAlH₄ in dry ether (or H₂ gas over a Ni catalyst).
4. 0.0400 × 78.5 = **3.14 g**.
5. Mr = 44.0; 16.0 ÷ 44.0 × 100 = **36.4%**.
6. The benzenediazonium ion decomposes when warmer, giving nitrogen and phenol.
7. H₃N⁺CH(CH₃)COO⁻.
8. Glycine has two H atoms on its central carbon, so it has no chiral centre.
9. Mr = 71.0; 14.0 ÷ 71.0 × 100 = **19.7%**.
10. **115.0**.
11. A pale blue precipitate, which then disappears, giving a deep blue solution, once butylamine is in excess.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying phenylamine is basic "because of the benzene ring" without explaining delocalisation of the lone pair.
- Using one mole of amine with ethanoyl chloride when two are needed.
- Omitting "excess ammonia" or "sealed tube / ethanol" in the halogenoalkane route.
- Writing aqueous LiAlH₄; it must be in dry ether.
- Leaving out the NaOH step after Sn / HCl.
- Giving "room temperature" for diazotisation, or coupling in acid.
- Drawing the repeat unit of poly(ethenol) with a C=C, or a condensation repeat unit with –OH still on the end.
- Explaining poly(ethenol) solubility without naming hydrogen bonds with water.
- Forgetting that two different amino acids can make two dipeptides.
- Recording only the final colour in a test.
