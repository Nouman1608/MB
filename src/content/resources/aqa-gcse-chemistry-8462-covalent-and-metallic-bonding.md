## Covalent bonding: what it is (4.2.1.4)

A **covalent bond** forms when two atoms **share a pair of electrons**. Each atom gives one electron to the pair. Covalent bonding occurs in most non-metallic elements and in compounds of non-metals.

The bond is **strong**. The negative shared pair sits between the two positive nuclei, and both nuclei are attracted to it by **electrostatic attraction**.

For the non-metals here, the number of covalent bonds an atom forms equals the number of electrons it needs to fill its outer shell.

| Atom | Outer electrons | Electrons needed | Covalent bonds formed |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1 | 1 (to reach 2) | 1 |
| Cl | 7 | 1 | 1 |
| O | 6 | 2 | 2 |
| N | 5 | 3 | 3 |
| C | 4 | 4 | 4 |

One shared pair is a **single bond** (drawn as one line). Two shared pairs is a **double bond**. Three shared pairs is a **triple bond**.

## The three kinds of covalent substance

- **Small molecules.** A fixed, small number of atoms joined by covalent bonds, for example H₂O, CO₂, NH₃, CH₄, HCl, H₂, O₂, N₂ and Cl₂.
- **Very large molecules (polymers).** Long chains of repeating units joined by covalent bonds, for example poly(ethene).
- **Giant covalent structures.** Huge numbers of atoms, each joined to its neighbours by covalent bonds, in a network that continues in all directions, for example **diamond** and **silicon dioxide**.

### Recognising small molecules from a formula

A formula of only non-metal symbols with small numbers usually means small molecules. Two traps:

- A formula with a metal (NaCl, MgO) is ionic, not covalent.
- **SiO₂** looks like CO₂, but silicon dioxide is a giant covalent structure. For a giant structure the formula gives only the **ratio** of atoms, not the number in a molecule. Learn diamond (C) and silicon dioxide (SiO₂) as the named giant covalent examples.

### Recognising the type from a diagram

- Separate, identical small groups of atoms with space between them: **small molecules**.
- A chain drawn inside brackets with a small *n* after the bracket: a **polymer**.
- A network where the bonds run off the edge of the diagram in every direction: a **giant covalent structure**.

## Dot and cross diagrams for the eight molecules

In a dot and cross diagram, the electrons of one atom are dots and those of the other atom are crosses. Show the outer shell of each atom. Each **shared pair** (one dot and one cross) sits in the overlap between the two atoms. Electrons that are not shared stay in pairs on their own atom.

| Molecule | Shared pairs | Bond(s) | Non-shared pairs |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | 1 | H–H single | none |
| Cl₂ | 1 | Cl–Cl single | 3 on each Cl |
| HCl | 1 | H–Cl single | 3 on Cl |
| O₂ | 2 | O=O double | 2 on each O |
| N₂ | 3 | N≡N triple | 1 on each N |
| H₂O | 2 | two O–H single | 2 on O |
| NH₃ | 3 | three N–H single | 1 on N |
| CH₄ | 4 | four C–H single | none |

Check every diagram: each hydrogen has **2** electrons in its shell, and every other atom has **8** in its outer shell.

### Worked example 1: water

**Step 1.** Outer electrons: oxygen has 6, each hydrogen has 1.

**Step 2.** Oxygen needs 2 more electrons, so it forms 2 bonds, one with each hydrogen.

**Step 3.** Draw the O outer shell overlapping each H shell. In each overlap put one dot (from O) and one cross (from H).

**Step 4.** Oxygen has 6 − 2 = 4 electrons left. Draw them as **2 pairs** of dots on the oxygen, outside the overlaps.

**Step 5.** Check: oxygen has 2 + 2 shared + 4 unshared = 8. Each hydrogen has 2.

```
Line form:   H–O–H
Molecular formula: H₂O
```

### Worked example 2: nitrogen

**Step 1.** Each nitrogen atom has 5 outer electrons and needs 3 more.

**Step 2.** The atoms share **three pairs**: a triple bond.

**Step 3.** In the overlap draw 3 dots and 3 crosses, arranged as three dot-cross pairs.

**Step 4.** Each nitrogen has 5 − 3 = 2 electrons left: **one** non-shared pair on each atom.

**Step 5.** Check: each N has 6 shared + 2 unshared = 8.

```
Line form:   N≡N
```

O₂ works the same way: each O needs 2, so a double bond, with 2 non-shared pairs on each atom.

## Representing covalent bonds with lines

The specification asks you to use **a line for a single bond** in three settings.

**Small molecules.** Methane is drawn with C in the centre and four lines to four H atoms. Double and triple bonds are two and three lines (O=O, N≡N).

**Polymer repeating units.** A polymer is drawn as one repeating unit inside brackets, with a bond line passing through each bracket and *n* written after it. For poly(ethene):

```
   [  H   H  ]
   [  |   |  ]
 --[--C---C--]--
   [  |   |  ]
   [  H   H  ] n
```

Here **n is a large number**: the number of repeating units in one chain. The lines crossing the brackets show the chain continues.

**Part of a giant covalent structure.** You draw a few atoms with lines between them, and lines leaving the edge to show the structure continues. In diamond each carbon atom is bonded to **four** other carbon atoms. In silicon dioxide each silicon atom is bonded to **four** oxygen atoms and each oxygen atom to **two** silicon atoms. Since each oxygen is shared between two silicons, there are 4 ÷ 2 = 2 oxygen atoms for every silicon atom, which is why the formula is SiO₂.

## Deducing a molecular formula from a model

A **molecular formula** shows the actual number of each type of atom in one molecule. To deduce it from a model or diagram, count each type of atom and write the counts as subscripts.

### Worked example 3: a ball and stick model

A ball and stick model shows: two black balls joined to each other; three white balls on the first black ball; two white balls on the second black ball; and one red ball on the second black ball, which carries one more white ball. (Black = carbon, white = hydrogen, red = oxygen.)

**Step 1.** Count carbon: 2.

**Step 2.** Count hydrogen: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6.

**Step 3.** Count oxygen: 1.

**Step 4.** Write carbon, then hydrogen, then others: **C₂H₆O**.

**Step 5.** Check: each C has 4 sticks, the O has 2, each H has 1.

```
     H   H
     |   |
 H – C – C – O – H
     |   |
     H   H
```

Do not cancel the numbers: a molecular formula describes one whole molecule, not a ratio.

## Limitations of the different diagrams

Learn one specific limitation of each model.

| Representation | What it shows well | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Dot and cross | Which atom each electron came from; shared and unshared pairs | Usually 2D, so no shape; atoms not to scale; electrons from different atoms are really identical |
| 2D displayed (lines) | Which atoms are bonded, and single/double/triple bonds | No 3D shape; no unshared electrons; no electrons at all |
| Ball and stick | 3D shape and bond directions | Bonds are not sticks; atoms are drawn far apart, but really touch; atoms not to scale; no electrons |
| 3D space-filling | Relative sizes of atoms and the overall shape | Hard to see the bonds or count them; no electrons |
| Any diagram of a giant structure | How the atoms are linked | Shows only a tiny part; the real structure continues in all directions with an enormous number of atoms |

## Metallic bonding (4.2.1.5)

Metals are **giant structures of atoms arranged in a regular pattern**. The electrons in the **outer shell** of each metal atom become **delocalised**: they are no longer attached to one atom and are **free to move through the whole structure**.

What remains of each atom is a positive ion. The **sharing of the delocalised electrons** between all the atoms gives **strong metallic bonds**. The bond is an electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the negative delocalised electrons, and it acts throughout the structure.

The usual diagram shows rows of + circles in a regular arrangement with electrons (e⁻) in the spaces between them: a **metallic giant structure**.

Metallic bonding occurs in metallic elements and in alloys.

### Worked example 4: describing the bonding in magnesium

*Describe the structure and bonding in magnesium.*

**Point 1.** Magnesium is a giant structure of atoms arranged in a regular pattern.

**Point 2.** Magnesium is in Group 2, so each atom has **2 outer electrons**. These become delocalised, leaving **Mg²⁺ ions**.

**Point 3.** The delocalised electrons are free to move through the whole structure.

**Point 4.** There is strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons. This sharing of delocalised electrons is the strong metallic bond.

The links between metallic bonding and properties such as conductivity and malleability are taught in 4.2.2.7 and 4.2.2.8.

## Common errors

- Writing that covalent bonds are weak. The **bonds** are strong. Small molecular substances have low melting points because of weak forces **between** molecules, which is section 4.2.2.4, not because the covalent bonds break.
- Putting the shared pair outside the overlap, or drawing two dots (or two crosses) as a shared pair between two different atoms.
- Forgetting the non-shared pairs on O, N or Cl, so the outer shell does not total 8.
- Drawing N₂ with a single or double bond. It needs a triple bond.
- Calling silicon dioxide a small molecule because its formula looks like CO₂.
- Cancelling a molecular formula. Ethane (H₃C–CH₃) is C₂H₆; writing CH₃ describes no real molecule.
- Saying metals have "free electrons" without saying the electrons are **delocalised** and come from the **outer shell**.
- Describing metallic bonding as attraction between "atoms and electrons" with no mention of electrostatic attraction or of sharing through the whole structure.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which paper are covalent and metallic bonding examined on in AQA GCSE Chemistry 8462?**
Sections 4.2.1.4 and 4.2.1.5 sit in Topic 2, Bonding, structure, and the properties of matter, which is examined on Paper 1. Paper 1 is 1 hour 45 minutes, worth 100 marks and 50% of the GCSE.

**Are covalent bonds weak because simple molecules have low melting points?**
No. Covalent bonds are strong. Small molecular substances have low melting points because the forces between molecules are weak, not because the covalent bonds inside them are weak.

**How do you work out a molecular formula from a ball and stick model?**
Count the atoms of each element in the model, then write carbon first, hydrogen next and other elements after. Do not cancel the formula to a ratio: ethane is C₂H₆, not CH₃.

**What causes the strong bonding in a metal such as magnesium?**
Metal atoms lose their outer electrons, which become delocalised, leaving positive ions in a regular pattern. Strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is the metallic bond.
