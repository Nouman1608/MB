## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Natural selection | Differential survival and reproduction of individuals with heritable traits, changing a population over generations |
| Paradigm shift | A fundamental change in the accepted framework of a science (Lamarckism to Darwin) |
| Mutation | A change in DNA base sequence; makes new alleles |
| Carrying capacity | The largest population an environment can sustain |
| Density-independent factor | Affects survival regardless of population size, such as a frost |
| Intraspecific competition | Competition between members of the same species |
| Fitness | Survival value plus reproductive potential of a genotype |
| Heritable | Passed on through alleles in gametes |
| Sexual selection | Selection by mate choice or competition for mates |
| Gene pool (HL only) | All genes and all their alleles in a population |
| Allele frequency (HL only) | Proportion of gene copies that are a given allele |
| Artificial selection (HL only) | Humans choose which individuals breed |

## The logic of natural selection

1. Populations show **variation**, from mutation and sexual reproduction.
2. More offspring are produced than the environment can support, so there is a **struggle** for resources.
3. Individuals differ in **adaptation**, so some survive and breed more.
4. If the traits are **heritable**, their alleles are more common in the next generation.
5. Repeated over many generations, the population changes.

Test each link before you write an answer. If any step is missing, the explanation is incomplete.

## Sources of variation (D4.1.2)

- **Mutation** makes new alleles. Only changes in gamete-forming cells matter for inheritance.
- **Meiosis**: crossing over and independent assortment give new combinations.
- **Random fertilisation**: any sperm can fuse with any egg.
- Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles, only new combinations.

## Selection pressures (D4.1.3 to D4.1.5)

- Biotic: food, predators, disease, mates, nesting sites.
- Abiotic: temperature, drought, salinity, pH, fire. Extremes such as frost are density-independent.
- Competition for food or space is density-dependent.
- Individuals do not choose to adapt. Variation exists first and the environment filters it.
- Fitness is judged by the number of offspring that survive to breed.

|---|---|---|
| Bacterial resistance gene | Yes | In the DNA, passed on |
| Strong arm muscles from lifting | No | Environmental effect, not in gene base sequence |
| Lost tail from an accident | No | Injury does not alter the gametes |

## Sexual selection and Endler's guppies (D4.1.7 and D4.1.8)

- Traits that signal health, such as bright colour or elaborate displays, improve mating success.
- Costs include predation. The final trait balances mating benefit against survival cost.
- Birds of paradise: females choose, males with the most striking plumage and displays mate more.
- Guppy pattern: **fewer predators means brighter males**; **more predators means duller males**.
- Reading a table: find the starting value, compute percentage change = (final − start) ÷ start × 100, then link the direction to the pressure that was varied.

## HL only: gene pools and allele frequency (D4.1.9 to D4.1.11)

**Method for allele frequency from counts**

1. Total alleles = 2 × number of individuals.
2. Count copies of one allele (homozygote counts twice, heterozygote once).
3. Divide by total alleles.
4. Frequency of the other allele = 1 − that value.

**Worked reminder.** 18 AA, 16 Aa, 6 aa: 40 individuals, 80 alleles. A = (2 × 18 + 16) ÷ 80 = 52 ÷ 80 = **0.65**, so a = 0.35.

Isolated populations differ because there is no gene flow, conditions differ and mutations arise separately. When using a database, note population, sample size and allele, and include a human example.

Neo-Darwinism: natural selection plus genetics. Evolution is a change in allele frequency in the gene pool.

## HL only: Hardy-Weinberg (D4.1.13 and D4.1.14)

| Quantity | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency of dominant allele | p | 1 − q |
| Frequency of recessive allele | q | 1 − p |
| Homozygous dominant | p² | |
| Heterozygous | 2pq | |
| Homozygous recessive | q² | |

Equations: p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1.

**Method in steps**

1. Identify what is given: usually the recessive phenotype frequency, which equals q².
2. Square-root to get q.
3. p = 1 − q.
4. Compute p², 2pq, q².
5. Check that the three genotype frequencies add to 1.
6. For numbers of individuals, multiply by the population size.

**Worked reminder.** A recessive trait occurs in 0.49% of a population. q² = 0.0049, q = 0.07, p = 0.93. Carriers = 2 × 0.93 × 0.07 = 0.1302, about **13.0%**.

**Worked reminder: effect of selection.** Start with q = 0.3, so p = 0.7 and genotypes AA 0.49, Aa 0.42, aa 0.09. If aa individuals have a relative survival of 0.4, aa becomes 0.09 × 0.4 = 0.036. Total = 0.49 + 0.42 + 0.036 = 0.946. New q = (0.42 ÷ 2 + 0.036) ÷ 0.946 = 0.246 ÷ 0.946 = **0.260** (3 s.f.). The recessive allele has fallen from 0.3 to 0.26 in one generation, and it falls slowly because it is hidden in heterozygotes.

If you are given the number of heterozygotes or homozygous dominants only, use the matching term: p² gives p directly.

**Conditions for genetic equilibrium**: large population; random mating; no mutation; no migration; no selection. If observed genotypes differ from expected, at least one is unmet. Example: too few heterozygotes suggests non-random mating or selection against heterozygotes.

## HL only: artificial selection (D4.1.15)

- Humans pick parents with desired traits in crops and domesticated animals, and repeat each generation.
- Examples: higher-yielding crops, dogs bred for behaviour, cattle for milk.
- Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is **natural** selection: the antibiotic is a selection pressure, but nobody chooses which bacteria breed.

## Frame for an explain question

For any "explain how a population changed" question, write four short linked points: there was heritable variation; more offspring were produced than survive; individuals with the advantageous trait survived and reproduced more; so the allele became more frequent. Add the named selection pressure from the stem. For HL, finish by stating the change in allele frequency.

## Must-know distinctions

- Natural selection (environment filters) against artificial selection (humans choose).
- Mutation (new alleles) against sexual reproduction (new combinations).
- Acquired (not heritable) against inherited characteristics.
- Density-dependent against density-independent factors.
- Natural selection (survival) against sexual selection (mating success).
- Allele frequency against genotype frequency.

### Answers

1. A fundamental change in the accepted framework of a field of science. Darwin's theory replaced Lamarckism.
2. Mutation makes new alleles. Sexual reproduction makes new combinations of alleles.
3. A severe frost (also drought or fire).
4. How well a genotype does at surviving and reproducing: its survival value and reproductive potential.
5. It was gained through use, an environmental effect, not coded in the base sequence of genes, so not passed on in gametes.
6. Males become brighter or more colourful over generations because female preference is no longer opposed by predation.
7. The complete set of genes in a population, including every allele of each.
8. Disruptive selection.
9. q = 0.07, p = 0.93, 2pq = 0.1302, so about **13.0%**.
10. q = 0.2, 2pq = 0.32, 0.32 × 600 = **192**.
11. Large population, random mating, no mutation, no migration, no selection.
12. Natural selection.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying individuals evolve or adapt during their lives, rather than populations changing.
- Writing "survival of the fittest" without linking to reproduction and heritability.
- Omitting the step that variation is heritable.
- Calling mutation the source of new combinations; it makes new alleles.
- Using p² or q instead of q² for the recessive phenotype.
- Forgetting to take the square root of q².
- Not checking that genotype frequencies sum to 1.
- Describing a graph without naming the selection type and the direction of change.
- Describing antibiotic resistance as artificial selection.
- Giving percentage change without the sign or without dividing by the starting value.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the steps of natural selection?**
Populations show heritable variation; more offspring are produced than can survive; individuals better adapted survive and reproduce more; so their alleles become more common over generations.

**What is the difference between mutation and sexual reproduction as sources of variation?**
Mutation changes the DNA base sequence and creates new alleles; sexual reproduction, through crossing over, independent assortment and random fertilisation, creates new combinations of existing alleles.

**What are the conditions for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium?**
Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium requires a large population, random mating, no mutation, no migration and no selection; if observed genotypes differ from expected, at least one condition is not met.

**What are directional, stabilizing and disruptive selection?**
Directional selection favours one extreme so the mean shifts; stabilizing selection favours the mean so the spread narrows; disruptive selection favours both extremes and can give two peaks. All three change allele frequencies.

**What is sexual selection?**
Sexual selection is selection by mate choice or competition for mates; traits such as bright colour improve mating success but may carry survival costs, as in guppies, where males are brighter when predators are fewer.
