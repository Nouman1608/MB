**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **A Level** subtopic
37.2: the terms stationary phase, mobile phase and retention time; interpreting chromatograms "in
terms of the percentage composition of a mixture"; and explaining retention times "in terms of
interaction with the stationary phase".

## The terms

| Term | Meaning in GLC |
|---|---|
| stationary phase | **high boiling point, non-polar liquid** coated on a **solid support** inside the column |
| mobile phase | **unreactive (carrier) gas**, e.g. nitrogen or helium |
| retention time | time from injection to the **maximum** of a component's peak at the detector |
| chromatogram | trace of detector response against time; one peak per separated component |

- Stationary liquid has a **high boiling point** so it stays in the column at oven temperatures.
- Carrier gas is **unreactive** so it does not change the sample or the stationary phase.
- The sample must be **volatile**: it is vaporised on injection.

## Retention time depends on conditions

| Change | Effect on retention times |
|---|---|
| higher column temperature | shorter |
| faster carrier gas flow | shorter |
| longer column | longer |
| different stationary phase | changes, and the order may change |

So compare retention times with a pure known sample **on the same instrument under the same
conditions**.

## Percentage composition

    % of X = area of peak X ÷ total area of all peaks × 100

- Area is proportional to amount.
- Triangular peaks: **area = ½ × base × height**.
- Use **heights** only if all the peaks have the **same width**.
- Assumes the detector responds equally to each component (the 9701 convention).

**Worked mini-example.** Areas 15, 45 and 40 units: total 100, so 15%, 45% and 40%.
Triangular peaks with base 0.2 min and height 6.0, and base 0.4 min and height 4.5: areas 0.60 and
0.90, so 40.0% and 60.0% (heights alone would wrongly suggest 57% and 43%).

## Explaining retention times

- Non-polar stationary phase → main attraction is **instantaneous dipole–induced dipole**.
- **Stronger attraction to the stationary phase** → dissolves more → **longer** retention time.
- More electrons / longer chain → stronger attractions → longer time.
- Branched isomers: less surface contact → weaker attractions → **shorter** time than the
  straight-chain isomer.
- Order on a non-polar column often matches **increasing boiling point**, but explain using the
  **stationary phase**.

| Compounds | Order out of the column (first → last) | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| pentane, hexane, heptane | pentane → hexane → heptane | more electrons, stronger attraction to stationary phase |
| 2,2-dimethylpropane, pentane | 2,2-dimethylpropane → pentane | branching reduces surface contact |

## Interpreting a chromatogram

1. Count the peaks: at least that many components.
2. Match retention times with references run under the same conditions.
3. Measure or calculate peak areas.
4. Convert to percentages of the total area.
5. State conclusions with "probably" unless confirmed under a second set of conditions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| carrier gas | the unreactive gas that is the mobile phase |
| column | long, narrow tube holding the stationary phase |
| detector | device at the column outlet that responds to each component |
| peak area | area under a peak, proportional to the amount of that component |
| volatile | easily vaporised |
| instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces | attractions between non-polar molecules, stronger with more electrons |

## Exam traps

- In GLC the stationary phase is a **liquid** (on a solid support), not a solid.
- The carrier gas is **unreactive**; never say it reacts with the sample.
- Use **areas**, not heights, unless the widths are equal.
- Retention time is read at the **peak maximum**.
- Do not compare retention times measured under **different conditions**.
- Explain with **interaction with the stationary phase**; boiling point alone is incomplete.
- A single peak does not prove a pure sample: two components could have the same retention time.

## Self-test

1. Describe the stationary phase in GLC.
2. Name a suitable mobile phase and say why it must be unreactive.
3. Define retention time.
4. Why must the stationary liquid have a high boiling point?
5. State two conditions that affect retention time.
6. Peak areas are 24, 36 and 60. Calculate the percentage of the second component.
7. A triangular peak has base 0.30 min and height 7.0. Calculate its area.
8. When can peak heights be used instead of areas?
9. Which leaves the column first, octane or hexane? Explain.
10. Why does 2-methylbutane have a shorter retention time than pentane?
11. How would raising the column temperature affect all retention times?
12. A sample gives one peak at the same retention time as pure ethanol. Is the sample definitely pure
    ethanol?

**Answers:**

1. A non-polar liquid with a high boiling point, coated on a solid support in the column.
2. Nitrogen or helium; so that it does not react with the sample or stationary phase.
3. Time from injection to the maximum of the component's peak at the detector.
4. So it stays liquid in the heated column and is not carried away by the gas.
5. Any two: column temperature; carrier gas flow rate; column length; type of stationary phase.
6. 36 / 120 × 100 = 30%.
7. ½ × 0.30 × 7.0 = 1.05.
8. When all the peaks have the same width.
9. Hexane: it has fewer electrons, so weaker instantaneous dipole–induced dipole attractions to the
   stationary phase, and spends less time dissolved in it.
10. It is branched, so it has less surface contact and weaker attractions to the stationary phase.
11. They would all decrease, because components spend more time as vapour.
12. No: another compound could have the same retention time; it is probably ethanol.
