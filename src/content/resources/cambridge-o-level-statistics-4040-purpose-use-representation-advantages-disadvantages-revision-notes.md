These notes condense section 2.3 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: "The purpose and use of various forms of representation, their advantages and disadvantages". Explanations and model answers are in the [study guide on choosing representations](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-purpose-use-representation-advantages-disadvantages/); written practice is in the [practice questions on choosing representations](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-purpose-use-representation-advantages-disadvantages-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Purpose | What the reader needs to see: shares, amounts, comparison, overlap, distribution |
| Advantage | Something the form shows clearly or keeps, compared with another form |
| Disadvantage | Something the form hides, loses or makes hard to judge |
| Raw data | The original individual values, before grouping or summarising |
| Proportion | A part as a fraction or percentage of its own whole |
| Common baseline | A shared starting line that lets bar lengths be compared directly |
| Misleading diagram | A diagram whose design gives a false impression of the data |

## One line per form

| Form | Best for | Main advantage | Main disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Table | Exact values; two-way classification | Values kept for calculation | Patterns slow to see |
| Pictogram | Simple counts for a general audience | Eye-catching | Part-symbols imprecise |
| Pie chart | Shares of one whole | Largest share obvious | Frequencies need the total; many sectors unreadable |
| Comparative pie charts | Shares of two wholes of different size | Shows shares and relative size | Size difference hard to judge by eye |
| Bar chart | Amounts in categories | Easy to compare and read values | No proportions; not for continuous data |
| Dual bar chart | Two groups, same categories | Side-by-side comparison | Crowded with many categories |
| Sectional bar chart | Totals and their make-up | Total and parts in one bar | Upper sections lack a common baseline |
| Percentage bar chart | Proportions in groups of different size | Fair comparison of shares | Totals lost |
| Venn diagram | Overlapping categories | "Both", "either", "neither" visible | Cluttered beyond three sets |
| Stem-and-leaf | Small numerical data sets | Keeps every value; shows shape | Impractical for large data sets |
| Box-and-whisker | Comparing distributions | Median and spread compared quickly | Hides individual values, mean and n |

## Decision questions

1. Do I need exact values? Table or stem-and-leaf.
2. Is it one whole split into parts? Pie chart.
3. Am I comparing groups of different sizes? Use proportions: percentage bar chart or comparative pie charts.
4. Do the totals matter as well? Sectional bar chart or comparative pie charts.
5. Do categories overlap? Venn diagram or two-way table.
6. Am I comparing spread and centre of numerical data? Box-and-whisker diagrams on one scale.

## Phrases that earn credit

- "... because the groups are of different sizes, so proportions should be compared."
- "... because it keeps the original values, so the median can be found exactly."
- "... because each bar starts from the same baseline, so heights can be compared directly."
- "... but the total number in each group is not shown."
- "... because the area, not just the height, of the picture is enlarged."

## Misleading features to spot

- Vertical axis not starting at zero on a bar chart.
- Pictures enlarged in both height and width to show a single change.
- Symbols of unequal size or spacing in a pictogram.
- Unequal steps on a scale.
- Same-size pie charts for different totals.
- Missing key, title, units or total.

**Worked reminder.** A bar chart's axis starts at 90. Bars show 95 and 100. The second bar looks twice as tall (10 against 5 units above the axis start) although the value is only about 5% larger. Starting at zero fixes this.

**Worked reminder.** A picture of a house is drawn 1.5 times as tall and 1.5 times as wide to show a 50% rise in prices. Its area is 1.5² = 2.25 times the original, suggesting a 125% rise.

## Exam traps

- Reasons that fit any diagram: "it is clearer", "it is easier".
- Suggesting a pie chart for continuous data or for many categories.
- Forgetting that a percentage bar chart and a pie chart hide totals.
- Claiming a box-and-whisker diagram shows the mean.
- Suggesting stem-and-leaf for several hundred values.
- Stating an advantage of X without comparing it with the Y named in the question.

## Quick self-test

1. Which form shows the share of a whole most directly?
2. Give one disadvantage of a pie chart with 14 categories.
3. Two schools of 300 and 1200 pupils: which bar-type chart compares the proportions of pupils travelling by each method?
4. Give one advantage of a sectional bar chart over a percentage bar chart.
5. Give one advantage of a stem-and-leaf diagram over a box-and-whisker diagram.
6. Give one advantage of box-and-whisker diagrams for comparing five classes' test marks.
7. Why is a dual bar chart not ideal for comparing groups of very different sizes?
8. What does a Venn diagram show that a bar chart of the same categories cannot?
9. Why might a table be preferred to a diagram?
10. A bar chart's vertical axis starts at 50. Why is this misleading?
11. A pictogram picture is doubled in height and width. By what factor does its area increase?
12. Name one thing a box-and-whisker diagram does not show.

### Answers

1. A pie chart.
2. Sectors are thin and hard to label or compare.
3. A percentage bar chart.
4. It shows each group's total as well as its parts.
5. It keeps the individual values (and shows the shape in detail).
6. Medians and spreads of all five can be compared at a glance on one scale.
7. It compares counts, which depend on group size; proportions would be fairer.
8. How many items belong to more than one category.
9. It gives exact values for calculation.
10. Differences between bars look larger than they are.
11. By a factor of 4.
12. Any one: individual values, the mean, the number of observations.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many reasons do I need when I choose a diagram?**
One reason per mark, each linked to the data or aim in the question.

**Can I suggest a diagram not named in section 2.2?**
For continuous grouped data a histogram or cumulative frequency graph, from topics 3 and 4, may be appropriate. For section 2.3 questions, choose from the forms the question offers when it gives a list.
