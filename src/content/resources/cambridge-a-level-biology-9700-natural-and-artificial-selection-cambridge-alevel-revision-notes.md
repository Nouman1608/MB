These notes condense section **17.2 Natural and artificial selection** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus (examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027). It is A Level content, examined in Paper 4. For explanations and worked examples, read the [17.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-natural-and-artificial-selection-cambridge-alevel/); for exam-style questions, use the [17.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-natural-and-artificial-selection-cambridge-alevel-practice/).

## Core recall

### Natural selection in six links

1. Overproduction of offspring.
2. Competition for limited resources: the struggle for existence.
3. Variation, caused partly by different alleles.
4. Best adapted individuals more likely to survive and reproduce.
5. They pass on their alleles.
6. Frequency of advantageous alleles increases over generations.

### Forces of selection

| Force | Favours | Mean | Variation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilising | Intermediates | Unchanged | Reduced |
| Directional | One extreme | Shifts | May fall |
| Disruptive | Both extremes | May split into two peaks | Increased |

- Stabilising: stable environment (example: human birth mass).
- Directional: changing environment (example: antibiotic resistance).
- Disruptive: two different niches or backgrounds favour two extremes.

### Chance effects

| Effect | What happens | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic drift | Random change in allele frequency between generations | Greatest in small populations; alleles may be lost or fixed |
| Founder effect | A few individuals start a new isolated population | Allele frequencies differ from the original; some alleles absent |
| Bottleneck effect | Population drastically reduced, then recovers | Low genetic diversity persists |

### Antibiotic resistance

Random mutation → resistance allele → antibiotic kills non-resistant bacteria → resistant bacteria survive and divide by binary fission → allele frequency increases; plasmids can transfer resistance between bacteria.

### Hardy–Weinberg

```
p + q = 1
p² + 2pq + q² = 1
```

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| p | Frequency of the dominant allele |
| q | Frequency of the recessive allele |
| p² | Homozygous dominant frequency |
| 2pq | Heterozygous (carrier) frequency |
| q² | Homozygous recessive frequency |

**Method**: q² from the recessive phenotype → q = √q² → p = 1 − q → 2pq and p².

**Conditions**: large population; random mating; no mutation; no migration; no selection.

### Selective breeding

- Principles: choose individuals with desired traits → breed them → select best offspring → repeat for many generations.
- Humans are the selective agent; genetic diversity usually falls.

| Example | Key points |
|---|---|
| Wheat and rice disease resistance | Cross high-yield variety with resistant variety; test offspring with the pathogen; select resistant, high-yield plants; backcross and repeat |
| Maize | Inbreeding → homozygous, uniform inbred lines with low vigour; crossing two inbred lines → heterozygous, uniform, vigorous F1 hybrids; buy new seed each year |
| Dairy cattle | Progeny testing of bulls by daughters' milk yield; best bulls used for artificial insemination of high-yielding cows |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Selective agent | Environmental factor that makes some phenotypes more likely to survive and reproduce |
| Allele frequency | Proportion of all copies of a gene in a population that are one particular allele |
| Gene pool | All the alleles of all the genes in a population |
| Genetic drift | Random change in allele frequencies, important in small populations |
| Founder effect | Loss of genetic variation when a new population is started by a few individuals |
| Bottleneck | Sharp reduction in population size that reduces genetic diversity |
| Inbreeding depression | Reduced vigour from homozygosity of harmful recessive alleles |
| Hybrid vigour | Increased vigour of offspring that are heterozygous at many loci |
| Progeny testing | Judging an animal's genetic value from the performance of its offspring |

## Exam traps

- Organisms do not "adapt on purpose"; **allele frequencies** change.
- The antibiotic does not cause the mutation; it **selects** existing resistant bacteria.
- Carriers = **2pq**, not p².
- Always start Hardy–Weinberg from **q²**, unless genotypes can be counted directly.
- Drift is **random** in direction; selection is consistent and linked to the environment.
- Inbred lines are homozygous; F1 hybrids are heterozygous.
- Bulls are judged by their **daughters**, because bulls produce no milk.
- "Survival of the fittest" alone scores little: add reproduction and passing on alleles.

## Self-test

1. What is meant by the "struggle for existence"?
2. Which type of selection keeps the mean the same but reduces variation?
3. Give an example of directional selection.
4. Why does genetic drift have a bigger effect in small populations?
5. What is the founder effect?
6. Outline how a population of bacteria becomes resistant to an antibiotic.
7. In a population, 9 % show a recessive phenotype. Calculate q.
8. Using your answer to 7, calculate the frequency of heterozygotes.
9. State three conditions needed for the Hardy–Weinberg principle to apply.
10. Why are maize inbred lines uniform?
11. Why do farmers buy new hybrid maize seed every year?
12. How is a bull selected for breeding to improve milk yield?

### Answers

1. Competition between the many offspring produced for limited resources, together with predation and disease, so not all survive.
2. Stabilising selection.
3. Selection for antibiotic-resistant bacteria when an antibiotic is used.
4. Chance events (which individuals happen to reproduce) affect a larger proportion of the gene pool.
5. A few individuals start a new population, so its allele frequencies differ from the original population and some alleles may be missing.
6. A random mutation gives resistance; the antibiotic kills non-resistant bacteria; resistant ones survive, reproduce and pass on the allele, so its frequency rises.
7. q² = 0.09, so q = **0.3**.
8. p = 0.7; 2pq = 2 × 0.7 × 0.3 = **0.42**.
9. Any three: large population; random mating; no mutation; no migration; no selection.
10. They are homozygous at most loci, so all plants are genetically almost identical.
11. Seed from F1 hybrids gives a variable F2 generation, losing uniformity and vigour.
12. By progeny testing: the milk yields of his daughters are recorded, and bulls with high-yielding daughters are chosen.
