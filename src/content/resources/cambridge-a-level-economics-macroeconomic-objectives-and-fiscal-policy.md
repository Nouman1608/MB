## 5.1.1 Policy objectives

A government has three macroeconomic objectives in this unit.

- **Price stability**: low and steady inflation, so that the price level is predictable. The aim is usually a low target rate.
- **Low unemployment**: few people who are able, willing and looking for work are without a job.
- **Economic growth**: a rise in real GDP over time.

Three types of policy can pursue these objectives: fiscal policy (the budget, this unit), monetary policy (interest rates and the money supply, section 5.3) and supply-side policy (section 5.4). Fiscal measures link to each objective as follows.

| Objective | Typical problem | Fiscal response |
|---|---|---|
| Price stability | Demand-pull inflation | Lower government spending or higher taxes, so AD grows more slowly |
| Low unemployment | Cyclical unemployment in a recession | Higher spending or lower taxes, so AD rises and firms hire |
| Economic growth | Slow growth in output | Short run: raise AD. Long run: capital spending and tax incentives that raise capacity |

### Worked example: checking progress on the objectives

Country A has a target inflation rate of 2%. The consumer price index (CPI) rises from 104.0 to 106.6.

Inflation = (106.6 − 104.0) ÷ 104.0 × 100 = **2.5%**, which is above target.

Nominal GDP rises by 6.3% while prices rise by 2.5%. Real growth is 1.063 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = 0.0371, so real GDP grows by **3.7%**. Dividing by the price index removes the price effect; subtracting 2.5 from 6.3 gives 3.8, which is only an approximation.

The labour force is 32 million and 1.6 million are unemployed. The unemployment rate = 1.6 ÷ 32 × 100 = **5.0%**. If 0.8 million more people find work and the labour force stays the same, the rate falls to 0.8 ÷ 32 × 100 = **2.5%**.

## 5.2.1 and 5.2.2 The budget: deficit and surplus

The **government budget** is the plan of government revenue and government spending for a period, usually a year. Revenue comes mainly from taxes. Spending covers current items and capital items.

- **Budget deficit**: spending is greater than revenue. The government must borrow the difference.
- **Budget surplus**: revenue is greater than spending. The government can repay debt or build up reserves.
- **Balanced budget**: revenue equals spending.

### Worked example: Country A's budget (cu billion)

| Revenue | | Spending | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | 310 | Current | 560 |
| Corporation tax | 90 | Capital | 130 |
| Sales tax | 150 | | |
| Excise duties | 60 | | |
| Other | 40 | | |
| **Total** | **650** | **Total** | **690** |

Budget balance = 650 − 690 = **−40**, a deficit of 40. With GDP of 2,600, the deficit is 40 ÷ 2,600 × 100 = **1.5% of GDP**. Direct taxes (income and corporation) are 400 ÷ 650 = **61.5%** of revenue.

Next year revenue is 700 and spending 680. The balance is +20, a **surplus**.

In a recession a deficit can widen without a policy change, because tax receipts fall and benefits rise.

## 5.2.3 The national debt

The **national debt** is the total amount the government owes, built up from past borrowing. A deficit is a flow in one year; the debt is a stock that adds up those flows. A surplus used to repay lenders reduces the debt.

Country A starts the year owing 900. After a deficit of 40 the debt is 900 + 40 = **940**, which is 940 ÷ 2,600 × 100 = **36.2% of GDP**. The ratio matters more than the raw sum. If the next year ends with a surplus of 20 that is used to repay lenders, the debt falls to 920.

**Why the debt matters** (its significance):

- **Interest payments** are an opportunity cost. At an average interest rate of 3.5% the bill is 0.035 × 940 = 32.9 cu billion, or 5.1% of revenue. That money cannot go to schools or roads.
- **Future taxes** may need to rise, or other spending fall, to pay lenders.
- Debt owed to **foreign lenders** sends interest abroad. Debt owed to domestic lenders stays in the economy.
- A large, rising debt can make lenders ask for **higher interest**.

## 5.2.4 Taxation

### Types

- **Direct tax**: levied on the income or profit of the person or firm that pays it to the government, such as income tax or corporation tax.
- **Indirect tax**: added to the price of purchases and handed over by the seller, such as a sales tax or an excise duty.

By how the tax burden changes with income:

- **Progressive**: the tax takes a higher proportion of income as income rises.
- **Proportional**: the same proportion at all incomes.
- **Regressive**: a lower proportion as income rises.

### Marginal and average rates

- **Average rate of taxation (art)** = total tax ÷ total income × 100.
- **Marginal rate of taxation (mrt)** = change in tax ÷ change in income × 100. It is the rate on the next unit of income.

A tax is progressive when the average rate rises with income, which happens when the marginal rate is above the average rate.

### Worked example: Country A's income tax

The first 10,000 cu of income is taxed at 0%. Income from 10,000 to 40,000 is taxed at 20%. Income above 40,000 is taxed at 40%.

| Income | Tax | Average rate | Marginal rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 0 | 0% | 20% on the next unit |
| 25,000 | 3,000 | 12.0% | 20% |
| 40,000 | 6,000 | 15.0% | 20% up to 40,000 |
| 60,000 | 14,000 | 23.3% | 40% |
| 100,000 | 30,000 | 30.0% | 40% |

At 60,000: tax = 0.20 × 30,000 + 0.40 × 20,000 = 6,000 + 8,000 = 14,000. Average rate = 14,000 ÷ 60,000 = **23.3%**; the marginal rate is **40%**. The average rate climbs as income climbs, so the tax is **progressive**.

### Regressive and proportional examples

- **Proportional**: a flat 15% on all income takes 3,000 from 20,000 and 9,000 from 60,000, which is 15% of each.
- **Regressive**: a fixed annual licence fee of 120 takes 120 ÷ 20,000 = 0.6% of one income and 120 ÷ 60,000 = 0.2% of another.
- **Indirect taxes** can be regressive in effect. Household P (income 20,000) spends 16,000 on taxed goods and pays 10% of that, which is 1,600 or **8%** of income. Household R (income 80,000) spends 40,000 on taxed goods, pays 4,000, and loses **5%** of income. The rate is the same, but the burden is heavier on the poorer household because it spends more of its income.

### Reasons for taxation

- **Raise revenue** to pay for spending.
- **Redistribute income**, using progressive taxes.
- **Discourage demerit goods** or activities with negative externalities, by raising their price.
- **Influence aggregate demand** as part of fiscal policy.

## 5.2.5 Government spending

- **Current spending** is on goods and services used up in the year: wages of public employees, benefits, debt interest, and day-to-day running costs.
- **Capital (investment) spending** adds to the stock of assets that last: roads, schools, hospitals, power lines.

In Country A capital spending is 130 ÷ 690 × 100 = **18.8%** of the total.

**Reasons for government spending:**

- **Provide public goods and merit goods** that markets under-provide.
- **Redistribute income** through benefits and subsidised services.
- **Support growth** by building infrastructure and funding education, which raise productive capacity.
- **Influence aggregate demand**, to move output toward full employment.

## 5.2.6 Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy

- **Expansionary fiscal policy** raises AD by increasing government spending, cutting taxes, or both. It usually makes a deficit larger or a surplus smaller.
- **Contractionary fiscal policy** lowers AD by cutting spending, raising taxes, or both. It usually makes a deficit smaller or a surplus larger.

A rise of 60 in spending takes Country A's deficit from 40 to 100 before any feedback, because higher output would bring in some extra tax.

## 5.2.7 AD/AS analysis

Country A has AD: Y = 1,800 − 6P and short-run aggregate supply SRAS: Y = 600 + 6P, with Y in cu billion and P an index. Full employment output is 1,300, and SRAS becomes vertical there. Equilibrium: 1,800 − 6P = 600 + 6P, so P = **100** and Y = **1,200**. Assume each worker produces 40,000 cu a year, so employment = Y ÷ 40 = 30 million.

**1. Expansionary policy with spare capacity.** Spending rises by 60, shifting AD to Y = 1,860 − 6P. Then 1,260 = 12P, so P = **105** and Y = **1,230**.

- Prices up 5.0%, real output up 2.5%, employment 1,230 ÷ 40 = 30.75 million, a rise of **0.75 million**.

**2. Expansionary policy at full employment.** A rise of 240 gives AD: Y = 2,040 − 6P. The intersection with the vertical part of SRAS is Y = 1,300, so P = (2,040 − 1,300) ÷ 6 = **123.3**. Output rises only 8.3% (to capacity), but prices rise **23.3%**.

**3. Contractionary policy.** Spending falls by 90, shifting AD to Y = 1,710 − 6P. Then 1,110 = 12P, so P = **92.5** and Y = **1,155**. Prices fall 7.5%, output falls 3.75%, and employment falls by 1.125 million.

**Chain of reasoning (expansionary):** spending rises, so AD shifts right; with spare capacity firms raise output and hire, so output and employment rise with a small price rise; tax receipts rise, partly offsetting the larger deficit.

**Tax cut or spending rise?** If households spend 70% of extra income, a tax cut of 100 raises AD by 70 at first, whereas spending of 100 raises it by the full 100. The tax cut shifts AD to Y = 1,870 − 6P, giving P = 105.8 and Y = 1,235; the spending rise gives AD: Y = 1,900 − 6P, so P = 108.3 and Y = 1,250.

### Evaluation

- **Spare capacity**: nearer full employment, more of the effect falls on prices.
- **Time lags**: spending takes time to plan and to build.
- **Leakages**: saving and imports reduce the rise in demand.
- **Debt**: a larger deficit adds to the national debt and later interest.
- **Capital spending** may shift long-run supply, helping growth without raising inflation.
- **Judgement**: expansion suits cyclical unemployment; contraction suits demand-pull inflation.

## Common errors

- Confusing the deficit (one year) with the national debt (accumulated stock).
- Calling a tax progressive because the amount of tax rises with income. It must take a higher proportion.
- Using the marginal rate when asked for the average rate.
- Shifting AD the wrong way, or showing a price rise when the economy has plenty of spare capacity.
