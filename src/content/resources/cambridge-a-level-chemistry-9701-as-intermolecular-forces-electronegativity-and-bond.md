This guide covers subtopic **3.6, Intermolecular forces, electronegativity and bond properties**, from Topic 3, Chemical bonding, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The [Chemical Bonding: Shapes and Intermolecular Forces](/resources/as-chemical-bonding-shapes-and-intermolecular-forces/) guide introduces the force types; this page concentrates on the skill most questions test: **comparing boiling points** by deciding which forces are present and which is strongest. Condensed notes are in the [intermolecular forces revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-intermolecular-forces-electronegativity-and-bond-revision-notes/), and you can practise with the [intermolecular forces practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-intermolecular-forces-electronegativity-and-bond-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

- describe **hydrogen bonding**, limited to molecules containing N–H and O–H groups, including ammonia and water
- use hydrogen bonding to explain the **anomalous properties of water**: its relatively high melting and boiling points, its relatively high surface tension, and the density of ice compared with liquid water
- use **electronegativity** to explain **bond polarity** and **dipole moments** of molecules
- describe **van der Waals' forces** as the intermolecular forces between molecular entities other than those due to bond formation, using the term as a **generic** term for all intermolecular forces
- describe the types: **instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id)** forces, also called London dispersion forces, and **permanent dipole–permanent dipole (pd-pd)** forces, including hydrogen bonding
- understand that hydrogen bonding is a **special case of pd-pd** forces where hydrogen is bonded to a highly electronegative atom
- state that, in general, **ionic, covalent and metallic bonding are stronger** than intermolecular forces

## Bond polarity

When two atoms with **different electronegativities** share a pair of electrons, the more electronegative atom pulls the pair towards itself. It gains a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other atom a partial positive charge (δ+). The bond is **polar**. The larger the electronegativity difference, the more polar the bond. A bond between identical atoms, as in Cl₂, is non-polar.

## Dipole moments of molecules

A molecule has a **permanent dipole moment** (is polar) only if its bond dipoles **do not cancel**. Whether they cancel depends on the **shape** (subtopic 3.5).

| Molecule | Polar bonds? | Shape | Bond dipoles cancel? | Polar molecule? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ | yes (C=O) | linear | yes | no |
| BF₃ | yes | trigonal planar | yes | no |
| CCl₄ | yes | tetrahedral | yes | no |
| SF₆ | yes | octahedral | yes | no |
| CHCl₃ | yes | tetrahedral, but with one different atom | no | yes |
| NH₃ | yes | pyramidal | no | yes |
| H₂O | yes | non-linear | no | yes |

The pattern: a **symmetrical** molecule with identical atoms around the centre is non-polar even when its bonds are polar. A lone pair on the central atom, or a mix of different surrounding atoms, usually produces a polar molecule.

## The two types of van der Waals' force

**Van der Waals' forces** is the umbrella term for all intermolecular forces. The syllabus divides them into two types.

### Instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id) forces

Electrons in a molecule are always moving. At any instant their distribution may be uneven, creating an **instantaneous dipole**. This induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule by repelling or attracting its electrons. The two dipoles attract. The dipoles constantly disappear and re-form, but the attraction is always present on average.

id-id forces act between **all** molecules, polar or not. They are stronger when:

- the molecule has **more electrons** (a larger electron cloud is more easily distorted), and
- the molecules have a **larger surface area of contact**.

The halogens show the first factor clearly. Boiling points rise from fluorine (−188 °C) to chlorine (−34 °C) to bromine (59 °C); iodine is a solid at room temperature. The second factor explains isomers: pentane boils at 36 °C, while its compact isomer 2,2-dimethylpropane, with the same number of electrons, boils at about 10 °C, because the near-spherical molecules touch over a smaller area.

### Permanent dipole–permanent dipole (pd-pd) forces

Polar molecules have permanent dipoles. The δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of its neighbour. These forces act **in addition to** id-id forces.

A fair comparison holds the id-id contribution roughly constant. Hydrogen chloride and fluorine each have 18 electrons, so their id-id forces are similar. HCl boils at −85 °C, far above fluorine at −188 °C, because HCl molecules are polar and also attract each other by pd-pd forces. F₂ is non-polar.

### Hydrogen bonding, a special case of pd-pd forces

When hydrogen is bonded to a highly electronegative atom (in this syllabus, N or O), the bond is very polar and the hydrogen carries a large δ+ charge. Hydrogen has no inner electrons, so its nucleus is barely shielded. It is strongly attracted to a **lone pair** on an N or O atom of a neighbouring molecule. This is a **hydrogen bond**.

To describe or draw a hydrogen bond, include:

- the δ+ on H and δ− on N or O
- the **lone pair** on the N or O atom of the second molecule
- the hydrogen bond drawn as a dashed line from the H to that lone pair
- the three atoms (N/O–H···N/O) roughly in a **straight line**

## Water and ammonia compared

Water forms more hydrogen bonds per molecule than ammonia. Each H₂O molecule has **two** δ+ hydrogen atoms and **two** lone pairs, so on average each molecule takes part in two hydrogen bonds. NH₃ has three hydrogen atoms but only **one** lone pair, so it can form only one hydrogen bond per molecule on average. This is why water (100 °C) boils much higher than ammonia (−33 °C).

## The anomalous properties of water

Compare the Group 16 hydrides: H₂S −60 °C, H₂Se −41 °C, H₂Te −2 °C. From H₂S to H₂Te the boiling point rises with the number of electrons (stronger id-id forces). By that trend water should boil well below −60 °C. It boils at 100 °C because of **hydrogen bonding**. The Group 14 hydrides have no hydrogen bonding and rise smoothly: CH₄ −162 °C, SiH₄ −112 °C, GeH₄ −88 °C, SnH₄ −52 °C.

- **High melting and boiling points**: extra energy is needed to overcome hydrogen bonds, which are the strongest intermolecular force.
- **High surface tension**: molecules at the surface are pulled inwards and sideways by hydrogen bonds to neighbours, with no balancing pull from above, so the surface behaves like a stretched skin.
- **Ice is less dense than water**: in ice every molecule is hydrogen-bonded to four others in a regular three-dimensional arrangement that holds the molecules further apart than in the liquid. When ice melts, some hydrogen bonds break and molecules move closer together, so the liquid is denser.

## Worked example: ranking boiling points

**Question.** Place propane (C₃H₈, 26 electrons), ethanol (C₂H₅OH, 26 electrons) and methoxymethane (CH₃OCH₃, 26 electrons) in order of increasing boiling point. Explain your order. [4]

**Answer.** Propane < methoxymethane < ethanol.

- All three have the same number of electrons, so their id-id forces are similar. *(one mark)*
- Propane is non-polar: id-id forces only. *(one mark)*
- Methoxymethane is polar (C–O bonds, non-linear around O) but has no O–H group, so it has pd-pd forces but no hydrogen bonds. *(one mark)*
- Ethanol has an O–H group, so it forms hydrogen bonds, the strongest intermolecular force. *(one mark)*

(For reference: propane boils at −42 °C and ethanol at 78 °C.)

## Bonds versus intermolecular forces

In general, **ionic, covalent and metallic bonds are stronger** than intermolecular forces. When a simple molecular substance melts or boils, only the intermolecular forces are overcome; the covalent bonds inside the molecules stay intact. Boiling water produces H₂O molecules, not hydrogen and oxygen.

## Common mistakes

- Saying non-polar molecules have "no intermolecular forces". All molecules have id-id forces.
- Treating van der Waals' forces as only the weakest type. In this syllabus, it is the umbrella term.
- Explaining a polarity difference without referring to **shape** and whether dipoles cancel.
- Comparing molecules with very different numbers of electrons and putting the difference down to pd-pd forces alone.
- Drawing a hydrogen bond without the lone pair, or to a hydrogen atom bonded to carbon.
- Explaining ice's density by "hydrogen bonds are strong" rather than by the **open, ordered arrangement**.
- Writing that boiling breaks covalent bonds in a simple molecular substance.

## Exam technique

- For "explain the difference in boiling points": name the forces in **each** substance, state which is stronger and why, then link to the energy needed to separate molecules.
- For "describe hydrogen bonding": use a labelled diagram with partial charges, lone pair and a linear arrangement.
- For polarity: give the electronegativity reason for polar bonds, then use shape to say whether dipoles cancel.
