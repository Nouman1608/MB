Assignment A of 4ET1 Component 3 is one coursework essay of about 650–800 words on a modern drama set
text, marked out of 30 (AO1 15, AO2 15). Section A practises stagecraft analysis on a short scene
written for this page. Section B gives full assignment tasks in the specification's style, with
indicative content. Choose the task on the play you have studied.

## Section A: Stagecraft skills

Read this original scene, then answer Questions 1–6.

> *A railway platform at dawn. Benches, a single lamp. Grey light. LENA, sixteen, sits on a suitcase.
> Her grandfather ARVID stands apart, facing the empty track.*
>
> LENA: The train's late.
>
> ARVID: It's never late. We're early.
>
> *A whistle, far off. LENA stands. ARVID does not turn.*
>
> LENA: You could come with me.
>
> ARVID: *(still facing the track)* Somebody has to keep the house warm.
>
> *The whistle again, closer. The lamp flickers and goes out. In the darkness, the sound of the
> train arriving.*

**1.** State how the 30 marks for Assignment A are divided, and explain why a paragraph on the
historical background of a play earns no separate credit. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two stage directions in the scene and explain the effect of each on an audience.
**[4]**

**3.** Explain one thing an audience in a theatre would experience in the final stage direction that
a reader of the script might not. **[2]**

**4.** Rewrite this narrative sentence as an analytical one: "Arvid doesn't turn round when Lena
talks to him." **[3]**

**5.** A teacher offers two possible tasks: (a) "Write about *An Inspector Calls*." (b) "Explore the
ways in which Priestley presents the generation gap in *An Inspector Calls*. You must consider
language, form and structure." Explain which is the better Component 3 task and why. **[2]**

**6.** Explain what "form" means when writing about a play for AO2. **[2]**

## Section B: Assignment A tasks (30 marks each)

**7.** Explore the ways in which Samuels presents the relationship between mothers and daughters in
*Kindertransport*. You must consider language, form and structure. **[30]**

**8.** How does Stephens use staging to present Christopher's experience of the world in *The Curious
Incident of the Dog in the Night-time*? You must consider language, form and structure. **[30]**

**9.** Explore the ways in which Soyinka presents Elesin's failure in *Death and the King's Horseman*.
You must consider language, form and structure. **[30]**

**10.** "Catherine is the character who changes most in *A View from the Bridge*." To what extent do you
agree? You must consider language, form and structure. **[30]**

## Answers

**1.** AO1 15, AO2 15 [1]. Context (AO4) is assessed only in Assignment B, so background earns credit
only if it supports a point about the play's methods or meaning [1].

**2.** Any two, 1 mark for the direction and 1 for its effect:

- "Her grandfather ARVID stands apart, facing the empty track": the physical distance shows the
  emotional distance between them before a word is spoken.
- "ARVID does not turn": his refusal to look at Lena suggests he cannot bear the goodbye; the
  audience reads feeling from what he avoids.
- "The whistle again, closer": sound builds tension and marks the time running out.
- "The lamp flickers and goes out": the loss of light makes the parting final and leaves the
  audience unable to see their last moment together.

**3.** In the theatre, the audience is plunged into darkness and hears the train without seeing the
goodbye [1], so they share the characters' loss instead of reading about it [1].

**4.** For example: "The playwright keeps Arvid's back to Lena throughout the scene, so the audience
reads his grief in what he refuses to look at; the stage direction turns silence into feeling." [1
for naming the playwright's choice, 1 for the effect on the audience, 1 for linking to meaning]

**5.** (b) [1]: it names one focus, invites an argument through "explore the ways in which", and
includes the AO2 requirement; (a) gives no focus and invites retelling [1].

**6.** The type of text and how it works as drama [1]: for example staging, stage directions,
entrances and exits, the use of a narrator, act structure and the audience's experience in
performance [1].

### Level guidance for Questions 7–10

Use the five-level grid on a best-fit basis. Level 2 responses are "largely narrative" with some
comment on methods. Level 3 shows "sound" knowledge and clearly relevant examples. Level 4 sustains
analysis of language, form and structure. Level 5 shows an assured, perceptive argument with
"cohesive evaluation" of the playwright's methods. Credit valid alternatives to the points below.

### Question 7: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (15) | Helga sending the young Eva to England for safety; Lil becoming a second mother; Evelyn's guarded, controlling relationship with her own daughter Faith; Helga's return after the war and Eva's rejection of her; Faith's discovery of the past |
| AO2 (15) | Past and present on stage at once, so mothers and daughters from different times share the same space; the attic and its boxes as a structure for slowly revealed memory; the menacing folk-tale figure who links separation with fear; shifts between German and English as a sign of lost identity |

**Model paragraph.** "Samuels never lets the past leave the stage. Because the young Eva and the
adult Evelyn are present at the same time, the audience watches the child's fear and the woman's
coldness side by side, and understands that Evelyn's control over Faith is the scar of her own
separation. The structure makes the cause and the effect visible in one moment."

### Question 8: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (15) | Christopher's investigation into the dog's death; the revelation about his mother and father; his journey to London; his maths exam; his growing independence |
| AO2 (15) | A largely bare, non-naturalistic stage that becomes many places; an ensemble who play objects, crowds and the noise of the station; Siobhan reading from Christopher's book, which turns his narration into theatre; the second half presented as a play Christopher's school stages; moments of sensory overload created through sound and movement |

A strong response argues that the staging places the audience inside Christopher's perception,
rather than only describing the devices.

### Question 9: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (15) | Elesin's confident boasting in the market; his marriage on the day of his ritual death; Pilkings's intervention and Elesin's arrest; Olunde's death in his father's place; Elesin's final despair and death |
| AO2 (15) | Rich poetic language and song in the market scenes contrasted with flat, practical English at the Residency; the Praise-Singer and Iyaloja as voices of the community who judge Elesin; structure that delays the ritual so tension builds; the reversal in which the son fulfils the father's duty |

Context is not assessed here, but a brief reference to the colonial setting is acceptable if it
supports a point about the contrast of settings.

### Question 10: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (15) | Catherine's early dependence on Eddie's approval; her wish to take a job; her love for Rodolpho; her confrontation with Eddie after he informs; balance against Eddie's or Beatrice's change to reach a judgement |
| AO2 (15) | Stage directions showing Catherine's eagerness to please early on; her changing speech, from hesitant to direct and angry; the private scene with Rodolpho as a turning point; Alfieri's framing, which keeps Eddie at the centre and so limits how far her change is the play's focus |

A "to what extent" task needs a judgement: for example, that Catherine changes most visibly, but
Eddie's fixed refusal to change is what drives the tragedy.
