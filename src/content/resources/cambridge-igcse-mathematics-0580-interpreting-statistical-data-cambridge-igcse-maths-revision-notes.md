These notes condense **9.2 Interpreting statistical data** from Topic 9 Statistics of the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. The three outcomes are: read, interpret and draw inferences from tables and statistical diagrams; compare sets of data using tables, graphs and statistical measures; and appreciate restrictions on drawing conclusions from given data. Core (C9.2) compares averages and **ranges**; Extended (E9.2) compares averages and **measures of spread**, including the interquartile range. Lines marked **(Extended)** are for Extended candidates only.

Full explanations and worked examples are in the [interpreting statistical data study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-interpreting-statistical-data-cambridge-igcse-maths/). Test yourself afterwards with the [interpreting data practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-interpreting-statistical-data-cambridge-igcse-maths-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Read | Take a value directly from a table or diagram |
| Interpret | Say what a value or pattern means in context |
| Inference | A conclusion the data support but do not state directly |
| Average | A typical value: mean, median or mode |
| Spread | How varied the data are: range, or (Extended) interquartile range |
| Extreme value | A value far from the others, which can distort the mean and the range |
| Proportion | A part of a group as a fraction, percentage or angle of a pie chart |
| Sample | The part of a population from which data were collected |
| Representative | A sample that reflects the whole population fairly |
| Misleading diagram | A chart whose design gives a false impression of the data |

## Before you read any table or diagram

```
1. Title: what is being shown, and about whom?
2. Axes / column headings: what is measured, in what units?
3. Scale: where does each axis start? Is the scale even?
4. Key: what does one symbol or one colour stand for?
5. Totals: how many items in each group?
```

Step 5 matters for every fraction, percentage and comparison.

## True, false or cannot tell

For any statement about a table, ask:

- Does the table contain **this group**? If the statement is about people not surveyed: cannot tell.
- Does it contain **this detail**? Exact values inside a grouped class: cannot tell.
- If both, work it out: true or false, with the numbers that show it.

"Cannot tell" is a valid answer and is often the intended one.

## Writing a comparison

Use this frame for every "compare" question:

```
[Set A]'s [average] ([value]) is higher/lower than [set B]'s ([value]),
so [meaning in context].
[Set A]'s [spread] ([value]) is bigger/smaller than [set B]'s ([value]),
so [set A/B] is more consistent.
```

**Small reminder (Core).** Time to solve a puzzle, in minutes. Group A: mean 12, range 5. Group B: mean 15, range 9.

- Group A's mean (12 minutes) is lower than Group B's (15 minutes), so Group A were quicker on average.
- Group A's range (5 minutes) is smaller than Group B's (9 minutes), so Group A's times were more consistent.

**Small reminder (Extended).** Masses of two rugby teams. Team P: median 64 kg, IQR 8 kg. Team Q: median 71 kg, IQR 15 kg.

- Team Q's median (71 kg) is higher, so a typical Team Q player is heavier.
- Team P's IQR (8 kg) is smaller, so the middle half of Team P's masses are closer together.

## Which average? Which spread?

| Situation | Best average | Best spread |
|---|---|---|
| No extreme values, numerical data | Mean (uses every value) | Range or IQR |
| One or two extreme values | Median | (Extended) IQR |
| Qualitative data | Mode | Not meaningful |
| Most common value needed (sizes, choices) | Mode | Not needed |

Compare like with like: mean with mean, median with median, range with range.

## Misleading diagrams: what to look for

- Vertical axis **not starting at 0**: small differences look large.
- **Uneven** scale on an axis.
- Pictogram symbols of **different sizes**, or a missing key.
- **3D** pie charts or bars, where the front looks bigger.
- **Missing labels or units.**

**Small reminder.** Two bars show 95 and 100 on an axis starting at 90. The bars are 5 and 10 units tall, so one *looks* twice the other. The real increase is 5 ÷ 95 × 100 = 5.26% (3 s.f.).

## Proportion or number?

- Percentages, fractions and pie chart angles are **proportions**.
- To compare **numbers**, multiply each proportion by its own group total.

**Small reminder.** 40% of 150 = 60; 25% of 300 = 75. The smaller percentage gives the bigger number.

## Restrictions on conclusions: the checklist

| Question to ask | Example of a problem |
|---|---|
| Is the sample big enough? | 12 people asked about a whole city |
| Is it representative? | Asking only people outside a gym about exercise |
| Does the claim go beyond the data? | Predicting next year from three months |
| Has detail been lost? | Grouped classes; an average with no spread |
| Is the comparison fair? | Groups of different sizes compared by number |
| Does a link prove a cause? | Two things rising together need not be cause and effect |

## Exam traps

- Saying "true" or "false" when the data cannot decide.
- A comparison with numbers only and no context.
- Comparing only the averages, and forgetting spread (or the other way round).
- "Bigger range so higher scores": range measures spread, not size.
- Using the mean as "typical" when one value is extreme.
- Comparing two pie charts' angles as if they were numbers of people.
- Not noticing that the vertical axis starts above 0.
- Reasons like "the data could be wrong", which are not linked to the context.

## Quick self-test

1. What is the difference between reading a value and making an inference?
2. A table groups times as 10 ≤ t < 20 with frequency 6. Can you tell how many people took exactly 15 minutes?
3. Group A: mean 12, range 5. Group B: mean 15, range 9. Which group is more consistent? Give a reason.
4. Choose the best average for 2, 3, 3, 4, 30. Give a reason.
5. Which average must you use for favourite colours?
6. (Extended) Which measure of spread is not affected by one extreme value?
7. Bars of 95 and 100 are drawn on an axis starting at 90. Why is the chart misleading?
8. Which is larger: 40% of 150 or 25% of 300?
9. In a pie chart for a class of 30, football has 120°. In a pie chart for a class of 24, football has 150°. Which class has more football fans?
10. 50 people outside a gym are asked how often they exercise; 40 exercise every week. Give one reason why "80% of the town exercises every week" may be wrong.
11. Sales rose in January, February and March. Can you be sure they will rise in April?
12. Why is it unfair to compare the mean of one data set with the median of another?

### Answers

1. Reading takes a value directly from the data; an **inference** is a conclusion the data support but do not show directly.
2. **No**: grouping hides the individual values.
3. **Group A**: its range (5) is smaller.
4. **Median (3)**: the mean is pulled up by the extreme value 30.
5. **The mode**: the data are qualitative.
6. **The interquartile range.**
7. The bars show only the amount above 90 (5 and 10), so 100 **looks twice** 95, but it is only 5.26% more.
8. 40% of 150 = 60; 25% of 300 = 75, so **25% of 300**.
9. 120/360 × 30 = 10 and 150/360 × 24 = 10: **the same number (10)**.
10. The sample is **not representative**: people at a gym are more likely to exercise.
11. **No**: that goes beyond the data, and many things could change sales.
12. They measure different things, so a difference could come from the **choice of average**, not the data.
