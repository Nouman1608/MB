Content tested: "Two armed camps and increasing tension between the powers, up to 1913" and "The outbreak of the First World War".

## Section A: knowledge (3-mark questions)

**1.** Give three features of the alliance system in Europe by 1907. **[3]**

**2.** Give three features of the Anglo-German Naval Race. **[3]**

**3.** Give three events in the Balkans between 1908 and 1913 that increased tension. **[3]**

**4.** Give three ways in which the assassination at Sarajevo led to war between Austria-Hungary and Serbia. **[3]**

## Section B: explanation and judgement

**5.** Explain one way in which the Moroccan Crises affected relations between the Great Powers. **[6]**

**6.** Explain one way in which Kaiser Wilhelm II's foreign policy increased tension in Europe. **[6]**

**7.** Explain one way in which the Bosnian Crisis of 1908-09 made war more likely. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which the alliances turned the crisis of July 1914 into a general war. **[9]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why Britain declared war on Germany in August 1914. **[9]**

**10.** "The arms race was the main reason why war broke out in 1914." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**11.** "The July crisis of 1914 failed because Germany wanted a war." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**12.** "Nationalism in the Balkans was more important than rivalry between the Great Powers in causing the First World War." How far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** 3-mark questions: [1] per relevant point. 6-mark: way identified [1], precise knowledge [2], explanation of how it affected the development [2], further developed detail or consequence [1]. 9-mark: each of two ways or reasons [4] (identified [1], precise knowledge [1], explained link [2]) plus [1] for accurate, well-sequenced detail. 12-mark: the given factor [3], at least one other factor [4], comparison in a line of reasoning [3], supported judgement [2].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy (1882); the Franco-Russian Alliance of 1894; the Entente Cordiale between Britain and France (1904); the Anglo-Russian Agreement of 1907; Britain had ended Splendid Isolation; Germany feared encirclement; the alliances divided Europe into two armed camps.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: Germany passed Navy Laws from 1898 under Admiral Tirpitz to build a large fleet; Britain launched HMS Dreadnought in 1906 and Germany copied it; Britain responded by building more ships to keep its lead; the race was costly and increased mistrust; Britain regarded the German fleet as a threat to its empire and trade; it pushed Britain towards France and Russia.

**3.** Any three, [1] each: Austria-Hungary's annexation of Bosnia and Herzegovina in 1908; Russia's humiliation in the Bosnian Crisis; the first Balkan War (1912) in which the Balkan states defeated the Ottoman Empire; the second Balkan War (1913) among the Balkan states; Serbia's gains in 1913; the creation of Albania.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia, as Princip was a Bosnian Serb linked to the Black Hand; Germany promised support (the blank cheque); Austria-Hungary sent an ultimatum on 23 July with ten demands; Serbia accepted most but not all; Austria-Hungary declared war on 28 July.

**5.** Way identified [1]: the crises strengthened the Entente between Britain and France. Precise knowledge [2]: in 1905 the Kaiser visited Tangier and supported Moroccan independence, but at the Algeciras Conference in 1906 most powers backed France; in 1911 the German gunboat *Panther* was sent to Agadir. Explanation [2]: Germany had hoped to split Britain and France, but the crises made Britain regard Germany as a threat, so Britain supported France and held military talks with it. Further development [1]: Germany felt isolated and surrounded, which increased its fear of encirclement.

**6.** Way identified [1]: Weltpolitik and the building of a large navy challenged Britain. Precise knowledge [2]: from 1898 Tirpitz's Navy Laws expanded the German fleet; Wilhelm II wanted "a place in the sun" and overseas colonies. Explanation [2]: Britain saw the fleet as a threat to its security and its empire, so it began to build Dreadnoughts and to settle quarrels with France and Russia. Further development [1]: this created two armed camps and made Britain more likely to support France in a crisis.

**7.** Way identified [1]: it increased the hostility between Austria-Hungary and Serbia and Russia. Precise knowledge [2]: in 1908 Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia and Herzegovina, which contained many Serbs; Serbia protested and Russia was forced to back down in 1909 when Germany supported Austria-Hungary. Explanation [2]: Serbia's resentment grew and secret groups such as the Black Hand were encouraged; Russia was humiliated and determined not to back down in another crisis. Further development [1]: in 1914, Russia was ready to support Serbia.

**8.** Way one [4]: Russia's support for Serbia [1]; Russia saw itself as the protector of the Slavs and mobilised in late July [1]; so a war between Austria-Hungary and Serbia drew in Russia, and Germany saw the mobilisation as a threat [2]. Way two [4]: Germany's alliance with Austria-Hungary [1]; Germany gave a blank cheque and then declared war on Russia (1 August) and on France (3 August) [1]; so the Franco-Russian Alliance brought France in and the war spread across Europe [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example the Schlieffen Plan or dates of the declarations.

**9.** Reason one [4]: the invasion of Belgium [1]; Britain had guaranteed Belgian neutrality in the Treaty of London (1839) and Germany invaded on 4 August [1]; so Britain could not stay out without abandoning a treaty obligation and allowing Germany control of the Channel ports [2]. Reason two [4]: the Entente with France [1]; the Entente Cordiale (1904) and military talks since the Moroccan Crises meant a moral commitment to France [1]; a German victory over France would leave Germany dominant in Europe and threaten Britain [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example the Anglo-German Naval Race.

**10.** Given factor [3]: Germany and Britain built Dreadnoughts; armies expanded in France, Germany and Russia; mistrust increased; military plans such as the Schlieffen Plan depended on speed. Other factors [4]: the alliance system; nationalism in the Balkans and the Black Hand; the Moroccan and Bosnian Crises; the assassination at Sarajevo; Germany's blank cheque. Comparison [3]: the arms race made the powers fearful and ready to fight, but it did not itself start the war; without the assassination and the alliances, an arms race might have continued without war, and the war began in the Balkans, not over the navies. Judgement [2]: for example, the arms race created the conditions for rapid escalation, but the alliances and the Balkan crisis were the more important causes of this war.

**11.** Given factor [3]: Germany gave the blank cheque to Austria-Hungary; it may have hoped for a short war before Russia grew stronger; it declared war on Russia and France; the Schlieffen Plan required an invasion of Belgium. Other factors [4]: Austria-Hungary's ultimatum was designed to be hard to meet; Serbia's refusal of one demand; Russia's mobilisation; French support for Russia; Britain's failure to warn Germany; the Kaiser's last-minute attempts to restrain Austria came too late. Comparison [3]: Germany's role was crucial because of the blank cheque and the Schlieffen Plan, but other powers made decisions that made a settlement hard, so Germany was one of several responsible. Judgement [2]: for example, Germany bears a major share of the blame for turning a local dispute into a European war, but Austria-Hungary began the crisis and Russia's response widened it.

**12.** Given factor [3]: Serbian nationalism and Slav nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary; the Black Hand; Princip's assassination; the Bosnian Crisis and the Balkan Wars. Other factors [4]: rivalry between Britain and Germany; the naval race; colonial rivalry and the Moroccan Crises; the alliance system; the Schlieffen Plan. Comparison [3]: nationalism started the crisis in the Balkans, but it was the great-power alliances and plans that turned a regional dispute into a world war; without those rivalries, Austria-Hungary and Serbia might have fought a local war. Judgement [2]: for example, nationalism supplied the spark, but great-power rivalry explains why the fire spread, so rivalry between the Great Powers was the more important.
