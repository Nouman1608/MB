## Cash-flow

**Cash-flow** is the movement of money into and out of a business over a period. **Cash inflows** (receipts) include payments from customers, loans received and the sale of assets. **Cash outflows** (payments) include paying suppliers, wages, rent, loan repayments and buying equipment. **Net cash flow** is inflows minus outflows for the period. The **opening balance** plus net cash flow gives the **closing balance**, which becomes the next period's opening balance.

A business must be able to pay its bills as they fall due. Even a business with plenty of orders can fail if cash runs out first, which is why cash is called the lifeblood of a business.

## Forecast and statement

| | Cash-flow forecast | Cash-flow statement |
|---|---|---|
| Looks at | The future | The past |
| Based on | Estimates of sales, costs and timing | Actual cash movements |
| Purpose | Plan for shortages and surpluses; support a loan application; set targets | Report where cash came from and went; satisfy owners, lenders and the law |
| Layout | Monthly columns of receipts, payments, net flow, opening and closing balance | Cash from operating activities, investing activities and financing activities |
| Used by | Managers, lenders | Owners, investors, lenders, managers |

## Cash and profit

| | Profit | Cash-flow |
|---|---|---|
| Based on | Revenue earned less costs incurred in the period | Money actually received and paid |
| Credit sales | Counted when the sale is made | Counted when the customer pays |
| Buying equipment | Spread over its life as depreciation | The full price leaves in the month it is paid |
| Loans | Not income; interest is a cost | The loan is an inflow when received; repayments are outflows |
| Depreciation | A cost | Not a cash movement |

A profitable business can run out of cash if customers pay slowly, it builds up stock, or it buys equipment. A loss-making business may have cash for a while, for example if it has just taken out a loan.

### Worked example 1: a six-month forecast

Marlow Bikes has an opening balance of £8,000 on 1 January. Half of each month's sales are paid for in that month and half in the next. December sales were £28,000. Stock purchases, wages and overheads are paid in the month. Wages are £7,000 and overheads £5,000 each month. A loan repayment of £4,000 is made in March and June, and £12,000 is paid for equipment in April.

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales (invoiced) | £30,000 | £34,000 | £40,000 | £28,000 | £26,000 | £30,000 |
| Receipts | £29,000 | £32,000 | £37,000 | £34,000 | £27,000 | £28,000 |
| Stock purchases | £18,000 | £20,000 | £22,000 | £16,000 | £15,000 | £17,000 |
| Wages and overheads | £12,000 | £12,000 | £12,000 | £12,000 | £12,000 | £12,000 |
| Loan repayment | | | £4,000 | | | £4,000 |
| Equipment | | | | £12,000 | | |
| Total payments | £30,000 | £32,000 | £38,000 | £40,000 | £27,000 | £33,000 |
| Net cash flow | −£1,000 | £0 | −£1,000 | −£6,000 | £0 | −£5,000 |
| Opening balance | £8,000 | £7,000 | £7,000 | £6,000 | £0 | £0 |
| Closing balance | £7,000 | £7,000 | £6,000 | £0 | £0 | −£5,000 |

Working for January receipts: half of January sales (£15,000) plus half of December sales (£14,000) = **£29,000**. For April: half of April sales (£14,000) plus half of March sales (£20,000) = **£34,000**.

Interpretation: total sales in the six months are £188,000 and the business is trading, but the closing balance falls to zero in April and to **−£5,000** in June. The forecast gives early warning of a problem in June, with plenty of time to act. The causes are the equipment purchase in April and the loan repayments, which are not costs in the profit calculation in the same way.

### Worked example 2: a fall in sales

Suppose sales in March, April and May turn out 10% below forecast (£36,000, £25,200 and £23,400). Receipts are then £35,000 in March, £30,600 in April, £24,300 in May and £26,700 in June. Payments stay the same, because stock was already bought.

| | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net cash flow | −£3,000 | −£9,400 | −£2,700 | −£6,300 |
| Closing balance | £4,000 | −£5,400 | −£8,100 | −£14,400 |

The overdraft needed rises from £5,000 to **£14,400**, an increase of £9,400. The effect of a fall in revenue lasts for several months, because half of each month's sales are not paid until the next month. A rise in costs would have the same direction of effect but would be felt at once, since costs are paid in the month.

### Worked example 3: the cash-flow statement

Hartwell Joinery (a different business) reports a profit before tax of £60,000. In the year: depreciation was £12,000; receivables rose by £15,000; inventory rose by £8,000; payables rose by £5,000; it bought equipment for £40,000; it received a new loan of £20,000; it paid dividends of £10,000. Opening cash was £6,000. (Interest and tax are ignored for simplicity.)

| | £ |
|---|---|
| Profit before tax | 60,000 |
| Add depreciation (not a cash cost) | 12,000 |
| Less increase in receivables | −15,000 |
| Less increase in inventory | −8,000 |
| Add increase in payables | 5,000 |
| **Cash from operating activities** | **54,000** |
| Purchase of equipment | −40,000 |
| **Cash from investing activities** | **−40,000** |
| New loan | 20,000 |
| Dividends paid | −10,000 |
| **Cash from financing activities** | **10,000** |
| **Net change in cash** | **24,000** |
| Opening cash | 6,000 |
| **Closing cash** | **30,000** |

Profit was £60,000, but cash rose by only £24,000. The difference comes mainly from the equipment purchase and the money tied up in receivables and inventory. Reading the statement tells lenders and owners whether the business generates cash from trading (£54,000 from operations) or depends on borrowing.

## Evaluating forecasts and statements

**Usefulness of forecasts.** They warn of shortages, show when to arrange finance, help decide whether a purchase is affordable, support loan applications and allow "what if" tests like Example 2. **Limits:** they rely on estimates of sales and payment timing, which are often wrong, especially for new businesses; they can be time-consuming; they must be updated; and the forecast can give false comfort if assumptions are too optimistic.

**Usefulness of statements.** They show what really happened and whether cash came from trading, borrowing or selling assets. **Limits:** they are historic, so they cannot prevent a problem, and they give less detail on the timing within the year.

**Impact on stakeholders.** Lenders use the forecast to decide whether to lend and on what terms; suppliers may judge whether to offer credit; employees depend on the business paying wages; owners need to know whether dividends can be paid.

## Strategies to overcome forecast cash-flow problems

### Worked example 4: choosing a strategy

Marlow Bikes' base forecast shows a June closing balance of −£5,000 and zero in April and May. Options:

| Strategy | Effect on the forecast | Cost or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Overdraft of £10,000 | Covers the shortfall in every month | Interest; bank may refuse or withdraw it |
| Lease the equipment at £1,000 a month from April instead of paying £12,000 | Closing balance rises to £11,000 in April, £10,000 in May and £4,000 in June | Higher total cost over time; payments continue; no ownership |
| Speed up payments: customers pay 70% in month rather than 50% | Cash rises in early months (£8,400 in March), but June is still −£4,600 | Discounts may be needed; customers may refuse |
| Delay paying suppliers | Short-term relief | Damage to relationships; loss of discounts |
| Reduce stock purchases | Cuts payments | Stock-outs, lost sales |
| Sale and leaseback of an asset | Raises cash | Rent paid in future; lose ownership |
| Cut costs or delay spending | Immediate | May harm sales or quality |

Leasing solves the problem almost completely: the June balance is +£4,000. Faster collection helps only slightly: it brings cash forward by a month but does not add any, so the equipment payment and loan repayments still leave a gap. An overdraft is flexible but costs interest; if Marlow pays 12% a year on an average of £5,000 for three months, the cost is about £150. Judgement: leasing is best for the equipment, because the problem is a one-off large purchase; an overdraft facility should be arranged as a safety net for the sales risk shown in Example 2. This would change if the lease terms were poor.

## Common mistakes

- Treating profit as cash.
- Putting credit sales in the month of sale in a cash forecast.
- Forgetting that opening balance equals the previous closing balance.
- Counting depreciation as a cash outflow.
- Suggesting a strategy without a cost or risk.

## Exam technique

Show receipts and payments for each month, then net flow, opening and closing balance. For "analyse", comment on the lowest balance and when it occurs. For "evaluate strategies", give the effect, a cost or risk, and a judgement tied to the cause of the problem.
