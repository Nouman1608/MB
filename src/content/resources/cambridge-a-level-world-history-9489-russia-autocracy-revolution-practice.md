## Section A

**1.** Identify three institutions on which Tsarist autocracy relied in the 1880s. **[3]**

**2.** State two effects of Russification on the non-Russian peoples of the Empire. **[2]**

**3.** Give three reasons why Marxism attracted support in Russia by the 1890s. **[3]**

**4.** Identify two ways in which the Fundamental Laws of 1906 limited the Duma. **[2]**

**5.** State three weaknesses of the Tsarist government during the First World War. **[3]**

**6.** Identify three decrees issued by Sovnarkom in its first weeks. **[3]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why the Bolsheviks closed the Constituent Assembly in January 1918. **[4]**

## Section B

**8.** Explain why Stolypin's agrarian reforms had only limited success by 1914. **[10]**

**9.** "The failure to end the war was the main reason for the collapse of the Provisional Government." How far do you agree? **[20]**

**10.** Read the two invented sources below. Compare Sources A and B as evidence about the October Manifesto.

*Source A (invented)* -- From a liberal newspaper in Moscow, October 1905. "Russia awoke this morning a constitutional state. The Tsar has granted what the nation demanded: freedom of conscience, speech and assembly, and a Duma without whose consent no law shall stand. The task of every patriot now is to make these promises real, peacefully and lawfully."

*Source B (invented)* -- From a leaflet issued by a workers' committee in St Petersburg, October 1905. "Do not be deceived. The same Tsar who shot us in January now offers paper. Who will enforce these freedoms? The police and the Cossacks still stand in the streets. The general strike must continue until there is a constituent assembly elected by all the people." **[15]**

**11.** Read the two further invented sources below, together with Sources A and B in Question 10. How far do Sources A to D support the view that the Tsarist regime survived 1905 because its opponents were divided?

*Source C (invented)* -- From a report by a provincial governor to the Minister of the Interior, December 1905. "The troops returning from Manchuria have restored order in the district. The peasant disturbances on the estates have been put down and the ringleaders tried by field court. The garrison is reliable."

*Source D (invented)* -- From the memoirs of a former Duma deputy, published abroad in 1925. "In the autumn of 1905 we liberals took the Manifesto and went home to form parties. The workers went on striking alone, and in December they rose alone in Moscow. The government had only to wait and then strike them down one by one." **[25]**

**12.** Explain why the Kronstadt rising led to the introduction of the New Economic Policy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Tsar himself, with unlimited authority (1); the appointed State Council and ministers answerable only to him (1); the army, used against internal disorder (1); the Orthodox Church, which taught obedience (1); the bureaucracy and police, including the Okhrana (1). **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: Russian was enforced in schools and government, provoking resentment among Poles, Finns, Ukrainians and Baltic peoples (1); Jews faced pogroms and the May Laws of 1882, and many emigrated or joined revolutionary groups (1); national grievances grew that fed later opposition, especially in 1905 (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: industrialisation created a growing factory working class (1); harsh working and living conditions in the cities made workers receptive (1); the failure of Populism to rouse the peasants led some radicals to look to the proletariat instead (1); Plekhanov's Emancipation of Labour group (1883) spread Marxist ideas (1); the famine of 1891-92 discredited the regime and pushed the educated towards politics (1). **[3]**

**4.** Any two, 1 mark each: the Tsar kept "supreme autocratic power" and an absolute veto (1); ministers were appointed by and responsible to the Tsar, not the Duma (1); the Tsar could dissolve the Duma and rule by decree under Article 87 when it was not sitting (1); the State Council, half appointed, acted as an upper house (1). **[2]**

**5.** Any three, 1 mark each: frequent changes of ministers (1); the Tsar took personal command in 1915, linking himself to defeat (1); the Tsarina and Rasputin were left with influence over government (1); the Duma's Progressive Bloc was ignored (1); supply and transport of food and munitions broke down (1). **[3]**

**6.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Decree on Peace (1); the Decree on Land, approving peasant seizures (1); the Decree on Workers' Control (1); the Declaration of the Rights of the Peoples of Russia (1); a decree restricting the press (1). **[3]**

**7.** Two reasons, 2 marks each: the Socialist Revolutionaries won the most seats and the Bolsheviks only about a quarter, so the Assembly would have removed Sovnarkom (2); Lenin argued that soviets were a higher form of democracy than a "bourgeois" parliament and that the election lists were out of date after the SR split (2). **[4]**

**8.** Indicative content. **Peasant attitudes:** many peasants valued the security of the commune and distrusted separation; by 1914 only a minority of households had left it. **Practical obstacles:** consolidating scattered strips required surveys and officials; the process was slow. **Land and population:** rapid population growth meant plots remained small; the Peasant Land Bank helped some buy land, but noble estates were not touched. **Resettlement:** migration to Siberia relieved pressure for some, but many settlers returned. **Time:** Stolypin said he needed twenty years of peace; he was assassinated in 1911 and war came in 1914. **Politics:** the reforms were pushed through by decree under Article 87, angering the Duma and the court. **Linking:** the reform depended on peasants' willingness and on time, and Russia had little of either. **[10]**

**9.** Indicative content. **For:** the June offensive of 1917 failed, desertion grew and the army collapsed; continuing the war kept inflation and food shortages high; the July Days began with soldiers refusing to go to the front; Bolshevik "Peace" was their most powerful slogan. **Against:** the government delayed land reform until a Constituent Assembly, while peasants seized land anyway; dual power and Order No. 1 meant it never controlled the army or Petrograd; the Kornilov affair destroyed Kerensky's credibility with both right and left and armed the Red Guards; Lenin's leadership and Trotsky's organisation gave the Bolsheviks the means to take power. **Judgement:** strong answers show how factors connect -- the war made every other problem worse -- before deciding whether war was the root cause or whether Kornilov was the decisive trigger. **[20]**

**10.** Indicative content. **Similarities:** both describe the Manifesto as a promise of rights and some form of representative body. **Differences:** A sees Russia as now "a constitutional state" and calls for lawful co-operation; B dismisses it as "paper" and calls for continued strikes and a constituent assembly. A trusts the Tsar's word; B points to the police and Cossacks. **Explaining:** A is a liberal paper, voicing the propertied middle class who wanted order and reform -- the future Kadets and Octobrists; B is a workers' committee, close to the Soviet and the socialist parties, writing after Bloody Sunday. Knowledge: the Manifesto did split the opposition, as the liberals moved to elections while the Soviet continued the strike; the Fundamental Laws later bore out B's suspicion. **[15]**

**11.** Indicative content. **Support:** A and B show the opposition splitting over the Manifesto; D, written by a liberal, admits that liberals "went home" and the workers were defeated "one by one". Knowledge: the Kadets and Octobrists formed; the Soviet leaders were arrested in December; the Moscow rising was isolated. **Challenge:** C stresses the reliability of the army returning from Manchuria and the use of field courts -- repression and military loyalty, not division alone. Knowledge: the Treaty of Portsmouth freed troops; the Semyonovsky Guards crushed Moscow; foreign loans in 1906 helped the government. **Provenance:** C is an official report wishing to show success; D is written in exile after 1917, possibly regretting liberal choices; B is propaganda for the strike. **Judgement:** the sources show division was essential, because it allowed concession and force to be used separately, but C suggests force was what finally saved the regime. **[25]**

**12.** Indicative content. **The meaning of Kronstadt:** the sailors had been heroes of 1917; their demands for freely elected soviets, freedom of speech and an end to grain requisitioning showed that even loyal supporters had turned against War Communism. **Wider unrest:** peasant revolts such as that in Tambov and strikes in Petrograd showed the same anger. **Economic collapse:** industrial output and the grain harvest had fallen sharply; famine was approaching. **Timing:** the rising coincided with the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921, giving Lenin the occasion to announce a change. **Lenin's reasoning:** a tactical retreat would win back peasants while the party kept political power, which is why the NEP was paired with the ban on factions. **Linking:** Kronstadt did not cause the economic crisis but made it politically impossible to ignore. **[10]**
