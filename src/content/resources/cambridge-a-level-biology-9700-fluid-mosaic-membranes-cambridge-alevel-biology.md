## 4.1.1 Why a bilayer forms

A phospholipid has a polar, **hydrophilic** head (glycerol and phosphate) and two non-polar, **hydrophobic** fatty acid tails. Water molecules form hydrogen bonds with the heads but cannot interact with the tails. In water, phospholipids therefore arrange themselves so that the tails are hidden from water and the heads are in contact with it. Between two watery compartments, the cytoplasm and tissue fluid, the most stable arrangement is a **bilayer**: two layers of heads facing outwards, tails meeting in the middle.

Three consequences follow from the same interactions:

- **The bilayer seals itself.** An open edge would expose tails to water, so membranes form closed compartments, and small tears close up.
- **Phospholipids move sideways but rarely flip.** Moving within a layer keeps tails in the core; flipping across would drag a polar head through the hydrophobic core, so it is uncommon. This is the "fluid" in the model.
- **The core is a barrier to polar substances.** Ions and polar molecules are surrounded by water and cannot easily enter the hydrophobic core.

### How proteins are positioned

The "mosaic" is the scattered pattern of proteins in the bilayer. Their position is decided by the R groups of their amino acids.

- **Transmembrane (intrinsic) proteins** span the bilayer. The section in the core has amino acids with hydrophobic R groups; the sections exposed to the cytoplasm and tissue fluid have hydrophilic R groups. Channel proteins, carrier proteins and many receptors are of this kind.
- **Extrinsic (peripheral) proteins** sit on the inner or outer surface, held by interactions with phospholipid heads or with other proteins. They do not enter the core.

A protein cannot simply drift out of the membrane, because that would expose its hydrophobic region to water. It can, however, move sideways.

## 4.1.2 Where cholesterol, glycolipids and glycoproteins sit

| Component | Position |
|---|---|
| Cholesterol | Between phospholipid molecules in both layers; its small hydrophilic –OH group lies near the phosphate heads and its hydrophobic rings among the fatty acid tails |
| Glycolipids | In the outer layer only; the carbohydrate chain projects into the tissue fluid |
| Glycoproteins | Proteins in the membrane with carbohydrate chains attached on the outer surface only |

The carbohydrate chains of glycolipids and glycoproteins together form a carbohydrate-rich layer on the outside of the cell. None is on the cytoplasmic face.

## 4.1.3 Roles of the components

| Role | Components responsible | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Stability** | Phospholipids; cholesterol; glycolipids and glycoproteins | Hydrophobic interactions hold the bilayer together; cholesterol packs between phospholipids; carbohydrate chains form hydrogen bonds with water outside |
| **Fluidity** | Phospholipids; cholesterol | Phospholipids move sideways; cholesterol limits movement when warm and prevents tight packing when cold |
| **Permeability** | Phospholipid core; cholesterol; proteins | The core blocks ions and polar molecules; cholesterol reduces leakage of these; proteins provide specific routes |
| **Transport** | Channel and carrier proteins | Channels are water-filled pores for specific ions; carriers bind specific molecules and change shape |
| **Cell signalling** | Receptor proteins and glycoproteins | A binding site complementary to a specific ligand |
| **Cell recognition** | Glycoproteins and glycolipids | Carbohydrate chains act as cell surface antigens, recognised by other cells |

### What else affects fluidity

- **Temperature:** higher temperatures give phospholipids more kinetic energy, so the membrane becomes more fluid and more permeable.
- **Unsaturated fatty acid tails:** a C=C double bond puts a kink in the tail, so tails cannot pack tightly and the membrane is more fluid.
- **Cholesterol:** buffers both effects, keeping fluidity within a working range.

## Interpreting permeability data

Beetroot cells store a red pigment in their vacuoles. The pigment must cross the vacuole membrane and the cell surface membrane to leak out, so the colour of the surrounding water shows how permeable these membranes are. Discs of equal size are washed to remove pigment from cut cells, then placed in water at different temperatures for the same time. A colorimeter measures the absorbance of the water.

**Worked example 1 (invented results).**

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absorbance | 0.05 | 0.06 | 0.09 | 0.32 | 0.71 | 0.85 |

**Describe:** absorbance rises slowly from 20 °C to 40 °C (by 0.04), then steeply from 40 °C to 60 °C (by 0.62), then less steeply to 70 °C.

**Explain:** up to 40 °C, increased kinetic energy makes the bilayer slightly more fluid, so a little more pigment leaks. Above 40 °C, membrane proteins **denature** as hydrogen bonds and other bonds holding their tertiary structure break, leaving gaps, and the phospholipids become much more fluid. The membranes lose their barrier function and pigment escapes. By 70 °C most membranes are badly damaged, so there is little extra pigment left to release.

**Control:** disc size and number, volume of water, time, and washing method. Repeats at each temperature give a mean and show spread.

## 4.1.4 Cell signalling

The syllabus asks for three stages that lead to a specific response:

1. **Secretion:** a cell releases a specific chemical, the ligand, often by exocytosis.
2. **Transport:** the ligand travels to target cells, in the blood for a hormone or by diffusion across a short gap for a local signal.
3. **Binding:** the ligand binds to a **cell surface receptor** whose binding site is complementary in shape to it. Binding changes the receptor's shape, which sets off a response inside the cell.

**Specificity** comes from the receptor, not the ligand's route: a hormone reaches almost every cell, but only cells with the matching receptor respond. A cell with different receptors responds to different signals, and the same ligand can cause different responses in different cell types.

**Worked example 2: reasoning from evidence.** Liver cells normally respond to a protein hormone. After treatment with an enzyme that digests the outer parts of membrane proteins, they no longer respond, although the hormone is still present. Conclusion: the receptor is a protein (or glycoprotein) on the outer surface; once its binding site is destroyed, the hormone cannot bind, so stage 3 fails.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Explain how the structure of the cell surface membrane controls the movement of substances into a cell. **[5]**

> The phospholipid bilayer has a hydrophobic core of fatty acid tails, so ions and polar molecules cannot easily pass through. *(Links the core to permeability.)*
>
> Small non-polar molecules such as oxygen dissolve in the core and diffuse through. *(Contrast.)*
>
> Channel proteins provide water-filled pores for specific ions, and carrier proteins bind specific molecules and change shape to move them. *(Transport roles, with specificity.)*
>
> Because different cells have different channel and carrier proteins, each cell controls which substances enter. *(Develops the idea of control.)*
>
> Cholesterol between the phospholipids reduces the permeability of the bilayer to ions and polar molecules. *(Fifth distinct point.)*

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the model: name the bilayer, which way heads and tails face, the scattered proteins and the outward-facing carbohydrate chains.
- **Explain** positions: always give the interaction, such as "heads are hydrophilic so they face the water".
- **Outline** signalling: three stages, each in one clear sentence; do not skip transport.
- **Suggest** with unfamiliar data: apply denaturation, fluidity and the hydrophobic core to the new context.

## Common mistakes

- Saying phospholipid heads are "attracted to each other" rather than to water.
- Placing glycolipids or glycoproteins on the inner face.
- Writing "cholesterol makes the membrane more rigid" without the temperature condition.
- Saying the membrane is "fluid because it is liquid", rather than describing sideways movement.
- Saying the ligand enters the cell to cause the response; the syllabus outcome concerns binding to cell surface receptors.
- Explaining pigment leakage at high temperature only by denatured proteins, and forgetting the phospholipids.
