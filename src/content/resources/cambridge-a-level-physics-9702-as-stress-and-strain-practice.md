## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a tensile force and a compressive force acting on a rod. **[2]**

**2.** Define the Young modulus of a material and state its SI unit. **[2]**

**3.** A spring has an unstretched length of 120 mm. When a load of 4.0 N hangs from it, its length is 152 mm. Calculate the spring constant. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the Young modulus, rather than the spring constant, is used to compare the stiffness of different materials. **[2]**

**5.** Show that the unit of the Young modulus can be written as kg m⁻¹ s⁻². **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A copper wire has an original length of 2.20 m and a diameter of 0.38 mm. The Young modulus of copper is 1.2 × 10¹¹ Pa. A load of 30 N is applied and the wire obeys Hooke's law.

**(a)** Calculate the cross-sectional area of the wire. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the stress in the wire. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the extension of the wire. **[2]**

**(d)** The wire is replaced by a copper wire of the same length but diameter 0.76 mm. State, with a reason, the extension produced by the same load. **[2]**

**7.** A spring obeys Hooke's law. With a load of 2.0 N its length is 0.215 m; with a load of 5.0 N its length is 0.260 m.

**(a)** Calculate the spring constant. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the unstretched length of the spring. **[2]**

**8.** A steel cable supports a load of 1.2 × 10⁴ N. The Young modulus of the steel is 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. The designer requires the strain in the cable not to exceed 1.0 × 10⁻³.

**(a)** Calculate the minimum cross-sectional area of the cable. **[3]**

**(b)** The cable is 40 m long. Calculate its extension when the strain is 1.0 × 10⁻³. **[1]**

**9.** A solid aluminium cylinder of length 0.80 m and diameter 4.0 cm stands vertically and supports a compressive load of 50 kN. The Young modulus of aluminium is 7.0 × 10¹⁰ Pa. Assume Hooke's law applies.

**(a)** Calculate the compressive stress in the cylinder. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the decrease in length of the cylinder. **[2]**

**10.** Describe an experiment to determine the Young modulus of a metal in the form of a wire. Include the measurements taken, the instruments used and how the result is obtained from a graph. **[6]**

**11.** In a Young modulus experiment, a student records the following:

- original length L = 2.50 ± 0.01 m
- diameter d = 0.35 ± 0.01 mm
- gradient of the force–extension graph = 8.0 × 10³ N m⁻¹, with an uncertainty of 4%

**(a)** Calculate the Young modulus of the metal. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the Young modulus. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest how the student could reduce the largest contribution to this uncertainty. **[1]**

**12.** A steel wire P and a brass wire Q have the same length and the same diameter. They are joined end to end and hang vertically, supporting a single load. The Young modulus of steel is twice that of brass. Both wires obey Hooke's law. Determine the ratio, P : Q, of

**(a)** the stresses in the wires **[1]**

**(b)** the strains in the wires **[1]**

**(c)** the extensions of the wires. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** A tensile force **stretches** the rod, increasing its length [1]. A compressive force **squashes** the rod, decreasing its length [1].

**2.** The Young modulus is the ratio of **stress to strain** for a material obeying Hooke's law [1]. Unit: **pascal** (Pa, N m⁻²) [1].

**3.** x = 152 − 120 = 32 mm = 0.032 m [1]. k = F/x = 4.0 ÷ 0.032 = **125 N m⁻¹** [1].

**4.** The spring constant depends on the **dimensions** of the sample (length and cross-sectional area) as well as the material [1]. The Young modulus uses stress and strain, which remove the dimensions, so it depends **only on the material** [1].

**5.** Pa = N m⁻², and N = kg m s⁻² [1], so Pa = kg m s⁻² × m⁻² = **kg m⁻¹ s⁻²** (strain has no unit) [1].

**6. (a)** r = 0.19 × 10⁻³ m [1]; A = πr² = **1.13 × 10⁻⁷ m²** [1].

**(b)** Stress = 30 ÷ 1.13 × 10⁻⁷ = **2.6 × 10⁸ Pa** [1].

**(c)** Strain = stress ÷ E = 2.65 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.2 × 10¹¹ = 2.2 × 10⁻³ [1]; x = strain × L = 2.2 × 10⁻³ × 2.20 = **4.8 × 10⁻³ m** (4.8 mm) [1].

**(d)** Doubling the diameter makes the area **four times larger**, so the stress and strain are a quarter as large [1]; the extension is **1.2 × 10⁻³ m** (1.2 mm) [1].

**7. (a)** k = ∆F/∆x = (5.0 − 2.0) ÷ (0.260 − 0.215) [1] = 3.0 ÷ 0.045 = **67 N m⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** Extension under 2.0 N = 2.0 ÷ 66.7 = 0.030 m [1]; unstretched length = 0.215 − 0.030 = **0.185 m** [1].

**8. (a)** Maximum stress = E × strain = 2.0 × 10¹¹ × 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 2.0 × 10⁸ Pa [1]. A = F ÷ stress [1] = 1.2 × 10⁴ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁸ = **6.0 × 10⁻⁵ m²** [1].

**(b)** x = strain × L = 1.0 × 10⁻³ × 40 = **0.040 m** [1].

**9. (a)** A = π(0.020)² = 1.26 × 10⁻³ m² [1]; stress = 5.0 × 10⁴ ÷ 1.26 × 10⁻³ = **4.0 × 10⁷ Pa** [1].

**(b)** Strain = 3.98 × 10⁷ ÷ 7.0 × 10¹⁰ = 5.7 × 10⁻⁴ [1]; decrease in length = 5.7 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.80 = **4.5 × 10⁻⁴ m** (0.45 mm) [1].

**10.** Any six of the following [1] each, to a maximum of 6:

- A long, thin wire is clamped at one end and passes over a pulley (or hangs beside a reference wire), with a marker or vernier scale.
- The original length L is measured from the clamp to the marker with a metre rule or tape measure.
- The diameter is measured with a micrometer at several places and orientations, and the mean is used.
- A = πd²/4 is calculated.
- Loads are added in steps, with F = mg for each.
- The extension for each load is read from the scale (vernier) as the change in marker position.
- The loads are removed to check the wire returns to its original reading, so it has not been deformed beyond the elastic limit.
- A graph of F against x is plotted; E = gradient × L/A (or stress against strain, with E = gradient).

**11. (a)** A = π(0.175 × 10⁻³)² = 9.62 × 10⁻⁸ m² [1]. E = gradient × L/A = 8.0 × 10³ × 2.50 ÷ 9.62 × 10⁻⁸ = **2.1 × 10¹¹ Pa** [1].

**(b)** % uncertainty in d = 0.01 ÷ 0.35 × 100 = 2.9%, so in A = 5.7% [1]. % uncertainty in L = 0.4% [1]. Total = 5.7 + 0.4 + 4 = **10%** [1].

**(c)** The diameter contributes most: measure it with a more precise instrument (finer resolution) or take repeated readings at many points along the wire and average them [1].

**12. (a)** Same load and same area, so the stresses are equal: **1 : 1** [1].

**(b)** Strain = stress/E, and E(P) = 2E(Q), so strains are **1 : 2** [1].

**(c)** Same original length, so extensions follow the strains: **1 : 2** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the extension as 152 mm (the new length) rather than 32 mm (Question 3).
- Using the diameter as the radius in πr², or not converting mm to m (Questions 6, 9 and 11).
- Forgetting that the area scales with diameter squared (Question 6(d)).
- Doubling only the uncertainty in L, or forgetting to double the uncertainty in d (Question 11).
- In "describe" answers, naming apparatus without saying what is measured and how E is calculated (Question 10).
- Assuming two wires carrying the same load must have the same extension (Question 12).
