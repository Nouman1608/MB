## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *primary socialisation*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** of the functions of the family described by Murdock. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between the *instrumental role* and the *expressive role*. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by the *loss of functions* of the family. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** way in which the family may act as a unit of consumption. **[3]**

**6.** Explain what Marxist feminists mean when they say the family reproduces labour power. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain **two** ways in which the family may benefit capitalism. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** criticisms feminists make of the functionalist view of the family. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the Marxist theory of the family. **[8]**

**10.** Evaluate Parsons' theory that the nuclear family fits the needs of industrial society. **[8]**

**11.** *"The family has lost most of its functions in modern society."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the quotation. **[10]**

**12.** "The family benefits men more than any other group." Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers

### 1. Primary socialisation [2]

The first stage of learning a society's norms, values and language in early childhood [1], mainly within the family, through imitation, praise and punishment [1]. **[2]**

### 2. Murdock's functions [2]

Any two, one mark each: sexual, reproductive, economic, educational [1] [1]. **[2]**

### 3. Instrumental and expressive roles [4]

- The instrumental role is Parsons' term for the breadwinner role [1], earning income and linking the family to the world of work, traditionally given to the husband [1].
- The expressive role is the caring role [1], providing emotional support and socialising children, traditionally given to the wife [1]. **[4]**

### 4. Loss of functions [3]

The view, associated with Parsons, that as societies industrialised [1], specialist institutions such as schools, hospitals, factories and the welfare state took over functions the family once performed, such as education, health care and production [1], leaving the family with fewer, more specialised functions such as primary socialisation and emotional support [1]. **[3]**

### 5. Unit of consumption [3]

Way identified [1], explained [1], linked to capitalism or an example [1]. For example: families buy housing, cars, appliances and children's goods [1], and advertising targets parents and children, who put pressure on parents to buy [1]; Marxists argue this spending creates profit for the capitalist class [1]. **[3]**

### 6. Reproducing labour power [3]

Women's unpaid domestic work, such as cooking, cleaning and caring [1], keeps current workers fit for work and raises the next generation of workers [1]; Benston (1969) argued this is done at no direct cost to employers, so capitalism benefits from women's unpaid labour [1]. **[3]**

### 7. How the family benefits capitalism [6]

Up to 3 marks per way.

- **Ideological control:** children learn to accept authority and hierarchy, preparing them to obey employers.
- **Reproduction of labour power:** the family raises and maintains workers at no direct cost to capitalism.
- **Consumption:** families buy goods that create profit.
- **Inheritance:** Engels (1884) argued the monogamous family passes private property to legitimate heirs, preserving class inequality.
- **Safety valve:** family life absorbs workers' frustrations, reducing the threat of conflict at work. **[6]**

### 8. Feminist criticisms of functionalism [6]

Up to 3 marks per criticism.

- **Naturalising gender roles:** Parsons' expressive role treats women's caring and domestic work as natural, when Oakley (1974) argues the housewife role is socially constructed.
- **Ignoring inequality:** the family benefits men more than women, through unpaid labour and power over decisions (Delphy and Leonard, 1992).
- **Ignoring the dark side:** domestic violence and abuse, mostly by men against women and children, contradict the idea of the family as a "warm bath".
- **Dual burden:** women in paid work still do most housework, so the stabilisation of adult personalities is unequal. **[6]**

### 9. Evaluating the Marxist theory [8]

Up to 4 marks for strengths and up to 4 for limitations.

**Strengths:**
- Links the family to wider economic inequality, showing how class is reproduced across generations through inheritance [1].
- Explains why the family socialises children to accept authority, with evidence from parenting and schooling [1].
- Shows how consumption and advertising shape family life, which is visible in modern consumer societies [1].
- Zaretsky (1976) explains why so much emotional weight is placed on the family in capitalist societies [1].

**Limitations:**
- Assumes a single family type serving capitalism and ignores family diversity [1].
- Feminists argue it neglects gender inequality within the family [1].
- Functionalists argue it ignores real benefits members gain, such as love and support [1].
- Treats family members as passive and does not explain families in non-capitalist societies well [1]. **[8]**

### 10. Evaluating Parsons' functional fit theory [8]

**Strengths:**
- The small nuclear family can move easily for work, fitting the geographical mobility industrial economies need [1].
- Achieved status at work replaces ascribed kin status, which fits a meritocratic labour market [1].
- Explains the decline of the extended family as a unit of production [1].
- Fits the specialisation of institutions in modern societies [1].

**Limitations:**
- Laslett found small nuclear households common before industrialisation [1].
- Anderson (1971), using the 1851 census of Preston, found extended households helped families during industrialisation [1].
- Young and Willmott (1957) found strong extended family networks in East London [1].
- The theory is based on a narrow, middle-class American model and treats gender roles as natural [1]. **[8]**

### 11(a). Explaining the view [6]

- Parsons argued that industrialisation brought structural differentiation [1].
- Production moved from the household to factories [1].
- Schools took over formal education [1].
- Health services and the welfare state took over care of the sick, elderly and poor [1].
- The family kept only primary socialisation and stabilisation of adult personalities [1].
- Application: in many societies, children now spend most of the day in school and adults work outside the home [1]. **[6]**

### 11(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument.

- **Fletcher (1966):** the family's functions have become more important; the state supports families, and parents take more responsibility for children's health, education and welfare.
- **Parsons himself:** the family has specialised and performs its core functions better, so "lost most" overstates the change.
- **Continuing economic role:** families still pool income, buy goods, care for relatives and, in many societies, run family businesses or farms.
- **Cross-cultural evidence:** in many societies extended families still provide welfare, childcare and support for older members.

Credit any two developed arguments. **[10]**

### 12. Essay: does the family benefit men most? [26]

Marked by levels across AO1, AO2 and AO3.

**Indicative content for the view:**
- Radical feminists: Delphy and Leonard (1992), the family as a patriarchal economic system.
- Oakley (1974): the housewife role as unpaid, low-status work.
- Ansley (1972): wives absorb men's frustrations.
- Unequal domestic labour, the dual burden and emotion work.
- Domestic violence, mostly by men.

**Indicative content against the view:**
- Functionalists: the family benefits all members and society (Murdock, Parsons).
- Marxists: the main beneficiary is capitalism, not men as such.
- Liberal feminists: inequality is declining with women's employment and legal change.
- Benefits to children: protection, socialisation, investment in their future.
- Postmodern and personal-life views: people choose relationships that meet their needs; women initiate many divorces.

**Evaluation:** it depends on family type, class, ethnicity and society; distinguish benefits (income, care, emotional support) and who gains each; compare perspectives directly.

**Level guidance:** top-level answers contrast feminist, Marxist and functionalist arguments with accurate evidence and reach a supported judgement; middle-level answers explain views with limited evaluation; low-level answers describe one perspective.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about Marxist feminism as if it were the only feminist view.
- Evaluating a theory with limitations only.
- Not linking Laslett or Anderson to Parsons' fit thesis.
- Counter-arguments to the loss of functions that do not mention Fletcher or specialisation.
- Essays that describe perspectives in turn without answering "who benefits most".
