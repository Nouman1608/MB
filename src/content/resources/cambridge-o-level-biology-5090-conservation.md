This guide covers subtopic **19.5 Conservation** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to discuss reasons for conservation of species (maintenance of biodiversity, reducing extinction and protecting vulnerable environments); to explain how forests can be conserved using education, protected areas, quotas and replanting; to explain how fish stocks can be conserved using education, closed seasons, protected areas, controlled net types and mesh size, quotas and monitoring; and to describe a sustainable resource as one that is produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment so that it does not run out.

The [Relationships of organisms topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-relationships-of-organisms-with-one-another-and-with-the-env/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on conservation, with the reasoning behind each method. Use the [19.5 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-conservation-practice/) to test yourself.

## What is conservation?

**Conservation** is the protection and careful management of species and their habitats, so that they are not lost and resources are not used up. It is a response to the harm described in the previous subtopic: deforestation, over-harvesting, pollution and the loss of habitats.

## Reasons for conservation

The syllabus asks you to discuss reasons with reference to three ideas.

### Maintenance of biodiversity

A high biodiversity means ecosystems with many species. Each species has a role in food webs, nutrient cycles and pollination. If species are lost, the ecosystem becomes less stable and other species that depend on them may also decline. Biodiversity also gives people food, medicines, materials and genetic variety for use in selective breeding and genetic modification. Once the genes of a species are lost, they cannot be recovered.

### Reducing extinction

**Extinction** is the permanent loss of a species. When it has happened, it cannot be reversed. A species can become extinct when its habitat is destroyed, when it is over-harvested, or when non-native species or pollution harm it. Conservation aims to protect species at risk, to give them a chance to recover, and to keep the variety of life on Earth. Species also have value in themselves, and many people believe that we have a duty to protect them for future generations.

### Protecting vulnerable environments

Some environments, such as rainforests, coral reefs and wetlands, contain many species and are easily damaged. They are slow to recover. Protecting them keeps the species that depend on them. They also provide services for people: forests take in carbon dioxide and protect soil, wetlands reduce flooding, and coral reefs protect coasts and support fisheries.

### Worked example 1: writing a discussion

*Discuss why it is important to conserve a rainforest.*

- It has very high biodiversity, so clearing it would destroy a large number of species. [Biodiversity]
- Some species live only in that forest, so they could become extinct. [Extinction]
- Trees take in carbon dioxide, which reduces global warming, and tree roots stop soil being washed away and flooding. [Vulnerable environment]
- Some forest plants may be sources of future medicines or crops. [Resources]
- A counter-point: people living nearby also need land, food and work, so conservation has to balance their needs. [Balance]

## Conserving forests

Four methods are listed in the syllabus.

| Method | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Education | People are taught why forests matter, so local people, tourists and companies change their behaviour, for example by not cutting trees and buying wood from managed sources |
| Protected areas | National parks and reserves have laws against cutting, so the habitat and its species are protected |
| Quotas | Limits on the amount of timber that can be cut each year, so that the trees can regrow and the forest is not used up |
| Replanting | New trees are planted where trees have been cut, which replaces the lost trees, restores habitats and soil and takes in carbon dioxide |

These methods work best together. For example, a quota controls the harvest, replanting keeps the forest producing wood in the long term, and education explains the reasons.

### Worked example 2: a timber quota

*A forest contains 50 000 mature trees and grows 2 000 new mature trees each year. A company wants to cut 3 500 trees a year.*

1. Cutting rate minus growth rate = 3 500 - 2 000 = 1 500 trees lost each year.
2. Time for the forest to be used up = 50 000 divided by 1 500 = about 33 years.
3. A sustainable quota would be 2 000 trees a year or fewer, which is equal to the growth. The forest then stays at about 50 000 trees.

## Conserving fish stocks

A **fish stock** is the population of a species of fish in a fishing area. If too many fish are caught, especially young fish before they breed, the population falls and may collapse. Methods listed in the syllabus are:

| Method | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Education | Fishermen and consumers learn about the need to protect stocks; consumers choose sustainable fish |
| Closed seasons | Fishing is banned during the breeding season, so fish can reproduce and the numbers can recover |
| Protected areas | Some areas, such as breeding and nursery grounds, are closed to fishing, so that populations can build up |
| Controlled net types | Banning methods such as very large trawl nets that damage the sea bed and catch non-target species |
| Mesh size | Nets with a large enough mesh let young, small fish escape, so they can grow and breed |
| Quotas | A limit on the mass or number of fish that each boat or country can catch |
| Monitoring | Regular surveys, and checks on catches and boats, to measure the stocks and make sure the rules are followed |

### Worked example 3: net mesh

*A net catches fish of 12 cm or longer. Fish become able to breed when they are 20 cm long. Explain the effect of using a mesh that lets fish shorter than 25 cm escape.*

- Fish between 12 and 25 cm now escape.
- Fish of 20 to 25 cm are able to breed, so they will survive to breed again.
- The fish stock is not reduced as fast, so the population can recover.
- The fishermen catch only larger, older fish, which are fewer but larger, and the fishery can continue in the future.

## Sustainable resources

**A sustainable resource is one that is produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment so that it does not run out.** Trees, fish and crops can be sustainable if the rate of harvesting is no more than the rate of regrowth. Fossil fuels are not, because they take millions of years to form.

### Worked example 4: sustainable or not?

*A lake has 4 000 fish. Each year the fish produce 600 new fish that survive to adulthood. Fishermen catch 500 fish each year.*

1. Catch (500) is less than the new fish (600), so the population increases by 100 per year. This is sustainable.
2. If the catch rose to 800 fish a year, the population would fall by 200 a year. The stock would be gone in about 20 years (4 000 divided by 200). This is **not** sustainable.

## Conservation in practice: balancing needs

Conservation decisions are rarely simple, because people also depend on the resources. A fishing community may rely on cod for food and income, and a village next to a forest may need wood and farmland. A good conservation plan therefore tries to protect the resource while keeping the needs of people in view.

| Approach | Benefit to wildlife | Benefit to people |
|---|---|---|
| Sustainable quotas | Populations can recover | The resource continues to provide income |
| Protected areas with tourism | Habitats are protected | Local jobs in guiding and hospitality |
| Replanting with local species | Habitats are restored | Future supply of timber |
| Education programmes | Fewer harmful practices | People understand and support the rules |

### Worked example 5: reading a recovery graph

*A protected area was set up in year 0 to protect a fish species. The estimated population was 20 000 at year 0, 26 000 at year 3 and 41 000 at year 8.*

1. Increase from year 0 to year 8 = 41 000 - 20 000 = 21 000.
2. Percentage increase = 21 000 divided by 20 000 x 100 = 105 per cent.
3. Mean increase per year = 21 000 divided by 8 = 2 625 fish a year.

The data suggest that the protected area allowed the fish to breed and the population to recover. A careful answer would add that other factors, such as fishing outside the area, could also affect the numbers, and that monitoring is needed to confirm the trend.

## Linking conservation to earlier subtopics

The methods in this subtopic are the responses to the problems in the previous one. Quotas and closed seasons respond to over-harvesting. Protected areas and replanting respond to deforestation and the loss of habitat. Education responds to all of them, since it changes behaviour. Biodiversity (19.3) is the measure of success: a conservation scheme works if the number of species and the size of populations are maintained or increase over time, and the sustainable use of resources (the definition above) is the long-term aim.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that conservation means stopping all use of resources. It means managing use so that it can continue.
- Defining a sustainable resource as one that is "not used". It is used no faster than it is replaced.
- Describing only what the method is, not how it helps (for example "closed seasons" without "so fish can breed").
- Saying that a quota is a ban. It is a limit.
- Writing that a larger mesh size catches more fish. It lets smaller fish escape.
- Forgetting monitoring in the fish stock methods.
- Writing a one-sided discussion without balance.

## Command words

- **Discuss**: give reasons for conservation, with balance.
- **Explain**: give each method and how it helps.
- **Describe**: give the definition of a sustainable resource.
