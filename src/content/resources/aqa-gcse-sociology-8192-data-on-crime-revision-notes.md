These notes condense 3.5.4 Data on crime, from the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, assessed in Paper 2. You must identify and describe the main sources of data on crime, describe "the pattern and trends in crime figures using relevant statistical data", explain the "dark figure" of crime (unreported and unrecorded crime), and compare functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives on the use of data on crime.

For full explanations, read the [data on crime study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-data-on-crime/). For the whole topic, see the [Crime and deviance revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-crime-and-deviance-revision-notes/). Then try the [data on crime practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-data-on-crime-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Official crime statistics | Crime figures produced by the state, mainly police recorded crime |
| Reported crime | Crime the public tells the police about |
| Recorded crime | Crime the police officially count |
| Crime rate | The amount of crime relative to the population, for example per 1,000 people |
| Dark figure of crime | Crime that is unreported or unrecorded, so missing from official statistics |
| Victim survey | A survey asking people which crimes they have experienced |
| Self-report study | A survey asking people which offences they have committed |
| Secondary data | Data collected by someone else, such as official statistics |
| Validity | Whether data give a true picture of what is being measured |
| Reliability | Whether the same method would give the same results if repeated |
| Trend (in relation to data) | The general direction of change over time |
| Discretion | Police judgement about whether to record or act |
| Corporate crime | Crime committed by or for a company |

## Three sources compared

| Source | How collected | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Police recorded crime** | Crimes reported to or discovered by police, recorded under Home Office counting rules; published by the Office for National Statistics | National, regular, cheap for sociologists; good for crimes almost always reported, such as homicide | Unreported and unrecorded crime missing; police discretion; changes in counting rules and priorities; National Statistics status withdrawn in 2014 over recording quality |
| **Crime Survey for England and Wales** | Large random sample of households interviewed about crimes experienced in the past twelve months; began 1982 as the British Crime Survey, renamed 2012 | Captures unreported crime; unaffected by police recording; long-term trend data | Misses crimes with no direct victim, homicide and crimes against businesses; relies on memory; sensitive crimes may still be hidden |
| **Self-report studies** | Anonymous questionnaires or interviews asking about one's own offending | Reveal hidden offending and offenders never caught; test who really offends | Lying, forgetting or exaggerating; mainly minor offences; mostly young samples |

## The dark figure: two filters

1. **Committed → reported?** Lost if the victim is unaware, sees it as trivial or private, fears reprisal, or distrusts the police.
2. **Reported → recorded?** Lost through police discretion, lack of evidence, the victim withdrawing, or counting rules.

Victim surveys reach some of filter 1; self-report studies reach undetected offending; nothing measures the whole dark figure.

## Trends to know in outline (England and Wales)

- CSEW estimates **peaked in the mid-1990s** and then **fell substantially** for many crimes, such as burglary and vehicle crime.
- Police recorded crime **rose in the later 2010s**, partly because of **improved recording** and greater willingness to report some offences.
- **Fraud and computer misuse** have been included in the CSEW since the mid-2010s and are among the most commonly experienced crimes.

## Describing data: a four-step routine

1. State the **overall direction** (rise, fall, stable).
2. Quote the **start and end figures**.
3. Note any **turning point or exception**.
4. Ask whether the change reflects **behaviour or recording**.

## Perspectives in one line each

- **Functionalist:** official statistics broadly show who offends; theories such as Merton's explain the patterns.
- **Marxist:** statistics are a social construct reflecting the policing of the working class; crimes of the powerful are hidden.
- **Feminist:** domestic and sexual violence against women are heavily under-reported, so statistics understate harm to women.

## Exam traps

- The dark figure is **unreported and unrecorded** crime, not "unsolved" crime.
- **Victim survey** = victims; **self-report** = offenders.
- A rise in recorded crime is **not** proof of more offending.
- Only **three** perspectives are listed for 3.5.4: functionalist, feminist, Marxist.
- In data questions, **quote figures** and show working for calculations.
- Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.

## Self-test

1. What two steps must happen for a crime to appear in police recorded crime?
2. Define the dark figure of crime.
3. Give two reasons why victims do not report crimes.
4. Give one reason why a reported crime may not be recorded.
5. When did the British Crime Survey begin, and what is it now called?
6. Give one type of crime the CSEW cannot measure.
7. What is a self-report study?
8. Give one limitation of self-report studies.
9. Describe the long-term trend in CSEW estimates since the mid-1990s.
10. Why might police recorded crime rise even if offending does not?
11. How do Marxists view official crime statistics?
12. Why do feminists argue official statistics understate crimes against women?

### Answers

1. It must be reported to (or discovered by) the police, and then recorded by them.
2. Crime that is unreported or unrecorded, so it does not appear in official statistics.
3. Any two: thought too trivial; seen as a private matter; belief the police cannot help; fear of reprisal; distrust of the police; unaware a crime happened.
4. Police discretion, lack of evidence, the victim withdrawing, or counting rules.
5. 1982; the Crime Survey for England and Wales.
6. Any one: homicide; crimes against businesses; many corporate crimes; crimes with no direct victim.
7. A survey asking people which offences they have committed, whether or not they were caught.
8. Respondents may lie, forget or exaggerate; or studies focus on minor offences.
9. Estimates fell substantially for many types of crime.
10. Better recording practice, changes in counting rules, more police activity or more willingness to report.
11. As a social construct reflecting who is policed and prosecuted, hiding crimes of the powerful.
12. Domestic and sexual violence are heavily under-reported and under-recorded.
