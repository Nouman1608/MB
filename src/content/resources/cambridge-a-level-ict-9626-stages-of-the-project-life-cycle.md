This guide teaches **section 15.1 The stages of the project life cycle** of Topic 15 Project management in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Information Technology 9626 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is A Level content, examined in Paper 3 Advanced Theory. The [project management revision notes](/resources/a-level-cambridge-ict-project-management-revision-notes/) summarise the whole topic; this page goes deeper on the four stages. Use it with the [project life cycle practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-ict-9626-stages-of-the-project-life-cycle-practice/).

## What section 15.1 covers

| Stage | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Project initiation | Identify objectives, resources required, success criteria, stakeholders and their needs, scope, and develop high-level schedules |
| Project planning | Plan resources in detail and schedule tasks |
| Project execution and monitoring | Implement the plan, monitor progress against time, cost and quality, and report to stakeholders |
| Project close | Complete the project and review it, and explain why reviews are held |

A **project** is a temporary piece of work with a clear start and end, a defined goal, a budget and a limited set of resources, which creates a unique product, service or result. It differs from routine work, which is repeated. **Project management** is planning, organising and controlling the work so that the goal is met on time, within budget and to the required quality. These three limits (time, cost, quality), together with scope, are the main constraints on any project.

The stages follow one another, but there is feedback: problems found in monitoring can send the team back to planning.

## Stage 1: project initiation

Initiation decides **whether** and **why** to do the project, and defines it in outline. It produces a short document often called a project brief or charter.

### Objectives

Objectives state what the project is to achieve. They should be **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. For example: "Replace the school's paper registers with an electronic attendance system, in use in all classrooms by the start of next term, at a cost of no more than £25,000." A vague objective such as "improve registration" cannot be checked.

### Resources required

An outline of what is needed: **people** (skills and numbers), **hardware and software**, **money**, **time**, premises and training. At this stage, the estimates are rough, to decide whether the project is affordable.

### Success criteria

The measures by which the project will be judged a success: delivered by the date, within the budget, meets the quality standards, and meets stakeholders' needs. Example: "Teachers record attendance in under 2 minutes; 98% of registers are submitted by 9:30 each morning."

### Stakeholders and their needs

A **stakeholder** is anyone affected by, or with an interest in, the project. Identify each group and what it needs.

| Stakeholder | Interest or need |
|---|---|
| Sponsor / senior management | Value for money; delivery on time |
| Project team | Clear tasks, reasonable workload, the tools needed |
| End users (teachers) | A system that is quick and easy to use |
| Customers or students | Accurate records; privacy |
| Suppliers | Clear specifications, payment on time |
| Regulators | Compliance with data protection law |

Stakeholders may have conflicting needs, such as management wanting a low cost while users want more features, so the manager must negotiate and prioritise.

### Project scope

The **scope** states exactly what is included in the project, and what is **not**. A clear scope prevents **scope creep**, in which extra features are added without extra time or money. Example: the attendance system covers recording and reporting, but does not include a new school website.

### High-level schedule

An outline timetable showing main phases and milestones (key checkpoints), without detail.

**Worked example 1: a high-level schedule.** Four phases are expected to take: requirements 3 weeks, build 8 weeks, testing 4 weeks, training and launch 3 weeks. If they run one after the other, the total is 3 + 8 + 4 + 3 = **18 weeks**. If the sponsor wants the system live in 15 weeks, then either the scope is reduced or some phases must overlap (for example, training can begin during the last week of testing), or more resources must be added.

## Stage 2: project planning

Planning converts the outline into a detailed, workable plan.

### Detailed planning of resources

For every task, decide **who** will do it, what **equipment and software** are needed, how much **money**, and when. Compare the demand with availability and resolve clashes, such as one person being needed on two tasks at once. Plan for training, for risks and for contingency (spare time and money).

**Worked example 2: resource cost.** The build phase needs 3 developers for 20 working days each. Effort = 3 × 20 = **60 person-days**. At a cost of £240 per developer per day, the cost = 60 × 240 = **£14,400**. If only 2 developers are available, the same work takes 60 ÷ 2 = 30 working days, so the phase lasts longer, but the cost stays the same.

### Scheduling of tasks

- Break the work into tasks (a **work breakdown structure**).
- Estimate the **duration** of each task.
- Identify **dependencies**: tasks that cannot start until others finish.
- Set **milestones** and the order of tasks.
- Assign dates and people.

Tools such as Gantt charts and PERT charts present the schedule (section 15.3). A realistic plan also includes a **risk plan**, a communications plan and a quality plan describing how checks will be made.

## Stage 3: project execution and monitoring

### Implementing the plan

The team carries out the tasks. The manager allocates work, removes obstacles, runs team meetings, manages suppliers, and controls changes. Any request to change the scope, time or cost goes through a **change control** process, so that the effects are assessed and approved before work changes.

### Monitoring progress against time, cost and quality

| Measure | How monitored |
|---|---|
| Time | Compare actual dates with the schedule; track milestones; note delays |
| Cost | Compare actual spending with the budget |
| Quality | Testing, inspections and reviews against the success criteria |

Corrective action is taken when the project drifts: adding staff, reducing scope, reordering tasks, or approving more time.

**Worked example 3: simple progress check.** A project has a budget of £40,000. At week 10, the plan said that 50% of the work would be complete. In fact, only 40% is complete and £24,000 has been spent.

- Planned value of work = 50% × 40,000 = £20,000.
- Earned value (value of the work actually done) = 40% × 40,000 = **£16,000**.
- Schedule variance = earned − planned = 16,000 − 20,000 = **−£4,000**, so the project is **behind schedule**.
- Cost variance = earned − actual spend = 16,000 − 24,000 = **−£8,000**, so it is **over budget**.
- At this rate, each £1 spent has produced 16,000 ÷ 24,000 = £0.67 of work, so the full project would cost about 40,000 ÷ 0.67 = **£60,000** unless corrective action is taken.

This shows why monitoring matters: it gives an early warning, while action is still possible.

### Reporting to stakeholders

Regular reports tell stakeholders about progress, costs, risks, problems and decisions needed. Reports should be short, honest and fitted to the audience: the sponsor needs a summary of time, cost and risk; users want to know when the system will arrive; the team needs details. Regular meetings, dashboards and written reports are common methods. Good reporting builds trust and allows early decisions.

## Stage 4: project close

### Project completion

The deliverables are handed over and **accepted** by the client against the success criteria. Closing tasks include final testing and sign-off, handing over documentation and training, transferring support, releasing the team and equipment, paying suppliers, and closing the accounts.

### Project review

After completion, the team and stakeholders carry out a **project review** (sometimes called post-implementation review or lessons learned). It compares the results against the objectives, schedule, budget and quality, and records what went well and what went badly.

**Why reviews are held:**

- To check whether the objectives and success criteria were met.
- To learn lessons for future projects, so mistakes are not repeated.
- To identify good practice to be reused.
- To give feedback to the team and recognise achievement.
- To check that stakeholders are satisfied.
- To provide a record for audit and accountability.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the stages as completely separate, with no feedback between them.
- Writing objectives that cannot be measured.
- Forgetting stakeholders other than the client, such as users and suppliers.
- Confusing scope (what is included) with schedule (when).
- Saying monitoring happens only at the end. It continues throughout.
- Describing project close as just "handing over". The review is an important part.

## Exam technique

- **Name the stage** and give the activities in it, using the syllabus words.
- **Explain why** each activity matters, such as "success criteria allow the project to be judged at the end".
- **Scenario questions**: use details from the scenario (the type of project, stakeholders, timescale).
- **Calculations**: show each step and give the unit (weeks, person-days, pounds).
