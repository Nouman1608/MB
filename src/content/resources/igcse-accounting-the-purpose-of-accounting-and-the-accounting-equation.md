## 1.1 Book-keeping and accounting

**Book-keeping** is the recording side. Hamid Karimi runs Karimi Wheels, a bicycle repair workshop. Each time he buys spare parts, issues a bill to a customer or pays a supplier, the transaction is entered in the business records from a source document. This work is routine and follows fixed rules. It answers the question "what happened?".

**Accounting** starts where the recording stops. The recorded transactions are classified and summarised, financial statements such as the statement of profit or loss are prepared, and the results are analysed and communicated. It answers the question "what does this mean, and what should we do?".

| | Book-keeping | Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Main task | Recording transactions in the books | Summarising, presenting and interpreting the records |
| Output | Up-to-date ledger accounts | Financial statements, ratios, comments and advice |
| Skill level | Following set procedures | Judgement, analysis and communication |
| Example at Karimi Wheels | Entering a $45 chain purchase from a supplier | Telling Hamid that parts costs rose faster than repair income this quarter |

Book-keeping is the foundation. If it is careless, every statement built on it is unreliable. But entering figures does not tell the owner whether the business is doing well, so both are needed.

## 1.1 Why profit and loss is measured

**Profit** is the amount by which income earned in a period exceeds the expenses of earning it. If expenses are greater, the business has made a **loss**. The measurement is made for a stated period, usually a year, and is shown in the statement of profit or loss.

Olivera Candles is owned by Ines Olivera. Her figures for the year to 30 June, in dollars:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 86,400 |
| Materials | 31,200 |
| Rent | 12,000 |
| Wages | 18,600 |
| Other expenses | 6,300 |
| **Total expenses** | **68,100** |
| **Profit for the year** | **18,300** |

Profit is 86,400 − 68,100 = 18,300. The year before, profit was 14,640. The increase is 3,660, which is 3,660 ÷ 14,640 = 25.0% more. Measuring this gives several things:

- **A verdict on performance.** The owner sees whether the effort and money put in produced a return, and by how much.
- **A guide to withdrawals.** Ines took 12,000 for personal use, leaving 6,300 of profit in the business. If she had taken 20,000, she would have drawn out more than the business earned.
- **A comparison over time.** A 25.0% rise in profit tells her more than the profit figure alone.
- **Evidence for outsiders.** A lender deciding whether to give a loan, or a tax authority working out tax, wants a profit figure produced by recognised methods.
- **A basis for decisions.** Whether to raise prices, cut a cost or expand depends on what the profit figure shows.

A loss is equally informative. It warns the owner before cash runs out that something must change.

## 1.1 Monitoring progress and supporting decisions

Accounting information is useful because it is compared with something: an earlier period, a plan, or another business. Gurpreet Dhillon owns Dhillon Print Studio. Her monthly figures for the first half of the year, in dollars:

| Month | Revenue | Expenses | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| January | 7,200 | 6,900 | 300 |
| February | 7,650 | 7,100 | 550 |
| March | 8,100 | 7,300 | 800 |
| April | 8,400 | 7,350 | 1,050 |
| May | 8,820 | 7,500 | 1,320 |
| June | 9,300 | 7,640 | 1,660 |
| **Total** | **49,470** | **43,790** | **5,680** |

Revenue grew by 29.2% from January to June (9,300 ÷ 7,200 = 1.292) while expenses grew by only 10.7% (7,640 ÷ 6,900 = 1.107). That is how she **monitors progress**: the business is becoming more profitable each month.

The same figures support **decisions**. A steady rise in profit might justify hiring a part-time assistant. If profit had fallen while revenue rose, she would look at which expense was climbing before agreeing to take on more work. Accounting information does not make the decision; it gives the owner facts to base it on. Other decisions of the same kind include whether to buy more equipment, how much credit to allow customers, and whether to ask the bank for a loan.

## 1.2 Assets, liabilities and owner's equity

These are the three building blocks of the accounting equation.

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Asset** | A resource owned by the business that has value | Premises, equipment, vehicles, inventory, trade receivables, bank balance, cash |
| **Liability** | An amount the business owes to someone outside it | Trade payables, bank loan, bank overdraft |
| **Owner's equity** | The owner's claim on the business: what is left of the assets after the liabilities are paid | Capital paid in, plus profits kept in the business, less drawings |

Note the direction of each credit item. **Trade receivables** are customers who owe the business money, so they are an asset. **Trade payables** are suppliers the business owes, so they are a liability. A **bank overdraft** is borrowing, so it is a liability even though it appears next to the bank.

Owner's equity changes in four ways:

- It **increases** when the owner pays in more capital.
- It **increases** when the business makes a profit.
- It **decreases** when the business makes a loss.
- It **decreases** when the owner takes **drawings**, meaning money or goods taken out for personal use.

You will also see the word capital used for the owner's equity, particularly the amount the owner first paid in.

## 1.2 The accounting equation

Everything the business owns is financed either by outsiders (liabilities) or by the owner (owner's equity). So:

**Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity**

Rearranged: Owner's equity = Assets − Liabilities, and Liabilities = Assets − Owner's equity.

Here is Karimi Wheels at 31 December (dollar amounts):

```
Assets                    dollars       Liabilities         dollars
Equipment                  14,500       Trade payables        2,700
Inventory of parts          3,200       Bank loan             7,500
Trade receivables           1,850       Total liabilities    10,200
Bank                        6,400
Cash                          350       Owner's equity       16,100
Total assets               26,300       Liabilities + equity 26,300
```

Total assets are 26,300 and total liabilities 10,200, so owner's equity is 26,300 − 10,200 = 16,100. The two sides agree because the equity figure is what is left over.

## 1.2 Applying the equation

### Finding a missing figure

Write the equation, put in what you know, and solve for the gap.

- Assets 64,500 and liabilities 21,300: owner's equity = 64,500 − 21,300 = **43,200**.
- Liabilities 18,000 and owner's equity 52,500: assets = 18,000 + 52,500 = **70,500**.
- Assets 39,800 and owner's equity 27,350: liabilities = 39,800 − 27,350 = **12,450**.

### Following transactions

Every transaction changes at least two items, and the equation still balances afterwards. Moira Waddell starts Waddell Pastries (dollar amounts):

| Transaction | Assets | Liabilities | Owner's equity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Moira pays 30,000 into a business bank account | 30,000 | 0 | 30,000 |
| 2. Buys an oven for 9,000 by bank transfer | 30,000 | 0 | 30,000 |
| 3. Buys ingredients for 2,400 on credit | 32,400 | 2,400 | 30,000 |
| 4. Takes a bank loan of 8,000 | 40,400 | 10,400 | 30,000 |
| 5. Sells goods that cost 1,100 for 1,900 cash | 41,200 | 10,400 | 30,800 |
| 6. Pays rent of 600 from the bank | 40,600 | 10,400 | 30,200 |
| 7. Pays the supplier 1,500 | 39,100 | 8,900 | 30,200 |
| 8. Moira takes 700 cash as drawings | 38,400 | 8,900 | 29,500 |

Transaction 2 swaps one asset for another, so the total does not move. Transaction 3 raises assets and liabilities together. Transaction 5 raises assets by 800 because the goods sold earned 800 more than they cost: that 800 is profit, so owner's equity rises. Transaction 6 is an expense, which reduces profit and therefore owner's equity. In transaction 8, drawings reduce owner's equity but are not an expense. After each line, check that assets equal liabilities plus owner's equity; the last line gives 38,400 = 8,900 + 29,500.

### Working out profit from two positions

Gurpreet's position at the start and end of the year, in dollars:

| | Start | End |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | 52,000 | 61,800 |
| Liabilities | 17,500 | 15,300 |
| Owner's equity | 34,500 | 46,500 |

Owner's equity rose by 12,000. During the year she paid in 4,000 of extra capital and took 9,600 in drawings. Profit = closing equity − opening equity − capital introduced + drawings = 46,500 − 34,500 − 4,000 + 9,600 = **17,600**.

## Common errors

- Treating drawings as an expense. Drawings reduce owner's equity directly and are not deducted when finding profit.
- Calling profit "the cash we have". Profit is a measure of performance; the bank balance can differ from it.
- Listing a bank overdraft as an asset.
- Swapping trade receivables and trade payables.
- Saying that borrowing increases owner's equity. A loan raises assets and liabilities by the same amount.
- Writing book-keeping and accounting as if they mean the same thing, or defining accounting as "writing in the books".
- Stopping after one half of a transaction. Ask what the second change is.
