## Questions

**1.** A launderette has fixed costs of 450 a week. At 300 loads a week its average variable cost is 2.50. Calculate average fixed cost, total cost and average cost. **[4]**

**2.** Explain the difference between fixed costs and variable costs, using a print shop as an example. **[4]**

**3.** A print shop has fixed costs of 200 a week. At 40 units its variable costs are 280.

**(a)** Calculate average fixed cost, average variable cost and average cost at 40 units. **[3]**
**(b)** The rent rises so that fixed costs become 300. Calculate the new average cost at 40 units, the percentage change in average cost (to 1 d.p.), and state the effect on average variable cost. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what separates the short run from the long run, and state what determines the shape of the long-run average cost curve. **[3]**

**5.** A tile maker's long-run average cost (LRAC) per tile is below. Output is in hundreds of tiles a week.

| Output | 50 | 100 | 150 | 200 | 250 | 300 | 350 | 400 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LRAC | 14.0 | 11.2 | 9.8 | 9.0 | 9.0 | 9.0 | 9.9 | 11.2 |

**(a)** By what percentage does LRAC change as output goes from 50 to 100? **[2]**
**(b)** State the minimum efficient scale. **[1]**
**(c)** Output rises from 300 to 400. Calculate the percentage rise in output and in LRAC, and give one possible cause. **[3]**

**6.** Classify each as an internal or an external economy of scale, giving a reason. **(i)** A firm orders in bulk and gets a lower price per unit. **(ii)** A specialist hardware supplier opens beside a cluster of furniture makers, cutting delivery costs for all. **(iii)** A large firm borrows at a lower interest rate than a small one. **(iv)** A local college starts a course in the skills that the industry needs. **[4]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why a firm may suffer diseconomies of scale. **[4]**

**8.** A flower stall sells 90 bunches a day at 12 each. After a price cut to 10 it sells 110 bunches.

**(a)** Work out total revenue at the two prices. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in total revenue (to 1 d.p.). **[1]**
**(c)** State the average revenue at the lower price. **[1]**

**9.** A hat stall faces the demand and cost data below (fixed costs are included in total cost).

| Hats | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price | 30 | 27 | 24 | 21 |
| Total cost | 550 | 670 | 790 | 950 |

**(a)** Calculate total revenue at each output. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate profit at each output and state which output gives the most profit. **[2]**
**(c)** At the most profitable output, calculate profit per unit and check it against your answer to (b). **[1]**
**(d)** (A2 skill) Calculate the marginal revenue and marginal cost per hat between 40 and 50 hats. **[2]**

**10.** A firm sells 60 units at a price of 18.

**(a)** Calculate average revenue and total revenue. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a firm's average revenue curve and its demand curve are the same line. Then calculate total revenue at 60 units if demand rises so that the price at every quantity is 2 higher. **[2]**

**11.** A furniture maker plans to double the size of its workshop. Evaluate whether this will lower its average costs. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** AFC = 450 ÷ 300 = 1.50 [1]. VC = 2.50 × 300 = 750 [1]. TC = 450 + 750 = 1,200 [1]. AC = 1,200 ÷ 300 = **4.00** [1] (check: 1.50 + 2.50). **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing each formula before substituting makes your method easy to follow, and the AFC + AVC check catches slips.

**2.** Fixed costs stay the same whatever the output while the short run lasts [1], for example the rent or lease of the print shop's premises or printer [1]. Variable costs change as output changes [1], for example paper and ink, which rise with the number of jobs printed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns less when it has no example, and an example earns less when the link to output is missing.

**3. (a)** AFC = 200 ÷ 40 = **5.0** [1]. AVC = 280 ÷ 40 = **7.0** [1]. AC = 5.0 + 7.0 = **12.0** [1].
**(b)** New TC = 300 + 280 = 580, so AC = 580 ÷ 40 = **14.5** [1]. Change = (14.5 − 12.0) ÷ 12.0 × 100 = **+20.8%** [1]. AVC is **unchanged at 7.0** because variable costs did not change [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Percentage changes are divided by the original value, not the new one, and answers should state whether the change is a rise or a fall.

**4.** In the short run one or more inputs are locked in [1]. In the long run every input can be varied [1]. The shape of the LRAC curve is determined by economies and diseconomies of scale [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Describe what can and cannot be changed rather than giving months or years, because the two periods are not set lengths of time.

**5. (a)** (11.2 − 14.0) ÷ 14.0 × 100 [1] = **−20.0%** [1].
**(b)** LRAC is lowest at 9.0, first reached at **200** (hundred tiles) [1].
**(c)** Output: (400 − 300) ÷ 300 = **+33.3%** [1]. LRAC: (11.2 − 9.0) ÷ 9.0 = **+24.4%** [1]. Cause, for example: communication and co-ordination problems in a larger organisation raise cost per tile [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a cause that explains why unit costs rise, not just the label "diseconomies of scale". MES is output, so include units.

**6.** (i) Internal, because the firm's own larger orders earn the discount [1]. (ii) External, because the supplier arrived because of the cluster and every firm gains [1]. (iii) Internal (financial), because it depends on the firm's own size [1]. (iv) External, because the whole industry benefits from the trained workers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The test that works is whether the growth is of the firm or of the industry or area; a reason that uses it is more convincing than a bare label.

**7.** Any two developed reasons: Co-ordination and communication: more layers of management slow decisions [1], so mistakes and delays raise cost per unit [1]. Worker motivation: staff in a very large firm feel remote from its aims [1], so effort falls and output per worker falls, raising unit cost [1]. (Other valid: loss of control, duplication.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A cause is only developed once you show what it does to cost per unit; two causes with no consequence are each half-finished.

**8. (a)** TR at 12 = 12 × 90 = **1,080** [1]. TR at 10 = 10 × 110 = **1,100** [1].
**(b)** (1,100 − 1,080) ÷ 1,080 × 100 = **+1.9%** [1].
**(c)** AR = 1,100 ÷ 110 = **10** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* TR rising does not show profit rising; any extension should mention what happens to total costs when 20 more bunches are sold.

**9. (a)** TR = price × quantity [1]: **600, 810, 960, 1,050** [1].
**(b)** Profit = TR − TC: 600 − 550 = 50; 810 − 670 = 140; 960 − 790 = 170; 1,050 − 950 = 100 [1]. Highest profit is **170, at 40 hats** [1].
**(c)** AC = 790 ÷ 40 = 19.75; profit per unit = 24 − 19.75 = **4.25** [1]; and 4.25 × 40 = 170, which matches.
**(d)** MR = (1,050 − 960) ÷ 10 = **9** [1]. MC = (950 − 790) ÷ 10 = **16** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Setting out TR and TC for each row before subtracting means one slip does not hide the rest of your method.

**10. (a)** AR = price = **18** [1]. TR = 18 × 60 = **1,080** [1].
**(b)** At each price consumers buy the quantity demanded, so plotting AR (price) against quantity traces the demand curve [1]. New price 20, so TR = 20 × 60 = **1,200** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Make clear that AR equals price only when one price is charged to all buyers.

**11.** Knowledge and analysis: Doubling the workshop is a long-run change, so it can bring economies of scale [1]. Technical: larger machines or a longer production line cut cost per unit [1]. Purchasing: bigger timber orders earn a lower price per unit, so AC falls [1]. Managerial or financial economies may also apply, for example specialist managers or cheaper loans [1]. But a larger workshop may bring diseconomies, such as poorer communication [1], which could raise AC [1]. Evaluation: the outcome depends on whether the firm is already near its minimum efficient scale [1]. It depends on whether demand is high enough to use the extra capacity, otherwise AC is higher [1]. Building costs and delays come first, so any fall in AC may take time [1]. Judgement: AC is likely to fall if the firm is currently small and demand is growing, but not if it is already at MES [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that names the conditions under which the answer changes is stronger than a list of points; apply each point to the furniture maker.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the wrong output, or not subtracting fixed cost to find variable cost.
- Dividing a percentage change by the final value instead of the starting value.
- Leaving out the minus sign for a loss or a fall.
- Explaining the short-run U-shape with economies of scale.
- Labelling internal and external economies without a reason.
- Naming a diseconomy without saying why unit cost rises.
- Treating higher revenue as higher profit.
- Giving the minimum efficient scale as a cost instead of an output.
