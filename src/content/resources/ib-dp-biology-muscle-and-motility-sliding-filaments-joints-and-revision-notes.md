## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Motile | Able to move the whole body from place to place |
| Sessile | Fixed in one place; parts may still move |
| Sarcomere | Unit of a myofibril between two Z lines |
| Actin | Protein of the thin filaments |
| Myosin | Protein of the thick filaments; heads bind actin |
| Titin | Elastic protein linking thick filaments to Z lines |
| Motor unit | One motor neuron plus the fibres it controls |
| Neuromuscular junction | Synapse between motor neuron and muscle fibre |
| Antagonistic pair | Two muscles with opposite actions at a joint |
| Tendon | Joins muscle to bone |
| Ligament | Joins bone to bone |
| Range of motion | Angle a joint moves through in one direction |

## B3.3.1 Movement

- Movement is universal in living organisms.
- Motile example: housefly, moving by wings and legs to feed and find mates.
- Sessile example: oak tree. Leaves turn towards light, stomata open and close, shoots grow towards light.
- Sessile does not mean "does not move".

## B3.3.2 Sliding filament model

| Region | Contains | Change in contraction |
|---|---|---|
| A band | Whole length of the thick filaments | Unchanged |
| I band | No overlap, thin filaments alone | Reduced |
| H zone | Centre of the A band, no thin filaments | Narrows or vanishes |
| Sarcomere (Z to Z) | Everything | Shorter |

**Method in steps: the cross-bridge cycle**

1. Calcium ions leave the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
2. Calcium binds troponin; tropomyosin moves; binding sites on actin are exposed.
3. Myosin head binds actin (cross-bridge).
4. Power stroke: the head pulls actin towards the centre and sheds ADP and phosphate.
5. New ATP attaches; the head lets go.
6. ATP is hydrolysed; the head resets and binds again.

**Worked reminder.** Sarcomere 2.8 µm shortens to 2.1 µm. Shortening = 0.7 ÷ 2.8 × 100 = 25%. With an A band of 1.6 µm, the I band changes from 1.2 µm to 0.5 µm, a fall of 58.3%. The filaments slid. They did not shorten.

## B3.3.3 Titin and antagonism

- Titin is a giant elastic protein.
- It stores energy when stretched and helps the sarcomere recoil.
- It limits stretch, so it prevents overstretching.
- Force comes only from contraction, so a second muscle must reverse the movement.
- Contraction of one muscle of the pair stretches the other.

**Must-know distinction: contraction versus relaxation.** Contraction uses ATP and cross-bridges. Relaxation is the end of the calcium signal; the muscle is then lengthened by its antagonist, with titin helping recoil.

## B3.3.4 Motor units

- Motor neuron axon branches; each branch ends at one fibre at a neuromuscular junction.
- Impulse arrives, acetylcholine is released, it binds receptors on the sarcolemma, and the fibre depolarises.
- Depolarisation spreads into the fibre and releases calcium.
- All fibres of one unit contract together.
- Small units (few fibres) give fine control; large units give strong force.
- More units recruited means more force.

**Worked reminder.** 9 units of 60 fibres each, with 0.04 N per fibre: 9 × 60 = 540 fibres; 540 × 0.04 = 21.6 N.

## B3.3.5 Skeletons

| Feature | Exoskeleton | Endoskeleton |
|---|---|---|
| Example | Arthropod such as a crab or insect | Vertebrate such as a human |
| Position | Outside the body | Inside the body |
| Muscle attachment | Inner surface of the hard covering | Outer surface of bone, by tendons |

- Both give anchorage for muscles and act as levers.
- Moment rule for balance: muscle force × its distance from the pivot = load × its distance from the pivot.

**Worked reminder.** Load 15 N at 24 cm; muscle inserts 3 cm from the pivot. Force = 15 × 24 ÷ 3 = 120 N.

## B3.3.6 Synovial joint (hip)

- Ball-and-socket: head of the femur in the socket of the pelvis.
- Cartilage: reduces friction, absorbs shock.
- Synovial fluid: lubricates, nourishes cartilage, cushions.
- Ligaments: stabilise, bone to bone.
- Tendons: transmit muscle force to bone.
- Muscles: contract in antagonistic pairs to move the joint.

## B3.3.7 Range of motion

- Measure with a goniometer, or with computer analysis of images.
- Compare several dimensions of the same joint, such as flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and rotation.
- Range = largest angle minus smallest angle for that movement.
- Use repeat readings and a mean.

**Worked reminder.** Flexion of 150° and extension of 5° from one starting position give a flexion-extension range of 145°. Another person's mean of 95° against 70° is a 35.7% increase.

**Method in steps: using a goniometer**

1. Fix the pivot of the goniometer over the joint centre.
2. Align one arm with each bone.
3. Read the angle at the start and end of the movement.
4. Repeat, then take the mean.

## B3.3.8 Intercostal muscles

| Muscle layer | Effect on ribs | Breathing role |
|---|---|---|
| External intercostals | Raise ribs up and out | Inhalation |
| Internal intercostals | Pull ribs down and in | Forced exhalation |

- Fibres in the two layers run in different directions, so they pull the ribcage opposite ways.
- Contraction of either layer stretches its partner, and the partner's titin stores potential energy.

## B3.3.9 Reasons for locomotion

| Reason | Example |
|---|---|
| Foraging | Wolf pack hunting over a large territory |
| Escape from danger | Scallop swimming away from a starfish |
| Searching for a mate | Male moth following pheromones |
| Migration | Wildebeest following rainfall |

## B3.3.10 Marine mammals

- **Streamlining:** reduces drag.
- **Flippers:** forelimbs modified for steering and balance.
- **Fluke:** tail moves up and down for thrust.
- **Airways:** blowhole; a large fraction of lung air is replaced per breath; periodic breathing between dives.

**Worked reminder.** A breath exchanges 14 L of a 16 L lung: 14 ÷ 16 × 100 = 87.5%.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Thick versus thin filament:** thick is myosin, found only in the A band; thin is actin (with troponin and tropomyosin), attached at the Z line.
- **Motile versus sessile:** whole-body movement versus fixed position. Both can move internal or growing parts.
- **Tendon versus ligament:** tendon links muscle to bone; ligament links bone to bone.
- **Exoskeleton versus endoskeleton:** muscles attach inside a hard outer case, or outside internal bones.
- **Flexion versus extension:** flexion reduces the angle at a joint; extension increases it. Abduction moves a limb away from the midline and adduction brings it back.
- **External versus internal intercostals:** one raises the ribs, the other lowers them.
- **Fish tail versus whale fluke:** side-to-side beat versus up-and-down beat.

## Data and graph reminders

- When you are given sarcomere lengths, find the change, then divide by the starting value for a percentage.
- When asked what a constant A band shows, link it to thick filaments keeping their length, so sliding is the explanation.
- When comparing ranges of motion, use means of repeated readings and quote the units (degrees).
- When asked to evaluate a goniometer method, mention error in lining up the arms, differences in starting posture and a small number of repeats.

## Quick self-test

1. Name one motile and one sessile species and state one movement of the sessile one.
2. Which band stays the same length during contraction?
3. State what happens to the H zone.
4. Give two roles of titin.
5. Why do muscles work in antagonistic pairs?
6. Define a motor unit.
7. A muscle has 8 motor units of 50 fibres each at 0.04 N per fibre. How much force do all the units give?
8. Name the bones that meet at the hip.
9. Which structure joins bone to bone?
10. State two adaptations of marine mammals for swimming.

### Answers

1. Housefly (motile) and oak tree (sessile); the oak turns leaves to the light.
2. The A band.
3. It narrows or disappears.
4. It helps sarcomere recoil after stretch, and it prevents overstretching.
5. Muscle pulls only during contraction, so another muscle must pull the other way.
6. One motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it controls.
7. 8 × 50 = 400 fibres; 400 × 0.04 = **16 N**.
8. The femur and the pelvis.
9. A ligament.
10. Any two of: streamlined body, flippers, fluke moving up and down, blowhole with periodic breathing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that actin or myosin filaments "shorten".
- Forgetting that the A band stays constant.
- Describing the sarcomere without naming the I band, H zone or Z line when asked for observations.
- Mixing up tendons and ligaments.
- Saying muscles "push" or that the biceps "extends" the arm.
- Describing the fluke as moving side to side.
- Not stating the direction in which the ribs move when comparing external and internal intercostals.
- Giving a range of motion without units or without saying which movement was measured.
- Listing a reason for locomotion without a named example.

## Frequently asked questions

**What happens to the bands of a sarcomere during contraction?**
The A band stays the same length, the I band becomes shorter, the H zone narrows or disappears, and the distance between Z lines falls, because the filaments slide past each other.

**What are the roles of titin?**
Titin is a giant elastic protein that stores energy when a sarcomere is stretched, helping it recoil, and limits stretch so the sarcomere is not overstretched.

**What is the difference between motile and sessile organisms?**
A motile organism, such as a housefly, moves its whole body from place to place; a sessile organism, such as an oak tree, stays fixed but still moves parts, for example turning leaves towards light.

**How do the external and internal intercostal muscles work?**
External intercostals raise the ribs up and out for inhalation, and internal intercostals pull them down and in for forced exhalation; contraction of either layer stretches the other. This is HL-only content in IB DP Biology B3.3.
