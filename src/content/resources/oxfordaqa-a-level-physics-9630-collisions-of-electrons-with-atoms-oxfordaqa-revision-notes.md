Data: h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; hc = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ J m.

## Core recall

- Energy levels in atoms are discrete and negative; zero is the ionised state.
- **Excitation**: an electron moves to a higher level; the atom stays neutral.
- **Ionisation**: an electron is removed; a positive ion is left.
- A free electron can excite an atom if its kinetic energy is **at least** the gap; it keeps the remainder.
- A photon is absorbed only if its energy **exactly** equals a gap, or is enough to ionise.
- De-excitation emits a photon with hf = E1 – E2.
- Line spectra show only fixed photon energies, so energy levels must be discrete.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electronvolt (eV) | Energy gained by an electron accelerated through 1 V; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J |
| Ground state | The lowest energy level of an atom |
| Excitation energy | Energy needed to move an electron from the ground state to a given higher level |
| Ionisation energy | Energy needed to remove an electron from the ground state completely |
| Emission line spectrum | Bright lines at particular wavelengths from an excited gas |
| Absorption line spectrum | Dark lines in a continuous spectrum where a cool gas has absorbed photons |
| Phosphor | Coating that absorbs ultraviolet photons and emits visible light |
| Thermionic emission | Release of electrons from a heated metal filament |
| Characteristic X-rays | Line X-rays at wavelengths fixed by the target metal |

## Conversions

| From | To | Do |
|---|---|---|
| eV | J | × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ |
| J | eV | ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ |
| Photon energy E | Frequency | f = E/h |
| Photon energy E | Wavelength | λ = hc/E |
| Tube voltage V | Minimum X-ray wavelength | λ(min) = hc/eV |

## Collision outcomes

| Free electron's energy | Result |
|---|---|
| Below the first gap | No excitation; elastic collision |
| Between first gap and ionisation energy | Excitation to any reachable level; electron keeps the rest |
| Above ionisation energy | Ionisation possible |

Number of lines from the top n levels: n(n − 1)/2.

## Fluorescent tube sequence

1. Free electrons accelerated by the p.d.
2. Collisions excite (and some ionise) mercury atoms.
3. De-excitation emits ultraviolet photons.
4. The phosphor absorbs ultraviolet and re-emits visible photons of lower energy, in several smaller steps.

## Hydrogen levels to know how to use

Levels (eV): −13.6, −3.40, −1.51, −0.85.

| Transition | ΔE / eV | λ / nm | Region |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 → 2 | 1.89 | 658 | Visible (red) |
| 4 → 2 | 2.55 | 488 | Visible (blue-green) |
| 2 → 1 | 10.2 | 122 | Ultraviolet |

## X-ray tube and spectrum

- Evacuated tube; heated filament releases electrons; high p.d. accelerates them to a metal target, which needs cooling.
- **Continuous spectrum**: electrons decelerating in the target; cut-off at λ(min) = hc/eV.
- **Line spectrum**: inner electron knocked out, outer electron drops in, photon of fixed energy emitted. Depends only on the target metal.
- Higher voltage: shorter λ(min), more intense, lines unchanged.
- Higher filament current: more intense, same shape and λ(min).
- Medical uses: imaging bones (bone absorbs more), CT scans, radiotherapy for tumours. X-rays ionise, so doses are kept low.

## Exam traps

- "Exactly the gap" applies to photons, not to colliding electrons.
- Use the difference between two levels, not one level's value.
- Keep units consistent: all eV or all J.
- Only tube voltage changes λ(min); current changes intensity.
- Characteristic lines do not move when the voltage changes.
- The phosphor emits lower-energy photons than it absorbs, not higher.
- An atom ionised from the ground state needs the full ionisation energy, not the gap to the next level.

## Self-test

1. Convert 4.2 eV to joules.
2. Convert 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J to eV.
3. State the difference between excitation and ionisation.
4. An atom has levels −6.0 eV and −2.4 eV. Find the wavelength of the photon emitted in a transition between them.
5. An electron with 3.0 eV hits an atom whose first gap is 2.1 eV. What energy does the electron keep if it excites the atom?
6. A photon of 3.0 eV meets the same atom, which has no gap of 3.0 eV. What happens?
7. How many lines can an atom excited to its fifth level produce?
8. Why is a phosphor coating needed in a fluorescent tube?
9. Why are line spectra evidence for discrete energy levels?
10. An X-ray tube runs at 50 kV. Find λ(min).
11. What happens to the characteristic lines if the tube voltage is increased?
12. Give one medical use of X-rays and the property it relies on.

### Answers

1. 4.2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **6.72 × 10⁻¹⁹ J**.
2. 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **2.25 eV**.
3. Excitation raises an electron to a higher level within the atom; ionisation **removes** it completely, leaving an ion.
4. ΔE = 3.6 eV = 5.76 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/5.76 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **3.45 × 10⁻⁷ m** (345 nm).
5. 3.0 − 2.1 = **0.9 eV**.
6. It is **not absorbed** (it passes through), because its energy does not match a gap.
7. 5 × 4/2 = **10 lines**.
8. The mercury emits mainly **ultraviolet**; the phosphor absorbs it and emits **visible** light.
9. Only certain photon energies are emitted, so only certain **energy differences**, and hence only certain levels, exist.
10. λ(min) = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/(1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁴) = **2.49 × 10⁻¹¹ m**.
11. They stay at the **same wavelengths** (they become more intense).
12. Imaging bones, because **bone absorbs X-rays more than soft tissue**; or radiotherapy, because X-rays **ionise** and damage tumour cells.
