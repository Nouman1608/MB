This guide covers **Topic 5, The effects of using IT**, of Cambridge
IGCSE Information and Communication Technology 0417. It follows [4.2,
Network issues and
communication](/resources/igcse-ict-network-issues-and-communication/)
and shifts from technical infrastructure to the broader social and
physical impact of IT use -- a topic examined through discussion and
evaluation of trade-offs rather than pure factual recall.

## 5.1 Microprocessor-controlled devices in the home

You need to know the positive and negative effects of microprocessors
and smart devices in monitoring and controlling devices in the home,
across five named dimensions: impact on lifestyle, leisure time,
physical fitness, security of data, and the degree of social
interaction. A smart heating or lighting system, for example, can
positively affect lifestyle by automating routine tasks and saving
time, but may negatively affect physical fitness if it removes small
amounts of everyday physical activity, and raises data security
concerns since a networked smart device is a potential target for
unauthorised access. Answering a question on this subtopic well means
naming a specific dimension (not just "it's good" or "it's bad") and
explaining the mechanism behind the effect.

## 5.1 Microprocessor-controlled devices in transport

You need to know the positive and negative effects of microprocessors
and smart devices in monitoring and controlling transport, covering
security of data, autonomous vehicles, and transport safety
specifically. Autonomous (self-driving) vehicles are a particularly
rich example: potential positive effects include reduced human error
in driving and the ability for a passenger to use travel time
productively, while potential negative effects include job displacement
for professional drivers, new safety questions around a system's
decision-making in unusual situations, and the data-security risk of a
vehicle's control systems being accessed without authorisation.

## 5.2 Potential health problems from prolonged IT use

You need to know a named set of health issues linked to prolonged use
of IT equipment -- repetitive strain injury (RSI), back problems, eye
problems, and headaches -- along with the causes of each and strategies
for preventing them.

| Health issue | Typical cause | Prevention strategy |
|---|---|---|
| RSI | Repeated small movements, e.g. typing or mouse use | Take regular breaks; use an ergonomic keyboard/mouse |
| Back problems | Poor posture, unsuitable chair or desk height | Use an adjustable chair; keep screen at eye level |
| Eye problems | Prolonged screen focus, poor lighting, glare | Follow a break routine (e.g. look away periodically); use anti-glare screens; ensure good lighting |
| Headaches | Eye strain, screen glare, poor posture, dehydration | Take breaks; adjust screen brightness/contrast; stay hydrated |

Exam answers on this subtopic are expected to link a named health
issue to its specific cause and a specific, practical prevention
strategy, rather than a single generic answer such as "take breaks"
applied identically to every health issue -- each issue has causes and
countermeasures worth stating distinctly.

## A worked example: smart home heating

A smart thermostat that learns a household's routine and adjusts
heating automatically illustrates several of the named dimensions at
once. **Lifestyle**: reduces the need to manually adjust heating,
freeing time for other activities. **Leisure time**: by reducing a
routine chore, it may modestly increase time available for leisure.
**Physical fitness**: largely neutral for this specific device, though
the broader trend of automating small household tasks is sometimes
raised as a factor in reduced everyday physical activity. **Security of
data**: the thermostat is network-connected and typically linked to an
account, so it represents a potential target if that account or the
home network is not properly secured. **Social interaction**: largely
unaffected directly, though smart-home ecosystems in general are
sometimes discussed as reducing face-to-face household coordination
(such as discussing who last adjusted the heating) since the system
now manages it automatically. Working through a single device across
all five named dimensions like this, rather than jumping between
unrelated examples, is good practice for the "discuss the effects of"
style of question this subtopic favours.

## How to approach it

For 5.1, practise structuring an answer around the specific named
dimension a question asks about (lifestyle, leisure time, physical
fitness, data security, or social interaction for the home; data
security, autonomous vehicles, or transport safety for transport)
rather than giving a general, undirected opinion about smart
technology -- Cambridge's mark schemes reward answers that engage with
the named factor directly. For 5.2, learn the four health issues
together with their specific cause and prevention pairing (as in the
table above) rather than a single blended list of "IT health tips",
since exam questions frequently ask about one named issue at a time
and expect an answer specific to that issue's actual physical cause.
When discussing advantages and disadvantages in either subtopic, aim
to state both sides explicitly and reach a brief, reasoned judgement
where the question asks for one, since Cambridge's "discuss" and
"evaluate" command words in this topic specifically look for balanced
consideration rather than a one-sided answer.

## Why this topic sits where it does

Topic 5 acts as a bridge between the technical topics before it
(Topics 1-4, covering hardware, devices, storage and networks) and the
applied real-world systems that follow in Topic 6, ICT applications.
Where Topics 1-4 ask "how does this technology work", Topic 5 asks
"what happens to people and society when this technology is used" --
a shift in the type of thinking the topic expects, from factual recall
toward reasoned discussion of trade-offs, which is worth recognising
explicitly when revising so you approach 5.1 and 5.2 questions with
the right kind of answer structure rather than trying to recall a
single "correct" fact.
