These questions cover **3.3.13.3 Action of anticancer drugs** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3, with the linked cisplatin statement in 3.2.5.3. It is International A-level only content, assessed in Unit 4 and able to reappear in Unit 5. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [anticancer drugs study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-13-3/) or the [anticancer drugs revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-13-3-revision-notes/) first. Take Ar values as K = 39.1, Pt = 195.1, Cl = 35.5, N = 14.0, H = 1.0 and C = 12.0.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Give the formula of cisplatin and deduce the oxidation state of platinum in it. **[2]**

**2.** State the shape of cisplatin and the Cl–Pt–Cl bond angle. **[2]**

**3.** Which statement describes the bond formed between platinum and DNA when cisplatin acts as a drug?

A: An ionic bond between Pt²⁺ and a negatively charged chloride ion on DNA.

B: A co-ordinate bond in which a nitrogen atom on DNA donates a lone pair to platinum.

C: A hydrogen bond between an NH₃ ligand and a base on DNA.

D: A covalent bond in which platinum donates a lone pair to a nitrogen atom on DNA.

**[1]**

**4.** Platinum in cisplatin has a 2+ charge, yet cisplatin is a neutral molecule. Explain why. **[2]**

**5.** Calculate the percentage by mass of platinum in cisplatin. Give your answer to three significant figures. **[2]**

**6.** When cisplatin reacts with DNA, state which ligands are replaced and which ligands remain bonded to platinum. **[2]**

**7.** Explain why cisplatin can harm healthy cells as well as cancer cells. **[2]**

### Section B

**8.** Square planar complexes of formula [MA₂B₂] can exist as cis and trans isomers.

**(a)** Describe the arrangement of the two chloride ligands in cisplatin and in its trans isomer. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why a tetrahedral complex of formula [MA₂B₂] cannot show cis–trans isomerism. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why the trans isomer cannot bind to DNA in the same way as cisplatin. **[2]**

**9.** In the cell, cisplatin reacts with nitrogen atoms on guanine bases in DNA. Represent a guanine nitrogen with its lone pair as G–N:.

**(a)** Explain how a nitrogen atom on guanine forms a bond to platinum. **[2]**

**(b)** Write an equation for the replacement of both chloride ligands by guanine nitrogen atoms. Show the charge on the platinum-containing product. **[2]**

**(c)** State the co-ordination number of platinum before and after the reaction. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain how this reaction stops a cancer cell from dividing. **[2]**

**10.** Cisplatin can be made by a ligand substitution reaction between potassium tetrachloroplatinate(II), K₂[PtCl₄], and ammonia.

**(a)** Write an ionic equation for the reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** Deduce the oxidation state of platinum in [PtCl₄]²⁻ and state whether it changes during the reaction. **[2]**

**(c)** A student reacts 0.623 g of K₂[PtCl₄] with excess ammonia and obtains 0.315 g of cisplatin. Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**

**11.** A research group studies a complex of platinum(II) in which one chloride ligand of cisplatin has been replaced by a third ammonia ligand, giving [Pt(NH₃)₃Cl]⁺.

**(a)** Explain why this complex carries a 1+ charge. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest how the way this complex binds to DNA would differ from the way cisplatin binds, and why it might be less effective at stopping DNA replication. **[3]**

**12.** Cisplatin is widely used to treat some cancers, although it has serious side effects.

Evaluate the use of cisplatin as an anticancer drug. In your answer, explain how it works, why it causes adverse effects, and how society and doctors decide whether its use is justified. **[6]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** **[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]** [1]; x + 2(0) + 2(−1) = 0, so Pt is **+2** [1] **[2]**

**2.** **Square planar** [1]; **90°** [1] **[2]**

**3.** **B** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* D reverses the direction of electron donation. In every co-ordinate bond to a metal ion, the ligand donates the electron pair.

**4.** Each chloride ligand carries a 1− charge and NH₃ is neutral [1]; the two 1− charges cancel the 2+ on platinum, giving an overall charge of zero [1] **[2]**

**5.** Mr = 195.1 + 2(14.0) + 6(1.0) + 2(35.5) = 300.1 [1]; % Pt = 195.1 ÷ 300.1 × 100 = **65.0%** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Six hydrogen atoms, not three: there are two NH₃ ligands. Using Mr 297.1 gives 65.7% and loses the second mark.

**6.** The two **chloride** ligands are replaced (by nitrogen atoms on DNA) [1]; the two **ammonia** ligands remain bonded [1] **[2]**

**7.** Cisplatin binds to DNA whether or not the cell is cancerous, so it also binds to the DNA of healthy cells [1]; this stops healthy cells replicating their DNA and dividing, causing side effects such as kidney damage or hearing loss [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Because it is toxic" scores nothing. The mark is for linking the side effects to the same DNA reaction that makes the drug work.

### Section B

**8.**

**(a)** In cisplatin the two chloride ligands are next to each other, at 90° (cis) [1]; in the trans isomer they are opposite each other, at 180° [1]

**(b)** In a tetrahedral shape every ligand position is adjacent to every other position [1], so there is only one possible arrangement of two A and two B ligands [1]

**(c)** In the trans isomer the two leaving chloride ligands point in opposite directions [1], so platinum cannot bond to two nitrogen atoms that are close together on the same stretch of DNA [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), "trans is the wrong shape" is too vague; the answer needs the positions of the leaving groups and the consequence for binding two nearby sites.

**9.**

**(a)** The nitrogen atom has a lone pair of electrons [1]; it donates the pair to platinum, forming a co-ordinate bond, as it replaces a chloride ligand [1]

**(b)** [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] + 2 G–N: → [Pt(NH₃)₂(N–G)₂]²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ [1]; product charge shown as **2+** with two Cl⁻ released [1]

**(c)** **4** before and **4** after [1]

**(d)** Platinum bonds to two nitrogen atoms close together, which distorts the DNA [1]; the distorted DNA cannot be replicated, so the cell cannot divide [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* An equation that keeps the chlorides on platinum, or shows Cl bonding to G, cannot score in (b).

**10.**

**(a)** **[PtCl₄]²⁻ + 2NH₃ → [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] + 2Cl⁻** [1]

**(b)** x + 4(−1) = −2, so Pt is **+2** [1]; it does **not** change: the reaction is a ligand substitution, not a redox reaction [1]

**(c)** Mr of K₂[PtCl₄] = 2(39.1) + 195.1 + 4(35.5) = 415.3; amount = 0.623 ÷ 415.3 = 1.500 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; theoretical mass of cisplatin = 1.500 × 10⁻³ × 300.1 = 0.450 g [1]; yield = 0.315 ÷ 0.450 × 100 = **70.0%** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing the masses directly (0.315 ÷ 0.623) gives 50.6% and scores nothing for the yield. Convert to moles and use the 1 : 1 ratio first.

**11.**

**(a)** Pt is +2, the three NH₃ ligands are neutral and the single chloride is 1−, so the overall charge is 2 − 1 = **+1** [1]

**(b)** Only one chloride ligand can be replaced [1], so platinum can bond to only one nitrogen atom on the DNA [1]; it cannot link two nearby sites, so it distorts the DNA less and is less able to stop replication [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Suggest" means applying the cisplatin mechanism to a new complex. Count the leaving groups first; that count drives the whole answer.

**12.** Indicative content; award marks by level for a balanced evaluation with chemical explanation and a judgement [6] **[6]**

- How it works: chloride ligands are replaced by nitrogen atoms on DNA; each nitrogen forms a co-ordinate bond to platinum using its lone pair; platinum binds two nearby sites because the chlorides are cis; DNA is distorted and cannot replicate, so cancer cells stop dividing.
- Benefit: tumours can shrink; the drug can extend life or cure some cancers.
- Why adverse effects occur: the drug also binds to the DNA of healthy cells and stops them dividing.
- Adverse effects: kidney damage, hearing loss, sickness.
- Limiting harm: lowest effective dose, treatment in cycles so healthy tissue recovers, combination with other treatments, monitoring of kidney function and hearing.
- Deciding: trials and regulators judge whether benefit outweighs risk for a use; the patient gives informed consent; the balance depends on how serious the cancer is.
- Judgement: for many patients with a serious cancer, the benefit outweighs the adverse effects, provided they are monitored.

Levels guide: 5 to 6 marks for mechanism, adverse effects explained by the same chemistry, and a supported judgement; 3 to 4 marks for two of these done well; 1 to 2 marks for isolated points with little explanation.

*Examiner insight:* Answers that list side effects without the chemical reason, or that never reach a judgement, cannot reach the top level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Showing the bond to DNA forming through chlorine instead of nitrogen.
- Stopping at "binds to DNA" without the consequence for replication and cell division.
- Giving three hydrogens instead of six when calculating the Mr of cisplatin.
- Writing "it is toxic" in place of "it also binds to the DNA of healthy cells".
- Evaluations with no conclusion.
