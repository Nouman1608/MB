This guide goes deeper into one sub-topic of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: **20.2 Habitat destruction**, the second part of topic 20, Human influences on ecosystems. It has four learning outcomes, and all four are **Core**, so every candidate needs them on every paper. There is no Supplement content in 20.2.

The [human influences on ecosystems study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-human-influences-on-ecosystems/) lists the reasons for habitat destruction and the five effects of deforestation. This page turns each effect into a chain of cause and consequence, gives examples for each reason and shows how to handle data on species and forest loss. Afterwards, use the [habitat destruction revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-habitat-destruction-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/) and the [habitat destruction practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-habitat-destruction-cambridge-igcse-biology-practice/).

## What sub-topic 20.2 asks you to do

| Outcome | Syllabus wording (summarised) | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 20.2.1 | Describe biodiversity as the number of different species that live in an area | Core |
| 20.2.2 | Describe the reasons for habitat destruction, including: (a) increased area for housing, crop plant production and livestock production; (b) extraction of natural resources; (c) freshwater and marine pollution | Core |
| 20.2.3 | State that through altering food webs and food chains, humans can have a negative impact on habitats | Core |
| 20.2.4 | Explain the undesirable effects of deforestation as an example of habitat destruction, to include: reducing biodiversity, extinction, loss of soil, flooding and increase of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere | Core |

Outcomes 1 to 3 use **describe** and **state**. Outcome 4 says **explain**, so each effect needs its cause: what happens when trees are removed, and why that leads to the effect.

## 20.2.1 Biodiversity

> Biodiversity is the **number of different species** that live in an area.

Count species, not individuals. A field with 10 000 wheat plants and 3 other species has a biodiversity of 4 species. A hedge 50 m long with 30 plant species and many insects and birds has far higher biodiversity, even with fewer individuals.

### Worked example 1: comparing biodiversity

Students recorded the species they found in equal areas of three habitats (illustrative data).

| Habitat | Plant species | Insect species | Bird species |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural forest | 46 | 120 | 28 |
| Oil palm plantation | 4 | 31 | 9 |
| Housing estate | 18 | 40 | 12 |

- Total species: forest 194, plantation 44, housing 70.
- **Highest biodiversity:** natural forest.
- Percentage of the forest's species found in the plantation: 44 ÷ 194 × 100 = **22.7%** (to 3 significant figures).
- **Explanation:** the plantation is a monoculture with one tree species, so it offers fewer kinds of food and shelter, and fewer species can live there.

## 20.2.2 Reasons for habitat destruction

### (a) Increased area for housing, crops and livestock

As the human population grows, land is cleared for houses, roads and towns, for fields of crops and for grazing animals. Forests are felled, wetlands are drained and grasslands are ploughed. Each change replaces a varied habitat with one used by very few species.

### (b) Extraction of natural resources

Natural resources are materials taken from the environment. Examples:

- **mining** for metal ores and coal, which removes soil and vegetation and leaves spoil heaps;
- **quarrying** for stone, sand and gravel;
- **logging** for timber and paper;
- **drilling** for oil and gas, with roads, pipelines and the risk of spills.

### (c) Freshwater and marine pollution

Pollution can destroy a habitat without removing it. Untreated sewage and fertiliser run-off reduce dissolved oxygen in rivers and lakes; oil spills coat shorelines; plastic litters beaches and the sea. The water remains, but many species can no longer live in it. The mechanisms are taught in the [pollution study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-pollution-cambridge-igcse-biology/).

## 20.2.3 Altering food webs and food chains

Humans also damage habitats by changing who eats whom. The syllabus asks you to **state** the idea, and you should be able to illustrate it:

- removing a predator by hunting lets its prey increase, and the prey may then overgraze the plants;
- overfishing one species removes food for seabirds and seals;
- introducing a species with no natural predators lets it spread and replace native species;
- killing insects with pesticides removes food for insect-eating birds.

The habitat may look the same, but its food web, and so its community, has changed for the worse. See the [food chains and food webs study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-food-chains-and-food-webs-cambridge-igcse-biology/) for how to trace these effects.

## 20.2.4 Undesirable effects of deforestation

Deforestation is the clearing of forest, usually by felling or burning, to use the land for something else. Learn each effect as a chain.

### Reducing biodiversity

Trees provide food (leaves, fruit, nectar), shelter and nesting sites for many species, and each layer of a forest, from canopy to leaf litter, supports different organisms. **Remove the trees → food and shelter are lost → many species die or leave → fewer species in the area.**

### Extinction

Some species live in only one forest, or need very large areas of it. **Clear that forest → the species has nowhere else to live → its population falls to zero → the species becomes extinct.** Extinction is permanent: the species and its alleles are lost.

### Loss of soil

In a forest, tree roots bind the soil, the canopy intercepts heavy rain, and leaf litter covers the ground. **Remove the trees → roots no longer hold the soil, rain hits the bare ground directly → soil is washed away by rain or blown away by wind (erosion).** Without the litter and its decomposers, the remaining soil also receives fewer mineral ions, so it becomes less fertile.

### Flooding

Trees take up water through their roots and lose it by transpiration, and the canopy holds rain before it reaches the ground. **Remove the trees → less water is absorbed and transpired, more rain reaches the ground at once → more water runs off the surface into rivers → rivers overflow.** Eroded soil carried into rivers can also block them, adding to flooding downstream.

### Increase of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere

Trees remove carbon dioxide from the air by photosynthesis and store the carbon in wood. **Remove the trees → less carbon dioxide is removed by photosynthesis; burning the trees releases carbon dioxide by combustion; decomposing wood releases carbon dioxide through the respiration of decomposers → carbon dioxide in the atmosphere increases.** This links to the carbon cycle in the [nutrient cycles study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-nutrient-cycles-cambridge-igcse-biology/).

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain how deforestation can lead to flooding and to an increase in carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. [4]

**Answer:** With fewer trees, less water is taken up by roots and lost by transpiration, and less rain is intercepted by leaves (1), so more water runs off into rivers, which then overflow (1). There are fewer trees to absorb carbon dioxide for photosynthesis (1), and burning the felled trees releases carbon dioxide (1).

**Why it scores:** each effect has a cause and a consequence. "Deforestation causes flooding and global warming" names effects but explains nothing.

### Worked example 3: forest-loss data

A region had 8.4 million hectares of forest. Ten years later it had 6.3 million hectares (illustrative figures).

- Area lost: 8.4 − 6.3 = **2.1 million hectares**.
- Percentage lost: 2.1 ÷ 8.4 × 100 = **25%**.
- Mean rate of loss: 2.1 ÷ 10 = **0.21 million hectares per year**.

Always divide by the **original** area when calculating a percentage decrease.

## Common mistakes

- Defining biodiversity as "the number of organisms" or "the variety of life" without "different species".
- Giving "pollution" as a reason without saying freshwater or marine pollution makes the habitat unsuitable.
- Writing "deforestation causes soil erosion" with no mention of roots or rain.
- Explaining flooding without transpiration or interception.
- Giving only one reason for the increase in carbon dioxide; there are two or three.
- Saying deforestation "destroys the ozone layer". That is not in this sub-topic.
- Treating reduced biodiversity and extinction as the same; extinction is the permanent loss of a whole species.

## Exam technique for 20.2 command words

- **Describe** biodiversity: "the number of different species that live in an area".
- **Describe** reasons: name the reason and give an example of the land use or resource.
- **State** (outcome 3): humans can have a negative impact on habitats by altering food webs and food chains; add an example if marks allow.
- **Explain** deforestation effects: trees removed → what stops happening → effect.
- **Calculate**: subtract, divide by the original, give units.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is habitat destruction Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
Sub-topic 20.2 is entirely Core: biodiversity, the reasons for habitat destruction, food-web impacts and the effects of deforestation.

**What are the five effects of deforestation in the syllabus?**
Reducing biodiversity, extinction, loss of soil, flooding and an increase of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere.

**Why does deforestation increase carbon dioxide in the air?**
Fewer trees remove carbon dioxide by photosynthesis, and burning or decomposing the felled trees releases carbon dioxide.
