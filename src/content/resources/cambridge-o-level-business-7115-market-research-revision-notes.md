Condensed recall for subtopic **3.2 Market research**. For worked examples, questionnaire design and data analysis, use the [Market research study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-market-research/).

## Why research matters

**Market-orientated** business: research customer wants first, then design the product. **Product-orientated** business: make the product first, then try to sell it.

Uses of market research information:

- check demand before a launch
- find preferred features, prices and packaging
- choose segments to target
- learn about competitors
- explain falling sales or complaints
- judge whether promotion worked.

## Primary versus secondary

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Data | New, for this business's purpose | Already exists, collected by others |
| Benefits | Up to date; specific; not available to rivals | Quick; often cheap; market overview |
| Limitations | Costly; slow; depends on sample and questions | May be out of date; not specific; rivals have it; reports can be expensive |

## Primary methods

| Method | Plus | Minus |
|---|---|---|
| Postal questionnaire | Wide area; cheap per person | Low response; slow |
| Online survey | Cheap; fast; easy to analyse | Only online users; careless answers |
| Interview | Explain questions; follow-up | Costly; interviewer influence |
| Focus group | In-depth reasons and feelings | Small, may not represent the market |
| Observation | Shows real behaviour | Does not explain why |

## Secondary methods

Online sources (competitor websites, reviews); government statistics (population, incomes, spending); commercial market research reports; the business's own records; newspapers and magazines.

## Questions and sampling

- **Closed questions**: fixed answers, easy to count.
- **Open questions**: reasons and ideas, harder to analyse.
- **Avoid leading questions**, which push respondents to an answer.
- **Population**: everyone in the target market. **Sample**: the group actually asked.
- Sampling is needed because surveying everyone costs too much and takes too long.
- The sample must **represent** the target market; larger samples are usually more reliable.

## Accuracy factors

- Sample too small or unrepresentative
- Leading or unclear questions
- Dishonest or careless answers
- Interviewer influence
- Low response rate
- Secondary data out of date or collected for another purpose

## Using results

1. State the **trend** or main finding.
2. Quote **figures** from the chart or table.
3. **Compare** groups or time periods.
4. Say what the business should **do**.
5. Note a **limitation** of the data.

```
percentage = number giving an answer ÷ total in sample × 100
```

## Choosing a method for a case

- **Small budget, existing customers nearby**: short in-store questionnaire or taste test.
- **Customers mainly online**: online survey.
- **Need reasons and feelings about a new product**: focus group.
- **Need market size or population data first**: government statistics, then primary research.
- **Large firm entering a new market**: commercial report plus primary research.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Market research | Collecting and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market |
| Market-orientated | Basing products on what research shows customers want |
| Product-orientated | Making a product first, then trying to sell it |
| Primary research | Collecting new data for the business's own purpose |
| Secondary research | Using data that already exists |
| Questionnaire | A set of questions used to collect data from respondents |
| Focus group | A small group of people discussing a product in depth |
| Sample | The part of the target market chosen to take part in research |
| Response rate | The percentage of people asked who reply |
| Leading question | A question worded to encourage a particular answer |

## Exam traps

- **Secondary is not always free**: commercial reports cost money.
- **Primary is not always accurate**: a poor sample or leading questions spoil it.
- **Link every method to the case**, such as an online survey for a business with online customers.
- **Do not just repeat chart numbers**: draw a conclusion and an action.
- **"Probably would buy" ≠ will buy**.
- **Sampling is a necessity, not a weakness**, but the sample must be representative.

## Self-test

1. What is a market-orientated business?
2. Give two uses of market research information.
3. Give one benefit and one limitation of primary research.
4. Give two sources of secondary research.
5. Why might a postal questionnaire give unreliable results?
6. Give one benefit of a focus group.
7. Why do businesses use samples?
8. What is a leading question? Give an example.
9. State three factors that affect the accuracy of market research.
10. Of 400 people surveyed, 140 prefer a new flavour. What percentage is this?
11. Why might government statistics not be useful for a small café?
12. A chart shows sales rising each month after an advertising campaign. What conclusion could be drawn, and what is one limitation?

## Answers

1. One that researches what customers want before designing and selling products.
2. Any two: check demand; find preferred features or prices; choose segments; study competitors; explain falling sales; judge promotion.
3. Benefit: up to date and specific to the business's needs. Limitation: expensive and slow.
4. Any two: internet; government statistics; commercial reports; internal records; newspapers.
5. Few people reply, and those who do may not be typical of the target market.
6. In-depth discussion reveals customers' reasons and feelings about a product.
7. Surveying the whole target market would cost too much and take too long.
8. A question worded to push an answer, such as "Don't you think our prices are fair?"
9. Any three: sample size; representativeness; question design; honesty; interviewer influence; response rate; age of secondary data.
10. 140 ÷ 400 × 100 = **35%**.
11. They cover the whole country or region, not the café's local customers, and may be out of date.
12. The campaign may have increased sales; but other factors, such as seasonal demand or a rival closing, could explain the rise.
