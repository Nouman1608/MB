## Questions

**1.** For each reaction, identify the Lewis acid and the Lewis base.

**(a)** BF₃ + F⁻ → BF₄⁻ **[1]**
**(b)** Ni²⁺ + 6NH₃ → [Ni(NH₃)₆]²⁺ **[1]**
**(c)** CH₃NH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃NH₃⁺ **[1]**

**2.** Explain why every Brønsted–Lowry base is also a Lewis base, but not every Lewis acid is a Brønsted–Lowry acid. Give one example of a Lewis acid that is not a Brønsted–Lowry acid. **[3]**

**3.** Boron trifluoride reacts with methanol, CH₃OH, to form a single adduct.

**(a)** Draw the Lewis formula of the adduct, showing all lone pairs, and identify the coordination bond. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the electron domain geometry around boron changes in this reaction. **[2]**
**(c)** State whether the coordination bond in the adduct differs from a B–O bond formed in any other way, once it has formed. **[1]**

**4.** Deduce the charge on each complex.

**(a)** Co³⁺ with four NH₃ and two Cl⁻ ligands **[1]**
**(b)** Fe²⁺ with five CN⁻ ligands and one H₂O ligand **[1]**
**(c)** V³⁺ with four Cl⁻ and two H₂O ligands **[1]**
**(d)** Deduce the oxidation state of chromium in [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻. **[1]**

**5.** Explain, in terms of Lewis theory, how a water molecule acts as a ligand towards a transition element cation, and identify the donor atom in the cyanide ligand. **[3]**

**6.** Two halogenoalkanes, A (primary) and B (tertiary), are each reacted with aqueous hydroxide ions. Doubling [OH⁻] doubles the rate for A but has no effect on the rate for B.

**(a)** Deduce the rate equation for each reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** Under starting conditions the rate for A is 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ and the rate for B is 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Calculate each new rate if both [halogenoalkane] and [OH⁻] are tripled. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how the two energy profiles differ. **[1]**

**7.** 1-iodohexane reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Describe and explain the mechanism, including the transition state. **[4]**

**8.** A pure sample of one enantiomer of 2-iodooctane reacts with hydroxide ions by an SN2 mechanism.

**(a)** Describe the stereochemistry of the octan-2-ol formed and explain why. **[3]**
**(b)** State what would be formed instead if the reaction went entirely by SN1, and explain. **[2]**

**9.** Use these values: average bond enthalpies C–Cl 324 kJ mol⁻¹, C–Br 285 kJ mol⁻¹, C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Predict the order of rate of hydrolysis for chlorocyclohexane, bromocyclohexane and iodocyclohexane, fastest first. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain your order, referring to the data and the leaving group. **[2]**
**(c)** A student argues that chlorocyclohexane should react fastest because C–Cl is the most polar bond. Explain why this is wrong. **[1]**

**10.** Cycloheptene, C₇H₁₂, a ring of seven carbons with one C=C, is a symmetrical alkene.

**(a)** Name the product with bromine. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe and explain the mechanism for its reaction with bromine. **[4]**
**(c)** Name the product with hydrogen chloride. **[1]**

**11.** 1-ethylcyclohexene reacts with water in the presence of an acid catalyst.

**(a)** Draw or describe the two carbocations that could form when H⁺ adds. **[2]**
**(b)** Predict the major product and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe the remaining steps of the mechanism from the carbocation to the alcohol, and state why the acid is a catalyst. **[3]**

**12.** Benzene reacts with the nitronium ion, NO₂⁺.

**(a)** Explain why benzene undergoes substitution rather than addition, despite being unsaturated. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe and explain the mechanism by which benzene reacts with NO₂⁺. **[4]**
**(c)** In the mixture of concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids that produces NO₂⁺, state the role of HNO₃ in Brønsted–Lowry terms. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Acid **BF₃**, base **F⁻** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Acid **Ni²⁺**, base **NH₃** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Acid **H⁺**, base **CH₃NH₂** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both roles in each part; identifying only the acid leaves the answer incomplete.

**2.** A Brønsted–Lowry base accepts H⁺ by donating a lone pair to it [1], so it is an electron-pair donor, which is the Lewis definition of a base [1]. A Lewis acid need not contain transferable hydrogen, for example **BF₃ or Fe³⁺** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The example must contain no H⁺ to donate; quoting HCl as a Lewis-only acid gains nothing.

**3. (a)** Oxygen keeps one lone pair and bonds to CH₃, H and B; boron bonds to three F atoms (each with three lone pairs) and to O [1]. The **O–B bond is the coordination bond**, both electrons from oxygen [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** BF₃ has three electron domains: **trigonal planar** [1]. The adduct has four around boron: **tetrahedral** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** **No difference**; once formed, it is an ordinary covalent bond [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Lone pairs on every fluorine and the remaining pair on oxygen are part of a Lewis formula; omitting them leaves the structure incomplete.

**4. (a)** +3 + 2(−1) = **+1**, [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺ [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** +2 + 5(−1) = **−3**, [Fe(CN)₅(H₂O)]³⁻ [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** +3 + 4(−1) = **−1**, [VCl₄(H₂O)₂]⁻ [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** x + 6(−1) = −3, so **+3** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The answer is the overall charge of the complex, written outside the square bracket; repeating the metal ion's own charge misses the ligand contribution.

**5.** The oxygen of water has a **lone pair** that it donates to the cation [1], forming a **coordination bond**; water is the Lewis base and the cation the Lewis acid [1]. In CN⁻ the donor atom is **carbon** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Water bonds to the metal" is not enough; the answer needs donation of a lone pair.

**6. (a)** A: **rate = k[RX][OH⁻]** [1]. B: **rate = k[RX]** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** A: 2.4 × 10⁻⁵ × 3 × 3 = **2.2 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]. B: 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 3 = **4.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A (SN2) has **one hump**; B (SN1) has **two humps with a carbocation intermediate** between them [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Treat each reaction separately in (b); tripling only the halogenoalkane for A ignores the hydroxide term.

**7.** Curly arrow from **an oxygen lone pair of the hydroxide ion to C1**, attacking from the side opposite iodine [1]. Curly arrow from the **C–I bond to I** [1]. **Transition state**: [HO···C···I]⁻ in brackets with ‡, other three groups in one plane, overall negative charge [1]. Products hexan-1-ol and I⁻; one step because C1 is **primary**, uncrowded, and a primary carbocation would be unstable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An arrow must start at a lone pair or a bond, not at the minus sign, and must end on the δ+ carbon.

**8. (a)** A **single enantiomer** of octan-2-ol forms [1]. OH⁻ attacks from the side **opposite** iodine [1], so the configuration at C2 is **inverted** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** A **racemic mixture** [1], because the planar carbocation can be attacked equally from either face [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Inversion" alone, with no link to back-side attack, explains nothing about why it happens.

**9. (a)** **Iodocyclohexane > bromocyclohexane > chlorocyclohexane** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** C–I (228) is weaker than C–Br (285) and C–Cl (324), so it breaks most easily [1]; I⁻ is the most stable, best leaving group [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The slow part of the reaction is **C–X bond cleavage**, so the weakest bond, not the most polar one, controls the rate [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the numbers given; "C–I is weakest" with the values quoted is stronger than a general statement.

**10. (a)** **1,2-dibromocycloheptane** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The high electron density of the π bond repels electrons in Br₂, making the nearer Br δ+ [1]. Curly arrow from the **π bond to Brδ+** and from the **Br–Br bond to the far Br** [1]. A **carbocation** and Br⁻ form [1]. Curly arrow from a **Br⁻ lone pair to C⁺** [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** **chlorocycloheptane** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The induced dipole on Br₂ must be shown or stated; a non-polar Br₂ with no δ+ cannot act as an electrophile.

**11. (a)** H⁺ on the CH of the ring gives a **tertiary carbocation** on the carbon bearing the ethyl group [1]; H⁺ on that ethyl-bearing carbon gives a **secondary carbocation** on the neighbouring ring carbon [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **1-ethylcyclohexan-1-ol** [1], because the tertiary carbocation is more stable, stabilised by three alkyl groups releasing electron density [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A water molecule donates an **oxygen lone pair to C⁺** [1]. The protonated alcohol **loses H⁺** [1]. H⁺ is used in step 1 and **regenerated**, so it is a catalyst [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Justify the major product with carbocation stability; quoting the "more hydrogens" rule alone does not explain.

**12. (a)** Benzene's six π electrons are **delocalised** over the ring, which gives extra stability [1]; addition would destroy this, but substitution **keeps the ring intact** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Curly arrow from the **delocalised ring to N of NO₂⁺** [1]. **Intermediate**: horseshoe over five carbons with +, sixth carbon bearing H and NO₂ [1]. Curly arrow from the **C–H bonding pair back into the π system** [1]; **H⁺ lost**, delocalisation restored, nitrobenzene formed [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** HNO₃ acts as a **base**: it accepts H⁺ from H₂SO₄ [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The horseshoe must leave out the carbon carrying both H and NO₂; a full circle in the intermediate is a common way to lose credit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping "donor" and "acceptor" in Lewis definitions.
- Choosing a hydrogen-containing acid as an example of a Lewis-only acid.
- Leaving lone pairs off Lewis formulas of adducts.
- Forgetting that cyanide donates through carbon.
- Including the nucleophile in an SN1 rate equation, or leaving it out of SN2.
- Drawing front-side attack in SN2, which hides the inversion.
- Explaining halogenoalkane reactivity by bond polarity.
- Missing the induced dipole on Br₂.
- Picking the alcohol from the less stable carbocation.
- Drawing a full circle in the benzene intermediate.
