These notes condense section 12.1, Sustainable energy supplies, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026 (Paper 4, Topic 12 option). For full explanations and a worked calculation, see the [12.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-sustainable-energy-supplies/); then test yourself with the [12.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-sustainable-energy-supplies-practice/).

## The four syllabus statements

1. Renewable and non-renewable energy resources.
2. National-scale factors affecting demand, supply and the balance between sources: sustainability, level of development, resource endowment, climate, income, technology, pollution, energy policy, energy security.
3. Trends in consumption of fossil fuels, nuclear power and renewables (HEP, wind, biofuels) in LICs, MICs and HICs.
4. Environmental impacts of energy production, transport and usage at local and global scales.

## Resource types

| Type | Examples | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Non-renewable (stock) | Coal, oil, gas, uranium | Finite; used faster than formed |
| Renewable flow | Wind, solar, HEP, tidal, geothermal | Cannot be used up; output varies |
| Renewable but depletable | Fuelwood, charcoal, biofuels | Sustainable only if use ≤ regrowth |
| Secondary energy | Electricity | Converted from a primary source |

## Data skills

- **Capacity** (MW) = maximum instantaneous output. **Generation** (MWh, GWh, TWh) = output over time.
- **Capacity factor** = generation ÷ (capacity × hours) × 100. One year = 8 760 hours.
- Intermittent sources have lower capacity factors, so need more capacity, storage or back-up.
- **Share** and **total** can move in opposite directions: coal's share can fall while coal use rises if total demand grows faster.

## Factors sorted by what they affect

| Factor | Mainly affects | One-line effect |
|---|---|---|
| Level of development, income | Demand | Industry, cars and appliances raise use per person |
| Climate | Demand and supply | Heating and cooling demand; solar, wind and HEP potential |
| Resource endowment | Supply | Countries build systems round domestic resources |
| Technology | Both | Efficiency cuts demand; fracking and cheaper renewables widen supply |
| Pollution | Mix | Smog and emissions push away from coal |
| Energy policy | Mix | Taxes, subsidies and targets steer investment |
| Energy security | Mix | Import dependence encourages diversification |
| Sustainability | Mix | Can the system continue without harming future supply? |

## Two models

- **Energy ladder:** dung and crop waste → fuelwood, charcoal → kerosene, LPG → electricity as household income rises. **Fuel stacking**: several fuels used together.
- **Energy trilemma:** security, equity (affordability), environmental sustainability. Gains in one often cost another.

## Trends at a glance

- **HICs:** coal falling; gas and renewables rising; nuclear mixed; demand per person steady or falling.
- **MICs:** total demand rising fast; coal still central where abundant; largest absolute growth in wind and solar, especially China; large HEP schemes.
- **LICs:** low use per person; traditional biomass dominant in homes; HEP the main grid source in some; much unused potential.
- **Biofuels:** sugar-cane ethanol in Brazil; palm oil biodiesel in Indonesia; blending mandates in many HICs.

## Impacts at a glance

| Stage | Local | Global |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Mining scars, flooded valleys, radioactive waste, turbine noise | Carbon dioxide and methane emissions |
| Transport | Pipeline routes, tanker spills, transmission lines | Methane leaks, shipping emissions |
| Usage | Urban air pollution, indoor smoke from biomass | Enhanced greenhouse effect |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Energy mix | The share of each source in a country's energy supply |
| Energy security | Reliable, affordable access to sufficient energy |
| Resource endowment | The natural resources a country possesses |
| Intermittency | Variation in output of wind and solar with weather and time |
| Capacity factor | Actual generation as a percentage of maximum possible |
| Energy intensity | Energy used per unit of GDP |
| Energy poverty | Lack of access to modern, affordable energy |
| Fuel stacking | Using several household fuels at once |
| Baseload | The minimum constant demand, often met by steady sources |
| Peak load | Highest demand periods, needing flexible supply |
| First-generation biofuel | Fuel made from food crops such as sugar cane or maize |
| Carbon intensity | Carbon dioxide emitted per unit of energy |

## Exam traps

- "Renewable" is not the same as "sustainable": fuelwood and biofuels can be overused.
- MW is capacity; MWh is energy. Do not mix them.
- Electricity mix ≠ total energy mix.
- Nuclear is non-renewable but low-carbon; say both.
- HEP is low-carbon but has large local impacts.
- Always say local or global when describing an impact.
- A trend needs direction, rate and an example.

## Self-test

1. Give one stock resource and one flow resource.
2. Why is fuelwood called renewable but depletable?
3. Distinguish capacity from generation.
4. A 500 MW solar farm generates 876 GWh in a year. Calculate its capacity factor.
5. Name three of the nine factors listed in 12.1.
6. Which factors mainly affect demand?
7. Describe the energy ladder.
8. Name the three parts of the energy trilemma.
9. How can coal's share fall while coal use rises?
10. Give one local and one global impact of HEP.
11. Why can palm oil biodiesel increase carbon emissions?
12. Give one trend in energy consumption in LICs.

### Answers

1. Stock: coal, oil, gas or uranium. Flow: wind, solar, HEP, tidal or geothermal.
2. It regrows, but can run out locally if cut faster than it regrows.
3. Capacity is maximum output at a moment (MW); generation is output over time (MWh).
4. 500 × 8 760 = 4 380 000 MWh = 4 380 GWh; 876 ÷ 4 380 × 100 = 20%.
5. Any three: sustainability, level of development, resource endowment, climate, income, technology, pollution, energy policy, energy security.
6. Level of development, income, climate (and technology, through efficiency).
7. As incomes rise, households switch from dung and wood to charcoal, kerosene or LPG, then electricity.
8. Security, equity (affordability), environmental sustainability.
9. If total energy demand grows faster than coal use.
10. Local: flooding of valleys, displacement or trapped sediment. Global: low carbon emissions (or methane from tropical reservoirs).
11. Clearing forest or peatland for plantations releases more carbon than the fuel saves.
12. Low use per person, reliance on traditional biomass, or growth in HEP.
