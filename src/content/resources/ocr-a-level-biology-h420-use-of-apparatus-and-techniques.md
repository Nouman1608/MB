Section 1.2.2 lists the apparatus and techniques that you must use through a minimum of 12 assessed practicals. They are the tools of A Level Biology, and the written papers regularly ask you to describe how they are used, to calculate with their results, or to explain why a step in a procedure is done. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) there are twelve statements, (a) to (l), and each links to one or more of the Practical Activity Groups (PAG1 to PAG12).

This guide covers learning outcomes 1.2.2(a) to (l) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 1, Development of practical skills in biology. It goes into more detail than the [development of practical skills study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-development-of-practical-skills/). Use the [use of apparatus and techniques revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-use-of-apparatus-and-techniques-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [use of apparatus and techniques practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-use-of-apparatus-and-techniques-practice/) to test yourself.

## Measuring with apparatus (a and b)

Choose an instrument with a suitable range and resolution.

| Quantity | Instrument | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mass | Balance (0.01 g) | Tare with the container; blot tissue |
| Time | Stopwatch | Define the end point clearly |
| Volume | Syringe, graduated pipette, measuring cylinder, burette | Smaller instruments have a smaller uncertainty |
| Temperature | Thermometer, thermostatic water bath | Allow time to reach a steady reading |
| Length | Ruler, graticule | Read at eye level |
| pH | pH meter or universal indicator | Calibrate the meter with buffers |

**Colorimeter.** A colorimeter measures how much light a coloured solution absorbs. A filter of a colour that is **complementary** to the solution's colour is chosen, because that is the light the solution absorbs most. The instrument is set to zero with a **blank** (the solvent without the coloured substance). To find the concentration of an unknown, draw a **calibration curve**.

**Worked example 1.** Standards of a coloured substance give these absorbances.

| Concentration / mmol dm⁻³ | 0 | 0.2 | 0.4 | 0.6 | 0.8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absorbance | 0.00 | 0.15 | 0.30 | 0.45 | 0.60 |

The points lie on a straight line through the origin, with a gradient of 0.60 ÷ 0.80 = 0.75 per mmol dm⁻³. An unknown sample has an absorbance of 0.36. Its concentration is 0.36 ÷ 0.75 = **0.48 mmol dm⁻³**. On a graph you would read across from 0.36 to the line and then down to the concentration axis.

**Potometer.** A potometer measures the rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot, as an estimate of transpiration. The shoot is cut under water and the apparatus is assembled under water, so that no air enters the xylem. An air bubble is introduced into a capillary tube, and the distance it moves in a measured time is the measurement of uptake. Seal joints with petroleum jelly. The apparatus should be reset by returning the bubble to the start between readings.

## Serial dilutions (c)

A serial dilution gives a range of concentrations by repeating the same dilution step.

**Worked example 2 (ten-fold).** Start with 1.0 mol dm⁻³ solution. Add 1 cm³ of it to 9 cm³ of distilled water in tube 1 and mix: 0.1 mol dm⁻³. Take 1 cm³ from tube 1 into 9 cm³ of water in tube 2: 0.01 mol dm⁻³. Repeat for tube 3: 0.001 mol dm⁻³. Each tube is one-tenth of the one before. Use a clean pipette tip or syringe each time, or rinse the pipette, to avoid carry-over.

**Two-fold:** 5 cm³ of solution plus 5 cm³ of water halves the concentration at each step: 1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 mol dm⁻³.

The volumes of liquid must be measured accurately, because an error at one stage carries through all later tubes.

## Light microscope and graticule (d)

Start with the **low-power** objective, because it has a wider field of view and is easier to focus. Use the coarse focus first, then the fine focus. Move to high power only after the specimen is centred and in focus. Never lower the objective towards the slide while looking through the eyepiece.

**Calibrating an eyepiece graticule.** The graticule is a scale in the eyepiece with arbitrary divisions. To convert it to a real length, line it up against a **stage micrometer**, a slide with a scale of known size.

**Worked example 3.** 50 graticule divisions line up with 40 divisions of a stage micrometer. Each stage division is 10 μm (0.01 mm).

40 stage divisions = 40 × 10 = 400 μm.

One graticule division = 400 ÷ 50 = **8.0 μm**.

A cell that measures 6.5 graticule divisions has a length of 6.5 × 8.0 = **52 μm**. The calibration works only for that objective lens, so repeat it for each.

## Scientific drawings (e)

A scientific drawing records what you see. Follow these rules.

- Use a sharp pencil and draw clear, continuous lines. Do not sketch, shade or colour.
- Make the drawing large, using at least half of the space, and keep the proportions accurate.
- Draw only what you can see, and draw the outlines of cells in a tissue plan, not individual cells unless the question requires them.
- Label with ruled lines that do not cross, touch the structure they label and do not have arrowheads. Write labels horizontally.
- Add a title and the **magnification** or a **scale bar**.

**Worked example 4 (magnification).** The cell in worked example 3 is 52 μm long, or 0.052 mm. If your drawing of it is 78 mm long:

magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object = 78 ÷ 0.052 = **× 1500**.

Convert both sizes to the same unit before dividing.

## Qualitative reagents (f)

| Test | Reagent | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| Starch | Iodine in potassium iodide solution | Blue-black colour |
| Reducing sugar | Benedict's reagent, heated | Blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate |
| Non-reducing sugar | Boil with dilute acid, neutralise, then Benedict's test | Brick-red precipitate after the acid step, none before |
| Protein | Biuret reagent | Blue to purple or lilac |
| Lipid | Emulsion test: dissolve in ethanol, add water | Cloudy white emulsion |

A negative result is the colour of the reagent: blue for Benedict's and biuret, brown-orange for iodine. Always give the colour before and after.

## Chromatography and electrophoresis (g)

**Paper or thin layer chromatography** separates compounds because they differ in solubility in the solvent and in attraction to the stationary phase. Draw the **origin line in pencil** (ink would run), place small spots on it, stand the paper in solvent with the origin **above** the solvent level, and remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top, marking the **solvent front** at once.

Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent.

**Worked example 5.** A spot moves 4.2 cm while the solvent front moves 7.0 cm. Rf = 4.2 ÷ 7.0 = **0.60**. Rf has no unit and is always less than 1. Compare it with Rf values of known compounds run under the same conditions.

**Electrophoresis** separates charged molecules in an electric field through a gel. Molecules move at rates depending on their charge and size.

## Organisms, aseptic technique and dissection (h to j)

**Safe and ethical use of organisms (h).** Treat living organisms with care. Use the minimum number needed, avoid harm and distress, and return animals to their habitat. Human volunteers must give informed consent and be free to withdraw.

**Aseptic technique (i).** The aim is to prevent contamination of the culture and the surroundings.

1. Disinfect the bench and wash hands.
2. Flame the inoculating loop until red hot, then let it cool.
3. Lift the lid of the dish only slightly, and keep it close to the dish, working near a Bunsen flame.
4. Flame the neck of a broth bottle after opening and before closing.
5. Seal the dish with two or four short strips of tape, not all the way round, so that air can enter and anaerobic pathogens are not encouraged.
6. Incubate the dish upside down, so that condensation does not drip onto the agar, at a modest temperature such as 25 °C, which reduces the chance of growing pathogens.

**Worked example 6.** In an antibiotic test the clear zone around a disc has a diameter of 14 mm, so a radius of 7 mm. Area = π × 7² = **154 mm²**. A larger area shows stronger inhibition of bacterial growth.

**Dissection (j).** Use a sharp scalpel or scissors on a dissection board, cutting away from the body. Keep specimens moist, record what you see with a drawing, and dispose of tissue as instructed.

## Sampling in fieldwork (k)

A **quadrat** samples a small area. Use **random sampling** for a habitat that is uniform, choosing coordinates with random numbers. Use a **transect** where conditions change, such as from the shore up a beach, and sample at regular intervals.

**Worked example 7.** Twelve quadrats of 0.25 m² are placed at random. Together they contain 54 plants.

Mean per quadrat = 54 ÷ 12 = 4.5. Density = 4.5 ÷ 0.25 = **18 plants per m²**. If the habitat measures 400 m², the estimated population is 18 × 400 = **7200 plants**.

A larger sample reduces the effect of chance. Record percentage cover where counting individuals is hard.

## ICT (l)

A **data logger** with sensors records temperature, light, pH or oxygen at set intervals and displays the results as a graph. It is more precise than human timing and collects data for a long time. **Computer modelling** lets you test ideas that cannot be done in the laboratory. **Spreadsheets** and graph software help process data.

## Exam technique

- **Give reasons** for each step: "flame the loop to kill microorganisms that would contaminate the culture".
- **Put the calculation in order**: write the formula, convert units, substitute, and give the unit.
- **Name the apparatus precisely**: graduated pipette, gas syringe, stage micrometer.
- **State the positive result** for each reagent, including the colours.

## Common mistakes

- Using ink to draw the origin line in chromatography.
- Letting the solvent level be above the origin line.
- Mixing mm and μm in a magnification calculation.
- Starting with the high-power objective.
- Sealing a culture dish all the way round.
- Describing a Benedict's result as "red" without noting the heating.
