This guide covers one content point of the developmental psychology option in IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2019): **"development of empathy and theory of mind"**, part of the topic "developing an identity". The [developmental psychology study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-developmental-psychology-study-guide/) covers Piaget, Vygotsky, attachment and identity; the option's revision notes list the Sally-Anne study in one line. This page explains what theory of mind and empathy are, how each develops, the main research methods and studies, and how to use them in a 22-mark essay. The course described here has its last examination session in November 2026.

## Two related abilities

**Theory of mind** is the understanding that other people have mental states (beliefs, desires, intentions and knowledge) that can differ from one's own and from reality, and that these mental states guide behaviour. The term was introduced by Premack and Woodruff (1978), who asked whether chimpanzees attribute mental states to others.

**Empathy** is the capacity to understand and share another person's feelings. Psychologists often divide it into:

- **affective empathy**: feeling an emotional response that matches, or responds to, another's emotion;
- **cognitive empathy**: understanding what another person feels and why, which depends partly on theory of mind.

The two develop together but are not the same. A child can be upset by another child's crying (affective) long before understanding why that child is upset (cognitive). In an essay, keep them distinct and show how they connect.

## Testing theory of mind: false-belief tasks

The key test is whether a child understands that someone can hold a **false belief**. If a child predicts behaviour from what is really true, rather than from what the other person believes, they have not yet shown this understanding.

**Unexpected location: Wimmer and Perner (1983).** Children heard a story, acted out with toys, in which a boy called Maxi puts chocolate in one cupboard and goes out. While he is away, his mother moves it to another cupboard. Children were asked where Maxi would look when he returned. Most of the youngest children pointed to where the chocolate really was; correct answers became much more common with age, and most of the oldest children answered correctly.

**Unexpected contents: Perner, Leekam and Wimmer (1987).** Children were shown a familiar sweet tube and asked what was inside. After answering "sweets", they were shown that it contained a pencil, and asked what another child, who had not seen inside, would think was in the tube. Many three-year-olds answered "a pencil", attributing their own new knowledge to the other child, while most older children said "sweets".

**Sally-Anne: Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985).** Using dolls, Sally puts a marble in her basket and leaves; Anne moves it to her box. Most typically developing children and children with Down syndrome said Sally would look in her basket, while most of the autistic children, despite a higher average verbal mental age than the Down syndrome group, said she would look in the box. Baron-Cohen later developed this into the idea of **"mindblindness"**: difficulty attributing mental states may underlie some social difficulties in autism. Evaluation: dolls rather than people, a small sample, and not all autistic children failed, so a deficit in theory of mind cannot be the whole explanation.

## When does theory of mind develop?

**Wellman, Cross and Watson (2001)** carried out a meta-analysis of false-belief studies. Across many studies and countries, there was a consistent shift between about three and five years: younger children usually failed and older children usually passed. Changes to the task, such as making the deception more explicit, improved performance at every age but did not remove the age trend. This suggests a real conceptual change rather than a quirk of one task.

**Callaghan et al. (2005)** gave the same false-belief task to children in Canada, India, Peru, Samoa and Thailand. Children in all five cultures began to pass at around the same age, about five, which supports a universal developmental pattern despite very different upbringings.

**Onishi and Baillargeon (2005)** challenged the age of three to five. Using a non-verbal **violation-of-expectation** method, they showed fifteen-month-old infants an actor who hid a toy and then, in some trials, did not see it being moved. Infants looked longer when the actor searched in the place where the toy really was, as if surprised that she acted against her false belief. The finding suggests that an implicit understanding of belief may exist long before children can answer verbal questions. Critics argue that longer looking can be explained by simpler rules about where people usually search, so the interpretation is debated.

Putting the evidence together: explicit, verbal false-belief understanding develops around four to five across cultures; some implicit sensitivity may appear in infancy; and language, social experience and executive function help the explicit form emerge.

## How empathy develops

Martin Hoffman proposed a sequence of stages in the development of empathy:

| Stage | Approximate timing | What the child shows |
|---|---|---|
| Global empathy | first year | distress at others' distress, without a clear sense of whose distress it is |
| Egocentric empathy | second year | tries to help, but in ways that would comfort themselves (offering their own toy) |
| Empathy for another's feelings | from about two to three years | recognises that others' feelings differ from their own and responds to them |
| Empathy for another's life condition | late childhood | responds to a person's or group's general situation, such as poverty, not just a single event |

Evidence for the early stages:

- **Sagi and Hoffman (1976)** found that newborn babies cried more in response to the recorded cry of another newborn than to a synthetic sound of similar loudness, which is often described as **reactive crying** and treated as an early form of affective empathy.
- **Zahn-Waxler and colleagues (1992)** trained mothers to record their children's reactions to distress at home during the second year of life. Concern for others and attempts to help, such as comforting or sharing, increased with age through this period.
- **Warneken and Tomasello (2006)** found that eighteen-month-old infants spontaneously helped an unfamiliar adult who dropped a pen or could not open a cupboard while carrying something, but did not do so in matched control conditions where the adult dropped the pen deliberately. This suggests they understood the adult's goal, a step towards cognitive empathy.

**Evaluation.** Reactive crying may be emotional contagion rather than empathy in any full sense. Maternal reports may be biased by what mothers hope to see. Stage accounts describe a sequence, but children vary widely, and culture shapes how distress is expressed and responded to.

## Worked example: planning a 22-mark essay

**Question:** "Discuss research on the development of theory of mind."

1. **Focus:** define theory of mind and false belief; state the issue: at what age does it develop, and what drives it?
2. **Knowledge:** the logic of false-belief tasks; unexpected-location and unexpected-contents versions; implicit versus explicit understanding.
3. **Research:** Wimmer and Perner (1983); Wellman, Cross and Watson (2001); Callaghan et al. (2005); Onishi and Baillargeon (2005).
4. **Discussion:** verbal tasks may underestimate young children because of language and memory demands; meta-analysis shows a robust age trend; cross-cultural synchrony suggests a universal pattern; violation-of-expectation findings are open to simpler interpretations; most studies are cross-sectional.
5. **Conclusion:** explicit theory of mind develops at around four to five in many cultures, while the age at which some understanding first appears depends on how it is measured.

## Common mistakes

- Treating theory of mind and empathy as the same thing.
- Describing the Sally-Anne study as proof that autism is caused by a lack of theory of mind.
- Giving exact ages as if every child passes on a birthday.
- Ignoring task demands: a child may fail a verbal question while understanding the idea.
- Forgetting the cultural evidence when discussing whether development is universal.

## Exam technique

**Discuss** needs a balanced range of research and a conclusion; **evaluate** a judgement of strengths and limitations; **to what extent** a conclusion about degree, for example how far theory of mind is universal. Describe at least one false-belief procedure precisely, because the logic of the task is what earns knowledge marks, and finish each paragraph by saying what the study shows about development.
