This guide focuses on one half of Component 3 of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Literature
(4ET1): **Assignment A, Modern Drama**. Component 3 is the coursework alternative to the Component 2
exam. For the rules that apply to both assignments, start with the
[Component 3 study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-component-3/).

## What Assignment A covers

Assignment A is **one essay of about 650–800 words on a modern drama set text**, worth 30 marks: AO1
15 and AO2 15. The task is set by your teacher, and you may take part in choosing or shaping it. Your
centre marks the work and Pearson moderates a sample.

Under the Issue 3 specification the modern drama texts are *A View from the Bridge* (Arthur Miller),
*An Inspector Calls* (J B Priestley), *The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-time* (Mark Haddon,
adapted by Simon Stephens), *Kindertransport* (Diane Samuels) and *Death and the King's Horseman*
(Wole Soyinka). Set-text lists can change, so check the specification in force for your entry.

| AO | Marks | In a drama essay this means |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | 15 | Knowing the whole play, answering the task with an argued personal response, in a critical style |
| AO2 | 15 | Analysing how the playwright uses language, form and structure, including stagecraft, to create meaning and effect |

Context (AO4) is **not** assessed in Assignment A. A sentence of background can help an argument, but
it earns no separate marks, so it should be short and should serve a point about the play.

## Key ideas

### 1. A play is a script for performance

Half the marks are AO2, and for a play "form" includes everything an audience sees and hears. Examples
of dramatic methods you can analyse:

| Method | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Stage directions | What does the playwright insist on, and why? Silences, pauses, movement, sound |
| Entrances and exits | Who arrives at the worst moment? Who is left alone on stage? |
| Space and set | Is the stage realistic or symbolic? What stays visible throughout? |
| Doubling and ensemble | Does one actor play several roles? Does a chorus or ensemble become objects or crowds? |
| Time | Does the play move in a straight line, jump between past and present, or frame the action with a narrator? |
| Dialogue | Who speaks most, who interrupts, who changes register, who is silent? |
| Audience position | Does the audience know more than the characters (dramatic irony) or less? |

Each modern drama text uses some of these strongly. *Kindertransport* puts past and present on stage
at the same time, so the young Eva and the adult Evelyn can share the space. *The Curious Incident of
the Dog in the Night-time* uses a non-naturalistic stage and an ensemble to show how Christopher
experiences the world. *Death and the King's Horseman* moves between the market and the colonial
residence, so the structure itself sets two worlds against each other.

### 2. Write about "the audience", not just "the reader"

"The audience sees..." or "on stage, this moment..." signals that you understand form. Strong essays
also notice that different productions can make different choices, and that the script leaves some
decisions open.

### 3. The task is your target

A Component 3 task is narrower than "write about the play". A good title names **one focus** and
invites argument, for example "Explore the ways in which Samuels presents the effects of separation
in *Kindertransport*." Every paragraph should answer that wording.

### 4. AO1 and AO2 are equal

Fifteen marks each means every paragraph needs both: a point that answers the task, and an analysis
of how the playwright makes it. A paragraph of plot earns AO1 only at a low level; a list of devices
without an argument does not reach the higher AO2 levels either.

## Worked example 1: analysing stagecraft in a short original scene

This short scene was written for this guide to practise the skill.

> *A kitchen. Night. MARA sits at the table with an unopened letter. The clock is loud. Her brother
> TOMAS enters, coat still on.*
>
> TOMAS: You haven't opened it.
>
> MARA: It's addressed to Father.
>
> *Pause. TOMAS reaches for the letter. MARA puts her hand flat on it.*
>
> TOMAS: He's been gone six years, Mara.
>
> *The clock stops. Neither of them notices.*

**Analysis:** The loud clock makes the audience feel time pressing on Mara while she refuses to act.
The playwright uses an action rather than words when Mara presses her hand on the letter: her silence
shows she is still guarding her father's place in the family. The final stage direction gives the
audience information the characters do not have; the stopped clock suggests that time in this house
has frozen around the father's absence, and that neither sibling can see it. An essay would argue
that the scene presents grief as a refusal to let the past move on, and support that with these three
methods.

## Worked example 2: planning a 750-word essay

**Task:** Explore the ways in which Soyinka presents duty in *Death and the King's Horseman*. You must
consider language, form and structure.

| Section | Words | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | 60 | A clear argument: duty is presented as both glorious and fragile, and its failure brings disaster to the community |
| Paragraph 1 | 160 | Elesin's confident early performance in the market: praise-songs, rhythm and celebration as a public display of duty |
| Paragraph 2 | 160 | His request for a bride on the day he must die: how the structure introduces delay and doubt |
| Paragraph 3 | 160 | The scenes at the Residency: how cutting between worlds shows Pilkings misunderstanding duty |
| Paragraph 4 | 160 | Olunde's choice and Elesin's final failure: reversal of father and son as the play's climax |
| Conclusion | 50 | Return to the argument: duty matters because the whole community depends on it |

Total: 750 words. The plan uses the beginning, middle and end of the play, and each paragraph names
a method (song and verse, structure, setting, reversal) as well as a moment.

## The coursework process

1. **Understand the task.** Underline the focus and the command words.
2. **Gather evidence.** Choose a handful of moments from across the play, with short quotations or
   precise references to stage directions.
3. **Plan an argument**, not a sequence of scenes.
4. **Draft.** Your teacher may give general feedback on how to improve, but may not correct or
   rewrite your work.
5. **Revise and submit.** Once the work has been marked it cannot be rewritten.
6. **Authenticate.** You and your teacher sign to confirm the work is your own. Acknowledge any
   sources you used.

## Command words in Component 3 tasks

- **Explore the ways in which...** asks for range: several methods and several parts of the play.
- **How does [playwright] present...** keeps the playwright's choices at the centre.
- **To what extent...** asks you to weigh a view, agreeing in part, and to reach a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling.** A plot summary with a few quotations sits in the lower levels.
- **Treating the play as a novel.** Ignoring stage directions, staging and the audience loses much of
  AO2's form marks.
- **Long context paragraphs.** Context is not assessed in Assignment A; save it for Assignment B.
- **Drifting from the task.** Writing everything you know about a character instead of the angle the
  task asks for.
- **Feature-spotting.** Naming "dramatic irony" without explaining its effect at that moment.
- **Writing far beyond the advisory length.** There is no penalty, but focus usually slips.
