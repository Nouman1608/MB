Condensed recall for **1.6 Urban Settlements** (Cambridge IGCSE Geography 0460, Theme 1). For the
full explanation, read the
[Population and Settlement study guide](/resources/igcse-geography-population-and-settlement/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Land use** | What a piece of urban land is used for: commercial, industrial, residential, transport, open space |
| **CBD** | Central Business District: the commercial core of a city |
| **Accessibility** | How easy a place is to reach for workers, shoppers and goods |
| **Bid rent** | The amount a land user is willing to pay for a site, which falls with distance from the centre |
| **Urban sprawl** | Unplanned outward spread of a built-up area into the countryside |
| **Rural-urban fringe** | The transition zone where built-up land meets open countryside |
| **Squatter settlement** | Housing built illegally on land the residents do not own |
| **Urban renewal / regeneration** | Improving a run-down urban area through new housing, jobs and services |

## Land-use zones, centre to edge

| Zone | Typical features |
|---|---|
| **CBD** | Shops, offices, highest land values, tallest buildings, best accessibility |
| **Inner city** | Older terraced housing, former industry, often run-down |
| **Suburbs** | Newer, lower-density housing, gardens, more space |
| **Rural-urban fringe** | Where the city meets the countryside; large new developments, open land |

**Why are land values highest in the CBD?** It is the most accessible point, with the best transport
links, so demand for space is greatest and the space is the most expensive. High prices push
buildings upwards.

Real cities only roughly follow this pattern: relief, rivers, transport routes and planning
distort it.

### Why each zone looks the way it does

- **CBD:** shops and offices need the most customers and workers, so they outbid other users for the
  most accessible land. Because land is so costly, firms build upwards and use every square metre;
  few people live there, so the CBD can be quiet at night.
- **Inner city:** in many older cities in high-income countries this zone grew when factories were
  built close to the centre and workers needed housing within walking distance, giving dense
  terraced streets. When older industries closed, it was left with derelict land and poorer housing.
- **Suburbs:** better public transport and wider car ownership let people live further out on
  cheaper land, so plots are bigger, housing is detached or semi-detached and there are more parks.
- **Rural-urban fringe:** land is cheapest and road access is good, so it attracts out-of-town
  shopping centres, business parks, airports, sewage works and new housing estates.

### Different patterns in developing countries

In many cities in low- and middle-income countries the pattern is partly reversed. The **wealthy
often live near the centre** or in protected districts with good services, while the poorest
migrants build **squatter settlements on the edge**, on steep slopes, floodplains or beside
railways and industry -- land that nobody else wanted. Industry often follows main roads out of the
city rather than forming a ring. Be ready to say which type of city a land-use model fits, and why.

## Bid rent: a worked explanation

Land values (and the rent users are willing to bid) **fall with distance from the CBD**.

1. Shops bid the most at the centre because their profits depend on passing customers.
2. Offices bid slightly less but still need central access, so they take the next ring.
3. Industry and housing need more space, so they locate where land is cheaper.
4. Small **secondary peaks** in land value appear where main roads cross, often with a suburban
   shopping parade.

A good answer links each zone to **who can afford the land and why they need that location**.

## Problems by zone

- **Inner city:** old and poor-quality housing, derelict land, unemployment, crime, traffic
  congestion, poor environment.
- **Rural-urban fringe:** loss of farmland and green space through **urban sprawl**, more commuting
  and traffic. Cheap, spacious sites attract developers.
- **Rapid growth in developing countries:** shortage of housing, congestion, pollution and strain
  on water and sanitation.
- **CBD:** high rents push out small shops; congestion and air pollution; competition from
  out-of-town retail and online shopping can leave empty units.

**Responses to these problems:** inner-city redevelopment (clearing and rebuilding) or renewal
(improving existing buildings); pedestrianised streets, park-and-ride and congestion charges in the
CBD; **green belts** that restrict building at the city edge; new towns to take growth away from an
overcrowded city.

## Brownfield and greenfield

| | Brownfield | Greenfield |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Previously built on, now reused | Not built on before, often at the edge |
| Advantage | Saves countryside; services already nearby | Cheap and spacious |
| Disadvantage | Possible contamination, higher cost | Loses farmland and green space |

Developers often prefer greenfield sites because they are cheaper to build on and easy to reach by
road; planners often prefer brownfield sites because they bring life back to run-down areas. This
conflict is a common evaluation point.

## Squatter settlements

A **squatter settlement** is housing built illegally on land the residents do not own, usually from
poor materials and often without water, sewage or electricity. They grow where migrants arrive
faster than formal housing is built.

**Typical problems:** overcrowding; disease spread by untreated sewage and unsafe water; fire risk
from closely packed buildings; landslides on steep slopes or flooding on low land; insecure tenure,
so residents risk eviction; few formal jobs, so many work in the **informal sector**.

**Responses:** self-help schemes (residents improve their own homes with support),
site-and-service schemes (the authority provides plots with basic services and residents build),
improving services and transport.

**Evaluating responses:** self-help and site-and-service schemes are cheap and keep communities
together, but they do not create jobs, and they cannot keep pace if migration continues faster than
upgrading. Giving residents **legal title** to their land encourages them to invest in their homes.
Large clearance and rehousing schemes can move people far from their work.

## Case study card: Rio de Janeiro, Brazil

The syllabus asks for a case study of an urban area. Rio de Janeiro is well documented:

- **Land use:** the historic **CBD (Centro)** near the port; wealthy coastal districts of the
  **Zona Sul** such as Copacabana and Ipanema; newer high-rise and gated housing spreading west to
  **Barra da Tijuca**; industry and poorer suburbs to the north.
- **Squatter settlements:** **favelas** occupy steep hillsides (morros) and other unwanted land, many
  close to wealthy districts. **Rocinha** is the best-known and one of the largest.
- **Problems:** landslides on steep slopes after heavy rain, poor sanitation, drug-related crime,
  traffic congestion and a marked contrast between rich and poor districts.
- **Responses:** the **Favela-Bairro** upgrading programme from the 1990s paved streets, added
  drainage, sanitation and community buildings in many favelas rather than demolishing them; police
  "pacification" units were placed in some favelas from 2008, with mixed results; new transport
  links were built for the 2014 World Cup and the 2016 Olympics.

Use your own teacher's case study if it differs; the structure (zones, problems, responses,
evaluation) is what matters.

## Exam traps

- Placing the zones in the wrong order, or calling the inner city the newest part of town.
- Giving "expensive" as the reason for high land values without saying why (accessibility, demand).
- Mixing up brownfield and greenfield.
- Describing squatter settlements without a response, or without saying what a response cannot fix.
- Forgetting that real cities only roughly follow the zone pattern.
- Applying a high-income city model to a city in a developing country without adapting it.

## Command words

- **Describe** the land use on a map: name the zone, quote grid references or distances and give
  the features you can see.
- **Explain** a pattern: give the reason (accessibility, land value, age of buildings) for each
  feature.
- **Evaluate** a scheme: say who benefits, who loses and what it cannot achieve.

## Self-test

1. Name the four zones from the centre outwards.
2. Why are land values highest in the CBD?
3. Give two problems of the inner city.
4. Define a brownfield site.
5. Name one response to squatter settlement growth.
6. Why do the CBDs of large cities have tall buildings?
7. Why might developers prefer greenfield sites?
8. Give two hazards faced by squatter settlements built on hillsides or floodplains.
9. How does the location of wealthy and poor housing often differ in a city in a developing country
   compared with one in a high-income country?
10. Give one advantage and one limitation of a site-and-service scheme.
11. What is a green belt and why is it used?

**Answers:** 1. CBD, inner city, suburbs, rural-urban fringe. 2. It is the most accessible place, so
demand is highest. 3. For example old housing and unemployment. 4. Land that was built on before and
is reused. 5. For example a site-and-service scheme or a self-help scheme. 6. Land is so expensive
that firms build upwards to get the most floor space from a small plot. 7. The land is cheaper,
uncontaminated and spacious, with good road access. 8. Landslides and flooding. 9. In a developing
country the wealthy often live near the centre and the poor in squatter settlements at the edge; in a
high-income city the poorer housing is often in the inner city and wealthier housing in the suburbs.
10. Advantage: cheap, gives basic services and secure plots; limitation: it does not create jobs and
may not keep pace with migration. 11. A zone of protected open land around a city where building is
restricted, used to limit urban sprawl.
