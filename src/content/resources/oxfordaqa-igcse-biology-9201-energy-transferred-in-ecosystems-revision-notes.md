Condensed recall for **3.3.1 Energy transferred in ecosystems** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered. Statement d is marked

## The essentials

- **Radiation from the Sun** is the source of energy for most communities of living organisms.
- Plants and algae transfer about **1 %** of the incident light energy for photosynthesis.
- That energy is **stored in the substances that make up the cells** of the plants.
- Only about **10 %** of the biomass from each trophic level is transferred to the level above.
- Biomass at each stage can be drawn to scale as a **pyramid of biomass**.
- Efficiency of food production can be improved by **reducing the number of stages** in a food chain (Biology
  only).
- Pyramids of numbers and the construction of food webs and chains are **not** required.

## Why only about 10 % is passed on

| Reason | Detail |
|---|---|
| Waste materials | Some materials and energy are always lost in the organisms' waste, such as faeces and urine |
| Respiration | Supplies all the energy needs for living processes, including movement; much of this energy is transferred to the surroundings |
| Parts not eaten | Not all of an organism is eaten (for example, bones or roots), so not all of its biomass is passed on |

Energy transferred to the surroundings is not available to the next trophic level.

## The numbers

```
Sun → producer:         about 1 % of incident light energy
trophic level → next:   about 10 % of the biomass
percentage transfer = biomass at upper level ÷ biomass at lower level × 100
```

Worked reminder: a field receives 3.6 × 10⁶ kJ per m² per year.

```
producers store 1 %:        0.01 × 3.6 × 10⁶ = 3.6 × 10⁴ kJ
primary consumers receive:  10 % of 3.6 × 10⁴ = 3.6 × 10³ kJ
```

Second reminder: 800 kg of grass supports 96 kg of herbivores. Transfer = 96 ÷ 800 × 100 = **12 %**.

## Pyramids of biomass

- Each stage is a horizontal bar, drawn to scale, with producers at the bottom.
- Bars are the same height and centred on a line.
- Length of a bar = biomass ÷ scale (for example, 4 000 kg at 1 cm per 500 kg = 8 cm).
- Label each bar and show the scale.
- Interpret: each bar is smaller than the one below, showing that biomass falls at each stage.
- A pyramid of biomass shows **mass**, not the number of organisms.

## Efficiency of food production (Biology only)

- Fewer stages mean fewer places where energy is lost in waste and respiration.
- Eating crops directly makes more energy available to people from the same area of land than feeding the crops to
  animals and eating the animals.
- Restricting an animal's movement and keeping it warm can reduce energy lost in respiration so that more is
  stored as growth. The cost may be animal welfare.

Worked reminder: crop energy from a field 2.0 × 10⁵ kJ. Two stages up (producer to herbivore to carnivore) leaves
2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.10 × 0.10 = **2 000 kJ** for the carnivore, compared with all 2.0 × 10⁵ kJ if people eat the crop.

## Model answers to learn

- **Why is only about 10 % of biomass passed on to the next level? (2 marks):** "Some materials and energy are lost in waste materials such as faeces and urine. Respiration supplies the energy for living processes such as movement, and much of this is transferred to the surroundings."
- **Why can feeding crops directly to people feed more people? (2 marks):** "There is one fewer stage in the food chain, so less energy is lost in waste and respiration."
- **Why do food chains rarely have more than four or five stages? (1 mark):** "Only about 10 % of the biomass is passed on at each stage, so little is left after four or five stages."

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Incident energy | Light energy that falls on the plants |
| Biomass | The mass of living material (dry mass is often used) |
| Trophic level | A feeding level in a food chain |
| Producer | Plant or alga that makes food by photosynthesis |
| Consumer | Organism that eats other organisms |
| Pyramid of biomass | Scaled diagram showing the biomass at each stage of a food chain |
| Respiration | Process in cells that transfers energy for living processes |
| Waste materials | Materials such as faeces and urine, which still contain biomass |

## Exam traps

- It is **1 %** from the Sun to the plant, and about **10 %** from one trophic level to the next.
- Energy is **transferred**, not used up or lost from existence.
- Always give **two** reasons for loss: waste materials and respiration.
- Pyramids of **numbers** are not required. Use biomass.
- A scale and labels are needed when constructing a pyramid.
- Percentage transfer divides the **upper** level by the **lower** level.
- Evaluating farming methods needs both a benefit and a drawback.

## Self-test

1. What is the source of energy for most communities of living organisms?
2. What percentage of the incident light energy do plants and algae transfer for photosynthesis?
3. Where is this energy stored?
4. What percentage of the biomass is transferred from one trophic level to the next?
5. Give the two reasons given in the specification for this loss.
6. What does a pyramid of biomass show?
7. A scale of 1 cm per 200 kg is used. What bar length shows 600 kg?
8. Calculate the percentage transfer from 2 400 kg of producers to 288 kg of primary consumers.
9. A field stores 4.0 × 10⁴ kJ in plants. How much energy would be expected in secondary consumers?
10. Explain why reducing the number of stages in a food chain improves the efficiency of food production.

### Answers

1. The Sun (radiation from the Sun).
2. About 1 %.
3. In the substances that make up the cells of the plants.
4. About 10 %.
5. Some materials and energy are lost in waste materials; respiration supplies energy for living processes (including movement) and much of it is transferred to the surroundings.
6. The biomass at each stage of a food chain, drawn to scale.
7. 600 ÷ 200 = **3.0 cm**.
8. 288 ÷ 2 400 × 100 = **12 %**.
9. 10 % of 4.0 × 10⁴ = 4.0 × 10³ kJ to primary consumers; 10 % again = **400 kJ** to secondary consumers.
10. There are fewer stages at which energy is lost in waste and respiration, so more of the energy from the producers reaches the final consumer.
