This guide goes deeper into one sub-topic of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: **19.4 Populations**, the last part of topic 19, Organisms and their environment. It has seven learning outcomes. Outcomes 1 to 6 are Core. Outcome 7, explaining each phase of the sigmoid growth curve, is Supplement content, so it is **Extended only** (Papers 2 and 4).

The [organisms and their environment study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-organisms-and-their-environment/) gives the definitions and a table of the four phases. This page builds the skills that population questions test: sorting examples into population, community and ecosystem, linking each factor to birth and death rates, and reading and calculating from graphs. Afterwards, use the [populations revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-populations-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/) and the [populations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-populations-cambridge-igcse-biology-practice/).

## 19.4.1 to 19.4.3 Population, community, ecosystem

Each description is built from the one before it.

- A **population** has three conditions: **one species**, **same area**, **same time**.
- A **community** is **all the populations** of different species in an ecosystem.
- An **ecosystem** is the community **plus** its non-living environment (water, soil, air, light, temperature), **interacting** together.

**Worked example 1: sorting examples.**

| Example | Term | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| All the frogs of one species in a pond this spring | population | one species, one area, one time |
| All the frogs, fish, insects, algae and plants in the pond | community | all populations, no non-living parts |
| The pond: its organisms, the water, mud, dissolved oxygen and light | ecosystem | community and environment, interacting |
| Frogs of three different species in a pond | none of these | more than one species, but not all of the populations |

The last row is a favourite trap: "all the frogs" is not a population if there are several frog species.

## 19.4.4 Factors affecting the rate of population growth

A population grows when **births** (and arrivals) exceed **deaths**. The syllabus limits the factors to four. Learn how each one acts on births or deaths.

| Factor | Effect when it increases | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Food supply** | growth rate rises | more energy for reproduction, fewer deaths from starvation |
| **Competition** | growth rate falls | individuals compete for food, space, water or mates, so fewer survive or breed |
| **Predation** | growth rate falls | more individuals are killed and eaten |
| **Disease** | growth rate falls | more individuals die or are too weak to reproduce |

Disease spreads faster in a crowded population, and competition is more intense when numbers are high. So as a population grows, these factors become stronger, which is why growth slows.

## 19.4.5 The sigmoid curve: identifying the phases

A population of organisms placed in a new environment with **limited resources**, such as yeast in a flask of sugar solution, follows a characteristic pattern.

```
number
  |                 stationary
  |               ___________
  |              /           \   death
  |             /             \
  |  exponential               \
  |           /
  |  lag ____/
  |_______________________________ time
```

| Phase | How to recognise it on a graph |
|---|---|
| Lag | line almost flat at the start; numbers rise slowly |
| Exponential (log) | line gets steeper and steeper; numbers rise fastest |
| Stationary | line levels off; numbers stay roughly constant |
| Death | line falls |

The S-shape is formed by the lag, exponential and stationary phases. The death phase follows when resources run out.

## 19.4.6 Interpreting population graphs

### Worked example 2: a bacterial culture

Numbers of bacteria in a culture were counted every hour (illustrative data):

| Time / h | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number / millions | 2 | 3 | 6 | 15 | 34 | 52 | 58 | 59 | 59 |

**(a) Identify the phases.** Lag: 0 to about 1 h. Exponential: about 1 to 5 h. Stationary: about 6 to 8 h.

**(b) Calculate the mean rate of growth between 2 h and 4 h.**

```
rate = change in number ÷ time taken
     = (34 − 6) ÷ (4 − 2)
     = 28 ÷ 2
     = 14 million bacteria per hour
```

**(c) Compare this with the rate between 6 h and 8 h.** (59 − 58) ÷ 2 = 0.5 million per hour, so growth has almost stopped.

Always show the subtraction, divide by the time interval, and give the unit.

### Worked example 3: predator and prey

Some graphs show two populations together, such as hares and the lynx that eat them, rising and falling over several years.

- The **prey** population rises first, because there is plenty of food and few predators.
- The **predator** population rises **after** it, because more prey means more food.
- More predators eat more prey, so the prey population falls.
- With less food, the predator population falls next, and the cycle repeats.

The key observation for marks: **the predator peaks come after the prey peaks**. Use the years on the axis to quote the delay.

## 19.4.7 Explaining each phase (Extended only)

Tie every phase to birth rate, death rate and, where appropriate, a **limiting factor**: a factor in short supply that restricts growth.

| Phase | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Lag | Few individuals present, so few are reproducing; individuals are adjusting to the new conditions or maturing (for example making enzymes for a new food source) |
| Exponential | Food and space are plentiful, there is little competition, predation or disease; the birth rate is much higher than the death rate, and each new individual soon reproduces too, so numbers double regularly |
| Stationary | Limiting factors take effect: food, water, space or oxygen run short, and waste products build up; competition increases, so the birth rate falls and the death rate rises until **birth rate equals death rate** |
| Death | Food runs out or toxic waste (such as ethanol from yeast) reaches harmful levels; the **death rate is higher than the birth rate** |

### Worked example 4: annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain why the population of yeast in a flask of sugar solution stops increasing. [3]

**Answer:** As the population grows, the sugar becomes a limiting factor (1). Competition for the remaining sugar increases and waste products such as ethanol build up (1). The birth rate falls and the death rate rises until they are equal, so the population stays constant (1).

**Why it scores:** it names a limiting factor, says what changes as numbers rise, and ends with birth rate equal to death rate.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a population without "one species" or "same time".
- Calling all the animals in a habitat a community; a community includes plants and microorganisms too.
- Defining an ecosystem without "interacting".
- Giving factors outside the syllabus list when asked to state them; stick to food supply, competition, predation and disease.
- Saying the stationary phase is when "all organisms stop reproducing". Births continue but equal deaths.
- Reading the graph at the wrong point or omitting units in a rate calculation.
- (Extended) Explaining the exponential phase as "they reproduce a lot" without saying resources are plentiful and births exceed deaths.

## Exam technique for 19.4 command words

- **Describe** (terms): use all parts of the syllabus wording.
- **State** the factors: the four named factors, each with its effect.
- **Identify** a phase: name it using the syllabus terms (lag, exponential or log, stationary, death).
- **Interpret** or **calculate**: quote values from the axes; show working; give units.
- **Explain** (Extended): limiting factor + effect on birth or death rate.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the sigmoid curve Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
Identifying the four phases and interpreting population graphs are Core. Explaining the factors that cause each phase, using limiting factors, is Extended only.

**What is the difference between a community and an ecosystem?**
A community is all the populations of different species living in an ecosystem. An ecosystem is the community together with its non-living environment, interacting together.

**What happens to birth and death rates in the stationary phase?**
The birth rate equals the death rate, so the population size stays roughly constant.
