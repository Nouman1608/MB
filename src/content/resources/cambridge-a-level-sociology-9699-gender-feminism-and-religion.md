Section 12.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks whether religion oppresses
women, and whether that is changing. The syllabus names three content points: feminist
perspectives on religion; explanations for patriarchy and gender inequality in religion; and moves
towards gender equality in religious organisations, with evidence that religious practices may
benefit women. It ties the section to the key concepts of *Socialisation, culture and identity*
and *Power, control and resistance*, so essays should keep asking who holds religious authority
and how far women exercise agency within religion.

The [secularisation, feminism and post-modern religion guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-influence-of-religion/)
introduces the core names. This guide separates the feminist positions more carefully, organises
the explanations of inequality into types, and adds further evidence on both sides.

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology 9699 (2024–2026), Paper 4 Section C (Religion),
heading 12 *The influence of religion*, sub-topic 12.2:

- Feminist perspectives on religion.
- Explanations for patriarchy and gender inequality in religion.
- Moves towards gender equality in religious organisations and evidence that religious practices
  may benefit women.

Paper 4 essays carry 35 marks each and must explore contrasting views or evidence.

## Feminist perspectives: not one view but several

The most common weakness in 12.2 essays is writing "feminists say religion is patriarchal" as if
all feminists agree. Learn the positions as a spectrum.

| Perspective | Core claim about religion | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Radical feminist** | Religion is a central institution of patriarchy; male images of God make male power seem sacred | Mary Daly, *Beyond God the Father* (1973): "if God is male, then the male is God" |
| **Marxist feminist** | Religion is ideology serving both capitalism and patriarchy; it compensates women for exploitation and keeps them in domestic roles | Builds on Marx's "opium of the people" applied to gender |
| **Liberal feminist** | Religion contains inequality but can be reformed from within through equal access to roles | Campaigns for women's ordination |
| **Existentialist** | Religion offers women false compensation for second-class status | Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex* (1949) |
| **Feminists within faith traditions** | Patriarchy comes from male interpretation and culture, not the faith's core message | Nawal El Saadawi; Islamic and Christian feminist theologians |
| **Agency-based approaches** | Women actively use religion for their own purposes; Western liberal ideas of freedom may misread their choices | Linda Woodhead; Saba Mahmood |

Mary Daly began as a Catholic theologian seeking reform and later rejected Christianity
altogether, so her work shows the move from a liberal to a radical position. This kind of detail
lifts an answer above description.

## Explanations for patriarchy and inequality in religion

Group explanations by *where* the inequality is located. This makes evaluation easier, because
each type responds to a different kind of change.

1. **Organisational.** Leadership roles such as priest, bishop, imam or rabbi have been reserved
   for men in many traditions, so men control teaching, ritual and resources.
2. **Textual and interpretive.** Sacred texts were written, translated and interpreted mainly by
   men, and passages about obedience or modesty have been read in ways that favour men.
3. **Theological imagery.** God described as Father or King, and male founders and prophets, link
   authority with masculinity.
4. **Ritual and purity rules.** Ideas of female impurity, for example around menstruation or
   childbirth, have restricted women's access to sacred spaces or rituals in some traditions.
5. **Religious law and the family.** Rules on marriage, divorce, inheritance and dress can
   reinforce men's authority in the home.
6. **Historical.** Karen Armstrong argues that early religions often worshipped female deities and
   that male dominance became entrenched with the rise of monotheism; inequality is therefore a
   historical development, not a permanent feature of religion.

**Evaluating these explanations**

- They show that inequality is socially produced, which supports the claim that it can change.
- Radical feminists treat the explanations as evidence of a patriarchal core; liberal and
  faith-based feminists treat them as distortions that can be corrected.
- Explanations based on one tradition should not be generalised to all religions; essays that
  compare more than one faith score higher.

## Moves towards gender equality

The syllabus asks for "moves towards" equality, which implies a process that may be incomplete.

- **Church of England:** women ordained as priests from 1994; the first woman bishop consecrated
  in 2015.
- **Judaism:** Regina Jonas was ordained as a rabbi in Germany in 1935; Sally Priesand became the
  first woman ordained by the American Reform movement in 1972. Women serve as rabbis in Reform,
  Liberal and other non-Orthodox movements, while Orthodox Judaism generally does not ordain them.
- **Islam:** among Hui Muslims in China there is a long tradition of women's mosques led by women
  religious leaders who lead other women in prayer; women scholars and teachers also have
  established roles in many Muslim communities.
- **Roman Catholic Church:** priesthood remains restricted to men, which is useful as a
  counter-example.

**Evaluation:** formal access to roles does not guarantee equal influence. Women may be ordained
but under-represented in senior posts, and change in one denomination may provoke resistance or
division elsewhere.

## Evidence that religious practices may benefit women

- **Helen Watson (1994):** some Muslim women chose the veil as an expression of identity and as a
  way of avoiding sexual objectification.
- **Elizabeth Brusco**, *The Reformation of Machismo* (1995): in Colombia, women's conversion to
  evangelical Protestantism encouraged husbands to give up drinking and gambling and to commit to
  family life, so the household gained resources and stability.
- **Saba Mahmood**, *Politics of Piety* (2005): women in a mosque movement in Cairo cultivated
  piety through religious study. Mahmood argued that their agency should be understood on its own
  terms, not only as resistance to or acceptance of male power.
- **Linda Woodhead:** women may use religion for their own ends, gaining community, status,
  meaning and respect: "religious forms of feminism".
- **Practical benefits:** religious groups can offer women leadership in women-only spaces,
  social support networks and a moral framework that criticises male violence or neglect.

**Critical response:** radical feminists may see these benefits as making the best of a
patriarchal system rather than changing it. A strong answer weighs agency (women choose and use
religion) against structure (the choices are made within rules set mainly by men).

## Worked example: model evaluative paragraph

*Original practice question:* "Religious practices benefit women more than they oppress them."
Evaluate this view. **[35]**

> Brusco's study of evangelical converts in Colombia supports the view that religion can benefit
> women in practical ways: conversion reshaped men's behaviour, redirecting money and attention
> to the household. However, the benefit operated through a theology that still placed the
> husband at the head of the family, so women gained security without gaining formal authority.
> This suggests that the question sets up a false choice: religion can benefit women within a
> structure that remains patriarchal. A radical feminist such as Daly would see this as
> adaptation to oppression, while Woodhead would see a form of religious feminism. The study
> therefore supports the view in terms of everyday life chances but not in terms of power within
> the religious organisation.

**Why this works:** it uses evidence precisely, interprets it, introduces two contrasting
perspectives, and reaches a qualified mini-judgement linked back to the wording of the question.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "feminism" as one view.
- Generalising from one religion to all religions.
- Writing about veiling only as oppression or only as choice, without weighing both.
- Listing dates of ordination reforms without evaluating what formal change achieves.
- Ignoring the syllabus phrase "evidence that religious practices may benefit women", which
  needs its own developed section.

## Command words in Paper 4

**Evaluate** requires judgement. For 12.2, the most effective judgements specify which women,
which religion and which aspect of inequality (leadership, doctrine, family life) the conclusion
applies to.
