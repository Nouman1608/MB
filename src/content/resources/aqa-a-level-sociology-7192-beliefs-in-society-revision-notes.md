These notes condense the [Beliefs in society study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-beliefs-in-society/); read that first if any idea here is new.

They cover section 4.2.5 Beliefs in Society of the AQA AS and A-level Sociology (7191, 7192) specification, Version 1.2 (14 October 2021), for AQA A-level Sociology (7192). The topic is assessed in Paper 2: Topics in Sociology, Section B. Track your progress on the [AQA A-level Sociology hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/sociology/) and [checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/sociology/), then test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-sociology-7192-beliefs-in-society-practice/).

## Paper 2 at a glance

- Paper 2: Topics in Sociology is a 2 hour written exam, 80 marks, 33.3% of the A-level.
- Section A: one from option 1: 4.2.1, 4.2.2, 4.2.3 or 4.2.4. Section B: one from option 2: 4.2.5, 4.2.6, 4.2.7 or 4.2.8.
- Section B is extended writing, 40 marks. Beliefs in Society (4.2.5) is one of the four option 2 topics, alongside Global Development, The Media, and Stratification and Differentiation.
- The question split inside Section B is not printed in the specification; your teacher will confirm it.
- The five content points: (1) ideology, science and religion, including Christian and non-Christian traditions; (2) social change and stability; (3) religious organisations and New Age movements; (4) social groups; (5) religion in the contemporary world, secularisation, globalisation and the spread of religions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ideology | Ideas that serve a group's interests, often by making inequality look natural |
| Hegemony | Gramsci: rule by consent, achieved through ideas |
| Open / closed belief system | Open: can be revised by evidence. Closed: explains away challenges |
| Paradigm | Kuhn: a science's shared framework of assumptions and methods |
| Substantive definition | Defines religion by its content, such as belief in the supernatural (Weber) |
| Functional definition | Defines religion by what it does, such as integration (Durkheim) |
| Collective conscience | Shared norms and values that bind a society |
| Civil religion | Belief system that attaches sacred qualities to the nation (Bellah) |
| Theodicy of disprivilege | Religious explanation of suffering that appeals to the poor (Weber) |
| Liberation theology | Catholic movement in Latin America siding with the poor |
| Sect / cult | Sect: exclusive, high commitment. Cult: loose, tolerant, individual |
| Denomination | Niebuhr's middle type, accepts other faiths and society |
| NRM | New religious movement (Wallis: world-rejecting, -accommodating, -affirming) |
| Holistic milieu | Spiritual activities focused on the self and well-being (Heelas and Woodhead) |
| Secularisation | Religion losing social significance (Wilson) |
| Vicarious religion | A minority practising religion on behalf of a majority (Davie) |
| Existential security | Feeling of safety from risks to survival (Norris and Inglehart) |
| Cultural defence / transition | Religion uniting a threatened group / helping migrants adjust (Bruce) |
| Fundamentalism | Return to basic texts and practices, often against modern change |

## Theories and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding or claim | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Popper | Science | Science is open because theories must be falsifiable | Kuhn shows scientists defend paradigms |
| Merton (1942) | Norms of science | Communism, universalism, disinterestedness, organised scepticism | Ideal norms may not match real lab behaviour |
| Polanyi; Evans-Pritchard | Closed systems; Azande | Failed oracles explained away, protecting the belief | Science can do this too |
| Kuhn (1962) | Paradigms | Normal science resists anomalies until revolution | Revolutions show science does change |
| Mannheim | Ideology vs utopia | Privileged and underprivileged groups produce partial worldviews | Assumes intellectuals can rise above interests |
| Durkheim (1912) | Totemism, Arunta | Worshipping the totem is worshipping society | Fits small societies better than multi-faith ones |
| Malinowski | Trobriand Islanders | Ritual for risky ocean fishing, not lagoon fishing | Underplays religion's role in conflict |
| Bellah (1967) | Civil religion | US national rituals integrate a diverse society | Is civil religion really religion? |
| Marx | Opium of the people | Religion eases and disguises exploitation | Ignores religion inspiring change |
| Weber (1905) | Protestant ethic | Calvinist asceticism helped the spirit of capitalism | Kautsky: capitalism came first |
| Bruce | Civil rights movement | Black churches were a resource for change | New Christian Right largely failed |
| Maduro; liberation theology | Neo-Marxism | Religion can be relatively autonomous and radical | The Vatican criticised the movement |
| Worsley | Cargo cults, Melanesia | Millenarian movements as early protest | Mainly in colonial settings |
| Troeltsch; Niebuhr | Types | Church, sect, denomination | Based on Christian Europe |
| Wallis (1984) | NRMs | World-rejecting, accommodating, affirming | Some movements fit more than one type |
| Stark and Bainbridge | Market theory; deprivation | Competition and compensators explain religiosity | Hadaway et al. (1993): US attendance over-reported |
| Heelas and Woodhead (2005) | Kendal | Holistic milieu growing but much smaller than congregations | No spiritual revolution yet |
| Miller and Hoffmann (1995) | Gender | Women more risk-averse and socialised into religion | Gender gap varies by faith |
| Pryce (1979) | Bristol African-Caribbeans | Pentecostalism's values of hard work and thrift helped members adapt | One city, one period |
| Voas and Crockett (2005) | Age | Each generation less religious than its parents | Survey data may miss private belief |
| Davie | Believing without belonging | Belief persists as attendance falls | Belief is falling too |
| Lyon (2000) | Jesus in Disneyland | Religion relocated into consumption | Weak commitment may be decline |
| Norris and Inglehart (2004) | Existential security | Insecure societies stay religious and grow faster | Focuses on demand, not supply |
| Huntington (1996) | Clash of civilisations | Conflict follows religious-civilisational lines | Ignores divisions within religions |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Conservative force vs force for change:** conservative can mean upholding tradition (beliefs) or upholding the existing social order (function). Weber and Bruce show change; functionalists and Marxists show stability.
- **Church vs sect:** both claim a monopoly of truth, but churches accept society and sects reject it.
- **Sect vs cult:** sects are exclusive and demanding; cults are tolerant and individualistic.
- **Religion vs spirituality:** organised, external authority vs self-directed belief (Heelas).
- **Belief, belonging and practice:** three different measures of religiosity that can move in different directions.
- **Decline vs change:** secularisation theorists see decline; Davie, Lyon and Hervieu-Léger see religion changing form.
- **Cultural defence vs cultural transition:** defending identity under threat vs adjusting after migration.
- **UK vs global picture:** census figures show decline in England and Wales, but Norris and Inglehart and Berger argue the world as a whole is not secularising.

## Mini essay plan

*Question type: evaluate the view that religion is in decline in society today.*

1. **Introduction.** Define secularisation (Wilson). Name the measures: belief, belonging, practice.
2. **Evidence for decline.** Census 2021 for England and Wales: Christian 46.2%, down from 59.3% in 2011; "no religion" 37.2%. Bruce: technological worldview and diversity. Voas and Crockett: generational decline.
3. **Explanations.** Weber's disenchantment; Parsons's structural differentiation; Berger's sacred canopy.
4. **Against: changing form.** Davie's vicarious religion; Lyon's relocation; Hervieu-Léger's spiritual shoppers; Heelas and Woodhead on the holistic milieu.
5. **Against: global and market views.** Stark and Bainbridge's religious market; Norris and Inglehart's existential security; growth of Pentecostalism; migration and the spread of Islam, Hinduism and Sikhism to the UK.
6. **Evaluate.** Measurement problems; the UK is not the world; Bruce says New Age commitment is too weak to count as revival.
7. **Judgement.** Base it on your evidence; for example, organised Christianity in the UK is declining, while the global picture is more mixed.

## Quick self-test

1. What did Merton call the four norms of science?
2. Which study showed how the Azande protected belief in the poison oracle?
3. What did Durkheim say people really worship when they worship the totem?
4. Why did Trobriand Islanders use ritual for ocean fishing but not lagoon fishing?
5. Name the religious doctrine at the centre of Weber's Protestant ethic.
6. Give Wallis's three types of NRM.
7. What is a "theodicy of disprivilege"?
8. What did Heelas and Woodhead conclude about a spiritual revolution in Kendal?
9. What two roles does Bruce say religion plays for minority ethnic groups?
10. What does Davie mean by "believing without belonging"?
11. What is existential security theory?

### Answers

1. Communism, universalism, disinterestedness and organised scepticism.
2. Evans-Pritchard's study, used by Polanyi as an example of a closed belief system.
3. Society itself.
4. Ocean fishing was dangerous and uncertain; ritual helped people cope with stress.
5. Predestination (Calvinism).
6. World-rejecting, world-accommodating and world-affirming.
7. A religious explanation of suffering, often promising later reward, that appeals to marginal groups.
8. The holistic milieu was growing but still much smaller than the congregational domain, so a spiritual revolution had not yet taken place.
9. Cultural defence and cultural transition.
10. People keep private religious beliefs but no longer attend or join religious organisations.
11. Norris and Inglehart's view that people who feel secure from risks to survival need religion less.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining secularisation only as "fewer people going to church". Include belief and social significance.
- Quoting census figures without saying they come from England and Wales, or treating them as world data.
- Saying Weber argued Calvinism alone caused capitalism.
- Describing every new movement as a "cult" in the everyday negative sense.
- Writing about Christianity only when the specification names non-Christian traditions.
- Listing studies without linking them to the question's view.
- Treating Huntington's thesis as a description of fact rather than a contested argument.
- Leaving out research design, which the specification makes integral (for example, how Kendal was studied or how attendance is counted).
- Ending with a judgement that does not follow from the evidence you used.
