## Paper 2 at a glance

1 hour 45 minutes, 80 marks, two sections of 40 marks each.

| Section | Focus |
|---|---|
| A: Reading | Two unseen non-fiction extracts, one 19th-century + one 20th/21st-century, on a shared theme |
| B: Writing | One writing task on the same theme, presenting your own viewpoint |

## Section A: comparing, not describing

The specification's own aims are precise: "read and evaluate texts critically" and "make comparisons
between texts." A weak answer describes Text A's viewpoint, then Text B's, then bolts on "they are
different" at the end. A strong answer works through **specific, comparable points** — attitude to
the same event, choice of emotive language, use of a particular structural device — discussing both
texts together throughout, not in two separate halves.

**Why one 19th-century text?** It forces comparison across real historical distance — tone, assumed
audience and rhetorical convention differ between a Victorian travel account and a modern opinion
piece in ways two contemporary writers are less likely to show.

## Section B: writing to a viewpoint

Not neutral writing — a clearly presented, persuasive viewpoint on the Section A theme. Different
skill set from Paper 1: rhetorical questions, direct address, structured argument, deliberate tone —
not narrative or descriptive technique.

## Worked example: comparing two viewpoints

Two texts on the same theme (say, city life): one Victorian, praising urban progress; one modern,
critical of overcrowding.

```
Point of comparison:  attitude to the city itself
Text A (Victorian):   celebratory language ("magnificent," "thriving") --
                       positions the city as a symbol of progress
Text B (modern):       critical language ("crowded," "exhausting") --
                       positions the city as a source of strain
Analysis:              both use emotive, evaluative language, but
                       towards opposite ends -- comparing WHY each
                       writer chose their stance (era, evident purpose)
                       scores higher than simply noting the contrast
```

## Reading the two sources efficiently

Because Section A always presents two sources on a shared theme, read the shorter or more accessible
source first to establish the shared topic and general viewpoint landscape, then read the second
source actively looking for points of agreement and difference with the first, rather than reading
both sources cold in sequence without any comparative frame in mind.

## Section A question types

The four Section A questions build in demand across the paper: identifying and interpreting explicit
and implicit information; explaining a writer's use of language to achieve effects; comparing how
writers convey their differing perspectives across both texts; and evaluating a writer's methods
against a given statement. Because the final comparison/evaluation question typically carries the most
marks, allocate reading and planning time so it is not rushed at the end — a common and avoidable
timing mistake is spending disproportionate time on the earlier, lower-tariff questions and leaving too
little for the highest-value one.

## Register and audience in Section B

Because Section B's writing task specifies a form (such as an article, letter or speech), match your
register and structural conventions to that specific form, not a generic persuasive-writing template —
a letter has a different expected opening and sign-off than a speech, and examiners credit writing that
demonstrates awareness of its specified audience and purpose, not just accurate technical writing in
the abstract.

## Exam traps

- Summarising each text separately with comparison only at the end, rather than throughout.
- Writing Section B neutrally instead of committing to a clear, persuasive viewpoint.
- Running out of time on Section B because Section A's comparison took too long — both are worth 40
  marks, so time them evenly.
- Forgetting the Spoken Language endorsement is separately reported and carries 0% weighting — it
  does not affect the written grade at all.

## Building a Section A answer plan

For the comparison/evaluation question, a reliable structure is: identify a comparable point of
content or attitude in both texts; quote or closely reference each writer's specific language choice
around that point; explain the effect of each choice on a reader; then explicitly state how the two
approaches differ and why (audience, era, purpose). Repeating this four-step pattern for two or three
well-chosen points across the answer, rather than covering many points shallowly, tends to score more
reliably against the mark scheme's higher bands.

## Self-test

1. How many marks is each section worth, and how long is the paper?
2. Why does the specification pair a 19th-century text with a modern one?
3. What is the key difference between a weak and a strong Section A comparison?
4. What kind of writing skills does Section B specifically test?
5. Does the Spoken Language endorsement affect the overall GCSE grade?

Building in a two-minute check at the end of each section — rereading your answer against the exact
question wording — catches the most common and avoidable slips, such as drifting away from the
comparison the question actually asked for or forgetting to address both texts equally in Section A.

**Answers:** 1. 40 marks each, 1 hour 45 minutes total. 2. To force comparison across real historical
distance — differences in tone, audience and convention are more visible between a historical and a
modern text than between two contemporary ones. 3. A weak answer describes each text separately with
comparison as an afterthought; a strong answer works through specific comparable points across both
texts throughout the answer. 4. Persuasive, viewpoint-driven writing — rhetorical questions, direct
address, structured argument and deliberate tone, distinct from Paper 1's narrative/descriptive
skills. 5. No — it is reported separately and carries 0% weighting toward the overall grade.
