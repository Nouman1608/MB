**These notes condense how to answer questions on pairs of texts in Paper 2 Section A of OxfordAQA
International GCSE English Language (9270).** The specification says "questions will be asked on
individual texts and pairs of texts. At least one question will require students to consider two
texts." For full explanation and a model answer, use the
[questions on pairs of texts study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-questions-on-pairs-of-texts/);
for the section as a whole, see the
[Paper 2 Section A reading revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-a-reading-revision-notes/).

## The objective

**R2**: "Collate and synthesise detail from one or more sources, making cross-references and
comparisons as appropriate." R3 (perspectives) and R4 (methods, and "evaluate") often apply too.

## Three jobs

| Job | Signal words | Produce |
|---|---|---|
| Synthesis | "Using both texts, summarise…", "What do both tell you…" | One combined picture |
| Compare ideas or perspectives | "Compare the writers' attitudes…" | Similarities and differences in view |
| Compare methods | "Compare how the writers present…" | Similarities and differences in technique, with effects |

Combined questions need both: underline each part.

## Comparison axes

Purpose · Audience · Perspective · Tone · Form · Evidence · Structure

Choose two or three axes where the texts genuinely differ or surprisingly agree.

## The grid

```
               Text 1                    Text 2
Perspective    ...                       ...
Method 1       ... + quotation           ... + quotation
Method 2       ... + quotation           ... + quotation
Effect         ...                       ...
```

Each row becomes one integrated paragraph.

## Balance rules

1. Name **both** texts in every topic sentence.
2. Give **each** text its own evidence in every paragraph.
3. End each paragraph with **what the comparison shows**.

## Pairs of different kinds

| Pair | Compare |
|---|---|
| Fiction and non-fiction | Facts and explanation against character and situation |
| Visual and written | Instant impact against step-by-step argument |
| Brief and long | What is compressed against what is developed |

With very different forms, comparing **methods** is often most revealing.

## Synthesis sentence frame

*"Both texts suggest that… Text 1 shows… while Text 2 shows…; together they suggest that…"*
The final clause turns two details into synthesis.

## Comparative structures

| Similarity | Difference | Weighing |
|---|---|---|
| both, similarly, likewise, in the same way | whereas, while, by contrast, unlike, however | more directly, more vividly, less convincingly |

## Evaluation in a pair

Judge each text **against its own purpose** first, then compare: a report may be more useful; a
story more moving.

## Weak and strong compared

- **Weak:** "Text 1 is a report. It gives advice. Text 2 is a story. It is about an old woman." Each
  text is described separately; nothing is compared.
- **Strong:** "Whereas the report turns the heatwave into a list of instructions for 'residents', the
  story follows one named woman through the days, so the reader understands the danger as a lived
  experience rather than a set of rules." Both texts, one shared point, methods and effect.

## Paragraph engine for a comparison

```
TOPIC     shared point or key difference, naming both texts
TEXT 1    method + short quotation + effect
TURN      comparative connective
TEXT 2    method + short quotation + effect
SO WHAT   what the comparison reveals about purpose or perspective
```

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Synthesis | Combining details from texts into one account |
| Comparison | Setting texts side by side to show similarity and difference |
| Cross-reference | Linking a detail in one text to a detail in another |
| Comparison axis | An aspect on which both texts can be measured |
| Integrated paragraph | A paragraph that discusses both texts together |
| Perspective | A writer's viewpoint |
| Register | Level of formality and type of language |
| Evaluate | Judge how effectively a writer achieves a purpose |

## First-minute routine

1. Box the two texts the question names.
2. Underline the job: synthesise, compare ideas, compare methods, or a mix.
3. Choose two or three axes.
4. Fill the matching rows of your grid with short quotations.

## Exam traps

- Two mini-answers joined by "on the other hand".
- One text dominating.
- Trivial axes ("both use full stops").
- Synthesising when asked to compare, or the reverse.
- Using the wrong pair or a third text.
- Ignoring form when the texts are different kinds.

## Self-test

1. How many texts does the Paper 2 stimulus contain, and how many questions at least require two?
2. Quote R2.
3. Name the three jobs a pair question can ask for.
4. List five comparison axes.
5. Why avoid an axis where the texts are identical?
6. State the three balance rules.
7. What should the final clause of a synthesis sentence do?
8. When comparing fiction with non-fiction, what can you compare?
9. Why compare methods when the forms are very different?
10. How should you evaluate two texts with different purposes?
11. Give two connectives for similarity and two for difference.
12. What is an integrated paragraph?

## Answers

1. Five or six texts; at least one question.
2. "Collate and synthesise detail from one or more sources, making cross-references and comparisons
   as appropriate."
3. Synthesis; comparison of ideas or perspectives; comparison of methods.
4. Any five: purpose, audience, perspective, tone, form, evidence, structure.
5. Because there is little to say, and the answer will not show understanding of difference.
6. Name both texts in every topic sentence; give each its own evidence; end with what the comparison
   shows.
7. Say what the details show together.
8. How each creates understanding: facts and explanation in one, character and situation in the
   other.
9. Because different forms work in different ways, so methods reveal more than shared ideas.
10. Judge each against its own purpose first, then compare.
11. Similarity: any two of both, similarly, likewise, in the same way. Difference: any two of
    whereas, while, by contrast, unlike, however.
12. A paragraph that discusses both texts together around one shared point.
