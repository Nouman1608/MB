These notes condense sub-topic (g) Acids, bases and salt preparations (points 2.34 to 2.43C) of Topic 2 in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). Points 2.40C, 2.41C and 2.43C are **Paper 2 only**; the rest can appear on either paper. Worked examples and an annotated six-mark method are in the [acids, bases and salt preparations study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-acids-bases-and-salt-preparations/). Then try the [acids, bases and salt preparations practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-acids-bases-and-salt-preparations-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Soluble | Dissolves in water to form a solution |
| Precipitate | An insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed |
| Spectator ion | An ion present on both sides of an equation, unchanged |
| Proton donor | An acid: it gives away H⁺ |
| Proton acceptor | A base: it takes H⁺ |
| Base | A metal oxide, metal hydroxide or ammonia |
| Alkali | A base that is soluble in water |
| Excess | More than is needed, so some is left over after the reaction |
| Filtrate | The liquid that passes through the filter paper |
| Residue | The solid left on the filter paper |
| Saturated solution | Holds as much dissolved solute as it can at that temperature |
| Water of crystallisation | Water held inside a crystal's structure, as in CuSO₄·5H₂O |

## 2.34 Solubility in one pass

Check the ions in this order:

```
1. Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻ present?   → soluble
2. Chloride?  insoluble only if Ag⁺ or Pb²⁺
3. Sulfate?   insoluble only if Ba²⁺, Ca²⁺ or Pb²⁺
4. Carbonate? insoluble (unless step 1 applied)
5. Hydroxide? insoluble, except Na⁺, K⁺; Ca(OH)₂ slightly soluble
```

To predict a precipitate from two solutions, swap the partners and test both new compounds. If either is insoluble, it precipitates.

## 2.35–2.36 Proton transfer

- Follow the H⁺: the species losing it is the acid; the species gaining it is the base.
- Oxide ion: `O²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O`
- Hydroxide ion: `OH⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O`
- Ammonia: `NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺`
- Carbonate ion: `CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂`
- A metal reacting with acid gives electrons to H⁺; it does not accept a proton, so it is not a base.

## 2.37 Salts from the three acids

| Acid | Anion | Salt family |
|---|---|---|
| hydrochloric, HCl | Cl⁻ | chlorides |
| sulfuric, H₂SO₄ | SO₄²⁻ | sulfates |
| nitric, HNO₃ | NO₃⁻ | nitrates |

**Formula method:** write the two ions, balance the charges, use brackets for more than one polyatomic ion.

```
Al³⁺ + NO₃⁻         → Al(NO₃)₃
NH₄⁺ + SO₄²⁻        → (NH₄)₂SO₄
Fe³⁺ + Cl⁻          → FeCl₃
```

**Equation reminders:**

```
ZnO(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Zn(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
2KOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
```

Nitric acid with metals is excluded from the specification. Copper does not react with the dilute acids, so copper salts come from copper(II) oxide or carbonate.

## 2.38 Bases and alkalis

- All alkalis are bases; few bases are alkalis.
- Soluble bases you need: sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, ammonia solution; calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble.
- Insoluble bases: most metal oxides and hydroxides, such as copper(II) oxide and magnesium hydroxide.

## Choosing the method

```
Salt insoluble?                       → precipitation (2.41C)
Salt soluble, reactant insoluble?     → excess solid + filter (2.39)
Salt soluble, Na/K/NH₄ salt?          → titration (2.40C)
```

## The three methods as step lists

**Excess insoluble solid (2.39, practical 2.42)**

```
warm acid → add solid until some remains → filter (keep filtrate)
→ heat to crystallisation point → cool → filter crystals → dry
```

**Acid + alkali (2.40C, Paper 2 only)**

```
titrate with indicator → note volume → repeat with no indicator
→ partly evaporate → crystallise → filter → dry
```

**Precipitation (2.41C, practical 2.43C, Paper 2 only)**

```
mix two soluble solutions → filter (keep residue)
→ wash with distilled water → dry
```

## Purpose of each step

| Step | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Excess solid | Uses up all the acid |
| Filter after excess | Removes unreacted solid |
| Heat only to crystallisation point | Saturates the solution; keeps water of crystallisation |
| No indicator on the repeat | Stops the salt being contaminated |
| Wash the precipitate | Removes the soluble by-product |
| Dry | Removes surface water |

## Practical observations

- **Copper(II) sulfate (2.42):** black solid dissolves, colourless solution turns blue, black solid remains once the acid is used up, blue crystals form.
- **Lead(II) sulfate (2.43C):** white precipitate forms immediately. Lead compounds are toxic: gloves, eye protection, safe disposal.

## Exam traps

- Barium chloride and calcium nitrate are soluble; only the listed exceptions are not.
- "The salt is soluble so use precipitation" is backwards: precipitation makes **insoluble** salts.
- In precipitation, the solid you want is the **residue**; in the excess method, the salt is in the **filtrate**.
- Choosing an insoluble reactant for precipitation, such as copper(II) carbonate to make copper(II) hydroxide, gives no reaction you can use.
- Writing MgNO₃ or KSO₄: balance the charges.
- Using universal indicator in the titration step.

## Quick self-test

1. Classify as soluble or insoluble: lead(II) nitrate, ammonium carbonate, calcium hydroxide, silver chloride.
2. In `CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O`, name the proton donor and the proton acceptor.
3. Write the formula of aluminium nitrate.
4. Write a balanced equation for zinc oxide reacting with dilute nitric acid.
5. Explain why copper(II) sulfate is not made by adding copper to dilute sulfuric acid.
6. State the method used to make potassium sulfate and name the two reactants.
7. Suggest two solutions that could be mixed to make barium carbonate.
8. When magnesium oxide is added to dilute hydrochloric acid in excess, is the magnesium chloride in the filtrate or the residue?
9. Give two observations that show copper(II) oxide has reacted completely with the acid present.
10. Calculate the mass of zinc chloride formed when 3.25 g of zinc reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid. (Ar: Zn = 65, Cl = 35.5)
11. Write the ionic equation for the precipitation of calcium carbonate.
12. Explain why magnesium is not a base, although it reacts with acids.

### Answers

1. Lead(II) nitrate soluble (all nitrates); ammonium carbonate soluble (ammonium rule); calcium hydroxide slightly soluble; silver chloride insoluble.
2. Sulfuric acid is the proton donor; copper(II) oxide (the oxide ion) is the proton acceptor.
3. Al(NO₃)₃.
4. `ZnO(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Zn(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l)`
5. Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it does not react with dilute sulfuric acid. Copper(II) oxide or carbonate is used instead.
6. Titration of potassium hydroxide solution with dilute sulfuric acid, then repeating without indicator and crystallising.
7. Barium nitrate (or barium chloride) solution and sodium carbonate (or potassium carbonate) solution.
8. The filtrate. The residue is the unreacted magnesium oxide.
9. The solution has turned blue and black solid remains undissolved even after stirring.
10. Moles Zn = 3.25 ÷ 65 = 0.0500 mol. Mr ZnCl₂ = 65 + 71 = 136. Mass = 0.0500 × 136 = **6.80 g**.
11. `Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s)`
12. Magnesium atoms give electrons to hydrogen ions, forming hydrogen gas. A base accepts protons; magnesium does not, and the reaction is not neutralisation.
