## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Distinguish between a fixed cost and a variable cost, using a cafe as your example. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why marginal cost usually falls and then rises as a firm increases output in the short run. **[3]**

**2.** A courier firm's total cost (TC) for different outputs of deliveries (hundreds) is: 0 = 50; 1 = 110; 2 = 150; 3 = 180; 4 = 215; 5 = 270; 6 = 350.
**(a)** State the fixed cost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate average variable cost at output 5. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the marginal cost of the sixth hundred. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate average cost at output 6, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**3.** A firm's average cost at 5 units is 20.
**(a)** The sixth unit costs 8 to make. Calculate the new average cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what this shows about the relationship between marginal cost and average cost. **[2]**

**4.** A garage services 400 cars a year. Its annual licence fee, a fixed cost, rises by 1,200.
**(a)** Calculate the change in average cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why marginal cost and average variable cost are unchanged. **[2]**

**5.** A box-making firm produces 600 boxes a day. Method A uses 9 workers and 1 press. Method B uses 4 workers and 2 presses. A press costs 180 a day.
**(a)** The daily wage is 65. Calculate the cost per box of each method and state which is cheaper. **[3]**
**(b)** The wage falls to 30. Repeat the calculation. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the firm may not switch method immediately. **[1]**

**6.** A worker is paid 84 a day and produces 42 units.
**(a)** Calculate unit labour cost. **[1]**
**(b)** After training, output per worker is 56 and pay is 98. Calculate the new unit labour cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain whether the firm's costs have improved. **[2]**

**7.** For each case, state whether it is an internal or external economy or diseconomy of scale. **(i)** A firm secures a discount on flour for ordering in bulk. **(ii)** A local college trains skilled workers for all firms in an industrial cluster. **(iii)** Managers in a very large firm struggle to coordinate departments. **(iv)** Congestion in the cluster raises delivery times and costs for every firm. **(v)** A larger firm borrows at a lower interest rate. **[5]**

**8.** A firm employs 6 workers at 50 a day and 3 machines at 120 a day, producing 220 units. It doubles every input and produces 480 units.
**(a)** Calculate total cost and average cost before and after. **[3]**
**(b)** State the returns to scale and the effect on average cost. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why increasing returns might not lower average cost. **[1]**

**9.** An industry's long-run average cost (LRAC) is, for output in thousands: 5 = 14.0; 10 = 10.5; 15 = 8.4; 20 = 7.0; 25 = 6.2; 30 = 6.2; 35 = 6.2; 40 = 6.2.
**(a)** State the minimum efficient scale. **[1]**
**(b)** Market demand is 150,000 units. Calculate the maximum number of firms at that scale. **[2]**
**(c)** A new entrant expects to sell 10,000 units. Calculate its cost disadvantage per unit, in money and as a percentage of the lowest LRAC, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how minimum efficient scale affects barriers to entry. **[3]**

**10.** Explain two reasons why a large firm may suffer internal diseconomies of scale, and one reason why its LRAC might be L-shaped rather than U-shaped. **[6]**

**11.** In industry Z, LRAC is flat beyond minimum efficient scale, which is 40% of the market. Firm P produces at that scale at a cost of 6.2 per unit. Firm S, in an industrial cluster with external economies, has a cost of 7.4 per unit. Evaluate whether P's economies of scale give it a lasting advantage over S. **[9]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A fixed cost does not change with output in the short run, for example rent [1]. A variable cost changes with output, for example coffee beans [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At low output extra workers allow specialisation and better use of fixed equipment, so extra output costs less [1]. Eventually the fixed factor is crowded and diminishing returns set in [1], so each extra unit needs more input and marginal cost rises [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the fixed factor and link diminishing returns to the cost of the extra unit, not just to total output.

**2. (a)** Fixed cost is TC at zero output: **50** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** VC = 270 minus 50 = 220 [1]; AVC = 220 / 5 = **44** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** MC = 350 minus 270 = **80** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** AC = 350 / 6 [1] = **58.3** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula and the substitution so your method is clear even if the arithmetic slips.

**3. (a)** Total cost = 5 × 20 = 100 [1]. New average = (100 + 8) / 6 = **18** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** When MC is below AC, AC falls [1]. When MC is above AC (a sixth unit at 32 gives 22), AC rises, so MC cuts AC at its minimum [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use numbers in this explanation; a claim about averages with no figures is hard to distinguish from a guess.

**4. (a)** 1,200 / 400 [1] = **3 per car** increase [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MC is the cost of one extra car and the licence fee does not vary with output [1]. AVC contains only variable costs, so it excludes the fee [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State which curves move and which do not; AC and AFC rise by the same amount.

**5. (a)** A: 9 × 65 + 180 = 765, so 1.275 per box [1]. B: 4 × 65 + 2 × 180 = 620, so 1.03 per box [1]. **B is cheaper** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** A: 9 × 30 + 180 = 450 (0.75 per box); B: 4 × 30 + 360 = 480 (0.80) [1]. **A is now cheaper** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Presses are fixed in the short run, so replacing equipment takes time and may involve retraining [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing totals is not enough on its own; finish with a clear statement of the cheaper method.

**6. (a)** 84 / 42 = **2.00** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 98 / 56 = **1.75** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Unit labour cost fell by 12.5% [1] because output per worker rose by one third while pay rose by one sixth, so productivity outpaced pay [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A pay rise alone does not show higher costs; compare it with productivity.

**7.** (i) Internal economy [1]. (ii) External economy [1]. (iii) Internal diseconomy [1]. (iv) External diseconomy [1]. (v) Internal economy (financial) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Ask who gains or loses: the single firm (internal) or all firms in the area (external).

**8. (a)** Before: 6 × 50 + 3 × 120 = 660, AC = 660 / 220 = 3.00 [1]. After: 1,320, AC = 1,320 / 480 = 2.75 [1]. Both totals correct [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Inputs doubled but output rose 2.18 times: **increasing returns** [1]. AC fell by 8.3%, an **economy of scale** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** If buying more raises input prices (for example wages of 58 and machines at 140 give 1,536 / 480 = 3.20), AC could rise despite increasing returns [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep returns to scale (output) and economies of scale (unit cost) as separate ideas in your wording.

**9. (a)** **25,000 units** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 150,000 / 25,000 [1] = **6 firms** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 10.5 minus 6.2 = **4.3** per unit [1]; 4.3 / 6.2 = **69.4%** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Entrants selling below MES have higher unit costs [1]. Incumbents can cut price to a level at which the entrant makes a loss [1]. A high MES relative to the market means entry needs large, risky, sunk start-up spending and leaves room for few firms [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare MES with market size; MES alone says nothing about barriers.

**10.** Communication slows because decisions pass through many layers [1], so responses are late and costs rise [1]. Workers feel remote from decisions, so motivation and effort fall [1], raising unit costs [1]. LRAC may be L-shaped if technology, delegation or multiple plants offset these problems, so unit cost stays flat [1] and larger output brings no penalty [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs a link to unit cost, not just a description of the problem.

**11.** Economies of scale lower long-run average cost as output rises [1]. S's cost is 19.4% above P's (7.4 against 6.2) [1]. P could set a price of 7.0, earning 0.8 a unit while S loses 0.4 a unit [1]. With MES at 40% of the market, only two firms can reach efficient scale, so entry is hard [1]. Against this: LRAC is flat beyond MES, so growth past that scale adds no cost advantage [1]. S's external economies, such as shared suppliers and skilled labour, can narrow the gap [1]. S may serve a niche where price matters less [1]. P may later face diseconomies if it expands [1]. Judgement: the advantage is real but is lasting only if S cannot reduce costs or differentiate, which depends on the size of the gap and on time [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures given, then weigh the advantage against the counter-arguments and reach a supported conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Skipping the working on cost tables, so one slip removes the whole answer.
- Writing "costs fall" without saying whether total, average or marginal.
- Treating a change in output as a shift of a cost curve.
- Mixing up internal and external economies.
- Giving a reason for economies of scale with no link to unit cost.
- Using returns to scale and economies of scale as if they were the same idea.
- Discussing MES without comparing it with market size.
- Evaluation that lists points but reaches no judgement.
