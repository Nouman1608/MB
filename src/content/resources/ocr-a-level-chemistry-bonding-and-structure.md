## Ionic bonding: (a) to (c)

An **ionic bond** is the electrostatic attraction between positive and negative ions. Metal atoms lose electrons to form cations; non-metal atoms gain them to form anions.

In a dot-and-cross diagram, enclose each ion in square brackets and write its charge at the top right, outside the brackets. Use dots for one element's electrons and crosses for the other's. Show outer shells only unless asked.

**Worked example 1: barium fluoride, BaF₂**

Ba (Group 2) has two outer electrons; F (Group 17) has seven. One Ba gives one electron to each of two F atoms, so Ba : F = 1 : 2. Draw [Ba]²⁺ with an empty outer shell, and two [F]⁻ ions each with eight outer electrons: seven dots and one cross.

**Giant ionic lattice.** In solid NaCl each Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺. The oppositely charged ions attract strongly **in all directions**, building one huge three-dimensional lattice. There are no molecules.

| Property | Ionic compounds | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Melting and boiling points | High | Much energy is needed to overcome the strong attractions between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice. Higher charges and smaller ions attract more strongly. |
| Solubility | Many dissolve in water | Polar water molecules attract the ions (δ− O towards cations, δ+ H towards anions), pull them from the lattice and surround them. Some, such as calcium carbonate, are insoluble because the lattice attractions are too strong. |
| Conductivity as a solid | None | Ions are fixed in the lattice. |
| Conductivity when molten or aqueous | Conducts | Ions are mobile and carry charge. |

## Covalent bonding: (d) to (f)

A **covalent bond** is the strong electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the bonded atoms.

In a dot-and-cross diagram, put shared pairs where the outer shells overlap and show every lone pair.

- **Single bond**: one shared pair. **Double** or **triple bond**: two or three shared pairs.
- **Dative covalent (coordinate) bond**: a shared pair whose **two** electrons are both supplied by one atom. The donor needs a lone pair; the acceptor needs space for it. In a displayed formula, draw it as an arrow from donor to acceptor.

**Worked example 2: sulfur dichloride, SCl₂.** S shares one electron with each Cl. Around S: two bonding pairs and two lone pairs. Each Cl: one bonding pair, three lone pairs.

**Worked example 3: carbonyl sulfide, OCS.** C shares two of its electrons with O and the other two with S, giving O=C=S. C has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs; O and S each keep two lone pairs.

**Worked example 4: H₃N→BCl₃.** Boron in BCl₃ has only three bonding pairs (six electrons), so it can accept a pair. Nitrogen in NH₃ donates its lone pair into that space, forming N→B. Boron now has four bonding pairs. Once formed, a dative bond is identical to any other covalent bond between the same atoms.

From Period 3 onwards, the central atom can hold more than eight electrons: SeF₆ has six bonding pairs round Se.

**Average bond enthalpy** measures covalent bond strength: the larger the value, the stronger the bond. Carbon–carbon values rise from C–C to C=C to C≡C. No definition or calculation is needed here; that comes in 3.2.1.

## Shapes of molecules and ions: (g) and (h)

Electron pairs round a central atom repel and move as far apart as possible. Lone pairs are closer to the central atom and repel more. Two lone pairs repel most, then a lone pair and a bonding pair, and two bonding pairs least. Each lone pair closes the bond angle by about 2.5°.

| Pairs | Bonding | Lone | Shape | Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2 | 0 | Linear | 180° | BeCl₂ |
| 3 | 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BCl₃ |
| 4 | 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄ |
| 4 | 3 | 1 | Pyramidal | 107° | NH₃ |
| 4 | 2 | 2 | Non-linear | 104.5° | H₂O |
| 6 | 6 | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | SeF₆ |

A double or triple bond counts as **one** bonding region, so OCS, with two double bonds, is linear (180°). With six pairs of which two are lone pairs, as in XeF₄, the lone pairs sit opposite each other and the shape is square planar (90°).

**Method**

1. Take the central atom's outer electrons.
2. Add one per singly bonded atom.
3. Add one per negative charge; subtract one per positive charge.
4. Halve it for the number of pairs. Subtract the bonded atoms to get the lone pairs.
5. Name the shape from the positions of the atoms, then give the angle.

**Worked example 5: SCl₂.** 6 + 2 = 8 electrons, 4 pairs: 2 bonding, 2 lone. **Non-linear, 104.5°**, the same arrangement as in water.

**Worked example 6: SnCl₆²⁻.** Tin is in Group 14: 4 + 6 + 2 = 12 electrons, 6 pairs, all bonding. **Octahedral, 90°**.

**3-D diagrams.** A solid line is in the plane of the paper, a solid wedge comes towards you and a dashed wedge goes away. A tetrahedral molecule has two lines, one wedge and one dashed wedge. Show lone pairs on pyramidal and non-linear species.

## Electronegativity and polarity: (i) and (j)

**Electronegativity** is an atom's ability to attract the bonding electrons in a covalent bond. Values rise towards fluorine: across a period and up a group. Use these Pauling values:

| H | C | N | O | F | Si | S | Cl | Ba |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.20 | 2.55 | 3.04 | 3.44 | 3.98 | 1.90 | 2.58 | 3.16 | 0.89 |

Identical atoms (difference 0) give a non-polar bond, a small difference gives a polar covalent bond and a large difference means mostly ionic bonding. C–H differs by 0.35, C–F by 1.43 and Ba–F by 3.09, which fits BaF₂ being ionic.

A **polar bond** forms between atoms of different electronegativity. The more electronegative atom pulls the shared pair towards itself and becomes δ−; the other becomes δ+. This is a **permanent dipole**. A **polar molecule** needs polar bonds whose dipoles do **not** cancel because of the shape. The specification's example: H₂O and CO₂ both have polar bonds, but linear CO₂ has no overall dipole, while non-linear H₂O does.

**Worked example 7: SiH₄ and SiH₃Cl.** Both are tetrahedral. In SiH₄ four identical Si–H dipoles point symmetrically and cancel: **non-polar**. In SiH₃Cl one bond is the much more polar Si–Cl (difference 1.26, against 0.30 for Si–H), so the dipoles do not cancel: **polar**.

## Intermolecular forces: (k) and (l)

Intermolecular forces act **between** molecules and are much weaker than covalent bonds **within** them.

**Induced dipole–dipole interactions (London forces).** Moving electrons create an uneven charge at any instant: an **instantaneous dipole**. It induces a dipole in a neighbour, and the two attract. London forces act between all molecules and get stronger with more electrons. Use these boiling points: Ne −246 °C, Ar −186 °C, Kr −153 °C, Xe −108 °C. Down the group each atom has more electrons, so the forces and boiling points increase.

**Permanent dipole–dipole interactions.** The δ+ end of one polar molecule attracts the δ− end of another, in addition to London forces. Both types can be called van der Waals' forces.

**Worked example 8: H₂S and SiH₄.** Each has 18 electrons, so London forces are similar. Non-linear H₂S is polar; symmetrical SiH₄ is not. H₂S boils at −60 °C, 52 °C above SiH₄ (−112 °C), because of its permanent dipole–dipole interactions.

**Hydrogen bonding** happens between a lone pair on N, O or F in one molecule and the H of an –NH, –OH or HF group in another. N, O and F are so electronegative, and H so small, that the H is strongly δ+ and can get close to the lone pair. In a diagram, label δ+ and δ−, draw the lone pair, and draw the hydrogen bond as a dashed line from the lone pair to the H, with O–H···O close to 180°. Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular force.

## Anomalous properties of water: (m)

**Ice is less dense than water.** In ice each molecule hydrogen bonds to four others, tetrahedrally, holding them in an open lattice with lots of space. On melting, some hydrogen bonds break and the molecules move closer. Use ice 0.917 g cm⁻³ and water 1.00 g cm⁻³: water expands by about 9% when it freezes, and ice floats.

**High melting and boiling points.** Use these boiling points: H₂S −60 °C, H₂Se −41 °C, H₂O 100 °C. From H₂S to H₂Se the rise comes from more electrons and stronger London forces. On that trend water would boil well below −60 °C. It actually boils 160 °C above H₂S because hydrogen bonds must also be broken.

## Simple molecular lattices: (n) and (o)

A **simple molecular lattice** is a regular arrangement of small covalent molecules held by intermolecular forces: I₂ molecules held by London forces in solid iodine, and H₂O molecules held by hydrogen bonds in ice.

| Property | Simple molecular | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Melting and boiling points | Low | Only weak intermolecular forces are overcome; covalent bonds do not break. |
| Solubility | Non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents (iodine in hexane); substances that hydrogen bond, such as simple alcohols, dissolve in water | Similar intermolecular forces form between solute and solvent. |
| Conductivity | None in any state | No ions or delocalised electrons. |

**Worked example 9: methanol and ethane.** Both have 18 electrons. Methanol has an –OH group and hydrogen bonds; ethane has only London forces. Methanol boils at 65 °C, ethane at −89 °C: a 154 °C gap from the hydrogen bonds.

## Common errors

- Writing "molecules" or "intermolecular forces" for an ionic lattice.
- Claiming that boiling iodine or water splits its covalent bonds.
- Missing lone pairs, or writing charges inside the brackets.
- Counting a double bond as two regions when predicting shape.
- Calling NH₃ tetrahedral: name the shape from the atoms.
- Drawing a hydrogen bond to an H bonded to carbon.
- Explaining London forces by mass rather than number of electrons.
