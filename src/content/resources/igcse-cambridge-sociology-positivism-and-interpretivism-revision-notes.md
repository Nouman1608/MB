Key question 1.1 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) asks *How do sociologists approach the study of
society?* The answer is that there are two main approaches — positivism, which studies society
scientifically with quantitative data, and interpretivism, which seeks meanings through qualitative
data — plus ways of combining them, and a set of concepts (bias, validity, reliability and so on)
used to judge any research choice. These ideas underpin every part of Paper 1 Question 1.

Fuller explanation is in the
[research approaches, data and investigation study guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-sociology-research-approaches-data-and-investigation/).

## Syllabus coverage

0495 syllabus 2025–2027, **1.1 How do sociologists approach the study of society?**

- 1.1.1 The positivist approach — scientific method; macro view; social facts; quantitative data;
  causation and correlation; patterns and trends; objectivity and reliability.
- 1.1.2 The interpretivist approach — micro view; meanings and motivations; qualitative data;
  in-depth; subjectivity; validity and verstehen.
- 1.1.3 Approaches that combine methods — triangulation; longitudinal studies.
- 1.1.4 Analysis and evaluation of research choices — bias; interviewer effect; Hawthorne/observer
  effect; validity; reliability; representativeness; generalisability.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Positivism** | Approach that studies society like a natural science, using quantitative data to find causes |
| **Interpretivism** | Approach that seeks to understand the meanings people give to their actions |
| **Scientific method** | Hypothesis, measurement, testing and conclusions, aiming at objectivity |
| **Macro view** | Looking at society as a whole and its structures |
| **Micro view** | Looking at small-scale interaction between individuals |
| **Social facts** | Features of society outside the individual that shape behaviour (Durkheim) |
| **Correlation** | Two variables change together |
| **Causation** | One variable produces a change in another |
| **Verstehen** | Empathetic understanding of others' meanings (Weber) |
| **Objectivity** | Freedom from the researcher's personal values |
| **Subjectivity** | Influence of personal views and interpretations |
| **Triangulation** | Using several methods or sources to check findings |
| **Longitudinal study** | Research on the same group at intervals over time |
| **Generalisability** | How far findings apply to the wider population |

## Positivism versus interpretivism at a glance

| Feature | Positivism | Interpretivism |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | Macro | Micro |
| Data | Quantitative | Qualitative |
| Seeks | Patterns, trends, causes | Meanings, motivations |
| Values | Reliability, objectivity | Validity, depth |
| Typical methods | Questionnaires, structured interviews, official statistics | Unstructured interviews, participant observation, personal documents |
| Classic example | Durkheim's *Suicide* (1897) | Interviews exploring why people feel as they do |
| Main criticism | Ignores meaning; researcher imposes categories | Subjective, small samples, hard to repeat |

## Combining approaches

- **Triangulation:** for example, a questionnaire to find how many students feel stressed, followed
  by unstructured interviews to understand why. Strength: cross-checks validity and reliability.
  Limitation: takes more time and money.
- **Longitudinal studies:** for example, birth cohort studies following people from birth into
  adulthood. Strength: show change and cause over time. Limitations: cost, attrition (people
  dropping out), and findings can date.

## Evaluating research choices

| Problem | What happens | How to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Bias | Researcher's views shape questions or interpretation | Neutral wording; peer checking |
| Interviewer effect | Respondents react to the interviewer's age, gender, ethnicity or status | Matched interviewers; self-completion questionnaires |
| Hawthorne / observer effect | People behave differently when watched | Covert observation (but ethical issues); longer observation so people relax |
| Low validity | Data does not reflect reality | Open questions; qualitative methods |
| Low reliability | Results differ if repeated | Standardised questions and procedures |
| Unrepresentative sample | Some groups missing | Stratified or random sampling |
| Low generalisability | Findings only fit the group studied | Larger, representative samples |

## Worked example: a 6-mark "explain" answer

*Explain why positivist sociologists prefer quantitative data.* (practice)

1. Positivists see sociology as a science, so they want data that can be **measured and compared**
   statistically to identify **patterns and trends**.
2. Quantitative methods such as questionnaires are **standardised**, so another researcher could
   repeat them, making results **reliable**.
3. Numbers allow the researcher to look for **correlation and causation**, testing a hypothesis
   while keeping their own values out of the research (**objectivity**).

Each point identifies a reason and develops it with a syllabus term, which is how such answers earn
full marks.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Saying interpretivists "do not use evidence" — they use qualitative evidence.
- Mixing up **validity** (truth) and **reliability** (repeatability).
- Assuming correlation proves causation.
- Describing triangulation as "using three methods" — it means using more than one.
- Forgetting that the Hawthorne effect can affect any method where people know they are studied.

## Quick self-test

1. Give two features of the positivist approach.
2. What is verstehen?
3. Give one strength and one limitation of a longitudinal study.
4. Explain the interviewer effect with an example.
5. Why might a case study have low generalisability?

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name sociologists in 0495 methods answers?** Not usually, but a well-known example
such as Durkheim's study of suicide can strengthen an explanation of positivism.

**Which approach should I choose in Question 1(e)?** Either, if your methods and sampling fit it and
the research topic; explain the reasons for your choices.

**Is the Hawthorne effect the same as the observer effect?** The syllabus treats them together:
both describe people changing behaviour because they know they are being studied.
