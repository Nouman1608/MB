## What the specification lists

- Nature and purpose of investment appraisal.
- Calculate and interpret payback period, average rate of return (ARR) and net present value (NPV).
- Explain how quantitative and qualitative aspects affect a business.
- Evaluate the usefulness of the different methods.
- Recommend and justify investments using quantitative and qualitative appraisal.

## Core recall

### Purpose

Investment appraisal helps decide whether a project is worth its cost, or which of several is best. It matters because the money is spent now, the benefits come later and are uncertain, funds are limited and decisions are hard to reverse.

### The three methods

| Method | Steps | Decision rule |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | Add inflows year by year until the cost is recovered; add the fraction of the last year (still to recover ÷ that year's inflow) | Shorter is better; compare with a target |
| ARR | (Total inflows − cost) ÷ years ÷ cost × 100 | Higher is better; compare with a target return or the cost of finance |
| NPV | Multiply each inflow by its discount factor, add, subtract the cost | Accept if positive; the higher the better |

### Discounting

- Money now is worth more than the same money later.
- A higher discount rate gives lower present values and a lower NPV.
- Discount factors are usually supplied.
- NPV depends on the rate chosen.

### Strengths and weaknesses

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | Simple; focus on risk and cash | Ignores cash after payback and timing |
| ARR | Uses all flows; a percentage | Ignores timing |
| NPV | Time value of money; money value of benefit | Needs a rate; harder to explain |

### Qualitative factors

Strategic fit, risk and reliability of forecasts, staff, brand and customers, environment and ethics, flexibility, legal and external factors.

### Recommend and justify: five steps

1. Choose a project.
2. Use at least two measures.
3. Add qualitative points.
4. Comment on risk and reliability.
5. Give a judgement and what would change it.

## Quick worked reminder

Calder Print (invented): Project A, cost £120,000, inflows £30,000, £35,000, £45,000, £40,000, £30,000 (total £180,000). Payback 3 + 10,000 ÷ 40,000 = 3.25 years. ARR = 60,000 ÷ 5 ÷ 120,000 = 10%. NPV at 8% = £143,335 − £120,000 = +£23,335; at 12% = +£9,175. Project B (same cost, inflows £60,000 to £10,000) pays back in 2.33 years, ARR 8.3%, NPV at 8% +£23,740.

## Method checklist for each calculation

### Payback

1. List the cumulative inflow at the end of each year.
2. Find the last year in which the total is still below the cost.
3. Work out how much is still to recover at that point.
4. Divide that amount by the inflow of the next year.
5. Add the fraction to the whole years. State the answer in years and months if asked.

### ARR

1. Add all the inflows.
2. Subtract the initial cost to get the total net return.
3. Divide by the number of years to get the average annual return.
4. Divide by the initial cost and multiply by 100.

### NPV

1. Write a column of inflows and a column of discount factors.
2. Multiply each pair to get the present values.
3. Add the present values.
4. Subtract the initial cost.
5. State whether the project should be accepted, and say what rate you used.

## Writing the evaluation

- Compare the methods: say which project is best on each measure.
- Explain any disagreement. Payback and NPV favour early cash; ARR ignores timing.
- Bring in at least one qualitative factor tied to the case.
- State the risk: forecasts for later years are less reliable.
- Finish with a clear choice and what would change it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Investment | Spending now to earn benefits over several years |
| Payback period | Time taken to recover the initial cost |
| ARR | Average annual return as a percentage of the cost |
| NPV | Present value of inflows less the initial cost |
| Discount factor | A multiplier that converts a future sum to its present value |
| Discount rate | The rate used for discounting |
| Time value of money | A sum is worth more now than later |
| Cash inflow | Net cash received from the project in a period |

## Exam traps

- NPV: subtract the initial cost at the end.
- ARR: use the average annual return, not the total.
- Payback: do not forget the fraction of the final year.
- A positive NPV is an estimate, not a guarantee.
- Methods can disagree; say which you prefer and why.

## Self-test

1. State the purpose of investment appraisal.
2. A project costs £50,000 and returns £15,000 a year for five years. Calculate payback.
3. Calculate ARR for the same project.
4. Why is NPV considered more reliable than payback?
5. Give one weakness of ARR.
6. A year 1 inflow of £20,000 has a discount factor of 0.909. Calculate its present value.
7. Total present value is £86,320 and cost £80,000. Calculate NPV.
8. What does a negative NPV suggest?
9. What happens to NPV if the discount rate rises?
10. Give two qualitative factors.
11. Why is payback useful when cash is short?
12. Why might a business reject a project with a positive NPV?

### Answers

1. To decide whether an investment is worth its cost, or which of several projects is best.
2. £50,000 ÷ £15,000 = **3.33 years** (about 3 years and 4 months).
3. Total inflows £75,000; return £25,000; per year £5,000; £5,000 ÷ £50,000 × 100 = **10%**.
4. It takes account of the time value of money and uses all cash flows.
5. It ignores when the cash arrives (timing).
6. £20,000 × 0.909 = **£18,180**.
7. £86,320 − £80,000 = **+£6,320**.
8. The project returns less than the required rate, so it would destroy value.
9. It falls, because future cash is worth less in present terms.
10. Any two: strategic fit, risk, staff morale, brand, environmental impact, flexibility.
11. It shows how quickly the cash is recovered, so risk to liquidity is lower.
12. It may be too risky, need more cash than available, conflict with strategy or damage the brand or staff morale.
