This guide goes deeper into section 3.5 (Profitability and liquidity ratio analysis) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024. The section applies to SL and HL. You must calculate five ratios, interpret them, and judge strategies to improve them.

The broader [3.5 and 3.6 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-profitability-and-liquidity-ratio-analysis-and-efficiency-ratio-analysis-hl-only/) sets out the formulae with one-year examples. This page works through a two-year comparison, shows how the ratios explain each other, and tests strategies with numbers so that you can see their trade-offs. Companion pages: the [3.5 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-3-5-revision-notes/) and the [3.5 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-3-5-practice/). ## What section 3.5 asks for

| Content point | Level |
|---|---|
| Gross profit margin, profit margin and return on capital employed (ROCE) | SL and HL |

| Current ratio and acid test (quick) ratio | SL and HL |
| Possible strategies to improve these liquidity ratios | SL and HL |

## The five formulae

| Ratio | Formula | Source of the data |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | (gross profit ÷ sales revenue) × 100 | Statement of profit or loss |
| Profit margin | (profit before interest and tax ÷ sales revenue) × 100 | Statement of profit or loss |
| ROCE | (profit before interest and tax ÷ capital employed) × 100 | Both statements |
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities | Statement of financial position |
| Acid test (quick) ratio | (current assets − stock) ÷ current liabilities | Statement of financial position |

Capital employed = non-current liabilities + equity. According to the guide's appendix, candidates receive a copy of the formulae for the examination; knowing them well still saves time.

## A two-year analysis

### Data: Pinecrest Kitchens

Pinecrest Kitchens is an invented maker of fitted kitchens. All figures are invented and in dollars.

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 800,000 | 900,000 |
| Cost of sales | 480,000 | 558,000 |
| Expenses | 200,000 | 216,000 |
| Non-current liabilities | 200,000 | 300,000 |
| Equity | 400,000 | 420,000 |
| Stock | 90,000 | 150,000 |
| Debtors | 60,000 | 75,000 |
| Cash | 30,000 | 5,000 |
| Creditors | 100,000 | 130,000 |
| Overdraft | 20,000 | 50,000 |

### Step 1: build the subtotals

| Subtotal | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit (sales − cost of sales) | 320,000 | 342,000 |
| Profit before interest and tax (gross profit − expenses) | 120,000 | 126,000 |
| Capital employed | 600,000 | 720,000 |
| Current assets | 180,000 | 230,000 |
| Current liabilities | 120,000 | 180,000 |

### Step 2: calculate the ratios

| Ratio | Year 1 | Year 2 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 40.0% | 38.0% | −2.0 percentage points |
| Profit margin | 15.0% | 14.0% | −1.0 percentage point |
| ROCE | 20.0% | 17.5% | −2.5 percentage points |
| Current ratio | 1.50 | 1.28 | Down |
| Acid test ratio | 0.75 | 0.44 | Down sharply |

Sample working for Year 2: gross profit margin = 342,000 ÷ 900,000 × 100 = 38.0%. ROCE = 126,000 ÷ 720,000 × 100 = 17.5%. Acid test = (230,000 − 150,000) ÷ 180,000 = 0.44 (to two decimal places).

### Step 3: let the ratios explain each other

Good interpretation connects the ratios rather than commenting on each in isolation.

* **Gross profit margin fell by 2 points.** Cost of sales rose from 60% to 62% of revenue. Either input costs rose or prices were cut to win the extra sales.
* **Profit margin fell by only 1 point.** Expenses fell from 25.0% of revenue (200,000 ÷ 800,000) to 24.0% (216,000 ÷ 900,000), which partly offset the weaker gross margin. So overheads are not the main problem; the cost of sales is.
* **ROCE fell more than the margins.** Profit rose by only 5% (120,000 to 126,000), while capital employed rose by 20% (600,000 to 720,000) because of new long-term borrowing. The extra capital has not yet earned a matching return.
* **Liquidity weakened.** Stock jumped by 60,000 while cash fell to 5,000 and the overdraft grew. The acid test of 0.44 means that, without selling stock, Pinecrest holds 44 cents of quick assets for each dollar of short-term debt.

A strong conclusion ties these together: Pinecrest grew its sales, but growth was bought with lower margins, extra borrowing and a build-up of stock, and the cash position is now the most pressing concern.

## Testing strategies with numbers

The guide asks for "possible strategies". Testing a strategy on the case figures shows both its benefit and its risk.

### Profitability strategy: raise prices by 5%

If Pinecrest raises prices by 5% and sells the same number of kitchens in Year 2, sales revenue becomes 945,000 while cost of sales stays 558,000.

* Gross profit = 387,000, so gross profit margin = **41.0%** (to one decimal place).
* Profit before interest and tax = 387,000 − 216,000 = 171,000, so profit margin = **18.1%** and ROCE = 171,000 ÷ 720,000 × 100 = **23.8%**.

But demand may fall. If volume drops by 8%, revenue is 869,400 and cost of sales is 513,360. Gross profit margin stays 41.0% (price and unit cost are unchanged), but profit before interest and tax falls to 140,040, a profit margin of 16.1%. That is still above the Year 2 figure of 126,000. Profit would fall below 126,000 only if volume dropped by more than about 11.6%. Whether that is likely depends on how price-sensitive kitchen buyers are and on rivals' prices, which the case would need to show.

### Profitability strategy: reduce capital employed

ROCE can rise by lowering capital employed, for example by selling an unused non-current asset and repaying part of the long-term loan. The trade-off is lost capacity if the asset is needed later, and use of cash that the business may need for liquidity.

### Liquidity strategy: clear excess stock and cut the overdraft

Suppose Pinecrest sells 40,000 of surplus stock at cost and uses 30,000 of the cash to reduce its overdraft.

* Current assets: 230,000 − 40,000 (stock) + 40,000 (cash) − 30,000 (cash used) = 200,000.
* Current liabilities: 180,000 − 30,000 = 150,000.
* Current ratio = 200,000 ÷ 150,000 = **1.33**.
* Stock now 110,000, so acid test = (200,000 − 110,000) ÷ 150,000 = **0.60**.

Both ratios improve, and the acid test improves most because stock has been turned into cash. The risk: if the stock was held for confirmed orders, selling it could delay deliveries.

### Other liquidity strategies

* Collect debts faster, for example with early payment discounts (which cut revenue slightly).
* Negotiate longer credit from suppliers (which may harm relationships).
* Replace the overdraft with a long-term loan: current liabilities fall, but long-term debt and interest rise.
* Raise new share capital: cash rises without new debt, but ownership is diluted.

## How to interpret without a "correct" value

The guide does not set target values, and textbook rules of thumb vary by industry. A retailer selling for cash every day can operate with a low acid test; a manufacturer with long production times may need more. Judge a ratio against:

* the same business in earlier years (trend)
* similar businesses in the same industry
* the business's own objectives, such as rapid growth or financial safety

## Common mistakes

* Using profit after interest in the profit margin or ROCE.
* Forgetting to subtract stock in the acid test.
* Writing ratio changes as percentages when they are percentage points.
* Treating a higher current ratio as always better; idle stock and cash earn nothing.
* Suggesting a strategy that affects the wrong ratio, such as cutting rent to raise gross profit margin.
* Recommending a strategy without stating its risk in the case.

## Exam technique

* **Calculate**: show the formula, substitution and answer with the correct unit (% or ratio).
* **Comment** or **analyse**: compare with another year or business and explain the cause using case figures.
* **Evaluate** or **recommend** a strategy: test it on the numbers where possible, give its trade-off, and judge.
