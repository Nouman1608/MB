## What the guide states

- Students develop skills to organise, document and communicate their learning using a variety of media and presentation formats.
- Year 5 strands: (i) communicate information and ideas effectively using an appropriate style for the audience and purpose; (ii) structure information and ideas in a way that is appropriate to the specified format; (iii) document sources of information using a recognised convention.
- Maximum 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8.
- In the on-screen examination: Communicating task 12 marks for Criterion C, plus 8 marks in the Thinking critically task, so 20 of the 80 marks. Response types listed: creative writing, blog, article, letter, presentation, poster or infographic, speech.

| Band | Style | Structure | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | limited | limited | limited |
| 3-4 | satisfactory, somewhat appropriate | somewhat appropriate | sometimes documents |
| 5-6 | accurate, mostly appropriate | mostly appropriate | often documents |

## Three questions before you write

1. **Who** is the audience?
2. **Why** am I writing: inform, persuade, explain, narrate, argue or reflect?
3. **What** format has the task named, and what are its conventions?

Write the answers at the top of your plan.

## Style: formal against informal

| Feature | Formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|
| Words | precise subject terms | everyday words, terms explained |
| Sentences | longer, objective | shorter, direct |
| Person | third person | first and second person |
| Tone | measured, respectful | friendly, lively |
| Devices | evidence, linking words | questions, examples, humour |

Match the style to the audience: a council committee needs a formal tone, a class blog can be informal, a talk to younger children needs simple words and clear examples.

## Purpose words and what they need

- **Inform**: facts, clear order, neutral tone.
- **Persuade**: a clear position, evidence, emotional appeal used carefully, a call to action.
- **Explain**: reasons and causes, linking words such as "because" and "as a result".
- **Narrate or create**: setting, viewpoint, sequence and descriptive detail.
- **Argue**: claim, evidence, counter-argument, conclusion.

## Format conventions

| Format | Must have |
|---|---|
| Letter | address and date, greeting, purpose in the first paragraph, one idea per paragraph, closing request, correct sign-off |
| Article | headline, summary introduction, body in logical order, conclusion or quotation |
| Blog | title, personal voice, subheadings, direct address, closing thought |
| Speech | greeting, hook, signposts, rhetorical devices, strong ending |
| Presentation | title slide, one idea per slide, key words, visuals, summary and sources slides |
| Poster or infographic | title, headed sections, short facts, labelled visuals, sources |
| Creative writing | setting, viewpoint, sequence, descriptive detail, purposeful ending |

## Structuring ideas

- One main idea per paragraph.
- Topic sentence, evidence, link back to the task.
- Linking words: however, therefore, in contrast, for example, as a result.
- Plan the order of points first.
- Reread the instructions at the end and tick each requirement (length, audience, format).

## Documenting sources: the rules

- Choose one recognised convention and use it throughout.
- Cite in the text every time you use another person's fact, figure, quotation, image or idea.
- Put a reference list or bibliography at the end of the work with every source in it.
- Give the details the convention asks for, usually author or organisation, year, title, publisher or website, and date accessed.
- Order the list as the convention requires (often alphabetical).
- Quote briefly, use quotation marks, and cite. Paraphrase in your own words and cite.
- Check that each in-text citation matches an entry in the list and that each entry is cited.

### Example (invented sources)

In the text: "Cycling to school increased after the new lane opened (Okafor, 2023)."
Reference list: Okafor, T. (2023) *Cycling in small towns*. Nairobi: Savannah Books.

## Judging a source quickly

- **Origin**: who made it and when?
- **Purpose**: why was it made?
- **Evidence**: does it give data or sources?
- **Balance**: does it show more than one view?

A reliable source helps your argument. The questions are explored further in Criterion D.

## Command terms

| Term | Guide meaning |
|---|---|
| Communicate | (strand verb) present information and ideas to an audience |
| Document | credit sources by referencing or citing, following a recognised system, in the text and in a reference list or bibliography |
| Structure | (strand verb) organise information in a suitable order and layout |
| Summarize | abstract a general theme or major point(s) |
| Use | apply knowledge or rules to put theory into practice |

## Where marks are usually lost

- One style used for every task.
- The wrong register for the audience.
- Not following the format (no opening in a speech, no headings in a poster).
- Ideas not in a logical order.
- Citations missing in the text or in the list.
- Mixed referencing styles.
- Failing to meet instructions on length or audience.

## Quick self-test

1. A task asks for a speech to Year 7 about why a river needs protecting. Give two changes you would make compared with a report for local government.
2. Name three features of a good letter to a newspaper.
3. What two places must sources be credited?
4. A student quotes two lines from a website but writes no citation. What mark strand is affected?
5. Give one reason to use headings and short text on a poster.

### Answers

1. Simpler words and shorter sentences; direct address ("you") and vivid examples; a clear call to action; spoken devices such as repetition and signposts. (Any two.)
2. Any three of: address and date, greeting, clear purpose in the first paragraph, paragraphs with one idea each, a closing request, correct sign-off, formal tone.
3. In the text (in-text citation) and in the reference list or bibliography at the end.
4. Strand iii: documenting sources. The student also risks passing off another person's words as their own.
5. Readers scan posters quickly, so headings and short text make the main points easy to find.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to use a particular referencing style?**
No. The guide asks for a recognised convention. Use the one your school teaches and be consistent.

**Is length part of the criterion?**
The task instructions are part of strand ii (structure according to the format), so follow any length or format instruction.

**Can I mix styles in one piece?**
Keep the style consistent unless the task asks for a change, for example a quotation inside a formal report.
