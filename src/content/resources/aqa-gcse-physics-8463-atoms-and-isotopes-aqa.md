4.4.1.1 The structure of an atom, 4.4.1.2 Mass number, atomic number and isotopes, and 4.4.1.3 The development

## What the specification asks for

- Recall that atoms have a radius of about **1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m**, and that a nucleus has a radius **less than 1/10 000**
  of the atom's; recognise these expressions in standard form.
- Describe the basic structure: a positive nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by negative electrons, with
  most of the mass in the nucleus.
- Describe electron energy levels and how absorbing or emitting electromagnetic radiation changes them.
- Use atomic number and mass number; define isotopes; describe how atoms become positive ions.
- Relate differences between isotopes to differences in their conventional representations.
- Describe why the alpha scattering evidence changed the model of the atom, and the difference between the plum
  pudding and nuclear models.

## How small is an atom?

An atom's radius is about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m: one ten-billionth of a metre. The nucleus has a radius less than one
ten-thousandth of that.

**Worked example 1 (standard form).** A nucleus has a radius of 5 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. The atom has a radius of
1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. How many times larger is the atom's radius? Does this fit the specification's statement?

```
ratio = (1 × 10⁻¹⁰) ÷ (5 × 10⁻¹⁵)
      = 0.2 × 10⁵
      = 2 × 10⁴  = 20 000
```

The atom's radius is 20 000 times the nucleus's, so the nucleus radius is 1/20 000 of the atom's. This is less
than 1/10 000, as the specification states.

Yet **most of the mass** of the atom is in that tiny nucleus, because protons and neutrons are far more massive
than electrons. Almost all of the atom's volume is the space occupied by its electrons.

## Electrons and energy levels

Electrons are arranged at different distances from the nucleus, called **energy levels**. An electron can move
between levels:

- **Absorbing** electromagnetic radiation moves an electron **further from** the nucleus, to a **higher** energy
  level.
- **Emitting** electromagnetic radiation moves an electron **closer to** the nucleus, to a **lower** energy level.

The direction is easy to remember from energy: going up a level needs energy in, so radiation is absorbed; coming
down releases energy, so radiation is emitted.

## Atomic number, mass number and nuclear notation

| Quantity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Atomic number (Z) | Number of protons; the same for every atom of an element |
| Mass number (A) | Total number of protons and neutrons |
| Number of neutrons | Mass number − atomic number |
| Number of electrons in a neutral atom | Equal to the number of protons |

In nuclear notation the **mass number is written top left** and the **atomic number bottom left** of the
chemical symbol.

**Worked example 2.** Find the number of each particle in an atom of ⁵⁶₂₆Fe (iron-56).

```
protons   = atomic number          = 26
neutrons  = 56 − 26                = 30
electrons = protons (neutral atom) = 26
```

Because an atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, the positive and negative charges cancel and the atom
has **no overall charge**.

## Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with **different numbers of neutrons**. They have the same atomic
number (same number of protons) but different mass numbers.

**Worked example 3.** Compare the isotopes ¹²₆C and ¹⁴₆C.

| | ¹²₆C | ¹⁴₆C |
|---|---|---|
| Protons | 6 | 6 |
| Neutrons | 6 | 8 |
| Electrons (neutral atom) | 6 | 6 |
| Overall charge | 0 | 0 |
| Mass number | 12 | 14 |

The specification asks you to **relate differences between isotopes to their representations**. The bottom number
is the same (same element); the top number differs (different mass, because of extra neutrons). The charge is the
same, because neutrons have no charge.

## Ions

An atom becomes a **positive ion** if it **loses one or more outer electrons**. The number of protons stays the
same, so there is now more positive charge than negative.

**Worked example 4.** A magnesium atom (atomic number 12) loses two outer electrons. Describe the ion formed.

```
protons   = 12  (unchanged)
electrons = 12 − 2 = 10
charge    = +12 − 10 = +2   → a positive ion with charge 2+
```

Losing electrons never changes the element: the atomic number, set by the protons, stays the same.

## How the model of the atom developed

The model changed as new evidence appeared. This is how scientific models develop: a model is kept while it
explains the observations, and changed or replaced when new evidence contradicts it.

1. **Indivisible spheres.** Before the electron was discovered, atoms were thought to be tiny spheres that could
   not be divided.
2. **Plum pudding model.** The discovery of the electron showed atoms contain smaller, negative particles. The
   model became a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
3. **Nuclear model.** The alpha particle scattering experiment showed the mass of an atom is concentrated at the
   centre, in a nucleus, and that the nucleus is charged.
4. **Bohr's energy levels.** Niels Bohr adapted the nuclear model, suggesting electrons orbit the nucleus at
   specific distances. His theoretical calculations agreed with experimental observations.
5. **Protons.** Later experiments showed the positive charge of any nucleus could be divided into a whole number of
   smaller particles, each with the same positive charge: protons.
6. **Neutrons.** The experimental work of James Chadwick provided evidence for neutrons in the nucleus, about
   20 years after the nucleus became an accepted idea.

Details of the experiments behind Bohr's model and Chadwick's work are **not** required.

### Reasoning from the scattering evidence

Positively charged alpha particles were fired at very thin gold foil.

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Most passed straight through | Most of the atom is empty space |
| Some were deflected through small angles | There is a concentrated charge that repels alpha particles passing nearby |
| A very few bounced back | The mass and charge are concentrated in a tiny, dense centre |

The plum pudding model could not explain the bounce-back: a spread-out positive charge would be too weak at any
point to turn an alpha particle round. So the plum pudding model was replaced by the nuclear model.

## Common mistakes

- Saying mass number is the number of neutrons, or atomic number the number of neutrons.
- Giving isotopes different numbers of protons; then they would be different elements.
- Saying a positive ion has gained protons. It has lost electrons.
- Writing that electrons move closer to the nucleus when they absorb radiation.
- Saying the nucleus is "about 1/10 000" of the atom's radius. The specification says **less than**.
- In model-history questions, describing the plum pudding model as having a nucleus.

## Exam technique

- **Describe the difference** between two models: give a matching pair (where the positive charge is, where the
  mass is, whether there is empty space).
- **Explain why the model changed**: link one observation (alpha particles bouncing back) to the conclusion it
  forces (a tiny, dense, charged nucleus).
- **Calculate** particle numbers: show neutrons = mass number − atomic number.
- **Use standard form** carefully: divide the numbers and subtract the powers.
