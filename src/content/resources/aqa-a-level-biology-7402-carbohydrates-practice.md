## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Name the monosaccharides produced by the complete hydrolysis of (a) lactose and (b) sucrose. **[2]**

**2.** State the molecular formula of maltose and explain how it relates to the formula of glucose. **[2]**

**3.** In a diagram of glucose, the OH group on carbon 1 is above the plane of the ring. Name this isomer and the polysaccharide it forms. **[2]**

**4.** Name the type of glycosidic bond found at a branch point in amylopectin. **[1]**

**5.** A student heats a maltose solution with the enzyme maltase, then tests the product with Benedict's solution and gets a brick-red precipitate. She concludes that the maltose has been hydrolysed. Explain why this conclusion is not valid. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the enzyme amylase can hydrolyse starch but not cellulose. **[3]**

**7.** Describe how you would test a solution for starch, and give the result if starch is present. **[2]**

**8.** Muscle cells store glucose as glycogen. Explain why this is better than storing the same amount of glucose as free glucose molecules. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**9.** A student compared the reducing sugar content of four fruit juices by heating 2 cm³ of each with 5 cm³ of Benedict's solution in a water bath at 90 °C and recording the time until the first colour change.

| Juice | Time to first colour change (s) |
|---|---|
| W | 42 |
| X | 115 |
| Y | 68 |
| Z | No change after 300 |

**(a)** Rank the juices from highest to lowest reducing sugar concentration. **[1]**

**(b)** Give two variables, other than those already stated, that the student should control. **[2]**

**(c)** Juice Z may still contain sugar. Describe how the student could find out whether Z contains a non-reducing sugar. **[4]**

**10.** A technician made glucose standards, carried out the Benedict's test on each for the same time, then filtered, dried and weighed the precipitate.

| Glucose (g dm⁻³) | 0 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dry precipitate (mg) | 0 | 21 | 42 | 63 | 84 |

**(a)** An unknown solution gave 49 mg. Calculate its glucose concentration. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why Benedict's solution must be in excess in every tube. **[2]**

**(c)** Give one advantage of weighing the precipitate rather than judging its colour. **[1]**

**11.** Plant cells store starch and have cell walls made of cellulose. Both are polymers of glucose.

**(a)** Explain how the structure of amylose makes it suitable as an energy store. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how the structure of cellulose stops a plant cell bursting when water enters by osmosis. **[4]**

**12.** Glycogen is described as "more highly branched" than amylopectin. A student says this means glycogen contains more energy per gram. Evaluate this statement. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Glucose and galactose [1]. (b) Glucose and fructose [1]. **[2]**

**2.** C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ [1]; it is two glucose molecules (2 × C₆H₁₂O₆ = C₁₂H₂₄O₁₂) minus one water molecule removed in the condensation reaction [1]. **[2]**

**3.** β-glucose [1]; cellulose [1]. **[2]**

**4.** α-1,6 glycosidic bond [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Maltose is itself a reducing sugar [1], so it gives a positive Benedict's result whether or not it has been hydrolysed to glucose; the test cannot distinguish the two [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* A valid method would need a technique that separates maltose from glucose, such as chromatography with standards.

**6.** Amylase is specific: its active site is complementary to α-glycosidic bonds between α-glucose units [1]. Cellulose is made of β-glucose with alternate units inverted [1], so its bonds have a different shape that does not fit the active site, and no enzyme-substrate complex forms [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Add iodine in potassium iodide solution to the sample (no heating) [1]; it changes from yellow-brown to blue-black if starch is present [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Glycogen is insoluble, whereas free glucose is soluble [1]. Free glucose would lower the water potential of the cytoplasm so water would enter by osmosis [1]; glycogen does not affect water potential and is too large to diffuse out of the cell [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** W, Y, X, Z [1].

**(b)** Any two: same concentration of Benedict's solution; same size of tube or water-bath volume; same person judging the first colour change, or a colour standard; juices at the same starting temperature [1][1].

**(c)** Heat a fresh sample of Z with dilute hydrochloric acid [1]; neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate [1]; add Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath [1]; a green to brick-red precipitate shows a non-reducing sugar was present [1]. **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* The first test on Z is already negative, so the answer can go straight to hydrolysis. Leaving out neutralisation is the most common lost mark.

**10. (a)** Precipitate rises by 7 mg per 1 g dm⁻³ (21 ÷ 3) [1]. 49 ÷ 7 = **7.0 g dm⁻³** [1].

**(b)** If Benedict's solution ran out, extra sugar would produce no extra precipitate [1], so the mass would no longer be proportional to sugar concentration and higher concentrations would be underestimated [1].

**(c)** It gives a numerical (quantitative) result rather than a subjective judgement of colour [1]. **[5]**

**11. (a)** Amylose is a chain of α-glucose that coils into a helix [1], making it compact so a lot of glucose is stored in a small space [1]; it is insoluble, so it does not affect the water potential of the cell [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Cellulose is made of β-glucose, so alternate units are inverted and the chains are straight and unbranched [1]. Many hydrogen bonds form between parallel chains [1], which group into microfibrils with high tensile strength [1]. The wall resists the pressure of the expanding cell contents, so the cell does not burst [1]. **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers finish with the link to the cell not bursting. Stopping at "strong" leaves the question unanswered.

**12.** The statement is incorrect [1]. Both glycogen and amylopectin are made of α-glucose, so a gram of either releases a similar amount of glucose on hydrolysis [1]. Branching does not add energy; it creates more free ends [1], so enzymes can hydrolyse glucose from many ends at once, giving faster release, which suits animals with high metabolic rates [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a judgement. Say clearly whether the claim is right before explaining.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping the α and β positions on carbon 1.
- Naming cellulose bonds between chains as glycosidic rather than hydrogen bonds.
- Missing the water potential link when explaining storage.
- Forgetting that maltose and lactose are reducing sugars.
- Leaving out neutralisation in the non-reducing sugar test.
- Linking branching to energy content rather than rate of release.
