These notes cover strand 1 of geographical enquiry, "Suitable question for geographical enquiry", in section 3.3.2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3, together with the planning, background research and health and safety aspects on which exam questions can be set. For the whole unit, see the [Geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/); for full explanations and a worked plan, read the [enquiry planning study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-enquiry-planning/).

## Specification checklist (strand 1)

- "The factors that need to be considered when selecting suitable questions/hypotheses for geographical enquiry."
- "The geographical theory/concept underpinning the enquiry."
- "Appropriate sources of primary and secondary evidence, including locations for fieldwork."
- "The potential risks of both human and physical fieldwork and how these risks might be reduced."

Also examinable: defining aims, choosing a location, setting a hypothesis or question, background research from secondary sources (including the internet) **before** data collection, and health and safety.

## The sequence of enquiry

The specification's six strands run in order, and planning is the first: (1) suitable question → (2) selecting, measuring and recording data → (3) processing and presenting → (4) describing, analysing and explaining → (5) conclusions → (6) evaluation. A weak plan weakens every later strand, because the conclusion must answer the question the plan set.

## Where it is tested

- **Paper 3 Section B:** fieldwork in **unfamiliar** contexts.
- **Paper 3 Section C:** **your own** enquiry; you must identify its title.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aim | What the enquiry sets out to find out |
| Enquiry question | A question the data will answer |
| Hypothesis | A testable statement predicting a result |
| Sub-question | A smaller question building towards the main one |
| Variable | Something measured that can change, such as width or pedestrian count |
| Underpinning theory | The geographical idea that explains why a pattern is expected |
| Primary evidence | Collected first-hand by you or your group |
| Secondary evidence | Collected by someone else |
| Risk assessment | A list of hazards with their likelihood, seriousness and precautions |
| Bibliography | List of sources consulted and used |

## Suitability factors (the seven checks)

1. Geographical, linked to theory.
2. Testable, with measurable variables.
3. Specific: named place (and time).
4. Manageable in time and with equipment.
5. Safe and accessible.
6. Likely to show variation between sites.
7. Supported by secondary data.

## Theory and expected pattern

| Focus | Expected pattern |
|---|---|
| River channel | Width, depth, velocity increase downstream; sediment smaller and rounder |
| Town land use | Shops and offices at the centre; housing density falls outwards |
| Sand dunes | Vegetation cover and variety increase inland (succession) |
| Microclimate | Warmer over tarmac and between buildings than over grass |
| Environmental quality | Varies between inner and outer areas of a town |

## Choosing a location

Shows the feature clearly; has enough sites to show change; safe, legal, permitted; reachable in time; has secondary data.

## Background research sources

Large-scale maps; census and local authority data; GIS and satellite imagery; previous studies, local news, authority websites. Record them all for the bibliography.

## Risk and precaution pairs

| Risk | Precaution |
|---|---|
| Deep or fast water | Check levels; shallow sections only; never alone |
| Slippery rocks | Suitable footwear; careful footing |
| Incoming tide | Check tide times; set turn-back time |
| Falling rock at cliffs | Keep away from cliff foot |
| Traffic | Pavements, crossings, high-visibility clothing |
| Strangers | Pairs or groups; public places; identification |
| Weather | Forecast; water, sun protection, waterproofs |

## Condensed example

Section B-style question: "Suggest a suitable hypothesis for an enquiry into a beach, and explain why it is suitable."
- **Hypothesis:** "Pebble size decreases along the beach in the direction of longshore drift."
- **Why suitable:** pebble size is measurable with a ruler at regular sites; it links to the theory that attrition makes sediment smaller as it is transported; it is safe at low tide with tide times checked; there will be enough variation over a long beach.

## Exam traps

- Questions with nothing measurable.
- No theory, so no reason for the prediction.
- Section C answers with no named place or title.
- Risk with no matching precaution, or "be careful".
- Background research after the data collection.
- Plans too big for the time available.
- Confusing an aim with a hypothesis.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish an aim from a hypothesis.
2. Why must a hypothesis be testable?
3. Give four of the seven suitability checks.
4. Why does an enquiry need underpinning theory?
5. What pattern does dune succession predict?
6. Name three secondary sources for background research.
7. When should background research be done, according to the specification?
8. Give a risk and precaution for river fieldwork.
9. Give a risk and precaution for questionnaires in a town.
10. What must you identify at the start of Section C answers?
11. Improve this question: "What is the river like?"
12. Why should you check that a location is likely to show variation?

**Answers**

1. An aim states what the enquiry sets out to find out; a hypothesis is a testable statement predicting the result.
2. So the data can show whether it is supported or not.
3. Any four: geographical with theory; testable; specific; manageable; safe and accessible; likely to show variation; supported by secondary data.
4. It gives a reason to expect a pattern, which the hypothesis predicts and the conclusion tests.
5. Vegetation cover and variety increase inland as dunes become older and more stable.
6. Any three: maps; census data; local authority data; GIS; satellite imagery; previous studies; news reports; authority websites.
7. Before any data collection.
8. For example: deep water; work only in shallow sections and never alone.
9. For example: approaching strangers; work in pairs in public places.
10. The title of your individual enquiry.
11. For example: "Does the width of the (named) river increase with distance downstream?"
12. If all sites give the same result, there is no pattern to analyse.
