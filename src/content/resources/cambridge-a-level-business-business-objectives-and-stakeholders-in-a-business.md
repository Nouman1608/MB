## 1.4.1 Objectives by type of organisation

An **objective** is a specific goal that a business sets itself to achieve. The syllabus asks about three kinds of organisation.

- **Private-sector businesses** are owned by individuals or shareholders. Typical objectives are survival, profit, growth, market share, sales revenue and returns for owners.
- **Public-sector organisations** are owned and run by the state or local government. Typical objectives are providing a service to all citizens, keeping within a set budget, and meeting service standards. Profit is usually not the main aim.
- **Social enterprises** trade to meet a social or environmental purpose. They need revenue to survive, but they put most of their surplus back into the cause rather than paying it out to owners.

Objectives matter because they give direction, let managers judge success, support motivation, and keep departments pulling the same way. Without them, a decision has nothing to be tested against.

### Corporate social responsibility and the triple bottom line

**Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** means a business accepts responsibilities to society beyond making a profit. The **triple bottom line** judges performance on three sets of objectives: economic (financial), social and environmental. A business using it reports on all three, not profit alone.

### Mission statement, aims, objectives, strategy and tactics

These form a chain from the broad to the specific.

| Level | Meaning | Example (Sundrift Stationery) |
|---|---|---|
| Mission statement | The overall purpose and values | "Make good paper goods that last" |
| Aim | A broad long-term intention | Become the leading independent stationery brand in the region |
| Objective | A measurable goal | Raise sales revenue by 15% within 12 months |
| Strategy | The long-term plan to reach the objective | Expand into corporate gift orders |
| Tactics | Short-term actions that carry out the strategy | A launch discount for the first 20 corporate customers |

A common error is to treat a mission statement as a target. It has no number and no deadline.

## 1.4.2 Objectives and business decisions

### Stages of decision-making

The syllabus asks for the stages and the role of objectives in them. Textbooks describe the stages in slightly different ways, so this is a standard form: set objectives, gather information, identify options, choose an option, implement it, and review the outcome. Objectives matter at three points. They start the process, they act as the test when options are compared, and they are the benchmark in the final review.

### How objectives change over time

Objectives are not fixed. A business may change them because of:

- its stage of development (survival first, then growth or market share);
- a recession or a boom;
- new competitors or new technology;
- a change of owner, manager or law;
- pressure from stakeholders.

### Translating objectives into targets and budgets

A **target** breaks an objective into figures for a department, product or period. A **budget** is a financial plan setting the money available or expected.

**Worked example.** Sundrift Stationery had sales revenue of $480,000 last year. Its objective is a 15% rise within 12 months.

1. Revenue target: 480,000 × 1.15 = 552,000.
2. Quarterly targets, using the seasonal pattern of 20%, 20%, 25% and 35%: 110,400, 110,400, 138,000 and 193,200. These add back to 552,000.
3. A marketing budget set at 4% of the target: 0.04 × 552,000 = 22,080.

The managers now have figures they can be held to, and the review stage can compare actual results with them.

### Communicating objectives and the effect on the workforce

Employees can only work towards objectives they know about. Clear communication gives direction and can raise motivation, particularly if staff help shape the targets. Poor communication causes confusion, and unrealistic targets can cause stress, resentment or resignations. If an objective means job losses or a new way of working, staff are likely to resist unless the reasons are explained.

### SMART objectives

A SMART objective is **specific, measurable, achievable, realistic** and **time-limited**. "Become the best" fails every test. "Increase sales revenue from the school market by 8% by the end of the next financial year" passes. Be ready to say which element of a given objective is missing, and note that "achievable" and "realistic" overlap, so use the context to separate them: achievable concerns resources, realistic concerns whether the target is sensible in the market.

### Ethics

**Ethics** are the moral principles that guide behaviour. They can shape objectives, for example by rejecting profit from unsafe products, and they can shape activities, such as fair pay for suppliers. An ethical stance may raise costs or limit sales in the short term. It can also build reputation and customer loyalty.

## 1.5.1 Business stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is an individual or group with an interest in the activities of a business. Shareholders are one group among many.

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Internal (inside the business) | Owners and shareholders, directors and managers, employees |
| External (outside it) | Customers, suppliers, lenders, government, local community, pressure groups |

Owners can be classed as internal because they work in or control the business. Some books place shareholders of a large company outside day-to-day operations, so say how you are classing them if the context is unclear.

### Roles, rights and responsibilities

| Stakeholder | Role | Rights | Responsibilities |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employees | Produce and serve | Safe conditions, agreed pay, fair treatment | Work to the contract, follow safety rules |
| Shareholders | Provide capital and own the company | Vote at meetings, share in profits through dividends | Accept the risk of loss |
| Suppliers | Provide inputs | Payment on agreed terms | Deliver goods of agreed quality on time |
| Customers | Buy and give revenue | Safe, accurately described goods | Pay the agreed price |
| Government | Sets rules and collects tax | Taxes owed, laws obeyed | Apply the law fairly |
| Local community | Hosts the business | A reasonable environment | Support lawful activity |

Keep legal rights general. Name the right, not a statute.

## 1.5.2 Relative importance, influence and accountability

Stakeholders differ in their **influence** (power to affect the decision) and in how much a decision affects them. A major lender, a large customer or the government usually has more influence than a small community group, though a well-organised campaign can change that.

### Impact of decisions and reactions

For any decision, ask who gains, who loses, and what each group can do about it. Reactions range from support, to complaints, to withdrawing something the business needs: labour, custom, credit or supply.

### Impact of stakeholder aims on decisions

Stakeholders also shape decisions before they are made. Lenders may refuse finance for a risky plan, and customers' tastes decide which products are worth launching.

### Accountability

A business is **accountable** when it explains and justifies its actions to those affected. It needs to be, because stakeholders hold things it relies on: investors want clear reporting, customers want honest claims, and trust, once lost, is hard to rebuild. Annual reports, meetings and consultation are common ways to do it.

### Conflict

Conflict arises because aims differ. Higher pay for staff reduces profit; lower prices for customers reduce margins; a supplier wants higher prices than the business wants to pay.

### Changing objectives and the effect on stakeholders

**Worked example.** Ravensley Transit Authority is a local government body whose objective was low fares for all. Pressure on public funds leads it to adopt a new objective: cover 80% of running costs from fares.

- Passengers on low incomes face higher fares and may react by using the service less.
- Employees may face tougher efficiency targets, but jobs could be safer if the service is financially stable.
- Local taxpayers pay less, so they probably welcome the change.
- Local shops may lose customers if passenger numbers fall.

A strong answer weighs these groups against each other and says which matters most in this case.

## A third type: a social enterprise in practice

Brightstitch Cooperative repairs and resells school uniforms and puts its surplus into free uniforms for low-income families. Its mission is social, its financial objective is to break even, and its environmental objective is less textile waste. Its stakeholders therefore care about the triple bottom line, not profit alone. Use this model when asked how objectives differ by sector.

## Common errors

- Using "stakeholder" and "shareholder" as if they mean the same thing.
- Listing stakeholders without saying how a decision affects each.
- Calling any aim a SMART objective without checking for a deadline and a measure.
- Writing that all stakeholders can be satisfied at once.
- Ignoring the context: the same decision has different effects in a small firm and a large one.
