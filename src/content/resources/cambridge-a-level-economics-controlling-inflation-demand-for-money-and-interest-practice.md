## Questions

**1.** State the three motives for holding money in Keynes's liquidity preference theory. **[3]**

**2.** A bond pays a fixed 12 cu a year for ever.

**(a)** Calculate its price when the market interest rate is 6%, and when it is 8%. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the price when the rate rises from 6% to 8%. **[2]**

**3.** In Country A the demand for money is 160 − 10r, where r is the interest rate in percent. The money supply is 100.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium interest rate. **[2]**
**(b)** The central bank raises the money supply to 120. Find the new rate. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a rise in the money supply might fail to lower the rate in a liquidity trap. **[2]**

**4.** In Country B the demand for loanable funds is 140 − 8r and the supply is 20 + 4r, in billions of cu.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium rate and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Government borrowing adds 24 to demand at every rate. Find the new rate and quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what happens to private borrowing. **[2]**

**5.** The marginal propensity to withdraw in Country C is 0.25. The government cuts its spending by 5 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in AD. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the fall in the price level may be smaller than the fall in AD. **[2]**

**6.** Inflation in Country D is 7%. The policy interest rate is 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the approximate real interest rate. **[1]**
**(b)** The rate is raised to 9%. Calculate the new approximate real rate. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why a rise to 7% would not tighten policy. **[2]**

**7.** A firm in Country E imports a component priced at 60 foreign units (fu). The exchange rate is 0.75 fu per cu, then rises to 0.80 fu per cu.

**(a)** Calculate the cu price of the component at each rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the price. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one drawback of relying on a stronger currency to reduce inflation. **[2]**

**8.** In Country A wages rise by 6% and output per worker by 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in unit labour costs. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain whether demand-side or supply-side policy is better suited to this cost pressure. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why the demand for money slopes down against the interest rate. **[4]**

**10.** Inflation in Country G is 8%. Analysts say about half comes from dearer imported fuel and half from strong consumer demand. The central bank is considering a rise in its policy rate. Evaluate this policy. **[8]**

**11.** In Country H households raise their saving and the central bank raises the money supply.

**(a)** Using loanable funds theory, explain the effect of higher saving on the interest rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Using Keynesian theory, explain the effect of the larger money supply. **[2]**
**(c)** Assess whether the interest rate must fall. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Transactions [1], precautionary [1], speculative [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the motives and avoid a vague label such as "spending". Credit goes to precise terms.

**2. (a)** Price = coupon ÷ rate. At 6%: 12 ÷ 0.06 = 200 cu [1]. At 8%: 12 ÷ 0.08 = 150 cu [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Change = 150 − 200 = −50 [1]. As a percentage of 200: **−25%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A percentage change needs the starting price as the base. Using 150 as the base is a common slip.

**3. (a)** 160 − 10r = 100 [1], so **r = 6%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 160 − 10r = 120 [1], so **r = 4%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At a very low rate, bond prices are high and people expect them to fall [1], so extra money is held in the demand for money and the rate does not fall further [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the equation set equal before the answer. Part (c) needs the reason people hold the extra money, not only the term "liquidity trap".

**4. (a)** 140 − 8r = 20 + 4r [1], so 120 = 12r: **r = 10%, quantity 60** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 164 − 8r = 20 + 4r [1], so 144 = 12r: **r = 12%, quantity 68** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The higher rate reduces private demand for funds [1]: at 12% private demand is 140 − 96 = 44, down from 60, so private borrowing falls by 16 (crowding out) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate private and government borrowing in part (c). Total borrowing rises while private borrowing falls.

**5. (a)** 1 ÷ 0.25 = **4** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 5 × 4 = a fall of **20 cu** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** If the economy has spare capacity, the short-run aggregate supply curve is flat [1], so most of the fall in AD reduces real output and employment and little reduces prices [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the price level to the shape of SRAS. A general claim that prices are sticky earns less than a stated chain.

**6. (a)** 5 − 7 = **−2%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 9 − 7 = **+2%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** At 7% the real rate would be 0% [1], so borrowing would still cost nothing in real terms and spending would not be discouraged [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction, and quote the real rate as a signed number with its unit.

**7. (a)** 60 ÷ 0.75 = **80 cu** [1]; 60 ÷ 0.80 = **75 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (75 − 80) ÷ 80 = **−6.25%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Exports become dearer in foreign currency, so export demand falls [1], which reduces net exports and can cut output and employment [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the exchange rate when the rate is quoted as foreign units per cu. Check the direction against common sense.

**8. (a)** 1.06 ÷ 1.03 = 1.0291 [1], so unit labour costs rise by **2.91%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The cause is a cost rise, shifting SRAS left [1]. Lower AD would reduce output and employment but not unit costs [1]. Supply-side policy raising productivity attacks the cause, though it is slow [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the policy to the cause named in the data. A judgement that ignores the figures reads as generic.

**9.** Speculative demand falls as the interest rate rises [1]. Bond price is inversely related to the rate [1]. At high rates bond prices are low and people expect them to rise, so they hold bonds and less money [1]. At low rates prices are high and people expect a fall, so they hold more money [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each step needs the link before the conclusion. Describing the slope without the bond price reasoning leaves the explanation incomplete.

**10.** Higher rates make borrowing dearer and saving more rewarding [1], so investment and consumption fall and AD shifts left, with a multiplier effect [1]. This helps against the demand-pull half [1]. It does little against imported fuel costs, which are cost-push and set abroad [1]. There are time lags before spending responds [1]. The cost is slower growth and higher unemployment [1]. A stronger currency, or supply-side measures, may help with imported costs [1]. Judgement: a rate rise is partly effective, suited to the demand half, and the case for it improves if the economy is near full capacity and expectations of inflation are falling [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Split the case into the two causes and judge the policy against each. A balanced answer reaches a conclusion that follows from that split.

**11. (a)** Saving shifts the supply of loanable funds right [1], so the equilibrium rate falls [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The vertical money supply shifts right [1], so at the old rate there is excess supply of money and the rate falls [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Both theories point to a lower rate [1]. In a liquidity trap the rate cannot fall further [1]. Higher expected inflation, or a government deficit raising demand for funds, would push the rate up [1]. So the rate falls unless those offsetting factors are large [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State both predictions first, then test them. Credit tends to go to answers that name a specific offsetting factor.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a ratio such as a multiplier without stating what it is the reciprocal of.
- Taking the percentage change in bond price from the wrong base.
- Describing policy effects without naming the curve that shifts.
- Using nominal interest rates where the real rate drives decisions.
- Giving evaluation that is not linked to the causes of inflation in the data.
- Treating the bond price and the interest rate as moving together.
- Confusing a movement along the demand for money curve with a shift.
- Mixing up loanable funds and liquidity preference in one explanation.
- Ending an evaluation with no judgement.
