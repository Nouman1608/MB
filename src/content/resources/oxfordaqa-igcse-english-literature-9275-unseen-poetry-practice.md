## Section A: short skills questions

Read Poem A, then answer Questions 1 to 6.

> **Poem A: Exam Hall, June**
>
> Two hundred desks in rows like a harvest,
> and every head bent the same way, as if
> listening for rain. The clock is loud.
> The clock is louder.
> Somewhere a pen begins to run
> and the rest of us chase it.

**1.** What is the situation described in the poem? Answer in one sentence. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the effect of the simile "in rows like a harvest". **[3]**

**3.** How does the poet use repetition in lines 3 and 4 to create tension? **[4]**

**4.** Identify one example of enjambment and explain its effect. **[3]**

**5.** How does the final image, "a pen begins to run / and the rest of us chase it", present the
speaker's feelings? **[4]**

**6.** The speaker uses "us" in the final line. What does this suggest? **[2]**

**7.** Explain why you should always comment on the ending of an unseen poem. **[2]**

## Section B: exam-style questions

Read Poem B, then answer Question 8.

> **Poem B: Ironing**
>
> My father ironed on Sundays, badly,
> the shirts my mother used to press,
> the creases crossing like two roads
> that never meet a town.
>
> He hummed a tune I didn't know,
> stopped, began it over,
> as though there were a right way in
> and he could find it if he tried.
>
> The kitchen smelled of steam and starch.
> He held each shirt up to the window,
> checking, the way you'd check a letter
> for the line you'd missed.
>
> I never told him they were crooked.
> I wear them still.

**8.** How does the poet present the father's grief in *Ironing*? **[30]**

Read Poem C, then answer Question 9.

> **Poem C: Monsoon Market**
>
> The first drops hit the tin roofs like applause
> and the whole market answers:
> awnings thrown open, crates dragged in,
> a man with a radio shouting the price of mangoes
> over a song about the sea.
>
> Children stand where the gutters overflow
> and let the water have their feet.
> Their mothers call them, not too hard.
>
> By evening the street is a river of lamps,
> each stall a small boat, lit and floating,
> and no one is in any hurry to be dry.

**9.** Explore how the poet presents the arrival of the rain in *Monsoon Market*. **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The speaker describes sitting in a crowded exam hall at the start of an exam [1].

**2.** Up to three marks. The simile compares the desks to rows of crops [1], suggesting uniformity
and order, as if the students are all the same [1]; "harvest" may also hint that they are being
gathered in or judged, linking to the pressure of the exam [1].

**3.** Up to four marks. Repeating "The clock is loud. / The clock is louder." creates a sense of
growing pressure [1]. The comparative "louder" shows the sound increasing in the speaker's mind,
not in reality [1]. The short, end-stopped sentences mimic the ticking of the clock [1], and placing
the second on a line of its own isolates it and makes the anxiety feel intense [1].

**4.** One mark for a correct example and up to two for effect. For example, "as if / listening for
rain" [1]: the break after "as if" makes the reader wait, mirroring the students' anxious waiting [1],
and the image of listening for rain suggests a hush or expectation of something about to arrive [1].
Credit "begins to run / and the rest of us chase it" with a valid explanation.

**5.** Up to four marks. The metaphor of a pen that "begins to run" suggests the first student to
start writing sets off a race [1]. "Chase" presents the speaker and others as anxious followers,
afraid of being left behind [1]. The image is slightly comic, which may lighten the tension [1], but
it also shows how pressure spreads through the room [1].

**6.** Up to two marks. "Us" includes the speaker among the other students [1], suggesting a shared
experience of anxiety, so the reader may recognise the feeling too [1].

**7.** Any two: endings often resolve or change the poem's meaning [1]; they reveal the final attitude
or tone [1]; commenting on them shows you have followed the whole poem's development [1].

### Section B

These questions are marked holistically against level descriptors, not by adding up points. The
indicative content below shows what a high-level response might include; other valid readings should
be credited.

**8. *Ironing*.**

AO1 (ideas and interpretation):

- The poem presents grief indirectly: the mother's absence is implied by "used to press", never
  stated.
- The father's grief appears as an effort to continue her routine, and the speaker's love appears in
  her silence and in still wearing the shirts.

AO2 (methods):

- **"badly"**, placed after a comma at the end of the first line, is honest and affectionate; it
  sets up the father's struggle.
- **The simile** of creases "crossing like two roads / that never meet a town" suggests directions
  that lead nowhere, reflecting his loss of purpose; the enjambment delays the disappointing
  "never meet a town".
- **The broken tune** ("stopped, began it over") shows his attempts to find a "right way in", which
  could suggest a way back into normal life or into grief itself.
- **Sensory detail** ("steam and starch") makes the domestic scene vivid and intimate.
- **The letter simile** in stanza 3 presents each shirt as a message he checks for "the line you'd
  missed", hinting at regret or things left unsaid.
- **Structure:** three quatrains move from the father's actions to the final couplet, which shifts to
  the speaker. The short, plain final lines carry the strongest emotion. "I wear them still" turns his
  imperfect effort into something treasured.

AO3 (personal response):

- A personal reading of whether the poem is mainly about the father's grief, the child's, or both,
  supported by evidence and expressed with accurate terminology.

Level guidance: a lower-level answer paraphrases the poem with some identified features; a mid-level
answer clearly explains the effects of several methods; a top-level answer explores how small
domestic details build a picture of grief that is never named, and analyses the shift in the final
couplet.

**9. *Monsoon Market*.**

AO1:

- The rain is presented as a joyful, communal event, not a nuisance: it brings the market to life.
- The poem moves from sudden arrival to evening calm, presenting rain as transformation.

AO2:

- **The opening simile** "like applause" makes the rain a performance that the market welcomes;
  "answers" personifies the market as responding to it.
- **Listing and energetic verbs** ("thrown open", "dragged in", "shouting") create noise and movement.
- **Juxtaposition** of the price of mangoes with "a song about the sea" blends everyday trade with
  something larger and dreamlike.
- **The children** who "let the water have their feet" show pleasure and surrender; "not too hard"
  suggests the adults share their delight.
- **The final stanza's metaphors**: the street as "a river of lamps", stalls as "small boat[s]", turn
  the market into something magical.
- **Structure:** three stanzas move from sound and bustle to quiet light, and the final line, "no one
  is in any hurry to be dry", ends on contentment.

AO3:

- A personal judgement, for example that the poem celebrates community and the pleasure of a shared
  moment, with tentative alternative readings.

Level guidance: a lower-level answer lists images of rain; a mid-level answer explains several
methods and their effects; a top-level answer explores how the poem's structure moves from noise to
stillness and why the poet presents rain as a gift rather than a threat.

## How to use this page

Use Section A as a 20-minute warm-up. Then answer Question 8 or 9 in 45 minutes without looking at the
answers. Compare your work with the indicative content, and note which methods you missed. On Route A,
practise moving straight on to an unseen prose task afterwards, so you rehearse the full Paper 2a
sequence.
