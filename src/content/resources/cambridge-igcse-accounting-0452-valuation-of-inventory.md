## What 4.5 covers

The syllabus lists three points:

1. the valuation of inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value
2. how to calculate the value of inventory
3. the effect of an incorrect valuation of inventory on gross profit, profit for
   the year, equity and asset valuation

## The rule in one line

Each item, or group of identical items, is valued at the **lower of cost and net
realisable value (NRV)**, where NRV is the expected selling price less any costs
still to be paid to make the sale. Prudence means an expected loss is recognised
as soon as it is foreseen; realisation means an expected profit is not recognised
until the goods are sold. Together they mean inventory can be written **down**
below cost but never **up** above it.

NRV falls below cost for four common reasons, and an exam scenario usually
signals one of them:

- **damage**, so the goods need repair or can only be sold at a reduced price
- **obsolescence**, for example last season's model or a discontinued design
- **a fall in selling price** in the market
- **high selling costs**, such as commission or delivery to a remote customer

## Worked example 1: valuing a mixed inventory

Halden Sports counted its inventory at 31 December 2028.

| Item | Units | Cost per unit | Information | NRV per unit | Value used |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Footballs | 120 | 9.50 | sell at 16.00, commission 1.20 | 14.80 | cost: 120 × 9.50 = 1,140 |
| Tennis rackets (old model) | 25 | 62.00 | reduced price 70.00, restringing 6.00, delivery 4.00 | 60.00 | NRV: 25 × 60.00 = 1,500 |
| Treadmills (damaged) | 3 | 480.00 | sell at 420.00 after repair costing 75.00 | 345.00 | NRV: 3 × 345.00 = 1,035 |
| Team shirts (obsolete design) | 200 | 7.00 | sell as scrap at 1.50, collection 0.30 | 1.20 | NRV: 200 × 1.20 = 240 |
| **Total** | | | | | **3,915** |

At cost the inventory would be 1,140 + 1,550 + 1,440 + 1,400 = 5,530. The
write-down is therefore **1,615**:

```
Rackets     25 × (62.00 − 60.00)     =    50
Treadmills   3 × (480.00 − 345.00)   =   405
Shirts     200 × (7.00 − 1.20)       = 1,160
Total write-down                       1,615
```

Notice that the footballs' NRV (14.80) is well above cost but adds nothing to the
value. Only losses are recognised.

## Worked example 2: where the write-down goes

Halden's other figures for 2028 were revenue 61,000, opening inventory 4,200 and
purchases 38,600. Compare the cost of sales before and after applying the rule.

```
                                At cost        Lower of cost and NRV
Revenue                          61,000             61,000
Opening inventory                 4,200              4,200
Purchases                        38,600             38,600
                                 42,800             42,800
Closing inventory                (5,530)            (3,915)
Cost of sales                    37,270             38,885
Gross profit                     23,730             22,115
```

The write-down of 1,615 is not entered as a separate expense: it is built into
cost of sales through the lower closing inventory. Gross profit, profit for the
year, equity and the inventory shown under current assets are all 1,615 lower
than they would have been at cost.

## How an error travels

```
Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory
```

- Closing inventory **overstated** → cost of sales understated → gross profit
  and profit for the year overstated → equity overstated → current assets
  overstated.
- Opening inventory **overstated** → cost of sales overstated → gross profit and
  profit for the year **understated**. Opening inventory is not in this year's
  statement of financial position, so this year's inventory asset is unaffected.

| Error | Gross profit | Profit for the year | Inventory asset at year end | Equity at year end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Closing overstated | Overstated | Overstated | Overstated | Overstated |
| Closing understated | Understated | Understated | Understated | Understated |
| Opening overstated | Understated | Understated | Not affected | Not affected* |
| Opening understated | Overstated | Overstated | Not affected | Not affected* |

\*An opening error came from last year's closing figure, so last year's profit and
opening equity were already wrong in the other direction. This year's profit
error reverses it, and closing equity ends up correct, provided this year's
closing inventory is right.

## Worked example 3: opening and closing both wrong

Tolland Hardware's draft results for 2028 show gross profit 31,400, profit for the
year 12,600, closing inventory 16,800 and closing equity 58,000. An investigation
finds:

1. Opening inventory at 1 January 2028 was **overstated by 1,500**: last year's
   count included goods already sold and invoiced.
2. Closing inventory at 31 December 2028 was **understated by 2,200**: a storeroom
   was missed.

Both errors made cost of sales too high:

```
Cost of sales overstated by     1,500 + 2,200 = 3,700
Corrected gross profit          31,400 + 3,700 = 35,100
Corrected profit for the year   12,600 + 3,700 = 16,300
Corrected closing inventory     16,800 + 2,200 = 19,000
```

Equity needs more care. Last year's profit was overstated by 1,500, so opening
equity was 1,500 too high. This year's profit is 3,700 too low.

```
Closing equity: 58,000 − 1,500 + 3,700 = 60,200
```

The net change of 2,200 equals the closing inventory error, which is a useful
check: at any year end, equity is wrong by exactly the amount the closing
inventory is wrong.

## Effects on ratios

Because inventory errors move gross profit, they change the gross profit margin
and mark-up. A closing overstatement also raises current assets, so the current
ratio looks stronger. The acid test is not affected, because it excludes inventory.
These links are tested in
[Interpretation of accounting ratios](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-interpretation-of-accounting-ratios/).

## Common mistakes

- Valuing the whole inventory at total cost or total NRV instead of item by item.
- Raising an item to NRV because NRV is above cost.
- Deducting repair costs from cost instead of from the selling price.
- Showing a write-down as an extra expense **and** reducing closing inventory,
  so the loss is counted twice.
- Saying an opening inventory error affects this year's inventory asset.
- Forgetting that an opening error also affected last year's profit and so
  this year's opening equity.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show NRV per unit, state which figure is lower, then multiply.
  A short table earns method marks even if one figure slips.
- **State the effect:** give direction **and** amount for each measure the
  question names, for example "gross profit overstated by 2,200".
- **Explain:** follow the chain from inventory to cost of sales to gross profit to
  profit for the year to equity.
- **Prepare** a corrected statement: rework cost of sales in full rather than
  adjusting gross profit by a guess.
