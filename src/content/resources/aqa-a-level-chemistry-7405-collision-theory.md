## The model

Chemical reactions involve breaking bonds in the reactants and making new bonds in the products. For this to happen, particles must first **collide**. Collision theory says:

1. Particles must **collide**.
2. The collision must have **energy equal to or greater than the activation energy**.

A collision that meets both conditions is called a **successful collision** (an effective collision). Only successful collisions produce products.

In practice the particles must also meet in a suitable orientation, so that the reacting parts of the molecules touch. The specification focuses on the energy condition, and this guide does the same, but orientation is one reason why many collisions with enough energy still fail.

## Activation energy

**Activation energy, Eₐ, is the minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur.**

The energy is needed to start breaking bonds. The particles lose kinetic energy as they approach each other and their electron clouds repel. If the kinetic energy is below the activation energy the particles simply bounce apart. If it is above, the bonds in the reactants stretch enough for new bonds to form.

### On an enthalpy profile

A reaction profile has enthalpy on the vertical axis and progress of reaction on the horizontal axis. The curve rises from the level of the reactants to a peak and then falls to the level of the products.

- The height from the reactants to the peak is the **activation energy**.
- The difference between the levels of reactants and products is the **enthalpy change**, ΔH.

These are different quantities. In an exothermic reaction the products are lower than the reactants and ΔH is negative, but the activation energy is still positive: energy must be supplied before energy is released.

```
 enthalpy
   ▲          ____
   │         /    \       peak: activated state
   │ ______ /      \
   │ reactants      \______ products (exothermic)
   │   Eₐ ↑           ΔH ↓
   └──────────────────────► progress of reaction
```

## Why most collisions do not lead to reaction

In a gas at room temperature the particles collide billions of times every second, but the reaction rate is far slower than the collision rate. The reason is that **only a tiny fraction of collisions involve particles with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy.**

The particles in a gas have a wide range of energies. Most have energy well below the activation energy. When they collide, they bounce off each other without reacting. The more demanding the reaction (the higher the Eₐ), the smaller the fraction that react.

### Worked example 1: a mixture that does not react

A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen can be stored at room temperature for years without any visible change, although the gases have energy and collide constantly. Explain.

The reaction between hydrogen and oxygen has a high activation energy, because the strong H–H and O=O bonds must be broken. At room temperature almost none of the collisions have enough energy to overcome this. A spark gives local particles the energy to react, and the reaction then releases heat, which supplies the activation energy for more molecules.

### Worked example 2: comparing two reactions

Reaction P has an activation energy of 40 kJ mol⁻¹ and reaction Q has 120 kJ mol⁻¹. Both are carried out at the same temperature and concentration. Which is faster?

At the same temperature the particles have the same distribution of energies. A larger fraction of collisions have energy of at least 40 kJ mol⁻¹ than of at least 120 kJ mol⁻¹, so reaction P has more successful collisions per second and is faster.

### Worked example 3: explaining a slow reaction

Explain why two reactions with the same collision frequency can have different rates.

Rate depends on the frequency of **successful** collisions, not of all collisions. If one reaction has a higher activation energy, a smaller fraction of its collisions are successful, so its rate is lower even though the total collision frequency is the same.

## Rate and collisions

The rate of reaction depends on the **frequency of successful collisions**. It can be increased by:

- increasing the **frequency of collisions** (higher concentration, higher pressure for gases, larger surface area, higher temperature), or
- increasing the **proportion of collisions that are successful** (higher temperature, or a catalyst that lowers the activation energy).

The later subtopics expand on each factor. The key to the distinction is that a change in concentration changes how often collisions happen, but not the energy of each, while a change of temperature changes both the frequency and the energy.

## Writing a full-mark answer

A model answer to "explain why most collisions do not lead to reaction":

*"Only collisions in which the particles have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy can lead to reaction. Most particles have energy lower than the activation energy, so when they collide they do not react."*

Include "equal to or greater than" or "at least", the term "activation energy", and the idea that **most particles have less than this**.

## Common mistakes

- Defining activation energy as "the energy needed to start a reaction" without saying "minimum" and "colliding particles".
- Confusing activation energy with the enthalpy change on a profile.
- Saying that most collisions fail because the particles "do not collide" (collisions are very frequent).
- Writing that collisions need energy "greater than" the activation energy, instead of "equal to or greater than".
- Saying that a catalyst changes the energy of the particles.
- Describing activation energy as the energy released.
- Writing "the particles do not have enough activation energy".

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define** activation energy: give "minimum energy", "colliding particles", "for a reaction to occur".
- **Explain** why a reaction is slow: link low rate to a small proportion of collisions with energy at least equal to Eₐ.
- **Sketch** a profile: label the axes, reactants, products, Eₐ and ΔH.
- **Suggest** why particles do not react: state that their energy is less than Eₐ.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a reaction with a large negative ΔH always happen quickly?**
No. A reaction can be strongly exothermic yet slow, because the activation energy is high.

**Is activation energy per particle or per mole?**
It is given per mole, in kJ mol⁻¹, in this course.

**Why does a spark start a reaction that then continues?**
The spark supplies energy locally. The heat released by the reaction raises the energy of neighbouring molecules, so the reaction spreads.
