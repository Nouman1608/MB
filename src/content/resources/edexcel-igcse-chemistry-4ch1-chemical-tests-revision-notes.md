## The answer pattern for every test

> **Reagent** (named in full) → **method** (warm, damp paper, bubble through) → **observation** (with a colour or a sound).

"Precipitate" with no colour scores nothing. "Sulfate present" is a conclusion, not an observation.

## 2.44 Gas tests

| Gas | Do this | See this |
|---|---|---|
| H₂ | Lighted splint at mouth of tube | Squeaky pop |
| O₂ | Glowing splint into gas | Relights |
| CO₂ | Bubble through limewater | Limewater turns milky |
| NH₃ | Damp red litmus paper | Turns blue |
| Cl₂ | Damp litmus paper | Bleached white |

Limewater equation:

```
Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)
                         (the white solid that makes it milky)
```

Memory hooks:

- **G**lowing splint → **O**xygen relights it. **L**ighted splint → hydrogen **pops**.
- Ammonia is the only common **alkaline** gas, so **red** litmus goes **blue**.
- Chlorine is a **bleach**. Blue litmus may go red first; the result you write is "bleached".
- Paper must be **damp** for both ammonia and chlorine.

## 2.45 Flame test method in four steps

```
1. Clean a nichrome/platinum wire: dip in conc. HCl, heat in flame until no colour.
2. Dip the clean wire in conc. HCl, then into the solid sample.
3. Hold it in a blue (non-luminous) Bunsen flame.
4. Record the flame colour.
```

Why: a dirty wire gives a colour from the last sample; a yellow flame hides the result.

## 2.46 Flame colours

| Ion | Colour |
|---|---|
| Li⁺ | red |
| Na⁺ | yellow |
| K⁺ | lilac |
| Ca²⁺ | orange-red |
| Cu²⁺ | blue-green |

- Lithium is **red**; calcium is **orange-red**. Keep the words exact.
- Sodium's strong yellow can mask a weaker colour such as lilac.
- A flame test tells you the **metal ion only**.

## 2.47 Cation tests with sodium hydroxide solution

| Ion | Add NaOH(aq) | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cu²⁺ | a few drops | blue precipitate |
| Fe²⁺ | a few drops | green precipitate |
| Fe³⁺ | a few drops | brown precipitate |
| NH₄⁺ | then **warm** | no precipitate; gas turns damp red litmus blue |

Ionic equations (the hydroxide count equals the metal ion's charge):

```
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s)
Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)
Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)
NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(g) + H₂O(l)
```

Full equation for an ammonium salt you may not have met:

```
(NH₄)₂SO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2NH₃(g) + 2H₂O(l)
```

Green iron(II) hydroxide darkens to brown on standing in air. Record the colour you see at once.

## 2.48 Anion tests

| Ion | Add | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | dilute HNO₃, then AgNO₃(aq) | white precipitate (AgCl) |
| Br⁻ | dilute HNO₃, then AgNO₃(aq) | cream precipitate (AgBr) |
| I⁻ | dilute HNO₃, then AgNO₃(aq) | yellow precipitate (AgI) |
| SO₄²⁻ | dilute HCl, then BaCl₂(aq) | white precipitate (BaSO₄) |
| CO₃²⁻ | dilute HCl | fizzing; gas turns limewater milky (CO₂) |

Ionic equations:

```
Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)
Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
```

A full carbonate equation, for a coloured carbonate:

```
CuCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
```

### Which acid goes with which test

| Test | Acid to use | Acid to avoid, and why |
|---|---|---|
| Silver nitrate (halides) | nitric | hydrochloric -- adds Cl⁻, always gives white precipitate |
| Barium chloride (sulfate) | hydrochloric | sulfuric -- adds SO₄²⁻, always gives white precipitate |

The acid is added first to remove carbonate ions, which would otherwise form a precipitate too.

### Halide colours in order

Going down Group 7 the precipitate darkens: **white → cream → yellow** (Cl → Br → I).

## 2.49 Test for water

```
Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate:  white → blue
CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s)
```

This shows water is **present**, not that it is **pure**.

## 2.50 Physical test for pure water

| Property | Pure water (normal pressure) | Impure water |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling point | exactly 100 °C | above 100 °C, often over a range |
| Freezing point | exactly 0 °C | below 0 °C |

Method: heat the sample and read the boiling temperature with a thermometer.

## Method in steps: identifying an unknown salt

```
1. Flame test on the solid            → metal ion (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cu²⁺)
2. Dissolve; add NaOH(aq)             → Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺ by precipitate colour
   No precipitate? Warm, damp red litmus → NH₄⁺ if it turns blue
3. New sample: add dilute HCl         → fizzing + milky limewater = CO₃²⁻
4. New sample: HNO₃ then AgNO₃(aq)    → Cl⁻ / Br⁻ / I⁻ by precipitate colour
5. New sample: HCl then BaCl₂(aq)     → white precipitate = SO₄²⁻
6. Combine cation and anion; balance the charges for the formula
```

Use a fresh sample for each test. Reagents from one test would interfere with the next.

## Small worked reminders

**Reminder 1.** A pale green solution gives a green precipitate with sodium hydroxide. With dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate it gives a yellow precipitate.

- Green hydroxide → Fe²⁺. Yellow silver halide → I⁻.
- Charges: Fe²⁺ needs two I⁻. The salt is **iron(II) iodide, FeI₂**.

**Reminder 2.** A white solid gives no flame colour. Its solution gives no precipitate with sodium hydroxide, but on warming a gas turns damp red litmus blue. With dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate it gives a white precipitate.

- Gas is ammonia → NH₄⁺. White silver halide → Cl⁻.
- The salt is **ammonium chloride, NH₄Cl**.

**Reminder 3.** A white solid gives a lilac flame. With dilute hydrochloric acid and barium chloride it gives a white precipitate.

- Lilac → K⁺. White precipitate with acidified BaCl₂ → SO₄²⁻.
- Charges: two K⁺ balance one SO₄²⁻. The salt is **potassium sulfate, K₂SO₄**.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Glowing vs lighted splint.** Glowing tests for oxygen; lighted tests for hydrogen.
- **Red vs blue litmus.** Damp **red** litmus for ammonia (goes blue). Any damp litmus for chlorine (bleached).
- **Fe²⁺ vs Fe³⁺.** Green vs brown hydroxide. Always write the charge or the Roman numeral.
- **NH₄⁺ vs metal ions.** Ammonium gives no precipitate; you must warm and test the gas.
- **Bromide vs iodide.** Cream vs yellow.
- **Sulfate vs carbonate.** Sulfate gives a precipitate with acidified BaCl₂; carbonate fizzes with acid and gives no precipitate.
- **Present vs pure.** Copper(II) sulfate shows water is present; the boiling point shows if it is pure.
- **Flame test vs NaOH test.** Cu²⁺ is the only ion in both lists (blue-green flame; blue precipitate).

## Quick self-test

1. Which gas puts out a lighted splint with a squeaky pop?
2. Describe the test for ammonia, with its result.
3. Give the flame colour of Cu²⁺.
4. A sample gives a red flame. Which ion from the specification list is present?
5. Why is the wire cleaned in concentrated hydrochloric acid before a flame test?
6. Sodium hydroxide solution gives a brown precipitate. Name the ion present.
7. Write the ionic equation for the reaction of iron(II) ions with hydroxide ions.
8. Dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate give a cream precipitate. Name the ion present.
9. Name the two reagents used to test for sulfate ions, and the positive result.
10. A white powder gives a yellow flame. It fizzes with dilute hydrochloric acid and the gas turns limewater milky. Name the compound.
11. Give the colour change when water is added to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate.
12. A clear liquid freezes at −2 °C. Is it pure water? Explain.

### Answers

1. Hydrogen.
2. Hold damp red litmus paper in the gas; it turns blue.
3. Blue-green.
4. Lithium ion, Li⁺.
5. To remove traces of other substances, which would colour the flame.
6. Iron(III), Fe³⁺.
7. `Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)`
8. Bromide, Br⁻.
9. Dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution; white precipitate.
10. Sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃ (yellow flame = Na⁺; fizzing plus milky limewater = CO₃²⁻).
11. White to blue.
12. No. Pure water freezes at 0 °C; dissolved impurities lower the freezing point.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the gas is tested with a splint" without saying glowing or lighted.
- Holding dry litmus paper in ammonia or chlorine.
- Giving "turns red" as the chlorine result instead of "bleached".
- Stating "precipitate forms" with no colour.
- Writing "iron" for a green precipitate; the answer needs iron(II) or Fe²⁺.
- Leaving out "warm" in the ammonium test, or saying the litmus is dipped into the solution.
- Naming "acidified silver nitrate" correctly but then adding hydrochloric acid in the method.
- Swapping the cream and yellow halide precipitates.
- Saying "bubbles" for carbonate without testing the gas with limewater.
- Claiming that a blue colour with anhydrous copper(II) sulfate proves the water is pure.
