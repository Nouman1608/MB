These notes condense **3.1.3 Price determination in a competitive market** in AQA A-level Economics (7136).
For explanations and worked examples, read the [study guide for this sub-section](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-price-determination-in-a-competitive-market-aqa-alevel-economics/),
then use the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-price-determination-in-a-competitive-market-aqa-alevel-economics-practice/).
All figures are invented.

## Demand (3.1.3.1)

- A demand curve shows the relationship between **price and quantity demanded**, ceteris paribus.
- **Movement along:** caused only by a change in the good's own price (extension or contraction).
- **Shift:** caused by any other determinant: income, prices of substitutes and complements, tastes,
  advertising, population, expectations, interest rates and credit.

## Supply (3.1.3.3)

- A supply curve shows the relationship between **price and quantity supplied**.
- Higher prices imply higher profits, giving firms an incentive to expand production: the curve slopes up.
- **Shifts:** costs of production (wages, raw materials, energy), productivity and technology, indirect taxes
  and subsidies, number of firms, weather for farm goods.
- Under **perfect competition**, the supply curve is the **marginal cost curve**.

## Elasticity formulae

| Elasticity | Formula | Key values |
|---|---|---|
| PED | %Δ quantity demanded ÷ %Δ price | < 1 (ignoring sign) inelastic; > 1 elastic; 1 unitary |
| YED | %Δ quantity demanded ÷ %Δ income | > 0 normal; < 0 inferior |
| XED | %Δ quantity demanded of X ÷ %Δ price of Y | > 0 substitutes; < 0 complements; 0 unrelated |
| PES | %Δ quantity supplied ÷ %Δ price | < 1 inelastic; > 1 elastic |

## PED and total revenue

| PED (ignoring sign) | Price rises | Price falls |
|---|---|---|
| Inelastic (< 1) | revenue rises | revenue falls |
| Unitary (= 1) | no change | no change |
| Elastic (> 1) | revenue falls | revenue rises |

## What influences each elasticity

- **PED:** number and closeness of substitutes; share of income spent; necessity or habit; time period; how
  widely the market is defined.
- **YED:** whether the good is a necessity or a luxury; the level of income already reached.
- **XED:** how close the substitute or complement is.
- **PES:** spare capacity; stocks; ease of switching factors; production time; time period.

## Equilibrium (3.1.3.5)

- **Equilibrium:** quantity demanded equals quantity supplied; no tendency to change.
- **Disequilibrium:** they differ at the current price.
- **Excess demand** (shortage) pushes price up; **excess supply** (surplus) pushes price down.
- Assumptions: many buyers and sellers, prices free to adjust, good information, ceteris paribus.

## Two curves shifting

| Shift | Certain | Uncertain |
|---|---|---|
| D right, S right | quantity up | price |
| D left, S left | quantity down | price |
| D right, S left | price up | quantity |
| D left, S right | price down | quantity |

## Which effect dominates

- Demand shift + inelastic supply → mostly a **price** change.
- Demand shift + elastic supply → mostly a **quantity** change.
- Supply shift + inelastic demand → mostly a **price** change; revenue moves with price.
- Supply shift + elastic demand → mostly a **quantity** change; revenue moves against price.

## Related markets (3.1.3.6)

| Relationship | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Joint demand | Goods bought together | Printers and ink |
| Competitive demand | Goods that are substitutes | Butter and margarine |
| Composite demand | One good with several uses | Wheat for bread and for animal feed |
| Derived demand | Demand for an input comes from demand for the output | Bricklayers and new houses |
| Joint supply | Producing one good also produces another | Lamb and wool |

## Worked reminders (invented)

- Qd = 600 − 20P, Qs = 100P − 120: 720 = 120P, so **P = 6**, **Q = 480**.
- At P = 5: Qd = 500, Qs = 380, excess demand **120**.
- Price rises 5%, quantity demanded falls 2%: PED = −2 ÷ 5 = **−0.4**, inelastic; revenue rises.
- Income rises 10%, demand for a good falls 3%: YED = **−0.3**, inferior.

## Must-know distinctions

- Change in demand (shift) versus change in quantity demanded (movement).
- Excess demand versus higher demand.
- Inferior good (negative YED) versus inelastic demand.
- Substitutes (positive XED) versus complements (negative XED).
- Composite demand (one good, many uses) versus joint demand (several goods used together).

## Quick self-test

1. What causes a movement along a demand curve?
2. Give three causes of a rightward shift in supply.
3. A price rises by 8% and quantity demanded falls by 12%. Calculate PED and state the effect on total revenue.
4. Income rises by 5% and demand rises by 9%. Calculate YED and classify the good.
5. The price of good Y rises by 10% and demand for good X falls by 4%. Calculate XED and state the relationship.
6. Price rises by 20% and quantity supplied rises by 6%. Calculate PES and give one likely reason for the
   value.
7. Explain how excess supply is removed.
8. Demand shifts right and supply shifts left. What can be said about price and quantity?
9. Give an example of composite demand.
10. Explain why the demand for steelworkers is a derived demand.
11. Why is a shift in demand likely to raise price sharply in the short run in the housing market?
12. In which market structure is the supply curve the marginal cost curve?

### Answers

1. A change in the good's own price.
2. Any three: lower wages or raw material costs, better technology, a subsidy, more firms entering, higher
   productivity, good weather for farm goods.
3. PED = −12 ÷ 8 = **−1.5**, elastic; total revenue **falls**.
4. YED = 9 ÷ 5 = **+1.8**; a **normal good** (demand grows faster than income).
5. XED = −4 ÷ 10 = **−0.4**; **complements**.
6. PES = 6 ÷ 20 = **0.3**, inelastic; for example little spare capacity or a long production period.
7. Sellers with unsold stock cut prices; quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until they
   are equal.
8. Price **rises**; the change in quantity is **uncertain**, depending on the relative size of the shifts.
9. For example, wheat used for bread and for animal feed, or land used for housing and for farming.
10. Firms employ steelworkers because consumers and firms demand steel products; demand for the workers comes
    from demand for the output.
11. Housing supply is **price inelastic** in the short run because new homes take years to build.
12. **Perfect competition.**
