## Bonding as a continuum (Structure 2.4.1)

The three bonding models are idealised end points that few real substances match perfectly.

| Model | Particles | What holds them | Typical properties |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Cations and anions in a lattice | Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions | High melting point, brittle, conducts only when molten or in solution |
| Covalent | Atoms sharing electron pairs | Attraction of two nuclei to a shared pair | Molecular: low melting point, non-conducting. Network: very high melting point, hard |
| Metallic | Cations in a sea of delocalized electrons | Attraction between cations and delocalized electrons | Conducts as a solid, malleable, ductile, lustrous |

A bond between atoms of slightly different electronegativity is polar covalent: covalent with some ionic character. A lattice where a small, highly charged cation distorts the anion's electron cloud is ionic with some covalent character. So ask "how far towards each end?" rather than "ionic or covalent?". This is a limitation of neat categories.

### Worked example 1: explaining a material's use

A furnace uses magnesium oxide bricks, copper cable and silicon carbide cutting discs. Explain each choice.

1. **Magnesium oxide** is ionic. Small, doubly charged Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions attract very strongly, so much energy is needed to separate them and the bricks stay solid.
2. **Copper** is metallic. Its delocalized electrons move under a potential difference, so it conducts; non-directional bonding lets layers slide, so it can be drawn into wire.
3. **Silicon carbide** is a covalent network. Each atom has four strong covalent bonds in three dimensions, and many must break to scratch it, so it is extremely hard.

## The bonding triangle (Structure 2.4.2)

A bonding triangle (also called a van Arkel–Ketelaar triangle) turns the continuum into a graph. For a binary compound of elements A and B:

- the **horizontal axis** is the average electronegativity, (χA + χB) / 2
- the **vertical axis** is the electronegativity difference, Δχ = |χA − χB|

According to the guide, the data booklet contains both a bonding triangle diagram and electronegativity values.

The corners are the pure models. With Pauling values, caesium (0.79, Δχ = 0) is the **metallic** corner, fluorine (3.98, Δχ = 0) the **covalent** corner, and caesium fluoride (Δχ = 3.19, average 2.385) the **ionic** corner.

- **Higher up** (large Δχ): more ionic character.
- **Low down and to the left** (small Δχ, low average): metallic.
- **Low down and to the right** (small Δχ, high average): covalent; both elements hold electrons tightly.
- **Between regions**: intermediate character, such as polar covalent.

The boundaries are gradual, not sharp lines. You will not have to calculate a percentage ionic character.

### Worked example 2: placing compounds

Use these Pauling electronegativity values: K 0.82, Cl 3.16, Al 1.61, Si 1.90, C 2.55, Ni 1.91. Place KCl, AlCl₃, SiC and NiAl in the triangle.

| Compound | Δχ | Average χ | Position | Bonding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KCl | 3.16 − 0.82 = 2.34 | (0.82 + 3.16) / 2 = 1.99 | High up, near the middle | Mainly ionic |
| AlCl₃ | 3.16 − 1.61 = 1.55 | (1.61 + 3.16) / 2 = 2.385 | Middle height, right of centre | Polar covalent |
| SiC | 2.55 − 1.90 = 0.65 | (1.90 + 2.55) / 2 = 2.225 | Low, right of centre | Covalent |
| NiAl | 1.91 − 1.61 = 0.30 | (1.61 + 1.91) / 2 = 1.76 | Very low, left of centre | Metallic |

Only the two elements matter: AlCl₃ has three chlorines, but you use one value for each element.

### Worked example 3: predicting properties

Predict and compare the properties of KCl and AlCl₃ from their positions.

- KCl lies well into the ionic region: predict a high melting point, a brittle solid, and conduction only when molten or dissolved.
- AlCl₃ lies lower, between ionic and covalent: predict a much lower melting point and little conduction. Aluminium chloride in fact sublimes at about 450 K, and its vapour is made of Al₂Cl₆ molecules.
- NiAl should conduct as a solid; SiC should be a hard, high-melting non-conductor.

Structure 3.1 uses the same reasoning for the bonding trend in the period 3 oxides.

## Alloys (Structure 2.4.3)

An **alloy** is a metal mixed with one or more further elements, metal or non-metal. It is a mixture, not a compound, because its composition can vary. Alloys usually have enhanced properties.

A metal can take in foreign atoms because **metallic bonding is non-directional**: the delocalized electrons attract cations equally in all directions, so a different-sized atom can sit in the lattice without breaking a particular bond.

- **Harder and stronger.** Different-sized atoms distort the regular layers of cations, so the layers cannot slide as easily.
- **Still conducts.** The delocalized electrons remain mobile.
- **Other changes.** Some alloys resist corrosion better; some melt lower.

| Alloy | Main components | Improved property |
|---|---|---|
| Bronze | Copper with tin | Harder than copper; resists corrosion |
| Brass | Copper with zinc | Harder than copper; easy to machine; attractive colour |
| Stainless steel | Iron with chromium (often nickel), a little carbon | Resists rusting; strong |

You do not need to learn specific alloys, only the explanation.

### Worked example 4

Explain why brass is harder than copper, yet both conduct. Copper's identical cations form regular layers that slide easily. In brass, zinc atoms of a different size fit into the lattice, which non-directional bonding allows. They disrupt the layers, so more force is needed to make them slide. Both keep mobile delocalized electrons, so both conduct.

## Polymers and plastics (Structure 2.4.4)

A **polymer** is a macromolecule made from many small repeating subunits called **monomers**. The section of chain that repeats is the **repeating unit**.

| Natural polymers | Synthetic polymers |
|---|---|
| Proteins (from 2-amino acids) | Poly(ethene) |
| Starch and cellulose (from glucose) | Poly(chloroethene), PVC |
| DNA (from nucleotides) | Polyamides such as nylons |
| Natural rubber | Polyesters |

**Plastics** are synthetic polymers that can be moulded. Their common properties follow from their structure:

| Property | Structural reason |
|---|---|
| Low density | Light atoms (mostly C and H), loosely packed chains |
| Electrical insulators | Electrons held in localized bonds; no mobile charges |
| Can be softened and moulded | Warming overcomes the intermolecular forces between chains; chain bonds stay intact |
| Strong and flexible | Long chains tangle, with many intermolecular forces along each one |
| Unreactive, slow to biodegrade | Strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds |

Longer, less branched chains pack closer, so the plastic is stronger and denser. Covalent cross-links between chains stop a plastic softening on heating. Low reactivity also makes plastic waste persist; Structure 3.2 asks what makes some plastics biodegradable.

## Addition polymers (Structure 2.4.5)

In **addition polymerization**, the π bond of each monomer's C=C breaks and each carbon forms a new C–C bond to a neighbouring monomer. Nothing else forms, so the **atom economy is 100%**.

To draw the repeating unit:

1. Draw the C=C carbons joined by a single bond, keeping all their groups.
2. Add a continuation bond on each side through square brackets, and write n after.

### Worked example 5: from monomer to repeating unit

Propenamide, CH₂=CHCONH₂, polymerizes to make a water-absorbing polymer. Draw its repeating unit and work out the molar mass of a chain of 2000 units.

Repeating unit: **–[–CH₂–CH(CONH₂)–]–ₙ**, a two-carbon backbone with continuation bonds through the brackets.

CONH₂ is a side group, not part of the backbone. Each repeating unit has the same atoms as one monomer, C₃H₅NO. Taking C = 12.01, H = 1.01, O = 16.00 and N = 14.01: M = 3(12.01) + 5(1.01) + 14.01 + 16.00 = 71.09 g mol⁻¹. A chain of 2000 units has M ≈ 2000 × 71.09 = **1.42 × 10⁵ g mol⁻¹** (ignoring the end groups).

### Worked example 6: from polymer to monomer

A polymer has the repeating unit –CH₂–C(CH₃)Cl–. Deduce the monomer.

Put a double bond between the two backbone carbons and remove the continuation bonds: CH₂=C(CH₃)Cl, **2-chloropropene**.

## Condensation polymers (Structure 2.4.6, HL only)

In **condensation polymerization**, each monomer has two functional groups. When two groups react, they form a link and release a small molecule, usually water. Because a by-product forms, the atom economy is below 100%.

- **Polyesters**: a carboxyl group (–COOH) reacts with a hydroxyl group (–OH) to give an **ester link**, –COO–.
- **Polyamides**: a carboxyl group reacts with an amino group (–NH₂) to give an **amide link**, –CONH–.

The groups can be on two different monomers or on the two ends of one monomer.

### Worked example 7: a polyester

Butane-1,4-diol, HO(CH₂)₄OH, reacts with butanedioic acid, HOOC(CH₂)₂COOH. Draw the repeating unit and find the atom economy.

1. H from the diol's –OH and OH from the acid's –COOH leave as H₂O; the diol's O joins the acid's C=O, at both ends.
2. Repeating unit: **–[–O(CH₂)₄O–CO(CH₂)₂CO–]–ₙ**, with two ester links and two water molecules released per unit.
3. Atom economy = 172.20 / (90.14 + 118.10) × 100 = **82.7%**.

### Worked example 8: polyamides

(a) 4-Aminobutanoic acid, H₂N(CH₂)₃COOH, has both groups on one molecule. Each monomer links head-to-tail: **–[–NH(CH₂)₃CO–]–ₙ**, releasing one H₂O per unit.

(b) Pentane-1,5-diamine and hexanedioic acid give **–[–NH(CH₂)₅NH–CO(CH₂)₄CO–]–ₙ**, releasing two H₂O per unit.

### Biological macromolecules

All biological macromolecules form by condensation and break down by **hydrolysis**, the reverse reaction, in which water splits each link.

- **Proteins**: 2-amino acids join through amide (peptide) links. A chain of 120 amino acids releases 119 water molecules.
- **Polysaccharides** such as starch and cellulose: glucose units join, releasing water at each link.
- **Nucleic acids** (DNA, RNA): nucleotides join by condensation.

### Worked example 9

Starch is fully hydrolysed to glucose. Find the mass of glucose from 8.10 g of starch.

Each chain unit is C₆H₁₀O₅ (162.16 g mol⁻¹); adding water gives glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆ (180.18 g mol⁻¹). Moles of units = 8.10 / 162.16 = 0.04995 mol, so mass of glucose = 0.04995 × 180.18 = **9.00 g**. The mass rises because water is added.

## Common errors

- Multiplying by a subscript: Δχ for AlCl₃ uses one Cl value.
- Calling an alloy a compound.
- Saying alloys are harder "because the bonds are stronger" instead of "layers cannot slide".
- Putting a side group into the backbone of an addition polymer, or leaving out continuation bonds and n.
- (HL) Forgetting to remove H₂O at each link, or confusing the amide link –CONH– with an amine.
