Subtopic 13.4 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers the definition of gravitational potential, ϕ = –GM/r, and the potential energy of two point masses, E_P = –GMm/r. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Gravitational potential study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-potential/); to apply the ideas, try the [Gravitational potential practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-potential-practice/).

## Definition

> Gravitational potential at a point is the **work done per unit mass** in bringing a **small test mass** from **infinity** to the point.

- Unit: **J kg⁻¹**. Scalar.
- Zero at infinity; **negative** everywhere else.
- Increases (towards zero) as r increases.

## Equations (on the data sheet)

```
ϕ = –GM / r                      (point mass, or outside a uniform sphere)
E_P = mϕ = –GMm / r               (two point masses)
ΔE_P = mΔϕ = GMm (1/r₁ – 1/r₂)     moving from r₁ to r₂
```

- r from the **centre**.
- ϕ ∝ 1/r (field strength ∝ 1/r²).
- Final minus initial: moving outwards gives ΔE_P > 0.

## Why negative

Potential is zero at infinity. Gravity attracts, so the field does positive work on a mass brought in from infinity; the work done **on** the mass is negative. Energy must be supplied to move a mass outwards.

## Potential compared with field strength

| | Field strength g | Potential ϕ |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Force per unit mass | Work done per unit mass from infinity |
| Unit | N kg⁻¹ | J kg⁻¹ |
| Type | Vector | Scalar |
| Point mass | GM/r² | –GM/r |
| Doubling r | ÷ 4 | ÷ 2 (less negative) |
| Two bodies | Vector sum (may cancel) | Simple sum (both negative, never cancel) |

## mgΔh versus mΔϕ

- mgΔh assumes **uniform** g: valid only when Δh ≪ R.
- For large height changes, mgΔh **overestimates** ΔE_P because g falls with height.
- Example: 1500 kg raised 2.0 × 10⁶ m: mΔϕ gives 2.2 × 10¹⁰ J; mgΔh gives 2.9 × 10¹⁰ J.

## Satellite energies (circular orbit)

```
E_K = GMm / 2r        E_P = –GMm / r        E_K = –½E_P
```

Moving to a higher orbit: E_P increases, E_K decreases by half as much, so energy must be supplied overall.

## Method for any energy-change question

1. Convert every height to a distance from the **centre** (r = R + h).
2. Find ϕ at the start and the end, or go straight to ΔE_P = GMm(1/r₁ – 1/r₂).
3. Work out **final minus initial**; a positive answer means energy is supplied.
4. If the object is in orbit at both ends, add ΔE_K = (GMm/2)(1/r₂ – 1/r₁).
5. If it falls freely, set the loss of E_P equal to the gain of E_K to find a speed.
6. Check the size: for a small height change the answer should be close to mgΔh.

## Values worth recognising

| Location | ϕ / J kg⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| Earth's surface | –6.25 × 10⁷ |
| r = 2R | –3.13 × 10⁷ |
| Geostationary radius | –9.4 × 10⁶ |
| Infinity | 0 |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gravitational potential ϕ | Work done per unit mass bringing a small test mass from infinity to the point |
| Gravitational potential energy E_P | Energy of two masses due to their separation; E_P = mϕ |
| Potential difference Δϕ | Difference in potential between two points; ΔE_P = mΔϕ |
| Infinity | Reference position where ϕ = 0 |
| Scalar | Quantity with magnitude (and sign) but no direction |

## Exam traps

- Leaving out the minus sign.
- Writing 1/r² for potential.
- Defining potential without "per unit mass", "small test mass" or "from infinity".
- Using mgΔh for a satellite launch.
- Treating potentials of two bodies as vectors and subtracting them.
- Giving ΔE_P with the wrong sign: work out ϕ_final – ϕ_initial.
- Assuming the zero-field point between two bodies has zero potential.

## Self-test

1. Define gravitational potential.
2. Why is gravitational potential negative at every finite distance?
3. State the unit of gravitational potential.
4. The potential at the Earth's surface is –6.25 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹. What is it at r = 4R?
5. Calculate E_P for a 2.0 kg mass at the Earth's surface.
6. A 50 kg mass moves from a point where ϕ = –5.0 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ to one where ϕ = –3.0 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹. Find the change in its potential energy.
7. Why does mgΔh overestimate the energy needed to lift a rocket to a high orbit?
8. Two bodies each produce a potential of –2.0 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ at a point. What is the total potential there?
9. State the relationship between E_K and E_P for a satellite in a circular orbit.
10. A satellite moves to a higher circular orbit. State what happens to its E_P, its E_K and its speed.
11. Is potential a scalar or a vector?
12. A planet has M = 3.0 × 10²⁴ kg and R = 4.0 × 10⁶ m. Calculate ϕ at its surface.

**Answers:** 1. The work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to the point. 2. Potential is zero at infinity and gravity is attractive, so the work done on a mass brought in from infinity is negative. 3. J kg⁻¹. 4. ϕ ∝ 1/r: –6.25 × 10⁷ ÷ 4 = –1.56 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹. 5. E_P = mϕ = 2.0 × (–6.25 × 10⁷) = –1.25 × 10⁸ J. 6. ΔE_P = mΔϕ = 50 × (+2.0 × 10⁷) = +1.0 × 10⁹ J. 7. It assumes g stays constant, but g decreases with height. 8. –4.0 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹. 9. E_K = –½E_P (E_K is half the magnitude of E_P). 10. E_P increases (less negative); E_K decreases; speed decreases. 11. Scalar. 12. ϕ = –6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 3.0 × 10²⁴ ÷ 4.0 × 10⁶ = –5.0 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹.
