These questions cover Topic 8, Coastal environments (sections 8.1 to 8.4), of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The topic is assessed in Paper 3, Advanced Physical Geography Options, where you answer on two of the four options; each option has a 10-mark structured question and a choice of two 20-mark essays. All places and data in the questions are fictional unless a question asks you to use your own case study.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term "fetch". **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between cavitation and hydraulic action. **[3]**

**3.** Outline how two different sub-aerial processes contribute to cliff retreat. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows cliff-top retreat at five survey posts on the fictional Brenmouth coast, listed from north to south. The cliffs at posts A to D are glacial till; post E is on a sandstone headland. Rock groynes and a revetment were built at post B in 2012. The dominant longshore drift is from north to south.

| Post | Distance from cliff top to fixed marker, 2010 (m) | Distance, 2025 (m) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 42.0 | 15.0 |
| B | 38.0 | 36.5 |
| C | 50.0 | 8.0 |
| D | 45.0 | 16.5 |
| E | 30.0 | 28.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean annual rate of cliff retreat at post C between 2010 and 2025. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the pattern of retreat shown in the table. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest reasons for the differences in retreat between the posts. **[5]**

**5.** A student measured pebbles at six sites, 1 km apart, along the fictional Carrow Bay beach from west to east. Waves mostly approach from the south-west.

| Site | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean long axis (mm) | 62 | 51 | 44 | 37 | 30 | 24 |
| Mean roundness (1 = very angular, 6 = very rounded) | 2.8 | 3.1 | 3.6 | 4.0 | 4.4 | 4.9 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage decrease in mean long axis from site 1 to site 6. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the changes in pebble size and roundness along the beach. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the formation of a compound spit. You may use a labelled diagram. **[5]**

**7.** This question is about the distribution of coral reefs.

**(a)** Outline four conditions needed for the growth of reef-building corals. **[4]**
**(b)** Suggest why coral reefs are rare along the western coasts of continents in the tropics. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows live coral cover at four sites on the fictional Lirau Reef. All four sites had the same peak sea surface temperature anomaly (+1.6°C) during a marine heatwave in 2022.

| Site | Description | Coral cover 2019 (%) | Coral cover 2024 (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Near a river mouth draining farmland | 38 | 22 |
| 2 | Tourist boat anchorage | 45 | 30 |
| 3 | No-take marine reserve | 52 | 47 |
| 4 | Outer reef, deep water nearby | 60 | 51 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in coral cover at site 1 between 2019 and 2024. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why coral loss differed between the sites even though heat stress was the same. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how changes in relative sea level can produce rias and raised beaches. **[6]**

**10.** "Most coastal landforms are the result of past conditions rather than present-day processes." How far do you agree? **[20]**

**11.** With reference to examples, evaluate the view that global warming is now a greater threat to coral reefs than local human activity. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The distance of open water [1] over which the wind blows to generate waves [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "The length of the sea" does not score; you need the idea of wind blowing across uninterrupted water.

**2.** Hydraulic action is the force of water, and air compressed by breaking waves, in joints and cracks [1]. Cavitation is the collapse of air bubbles in the water [1], which releases small shock waves that weaken the rock surface [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Many answers describe both as "air in cracks"; the bubble-collapse mechanism is what earns the cavitation marks.

**3.** Weathering, such as salt crystallisation or wetting and drying, weakens and loosens rock on the cliff face [1], so it is more easily removed [1]. Mass movement, such as rotational slumping in saturated clay, moves material down to the cliff foot [1], where waves can remove it and the cliff top retreats [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline how" needs the effect on retreat for each process, not just a named process.

**4. (a)** 50.0 - 8.0 = 42.0 m over 15 years [1]; 42.0 / 15 = 2.8 m per year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Retreat is greatest at post C (42.0 m) [1]. Posts A and D retreat by similar, large amounts (27.0 m and 28.5 m) [1]. Posts B and E retreat very little (1.5 m each) [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Post B is protected, so the groynes and revetment absorb wave energy [1]. Post C lies just south of the groynes, so sediment moving south is trapped and its beach is starved, leaving the cliff foot exposed [1]. Posts A to D are weak glacial till, which slumps and is easily eroded [1], whereas the sandstone at E is more resistant [1]. E may also be a headland where the cliff foot is protected by a rock platform or by debris that is removed more slowly [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Top answers use the data and context together: the groyne location and drift direction explain post C, which is the key anomaly.

**5. (a)** (62 - 24) / 62 x 100 [1] = 61.3% decrease [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Pebbles get smaller and rounder from west to east [1]. Waves from the south-west drive longshore drift eastwards [1], so pebbles further east have travelled further [1]. Attrition during transport knocks off corners and reduces their size [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the wave direction given in the stem to the drift direction; a general account of attrition alone caps the answer.

**6.** Longshore drift moves sediment along the coast [1]. Where the coastline changes direction, for example at an estuary, deposition continues out into open water [1]. Waves refracting round the end, or a change in wind and wave direction, curve the tip into a recurve [1]. As the spit grows, new recurves form and older ones are left along the landward side, giving a compound spit [1]. Sheltered water behind the spit allows mud to settle and saltmarsh to form [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Compound" must be explained; a description of a simple spit with one hook stops at about three marks.

**7. (a)** Warm water, typically 23-29°C [1]; shallow water, mostly within roughly the top 30 m, so light reaches the zooxanthellae for photosynthesis [1]; clear water with little sediment [1]; normal sea salinity [1]. Also credit wave action to bring oxygen and food, or only brief exposure to air at low tide, in place of any of these. **[4]**
**(b)** Cold ocean currents [1] and upwelling of cold deep water lower sea temperatures below what corals need [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* For (a), give a value or reason with each condition; a bare list such as "warm, shallow, clear" earns less.

**8. (a)** (22 - 38) / 38 x 100 [1] = -42.1%, a decrease of about 42% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Site 1 receives sediment that cuts light and nutrients that encourage algae, adding stress to heat-stressed corals [1]. At site 2, anchors and boat traffic break coral physically, so recovery is slower [1]. Site 3 is protected from fishing and damage, so healthy fish populations graze algae and the reef recovers faster [1]. Site 4 may be cooled by deeper water mixing, and is far from land-based pollution [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Suggest" rewards reasons tied to each site's description; repeating "global warming" for every site earns nothing, since heat stress was the same.

**9.** A rise in sea level relative to the land, for example eustatic rise after the last glaciation, floods the lower parts of river valleys [1]. This produces a ria: a drowned river valley with a winding plan and a depth that decreases inland [1]. A raised beach forms where the land rises relative to the sea [1], for example through isostatic rebound after the weight of an ice sheet is removed [1]. The former beach and wave-cut platform are left above the reach of present waves [1], often backed by a relict cliff [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the type of sea level change (eustatic or isostatic) for each landform; vague "sea level changed" answers rarely reach full marks.

**10.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define past conditions (earlier sea levels, glacial sediment supply) and present processes (waves, tides, drift, sub-aerial processes) [1]. Set out the scale argument: small landforms change fast, large ones reflect long timescales [1].

*Agree:* raised beaches and relict cliffs formed at earlier, higher relative sea levels and are no longer shaped by waves [1]. Rias and fjords are drowned valleys formed by post-glacial sea-level rise [1]. Many shingle ridges were built from sediment pushed onshore as the sea rose after the last glaciation [1]. That supply has largely ended, so these beaches now lose material [1]. Glacial till cliffs, such as at Holderness, exist because of past glaciation [1].

*Disagree:* beach profiles change with each storm and calm spell [1]. Swash and backwash rebuild berms in days or weeks [1]. Longshore drift still feeds spits; Spurn Head is built from sediment eroded from Holderness today [1]. Embryo dunes form and change each year as sand blows inland [1]. Cliffs, arches and stacks continue to change through present erosion and mass movement [1]. Saltmarshes and mangroves grow as they trap present-day sediment [1].

*Interaction:* most landforms are present processes acting on a form inherited from the past [1]. For example, present waves rework older sediment stores [1]. Human activity now changes processes too, as at Mappleton, where groynes have changed sediment supply [1].

*Judgement:* large-scale forms (rias, fjords, raised beaches, many barrier beaches) are mainly inherited [1]. Small-scale forms (beach profiles, dunes, spit tips) are mainly present-day [1]. The statement is partly true and depends on scale and time [1]. A reasoned final view that weighs both sides [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* High-level answers vary their judgement by landform and scale, and use at least one real named example for each side.

**11.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define global warming as a global, long-term rise in sea temperature [1], and local human activity as pollution and physical damage at a reef [1].

*Global warming as the greater threat:* water above the usual summer maximum makes corals expel zooxanthellae, causing bleaching [1]. Mass bleaching events on the Great Barrier Reef include those of 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2022 and 2024 [1]. Bleaching affects remote reefs far from any local source of pollution [1]. Ocean acidification from dissolved carbon dioxide slows skeleton growth [1]. Faster sea-level rise may outpace reefs that are already stressed [1]. No local plan can stop a marine heatwave [1].

*Local activity as the greater threat:* farm runoff adds nutrients that feed algae, and sediment cuts out light [1]. Anchors, trampling and blast fishing physically break corals [1]. Overfishing removes grazers that keep algae in check [1]. These pressures act every day, while heatwaves are intermittent [1].

*Interaction:* local stress lowers resilience, so polluted or damaged reefs recover more slowly after bleaching [1]. Management such as the 2004 Great Barrier Reef rezoning, which raised no-take zones from 4.6% to 33.3% of the park, reduces local pressure [1]. But bleaching has continued since the rezoning [1], which suggests that local management alone cannot protect reefs [1].

*Judgement:* at a global scale, warming is the greater threat because it affects every reef [1]. At a local scale, a heavily polluted reef may be lost to runoff first [1]. The two threats combine, so the most effective strategy reduces both [1]. A clear final answer to "how far" [1]. **[20]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs specific evidence on both sides and a judgement on scale; a list of threats with no weighing stays in the lower levels.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculations with no working shown, or with the wrong time period used.
- Describing the pattern in a table without quoting figures.
- Ignoring the context given in a stem, such as drift direction or the position of defences.
- Naming erosion processes with no description of how each works.
- Writing "global warming" as the answer to every coral question.
- Spit and tombolo answers without a sequence of formation.
- Essays that list points without weighing them or reaching a judgement.
- Case study references with no place, date or scheme named.
