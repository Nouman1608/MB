This guide teaches subtopic **1.6 The four operations** from the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027: *use the four operations for calculations with integers, fractions and decimals, including correct ordering of operations and use of brackets*. The syllabus notes say this includes negative numbers, improper fractions, mixed numbers and practical situations such as temperature changes. Core (C1.6) and Extended (E1.6) are identical. Because the non-calculator Papers 1 and 2 depend on it, every method here is written out by hand.

Use it with the [four operations revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-the-four-operations-revision-notes/) and the [four operations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-the-four-operations-practice/). Converting between fractions, decimals and percentages is in [subtopic 1.4](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-fractions-decimals-and-percentages/).

## Order of operations

Work in this order:

1. **Brackets** (innermost first)
2. **Indices**: powers and roots
3. **Division and multiplication**, left to right
4. **Addition and subtraction**, left to right

Division and multiplication have equal priority, so 12 ÷ 4 × 3 is (12 ÷ 4) × 3 = 9, not 12 ÷ 12 = 1. The same applies to addition and subtraction: 10 − 4 + 3 = 9.

A fraction bar acts as a bracket round its numerator and its denominator: (8 + 4) / (5 − 2) = 12 / 3 = 4.

**Worked example 1.** Work out 24 − 6 × (5 − 8)² ÷ 9.

```
Brackets:            5 − 8 = −3
Indices:             (−3)² = 9
× and ÷, left to right:  6 × 9 = 54, then 54 ÷ 9 = 6
Subtraction:         24 − 6 = 18
```

Writing one line per step keeps the order visible and protects method marks.

**Using brackets to change a result.** Insert one pair of brackets so that 2 + 6 × 5 − 3 = 14.

```
Without brackets:   2 + 30 − 3 = 29
(2 + 6) × 5 − 3   = 40 − 3 = 37
2 + 6 × (5 − 3)   = 2 + 12 = 14   ✓
```

Testing each position in turn is quicker than guessing.

## Negative numbers

**Adding and subtracting.** Subtracting a negative is the same as adding: 5 − (−3) = 8. Adding a negative is the same as subtracting: 5 + (−3) = 2.

**Multiplying and dividing.**

| Signs | Result | Example |
|---|---|---|
| same signs | positive | (−6) × (−4) = 24; (−20) ÷ (−5) = 4 |
| different signs | negative | (−6) × 4 = −24; 20 ÷ (−5) = −4 |

**Worked example 2.** Work out (a) (−3) × (−4) − 20 ÷ (−5), (b) −2 − (−7) + (−4).

```
(a) 12 − (−4) = 12 + 4 = 16
(b) −2 + 7 − 4 = 1
```

**Practical situations.** At 6 am the temperature is −4.5 °C. By noon it has risen by 7.8 °C, and by midnight it has fallen 9.2 °C from the noon value.

```
Noon:      −4.5 + 7.8 = 3.3 °C
Midnight:   3.3 − 9.2 = −5.9 °C
Change from 6 am to midnight: −5.9 − (−4.5) = −1.4, a fall of 1.4 °C
```

A number line sketch is a quick check: from −4.5 up 7.8 crosses zero after 4.5, leaving 3.3 above it.

## Integers without a calculator

**Long multiplication.** 368 × 24: 368 × 20 = 7360 and 368 × 4 = 1472, so 368 × 24 = 8832.

**Long or short division.** 1035 ÷ 23: 23 × 40 = 920, leaving 115; 23 × 5 = 115, so the answer is 45.

Any written method is accepted if it is clear; the grid method is as valid as column multiplication.

## Fractions and mixed numbers

**Adding and subtracting.** Change mixed numbers to improper fractions (or deal with whole numbers separately), use a common denominator, then simplify.

**Worked example 3.** Work out (a) 2¾ + 1⅚, (b) 3⅓ − 1⅘.

```
(a) 11/4 + 11/6 = 33/12 + 22/12 = 55/12 = 4 7/12
(b) 10/3 − 9/5  = 50/15 − 27/15 = 23/15 = 1 8/15
```

In (b), subtracting the whole numbers first gives 2 + (1/3 − 4/5), and 1/3 − 4/5 is negative. Improper fractions avoid that complication.

**Multiplying.** Change mixed numbers to improper fractions, cancel common factors, then multiply.

```
1⅗ × 2 1/12 = 8/5 × 25/12
            = (8 × 25)/(5 × 12)
cancel 8 and 12 by 4, 25 and 5 by 5:  (2 × 5)/(1 × 3) = 10/3 = 3⅓
```

**Dividing.** Multiply by the reciprocal of the divisor.

```
3¾ ÷ 1⅞ = 15/4 ÷ 15/8 = 15/4 × 8/15 = 8/4 = 2
```

Multiplying mixed numbers by multiplying the whole numbers and the fractions separately (1 × 2 and 3/5 × 1/12) is wrong; always convert first.

## Decimals without a calculator

**Multiplying.** Multiply as whole numbers, then place the decimal point so the answer has as many decimal places as the two numbers together.

```
3.6 × 0.07:   36 × 7 = 252;  1 + 2 = 3 decimal places  ->  0.252
2.4 × 1.25:   24 × 125 = 3000;  1 + 2 = 3 decimal places  ->  3.000 = 3
```

**Dividing.** Multiply both numbers by the same power of 10 so the divisor is a whole number.

```
4.83 ÷ 0.3  = 48.3 ÷ 3  = 16.1
0.552 ÷ 0.24 = 55.2 ÷ 24 = 2.3
```

Check with an estimate: 4.83 ÷ 0.3 is about 5 ÷ 0.3, which is more than 15, so 16.1 is sensible. Dividing by a number less than 1 makes the answer larger.

## Worked example 4: a practical fraction problem

A recipe needs 2¼ cups of flour for one cake. Mina has 10 cups. How many complete cakes can she make, and how much flour is left?

```
10 ÷ 2¼ = 10 ÷ 9/4 = 10 × 4/9 = 40/9 = 4 4/9
Complete cakes: 4
Flour used: 4 × 2¼ = 9 cups; left: 10 − 9 = 1 cup
```

"Complete cakes" means rounding down, whatever the fraction.

## Common errors

- Working left to right regardless of operation: 3 + 4 × 2 = 14 instead of 11.
- Multiplying before dividing when division comes first: 12 ÷ 4 × 3.
- Sign errors with two negatives: −3 − (−5) is 2, not −8.
- Squaring a negative without brackets: (−3)² = 9 but −3² = −9.
- Adding fractions by adding numerators and denominators: 1/4 + 1/6 ≠ 2/10.
- Dividing by a fraction without inverting the second fraction, or inverting the first.
- Losing the decimal point when multiplying decimals.

## Exam technique

- **Work out** on a non-calculator paper: show every intermediate value; an arithmetic slip then costs one mark, not all of them.
- **Give your answer as a mixed number in its simplest form**: convert at the end and cancel fully.
- **Show that** a calculation equals a given value: every step must appear, with no jumps that rely on a calculator.
- **Insert brackets to make a statement true**: test each possible position systematically.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does BIDMAS mean division always comes before multiplication?**
No. Division and multiplication have equal priority and are worked from left to right, and the same is true of addition and subtraction.

**Can I add mixed numbers by adding the whole numbers and fractions separately?**
Yes, when the fraction part does not become negative. For subtraction such as 3⅓ − 1⅘, converting to improper fractions is safer.
