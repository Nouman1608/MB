## Questions

**1.** Define price elasticity of demand. **[2]**

**2.** (calculator-free) The price of a good rises from 50 to 55 and the quantity demanded falls from 800 to 720. Calculate PED and state what type of elasticity it shows. **[3]**

**3.** (calculator allowed) Pellam Tea sells a blend at 5.00 a packet and sells 2,400 packets a week. Its PED is -0.5. The firm raises the price by 8%.

**(a)** Calculate the new weekly quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in total revenue. **[2]**

**4.** A fictional gym cuts its membership fee. The fee falls from 20 to 16 a month and monthly members rise from 100 to 150.

**(a)** Calculate PED. **[3]**
**(b)** Draw a diagram showing the change in total revenue, with the figures labelled. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how each factor affects PED for the good named.

**(a)** The number of close substitutes: a brand of breakfast cereal. **[2]**
**(b)** The proportion of income spent: table salt. **[2]**
**(c)** Time: petrol, after a permanent price rise. **[2]**

**6.** (HL only) The demand for a good in a fictional market is Q = 240 - 6P.

**(a)** Calculate PED when price rises from 10 to 12. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate PED when price rises from 30 to 32. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on the two results. **[1]**

**7.** (calculator-free) In a fictional country, household income rises from 3,000 to 3,300 a month. Weekend city breaks purchased rise from 120 to 162.

**(a)** Calculate YED. **[3]**
**(b)** Classify the good. **[1]**

**8.** Good R has a YED of -0.5. Incomes rise from 4,000 to 4,400 and the original quantity demanded is 200.

**(a)** Calculate the new quantity demanded. **[2]**
**(b)** Draw the Engel curve through the two points. **[3]**

**9.** (HL only) In the fictional economy of Karvia, YED is 0.3 for agricultural goods, 1.1 for manufactures and 1.6 for services. Income rises by 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in demand for each. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain what these results imply for the sectoral structure of Karvia. **[3]**

**10.** Doverly Loaves, a fictional bakery chain, faces price elastic demand for its rolls. Discuss whether it should cut its price to raise revenue. **[8]**

**11.** (calculator allowed) Halvor Ferries, a fictional operator, records fares and weekly passengers: fare 30 with 1,000 passengers; fare 36 with 880; fare 42 with 800.

**(a)** Calculate PED between fares 30 and 36. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate PED between fares 36 and 42. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate total revenue at each fare. **[1]**
**(d)** Recommend a fare, with one reason for caution. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** PED measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a change in the good's own price [1]. It is the quantity response in per cent divided by the price change in per cent [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the idea of responsiveness and the formula together; either alone leaves the definition incomplete.

**2.** Price change = 5 / 50 = +10% [1]. Quantity change = -80 / 800 = -10% [1]. PED = -10 / 10 = **-1.0**, so demand is unitary elastic (size 1) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Without a calculator, choose the starting values for each percentage and show each fraction; a bare answer gives no evidence of method.

**3. (a)** Quantity change = -0.5 x 8 = -4% [1]. New quantity = 2,400 x 0.96 = **2,304 packets** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New price = 5.00 x 1.08 = 5.40, so revenue is 5.40 x 2,304 = 12,441.60 against 12,000 before [1]. Revenue **rises by 441.60** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculate old and new revenue separately and state the change; this avoids relying on the rule alone.

**4. (a)** Price change = -4 / 20 = -20% [1]. Quantity change = +50 / 100 = +50% [1]. PED = 50 / -20 = **-2.5** (price elastic) [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Axes: price (vertical), quantity (horizontal). Revenue rectangle before: 20 x 100 = 2,000 [1]. Rectangle after: 16 x 150 = 2,400, so the larger rectangle shows revenue rising by 400 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A revenue diagram needs both rectangles labelled with price and quantity, plus a clear statement of which is larger.

**5. (a)** A brand of cereal usually has many close rivals, so consumers can switch easily [1]. Demand is therefore relatively elastic [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Salt is a very small share of income, so a price rise is barely noticed [1]. Demand is therefore relatively inelastic [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** In the short run drivers cannot change vehicles or journeys, so demand is inelastic [1]. Over time they can switch to efficient cars or other transport, so demand becomes more elastic [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each determinant to the named good and give the direction of the effect; a list of determinants without application is thin.

**6. (a)** At P = 10, Q = 180; at P = 12, Q = 168. Price +20%, quantity -6.67% [1]. PED = **-0.33** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At P = 30, Q = 60; at P = 32, Q = 48. Price +6.67%, quantity -20% [1]. PED = **-3.0** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The slope is the same, but PED size is below 1 at the lower price and above 1 at the higher price, so PED changes along a straight demand curve [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the starting price and quantity for each range, because the result depends on where on the line you begin.

**7. (a)** Income change = 300 / 3,000 = +10% [1]. Quantity change = 42 / 120 = +35% [1]. YED = 35 / 10 = **+3.5** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** YED is positive and above 1: a normal good with income elastic demand, typical of a luxury or service [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the sign and the size in your classification shows you understand both what the number means and why.

**8. (a)** Income change = 400 / 4,000 = +10% [1]. Quantity change = -0.5 x 10 = -5%, so new quantity = **190** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Income up the side, quantity along the base, both axes labelled [1]. Plot (200, 4,000) and (190, 4,400) [1]. Draw the curve sloping backwards, because quantity falls as income rises (an inferior good) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Label both axes with income and quantity, because an Engel curve with unlabelled or reversed axes is easy to misread.

**9. (a)** Agriculture 0.3 x 5 = **+1.5%** [1]. Manufactures 1.1 x 5 = **+5.5%** [1]. Services 1.6 x 5 = **+8.0%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Demand for agriculture grows more slowly than income [1]. Demand for services grows faster than income [1]. Over time the share of services in output rises and the share of agriculture falls, which changes the sectoral structure [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Move from the numbers to the economy-wide consequence; stopping at the calculations leaves the explanation unmade.

**10.** Indicative points, each worth **[1]**:
Elastic demand means the percentage rise in quantity exceeds the percentage fall in price [1]. A price cut then raises revenue because the extra sales outweigh the lower price on existing sales [1]. Example: price 2.00 to 1.80 (-10%), quantity 1,000 to 1,200 (+20%), PED = -2.0, revenue 2,000 to 2,160 [1]. Demand is probably elastic because rolls have many close substitutes [1]. Against: revenue is not profit, and extra output has extra costs [1]. PED is an estimate for a particular range and may not hold for a larger cut [1]. Rivals may match the cut, removing the gain, or capacity limits may restrict output [1]. A judgement based on the objective and costs, such as yes if profit rises and no if costs rise faster [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Discuss requires a balanced review, so give arguments on both sides and finish with a reasoned conclusion.

**11. (a)** Price +20%, passengers -12% [1]. PED = **-0.6** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Price +16.67%, passengers -9.09% [1]. PED = **-0.55** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 30 x 1,000 = 30,000; 36 x 880 = 31,680; 42 x 800 = 33,600 [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Demand is inelastic over both ranges, so revenue rises as fares rise; recommend fare 42 [1]. Caution: PED is estimated for these ranges only and may become elastic at higher fares, or costs and rival ferries may change the result [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A recommendation should rest on the calculated PED and name a limitation of the data.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking each percentage change from the end figure instead of the start.
- Inverting the PED formula by putting price change over quantity change.
- Quoting a size such as 0.6 and then calling demand elastic.
- Naming a determinant but never tying it to the named good.
- Drawing revenue rectangles without labelled axes or labelled figures.
- Saying revenue falls after a price rise without stating that demand is elastic.
- Describing PED as constant along a straight line, when the HL guide content shows it changes.
- Treating revenue as profit in a discussion.
- Answering "classify" with only "normal" and no mention of the size of YED.
