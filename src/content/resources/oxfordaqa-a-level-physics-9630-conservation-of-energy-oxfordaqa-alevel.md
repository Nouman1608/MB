This guide goes deeper into **3.2.8 Conservation of energy** in Section 3.2 Mechanics and materials of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. Energy methods often solve in one line a problem that would take several steps with forces and SUVAT, and they work even when the acceleration is not uniform.

## What the specification asks for

- the principle of conservation of energy
- **ΔEp = mgΔh** and **Ek = ½mv²**
- quantitative and qualitative application of energy conservation to examples involving gravitational potential energy, kinetic energy, elastic potential energy and work done against resistive forces.

## The principle

**Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred from one store to another.** The total energy of a closed system is constant.

In mechanics questions this becomes an energy account:

**energy at the start = energy at the end + work done against resistive forces**

Work done against friction or air resistance is not "lost" from the universe: it is transferred to the internal energy of the surroundings and the object (they warm up) and sometimes to sound.

## The energy stores in this subtopic

| Store | Equation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Kinetic | Ek = ½mv² | Depends on speed squared; always positive |
| Gravitational potential (change) | ΔEp = mgΔh | Δh is the vertical height change; valid where g is uniform |
| Elastic potential | E = ½FΔL = ½k(ΔL)² | From 3.2.9; area under a force–extension graph within the limit of proportionality |
| Work against resistive forces | W = Fs | F is the average resistive force; s the distance moved against it |

Only the **vertical** height change matters for ΔEp. A cyclist freewheeling down a winding road loses the same gravitational potential energy as one going straight down, if both drop the same height.

## Worked example 1: a pendulum

**Question.** A pendulum bob is pulled aside so that it rises 0.20 m above its lowest point, then released. Ignoring air resistance, find its speed at the lowest point.

**Method.** Ep lost = Ek gained: mgΔh = ½mv², so v = √(2gΔh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 0.20) = **1.98 m s⁻¹**.

**Comment.** The mass cancels. Any bob released from the same height has the same speed at the bottom, whatever its mass, as long as resistive forces can be ignored. The path is curved and the acceleration is not uniform, so SUVAT could not be used here.

## Worked example 2: speed after falling, whatever the direction of throw

**Question.** A stone is thrown at 12 m s⁻¹ from the top of a cliff 20 m above the sea. Ignoring air resistance, find its speed when it hits the water.

**Method.** ½mv² = ½mu² + mgΔh, so v² = u² + 2gΔh = 12² + 2 × 9.81 × 20 = 536.4, giving v = **23.2 m s⁻¹**.

**Comment.** The answer does not depend on the direction of the throw: upwards, downwards or horizontally, the stone lands at 23.2 m s⁻¹. Energy is a scalar. The direction of the final velocity does depend on the throw, but energy alone cannot give it.

## Worked example 3: a skier and resistive forces

**Question.** A 70 kg skier starts from rest and descends a slope 120 m long, dropping a vertical height of 25 m. At the bottom her speed is 18 m s⁻¹. Find the work done against resistive forces and the average resistive force.

**Method.**

1. Ep lost = 70 × 9.81 × 25 = 17 170 J.
2. Ek gained = ½ × 70 × 18² = 11 340 J.
3. Work against resistive forces = 17 170 − 11 340 = **5830 J** (5.8 kJ).
4. Average resistive force = 5830 ÷ 120 = **49 N**.

**Comment.** The resistive force acts along the whole 120 m path, so divide by the slope length, not by the 25 m height. About a third of the potential energy went into heating the snow, skis and air.

## Worked example 4: spring to kinetic to potential

**Question.** A spring of spring constant 400 N m⁻¹ is compressed by 0.030 m and used to fire a 0.020 kg ball vertically upwards. Ignoring air resistance, find the maximum height the ball reaches above its starting position.

**Method.**

1. Elastic potential energy = ½kx² = ½ × 400 × 0.030² = 0.18 J.
2. At the top all of it is gravitational potential energy: 0.020 × 9.81 × h = 0.18.
3. h = **0.92 m**.

**Comment.** The height is measured from the ball's starting position, with the spring compressed. The ball leaves the spring 0.030 m higher, so it rises about 0.89 m above the point where it loses contact. Read which reference point a question uses.

## Worked example 5: a bouncing ball

**Question.** A ball dropped from 1.50 m rebounds to 1.05 m. What fraction of its energy is transferred to other stores in the bounce?

**Method.** At the top of each flight the ball has only gravitational potential energy, so energy is proportional to height. Fraction kept = 1.05 ÷ 1.50 = 0.70. Fraction transferred = **0.30** (30%), mostly to internal energy of the ball and floor and to sound.

## Qualitative energy questions

Examiners also ask you to *describe* the transfers. Name each store at each stage and say where the work done against resistive forces goes.

**Example: a roller-coaster car.** A motor does work on the car to raise it up the first hill, increasing its gravitational potential energy. On the way down, gravitational potential energy is transferred to kinetic energy. Because of friction and air resistance, some energy is transferred to internal energy of the track, wheels and air, so the car cannot rise to the height of the first hill again. That is why the first hill is always the highest.

**Example: a bungee jump.** On the way down: gravitational potential energy → kinetic energy; once the cord is taut, gravitational potential and kinetic energy → elastic potential energy. At the lowest point the jumper is momentarily at rest, so kinetic energy is zero and elastic potential energy is at its maximum. Some energy is transferred to internal energy in the cord and the air at every stage.

## Common mistakes

- Using the length of a slope instead of the vertical height in mgΔh.
- Forgetting to square the speed, or squaring the whole ½mv.
- Saying energy is "lost" without saying where it goes.
- Dividing work against friction by the height instead of the distance travelled.
- Leaving out the initial kinetic energy when an object is thrown rather than dropped.
- Using energy to find a direction: energy gives speed only.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** the principle of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between stores; the total is constant.
- **Calculate**: write the energy account in words first ("Ep lost = Ek gained + work against friction"), then substitute.
- **Show that**: give the value one significant figure more precisely than the value quoted.
- **Describe** energy changes: name every store, in order, and include the transfer to internal energy.
- **Explain** why a real result is smaller than an ideal one, such as a lower rebound height: work is done against resistive forces, transferring energy to internal energy of the surroundings.
