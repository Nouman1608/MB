## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following best describes the internal working model? Choose **one** letter.

A An innate behaviour that triggers care from adults
B A mental template of relationships based on the primary attachment
C The time window in which attachment must form
D A secondary reinforcer associated with food

**[1]**

**2.** Which **one** of the following was found by van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988)? Choose **one** letter.

A Insecure-resistant attachment was the most common type in Japan
B Variation within countries was greater than variation between countries
C Secure attachment was rare outside the USA
D Insecure-avoidant attachment was the most common type in West Germany

**[1]**

**3.** Outline Harlow's research into attachment in monkeys. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a critical period, with reference to Bowlby's monotropic theory. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** criticism of learning theory as an explanation of attachment. **[3]**

### Section B: applied and data questions

**6.** An observer recorded two infants in the Strange Situation.

- Infant 1 played happily with toys, showed little reaction when the mother left or when the stranger entered, and continued playing when the mother returned.
- Infant 2 stayed close to the mother and explored little, screamed when she left, and on her return clung to her and then kicked to be put down.

Identify the attachment type of each infant and justify your answers with behaviours from the descriptions. **[4]**

**7.** Researchers used the Strange Situation in two countries. In Country A, 72 of 120 infants were secure, 30 insecure-avoidant and 18 insecure-resistant. In Country B, 96 of 160 infants were secure, 24 insecure-avoidant and 40 insecure-resistant.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of infants classed as insecure-resistant in each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the researchers should be cautious about concluding that culture caused the difference. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what is meant by an imposed etic, using this study as an example. **[2]**

**8.** In the 1950s, Ellie, aged 18 months, spent three weeks in hospital, and her parents were allowed to visit for only one hour a week.

**(a)** Use Bowlby's theory of maternal deprivation to explain why a psychologist at the time might have been concerned about Ellie. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **one** limitation of the theory. **[2]**

**9.** Two children were raised in the same poor-quality institution. Nina was adopted by a British family at four months old; Luca was adopted at two and a half years old.

Use the findings of the English and Romanian Adoptees project to explain how their development might differ. **[4]**

**10.** Ruby, aged 24, was classed as insecure-avoidant as an infant. She tells a friend she prefers not to rely on partners and feels uncomfortable when they want to be close. Use the internal working model to explain Ruby's adult relationships. **[3]**

### Section C: extended writing

**11.** Evaluate learning theory as an explanation of attachment. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss the influence of early attachment on childhood and adult relationships. Refer to the internal working model in your answer. **[16]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A describes social releasers, C a critical period and D the caregiver in learning theory.

**2.** B [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Secure attachment was the most common type in every country, so A and D are wrong even though those types were relatively high there.

**3.** Harlow reared infant rhesus monkeys with two wire "mothers", one plain and one covered in soft cloth [1]. Even when only the wire mother dispensed milk, the monkeys spent most time with the cloth mother and clung to it when frightened [1]. This showed contact comfort mattered more than food [1]. Monkeys reared without real mothers were later aggressive and unsociable, bred less and were poor mothers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The later effects of maternal deprivation earn the fourth mark; without them, the answer covers only half the research.

**4.** A critical period is a fixed time window, which Bowlby put at about the first two and a half years [1], during which an attachment must form; if it does not, Bowlby argued, it will be much harder to form later and development will be harmed [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the approximate time to show the link to Bowlby specifically.

**5.** Learning theory claims attachment depends on food as a primary reinforcer [1]. Harlow found monkeys attached to a cloth mother that gave no food rather than a wire mother that did [1]. This suggests contact comfort, not feeding, is the basis of attachment, which challenges the central claim of the theory [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Point, evidence, then what it means for the theory: three sentences, three marks.

**6.** Infant 1 is insecure-avoidant [1]: low separation anxiety (little reaction when the mother left) and little response at reunion (continued playing) [1]. Infant 2 is insecure-resistant [1]: high separation anxiety (screaming) and both seeking and resisting contact at reunion (clinging, then kicking to be put down) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each justification mark needs behaviours quoted from the description, not a textbook definition.

**7. (a)** Country A: 18/120 × 100 = 15% [1]. Country B: 40/160 × 100 = 25% [1].
**(b)** Each sample may not represent its whole country; for example, both may come from one city or social class [1]. Variation within countries can be larger than between them, so the difference may reflect the samples chosen rather than culture [1].
**(c)** An imposed etic is applying a method developed in one culture as if it were valid in all [1]. The Strange Situation was developed in the USA; if infants in Country B are rarely separated from their mothers, high distress may reflect unfamiliarity, not insecure attachment [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare percentages, not raw numbers; the samples are different sizes.

**8. (a)** Bowlby argued that continuous care from the mother is essential during the critical period, up to about two and a half years [1]. Ellie, at 18 months, experienced prolonged separation with very limited contact [1]. Bowlby would predict lasting harm, such as emotional problems including affectionless psychopathy or intellectual delay [1].
**(b)** The 44 thieves evidence is retrospective and correlational [1], and Bowlby made the diagnoses himself, so the link between separation and later problems may be due to bias or other factors, such as family conflict [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply Ellie's age and the visiting restriction; a description of the theory without them earns one mark in (a).

**9.** Nina was adopted before about six months, so the research suggests she is likely to recover well and develop normally, catching up intellectually [1] [1]. Luca was adopted much later, so he is more likely to show disinhibited attachment, being indiscriminately friendly to strangers [1], and to have lower intellectual ability on average [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The six-month point is the key finding to use; refer to each child's age.

**10.** The internal working model is a template of relationships formed from the primary attachment [1]. Ruby's avoidant infant attachment would have taught her that caregivers are unresponsive, so she expects partners to be the same [1]. As a result she avoids depending on them and is uncomfortable with closeness, matching the adult avoidant style Hazan and Shaver described [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use Ruby's own words in the answer to secure the application marks.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. These indicative points show the content a strong answer could use.

- Learning theory explains attachment through classical conditioning (caregiver associated with food) and operant conditioning (feeding reduces the hunger drive) [1].
- Harlow's monkeys preferred the cloth mother, showing comfort mattered more than food [1].
- Lorenz's goslings imprinted on him before feeding, suggesting attachment can be innate rather than learned [1].
- Human infants often form their strongest attachment to the most responsive person, not the one who feeds them [1].
- The theory does explain why infants seek caregivers who meet their needs, and conditioning can play some part [1].
- It is a reductionist account, reducing a complex emotional bond to stimulus–response links [1].
- Much supporting conditioning research was carried out with animals, limiting generalisation to humans [1].
- Judgement: learning theory may explain some attachment behaviour, but innate explanations such as Bowlby's account for the evidence better [1].

**[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation-only question still benefits from one sentence of description, but the marks are for evaluation.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. A strong answer might use this plan.

*AO1 (description)*

- Bowlby proposed that the primary attachment creates an internal working model [1].
- The model contains expectations about whether others are reliable and whether the self is worth caring for [1].
- It is predicted to shape childhood relationships, such as friendships and peer relationships [1].
- It is predicted to shape adult romantic relationships [1].
- It is also predicted to shape adults' parenting, passing attachment types between generations [1].
- Hazan and Shaver (1987) collected replies to a newspaper "love quiz" about relationship experiences and early attachment [1].
- Adult attachment types appeared in roughly similar proportions to infant types [1].
- Secure adults reported longer-lasting, happier relationships than insecure adults [1].

*AO3 (evaluation)*

- Hazan and Shaver support continuity between early attachment and adult love [1].
- The evidence is correlational, so other factors, such as family conflict, could explain both early and later relationships [1].
- Adults' recall of childhood relationships is retrospective and may be inaccurate [1].
- The temperament hypothesis (Kagan) suggests an innate temperament could shape both infant attachment and later relationships [1].
- Many people with insecure early attachment form secure adult relationships, so the model is a tendency, not a fixed outcome [1].
- The theory is deterministic if taken literally, which could lead people to blame early experiences for current problems [1].
- Late-adopted children in the English and Romanian Adoptees project could still form attachments, showing change is possible [1].
- Judgement: early attachment influences later relationships, but the influence is probabilistic and interacts with later experience [1].

**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* The question names both childhood and adult relationships; covering only adult relationships limits the answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming attachment types without quoting behaviours from the scenario.
- Comparing raw numbers rather than percentages across samples of different sizes.
- Saying a type other than secure was most common in a country.
- Mixing up the monotropic theory and the maternal deprivation theory.
- Ignoring the age at adoption in institutionalisation questions.
- Treating the internal working model as fixed.
- Leaving out a judgement in 8- and 16-mark answers.
