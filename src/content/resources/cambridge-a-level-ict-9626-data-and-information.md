## Data and information

**Data** is raw facts, figures or symbols that have no context or meaning on their own. The values `42`, `Okafor` and `14/03/2026` are data. You cannot tell what they describe.

**Information** is data that has been given **context and meaning**, so that a person can understand it and use it to make a decision. "Student Okafor scored 42 out of 50 in the test on 14 March 2026" is information. The same three values are now linked to a person, a score and a date.

The key phrase is the one used in the syllabus: data becomes information **through context and meaning**. Processing (sorting, calculating, labelling, presenting in a table or chart) is usually how context is added, but the context itself is what makes the difference.

### Worked example 1: turning data into information

A school records these values from a sensor in a classroom: `19`, `21`, `24`, `26`.

1. On its own, the list is data. It could be shoe sizes, ages or temperatures.
2. Add context: the readings are air temperatures in degrees Celsius, taken at 09:00, 11:00, 13:00 and 15:00 in Room 12.
3. Add meaning by processing: the temperature rose by 7 degrees in six hours and passed 25 degrees at 15:00.
4. The caretaker can now act. The room is getting too warm in the afternoon, so the blinds should be closed or a window opened.

Notice that the numbers did not change. The context and the interpretation did.

### One person's information is another system's data

The same item can be information to one person and raw data to another system. A single receipt is information for the customer. To a supermarket's analysis software, thousands of receipts are data that can be processed into information about which products sell best on Fridays. This is why exam answers should always refer to the **context** rather than simply saying that information is "processed data".

## Direct data

**Direct data** is data collected **first-hand, for the specific purpose** the collector has in mind. The person who will use it designs the collection, so it is tailored to the question.

### Sources of direct data

| Source | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire | A set of written or online questions answered by many people | Finding customer opinions about a new service |
| Interview | A researcher asks questions face to face, by phone or by video call | Finding detailed reasons for a user's views |
| Data logging | Sensors record readings automatically over time | Recording river level, temperature or pollution |
| Observation | The researcher watches and records what happens | Counting how many people use a footpath, or watching how staff use a system |

**Questionnaires.** They reach many people cheaply and the answers are easy to compare, especially with tick-box questions. The weaknesses are that people may not return them, may give rushed or dishonest answers, and closed questions cannot capture an unexpected view.

**Interviews.** The interviewer can explain a question, follow up an interesting answer and read body language. The weaknesses are that they take a lot of time, only a few people can be interviewed, and the interviewer's manner can influence the answers.

**Data logging.** Sensors take readings at fixed intervals with no human present, so the readings are accurate, frequent and available at any hour, including dangerous or remote places. The weaknesses are the cost of the equipment and the fact that a faulty or badly placed sensor produces wrong data in large quantity.

**Observation.** It shows what people actually do rather than what they say they do. The weaknesses are that it is slow, people may behave differently when they know they are watched, and the observer may record events subjectively.

### Advantages of direct data

- It is collected for the exact purpose, so it is **relevant** and in the right format.
- The collector **knows its source** and how it was gathered, so its reliability can be judged.
- It is usually **up to date**, because it has just been collected.
- The collector **controls** which questions are asked, so no unwanted detail has to be filtered out.
- The data belongs to the collector, so it may give a competitive edge.

### Disadvantages of direct data

- It can be **slow and expensive** to collect, especially with a large sample.
- It may need **specialist equipment** or trained staff.
- A **small sample** may not represent the whole population.
- Collecting personal data brings **legal and ethical duties**, such as consent and secure storage.

## Indirect data

**Indirect data** was **originally collected for some other purpose** and is then reused by someone else. The person using it did not design the collection and may know little about how it was gathered.

### Sources of indirect data

| Source | What it is | Example of reuse |
|---|---|---|
| Weather data | Records published by a national weather service | A farmer choosing when to plant a crop |
| Census data | Population counts collected by a government | A council planning how many school places are needed |
| Electoral register | A list of people registered to vote | A market researcher selecting households in an area |
| Businesses collecting personal information, used by third parties | Customer details gathered by one organisation and sold or shared | An advertiser targeting people who have bought similar products |
| Research from textbooks, journals and websites | Published findings and statistics | A student writing a report using existing studies |

### Advantages of indirect data

- It is **quick and cheap** to obtain, because someone else has already done the work.
- It can cover a **very large scale**, such as a whole country's population, that one organisation could not collect itself.
- It may cover **long periods**, allowing trends over many years to be seen.
- Official sources are often **checked and reliable**.

### Disadvantages of indirect data

- It may be **out of date**, for example a census taken years ago.
- It may be **irrelevant or in the wrong format**, so it needs cleaning and reorganising.
- The collector's **methods are unknown**, so its accuracy cannot always be checked.
- It may be **biased**, for example research funded by a company with an interest in the result.
- There may be **copyright or licence** restrictions, or a cost to buy it.
- Personal data obtained from third parties raises **privacy issues**, because the people involved may not have agreed to this new use.

### Worked example 2: choosing direct or indirect data

Marsh Lane Bakery wants to decide whether to open a second shop. Consider two tasks.

**Task A: find out how many people live within 2 km of the proposed site and their age groups.** Indirect data is the better choice. Census data already gives population by area and age, and the bakery could not afford to survey thousands of households. The disadvantage is that the census may be several years old, so the owner should check how recent it is.

**Task B: find out which new flavour of bread local people would buy.** Direct data is the better choice. No existing source will answer the question for this shop's customers. A short questionnaire at the till, plus a tasting day where staff observe reactions, gives relevant and current answers.

A strong answer names the type, links it to the purpose and states one limitation. In many real projects both are used: indirect data to size the market, direct data to test the product.

## Choosing between them

| Question to ask | Points towards direct | Points towards indirect |
|---|---|---|
| Does an existing source answer the question? | No | Yes |
| How much time and money are available? | Plenty | Little |
| How specific must the data be? | Very specific | General is acceptable |
| How much does freshness matter? | Very important | Less important |
| Can the collector check reliability? | Needed | Source is trusted |

## Common mistakes

- Defining information as "processed data" without mentioning **context and meaning**.
- Saying direct data is "more accurate". It is more **relevant** and its reliability is known, but a badly designed questionnaire can be very inaccurate.
- Treating indirect data as always poor. Government statistics are often more reliable than a small survey.
- Listing sources without saying which type they are. Questionnaires, interviews, data logging and observation are **direct**. Census data, weather records, the electoral register, data bought from businesses and published research are **indirect**.
- Giving a generic advantage such as "it is cheaper" without saying why, for example "because the data has already been collected by someone else".
- Forgetting that the same data can be direct for the collector and indirect for the next user.

## Exam technique

- **Define** command: one precise statement, for example "Data is raw facts and figures with no context or meaning." Then add the contrasting definition for information.
- **Describe** a source: say how the data is collected, not just its name.
- **Compare** direct and indirect data: use words such as "whereas" and make each point about both sides.
- **Suggest** command: apply the idea to the scenario. Name the type, give a source, and link it to the purpose.
- When asked for a **disadvantage**, make it specific to the type of data and the situation. "It takes a long time to survey 2,000 customers" scores better than "it is slow".
