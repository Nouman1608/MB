This guide goes deeper into **3.3.5.1 Oxidation of alcohols**, the first subtopic of section 3.3.5 Alcohols in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2 (Organic 1 and Physical 1) and open to the synoptic Unit 5 paper. The [alcohols study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-alcohols/) introduces the whole section; this page adds full redox equations, boiling-point reasoning, structure deduction and more practice with naming.

## What the specification asks for

- "Alcohols are classified as primary, secondary and tertiary."
- "Primary alcohols can be oxidised to aldehydes which can be further oxidised to carboxylic acids."
- "Secondary alcohols can be oxidised to ketones."
- "Tertiary alcohols are not easily oxidised."
- "Acidified potassium dichromate(VI) is a suitable oxidising agent."

Skills: write equations for these oxidations ("equations showing [O] as oxidant are acceptable"); explain how the method used to oxidise a primary alcohol determines whether an aldehyde or carboxylic acid is obtained; and use chemical tests, including Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent, to distinguish aldehydes from ketones.

## Classifying alcohols systematically

Classify by the number of carbon atoms bonded to the carbon that carries the –OH group. A good test of the method is to classify every alcohol with formula C₅H₁₂O.

| Alcohol | Structure | Carbons on C–OH carbon | Class |
|---|---|---|---|
| pentan-1-ol | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂OH | 1 | primary |
| pentan-2-ol | CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₂CH₃ | 2 | secondary |
| pentan-3-ol | CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ | 2 | secondary |
| 2-methylbutan-1-ol | HOCH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ | 1 | primary |
| 3-methylbutan-1-ol | HOCH₂CH₂CH(CH₃)₂ | 1 | primary |
| 2-methylbutan-2-ol | (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂CH₃ | 3 | tertiary |
| 3-methylbutan-2-ol | CH₃CH(OH)CH(CH₃)₂ | 2 | secondary |
| 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol | (CH₃)₃CCH₂OH | 1 | primary |

Four primary, three secondary, one tertiary. Note 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol: the molecule is crowded, but its C–OH carbon is bonded to only one carbon, so it is primary and can be oxidised.

### What oxidation removes

Oxidation to C=O removes **two hydrogen atoms**: the H of the –OH group and an H on the C–OH carbon. That is why the hydrogen count on the C–OH carbon predicts the outcome:

- two H (primary): one H is removed to make –CHO, and the remaining aldehyde H can be replaced by OH in a second step to make –COOH;
- one H (secondary): it is removed to make C=O, and no H is left, so a ketone is the end point;
- no H (tertiary): a C=O could only form by breaking a C–C bond, which acidified dichromate does not do.

Methanol, CH₃OH, has three H on its C–OH carbon and behaves like a primary alcohol, giving methanal and then methanoic acid.

## Writing the equations

### With [O]

[O] stands for an oxygen atom supplied by the oxidising agent. Balance atoms only; charges do not appear.

- Primary to aldehyde: RCH₂OH + [O] → RCHO + H₂O
- Aldehyde to acid: RCHO + [O] → RCOOH
- Primary to acid directly: RCH₂OH + 2[O] → RCOOH + H₂O
- Secondary to ketone: R₂CHOH + [O] → R₂CO + H₂O

### As full redox equations (a synoptic extension)

The [O] shorthand hides the dichromate. Questions that link to [redox](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-oxidation-reduction-and-redox-equations/) may ask for the full equation, built from half-equations:

```
Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O               (reduction: orange → green)
CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ → CH₃COCH₃ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻                (oxidation of propan-2-ol)
× 3 and add, then cancel 6H⁺:
Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 8H⁺ + 3CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ → 2Cr³⁺ + 3CH₃COCH₃ + 7H₂O
```

For a primary alcohol going all the way to the acid, the alcohol half-equation releases four electrons, for example CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + H₂O → CH₃CH₂COOH + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻, so the ratio becomes 2 Cr₂O₇²⁻ : 3 alcohol.

## How the method decides the product

The key physical fact is the **boiling point order** for compounds with the same carbon skeleton. For the three-carbon series:

| Compound | Boiling point / °C | Strongest forces between molecules |
|---|---|---|
| propanal | about 48 | permanent dipole–dipole (C=O) |
| propan-1-ol | about 97 | hydrogen bonds (O–H) |
| propanoic acid | about 141 | hydrogen bonds (O–H and C=O, stronger overall) |

The aldehyde has no O–H group, so its molecules cannot hydrogen bond to each other, and it boils well below both the alcohol and the acid.

- **To make propanal:** heat propan-1-ol (in excess) gently with acidified dichromate in apparatus set for distillation. Propanal is the most volatile substance present, so it distils out **as it forms** and is not oxidised further.
- **To make propanoic acid:** heat with **excess** acidified dichromate **under reflux**. Any propanal that boils off condenses and returns, so it stays in contact with the oxidising agent until it has become the acid. The acid is distilled off afterwards.

## Telling aldehydes from ketones

Aldehydes are oxidised by mild oxidising agents; ketones are not.

| Test | Positive (aldehyde) | Ketone | What is reduced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm with Fehling's solution | blue solution → red precipitate | stays blue | Cu²⁺ → Cu⁺ (in Cu₂O) |
| Warm with Tollens' reagent | silver mirror | no silver mirror | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + e⁻ → Ag + 2NH₃ |

Warm in a water bath, because organic compounds are flammable. Acidified dichromate cannot tell an aldehyde from a primary or secondary alcohol, because all three turn it green.

### Worked example 1: deducing a structure

*Alcohol Q, C₄H₁₀O, turns acidified potassium dichromate(VI) green when the product is distilled off as it forms. The distillate gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent. Q has a branched chain. Identify Q and the distillate.*

- Green colour: Q is primary or secondary.
- Silver mirror: the distillate is an aldehyde, so Q is **primary**.
- Primary C₄H₁₀O alcohols: butan-1-ol (unbranched) and 2-methylpropan-1-ol (branched).
- Q is **2-methylpropan-1-ol**, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH; the distillate is **2-methylpropanal**, (CH₃)₂CHCHO.

### Worked example 2: a yield calculation

*3.00 g of propan-1-ol (Mr 60.0) is heated under reflux with excess acidified dichromate. 2.96 g of propanoic acid (Mr 74.0) is collected. Calculate the percentage yield.*

```
n(propan-1-ol)    = 3.00 ÷ 60.0 = 0.0500 mol
n(propanoic acid) = 2.96 ÷ 74.0 = 0.0400 mol
yield             = 0.0400 ÷ 0.0500 × 100 = 80.0 %
```

## Common mistakes

- Classifying by counting all carbons in the molecule, or by branching.
- Writing –COH for an aldehyde (it is –CHO).
- Using one [O] for the direct primary-to-acid equation; it needs 2[O].
- Leaving H₂O out of alcohol-to-aldehyde or alcohol-to-ketone equations.
- Saying the aldehyde boils lower because it has "weaker bonds" or "a smaller mass"; the reason is no hydrogen bonding between its molecules.
- Giving "no reaction" instead of "stays blue" or "no silver mirror" for a ketone.
- Using dichromate to distinguish an aldehyde from an alcohol.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Classify**: state the number of carbons bonded to the C–OH carbon.
- **Write an equation**: [O] is accepted; balance and include water where formed.
- **Explain how the method determines the product**: name the apparatus (distillation or reflux), the reagent in excess and the boiling-point reason.
- **Describe a test**: reagent, warm in water bath, observation for **both** compounds.
- **Deduce**: link each observation to a conclusion before naming the compound.
