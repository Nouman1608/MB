## Key definitions

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Biodiversity | The variety of living organisms, considered from a small local habitat up to the whole Earth |
| Community | The populations of all the different species that live together in one habitat |
| Species richness | A count of the different species in a community |
| Index of diversity | A measure relating the number of species in a community to the number of individuals in each species |
| Monoculture | One crop grown over a large area (an example of a farming technique) |

## Scales of biodiversity

- Small local habitat: a pond, a hedge base, a rotting log.
- Larger habitat: a woodland, a lake, a large field.
- Region or country: built up from many surveys.
- The Earth: only estimated, because many species are not yet described.
- Compare like with like: same scale, same sampling method, same effort.

## Richness and the index of diversity

| Measure | What it uses | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Species richness | Number of species only | Ignores how many individuals there are of each, so an even community and a dominated one can score the same |
| Index of diversity, d | Number of species and individuals in each | Needs individuals to be counted, and is unreliable for small samples |

**Formula**

d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1)

N is the total number of organisms of all species. n is the number of organisms of one species. Σ means the sum over all species.

### Method in steps

1. Find N by adding every count. Work out N(N − 1).
2. For each species work out n(n − 1).
3. Add those to get Σn(n − 1).
4. Divide: d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1).
5. Give 2 or 3 significant figures, then say what the value means.

**Worked reminder.** A sample has species counts 8, 4 and 2. N = 14, so N(N − 1) = 14 × 13 = 182. Σn(n − 1) = 56 + 12 + 2 = 70. d = 182 ÷ 70 = **2.6**.

**Second worked reminder.** Site 1 has counts 16, 4 and 1 and site 2 has 9, 8 and 4. Both have N = 21, so N(N − 1) = 420. Site 1: Σn(n − 1) = 240 + 12 + 0 = 252, so d = 420 ÷ 252 = **1.67**. Site 2: Σn(n − 1) = 72 + 56 + 12 = 140, so d = 420 ÷ 140 = **3.0**. Both have three species, but site 2 has the more even spread and so the higher d.

**Special cases**

- One species only: d = 1, the lowest possible value.
- Every species has one individual: Σn(n − 1) = 0, so d cannot be calculated.
- A species with n = 0 is not in the sample, so ignore it.
- A species with n = 1 adds 0 to the denominator.
- Equal counts across species give a high d. One dominant species gives a low d.

## Interpreting d

- Higher d: more species and a more even spread, so a more diverse community.
- Lower d: fewer species or one dominating, often a community more vulnerable to change.
- Compare values only when sampling effort and method are the same.
- Say whether the sample is large enough and random. Small samples may miss species.
- Describe the difference in words and quote both values, for example "d is higher at A (4.7) than at B (1.6)".
- For percentage change: (new − old) ÷ old × 100.

## Farming and biodiversity

| Technique | Effect on biodiversity |
|---|---|
| Monoculture | Fewer plant species, so fewer food sources and habitats for animals |
| Hedgerow removal | Loss of nesting, shelter and food, and fewer routes between habitats |
| Herbicides | Wild plants killed, so less food for insects and animals that eat them |
| Pesticides | Non-target insects killed, so less food for insect-eating animals |
| Drainage, ploughing grassland | Habitat lost or changed |
| Fertiliser | Nutrient run-off can alter nearby water communities |
| Overgrazing | Fewer plant species survive |

Chain to write: habitat or food removed, so fewer niches, so fewer species, so lower d.

## Conservation and farming: the balance

- Farming gives food and income, and intensive methods give high yield from less land.
- Conservation protects species, and wild species can help farming, such as pollinators and pest predators.
- Compromises: hedgerows kept, sown field margins and buffer strips, crop rotation, fallow land, fewer pesticides, lower stocking density, protected wet areas.
- Evaluate with data: how much did d rise, and how much did yield fall?
- Give a judgement: say which factors matter most and why.

## Answering the command words

| Command word | What to write |
|---|---|
| Calculate | Show N(N − 1), each n(n − 1), the total and the division, then the answer to a sensible number of significant figures |
| Compare | Give both values of d, say which is higher, and link the difference to richness and evenness |
| Explain | Give the mechanism: what changed in the habitat, why that changes the species present, and what happens to d |
| Suggest | Offer a reasonable biological reason that fits the data supplied |
| Evaluate | Weigh evidence on both sides, say what the data cannot show, then reach a conclusion |

## Sampling points for diversity data

- Use random sampling, for example random coordinates for quadrats or trap positions, so that the sample is not biased by where you choose to look.
- Use the same method, effort and time of year in each habitat being compared.
- Take enough samples that rare species are likely to appear.
- Repeat on different days if you can, and quote values with a sensible number of significant figures.

## Must-know distinctions

- Richness counts species; the index uses species and numbers.
- Species richness is not the same as the number of individuals.
- Community means many species in a habitat; population means one species.
- Higher d means more varied, not more abundant.
- The index is calculated from a sample, not from the entire community.

## Quick self-test

1. Define species richness.
2. A sample has two species with 10 individuals each. Calculate d to 3 significant figures.
3. A sample has two species with 9 and 1 individuals. Calculate d to 3 significant figures.
4. A sample has counts 7, 5, 3 and 1. Calculate d to 3 significant figures.
5. A sample contains 20 individuals, all of one species. What is d?
6. Six species with 3 individuals each. Calculate d.
7. Why is d a better measure than richness for comparing two meadows?
8. Explain why a species with one individual adds nothing to Σn(n − 1).
9. Give two ways hedgerow removal reduces biodiversity.
10. Why should two values of d be compared only if the sampling effort was the same?
11. A farm adds sown field margins and d rises from 1.5 to 4.9. State one further piece of data needed before judging the margins a success.
12. Name two features of a sample that make a calculated d unreliable.

### Answers

1. A count of the different species in a community.
2. N = 20, N(N − 1) = 380. Σn(n − 1) = 90 + 90 = 180. d = 380 ÷ 180 = **2.11**.
3. N = 10, N(N − 1) = 90. Σn(n − 1) = 72 + 0 = 72. d = 90 ÷ 72 = **1.25**.
4. N = 16, N(N − 1) = 240. Σn(n − 1) = 42 + 20 + 6 + 0 = 68. d = 240 ÷ 68 = **3.53**.
5. N(N − 1) = 380 and Σn(n − 1) = 380, so d = **1** (the lowest possible value).
6. N = 18, N(N − 1) = 306. Each species gives 6, so Σn(n − 1) = 36. d = 306 ÷ 36 = **8.5**.
7. It uses the number of individuals of each species as well as the number of species, so it shows whether one species dominates.
8. n(n − 1) = 1 × 0 = 0.
9. Any two from: loss of nesting sites or shelter; loss of food sources; fewer routes between habitats; loss of plant species.
10. More traps, nights or area would catch more individuals and species, so a difference in d could be due to effort instead of the habitat.
11. The effect on crop yield (or on income), so the gain in biodiversity can be weighed against the cost.
12. A small number of individuals sampled; sampling that is not random; species wrongly identified; a sample taken at only one time of year.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using n² or N² instead of n(n − 1) and N(N − 1).
- Adding the species counts for N but forgetting to subtract 1 inside N(N − 1).
- Including species with n = 0 as if they were present.
- Dividing the wrong way round, so d comes out below 1.
- Stating richness as a number of individuals.
- Describing d only as "higher" without saying it means more species or a more even spread.
- Giving a farming effect with no mechanism, such as "pesticides harm wildlife" instead of "kill insects that are food for birds".
- Evaluating the conservation and farming balance from one side only.
- Comparing d values without commenting on sample size or method.
