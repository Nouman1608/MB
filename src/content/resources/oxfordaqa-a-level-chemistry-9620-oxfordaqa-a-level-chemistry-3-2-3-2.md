This guide goes deeper into **3.2.3.2 Uses of chlorine and chlorate(I)** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification. The point covers "the reaction of chlorine with water to form chloride ions and chlorate(I) ions", "the reaction of chlorine with water to form chloride ions and oxygen", the use of chlorine in water treatment, and "the reaction of chlorine with cold, dilute, aqueous NaOH and uses of the solution formed". You should also appreciate that society assesses the advantages and disadvantages when deciding if chemicals should be added to water supplies, and that the health benefits of water treatment by chlorine outweigh its toxic effects. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1.

## Names and oxidation states

| Species | Name | Oxidation state of Cl |
|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ | chlorine | 0 |
| Cl⁻ | chloride ion | −1 |
| HCl | hydrogen chloride (hydrochloric acid in water) | −1 |
| ClO⁻ | chlorate(I) ion | +1 |
| HClO | chloric(I) acid | +1 |
| NaClO | sodium chlorate(I) | +1 |

The Roman numeral in "chlorate(I)" is the oxidation state of chlorine. In ClO⁻, oxygen is −2 and the overall charge is −1, so chlorine must be +1.

## Disproportionation

A **disproportionation** reaction is one in which the same element is simultaneously oxidised and reduced. Chlorine starts at 0 and can go both up (to +1) and down (to −1) in the same reaction. Spotting this is a common exam task.

## Reaction 1: chlorine with water, forming chloride and chlorate(I)

```
Cl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + HClO(aq)
or, as ions:   Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ 2H⁺ + Cl⁻ + ClO⁻
```

- One chlorine atom goes from 0 to −1 (reduced); the other goes from 0 to +1 (oxidised). This is **disproportionation**.
- The reaction is reversible; in chlorine water a mixture of Cl₂, H⁺, Cl⁻ and chloric(I) acid is present.
- Both products are acids, so the solution is acidic. Chloric(I) acid is also a bleach. A few drops of universal indicator in chlorine water first turn red and then lose their colour.

## Reaction 2: chlorine with water in sunlight, forming chloride and oxygen

```
2Cl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 4H⁺(aq) + 4Cl⁻(aq) + O₂(g)
```

- Chlorine goes from 0 to −1 only, so it is reduced.
- Oxygen goes from −2 in water to 0 in O₂, so it is oxidised.
- This is a redox reaction but **not** disproportionation, because two different elements change oxidation state.

This reaction explains why chlorine water slowly loses its bleaching power when left in sunlight: chlorine is converted into chloride ions and oxygen gas escapes.

### Worked example 1: classifying three reactions

*For each reaction, state whether chlorine is oxidised, reduced or both, and whether it is disproportionation.*

```
(a) Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂             Cl: 0 → −1         reduced only; not disproportionation
(b) Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O       Cl: 0 → −1 and +1  both; disproportionation
(c) 2Cl₂ + 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4Cl⁻ + O₂       Cl: 0 → −1         reduced only; O oxidised; not disproportionation
```

## Chlorine in water treatment

Chlorine, or a chlorate(I) compound, is added to drinking water supplies to **kill bacteria** and other disease-causing micro-organisms. This prevents the spread of water-borne diseases such as cholera and typhoid. A small amount remains dissolved as the water travels through pipes, which keeps it safe until it reaches the tap.

The disadvantages:

- Chlorine is a **toxic** gas, so storing and handling it carries risk.
- Chlorine can react with organic matter in water to form **chlorinated organic compounds**, some of which are potentially harmful.
- Some people dislike the taste or smell of chlorinated water.

The specification asks you to **appreciate that the benefits to health outweigh the toxic effects**. The concentrations used are very low, while the diseases prevented can be fatal and spread rapidly through a whole population.

### How society decides whether to add a chemical to water

Adding anything to a public water supply affects everyone who uses it, so the decision balances:

- **benefit**: how much harm is prevented, and for how many people
- **risk**: toxicity at the dose used, and any harmful by-products
- **choice**: individuals cannot easily opt out of a public supply
- **alternatives**: other methods of treatment, and whether they give the same protection.

Chlorination is widely accepted because the benefit is large and immediate. Adding fluoride to water to reduce tooth decay is a contrasting example that is more debated, because the benefit is smaller and people disagree about the balance.

## Reaction 3: chlorine with cold, dilute sodium hydroxide

```
Cl₂(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + NaClO(aq) + H₂O(l)
ionic:   Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O
```

Chlorine is again **disproportionated**: 0 → −1 in NaCl and 0 → +1 in NaClO. The solution formed contains sodium chlorate(I), which is used as **bleach**. It is used for bleaching and for disinfecting surfaces, because it kills bacteria, and it can be used in water treatment.

Compare this with reaction 1. In water the equilibrium lies to the left, so little ClO⁻ forms. In sodium hydroxide the H⁺ that would be produced is removed by OH⁻, so the reaction goes to completion and much more chlorate(I) is formed.

### Worked example 2: making sodium chlorate(I)

*7.10 g of chlorine is bubbled into excess cold, dilute sodium hydroxide and the solution is made up to 500 cm³. Calculate the mass of sodium chlorate(I) formed and its concentration. (Mr: Cl₂ = 71.0, NaClO = 74.5)*

```
n(Cl₂)        = 7.10 / 71.0 = 0.100 mol
n(NaClO)      = 0.100 mol          (1 Cl₂ : 1 NaClO)
mass          = 0.100 × 74.5 = 7.45 g
concentration = 0.100 / 0.500 = 0.200 mol dm⁻³
```

### Worked example 3: oxygen from chlorine water

*In sunlight, a sample of chlorine water releases 2.40 × 10⁻³ mol of oxygen. Calculate the mass of chlorine that reacted and the amount of H⁺ formed.*

```
2Cl₂ : 1O₂ : 4H⁺
n(Cl₂) = 2 × 2.40 × 10⁻³ = 4.80 × 10⁻³ mol;   mass = 4.80 × 10⁻³ × 71.0 = 0.341 g
n(H⁺)  = 4 × 2.40 × 10⁻³ = 9.60 × 10⁻³ mol
```

## Common mistakes

- Calling the sunlight reaction disproportionation.
- Writing NaClO₃ (sodium chlorate(V)) for the bleach. The bleach is NaClO, chlorate(I).
- Giving the oxidation state of Cl in ClO⁻ as −1.
- Forgetting to say the reaction with NaOH needs cold, dilute alkali.
- Answering "evaluate chlorination" with only benefits, or only risks.
- Saying chlorine is added to water "to make it clean"; say it **kills bacteria**.

## Exam technique

- **"Explain why this is a disproportionation reaction"**: give the oxidation state of chlorine before and both after, and say the same element is both oxidised and reduced.
- **"Give a use of the solution formed"**: bleach, or disinfecting surfaces by killing bacteria.
- **"Evaluate"** or **"discuss"** chlorination: give at least one benefit, at least one risk, and a reasoned conclusion.
- **"Write an equation"**: both the full and ionic forms are usually credited, but balance charge in the ionic one.
