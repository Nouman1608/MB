Sub-topic 7.1 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus (2027-2029) is about where people live and how a population is made up. It has four outcomes: describing world population density and distribution (7.1.1), interpreting population pyramids (7.1.2), describing the World Bank income groups (7.1.3), and comparing the population structure of a low-income country (LIC) with that of a high-income country (HIC) (7.1.4). Most of the marks come from reading data accurately, so this guide concentrates on the skills of describing patterns and interpreting pyramids, with worked calculations.

For the whole of Topic 7, see the [human population study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-human-population-study-guide/).

## Density and distribution (7.1.1)

The syllabus gives three ideas:

- **Sparsely populated** places contain few people; **densely populated** places contain many people.
- **Population density** is the number of people living in an area, usually measured in **people per km²**.
- **Population distribution** is how people are spread over an area.

Density is a single number for an area. Distribution is the pattern. Two countries can have the same density but very different distributions: in one, people may be spread evenly; in the other, almost everyone may live in a few river valleys or coastal cities.

### Describing the world pattern

On a world map of density, the densely populated regions include South Asia, East Asia, South-East Asia and much of Europe, together with river valleys such as the Nile and coastal plains on every inhabited continent. Sparsely populated regions include hot deserts such as the Sahara, cold polar regions, high mountains, and dense tropical forest such as the Amazon basin. Antarctica has no permanent population.

To describe a distribution in an exam:

1. State the overall pattern ("uneven", "concentrated along the coast", "clustered in river valleys").
2. Name places or directions, using the map's labels or the 8-point compass.
3. Quote values from the key, with units.
4. Mention an exception (an isolated dense city in a sparse region, for example).

### Worked example 1: density hides distribution

*The figures are invented for practice.* A country has 52 million people and an area of 640 000 km².

- Overall density = 52 000 000 / 640 000 = **81.25 people per km²** (about 81).

But 80% of the people live on the 10% of the land that forms a fertile coastal plain.

- Coastal plain: 0.8 x 52 000 000 = 41 600 000 people on 0.1 x 640 000 = 64 000 km², so density = 41 600 000 / 64 000 = **650 people per km²**.
- Rest of the country: 10 400 000 people on 576 000 km², so density = **about 18 people per km²**.

The national figure of 81 describes almost nowhere in the country. This is why a question on distribution needs more than one density value.

## Interpreting population pyramids (7.1.2)

A population pyramid is a pair of horizontal bar charts placed back to back: males on one side, females on the other, with age groups (usually five-year bands) from youngest at the bottom to oldest at the top. Bars show the percentage (or number) of the population in each group.

The syllabus limits interpretation to three things.

**(a) Young and elderly dependent population.** Dependants are people who are usually not working and rely on others: the **young dependants** (under 15) and the **elderly dependants** (65 and over).

**(b) Working population.** The economically active age group, **15 to 64**. They pay most of the taxes that fund schools, healthcare and pensions.

**(c) Female and male population.** Compare the two sides. In most countries more boys than girls are born, but women usually live longer, so the top of the pyramid is often wider on the female side. A big difference among working-age adults may suggest migration, for example many young men leaving to work elsewhere.

### What shapes tell you

| Feature | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Wide base | Many young children: high birth rate |
| Sides narrowing quickly | Fewer people survive to older ages: higher death rate, lower life expectancy |
| Narrow base | Few children: low birth rate |
| Straight or bulging middle and wide top | Many people live to old age: low death rate, high life expectancy |
| A notch in one age band | A past event, such as a war or a period of very low births |
| One side much wider in working ages | Migration of one sex |

### Worked example 2: two pyramids as data

*The data are invented for practice.* Percentages of total population:

| Age group | Country A male | Country A female | Country B male | Country B female |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0-14 | 21.0 | 20.0 | 7.5 | 7.0 |
| 15-64 | 28.0 | 28.0 | 32.0 | 31.5 |
| 65+ | 1.4 | 1.6 | 9.5 | 12.5 |

Totals by group:

- Country A: young 41%, working 56%, elderly 3%.
- Country B: young 14.5%, working 63.5%, elderly 22%.

**Dependent population**: Country A = 41 + 3 = **44%**; Country B = 14.5 + 22 = **36.5%**.

**Dependency ratio** (dependants per 100 working-age people) = dependants / working population x 100.
- Country A: 44 / 56 x 100 = **78.6**.
- Country B: 36.5 / 63.5 x 100 = **57.5**.

**Female and male population**: in Country B women make up 12.5 / 22 x 100 = **56.8%** of the over-65s, showing longer female life expectancy.

**Interpretation**: Country A's dependants are mostly young, so it needs schools, child healthcare and future jobs. Country B's dependants are increasingly elderly, so it needs pensions, healthcare for older people and care homes, with fewer young people entering the workforce.

## World Bank income groups (7.1.3)

The World Bank classifies countries by **gross national income (GNI) per person**, which is roughly the average income per person in a year. The syllabus names three groups:

- **Low-income countries (LICs)**
- **Middle-income countries (MICs)**, which the World Bank divides further into lower-middle and upper-middle income
- **High-income countries (HICs)**

The dollar thresholds between the groups are revised each year, so you do not need to learn them. Remember that a country's group can change as its income changes.

## Comparing an LIC and an HIC (7.1.4)

| Feature | Typical LIC | Typical HIC |
|---|---|---|
| Base | Wide: high birth rate | Narrow: low birth rate |
| Shape of sides | Concave, narrowing quickly | Straighter, narrowing slowly |
| Top | Narrow: few elderly | Wide: many elderly |
| Main dependants | Young | Increasingly elderly |
| Life expectancy | Lower | Higher |
| Female and male | Small differences | More women than men in the oldest groups |

A **compare** answer needs both countries in every sentence, using comparative words such as "wider", "higher" or "whereas". Two separate descriptions earn fewer marks.

## Common mistakes

- Giving density with no unit or in people per m². Use people per km².
- Using "distribution" and "density" as if they meant the same thing.
- Adding the percentages for one side only when the question wants the total for both sexes.
- Reading the wrong age band: check the axis labels before quoting a value.
- Calling every wide-based pyramid an LIC. Interpret the shape; only identify the income group if the question asks you to suggest it.
- Comparing pyramids by describing one and then the other, with no comparative words.

## Exam technique

**Describe** a pyramid: overall shape, then base, middle and top, with at least two values quoted (age band, sex, percentage). **Interpret** or **explain**: link each feature to birth rate, death rate, life expectancy or migration. **Calculate**: show the addition before the division. **Compare**: write paired statements.

## Check your understanding

1. Define population density. *The number of people living in an area, usually in people per km².*
2. Which ages form the working population? *15 to 64.*
3. A country has 12 million people in 300 000 km². What is its density? *40 people per km².*
4. Young 30%, elderly 10%. Calculate the dependency ratio. *40 / 60 x 100 = 66.7.*
5. What does a wide top on the female side suggest? *Women live longer than men.*
