## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain the purpose of a decision tree. **[2]**

**2.** A chance node has three branches. Two have probabilities of 25% and 0.4. Calculate the probability of the third branch, as a decimal and as a percentage. **[2]**

**3.** An outcome of £80,000 has a probability of 0.45 and an outcome of £30,000 has a probability of 0.55. Calculate the expected value. **[2]**

**4.** On a decision tree, which **one** of these shows a point where the outcome depends on chance?

A: A square.

B: A circle.

C: A triangle.

D: A figure written on a branch. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why the expected value of an option is not the amount a business will receive. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Ravensworth Ales must choose how to bottle a new beer.

Option A: build its own bottling plant for £200,000. Demand could be high (probability 0.5, payoff £500,000), medium (0.3, £250,000) or low (0.2, £50,000).

Option B: pay a contract bottler £30,000. Sales could be good (0.8, £150,000) or weak (0.2, £70,000).

**(a)** Calculate the expected value and net gain of each option. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the economic outcome of each of the five branches. **[3]**

**(c)** Evaluate the outcomes shown on this decision tree. **[6]**

**7.** Using Question 6, evaluate how Ravensworth Ales should respond to the information in the decision tree. **[8]**

**8.** Bramble Hill Studios can choose Option X, which costs £60,000 and pays £200,000 if it succeeds or £20,000 if it fails, or Option Y, which has a net gain of £50,000.

**(a)** Let p be the probability that Option X succeeds. Calculate the value of p at which the two options have equal net gains. **[3]**

**(b)** The managers estimate p at 0.55. Assess the importance of this estimate to the decision. **[4]**

**9.** Calder Toys can launch a new game for £80,000. Demand will be high (0.4) or low (0.6). Low demand brings £60,000. If demand is high, Calder must then decide whether to expand production. Not expanding brings £180,000. Expanding costs a further £50,000 and brings £400,000 with probability 0.75 or £150,000 with probability 0.25.

**(a)** Calculate whether Calder should expand if demand is high, and the net gain of launching. **[6]**

**(b)** State **one** reason why the probability of high demand might be unreliable. **[1]**

**10.** Penrose Cosmetics estimates that launching a new range directly has an expected net gain of £70,000, but a 35% chance of a £90,000 loss. A test market would cost £12,000. With the test, the expected value before deducting its cost is £78,000, and the worst outcome is a loss of £12,000. Penrose has cash reserves of £60,000.

**(a)** Calculate the value of the test-market information and state whether the test is worth its cost on expected value. **[3]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether Penrose Cosmetics should run the test market. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** To compare options under risk by showing the choices, possible outcomes, their probabilities and payoffs [1], so the business can calculate expected values and choose the option with the best average result while seeing the assumptions [1]. **[2]**

**2.** 1 − 0.25 − 0.4 = **0.35** [1] = **35%** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** (0.45 × 80,000) + (0.55 × 30,000) = 36,000 + 16,500 [1] = **£52,500** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** The expected value is a probability-weighted average of possible outcomes [1]; for a single decision only one outcome will actually happen, so the business will receive one of the payoffs, not the average [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Option A: (0.5 × 500,000) + (0.3 × 250,000) + (0.2 × 50,000) = 250,000 + 75,000 + 10,000 = **£335,000** [1]; net gain **£135,000** [1]. Option B: (0.8 × 150,000) + (0.2 × 70,000) = 120,000 + 14,000 = **£134,000** [1]; net gain **£104,000** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Option A: high +£300,000; medium +£50,000 [1]; low −£150,000 [1]. Option B: good +£120,000; weak +£40,000 [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** On expected value Option A is better by £31,000 [1]. But Option A has a 20% chance of a £150,000 loss [1], while Option B cannot make a loss: its worst outcome is a £40,000 gain [1]. The range for A is £450,000 (−£150,000 to +£300,000) compared with £80,000 for B [1], so A is far riskier for a relatively small extra expected gain [1]. Judgement: the outcomes favour B unless Ravensworth can afford a £150,000 loss and is confident in the probabilities for A [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Check the worst case: Ravensworth should compare A's possible £150,000 loss with its reserves and borrowing [1]; if it could not survive that loss, it should choose B regardless of A's higher expected value [1].
Test the probabilities: A's advantage is only £31,000, so a small fall in the chance of high demand could reverse the choice [1]; the probabilities for a new beer are likely to be estimates [1].
Qualitative factors: owning a plant gives control over quality and capacity for future beers [1], while contract bottling keeps flexibility and avoids a large fixed commitment [1].
Timing: the tree ignores when payoffs arrive; a plant may take years to pay back, so investment appraisal should be used too [1].
Judgement: Ravensworth should start with contract bottling, which has a good expected gain and no chance of loss, and build its own plant later if demand proves high [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** Net gain of X = 200,000p + 20,000(1 − p) − 60,000 = 180,000p − 40,000 [1]. Set equal to 50,000: 180,000p = 90,000 [1], so **p = 0.5** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** At 0.55, X has the higher net gain: 180,000 × 0.55 − 40,000 = £59,000, compared with £50,000 for Y [1]. But the estimate is only 0.05 above the switching point [1], so if it is slightly too optimistic, Y would be the better choice [1]. The estimate is therefore very important: the business should check its source before choosing X [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Expand: (0.75 × 400,000) + (0.25 × 150,000) = 300,000 + 37,500 = £337,500 [1]; net of £50,000 = £287,500 [1]. This beats not expanding (£180,000), so Calder should expand [1]. Launch chance node: (0.4 × 287,500) + (0.6 × 60,000) = 115,000 + 36,000 = £151,000 [1]; net gain of launching = 151,000 − 80,000 = **£71,000** [1], which is better than not launching (£0), so the tree recommends launching and expanding if demand is high [1]. **[6]**

**(b)** For example: it is a new game, so there is no sales history and the figure is a judgement that may reflect managers' optimism [1]. **[1]**

**10. (a)** Value of the information = 78,000 − 70,000 = **£8,000** [1]. The test costs £12,000 [1], so on expected value it is not worth it: the net expected value with the test is £66,000, below £70,000 [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** For launching directly: it gives the higher expected net gain, £4,000 more than testing [1], and the business reaches the whole market sooner, before rivals can respond [1].
For testing: launching directly carries a 35% chance of a £90,000 loss, which is larger than Penrose's £60,000 reserves [1], so a failure could threaten its survival [1]. The test limits the worst outcome to a loss of £12,000 [1].
Judgement: Penrose should run the test, because giving up £4,000 of expected value is a small price for avoiding a one-in-three chance of a loss it could not survive [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not subtracting the cost of the option from its expected value.
- Probabilities at a node that do not add up to 1.
- Choosing on expected value alone, without the worst case.
- Working forwards instead of right to left in a two-stage tree.
- Giving the value of information without comparing it with the cost of obtaining it.
