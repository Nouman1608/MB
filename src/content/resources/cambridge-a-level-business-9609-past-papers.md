This page gathers past-paper information for Cambridge International AS & A Level Business (9609), for every series from March 2020 to November 2025. For each series you get the grade thresholds, copied from Cambridge's grade-threshold documents, and our notes on what the examiners said, written in our own words from Cambridge's Principal Examiner Reports for Teachers. We do not host question papers or mark schemes. The links below take you to Cambridge's own pages.

## Where to get the official papers

- [Cambridge International AS & A Level Business (9609) syllabus page](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/programmes-and-qualifications/cambridge-international-as-and-a-level-business-9609/)
- [9609 past papers on the Cambridge International website](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/programmes-and-qualifications/cambridge-international-as-and-a-level-business-9609/past-papers/)

Cambridge publishes recent papers, mark schemes, examiner reports and grade thresholds on the syllabus's past-papers page; older series are available to schools through the School Support Hub.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, timed and without notes. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, including the level descriptors for the longer answers. Then read the examiner report for that series, or our notes below, to see why answers like yours lost marks. The mark scheme tells you what earns credit. The report tells you what candidates actually got wrong.

### Which syllabus were the papers set on?

Papers from March 2023 to November 2025 were set on the revised syllabus, with four papers: Paper 1 Business Concepts 1, Paper 2 Business Concepts 2, Paper 3 Business Decision-Making and Paper 4 Business Strategy. These are the closest match to the current syllabus, for exams in 2026, 2027 and 2028, so they are your best practice papers. Still check the syllabus version on the paper's front cover against the current syllabus, because the paper structure and topic details can change between versions.

Papers from March 2020 to November 2022 were set on the earlier syllabus, which had three papers: Paper 1 Short Answer and Essay, Paper 2 Data Response and Paper 3 Case Study. Much of the content is still useful for topic practice, but the paper structure is different, so do not use them as timed mocks for the current papers. Always use the current published formulae list when you practise on them.

## What examiners keep saying

These are the mistakes that come back series after series. They are grouped by the syllabus's own topic areas. The series named after each point are the ones whose reports raised it.

### Business and its environment

- **Business structure.** A public limited company is treated as a government-owned or public-sector body. Features that every company shares, such as limited liability, are offered as special benefits of a plc. November 2020, November 2021, March 2022, November 2022, June 2023, March 2024, November 2025.
- **Stakeholders in a business.** Answers take the wrong viewpoint: the business when the question asked about employees, the employer when it asked about employee aims, or the other way round. The business is never its own stakeholder. November 2020, March 2021, June 2021, November 2021, June 2022, November 2024, March 2025, June 2025, November 2025.
- **External influences on business activity.** Exchange-rate effects are put the wrong way round. Interest-rate and tax changes are not followed through to their effect on the business. Corporation tax is treated as an operating cost, or mixed up with income tax. March 2020, March 2021, June 2021, March 2022, November 2023, March 2025, November 2025.

### Human resource management

- **Workforce planning** is answered as motivating or managing current staff, or as recruitment, training or organisational structure. It means auditing the skills you have now and forecasting the labour you will need. March 2020, November 2020, June 2021, June 2022, November 2022, November 2023, November 2024.
- **Recruitment and selection.** Recruitment methods are given when selection was asked for, and the reverse. Job description and person specification are swapped. June 2021, November 2021, November 2022, November 2023, June 2024, June 2025, November 2025.
- **Motivation and leadership.** Fringe benefits are counted as non-financial motivators. A question on a named theory (McClelland, Herzberg, Maslow's self-actualisation) is answered with motivation in general or with other theorists. Theory X and Theory Y are swapped. Leadership styles are confused, and decentralisation or a flat structure is treated as democratic leadership. November 2020, March 2021, June 2021, November 2021, November 2022, March 2023, November 2023, June 2024, November 2024, March 2025, June 2025.

### Marketing

- **Elasticity** (price, income, cross and promotional). The formula is inverted, the wrong base is used for a percentage change, the negative sign is dropped or the direction of change is ignored. Some answers say inelastic demand does not change at all. November 2020, November 2021, June 2022, November 2022, March 2023, June 2023, March 2024, June 2024, November 2024, November 2025.
- **Marketing analysis and strategy.** The Boston Matrix is confused with the product life cycle. The marketing mix is worked through one element at a time instead of as one coordinated strategy. March 2020, June 2021, November 2021, November 2022, March 2024, November 2024, June 2025.
- **Market research and pricing.** Sampling methods are given for research methods, or reasons for research are given when the question asked why firms sample. Price skimming is described as a low price. November 2021, November 2022, March 2024, March 2025, November 2025.

### Operations management

- **Quality and operations concepts.** TQM is treated as adding inspectors, and quality assurance is confused with quality control. Efficiency is confused with productivity or effectiveness, CAD with CAM, and process innovation with product innovation. ERP is often unknown, and critical path nodes are filled in wrongly. November 2020, June 2021, March 2022, June 2022, November 2022, June 2023, June 2024, November 2024, March 2025, June 2025, November 2025.

### Finance and accounting

- **Investment appraisal.** ARR is worked out with the superseded formula, or with the capital cost instead of the average investment, or not given as a percentage. NPV leaves out the initial outlay. June 2021, November 2021, November 2022, March 2023, June 2023, November 2023, June 2024, November 2025.
- **Analysis of published accounts.** Reserves, retained earnings or new borrowing are left out of capital employed. The acid test is confused with the current ratio. Percentage signs and units are missing. March 2020, November 2020, March 2021, November 2021, March 2022, June 2022, June 2023, March 2024.
- **Profit, cash and costs.** Cash is confused with profit, and profit with profit margin or profitability. Fixed costs are included in a contribution decision, and contribution costing is often unknown. Variances are labelled by their sign rather than by their effect on the business. March 2020, November 2020, March 2021, June 2021, November 2021, November 2022, June 2023, June 2024, November 2024, June 2025.
- **Sources of finance.** Overdrafts are treated as long-term loans or used for capital spending. Leasing is mixed up with sale and leaseback or with hire purchase. June 2021, November 2021, June 2022, June 2023, March 2024, June 2024, November 2024, June 2025.

### Business strategy (A Level)

- **Strategic tools and approaches.** Answers pick an option, repeat the appendix or carry out a SWOT or PEST, when the question asked how useful a technique or approach is. On Paper 4, candidates give advice when asked to judge a past strategy, and analyse single elements instead of the strategy as a whole. March 2020, November 2020, March 2021, June 2021, November 2021, March 2022, June 2022, November 2022, March 2023, June 2023, November 2023, March 2024, June 2024, November 2024, June 2025, November 2025.

### Exam technique the reports repeat

- **Match the command word.** Identify needs a word or two. Explain needs a point linked to the business. Analyse needs a chain of consequences and no conclusion. Evaluate needs a justified judgement in context.
- **Give exactly the number of points asked for.** Extra points earn nothing and leave less time to develop the ones that count. Two well-developed points usually beat a list.
- **Define precisely.** One or two sentences, covering every part of the term, without reusing the words of the term.
- **Evaluate throughout.** In the long answers about half the answer should be judgement. Put a supported verdict at the end, not a one-line verdict at the start. Use the question's own wording and say what your verdict depends on in this business.
- **Show every calculation step.** State the formula, lay out each stage, give units and signs, and learn the published formulae list. Reuse your own calculated figures as evidence later.
- **Plan your time.** The reports suggest about 1.5 minutes per mark and 10-15 minutes reading the case. On Paper 4, allow about 15 minutes to read and plan, then about 30 minutes per essay.

## November 2025

The report's main theme was the gap between secure knowledge and weaker application and evaluation. Motivation, training, CSR and sales forecasting were generally well known. Less familiar terms tripped candidates up: liquidation, vertical mergers, the triple bottom line, hostile takeovers, outsourcing, segmentation and multinationals. Paper 3 exposed weak number skills, including centred moving averages, NPV and the current ARR formula, and some candidates knew nothing about critical path analysis or exchange-rate effects. Stronger scripts used case figures and industry detail and judged as they went. Weaker ones retold the case, over-analysed and ended with a summary rather than a justified verdict.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 11: liquidation confused with liquidity; general or horizontal mergers discussed when vertical mergers were asked for. On Paper 13, what makes a takeover "hostile" was not explained.
- Paper 12: the triple bottom line was unknown or given an invented meaning; share issues were treated as internal finance.
- Papers 21, 22 and 23: segmentation confused with product differentiation, outsourcing treated as hiring staff, a multinational defined as any exporter, and selection methods given for recruitment.
- Papers 31, 32 and 33: the old ARR formula, capital cost used in place of average investment, the wrong payback year, and NPV without its sign or the outlay.
- Paper 32: moving averages not centred, and appreciation and depreciation effects reversed. On Paper 31, the sign of income elasticity was misread.
- Papers 41 and 43: forward-looking advice when the question asked you to evaluate a past strategy, completing a SWOT or PEST, and recommending a mix of approaches instead of one justified choice.

## June 2025

Examiners wanted exact knowledge of syllabus terms, and finance was the weakest area across the series. Budgeting terms, variance analysis, dividend cover, time-series forecasting and sale and leaseback all caused trouble, and some candidates left finance questions blank. Human resources, operations ideas such as quality assurance and ERP, and research and development caused further confusion. The best scripts pulled figures and specific detail from the case, took each chain of reasoning through to an effect on the business and judged in context throughout. Weaker scripts restated the case, wrote about the wrong stakeholder or finished with a single-sentence verdict.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 13: incremental budgets and variance analysis were defined vaguely or left blank.
- Paper 21: generic advantages that would suit any source of finance. On Paper 12, a weak grasp of sale and leaseback.
- Paper 22: commission treated as a cut in revenue rather than a cost, and job production equated with batch or customised production.
- Paper 32: quality assurance not separated from quality control, R&D confused with market research, and dividend cover worked out with the wrong formula or profit figure.
- Paper 31: seasonal variations not averaged or not applied to the trend. On Papers 31 and 43, candidates had no working definition of ERP.
- Papers 42 and 43: filling in a planning tool rather than judging how useful it is, and recommending a mix of approaches.

## March 2025

This series rewarded secure definitions and discipline with command words. Paper 12 was seen as accessible, but finance and accounting terms were handled less confidently than people or marketing topics. On the AS papers, examiners wanted analysis built as a chain of linked consequences, not a jump straight to "more profit", and short, direct answers to identify and explain questions. Evaluation was the biggest dividing line at every level. Stronger scripts made justified judgements specific to the context as they went. Weaker ones added one unsupported sentence at the end, or nothing. On Paper 12, misreading whose viewpoint a question asked for also cost marks.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 12: a cost type defined by giving an example of it, intrapreneur not told apart from entrepreneur, and sustainability confused with lean production.
- Paper 22: break-even worked out by trial and error because the formula was not secure, and no way found to work back from market share to total market size.
- Paper 22: price skimming confused with penetration pricing or just "low prices", democratic and autocratic styles confused, and profit or market size given as a measure of business size.
- Paper 32: liquidity treated as profit, with unrealistic fixes such as a flotation for working capital; network nodes and float filled in wrongly; income tax mixed up with corporation tax.
- Paper 42: working through the data in date order, which rarely reached strategic analysis; drifting away from the named leadership style; trying to use every piece of data and running out of time.

## November 2024

The papers leaned on budgets and variances, costing methods, market research, workforce planning, capacity utilisation, growth and mergers, motivation, product portfolio analysis, liquidity and ratios, decision trees, elasticity, TQM, critical path analysis and corporate planning. Gaps in knowledge of key terms cost many candidates heavily, and evaluation was again a weakness, often just a restatement of the analysis. Stronger scripts defined terms precisely, drew context from case figures and their own calculations, and reached a judgement in the question's own words. Weaker scripts guessed a term's meaning from one of its words, over-developed short answers and answered a prepared question rather than the one set.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 11: zero budgeting, résumé and workforce planning guessed from everyday meanings; external growth confused with external finance; fringe benefits called non-financial motivators.
- Paper 12: full and contribution costing poorly known, with many blank answers; employer aims discussed when employee aims were asked for.
- Paper 22: the Boston Matrix confused with the product life cycle, and workforce planning treated as general HRM. On Paper 21, knowledge of the Boston Matrix was insecure.
- Paper 31: liquid assets miscalculated and TQM thought to need more inspectors; on Paper 33, TQM was confused with quality control. On Paper 32, finance costs were deducted when finding operating profit and profit was treated as profitability.
- Paper 33: income elasticity rearranged wrongly and its direction ignored.
- Papers 41, 42 and 43: retelling the case, judging future success instead of the past strategy, prepared answers on strategic approaches, and advice given instead of a judgement on impact.

## June 2024

Topics included inventory management, recruitment and training, costing methods, budgets and variances, break-even, sources of finance, motivation theory, supply chain management, investment appraisal, elasticity, sales forecasting, TQM, CSR, and HR, operations and corporate-planning strategy. The reports again pick out evaluation as a weakness: scripts often gave a short summary rather than a supported judgement in context. Stronger scripts defined terms precisely, used case figures and their own results as context, and built two-sided chains of analysis. Weaker ones confused paired concepts, wrote introductions that earned nothing, or answered a prepared question instead of the one on the paper.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 12: job description confused with person specification, and contribution costing widely guessed at. On Paper 23, recruitment and selection were confused.
- Paper 13: cash flow confused with profit, Theory X swapped with Theory Y, and added value defined using cost instead of price. On Paper 11, just-in-case was confused with just-in-time.
- Paper 22: variances labelled favourable or adverse by their sign rather than by their effect on the business.
- Papers 31, 32 and 33: capital cost used instead of average investment in ARR, or the superseded ARR formula used.
- Papers 31 and 33: the sign of elasticity dropped or misread. On Paper 31, higher revenue was assumed to mean higher profit. On Paper 32, the centred moving average was unknown or skipped and TQM was thought to add inspectors.
- Paper 42: one element, such as lean production, analysed alone instead of the operations strategy as a whole. On Paper 43, an analysis was carried out rather than a judgement on the approach.

## March 2024

This series drew on sampling, entrepreneurship, capacity utilisation, sources of finance, break-even and margin of safety, labour turnover, pricing, legal structure, payment systems, ratios, price elasticity, economies of scale, organisational structure, exchange rates, and marketing and operations strategy. The reports stress that evaluation carries the largest share of marks in the long answers, yet on Paper 22 many scripts wrote very little judgement. Stronger scripts picked the most relevant data, set out calculations step by step and tied their judgement to the exact wording of the question. Weaker scripts opened with unrewarded definitions, covered too many points thinly or mixed up terms that sound alike.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 12: dismissal mixed up with redundancy, several reasons for finance explained when one was needed, and a capacity question answered mainly about the opposite capacity situation.
- Paper 22: current output read from the end of the break-even graph lines rather than from the information given, and the labour turnover formula inverted or the final comparison left out.
- Paper 22: price skimming taken to mean a low price, plcs confused with public-sector bodies, performance-related pay treated as piece rate, and leasing confused with sale and leaseback.
- Paper 32: parts left out of capital employed and wrong units on ratios; the elasticity formula not rearranged and its sign dropped; geographical structure reduced to hiring locally or expanding abroad.
- Paper 42: the marketing mix analysed element by element rather than as a strategy, the question's evaluative term swapped for another, and the existing operations strategy evaluated when advice was asked for.

## November 2023

The papers covered workforce planning, operations methods, break-even and margin of safety, motivation theory (McClelland in particular), recruitment documents, business failure, finance, supply chains, ethics, costing, just-in-time, critical path analysis, ratios, net present value, lean production, corporate culture and strategic approaches such as blue ocean strategy. Evaluation was again the main weakness: many scripts analysed well but ended with a summary rather than a judgement in context. Stronger answers used precise definitions that did not repeat the term, built chains around two or three points and anchored conclusions in the named business. Weaker scripts misread the question's focus, repeated the case or lacked key terms and formulae.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 11: workforce planning confused with organisational structure, margin of safety confused with break-even, a McClelland question answered with other theorists, and mass marketing confused with mass production.
- Paper 12: job description mixed up with person specification, and bankruptcy not recognised as a legal process. On Paper 13, trade credit was not understood and venture capital was confused with joint ventures.
- Papers 13 and 21: supply chain management reduced to physical supply or delivery. On Paper 22, direct costs were treated as variable costs, and circular definitions lost marks on Papers 22 and 23.
- Paper 31: a product-based structure read as product orientation, and dividend cover worked out from the wrong figures.
- Paper 32: corporation tax treated as a cost, the capital cost not netted off in NPV, and units missing on capacity utilisation and inventory turnover.
- Papers 42 and 43: no knowledge of blue ocean strategy, overall success judged instead of the effect of culture, and methods listed without definitions.

## June 2023

## How to use this page

1. **Sit a paper timed.** Choose a paper from March 2023 onwards if you can, since those match the current four-paper structure. Download it with its mark scheme from the official past-papers page and sit it in exam conditions.
2. **Mark it strictly.** Use the mark scheme and its level descriptors. Be honest about analysis and evaluation marks, because that is where the reports say most marks go.
3. **Read the notes for that series.** Compare your answers with the "Where marks were lost" points above, then check the [What examiners keep saying](#what-examiners-keep-saying) list for your topic.
4. **Fix the weak topics.** Work through the [9609 revision checklist](/checklists/cambridge/a-level/business/) and the topic resources on the [Cambridge A Level Business hub](/boards/cambridge/a-level/business/). Check what each command word asks for on our [command words page](/command-words/).
5. **Re-test.** Take the free [AS Level diagnostic](/practice/9609/diagnostic/as/) or [A Level diagnostic](/practice/9609/diagnostic/a-level/) (all our diagnostics are listed on the [diagnostics page](/diagnostics/)), and use the [9609 self-check question bank](/practice/9609/) for quick practice on single topics.
6. **Plan ahead.** See the [exam calendar](/exam-calendar/) for exam dates, and our [grade thresholds page](/grade-thresholds/) for June 2026 thresholds for our flagship syllabuses.
7. **Get help if you are stuck.** Book a [free trial class](/trial/) with one of our teachers.
