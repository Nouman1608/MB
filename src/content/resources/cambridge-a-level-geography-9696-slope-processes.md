This guide covers **3.3 Slope processes** in Topic 3, Rocks and weathering, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus asks for **slope processes, the conditions under which each occurs and their effects on slopes**, under two headings:

- **Mass movement:** heaves, flows, slides and falls.
- **Water and sediment movement on slopes:** rainsplash and surface runoff (sheetwash and rills).

Notice the three-part demand: for every process you need the **mechanism**, the **conditions** and the **effect on the slope**. The [3.3 slope processes revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-slope-processes-revision-notes/) cover the mechanisms and the stress-strength balance; this guide concentrates on conditions, effects on slope form, field evidence and calculations. Practise with the [3.3 slope processes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-slope-processes-practice/).

## The organising idea: speed and water content

The four mass movement groups sit along two gradients. **Speed** increases from heaves (millimetres a year) to falls (free fall). **Water content** is low in heaves and falls, rises through slides and is highest in flows. A useful way to classify any movement is to ask three questions:

1. Does the material move as a coherent block (slide, fall) or does it deform internally (heave, flow)?
2. How fast does it move?
3. How much water is involved?

## Mass movement: conditions and effects

| Group | Typical conditions | Effect on the slope |
|---|---|---|
| Heave (soil creep) | Soil-covered slopes of moderate angle; repeated wetting and drying or freezing and thawing; vegetated slopes in humid climates | Slow, widespread downslope movement of the whole soil layer; rounds the upper slope into a **convex** profile; soil thickens at the slope foot |
| Flows (earthflows, mudflows, debris flows) | Saturated, fine or poorly sorted material; heavy rain or snowmelt; little vegetation; steep gullies for debris flows | Material is stripped from source areas and spread as **lobes** or fans at the base; leaves scars and channels |
| Slides (translational and rotational) | Weak or jointed rock, clay, or bedding planes dipping downslope; slopes undercut at the base; high pore water pressure | Leaves a **scar** at the top; rotational slides leave back-tilted blocks and a **stepped** profile; translational slides leave a smooth slip plane |
| Falls | Steep, bare rock faces; joints opened by freeze-thaw, pressure release or root action; undercutting | **Parallel retreat** of the free face; accumulation of a **scree (talus) slope** at its foot |

Three points from the table earn credit in "effects" questions:

- **Creep shapes convex upper slopes.** Because the amount of soil moving past each point increases downslope, the slope steepens progressively below the crest, giving a rounded convex form.
- **Falls create two-part slopes.** The free face retreats while keeping its angle, and scree builds up below it at an angle of rest usually between about 30° and 40°. Over time the scree can bury the lower part of the face.
- **Slides reset slopes.** A single rotational slide can lower the slope angle overall but steepen the scar; repeated slides produce a hummocky, terraced slope.

## Water and sediment movement: conditions and effects

- **Rainsplash** occurs where raindrops strike bare or thinly vegetated soil. Each impact detaches particles and throws them outwards. On a slope, particles thrown downslope travel further than those thrown upslope, so the net movement is downhill. Conditions: intense rain with large drops, little vegetation or litter, fine sandy or silty soils that are easily detached. Effect: gradual lowering of the surface and **surface sealing**, which reduces infiltration and so encourages the next process.
- **Sheetwash** is a thin, unchannelled sheet of overland flow. Conditions: rainfall intensity exceeding the infiltration capacity, smooth surfaces such as bare fields, and the upper parts of slopes before flow has concentrated. Effect: removal of the finest soil evenly across the surface, which can be hard to see until soil depth has fallen.
- **Rills** form where sheetwash concentrates into small channels a few centimetres deep. Conditions: longer and steeper slopes (so flow builds up), and erodible soils, often on ploughed land running downslope. Effect: concentrated erosion in lines down the slope; rills can deepen and merge into gullies if not removed by ploughing or vegetation.

These processes operate in sequence down a slope: rainsplash dominates near the crest where little water has collected, sheetwash on the upper and middle slope, and rills lower down where runoff is deepest and fastest.

## Worked example 1: a factor of safety calculation

*On a fictional clay slope, shear strength is 140 kPa and shear stress is 100 kPa in dry conditions. After prolonged rain, the strength falls to 95 kPa because of rising pore water pressure, and the stress rises to 105 kPa because of the weight of the water. Calculate the factor of safety before and after the rain.*

Factor of safety = shear strength / shear stress.

- Dry: 140 / 100 = **1.40**, so the slope is stable.
- Wet: 95 / 105 = **0.90**, which is below 1, so failure is expected, most likely as a rotational slide in clay.

Rain affects both sides of the ratio, which is why prolonged rain is the most common trigger of slides and flows.

## Worked example 2: rainsplash measurements

*Students placed splash boards across a bare 10° slope. In one storm, 620 g of soil was splashed downslope and 380 g upslope. Calculate the net downslope movement and the downslope share.*

Net downslope = 620 - 380 = **240 g**.
Downslope share = 620 / (620 + 380) x 100 = **62%**.

On a steeper slope the downslope share would rise, because the difference in travel distance up and down the slope becomes greater.

## Field evidence for each process

Being able to recognise processes from photographs is useful in Section A:

- **Heave:** terracettes (small steps along the contours), trees with curved bases, leaning fences and walls, and soil banked up on the upslope side of walls.
- **Flows:** tongue-shaped lobes of debris, fans at the mouths of gullies, mud-covered surfaces.
- **Slides:** curved scars at the top of slopes, back-tilted blocks, tension cracks above the scar.
- **Falls:** fresh, angular blocks on scree, light-coloured scars on rock faces.
- **Surface runoff:** exposed roots and stones standing on small pedestals of soil (protected from rainsplash), lines of rills on fields.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain the conditions under which rotational slides occur and their effects on a slope." [6]*

> Rotational slides typically occur in clay or where weak rock such as clay lies beneath more permeable rock **(material)**. Heavy or prolonged rain soaks into the permeable layer and saturates the clay, raising pore water pressure and reducing friction, while adding weight **(trigger, both sides of the balance)**. Undercutting by a river or the sea removes support at the base **(second trigger)**. The mass then moves along a curved slip surface, rotating backwards as it slides **(mechanism)**. This leaves a steep, curved scar at the top and a back-tilted block below **(effect)**, and repeated slides give the slope a stepped, hummocky profile **(long-term effect)**.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the process but not the conditions or the effect on the slope, as the syllabus demands.
- Calling soil creep a "flow"; it is a heave process.
- Treating rainsplash as negligible; it seals the surface and starts runoff.
- Describing rills and gullies as the same scale of feature.
- Using "landslide" for every movement instead of classifying it.
- Forgetting that water raises stress (weight) as well as lowering strength.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a process: give its mechanism, the conditions it needs, and its effect on slope form.
- **Describe** a photograph: identify evidence and name the likely process.
- **Calculate** factors of safety and percentages step by step, and interpret the result.
- **Compare** two processes: compare speed, water content, material and effect directly.
