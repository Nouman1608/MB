These notes condense content item 2.3.2 from Topic 2.3 Making operational decisions in Theme 2 of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022: managing stock and the role of procurement. Worked examples are in the [managing stock study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-managing-stock-and-working-with-suppliers/) and exam-style questions in the [managing stock practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-managing-stock-and-working-with-suppliers-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented.

## The specification in one view

- **Managing stock:** interpretation of bar gate stock graphs; the use of just in time (JIT) stock control.
- **The role of procurement:** relationships with suppliers (quality, delivery: cost, speed, reliability; availability; cost; trust); the impact of logistics and supply decisions on costs, reputation and customer satisfaction.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stock | Materials, components, work in progress and finished goods a business holds |
| Bar gate stock graph | A graph of stock level over time: falling lines for usage, vertical jumps for deliveries |
| Buffer stock | A minimum level kept as a safety cushion |
| Reorder level | The stock level at which a new order is placed |
| Reorder quantity | The amount ordered each time |
| Lead time | The time between ordering and receiving stock |
| Just in time (JIT) | Ordering stock to arrive just as it is needed, with little or no buffer |
| Procurement | Finding and buying the goods and services a business needs |
| Logistics | Moving and storing goods through the supply chain |

The graph labels are standard textbook terms; the specification asks you to interpret the graphs.

## Reading a bar gate graph in five steps

1. **Maximum:** the highest point, just after a delivery.
2. **Buffer:** the lowest point in a normal cycle.
3. **Usage rate:** the fall in stock per week (the slope).
4. **Reorder quantity:** the height of the vertical jump.
5. **Lead time:** the gap between reaching the reorder level and the delivery.

**Standard relationships:**

- Reorder level = (usage per period × lead time) + buffer stock
- Weeks stock will last = stock ÷ usage per week

**Quick calculation.** Usage 300 units a week, lead time 3 weeks, buffer 400 units. Reorder level = (300 × 3) + 400 = **1,300 units**. With a maximum of 2,500, a delivery arriving at the buffer would be 2,500 − 400 = **2,100 units**.

## Warning signs on a graph

- Stock falls below the buffer: demand rose or a delivery was late.
- Stock reaches zero: the business ran out and lost sales.
- Stock jumps above the usual maximum: an extra or oversized delivery, tying up cash and space.
- A steeper slope: usage has increased, so the reorder level may need raising.

## JIT summary

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Less cash tied up in stock | Needs very reliable suppliers |
| Lower storage costs | Late delivery stops production or sales |
| Less waste and obsolete stock | More frequent deliveries; bulk discounts lost |
| Problems noticed quickly | Hard to meet sudden rises in demand |

Suits: predictable demand, nearby reliable suppliers, perishable goods.

**Applied example.** A florist ordering fresh flowers each morning gains from JIT, because flowers wilt within days and stock left over is wasted. A garden centre selling barbecues in a heatwave would struggle with JIT, because demand can jump faster than suppliers can deliver.

## Supplier relationships: the five factors

| Factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Quality | Do supplies meet our standard? |
| Delivery | Cost of delivery? Speed (lead time)? Reliability (on time, complete)? |
| Availability | Can they supply enough, including at short notice? |
| Cost | Price, discounts, payment terms? |
| Trust | Will they keep promises? Can we work closely, for example on JIT? |

## Logistics and supply decisions

| Affects | Example |
|---|---|
| Costs | Cheap but slow suppliers mean more buffer stock to store |
| Reputation | Frequent stock-outs make a business look unreliable |
| Customer satisfaction | Fast, accurate delivery to customers raises satisfaction |

Trade-off: cutting costs often risks reputation and satisfaction.

## Exam traps

- Taking the reorder level from the lowest point (that is the buffer).
- Mixing up lead time and reorder quantity.
- Choosing a supplier on price alone.
- Saying JIT means no stock ever.
- Listing supplier factors without applying them.

## Self-test

1. Define lead time.
2. What does the vertical line on a bar gate stock graph show?
3. What is the purpose of buffer stock?
4. Usage is 50 units a day, lead time is 4 days and buffer stock is 100 units. Calculate the reorder level.
5. A shop holds 900 units and uses 150 a week. How many weeks will its stock last with no deliveries?
6. Give two benefits of JIT.
7. Give one risk of JIT.
8. Name the three parts of delivery listed in the specification.
9. Define procurement.
10. Give one way a supply decision can affect a business's reputation.
11. On a graph, stock falls below the buffer level before a delivery arrives. Give one possible reason.
12. Why might a business choose a supplier that is not the cheapest?

### Answers

1. The time between placing an order and receiving the stock.
2. A delivery arriving, raising the stock level immediately.
3. A safety cushion in case a delivery is late or demand rises.
4. (50 × 4) + 100 = **300 units**.
5. 900 ÷ 150 = **6 weeks**.
6. Any two: less cash tied up; lower storage costs; less waste; problems noticed quickly.
7. A late delivery could stop production or leave the business unable to meet orders.
8. Cost, speed, reliability.
9. Finding and buying the goods and services a business needs from suppliers.
10. Unreliable suppliers lead to empty shelves or late orders, so customers see the business as unreliable.
11. Demand was higher than expected, or the delivery was late.
12. Better quality, faster or more reliable delivery, greater availability, or a trusted relationship can be worth more than a lower price.
