## Section A

**1.** Which cells produce antibodies?

A: lymphocytes

B: phagocytes

C: platelets

D: red blood cells

**[1]**

**2.** Which type of immunity does not involve the production of memory cells?

A: active immunity after an infection

B: active immunity after vaccination

C: immunity after a booster vaccination

D: passive immunity from breast milk

**[1]**

**3.** State what is meant by an antigen. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** ways in which a baby can gain passive immunity. **[2]**

**5.** Define active immunity. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A vaccine against a bacterial disease contains weakened bacteria.

**(a)** Outline how the vaccine gives immunity to the disease. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why a person who has been vaccinated does not usually become ill when infected by the real bacteria. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why a vaccine against one disease does not protect against a different disease. **[2]**

**7.** The graph data show the concentration of antibodies in a person's blood after two exposures to the same antigen.

| | First exposure | Second exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Day on which antibodies were highest | 10 | 3 |
| Highest concentration (arbitrary units) | 8 | 96 |

**(a)** Calculate how many times higher the peak concentration was after the second exposure. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate how many days sooner the peak occurred after the second exposure. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain the difference between the two responses. **[3]**

**8.** In a town of 20 000 people, 18 600 have been vaccinated against a disease.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of people who have not been vaccinated. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how vaccinating most of the population helps to control the spread of the disease. **[3]**

**9.** Compare active and passive immunity. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why breast-feeding is important for the development of immunity in a young baby. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how HIV leads to a weakened immune system. **[3]**

**12.** Antibodies have a specific shape. Explain what this means and why it matters. **[3]**

**13.** A new strain of an influenza virus has antigens with a different shape from last year's strain. Suggest why last year's vaccine may not protect against it. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A [1]. **[1]**

**2.** D [1]. **[1]**

**3.** A molecule, usually on the surface of a pathogen [1], that has a specific shape and that the body recognises as foreign (and that stimulates antibody production) [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Across the placenta [1]; in breast milk [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Defence against a pathogen [1] by antibody production in the body [1]. **[2]**

**6.**

**(a)** The weakened bacteria (or their antigens) are given to the person [1]. The antigens stimulate lymphocytes to produce antibodies [1], and memory cells are produced, giving long-term immunity [1].

**(b)** The memory cells quickly recognise the antigens [1] and produce large numbers of antibodies fast, so the bacteria are destroyed before they cause illness [1].

**(c)** Each pathogen has its own antigens with a specific shape [1], and antibodies have a complementary shape that fits only one antigen, so antibodies for one disease do not fit another [1]. **[7]**

**7.**

**(a)** 96 divided by 8 = 12 times [1].

**(b)** 10 - 3 = 7 days [1].

**(c)** After the first exposure, few lymphocytes could make the correct antibody, so the response was slow and small [1]. Memory cells were formed [1]. After the second exposure, the memory cells responded fast and divided to give many lymphocytes, producing a higher level of antibodies sooner [1]. **[5]**

**8.**

**(a)** Not vaccinated = 20 000 - 18 600 = 1400 [1]. 1400 divided by 20 000 x 100 = 7 per cent [1].

**(b)** Few people are left who can catch the disease [1], so the pathogen is less likely to pass from person to person [1]. Unvaccinated people are protected because they are less likely to meet an infected person [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Any four of: in active immunity the person makes their own antibodies but in passive immunity the antibodies come from another individual [1]; active is gained by infection or vaccination but passive by the placenta or breast milk [1]; active produces memory cells but passive does not [1]; active gives long-term protection but passive is short term [1]; active is slow to start but passive gives immediate protection [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Breast milk contains antibodies from the mother [1], which give the baby passive immunity [1] against pathogens while its own immune system is still developing [1]. **[3]**

**11.** HIV infects and destroys lymphocytes [1]. The number of lymphocytes decreases, so fewer antibodies can be produced [1], and the person cannot defend against infections as well [1]. **[3]**

**12.** The shape of an antibody is complementary to the shape of one particular antigen [1]. It binds only to the antigen that it fits, like a lock and key [1], so a different antibody is needed for each pathogen [1]. **[3]**

**13.** Antibodies have a shape complementary to one specific antigen [1]. The memory cells and antibodies from last year's vaccine do not fit the changed antigens of the new strain [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying that phagocytes make antibodies.
- Saying that a vaccine contains the active, dangerous pathogen.
- Giving memory cells in passive immunity.
- Saying that a vaccine gives passive immunity.
- Not saying that antibodies are specific to one antigen.
