These questions practise the "Initial preparation and research proposal" section of Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, where each candidate "must write a research proposal and discuss this with their teacher". For the component as a whole, see the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The Research Report is coursework, so these questions practise the decisions behind the proposal; they are not past-paper questions. All projects and data are fictional. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [initial preparation and research proposal study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-initial-preparation-and-research-proposal/).

## Section A

**1.** State **four** things that the syllabus says a research proposal should outline. **[4]**

**2.** State **two** things the teacher must do with the proposal. **[2]**

**3.** Classify each title as a topic, a loaded question or an open question suitable for debate. **[4]**

- **(i)** "Urban farming"
- **(ii)** "Why is online gaming harmful to teenagers?"
- **(iii)** "Should schools ban mobile phones during lessons?"
- **(iv)** "Is nuclear power a good solution to climate change?"

## Section B

**4.** A candidate proposes the question "How many countries have introduced a sugar tax?" Explain why this is not suitable and rewrite it as a better research question. **[3]**

**5.** A candidate writes this question: "Has tourism ruined small island communities?" Identify **two** problems and suggest an improved question. **[4]**

**6.** For the question "Should governments subsidise electric cars?" identify **three** key concepts and, for **one** of them, explain how two perspectives might use it differently. **[5]**

**7.** A candidate's proposal says: "Debate: I will show that fast fashion is harmful." Rewrite the sentence as an outline of the debate that would be suitable for the proposal. **[3]**

**8.** A candidate lists as sources: "the internet, books, and some experts." Explain why this is weak and rewrite it as **three** better examples of sources for a research project on the question "Should cities restrict private cars in their centres?" **[4]**

**9.** A candidate has chosen the topic "the history of everything in the twentieth century". Explain what is wrong with the scale of this topic and suggest a narrower topic with a debatable question. **[4]**

**10.** A teacher is asked to approve this proposal: research question "Should social media companies be regulated more strictly?"; key concepts "regulation", "freedom of expression", "harm"; sources "a government report, two academic articles and a campaign website". The proposal has no outline of the debate. Explain what the candidate should add, with an example of what it might say. **[4]**

**11.** Halfway through the research, a candidate changes her question from "Should plastic bags be banned?" to "Should single-use plastic packaging be banned in supermarkets?" Explain how she should deal with this in the report and in the research log. **[4]**

**12.** "The research proposal is only a formality because it is not externally marked." Evaluate this claim. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The research question [1]; the key concepts relevant to the question [1]; an outline of the debate to be addressed [1]; examples of sources which will be used [1]. **[4]**

**2.** Any two: review the proposal to confirm that the concepts and debate are relevant [1]; confirm the question gives scope to meet the assessment criteria [1]; formally approve it before the candidate starts the research [1]; add comments on the form [1]; check safety issues [1]. **[2]**

**3.** (i) topic [1]; (ii) loaded question [1]; (iii) open question [1]; (iv) open question [1]. **[4]**

**4.** It asks for a number, which is factual [1], so it does not lead to different perspectives to compare and evaluate [1]. Better, for example: "Should governments introduce a sugar tax to reduce obesity?" [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any two problems [2]: "ruined" is a loaded word that assumes tourism has been harmful [1]; "small island communities" is vague, covering many places with different situations [1]; the question does not lead directly to a debate [1]. Improved question, for example: "Is tourism good for the economies of small island communities?" or a question about one named place [2]. **[4]**

**6.** Any three key concepts, for example subsidy, externality, sustainability, equity [3]. For one concept, two perspectives: supporters might treat "externality" as the pollution that subsidies help to reduce [1], whereas critics might treat it as a wider issue, including the environmental costs of making batteries, which subsidies do not remove [1]. **[5]**

**7.** For example: "Some argue that fast fashion makes clothing affordable and creates jobs; others argue that it damages the environment and exploits workers [2]. I will compare the evidence and assumptions of each before making a judgement [1]." **[3]**

**8.** The list is vague: it names no specific source and no type of source [1], so the teacher cannot judge whether evidence exists on each side of the debate. Three examples, one mark each [3]: a city council transport report on traffic in the centre; an academic study of the effects of congestion charging on air quality; statistics on retail sales from a trade association; an interview with a local shop owner. **[4]**

**9.** The topic is far too large [1], so it could not be covered or argued in up to 5000 words and would produce a general summary [1]. A narrower topic: for example "the role of radio in rural development in one country in the 1960s" [1], with a debatable question such as "Did radio improve rural development more than schools?" [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The outline of the debate is missing [1]. The candidate should name the main perspectives and how they differ [1]. For example: "Supporters argue that stricter regulation protects users from harm; critics argue that it limits freedom of expression and gives governments too much power [1]. I will assess the evidence and assumptions of each and reach a supported judgement [1]." **[4]**

**11.** In the report she should explain how and why the question developed [1], for example that the first question was too broad and a narrower one allowed better evidence [1]. In the research log she should record the decision to change, the reason, and what the change affects [1], such as the sources and methods [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Indicative content, up to 8 marks: up to 2 for the case for the claim, up to 5 for the case against it, and 1 for a judgement.

For the claim: the proposal is not externally marked, so it does not itself receive marks [1]; it is a form approved by the teacher [1]; the question may change during the work [1].

Against the claim: the proposal fixes the question, concepts and planned debate that later criteria assess [1]; a poor question limits what the whole report can achieve [1]; the teacher's review checks scope and safety before work begins [1]; the form is submitted with the report, so Cambridge sees it [1]; the report must explain how and why the question developed, and the proposal is the starting point [1].

Judgement: for example, the proposal is not marked but it is far from a formality, because it determines whether the later marks are achievable [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 3**: calling a loaded question "open" because it is phrased as a question.
- **Question 4**: saying a factual question is "too easy" instead of saying it has no perspectives.
- **Question 6**: naming concepts without saying how perspectives use them differently.
- **Question 8**: listing generic sources such as "the internet" instead of specific kinds of source.
- **Question 12**: only stating that the proposal is not marked, without evaluating its effect on later marks.
