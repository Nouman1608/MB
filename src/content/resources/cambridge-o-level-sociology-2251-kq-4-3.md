Key question 4.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *How is family life changing?* It is the
part of the Family topic most often tested through "explain why" questions, so the skill it rewards is
linking a cause to an effect. This guide works through each of the five syllabus sections as chains of
cause and consequence, and shows how to bring sociological perspectives into an answer about change.

## What the syllabus covers

| Section | Content named in the 2251 syllabus (2025–2027) |
|---|---|
| 4.3.1 Demographic factors and their effects on family life | Family size; birth rates; life expectancy; ageing population; having children in later life |
| 4.3.2 | The impact of industrialisation and urbanisation on family structures and roles |
| 4.3.3 Explanations of changing patterns and trends in marriage, divorce and cohabitation | Legislation; societal attitudes and values; role of the internet; secularisation; cultural expectations; changes in the status and power of women / female empowerment |
| 4.3.4 Alternatives to marriage | Cohabitation; singlehood; lone parents; civil partnerships |
| 4.3.5 Changing family roles | Conjugal roles (more diverse family types; segregated to joint roles, e.g. symmetrical family, dual worker family; the pivot/sandwich generation); roles of children (economic burden/cost; child-centredness; boomerang children); roles of grandparents (childcare; economic support; wisdom and advice; dependent / a burden) |

## Demographic change as cause and effect (4.3.1)

**Demography** is the study of population: births, deaths and migration. The **birth rate** is the
number of live births per 1,000 people in the population per year. **Life expectancy** is the
average number of years a person born in a given year can expect to live.

Write each factor as a chain:

- **Falling birth rates → smaller families.** Causes include reliable contraception, falling infant
  mortality (parents no longer have extra children in case some die), women's education and careers,
  and the rising cost of raising children. *Effect:* fewer siblings and cousins, more resources per
  child, and families that are "thin" across each generation.
- **Rising life expectancy → more living generations.** Better diet, sanitation and medicine mean
  grandparents and great-grandparents are more often alive. *Effect:* the beanpole family, longer
  marriages (which may raise the chance of divorce), and longer periods of widowhood.
- **Ageing population → new care responsibilities.** An ageing population has a rising share of older
  people, because of lower birth rates and longer lives. *Effect:* more older people living alone,
  more adult children caring for parents, and pressure on pensions and health services that shapes
  family decisions.
- **Having children in later life → a wider gap between generations.** Longer education, careers and
  housing costs delay parenthood. *Effect:* fewer children per couple, parents who are older and often
  better off, and a middle generation that may care for young children and frail parents at the same
  time.

## Industrialisation and urbanisation (4.3.2)

**Industrialisation** is the shift from agricultural work to factory and service work; **urbanisation**
is the movement of population from rural areas to towns and cities. The debate is about what these
changes did to family structure and roles.

- **Parsons (functional fit):** the pre-industrial extended family worked together as a unit of
  production; industry needed a mobile workforce, so the smaller nuclear family became dominant.
- **Young and Willmott** described stages: the pre-industrial family as a unit of production; an early
  industrial stage in which extended kin networks helped families cope with poverty; and a later
  **symmetrical** stage of home-centred nuclear families. Their study of families moving from Bethnal
  Green to the new estate they called Greenleigh found that daily contact with extended kin fell and
  couples became more home-centred.
- **Critics:** historians found nuclear households common before industrialisation and extended
  households common during it, so the link is not simple.
- **Urbanisation today:** in many countries young adults move to cities for work, forming nuclear or
  single-person households while keeping ties to rural relatives through visits, phones and money
  sent home. The result is often a modified extended family rather than the end of kinship.

## Why are marriage, divorce and cohabitation changing? (4.3.3)

Each named factor needs a mechanism, not just a label.

| Factor | Mechanism | Likely effect |
|---|---|---|
| Legislation | Laws change the cost, grounds or speed of divorce, and women's rights to property and custody | Easier, cheaper divorce raises divorce rates; laws on minimum marriage age delay marriage |
| Societal attitudes and values | Less stigma attaches to divorce, cohabitation and children born outside marriage | People feel freer to cohabit or leave a marriage |
| Role of the internet | Dating sites and apps widen the pool of partners; social media can create new sources of jealousy | Partner choice becomes more individual; some argue it also makes relationships easier to end |
| Secularisation | Religion has less influence on people's thinking and behaviour | Weaker pressure to marry, or to stay married, for religious reasons |
| Cultural expectations | Communities may expect marriage, early marriage or arranged marriage | Marriage stays near-universal in some groups; change is uneven between cultures |
| Female empowerment | Education, paid work and income give women independence | Women can delay marriage, choose not to marry, or leave unhappy marriages |

**Perspectives on the trends:**

- **Functionalist:** Ronald Fletcher argued that a high divorce rate reflects the high value placed on
  marriage. People expect more, so they end marriages that fail, and many remarry.
- **New Right:** rising divorce and cohabitation are signs of family breakdown that harm children.
- **Feminist:** rising divorce, much of it initiated by women in countries such as the UK, reflects women's
  greater independence and unwillingness to accept inequality.
- **Late modern and postmodern writers:** Anthony Giddens (1992) described the **pure relationship**,
  which lasts only as long as both partners are satisfied. Ulrich Beck and Elisabeth Beck-Gernsheim
  linked rising divorce to **individualisation**, as tradition gives way to personal choice.

## Alternatives to marriage (4.3.4)

- **Cohabitation**: a couple living together without marrying. For some it is a trial before
  marriage; for others a long-term alternative.
- **Singlehood**: not living with a partner, which may be chosen or result from divorce, widowhood or
  the cost of housing.
- **Lone parents**: raising children outside a couple, through divorce, separation, bereavement or
  choice.
- **Civil partnerships**: legally registered unions, available in some countries, which give partners
  rights similar to those of marriage.

## Changing family roles (4.3.5)

**Conjugal roles.** Young and Willmott's symmetrical family described couples whose roles were similar
though not identical. The **dual worker family**, with both partners in paid work, is now common in
many societies. Feminists reply that women in dual worker families often keep the main responsibility
for the home. More diverse family types mean conjugal roles also vary: in a lone-parent family there is
no partner to share with. The **pivot or sandwich generation** cares for children and ageing parents
at once.

**Children.** Philippe Ariès argued that childhood as a separate, protected stage is a modern idea; in
medieval Europe children joined adult work and life early. Today:

- Children are an **economic burden/cost** rather than a source of income, because of child labour
  laws, compulsory schooling and long periods of dependence.
- Families are more **child-centred**: decisions about leisure, spending and where to live often
  revolve around children.
- **Boomerang children** return to the parental home as adults, often because of housing costs, low
  pay, debt or unemployment.

**Grandparents.** With longer life expectancy, grandparents provide **childcare** that lets parents
work, **economic support** such as help with housing, and **wisdom and advice**. Later in life they may
become **dependent**, needing care that falls mainly on the middle generation.

## Worked example: an annotated "explain" answer

**Question:** Explain **two** reasons why divorce rates have risen in many societies.

**Answer:** "First, **legislation** has made divorce easier in many countries, for example by
reducing the grounds a person must prove or the time a divorce takes **[point]**. When divorce is
cheaper and quicker, couples in unhappy marriages who might once have stayed in an empty shell
marriage can end it legally **[mechanism and link to 4.1 concept]**. Second, **female empowerment**:
as more women are educated and earn their own income, they no longer depend on a husband financially
**[point]**, so they are able to leave a marriage that is unequal or abusive, which feminists see as
progress rather than breakdown **[mechanism and perspective]**."

*Why it works:* each reason is separate, explained with *how* it raises divorce, and connected to a
concept or perspective.

## Common mistakes

- Stating a demographic change without its effect on family life.
- Writing "the internet changed marriage" without saying how.
- Treating rising divorce as proof that marriage is unpopular, ignoring remarriage and Fletcher's
  argument.
- Assuming all societies are changing in the same direction or at the same speed.
- Using "symmetrical" to mean "equal": Young and Willmott meant *similar* contributions.
- Confusing boomerang children (adults returning home) with child-centredness.

## Exam technique for 2251 command words

- **Explain** answers need the chain: change → mechanism → effect on family life.
- **Discuss** whether roles are becoming more equal by setting the symmetrical family against feminist
  evidence.
- **Evaluate** a claim such as "marriage is in decline" by weighing divorce and cohabitation trends
  against remarriage and cultural variation, then judging.
- **Suggest** questions reward realistic reasons applied to a situation, for example why a couple may
  delay having children.
