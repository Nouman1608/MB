These questions cover section 3.5.1 Setting financial objectives of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [financial objectives study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-financial-objectives/) and the [financial objectives revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-financial-objectives-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A business has revenue of £90,000 and cost of sales of £54,000. What is its gross profit? **[1]**

A: £36,000

B: £54,000

C: £144,000

D: £4,860,000

**2.** Which **one** of the following would increase a firm's cash balance without increasing its profit? **[1]**

A: Selling goods at a higher price

B: Cutting the cost of materials

C: Receiving a bank loan

D: Reducing the wages bill

**3.** Define the term "return on investment". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why a business's profit may differ from its cash flow in the same period. **[3]**

**5.** Oakmere Café plans to spend £60,000 on a new kitchen. It expects the kitchen to add £9,000 a year to profit. The owner's target return on investment is 18%.

**(a)** Calculate the return on investment. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain whether the project meets the owner's target. **[2]**

**6.** Brackley Stationers reports the following for a year: revenue £800,000; cost of sales £480,000; operating expenses £220,000; interest paid £20,000. Tax is 20% of profit before tax.

**(a)** Calculate gross profit and the gross profit margin. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate operating profit and the operating profit margin. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate profit for the year. **[2]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by a cash flow objective, giving an example. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** benefits to a growing business of setting financial objectives. **[6]**

**9.** In one month Harlow Hire makes a profit of £40,000. Its revenue was £300,000, of which £90,000 is unpaid by customers, and it paid all its costs of £260,000. It also bought a van for £30,000 in cash. It started the month with £50,000 in the bank. Assess whether the owner should be concerned about the business's finances. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse how a financial objective of cutting costs by 5% might affect the marketing and operations functions. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways in which a strong focus on short-term profit could harm a business in the long term. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether profit should be the most important financial objective for a growing business. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **A** [1]. £90,000 − £54,000 = £36,000. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A loan brings cash in but is not revenue, so profit is unchanged; the other options raise profit as well. **[1]**

**3.** The profit gained from an investment [1] expressed as a percentage of the amount invested [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Profit records a sale when it is made, but cash flow records it only when the customer pays [1]. If a business makes a large sale on credit, profit rises immediately but cash arrives later [1], so the business may be profitable yet short of cash to pay wages and suppliers [1]. Accept: equipment is a cash outflow when bought but its cost is spread over years in profit. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 9,000 ÷ 60,000 [1] × 100 = **15%** [1].

**(b)** No, 15% is below the 18% target [1], so on this measure the project would be rejected unless the owner expects benefits not in the profit figure, such as higher sales from a better menu, or lowers the target [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** Gross profit = £800,000 − £480,000 = **£320,000** [1]; margin = 320 ÷ 800 × 100 = **40%** [1].

**(b)** Operating profit = £320,000 − £220,000 = **£100,000** [1]; margin = 100 ÷ 800 × 100 = **12.5%** [1].

**(c)** Profit before tax = £100,000 − £20,000 = £80,000; tax = 20% × £80,000 = £16,000 [1]; profit for the year = **£64,000** [1]. **[6]**

**7.** A target for the movement of money in and out of the business [1], for example to keep the bank balance above £30,000 at all times, or to reduce the time customers take to pay from 45 to 30 days [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Benefit 1: direction and control [1]. A target such as 10% operating margin tells managers what to aim for [1], and comparing actual results with it shows quickly if costs are rising or sales falling [1]. Benefit 2: reassuring lenders and investors [1]. A growing business often needs outside finance, and clear targets for profit and cash flow show it is well managed [1], making banks and shareholders more willing to lend or invest [1]. Credit also: coordination with other functions; motivation through bonuses. **[6]**

**9.** Concern: the business made a profit of £40,000 but net cash flow was negative [1]. Cash in = £300,000 − £90,000 = £210,000 [1]. Cash out = £260,000 + £30,000 = £290,000 [1]. Net cash flow = −£80,000, so the bank balance falls from £50,000 to −£30,000, an overdraft [1]. The overdraft may carry high interest and the bank can withdraw it [1]. Reasons for less concern: the £90,000 is owed and should arrive, and the van is a lasting asset [1]. The profit shows the underlying business is sound [1]. Judgement: the owner should be concerned in the short term, because a profitable business can fail if it cannot pay its bills [1]; action to speed up payment from customers or arrange a loan would reduce the risk [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Marketing: lower costs may cut the budget for advertising, so awareness and sales could fall [1]. Cheaper packaging or materials could change how customers see the brand, and quality [1]. Operations: it may be forced to find savings through bulk buying, less waste or better efficiency, which supports the target [1]. But cutting maintenance or training to save money could cause breakdowns and defects [1]. Interrelationship: savings in one area may cost more in another, for example cheaper parts leading to more returns [1], so the objectives of the functions must be coordinated [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Way 1: cutting investment [1]. Reducing spending on training, research or maintenance raises profit now but weakens the business's future ability to compete, so profits fall later [1]. Way 2: damaging relationships and reputation [1]. Squeezing suppliers or cutting quality to keep costs down may lose customers or cause suppliers to withdraw [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: profit is the source of funds for reinvestment and growth, since retained profit is the cheapest finance [1]. It rewards owners and shareholders and attracts new investors [1]. Without profit a business cannot survive in the long term [1]. Profit margins show how well costs are controlled [1]. Profitable firms can borrow more easily and on better terms [1]. Against: a growing business needs cash flow more than profit in the short run, because rapid growth ties up money in stock and receivables [1]. A narrow profit focus can lead to cutting costs in ways that harm quality or staff [1]. Other objectives such as market share, return on investment or social aims may matter more for some firms [1]. In its early years, a growing business may sacrifice profit to gain share [1]. Factors: stage of the business, owners' aims, and the finance available [1]. Judgement: profit is the long-term aim, but cash flow comes first in early growth, and a balanced set of objectives (profit, cash, ROI) is better than a single target [1]; the priority between them should change as the business matures [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* When comparing cash and profit, use figures from the case to show why the two differ.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating cash flow and profit as the same.
- Mixing up gross, operating and net profit.
- Giving margins as a percentage of costs rather than revenue.
- Forgetting that a loan or share issue gives cash but not profit.
- Setting an objective with no number or deadline.
- Not applying the answer to the business in the case.
