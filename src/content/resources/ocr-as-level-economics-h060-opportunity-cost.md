## Opportunity cost and trade-offs

**Opportunity cost** is the value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. It is a single alternative: the best one forgone, not everything rejected.

A **trade-off** is the relationship in which more of one thing means less of another. The trade-off describes the choice; the opportunity cost is what is actually given up.

**Example (invented).** A farmer owns a field. She can grow wheat, worth a profit of £9,000 a year; rent it to a neighbour for £7,500; or use it for a campsite earning £6,000. If she grows wheat, the opportunity cost is the £7,500 rent, the best alternative. It is not £13,500, the total of both rejected options, because she could only have chosen one of them. Notice that the farmer pays no money for using her own field. **Accounting cost** records money paid out; opportunity cost also includes the value of resources the decision-maker already owns, such as land and time.

Opportunity cost does not have to be measured in money. A student who spends Saturday revising gives up the next best use of that time, which might be paid work or sport.

## The production possibility curve

A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently used, with technology given.

**Country L (invented)** can produce food and machines.

| Point | Food (m tonnes) | Machines (thousand) | Machines given up for the extra 10m tonnes of food |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 50 | |
| B | 10 | 48 | 2 |
| C | 20 | 44 | 4 |
| D | 30 | 36 | 8 |
| E | 40 | 22 | 14 |
| F | 50 | 0 | 22 |

Plot machines on the vertical axis and food on the horizontal. Reading the diagram:

- **Points on the curve** (A to F) are productively efficient: more of one good can only be had by giving up some of the other.
- **A point inside the curve**, such as 25m tonnes of food and 30 thousand machines, shows unemployed or inefficiently used resources. At 25m tonnes, the curve allows 40 thousand machines (halfway between C and D), so 10 thousand machines are being lost.
- **A point outside the curve**, such as 30m tonnes and 40 thousand machines, is unattainable with current resources and technology.
- **Moving along the curve** from one point to another shows a choice, and the opportunity cost is the amount of the other good given up.

### Why the curve bows outward

The last column shows **increasing opportunity cost**. The first 10m tonnes of food cost only 2 thousand machines; the last 10m tonnes cost 22 thousand. Resources are not equally good at producing both goods. When the economy makes little food, it uses its most fertile land and most experienced farmers; as it makes more, it has to move engineers and factory land into farming, where they are much less productive. Each extra unit of food therefore needs more resources, and more machines are lost. That is why the PPC is concave to the origin. If resources were equally suited to both goods, opportunity cost would be constant and the PPC a straight line.

Opportunity cost per unit can be calculated too. Between D and E, 10m tonnes of food cost 14 thousand machines, so each million tonnes costs 1.4 thousand machines.

## What shifts the PPC

| Change | Effect on the PPC |
|---|---|
| More resources (a larger workforce, new capital, discovery of minerals) | Shifts outward |
| Better technology or a more skilled workforce | Shifts outward |
| Technology that helps only one good | Pivots outward along that good's axis only |
| Natural disaster, war or emigration of workers | Shifts inward |
| Using idle resources (falling unemployment) | No shift: a movement from inside the curve toward it |

The last row is a common source of lost marks. Bringing unemployed workers back into production moves the economy toward its existing curve; only a change in the quantity or quality of resources moves the curve itself.

There is an opportunity cost over time as well. Producing more capital goods today means fewer consumer goods now, but more capital tends to shift the PPC outward in future. Country L's choice between food and machines is also a choice between consumption now and growth later.

## How useful is opportunity cost?

**Useful because:**

- it makes hidden costs visible, such as the value of an owner's own time or land;
- it helps government compare uses of a budget: a new road might cost the equivalent of two schools;
- the PPC shows clearly why efficient use of resources matters and why growth widens choice.

**Limited because:**

- the next best alternative is often hard to identify and value, especially for non-market options such as leisure or the environment;
- decision-makers rarely have full information about all alternatives;
- the PPC simplifies to two goods and assumes a fixed level of technology.

A balanced judgement: the concept is always relevant, because every choice has one, but putting a precise value on it can be difficult.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Using the table for Country L, explain why the PPC is concave to the origin. [4]*

> Moving from A to B, Country L gains 10m tonnes of food by giving up 2 thousand machines, but moving from E to F the same gain in food costs 22 thousand machines. Opportunity cost therefore rises as food output increases. This happens because resources are not equally productive in both uses: as food output expands, resources better suited to making machines are moved into farming, so more of them are needed for each extra tonne. Rising opportunity cost gives the curve its bowed shape.

*Why it works:* it uses two figures from the data, states the pattern, gives the reason, and links it to the shape.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the total of all rejected options as the opportunity cost.
- Leaving out non-money costs such as time.
- Showing a fall in unemployment as an outward shift.
- Treating a point outside the PPC as inefficient (it is unattainable).
- Reading opportunity cost off the axes instead of as the change in the other good.

## Exam technique

- **Section A:** multiple-choice items often give a PPC table; work out the change in the other good carefully.
- **Section B:** when asked to draw, label both axes, the curve, and the points or shift you discuss.
- **Section C:** for an essay on the usefulness of opportunity cost, apply it to a named agent (a household, a firm, a government) and finish with a judgement about when it is most useful.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is opportunity cost always measured in money?**
No. It is the value of the next best alternative, which may be time, leisure or another good.

**Why is a point inside the PPC not the same as a point outside?**
A point inside shows that resources are idle or wasted; a point outside cannot be reached with current resources and technology.
