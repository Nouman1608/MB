## What the specification asks for

| Statement in 3.3.9.1 | What it means in an answer |
|---|---|
| The structures of carboxylic acids and esters | Draw, name and recognise –COOH and –COO– compounds, including isomers |
| Carboxylic acids are weak acids but will liberate CO₂ from carbonates | Explain "weak" by partial dissociation; write carbonate equations with the right ratio |
| Acids and alcohols react, with an acid catalyst, to give esters | Write the equilibrium equation; name the ester; link to Kc and Le Chatelier |
| Common uses of esters | Solvents, plasticisers, perfumes and food flavourings |
| Oils and fats are esters of propane-1,2,3-triol | Draw a triester and find its products |
| Ester hydrolysis in acid or alkali | Give the products for each condition and explain why alkali goes to completion |
| Soap and glycerol from alkaline hydrolysis | Use the 1 : 3 : 1 ratio of fat : salt : glycerol |
| Biodiesel | A mixture of methyl esters, made from a vegetable oil and methanol with a catalyst |

No mechanism is required for esterification or ester hydrolysis in 9620.

## Structures and isomers

Both families contain a carbonyl carbon bonded to a second oxygen. In an acid that oxygen carries H (–COOH). In an ester it carries a carbon group (–COO–R). Acids and esters with the same number of carbon atoms therefore share a general formula, CₙH₂ₙO₂, and are **functional group isomers**.

An ester can be written either way round, so always find the carbonyl carbon before naming. CH₃CH₂OOCCH₃ and CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ are the same compound, ethyl ethanoate: the carbonyl carbon belongs to the two-carbon ethanoate part, and the ethyl group sits on the single-bonded oxygen.

### Worked example 1: every isomer of C₄H₈O₂ with a –COO– group

Draw and name the carboxylic acids and esters with formula C₄H₈O₂.

1. **Acids**: four carbons including the –COOH carbon. A straight chain gives **butanoic acid**, CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH. A branch gives **2-methylpropanoic acid**, (CH₃)₂CHCOOH.
2. **Esters**: share four carbons between the acid part and the alcohol part.
   - one carbon in the acid part: **propyl methanoate**, HCOOCH₂CH₂CH₃, and **1-methylethyl methanoate**, HCOOCH(CH₃)₂
   - two carbons in the acid part: **ethyl ethanoate**, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃
   - three carbons in the acid part: **methyl propanoate**, CH₃CH₂COOCH₃
3. Total: two acids and four esters.

To tell the two families apart in the laboratory, add sodium carbonate solution. Only the acids fizz.

## Weak acids that still release CO₂

A carboxylic acid dissociates only partly in water, so its solution contains far fewer H⁺ ions than a strong acid of the same concentration:

`CH₃CH₂COOH ⇌ CH₃CH₂COO⁻ + H⁺`

That small concentration of H⁺ is still enough to react with carbonate ions. As H⁺ is used up the equilibrium shifts right, so all of the acid eventually reacts. Observations: effervescence, and the gas turns limewater cloudy. A carbonate ion needs **two** H⁺, so a monocarboxylic acid reacts with a carbonate in a 2 : 1 ratio.

### Worked example 2: propanoic acid and sodium carbonate

1.85 g of propanoic acid (Mr 74.0) reacts with excess sodium carbonate. Write the equation, then calculate the mass of CO₂ and its volume at 100 kPa and 298 K (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹).

1. `2CH₃CH₂COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃CH₂COONa + CO₂ + H₂O`
2. n(acid) = 1.85 ÷ 74.0 = 0.0250 mol, so n(CO₂) = 0.0250 ÷ 2 = 0.0125 mol.
3. Mass of CO₂ = 0.0125 × 44.0 = **0.550 g**.
4. V = nRT ÷ p = 0.0125 × 8.31 × 298 ÷ 100 000 = 3.10 × 10⁻⁴ m³ = **310 cm³**.

The salt is **sodium propanoate**. Name the salt with the "-oate" ending and the metal first.

## Esterification as an equilibrium

Warming a carboxylic acid with an alcohol and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid gives an ester and water:

`CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₃ + H₂O`  (propyl ethanoate)

The acid catalyst speeds up the approach to equilibrium but does not change its position. Because the reaction is reversible, Le Chatelier's principle explains the usual ways of raising the yield: use an excess of the cheaper reactant, or remove the ester or the water as it forms. The equilibrium constant is covered in the [9620 equilibria guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-equilibria-le-chateliers-principle/).

### Worked example 3: Kc for an esterification

0.400 mol of ethanoic acid and 0.600 mol of propan-1-ol are mixed with a little acid catalyst and left to reach equilibrium. Titration with sodium hydroxide, after allowing for the catalyst, shows that 0.120 mol of ethanoic acid remains. Calculate Kc.

1. Acid used = 0.400 − 0.120 = 0.280 mol, so 0.280 mol of ester and 0.280 mol of water form.
2. Propan-1-ol left = 0.600 − 0.280 = 0.320 mol.
3. Kc = [ester][water] ÷ ([acid][alcohol]). There are two moles on each side, so the volume cancels and amounts can be used directly.
4. Kc = (0.280 × 0.280) ÷ (0.120 × 0.320) = 0.0784 ÷ 0.0384 = **2.04** (no units).

The titration also measures the sulfuric acid catalyst, which is why a separate blank titration is subtracted. Missing that step makes the acid amount too large and Kc too small.

## Uses of esters

The specification names four uses. Learn one reason for each.

- **Solvents**: small esters such as ethyl ethanoate dissolve many organic substances and evaporate easily, so they are used in glues and nail varnish remover.
- **Plasticisers**: ester molecules added to a polymer such as PVC sit between the chains, weaken the forces between them and let the chains slide, making the plastic flexible.
- **Perfumes** and **food flavourings**: many small esters are volatile and have sweet, fruity smells.

## Oils, fats and their hydrolysis

A vegetable oil or animal fat is a **triester** of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol), HOCH₂CH(OH)CH₂OH. The three acid chains may be identical or different.

| Conditions | Products | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Heat under reflux with dilute acid | Carboxylic acid(s) + alcohol | Reversible, so hydrolysis is incomplete |
| Heat under reflux with NaOH(aq) | Carboxylate salt(s) + alcohol | The carboxylate ion cannot react with the alcohol, so the reaction goes to completion |

Alkaline hydrolysis of a fat gives **glycerol** and **soap**, the sodium (or potassium) salts of long-chain carboxylic acids.

### Worked example 4: making soap

17.8 g of glyceryl trioctadecanoate, (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₃C₃H₅ (Mr 890.0), is boiled with excess sodium hydroxide. Calculate the maximum masses of sodium octadecanoate (Mr 306.0) and glycerol (Mr 92.0).

1. `(C₁₇H₃₅COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3NaOH → 3C₁₇H₃₅COONa + C₃H₅(OH)₃`
2. n(fat) = 17.8 ÷ 890.0 = 0.0200 mol.
3. Soap: 3 × 0.0200 = 0.0600 mol, mass = 0.0600 × 306.0 = **18.4 g**.
4. Glycerol: 0.0200 mol, mass = 0.0200 × 92.0 = **1.84 g**.

## Biodiesel

Reacting a vegetable oil with **methanol** in the presence of a **catalyst** swaps the glycerol part of each ester for a methyl group. The specification only says "a catalyst"; a strong base such as potassium hydroxide is commonly used. Every acid chain becomes a separate methyl ester, and glycerol is released. A real oil contains triesters with different chains, so biodiesel is a **mixture** of methyl esters.

### Worked example 5: a mixed triester

A triester contains two hexadecanoate groups and one octadecanoate group on the same glycerol unit. Its formula is C₅₃H₁₀₂O₆ (Mr 834.0). Name the products with methanol and find the mass of methanol needed for 1.00 kg of the oil.

1. Products: **methyl hexadecanoate**, C₁₅H₃₁COOCH₃ (2 mol per mol of oil), **methyl octadecanoate**, C₁₇H₃₅COOCH₃ (1 mol), and **glycerol** (1 mol).
2. n(oil) = 1000 ÷ 834.0 = 1.199 mol.
3. n(methanol) = 3 × 1.199 = 3.597 mol; mass = 3.597 × 32.0 = **115 g**.
4. Check by mass conservation: 1000 + 115.1 = 647.5 (methyl hexadecanoate) + 357.3 (methyl octadecanoate) + 110.3 (glycerol) = 1115.1 g.

## Common mistakes

- Naming an ester from the order it is written instead of from the carbonyl carbon.
- Writing a 1 : 1 ratio for a carboxylic acid with a carbonate.
- Calling an acid strong because it fizzes with a carbonate; strength is about the extent of dissociation.
- Saying the catalyst increases the equilibrium yield of an ester.
- Giving the free acid, not the salt, after alkaline hydrolysis.
- Calling biodiesel a single compound, or saying it is made with ethanol; the specification names methanol.

## Exam technique for this subtopic

- **State** a use or a condition: one precise phrase earns the mark ("heat under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide").
- **Explain** why alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion: name the carboxylate ion and say it cannot react with the alcohol.
- **Deduce** a structure: link each observation to a conclusion, for example "fizzes with Na₂CO₃, so –COOH present".
- **Calculate**: write the equation first, show the mole ratio as its own step and give the answer to the same significant figures as the data.
