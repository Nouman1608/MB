These questions cover section 6.5.1 (Using X-rays), statements (a) to (g), of the OCR Advanced GCE in Physics A specification (H556), Version 3.0 (March 2026), for first assessment in 2017. Use e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. The rest energy of an electron is 0.511 MeV. Start with the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-x-rays-attenuation-and-cat-scanning/) or the [revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-x-rays-attenuation-and-cat-scanning-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** State the function of each of these parts of an X-ray tube: (i) the heater (cathode), (ii) the high voltage supply, (iii) the target metal, (iv) the vacuum. **[4]**

**2.** A tube operates at 70 kV.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum energy of an X-ray photon from the tube, in joules. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the shortest wavelength emitted. **[2]**

**3.** The same tube has a tube current of 9.0 mA. About 1% of the electrical power supplied becomes X-rays.

**(a)** Calculate the number of electrons that strike the target each second. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the thermal energy produced in the target in 2.0 s. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest one way the tube is designed to cope with this energy. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why the spectrum from an X-ray tube has (a) a smooth continuous background with a sharp minimum wavelength, and (b) narrow peaks at particular wavelengths. **[5]**

**5.** Name the attenuation mechanism described in each case.

**(a)** A low-energy photon changes direction without losing energy. **[1]**
**(b)** A photon is completely absorbed and an inner-shell electron is ejected. **[1]**
**(c)** A photon collides with a loosely bound outer electron and continues with less energy. **[1]**
**(d)** A photon near a nucleus becomes an electron and a positron. **[1]**
**(e)** Calculate the minimum photon energy for the process in (d), in MeV and in joules. **[2]**

**6.** A narrow X-ray beam of a single photon energy passes through 7.5 cm of a uniform material. 18% of the incident intensity is transmitted.

**(a)** Calculate the attenuation coefficient, in cm⁻¹. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the half-value thickness. **[2]**

**7.** A beam passes through 3.0 cm of soft tissue (μ = 0.22 cm⁻¹) followed by 2.0 cm of bone (μ = 0.55 cm⁻¹).

**(a)** Calculate the fraction of the incident intensity transmitted. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the fraction transmitted by 5.0 cm of soft tissue alone, and explain what the difference means for the image. **[3]**

**8.** A shielding material has an attenuation coefficient of 35 cm⁻¹ for a certain X-ray beam.

**(a)** Calculate the thickness needed to reduce the intensity to 1.0% of its incident value. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the extra thickness needed to reduce it from 1.0% to 0.10%. **[2]**

**9.** A student measures the count rate R of a narrow beam transmitted through different thicknesses x of a metal sheet.

| x / cm | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R / counts per second | 840 | 525 | 328 | 205 | 128 | 80 |

**(a)** Show that a graph of ln R against x should be a straight line, and state what its gradient represents. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate ln R for x = 0 and x = 5, and use them to find the attenuation coefficient. **[3]**
**(c)** State the unit of the attenuation coefficient and give one reason why a best-fit line through all points is better than using two points. **[2]**

**10.** An artery of internal width 0.60 cm lies in soft tissue. The blood has μ = 0.21 cm⁻¹. When it contains an iodine compound, its effective attenuation coefficient is 1.9 cm⁻¹.

**(a)** Explain why a barium meal is used to image the stomach on a plain X-ray image. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why barium and iodine are suitable contrast media. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the fraction of intensity transmitted across the artery without and with the iodine compound. **[2]**

**11.** Describe how a CAT scanner produces an image of a slice of the body, naming the components involved. **[6]**

**12.** A patient has had a plain X-ray image of the abdomen but the doctor suspects a small tumour behind other organs. A CAT scan is offered.

**(a)** Explain two advantages of a CAT scan over the plain X-ray image in this situation. **[4]**
**(b)** Discuss whether a CAT scan should be used to check a minor injury that a plain X-ray image can already assess. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) The heater is a hot filament that releases electrons by thermionic emission [1]. (ii) The high voltage supply accelerates the electrons from cathode to anode, giving each kinetic energy eV [1]. (iii) The target metal is where the electrons are stopped and X-ray photons are emitted [1]. (iv) The vacuum stops electrons colliding with gas molecules on the way [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each part needs a function, not a name. "The heater heats the tube" does not say that electrons are released.

**2.** **(a)** E = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 70 000 [1] = **1.12 × 10⁻¹⁴ J** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(1.12 × 10⁻¹⁴) [1] = **1.78 × 10⁻¹¹ m** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution in joules. Convert keV before using hc.

**3.** **(a)** n = I/e = 9.0 × 10⁻³/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ [1] = **5.6 × 10¹⁶ s⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Power in = VI = 70 000 × 9.0 × 10⁻³ = 630 W [1]. Thermal power = 0.99 × 630 = 624 W [1]. Energy in 2.0 s = 624 × 2.0 = **1.2 × 10³ J** [1] **[3]**
**(c)** Any one of: rotating anode, copper anode to conduct heat, oil or fins for cooling [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* State that 99% is thermal before multiplying by 0.99.

**4.** (a) Electrons are decelerated by target nuclei and lose varying amounts of energy [1], so photons of all energies up to a maximum are emitted [1]. The most energetic photon takes all of an electron's energy, eV = hc/λ_min, so there is a sharp minimum wavelength [1]. (b) An incoming electron can eject an inner-shell electron from a target atom [1]. An outer electron falls into the gap and emits a photon with energy equal to the difference between the levels, which is fixed by the target element, so a narrow peak appears [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each feature to its cause. A name with no mechanism rarely gains full credit.

**5.** **(a)** **Simple scatter** [1] **(b)** **Photoelectric effect** [1] **(c)** **Compton effect** [1] **(d)** **Pair production** [1] **(e)** Two particles are made, so E = 2 × 0.511 MeV = **1.02 MeV** [1] = 1.022 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (e) two particles are made, so use two rest energies.

**6.** **(a)** I/I₀ = e^(−μx), so ln(1/0.18) = μ × 7.5 [1]. μ = 1.715/7.5 = **0.229 cm⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** x½ = ln 2/μ = 0.693/0.2286 [1] = **3.03 cm** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the natural logarithm, not log to base 10, and quote the unit.

**7.** **(a)** Total exponent = 0.22 × 3.0 + 0.55 × 2.0 [1] = 0.66 + 1.10 = 1.76 [1]. I/I₀ = e^(−1.76) = **0.17** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** I/I₀ = e^(−0.22 × 5.0) = e^(−1.10) = **0.33** [1]. The beam through the bone path is about half as intense (0.17/0.33 = 0.52) [1], so the bone casts a shadow and gives contrast in the image [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Add the exponents for layers. Adding intensities is a common wrong route.

**8.** **(a)** I/I₀ = 0.010, so μx = ln 100 = 4.605 [1]. x = 4.605/35 [1] = **0.13 cm** (1.3 mm) [1] **[3]**
**(b)** From 1.0% to 0.10% is a factor of 10 [1]. Extra thickness = ln 10/35 = **0.066 cm** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor-of-ten reduction needs the same extra thickness, so (b) is quicker than starting again.

**9.** **(a)** Taking natural logs of R = R₀e^(−μx) gives ln R = ln R₀ − μx [1], which has the form y = c + mx. The gradient is −μ (minus the attenuation coefficient) [1] **[2]**
**(b)** ln 840 = 6.73 and ln 80 = 4.38 [1]. Gradient = (4.38 − 6.73)/5 = −0.47 [1]. μ = **0.47 cm⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
**(c)** Unit: cm⁻¹ [1]. A best-fit line uses every reading, so random errors average out [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the gradient its sign, then state μ as positive.

**10.** **(a)** Soft tissue in the stomach wall and its surroundings have similar attenuation coefficients, so there is little contrast [1]. Barium strongly absorbs X-rays, so the barium sulfate suspension outlines the inside of the stomach [1] **[2]**
**(b)** They have high atomic numbers (56 and 53) [1], so photons are strongly absorbed, mainly by the photoelectric effect, compared with soft tissue [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Without: e^(−0.21 × 0.60) = **0.88** [1]. With: e^(−1.9 × 0.60) = **0.32** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) name the property (high atomic number) and the consequence (strong absorption). "They show up well" is too vague.

**11.** The X-ray tube rotates around the patient [1] and produces a thin fan-shaped beam that passes through one slice of the body [1]. A ring of detectors records the transmitted intensity at many positions [1]. Because the tube turns, each part of the slice is crossed by beams from many different angles [1]. Computer software combines all the readings and calculates the attenuation of each small volume in the slice [1]. The display shows this as a grey-scale cross-sectional image, with brightness set by the attenuation [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give each component a role, in order. Names alone earn only part of the marks.

**12.** **(a)** First advantage: the CAT scan gives cross-sectional slices, so a tumour is not hidden behind overlapping organs as in a single flat image [1], and the doctor can see exactly where it lies [1]. Second advantage: small differences in attenuation between soft tissues are shown with better contrast [1], so a tumour with attenuation close to its surroundings can still be seen [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Benefit: if the plain image already answers the question, the CAT scan adds little diagnostic information [1]. Risk: a CAT scan usually gives a larger dose of ionising radiation, which can damage cells and slightly raises the chance of cancer [1]. It also costs more [1]. Conclusion: it should not be used when the plain image is sufficient, but would be justified if the plain image were unclear or the injury were serious [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A "discuss" question needs both sides and a reasoned conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting that about 99% of the electrical power becomes thermal energy, not X-rays.
- Describing characteristic lines or the continuous spectrum by name only, with no mechanism.
- Confusing the photoelectric effect (photon absorbed) with Compton scattering (photon survives with less energy).
- Using one electron rest energy instead of two for the pair production threshold.
- Adding intensities, or multiplying coefficients, for layers of different material.
- Using log to base 10 instead of the natural logarithm in the attenuation law.
