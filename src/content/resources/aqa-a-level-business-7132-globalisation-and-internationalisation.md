This guide goes deeper into section **3.9.3 Assessing globalisation and internationalisation** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.9 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **reasons for greater globalisation of business**, and **the importance of globalisation** and **of emerging economies** for business
- **reasons for targeting, operating in and trading with international markets**, with methods of entry including **export, licensing, alliances and direct investment**
- **factors influencing the attractiveness of international markets**
- **reasons for producing more and sourcing more resources abroad**, including **off-shoring and re-shoring**
- **ways of entering international markets and the value of different methods**, with targeting overseas markets possibly involving being a **multinational**
- **influences on buying, selling and producing abroad**
- **managing international business**, including **pressures for local responsiveness and pressures for cost reduction**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## Globalisation and internationalisation

**Globalisation** is the increasing integration of the world's economies, as trade, investment, people and information move more freely across borders. **Internationalisation** is a single business's decision to sell, source or produce abroad. A **multinational** is a business that owns or controls operations in more than one country.

### Reasons for greater globalisation

- **Trade liberalisation:** lower tariffs and quotas, and trade agreements.
- **Transport and communication:** cheaper shipping, air freight, container ports, the internet and mobile communications.
- **Technology:** online selling, digital payment, translation tools.
- **Financial liberalisation:** easier movement of money and investment.
- **Growth of multinationals,** which spread products, brands and methods.
- **Rising incomes and consumer tastes** converging in many markets.
- **Falling costs of information,** which lets firms manage far-away operations.

### Importance of globalisation for business

It creates both opportunities and threats:

| Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|
| Bigger markets and economies of scale | More competition from foreign firms at home |
| Cheaper inputs and labour | Dependence on distant supply chains; disruption |
| Spreading risk across markets | Exchange rate risk |
| Access to skills, technology and finance | Pressure on prices and margins |
| Learning from foreign rivals | Reputation risk from suppliers' conduct abroad |

### Importance of emerging economies

**Emerging economies** are fast-growing, lower- or middle-income countries moving towards industrialisation. They matter because they offer:

- **rising middle classes,** creating demand for branded goods, cars, financial services and consumer products
- **large populations** and potential growth for firms in mature markets
- **lower labour costs** and expanding manufacturing capacity
- **new competitors** that may grow into global rivals.

Risks include political instability, corruption, weak legal protection of intellectual property, infrastructure gaps, currency swings and consumer tastes that differ from home.

## Reasons for targeting international markets

- Saturated home market and slow growth
- Bigger sales and economies of scale
- Spreading risk across several economies
- Extending the life of a product that is mature at home
- Following customers or competitors abroad
- Lower costs, or access to resources and skills
- Government encouragement, such as export support

## Factors influencing the attractiveness of an international market

| Factor | Questions |
|---|---|
| Market size and growth | How many potential customers? How fast is demand growing? |
| Income and spending power | Can customers afford the product? |
| Competition | How strong are local and foreign rivals? |
| Political and legal stability | Are laws, tax and property rights stable? Is corruption a risk? |
| Trade barriers | Tariffs, quotas, licences, standards |
| Culture and language | Fit with tastes, religion and habits |
| Infrastructure | Roads, ports, internet, energy |
| Exchange rates | Is the currency stable? |
| Cost of entry | Distribution, marketing, adaptation |
| Labour availability and cost (for production) | Skills and wage levels |

### Weighted scoring

Firms compare markets with a table. Weights show the importance of each factor, and each country is scored from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent).

| Factor | Weight | Arland | Bexton | Corvia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Market size | 0.30 | 4 | 3 | 5 |
| Growth | 0.25 | 3 | 5 | 4 |
| Political stability | 0.20 | 5 | 3 | 2 |
| Competition (5 = weak) | 0.15 | 2 | 3 | 2 |
| Ease of entry | 0.10 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| **Weighted score** | | **3.65** | **3.50** | **3.40** |

For Arland: 0.30 × 4 + 0.25 × 3 + 0.20 × 5 + 0.15 × 2 + 0.10 × 4 = 1.20 + 0.75 + 1.00 + 0.30 + 0.40 = 3.65.

**Evaluation of the method:** it is structured and makes assumptions visible, but the weights and scores are subjective, a small change in weights can change the ranking (Arland leads Bexton by only 0.15), and it cannot capture risks that are rare but severe.

## Methods of entering international markets

| Method | Description | Value | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Export | Produce at home and sell abroad, directly or via agents or online | Low cost and risk; flexible; uses existing production | Transport costs, tariffs, exchange rate risk; less control over marketing and customers |
| Licensing | Allow a foreign firm to produce or sell using your brand, technology or design for a fee or royalty | Low investment; quick; local knowledge from partner | Less control over quality and brand; income limited; may train a future rival |
| Alliances (joint venture, strategic alliance) | Co-operate with a local or foreign partner, sharing costs and risks | Shares risk and cost; local knowledge; may be required by law | Conflicts; profit sharing; risk of knowledge leakage |
| Direct investment | Buy or build operations abroad (takeover, new factory, subsidiary) | Full control; close to customers; avoids tariffs and transport costs | High cost and risk; political risk; local knowledge needed |

*Licensing example.* A licensee sells £10m of the licensor's brand products abroad and pays a 6% royalty: **£600,000** a year to the licensor, with no investment in factories.

**Choosing between methods** depends on: the size of the opportunity, the resources and risk appetite of the firm, how much control it needs, the speed required, local rules (some countries limit foreign ownership), and the firm's experience. Firms often start with exporting and move to direct investment as the market grows.

## Producing and sourcing abroad: off-shoring and re-shoring

**Off-shoring** is moving production or services to another country, usually to cut costs. **Re-shoring** is bringing them back to the home country.

**Reasons for producing or sourcing abroad:** lower labour or material costs, access to skills or resources, being near customers, avoiding tariffs, and spreading risk.

**Reasons for re-shoring:** rising wages abroad, higher shipping costs, quality problems, long lead times and supply chain disruption, a wish for faster response to customers, reputational pressure over working conditions, and government incentives.

### Worked example: should Marchmont Garden Tools off-shore?

Marchmont makes a tool in the UK for **£25.00** a unit. Producing overseas would cost:

| Item | Per unit |
|---|---|
| Manufacturing | £15.00 |
| Shipping and handling | £3.00 |
| Import tariff (4% of £15) | £0.60 |
| Extra inventory (longer lead time) | £1.20 |
| Extra quality control | £0.70 |
| **Total** | **£20.50** |

Saving per unit: £25.00 − £20.50 = **£4.50**, which is 18%. On 200,000 units a year, the saving is **£900,000**. Setting up costs £1.5m once, so payback is 1.5m ÷ 0.9m = **1.67 years**.

**Judgement.** The financial case is strong, but costs not in the table could change the picture: redundancy costs at home, supply chain risk, the effect of exchange rate changes, and the brand impact if "made in Britain" matters to customers. If wages overseas rise 20% a year, the saving may vanish within a few years. Marchmont should test the decision with sensitivity analysis.

## Influences on buying, selling and producing abroad

- **Costs:** labour, materials, transport, tariffs and exchange rates.
- **Demand:** incomes, tastes, product fit.
- **Legal and political:** stability, regulation, taxes, trade agreements, sanctions.
- **Infrastructure and skills.**
- **Ethical and reputational:** working conditions, environmental standards, public opinion.
- **Technology:** ability to manage remote supply chains.
- **Competitors' actions.**

## Managing international business: local responsiveness against cost reduction

Multinationals face two opposing pressures:

| Pressure | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure for **cost reduction** | Standardise products and processes worldwide to gain economies of scale | One global product design; central purchasing; a few large factories |
| Pressure for **local responsiveness** | Adapt to local tastes, laws, language and customs | Different menus, languages, pack sizes, local pricing, local managers |

*Global standardisation* lowers costs but may fail to suit local customers. *Local adaptation* increases sales but raises costs and weakens control. Many firms use a middle path: a common core product or brand with local variations ("think global, act local"). The balance depends on how different customers are, how large the cost savings are, and how strict local rules are.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** choose a reason or a method and explain, step by step, its effect on cost, revenue, risk and control.
- **Evaluate:** compare methods for the firm's size, resources and objectives, consider risks, and state what the choice depends on.
- Use evidence from the case about the market, competitors and costs.

## Common mistakes

- Using "globalisation" and "internationalisation" interchangeably with no distinction.
- Assuming off-shoring is always cheaper once all costs are counted.
- Ignoring political and cultural risk.
- Confusing licensing (granting rights) with franchising or direct investment.
- Forgetting to consider the opposite pressures of cost reduction and local responsiveness.
