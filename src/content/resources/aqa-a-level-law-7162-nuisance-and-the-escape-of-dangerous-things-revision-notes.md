## Private nuisance: definition

Unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or some right over or in connection with it.

**Three types:** encroachment; physical damage to land; interference with comfort and enjoyment.

## Parties

| Question | Rule | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Who can sue? | Person with a proprietary interest (owner, tenant) | *Hunter v Canary Wharf* (1997) |
| Who can be sued? | Creator | General rule |
| | Occupier who adopts or continues a nuisance created by a trespasser | *Sedleigh-Denfield v O'Callaghan* (1940) |
| | Occupier who fails to act on a known natural hazard | *Leakey v National Trust* (1980) |
| | Landlord who authorised the nuisance | General rule |

## Unreasonableness factors

| Factor | Case | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Locality | *Sturges v Bridgman* (1879) | Character of the area matters for comfort cases |
| Locality irrelevant for physical damage | *St Helen's Smelting v Tipping* (1865) | Damage to trees and crops |
| Duration and time | *Halsey v Esso Petroleum* (1961) | Night-time noise, smell, smuts |
| Sensitivity | *Robinson v Kilvert* (1889) | Unusually sensitive use not protected |
| Foreseeability approach | *Network Rail v Morris* (2004) | Interference with amplified guitars unforeseeable |
| Malice | *Christie v Davey* (1893); *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett* (1936) | Deliberate harm is unreasonable |
| Overlooking | *Fearn v Tate Gallery* (2023) | Constant viewing into flats can be a nuisance |

**Planning permission:** not a defence; may change the locality (*Gillingham BC v Medway Dock*, 1993) or not (*Wheeler v JJ Saunders*, 1996); limited relevance to liability (*Coventry v Lawrence*, 2014).

## Rylands v Fletcher

**Origin (1868):** reservoir water burst into the claimant's mine through old shafts. Blackburn J: a person who brings onto land and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes keeps it "at his peril". House of Lords: use must be non-natural.

| Element | Case | Point |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Brought onto land and accumulated | *Giles v Walker* (1890) | Naturally growing thistles: no liability |
| 2 Likely to do mischief if it escapes | *Transco v Stockport MBC* (2003) | Ordinary water pipe not enough |
| 3 Non-natural use | *Rickards v Lothian* (1913) | Domestic water supply is natural |
| | *Transco* (2003) | Extraordinary and unusual use, exceptionally high risk |
| | *Cambridge Water* (1994) | Chemical storage almost a classic non-natural use |
| 4 Escape | *Read v J Lyons* (1947) | Explosion inside factory: no escape |
| 5 Foreseeable type of damage | *Cambridge Water* (1994) | Contamination of distant borehole not foreseeable |

**Claimant:** needs an interest in land (*Transco*, following *Hunter*).
**Damage:** to land and property; not personal injury.
**Fire:** *Stannard v Gore* (2012): tyres not exceptionally dangerous; fire, not tyres, escaped.

## Nuisance and Rylands compared

| | Private nuisance | Rylands v Fletcher |
|---|---|---|
| Typical harm | Continuing interference | Single escape |
| Key test | Unreasonable use in effect on neighbour | Non-natural use and escape |
| Fault | Not required as such | Strict liability |
| Claimant | Interest in land | Interest in land |
| Personal injury | Not recoverable | Not recoverable |

## Evaluation points

- **Balancing rights:** nuisance asks neighbours to "give and take", but outcomes depend heavily on locality and judicial impression.
- **Who can sue:** *Hunter* keeps nuisance tied to land, but leaves family members without a claim for the same disturbance.
- **Rylands narrowed:** after *Transco* few claims succeed, and personal injury is excluded, so negligence often does the real work.
- **Planning:** letting public planning decisions change a locality can remove private rights without compensation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Proprietary interest | A legal interest in land, such as ownership or a tenancy |
| Encroachment | Something physically crossing onto the claimant's land |
| Adopting a nuisance | Using a hazard created by another |
| Continuing a nuisance | Failing to end a hazard the occupier knows of |
| Non-natural use | An extraordinary and unusual use creating exceptionally high risk |
| Escape | Movement of the thing from the defendant's control to outside it |
| Strict liability | Liability without proof of fault |

## Exam traps

- Lodgers and family members without an interest cannot sue (*Hunter*).
- Planning permission is not a defence.
- *Rylands* never covers personal injury after *Transco*.
- Escape must be from land the defendant controls.
- Physical damage does not need the locality factor.

## Self-test

1. Define private nuisance. *Unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land or rights over it.*
2. Who can sue in private nuisance? *A person with a proprietary interest in the land affected.*
3. What did *Sedleigh-Denfield* decide? *An occupier who adopts or continues a nuisance created by a trespasser is liable.*
4. What did *Leakey* decide? *An occupier must take reasonable steps against a known natural hazard.*
5. Why was locality irrelevant in *St Helen's Smelting*? *The nuisance caused physical damage to property.*
6. What did *Network Rail v Morris* decide? *Unforeseeable interference with an unusually sensitive use was not a nuisance.*
7. How can planning permission affect nuisance? *It can change the character of the locality, but it is not a defence.*
8. What did *Fearn* (2023) decide? *Constant viewing into flats from a platform could be a nuisance.*
9. Why did *Giles v Walker* fail? *The thistles grew naturally; nothing was brought onto the land.*
10. What test for non-natural use did *Transco* give? *An extraordinary and unusual use creating an exceptionally high risk.*
11. Why did *Read v Lyons* fail? *There was no escape from the defendant's premises.*
12. Why did the claim fail in *Cambridge Water*? *Damage of that type was not foreseeable.*
