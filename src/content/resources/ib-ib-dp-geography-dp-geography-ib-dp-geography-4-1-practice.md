## Section A

**1.** Define the factor of safety of a slope. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between a rotational slide and a translational slide. **[2]**

**3.** State the depth range of an intermediate-focus earthquake. **[1]**

**4.** Outline the main difference between a shield volcano and a stratovolcano. **[2]**

**5.** Outline how a caldera forms. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A slope section has resisting forces of 300 kN and driving forces of 240 kN in dry conditions. After heavy rain, driving forces rise by 10 per cent and resisting forces fall by 15 per cent.

**(a)** Calculate the factor of safety in dry conditions. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the factor of safety after rain, to two decimal places, and state whether the slope is stable. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain the two ways in which rainwater changes the balance of forces. **[2]**

**7.** A volcanic island is 600 km from an active hotspot and is 7.5 million years old.

**(a)** Calculate the speed of plate movement in centimetres per year. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the islands in the chain become older with distance from the hotspot. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why deep-focus earthquakes occur almost only at subduction zones. **[3]**

**9.** Describe the sequence of eruption styles from Hawaiian to Plinian, and explain what controls it. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine the physical factors that influence the type and speed of mass movement. **[10]**

**11.** Explain how plate boundary type influences the nature of volcanic activity. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The ratio of the forces resisting movement on a slope to the forces driving it; a value below 1 means failure [1]. **[1]**

**2.** In a rotational slide, material moves along a curved, spoon-shaped surface, so the block tilts back as it moves [1]; in a translational slide, material moves along a flat surface such as a bedding plane, staying roughly parallel to the slope [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 70 to 300 km [1]. **[1]**

**4.** A shield volcano is a broad, gently sloping dome built of runny basaltic lava flows [1], whereas a stratovolcano is a steep cone of alternating layers of lava and ash from explosive eruptions of viscous magma [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A very large eruption empties much of the magma chamber beneath a volcano [1], so the overlying rock loses support and collapses into the chamber, leaving a large depression [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 300 / 240 = **1.25** [1].

**(b)** Driving forces = 240 x 1.10 = 264 kN [1]. Resisting forces = 300 x 0.85 = 255 kN [1]. Factor of safety = 255 / 264 = **0.97**, so the slope **fails** (below 1) [1].

**(c)** Water adds weight to the slope material, increasing the driving force [1]; pore water pressure pushes grains apart, reducing friction and cohesion and so lowering the resisting force [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** 600 km = 60,000,000 cm [1]. Speed = 60,000,000 / 7,500,000 = **8 cm per year** [1].

**(b)** The mantle plume stays in place while the plate moves over it [1], so each new volcano is carried away from the hotspot and becomes extinct, and the one furthest away formed first [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Earthquakes need brittle rock that can fracture [1]. At most depths the mantle is too hot and ductile to fracture [1], but at subduction zones cold, brittle oceanic lithosphere is carried down to depths of several hundred kilometres, where it can still rupture [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Hawaiian eruptions produce lava fountains and flows; Strombolian eruptions give regular bursts of lava fragments; Vulcanian eruptions are short, violent explosions clearing a blocked vent; Plinian eruptions produce sustained, very high ash columns [1] [1]. Explosivity increases along the sequence because magma viscosity rises, mainly with silica content and lower temperature [1], and more gas is trapped until it is released violently [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Slope angle:** steeper slopes increase shear stress, favouring falls and rapid slides [1].
- **Rock type and structure:** jointed rock favours falls and topples [1]; bedding planes parallel to the slope favour translational slides [1]; weak clays favour rotational slides and flows [1].
- **Water content:** little water gives falls and slides; saturated material gives flows [1]; pore water pressure reduces strength [1].
- **Vegetation:** roots bind soil and slow creep and slides; its removal increases risk [1].
- **Undercutting and seismic shaking:** remove support or add stress suddenly [1].
- **Examination:** factors interact; the same slope may creep for decades and then slide after extreme rain [1].
- **Conclusion:** water content is often the decisive factor in both type and speed, acting on slopes made susceptible by angle and geology [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Divergent boundaries:** decompression melting of the mantle produces basaltic magma [1]; low viscosity and gas escape easily, so eruptions are effusive, building lava plateaus or shields, as in Iceland [1].
- **Subduction boundaries:** water released from the sinking plate lowers the melting point of the overlying mantle [1]; magma rising through crust becomes more silica-rich and viscous [1]; gas is trapped, giving explosive eruptions and stratovolcanoes, as in Japan and the Andes [1].
- **Continental collision:** little volcanic activity, because neither plate subducts deeply [1].
- **Transform boundaries:** usually no volcanic activity, because crust is neither created nor destroyed [1].
- **Hotspots (contrast):** basaltic shield volcanoes away from boundaries, showing boundary type is not the only control [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Link each boundary type to a melting process and then to magma viscosity. That chain is what turns description into explanation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying a percentage change to the wrong force, or adding instead of multiplying.
- Forgetting to convert kilometres to centimetres.
- Describing eruption styles without the viscosity and gas explanation.
- Writing "landslide" for every form of mass movement.
