## What the specification asks

The content covers sole traders, partnerships, private limited companies (ltd), public limited companies (plc) and not-for-profit organisations. Students should be able to:

- understand the different legal structures that businesses adopt
- analyse the benefits and drawbacks of each, including **management and control, sources of finance available, liability and distribution of profits**
- understand the concept of limited liability and which legal structures benefit from it
- evaluate which legal structure would be most appropriate for a variety of business examples, including new start-ups and large established businesses

The specification adds that you are not expected to know the legal process of incorporation. Keep your answers on the four issues above; they are the criteria for every comparison.

## One business, four structures: a growth story

Following one fictional business through its life shows why structures change.

### Stage 1: sole trader

Tomasz starts Brightwell Bikes, repairing bicycles from a rented lock-up. He needs only tools and a little stock, paid for from £6,000 of savings. As a sole trader he makes every decision, keeps all the profit and can start trading straight away. The trade-off is **unlimited liability** and limited finance: he cannot borrow much, and if the business fails owing money, his personal possessions are at risk.

**Trigger for change:** demand outgrows one person. He is turning customers away.

### Stage 2: partnership

Tomasz takes on Leah, an experienced mechanic who invests £10,000. Now the business has more capital, two sets of skills and someone to cover holidays and illness. They agree in writing how profit is shared and who decides what. But decisions now need agreement, profit is split, and liability remains unlimited for both partners.

**Trigger for change:** they want to open a shop and sell new bikes, which needs a large loan and stock. Personal risk becomes too great.

### Stage 3: private limited company

They form Brightwell Bikes Ltd. Tomasz and Leah become shareholders and directors. Liability is now **limited**: if the company fails, they lose what they invested in shares but not their homes. Banks may lend more readily to a company, and they can sell shares privately to people they choose, such as family or a local investor, without losing control as long as they keep most of the shares. In return, there is more paperwork, and the company's accounts are filed publicly.

**Trigger for change:** after years of growth they plan fifty stores across the country, needing millions of pounds.

### Stage 4: public limited company

Floating on a stock exchange as Brightwell Bikes plc lets the company sell shares to the public, raising very large sums. Tomasz and Leah may end up owning only a small share. Directors must answer to many shareholders who expect dividends and a rising share price, and anyone can buy enough shares to try a takeover.

The lesson for the exam: a structure suits a **stage**, not a business for ever. Each move gains finance or protection and gives up some control or privacy.

## Liability in numbers

### Partners share the risk, but not neatly

In an ordinary partnership each partner can be pursued for the debts of the whole business, not just their "share".

**Worked example.** Brightwell's partnership closes with debts £30,000 greater than its assets. Leah has savings and a house; Tomasz has no assets left. Creditors can claim the full £30,000 from Leah, even if the debts came from Tomasz's decisions. This is why choosing a trustworthy partner, and a written partnership agreement, matter so much.

### Limited liability caps the loss

If Brightwell Bikes Ltd failed with the same shortfall, each shareholder would lose only the money paid for their shares. The company, as a separate legal identity, owes the debts.

## Control in numbers: share dilution

Selling shares raises finance but spreads ownership. Control in a company depends on who owns most of the shares.

**Worked example.** Brightwell Bikes Ltd has 10,000 shares. Tomasz owns 6,000 (60%), so with the majority he can outvote Leah. The company sells 5,000 new shares to an investor at £10 each.

```
Finance raised    = 5,000 × £10 = £50,000
Total shares now  = 10,000 + 5,000 = 15,000
Tomasz's share    = 6,000 ÷ 15,000 × 100 = 40%
```

Tomasz has kept all his shares but lost his majority. If the investor sides with Leah, Tomasz can be outvoted. This is the trade-off between **sources of finance** and **management and control** in one calculation.

## Distribution of profits

| Structure | What happens to profit |
|---|---|
| Sole trader | All belongs to the owner |
| Partnership | Shared as the partnership agreement sets out, which may reflect money invested or hours worked |
| Ltd and plc | Directors decide how much to pay as dividends; the rest is retained for investment |
| Not-for-profit | Any surplus is put back into the organisation's purpose |

**Worked example.** A plc makes £2.4 million profit after tax and pays out 40% as dividends on its 12 million shares.

```
Dividends paid    = 0.40 × £2,400,000 = £960,000
Dividend per share = £960,000 ÷ 12,000,000 = £0.08 (8p)
Retained profit   = £2,400,000 − £960,000 = £1,440,000
```

Paying more pleases shareholders now; retaining more funds growth that may raise the share price later. That tension is a common evaluation point.

## Not-for-profit organisations

Not-for-profit organisations, such as charities and community groups, exist mainly to benefit a cause. They still need to cover their costs and may trade, for example through a charity shop, but any surplus is reinvested in the cause rather than paid to owners. Their finance comes from donations, grants and trading income, which can be less predictable than a company's sales. When a case describes a business whose main purpose is social, consider whether a not-for-profit structure suits it better than a company.

## Choosing a structure: three short judgements

1. **A school-leaver starting a window-cleaning round** with a ladder and a van bought from savings. Little capital is needed and risk is low, so a sole trader suits, because it is simple and keeps full control. Unlimited liability matters little while debts are small.
2. **A family restaurant borrowing £200,000 to open a second site.** A large loan makes unlimited liability dangerous, so a private limited company suits: limited liability protects family homes, and shares can stay within the family to keep control.
3. **An established retailer needing £30 million to expand abroad.** Only a plc can raise this from many investors, though the founders accept loss of control, public scrutiny and takeover risk. If they value control more, they could grow more slowly as a large private company using loans.

Each judgement names the need, picks the structure that meets it, and states the cost of that choice.

## Exam technique for evaluate questions

For "evaluate which structure would be most appropriate":

- Identify what the business needs most: finance, control, protection, speed or simplicity.
- Compare at least two structures on the four issues, using case figures where possible.
- Recommend one, explain why it best fits the business's main need, and say what would change your answer.

## Common mistakes

- Saying limited liability means the company has limited debts. The owners' losses are limited.
- Assuming partners each owe only half the debts.
- Treating a plc as owned by the government.
- Recommending a plc for a small start-up because it "raises the most money".
- Saying shareholders automatically keep control after selling new shares.
- Ignoring the four issues and comparing structures on vague ideas such as "it is better".
