**3.1.3 Biopsychology** is Section C of Paper 1 (Cognition and Behaviour) in OxfordAQA International
GCSE Psychology (9218), Version 1.1. It explains how the nervous and endocrine systems produce
behaviour, how neurons communicate, how the brain divides its work, and how scanning and brain
damage reveal which areas do what. Expect labelling and definition questions as well as scenarios
that ask you to apply a process to a real situation.

## What the specification lists

- The divisions of the nervous system: **central** and **peripheral** (**somatic** and
  **autonomic**), and their basic functions
- The **endocrine system**: glands and hormones; the autonomic nervous system and the **fight or
  flight response**; the **James-Lange theory of emotion**
- **Sensory, relay and motor neurons**; **synaptic transmission** (release and reuptake of
  neurotransmitters); **excitation and inhibition**, and how these processes interact
- **Hebb's theory of learning and neuronal growth**
- Brain structure (**frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital lobes and cerebellum**) and localisation
  of function: **motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory** and **language areas** (Broca's and
  Wernicke's)
- **Penfield's study of the interpretive cortex**
- **Cognitive neuroscience**; scanning with **CT, PET and fMRI**; **Tulving's 'gold' memory study**
- How **neurological damage**, such as stroke or injury, can affect motor abilities and behaviour

## The nervous system

| Division | Made up of | Basic function |
|---|---|---|
| **Central nervous system** | Brain and spinal cord | Processes information and makes decisions; the spinal cord relays messages to and from the brain |
| **Peripheral nervous system** | All nerves outside the brain and spinal cord | Carries messages between the central nervous system and the body |
| **Somatic** (part of peripheral) | Sensory and motor nerves to skin and skeletal muscles | Voluntary movement and sensation |
| **Autonomic** (part of peripheral) | Nerves to internal organs and glands | Involuntary functions such as heart rate, breathing and digestion |

## The endocrine system and fight or flight

The **endocrine system** is a set of **glands** that release **hormones** into the bloodstream. Hormones
travel to target organs and change how they work. The system is slower than the nervous system but
its effects last longer. The **pituitary gland** in the brain controls many other glands; the
**adrenal glands** above the kidneys release **adrenaline**.

**Fight or flight.** When a threat is perceived, the autonomic nervous system activates the body and
the adrenal glands release adrenaline. Heart rate and breathing rate rise, pupils widen, muscles
tense, sweating increases and digestion slows. Energy is sent where it is needed to fight or run.
When the threat passes, the autonomic nervous system returns the body to a resting state.

**The James-Lange theory of emotion** says the order of events is: event, then **physiological
arousal**, then the brain's **interpretation** of that arousal, which is the emotion. "We feel afraid
because we tremble." Evaluation: it fits cases where people feel panic after noticing a racing heart,
but critics such as Cannon argued that bodily changes are too slow to explain how quickly emotions
appear, and that the same arousal occurs in quite different emotions, such as fear and excitement.

## Neurons and synaptic transmission

| Neuron | Where it is | Job | Structure clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sensory** | From receptors to the central nervous system | Carries messages from the senses | Long dendrites, short axon |
| **Relay** | Within the central nervous system | Connects sensory and motor neurons | Short dendrites, short axon |
| **Motor** | From the central nervous system to muscles and glands | Carries messages that cause action | Short dendrites, long axon |

A message travels along a neuron as an **electrical impulse**. At the end of the axon it reaches the
**synapse**, a tiny gap between neurons, where it becomes chemical:

1. The impulse reaches the axon terminal, where **vesicles** hold **neurotransmitters**.
2. Neurotransmitters are **released** into the synaptic gap.
3. They **bind to receptors** on the next neuron.
4. The effect is **excitatory** (the next neuron is more likely to fire) or **inhibitory** (less
   likely to fire).
5. Leftover neurotransmitter is taken back into the first neuron (**reuptake**) or broken down, so
   the signal stops.

**How the processes interact.** A neuron receives inputs from many others at once. Whether it fires
depends on the **overall balance** of excitatory and inhibitory signals: if excitation outweighs
inhibition, it fires; if not, it does not. This is why a single signal rarely decides behaviour on its
own.

## Hebb's theory of learning and neuronal growth

Hebb proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them
becomes **stronger and more efficient**. Groups of neurons that are repeatedly active together form
**cell assemblies** that represent a memory or skill. Learning therefore physically changes the brain,
and practice makes pathways quicker to activate. This links biopsychology to memory: rehearsal
strengthens synaptic connections.

## Brain structure and localisation of function

| Structure | Main functions | Named area |
|---|---|---|
| **Frontal lobe** | Planning, decision-making, voluntary movement | **Motor area**; **Broca's area** (speech production), usually in the left hemisphere |
| **Parietal lobe** | Processing touch, temperature and body position | **Somatosensory area** |
| **Temporal lobe** | Hearing, language comprehension, memory | **Auditory area**; **Wernicke's area** (understanding language), usually in the left hemisphere |
| **Occipital lobe** | Vision | **Visual area** |
| **Cerebellum** | Balance, posture and coordination of movement | — |

**Localisation of function** is the idea that specific areas carry out specific functions. Each side
of the motor area controls movement on the **opposite** side of the body.

**Penfield's study of the interpretive cortex.** Penfield operated on patients with epilepsy who were
**awake** during surgery. He stimulated points on the surface of the brain with a weak electric
current and recorded what patients reported. Stimulating parts of the **temporal lobe** sometimes
produced vivid experiences such as hearing music or reliving past events. Evaluation: it gives direct
evidence that particular areas are linked to particular experiences, but the patients' brains were
not typical because of their epilepsy, and only a minority reported such experiences, so the findings
may not generalise.

## Cognitive neuroscience and scanning

**Cognitive neuroscience** studies how brain structure and activity relate to thinking and behaviour.

| Scan | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CT** | Series of X-rays builds an image of structure | Quick, widely available | Shows structure, not activity; uses radiation |
| **PET** | A radioactive tracer shows which areas are most active | Shows activity during a task | Radioactive; expensive |
| **fMRI** | Magnetic fields detect changes in blood oxygen in active areas | Shows activity; no radiation | Expensive; slight delay between activity and signal |

**Tulving's 'gold' memory study.** A small group of participants, including Tulving, were injected
with a small dose of radioactive gold that travelled in the blood, so active brain areas could be
detected. They thought about personal experiences (**episodic** memory) or general facts
(**semantic** memory). Episodic thinking was linked to greater activity towards the **front** of the
cortex and semantic thinking towards the **back**. Evaluation: it supports localisation and the idea
that episodic and semantic memory are different, but the sample was tiny and the pattern was not
clear for every participant.

## Neurological damage

A **stroke** cuts off blood to part of the brain; an **injury** damages tissue directly. The effect
depends on where the damage is. Damage to the motor area on one side can cause weakness or paralysis
on the opposite side of the body. Damage to **Broca's area** causes slow, effortful speech, though
understanding remains. Damage to **Wernicke's area** causes fluent but meaningless speech and poor
understanding. Damage to the frontal lobe can change personality and self-control.

## Worked example

*Jun touches a hot pan and pulls his hand away. Describe the role of the three types of neuron.*
[3 marks]

```
1. Sensory neurons carry the message from heat receptors in his
   hand to the spinal cord.                                     [1]
2. Relay neurons in the spinal cord pass the message on to motor
   neurons (and to the brain).                                  [1]
3. Motor neurons carry the message to the arm muscles, which
   contract and pull the hand away.                             [1]
```

## Common mistakes

- Placing Broca's area in the temporal lobe; it is in the frontal lobe. Wernicke's is temporal.
- Saying hormones travel along nerves; they travel in the blood.
- Describing reuptake as the next neuron absorbing neurotransmitter; it is the sending neuron.
- Writing that the James-Lange theory puts emotion before arousal; it is the reverse.
- Saying CT shows brain activity; it shows structure only.

## Exam technique

- **Label / Identify:** precise names (relay neuron, cerebellum).
- **Explain a process:** use a sequence with connectives (first, then, as a result).
- **Apply:** name the structure or process and link it to the person's symptom or action.
- **Evaluate a study:** sample, artificiality and what the evidence supports.
