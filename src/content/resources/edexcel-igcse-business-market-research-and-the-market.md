It covers Topic 4, Marketing: section 4.1 Market research (4.1.1 to 4.1.3) and section 4.2 The market (4.2.1 to 4.2.3). The marketing mix is section 4.3 and is not covered here. Paper 1 uses a small business (up to 49 employees) and Paper 2 a large business (more than 250 employees). Both papers can draw on the whole content.

## 1. The purpose of market research (4.1.1)

Market research is the collection and analysis of information about customers, competitors and the market. The specification lists four purposes.

- **To identify and understand customer needs.** What do people want, how much will they pay, where and how do they like to buy?
- **To identify gaps in the market.** A gap is a want that no existing product meets well. A bakery that finds nobody nearby sells gluten-free bread has found one.
- **To reduce risk.** Launching a product is expensive. Research does not remove the risk, but it replaces guesses with evidence.
- **To inform business decisions.** Price, product design, where to open and how much stock to order are all easier to justify with evidence.

In an answer, link the purpose to the scenario: "Harlow Bakes would buy a second oven before knowing whether customers want sourdough, so research lowers the chance of a costly mistake."

## 2. Methods of market research (4.1.2)

**Primary research** is collected first-hand for the business's own purpose. **Secondary research** uses information that already exists, collected by someone else.

### Primary methods

| Method | What it is | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Survey | Questions put to a sample of people, in person, by phone or online | Reaches many people; results can be counted | Takes time and money; some people give careless answers |
| Questionnaire | A written set of questions that respondents complete themselves | Cheap; the same questions go to everyone | Low response rate; cannot ask follow-up questions |
| Focus group | A small group discussing a product with a leader | Explains why people think as they do | Small sample; strong voices can dominate |
| Observation | Watching what customers actually do, such as which shelf they walk to | Shows real behaviour, not claimed behaviour | Cannot show why they acted that way |
| Test marketing | Selling the product in a limited area or period before a full launch | Tests real buying with real money | Slow, and competitors can see the product early |

In standard usage, a survey is the whole exercise of asking a sample and a questionnaire is the written list of questions used in it. The specification lists both without defining them.

### Secondary methods

- **Internet:** competitor websites, review sites, news and search trends. Quick and often free, but quality varies.
- **Market reports:** published analyses of an industry, usually produced by research firms. Detailed, but may be expensive and not about your exact customers.
- **Government reports:** official statistics, for example on population, incomes or spending. Usually trustworthy and wide in scope, but may be out of date or too general.

**Primary or secondary?** Primary data fits the exact question and is not shared with rivals, but costs more and takes longer. Secondary data is cheaper and faster, but was collected for another purpose.

### Worked example 1: choosing a method

Harlow Bakes, a small business with 12 employees, is thinking of selling fresh sourdough. The owner has little money and wants an answer in a fortnight. **Recommend a method.**

1. Identify what must be found out: will local people buy sourdough, and at what price?
2. Weigh the options. Test marketing costs ingredients and time. A focus group gives opinions from perhaps eight people. A short questionnaire left on the counter is cheap and reaches regular customers.
3. Judgement: use a short questionnaire first, because the budget is small and the deadline is short. Try a limited test sale only if replies show enough interest.

## 3. Using data (4.1.3)

### Qualitative and quantitative data

**Quantitative data** is numerical and can be counted or measured: how many people would buy, average spend, a percentage. **Qualitative data** is descriptive and records opinions, feelings and reasons, such as "the packaging looks cheap".

Quantitative data shows how many and how much. Qualitative data shows why. A focus group usually produces qualitative data. A questionnaire with tick-box answers produces quantitative data, and one with open boxes also produces qualitative data.

**Worked example 2.** Harlow Bakes asks 240 customers whether they would buy sourdough weekly. 84 say yes.

```
Percentage yes = 84 / 240 x 100 = 35%
```

35% is quantitative. A customer comment, "I would buy it if it were sliced", is qualitative. The two together suggest a market that exists and a feature that might widen it.

### The role of social media

Social media can provide market research data cheaply and quickly. A business can run a poll, read comments and reviews, see which posts get most reactions, and see what customers say about competitors. Strengths: low cost, fast results and direct contact with customers. Weaknesses: respondents may not represent all customers, and some accounts may not be real customers.

### Reliability of data

Data is **reliable** when it can be trusted to give a true picture, so that repeating the research would give much the same result. Questions to ask about any data:

- **Sample size:** are enough people included? Ten people tell you little about a city.
- **Representative sample:** do the people questioned match the target market in age, income and location?
- **Bias:** were the questions leading, such as "Don't you agree our bread is excellent?" Were respondents only the business's friends?
- **Age of data:** is it recent? Spending patterns change.
- **Source:** who collected it, and do they gain from a particular result?

Unreliable data can lead to a wrong decision, such as stocking an unwanted product, which defeats the purpose of reducing risk.

## 4. Why marketing matters (4.2.1, part 1)

In standard teaching, marketing is how a business identifies, anticipates and satisfies customer needs profitably. The specification names these three reasons it matters.

- **Satisfying customer needs:** a business that provides what customers want sells more and receives fewer returns.
- **Building customer relationships:** friendly service, replies to complaints and personal offers make customers feel valued.
- **Keeping customer loyalty:** repeat customers cost less to serve than winning new ones, and they recommend the business to others.

### Market orientation and product orientation

| | Market orientation | Product orientation |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | What customers want, found through research | What the business can make or already makes |
| Role of research | Central | Limited |
| Risk | Research costs money and customer wants change | Product may not match demand |
| Fits | Competitive markets with changing tastes | A genuinely new product, or one where the maker's skill is the selling point |

The table is standard teaching. A market-oriented business designs its offer around research results; a product-oriented business makes the product first and then looks for buyers.

## 5. Market share, analysis, niche and mass marketing (4.2.1, part 2)

### Market share and market analysis

**Market share** is the proportion of total market sales that one business makes. The specification names market share but gives no formula, so this is the standard form:

```
Market share (%) = (business sales / total market sales) x 100
```

**Worked example 3.** Total sales of bread in a town last year were 360,000. Harlow Bakes sold 45,000 of it.

```
Market share = 45,000 / 360,000 x 100 = 12.5%
```

This year the town market grows 10% to 396,000, and Harlow Bakes grows its sales 10% to 49,500.

```
New market share = 49,500 / 396,000 x 100 = 12.5%
```

Sales rose, yet share is unchanged: the business only kept pace with the market. A fair judgement is that the business has not gained ground on competitors.

**Market analysis** means studying the market's size, growth, the share of each competitor and customer trends. A business uses it to decide whether to enter, expand or leave a market, and to set targets such as raising share by two percentage points.

### Niche and mass marketing

| | Niche marketing | Mass marketing |
|---|---|---|
| Target | A small, specialised part of the market | The whole market, or most of it |
| Typical product | Specialist, often higher price | Standard, often lower price |
| Advantages | Less competition; loyal customers; premium prices possible | Large sales volume; economies of scale |
| Disadvantages | Limited customer base; harmed if the niche shrinks | Strong competition; lower margins |

A shop selling handmade vegan cakes is niche. A supermarket chain selling bread to all shoppers is mass.

## 6. Responding to changes in the market (4.2.2)

Markets change. The specification names three causes.

- **Changing customer needs.** Tastes, technology and attitudes move. A business should keep researching, adapt products and drop what no longer sells. Response: add healthier recipes after research shows demand.
- **Changing customer or consumer spending patterns.** If incomes fall, people buy cheaper items or fewer of them. If they shift to online shopping, shops lose footfall. Response: introduce value ranges, change prices, add a delivery service.
- **Increased competition.** A new rival can take customers and share. Response: improve quality or service, change prices, promote more strongly or find a niche.

**Worked example 4.** Weekly spending on bakery products by a typical household in Harlow falls from 38 to 34. Percentage change:

```
(34 - 38) / 38 x 100 = -10.5% (to 1 d.p.)
```

A drop of about 10.5% would worry Harlow Bakes. A response is a small, lower-priced range. A drawback is lower profit on each sale, so the owner must check costs first.

## 7. Market segmentation (4.2.3)

A **market segment** is a group of customers who share characteristics. **Market segmentation** is dividing a market into such groups so that a business can target the product, price and promotion at the groups most likely to buy. The specification lists five ways to identify segments.

| Basis | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Where people live or work | A city-centre cafe targets office workers |
| Demographics | Features of the population such as gender, family size or occupation | A toy maker targets parents of young children |
| Lifestyle | How people live and what they value | A sports brand targets people who exercise regularly |
| Income | How much customers earn | A luxury chocolate maker targets high earners |
| Age | The age group of customers | A phone with large buttons targets older adults |

Segmentation helps a business aim its marketing money well. Disadvantages: research costs, and ignoring other segments may lose sales.

## Common errors

- Describing a focus group as quantitative. It mostly gives qualitative views.
- Calling data from a government report primary. The business did not collect it.
- Treating higher sales as a higher market share. Check the total market.
- Writing that segmentation "makes more profit" without explaining how targeting reduces wasted spending.
