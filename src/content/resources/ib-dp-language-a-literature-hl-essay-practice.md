## Questions

### Passage A -- Kate Chopin, "The Story of an Hour" (1894) (public domain; (…) marks cuts)

Louise Mallard has just been told that her husband has been killed in a railroad disaster.

> She knew that she would weep again when she saw the kind, tender hands folded in death; the face
> that had never looked save with love upon her, fixed and gray and dead. But she saw beyond that
> bitter moment a long procession of years to come that would belong to her absolutely. And she
> opened and spread her arms out to them in welcome.
>
> There would be no one to live for her during those coming years; she would live for herself.
> There would be no powerful will bending hers in that blind persistence with which men and women
> believe they have a right to impose a private will upon a fellow-creature. (…)
>
> And yet she had loved him — sometimes. Often she had not. What did it matter! (…)
>
> She arose at length and opened the door to her sister's importunities. There was a feverish
> triumph in her eyes, and she carried herself unwittingly like a goddess of Victory. (…)
>
> When the doctors came they said she had died of heart disease — of the joy that kills.

### Passage B -- a student's draft paragraph (original text written for Marlbridge)

> "The Story of an Hour" is about a woman called Louise Mallard whose husband dies in a train crash.
> Her sister Josephine tells her the news and she cries and goes upstairs. Chopin uses a lot of
> imagery in this part of the story. "There were patches of blue sky showing here and there through
> the clouds that had met and piled one above the other in the west facing her window." Louise then
> says "free, free, free" and realises she will have "no one to live for" in the future. The story
> is really about how marriage in the 1890s was a prison for women.

**1.** State three facts the IB subject brief gives about the HL essay. **[3]**

**2.** Which is the strongest line of inquiry for an HL essay on this story? Justify your choice.
(i) *Is "The Story of an Hour" a feminist story?* (ii) *What happens to Louise Mallard?*
(iii) *How does Chopin set Louise's private thoughts against the doctors' public verdict to make the
reader question who understands her heart?* (iv) *Imagery in "The Story of an Hour".* **[3]**

**3.** Analyse how the sentences "And yet she had loved him — sometimes. Often she had not. What did
it matter!" present Louise's feelings. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how Chopin presents Louise's future in the first paragraph of Passage A. Refer to
precise words. **[4]**

**5.** Explain the effect of the phrase "like a goddess of Victory", and why discussing it counts as
intertextuality in an essay on one work. **[3]**

**6.** Write a Time and space line of inquiry based on the sentence beginning "There would be no
powerful will", and explain what context it needs. **[3]**

**7.** Identify four weaknesses in Passage B. **[4]**

**8.** (a) Correct the two misquotations in Passage B. **[2]**
**(b)** Rewrite the imagery sentence and its quotation as one analytical sentence with a short,
embedded quotation. **[2]**

**9.** A student plans an introduction of 180 words, four body sections of 340 words and a
conclusion of 170 words.

**(a)** Find the total and say how far it is from the limit. **[2]**
**(b)** Revise the plan so it fits, and justify the change. **[2]**

**10.** Using Passage A and your knowledge of the whole story, write a line of inquiry, a thesis, a
three-section plan with a claim and evidence for each section, a word budget, and the first two
sentences of section 1. State how you will reference the story. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** It is a 1,200–1,500 word essay [1]. It is written on one work studied [1]. It is worth 20% of
the final grade at HL [1]. (Also accept: it is written coursework, listed under external
assessment.) **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Only state what the brief says; claims about criteria or drafting rules you have
not been given by your teacher cannot be credited as facts.

**2.** **(iii)** is strongest [1]. It names the method (private thoughts set against a public
verdict) and an effect to argue about (the reader questions the doctors) [1]. (i) invites a yes/no
label, (ii) invites plot summary and (iv) is a topic with nothing to prove [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong justification explains why the rejected options fail, not only why the
chosen one works.

**3.** The short sentences and the dash show Louise correcting herself: "sometimes" shrinks the love
that "she had loved him" seemed to affirm [1]. "Often she had not" is shorter and plainer, so the
truth needs fewer words once admitted [1]. The exclamation "What did it matter!" is a dismissal,
not a question; the narration voices Louise's own thought, so the reader follows her reasoning
from inside [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Weaker answers label "short sentences" and stop; stronger ones say what each
sentence does to the one before it.

**4.** "A long procession of years" turns time into a slow, ceremonial approach towards her [1].
"Belong to her absolutely" reverses the language of possession: she will own her years rather than
be owned [1]. She "opened and spread her arms out to them in welcome", so her body embraces the
future [1]. This contrasts with his "kind, tender hands folded in death": his hands are closed,
hers open, so grief is acknowledged but outweighed [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Precise single words ("absolutely", "folded") analysed closely earn more than
a long quotation paraphrased.

**5.** It alludes to the classical goddess of victory (Nike in Greek, Victoria in Roman
tradition) [1]. It raises a private moment at a bedroom door to the scale of a public triumph, and
"unwittingly" shows Louise does not know she is performing it [1]. It is intertextuality because
the story draws on another tradition; within one work, it also makes the ending's reversal
steeper, since the "Victory" lasts only until the front door opens [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the allusion is not enough; the credit comes from explaining what it
does in this work's argument.

**6.** Model: *How does Chopin present the "powerful will" of marriage as a burden imposed by "men
and women" alike, and how might readers in 1894 and today judge Louise's wish to "live for
herself" differently?* [1]. Context needed: how limited a married woman's legal and economic
independence was in the 1890s [1]. It changes the reading: in 1894 her wish may sound like a claim
to something the law did not give her; today it may sound ordinary, which makes the ending harder
to accept [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Context earns credit only when it is tied to how a specific phrase is read.

**7.** Any four: the paragraph opens with plot summary, not a claim [1]; it misreads the
plot, since the husband is reported dead but is alive [1]; "a lot of imagery" names a technique
without any effect [1]; the long quotation is dropped in as its own sentence and never
analysed [1]. (Also accept: misquotations; a sweeping claim about the 1890s with no evidence or
credit.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Diagnosing a weak paragraph precisely is the skill you need when revising your
own draft.

**8. (a)** "free, free, free!" needs its exclamation mark [1]. The text reads "no one to live for
her"; dropping "her" reverses the meaning [1].
**(b)** Model: *The "patches of blue sky" breaking through the clouds give Louise glimpses of an
open future before she can name it* [1], *so the weather prepares the reader for the word "free"* [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A short embedded quotation followed by analysis in the same sentence is stronger
than a long quotation with no comment.

**9. (a)** 180 + 4 × 340 + 170 = **1,710 words** [1], which is **210 words over** the 1,500
maximum [1].
**(b)** Cut one body section: 180 + 3 × 340 + 170 = **1,370 words** [1]. This keeps the analysis in
each remaining section and leaves room for revision, provided the cut section's point overlapped
with another [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Trimming every section equally usually cuts analysis; removing the weakest
section keeps the argument's depth.

**10.** Indicative model plan:

- Line of inquiry: *How does Chopin turn Louise's "heart trouble" into a judgement on marriage, and
  how far does the final sentence ask us to reject the doctors' reading?* [1]
- Thesis: the heart weakens under marriage and quickens at the thought of freedom; "the joy that
  kills" is dramatic irony, because the reader knows loss of freedom killed her [1].
- Section 1, confinement: "afflicted with a heart trouble" and "pressed down by a physical
  exhaustion" [1].
- Section 2, awakening: "free, free, free!" and arms "spread … in welcome" [1].
- Section 3, the verdict: "like a goddess of Victory" set against "of the joy that kills" [1].
- A deliberate lens, for example Time and space: how a reader in 1894 might hear "live for herself"
  [1].
- Budget: 150 + 3 × 370 + 140 = 1,400 words, inside 1,200–1,500 [1].
- Opening of section 1: *Chopin introduces Louise through a diagnosis, not a description: she is
  "afflicted with a heart trouble".* [1] *The medical word "afflicted" makes her fragile before she
  has spoken, which is exactly the reading the story will overturn.* [1]
- Referencing: page numbers from the class edition in the school's style, and a works cited list
  including the story [1]. **[10]**

The real essay is marked holistically against the IB assessment criteria your teacher will share,
not point by point; these points show what a strong plan contains.
*Examiner insight:* The strongest plans make every section answer the line of inquiry; weaker ones
list three themes that sit side by side.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing on a topic instead of a question, so the essay describes rather than argues.
- Spending the opening on plot the reader already knows.
- Naming "imagery" or "repetition" without analysing the particular words.
- Dropping long quotations in as separate sentences.
- Misquoting: a missing word such as "her" or a lost exclamation mark can change the meaning.
- Using context that never returns to a specific line.
- Explaining an allusion without saying what it does in the work.
- Planning over 1,500 words and cutting analysis at the end.
