## Questions

**1.** Opportunity cost and trade-off.

**(a)** Define opportunity cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Distinguish a trade-off from an opportunity cost. **[2]**

**2.** A self-employed designer (illustrative) has annual revenue of £64,000 and explicit costs of £21,000. She could earn £38,000 as an employee and could rent out her own office for £9,000.

**(a)** Calculate her accounting profit. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate her economic profit, showing your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what the answer to (b) tells her. **[2]**

**3.** Country C (illustrative) can produce these combinations of manufactured goods and services.

| Point | Manufactured goods | Services |
|---|---|---|
| P | 0 | 60 |
| Q | 20 | 56 |
| R | 40 | 48 |
| S | 60 | 32 |
| T | 80 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one unit of manufactured goods when moving from Q to R. **[2]**
**(b)** State and explain how opportunity cost changes along the frontier. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain whether the combination of 40 manufactured goods and 40 services is attainable and efficient. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why 60 manufactured goods with 50 services cannot be produced. **[2]**

**4.** Country D (illustrative) has a PPC/F for capital goods and consumer goods.

**(a)** Explain two factors that could shift the PPC/F outward. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain one factor that could shift it inward. **[2]**

**5.** An economy (illustrative) has a straight-line PPC/F: up to 80 units of X or 50 units of Y. New technology raises the maximum output of Y by 30%. The maximum of X is unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate the new maximum of Y. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one unit of Y, in units of X, before and after (2 decimal places). **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how the frontier changes. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the opportunity cost of Y falls. **[1]**

**6.** Explain how a PPC/F can illustrate scarcity, choice, opportunity cost and productive efficiency. **[8]**

**7.** A bicycle workshop (illustrative) has 10 workers paid £80 a day each. Each builds whole bicycles at 1.2 a day. After the work is divided into stations, total output is 21 bicycles a day.

**(a)** Calculate output before the change. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage rise in output. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate labour cost per bicycle before and after (to the nearest penny). **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage fall in labour cost per bicycle (1 decimal place). **[1]**

**8.** Evaluate the role of the division of labour in addressing the problem of scarcity. **[10]**

**9.** Tomas and Ilse (illustrative) each have 10 hours. Tomas bakes 4 loaves or makes 1 jug per hour. Ilse bakes 1 loaf or makes 4 jugs per hour. Without specialising, each spends 5 hours on each task.

**(a)** Calculate the output of each person and the total of each good. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total of each good if each specialises for the whole 10 hours. **[1]**
**(c)** They swap 15 loaves for 15 jugs. Show that both are better off than in (a). **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how the example shows that specialisation encourages exchange. **[2]**

**10.** A village market (illustrative) trades 15 types of goods by barter.

**(a)** Calculate the number of exchange rates under barter and the number of prices if money is introduced. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the "double coincidence of wants", using an example. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain how two functions of money overcome problems of barter. **[4]**

**11.** A government (illustrative) must choose between spending a fixed sum on a new rail line or on flood defences. Evaluate the usefulness of the concept of opportunity cost to the government in making this decision. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The value [1] of the next best alternative given up [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** A trade-off is the situation in which more of one thing means accepting less of another [1]. Opportunity cost is the value of the particular best alternative lost [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Put "next best" in the definition; "everything you give up" describes a list.

**2. (a)** 64,000 − 21,000 = **£43,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Opportunity costs are 38,000 + 9,000 = 47,000 [1]. Economic profit = 43,000 − 47,000 = **−£4,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** She would be £4,000 a year better off as an employee who rents out the office [1]. The costs are real even though no cash is paid out [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both implicit costs in the working so the method is visible.

**3. (a)** Services fall from 56 to 48, a loss of 8 [1]. 8 ÷ 20 = **0.4 services per unit** of manufactured goods [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** It rises [1] from 0.2 (P to Q) to 1.6 (S to T), because resources suit the two uses unequally [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** It is attainable because it lies inside the frontier [1]. It is inefficient because 48 services could be made with 40 manufactured goods, so resources are idle or misused [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** At 60 manufactured goods the maximum services are 32 [1]. The combination lies beyond the frontier because resources are limited: scarcity [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) divide by the units gained, not by the starting level. Quote the figures from the table when explaining (b).

**4. (a)** Any two, each with a link to capacity. Education and training raise labour quality [1], so more output can be made from the same workers [1]. Investment adds capital [1], which raises the maximum output of goods [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** A flood or war destroys capital and land [1], so maximum output falls and the curve shifts inward [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say what changes in the economy's ability to produce; a cause alone is only a label.

**5. (a)** 50 × 1.3 = **65** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Before: 80 ÷ 50 = **1.60** X [1]. After: 80 ÷ 65 = **1.23** X [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The frontier pivots outward on the Y axis [1], with the X intercept unchanged at 80 [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The same resources now make more Y, so fewer units of X are given up per unit of Y [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Describe a one-sided pivot, not a parallel shift, and say which intercept moves.

**6.** Scarcity: the frontier is the limit of what resources can produce [1], and points beyond it are unattainable [1]. Choice: any point on the frontier can be selected [1], and choosing one means rejecting the others [1]. Opportunity cost: the slope shows how much of one good is lost per unit gained of the other [1], shown by a move between two points [1]. Productive efficiency: points on the frontier are efficient, as no more of one good can be made without less of the other [1]. A point inside is inefficient because resources are unemployed or underused [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover all four ideas, each tied to the diagram, for example with a named unattainable point.

**7. (a)** 10 × 1.2 = **12** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** (21 − 12) ÷ 12 [1] × 100 = **75%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Daily wage bill is 10 × 80 = £800. Before: 800 ÷ 12 = **£66.67** [1]. After: 800 ÷ 21 = **£38.10** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** (66.67 − 38.10) ÷ 66.67 × 100 = **42.9%** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division from the wage bill, and carry unrounded values into the percentage.

**8.** Scarcity is limited resources against unlimited wants, so raising output from given resources eases it [1]. Division of labour raises output per worker through practice [1] and less time lost switching tasks [1]. Lower unit cost allows lower prices, so more wants can be met [1], and in effect the economy's PPC/F moves outward [1]. However, repetitive work can lower motivation and quality [1]. Workers who learn one task may lose wider skills, making it harder to change jobs [1]. Reliance on one stage or product is risky if demand falls, and gains need a market large enough for the extra output [1]. Overall it eases scarcity most where tasks are simple and the market is large, but motivation and dependence limit the gains [2]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the gains against the limits and decide which dominates. Two lists with no judgement leave the evaluation unfinished.

**9. (a)** Tomas: 20 loaves and 5 jugs. Ilse: 5 loaves and 20 jugs [1]. Totals: **25 loaves and 25 jugs** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Tomas 10 × 4 = 40 loaves; Ilse 10 × 4 = 40 jugs, so **40 loaves and 40 jugs** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Tomas has 25 loaves and 15 jugs, against 20 and 5 [1]. Ilse has 15 loaves and 25 jugs, against 5 and 20 [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Each specialist has a surplus of one good and none of the other [1], so to consume both they must trade [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c) compare each person's before and after for both goods; a total alone does not show each gains.

**10. (a)** Barter: 15 × 14 ÷ 2 = **105** exchange rates [1]. With money: **15** prices [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Each party must want what the other offers [1]: a potter who wants shoes needs a cobbler who wants pots [1]. Without a match, trade fails or searching wastes time, which limits specialisation [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Any two. Medium of exchange [1]: sellers accept money and spend it elsewhere, so no double coincidence is needed [1]. Unit of account [1]: every good has one price, so no need for 105 exchange rates [1]. (Store of value and standard of deferred payment are also acceptable.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each function to the barter problem it solves; a bare list of functions gains little.

**11.** Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone [1]. Choosing the rail line means flood defences are not built, so the cost is the protection lost [1]. This forces the government to compare options rather than judge the rail line alone [1]. It applies to any fixed budget [1]. However, the benefits of each project are hard to value in money, such as lives protected against time saved [1]. Future benefits are uncertain, so the value of the forgone option is uncertain too [1]. The next best alternative may be unclear where several options are close [1], and governments lack full information [1]. Overall it is useful because it forces the cost of the choice into view, but it cannot give a precise answer when benefits are non-monetary and uncertain [2]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the points to rail and flood defence, and state a view on how far the concept helps.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing several alternatives as the cost when only the best forgone one counts.
- Calling a point inside the frontier unattainable, or a point on it inefficient.
- Showing more employment as a shift of the PPC/F.
- Saying the division of labour raises output with no reason.
- Giving the gains and limits of specialisation without weighing them.
