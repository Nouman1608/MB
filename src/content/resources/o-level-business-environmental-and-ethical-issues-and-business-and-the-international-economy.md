## 6.2.1 Environmental concerns as opportunities and constraints

An environmental concern can be a constraint, because it limits or adds cost to what a business may do. It can also be an opportunity, because customers, governments and investors may reward a business that deals with it well.

### How business activity affects the environment

Factories burn fuel, farms clear land, transport uses oil and shops create packaging waste. Burning fossil fuels releases gases that add to global warming. Other effects include air and water pollution, noise, loss of wildlife habitat and the use up of non-renewable resources such as coal and minerals.

### Externalities

An externality is a cost or benefit of a business decision that falls on people who are not part of the transaction. Those people are neither the business nor its customer.

- External cost: a cost borne by third parties. Smoke from a plant harms the health of people nearby.
- External benefit: a gain to third parties. A new factory attracts shops and cafes that give local people work and income.

Private costs, such as wages and materials, are paid by the business. External costs are not, which is why a business can ignore them unless pressure or law makes it count them.

Worked example. Northlake Bottlers fills water bottles in a valley town. Its lorries make the road busy and noisy, and the plant lets warm water run into the river. Local anglers lose fish. The town also gains 90 jobs and a better road paid for by the firm.

- External costs: noise and congestion for residents, harm to river life for anglers.
- External benefits: jobs and the new road for the community.
- Costs and benefits to Northlake itself, such as wages or sales, are private, not external.

### Sustainable development

Sustainable development means meeting the needs of people today without harming the ability of future generations to meet their needs. A business can contribute by using renewable energy, cutting waste, recycling materials, using resources from managed sources, designing products to last and reducing transport distances.

### Responding to environmental pressures and opportunities

Pressure groups campaign to change business behaviour. Methods include publicity, petitions, protests and calling for consumer boycotts. Businesses respond because bad publicity can lower sales and damage the brand.

Reasons to respond: avoid lost sales, meet customer demand, follow the law, attract staff and investors, and charge higher prices for green products. Costs of responding: new equipment, higher input costs and staff training. A business that acts early may gain an advantage over rivals, but it must weigh that against the extra cost.

### Legal controls

Governments set rules, such as pollution controls on what a factory may release. Businesses that break them can face fines, orders to stop and loss of reputation. The law forces all firms in an industry to meet the same minimum standard, which stops a careless firm gaining a cost advantage. Compliance raises costs, though it can also push a business towards cleaner methods. Keep your description general: name the type of control rather than inventing exact laws.

Worked example. Northlake is told to fit a filter to stop warm water entering the river. The filter costs Rs 180,000. If it refuses and is fined Rs 250,000, the fine is Rs 70,000 higher than the filter, before counting damage to its name. A good judgement says the filter is cheaper, avoids reputation damage and could be used in advertising.

### Ethical issues

An ethical issue is a question of right and wrong in how a business behaves. Many conflicts are between profit and ethics: the cheapest route may not be the fair one.

- Child labour: cheap but wrong. The business could audit suppliers, end contracts with those that use it and buy from certified sources.
- Paying fair prices to suppliers: low prices raise margins but can leave farmers or small firms unable to live decently.
- Other examples: honest advertising, safe working conditions and treatment of staff.

Worked example. Zanthe Foods buys 20,000 kg of cocoa a year at Rs 300 per kg, a cost of Rs 6,000,000. Sales revenue is Rs 12,000,000 and other costs are Rs 4,200,000, so profit is Rs 1,800,000. The growers ask for a fair price of Rs 345 per kg.

- Price rise: 345 minus 300 is 45, so 45/300 = 15%.
- New cocoa cost: 20,000 × 345 = Rs 6,900,000, up by Rs 900,000.
- New profit: 12,000,000 minus 6,900,000 minus 4,200,000 = Rs 900,000, so profit falls by 50%.

Zanthe could raise its price by 5%, which adds Rs 600,000 to revenue, leaving profit at Rs 1,500,000, or Rs 300,000 less than before. The fair price costs profit, but it may protect supply, win ethical customers and avoid bad publicity. A reasoned judgement says it depends on whether customers will accept the price rise.

## 6.3.1 Globalisation

Globalisation is the growing links between countries in trade, finance, people and ideas, so that markets work as one world market. Reasons for it include cheaper and faster transport, better communications and the internet, lower trade barriers, and the growth of multinationals.

| Opportunities for a business | Threats to a business |
|---|---|
| Larger markets to sell into | More competition from foreign firms |
| Cheaper inputs or labour abroad | Home market lost to cheaper imports |
| Spread of risk across countries | Exposure to changes in other economies |
| Access to skills and technology | Need to adapt products to local tastes |

### Import tariffs and import quotas

An import tariff is a tax on imported goods. An import quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported. Governments may use either to protect local firms and jobs, to protect new industries, to raise money (tariffs) or to cut a trade deficit.

Worked example. Tavernor Fabrics, a local mill, sells cloth at Rs 560 per metre. Imported cloth costs Rs 500 per metre. A 20% tariff adds 500 × 0.20 = Rs 100, so imported cloth now sells at Rs 600, which is Rs 40 above the local mill. Local buyers now have more reason to buy from Tavernor. The cost: shoppers who liked the cheaper cloth lose, and other countries may reply with tariffs on the country's exports.

A quota gives a hard limit on volume, not a price rise. Local firms are protected because fewer imports can enter, but prices may rise as supply is short.

## 6.3.2 Multinational companies

A multinational company (MNC) operates in more than one country. It has its head office in one and production, sales or both in others.

Benefits to the business: access to new markets, lower labour and material costs, avoiding import tariffs by producing inside the country, spreading risk and economies of scale. Impact on stakeholders: shareholders may gain higher profits, managers get new careers, and local suppliers get new contracts. But workers at home may lose jobs if production moves, and some host communities may object to the firm.

| Benefits to the host country | Drawbacks to the host country |
|---|---|
| Jobs and training | Local businesses lose sales |
| Exports and foreign currency | Repatriation of profits |
| More choice for consumers | Pollution or poor conditions |
| Investment and new technology | Jobs may leave if the MNC moves |

Repatriation of profits means sending profits back to the home country, not reinvesting them where they were earned.

Worked example. Halcombe Electronics, from a larger country, opens a plant employing 300 local people and 120 imported managers and technicians. It makes Rs 8,000,000 profit a year and sends 75% to its head office. That is Rs 6,000,000 repatriated, with Rs 2,000,000 kept. The host country gains jobs and exports but loses most profit. Only 300 of the 420 jobs go to locals, or 71%, so the benefit is smaller than it first looks.

## 6.3.3 Exchange rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. Appreciation means a currency buys more foreign currency; depreciation means it buys less.

| If the home currency | Exporters | Importers |
|---|---|---|
| Appreciates | Prices abroad rise, competitiveness falls, sales may fall | Imports cheaper, costs fall, profit may rise |
| Depreciates | Prices abroad fall, competitiveness rises, sales may rise | Imports dearer, costs rise, profit may fall |

Profitability depends on the mix. Mereford Shoes imports leather and sells shoes abroad. If its currency depreciates, leather costs more but its shoes look cheaper abroad. Whether profit rises depends on which effect is larger, and on whether the business can change its supplier or price. This is how to evaluate.

## Common errors

- Naming costs to the business itself as external costs.
- Mixing up a tariff (a tax) and a quota (a limit on quantity).
- Writing "appreciation" when the currency has fallen in value.
- Saying an MNC always helps or always harms the host country.
- Describing an ethical issue without a business response.
