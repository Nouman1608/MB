## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between short-term and long-term sources of finance. **[3]**

**2.** Identify **two** internal sources of finance. **[2]**

**3.** A business borrows £30,000 on overdraft at 12% a year for 90 days. Calculate the interest charged, to the nearest pound. **[2]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is an external, long-term source of finance?

A: Retained profit.

B: Trade credit.

C: A debenture.

D: Selling surplus equipment. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a sole trader may find it harder to raise finance than a public limited company. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Corbin Pottery needs a kiln costing £60,000. The kiln lasts for ten years. The supplier offers leasing at £1,500 a month for 48 months. The bank offers a loan of £60,000 repaid over four years, with interest charged at 5% a year on the original £60,000.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost of leasing over 48 months, and how much more this is than the purchase price. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the total interest on the loan over four years, and the total repaid. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Corbin Pottery should lease the kiln or buy it with a loan. **[8]**

**7.** Hallam Foods has 300,000 shares in issue and expects a profit after tax of £150,000. It needs £400,000 to build a new unit and is considering issuing 100,000 shares.

**(a)** Calculate the earnings per share before the share issue. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the earnings per share after the share issue, assuming profit after tax is unchanged. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **two** other effects of the share issue on the existing shareholders. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the legal structure of a business affects the sources of finance available to it. **[5]**

**9.** Drayton Textiles has been profitable but is short of cash because customers take 60 days to pay while suppliers want payment in 30 days. It needs £50,000 for six months. Evaluate **two** possible short-term sources of finance for Drayton Textiles. **[9]**

**10.** Interest rates have risen sharply and banks are lending less. Evaluate how these external influences might affect the way in which a small manufacturer finances a £200,000 expansion. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Short-term sources provide finance for up to about a year, for example an overdraft [1]; long-term sources provide finance for longer than a year, for example a bank loan or shares [1]. Short-term finance suits temporary needs such as paying suppliers; long-term suits assets that last for years [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Any two from: retained profit; sale of assets; owner's savings; faster collection of receivables; reducing inventory [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** £30,000 × 12% × 90 ÷ 365 [1] = **£888** (to the nearest pound) [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** A sole trader cannot issue shares [1], and has limited assets and a short record, so banks may lend less or ask for personal guarantees, whereas a public limited company can sell shares to the public on a stock exchange and has more security to offer [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 48 × £1,500 = **£72,000** [1] [1]. £72,000 − £60,000 = **£12,000** more [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Interest = £60,000 × 5% × 4 [1] = **£12,000**, so the total repaid is **£72,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Leasing: no large outlay, and fixed monthly payments make cash-flow easy to plan [1]; maintenance may be included [1]. Total cost is £72,000, which is £12,000 more than the price [1], and Corbin does not own the kiln at the end [1].
Loan: the total repaid is also £72,000 (£60,000 plus £12,000 of interest), so the cash cost over four years is the same as leasing [1]. But Corbin then owns a kiln that lasts ten years, so it has six more years of use [1]. The loan raises gearing and the bank may want security [1].
Judgement: because the total cost is equal, the loan is better if Corbin can afford the repayments and wants to own the kiln for its ten-year life; leasing is better if cash is tight or technology may change [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** £150,000 ÷ 300,000 [1] × 100 = **50p** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** New shares = 400,000; £150,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 [1] = **37.5p** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Each existing shareholder's percentage ownership falls (a holder of 60,000 shares has 20% before and 15% after) [1] [1]. Control and voting power are diluted [1]; dividends have to be shared among more shares [1]. **[4]**

**8.** A sole trader or partnership cannot issue shares [1], so depends on savings, retained profit and loans [1]. A private limited company can issue shares, but only to people it chooses, not to the public [1]. A public limited company can sell shares on the stock exchange and can raise large sums [1]. Limited liability makes it easier to attract investors, since their loss is limited to what they invest [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Overdraft: it is flexible and quick, and interest is paid only on the amount used [1]. It suits a temporary six-month need [1]. But interest rates are high and the bank can withdraw it [1].
Factoring: it gives cash at once against unpaid invoices, which solves the gap between the 60 days customers take and the 30 days suppliers want [1]. The factor charges a fee, which is a cost [1], and may take over collecting payments, which could affect customer relationships [1].
Evaluation: the amount of £50,000 for six months is easily covered by an overdraft if the bank agrees [1]; factoring is better if the gap is permanent [1].
Judgement: Drayton should use an overdraft for the temporary need and consider factoring only if the payment gap is likely to continue [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Quantitative effect: a loan of £200,000 at a higher rate means higher interest, for example 10% gives £20,000 a year against £12,000 at 6% [1]. Availability: banks lending less may refuse or ask for more security [1].
Responses: use retained profit if the business has enough [1]; consider leasing equipment or phasing the expansion [1]; seek investors or a grant [1], but investors want a share of control [1].
Qualitative: the owners' attitude to risk and wish to keep control [1].
Evaluation: high rates make the project less profitable, so the expansion should go ahead only if the return exceeds the cost of finance [1].
Judgement: a smaller, phased expansion funded partly from retained profit reduces the risk from changing rates [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming sources without saying why they suit the case.
- Mixing up short-term and long-term finance.
- Ignoring the legal structure of the business.
- Comparing only cost and forgetting risk and control.
- Using the wrong number of shares for earnings per share.
