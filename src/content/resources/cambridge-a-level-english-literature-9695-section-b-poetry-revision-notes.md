## Section B in five lines

- One essay on one poetry set text, worth **25 marks**.
- **(a)**: discuss a focus across poems from your selection. **(b)**: analyse a **printed poem** from the
  selection.
- Assessed on **AO1 to AO4**, each 25% of the paper.
- Closed book: quotations for (a) must come from memory.
- Learn the poems the syllabus **names** in your selection, not the whole collection.

## Reading order for any poem

1. **Speaker and situation**: who, to whom, where, when.
2. **Movement**: what changes between opening and ending; where the turn falls.
3. **Form**: stanza pattern, line length, rhyme, metre, and any disruption.
4. **Language**: image clusters, diction, register, sound.
5. **Effect**: what each choice does to meaning or tone at that point.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition | Typical effect to discuss |
|---|---|---|
| Persona | The constructed speaking voice | Allows irony; the speaker may not be the poet |
| Apostrophe | Addressing an absent person, object or idea | Urgency, longing, or a one-sided relationship |
| Iamb | Unstressed then stressed syllable | Speech-like, steady movement |
| Trochee | Stressed then unstressed syllable | Force, chant, insistence |
| Iambic pentameter | Five iambs per line | The "default" line; departures stand out |
| Caesura | A pause within a line | Hesitation, emphasis, a break in thought |
| Enjambment | A sentence running over a line end | Momentum, overflow, delayed meaning |
| End-stopping | A line ending with punctuation | Control, finality, deliberate pacing |
| Volta | A turn in argument or mood | Reframes what came before |
| Half-rhyme | Near rhyme on consonants or vowels | Unease, incompleteness |
| Refrain | A repeated line or phrase | Insistence, memory, ritual |
| Anaphora | Repetition at the start of successive lines | Building rhetoric, accumulation |
| Juxtaposition | Placing contrasting images side by side | Tension, irony |
| Semantic field | Words from one area of meaning | Builds an attitude across a poem |

## Forms to recognise

- **Sonnet**: 14 lines with a turn; the turn's position is part of the argument.
- **Ballad**: quatrains, narrative, refrain; simple music can make dark subject matter more disturbing.
- **Dramatic monologue**: one speaker, silent listener; the speaker reveals more than intended.
- **Lyric**: a short poem of personal feeling; ask how feeling is shaped, not just what it is.
- **Free verse**: no fixed pattern; comment on where lines break and why.
- **Paired poems**: in a collection, poems may answer each other (as Blake's *Songs of Innocence* and
  *Songs of Experience* do), so a link between paired poems is strong AO1.

## Answer shapes

**(b) printed poem**
1. One-sentence gist and thesis tied to the question.
2. Follow the poem's movement: opening, turn, ending.
3. Short quotation, technique, effect, every time.
4. One or two brief links to other poems in the selection.
5. Conclusion that answers the question's wording.

**(a) across the selection**
1. Check how many poems the question asks for, and use exactly that number.
2. Choose poems that treat the focus differently.
3. Integrate: organise by point, moving between poems.
4. Keep line-level analysis in every paragraph.

## Memory preparation for (a)

For each named poem in your selection, keep a card with:
- four or five short quotations (three to six words each);
- one feature of form or structure and its effect;
- two themes or attitudes it could support;
- one other poem it pairs or contrasts with.

## Exam traps

- **Stanza-by-stanza paraphrase**: describing content rather than analysing method.
- **Technique tour**: listing devices without a shared argument.
- **Poet equals speaker**: treating every "I" as autobiography.
- **Scansion without point**: labelling metre correctly and stopping.
- **Ignoring the question's focus** in a (b) answer and writing a general commentary instead.
- **Spreading too thin** in (a): mentioning many poems without developing any.
- **Missing the turn**: stopping before the final lines, where many poems reframe themselves.

## Self-test

1. What is printed on the paper for a Section B (b) question?
2. Which assessment objectives are tested in Section B?
3. What is a persona, and why does it matter?
4. Define enjambment and give one possible effect.
5. What is the difference between a caesura and end-stopping?
6. How many syllables, and in what pattern, make up a line of regular iambic pentameter?
7. What is a volta?
8. What can half-rhyme suggest?
9. Why might a ballad's regular music make its content more disturbing?
10. In an (a) answer, what should you check first about the number of poems?
11. Why is integrating two poems better than writing about them one after the other?
12. What four things should a memory card for each set poem contain?

**Answers:** 1. One poem from the set selection. 2. AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO4. 3. The constructed speaking
voice; it may differ from the poet, which opens irony and interpretation. 4. A sentence running over a
line end; it can create momentum or delay meaning until the next line. 5. A caesura is a pause within a
line; end-stopping is punctuation at the end of a line. 6. Ten syllables in five iambs, each unstressed
then stressed. 7. A turn in argument or mood. 8. Unease or incompleteness, because the rhyme is not
quite fulfilled. 9. The contrast between song-like form and dark content unsettles the reader. 10.
Whether the question names a number of poems, so you write on exactly that many. 11. It keeps the essay
argued by point and makes comparison visible. 12. Short quotations, a feature of form with its effect,
themes it supports, and a poem it pairs with.
