**This guide covers two linked elements AQA lists for Option 2B Elements of Political and Social
Protest Writing:** "the connection of the smaller world to the larger world" and "the focus on human
organisation: domestically, in the work place, in local and national governments". The
[Elements of Political and Social Protest Writing study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-2b-elements-of-political-and-social/)
names three examples in a sentence each; this page shows the methods writers use to connect scales,
how each set text organises its worlds, and how to turn the idea into an argument.

## Why scale matters in protest writing

AQA notes that the genre "covers representations of both public and private settings". Protest
writing rarely stays at one scale. A text may show the state through a single household, or show what
a national decision means for one worker. The connection between scales is where the protest lies:
if a cruel law only existed in a government chamber, it would be abstract; when it reaches a kitchen
or a schoolroom, the reader feels it.

AQA's second phrase gives you four levels of "human organisation":

| Level | Typical setting | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic | Home, marriage, family | Who decides, and who obeys? |
| Workplace | Factory, mill, bank, farm | How is labour controlled and rewarded? |
| Local government | Town, parish, village, council | Who owns, who rules, who is excluded? |
| National government | Court, state, regime, war | How does power at the top reach the bottom? |

Strong answers trace a line through at least two of these levels.

## Methods that connect scales

- **Microcosm.** A small world that stands for a larger one. A household can be a miniature state.
- **Parody or rehearsal.** A small world imitates a larger one, exposing it.
- **Synecdoche.** A part represents a whole: a single worker for a class, a single room for a
  regime.
- **Juxtaposition.** Scenes at different scales placed side by side so that each comments on the
  other.
- **Chain of cause.** A decision at one level is followed step by step to its effect at another.
- **Frame.** An outer narrative places the inner story in a wider history.

## The set texts, scale by scale

**Henry IV Part I.** Shakespeare alternates scenes at court, among the rebels and in the Eastcheap
tavern. The tavern is not an escape from politics but a rehearsal of it: in Act 2 Scene 4, Falstaff
and Hal take turns playing the king and the prince, so the tavern stages a mock court. The
alternation lets each world judge the other, as Falstaff's wit exposes the honour the court values,
and the court's demands expose Falstaff's evasions.

**Hard Times.** Dickens builds a chain from schoolroom to nation. Gradgrind's classroom of facts, in
which Sissy Jupe is "girl number twenty" and Bitzer defines a horse in Latinate terms, produces the
minds that run Coketown's factories; Gradgrind himself enters Parliament. The domestic world of the
Gradgrind home, especially Louisa's marriage, shows the same philosophy damaging private life. The
novel's protest depends on the reader seeing that the same idea governs a child's lesson, a worker's
wage and a daughter's marriage.

**The Handmaid's Tale.** Gilead is experienced almost entirely through one household and one
narrator's room. The Commander's house is the regime in miniature: Wife, Handmaid, Marthas, driver,
each in an assigned place. The monthly Ceremony makes the state's control of reproduction a
domestic ritual. Atwood then adds a frame: the Historical Notes, set at an academic symposium in
2195, place Offred's story in a larger history and raise the question of how large-scale accounts
treat individual suffering.

**A Doll's House.** Ibsen links the home to the workplace through the bank. Torvald's promotion to
bank manager gives him power over Krogstad's job, and Krogstad's threat to Nora is an attempt to
keep his place at work. The household's security depends on the world of money and reputation
outside it, so the domestic drama is also an economic one.

**The Kite Runner.** Hosseini connects one household in Kabul to the history of Afghanistan. The
friendship of Amir and Hassan, master's son and servant, mirrors the hierarchy between Pashtun and
Hazara; the end of the monarchy, the Soviet invasion and the rule of the Taliban then reshape the
characters' lives. National events break the small world apart, and Amir's return to Kabul shows
what has happened to both.

**Songs of Innocence and of Experience.** Blake works by synecdoche. A single sweep, a single charity
child in "Holy Thursday", or the "mind-forg'd manacles" heard in every voice in "London" stand for a
whole society. The paired poems also connect the small world of a child's belief to the larger
institutions that exploit it.

**Tony Harrison's Selected Poems.** In "V", the poet's visit to his parents' grave in a Leeds
cemetery opens onto national division during the miners' strike, and the graffitied letter V
becomes the sign of opposition between groups. In "Marked with D", one father's death and one
bakery oven carry the weight of a class.

**Harvest.** Crace's village is a small world, narrated by Walter Thirsk, whose way of life is
ended by a larger economic change: the enclosure of common land for sheep. The novel shows a
national process arriving as a local catastrophe.

## Worked example: a set-text paragraph

**Question shape (original):** "In *Henry IV Part I*, the tavern matters as much as the court."
Discuss.

> Shakespeare structures the play so that the tavern and the court comment on each other, and the
> tavern scenes are where the audience learns most clearly what kingship involves. In Act 2 Scene 4,
> Falstaff and Hal improvise a scene in which each plays the king, so the small world of Eastcheap
> rehearses the larger world of the throne. The game is comic, but its ending is not: when Falstaff,
> playing Hal, pleads not to be banished, the prince, now playing his father, answers that he will.
> In a play-within-a-scene, the future king announces a political decision about his own pleasures.
> The tavern therefore matters as much as the court because it is where Hal prepares for power,
> learning the language of ordinary people that will serve him as a ruler. Yet the play's structure
> also limits the tavern's importance: the rebellion is defeated on the battlefield, not in
> Eastcheap, and Falstaff's comic counterfeit death at Shrewsbury sits beside real deaths, so the
> larger world finally sets the terms.

**Why it works.** It analyses a precise scene and its place in the structure (AO2), uses the idea of
rehearsal to connect scales, and weighs the view (AO5).

## Scale in the unseen passage

In Section A, ask of any extract:

- What is the smallest unit of organisation shown (a person, a room, a family)?
- What larger structure is present or implied (a company, a law, a war, a regime)?
- Which details connect the two (a uniform, a notice, a wage, a broadcast, a name)?
- Does the writer move between scales, or stay small and let the reader infer the larger world?

## Common mistakes

- **Writing about the "message" of the text** without showing how small and large worlds are linked.
- **Ignoring domestic power** because it seems unpolitical; AQA names it.
- **Context as a history lesson.** Use it to explain how the larger world reaches a specific scene.
- **Describing settings** instead of analysing what connects them.

## Exam technique

For a question on setting, power or society, plan a paragraph at each of two or three levels of
organisation and a final paragraph on how they connect. In Section C, pair a text that works by
microcosm with one that works by a chain of cause.
