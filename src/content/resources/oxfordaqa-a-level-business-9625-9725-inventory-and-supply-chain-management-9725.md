The [Operational performance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-operational-performance/) introduces the terms; this page concentrates on the skills the specification names: amending, completing and interpreting inventory control charts, judging JIT against JIC, and evaluating sourcing and supply chain decisions. ## What the specification asks for

**Inventory**

- Reasons to hold inventory and influences on the amount held.
- Inventory control: amending, completing and interpreting inventory control charts, including lead time, re-order quantities and levels, and buffer stock.
- Just in time (JIT) versus just in case (JIC).
- The value of effective inventory control.

**Supply chain management**

- Purpose and value of managing the supply chain, including relationships with suppliers and logistics from suppliers and to customers.
- Influences on sourcing supplies, including number and choice of suppliers.
- Impact of supply chain issues on a business.
- Purpose and value of supply chain transparency and supplier third party accreditations.

## Why hold inventory, and how much?

Inventory includes raw materials, work in progress and finished goods. Businesses hold it to:

- meet orders immediately and cover unexpected rises in demand;
- keep production running if a delivery is late;
- buy in bulk for discounts;
- build up goods ahead of a seasonal peak.

**Influences on the amount held:** how predictable demand is, the lead time and reliability of suppliers, storage space and cost, whether goods perish or go out of date, the cash available, the value of bulk discounts, and whether the firm uses JIT or JIC.

## Reading an inventory control chart

An inventory control chart plots inventory (vertical axis) against time (horizontal axis). The line falls as goods are used and jumps up when a delivery arrives. The horizontal lines and gaps you must identify:

- **Maximum inventory level**: the top of each jump.
- **Re-order level**: the inventory level at which an order is placed.
- **Buffer stock**: the minimum level, kept as a safety margin.
- **Lead time**: the horizontal gap between placing an order and its arrival.
- **Re-order quantity**: the size of each jump (the amount delivered).

Two relationships help you check a chart:

```
Re-order level    = buffer stock + (usage per period × lead time in periods)
Re-order quantity = maximum level − buffer stock (if each delivery arrives exactly at the buffer)
Usage per period  = fall in inventory ÷ number of periods
```

## Worked example 1: completing a chart

Pellow Garden Centre sells 300 bags of compost a week. It keeps a buffer stock of 400 bags, its maximum level is 1,900 bags and its supplier's lead time is 2 weeks.

```
Re-order level    = 400 + (300 × 2) = 1,000 bags
Re-order quantity = 1,900 − 400     = 1,500 bags
```

| End of week | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inventory before delivery | 1,900 | 1,600 | 1,300 | 1,000 | 700 | 400 |
| Event | | | | Order placed | | Delivery of 1,500 → 1,900 |

The order is placed when inventory reaches 1,000 at the end of week 3. Two weeks later, inventory has fallen to the buffer of 400 just as 1,500 bags arrive, restoring 1,900. Each cycle lasts 5 weeks.

## Worked example 2: amending the chart

**Change A: the lead time rises to 3 weeks and Pellow does not change its re-order level.**

| End of week | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inventory | 1,000 (order placed) | 700 | 400 | 100 → delivery → 1,600 |

Pellow eats 300 bags into its buffer, leaving only 100. One unusually busy week would have caused a stock-out. The fix: raise the re-order level to 400 + (300 × 3) = **1,300 bags**, so the order is placed at the end of week 2.

**Change B: a spring rush raises sales to 450 bags a week from week 4, with the original 2-week lead time.**

After the order at 1,000 (end of week 3), inventory falls to 550 (week 4) and 100 (week 5) before the delivery lifts it to 1,600. Again the buffer is used. Pellow could raise the re-order level for spring or increase the buffer.

**Interpreting.** When a chart shows inventory dipping below the buffer, ask: did usage rise, or did the lead time lengthen? The cure differs: higher usage calls for a higher re-order level or quantity; an unreliable supplier may call for a larger buffer or a new supplier.

## JIT versus JIC

| | Just in time | Just in case |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory held | Minimal | Large safety margin |
| Storage cost and cash tied up | Low | High |
| Risk of stock-out if supply fails | High | Low |
| Waste and obsolescence | Low | Higher |
| Best when | Suppliers are close and reliable; demand is steady | Supply is uncertain; demand is volatile; stock-outs are very costly |

Neither approach is right everywhere. A sandwich shop with fresh ingredients leans to JIT; a hospital holding vital medicines leans to JIC, because running out costs far more than storage.

**Value of effective inventory control.** It avoids both failures: stock-outs (lost sales, idle production, disappointed customers) and excess inventory (storage costs, cash tied up, waste). Good control also gives managers accurate data for ordering and forecasting.

## Supply chain management

The supply chain is every step from raw materials to the final customer. Managing it covers:

- **Relationships with suppliers**: long-term partnerships can bring better prices, priority when supplies are short, and joint work on quality.
- **Logistics from suppliers**: transport, timing and storage of inputs.
- **Logistics to customers**: warehousing, delivery and returns.

Its value is lower costs, reliable delivery, consistent quality and the ability to respond quickly to customers.

**Influences on sourcing supplies:** price, quality, reliability, lead time, location, capacity of the supplier, ethical and environmental standards, and the **number and choice of suppliers**.

| | One supplier | Several suppliers |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Close relationship; bulk discounts; simpler ordering | Spreads risk; competition between suppliers keeps prices down |
| Disadvantages | Supply stops if that supplier fails; weaker bargaining position over time | Less loyalty from each; more administration; quality may vary |

## Impact of supply chain issues

**Scenario.** Rowntree Bicycles (invented) buys all its gear sets from one overseas supplier. A fire closes the supplier's factory for six weeks.

- **Operations**: assembly stops once gear-set inventory runs out; staff are idle.
- **Marketing**: dealers cannot get bikes for the spring season; customers buy rival brands.
- **Finance**: revenue falls while fixed costs continue; emergency supplies from another firm cost more.
- **Human resources**: overtime is needed later to catch up, or temporary lay-offs now.

The scenario shows why the number of suppliers and the size of buffer stock are strategic decisions, not only operational ones.

## Transparency and third party accreditation

**Supply chain transparency** means knowing, and often publishing, who the suppliers are and how inputs are produced. **Third party accreditation** means an independent organisation checks that a supplier meets a standard, such as a recognised quality management standard or a fair-trade certification.

- **Purpose**: to check that suppliers meet quality, safety, labour and environmental standards.
- **Value**: reassures customers and investors, supports ethical marketing claims, and reduces the risk of a scandal if a supplier is found to use unsafe or exploitative practices.
- **Limits**: audits cost money, accredited suppliers may charge more, and checks happen at intervals, so problems can arise between them.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the re-order level (an inventory level) with the re-order quantity (an amount ordered).
- Measuring lead time vertically instead of horizontally on a chart.
- Saying JIT is always better because it is cheaper.
- Treating supply chain management as only transport.
- Mentioning transparency without explaining why customers or investors care about it.

## Exam technique

- When completing a chart or table, show the usage per period first, then work forward period by period.
- **Explain** an inventory decision by linking it to cost and to the risk of a stock-out.
- **Analyse** a supply chain issue by tracing its effects on at least two functions.
- **Evaluate** JIT or a sourcing choice by weighing cost savings against risk, and say what the decision depends on (supplier reliability, demand stability, cost of running out).
