## 3.1.12.1 Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases

- An **acid** is a proton donor.
- A **base** is a proton acceptor.
- Acid–base equilibria involve the **transfer of protons** (H⁺ ions).

```
HNO₃ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + NO₃⁻
NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
```

In the first equation HNO₃ donates a proton (acid) and water accepts it (base). In the second, water
donates a proton to NH₃, so water is the acid and NH₃ the base.

In the reverse of the ammonia reaction, NH₄⁺ donates a proton to OH⁻, so NH₄⁺ is the acid and OH⁻ the
base. Look at both directions of an equilibrium.

## 3.1.12.2 pH and how to find it

[H⁺] in aqueous solution covers a very wide range, so a logarithmic scale is used:

```
pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]          [H⁺] = 10^(−pH)
```

A fall of one pH unit means [H⁺] is ten times larger.

A **strong acid** such as HCl or HNO₃ is fully dissociated in water. For a monoprotic strong acid,
[H⁺] equals the acid concentration.

### Worked example 1

(a) [H⁺] = 3.6 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³. pH = −log₁₀(3.6 × 10⁻³) = **2.44**

(b) pH = 11.20. [H⁺] = 10^(−11.20) = **6.31 × 10⁻¹² mol dm⁻³**

(c) 0.0250 mol dm⁻³ HNO₃: [H⁺] = 0.0250 mol dm⁻³, so pH = −log₁₀(0.0250) = **1.60**

Give pH to 2 decimal places unless told otherwise.

## 3.1.12.3 The ionic product of water, Kw

Water is slightly dissociated:

```
H₂O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)        Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] / [H₂O]
```

So little water dissociates that [H₂O] is effectively constant, so it is combined with Kc:

```
Kw = Kc × [H₂O] = [H⁺][OH⁻]        units: mol² dm⁻⁶
```

At 298 K, Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶.

**Kw varies with temperature.** It rises with temperature, so the dissociation is endothermic. In pure
water [H⁺] = [OH⁻], so [H⁺] = √Kw. When Kw is larger, pure water has pH below 7 but is still **neutral**,
because [H⁺] still equals [OH⁻].

**Strong bases** such as NaOH and KOH are fully dissociated, so [OH⁻] equals the base concentration.
Then [H⁺] = Kw / [OH⁻].

### Worked example 2

(a) pH of 0.0400 mol dm⁻³ NaOH at 298 K.

```
[OH⁻] = 0.0400 mol dm⁻³
[H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0400 = 2.50 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³
pH = −log₁₀(2.50 × 10⁻¹³) = 12.60
```

(b) At a higher temperature, Kw = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶. In pure water,
[H⁺] = √(2.40 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 1.55 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³, so pH = **6.81**. The water is neutral.

## 3.1.12.4 Weak acids, Ka and pKa

**Weak acids and weak bases dissociate only slightly** in aqueous solution. Ethanoic acid and ammonia are
examples. For a weak acid HA:

```
HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻          Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]          units: mol dm⁻³
pKa = −log₁₀ Ka        Ka = 10^(−pKa)
```

Ka is the **acid dissociation constant**. A larger Ka (smaller pKa) means a stronger acid.

To relate pH, concentration and Ka, make two approximations:

1. [H⁺] = [A⁻], because each HA that dissociates gives one of each (H⁺ from water is ignored).
2. [HA] at equilibrium ≈ the starting concentration, because so little dissociates.

Then Ka = [H⁺]² / [HA], so **[H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA])**.

### Worked example 3: pH from Ka

Ethanoic acid has Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ (pKa 4.76). Find the pH of a 0.150 mol dm⁻³ solution.

```
[H⁺]² = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.150 = 2.61 × 10⁻⁶
[H⁺] = 1.62 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³
pH = 2.79
```

### Worked example 4: Ka from pH

A 0.0800 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak acid HA has pH 3.05. Find Ka and pKa.

```
[H⁺] = 10^(−3.05) = 8.91 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³
Ka = (8.91 × 10⁻⁴)² / 0.0800 = 9.93 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³
pKa = −log₁₀(9.93 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.00
```

## 3.1.12.5 Titrations, pH curves and indicators

### Calculations from titration results

Use the mean of concordant titres, moles = concentration × volume (dm³), then the equation ratio.

**Worked example 5.** 25.0 cm³ of NaOH needs a mean titre of 21.30 cm³ of 0.120 mol dm⁻³ HCl.

```
n(HCl) = 0.120 × 21.30/1000 = 2.556 × 10⁻³ mol = n(NaOH)   (1 : 1)
[NaOH] = 2.556 × 10⁻³ / 0.0250 = 0.102 mol dm⁻³
```

**Worked example 6: pH after mixing a strong acid and a strong base.** 20.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ HCl is
added to 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.

```
n(H⁺) = 0.150 × 0.0200 = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol
n(OH⁻) = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol
excess H⁺ = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol in 45.0 cm³
[H⁺] = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.0450 = 0.0111 mol dm⁻³      pH = 1.95
```

If OH⁻ is in excess instead, find [OH⁻] in the total volume, then use Kw.

### Shapes of pH curves

A pH curve plots pH against volume added. Each curve has a steep section around the **equivalence
point**, except weak acid with weak base.

| Acid + base | Start (acid in flask) | Steep section | pH at equivalence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong + strong | Low (about 1) | Long, spanning acidic to alkaline | About 7 |
| Weak + strong | Higher, rises then levels into a buffer region | Shorter, in the alkaline range | Above 7 |
| Strong + weak | Low (about 1) | Shorter, in the acidic range | Below 7 |
| Weak + weak | Higher | No sharp vertical section | Hard to read |

Explanations to learn:

- A weak acid starts at higher pH because it is only slightly dissociated.
- After the first additions of a strong base to a weak acid, the flask contains HA and A⁻. This is a
  buffer, so pH changes slowly.
- **At half-neutralisation**, [HA] = [A⁻], so Ka = [H⁺] and **pH = pKa**. This is how you can find Ka
  experimentally.
- At equivalence, the salt of a weak acid and a strong base gives a solution above pH 7. The salt of a
  strong acid and a weak base gives a solution below pH 7.

### Choosing an indicator

An indicator is suitable when its **whole colour-change range lies within the steep section** of the
curve.

| Indicator | Colour change range (pH) |
|---|---|
| Methyl orange | 3.1–4.4 |
| Phenolphthalein | 8.3–10.0 |

- Strong acid + strong base: either works.
- Weak acid + strong base: phenolphthalein.
- Strong acid + weak base: methyl orange.
- Weak acid + weak base: no indicator gives a sharp end point. Use a pH meter.

### Required practical 9

Investigate how pH changes when a **weak acid reacts with a strong base**, and when a **strong acid
reacts with a weak base**.

1. Calibrate the pH meter with buffer solutions of known pH.
2. Put a measured volume of the acid (or base) in a beaker, using a pipette.
3. Add the other solution from a burette in small portions. Stir or swirl, then record the pH.
4. Use smaller portions near the end point, where pH changes fastest.
5. Plot pH against volume added. Read the equivalence volume from the middle of the steep section.
6. For the weak acid, read the pH at half the equivalence volume. That pH equals pKa.

## 3.1.12.6 Buffer action

A **buffer solution** keeps an approximately constant pH despite dilution or the addition of small
amounts of acid or base.

- **Acidic buffer**: a weak acid and the salt of that weak acid, for example ethanoic acid and sodium
  ethanoate. It can be made by partly neutralising a weak acid with a strong base.
- **Basic buffer**: a weak base and the salt of that weak base, for example ammonia and ammonium chloride.

### How an acidic buffer works

The buffer contains large amounts of both HA and A⁻.

```
HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻
```

- **Add a little acid**: the extra H⁺ reacts with A⁻ to form HA. The equilibrium shifts left and
  [H⁺] stays nearly the same.
- **Add a little base**: OH⁻ reacts with HA (OH⁻ + HA → A⁻ + H₂O), or with H⁺ which is then
  replaced as more HA dissociates.

Both [HA] and [A⁻] are large, so their ratio, and the pH, changes only a little. Dilution changes both by
the same factor, so the ratio stays the same.

### How a basic buffer works

- Add acid: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺
- Add base: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O

### Calculating the pH of an acidic buffer

Rearrange Ka:

```
[H⁺] = Ka × [HA] / [A⁻]
```

Take [HA] as the acid added and [A⁻] as the salt. The volume cancels, so moles can be used.

**Worked example 7.** 1.00 dm³ of buffer contains 0.200 mol ethanoic acid and 0.150 mol sodium
ethanoate (Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³).

```
[H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.200/0.150 = 2.32 × 10⁻⁵      pH = 4.63
```

Add 0.0100 mol HCl. The H⁺ turns A⁻ into HA: HA = 0.210 mol, A⁻ = 0.140 mol.

```
[H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.210/0.140 = 2.61 × 10⁻⁵      pH = 4.58
```

The pH falls by only 0.05. The same acid in 1.00 dm³ of water would give pH 2.00.

**Worked example 8: buffer by partial neutralisation.** 15.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH is added to
50.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid.

```
n(HA) start = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol;  n(OH⁻) = 1.50 × 10⁻³ mol
HA left = 3.50 × 10⁻³ mol;  A⁻ formed = 1.50 × 10⁻³ mol
[H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 3.50/1.50 = 4.06 × 10⁻⁵      pH = 4.39
```

### Applications of buffers

- **Blood** is kept between pH 7.35 and 7.45, mainly by a carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate buffer.
- Fermentation and fabric dyeing need a set pH.
- pH meters are calibrated with buffer solutions of known pH.

## Common errors

- Using √(Ka[HA]) for a buffer. In a buffer [H⁺] ≠ [A⁻]; use Ka × [HA]/[A⁻].
- Forgetting the total volume after mixing a strong acid and strong base.
- Saying hot pure water is acidic because its pH is below 7.
- Choosing an indicator whose range only overlaps the steep section.
- Not updating moles of HA and A⁻ after adding acid or base to a buffer.
