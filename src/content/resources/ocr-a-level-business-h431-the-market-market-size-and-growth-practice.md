## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by competition. **[2]**

**2.** In a market for bicycle helmets, sales fell from 2.5 million units to 2.4 million units, while sales revenue rose from £50 million to £52.8 million.

**(a)** Calculate market growth by volume and by value. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the average price in each year. **[2]**

**3.** Tamsworth Brushes sells £3.6 million in a market worth £48 million. Calculate its market share, and the sales it would need for a 10% share of the same market. **[3]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is a key feature of monopolistic competition?

A: A single seller with no close substitutes.

B: A few large firms whose decisions depend on each other.

C: Many firms selling differentiated products, with low barriers to entry.

D: Very high barriers to entry. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a narrow definition of a market may give a misleading picture of a business's position. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Hollin Pasta makes fresh pasta for supermarkets. Its sales rose from £7.5 million to £8.1 million in a year. Over the same year, the fresh pasta market grew from £50 million to £60 million.

**(a)** Calculate Hollin Pasta's sales growth, the market growth and Hollin's market share in each year. **[4]**

**(b)** Analyse what these figures show about Hollin Pasta's performance. **[6]**

**7.** Kittiwake Kayaks makes sea kayaks in a small workshop. The UK market for sea kayaks is worth about £6 million a year. The owner is considering a £1.5 million factory that would allow output to treble. Explain **two** reasons why the size of the market is important to this decision. **[4]**

**8.** Brockley Broadband is a small internet provider with a 3% share of a regional market. Four large national providers hold 85% of the market between them and advertise heavily. Brockley is known for fast customer service. The market is growing by about 2% a year. Evaluate how Brockley Broadband could increase its market share. **[9]**

**9.** A regional bus-travel market is worth £200 million a year. The four largest operators earn £84 million, £46 million, £20 million and £10 million; the rest of the market is shared by many small operators.

**(a)** Calculate the four-firm concentration ratio. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse the relationship between this market structure and the decision making power of the largest operator. **[6]**

**10.** Lyndale Tea Rooms is the only café in a village, but there are 30 cafés in a town six miles away. The owner says she has "100% of the market" and plans a 25% price rise. Evaluate whether the owner's view of her market is a sound basis for this decision. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Rivalry between businesses to win the same customers [1], for example through price, quality, service or promotion [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Volume: (2.4 − 2.5) ÷ 2.5 × 100 = **−4%** [1]. Value: (52.8 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = **+5.6%** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Last year: 50 ÷ 2.5 = **£20** [1]. This year: 52.8 ÷ 2.4 = **£22** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Share = 3.6 ÷ 48 × 100 [1] = **7.5%** [1]. For 10%: 0.10 × 48 = **£4.8 million** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** **C** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** A narrow definition, such as "independent cafés in one street", may give the business a large share [1], but customers may easily switch to products outside that definition, such as chain cafés, so the business faces far more competition than its share suggests [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Hollin's sales growth = (8.1 − 7.5) ÷ 7.5 × 100 = **8%** [1]. Market growth = (60 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = **20%** [1]. Share last year = 7.5 ÷ 50 × 100 = **15%** [1]; this year = 8.1 ÷ 60 × 100 = **13.5%** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Hollin's sales grew by 8%, which looks healthy on its own [1]. But the market grew by 20%, so Hollin grew at less than half the market's rate [1] and its share fell by 1.5 percentage points [1]. To keep a 15% share it would have needed sales of £9 million, £0.9 million more than it achieved [1]. This suggests rivals are capturing most of the new demand, perhaps through new products or better supermarket deals [1]; if the trend continues, Hollin may lose shelf space and bargaining power with supermarkets [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Revenue potential: trebling output would require Kittiwake to sell a much larger share of a market worth only £6 million a year [1], which may not be possible without taking many customers from rivals or cutting prices [1].
Viability of fixed costs: a £1.5 million factory adds large fixed costs, which need high sales to spread them [1]; in a small market there may not be enough sales to cover those costs, so the investment could be unprofitable unless Kittiwake also exports or enters related markets [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Context: the market is an oligopoly with slow growth, so share must mostly come from rivals [1].
Lower prices: could attract price-sensitive customers [1], but large rivals could match cuts easily and absorb losses for longer, so a price war could harm Brockley most [1].
Differentiate on service: Brockley's fast customer service is something the large providers find hard to copy [1]; promoting it, perhaps with service guarantees, builds on an existing strength [1]. Its limitation is that it may attract only customers who value service, and word of mouth takes time [1].
Targeting a niche: focusing on small businesses or a particular area where service matters most [1] would let Brockley compete without matching national advertising budgets [1].
Judgement: Brockley should differentiate on service and target a niche rather than compete on price, because in a slow-growing oligopoly price cuts are likely to be matched; its success depends on whether enough customers will pay a little more for better service [1]. **[9]**

**9. (a)** (84 + 46 + 20 + 10) ÷ 200 × 100 = 160 ÷ 200 × 100 [1] = **80%** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The market is highly concentrated, an oligopoly, and the largest operator holds 42% (84 ÷ 200) [1]. This gives it considerable power over fares, because many passengers have few alternative operators on its routes [1]. However, the other large operators are likely to respond to its decisions [1]: a fare cut might be matched, reducing everyone's revenue, so it may prefer to compete on timetables, routes and comfort [1]. Its power is also limited by barriers that protect it, such as depots and vehicles, which make entry hard for new firms [1], but which may attract regulation of fares or services if it abuses its position [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The owner's view: in the village she has no direct competitor, so she has some power to raise prices [1].
Weaknesses of the view: customers with cars can easily go to the 30 cafés six miles away, so the relevant market is wider than the village [1]; indirect competitors, such as a pub serving coffee or making drinks at home, also limit her power [1].
A 25% price rise may cause regular customers to visit less often or go elsewhere [1], so revenue could fall rather than rise [1].
Her power is greater for customers without transport, such as elderly residents, and for walkers or visitors who want a café in the village itself [1].
Evaluation: her local monopoly is real but weak, because the barriers to switching are low [1]. Judgement: the "100% of the market" view is not a sound basis for a 25% rise; a smaller rise, tested on some items first, would be safer [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing value and volume growth, or not saying which is used.
- Treating rising sales as rising market share.
- Forgetting the ÷ old size step in growth calculations.
- Recommending price cuts in an oligopoly without considering retaliation.
- Defining the market too narrowly when judging a firm's power.
