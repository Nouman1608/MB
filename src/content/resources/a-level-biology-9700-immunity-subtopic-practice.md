These questions cover topic 11, Immunity, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, outcome by outcome: sub-topic 11.1 The immune system (outcomes 1–4) and sub-topic 11.2 Antibodies and vaccination (outcomes 1–6). It is AS Level content, examined on Papers 1 and 2, and Paper 4 can assume it. Calculators are allowed in all 9700 papers.

Revise first with the [immunity study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-immunity/) and [revision notes](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-immunity-revision-notes/). The [topic-level immunity practice questions](/resources/a-level-biology-immunity-practice/) are a separate set.

## Questions

**1.** (11.1.1) Neutrophils and macrophages are both phagocytes.

**(a)** State **two** ways in which the role of a macrophage differs from that of a neutrophil. **[2]**
**(b)** Neutrophils contain very large numbers of lysosomes. Explain why. **[2]**

**2.** (11.1.2) A patient receives a kidney from an unrelated donor.

**(a)** Explain what is meant by an antigen. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the patient's lymphocytes respond to cells of the transplanted kidney but not to the patient's own cells. **[3]**

**3.** (11.1.3) These events in a primary immune response to a virus are in the wrong order.

- A: B-lymphocytes divide by mitosis.
- B: A macrophage engulfs the virus and displays its antigens.
- C: Plasma cells secrete antibodies.
- D: A T-helper cell with a complementary receptor binds to the displayed antigen.
- E: T-helper cells divide and release cytokines.
- F: B-lymphocytes differentiate into plasma cells.

**(a)** Write the letters in the correct order. **[3]**
**(b)** Some viruses pass directly from one host cell to the next. Explain why T-killer cells, rather than antibodies, are needed to stop them. **[2]**

**4.** (11.1.3) A single B-lymphocyte is selected by an antigen. Assume it and all its descendants divide by mitosis every 12 hours.

**(a)** Calculate the number of cells in the clone after 4 days. **[2]**
**(b)** 75% of these cells become plasma cells, each secreting 2000 antibody molecules per second. Calculate the number of antibody molecules secreted in one hour, in standard form to 3 significant figures. **[2]**

**5.** (11.1.4) A person was injected with harmless antigen X on day 0. On day 28 they were injected with X again **and** with a different antigen, Y, for the first time. The table shows antibody concentrations in their blood.

| Day | 0 | 7 | 14 | 21 | 28 | 31 | 35 | 42 | 49 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anti-X / arbitrary units | 0 | 2 | 12 | 6 | 3 | 45 | 160 | 120 | 70 |
| Anti-Y / arbitrary units | -- | -- | -- | -- | 0 | 0 | 2 | 12 | 6 |

**(a)** Calculate how many times greater the peak of anti-X after day 28 is than the peak of anti-Y. **[1]**
**(b)** Compare the response to X after day 28 with the response to Y. Use figures from the table. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain these differences. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why the injection of X on day 0 did not speed up the response to Y. **[1]**

**6.** (11.2.1) Antibodies against measles virus do not bind to chickenpox virus.

**(a)** Explain why, with reference to antibody structure. **[2]**
**(b)** A scientist makes a modified antibody with only **one** antigen-binding site. Suggest why it would be less effective at helping phagocytes destroy bacteria. **[2]**

**7.** (11.2.2) Monoclonal antibodies are made by the hybridoma method.

**(a)** Outline the hybridoma method. **[4]**
**(b)** After fusion, cells are grown separately and only some are kept. Explain why. **[2]**

**8.** (11.2.3) Monoclonal antibodies are used in diagnosis and treatment.

**(a)** A rapid test detects a protein released by a pathogen into the blood. Blood flows along a strip carrying monoclonal antibodies attached to a coloured marker. Outline the principle of this test. **[3]**
**(b)** Some cancer cells carry very large numbers of one receptor protein. A monoclonal antibody against this receptor is used as a treatment. Suggest why it harms healthy cells less than a drug that kills all dividing cells. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why repeated doses of a mouse monoclonal antibody become less effective. **[2]**

**9.** (11.2.4) A person with a deep, dirty wound has never been vaccinated against tetanus. A doctor injects antibodies against tetanus toxin **and** gives a first dose of tetanus vaccine at the same visit.

**(a)** Name the type of immunity each injection gives, and explain why both are given. **[4]**
**(b)** Name the type of immunity gained by recovering from an infection, and state how it differs from the immunity the vaccine gives. **[2]**

**10.** (11.2.5) A vaccine contains one purified protein from the surface of a virus, and no viral genetic material.

**(a)** Explain how this vaccine gives long-term immunity although it cannot cause infection. **[4]**
**(b)** A second dose is given six months later. Explain the benefit. **[2]**

**11.** (11.2.6) The table shows data for one disease in a region where a vaccination programme began in 2000. Infants are too young to be vaccinated.

| Year | 2000 | 2005 | 2010 | 2015 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Children vaccinated / % | 40 | 70 | 92 | 95 |
| Total cases | 3600 | 1150 | 90 | 12 |
| Cases in infants | 410 | 160 | 9 | 2 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage decrease in total cases between 2000 and 2015. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why cases in infants fell, although infants were not vaccinated. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons why the disease has not been eradicated from the region. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Macrophages **present antigens** to lymphocytes [1]; macrophages are **long-lived**, while neutrophils die after phagocytosis (or: neutrophils arrive first, in large numbers) [1].
**(b)** Lysosomes contain **hydrolytic enzymes** that digest engulfed pathogens [1]; each neutrophil engulfs **many pathogens**, so needs many lysosomes to fuse with phagocytic vacuoles [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Macrophages are bigger" gains nothing in (a): a **role** is asked for.

**2. (a)** A molecule, usually a protein or glycoprotein [1], that is **recognised by the immune system** and **stimulates an immune response** [1].
**(b)** Lymphocytes **do not respond to self antigens** on the patient's own cells [1]; donor cells carry **different, non-self antigens** [1]; lymphocytes with **complementary receptors** bind these and are activated [1].
*Examiner insight:* "A foreign molecule" alone earns one mark at most in (a): self antigens are antigens too.

**3. (a)** **B, D, E, A, F, C** [3]. (Two marks if one adjacent pair is swapped; one mark if B is first and C last.)
**(b)** Antibodies act in blood and tissue fluid and **cannot enter host cells** [1]; T-killer cells **destroy infected cells displaying viral antigens**, removing the cells where the virus replicates [1].
*Examiner insight:* "T-killer cells kill the virus" is not credited; they kill infected **cells**.

**4. (a)** 96 hours ÷ 12 = 8 divisions [1]; 2⁸ = **256 cells** [1].
**(b)** 0.75 × 256 = 192; 192 × 2000 × 3600 [1] = **1.38 × 10⁹ molecules** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Error carried forward is allowed in (b) from a wrong (a); using 8 × 2 instead of 2⁸ loses the (a) accuracy mark.

**5. (a)** 160 ÷ 12 = **13.3 times** [1].
**(b)** Anti-X rises **sooner**: 45 AU on day 31 while anti-Y is still 0 [1]; anti-X peaks **7 days** after injection, anti-Y after **14 days** [1]; the anti-X peak is **much higher**, 160 AU against 12 AU (or: 70 AU against 6 AU on day 49) [1].
**(c)** The day 0 injection caused a primary response that left **memory cells** specific to X [1]; on day 28 these **many memory B-cells** recognised X at once, with no delay selecting a few matching lymphocytes [1]; they **rapidly divided and formed many plasma cells** (a secondary response), while Y caused only a primary response [1].
**(d)** Memory cells are **specific**: memory cells for X have receptors complementary to X, not to Y [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each comparison in (b) needs paired figures for **both** antigens.

**6. (a)** The **variable regions** form an **antigen-binding site with a shape complementary** to the measles antigen [1]; chickenpox antigens have a **different shape**, so they cannot bind [1].
**(b)** It binds only **one bacterium**, so cannot **link bacteria together (agglutination)** [1]; clumped bacteria are easier for phagocytes to engulf, so more would stay free [1].
*Examiner insight:* "The antibody is specific" repeats the stem; credit needs **variable region**, **shape** and **complementary**.

**7. (a)** A mouse is **injected with the antigen** [1]; **B-lymphocytes** making the matching antibody are **removed from its spleen** [1]; they are **fused with myeloma (cancer) cells** to form **hybridoma cells** [1]; the hybridoma making the required antibody is **cloned in culture** and the antibody collected [1].
**(b)** The spleen contains B-lymphocytes making **many different antibodies**, so many different hybridomas form [1]; only a clone of the cell making the **required antibody** gives a **monoclonal** antibody [1].
*Examiner insight:* The fusion mark needs **myeloma** (or cancer) cells named, not just "another cell".

**8. (a)** The antibody binds **only to the pathogen's protein** [1]; antibody–antigen complexes **build up at one place** on the strip [1]; the **coloured marker** shows there, so a line appears only if the pathogen is present [1].
**(b)** The antibody binds **only to cells carrying that receptor**, mainly cancer cells [1]; healthy dividing cells have few of these receptors, so are **not targeted** [1].
**(c)** Mouse antibodies are **non-self antigens** in humans [1]; the patient makes **antibodies against the mouse antibody**, which remove later doses [1].
*Examiner insight:* Without "binds only to this antigen", (a) does not state the principle.

**9. (a)** The antibody injection gives **artificial passive** immunity [1]; the vaccine gives **artificial active** immunity [1]. The antibodies act **at once** on the toxin; the vaccine's primary response takes days [1]. Injected antibodies are **broken down** and **no memory cells** form, so the vaccine is needed for **long-term** protection [1].
**(b)** **Natural active** immunity [1]; the antigen enters by infection, not by **deliberate** medical injection (both make memory cells) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each type needs both words; "passive" alone does not score.

**10. (a)** The protein is a **non-self antigen** [1]; it activates **matching T-helper cells and B-lymphocytes** (clonal selection) [1]; this **primary response** produces plasma cells and **memory cells** [1]; if the virus enters later, memory cells give a **fast secondary response** that destroys it **before symptoms** develop [1].
**(b)** It causes a **secondary response** [1] that produces **more memory cells**, so immunity is stronger and longer-lasting [1].
*Examiner insight:* Saying the vaccine contains antibodies loses the first mark in (a).

**11. (a)** (3600 − 12) ÷ 3600 × 100 [1] = **99.7%** [1].
**(b)** With 95% vaccinated, **most people were immune** [1], so there were **few infectious people** to pass on the pathogen [1]; so infants rarely met an **infected person** (herd immunity) [1].
**(c)** About 5% of children are **not vaccinated** and infants cannot be, so the pathogen can still spread [1]; **cases can be brought in** from other regions (or: the vaccine does not work in everyone, e.g. immunosuppressed people) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by the 2015 value is the usual error in (a); divide by the **original** value. Allow 99.67%.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining an antigen as just "a foreign molecule".
- Saying T-killer cells kill viruses, or that T-helper cells make antibodies.
- Leaving out **myeloma** cells, or the selection step, from the hybridoma method.
- Writing "passive" without "artificial" or "natural".
