This guide covers the first two bullets of content area **1.2.22** of Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Law, Issue 4, "Lay people in the law": "magistrates, including the Justices of the Peace Act 1979" and "jurors, including the Juries Act 1974", under the heading "Role, selection and appointment of people in law – advantages and disadvantages". The [sources of English law study guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-law-yla1-the-sources-of-english-law-its-enforcement-and-administration/) gives each in a single sentence. This page sets out how each group is selected, what it does, the cases that test it, and the evaluation points an answer needs.

## Why lay people?

A **lay person** has no legal qualification. English law gives them a decisive role: magistrates decide the great majority of criminal cases, and juries decide guilt in the most serious ones. The justification is **democratic participation**: justice is done by members of the community, not only by professionals. The criticisms are about **competence, consistency and representativeness**.

## Magistrates

### Qualifications and selection

- **Age:** appointed from 18; the compulsory retirement age was raised from 70 to 75 in 2022.
- **No legal qualifications** are required.
- **Six key qualities:** good character; understanding and communication; social awareness; maturity and sound temperament; sound judgement; commitment and reliability.
- **Commitment:** to sit for at least 13 full days (26 half-days) a year; employers must allow reasonable time off. The role is **unpaid**, though expenses and loss of earnings can be claimed.
- **Who cannot serve:** for example, people with certain criminal convictions, undischarged bankrupts, and some occupations whose work conflicts with the role, such as police officers.
- **Appointment:** vacancies are advertised; local **Advisory Committees** interview candidates (usually twice) and recommend appointments. Appointment was historically by the Lord Chancellor and is now made by the **Lord Chief Justice**.

The specification names the **Justices of the Peace Act 1979**, the statute that governed magistrates' appointment and organisation; much of that framework is now in the **Courts Act 2003**. Use the Act the specification names, and mention the later Act if your teacher asks you to.

### Training

New magistrates receive training organised through the **Judicial College**, with mentoring by experienced magistrates, observation visits and regular appraisal. Training covers the law they apply, structured decision-making and sentencing guidelines; it does not make them lawyers.

### Role

- **Criminal:** try all **summary** offences and most **triable either way** offences where the defendant agrees; hold first hearings for **indictable** offences before sending them to the Crown Court; decide **bail**; issue search and arrest **warrants**.
- **Sentencing:** their custodial powers are limited, and the limit for a single offence has been changed several times by Parliament in recent years, so check the current figure; most fines are now unlimited.
- **Youth court** for defendants aged 10 to 17, and the **family court**.
- They sit as a **bench of three**, advised on law and procedure by a **legal adviser** (justices' clerk), who must not take part in the decision. Professional **District Judges (Magistrates' Courts)** sit alone in busier courts.

**A case on bias:** in *R v Bingham Justices, ex parte Jowitt* (1974) the chairman of the bench said his principle was always to believe the police officer's evidence; the conviction was quashed because the defendant had not had a fair hearing.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Local knowledge of the area | Local knowledge may be limited or out of date |
| Cheap: unpaid, so criminal justice costs less | Can rely heavily on the legal adviser |
| Lay involvement: justice by the community | Profile tends to be older and from professional backgrounds |
| A bench of three balances individual views | Sentencing can be inconsistent between areas |
| Few successful appeals, suggesting sound decisions | Risk of becoming "case-hardened" and prosecution-minded (*Jowitt*) |

## Jurors

### History

In *Bushell's Case* (1670) a judge who had imprisoned jurors for acquitting against his direction was held to have acted unlawfully: jurors may give a verdict according to their conscience. This is the basis of **jury equity**.

### Qualifications (Juries Act 1974, s 1, as amended)

- Registered on the **electoral register**.
- Aged **18 to 75** (the upper limit was raised from 70 by the Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015).
- **Ordinarily resident** in the UK for at least **five years since the age of 13**.
- Not a mentally disordered person, and **not disqualified**.

**Disqualification:** anyone ever sentenced to life imprisonment or to a custodial sentence of five years or more; anyone who in the last ten years has served any part of a custodial sentence or received a suspended sentence or community order; and anyone currently on bail in criminal proceedings.

**Excusal and deferral.** The **Criminal Justice Act 2003** removed most categories of people who were ineligible or excused as of right, so judges, lawyers and police officers can now serve. In *R v Abdroikov* [2007] the House of Lords held that in two of three appeals a fair-minded observer would think there was a real possibility of bias where a police officer or a Crown Prosecution Service lawyer sat on the jury, and those convictions were quashed. Anyone may ask to be excused for a good reason, or to defer service to another time.

### Selection

1. The **Jury Central Summoning Bureau** selects names at random from the electoral register by computer.
2. Those summoned form a **panel** at the Crown Court.
3. Twelve are chosen by **ballot** in court for each trial.
4. **Vetting:** routine police checks for disqualification; wider authorised checks only in exceptional cases such as national security.
5. **Challenges:** either side may challenge a juror **for cause**, for example knowing the defendant; the prosecution may **stand by** a juror. The defence's right of peremptory challenge (without reasons) was abolished by the Criminal Justice Act 1988.

**Racial composition:** in *R v Ford* [1989] the Court of Appeal held there is no right to a racially mixed jury. In *Sander v UK* (2000) the European Court of Human Rights found a violation of the right to a fair trial where a juror's racist jokes were not dealt with adequately.

### Role

- **Crown Court:** juries decide the **facts** and return the **verdict**; the judge decides questions of law and passes sentence.
- **Majority verdicts** (Juries Act 1974, s 17): after at least two hours of deliberation, a verdict of 11–1 or 10–2 can be accepted.
- **Civil cases:** rare; available mainly for fraud, malicious prosecution and false imprisonment. The Defamation Act 2013 removed the presumption of jury trial in defamation. Juries are not normally used for personal injury (*Ward v James* [1966]).
- **Coroners' courts** use juries in some inquests.

### Secrecy and misconduct

Jury deliberations are secret; disclosing them is an offence (now under the Juries Act 1974, s 20D). In *R v Mirza* [2004] the House of Lords refused to look into what was said in the jury room. But in *R v Young (Stephen)* [1995] a conviction was quashed because jurors had used a ouija board in their hotel, which was outside the jury room. Jurors who research cases online face prison: in *R v Fraill* [2011] a juror who contacted a defendant on Facebook was jailed for contempt, and in *Attorney General v Dallas* [2012] a juror who researched the defendant online was also jailed. The Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015 made such research a criminal offence.

**Jury equity in action:** in *R v Ponting* (1985) and *R v Kronlid* (1996) juries acquitted defendants who appeared to have no legal defence.

**Trial without a jury:** the Criminal Justice Act 2003, s 44, allows trial by judge alone where there is a real danger of jury tampering; *R v Twomey* [2009] was the first such trial.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Public participation and confidence | No reasons given, so verdicts cannot be checked |
| Random selection gives a cross-section | Random selection can still produce an unrepresentative twelve |
| Independent of the state; jury equity (*Ponting*) | Jury equity can produce perverse acquittals |
| Twelve views reduce individual bias | Bias or misconduct may go undetected because of secrecy (*Mirza*) |
| Secrecy protects jurors from pressure | Internet research and media coverage (*Fraill*, *Dallas*) |

## Worked example: an evaluation plan

*Evaluate the use of juries in criminal trials.*

1. **AO1:** qualifications under the Juries Act 1974, selection, role in the Crown Court, majority verdicts.
2. **For:** participation; independence (*Bushell*, *Ponting*); a cross-section; secrecy reduces pressure.
3. **Against:** no reasons; misconduct (*Young*, *Fraill*); possible bias (*Sander*, *Abdroikov*); cost and time.
4. **Conclude:** for example, juries remain justified for serious offences because of their democratic value, but reforms such as the 2015 research offence are needed to protect fair trials.

## Common mistakes

- Saying magistrates are paid or legally qualified.
- Saying juries decide sentence, or that they sit in the magistrates' court.
- Giving old age limits: check the current figures.
- Writing that police officers can never sit as jurors: after the 2003 Act they can, subject to *Abdroikov*.
- Listing advantages without cases or a conclusion.
