These questions cover section 3.2.4 Globalisation of AQA GCSE Business (8132). Revise first with the [Globalisation study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-globalisation/) and the [Globalisation revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-8132-globalisation-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented. No question asks you to convert currencies.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *exchange rate*. **[1]**

**2.** State the **two** ways in which UK businesses compete internationally that are named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** benefits of globalisation for a UK business that exports. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** drawback of globalisation for a UK clothing retailer. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how a weaker pound could affect the profit of a UK business that imports all of its stock. **[3]**

**6.** A UK exporter receives £80 for each product it sells abroad. Each product costs £56 to make. After the pound strengthens, it agrees to accept £72 per product to keep its overseas price unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate the profit per product before and after the change. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in profit per product. **[1]**

### Section B: longer and case-study questions

**7.** Penhallow Knives makes high-quality kitchen knives by hand in Sheffield. Its knives cost more than mass-produced knives from abroad. Analyse how Penhallow Knives could use better design to compete internationally. **[6]**

**8.** Grafton Toys imports wooden toys from overseas manufacturers and sells them to UK shops. Analyse the effect of a stronger pound on Grafton Toys' profit and sales. **[6]**

**9.** Explain why a UK business that both imports components and exports finished products may be less affected by a change in the exchange rate than one that only exports. **[4]**

**10.** Ashby Engineering makes 40,000 machine parts a year and sells them across Europe. Its fixed costs are £200,000 a year. It wins new export orders that raise output to 50,000 parts.

**(a)** Calculate the fixed cost per part before and after the increase in output. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how this helps Ashby Engineering offer higher quality products at lower prices. **[2]**

**11.** Explain **one** risk to a UK business of having its products made by a supplier overseas. **[3]**

**12.** Whitlow Cider exports 60% of its output to customers in Europe and the USA. It buys all its apples from UK farms. The pound is expected to strengthen over the next year. Its main overseas rivals sell cheaper, mass-produced cider. Evaluate the likely impact of the stronger pound on Whitlow Cider. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The price of one currency in terms of another currency [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Offering better designs [1]; offering higher quality products at lower prices [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two of: a larger market with more potential customers [1]; risk spread across several countries [1]; lower unit costs from higher output; new ideas from overseas markets. **[2]**

**4.** Foreign clothing businesses can sell to UK customers, often online [1]. If they offer lower prices, UK shoppers may switch to them [1], so the UK retailer loses sales or has to cut its own prices, reducing its profit [1]. **[3]**

**5.** A weaker pound buys less foreign currency, so imported stock costs more in pounds [1]. The business's costs rise [1], so unless it raises its prices its profit per item falls; if it does raise prices, sales may fall [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Before: £80 − £56 = £24 [1]. After: £72 − £56 = £16 [1].
**(b)** Fall = £8; £8 ÷ £24 × 100 = 33.3% (to one decimal place) [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Penhallow cannot match the low prices of mass-produced knives [1], so it needs a reason for customers to pay more [1]. A distinctive design, such as an unusual handle shape or a blade designed for a particular style of cooking, makes its knives stand out from rivals [1] and is harder for mass producers to copy [1]. Customers who value design, such as professional chefs, may then pay a premium price [1], so Penhallow can earn a good profit per knife despite selling fewer than its rivals, and its handmade, Sheffield-made image supports that price abroad [1]. **[6]**

**8.** A stronger pound means each pound buys more foreign currency [1], so the toys Grafton imports cost fewer pounds [1]. If Grafton keeps its selling prices to UK shops the same, its profit per toy rises [1]. Alternatively, it could lower its prices to UK shops [1], making its toys more competitive against UK-made toys, so its sales may rise [1]. Either way, the stronger pound benefits Grafton, as long as the effect lasts long enough for it to plan its orders [1]. **[6]**

**9.** If the pound strengthens, the business's exports become dearer abroad, which may cut sales [1], but its imported components become cheaper, which lowers its costs [1]. If the pound weakens, exports become more competitive but components cost more [1]. In each case one effect partly offsets the other, so its profit changes less than that of a business that only exports [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Before: £200,000 ÷ 40,000 = £5 per part [1]. After: £200,000 ÷ 50,000 = £4 per part [1].
**(b)** Spreading fixed costs over more parts lowers the cost of each part by £1 [1], so Ashby can cut its price, or spend the saving on better materials and checks, without reducing its profit per part [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The business has less direct control over how the products are made [1]. If the overseas supplier uses poor-quality materials or treats its workers badly [1], customers may complain or the business may receive bad publicity, damaging its reputation and sales [1]. **[3]**

**12.** **Sales:** a stronger pound makes Whitlow's cider dearer for overseas customers [1]. With 60% of output exported, a large share of its sales is exposed [1], and customers could switch to the cheaper mass-produced rivals [1]. **Profit:** Whitlow buys all its apples in the UK, so its costs do not fall when the pound strengthens [1], unlike a business that imports inputs. If it cuts its pound price to keep its overseas price steady, profit per bottle falls [1]. **Factors that reduce the impact:** if its cider is seen as a premium, craft product, its customers may be less sensitive to price [1], and the rivals are competing mainly on price rather than quality [1]. **Judgement:** the impact is likely to be significant because so much of its output is exported and it has no import savings to offset the effect [1]. Its brand may protect some sales, so the size of the impact depends on how loyal its overseas customers are; it could also increase UK sales to reduce its dependence on exports [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reversing the effect of a stronger or weaker pound.
- Attempting currency conversions that are not required.
- Mentioning sales but not profit, or the reverse.
- Treating globalisation only as a threat.
- Ignoring whether the business imports, exports or both.
- Ending an evaluation without weighing how much the business depends on overseas trade.
