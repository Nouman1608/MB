## 1. What efficiency means

Scarcity means resources have alternative uses, so wasting them has an opportunity cost. **Economic efficiency** is achieved when resources are used so that no one can be made better off without someone else being made worse off. It has two parts:

- **Productive efficiency:** goods are produced using the fewest resources, so output is as large as possible from the inputs available. For a firm, it is producing at the lowest point of its average cost curve.
- **Allocative efficiency:** resources produce the mix of goods and services that society values most. For a single good, output is at the level where the value of the last unit to consumers (marginal benefit, shown by price) equals the cost of producing it (marginal cost).

A useful way to remember the difference: productive efficiency is about **how** goods are made; allocative efficiency is about **which goods, and how many**, are made.

## 2. Productive efficiency

### On the PPC

Every point **on** a PPC is productively efficient: more of one good can be produced only by giving up some of another. A point **inside** the curve is productively inefficient, because unemployed or badly organised resources could raise output of both goods.

**Worked example 1 (invented Country R).** Country R can produce these combinations of food and machines (thousand units):

| Combination | A | B | C | D | E |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 100 | 90 | 70 | 40 | 0 |
| Machines | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |

Country R actually produces 60 food and 30 machines.

- Holding machines at 30, the PPC (between B and C) allows food of 90 − (10 ÷ 20) × 20 = **80**. Country R is 20 thousand units of food short.
- Holding food at 60, the PPC (between C and D) allows machines of 40 + (10 ÷ 30) × 20 = **46.7**. Country R is about 16.7 thousand machines short.

The point lies inside the curve, so Country R is productively inefficient. A likely cause is unemployment of labour or capital. Moving to the curve costs nothing in terms of the other good, which is why productive inefficiency is pure waste.

### With average cost

A firm is productively efficient when it produces at the minimum of its average cost (AC), where AC = total cost ÷ output.

**Worked example 2 (invented Delta Bricks).**

| Output (thousand bricks) | 100 | 200 | 300 | 400 | 500 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cost (£) | 1,500 | 2,400 | 3,000 | 3,600 | 4,750 |
| Average cost (£ per thousand) | 15.00 | 12.00 | 10.00 | 9.00 | 9.50 |

The lowest AC, £9.00 per thousand bricks, is at 400 thousand bricks. Producing 200 thousand costs £12.00 per thousand, so £3.00 per thousand more resources are used than necessary.

## 3. Allocative efficiency

### Marginal benefit equals marginal cost

The demand curve shows the marginal benefit consumers receive from each extra unit. Where there are no external effects, the supply curve shows the marginal cost of producing each extra unit. If output is below the point where they cross, the next unit is valued more than it costs, so producing it would add to welfare. If output is beyond it, the last units cost more than they are worth. Welfare is greatest where **price = marginal cost**.

### Total surplus

**Worked example 3 (invented market for bicycle repairs).** Demand: Qd = 200 − 10P. Supply: Qs = 10P − 40. Q is repairs a week and P is in pounds.

- Equilibrium: 200 − 10P = 10P − 40, so P = **£12** and Q = **80**.
- Demand meets the price axis at £20 and supply at £4.
- Consumer surplus = ½ × 80 × (20 − 12) = **£320**.
- Producer surplus = ½ × 80 × (12 − 4) = **£320**.
- Total surplus = **£640**.

Suppose a licensing rule limits repairs to 60 a week. At Q = 60 buyers would pay £14 (from demand) while the marginal cost is £10 (from supply). The 20 lost repairs were each worth more than they cost. The **deadweight loss** is ½ × (80 − 60) × (14 − 10) = **£40**, so total surplus falls to £600. Allocative efficiency is lost because output is too low.

On a diagram, shade consumer surplus above the price, producer surplus below it, and the triangle between the curves from 60 to 80 as the welfare loss.

## 4. One without the other

The two kinds of efficiency are independent:

- An economy can sit on its PPC (productively efficient) but produce the wrong mix, for example too many weapons and too few hospitals compared with what people want. That point is productively efficient but allocatively inefficient.
- A firm can produce at minimum AC but at an output society does not value enough to cover that cost.
- Externalities break the link between market equilibrium and allocative efficiency: when private costs or benefits differ from social ones, the free market output is not the one society values most. This is the basis of topic 2.7 Market failure and externalities.

## 5. Efficiency in different economic systems

| System | How resources are allocated | Productive efficiency | Allocative efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | Price mechanism: signals, incentives, rationing | Profit motive and competition push firms to cut costs | Prices reflect consumer preferences, so resources move to goods people value, except where markets fail |
| Planned | State decides what, how and for whom | Weak incentives for managers and workers; targets can encourage waste | Planners lack information on preferences, so shortages and surpluses appear |
| Mixed | Markets plus government provision and regulation | Depends on competition and how well public services are managed | Government can correct market failure but may create government failure |

**Evaluation.** Market economies usually score better on productive efficiency because incentives reward cost cutting. But allocative efficiency in the market depends on there being no externalities and on consumers being well informed. A planned economy can, in principle, direct resources to goods with large social benefits, such as health care, but in practice has struggled to gather the information that prices supply automatically. Efficiency is also not the only goal: a market can be efficient yet leave some households unable to afford essentials, so judgements about systems weigh efficiency against equity.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Explain why an economy producing inside its PPC is not productively efficient." 

> A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its resources and technology *[definition]*. A point inside the curve, such as 60 food and 30 machines in Country R, means output of both goods could rise without giving anything up *[application with data]*. This happens when resources are unemployed, for example during a recession, or are poorly organised *[cause]*. Because more could be produced from the same inputs, resources are wasted, so the economy is productively inefficient *[conclusion linked to the definition]*.

## Common mistakes

- Defining productive efficiency as "producing a lot". It is producing at the lowest cost per unit, or on the PPC.
- Saying every point on a PPC is allocatively efficient. Only one point is, the mix society values most.
- Treating market equilibrium as allocatively efficient when the question involves an externality.
- Forgetting that allocative efficiency is judged at the margin, by comparing marginal benefit and marginal cost.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** define the efficiency, apply it to the case (a point on a PPC, a cost table, a market) and complete the chain to waste or welfare.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** for productive efficiency draw a PPC with a point inside; for allocative efficiency draw demand and supply with the surplus areas labelled.
- **Evaluate:** compare systems on both kinds of efficiency, name a condition (competition, information, externalities) and reach a judgement.
