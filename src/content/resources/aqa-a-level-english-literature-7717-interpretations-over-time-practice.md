> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, for practice on the NEA requirement to
> consider "different interpretations of the text(s) over time". They are **not** AQA tasks. The NEA
> itself is two essays of 1,250-1,500 words; the tariffs below are Marlbridge's own, so that each
> skill can be marked point by point. The student sentences quoted are **original**, written for this
> page.

## Section A: the requirement

**1.** State where in the NEA the "over time" requirement must be met, and how many texts it must
cover at minimum. **[2]**

**2.** A student plans one conventional essay and one re-creative piece, and intends to discuss
interpretations over time only in the commentary. Explain the problem. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why two modern critics who disagree do not, on their own, meet the requirement. **[2]**

**4.** Name the four moments of reception and give one source of evidence for any one of them. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why the requirement affects text selection as well as essay writing. **[3]**

## Section B: applying the requirement

**6.** A student writes: "Feminist critics think Bertha is Jane's double, but post-colonial critics
disagree." Explain two ways to improve this sentence so that it meets the requirement. **[4]**

**7.** Judge the following sentence and explain whether it meets the requirement: "Early readers saw
the poem as a simple moral tale, but by the late twentieth century, critics using feminist ideas
read the sisters' bond as the poem's real subject, partly because criticism had begun to value
relationships between women that earlier readings ignored." **[3]**

**8.** A student proposes a novel published two years ago for the NEA. Explain one advantage and one
risk of this choice in relation to interpretations over time. **[2]**

**9.** Using *Jane Eyre* or *Heart of Darkness*, write a topic sentence for a paragraph that discusses
interpretations over time and links to a section of the Critical anthology. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how the "literary value and the canon" section could be used to discuss a text's
changing reputation. **[3]**

**11.** Plan a paragraph using the five-step frame (reading and moment; reason; later reading and
moment; what changed; judgement) for any suitable prose or poetry text not on the exam set-text
lists. **[5]**

**12.** Explain why a student should avoid presenting the most recent interpretation as simply
correct. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** In a conventional response [1], on at least one text [1].

**2.** The requirement must be met in a conventional response [1], so placing it only in the
re-creative commentary does not satisfy it; the conventional essay must include it [1].

**3.** Their disagreement may come from different arguments rather than different moments of
reception [1]; the requirement asks for interpretations that differ over time, so the change and its
causes must be shown [1].

**4.** First reception; later critical traditions; the theoretical turn; present-day reading [2 for
all four, 1 for two or three]. One source [1]: for example, contemporary reviews for first reception,
a later critical study for the theoretical turn, or a rewriting such as *Wide Sargasso Sea*.

**5.** The specification says texts "must allow access to a range of critical views and
interpretations, including over time" [1]; a text with little reception history makes the
requirement hard to meet [1]; so students should check a text's critical history before committing
to it [1].

**6.** Two improvements, [2] each, with [1] for the idea and [1] for its application: date the
readings (Gilbert and Gubar in 1979; Spivak in 1985) so that change over time is visible; explain why
the readings differ (the rise of post-colonial criticism, attention to Bertha's Creole origins); state
what each reading sees and misses; use the change as evidence for the student's own judgement.

**7.** It meets the requirement [1]: it identifies two moments, early readers and late
twentieth-century critics [1], and it explains why the reading changed, because criticism had begun
to value relationships between women [1].

**8.** Advantage [1]: the student's own reading is fresh, and present-day responses are easy to find.
Risk [1]: the reception history is very short, so there may be too little evidence of interpretations
changing over time. A student who keeps such a text should plan to use it for the essay that does not
carry the requirement.

**9.** A topic sentence that names at least two moments or periods of reading [1] and links them to an
anthology section [1]: for example, "Readings of *Heart of Darkness* changed sharply after Achebe's
1975 lecture, which used ideas now central to post-colonial theory to question whether the novella's
critique of empire could be separated from its portrayal of Africans."

**10.** The section concerns why some texts are judged great and how judgements change [1]; a student
can trace a text's rise or fall in reputation [1] and explain what values caused the change, using
that history as evidence of interpretation over time [1].

**11.** One mark for each step of the frame completed relevantly: a first reading with its moment [1];
a reason for that reading [1]; a later reading with its moment [1]; an explanation of what changed
[1]; a judgement that uses the change [1].

**12.** Later readings also come from a particular moment and have their own blind spots [1]; AO5
treats interpretation as "a dynamic" process, so the essay should weigh what each reading reveals and
misses rather than declaring a winner [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming critics without dates or reasons for change.
- Meeting the requirement only in a re-creative commentary.
- Inventing early responses instead of using sources.
- Choosing a text with almost no reception history.
- Treating context (AO3) and interpretations over time (AO5) as the same thing: context explains
  when and where a text was written and read; interpretations over time explain how its meaning has
  been argued about.

For full practice, draft the paragraph from Question 11 and check it against the five-step frame,
then ask your teacher whether your sources are reliable enough to cite.
