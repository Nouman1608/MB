## Key terms (5.2.3(a) and (f))

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Oxidising agent | Electron acceptor; it is reduced and its oxidation number falls |
| Reducing agent | Electron donor; it is oxidised and its oxidation number rises |
| Half-equation | One reduction or one oxidation, with electrons shown |
| Standard hydrogen electrode | H₂(g) at 100 kPa over Pt, in 1 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq), at 298 K; E⦵ = 0.00 V by definition |
| Standard electrode potential, E⦵ | e.m.f. of a half-cell connected to the standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions (298 K, 100 kPa, 1 mol dm⁻³) |
| Standard cell potential, E⦵cell | E⦵(positive electrode) − E⦵(negative electrode) |
| Storage cell | Sealed cell; rechargeable types reverse the discharge reaction when charged |
| Fuel cell | Uses energy from a fuel reacting with oxygen to create a voltage; reactants supplied continuously |

Memory hook: the agent is named for what it does to the other species, not what happens to itself.

## Building equations (5.2.3(b))

**Method in steps: half-equations (acid)**

1. Balance the atom that changes.
2. Add H₂O to balance O.
3. Add H⁺ to balance H.
4. Add e⁻ to balance charge.
5. Scale both half-equations to the same electron count, add them, then cancel H⁺, H₂O or e⁻ that appear on both sides.

**Method in steps: oxidation numbers**

1. Mark the element whose oxidation number rises and the one whose oxidation number falls.
2. Multiply by the number of atoms changing.
3. Choose coefficients so total increase = total decrease.
4. Balance O with H₂O and H with H⁺ if needed, then check charge.

Worked reminder: in acid, Cr₂O₇²⁻ → Cr³⁺ gives Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O. Check: charge +6 on each side.

## Predicting electron transfer (5.2.3(c))

- Electrons lost = electrons gained. Use mole ratios to find a change in oxidation number, then the product.
- If one species is both oxidised and reduced, it is disproportionation (for example Cu⁺ forming Cu²⁺ and Cu, met again in 5.3.1).

## Redox titrations (5.2.3(d))

| Feature | Fe²⁺ / MnO₄⁻ | I₂ / S₂O₃²⁻ |
|---|---|---|
| Burette | KMnO₄(aq) | Na₂S₂O₃(aq) |
| Flask | Fe²⁺(aq) + excess dilute H₂SO₄ | I₂ released by adding the oxidising agent to excess KI(aq) |
| Indicator | None: MnO₄⁻ is self-indicating | Starch, added when the iodine colour is pale yellow |
| End point | First permanent pale pink | Blue-black colour just disappears |
| Key ratio | MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5 | I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2 |
| Watch out | No HCl (Cl⁻ is oxidised); read top of meniscus | Too little KI leaves oxidising agent unreacted |

Equations to know how to use:

- MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O
- I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻

## Titration calculations (5.2.3(e))

**Method in steps**

1. Choose concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³) and average them.
2. Moles in the burette solution = c × V (V in dm³).
3. Use the ratio from the equation. For a two-step iodometric method, chain the ratios: e.g. IO₃⁻ → 3I₂ → 6S₂O₃²⁻.
4. Scale up for the volumetric flask (25.0 cm³ from 250.0 cm³ is × 10).
5. Convert to the quantity asked for: concentration, mass, percentage purity, or a formula value such as x in a hydrate.

Worked reminder: an oxidising agent releases 2.40 × 10⁻⁴ mol of I₂ from excess KI. This needs 2 × 2.40 × 10⁻⁴ = 4.80 × 10⁻⁴ mol of S₂O₃²⁻. With 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ thiosulfate, the expected titre is 4.80 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 0.0200 = 0.0240 dm³, or 24.00 cm³.

For a non-familiar system, the question gives you a half-equation or an equation. Build the ratio from it before touching the numbers.

## Measuring cell potentials (5.2.3(g), PAG8)

| Half-cell type | Electrode | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Metal / metal ion | The metal, cleaned with emery paper | 1 mol dm⁻³ metal ions |
| Non-metal / ion | Platinum, with gas at 100 kPa if the element is a gas | 1 mol dm⁻³ ions |
| Two ions of one element | Platinum | Both ions at 1 mol dm⁻³, or equimolar |

Join half-cells with a salt bridge (e.g. filter paper soaked in saturated KNO₃(aq)) and a high-resistance voltmeter.

## Cell potentials and feasibility (5.2.3(h) and (i))

- E⦵cell = E⦵(more positive) − E⦵(less positive). Reduction happens at the more positive half-cell.
- A reaction is feasible if the oxidising agent's half-equation has the more positive E⦵, so E⦵cell is positive.
- Never multiply E⦵ when you scale a half-equation.

**Must-know distinctions**

| Feasible | Actually observed |
|---|---|
| Decided by E⦵ values (thermodynamics) | Also depends on rate (activation energy) |
| Assumes 1 mol dm⁻³, 100 kPa, 298 K | Real concentrations shift each half-cell equilibrium |

Concentration rule: raising the concentration of a species on the left of a half-equation makes that electrode potential more positive; raising one on the right makes it less positive.

## Storage and fuel cells (5.2.3(j) and (k))

- All the half-equations and potentials you need are supplied. Identify the more negative half-cell as the negative electrode, where oxidation happens on discharge.
- Overall equation: reverse the more negative half-equation, balance electrons, add.
- Rechargeable cell: charging reverses the overall discharge reaction.
- Lithium-based cells: benefits weighed against toxicity and fire risk.
- Fuel cell: the fuel is oxidised at the negative electrode; oxygen is reduced at the positive electrode. The cell runs while fuel and oxygen are supplied.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a reducing agent in terms of electrons.
2. Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻ is +1.36 V and Br₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻ is +1.09 V. Will chlorine oxidise bromide ions? Give E⦵cell.
3. Write the half-equation for Cr₂O₇²⁻ forming Cr³⁺ in acid.
4. 25.0 cm³ of Fe²⁺(aq) reacts with 18.40 cm³ of 0.0150 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Find [Fe²⁺].
5. A titration uses 21.60 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ Na₂S₂O₃. How many moles of I₂ reacted?
6. Why is starch added near the end of an iodine–thiosulfate titration?
7. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ ⇌ Al is −1.66 V and Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu is +0.34 V. Calculate E⦵cell.
8. In the cell from question 7, what happens to the cell potential if [Cu²⁺] is increased?
9. I₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2I⁻ is +0.54 V and Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺ is +0.77 V. Does iodine oxidise Fe²⁺?
10. Cu²⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cu⁺ is +0.15 V and Cu⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Cu is +0.52 V. Is the disproportionation of Cu⁺ feasible?
11. Why does an Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ half-cell need a platinum electrode?
12. State one reason a feasible reaction might not be seen.

### Answers

1. A species that donates electrons (and is itself oxidised).
2. Yes. E⦵cell = 1.36 − 1.09 = **+0.27 V**.
3. **Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O**.
4. n(MnO₄⁻) = 2.76 × 10⁻⁴ mol; n(Fe²⁺) = 1.38 × 10⁻³ mol; [Fe²⁺] = **0.0552 mol dm⁻³**.
5. n(S₂O₃²⁻) = 1.08 × 10⁻³ mol, so n(I₂) = **5.40 × 10⁻⁴ mol**.
6. With a lot of iodine present, the starch–iodine complex can be slow to break up, blurring the end point. Added late, it gives a sharp end point.
7. 0.34 − (−1.66) = **2.00 V**.
8. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu shifts right, the copper electrode becomes more positive, so the cell potential **increases**.
9. **No.** E⦵cell would be 0.54 − 0.77 = −0.23 V. The reverse, Fe³⁺ oxidising I⁻, is feasible.
10. **Yes.** 2Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Cu; E⦵cell = 0.52 − 0.15 = **+0.37 V**.
11. Neither species is a solid that conducts; inert platinum transfers electrons between the ions and the circuit.
12. High activation energy (slow rate), or non-standard concentrations change the electrode potentials.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the element ("manganese") instead of the agent (MnO₄⁻ or "manganate(VII) ions").
- Leaving electrons in a combined equation, or H⁺ on both sides.
- Using HCl to acidify a manganate(VII) titration, or saying "add indicator".
- Describing the thiosulfate end point as "turns blue-black" (that is when starch is added).
- Forgetting the dilution factor when only part of a volumetric flask is titrated.
- Using a 1 : 2 or 1 : 3 ratio in an iodate(V) titration instead of chaining to 1 : 6.
- Giving a cell potential with a negative sign for the positive electrode, or omitting "V".
- Saying a reaction "will happen" because E⦵cell is positive, with no mention of rate or concentration.
- Using a metal electrode (such as iron) for a half-cell of two iron ions.
