This guide covers the OCR A Level Business (H431) content headed "Different stakeholder and business objectives", in the area of study "Business objectives and strategy" (specification Version 2.1, January 2026; final assessment summer 2027). The specification asks you to "distinguish between organisational aims, corporate/business objectives, strategic objectives, tactical objectives and operational objectives", to "explain the importance of setting SMART objectives to a business and its stakeholders", to explain "the hierarchy of objectives", to "evaluate the impact and importance of setting aims and objectives", to explain how sector affects aims and objectives, to explain, evaluate and improve the communication of objectives, to explain why objectives "may need to change", and to "recommend and justify the aims and objectives for a business and how any changes may be implemented".

## Classifying an objective you have not seen before

Exam cases rarely label their objectives. Use four tests, in this order.

| Test | Aim | Corporate objective | Strategic objective | Tactical objective | Operational objective |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who sets it? | Owners, board | Board | Senior managers | Middle managers | Supervisors, team leaders |
| Scope | Whole organisation, its purpose | Whole business, measurable | Direction for the business or a major part | One department or function | One team, person or process |
| Time horizon | Open-ended | Several years | Medium to long term | Months to a year | Days to weeks |
| Resources at stake | None directly | Large | Large, hard to reverse | Moderate | Small |

A target can change level depending on the organisation. "Open a second shop by next summer" is strategic for a sole trader with one shop, because it commits most of the owner's capital and changes the direction of the business. For a chain with 300 shops, the same target is tactical: one regional manager's project. **State which test you used**, because the reasoning is what earns marks, not the label.

## SMART objectives tested with numbers

SMART is usually read as specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. The "measurable" and "achievable" tests often need a calculation.

**Brackenford Bakery Ltd** has sales revenue of £1.6 million. The managing director proposes the objective "increase sales revenue by 40% within 12 months".

- **Specific and measurable**: yes. The target is £1.6m × 1.40 = **£2.24 million**, an increase of £0.64m.
- **Time-bound**: yes, 12 months.
- **Achievable and realistic**: test against the evidence. The bakery's ovens run at 85% of capacity. If revenue rises in proportion to output, 40% more output needs 85% × 1.40 = 119% of current capacity, which is impossible without new ovens. The objective fails the achievable test unless investment is part of the plan.

A revised objective might be "increase sales revenue by 15% to £1.84 million within 12 months", which needs 85% × 1.15 = 97.75%, just under full capacity.

### Why SMART matters to the business and to stakeholders

- **To the business**: a measurable target allows progress to be monitored and corrective action taken early; a deadline creates urgency; achievable targets protect motivation, since staff who see a target as impossible may stop trying.
- **To employees**: they know exactly what is expected and can be appraised fairly against it.
- **To shareholders and lenders**: they can judge whether managers are performing, and lenders can see whether repayment is likely.
- **To customers and the community**: published targets (on delivery times or emissions, for example) let them hold the business to account.

The limitation is that SMART objectives reward what can be measured. A bakery measured only on revenue might cut product quality, which is harder to count, to hit the figure.

## The hierarchy of objectives in practice

The hierarchy works when each level gives "line of sight" to the level above: a production supervisor can see how reducing waste helps the tactical target on costs, which serves the strategic aim of competing on price, which supports the corporate objective on profit. It breaks down in three common ways:

1. **Departments pull in different directions**: sales wins orders with promised next-day delivery, while operations is set a target to cut overtime.
2. **Targets are passed down without reasons**, so staff meet the letter of the target rather than its purpose.
3. **The top level changes but the lower levels do not**, so teams keep working towards an old strategy.

Each failure is a strong evaluation point, because it shows why setting objectives is not enough on its own.

## Evaluating the impact and importance of setting aims and objectives

The importance of objectives **depends on the situation**, and good answers say on what:

- **Size**: a sole trader carries the objectives in their head; a business with 2,000 staff across several sites needs written, cascaded objectives to co-ordinate effort.
- **Market change**: in a stable market, multi-year objectives guide investment; in a fast-moving one, rigid targets can lock a business into the wrong course, so shorter review periods matter more than precise long-term figures.
- **Ownership**: a public limited company needs clear objectives to reassure shareholders and the market; a family business may value independence over measurable growth.
- **Quality of the objectives**: poorly chosen objectives can do more harm than none, by rewarding the wrong behaviour.

## How sector affects aims and objectives

| Sector | Who it answers to | Typical objectives | How success is measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| Private | Owners or shareholders | Profit, growth, market share, survival | Financial results |
| Public | Government and taxpayers | Service quality, access, value for money, staying within budget | Service targets, cost per user |
| Third | Trustees, members, funders | A social or environmental purpose; surpluses reinvested | Social impact, people helped |

The measurement column is the one students overlook. **Aldwyn Housing Association**, a third-sector landlord, might set the objective "reduce the average time to re-let an empty home from 35 days to 21 days within a year". There is no profit figure, but the objective is still SMART, and meeting it helps both its social purpose (more people housed) and its finances (less rent lost).

## Communicating objectives, and what goes wrong

Match the method to the audience:

| Audience | Suitable methods | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Senior managers | Board papers, strategy meetings | Detail and discussion needed |
| Staff in one location | Team briefings, noticeboards | Quick, allows questions |
| Staff spread across sites | Intranet, video briefings, email | Reaches everyone at once |
| Shareholders | Annual report, shareholder meetings | Formal, legally expected |
| Customers and community | Website, press releases | Public, wide reach |

**Consequences of mis-communication**, traced as a chain: staff misread a target → effort goes in the wrong direction → the objective is missed → managers blame staff → morale and trust fall → later messages are believed less. Externally, shareholders who were told one thing and see another may sell shares or vote against directors.

**Better communication** usually means two-way methods: explaining the reason behind the objective, inviting questions, and checking understanding. Evaluate on cost and time against the risk of error; for a complex change affecting many staff, the extra cost of face-to-face briefings is usually justified.

## Why objectives may need to change

Sort reasons into internal and external:

- **Internal**: new owners or managers, growth to a new size, the original objective has been met or clearly missed, a change in finance available.
- **External**: recession or recovery, a new competitor, new technology, changes in law or regulation, shifts in social attitudes.

Objectives also change over the life of a business: survival first, then growth, then profit and perhaps wider social goals.

## Worked example: recommend and justify

*Corvan Software Ltd has grown from 12 to 60 staff in three years. Its single objective has been "double revenue every two years". Staff turnover has risen to 25% a year, and customers complain about bugs. Recommend and justify an objective for the next two years, and how the change should be implemented.*

An annotated answer:

> "I recommend Corvan changes to a quality objective: cut customer-reported faults by half within 18 months, while keeping revenue growth at 15% a year **[SMART, linked to the case]**. Rapid growth has left quality behind, and complaints threaten the repeat business a software firm depends on **[justification from the evidence]**. With 25% turnover, about 15 of the 60 staff leave each year, so time is lost training replacements; a less frantic target should also help retention **[uses the data]**. To implement it, managers should explain why the objective is changing in team briefings, set each development team a tactical target on fault rates, and review progress each quarter **[implementation]**. The risk is that shareholders who expected revenue to double may resist, so the directors must show them that lost customers would hurt revenue more **[weighs a risk and concludes]**."

## Common mistakes

- Classifying an objective by its wording alone, without asking who sets it, its scope and its time span.
- Calling an objective SMART without testing "achievable" against evidence in the case.
- Treating every non-profit organisation as having no measurable objectives.
- Naming communication methods without matching them to the audience.
- Recommending an objective without saying how the change will be implemented.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish**: give a feature of each, side by side, with an example from the case.
- **Explain**: point, reason, consequence for this business.
- **Evaluate**: weigh the factors above (size, market change, ownership) and reach a judgement that says "it depends on..." and then decides.
- **Recommend and justify**: one clear recommendation, evidence from the case, a calculation where data is given, implementation steps and a risk.
