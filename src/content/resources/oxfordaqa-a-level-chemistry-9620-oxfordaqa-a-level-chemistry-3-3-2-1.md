## What the specification asks for

- Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons.
- Petroleum is a mixture consisting mainly of alkane hydrocarbons that can be separated by fractional distillation.

The section overview adds the context: alkanes are the main constituent of crude oil, an important raw material for the chemical industry.

## Alkanes: what the definition means

- **Hydrocarbon**: a compound of hydrogen and carbon **only**. Chloromethane is not a hydrocarbon; neither is ethanol.
- **Saturated**: contains only **single** carbon–carbon bonds. No more hydrogen atoms can be added.
- Chain alkanes fit **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**; cycloalkanes (rings) are also saturated hydrocarbons, but fit CₙH₂ₙ.

Each carbon in an alkane forms four single bonds arranged tetrahedrally (bond angle about 109.5°), so chains are zig-zag rather than straight. The bonds are non-polar or almost non-polar, because carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativities. That is why the only forces between alkane molecules are **van der Waals forces**.

### Worked example 1: using the general formula

*An alkane has Mr = 170.0. Find its molecular formula and the percentage by mass of carbon in it. (Ar: C = 12.0, H = 1.0)*

```
Mr(CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) = 12.0n + 1.0(2n + 2) = 14n + 2
14n + 2 = 170.0  →  n = 12
Molecular formula: C₁₂H₂₆
% carbon = (12 × 12.0) ÷ 170.0 × 100 = 144.0 ÷ 170.0 × 100 = 84.7 %
```

## Petroleum: a mixture

Petroleum (crude oil) is a **mixture**, mainly of alkanes ranging from one carbon atom to many tens of carbon atoms. Because it is a mixture, its components are not chemically bonded to one another and can be separated by a **physical** method. Fractional distillation separates the mixture into **fractions**, each a smaller mixture of hydrocarbons whose boiling points lie within a narrow range.

Two points examiners look for:

- A fraction is **not a pure compound**. It still contains many hydrocarbons with similar chain lengths.
- No covalent bonds are broken during fractional distillation. Molecules leave the liquid and condense again unchanged. (Breaking C–C bonds is cracking, in 3.3.2.2.)

## Why boiling point depends on chain length

When an alkane boils, the molecules separate from one another. The energy needed depends on the strength of the van der Waals forces between molecules, not on the covalent bonds within them.

| Alkane | Formula | Boiling point / °C (approximate) |
|---|---|---|
| methane | CH₄ | −162 |
| ethane | C₂H₆ | −89 |
| propane | C₃H₈ | −42 |
| butane | C₄H₁₀ | −1 |
| pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 36 |
| hexane | C₆H₁₄ | 69 |
| octane | C₈H₁₈ | 126 |
| decane | C₁₀H₂₂ | 174 |

As the chain gets longer:

1. each molecule has **more electrons**, so larger temporary (induced) dipoles can form
2. the molecules have a **larger surface area** in contact with neighbours
3. so the van der Waals forces are **stronger**, and more energy is needed to separate the molecules
4. so the **boiling point is higher**.

The first four alkanes are gases at room temperature; from pentane onwards they are liquids, and long chains are waxy solids.

### Branching lowers boiling point

Isomers have the same number of electrons, but branched molecules are more compact. They have **fewer points of contact** with neighbouring molecules, so the van der Waals forces are weaker.

| C₅H₁₂ isomer | Boiling point / °C (approximate) |
|---|---|
| pentane | 36 |
| 2-methylbutane | 28 |
| 2,2-dimethylpropane | 10 |

### Worked example 2: an annotated model answer

*Question: Hexane boils at about 69 °C; 2,2-dimethylbutane, an isomer, boils at about 50 °C. Explain the difference. (3 marks)*

> Both molecules have the same number of electrons, but 2,2-dimethylbutane is more branched and more compact [1]. Its molecules have less surface contact with one another, so the van der Waals forces between them are weaker [1]. Less energy is needed to overcome these forces, so its boiling point is lower [1].

The answer names the force, compares its strength, and links strength to energy. "Branched molecules have weaker bonds" scores nothing: no bonds break on boiling.

## How the fractionating column works

1. The crude oil is heated in a furnace until most of it has **vaporised**, and the hot vapour (with some liquid) enters near the **base** of the column.
2. The column has a **temperature gradient**: hottest at the bottom, coolest at the top.
3. Vapour rises through the column. As it rises it cools.
4. When a hydrocarbon reaches a level where the temperature is **below its boiling point**, it **condenses** and is collected on a tray at that level.
5. Hydrocarbons with **low boiling points** (short chains) stay as vapour longer and condense **higher up**; the smallest leave the top as gases.
6. Hydrocarbons with **high boiling points** (long chains) condense **low down**. The heaviest material does not vaporise and is removed at the bottom as a residue.

### Worked example 3: predicting where compounds collect

*A sample containing butane, hexane, octane and decane is fed into a column. Trays are at 180 °C, 140 °C and 100 °C going up the column, and anything still a vapour above the top tray leaves the column. Where is each alkane collected?*

```
Rule: a compound condenses at the first tray (going up) that is cooler than its boiling point.

decane   bp 174 °C  → condenses on the 140 °C tray (first level below 174 °C)
octane   bp 126 °C  → condenses on the 100 °C tray
hexane   bp 69 °C   → passes all trays; leaves the top as vapour, condenses when cooled
butane   bp −1 °C   → leaves the top as a gas
```

Hexane and butane both leave the top, so this column would not separate them; a cooler top tray would be needed. This is why real columns have many trays: closer temperature steps give narrower fractions.

## Trends across fractions

Moving from the top of the column to the bottom, the molecules get larger, and:

- boiling point **increases**
- **volatility** (how easily the liquid evaporates) **decreases**
- **viscosity** (resistance to flow) **increases**, because stronger van der Waals forces hold the molecules together
- the fractions become **harder to ignite**, because less vapour forms at a given temperature.

Each trend has the same cause: stronger van der Waals forces between longer molecules.

## Common mistakes

- Defining saturated as "has a full number of hydrogens" without saying **only C–C single bonds**.
- Calling a fraction a pure substance, or saying that each fraction is "one alkane".
- Saying fractional distillation "breaks up" or "cracks" crude oil.
- Explaining boiling points with "stronger covalent bonds" or "stronger bonds" rather than stronger van der Waals forces **between molecules**.
- Saying long chains condense "at the top because they are heavy and stay at the bottom" — reason from boiling point, not from weight.
- Getting the temperature gradient upside down.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State**: one fact, for example "alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons".
- **Explain** (boiling points): name van der Waals forces, compare their strength, link to the energy needed.
- **Describe** (the column): heating, temperature gradient, rising vapour, condensing at the level below boiling point, collection.
- **Suggest** (unfamiliar data): use the condensing rule with the given temperatures.
