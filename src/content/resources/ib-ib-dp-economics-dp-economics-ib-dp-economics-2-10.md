This guide goes deeper on sub-topic **2.10 Market failure: asymmetric information**, which is **HL only** in the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). SL students do not need it. The [guide to public goods and asymmetric information](/resources/ib-dp-economics-public-goods-and-asymmetric-information/) introduces 2.10 with examples from second-hand goods and fire insurance; this page uses different markets (health insurance, jobs and rentals), shows how adverse selection can unravel a whole market, and builds fuller evaluations of each response. Consolidate with the [2.10 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-10-revision-notes/) and practise with the [2.10 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-2-10-practice/).

All places and figures are invented. The main example is Talvane, whose currency is the tal.

## What 2.10 asks you to do

- Explain **asymmetric information**: one party to a transaction knows more than the other.
- Explain the two forms of market failure it causes: **adverse selection** and **moral hazard**.
- Evaluate **government responses** (legislation and regulation; provision of information) and **private responses** (signalling; screening).

## Why asymmetric information is a market failure

Competitive markets allocate resources efficiently only if buyers and sellers can judge what they are trading. When one side holds hidden knowledge, prices stop reflecting true quality or risk. Trades that would benefit both sides do not happen, or the wrong trades happen. Either way, resources are misallocated.

The key distinction is **timing**:

| | Adverse selection | Moral hazard |
|---|---|---|
| When | **Before** the transaction | **After** the transaction |
| What is hidden | A characteristic (quality, risk type) | An action (effort, care) |
| Typical example | Buyers of insurance know their health better than the insurer | An insured person takes less care |

## Adverse selection: how a market can unravel

**Worked example: health insurance in Talvane.** One hundred people consider buying a year's health cover from a single insurer. They know their own health; the insurer cannot tell them apart.

| Group | Number | Expected medical cost per person (tals) | Most each would pay for cover (tals) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low risk | 60 | 500 | 700 |
| High risk | 40 | 2,000 | 2,300 |

Each person would pay a little more than their expected cost, because cover removes uncertainty.

1. The insurer sets one premium for everyone at the average expected cost: (60 × 500 + 40 × 2,000) / 100 = (30,000 + 80,000) / 100 = **1,100 tals**.
2. Low-risk people will pay at most 700, which is below 1,100, so they **do not buy**.
3. Only high-risk people remain, so the insurer's average cost jumps to 2,000 and it raises the premium to **2,000 tals**.
4. High-risk people still buy (2,000 is below 2,300), but the 60 low-risk people are uninsured.
5. Each low-risk person would have gained 700 - 500 = 200 tals from cover priced at their own cost. That gain is lost: 60 × 200 = **12,000 tals** of welfare is missing from the market.

The hidden information drove the better risks out. The same mechanism appears in second-hand markets, where good-quality goods are withdrawn, and in credit markets, where lenders charging high interest rates attract riskier borrowers.

## Moral hazard: changed behaviour after the deal

Moral hazard arises when one party changes its behaviour after an agreement because it no longer bears the full cost of its actions, and the other party cannot observe or control that behaviour.

**Worked example: rental e-scooters in Talvane.** A rider who parks carefully has a 2% chance of the scooter being damaged on a hire; a careless rider has an 8% chance. Repairs cost 1,500 tals. Parking carefully takes effort the rider values at 10 tals.

- **With full cover and no excess:** damage costs the rider nothing either way, so the rider saves the 10 tals of effort and parks carelessly. The firm's expected repair cost rises from 0.02 × 1,500 = 30 to 0.08 × 1,500 = **120 tals** per hire.
- **With a 400-tal excess** (the rider pays the first 400 of any repair): careless expected cost = 0.08 × 400 = 32; careful expected cost = 10 + 0.02 × 400 = 18. Care is now cheaper for the rider, so the rider parks carefully.

The excess makes the rider bear part of the cost of carelessness, which reduces moral hazard. Other examples: workers on fixed pay whose effort cannot be observed, and large banks that take bigger risks if they expect the government to rescue them in a crisis.

## Responses

### Private responses

**Signalling:** the better-informed party sends a costly signal that reveals quality.

- A job applicant in Talvane earns a demanding professional certificate. If high-ability applicants find it much easier to pass than low-ability ones, the certificate separates the two groups, and employers can pay according to it.
- A firm offers a long guarantee, which is cheap for a reliable product and costly for an unreliable one.

A signal works only if it is too expensive for low-quality types to copy.

**Screening:** the less-informed party designs a test or choice that makes the other side reveal information.

- Insurers use medical questionnaires and check claims histories.
- Employers use probation periods and skills tests.
- Insurers offer a menu of contracts, such as a cheap policy with a high excess and an expensive one with no excess, so customers sort themselves by risk.

### Government responses

**Legislation and regulation**

- **Licensing** of doctors, pilots and electricians sets minimum standards, so consumers need not judge competence themselves.
- **Mandatory disclosure**: sellers must reveal known defects or the full cost of a loan.
- **Compulsory insurance**: if everyone must buy health or vehicle cover, low-risk people cannot leave the pool, which stops the unravelling in the health insurance example. At a pooled premium of 1,100, low-risk people pay more than their expected cost, so this raises a fairness question as well as an efficiency gain.

**Provision of information**

- Publishing hygiene ratings for restaurants, school inspection reports or energy-efficiency labels narrows the information gap at low cost to consumers.

### Evaluating the responses

| Response | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Signalling | Driven by the informed party; no public cost | Signals can be costly and wasteful if they reveal ability but add nothing to productivity |
| Screening | Lets insurers and employers price or hire more accurately | Some people may be priced out entirely; screening is costly and imperfect |
| Regulation and licensing | Sets enforceable standards across a whole market | Enforcement costs; may restrict entry and raise prices; rules lag behind new products |
| Compulsory insurance | Prevents adverse selection from unravelling the market | Low-risk people subsidise high-risk people; can weaken incentives to stay low-risk (moral hazard) |
| Provision of information | Cheap, and leaves choice with consumers | Information may be ignored, misread or out of date |

A strong judgement matches the response to the problem. Adverse selection about **types** suits screening, signalling, disclosure and compulsory pooling. Moral hazard about **actions** suits contracts that share costs, such as an excess, plus monitoring.

## Common mistakes

- Using "asymmetric information" to mean "consumers lack information". It means one side knows **more than the other**.
- Mixing up the two problems: adverse selection is hidden characteristics before the deal; moral hazard is hidden actions after it.
- Describing a response without saying which problem it solves.
- Forgetting that compulsory insurance has a distributional effect.
- Presenting 2.10 material in an SL answer: it is HL only.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** adverse selection as a chain: hidden information, one average price, better types leave, average quality or risk worsens, market shrinks or collapses.
- **Distinguish** adverse selection from moral hazard using timing and what is hidden.
- **Evaluate** a response by naming the problem it targets, one strength, one limitation and a judgement that depends on the market. Real-world examples you have researched strengthen Paper 1 part (b) answers.
- Simple expected-value arithmetic, as in the scooter example, makes moral hazard concrete in Paper 2 or HL Paper 3 answers, but always explain what the numbers show.
