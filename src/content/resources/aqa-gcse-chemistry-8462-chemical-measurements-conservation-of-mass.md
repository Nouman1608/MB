This guide covers **4.3.1 Chemical measurements, conservation of mass and the quantitative interpretation of
chemical equations** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards
(version 1.2). It includes 4.3.1.1 to 4.3.1.4 and is assessed on Paper 1. Every statement in it is for both
Foundation and Higher Tier, and there is no required practical, though the mass-change work links to
practical investigations.

For quick recall use the [chemical measurements and conservation of mass revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-measurements-conservation-of-mass-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-measurements-conservation-of-mass-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Section | Statement or skill |
|---|---|
| 4.3.1.1 | The law of conservation of mass: no atoms are lost or made, so the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants; balanced symbol equations; the use of multipliers in normal script and subscripts |
| 4.3.1.2 | Relative formula mass (Mr) is the sum of the relative atomic masses in the formula; the sums of the Mr values on each side of a balanced equation are equal; calculate percentage by mass |
| 4.3.1.3 | Explain mass changes in non-enclosed systems when a reactant or product is a gas, using the equation and the particle model |
| 4.3.1.4 | Uncertainty in measurements; represent the distribution of results; use the range about the mean as a measure of uncertainty |

Relative atomic masses (Ar) are given in the exam, normally on the periodic table. The values used here are H 1,
C 12, N 14, O 16, Na 23, Mg 24, Al 27, S 32, Cl 35.5, Ca 40, Fe 56 and Cu 63.5.

## Conservation of mass and balanced equations

In a chemical reaction no atoms are lost and none are made. The atoms are rearranged into new substances. So the
total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.

A **balanced symbol equation** shows the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.

| Notation | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Large number in front of a formula | Multiplies everything in that formula | 2H₂O means 2 molecules, 4 H and 2 O |
| Small subscript number | Number of atoms of the element just before it | H₂O has 2 H and 1 O |
| Brackets with a subscript | Multiplies everything inside the brackets | Ca(OH)₂ has 1 Ca, 2 O and 2 H |

### Worked example 1: balancing an equation

*Balance: Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃*

```
Right side: 2 Al and 3 O
Make O even: 6 O needs 3 O2 on the left and 2 Al2O3 on the right
   Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3
Now right side has 4 Al: put 4 Al on the left
   4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3
Check: Al 4 = 4;  O 6 = 6
```

Change only the large numbers in front of formulae. Never change a subscript, because that changes the substance.

### Worked example 2: balancing with brackets

*Balance: Na₂CO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂*

```
Na: 2 on the left, so 2 NaCl;    Cl: 2 on the right, so 2 HCl
   Na2CO3 + 2HCl -> 2NaCl + H2O + CO2
Check: Na 2=2, C 1=1, O 3 = 1+2 = 3, H 2=2, Cl 2=2
```

## Relative formula mass (Mr)

The **relative formula mass** of a compound is the **sum of the relative atomic masses** of the atoms in the
numbers shown in the formula.

### Worked example 3: calculating Mr

*Calculate the Mr of (NH₄)₂SO₄.*

```
(NH4)2SO4 contains: N 2, H 8, S 1, O 4
N: 2 x 14 = 28
H: 8 x 1  =  8
S: 1 x 32 = 32
O: 4 x 16 = 64
Mr = 28 + 8 + 32 + 64 = 132
```

Remember to multiply what is inside brackets by the number outside.

### Mr and balanced equations

In a balanced equation, the sum of the Mr values of the reactants, in the quantities shown, equals the sum of the
Mr values of the products.

### Worked example 4: checking an equation with Mr

*Show that mass is conserved: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂*

```
Mr CaCO3 = 40 + 12 + 3 x 16 = 100
Mr CaO   = 40 + 16 = 56
Mr CO2   = 12 + 2 x 16 = 44
Reactants: 100        Products: 56 + 44 = 100     -> equal
```

### Worked example 5: the multipliers count

*2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Show that the Mr values balance.*

```
Left:  2 x 24 + 32 = 80
Right: 2 x 40 = 80
```

## Percentage by mass in a compound

Percentage by mass of an element = (Ar × number of atoms of the element in the formula ÷ Mr of the compound) × 100.

### Worked example 6: percentage of iron in Fe₂O₃

```
Mr of Fe2O3 = 2 x 56 + 3 x 16 = 112 + 48 = 160
Mass of iron in the formula = 112
Percentage = 112 / 160 x 100 = 70%
```

### Worked example 7: percentage of magnesium in magnesium oxide

```
Mr MgO = 40;  Ar Mg = 24
Percentage = 24 / 40 x 100 = 60%
```

## Mass changes when a gas is involved

Sometimes a reaction seems to gain or lose mass. This is because a reactant or product is a **gas** whose mass
has not been taken into account. Atoms are still conserved.

- When a metal reacts with oxygen in an open container, the oxide is **heavier** than the metal because oxygen
  gas from the air is added.
- In the thermal decomposition of a metal carbonate, the solid left is **lighter** because carbon dioxide gas
  escapes into the air.

In the particle model, the gas particles are far apart and move away from the container, so their mass is no
longer in the solid.

### Worked example 8: mass gain

*4.8 g of magnesium burns in air and forms 8.0 g of magnesium oxide. Explain the change and find the mass of
oxygen that reacted.*

```
2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO. Oxygen from the air joins the magnesium atoms, so the oxide is heavier.
Mass of oxygen = 8.0 - 4.8 = 3.2 g
```

### Worked example 9: mass loss

*10.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated in an open crucible until no more changes. The solid left is 5.6 g.
Explain and calculate the mass of gas that escaped.*

```
CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2.  CO2 is a gas and escapes into the air.
Mass of gas lost = 10.0 - 5.6 = 4.4 g
(Check with Mr: 100 g of CaCO3 gives 56 g CaO and 44 g CO2; 10.0 g gives 5.6 g and 4.4 g.)
```

In a **closed** (enclosed) system, no gas can escape and the total mass stays the same.

## Chemical measurements and uncertainty

Whenever a measurement is made there is some **uncertainty** about the result. Repeating a measurement shows how
much the results vary.

### Worked example 10: mean and uncertainty

*Five volumes of gas, in cm³, were measured: 24.2, 24.6, 24.4, 24.0 and 24.3. Find the mean and the
uncertainty.*

```
Mean = (24.2 + 24.6 + 24.4 + 24.0 + 24.3) / 5 = 121.5 / 5 = 24.3
Range = 24.6 - 24.0 = 0.6
Uncertainty = range / 2 = 0.3
Result: 24.3 +/- 0.3 cm3
```

The range about the mean is the measure of uncertainty. Spread of results can also be shown in a table, a bar
chart or a dot plot.

## Common mistakes

- Changing the subscripts to balance an equation.
- Forgetting to multiply the atoms in brackets by the subscript outside, for example in Ca(OH)₂.
- Mixing up Ar (for an atom) and Mr (for a formula).
- Calculating percentage by mass using the wrong total, such as the mass of only part of the formula.
- Saying that mass is "lost" or "gained" without naming the gas.
- Forgetting to multiply by the balancing number when summing Mr values.
- Giving the mean without the unit.

## Exam technique

- **Balance:** count atoms on both sides before and after.
- **Calculate Mr:** write each element's contribution on its own line, then add.
- **Percentage by mass:** write the fraction first, then multiply by 100.
- **Explain a mass change:** name the gas, say whether it was added or escaped, and relate to the equation.
- **Uncertainty:** give mean ± half the range, with the unit.
