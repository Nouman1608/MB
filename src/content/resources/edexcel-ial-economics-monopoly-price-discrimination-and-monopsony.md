## 1. Assumptions and barriers (6a, 6b)

**Assumptions (6a).** A pure monopoly is the only seller in its market, so its demand curve is the market demand curve and slopes downwards. It sells a product with no close substitutes. It is a price maker, and it can choose a price or an output but not both. Barriers to entry stop rivals competing away its profit. Firms are assumed to maximise profit.

**Barriers to entry (6b).**

- Economies of scale: if minimum efficient scale is large compared with demand, a new small entrant has higher unit costs.
- Legal barriers: patents, licences and franchises.
- Branding and loyalty: an entrant must spend heavily on marketing.
- Sunk costs: money that cannot be recovered on leaving makes entry riskier.
- Limit pricing: the incumbent sets a price low enough to make entry look unprofitable.

**Barriers to exit** are costs of leaving: sunk costs, redundancy payments and long contracts. High exit costs also deter entry, because a firm that cannot leave easily is wary of arriving.

## 2. Profit-maximising equilibrium (6c)

A monopolist faces a falling demand curve, so selling an extra unit means cutting the price on every unit. Marginal revenue (MR) is therefore below price. With a straight-line demand curve, MR has the same intercept and twice the slope.

**Worked example.** A sole cement producer in a remote region of Country A (illustrative) has demand P = 120 − 4Q and costs TC = 240 + 24Q + 2Q², so MC = 24 + 4Q.

| Q | P (= AR) | TR | MR | MC | TC | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 96 | 576 | 72 | 48 | 456 | 120 |
| 7 | 92 | 644 | 64 | 52 | 506 | 138 |
| 8 | 88 | 704 | 56 | 56 | 560 | 144 |
| 9 | 84 | 756 | 48 | 60 | 618 | 138 |
| 10 | 80 | 800 | 40 | 64 | 680 | 120 |

Method:

1. TR = PQ = 120Q − 4Q², so MR = 120 − 8Q.
2. Set MR = MC: 120 − 8Q = 24 + 4Q, so 12Q = 96 and Q = 8.
3. Take the price from the demand curve, not from MC: P = 120 − 32 = 88.
4. TC = 240 + 192 + 128 = 560, so ATC = 70. Profit = (88 − 70) × 8 = 144 cu, which is supernormal profit.

The diagram, in words: MR cuts MC from below at output 8. Go up to the demand curve to read the price of 88. Supernormal profit is the rectangle between price 88 and ATC 70, over 8 units.

```
Price
 88  +------* <- price read from demand
 70  |//////|    ATC at Q = 8
 56  |......X <- MR = MC at Q = 8
     +------+----------- Quantity
            8
```

Shaded (//) area = supernormal profit = (88 - 70) x 8 = 144.

Because MR must be positive at the optimum, a monopolist never settles where demand is inelastic. At Q = 8, price elasticity of demand = (1/4) × (88/8) = 2.75, which is elastic (see the [elasticity guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-economics-rational-choice-demand-and-the-elasticities-of-demand/)).

## 3. Costs, benefits and efficiency (6d, 6h)

Take the competitive benchmark P = MC: 120 − 4Q = 24 + 4Q, giving Q = 12 and P = 72.

| | Monopoly | P = MC benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| Output | 8 | 12 |
| Price | 88 | 72 |
| Consumer surplus | 128 | 288 |
| ATC | 70 | 68 |

Consumer surplus is a triangle: ½ × 8 × (120 − 88) = 128, against ½ × 12 × (120 − 72) = 288. The deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and MC from output 8 to 12: ½ × 4 × (88 − 56) = 64 cu.

**Efficiency (6h).**

- Allocative: P = 88 exceeds MC = 56, so monopoly is allocatively inefficient. Output is too low.
- Productive: minimum ATC occurs at Q = √120 ≈ 10.95, where ATC is about 67.8. The monopolist produces 8 at ATC 70, so it is not productively efficient.
- Dynamic: supernormal profit can fund research and new products, which favours dynamic efficiency. Protection from rivals can also remove the pressure to innovate, and costs may drift upwards (X-inefficiency).

**Costs and benefits (6d).**

| | Costs | Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Firms | Regulation or fines; complacency; X-inefficiency | Supernormal profit; funds for investment; economies of scale; less risk |
| Consumers | Higher price, lower output, less choice, possible lower quality | Lower price if scale economies are big; innovation; standardised products |

**Worked evaluation: can monopoly give a lower price?** Demand is P = 120 − 4Q. A competitive industry with constant MC = 80 would give Q = 10 at P = 80. One firm exploiting scale economies has MC = 8, so MR = 120 − 8Q = 8 gives Q = 14 and P = 64. The monopoly price is lower. Only a large cost advantage produces this, and it depends on the savings being passed on. Judgement therefore turns on the size of the cost gap, regulation, and whether profits are reinvested.

## 4. Natural monopoly (6e)

A natural monopoly exists where long-run average cost keeps falling over the whole range of market demand. One firm can serve the market at lower unit cost than two or more, so competition would waste resources. Typical cases have a large fixed network and a low marginal cost.

**Worked example.** A pipeline operator has TC = 480 + 10Q, so MC = 10 and ATC = 480/Q + 10 always falls. Demand is P = 90 − 2Q.

| Rule | Q | P | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit maximising (MR = MC) | 20 | 50 | Profit 1,000 − 680 = 320 |
| Average cost pricing (P = ATC) | about 32.6 | about 24.7 | Normal profit |
| Marginal cost pricing (P = MC) | 40 | 10 | Loss of 480 |

For the second row: 90 − 2Q = 480/Q + 10 gives Q² − 40Q + 240 = 0, so Q = 20 + √160 ≈ 32.65.

**Implications.**

- Unregulated, the firm restricts output and charges 50. Regulation can set a price cap near average cost.
- Allocative efficiency needs P = MC, but that makes a loss equal to fixed cost (480), so a subsidy or a two-part tariff would be needed.
- Duplication is costly. If two firms each served 20 units, each would face ATC = (480 + 200)/20 = 34, against 22 for one firm serving 40. Competition raises costs.
- A regulator may lack the cost information needed to set the price correctly.

## 5. Third-degree price discrimination (6f, 6g)

**Definition.** Charging different prices to different groups for the same product, for reasons not related to cost.

**Conditions (6f).**

1. The firm has price-making power.
2. It can separate the markets, for example by age, location or time of purchase.
3. Resale (arbitrage) between the groups can be prevented.
4. The groups have different price elasticities of demand, so the profit-maximising price differs.

**Worked example.** A ferry operator (invented) has constant MC = 12. Adult demand is P = 100 − 2Q and student demand is P = 60 − 2Q.

| | Adults | Students |
|---|---|---|
| MR | 100 − 4Q | 60 − 4Q |
| MR = 12 gives Q | 22 | 12 |
| Price | 56 | 36 |
| PED at that point | 0.5 × 56/22 = 1.27 | 0.5 × 36/12 = 1.5 |
| Profit | 22 × 44 = 968 | 12 × 24 = 288 |

Total profit is 1,256. The group with the more elastic demand gets the lower price.

**Single price comparison.** Combined demand is Q = 80 − P (valid for prices up to 60), so P = 80 − Q and MR = 80 − 2Q. Setting MR = 12 gives Q = 34, P = 46, and profit 34 × 34 = 1,156. Discrimination adds 100 to profit.

**Costs and benefits (6g).**

- Consumers: adult consumer surplus falls from 729 (½ × 27 × 54) to 484 (½ × 22 × 44); student surplus rises from 49 to 144. Total consumer surplus falls from 778 to 628. With linear demands, total output is 34 either way, so total surplus falls by 50 as the profit gain (100) is smaller than the consumer surplus loss (150).
- Firms: higher profit, and spare capacity can be filled.
- Welfare can rise if discrimination opens a market that a single price would exclude. Suppose student demand is P = 30 − 2Q. A single price of 56 serves no students. Discrimination sells 4.5 units at 21, adding profit of 40.5 and consumer surplus of 20.25.

## 6. Monopsony (7a, 7b)

**Assumptions and conditions (7a).** A monopsony is a single (or dominant) buyer. In a labour market it is the main employer, and it is a wage maker facing an upward-sloping labour supply curve. Conditions that sustain it: workers are occupationally or geographically immobile, there are few alternative employers, and unions are weak or absent. It is usually assumed to pay one wage to all workers.

**Why MCL exceeds the wage.** To hire one more worker the firm must raise the wage for everyone, so the marginal cost of labour (MCL) lies above the supply curve (the average cost of labour).

**Worked example.** A sole employer in an isolated town (invented) faces labour supply W = 6 + 0.5L and marginal revenue product MRP = 30 − 0.5L. Total labour cost is 6L + 0.5L², so MCL = 6 + L.

| | Competitive labour market | Monopsony |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | S = MRP | MCL = MRP |
| Workers | 24 | 16 |
| Wage | 18 | 14 |

Under monopsony, 6 + L = 30 − 0.5L gives L = 16. The wage comes from the supply curve: 6 + 8 = 14. MRP at 16 workers is 22, so each worker is paid 8 less than the value of their contribution.

**Minimum wage.** At 18, the firm's MCL becomes 18 up to 24 workers and employment rises to 24. At 20, labour demand is 20 workers, so employment is 20: higher than 16, but below 24. At 22, employment is back at 16.

**Costs and benefits (7b).**

- Firms: lower costs and higher profit; risks include poor motivation, high turnover and hard-to-fill posts. The same logic applies to a dominant buyer of farm produce paying suppliers less.
- Consumers: lower prices if cost savings are passed on; possible lower quality or supply problems if suppliers cannot survive.
- Employees: lower wages and fewer jobs than competition would give; but jobs may not exist at all without the large employer, and it may train staff.

## Common errors

- Reading the monopoly price off MC instead of up to the demand curve.
- Drawing MR with a different price intercept from demand. For a straight-line demand curve they start together, and MR falls twice as fast.
- Calling any large firm a monopoly: the assumptions need one seller and high barriers.
- Saying price discrimination always harms consumers.
- Reading the monopsony wage from MCL instead of dropping down to the supply curve.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the assumptions of monopoly in Edexcel IAL Economics?**
In the Edexcel IAL Economics specification (YEC11, section 3.3.3 item 6), a pure monopoly is the only seller, so its demand curve is the downward-sloping market demand curve. It sells a product with no close substitutes, is a price maker that can choose price or output but not both, is protected by barriers to entry and is assumed to maximise profit.

**Why is marginal revenue below price for a monopolist?**
A monopolist faces a falling demand curve, so selling an extra unit means cutting the price on every unit sold. Marginal revenue is therefore below price, and for a straight-line demand curve MR has the same intercept and twice the slope.

**Why does a monopolist never produce where demand is price inelastic?**
Profit is maximised where MR equals MC, and marginal cost is positive, so MR must be positive at the optimum. Marginal revenue is positive only on the elastic part of a straight-line demand curve, so the monopolist sets output where demand is elastic.

**Can a monopoly charge a lower price than a competitive industry?**
It can if economies of scale give the single firm a much lower marginal cost than competitive firms would have, and the savings are passed on. Whether this happens depends on the size of the cost advantage, regulation and whether profits are reinvested.

**Why is the marginal cost of labour above the wage in a monopsony?**
To hire one more worker, a monopsonist must raise the wage for all its workers, not just the new one. The marginal cost of labour therefore lies above the labour supply curve, so the firm hires fewer workers at a lower wage than a competitive labour market would.
