## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of capital government expenditure? **[1]**

A: Salaries of teachers in state schools

B: Payments of state pensions

C: Construction of a new railway line

D: Interest paid on government debt

**2.** A tax takes 5% of income from a household earning 15,000 and 5% from a household earning 75,000. Which **one** describes this tax? **[1]**

A: Progressive

B: Proportional

C: Regressive

D: Indirect

**3.** Distinguish between a budget deficit and the national debt. **[2]**

**4.** A worker's income rises from 38,000 to 40,000 and the tax paid rises from 6,400 to 7,000. Calculate the marginal tax rate. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why VAT is usually described as a regressive tax. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how progressive income tax acts as an automatic stabiliser during a boom. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** Country M has the following invented income tax bands.

| Band of annual income | Rate |
|---|---|
| 0 to 15,000 | 0% |
| 15,001 to 45,000 | 20% |
| Above 45,000 | 40% |

**(a)** Calculate the tax paid and the average tax rate for an income of 35,000. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the tax paid and the average tax rate for an income of 90,000. **[3]**

**(c)** State the marginal tax rate for each income and explain whether the system is progressive. **[2]**

**8.** In a recession, Country N's budget deficit is 7% of GDP. The government's advisers estimate that the cyclical deficit is 3% of GDP.

**(a)** Calculate the structural deficit. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the government may need discretionary action to remove the structural deficit. **[2]**

**9.** Country P's national debt is 1,400 billion and its nominal GDP is 2,000 billion. Next year it borrows 80 billion and nominal GDP grows by 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the debt-to-GDP ratio before and after the year. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain one reason why a high and rising debt ratio may concern a government. **[2]**

**10.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, the Laffer curve and what it implies for a government considering a cut in the top rate of income tax. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how crowding out may reduce the effect of an increase in government spending on aggregate demand. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the effectiveness of fiscal policy in reducing unemployment during a recession. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. A railway is a long-lasting asset. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. The average rate is the same at both incomes. **[1]**

**3.** A budget deficit is the amount by which government spending exceeds revenue in one year, a flow [1]; the national debt is the total stock of debt accumulated from past borrowing [1]. **[2]**

**4.** MTR = 600 / 2,000 x 100 [1] = **30%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Lower-income households spend a larger share of their income on goods and services subject to VAT [1], so VAT takes a larger proportion of their income than of richer households' incomes, even at the same rate on spending [1]. **[2]**

**6.** As incomes rise in a boom, more income falls into higher tax bands [1], so tax revenue rises faster than income [1]. This takes spending power out of the economy automatically, slowing the growth of AD and reducing inflationary pressure [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Tax = 20% x (35,000 - 15,000) = **4,000** [1]. ATR = 4,000 / 35,000 = **11.4%** [1].

**(b)** Tax = 20% x 30,000 + 40% x 45,000 [1] = 6,000 + 18,000 = **24,000** [1]. ATR = 24,000 / 90,000 = **26.7%** [1].

**(c)** MTR is 20% at 35,000 and 40% at 90,000 [1]. The ATR rises from 11.4% to 26.7% as income rises, so the system is progressive [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Progressivity is judged by the average rate. Quote both ATRs.

**8. (a)** 7 - 3 = **4% of GDP** [1].

**(b)** The cyclical part disappears as the economy recovers and tax receipts rise [1], but the structural part reflects permanent spending and tax decisions, so it remains unless the government raises taxes or cuts spending [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Before: 1,400 / 2,000 = **70.0%** [1]. After: debt 1,480, GDP 2,060, ratio 1,480 / 2,060 = **71.8%** [1].

**(b)** Higher debt means higher interest payments, which take up spending that could go on public services [1], and lenders may demand higher interest rates if they doubt the government's ability to repay [1]. Other valid reasons: less room for stimulus in a future recession; burden on future taxpayers. **[4]**

**10.** The diagram has the tax rate on the horizontal axis and tax revenue on the vertical, with revenue zero at 0% and near zero at 100%, peaking in between [1]. Below the peak, higher rates raise revenue; above it, higher rates reduce revenue because people work less, avoid or evade tax [1]. If the top rate is above the peak, a cut could raise revenue and improve incentives [1]. But the peak's position is uncertain, so if the rate is below the peak a cut will reduce revenue [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Higher government spending financed by borrowing increases demand for loanable funds [1], which can push up interest rates [1]. Higher interest rates reduce private investment and consumption [1], so the net rise in AD is smaller than the rise in G; near full capacity, public use of resources can also displace private output [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Expansionary policy (higher G or lower T) shifts AD right, raising output and demand for labour [1].
- In a recession there is spare capacity, so the rise in AD mainly raises output rather than prices [1].
- Automatic stabilisers support incomes immediately without a decision [1].
- Capital spending can raise LRAS too, giving lasting benefits [1].
- Time lags may delay the effect until recovery has started [1].
- Tax cuts may be saved if households are worried about their jobs [1].
- Crowding out is weak in a recession, which supports effectiveness [1].
- High existing debt may limit the size of the stimulus or raise borrowing costs [1].
- Part of the extra spending leaks into imports, reducing the effect on domestic jobs [1].
- Judgement: fiscal policy is likely to be effective in a deep recession with spare capacity and manageable debt, especially through well-timed capital spending, but less so if lags are long or debt is already high [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each point to unemployment, not just to AD.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying the top rate to all income.
- Judging progressivity by the marginal rate.
- Confusing a deficit with the debt.
- Saying a rising deficit always means looser policy.
- Drawing the Laffer curve with mislabelled axes.
- Evaluating fiscal policy without reference to spare capacity.
