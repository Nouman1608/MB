This guide covers **Paper 1 Approaches, Issues and Debates**, an AS
Level paper for Cambridge International AS & A Level Psychology 9990,
sat by both AS-only candidates and those continuing to the full A Level.

## Where this fits in 9990

Paper 1 introduces the four core theoretical approaches psychology
uses to explain behaviour, before the syllabus moves on to specific
areas of application. Every later topic in 9990 is examined through
the lens of one or more of these approaches, so a shaky grasp of any
one of the four tends to resurface as difficulty explaining or
evaluating studies drawn from that approach later in the course. This
makes Paper 1 unusually foundational compared with most other AS
papers, since its content is not simply tested once and set aside but
is actively reused as the analytical toolkit for the rest of the
qualification.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE AS & A LEVEL PSYCHOLOGY 9990 — PAPER 1 APPROACHES, ISSUES
AND DEBATES**

- 1.1 The Biological Approach — explaining behaviour through
physiological processes, including the nervous system, hormones and
genetics
- 1.2 The Cognitive Approach — explaining behaviour through internal
mental processes such as memory, perception and thinking
- 1.3 The Learning Approach — explaining behaviour through
conditioning and observational learning
- 1.4 The Social Approach — explaining behaviour through the influence
of other people and the social context

## How to approach it

Each approach is examined both on its own terms and through named
core studies that illustrate it, so revision needs to pair the
theoretical assumptions of each approach with specific supporting
evidence — being able to describe a named study's method, findings and
a genuine criticism of it, for each of the four approaches, is
consistently tested. Evaluation is where most marks are gained or lost:
practise comparing approaches directly (for instance, contrasting how
the Biological and Learning Approaches would each explain the same
behaviour) rather than evaluating each approach only in isolation,
since "compare and contrast" style questions reward exactly that kind
of cross-approach thinking. Because this paper's title includes
"Issues and Debates," candidates should also be comfortable applying
general psychological debates — such as nature versus nurture, or
reductionism versus holism — to each of the four approaches specifically,
rather than treating those debates as a separate, disconnected block of
revision. A simple grid — one row per approach, columns for its core
assumption, a supporting named study, a criticism, and how it relates
to nature/nurture and reductionism/holism — is a genuinely useful
revision tool here, since it forces the cross-approach comparisons
the paper actually rewards rather than four separate, siloed summaries.

## The four approaches at a glance

| Approach | Core explanation | Typical evidence type |
|---|---|---|
| Biological | Behaviour explained through physiology — nervous system, hormones, genetics | Case studies, biological measurements, twin/adoption studies |
| Cognitive | Behaviour explained through internal mental processes — memory, perception, thinking | Lab experiments, often with reaction-time or recall measures |
| Learning | Behaviour explained through conditioning and observational learning | Controlled experiments, often involving animals or children |
| Social | Behaviour explained through the influence of others and social context | Field or lab experiments involving group behaviour |

## Why "issues and debates" is not a separate revision block

A common mistake is treating Issues and Debates as a fifth, standalone topic to revise once, rather
than a lens applied to each of the four approaches individually. Nature versus nurture, for example,
looks different from each approach's perspective: the Biological Approach leans toward nature
(genetics, physiology), the Learning Approach leans toward nurture (environment, conditioning), and
the Cognitive and Social Approaches typically sit somewhere between the two, depending on the
specific study being discussed. The same applies to reductionism versus holism — a Biological
explanation of behaviour is often criticised as reductionist for ignoring social or cognitive
factors, while a Social Approach explanation risks being criticised as failing to account for
individual biological differences. Being able to apply these debates approach-by-approach, rather
than reciting a generic definition of "nature versus nurture," is what separates a strong evaluative
answer from a weak one.

## Comparing approaches head-to-head

Because "compare and contrast" questions are a recurring format, it helps to have, for each pair of
approaches, at least one behaviour or phenomenon you can explain from both angles and then compare
directly — for instance, contrasting how the Biological Approach and the Learning Approach would
each account for the same behaviour, one through physiology and genetics, the other through
conditioning and environmental reinforcement. Preparing two or three such worked comparisons in
advance, across different pairings of the four approaches, is more efficient revision than trying to
improvise a comparison from scratch in the exam, since the underlying structure of a good comparison
(state each approach's explanation, then explicitly weigh them against each other) is reusable
regardless of which specific pairing comes up.

## Named studies are non-negotiable

Cambridge 9990's mark schemes consistently expect a **specific, named study** as evidence for each
approach, not a general description of "an experiment." For each of the four approaches, candidates
should be able to state: the named researcher(s) and study, its aim, its method (including sample
and procedure in outline), its key finding, and at least one genuine methodological or ethical
criticism. A study recalled only vaguely ("a psychologist did an experiment on memory") earns
substantially less credit than one recalled precisely, even where the underlying point being made is
correct.
