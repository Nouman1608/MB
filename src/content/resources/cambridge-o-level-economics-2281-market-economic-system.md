## 2.9.1 What a market economic system is

A **market economic system** is one in which resources are allocated mainly by the **price mechanism**: the interaction of demand and supply in markets, with land and capital mostly owned privately and decisions made by households and firms rather than by the government.

Every economy must answer three questions because resources are scarce: **what** to produce, **how** to produce it and **for whom**. A market system answers them like this:

| Question | Answer in a market system |
|---|---|
| What to produce? | Whatever consumers are willing and able to buy at a price that covers firms' costs |
| How to produce? | Whatever method gives the lowest cost, because that maximises profit |
| For whom? | Those with the income and wealth to pay the market price |

### Six features to know

1. **Private ownership of resources.** Households and firms own land, buildings and machines and decide how to use them.
2. **The profit motive.** Firms produce in order to make a profit, which rewards those that satisfy buyers at low cost.
3. **Consumer sovereignty.** Consumers' spending decides which products survive. Each purchase works like a vote for that product.
4. **Price signals.** Prices rise where demand exceeds supply and fall where supply exceeds demand, telling everyone where resources are wanted.
5. **Competition.** Many buyers and sellers mean no single one can set the price, and firms must keep costs and prices down to keep customers.
6. **A limited role for the government.** It enforces property rights and contracts and keeps law and order, but does not decide what is produced.

## How the price mechanism works: one change traced through

In an invented country, a popular health campaign makes cycling fashionable.

1. **Signal.** Demand for bicycles rises. At the old price there is a shortage, so the price of bicycles rises. Meanwhile demand for motor scooters falls, and their price falls.
2. **Incentive.** Higher prices raise profit for bicycle makers, so they expand output and new firms enter. Scooter makers face losses and cut output.
3. **Reallocation.** Bicycle makers hire more workers and buy more steel and factory space. Scooter makers release workers and capital. Wages for skilled bicycle mechanics rise, encouraging workers to retrain.
4. **Rationing.** While output catches up, the higher price decides who gets the bicycles that exist: those willing and able to pay most.

No official planned any of this. Millions of separate decisions, each guided by prices, moved resources from scooters to bicycles. That is the central claim made for the market system.

## 2.9.2 Advantages

Each advantage needs a reason, not just a label.

- **Choice and responsiveness.** Firms make what consumers will pay for, because that is where profit is. When tastes change, prices change and production follows quickly, as in the bicycle example.
- **Efficiency.** Competition forces firms to use the lowest-cost methods; a firm that wastes resources must charge more and loses customers to rivals.
- **Innovation.** The chance of profit encourages firms to develop new products and better methods, since a successful idea can earn high returns.
- **Incentives for effort and enterprise.** People keep what they earn, so there is a reward for working, saving, training and taking business risks.
- **No costly planning body.** Prices co-ordinate decisions automatically, so there is no need for officials to collect information and set output targets for every product.

## 2.9.2 Disadvantages

These are the drawbacks the mixed economic system (2.11) tries to correct, and several are the causes of market failure in 2.10.

- **Public goods may not be provided.** Products such as street lighting cannot easily be charged for, because non-payers cannot be excluded, so private firms have no reason to supply them.
- **Merit goods are under-consumed.** People may undervalue education or healthcare, or be unable to pay, so less is bought than is good for them and for society.
- **Demerit goods and external costs are over-produced.** Firms and consumers ignore costs they impose on others, such as pollution or the harm from smoking.
- **Inequality.** Income decides who gets goods, so those with little income or wealth go without, even for essentials, while the rich have wide choice.
- **Monopoly power.** Competition can disappear if one firm drives out or buys up its rivals; it can then raise prices and restrict output.
- **Factor immobility and instability.** Workers and capital may not move easily from declining to growing industries, so unemployment can persist. Output and jobs can also rise and fall sharply over time.

## Examples from a variety of countries

No country has a pure market system. Every government at least runs courts and enforces contracts, and most provide schools, defence and roads. What differs is how far each economy relies on markets.

- **Hong Kong** is often described as one of the most market-oriented economies, with low taxes and few barriers to trade. Even so, its government provides public housing, education and healthcare for large numbers of residents.
- **The United States** relies more on markets for healthcare than most other high-income countries: much healthcare is paid for through private insurance. This is often linked both to rapid medical innovation and to the problem that people without insurance can find care hard to afford.
- **Central and Eastern Europe.** After 1989, countries such as Poland moved from central planning towards market systems. Prices were freed, state firms were sold or closed, and private businesses grew. Shortages of consumer goods ended, but unemployment rose as inefficient state firms shut.
- **China** began market reforms from 1978, allowing farmers and private firms to sell output at market prices. Output grew rapidly over the following decades, although the state still owns many large firms.

Use examples like these as evidence, then link them to the question: each shows a benefit (choice, growth, efficiency) or a cost (unequal access, job losses during change) of relying on markets.

## Evaluating the market system

A strong judgement says what the answer **depends on**:

- **The product.** Markets work well for food, clothing and phones, where there are many sellers and buyers can judge quality. They work poorly for public goods, merit goods and products with large external costs.
- **Competition.** The efficiency argument holds only where firms face rivals. Where one firm dominates, consumers may pay more.
- **Income distribution.** Where incomes are very unequal, market outcomes may leave many people without essentials.
- **Time.** Moving towards markets can bring short-run costs (job losses, rising prices for some goods) and long-run gains (growth, choice).

## Common mistakes

- Defining the system as "no government". The government still sets and enforces the rules.
- Saying the government sets prices in a market economy. Demand and supply set them.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages as single words with no explanation.
- Confusing the market economic system (a way of allocating resources) with market failure (a situation where it allocates them poorly).
- Using a country example without linking it to the point being made.

## Exam technique for 2.9

- **Define**: mention the price mechanism, private ownership and decisions by households and firms.
- **Describe** the features: give each one with a short explanation.
- **Explain** an advantage or disadvantage as a chain: point, how it happens, what it leads to.
- **Discuss** whether the market system is the best way to allocate resources: give both sides, use an example, and finish with a judgement that says what the answer depends on.
