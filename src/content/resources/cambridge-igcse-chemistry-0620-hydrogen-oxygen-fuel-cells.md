This guide covers subtopic **4.2 Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells** in Topic 4, Electrochemistry,
for Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). The short comparison table in
[Electrolysis and Fuel Cells](/resources/electrolysis-and-fuel-cells/) lists the main
points; this page explains the chemistry behind each one and shows how to build a
balanced evaluation that earns full marks.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core**

- State that a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell uses **hydrogen and oxygen to produce
  electricity**, with **water as the only chemical product**.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only)

- Describe the **advantages and disadvantages** of using hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells **in
  comparison with gasoline/petrol engines in vehicles**.

The comparison is specifically with **petrol engines in vehicles**. An answer that compares a
fuel cell with a battery or a power station misses the point of the question.

## What a fuel cell does

A **fuel cell** is a device in which a fuel reacts with oxygen to produce **electricity
directly**. In a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell:

- **hydrogen** is fed to one side of the cell;
- **oxygen** (usually from the air) is fed to the other side;
- the two react, and the energy released is transferred as an **electric current** through
  an external circuit;
- the only chemical product is **water**.

The overall reaction is the same as burning hydrogen:

```
2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O
```

The difference is **how the energy is released**. When hydrogen burns there is a flame and
the energy appears mainly as heat. In a fuel cell there is no flame: the reaction is split
between the two sides of the cell, and much of the energy appears as **electrical energy**.

A fuel cell keeps working for as long as hydrogen and oxygen are supplied. That is the key
difference from an ordinary battery, which contains a fixed amount of reactants and stops
when they are used up.

**Link to redox (Topic 6.4).** Hydrogen **loses electrons** (is oxidised) and oxygen **gains
electrons** (is reduced). The electrons travel through the external circuit, which is the
current. Half-equations for the fuel cell are not required at 0620.

**Link to the mole (Supplement, 3.3).** The equation shows that hydrogen and oxygen react
in a **2 : 1** ratio by moles, and therefore by volume for gases at the same temperature
and pressure. 4 g of hydrogen (2 mol) gives 36 g of water (2 mol).

## The petrol engine for comparison

A petrol engine burns petrol (a mixture of hydrocarbons) inside the engine. The heat makes
hot gases expand and push the pistons, and the motion turns the wheels. Its exhaust
contains:

- **carbon dioxide** — from complete combustion; a greenhouse gas linked to climate change;
- **carbon monoxide** — from incomplete combustion; toxic;
- **oxides of nitrogen** — formed when nitrogen and oxygen from the air react at the high
  temperature inside the engine; they contribute to acid rain and photochemical smog;
- **unburnt hydrocarbons** and **particulates**.

These pollutants are covered in Topic 10 (Chemistry of the environment); here they matter
because a fuel-cell vehicle produces none of them at the point of use.

## Advantages of fuel cells compared with petrol engines (Supplement)

| Advantage | Why — the chemistry behind it |
|---|---|
| Only product is water | No carbon in the fuel, so no CO₂ or CO; no combustion at high temperature, so no oxides of nitrogen |
| Less pollution at point of use | Cleaner air in towns; no particulates or unburnt hydrocarbons from the vehicle |
| More efficient | Chemical energy goes **directly** to electrical energy; a petrol engine converts chemical → heat → movement, losing much energy as heat |
| Hydrogen can be made from water | By electrolysis, so it does not have to come from crude oil, a non-renewable resource |
| Fewer moving parts | Quieter running and less wear |

## Disadvantages of fuel cells compared with petrol engines (Supplement)

| Disadvantage | Why |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen is difficult to store | It is a gas with a very low density, so it must be stored under high pressure in strong, heavy tanks; petrol is an easily stored liquid |
| Hydrogen is highly flammable | Leaks can form explosive mixtures with air |
| Few refuelling stations | Petrol stations are widespread; hydrogen supply is limited |
| Making hydrogen uses energy and may release CO₂ | Much hydrogen is made from methane (a fossil fuel), which releases CO₂; electrolysis of water needs electricity, which may come from burning fossil fuels |
| Fuel cells are expensive | Vehicles and cells currently cost more to make than petrol engines |

The fourth disadvantage is the one examiners most want to see. A fuel-cell car has **no
harmful emissions from the vehicle**, but the hydrogen it uses may have been produced in a
way that does release carbon dioxide.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (Supplement): Describe two advantages and two disadvantages of using hydrogen–oxygen
fuel cells instead of petrol engines in cars.* **[4]**

> Advantage 1: the only product is water, so unlike a petrol engine no **carbon dioxide or
> carbon monoxide** is released ✓.
> Advantage 2: fuel cells are **more efficient** because chemical energy is converted
> directly into electrical energy, whereas a petrol engine loses much energy as heat ✓.
> Disadvantage 1: hydrogen is a gas that is **difficult and dangerous to store** — it needs
> high-pressure tanks and is highly flammable — whereas petrol is a liquid ✓.
> Disadvantage 2: hydrogen is often **made from fossil fuels such as methane**, which releases
> carbon dioxide, so the overall process is not pollution-free ✓.

Each point names the fuel cell feature **and** the contrast with petrol. Bare words such as
"cleaner" or "dangerous" would not score.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying a fuel cell produces "nothing"** or "no products". It produces water.
- **Saying fuel cells "burn" hydrogen.** There is no combustion; the energy is released as
  electricity.
- **Calling water vapour a pollutant** from fuel cells.
- **"Hydrogen is renewable" without qualification.** Hydrogen is only as clean as the
  method used to make it.
- **Comparing with batteries or electric cars** when the syllabus comparison is with petrol
  engines.
- **One-word points** ("cheaper", "safer") without saying why.

## Exam technique: command words

- **State** (Core): "uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity; water is the only
  product". Both reactants and the product are needed.
- **Describe advantages and disadvantages** (Supplement): give the number asked for of
  each, each with its reason and the comparison with petrol.
- **Suggest / evaluate**: weigh the vehicle's zero emissions against how hydrogen is made
  and stored, and reach a conclusion.
- **Write an equation**: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, with state symbols if asked.

## Quick checklist

- Reactants: hydrogen and oxygen; product: water only; output: electricity
- Overall equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
- *(Supplement)* at least three advantages and three disadvantages, each compared with a
  petrol engine
