## Section A

**1.** State the type of current supplied by the mains and the type supplied by a battery. **[2]**

**2.** State the equation linking energy transferred, current, voltage and time. **[1]**

**3.** Name two safety features that protect the user of an appliance with a metal case. **[2]**

**4.** State what happens to a fuse when the current through it becomes greater than its rating. **[2]**

**5.** Give one reason why a circuit breaker may be preferred to a fuse. **[1]**

**6.** Describe how the graph of voltage against time for an a.c. supply differs from the graph for a d.c. supply. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A toaster is rated 1.1 kW, 230 V.

**(a)** Calculate the normal operating current. **[2]**
**(b)** Select the most suitable fuse and justify your choice. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the energy transferred by the toaster in 2.5 minutes. **[3]**

**8.** An electric drill has a metal case, which is earthed. A fault causes the live wire to touch the case.

Explain how the earth wire and the fuse together protect the user. **[4]**

**9.** A food mixer has a plastic case and is double insulated.

**(a)** Explain why the mixer does not need an earth wire. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why a damaged plastic case should not be used, even on a double-insulated appliance. **[1]**

**10.** An electric kettle contains a heating element made of resistance wire.

**(a)** Explain, in terms of electrons and ions, why the element becomes hot when there is a current in it. **[3]**
**(b)** Name two other domestic appliances that use this heating effect. **[2]**
**(c)** The same heating effect happens in the cable connecting the kettle to the mains. Explain why this is a disadvantage, and how it is reduced. **[2]**

**11.** A student says: "A fuse should have a rating as high as possible so that it never blows."

Evaluate this statement. **[3]**

**12.** A room heater takes a current of 6.5 A from the 230 V mains.

**(a)** Calculate the power of the heater, in kW. **[3]**
**(b)** The heater is used for 3.0 hours each day. Calculate the energy it transfers each day, in MJ. **[3]**

**13.** Two appliances are used on 230 V: a 2.8 kW electric shower and a 15 W phone charger.

**(a)** Calculate the operating current of each. **[2]**
**(b)** Select a fuse for each from 3 A, 5 A and 13 A. Explain why one of them could not be protected properly by any of these fuses. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Mains: alternating current (a.c.) [1]. Battery: direct current (d.c.) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** energy transferred = current × voltage × time (E = I × V × t) [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two: insulation of the wires [1]; earthing of the case [1]; a fuse [1]; a circuit breaker [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The fuse wire heats up and melts [1], breaking the circuit so the current stops [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any one: it can be reset rather than replaced; it acts more quickly [1]. **[1]**

**6.** The a.c. graph is a wave that alternates between positive and negative values [1]; the d.c. graph is a horizontal line at a constant value on one side of zero [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** I = P ÷ V = 1100 ÷ 230 [1] = 4.8 A [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 5 A [1]: it is the smallest rating above the normal current of 4.8 A, so it does not blow in normal use but blows quickly if a fault increases the current [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** t = 2.5 × 60 = 150 s [1]; E = I × V × t = 4.78 × 230 × 150 (or P × t = 1100 × 150) [1] = 165 000 J (1.65 × 10⁵ J) [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Indicative points, one mark each:
- the case becomes connected to the live wire (it would be at a high voltage) [1]
- the earth wire provides a low-resistance path from the case to the ground [1]
- so a very large current flows through the live and earth wires, not through the user [1]
- this current is greater than the fuse rating, so the fuse melts and disconnects the live supply, leaving the case safe [1]

**[4]**

**9. (a)** The case is made of an insulator [1], so no part that the user can touch can become live, even if a wire inside comes loose [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** A crack or hole could let the user touch internal live parts, so the second layer of insulation no longer protects them [1]. **[1]**

**10. (a)** The current is a flow of electrons through the metal [1]. The electrons collide with the ions of the metal and transfer energy to them [1], so the ions vibrate more and the temperature of the wire rises [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Any two: toaster, electric heater, iron, hairdryer, immersion heater, electric shower [1] [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Energy is wasted heating the cable, and too large a current could make it overheat [1]; cables are made of copper, which has a low resistance, and are thick enough for the current they carry [1]. **[2]**

**11.** The statement is wrong [1]. A very high-rated fuse would not melt when a fault causes a larger-than-normal current, so the cable could overheat and cause a fire, or a user could be at risk [1]. The fuse should be rated just above the normal operating current, so it does not blow in normal use but does blow when there is a fault [1]. **[3]**

**12. (a)** P = I × V = 6.5 × 230 [1] = 1495 W [1] = 1.5 kW [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** t = 3.0 × 3600 = 10 800 s [1]; E = I × V × t = 6.5 × 230 × 10 800 = 16 146 000 J [1] = 16 MJ [1]. **[3]**

**13. (a)** Shower: I = 2800 ÷ 230 = 12 A (12.2 A) [1]. Charger: I = 15 ÷ 230 = 0.065 A [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Shower: 13 A [1]; charger: 3 A [1]. The charger's current is far below 3 A, so even a 3 A fuse would allow a fault current many times its normal current before melting; the shower's 13 A is only just above its normal current, so it is well protected [1]. **[3]**
