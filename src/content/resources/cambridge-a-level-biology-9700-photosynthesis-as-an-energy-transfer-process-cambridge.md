This guide covers section **13.1 Photosynthesis as an energy transfer process** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is A Level content, examined in Paper 4, and the chromatography outcome also supports the planning and analysis skills of Paper 5. The [Photosynthesis study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-photosynthesis/) introduces the whole of Topic 13; this page stays with section 13.1 and goes further into the reasoning examiners reward: following energy from one form to the next, predicting what happens when one step is blocked, and reading spectra and chromatograms. ## What 13.1 asks you to do

| Outcome | In short |
|---|---|
| 13.1.1 | Relate chloroplast structure, in diagrams and electron micrographs, to function |
| 13.1.2 | Explain that ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent stage are used in the Calvin cycle to make complex organic molecules |
| 13.1.3 | State that thylakoids (in stacks called grana) are the site of the light-dependent stage and the stroma of the light-independent stage |
| 13.1.4–5 | Describe the roles of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotene and xanthophyll; interpret absorption and action spectra |
| 13.1.6 | Describe and use chromatography, with Rf values, to identify pigments |
| 13.1.7–10 | Explain cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation and how the proton gradient drives ATP synthase |
| 13.1.11–12 | Outline the three stages of the Calvin cycle and the uses of GP and TP |

Details of electron carriers and of ATP synthase are not expected.

## The energy story in one chain

The title of the section is the key to it. Every outcome is a link in one chain of energy transfers:

1. **Light energy** is absorbed by pigments in the thylakoid membranes.
2. It is passed to chlorophyll a in a reaction centre, where it **raises an electron to a higher energy level** (photoactivation).
3. The energetic electron **releases energy** as it passes along the electron transport chain.
4. That energy is used to **pump protons** into the thylakoid space, so energy is now stored in a **proton gradient**.
5. Protons diffuse back through **ATP synthase**, and the energy is transferred to **ATP**.
6. Electrons that reach the end of the non-cyclic pathway are transferred to NADP, so energy is also held in **reduced NADP**.
7. In the stroma, ATP and reduced NADP transfer their energy to **triose phosphate (TP)**, from which carbohydrates, lipids and amino acids are made.

If you can say which form the energy is in at each step, you can answer most "explain" questions on this section. Notice that oxygen appears only as a by-product of replacing electrons lost from photosystem II; it carries no energy forward in the chain.

## Reading a chloroplast electron micrograph

Outcome 13.1.1 asks you to link what you see to what each part does. On an electron micrograph look for:

| Feature you can see | How to recognise it | Function it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Envelope | Two parallel membranes round the edge | Controls entry of CO₂, exit of sugars and other products |
| Grana | Dark stacks of flattened sacs | Light-dependent stage; huge membrane area for pigments, photosystems, carriers and ATP synthase |
| Thylakoids linking grana | Single membranes running between stacks | Connect the grana into one membrane system |
| Stroma | Pale granular matrix | Calvin cycle enzymes, including rubisco |
| Starch grains | Large pale or white oval areas | Store of carbohydrate made from TP |
| Lipid droplets | Small dark round spots | Lipid store |
| Ribosomes and DNA | Tiny dots; faint fibrous area | Make some chloroplast proteins |

Examiners reward a **structure plus consequence** pair. "There are many grana" scores less than "many stacked thylakoids give a large surface area for photosystems, so more light is absorbed".

## Pigments, photosystems and spectra

The four named pigments are held in the thylakoid membranes in groups called **photosystems**. Chlorophyll b, carotene and xanthophyll are **accessory pigments**: they absorb wavelengths that chlorophyll a absorbs less well and pass the energy to chlorophyll a at the **reaction centre**. A wider range of wavelengths can therefore be used.

**Absorption spectrum**: absorbance of a pigment against wavelength. **Action spectrum**: rate of photosynthesis against wavelength.

When interpreting spectra, make three moves:

- **Describe peaks with numbers.** For example, "absorption peaks at about 430 nm and 660 nm" scores; "it absorbs blue and red" may only score once.
- **Compare the action spectrum with the combined absorption.** Where they match, the absorbed light is being used for photosynthesis.
- **Explain any gap.** If the rate of photosynthesis at a wavelength is higher than chlorophyll a alone would explain, accessory pigments are absorbing at that wavelength and passing energy on.

## Chromatography: a second worked example

Paper chromatography (or thin-layer chromatography) separates pigments because each one has a different solubility in the solvent and a different attraction to the stationary phase.

**Worked example 1.** A leaf extract was run in a solvent until the solvent front was 120 mm from the origin. Spots were at 114 mm, 84 mm, 72 mm and 54 mm. A reference table for this solvent gave carotene 0.95, xanthophyll 0.70, chlorophyll a 0.60 and chlorophyll b 0.45.

```
114 ÷ 120 = 0.95   carotene
 84 ÷ 120 = 0.70   xanthophyll
 72 ÷ 120 = 0.60   chlorophyll a
 54 ÷ 120 = 0.45   chlorophyll b
```

Method points that often earn Paper 5 marks: draw the origin in **pencil** (ink would itself separate); apply a **small, concentrated** spot, letting it dry between applications; keep the origin **above** the solvent surface; cover the tank so the solvent vapour saturates the air; mark the solvent front as soon as the paper is removed.

## Cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation compared

| | Cyclic | Non-cyclic |
|---|---|---|
| Photosystems | PSI only | PSII and PSI |
| Electron source | Electrons from PSI return to PSI | Electrons from PSII; replaced by photolysis of water |
| Final electron acceptor | PSI | NADP (which becomes reduced NADP) |
| Products | ATP | ATP, reduced NADP, and oxygen as a by-product |

In both, photoactivation of chlorophyll happens, electrons release energy along the transport chain, and that energy pumps protons into the thylakoid space. The **oxygen-evolving complex** catalyses photolysis: water is split into protons, electrons and oxygen.

**Why have a cyclic pathway at all?** As background (you will not be asked to recall these numbers): reducing each GP to TP uses one ATP and one reduced NADP, and regenerating each RuBP uses one more ATP. For three turns of the cycle that is 9 ATP but only 6 reduced NADP. Cyclic photophosphorylation makes extra ATP without making reduced NADP, which helps match the supply to the Calvin cycle's demand.

## The Calvin cycle as an energy sink

In the stroma, **rubisco** fixes CO₂ by combining it with RuBP (5C), giving two GP (3C). GP is **reduced** to TP using reduced NADP and ATP; this is the step where energy from the light-dependent stage enters organic molecules. Most TP is used to **regenerate RuBP**, a step that uses ATP. The rest leaves the cycle: TP is used to make carbohydrates, lipids and amino acids, and GP is used to make some amino acids.

## Worked example 2: predicting the effect of a block

*A chloroplast suspension is photosynthesising in bright light. The light is switched off. Predict and explain the changes in GP and RuBP over the next minute.*

Model answer, annotated:

1. With no light, there is no photoactivation, so **no ATP or reduced NADP** is made. *(Identifies the first link that breaks.)*
2. GP cannot be reduced to TP, but rubisco still fixes CO₂ with the RuBP present, so **GP accumulates**. *(Explains both the "made" and "used" sides of GP.)*
3. Less TP is made, and there is no ATP for regeneration, so **RuBP is not regenerated**; it is used up in fixation and its concentration **falls**. *(Same two-sided reasoning for RuBP.)*

The same method handles a chemical that blocks PSII: photolysis stops, oxygen release stops and no reduced NADP is made, but cyclic photophosphorylation through PSI can still make some ATP. GP rises and RuBP falls, as above, because GP reduction needs reduced NADP.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the light-dependent stage "makes energy". Energy is **transferred** into ATP and reduced NADP.
- Writing "NADPH" when the syllabus term is **reduced NADP**: either may be accepted, but stay consistent.
- Stating that protons are pumped **out of** the thylakoid. They are pumped **into** the thylakoid space and return to the stroma through ATP synthase.
- Claiming oxygen comes from carbon dioxide. It comes from **photolysis of water**.
- Saying the Calvin cycle "makes glucose". Its direct product is **TP**.
- Calculating Rf from the bottom edge of the paper rather than from the origin.

## Exam technique for 13.1 command words

- **State** (13.1.3, 13.1.7): one clear fact, no explanation needed. "The stroma" is enough for the site of the Calvin cycle.
- **Describe** (13.1.1, 13.1.4, 13.1.6): give features or steps in order, using syllabus terms such as grana, thylakoid space and accessory pigment.
- **Explain** (13.1.2, 13.1.8–10): give the reason or mechanism, linking each step with "so" or "because". A list of terms without links rarely gets full marks.
- **Outline** (13.1.11): the main points only. Three stages with the right molecules and carbon numbers are what is needed.
- **Interpret** spectra: quote values from the graph and link them to pigments.
- **Predict**: state the direction of change, then justify it from what is made and what is used.
