## Why multicellular organisms need communication (5.1.1(a))

In a large multicellular organism most cells are far from the surface and are specialised, so they cannot sense or respond to everything themselves. Communication systems solve three problems.

- **Responding to the external environment.** Animals must react to temperature, predators and food. Plants must respond to light direction, gravity and touch.
- **Responding to the internal environment.** Blood glucose concentration, blood water potential, pH and core temperature must stay within limits that suit enzymes and membranes. Changes are detected and corrected.
- **Coordinating organs.** During exercise your muscles need more oxygen, so heart rate and breathing rate must rise together. Different organs must act as one system.

Animals use the nervous system, with electrical impulses along neurones and chemical transmitters between them, and the endocrine system, which releases hormones into the blood. Plants rely on chemical signals.

## Cell signalling (5.1.1(b))

Cell signalling means one cell releasing or displaying a signal that changes another cell's behaviour. The signal molecule binds to a **receptor** whose shape is complementary to it. Only cells with the right receptor, the **target cells**, respond. Cells without it ignore the signal.

### Signalling between adjacent cells

- **Across a synapse.** A neurone releases neurotransmitter into the narrow gap. It diffuses a very short distance and binds to receptors on the next cell. The effect is fast and brief.
- **Direct contact.** Some cells carry signal molecules in their surface membranes, as when a T helper cell binds to an antigen-presenting cell.
- **Channels between cells.** Animal cells can pass small molecules through gap junctions. Plant cells are joined by plasmodesmata, which link cytoplasm through the cell walls.

### Signalling between distant cells

- **Hormones.** An endocrine gland secretes a hormone into the blood. The blood carries it to every organ but only target cells with the matching receptor respond. Peptide hormones usually bind to receptors on the cell surface membrane. Steroid hormones can cross the membrane and bind to receptors inside the cell.

| Feature | Nervous communication | Hormonal communication |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Electrical impulses, then neurotransmitter at synapses | Hormone molecules in the blood |
| Speed | Very fast | Slower, as the blood must carry the hormone |
| Duration | Short; stops when the stimulus stops | Usually longer lasting |

## Principles of homeostasis (5.1.1(c))

**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of the internal environment within narrow limits, around a set point. It is a dynamic equilibrium: the value fluctuates slightly and is always being corrected. Enzymes work best in a narrow range of temperature and pH, and water potential must stay balanced for cells to avoid gaining or losing too much water.

A control loop has three parts: a **receptor** detects a stimulus (a change from the set point), information passes by nerve or hormone, and an **effector** (a muscle or gland) carries out a response. The receptors then detect the result, which closes the loop.

A receptor detects; an effector acts. A temperature-sensitive nerve ending in the skin is a receptor. A sweat gland is an effector.

### Negative and positive feedback

**Negative feedback** reverses a change. If a factor rises above the set point, the response lowers it. If it falls, the response raises it. This keeps the factor near the set point, which is why negative feedback is the basis of homeostasis. Systems often have two opposing effectors, so the factor can be corrected in both directions. That gives finer control than one effector alone.

**Positive feedback** increases the change. The response makes the stimulus larger, so the factor moves further from its starting value. It does not hold a factor steady, but suits a rapid, one-off event. Platelets that stick at a wound release chemicals that attract more platelets, speeding up clot formation. Contractions of the uterus during birth stimulate release of oxytocin, which stimulates stronger contractions.

## Temperature control (5.1.1(d))

An **endotherm** (for example a mammal or bird) produces most of its body heat by metabolism and keeps a fairly constant core temperature. An **ectotherm** (for example a lizard or a fish) gains most of its heat from the surroundings, so its body temperature changes with the environment.

### Endotherms: receptors, hypothalamus, effectors

- **Peripheral temperature receptors** in the skin detect surface temperature.
- The **hypothalamus** in the brain monitors the temperature of the blood and receives input from the skin. It acts as the coordination centre and sends impulses to the effectors.
- **Effectors in the skin and muscles** bring about the response.

| Response | When too cold | When too hot |
|---|---|---|
| Arterioles supplying skin capillaries | Vasoconstriction: skin receives less blood, so less heat escapes | Vasodilation: greater blood flow to the skin, so heat radiates away faster |
| Sweat glands | Little or no sweat | Sweat evaporates and takes heat from the skin |
| Skeletal muscle | Shivering: rapid contractions release heat from respiration | No shivering |
| Erector pili muscles | Hairs raised, trapping an insulating layer of air | Hairs lie flat |
| Metabolic rate | Increased, releasing more heat | Reduced heat production |

Behavioural responses add to these. Endotherms may seek shade, change posture, huddle together, put on or remove clothing, or become less active in the heat.

### Ectotherms: behavioural responses

Ectotherms control temperature by behaviour. They bask in the sun or press against warm ground to warm up. They orientate the body to the sun, or flatten and spread to increase the area exposed. They shelter in burrows or shade to cool down, and may be active at different times of day.

Endotherms need much more food, because heat production uses energy, but they stay active in the cold. Ectotherms need less food but may be sluggish when cold.

### Worked example: comparing a lizard and a mammal

A lizard and a small mammal are placed in a chamber warmed from 18 °C. After 40 minutes the lizard's body temperature has risen from 18.0 °C to 34.0 °C. In the same time the mammal's body temperature changes from 37.0 °C to 37.4 °C.

1. Lizard: change = 34.0 − 18.0 = 16.0 °C. Rate = 16.0 ÷ 40 = **0.40 °C per minute**.
2. Mammal: change = 37.4 − 37.0 = 0.4 °C. Rate = 0.4 ÷ 40 = **0.010 °C per minute**.
3. The lizard's temperature followed the environment because it relies on external heat. The mammal's barely changed because negative feedback kept its core temperature near the set point.

## Excretion (5.1.2(a))

**Excretion** means getting metabolic waste out of the body. It is different from egestion, which removes undigested food that has never entered body cells.

Two groups of waste matter most for this outcome.

- **Carbon dioxide** from respiration. It dissolves to form an acid in blood and lowers pH, which would disturb enzyme activity. The lungs excrete it.
- **Nitrogenous waste.** Excess amino acids cannot be stored. The liver removes the amino group, and the nitrogen leaves as ammonia, which is toxic. The liver converts ammonia to urea, which the kidneys remove in urine.

Removing waste protects metabolism, because enzymes need the right chemical conditions, and keeps blood composition stable.

## The mammalian liver (5.1.2(b))

### Gross structure

The liver is a large organ below the diaphragm. Two vessels bring blood in.

- The **hepatic artery** brings oxygenated blood from the heart.
- The **hepatic portal vein** brings blood rich in absorbed nutrients from the small intestine.

The **hepatic vein** takes blood away to the vena cava. The **bile duct** carries bile to the gall bladder and small intestine.

### Histology

The liver is built from **lobules**, roughly hexagonal units about 1 mm across.

- Each lobule has a **central vein**, a branch of the hepatic vein, running through the middle.
- At the corners, a branch of the hepatic artery, a branch of the hepatic portal vein and a branch of the bile duct run side by side.
- Blood from the artery and portal vein mixes in channels called **sinusoids** that run between rows of liver cells towards the central vein. Sinusoids are wide, leaky capillaries with a slow flow.
- **Hepatocytes** are the liver cells. They line the sinusoids, so they exchange materials with the blood easily. They have many mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, as they make and modify many substances.
- **Kupffer cells** are macrophages in the sinusoid walls. They engulf old red blood cells and foreign particles.
- Hepatocytes secrete bile into tiny **canaliculi** that lead to the bile duct. Bile flows the opposite way to the blood.

### Drawing stained sections (5.1.2(b)(ii))

At low power, draw a tissue plan only: the lobule outline, central vein and corner tracts, with no individual cells. At high power, draw a few cells with a sharp pencil, clear single lines and no shading. Use ruled label lines that never cross, and state the magnification.

### Worked example: magnification

A micrograph shows one hepatocyte measuring 36 mm across at a magnification of ×1500.

Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 36 ÷ 1500 = 0.024 mm, which is 0.024 × 1000 = **24 µm**.

### Roles of the liver

- **Storage of glycogen.** Hepatocytes convert excess glucose to glycogen and break it down to glucose when blood glucose falls. The hormones that control this are covered in a later part of Module 5.
- **Detoxification.** The liver breaks down toxins from the diet or from metabolism into less harmful substances. Hepatocytes convert ethanol to ethanal and then ethanoate, using enzymes, and break down hydrogen peroxide using catalase. They also metabolise many drugs.
- **Formation of urea.** Amino acids not needed are deaminated. Ammonia is released and joins with carbon dioxide in the **ornithine cycle** to form urea. The blood carries urea to the kidneys.

### Worked example: interpreting data

Ammonia in the blood of a patient is 0.24 mmol dm⁻³ in the hepatic portal vein and 0.03 mmol dm⁻³ in the hepatic vein.

1. Fall = 0.24 − 0.03 = 0.21 mmol dm⁻³.
2. Percentage fall = 0.21 ÷ 0.24 × 100 = **87.5%**.
3. Explanation: hepatocytes take up ammonia and convert it to urea, so blood leaving the liver contains less.

## Common errors

- Writing that the hypothalamus "sweats". It is the coordination centre; glands and muscles are effectors.
- Saying vasoconstriction "moves capillaries" nearer the skin. Capillaries do not move. Arterioles change diameter.
- Confusing excretion with egestion.
- Mixing up the liver's two blood supplies. Only the hepatic artery carries oxygenated blood in.
- Drawing cells at low power, or drawing shaded and overlapping lines.
