## Section A

**1.** Define the term "sustainable management". **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by "leakage" in forest conservation. **[2]**

**3.** Outline **two** threats to savanna ecosystems. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows data for the fictional rainforest district of Selvia. A scheme paying landowners to keep forest began in 2010.

| Year | Forest cover (%) | Land in payment scheme (km²) | Cattle (thousands) | Tourist arrivals (thousands) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 48 | 0 | 120 | 15 |
| 2010 | 39 | 20 | 135 | 40 |
| 2020 | 46 | 310 | 80 | 95 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in cattle numbers between 2010 and 2020. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the changes in forest cover between 2000 and 2020. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why it is difficult to judge how far the payment scheme caused the change in forest cover. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why community-based management may succeed where strictly protected areas fail. **[4]**

**6.** Explain **two** problems of managing livestock grazing sustainably in savanna areas. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** For a rainforest or savanna area you have studied, explain the main threats to it. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how the physical characteristics of rainforest or savanna ecosystems make sustainable management difficult. **[5]**

**9.** Assess the effectiveness of certification schemes, such as those for timber or palm oil, in reducing damage to tropical ecosystems. **[5]**

**10.** "Sustainable management of tropical environments succeeds only when conservation is more profitable than exploitation." With reference to a case study of either the rainforest or the savanna ecosystem, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Using an area or resource to meet present needs [1] without reducing its ability to meet the needs of future generations, environmentally, economically and socially [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Leakage happens when protecting one area simply moves damage, such as clearance, to another area [1], so the total loss is not reduced, for example when farmers excluded from a reserve clear forest beyond its boundary [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Overgrazing by livestock [1], which removes grass cover and exposes soil to erosion [1]. Poaching of wildlife [1], which reduces populations of species such as elephants and rhinos and the tourism income they bring [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit fuelwood and charcoal collection, crop conversion, fencing or changed fire regimes, each with its effect.

**4. (a)** (80 − 135) ÷ 135 × 100 [1] = −40.7%, a decrease of about 41% [1].

**(b)** Forest cover fell from 48% in 2000 to 39% in 2010 [1], then recovered to 46% by 2020, still below the 2000 level [1].

**(c)** Cattle numbers fell by about 41% at the same time, so declining ranching may explain the recovery [1]. Tourist arrivals more than doubled, so tourism income may have encouraged forest protection [1]. The data do not show whether land in the scheme was ever at risk of clearance, so the scheme's own effect cannot be separated [1]. **[7]**

**5.** Local people gain income or other benefits from conservation, so they have a reason to protect the area [1]. They have local knowledge of land and wildlife [1]. Strict protection can exclude people from land they depend on, causing conflict and illegal use [1]. Community schemes reduce enforcement costs, because residents monitor the land themselves [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Rainfall varies from year to year [1], so a herd that is sustainable in a wet year overgrazes the land in a drought [1]. Livestock are a store of wealth and security for herders [1], so households keep large herds even when grass is scarce, degrading the land [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit loss of mobility through fencing or land subdivision, which concentrates grazing.

**7.** Credit: a named, located area within one ecosystem [1]; a main threat with its cause, such as ranching driven by global beef demand [1]; a second threat with its cause [1]; the scale or rate of the threat, with data [1]; the groups responsible and why the activity is attractive to them [1]; a link between threats, such as roads opening land to settlers [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Rainforest nutrients are held mostly in the biomass, so clearance removes the main store [1]. Heavy rain on bare soil leaches nutrients and causes erosion, so farmland loses fertility quickly [1]. Secondary forest takes decades to recover its structure [1]. Savanna rainfall is variable, so safe stocking levels change from year to year [1]. Savanna depends on a balance of fire and grazing, so changes in either degrade it [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Certification uses consumer demand to reward producers who meet standards, such as no clearance of primary forest [1]. It has encouraged some large companies to change practices [1]. However, it covers only part of the market, as many buyers do not demand certified products [1]. Monitoring in remote areas is difficult and costly, so standards may not always be enforced [1]. Overall it is useful but limited, because uncertified production can still expand elsewhere [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* names the case study area and ecosystem [1]; defines exploitation and sustainable management [1].

*Agree:* where people gain income from conservation, they protect land: payments to landowners give standing forest a value [1]. Ecotourism and conservancy lease fees give savanna wildlife an economic value [1]. Community wildlife schemes made wildlife an asset rather than a pest [1]. Where clearance pays more, as with ranching or soy, laws alone are often broken [1].

*Disagree:* laws and enforcement can cut clearance even when it is profitable [1], for example through satellite monitoring backed by fines [1]. Land rights and secure tenure matter as well as profit [1]. Profit-based schemes are vulnerable to market shocks, such as the collapse of tourism during the pandemic [1]. Some values, such as biodiversity and indigenous culture, are not captured by profit [1]. Payments can reward land that was never at risk, so profit does not always change behaviour [1].

*Evaluation of evidence:* a named scheme where profit-based incentives worked, with evidence [1]; a named scheme where they failed or were limited [1]; recognition that other factors, such as economic change, also explain outcomes [1]. Who benefits from profits: elites or councils may capture them [1].

*Use of detail:* dates and figures from the case study [1].

*Judgement:* profitability is necessary but not sufficient [1]; it works best when combined with law, secure rights and stable markets [1]; a clear final view on "only" [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* top answers test the word "only", showing cases where law or rights mattered as much as profit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating percentage change from the wrong base year.
- Assuming that a scheme caused every improvement in the data.
- Writing about rainforest and savanna together in a case study answer.
- Threats with no causes or no named area.
- Essays that describe schemes without evidence of results.
