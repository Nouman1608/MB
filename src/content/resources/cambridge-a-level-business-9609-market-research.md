This guide goes deeper on **3.2 Market research**, part of AS Level topic 3 (Marketing) in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. The syllabus content is:

- **3.2.1 The purposes of market research**: identifying the main features of a market (size, growth, competitors) and customer and consumer characteristics, profiles, wants and needs.
- **3.2.2 Primary research and secondary research**: the distinction and main features of each; the usefulness of data from primary methods and from secondary sources.
- **3.2.3 Sampling**: the need for and limitations of sampling.
- **3.2.4 Market research data**: the reliability of the data collected; analysis of quantitative and qualitative data; interpretation of information presented in tables, charts and graphs.

The [combined guide to the nature of marketing and market research](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-the-nature-of-marketing-and-market-research/) defines these terms. This page concentrates on choosing research methods for a given business, spotting weaknesses in data, and handling the numerical and graphical information that appears in case studies. Revise with the [Market research revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-market-research-revision-notes/) and practise with the [Market research practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-market-research-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Customers and consumers

3.2.1 mentions both **customers** and **consumers**. The customer buys the product; the consumer uses it. They are often the same person, but not always: a parent buys cereal that a child eats, and a company buys software its staff use. Research may need to reach both, because the customer decides whether to buy while the consumer's experience decides whether the purchase is repeated.

## A research plan in five steps

1. **Set the objective**: what decision does the research support? ("Should we open on Sunday evenings?")
2. **Check secondary data first**: it is cheaper and may answer part of the question.
3. **Choose primary methods** for what remains.
4. **Decide the sample**: who, how many, and how they will be reached.
5. **Analyse and act**: summarise the data and judge how far it can be trusted before deciding.

Starting with secondary research is not a rule, but it is usually sensible: there is no point paying for a survey to find the size of a market if a trade association already publishes it.

## Choosing primary methods

| Method | Best for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire (online, phone, face to face) | Quantitative data from many people | Shallow answers; low response rates online |
| Interview | Detailed reasons from a few people | Expensive per person; interviewer can influence answers |
| Focus group | Reactions to ideas, packaging, adverts | Small and unrepresentative; one strong voice can dominate |
| Observation | What people actually do, such as which shelves they visit | Shows behaviour, not reasons |
| Test marketing | Real sales in a limited area before a full launch | Costly; rivals see the product early |

**Matching method to the case.** A start-up café with little money might observe passing trade at different times and run a short face-to-face questionnaire. A food manufacturer launching a new flavour might use focus groups to refine it and a test market to measure real sales.

### Question design

Poor questions produce unreliable data even with a perfect sample.

- **Closed questions** (tick boxes, rating scales) give quantitative data that is easy to analyse but may miss reasons.
- **Open questions** ("What would make you visit more often?") give qualitative data that explains behaviour but is hard to summarise.
- **Leading questions** push the respondent towards an answer: "Don't you agree our new menu is better value?" A neutral version is: "How would you rate the value of the new menu, from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent)?"
- **Ambiguous questions** mean different things to different people: "Do you eat out regularly?" is better as "How many times did you eat out in the last four weeks?"

## Usefulness of secondary sources

Secondary data can be **internal** (sales records, loyalty-card data, customer complaints) or **external** (government statistics, trade association reports, published company accounts, newspapers, commercial research reports).

When judging usefulness, ask:

- **Is it relevant?** National figures may not describe a small local market.
- **Is it recent?** Old data may miss new trends.
- **Who produced it, and why?** A trade body promoting its industry may present optimistic figures.
- **What did it cost?** Commercial reports can be expensive, but still cheaper than collecting the same data.

## Sampling: why and what goes wrong

A sample is needed because surveying a whole population is too costly and slow. The limitations come from the fact that a sample is only part of the population:

- **Unrepresentative samples**: surveying customers inside a shop misses people who never visit, often the very people the business wants to attract.
- **Small samples**: results can swing widely by chance. If 6 out of 10 people say they would buy, that is far less convincing than 600 out of 1 000.
- **Non-response**: people who reply may differ from those who do not; those with strong opinions are more likely to respond.
- **Cost trade-off**: a larger, better-chosen sample is more reliable but costs more and takes longer.

**Worked example.** Selby Gyms emails 2 000 members and receives 260 replies.

- Response rate = 260 ÷ 2 000 × 100 = 13%.
- 87% did not reply. If the least satisfied members ignored the email, satisfaction will be overstated.
- The sample also excludes non-members, so it cannot show why people choose rival gyms.

## Reliability: sources of bias

| Bias | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sample bias | Wrong people asked | Surveying only daytime shoppers about late opening |
| Question bias | Leading or unclear questions | "Would you like lower prices?" |
| Interviewer bias | Interviewer's manner influences answers | Respondents try to please a friendly interviewer |
| Respondent bias | People say what sounds good | Overstating how often they would buy healthy options |
| Out-of-date data | Data collected before a change | A survey taken before a new competitor opened |

## Analysing quantitative data

**Worked example.** Ardley Ices asks 400 people which new flavour they would buy.

| Flavour | Number |
|---|---|
| Salted caramel | 148 |
| Mango | 96 |
| Pistachio | 84 |
| Blackberry | 72 |

- Salted caramel: 148 ÷ 400 = 37%; mango 24%; pistachio 21%; blackberry 18%.
- Salted caramel leads by 13 percentage points over mango.
- The mean number choosing each flavour is 400 ÷ 4 = 100; only salted caramel is above it.

Before recommending, ask: who were the 400? If they were all visitors to a summer festival, they may be younger than Ardley's usual customers.

## Analysing qualitative data

Qualitative data explains *why*. To analyse it, group comments into themes and count how often each appears. If 30 of 50 open comments about a hotel mention noise, the business has a clear priority, and the comments may suggest a solution ("rooms facing the road are noisy"). The limitation is that the themes depend on the researcher's judgement.

## Interpreting tables, charts and graphs

- **Tables**: calculate totals, percentages or changes; do not simply copy figures.
- **Bar charts**: compare categories; check the scale starts at zero, or small differences may look large.
- **Line graphs**: show trends over time; identify the direction, the rate of change and any turning point.
- **Pie charts**: show shares of a whole; check the total they represent, since a larger share of a smaller market may be worth less.

A strong answer quotes a figure, manipulates it (a change or a percentage), and then says what it means for the decision.

## Is the research worth the cost?

**Worked example.** Kelso Furniture plans a new sofa range. Development and launch will cost $120 000, and the range is expected to earn $45 000 contribution a year. Research costing $15 000 would test designs and prices with customers first.

- Research adds 15 000 ÷ 120 000 × 100 = 12.5% to the launch cost.
- Without research, a range that sells poorly could lose most of the $120 000.
- With research, Kelso can change the design or price before committing, or cancel early.

The research is worth more when the launch is large, the market is unfamiliar and the cost of failure is high. It is worth less when the change is small, the firm already knows its customers well, or a delay would let a rival launch first.

## Common mistakes

- Recommending expensive primary research to a business with little money without justification.
- Treating survey results as certain without checking the sample.
- Confusing reliability (can it be trusted?) with usefulness (does it help this decision?).
- Copying figures from a table without interpreting them.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Identify**: name a method or source.
- **Explain**: give a feature of the method and why it suits or does not suit the business.
- **Analyse**: link the data to a decision, such as which segment to target.
- **Evaluate** or **Assess**: judge how reliable and useful the data is, and whether it justifies the decision.
- **Advise**: recommend a research approach for the business, considering its budget and its objective.
