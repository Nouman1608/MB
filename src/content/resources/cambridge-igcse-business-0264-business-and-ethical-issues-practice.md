## Questions

### Section A: short answer

**1.** Define the term **ethical decision**. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two ethical issues which may affect businesses. **[2]**

**3.** Outline the difference between a legal requirement and an ethical choice. **[2]**

**4.** State two advantages to a business of being ethical. **[2]**

**5.** Pinewood Chocolates agrees to pay cocoa growers $0.60 more per kilogram. It buys 15,000 kg a year. Calculate the extra yearly cost. **[2]**

**6.** Identify the ethical issue in each example.

**(a)** A factory pays its workers so little that they cannot afford rent and food. **[1]**

**(b)** A supermarket forces small farms to accept prices below their costs. **[1]**

**(c)** A furniture retailer buys timber from a company that illegally clears rainforest. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Corrie Clothing discovers that one of its suppliers employs children aged 12 in its factory.

**(a)** Explain two ways Corrie Clothing could respond. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why ending the contract with the supplier immediately may not be the most ethical response. **[2]**

**8.** Hendry Supermarkets buys 400 tonnes of potatoes a year from local farmers at $180 a tonne. The farmers say this does not cover their costs and ask for $210 a tonne. Hendry's customers mainly shop there because of its low prices.

**(a)** Calculate the extra yearly cost to Hendry Supermarkets of paying $210 a tonne. **[2]**

**(b)** Consider whether Hendry Supermarkets should agree to pay the higher price. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**9.** Explain two disadvantages to a business of being ethical. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how paying fair wages could benefit a hotel. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why a business might choose a supplier that does not damage the environment, even though it charges more. **[3]**

**12.** Explain how an ethical decision could create a conflict between two stakeholder groups. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A decision based on what is morally right [1], even when a cheaper or more profitable option is available [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: child labour; paying fair wages to employees; paying fair prices to suppliers; using suppliers who do not damage the environment. One mark for each of two different issues [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A legal requirement must be obeyed, and breaking it can lead to fines or legal action [1], whereas an ethical choice goes beyond what the law requires and is made voluntarily [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A better reputation that may increase sales [1]; more motivated employees who stay longer [1]. Also accept: loyal customers; reliable suppliers; less risk of boycotts; possibly higher prices. **[2]**

**5.** 0.60 x 15,000 [1] = **$9,000** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** **(a)** Paying fair wages to employees (unfair wages) [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Paying fair prices to suppliers [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Using suppliers who do not damage the environment [1]. **[1]**

**7.** **(a)** Switch to a supplier that does not use child labour [1], which ends Corrie's link with the practice quickly and protects its reputation, though it may cost more [1]. Require the current supplier to stop employing children by a set date and inspect it regularly [1], which keeps the supply and may improve conditions, but Corrie must pay for inspections and the supplier may not change [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** The children may lose their income, which their families may depend on [1], and may move to other work that is even more dangerous, so their situation may not improve [1]. **[2]**

**8.** **(a)** Extra cost per tonne = 210 - 180 = $30 [1]; 30 x 400 = **$12,000** a year [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** For paying: the farmers need a price that covers their costs, otherwise they may go out of business and Hendry loses a local supplier [1]; Hendry could promote the fact that it pays local farmers fairly, which may improve its reputation [1]. Against paying: $12,000 a year is a permanent extra cost that reduces profit unless prices rise [1]; Hendry's customers shop there for low prices, so even a small price rise could send some to rival supermarkets [1]. Judgement: Hendry should agree to pay the higher price [1], because $12,000 spread over 400 tonnes of potatoes adds only 3 cents per kilogram, a rise customers are unlikely to notice, while losing reliable local supply would be a larger problem [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Converting the extra cost into cost per kilogram ($30 per tonne is $0.03 per kg) is the kind of analysis that supports a convincing judgement.

**9.** Being ethical often raises costs, for example higher wages or supplier prices [1], which reduces profit unless the business raises its prices [1]. Competitors that are less ethical may keep their costs and prices lower [1], so the business may lose price-sensitive customers to them [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Staff who are paid fairly are likely to be better motivated [1], so they give better service to guests, which can lead to good reviews and repeat bookings [1]. They are also more likely to stay, which reduces the hotel's recruitment and training costs [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Customers, the media or pressure groups may criticise a business whose supplier pollutes [1]. Choosing a cleaner supplier protects the business's reputation and may attract customers who care about the environment [1], which can increase sales by more than the extra cost of the supplier [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Paying employees more than the legal minimum helps employees, who receive higher pay [1]. But it raises the business's costs and may reduce profit [1], so owners or shareholders may receive less, creating a conflict between employees and owners [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a business must be ethical because "it is the law".
- Writing "good for image" without explaining the effect on sales or loyalty.
- Treating the extra cost of being ethical as a one-off.
- Not naming which stakeholders gain and lose.
- Ignoring the type of customers in the case when judging a decision.
