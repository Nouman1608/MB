**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 9, Europe and the Second World War (1918 to 1949), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option examined on HL Paper 3, where you write two essays from different regional studies. The guide has six inquiry topics: successes and challenges of the League of Nations in Europe; reasons for the Second World War in Europe; reasons for Axis defeat and Allied victory; the impact of the war on **one European country** (to 1949); reasons for the persecution and mass murder of marginalized groups in Nazi-occupied Europe; and responses to it. This guide uses France as the country case.

## The League of Nations in Europe

*Aims, organization and structure.* The League was created in 1919 to keep peace through collective security, disarmament and arbitration. It had an Assembly of all members, a Council with permanent and elected members, and a Secretariat. Decisions generally needed unanimity. The USA never joined, and Germany and the USSR were absent for years (Germany joined in 1926; the USSR in 1934). It had no army and depended on members to apply sanctions.

*Disputes in the 1920s.* The League had some successes. In the **Åland Islands** dispute (1921) between Sweden and Finland, it awarded the islands to Finland with guarantees for the Swedish-speaking population. In **Vilna** (1920 to 1923), it failed to reverse Poland's seizure of the city from Lithuania. In **Corfu** (1923), after the murder of an Italian general on Greek soil, Mussolini bombarded and occupied the island; the matter was settled by the Conference of Ambassadors, which favoured Italy, and the League looked weak against a great power.

*Collective security and disarmament.* Locarno (1925) was a success for diplomacy outside the League, and the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) outlawed war on paper. The Disarmament Conference (1932 to 1934) failed when Germany demanded equality and left the League in 1933.

*Crises in the 1930s.* The Depression, and the failure to act over Manchuria in 1931, damaged confidence. In 1935 Italy invaded Abyssinia. The League condemned Italy and imposed sanctions, but they excluded oil, and Britain and France, fearing to push Mussolini toward Hitler, offered Italy a deal in the Hoare-Laval Pact. Sanctions were dropped in 1936. After this the League was seen as ineffective, though historians note it did useful work in health, refugees and labour standards.

## Reasons for the Second World War in Europe

*Italian and German foreign policies.* Mussolini sought an empire: Abyssinia (1935 to 1936), involvement in Spain, Albania (1939), then the Axis with Germany (1936) and the Pact of Steel (1939). Hitler aimed to overturn Versailles, unite German speakers and win *Lebensraum*, living space, in the east. He left the League (1933), introduced conscription (1935), remilitarized the Rhineland (March 1936), achieved the **Anschluss** with Austria (March 1938), took the Sudetenland after Munich (1938) and the rest of Czechoslovakia (March 1939). Historians debate whether Hitler had a fixed plan (a view associated with Trevor-Roper) or took opportunities as they came (A.J.P. Taylor's argument).

*Appeasement, Chamberlain and Munich.* **Appeasement** meant giving way to demands to avoid war. Reasons: fear of another war and its costs, Depression, belief Versailles was harsh, weakness of the armed forces and the empire, anticommunism and public opinion. **Neville Chamberlain** flew to Germany three times in September 1938. At Munich (29 to 30 September) Britain, France, Italy and Germany agreed that Germany would take the Sudetenland; Czechoslovakia was not invited. Critics, led by Churchill, said it encouraged Hitler; defenders say it bought time for rearmament. A fair judgement considers what was known in 1938.

*British, French and Soviet policies.* Britain and France were slow to rearm and divided; France relied on the Maginot Line and followed British lead. The USSR proposed collective security under Litvinov but was excluded from Munich and distrusted by Poland and the western powers. Stalin, who had purged his army, was also wary.

*Nazi-Soviet Pact and Poland.* In March 1939 Britain and France guaranteed Poland. On 23 August 1939 Germany and the USSR signed a non-aggression pact, with a secret protocol dividing eastern Europe. Germany invaded Poland on 1 September; Britain and France declared war on 3 September. The USSR invaded eastern Poland on 17 September.

## Axis defeat and Allied victory

*Political leadership.* The Grand Alliance of Britain, the USA and the USSR was formed after Germany invaded the USSR (June 1941) and Japan attacked Pearl Harbor (December 1941). Leaders **Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin** met at Tehran (1943), Yalta (February 1945) and Potsdam (July 1945). They agreed to demand unconditional surrender. The alliance had tensions, but common aims held until the victory. Hitler, by contrast, took close control of strategy and often ignored advice. Italy and Germany coordinated poorly.

*Economic factors.* Allied economic power was greater. The USA's industry produced vast quantities of weapons, and Lend-Lease supplied Britain and the USSR. Soviet industry was moved east and expanded. Germany's economy was only fully mobilised from 1942 under Speer, and it lacked oil. Allied bombing and the Atlantic blockade caused disruption.

*Strategic and military factors.* Germany failed to defeat Britain in 1940. Operation Barbarossa (June 1941) opened a huge eastern front, where the Red Army halted the Germans at Moscow, then won at **Stalingrad** (1942 to 1943) and Kursk (1943). The Allies drove the Axis from North Africa (El Alamein, 1942), invaded Italy (1943), and landed in Normandy on 6 June 1944. Mussolini was removed in July 1943. The Soviets advanced from the east, and Germany surrendered on 8 May 1945.

*Strengths and weaknesses.* Axis strengths early on were speed, tactics and surprise (blitzkrieg). Weaknesses were overstretch, limited resources and fighting a long war against a stronger coalition. Allied weaknesses early included poor preparation; strengths were resources, manpower and unity.

## Impact of the war on one country: France

*Social and economic impact.* France was defeated in six weeks in 1940. The armistice divided the country into an occupied north and a southern zone under Marshal **Pétain's** Vichy regime. The occupation drained resources through occupation costs and forced labour, and food shortages were severe. Liberation in 1944 left cities damaged and transport crippled.

*Political impact.* Vichy ended the republic, ruled authoritarian and collaborated, while **de Gaulle** led the Free French from London, and the Resistance grew, with the National Council of the Resistance formed in 1943. After liberation, the provisional government purged some collaborators. A new constitution founded the Fourth Republic in 1946. Postwar governments nationalised key industries, created social security and launched the Monnet Plan for reconstruction; Marshall Plan aid followed in 1948.

*Experiences of women.* Women led households through shortages, joined the Resistance, and some were punished as "horizontal collaborators", having their heads shaved after liberation. French women were granted the vote in 1944 and first voted in 1945.

*Marginalized groups.* Vichy introduced its own anti-Jewish laws in 1940 and 1941. About 75,000 Jews were deported from France, most to Auschwitz, including after the Vel d'Hiv round-up in Paris in July 1942, carried out by French police. Foreign Jews were hit first. Roma and others also suffered.

## Reasons for the persecution and mass murder of marginalized groups

*Long-term factors and ideology.* Centuries of religious antisemitism were reshaped in the nineteenth century into racial antisemitism. Nazi ideology saw Jews as a racial enemy, and Hitler's *Mein Kampf* spelt this out. Nazi racial thinking also targeted Roma, disabled people ("lives unworthy of life"), Slavs, homosexuals and political opponents. Persecution grew in stages: boycotts, the Nuremberg Laws (1935), Kristallnacht (1938).

*Military factors.* The invasion of Poland (1939) put millions of Jews under German control and led to ghettos. The invasion of the USSR (June 1941) brought mass shootings by the **Einsatzgruppen**, such as at Babi Yar near Kyiv in September 1941.

*Political factors.* In July 1941 Göring ordered Heydrich to prepare a "total solution". The **Wannsee Conference** (20 January 1942) coordinated the administration of the "Final Solution". Extermination camps such as Treblinka, Sobibor, Belzec and Auschwitz-Birkenau were built. About six million Jews were murdered. Historians debate when and how the decision to kill all Jews was made: the "intentionalist" view stresses Hitler's plan, while "functionalists" stress improvisation under war pressure.

*Collaboration.* Many individuals and groups took part or helped: German police and army units, local police and officials in occupied territories, and governments such as Vichy, Croatia and Hungary. Browning's study of Police Battalion 101 argued ordinary men became killers through conformity, while Goldhagen argued for a widespread "eliminationist" antisemitism. The two views remain debated.

## Responses to persecution and mass murder

*Resistance by individuals.* Individuals hid and rescued Jews at great risk, such as Irena Sendler in Warsaw and the villagers of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon in France. Raoul Wallenberg issued protective passes in Budapest in 1944.

*Collective protest and resistance.* The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (April to May 1943) was the most famous armed revolt. Prisoners revolted at Sobibor in 1943. Jewish partisans fought in forests. Denmark helped most of its Jewish population escape to Sweden in October 1943. Solidarity was exceptional; fear, hunger and German terror made resistance dangerous.

*International response.* The Evian Conference (1938) failed to open doors to refugees. The Allies knew by 1942, and the declaration of December 1942 condemned the murders, but they focused on military victory. The US War Refugee Board (1944) saved some lives. The Allies did not bomb the camps or rail lines to Auschwitz, a decision still debated.

*Legal responses.* The **Nuremberg Trials** of leading Nazis (1945 to 1946) were held under the London Charter, with charges including crimes against humanity. Of the 22 tried, 12 were sentenced to death. Later trials tried doctors, judges and others. The Genocide Convention (1948) followed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Must I use France?**
No. Choose one European country and use it throughout.

**Was appeasement a mistake?**
Historians disagree. Judge it by what leaders knew, what alternatives existed and what followed.
