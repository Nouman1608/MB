All countries, firms and figures are invented. ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of the following is an example of capital as a factor of production?

A: a forest

B: a bakery's oven

C: a shop assistant

D: a bank deposit

**[1]**

**2.** What is meant by enterprise? **[2]**

**3.** State the sector of the economy to which each activity belongs: (i) a copper mine, (ii) a car assembly plant, (iii) an insurance company. **[3]**

**4.** What is meant by land as a factor of production? **[2]**

**5.** A construction firm builds houses. State which sector of the economy it belongs to. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Kofi runs a tea plantation. He owns 200 hectares of hillside, employs 85 pickers, owns three tractors and a drying shed, and took out a loan to expand the business.

**(a)** Identify **one** example of each factor of production in Kofi's business. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why the loan is not itself a factor of production. **[2]**

**7.** The table shows invented shares of employment in Country G.

| Sector | Year 1 | Year 25 |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 62% | 38% |
| Secondary | 14% | 27% |
| Tertiary | 24% | 35% |

**(a)** Calculate the change in the primary sector's share of employment, in percentage points. **[1]**

**(b)** Total employment rose from 15 million in Year 1 to 24 million in Year 25. Calculate the number of secondary-sector workers in each year. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in the number of secondary-sector workers. **[2]**

**(d)** State whether Country G is more likely to be a developing or a developed economy, giving **one** reason from the data. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **one** reason why the tertiary sector tends to grow as an economy develops. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why a sector's share of employment may fall while its output rises. **[3]**

**10.** Explain **one** reason why the secondary sector's share of employment may fall in a developed economy. **[3]**

**11.** Using a product of your choice, explain how it passes through the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors. **[3]**

**12.** Assess whether economies always move from the primary sector to the secondary sector and then to the tertiary sector as they develop. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A forest is land, a shop assistant is labour and a bank deposit is money, not capital.

**2.** Organising the other factors of production [1]; and taking the risk of the business failing (or making decisions) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** (i) Primary [1]; (ii) secondary [1]; (iii) tertiary [1]. **[3]**

**4.** All natural resources, such as soil, minerals or water [1]; used in the production of goods and services [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Secondary [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Land: the 200 hectares of hillside [1]; labour: the 85 pickers [1]; capital: the tractors or drying shed [1]; enterprise: Kofi, who organises the business and takes the risk [1].

**(b)** Money is used to buy factors such as capital [1]; it does not itself produce goods or services [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** 62 − 38 = **24 percentage points** fall [1].

**(b)** Year 1: 0.14 × 15 = **2.1 million** [1]; Year 25: 0.27 × 24 = **6.48 million** [1].

**(c)** (6.48 − 2.1) ÷ 2.1 × 100 [1] = **208.6%** [1].

**(d)** Developing (industrialising) [1]; the primary sector still employs the largest share, though it is falling while the secondary sector grows [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), "24%" without "percentage points" is a common slip; the units show the examiner you understand the difference.

**8.** As incomes rise, households spend a larger share on services such as tourism, entertainment and healthcare [1]; so demand for services increases [1]; so firms in the tertiary sector expand and employ more workers [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Machinery and better methods raise output per worker [1]; so the sector can produce more with fewer workers [1]; for example, mechanised farms grow more food while workers move to factories and services [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Factories replace workers with machines [1]; so fewer workers are needed for the same output [1]; and some manufacturing moves to countries with lower costs, reducing factory jobs at home [1]. **[3]**

**11.** For example, cotton: grown and picked on farms (primary) [1]; spun, woven and sewn into shirts in factories (secondary) [1]; transported and sold in clothes shops (tertiary) [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Supporting the pattern: as incomes rise, demand moves from food to manufactured goods and then services [1]; farm mechanisation releases workers to factories [1]; many developed economies now have most workers in services [1]. Against: resource-rich countries may stay mainly primary in output for a long time [1]; some economies grow services such as tourism without a large manufacturing stage [1]. Judgement: the pattern is a useful tendency, not a rule, and depends on resources and trade [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating money as capital.
- Calling a salaried worker the entrepreneur.
- Writing "%" for a change in percentage share.
- Forgetting to multiply shares by total employment to get numbers.
- Assessing structural change with no exception or judgement.
