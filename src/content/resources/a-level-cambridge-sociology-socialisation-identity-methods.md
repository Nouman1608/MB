This guide covers **Paper 1 Socialisation, Identity and Methods of
Research**, one of two AS Level papers in Cambridge International AS
& A Level Sociology (9699). This topic area has two central aims:
examining the role of socialisation in creating social identities, and
introducing the research methods and concepts used to evaluate
sociological findings. These two aims are deliberately paired within
a single paper, since the methods content is needed to properly
evaluate the evidence base behind every claim about socialisation and
identity made elsewhere in the syllabus.

## Where this fits in 9699

Paper 1 establishes the theoretical and methodological foundations
that the rest of the qualification builds on -- Paper 2 (The Family)
at AS Level, and Papers 3-4 (Education, Globalisation, Media and
Religion) at A Level all draw on the concepts and research methods
introduced here. This means Paper 1's content is examined not only in
its own right but implicitly through every later paper, since a
candidate who cannot apply the positivist/interpretivist distinction
or the concept of socialisation confidently will find it resurfacing as
a weakness in Family, Education and every other later topic.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL SOCIOLOGY (9699) — PAPER 1
SOCIALISATION, IDENTITY AND METHODS OF RESEARCH**

- 1.1 The process of learning and socialisation — culture, roles,
norms, values and status in the social construction of reality; the
nurture versus nature debate; and agencies of socialisation and social
control (family, education, peer group, media, religion)
- 1.2 Social control, conformity and resistance — structure and
agency in shaping the individual-society relationship; factors
explaining conformity; mechanisms maintaining social order; and
explanations of deviance and non-conformity
- 1.3 Social identity and change — social class, gender, ethnicity and
age as elements of social identity, and how these identities change
due to globalisation and new or hybrid identities
- 2.1 Types of data, methods and research design — primary and
secondary, quantitative and qualitative data; the strengths and
limitations of different research methods; and the stages of research
design
- 2.2 Approaches to sociological research — case studies, social
surveys, ethnography, mixed methods, and the positivist and
interpretivist approaches
- 2.3 Research issues — the practical, ethical and theoretical issues
that arise in conducting sociological research

## How to approach it

Socialisation and identity (1.1-1.3) is best revised by tracing one
continuous argument -- how socialisation shapes identity, how
conformity and resistance are explained, and how identity then changes
over time -- rather than treating the three sub-topics as separate.
The structure/agency debate that runs through 1.2 in particular is
worth practising applying to specific examples, since it is a recurring
theoretical lens across the wider sociology syllabus, not a topic
confined only to Paper 1.
Methods of research (2.1-2.3) rewards precise, method-specific
knowledge of strengths and limitations rather than general statements,
since exam questions typically ask students to evaluate a named method
in a specific research context — a generic "strengths and weaknesses of
interviews" answer scores lower than one that explains why an
unstructured interview specifically suits a study of a sensitive or
hard-to-access topic. Because the positivist and
interpretivist approaches (2.2) recur as a framing device throughout
the qualification, get comfortable applying this distinction to new
research scenarios, not just defining the two approaches, since it is
this applied skill — not the definitions alone — that Cambridge's mark
schemes reward most heavily.

## The socialisation-to-identity argument, traced through

1.1 establishes that identity is not innate but constructed through socialisation — the family,
education, peer group, media and religion each transmit norms and values, sometimes reinforcing and
sometimes contradicting one another. 1.2 then asks how society maintains conformity to those norms
(formal and informal social control) and how it explains cases where conformity breaks down
(theories of deviance). 1.3 completes the argument by showing that the identities constructed
through 1.1's socialisation are not fixed: class, gender, ethnicity and age-based identities shift
over time, and globalisation in particular has generated new, hybrid identities that earlier
sociological theory did not originally account for. This forward-looking, change-focused framing of identity is deliberately what 1.3 adds beyond 1.1's more static account of how identity is initially formed.

Revising these three sub-topics as one
continuous chain — construction, maintenance, change — rather than three separate definitions
lists is what lets a candidate answer synoptic questions that span more than one sub-topic, which
Cambridge's Paper 1 questions frequently do rather than confining a question to a single numbered
sub-topic — a candidate revising 1.1-1.3 as isolated definitions lists is poorly placed to answer a
question that deliberately spans two or more of them.

## Applying positivism and interpretivism

The positivist approach favours quantitative, structured methods (social surveys, official
statistics) that aim for objectivity, reliability and the ability to generalise findings across a
population, reflecting positivism's roots in modelling sociology closely on the natural sciences. The interpretivist approach favours qualitative, less structured methods (unstructured
interviews, participant observation) that aim for validity — a rich, authentic understanding of
meaning from the research subjects' own perspective — even at some cost to reliability and
generalisability, reflecting interpretivism's view that human behaviour cannot be fully understood
through the same methods used to study the natural world. Exam questions consistently ask candidates to apply this framework to a specific,
sometimes unfamiliar research scenario, so practise identifying which approach a described method
sits closer to, and why, rather than only being able to define the two approaches in the abstract.
