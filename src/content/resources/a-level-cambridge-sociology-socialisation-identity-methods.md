This guide covers **Paper 1 Socialisation, Identity and Methods of
Research**, one of two AS Level papers in Cambridge International AS
& A Level Sociology (9699). This topic area has two central aims:
examining the role of socialisation in creating social identities, and
introducing the research methods and concepts used to evaluate
sociological findings.

## Where this fits in 9699

Paper 1 establishes the theoretical and methodological foundations
that the rest of the qualification builds on -- Paper 2 (The Family)
at AS Level, and Papers 3-4 (Education, Globalisation, Media and
Religion) at A Level all draw on the concepts and research methods
introduced here.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL SOCIOLOGY (9699) — PAPER 1
SOCIALISATION, IDENTITY AND METHODS OF RESEARCH**

- 1.1 The process of learning and socialisation — culture, roles,
norms, values and status in the social construction of reality; the
nurture versus nature debate; and agencies of socialisation and social
control (family, education, peer group, media, religion)
- 1.2 Social control, conformity and resistance — structure and
agency in shaping the individual-society relationship; factors
explaining conformity; mechanisms maintaining social order; and
explanations of deviance and non-conformity
- 1.3 Social identity and change — social class, gender, ethnicity and
age as elements of social identity, and how these identities change
due to globalisation and new or hybrid identities
- 2.1 Types of data, methods and research design — primary and
secondary, quantitative and qualitative data; the strengths and
limitations of different research methods; and the stages of research
design
- 2.2 Approaches to sociological research — case studies, social
surveys, ethnography, mixed methods, and the positivist and
interpretivist approaches
- 2.3 Research issues — the practical, ethical and theoretical issues
that arise in conducting sociological research

## How to approach it

Socialisation and identity (1.1-1.3) is best revised by tracing one
continuous argument -- how socialisation shapes identity, how
conformity and resistance are explained, and how identity then changes
over time -- rather than treating the three sub-topics as separate.
Methods of research (2.1-2.3) rewards precise, method-specific
knowledge of strengths and limitations rather than general statements,
since exam questions typically ask students to evaluate a named method
in a specific research context. Because the positivist and
interpretivist approaches (2.2) recur as a framing device throughout
the qualification, get comfortable applying this distinction to new
research scenarios, not just defining the two approaches.
