## The five syllabus points

1. Meaning of irrecoverable debts and irrecoverable debts recovered.
2. Journals and ledger accounts for irrecoverable debts.
3. Journals and ledger accounts for irrecoverable debts recovered.
4. The need for maintaining an allowance for irrecoverable debts.
5. Journals and ledger accounts for creating and adjusting the allowance.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Irrecoverable debt | An amount owed by a credit customer that the business has decided will not be paid |
| Write off | Remove the debt from the customer's account and charge it as an expense |
| Irrecoverable debt recovered | Cash later received for a debt written off in an earlier period |
| Reinstate | Put the written-off debt back on the customer's account before recording the cash |
| Allowance for irrecoverable debts | An estimate of the part of remaining trade receivables that may not be collected |
| Increase in allowance | Expense in the statement of profit or loss |
| Decrease in allowance | Income in the statement of profit or loss |
| Net trade receivables | Trade receivables less the allowance |

## Double entry at a glance

| Event | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Debt written off | Irrecoverable debts | Customer |
| Part payment in final settlement | Bank (cash received) and Irrecoverable debts (the rest) | Customer |
| Debt recovered: step 1 | Customer | Irrecoverable debts recovered |
| Debt recovered: step 2 | Bank | Customer |
| Allowance created or increased | Profit or loss | Allowance for irrecoverable debts |
| Allowance decreased | Allowance for irrecoverable debts | Profit or loss |
| Year end: irrecoverable debts | Profit or loss | Irrecoverable debts |
| Year end: debts recovered | Irrecoverable debts recovered | Profit or loss |

## The allowance in four steps

1. Write off known irrecoverable debts from trade receivables.
2. New allowance = percentage × remaining trade receivables.
3. Change = new allowance − existing allowance.
4. Positive change: expense. Negative change: income. In the statement of
   financial position, deduct the **whole new allowance** from trade receivables.

**Worked reminder.** Trade receivables 25,500 include a debt of 500 to be written
off. Allowance 2%. Existing allowance 420.

```
Remaining receivables   25,500 − 500       = 25,000
New allowance           2% × 25,000        =    500
Change                  500 − 420          =     80 increase (expense)
Statement of financial position: 25,000 − 500 = 24,500
Total charge to profit: 500 write-off + 80 = 580
```

## Why keep an allowance?

- **Prudence:** assets are not overstated; expected losses are recognised.
- **Matching:** the expected loss falls in the year of the credit sales.
- **Realistic figures:** net trade receivables show what the business expects
  to collect, so liquidity is judged on reliable numbers.

## Where things appear

| Item | Statement of profit or loss | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| Irrecoverable debts | Expense | Trade receivables already reduced |
| Irrecoverable debts recovered | Other income | Nothing at the year end (cash received) |
| Increase in allowance | Expense | Allowance deducted from receivables |
| Decrease in allowance | Other income | Allowance deducted from receivables |

## Exam traps

- **Write-offs never touch the allowance account** in 0452 answers: use the
  irrecoverable debts account.
- **Only the change** in the allowance goes to profit or loss; the full balance
  goes in the statement of financial position.
- **Write off before applying the percentage.**
- **A recovery is not revenue.** It is other income, not sales.
- **Reinstate first.** The customer's account must show the debt and then the
  payment.
- **Part payments.** Only the unpaid balance is irrecoverable.
- **A decrease in allowance** is not cash. Profit rises; bank does not.
- **Running balance format** is allowed. The allowance balance is always a credit.
- **Change in rate.** Apply the new rate to this year's receivables; do not
  recalculate earlier years.

## Self-test

1. Define an irrecoverable debt.
2. Give the journal entry to write off a debt of 260 owed by R Shah.
3. Why is a recovered debt first reinstated in the customer's account?
4. The allowance was 900. Receivables are 18,000 and the rate is 4%. Calculate
   the change and say whether it is an expense or income.
5. Where is an irrecoverable debt recovered shown in the statement of profit or loss?
6. State two reasons for maintaining an allowance.
7. A customer owing 1,500 pays 30% in final settlement. Calculate the irrecoverable debt.
8. A business creates an allowance for the first time: receivables 12,400, rate 5%.
   Give the journal entry.
9. Receivables 30,000; allowance 3%. Show the statement of financial position extract.
10. The allowance should have fallen by 200 but was not adjusted. State the effect
    on profit.
11. Does writing off an irrecoverable debt change total revenue?
12. Previous allowance 1,050. Receivables 40,000 before writing off 2,000. Rate 3%.
    Calculate the change.

### Answers

1. A debt owed by a credit customer that the business has decided will not be
   paid, so it is removed from receivables and charged as an expense.
2. Debit Irrecoverable debts 260; credit R Shah 260.
3. So the customer's record shows the debt was eventually paid, which helps when
   deciding whether to give credit again.
4. New allowance 720; change 720 − 900 = **180 decrease**, which is income.
5. As other income (added to gross profit).
6. Prudence (receivables not overstated) and matching (the expected loss is
   charged in the year of the sales).
7. Paid 450, so irrecoverable debt = **1,050**.
8. Debit Profit or loss 620; credit Allowance for irrecoverable debts 620.
9. Trade receivables 30,000, less allowance 900, gives **29,100**.
10. Profit is understated by 200.
11. No. Revenue was correctly recorded when the sale was made; the loss is an expense.
12. Remaining 38,000 × 3% = 1,140; change = 1,140 − 1,050 = **90 increase**.
