## What price is

**Price** is the amount a customer pays for a product. It is the only element of the mix that earns revenue directly, since the others create costs. Price also sends a message: a high price can signal quality, a low price can signal value or low quality. It interacts with costs, competitors, customers' income and the stage of the product's life.

## Pricing strategies

| Strategy | How it works | Suits | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Skimming** | High launch price, lowered later | New, innovative products with few rivals; recovers development costs quickly | Rivals enter; slow take-up |
| **Penetration** | Low launch price to win share fast | Competitive markets; where scale cuts costs | Low early profit; hard to raise price later |
| **Competition-based** | Price set by reference to rivals (match, undercut or price above) | Markets with similar products | Price war; ignores own costs |
| **Psychological** | Prices that influence perception: £9.99 not £10, prestige prices, price bundles | Consumer goods | Customers may feel misled; effect is uncertain |
| **Cost-plus (full-cost)** | Unit cost plus a mark-up | Where costs can be calculated; contracts | Ignores demand and rivals |
| **Marginal** | Price at or above the variable (marginal) cost only; used for extra output | Using spare capacity; clearing stock | Fixed costs not covered; may upset regular customers |
| **Contribution** | Price to give each unit a contribution to fixed costs and profit; products in a range may be priced with different contributions | Multi-product firms | Needs good cost data; must cover all fixed costs in total |

### Worked example 1: skimming against penetration

Orbis Tech launches a smart speaker. The variable cost is **£50** per unit. Management forecasts two pricing paths over two years.

| | Skimming | Penetration |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 price and sales | £120, 3,000 units | £60, 15,000 units |
| Year 1 contribution | (120 − 50) × 3,000 = **£210,000** | (60 − 50) × 15,000 = **£150,000** |
| Year 2 price and sales | £90, 9,000 units | £75, 14,000 units |
| Year 2 contribution | (90 − 50) × 9,000 = **£360,000** | (75 − 50) × 14,000 = **£350,000** |
| Two-year contribution | **£570,000** | **£500,000** |
| Two-year units | 12,000 | 29,000 |

**Interpretation.** Skimming earns £70,000 more contribution (570,000 − 500,000) and recovers development costs sooner. Penetration sells 29,000 units against 12,000, building a large customer base and market share that might support future sales of accessories and make it hard for rivals to enter. The better choice depends on the objective: profit and payback favour skimming; share and market dominance favour penetration. The figures are forecasts, so a lower response to the £60 launch price would remove its advantage.

### Worked example 2: cost-plus and marginal pricing

Brisk Bicycles makes a helmet. The variable cost is **£14** per unit, fixed overheads are **£180,000** a year and normal output is **12,000** helmets.

**Cost-plus (full-cost) price.**

1. Fixed cost per unit = £180,000 ÷ 12,000 = **£15**.
2. Full cost per unit = £14 + £15 = **£29**.
3. With a 40% mark-up, price = £29 × 1.40 = **£40.60**.
4. Contribution per helmet = £40.60 − £14 = **£26.60**.

**Marginal (contribution) pricing for a special order.** A retailer offers to buy a further **2,000** helmets at **£22** each. Brisk has spare capacity and the fixed costs are already covered by normal sales.

1. The offer is below the full cost of £29, but above the variable cost of £14.
2. Contribution per helmet = £22 − £14 = **£8**.
3. Extra contribution = 2,000 × £8 = **£16,000**.

**Evaluation.** Accepting adds £16,000 to profit if it uses spare capacity and does not lead regular customers to demand the lower price. If Brisk lacks spare capacity, or the order displaces full-price sales, it should refuse. Cost-plus is simple and ensures that costs are covered at normal output, but it ignores customers: if rivals charge £30, a price of £40.60 may sell nothing.

**Other strategies in brief.** *Competition-based* pricing suits a market with similar products, such as petrol, where a small price gap moves customers. *Psychological* pricing includes charm pricing: a price of £4.99 may feel closer to £4 than £5. A shop selling 10,000 items at £4.99 receives £49,900; if the price of £5.00 reduced sales to 9,000, revenue would be £45,000. This shows why firms try it, though the effect varies.

## Elasticities of demand

**Price elasticity of demand (PED)** measures how far quantity demanded responds to a change in price.

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

- If the number ignoring sign is **greater than 1**, demand is **elastic**: quantity falls by a larger percentage than price rises, so a price rise cuts revenue.
- If **less than 1**, demand is **inelastic**: a price rise increases revenue.
- If equal to 1, demand is **unit elastic**.

**Income elasticity of demand (YED)** = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in consumers' income. A positive value means a **normal good**; above 1 is often a luxury. A negative value means an **inferior good**.

**Cross elasticity of demand (XED)** = % change in quantity demanded of product A ÷ % change in the price of product B. A positive value means **substitutes**; a negative value means **complements**.

### Worked example 3: calculating and interpreting

**PED.** Brisk raises the price of a helmet from £10 to £11 and sales fall from 5,000 to 4,300.

1. % change in price = (11 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = **+10%**.
2. % change in quantity = (4,300 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **−14%**.
3. PED = −14 ÷ 10 = **−1.4**.

Demand is **elastic** (1.4 is greater than 1). Revenue before: £10 × 5,000 = £50,000. Revenue after: £11 × 4,300 = £47,300, a fall of £2,700. A price rise was the wrong move; a price cut would be expected to increase revenue.

**YED.** Consumers' incomes rise by 4% and demand for Brisk's premium helmet rises by 6%. YED = 6 ÷ 4 = **+1.5**: a normal good, and a luxury because the value is above 1. Demand for its budget helmet falls by 3.2% over the same period. YED = −3.2 ÷ 4 = **−0.8**: an inferior good. In a boom, Brisk should expand the premium range; in a recession the budget range may sell better.

**XED.** A rival raises its helmet price by 8% and Brisk's sales rise by 12%. XED = 12 ÷ 8 = **+1.5**, so the products are close substitutes. A helmet light sells 4.8% fewer units when helmet prices rise 8%: XED = −4.8 ÷ 8 = **−0.6**, so they are complements.

### Using elasticities

Uses: set prices that raise revenue; forecast how sales respond to a change; plan for the economic cycle (YED); anticipate rivals' pricing (XED); choose promotions for complementary goods. **Limits:** the figures come from past data and assume other factors stay the same; elasticity differs between groups and over time; and a calculation from a small price change may not apply to a big one.

## Importance of price

For the business, price decides revenue, margin and position against rivals. A small price change can change profit sharply: if the margin is 20%, a 5% price cut cuts the contribution per unit by about a quarter. For stakeholders: customers care about value; employees' jobs depend on sales; shareholders look at profit; suppliers may feel pressure to cut their own prices; competitors may react.

## Choosing a pricing mix

A **pricing mix** combines strategies for different products or stages. To recommend and justify one:

1. Use the objective (profit, share, survival).
2. Check costs (what price covers full cost and what is the floor?).
3. Check demand (is it elastic?) and rivals' prices.
4. Consider the product's life-cycle stage and the brand.
5. State the risk and how to review.

For example, Orbis might skim the speaker and use penetration pricing for its accessories to build a base, then lower the speaker price as rivals enter.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a PED without the sign, or confusing elastic and inelastic.
- Dividing quantities rather than percentage changes.
- Using cost-plus without checking what customers will pay.
- Rejecting a marginal-cost order because the price is below full cost, without checking spare capacity.
- Treating skimming as always better, or penetration as always cheap.

## Exam technique

Show the percentage change in each variable before dividing. Interpret every calculation in a sentence. For "evaluate", weigh benefits against risks using the objective and the market in the case.
