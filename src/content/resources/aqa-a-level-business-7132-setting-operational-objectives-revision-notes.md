Condensed notes on section **3.4.1 Setting operational objectives** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The full explanations and the Alderbank Furniture worked example are in the [operational objectives study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-operational-objectives/); exam-style questions are in the [operational objectives practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-operational-objectives-practice/).

## Specification content

- The value of setting operational objectives.
- Operational objectives include: costs; quality; speed of response; flexibility; environmental objectives; added value.
- Throughout section 3.4: technology (online business, links with customers and suppliers, inventory control, automation, robotics); ethical and environmental influences; market conditions and competition; competitiveness; links with other functions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Operations | Turning inputs into outputs that customers value |
| Operational objective | A specific target for how well operations performs |
| Unit cost | Average cost of producing one unit |
| Quality | Meeting or exceeding customers' expectations |
| Speed of response | How quickly the business meets orders or changes |
| Flexibility | Ability to change volume, product mix or specification quickly |
| Added value | Selling price minus cost of bought-in inputs |
| Order winner | A feature that makes customers choose the business |
| Order qualifier | A minimum standard customers expect |

## Why set operational objectives

- Link operations to **corporate strategy**.
- Provide **targets** that can be measured and improved.
- Guide decisions on capacity, technology, suppliers and staff.
- Improve **competitiveness** by excelling on what customers value.
- Help other functions (marketing promises, finance budgets, HR training).

**Limits:** objectives can conflict; poor information; focusing on one target (cost) can harm another (quality); markets change.

## The six objectives

| Objective | Aim | Measured by | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Costs | Lower unit or total cost | Unit cost, cost per order | Cutting corners harms quality |
| Quality | Meet or exceed expectations | Defect rate, returns, satisfaction | Costs money to achieve |
| Speed of response | Faster delivery or service | Lead time, delivery time | Needs spare capacity or stock |
| Flexibility | Change volume or specification quickly | Time to switch products | Fewer economies of scale |
| Environmental | Reduce harm: energy, waste, emissions | Waste %, carbon output | May raise short-run costs |
| Added value | Widen the gap between price and input cost | £ or % added value | Not the same as profit |

## Added value formulas

- **Added value** = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs
- **Added value (%)** = added value ÷ selling price × 100
- Increase it by raising the price customers will pay (brand, design, service) or cutting input costs.
- Added value must cover wages, rent and overheads before any profit remains.

## Trade-offs

| Clash | Why |
|---|---|
| Cost against quality | Better materials and checks cost more |
| Cost against speed | Fast delivery needs spare capacity |
| Cost against flexibility | Short varied runs cost more per unit |
| Speed against quality | Rushing causes errors |
| Cost against environment | Clean methods can cost more at first |

Decide the **order winners** (why customers choose you) and **order qualifiers** (minimum standards). A budget airline wins on cost and qualifies on punctuality; a luxury hotel wins on quality and qualifies on a fair price.

## Influences on the choice of objectives

Corporate strategy; competitors; customers' priorities; type of product (bespoke needs flexibility, mass needs low unit cost); resources; technology; ethical and environmental pressures.

## Worked example to remember

A chair sells for £120 and uses £48 of materials.

- Added value = £120 − £48 = **£72**.
- Added value % = 72 ÷ 120 × 100 = **60%**.
- If a better design lets the chair sell at £130 with the same materials: added value = £82 and 82 ÷ 130 × 100 = **63.1%**.
- Cutting the average unit cost from £86 to £80 is a fall of 6 ÷ 86 × 100 = **7.0%**.
- Cutting waste from 12% to 8% of £400,000 of materials saves £48,000 − £32,000 = **£16,000**.

## Exam traps

- Added value is **not** profit.
- Always name the objective that fits the business in the case.
- A cost objective does not automatically mean low quality, but the risk should be discussed.
- An objective needs a measure: "improve quality" alone is weak.
- Flexibility can reduce economies of scale.

## Self-test

1. Define "operational objective".
2. Give two reasons for setting operational objectives.
3. A product sells for £80 and uses £35 of bought-in materials. Calculate added value.
4. Calculate added value as a percentage of the selling price in question 3.
5. Give one way of raising added value without cutting input costs.
6. A firm's unit cost falls from £50 to £46. Calculate the percentage fall.
7. Give two measures of quality.
8. Why might a speed of response objective raise costs?
9. Give one reason a business might set an environmental objective.
10. What is the difference between an order winner and an order qualifier?
11. Give one conflict between operational objectives.
12. Why must marketing and operations coordinate on delivery promises?

### Answers

1. A specific target for how well the operations function performs, set within the corporate objectives.
2. Any two: link to strategy; measurable targets; guide decisions; improve competitiveness; coordinate functions.
3. £80 − £35 = **£45**.
4. 45 ÷ 80 × 100 = **56.25%** (accept 56.3%).
5. Any one: branding; better design; improved service; raising the price customers accept.
6. (50 − 46) ÷ 50 × 100 = **8%**.
7. Any two: defect rate; returns; complaints; customer satisfaction scores.
8. It may need spare capacity, extra stock or express transport.
9. Any one: legal duty; customer or investor pressure; saving costs from less waste; brand image.
10. An order winner is why customers choose the business; an order qualifier is a minimum standard needed to be considered.
11. Any one: cost against quality; cost against speed; cost against flexibility; speed against quality.
12. Marketing can promise only what operations can deliver; if a promise is broken, customers are lost and reputation is damaged.
