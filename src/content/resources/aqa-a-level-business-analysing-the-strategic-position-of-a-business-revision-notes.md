## 3.7.1 Mission, objectives and strategy

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mission | Purpose of the business: why it exists |
| Corporate objectives | Measurable goals for the whole business |
| Strategy | Long-term plan to reach the objectives |
| Tactics | Short-term actions that carry out the strategy |

- Chain: mission, then corporate objectives, then strategy, then tactics, then functional decisions.
- Influences on objectives: short termism, business ownership, internal environment, external environment.
- Strategy sets what each function must do; for example a low-cost strategy pushes operations to cut waste and marketing to stress price.
- SWOT: internal strengths and weaknesses, external opportunities and threats. Useful for organising evidence; weak because it is a list, subjective and dated. Always rank points and reach a choice.

## 3.7.2 Financial ratio analysis

| Ratio | Standard form | High or rising means |
|---|---|---|
| ROCE | operating profit / capital employed x 100 | Better return on funds used |
| Current ratio | current assets : current liabilities | Better liquidity (too high may mean idle assets) |
| Gearing | non-current liabilities / capital employed x 100 | More reliance on debt, more risk |
| Receivables days | receivables / revenue x 365 | Slower collection from customers |
| Payables days | payables / cost of sales x 365 | Longer taking credit from suppliers |
| Inventory turnover | cost of sales / inventories | Stock sells faster |

Capital employed = total equity + non-current liabilities. The specification names these ratios without formulas, so these are the standard forms.

**Worked reminder.** Norbrook Cafes (£000): operating profit 54; equity 300; non-current liabilities 100; current assets 150; current liabilities 120; revenue 600; cost of sales 360; receivables 50; payables 36; inventories 45.

- Capital employed 400, so ROCE = 54 / 400 x 100 = 13.5% and gearing = 100 / 400 x 100 = 25.0%.
- Current ratio = 150 / 120 = 1.25 : 1.
- Receivables days = 50 / 600 x 365 = 30.4; payables days = 36 / 360 x 365 = 36.5.
- Inventory turnover = 360 / 45 = 8.0 times.

**Method for a ratio question:** write the formula, substitute, give units (%, days, times), compare with last year or a rival, then say what it means and what else you would want to know.

**Value of ratios:** allow comparison over time and with other businesses; but backward-looking, depend on accounting choices, ignore non-financial factors, need a like-for-like benchmark.

## 3.7.3 Overall performance

- Non-financial data: operations (capacity utilisation, productivity, defects, delivery times), human resources (labour turnover, absenteeism, training), marketing (market share, satisfaction, retention). Analyse over time or against other businesses.
- Core competences: things done well that are hard to copy and valued by customers; source of competitive advantage.
- Short term (profit, cash) against long term (market position, skills, reputation). Cutting training lifts short-term profit and risks the long term.

| Model | Content | Value | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaplan and Norton's Balanced Scorecard | Financial; customer; internal business processes; learning and growth | Balances short and long term | Time to design; weighting measures |
| Elkington's Triple Bottom Line | Profit, People, Planet | Broader view of success | Social and environmental impact hard to measure |

## 3.7.4 Political and legal change

- Law (UK and EU, in general terms): competition, labour market, environment.
- Policy: enterprise, regulators, infrastructure, environment, international trade.
- Effect on strategy: where to operate, which products, whether to enter or leave a market. Effect on functions: labour costs, production methods, marketing rules, financing.
- Evaluation prompts: how big, how soon, how long to adapt, do rivals face the same rule, is there an opportunity as well as a threat?

## 3.7.5 Economic change

| Factor | Typical effect on business |
|---|---|
| GDP | Growth lifts demand, especially for luxuries |
| Taxation | Corporation tax cuts retained profit; income tax and VAT cut spending |
| Exchange rates | Stronger pound: exports dearer, imports cheaper. Weaker: the reverse |
| Inflation | Rising costs; pricing power matters |
| Fiscal and monetary policy | Taxes and spending; interest rates change borrowing cost and demand |
| Open trade v protectionism | Wider markets and rivals v shelter and higher import costs |

**Exchange rate reminder.** An export priced at €910 earns £700 at €1.30 per pound but only £650 at €1.40. A stronger pound lowers the sterling revenue, or forces a price rise that cuts sales.

- Data skill: state the change, size it, say who is affected, give implications for the UK, European Union or global market.
- Globalisation: reasons are cheaper transport and communication, freer trade, search for lower costs and bigger markets. Importance: markets, inputs, scale economies, but more rivals and currency risk.
- Emerging economies: fast-growing demand and production base; political and infrastructure risk.

## 3.7.6 Social and technological change

- Demographics and movement: ageing population, urbanisation and migration.
- Lifestyle and buying behaviour: convenience, health, ethical choices; growth of online businesses.
- CSR: for (reputation, loyalty, avoiding regulation) and against (cost, lower short-term profit).
- Shareholder concept: serve owners' returns. Stakeholder concept: balance all affected groups.
- Carroll's pyramid, base to top: economic, legal, ethical, philanthropic.
- Pressures for socially responsible behaviour: consumers, pressure groups, social media, employees, investors, regulators.
- Technology: affects every function and strategy; creates markets, ends products, lowers entry barriers.

## 3.7.7 Porter's five forces

| Force | Strong when | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Entry threat | Low barriers to entry | Profit competed away |
| Buyer power | Few or large buyers, easy switching | Lower prices |
| Supplier power | Few suppliers, specialised inputs | Higher costs |
| Rivalry | Many similar rivals, slow growth | Price competition, high promotion spend |
| Substitute threat | Easy alternatives | Price ceiling |

Forces change with technology, mergers, regulation and new products. Weak forces mean higher profit potential. Forces shape competitive strategy: build barriers, differentiate, secure customers or suppliers.

## 3.7.8 Investment appraisal

| Method | Formula or rule | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payback | Time to recover the initial cost | Simple; stresses risk | Ignores later cash and timing |
| Average rate of return | (total returns - cost) / years / cost x 100 | Uses all returns | Ignores timing |
| Net present value | Sum of discounted cash flows - cost | Allows for time value of money | Depends on discount rate and forecasts |

**Worked reminder.** Norbrook Cafes spends £50,000 on a new oven; cash flows are £20,000, £25,000 and £30,000 in years 1 to 3; the discount rate is 10%.

- Payback: after year 2, £45,000 recovered, so 2 + 5/30 = 2.17 years (about 2 years 2 months).
- ARR: total returns £75,000, net £25,000, so 25,000 / 3 / 50,000 x 100 = 16.7%.
- NPV: 20/1.1 + 25/1.21 + 30/1.331 = 18.18 + 20.66 + 22.54 = 61.38, so NPV = 61.38 - 50 = **+£11,380**.
- Sensitivity: with cash flows 10% lower, NPV falls to about +£5,240.

Factors influencing the decision: investment criteria, non-financial factors, risk and uncertainty. Sensitivity analysis changes one assumption at a time to see how much the result depends on it; it shows which forecasts matter but gives no probabilities.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish corporate objectives from tactics.
2. A business has operating profit £45,000, equity £250,000 and non-current liabilities £50,000. Calculate ROCE.
3. Using the same figures, calculate gearing.
4. Receivables are £40,000 and revenue is £480,000. Calculate receivables days to 1 decimal place.
5. Cost of sales is £420,000 and inventories £60,000. Calculate inventory turnover.
6. Name the four perspectives of the Balanced Scorecard.
7. What are the three Ps of the Triple Bottom Line?
8. Why might a stronger pound hurt an exporter?
9. Name Porter's five forces.
10. A £90,000 project returns £30,000, £40,000 and £50,000 in years 1 to 3. Calculate payback.
11. What does a positive NPV tell you?

### Answers

1. Corporate objectives are measurable, whole-business goals; tactics are short-term actions that carry out the strategy.
2. Capital employed = 300,000, so ROCE = 45,000 / 300,000 x 100 = 15.0%.
3. 50,000 / 300,000 x 100 = 16.7%.
4. 40,000 / 480,000 x 365 = 30.4 days.
5. 420,000 / 60,000 = 7 times.
6. Financial; customer; internal business processes; learning and growth.
7. Profit, People, Planet.
8. Its goods cost more abroad or its sterling revenue falls, reducing competitiveness or margins.
9. Entry threat, buyer power, supplier power, rivalry, substitute threat.
10. After year 2, £70,000 is recovered; £20,000 of £50,000 remains in year 3, so 2 + 20/50 = 2.4 years (about 2 years 5 months).
11. The project earns more than the discount rate, so it adds value on this measure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating a ratio result without a comparison or an explanation of what it means.
- Mixing up gearing (non-current liabilities over capital employed) with the current ratio.
- Writing days for payables using revenue instead of cost of sales.
- Describing a model such as Porter or Carroll without applying it to the business in the question.
- Treating every external change as a threat, with no opportunity or no judgement of size and timing.
- Discounting wrongly in NPV, or forgetting to subtract the initial investment.
- Recommending an investment on one method only, with no mention of risk, non-financial factors or sensitivity.
- Long definitions of CSR with no link to the business's stakeholders.
