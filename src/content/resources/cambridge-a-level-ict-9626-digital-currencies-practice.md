## Section A

**1.** Define the term cryptocurrency. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** differences between a virtual currency and a central bank digital base money. **[2]**

**3.** Describe a stored value card and give **one** use. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** examples of decentralised systems. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a payment made with a credit card can be reversed but a payment made with a cryptocurrency usually cannot. **[2]**

**6.** State what is meant by a distributed ledger. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A blockchain stores each block with a hash of its own contents and the hash of the previous block.

**(a)** State what a hash is. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why changing a transaction in an early block would be noticed. **[3]**

**8.** Dunmore Books takes £4,000 in sales each day. Its card provider charges 1.5% of each sale. A payment service that uses a cryptocurrency would charge 0.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the fee each day for card sales and for the cryptocurrency service. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the daily saving if all sales used the cryptocurrency service. **[1]**

**(c)** Dunmore Books is told this saving may not be real. Explain why. **[2]**

**9.** A customer pays Dunmore Books 0.4 of a coin when each coin is worth £2,500. The next day each coin is worth £2,200, and the shop sells the coin.

**(a)** Calculate how much the shop receives on the second day. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in the value of a coin. **[2]**

**(c)** Name the characteristic of cryptocurrencies that this shows. **[1]**

**10.** Describe how a mobile electronic wallet allows a customer to pay in a shop. **[3]**

**11.** Compare the use of debit cards in an electronic point of sale system with the use of Bitcoin. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss the impact of cryptocurrencies on governments. **[6]**

**13.** A central bank is considering issuing digital base money to the public. Evaluate this proposal from the point of view of citizens. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A digital currency [1] that uses cryptography to secure transactions and to control the creation of new units, usually with no central authority [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: virtual currency is controlled by a private company, but digital base money is issued by the central bank [1]; virtual currency is used inside a platform, but base money is legal tender everywhere in the country [1]; virtual currency value is set by the company, but base money has a stable value backed by the government [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A card that holds a fixed value paid in advance [1]. Use, for example: a prepaid card for a child's spending, a gift card, or a travel card [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two from: Bitcoin; Litecoin; peer-to-peer electronic monetary systems; mobile electronic wallets [1] [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A credit card is run by a bank or card company that can refund or reverse a payment [1]. A cryptocurrency network has no central owner to cancel a confirmed transaction, which is recorded permanently [1]. **[2]**

**6.** A record of all transactions [1] that is copied and held on many computers in a network instead of one central place [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** A fixed-length code calculated from the contents of the block [1].

**(b)** Changing the transaction changes the hash of that block [1]. The next block holds the old hash, so it no longer matches [1], and the mismatch continues through all the later blocks, and the other copies of the chain still show the original record [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Card: 4,000 × 0.015 = £60 [1]; cryptocurrency: 4,000 × 0.005 = £20 [1].

**(b)** £60 − £20 = £40 [1].

**(c)** The value of the cryptocurrency can change between the sale and the shop converting it to pounds [1], which could cost more than the saving. Any other valid reason, such as the cost of new equipment, may be accepted [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** 0.4 × 2,200 [1] = £880 [1].

**(b)** (2,500 − 2,200) ÷ 2,500 [1] = 0.12, which is 12% [1].

**(c)** Volatility [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The wallet app stores payment details or the keys needed to spend the currency [1]. The customer holds the phone close to the terminal or scans a code [1]. The payment is authorised, for example with a fingerprint or PIN, and then sent for approval, and the sale is recorded [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Debit card: the bank or card company controls the system and approves each payment [1]; payments can be refunded if wrong [1]; the provider holds a record of all spending [1]. Bitcoin: no central owner, as the network of computers checks transactions [1]; a confirmed payment cannot normally be reversed [1]; the value can change greatly over short periods [1]. Maximum 6 marks. **[6]**

**12.** Benefits: faster and cheaper international payments for businesses and citizens [1]; transactions can be tracked on the public ledger, which may help find crime [1]. Risks: governments lose some control over the money supply and interest rates when many people use another currency [1]; cryptocurrency may be used for money laundering and tax evasion [1]; it is difficult to regulate a system with no single owner [1]; price crashes can harm citizens and need government help [1]. Governments may respond by issuing their own digital money and regulating exchanges [1]. Maximum 6 marks. **[6]**

**13.** Benefits: stable value, because it is backed by the government [1]; fast and cheap payments, and access for people with no bank account [1]; safe from private companies going out of business [1]. Drawbacks: the government could see all payments, so privacy is reduced [1]; people need a device and internet access, which excludes some [1]; hackers could target a central system [1]. Conclusion supported by an argument, for example that it is worthwhile if privacy laws protect citizens [1]. Maximum 6 marks. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating cryptocurrency, digital currency and virtual currency as one thing.
- Saying blockchain is "secure" with no mention of linked hashes or shared copies.
- Percentage change worked out against the new price, not the old price.
- Listing benefits only in discuss and evaluate questions, with no balance or conclusion.
- Describing "the economy" generally without naming governments, businesses or individuals.
