The [assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-assessment-revision-notes/)
set out what each component weighs. These notes turn that into an exam-day plan -- how to work from
Paper 1's guiding question, build a genuinely comparative Paper 2 essay, and frame a tightly-scoped
individual oral -- with a worked scenario and a checklist, alongside the
[full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-language-and-literature-syllabus-guide/) already on
the site.

## Paper 1: answer the guiding question, not a generic commentary

Each unseen text on Paper 1 comes with a specific guiding question, and responses are expected to
work closely from that question rather than offering a broad close reading of everything noticeable
in the text. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise reading the guiding question first, before
annotating the text, and use it to filter which features to discuss -- a technically accurate
commentary that ignores the guiding question still loses marks, so the question should shape the
whole response, not just the introduction.

## Paper 2: build one argument across two texts, not two mini-essays

The comparative essay question is broad enough to answer using any two studied literary works, and
is assessed on how effectively evidence from both texts is selected and interwoven into **one
sustained argument** -- treating the two works as separate mini-essays stitched together is a common
way strong individual knowledge fails to translate into a strong comparative grade. **Exam-preparation
priority**: practise planning comparative paragraphs that discuss both texts together on the same
point (e.g. "both writers use structural fragmentation, though for different purposes"), rather than
planning one paragraph per text.

## The individual oral: frame the global issue narrowly

A tightly framed global issue, evidenced with specific textual detail from both a literary and a
non-literary text, consistently scores more highly than a broad theme only gestured at in general
terms. **Exam-preparation priority**: when choosing and preparing the global issue, practise stating
it as a specific, arguable claim (e.g. "how consumer advertising exploits anxiety about ageing," not
just "media and society"), and rehearse pointing to precise moments in both texts as evidence, since
focus and organisation are explicitly graded criteria.

## The HL essay: write independent argument, not an expanded exam answer

Because the HL essay is produced outside exam conditions with only a single general consultation,
examiners expect a higher standard of structured argument and textual evidence than an in-class
timed response. **Exam-preparation priority for HL students**: draft the essay's central line of
inquiry as a specific, arguable question well before the deadline, get that one consultation on the
draft itself (not just the topic), and revise at least once -- an essay that reads like a longer exam
answer, assembled close to the deadline, typically underperforms against the higher assessment
bands.

## Worked practice scenario: narrowing a global issue for the oral

A student has studied a novel about a family business and a set of print advertisements as their two
texts, and wants to explore "identity" as a global issue -- too broad to analyse precisely in the
time available.

```
Too broad:       "Identity in the novel and the advertisements."

Narrowed:         "How financial precarity shapes characters' and consumers'
                  sense of self-worth" -- specific enough to trace through
                  named moments: a character's shame at the family
                  business's decline in the novel, and an advertisement's
                  appeal to insecurity about social status.

Why it's
stronger:          The narrowed version gives a precise claim to argue,
                  which structures the whole response and makes it easy
                  to select focused, specific evidence from both texts --
                  rather than surveying "identity" generally, which
                  invites description over analysis.
```

This narrow-a-broad-theme-into-a-specific-claim step applies to any global issue chosen for the
oral, regardless of the two texts studied.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: practise reading a Paper 1 guiding question before annotating the text;
  practise planning Paper 2 paragraphs that discuss both texts together, point by point; narrow the
  oral's global issue into a specific, arguable claim well before the assessment; HL students should
  draft and revise the HL essay well ahead of the deadline, using the single consultation on an
  actual draft.
- **During Paper 1**: re-read the guiding question before finalising the response's structure, and
  check every paragraph still answers it.
- **During Paper 2**: check each body paragraph references both texts, not just one, before moving
  on.
- **During the individual oral**: keep evidence specific and drawn from precise moments in both
  texts, and manage time so both texts receive genuinely balanced treatment.

## Self-test

1. Why can a technically accurate Paper 1 commentary still lose marks?
2. What common structural mistake weakens an otherwise strong Paper 2 essay?
3. In the worked scenario, why is the narrowed global issue stronger than the broad one?
4. Why does the HL essay require a different writing approach from an in-class timed essay?

**Answers:** 1. Because each unseen text comes with a specific guiding question that the response is
expected to work from directly; ignoring it in favour of a broad, generic close reading loses marks
even when the textual observations are accurate. 2. Treating the two studied texts as separate
mini-essays rather than building one sustained argument that interweaves evidence from both. 3.
Because it gives a precise, arguable claim that can be traced through specific, named moments in both
texts, rather than surveying a broad theme in general terms that invites description over analysis.
4. Because it is produced independently, outside exam conditions, with only a single general
consultation, so examiners expect more sustained argument and more precisely selected textual
evidence than a timed response can typically produce.
