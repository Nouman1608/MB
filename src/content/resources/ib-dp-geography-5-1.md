This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 5.1 of Option E, Leisure, tourism and sport, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): changing leisure patterns. It is examined in Paper 1 if your school teaches this option. The [5.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-leisure-tourism-sport-changing-leisure-patterns-revision-notes/) already define leisure, recreation, tourism and sport, list the main influences, and cover the life cycle, unequal participation and commercialisation. This page adds what those notes leave short: how leisure is actually measured, how the main influences combine at different scales, why participation differs between countries as well as between groups, and how to turn leisure data into an analytical answer. Test yourself with the [5.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-5-1-practice/); the [Leisure, tourism and sport option study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-leisure-tourism-sport-study-guide/) gives the overview of all four sub-topics.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Classify leisure activities and show how tourism and sport fit within leisure.
- Explain why leisure patterns have changed over time.
- Explain why participation varies between places and between social groups.
- Interpret data on participation and be honest about its limits.

## How tourism and sport sit inside leisure

Think of three overlapping sets. **Leisure** is the largest: all discretionary time. **Recreation** is the activity done in that time, at home or away. **Tourism** is the part of recreation (plus business and other travel) that involves leaving the usual environment. **Sport** is a form of recreation that can happen locally, such as a weekly match, or can itself be a reason for tourism, such as travelling to a marathon or a ski resort.

This overlap matters in exams. A question about sport tourism draws on both sets: the motivation is sport, the geography is tourism (flows, destinations, impacts). Name the overlap rather than treating the two as separate topics.

### Primary and secondary resources

A useful distinction for explaining where leisure happens:

- **Primary resources** are the attractions themselves: natural features such as coastlines, mountains and snow, and cultural ones such as heritage sites and festivals.
- **Secondary resources** are the facilities that make use possible: accommodation, transport, booking systems, sports venues and equipment hire.

A region can have outstanding primary resources but little leisure use if secondary resources are missing. Investment in secondary resources, such as an airport or a ski lift, often explains a sudden change in a leisure pattern.

## How leisure is measured

Leisure is harder to count than population or income. Common sources are:

| Source | What it records | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Time-use surveys | Hours spent on each activity, from diaries | Small samples; people forget or round |
| Participation surveys | Share of people doing an activity at least once in a period | "Once a month" and "three times a week" both count |
| Membership and ticket data | Club members, entries, attendance | Misses informal activity; one person may count many times |
| Arrivals and bed-night data | Tourist numbers and length of stay | Misses day visitors and staying with friends |
| Spending data | Money spent on leisure goods and services | Free activities, such as walking, are invisible |

The choice of measure changes the pattern. Informal, free activities like walking or a street football game are undercounted, and they are disproportionately the activities of lower-income groups. So data can exaggerate the gap between rich and poor in leisure participation. Saying this earns analysis credit.

## Why patterns change over time

The revision notes list the influences. The deeper point is that they operate together and at different scales:

- **Global scale.** Time-space compression and rising incomes in emerging economies expand international leisure travel.
- **National scale.** Labour laws on working hours and paid holidays, school calendars, public holidays and government sport policy set the time available. Public investment in parks and pools sets what is accessible for free.
- **Local scale.** The presence of facilities, safe streets, green space and public transport decides what a household can actually do.

Change is also driven by **leisure being income-elastic**: as incomes rise, spending on leisure tends to grow faster than spending on essentials. That is why leisure industries grow quickly in emerging economies, and why leisure spending falls sharply in recessions.

### Sport participation and economic development

As countries develop, sport participation changes in three ways that are worth separating:

1. **Formalisation.** More sport moves from informal play into clubs, leagues and gyms.
2. **Commercialisation.** More sport becomes something people pay to do or watch.
3. **Spectatorship.** Watching sport, live or through media, often grows faster than playing it.

These trends can coexist with falling physical activity overall, because work and travel become more sedentary.

## Why participation varies between countries

When comparing countries, use a structured set of factors:

- **Income and cost** of equipment, membership and travel.
- **Climate and physical geography**: snow, coastlines and mountains shape which sports and outdoor activities are common.
- **Culture and tradition**: some sports are tied to national identity or historical diffusion.
- **Government policy**: physical education in schools, public facility funding, national health campaigns.
- **Demographic structure**: an older population shifts demand towards less strenuous activities and travel.
- **Gender norms and safety**, which can limit women's participation in public space.

A strong answer weighs these: for instance, climate makes a winter sport possible, but income and facilities decide who actually takes part.

## Worked example: reading participation data

A survey of an invented city reports the share of adults doing sport at least once a week.

| Income group | Men (%) | Women (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Lowest fifth | 30 | 18 |
| Middle fifth | 42 | 34 |
| Highest fifth | 55 | 51 |

1. **Describe the pattern.** Participation rises with income for both sexes: men from 30% to 55%, women from 18% to 51%.
2. **Quantify the gaps.** In the lowest fifth the gender gap is 30 - 18 = 12 percentage points; in the highest it is 55 - 51 = 4 points. The gap narrows as income rises.
3. **Explain.** Higher incomes pay for gym membership, childcare and travel to facilities, which reduces the time and cost barriers that fall most heavily on women in poorer households.
4. **Limit the data.** "At least once a week" treats a single walk the same as daily training, and informal activity may be missed, so the lowest group's participation may be understated.

That four-step pattern of describe, quantify, explain and limit fits most data-response parts.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine the reasons why leisure participation varies between social groups within one country.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Income is the clearest influence, because the cost of equipment, membership and travel excludes lower-income households from many formal activities **[factor and mechanism]**. However, income interacts with gender: unpaid care work reduces women's free time most in households that cannot buy childcare, so the gender gap is often widest among the poorest **[interaction between factors]**. Location adds a further layer, as inner-city areas with little green space and rural areas without facilities limit choice regardless of income **[spatial dimension]**. These differences may be exaggerated by surveys that miss informal and free activities, so the real gap is likely smaller than the data suggest, though still significant **[evaluates the evidence]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating tourism and sport as separate from leisure rather than parts of it.
- Explaining change with one influence when several act together at different scales.
- Comparing countries only by income, ignoring climate, culture and policy.
- Trusting participation data without noting what it misses.
- Describing your own leisure habits instead of geographical patterns.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a pattern with values and units; **explain** it with mechanisms.
- **Distinguish** needs the difference made explicit, such as primary versus secondary resources.
- **Examine** rewards showing how factors interact and how strong the evidence is.
- **Suggest** asks for plausible reasons linked to the source, not certainties.
- In data questions, compare like with like: percentage points for gaps between rates, percentages for relative change.
