This guide goes deeper on one subtopic of Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 (for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028): **1.2 Business structure**, part of AS Level topic 1, Business and its environment. A Level candidates need it too, because Papers 3 and 4 assume all AS content.

The syllabus splits 1.2 into two parts:

- **1.2.1 Economic sectors**: the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors and businesses within them; the public and private sectors and businesses within them; the reasons for and consequences of the changing relative importance of these sectors.
- **1.2.2 Business ownership**: the main features of sole traders, partnerships, private limited companies, public limited companies, franchises, co-operatives, joint ventures and social enterprises; the appropriateness of each; unlimited and limited liability and their importance; the advantages and disadvantages of changing from one type of ownership to another.

Use it with the [Business structure revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-business-structure-cambridge-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and the [Business structure practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-business-structure-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/). The [combined guide to business structure and size](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-business-structure-and-size-of-business/) has the definitions table for all eight ownership forms; this page builds on it with data, calculations and judgement. All businesses and figures below are invented, and sums of money are in dollars.

## 1.2.1 Reading sector change from data

Exam questions often give sector data and ask you to explain the change or its consequences. Here are the shares of national output in the invented economy of Norvana over 25 years.

| Sector | Share of output, then | Share of output, now | Change (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | 18% | 7% | -11 |
| Secondary | 32% | 26% | -6 |
| Tertiary | 45% | 57% | +12 |
| Quaternary | 5% | 10% | +5 |

Two points examiners look for:

1. **A falling share is not a falling total.** If Norvana's output doubled over the period, primary output went from 18 to 14 units out of 100 and 200 respectively: the sector shrank in relative importance but only slightly in absolute terms. Always say "relative importance".
2. **Output share and employment share differ.** Mechanised farming can produce a stable output with far fewer workers, so primary employment usually falls faster than primary output.

### Reasons for the change

| Reason | How it shifts the balance |
|---|---|
| Rising incomes | Spending shifts towards services such as leisure, health and finance |
| Technology and productivity | Machines replace workers in farming and manufacturing; digital technology creates quaternary activity |
| Global competition | Manufacturing moves to lower-cost countries, while the home economy imports goods |
| Depletion of resources | Mines and fisheries run down |
| Government policy | Privatisation shrinks the public sector; nationalisation or new public services expand it |

In many lower-income economies the pattern runs the other way for a period: **industrialisation** raises the secondary sector's share as workers leave agriculture for factories.

### Consequences for businesses

- **Opportunities** for firms in growing sectors: new markets, a larger pool of service-sector workers.
- **Threats** for firms in declining sectors: falling demand, pressure to cut costs or relocate.
- **Labour issues:** structural unemployment and skills shortages at the same time; firms may need to fund retraining.
- **Supply chains:** a manufacturer may come to rely on imported components as domestic suppliers close.

### Public and private sectors

The **public sector** consists of organisations owned and controlled by the state; the **private sector** consists of businesses owned by individuals or shareholders. Many economies have seen the private sector's share grow through privatisation, with the aim of raising efficiency and reducing the burden on taxpayers. A public-sector organisation usually pursues service objectives, so a question comparing a state hospital with a private clinic should start from their different objectives.

## 1.2.2 Choosing a form of ownership

The syllabus asks for **appropriateness**. Use five questions to decide which form suits a business:

1. **Risk:** how much could the owners lose, and do they need limited liability?
2. **Finance:** how much capital is needed, and from whom?
3. **Control:** how much decision-making power do the founders want to keep?
4. **Aims:** is the main purpose profit, member benefit or a social mission?
5. **Expertise:** does the business need skills the founder lacks?

### Applying the questions

| Business | Most appropriate form | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| A self-employed piano tuner | Sole trader | Low risk and capital; full control; simple to set up |
| Two architects pooling skills and clients | Partnership | Shared expertise and workload; professional reputation matters more than limited liability |
| A family bakery buying a $400 000 oven line | Private limited company | Heavy investment needs limited liability; the family keeps control because shares are not sold to the public |
| A food manufacturer seeking $50 million to build three factories | Public limited company | Only a share issue to the public can raise this much |
| Dairy farmers who want a fair price for milk | Co-operative | Members share ownership and benefits; aims are member-focused |
| A café employing young people leaving care | Social enterprise | Trades to fund a social mission; surplus reinvested |

### Liability in practice

**Unlimited liability** means the owner is personally responsible for all the business's debts. **Limited liability** caps an owner's loss at the amount invested in shares.

A deeper evaluation point: limited liability gives small companies **less protection than it appears**. Banks often refuse to lend to a new private limited company unless a director signs a **personal guarantee**.

*Worked example.* Inès Kovac sets up Kovac Signs Ltd with $15 000 of share capital and a bank loan of $40 000 that she personally guarantees. The company fails, owing the bank $40 000 and suppliers $12 000, with assets that sell for $20 000 (all taken by the bank).

1. The bank is owed $40 000 - $20 000 = $20 000 after selling the assets. Because of the guarantee, Inès must pay this herself.
2. Suppliers are owed $12 000. Limited liability protects her here: they cannot claim her personal assets.
3. Her total loss: $15 000 share capital + $20 000 guarantee = **$35 000**, far more than her shareholding.

Limited liability still matters: it protected her from the supplier debts, and it makes outside investors far more willing to buy shares. But the answer should not assume the owner's risk is always capped.

### Franchising: a calculation

A franchise is appropriate when a business wants a proven brand and system. Compare two options for a new coffee shop owner, Marek.

| | Independent café | Franchise outlet |
|---|---|---|
| Expected annual revenue | $320 000 | $400 000 (brand recognition) |
| Costs before royalty | $280 000 | $330 000 |
| Royalty: 6% of revenue | none | $24 000 |
| Initial franchise fee, $25 000 spread over 5 years | none | $5 000 |
| **Annual profit** | **$40 000** | **$41 000** |

The franchise earns only $1 000 a year more, despite $80 000 of extra revenue, because fees absorb most of the gain. The judgement then depends on risk: the franchise's revenue forecast rests on a known brand and so may be more reliable, while the independent café keeps freedom over the menu and avoids being harmed if the brand's reputation suffers elsewhere.

## Changing from one form to another

| Change | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader to partnership | More capital and skills; shared workload | Shared profit and decisions; liability for partners' actions |
| Sole trader or partnership to private limited company | Limited liability; separate legal identity; easier to raise capital | Accounts must be filed; set-up costs; less privacy |
| Private limited to public limited company | Very large sums from a share issue; shares easy to sell | Loss of control; takeover risk; short-term pressure from investors; costs of flotation |
| Public limited back to private limited | Founders regain control; less short-term pressure | Must buy back shares; loses access to public share capital |

*Worked example: dilution of control.* The founders of Halcroft Outdoor Ltd own all 2 000 000 shares. To build a national chain they float the business, issuing 3 000 000 new shares to the public. They now own 2 000 000 ÷ 5 000 000 = **40%**, so they no longer hold a majority and could be outvoted or face a takeover bid.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Assess whether becoming a public limited company is the best way for Halcroft Outdoor to finance its expansion. [12]

> Becoming a plc would raise a large sum without interest payments, which suits an expansion into a national chain. **(application to the case)** However, issuing 3 000 000 shares cuts the founders' stake to 40%, so they lose majority control and the business becomes open to a hostile takeover. **(analysis using the data)** Investors will also expect dividends and steady profit growth, which may conflict with the founders' long-term plans. **(further analysis)** Alternatives include a bank loan, which keeps control but adds interest costs and risk if sales disappoint, or bringing in a few private investors while staying a private limited company. **(considers alternatives)** On balance, if the founders value control and the expansion can be staged, remaining private and combining retained profit with a loan is more appropriate; flotation is justified only if rapid national growth is essential and the founders accept a minority stake. **(judgement stating what it depends on)**

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the public **sector** with a public **limited company**: a plc is in the private sector.
- Saying limited liability means the business cannot lose money.
- Listing features of a form without saying why it suits the business in the question.
- Describing sector change without separating reasons from consequences.
- Treating a falling share as a falling total.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Define** or **Identify**: a precise meaning or a named item.
- **Explain**: reason plus effect, linked to the business in the case.
- **Analyse**: a chain of cause and effect, such as limited liability → easier to attract investors → more capital → faster growth.
- **Assess**, **Evaluate** or **To what extent**: weigh both sides and reach a judgement that states what it depends on.
- **Advise** or **Justify**: recommend one form of ownership or one change and support it with evidence from the case.
