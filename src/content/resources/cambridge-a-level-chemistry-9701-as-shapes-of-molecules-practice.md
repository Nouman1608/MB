## Section B

**7.** Methane, ammonia and water have bond angles of 109.5°, 107° and 104.5° respectively.

**(a)** State the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs around the central atom in each molecule. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the decrease in bond angle from methane to water. **[3]**

**8.** Phosphorus forms PCl₃ and PCl₅.

**(a)** Predict the shape of PCl₅ and state its bond angles. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the counting method to show that phosphorus has one lone pair in PCl₃, and hence predict its shape. **[2]**

**9.** For each ion, calculate the number of lone pairs on the central atom and predict the shape.

**(a)** H₃O⁺ **[2]**

**(b)** NH₂⁻ **[2]**

**(c)** PF₆⁻ **[2]**

**10.** Carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide are both triatomic molecules.

**(a)** Explain why carbon dioxide is linear. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why sulfur dioxide is non-linear, and suggest a value for its bond angle. **[3]**

**11.** The tetrachloroaluminate ion, AlCl₄⁻, forms when AlCl₃ accepts a chloride ion.

**(a)** Deduce the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs around aluminium in AlCl₄⁻. **[1]**

**(b)** Predict the shape and bond angle, and name the molecule in the syllabus to which it is analogous. **[2]**

**12.** A student predicts that the carbonate ion, CO₃²⁻, is pyramidal because "it has the same AX₃ formula as NH₃".

**(a)** State the correct shape and bond angle of the carbonate ion. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the error in the student's reasoning. **[2]**

**13.** Solid phosphorus(V) chloride is made of two ions, PCl₄⁺ and PCl₆⁻, rather than PCl₅ molecules.

**(a)** Calculate the number of lone pairs on phosphorus in each ion. **[2]**

**(b)** Predict the shape and bond angle of each ion. **[2]**

**(c)** State the shape of a PCl₅ molecule in the vapour, and explain why it differs from both ions. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Octahedral [1]; 90° [1].

**2.** Valence shell electron pair repulsion [1]. Electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible [1].

**3.** B [1].

**4.** Tetrahedral [1]; 109.5° [1].

**5.** 120° and 90° (both needed) [1].

**6.** Lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair [1].

**7. (a)** CH₄: 4 bonding, 0 lone; NH₃: 3 bonding, 1 lone; H₂O: 2 bonding, 2 lone (all three correct for 2 marks, two correct for 1) [2].

**(b)** Each molecule has four electron pairs around the central atom [1]. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs [1]. As the number of lone pairs increases, the bonding pairs are pushed closer together, so the bond angle decreases [1].

**8. (a)** Trigonal bipyramidal [1]; 120° and 90° [1].

**(b)** (5 − 3) ÷ 2 = 1 lone pair, so four regions [1]; pyramidal (analogous to NH₃), bond angle about 107° [1].

**9. (a)** (6 − 3 − 1) ÷ 2 = 1 lone pair [1]; pyramidal [1].

**(b)** (5 − 2 + 1) ÷ 2 = 2 lone pairs [1]; non-linear [1].

**(c)** (5 − 6 + 1) ÷ 2 = 0 lone pairs [1]; octahedral [1].

**10. (a)** Carbon has two regions of electron density (two C=O double bonds) and no lone pairs [1]; these repel to be as far apart as possible, at 180° [1].

**(b)** Sulfur uses four of its six outer electrons in the two S=O bonds, leaving one lone pair [1]. The three regions (two bonds and a lone pair) repel, and the lone pair repels more strongly, so the molecule is bent [1]. Bond angle slightly less than 120° (the measured value is about 119°) [1].

**11. (a)** Four bonding pairs and no lone pairs [1].

**(b)** Tetrahedral, 109.5° [1]; analogous to CH₄ [1].

**12. (a)** Trigonal planar [1]; 120° [1].

**(b)** The shape depends on the number of electron pairs around the central atom, not on the formula pattern [1]. Carbon in CO₃²⁻ has three bonding regions and no lone pair, whereas nitrogen in NH₃ has a lone pair [1].

**13. (a)** PCl₄⁺: (5 − 4 − 1) ÷ 2 = 0 lone pairs [1]; PCl₆⁻: (5 − 6 + 1) ÷ 2 = 0 lone pairs [1].

**(b)** PCl₄⁺ is tetrahedral, 109.5° [1]; PCl₆⁻ is octahedral, 90° [1].

**(c)** Trigonal bipyramidal (120° and 90°) [1], because phosphorus has five bonding pairs and no lone pairs, compared with four pairs in PCl₄⁺ and six in PCl₆⁻ [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the arrangement of electron pairs (tetrahedral) instead of the shape (pyramidal or non-linear).
- Forgetting to add or subtract electrons for the charge on an ion.
- Counting double bonds as two regions.
- Giving one angle for PF₅ or PCl₅.
- Writing "lone pairs take up more space" without "repel more strongly".
- Assuming species with the same formula pattern must have the same shape.
