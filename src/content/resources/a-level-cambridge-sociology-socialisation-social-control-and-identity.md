Socialisation is the lifelong process through which people learn the culture of their society, and
in Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699) it is the first half of Paper 1. The
syllabus asks you to explain how culture is learned (1.1), why most people conform and some resist
(1.2), and how class, gender, ethnicity and age shape identities that are now changing (1.3).
Examiners reward answers that use the official concepts precisely and set structural views against
interactionist ones.

This guide goes deeper into the three socialisation sub-topics than the
[Paper 1 overview guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-socialisation-identity-methods/).
For the methods half of the paper, see the
[research methods, design and issues guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-research-methods-design-and-issues/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology 9699 (syllabus for 2024, 2025 and 2026), Paper 1,
section 1 *Socialisation and the creation of social identity*:

| Sub-topic | What the syllabus lists |
|---|---|
| **1.1 The process of learning and socialisation** | Culture, roles, norms, values, beliefs, customs, ideology, power and status as elements in the social construction of reality; the importance of socialisation, including the nature versus nurture debate; agencies of socialisation and social control (family, education, peer group, media, religion) |
| **1.2 Social control, conformity and resistance** | Structure and agency, including structuralist and interactionist views; why individuals conform (sanctions, social pressure, self-interest, social exchange); how order is maintained (power, ideology, force, consensus); explanations of deviance and non-conformity (subcultures, under-socialisation, marginalisation, cultural deprivation, social resistance) |
| **1.3 Social identity and change** | Class, gender, ethnicity and age in the construction of identity; how these identities may be changing through globalisation, increased choice and new or hybrid identities |

The syllabus links this section to four of its key concepts: *Socialisation, culture and identity*;
*Power, control and resistance*; *Structure and human agency*; and *Social change and development*.

## What does "the social construction of reality" mean?

Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann's *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966) argued that the
social world feels natural and fixed only because people learn it and then take it for granted. The
building blocks the syllabus names each play a part:

- **Culture** — the whole way of life of a group, passed between generations.
- **Values** — general beliefs about what is good or worth striving for.
- **Norms** — specific rules of behaviour that put values into practice.
- **Roles** — the expected behaviour attached to a social position (teacher, daughter, imam).
- **Status** — the social position itself; *ascribed* at birth or *achieved* through effort.
- **Customs** and **beliefs** — traditional practices and the ideas that justify them.
- **Ideology** — a set of ideas that serves the interests of a group, often disguising inequality.
- **Power** — the ability to get others to act as you want, which shapes whose norms become "normal".

A strong answer shows the elements working together: a value (respect for elders) produces norms
(standing when a grandparent enters), which attach to roles and statuses, and these feel natural
because they have been learned so early.

## Is human behaviour shaped by nature or nurture?

The **nature** side argues that behaviour is fixed by biology, instinct or genes. The **nurture**
side, which sociology broadly defends, argues that behaviour is learned. Classic evidence for
nurture includes:

- **Feral and isolated children.** "Genie", discovered in California in 1970 after years of
  extreme isolation, never fully acquired language, which suggests that human capacities depend on
  social contact at the right stage.
- **Cross-cultural variation.** Margaret Mead's *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies*
  (1935) described gender behaviour among the Arapesh, Mundugumor and Tchambuli of New Guinea
  that differed sharply from Western expectations. Her fieldwork has since been questioned, so use it
  as illustration rather than proof.
- **Gender socialisation.** Ann Oakley (*Sex, Gender and Society*, 1972, and later work) described
  how parents channel children into gendered behaviour through *manipulation* and *canalisation*.

For balance, note sociobiological claims and research on hormones; the strongest answers conclude
that biology sets possibilities, but socialisation decides which ones become normal.

## How do the agencies of socialisation and social control work?

| Agency | How it socialises | How it controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Family** | Primary socialisation: language, early norms, gender roles | Praise, punishment, withdrawal of affection |
| **Education** | Formal curriculum and hidden curriculum (punctuality, obedience, competition) | Detentions, grades, exclusion |
| **Peer group** | Secondary socialisation, especially in adolescence | Teasing, ostracism, acceptance |
| **Media** | Role models, representations, news framing | Shaming, moral panics, celebrity examples |
| **Religion** | Moral codes, ritual, sense of belonging | Promise of reward or fear of sanction; community disapproval |

Functionalists such as Talcott Parsons see these agencies passing on shared values that hold society
together. Marxists such as Louis Althusser (1971) describe the family, school, media and religion as
*ideological state apparatuses* that make capitalism look fair, while feminists stress that the same
agencies reproduce patriarchy.

## Why do most people conform?

The syllabus names four reasons, and each can be tied to a theory.

1. **Sanctions** — positive (rewards) and negative (punishments), formal or informal.
2. **Social pressure** — the wish to be accepted; Solomon Asch's line-judgement experiments (1951)
   showed people agreeing with an obviously wrong group answer.
3. **Self-interest** — people calculate that conformity pays; Travis Hirschi's *Causes of
   Delinquency* (1969) argued that attachment, commitment, involvement and belief bond people to
   society, so they have too much to lose by deviating.
4. **Social exchange** — George Homans (1958) described social life as an exchange of rewards and
   costs; conformity continues while the exchange is worthwhile.

## Structure or agency: how is social order maintained?

**Structuralist** views (functionalism and Marxism) start from society. Durkheim and Parsons see
order resting on **consensus** around shared values. Marxists see order resting on **power**: the
ruling class uses **ideology** to win consent (Antonio Gramsci's idea of *hegemony*) and **force**
(police, courts, army) when consent fails.

**Interactionist** views start from individuals. George Herbert Mead argued that the self develops
through interaction and taking the role of others, and Erving Goffman's *The Presentation of Self in
Everyday Life* (1959) showed people actively managing impressions. Order is therefore negotiated in
everyday encounters rather than imposed. Evaluation point: structuralists can be accused of
treating people as puppets; interactionists of ignoring the power that limits choice.

## How do sociologists explain deviance and non-conformity?

| Explanation (syllabus term) | Core idea | Example of a sociologist |
|---|---|---|
| **Under-socialisation** | Inadequate socialisation, often blamed on family breakdown | Charles Murray's underclass argument (1984, 1990) |
| **Cultural deprivation** | Groups lack the norms and skills that mainstream institutions reward | Used in debates on working-class achievement |
| **Subcultures** | Groups develop alternative values that reward deviance | Albert Cohen, *Delinquent Boys* (1955): status frustration |
| **Marginalisation** | Groups pushed to the edge of society have little stake in conforming | Linked to unemployment and racism |
| **Social resistance** | Deviance as a meaningful challenge to dominant power | Paul Willis, *Learning to Labour* (1977); Dick Hebdige, *Subculture: The Meaning of Style* (1979) |

Evaluate by asking who defines "deviance": interactionists argue that labels, not acts, make
deviance, while resistance theories risk romanticising behaviour that harms others.

## How are class, gender, ethnicity and age identities changing?

Section 1.3 moves from how identity is learned to how it is changing.

- **Social class.** Jan Pakulski and Malcolm Waters (*The Death of Class*, 1996) argued that
  consumption now matters more than occupation. Against this, Mike Savage and colleagues' Great
  British Class Survey (2013) still found clear class groupings based on economic, cultural and
  social capital.
- **Gender.** R.W. Connell's idea of *hegemonic masculinity* (1987) and Máirtín Mac an Ghaill's *The
  Making of Men* (1994) show masculinity as plural and contested, while Judith Butler (*Gender
  Trouble*, 1990) treats gender as performance.
- **Ethnicity.** Stuart Hall's writing on diaspora identity (1990s) and Homi Bhabha's idea of
  *hybridity* (*The Location of Culture*, 1994) describe new identities formed from more than one
  cultural source, accelerated by **globalisation** and migration.
- **Age.** Childhood, youth and old age are socially constructed; consumer markets and longer life
  expectancy are reshaping what each stage means.

The syllabus also names **increased choice**: postmodernists argue identities are now picked and
mixed, while structural sociologists reply that class, gender and ethnicity still limit the menu.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is 9699 Paper 1 mostly about theory or methods?** Both. Section A mixes short-answer and
two-part questions across socialisation and methods, and Section B offers a choice of two 26-mark
essays. Expect socialisation content and methods content in the same paper.

**Which theories should I use for socialisation essays?** Contrast structural views
(functionalist, Marxist, feminist) with interactionist ones, because 1.2 explicitly asks for the
difference between structuralist and interactionist views.

**Do I need named studies?** Named sociologists are not listed in the 9699 syllabus, but accurate,
relevant evidence is how AO1 and AO3 marks are earned. One well-used study beats five name-drops.

**How does 1.3 link to globalisation in Paper 4?** Hybrid identity and cultural defence reappear in
Paper 4 section 7.2, so learning them now saves time at A Level.
