OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) explores the physical
processes that shape the Earth, the challenges facing people in a rapidly
changing world, and the skills geographers use to gather and interpret
evidence. It suits students who want to understand the world around them
and to work with maps, data and fieldwork. The specification was first
taught in September 2018 and first examined in May/June 2020. It is a
linear qualification with no coursework, assessed by three papers
weighted 36%, 36% and 28%. Exams take place in May/June only, with no
November series from 2026.

## How the course is organised

The content is in three parts, matching the three papers.

**Living with the Physical Environment.** The physical geography half of
the course: natural processes, landscapes and the ways people interact
with the physical world.

**Challenges in the Human Environment.** The human geography half. Its
Section A includes urban growth and challenges, and a study of world
cities, London and New York. This gives you a detailed comparison of two
major cities alongside the wider patterns of urban change.

**Geographical and Fieldwork Skills.** The skills strand: working with
maps, graphs and data, and planning, carrying out and evaluating
geographical enquiry.

## How it's assessed

| Paper | Title | Time | Marks | Weighting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 | Living with the Physical Environment | 90 minutes | 80 | 36% |
| Paper 2 | Challenges in the Human Environment | 90 minutes | 80 | 36% |
| Paper 3 | Geographical and Fieldwork Skills | 75 minutes | 60 | 28% |

All three papers are externally assessed written exams, sat at the end of
the course, with a single untiered entry. Paper 3 is shorter and carries
fewer marks than the two content papers, but at 28% it is still more than
a quarter of the grade.

## How the parts fit together

Papers 1 and 2 divide the content cleanly between physical and human
geography, but the most interesting questions in geography sit where the
two meet. Physical hazards affect cities; urban growth changes rivers,
coasts and land use; and people's decisions shape how they respond to
the physical world. Looking for those links makes both papers easier,
because a case study learned for one can often support an argument in
the other.

Paper 3 draws on both halves. The skills it tests, reading maps,
interpreting graphs and data, and evaluating fieldwork methods and
results, are used constantly in Papers 1 and 2 as well. Practising those
skills in the context of your physical and human topics is therefore
more effective than treating them as a separate unit. The world cities
study of London and New York is also a natural place to practise data
interpretation, since comparing two cities often involves statistics,
maps and photographs.

## Case studies and examples

Geography answers are strongest when general points are supported by
named, located examples. Across the course you will meet places at very
different scales, from a single landform or neighbourhood to whole
cities such as London and New York, and examiners reward answers that
use those places precisely rather than vaguely.

It helps to think of case studies as evidence rather than as stories to
retell. For each one, note what it shows, which part of the
specification it supports, and how it might be used in a question that
asks you to explain, compare or evaluate. A single well-understood
example can often serve several topics: a city facing rapid growth, for
instance, may also illustrate environmental pressure or a physical
hazard. Building a small set of flexible examples is usually more
effective than trying to learn a large number thinly, and it leaves you
better prepared for the variety of questions the three papers can ask.

## Planning your study

Many classes start with Living with the Physical Environment, because
physical processes give a clear foundation in geographical systems and
vocabulary. Challenges in the Human Environment, including urban growth
and the world cities study, then follows.

Work on geographical and fieldwork skills throughout rather than at the
end. Each time you meet a new topic, practise the relevant skill with
it: a map for a landform, a graph for population change, a data table for
an urban comparison. If your class carries out fieldwork, write up your
method, results and evaluation soon afterwards, while the details are
fresh.

Keep a case-study file with a consistent format, recording place, facts,
figures and significance. Begin exam-style questions on each topic once
it has been taught, and move to full timed papers in the final months.
The course's exam-preparation page explains how to prepare for three
papers of unequal weight, so use it for paper-level planning.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography 9230 assessed?**
By three written papers: Paper 1 and Paper 2 are each 90 minutes, 80 marks and 36% of the grade, and Paper 3 is 75 minutes, 60 marks and 28%.

**Is there coursework in 9230?**
No. All three components are externally assessed exams, although Paper 3 tests geographical and fieldwork skills.

**What is the world cities study?**
Within Challenges in the Human Environment, Section A includes a study of world cities, London and New York, alongside urban growth and challenges.

**Can I sit 9230 in November?**
The board's page states that exams for this specification take place in May/June only, with no November series from 2026.

**When was 9230 first examined?**
First teaching was in September 2018 and the first exams were in May/June 2020.
