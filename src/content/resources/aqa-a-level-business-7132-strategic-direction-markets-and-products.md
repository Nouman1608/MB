This guide goes deeper into section **3.8.1 Strategic direction: choosing which markets to compete in and what products to offer** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.8 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **factors influencing which markets to compete in and which products to offer**
- **strategic direction**, to include the Ansoff matrix and the value of **market penetration, market development, new product development and diversification**
- **the reasons for choosing, and the value of, different options for strategic direction**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## Strategic direction

**Strategic direction** is the choice of where the business will compete: in which markets, with which products. It follows from the business's mission and objectives (section 3.7.1) and from the analysis of its internal position and external environment (sections 3.7.2 to 3.7.7). The choice commits large resources for several years, so it is a strategic decision, not a tactical one.

## The Ansoff matrix

Igor Ansoff's matrix sorts growth options by whether the **market** and the **product** are existing or new.

| | Existing products | New products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration (lowest risk) | New product development |
| **New markets** | Market development | Diversification (highest risk) |

### Market penetration

Selling more of the existing products in the existing markets. Methods include promotion, price cuts, loyalty schemes, better distribution and taking share from rivals.

- **Value:** lowest risk, because the business knows its products, customers and competitors; uses existing capabilities; quick to start.
- **Limits:** growth is capped by the size of the market; if the market is mature or in decline, share can only be gained from rivals who may retaliate; price cuts reduce margins.
- **Best when:** the market is growing, the firm has spare capacity, or rivals are weak.

### Market development

Selling existing products in new markets: new regions or countries, new customer segments, or new uses.

- **Value:** extends the life of products; spreads risk across markets; can use existing production.
- **Limits:** new markets may have different tastes, laws and competitors; research, distribution and promotion cost money; products may need adapting; the business lacks local knowledge.
- **Best when:** the home market is saturated, the product has a distinctive appeal, and the business can reach the new market at acceptable cost.

### New product development (NPD)

Developing new products for existing markets.

- **Value:** responds to changing customer needs; keeps the product range fresh; strengthens loyalty; builds on existing customer knowledge and relationships; can create competitive advantage.
- **Limits:** research and development are costly and uncertain; many new products fail; takes time; may cannibalise existing sales; needs technical and design skills.
- **Best when:** customers' needs are changing, technology is advancing, or the business has strong research capability.

### Diversification

New products in new markets. It can be **related** (new activity linked to the existing business, for example a bakery opening cafes) or **unrelated** (a conglomerate move, for example a bakery buying a hotel).

- **Value:** spreads risk across different industries, so a downturn in one is offset by another; provides growth when the core market is saturated or declining; may use spare resources.
- **Limits:** highest risk, because the business has no experience in the new market or product; requires large investment; risks losing focus; management may lack expertise; synergies may not materialise.
- **Best when:** the existing market has little growth, and the business has the funds and risk tolerance for a bold move, or can buy an established firm.

## Factors influencing the choice of markets and products

| Factor | How it influences the choice |
|---|---|
| Market growth and size | Fast-growing markets favour penetration or development; saturated markets push firms towards new markets or products |
| Competition | Strong rivals make penetration harder; weak competition in a new market favours development |
| Resources and finances | Diversification and development cost more; limited funds favour penetration |
| Core competences | Strategies that use the firm's strengths succeed more often |
| Risk attitude | Cautious owners prefer low-risk options; entrepreneurial leaders accept more risk |
| Corporate objectives | A target for fast growth may require riskier moves |
| Product life cycle | Products in decline need new products or new markets |
| Technology and customer trends | Digital changes may open new markets or make products obsolete |
| Law and regulation | Entry restrictions, standards or tariffs may block or favour a market |
| Ownership | A plc with shareholder pressure may favour acquisitions; a family business may prefer stability |

## Worked example: Lowry Outdoor

Lowry Outdoor sells waterproof clothing in the UK, where the market is worth £50m and the firm has a 12% share (£6m). It considers four options. Estimates are for extra annual revenue after three years.

| Option | Ansoff category | Investment (£m) | Extra revenue if successful (£m) | Probability of success | Expected extra revenue (£m) | Expected revenue per £1 invested |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Increase advertising and discounts to reach 15% share | Penetration | 0.4 | 1.5 | 80% | 1.2 | 3.00 |
| Sell in Germany | Market development | 1.2 | 3.0 | 60% | 1.8 | 1.50 |
| Launch electric heated jackets | NPD | 1.5 | 4.0 | 50% | 2.0 | 1.33 |
| Open a chain of activity centres | Diversification | 2.5 | 6.0 | 30% | 1.8 | 0.72 |

A rise from 12% to 15% of a £50m market is £6m to £7.5m, which is the £1.5m extra revenue in the first row.

**Analysis.** The table shows the general pattern: the further from the core business, the greater the possible reward but the lower the chance of success and the higher the cost. Penetration offers the best expected return per pound invested but has the lowest ceiling (a 15% share). Diversification has the largest possible gain but the weakest expected return.

**Judgement.** Revenue is not profit, and the probabilities are judgements, so the numbers are a guide, not an answer. A sensible strategy for Lowry is probably to pursue penetration first, because it is low-cost and fast, while testing a small pilot in Germany. The company might later develop the heated jackets if its research is promising and the funds are available. Diversification into activity centres has little connection to its core competences and is hard to justify unless the market is declining.

## Using the matrix in an exam

- **Identify** which category an action falls into. Check both the product and the market: selling an existing product to a new segment is market development, not NPD.
- **Link to the business's circumstances**: market growth, finance, competition and risk.
- **Weigh risk and reward** and say which factor decides the matter.
- **Remember combinations**: businesses often pursue more than one option, and options can be sequential.

## Limitations of the Ansoff matrix

- It focuses on products and markets and ignores other factors, such as financing and the firm's resources.
- It gives no guidance on how to grow (organically or by acquisition) or on timing.
- The categories overlap in practice: is a new flavour a new product or a variation?
- It does not account for competitors' reactions.
- Risk rankings are generalisations. A well-researched diversification may be less risky than a poorly planned market development.

## Applying the factors to a decision

When choosing direction, sort the information into three questions.

1. **Where is the market going?** Is demand growing, flat or falling? What are the trends in tastes, technology and regulation? A shrinking market makes penetration harder and increases pressure to look elsewhere.
2. **What can the firm do well?** Which core competences does it have, and do they travel to the new product or market? A firm with strong design skills can move into related products; a firm with strong distribution can enter new regions.
3. **What can it afford to risk?** How much cash can it invest, and what happens if the project fails? A business with thin margins cannot afford a failed diversification.

The answers point to an option, and a final recommendation should state the deciding factor.

## Quantitative checks

| Measure | Use |
|---|---|
| Market share (sales ÷ market size) | Shows the room for penetration |
| Expected revenue (probability × revenue) | Compares options with different risks |
| Payback or NPV of the investment | Tests affordability |
| Break-even output | Shows how much must be sold for the option to pay |

These figures guide, but do not replace, judgement about fit with the firm's mission and capabilities.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** explain why the option suits the case, using evidence on market growth, competitors, resources.
- **Evaluate:** compare options, weigh risk against reward, identify the deciding factor, and conclude.
- Do not just define the four boxes. Use them to justify a recommendation.

## Common mistakes

- Classifying a strategy wrongly, for example calling a new market for an existing product "diversification".
- Describing risk as fixed. It depends on the firm's skills and funds.
- Recommending diversification for a business with little cash and no experience.
- Forgetting that one strategy may support another.
- Failing to connect the choice to the objectives.
