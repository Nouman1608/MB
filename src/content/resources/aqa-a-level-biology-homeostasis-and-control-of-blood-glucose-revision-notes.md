## 3.6.4.1 Homeostasis and feedback

**Homeostasis**: physiological control systems in mammals keep the internal environment within restricted limits.

| Variable | Why it must stay stable |
|---|---|
| Core temperature | Enzyme activity. Too cool: fewer collisions and enzyme-substrate complexes. Too warm: hydrogen bonds break, active site changes, denaturation |
| Blood pH | Changes the charges on R groups, disrupting ionic and hydrogen bonds, so the active site changes shape |
| Blood glucose | Supply of respiratory substrate (low glucose starves cells, especially neurones); water potential of blood (high glucose lowers it, so water leaves cells by osmosis) |

**Negative feedback**: a change is detected, a response reverses it and the system returns to its original level.

Steps in a negative feedback loop:

1. Receptor detects a departure from the set point.
2. Coordinator (nervous or endocrine system) processes the information.
3. Effector acts to reverse the change.
4. The stimulus shrinks, so the response is switched off.

**Separate mechanisms for each direction** (for example insulin against glucagon, vasodilation against vasoconstriction) give a greater degree of control because the level can be corrected from either side.

**Positive feedback**: a change causes more of the same change. Examples are the opening of voltage-gated sodium ion channels and oxytocin release in labour. It moves the system away from its starting point, so it is not homeostatic.

| | Negative feedback | Positive feedback |
|---|---|---|
| Effect of response | Reverses the change | Increases the change |
| Result | Stable value near a set point | Rapid, runaway change |
| Role | Homeostasis | Amplification of a process |

How to interpret a feedback data set:

1. Identify the stimulus and the response.
2. Ask: does the response oppose the stimulus (negative) or add to it (positive)?
3. Quote values from the data, including a percentage change if useful.
4. Say what is kept constant, if anything.

## 3.6.4.2 Blood glucose control

**What raises blood glucose**: absorption of glucose after digestion, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis.
**What lowers it**: respiration of glucose in cells, glycogenesis.

**Liver processes**

| Process | Change | Effect on blood glucose |
|---|---|---|
| Glycogenesis | Glucose to glycogen | Lowers |
| Glycogenolysis | Glycogen to glucose | Raises |
| Gluconeogenesis | Glycerol and amino acids to glucose | Raises |

**Hormones**

| Hormone | Source | Trigger | Actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Insulin | Beta cells, islets of Langerhans | High blood glucose | Binds to surface receptors; more glucose channel proteins in surface membranes; activates enzymes for glucose to glycogen |
| Glucagon | Alpha cells, islets of Langerhans | Low blood glucose | Binds to surface receptors; activates enzymes for glycogen to glucose and for glycerol and amino acids to glucose |
| Adrenaline | Adrenal glands | Stress or danger | Binds to surface receptors; activates enzymes for glycogen to glucose |

**Second messenger model (adrenaline and glucagon)**

1. Hormone (first messenger) binds to receptor on the cell surface membrane.
2. Adenylate cyclase is activated.
3. Adenylate cyclase makes cyclic AMP (cAMP) from ATP; cAMP is the second messenger.
4. cAMP switches on protein kinase.
5. Protein kinase activates enzymes that convert glycogen to glucose.
6. The signal is amplified at each stage.

Memory line: hormone, receptor, adenylate cyclase, ATP to cAMP, protein kinase, enzymes.

**Diabetes**

| | Type I | Type II |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Immune system destroys beta cells; little or no insulin | Target cell receptors lose responsiveness to insulin, sometimes with inadequate insulin secretion |
| Typical risk factors | Not linked to lifestyle | Obesity, diet high in refined carbohydrate and fat, inactivity, age, family history |
| Control | Insulin injections, monitoring, managing carbohydrate in the diet | Manipulating the diet, exercise, weight loss; sometimes drugs or insulin |

**Evaluating positions** (health advisers and the food industry on type II diabetes)

- Health advisers: link a diet high in sugar, fat and energy, and low activity, to obesity and type II diabetes. They recommend labelling, lower sugar and more exercise. Evidence is mostly from population studies, which show correlation.
- Food industry: emphasises personal choice and balance, and points to reformulation and labelling. It has a commercial interest.
- To evaluate: judge sample size, correlation against cause, funding and who gains.

## Required practical 11

1. Make a dilution series of glucose solution from a stock, using a measured volume of stock and distilled water.
2. React each with the same volume of Benedict's reagent, heated for the same time at the same temperature (a typical method; follow your school's version).
3. Remove the precipitate, then measure with a colorimeter using a red filter, zeroed on distilled water.
4. Plot absorbance against concentration to give the calibration curve.
5. Read the unknown "urine" sample's concentration from the curve, within the range of the standards.

Volume of stock = (required concentration × final volume) ÷ stock concentration.
With Benedict's reagent, absorbance falls as glucose concentration rises, because the blue colour is used up.
If the sample was diluted before testing, multiply the answer by the dilution factor.

Worked reminder: stock 100 mmol dm⁻³, make 20 cm³ of 25 mmol dm⁻³. Stock volume = 25 × 20 ÷ 100 = 5.0 cm³, plus 15.0 cm³ of water.

## Worked reminders

**Percentage change**: (new − original) ÷ original × 100. Skin blood flow of 6 units rising to 27 units is (27 − 6) ÷ 6 × 100 = 350%.

**Mean rate**: change in glucose ÷ time. A fall from 7.8 to 4.9 mmol dm⁻³ over 90 minutes is 2.9 ÷ 90 = 0.032 mmol dm⁻³ min⁻¹.

**Reading a calibration curve**: a line of best fit with absorbance = 0.62 − 0.00245 × concentration gives, for an absorbance of 0.37, a concentration of (0.37 − 0.62) ÷ (−0.00245) = about 102 mmol dm⁻³.

**Answer frame for a glucose control question**:

1. State the stimulus and the receptor (beta cells for a rise, alpha cells for a fall).
2. Name the hormone and say it is carried in the blood.
3. State what it binds to on target cells.
4. Give the enzymes or channels affected and the process (glycogenesis, glycogenolysis or gluconeogenesis).
5. State the effect on blood glucose and that the stimulus then declines.

## Must-know distinctions

- Glycogenesis (make glycogen) versus glycogenolysis (break glycogen down) versus gluconeogenesis (make glucose from non-carbohydrates).
- First messenger (the hormone) versus second messenger (cAMP).
- Insulin binds to a surface receptor and never enters the cell.
- Type I (too little insulin) versus type II (poor response to insulin).
- Negative feedback (corrects) versus positive feedback (amplifies).

## Quick self-test

1. Define negative feedback.
2. Give two reasons why blood glucose concentration must be kept within limits.
3. Name the three processes carried out by the liver in blood glucose control.
4. Which cells in the pancreas secrete insulin?
5. How does insulin increase the uptake of glucose by target cells?
6. Name the enzyme that produces the second messenger and state the reaction it catalyses.
7. Name the enzyme activated by cAMP.
8. A blood glucose reading fell from 9.0 to 5.4 mmol dm⁻³ after a meal. Calculate the percentage fall.
9. Calculate the volume of stock needed to make 20 cm³ of a 25 mmol dm⁻³ solution from a 100 mmol dm⁻³ stock.
10. A "urine" sample diluted 1 in 2 reads as 87 mmol dm⁻³ on a calibration curve. What was the concentration of the original sample?
11. State the cause of type I diabetes.
12. Name one example of positive feedback.

### Answers

1. A change is detected and a response reverses it, restoring the system to its original level.
2. Glucose is the respiratory substrate, so low levels starve cells of ATP; a high level lowers the water potential of the blood, so water leaves cells by osmosis.
3. Glycogenesis, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
4. Beta cells of the islets of Langerhans.
5. It binds to receptors, which causes more glucose channel proteins to be included in the surface membrane.
6. Adenylate cyclase converts ATP to cyclic AMP.
7. Protein kinase.
8. (9.0 − 5.4) ÷ 9.0 × 100 = **40%**.
9. 25 × 20 ÷ 100 = **5.0 cm³** (plus 15.0 cm³ distilled water).
10. 87 × 2 = **174 mmol dm⁻³**.
11. Destruction of beta cells by the immune system, so little or no insulin is made.
12. Entry of sodium ions opening more voltage-gated sodium channels, or oxytocin release during labour.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "blood sugar is controlled" without naming the receptor, the hormone and the effector.
- Saying glucose is "stored" without naming glycogen and the liver.
- Describing the second messenger sequence out of order, or leaving out ATP to cAMP.
- Confusing the first messenger (adrenaline, glucagon) with the second messenger (cAMP).
- Forgetting to multiply by the dilution factor in the practical.
- Reading a calibration curve outside the range of the standards.
- Saying negative feedback "stops" a change rather than reversing it and returning to the set point.
- Giving only one side in an evaluation of health advisers and the food industry.
