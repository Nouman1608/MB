This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. The emergence of Central Asian republics (1917 to 2020) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. The guide says that every examination question will require you to refer to **one** Central Asian republic. The suggested examples are Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kazakhstan and Tajikistan. This guide uses **Kazakhstan** as its main case, with **Uzbekistan** and others as comparisons, so adapt the points to your own republic.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-central-asian-republics-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-central-asian-republics-practice/).

## Impact of the Russian Revolution and Civil War (1917 to 1924)

**Background.** Russia conquered most of Central Asia in the second half of the nineteenth century. Russian and Ukrainian settlers moved to the steppe in large numbers. A great revolt in 1916 against conscription for labour service shook the region and left bitter memories.

**Economic and social impact.** After the revolutions of 1917, war, requisitioning of grain and livestock, and the cutting of rail links caused famine and disease in many areas. In Turkestan, the shortage of food, a collapse in the cotton trade and the arrival of Russian refugees brought hunger. Central Asian nomads and farmers often saw the Russian settlers as the real beneficiaries of Soviet power.

**Local elites and intelligentsia.** Two groups played key roles. Kazakh nationalists of the **Alash Orda** (led by Alikhan Bukeikhanov) formed an autonomous government in 1917 to 1920. In Turkestan, reformist Muslims known as **Jadids** wanted modern education and national revival. Many of them first cooperated with, then resisted, the Bolsheviks. Armed resistance, called the **Basmachi** movement, lasted into the late 1920s in Uzbekistan and Tajikistan.

**Bolshevik response.** The Red Army defeated the main opposition. The Bolsheviks dissolved the Alash Orda and ended the khanate of Khiva and the emirate of Bukhara in 1920. They then made concessions, with the New Economic Policy in 1921 and a policy of "indigenisation" (*korenizatsiia*) to recruit local people into the party.

**Autonomous republics.** The Kirghiz (later Kazakh) ASSR was created in 1920. In 1924 to 1925, the "national delimitation" divided Turkestan into new units, including the Uzbek and Turkmen republics and an autonomous Tajik region. These borders created national identities, but historians still debate whether they were meant to divide and control.

## Impact of Soviet control and policies (1920 to 1940)

**Political control.** The Communist Party controlled appointments. The **1936 constitution** made the larger units full Soviet republics, including Kazakhstan, which was given SSR status, but real power stayed in Moscow. Languages were first written in Latin script and then, in about 1940, in Cyrillic, which tied them to Russian. **Russification** was strengthened, with Russian as the language of promotion.

**Repression.** The Great Terror of 1937 to 1938 destroyed the first generation of local leaders, including Uzbek communists such as Faizulla Khojaev, and the Alash and Jadid intellectuals were shot or imprisoned. The system was totalitarian in intent, but its control was limited by distance, clan networks and the lack of local staff, and historians debate how far the state controlled everyday life.

**Economic policies and collectivisation.** The Soviet state forced nomads to settle and farmers to join collective farms. In Kazakhstan, the campaign of 1929 to 1933 was catastrophic. Livestock herds were seized and slaughtered, and the Kazakh population suffered a famine in which probably over a million people died, perhaps a third or more of all Kazakhs. Many fled to China, Uzbekistan and elsewhere. The Turksib railway (1930) and mining developed the economy. In Uzbekistan, the economy was geared to cotton monoculture.

**Society, culture and identity.** The Soviet state attacked Islamic institutions, closing mosques and religious courts. In 1927 the *hujum* campaign pushed women to unveil, which gave some women new opportunities but also triggered a violent backlash and killings. Literacy rose sharply. Soviet policy encouraged "national in form, socialist in content" culture: national theatres and literature, but under party control.

## Impact of the Second World War (1941 to 1945)

- **Military.** Central Asians fought in the Red Army; Kazakh and Kyrgyz troops of the 316th Rifle Division (the "Panfilov men") helped defend Moscow in 1941. Hundreds of thousands did not return.
- **Political.** The war led to a more relaxed attitude to religion: in 1943 the state set up an official Muslim board for Central Asia in Tashkent.
- **Economic.** Factories and workers were evacuated to Central Asia from the west. Industry, especially mining and metal in Kazakhstan, grew. Farmers produced cotton, grain and meat for the front under severe quotas.
- **Social.** More than a million evacuees arrived, and the region took in deported peoples (Volga Germans, Chechens, Crimean Tatars and Koreans, who had been moved in 1937). Many deportees died of hunger and disease, and the republics became more mixed in ethnicity.

## Impact of changing Soviet policies

**Post-war reconstruction.** Central Asia remained a source of raw materials and cotton, and a place for secret projects such as the **Baikonur** space centre and the **Semipalatinsk** nuclear test site (456 tests between 1949 and 1989).

**Khrushchev.** After Stalin's death, repression eased. The **Virgin Lands Campaign** (from 1954) ploughed up many millions of hectares of grassland in northern Kazakhstan. It produced large harvests at first, but poor soil management led to erosion and falling yields. It brought in about a million Slavic settlers, and by 1959 Kazakhs were only about 30 per cent of the republic's people.

**Brezhnev.** Under Leonid Brezhnev (1964 to 1982), local party leaders such as **Dinmukhamed Kunaev** in Kazakhstan and **Sharaf Rashidov** in Uzbekistan stayed in office for decades, building patronage networks. Stability brought benefits but also corruption, as in the Uzbek "cotton affair", in which output figures were falsified.

**Environmental impact.** Diverting the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers for cotton irrigation caused the **Aral Sea** to shrink dramatically, ruining fisheries, harming health and creating toxic dust. Nuclear tests damaged the health of people near Semipalatinsk.

## Reasons for the establishment of independent republics

**Gorbachev's policies.** *Glasnost* (openness) allowed criticism of the past and the discussion of nationalism. *Perestroika* (restructuring) did not fix the economy. In Kazakhstan, the dismissal of Kunaev in December 1986 and his replacement by a Russian outsider, Gennady Kolbin, led to protests in Almaty (the Jeltoqsan events), which were put down by force. Ethnic violence flared in the Ferghana Valley (1989) and in Kyrgyzstan (1990). Environmental movements, such as the anti-nuclear Nevada–Semipalatinsk group, grew.

**Collapse of the USSR.** Most Central Asian leaders did not seek independence. In a March 1991 referendum, a large majority in the region voted to keep a reformed union. After the failed coup in Moscow in August 1991, republics declared independence one by one: Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan (31 August), Tajikistan (9 September), Turkmenistan (27 October), and Kazakhstan, last of all, on 16 December 1991. The USSR was dissolved on 26 December. The local party leaders, such as Nursultan Nazarbayev and Islam Karimov, became presidents.

## Challenges and successes of nation-building (1991 to 2020)

**Economic and social policies (Kazakhstan).** Oil and gas fields such as Tengiz attracted foreign investment. Growth was high in the 2000s, and the new capital, Astana, was built from 1997. But wealth was unequal, and the economy depended on energy prices. Uzbekistan kept state control of cotton and the economy.

**Political leadership and opposition.** Presidents tended to stay in office for long periods: Nazarbayev until 2019, Karimov until 2016. Opposition was restricted, elections were not seen as free, and protest was repressed (for example at Zhanaozen in 2011 and Andijan in Uzbekistan in 2005). Tajikistan fell into a civil war (1992 to 1997). Kyrgyzstan was the most open but had revolutions in 2005 and 2010.

**National identity, ethnicity and marginalised groups.** The new states promoted their own languages, history and symbols, and many Russians left. Kazakhstan's government supported both the Kazakh language and an official policy of ethnic harmony, but minorities such as Uyghurs and Germans, and Kazakhs who had grown up speaking Russian, felt pressure in different ways. Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan saw violence between ethnic groups in the Ferghana Valley, including in 2010.

**Foreign relations.** Kazakhstan followed a "multi-vector" policy of balance among Russia (a neighbour, trading partner and member of the Eurasian Economic Union), China (a major investor, with the Belt and Road Initiative announced in Astana in 2013) and the USA (for investment, and security cooperation after 2001). It gave up the nuclear weapons inherited from the USSR.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Sarah Cameron (*The Hungry Steppe*) explains the Kazakh famine as the result of Soviet policy towards nomads. Some Kazakh and other historians use the term genocide, while others reject it, a debate you can use.
- Debate over whether Soviet rule was "colonial" or "modernising". Adeeb Khalid argues that Soviet rule transformed the region, creating nations as well as destroying earlier ones.
- Marianne Kamp and Douglas Northrop on the hujum.
- Martha Brill Olcott and others on the leadership of post-Soviet states.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent did collectivisation transform Soviet Central Asia in the 1930s?"** Use one republic. Weigh the famine, the end of nomadism, the new farms and the Turksib railway against continuities in clan networks, Islam and local culture. Judge that the change was deep but incomplete.

## Common mistakes

- Treating five republics as one.
- Ignoring the pre-Soviet background.
- Giving exact casualty figures that are disputed.
- Describing independence as the result of a mass nationalist movement; most leaders were reluctant.
