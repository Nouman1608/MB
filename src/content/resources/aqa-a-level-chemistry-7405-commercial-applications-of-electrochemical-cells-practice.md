These questions cover section **3.1.11.2 Commercial applications of electrochemical cells** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 3. Learn the ideas first in the [Commercial applications of electrochemical cells study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-commercial-applications-of-electrochemical-cells/) and the [Commercial applications of electrochemical cells revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-commercial-applications-of-electrochemical-cells-revision-notes/). Electrode potentials needed for each question are given in the question.

## Questions

**1.** A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell and a nickel–cadmium cell are both used to power devices.

**(a)** State what is meant by a fuel cell. **[1]**

**(b)** Give one way in which a fuel cell differs from a rechargeable cell. **[1]**

**2.** A lithium cell has these simplified electrode reactions:

Li → Li⁺ + e⁻

Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻

**(a)** State which equation takes place at the negative electrode. **[1]**

**(b)** State the oxidation state of cobalt before and after the reaction at the positive electrode. **[1]**

**(c)** Write the overall equation for the cell reaction. **[1]**

**3.** A cell is made from these two half-cells.

Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Pb  E = –0.13 V

Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag  E = +0.80 V

**(a)** Identify the negative electrode. **[1]**

**(b)** Write the overall equation for the reaction that happens when the cell produces a current. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the EMF of the cell. **[1]**

**4.** A nickel–cadmium cell uses these electrode reactions in alkaline conditions.

NiO(OH) + H₂O + e⁻ ⇌ Ni(OH)₂ + OH⁻  E = +0.49 V

Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cd + 2OH⁻  E = –0.81 V

**(a)** Write the half-equation for the reaction at the negative electrode when the cell discharges. **[1]**

**(b)** Write the overall equation for the discharge reaction. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the EMF of the cell. **[1]**

**5.** The nickel–cadmium cell in Question 4 is recharged.

**(a)** Write the half-equation for the reaction at the negative electrode during recharging. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why this reaction does not happen when the cell is discharging. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a non-rechargeable cell stops producing a current after a time. **[2]**

**7.** An alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell has an electrolyte of aqueous potassium hydroxide. The electrode data are:

O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ ⇌ 4OH⁻  E = +0.40 V

2H₂O + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂ + 2OH⁻  E = –0.83 V

**(a)** Write the half-equation for the reaction at the negative electrode. **[1]**

**(b)** Write the half-equation for the reaction at the positive electrode, using one mole of oxygen molecules. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the EMF of the cell. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain how the cell generates an electric current. **[3]**

**8.** The electrodes of a fuel cell are porous and contain a platinum catalyst. Explain why. **[2]**

**9.** A company plans to replace its delivery vans with vehicles powered by hydrogen fuel cells. Evaluate this plan, giving two benefits and two risks or drawbacks. **[4]**

**10.** A student calculates the EMF of the nickel–cadmium cell in Question 4 as (–0.81) – (+0.49) = –1.30 V.

**(a)** Identify the error. **[1]**

**(b)** Another student doubles the nickel half-equation and then doubles the E value to +0.98 V. Explain why this is wrong. **[1]**

**11.** Lithium has a very negative electrode potential, –3.04 V for Li⁺/Li. Explain why lithium is a good choice of negative electrode for a cell used in a portable device. **[2]**

**12.** The electrolyte in a lithium cell does not contain water. Explain why. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A cell in which fuel and oxidant are supplied continuously from outside to generate a current [1]

**(b)** A fuel cell does not need to be electrically recharged and works as long as fuel is supplied; a rechargeable cell contains a fixed amount of reactant and has to be recharged using an electric current [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Li → Li⁺ + e⁻ [1]

**(b)** +4 before (in CoO₂) and +3 after (in Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻) [1]

**(c)** Li + CoO₂ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻ [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** The lead electrode (Pb/Pb²⁺), because it has the more negative E value [1]

**(b)** Pb → Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ and 2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag, combined: Pb + 2Ag⁺ → Pb²⁺ + 2Ag [2: 1 for reversing the lead half-equation and balancing electrons, 1 for the correct overall equation]

**(c)** EMF = +0.80 – (–0.13) = **+0.93 V** [1]. **[4]**

**4. (a)** Cd + 2OH⁻ → Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻ [1]

**(b)** Nickel half-equation doubled: 2NiO(OH) + 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2Ni(OH)₂ + 2OH⁻ [1]; overall: Cd + 2NiO(OH) + 2H₂O → Cd(OH)₂ + 2Ni(OH)₂ [1]

**(c)** EMF = +0.49 – (–0.81) = **+1.30 V** [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻ → Cd + 2OH⁻ [1]

**(b)** During discharge the cadmium electrode is the negative electrode where oxidation takes place, so cadmium is oxidised, not reduced [1]; the reduction only happens during recharging, when an external supply drives electrons into the cadmium electrode and reverses the reaction [1]. **[3]**

**6.** One of the reactants is used up [1]; the cell reaction cannot be reversed in practice, so the reactants are not replaced and the voltage falls to zero [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻ [1]

**(b)** ½O₂ + H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2OH⁻ [1]

**(c)** EMF = +0.40 – (–0.83) = **+1.23 V** [1]

**(d)** Hydrogen is oxidised at the negative electrode, releasing electrons [1]; the electrons flow through the external circuit to the positive electrode, where they reduce oxygen [1]; ions (hydroxide) move through the electrolyte to complete the circuit [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Porous electrodes give a large surface area where gas, electrolyte and catalyst can meet [1]; the platinum catalyst speeds up the electrode reactions so a larger current is produced [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Benefits, any two for [1] each, up to [2]: the only product at the point of use is water, so no carbon dioxide or particulates from the vehicle; fuel cells convert chemical energy to electrical energy efficiently; they are quiet; refuelling is quick compared with recharging. Risks or drawbacks, any two for [1] each, up to [2]: hydrogen is flammable and has to be stored under high pressure; most hydrogen is made from fossil fuels, which uses energy and releases carbon dioxide, so the plan may not reduce emissions overall; the platinum catalyst is expensive; refuelling infrastructure is limited. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a balanced answer. State each point as a benefit or risk to the company or society, not just as a fact.

**10. (a)** The student subtracted the positive electrode from the negative electrode; the EMF is E(positive) – E(negative) = +1.30 V [1]

**(b)** The electrode potential is independent of the number of electrons (or amount of substance) in the half-equation, so it must stay +0.49 V [1]. **[2]**

**11.** Lithium has a very negative electrode potential, so it releases electrons easily and gives a high EMF with a suitable positive electrode [1]; it is a light metal, so a lot of energy can be stored for a given mass, which suits portable devices [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Lithium reacts with water (so water must be absent) [1]. **[1]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the negative electrode from the wrong E value.
- Adding E values or multiplying them by the number of electrons.
- Leaving electrons in the overall equation.
- Omitting the movement of ions through the electrolyte.
- Evaluations that give only benefits or only risks.
