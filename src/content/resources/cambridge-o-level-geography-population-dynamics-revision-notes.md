Condensed notes for sub-topic 1.1 of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series.

## The three components

```
natural change   = birth rate - death rate        (per 1,000 per year)
total change     = natural change + net migration
% growth rate    = natural change per 1,000 / 10
```

- Natural change can be **negative** (natural decrease), as in Japan and several European
  countries.
- Infant mortality is per 1,000 **live births**, not per 1,000 people.

## Why world population grew so fast

- Roughly 1 billion around 1800; UN estimates 7 billion in 2011 and 8 billion in November 2022.
- **Death rates fell first**: food supply, clean water, sanitation, vaccination, antibiotics.
- **Birth rates fell later**: family decisions depend on culture, religion, education and the
  economic value of children, which change slowly.
- The **gap** between the two rates is the growth. Most growth now is in low-income countries,
  especially sub-Saharan Africa.

## Contrasting rates

| High birth rate because… | Low birth rate because… |
|---|---|
| Children are farm labour and old-age security | Children are expensive in cities |
| High infant mortality | Low infant mortality |
| Early marriage, low female education | Women in education and careers, later marriage |
| Little access to contraception | Contraception widely available |
| Religious or cultural value on large families | Smaller-family norms |

**High death rates:** poor healthcare, malnutrition, unsafe water, conflict, diseases such as
malaria and HIV/AIDS.
**Twist worth one mark:** an ageing high-income country can have a higher crude death rate than a
youthful low-income one.

## Over-, under- and optimum population

- **Over-population**: too many people for the resources and technology to give a decent standard
  of living. Example often used: **Bangladesh**.
- **Under-population**: too few people to use the resources fully. Example often used:
  **Australia** (large area, rich resources, planned immigration).
- **Optimum**: the population giving the highest standard of living.
- Density is **not** the test. The Netherlands is dense but not over-populated.

## Population policies

- **Anti-natalist**: lower the birth rate. **China's one-child policy** (about 1979–80), relaxed to
  two children from 2016 and three in 2021.
- **Pro-natalist**: raise it with allowances, tax relief, parental leave and childcare.
  **Singapore** switched from anti- to pro-natalist in the late 1980s.

**Evaluating China's policy**

| Successes | Problems |
|---|---|
| Slowed population growth | Birth rate was already falling before it began |
| Less pressure on food, jobs and housing | Sex ratio skewed towards boys |
| More resources per child | Ageing population and the "4-2-1" family |
| | Coercion and human rights concerns |

**Judgement:** effective at slowing growth, but the costs led to its reversal.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Birth rate | Live births per 1,000 people per year |
| Death rate | Deaths per 1,000 people per year |
| Natural increase | Birth rate exceeds death rate |
| Natural decrease | Death rate exceeds birth rate |
| Net migration | Immigrants minus emigrants |
| Life expectancy | Average years a newborn is expected to live |
| Fertility rate | Average number of children born per woman |
| Population policy | Government measures to influence population change |

## Exam traps

- Leaving out "per 1,000 per year".
- Forgetting migration in a total-change calculation.
- Saying birth rates rose to cause the population explosion.
- Equating dense with over-populated.
- Evaluating a policy with successes only.

## Self-test

1. Birth rate 18, death rate 7, net migration +3. Give natural change and total change.
2. Why did death rates fall before birth rates?
3. Define under-population in one sentence.
4. Give one reason a wealthy country might have a higher death rate than a poorer one.
5. Give two unintended consequences of China's one-child policy.

**Answers:** 1. Natural change 11 per 1,000 (1.1%); total change 14 per 1,000 (1.4%). 2. Medical and
sanitation improvements spread quickly, but family-size decisions depend on attitudes and economics
that take a generation to change. 3. Too few people to make full use of the available resources, so
living standards could rise if the population grew. 4. It has a larger proportion of elderly people.
5. Any two: a sex ratio with more boys than girls, a rapidly ageing population, the "4-2-1" family
burden, or a future shortage of workers.
