Section 6.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) is the classic explanation question in the education option. The syllabus lists four strands: the relationship between material factors and educational attainment; cultural explanations for patterns in social class and educational attainment, including parental attitudes, values, speech codes and cultural capital; in-school factors, including labelling, ability grouping and pupil subcultures; and compensatory education programmes. It sits in topic 6, Education and inequality, and is examined on Paper 3 – Education.

The [intelligence, social class and educational attainment overview](/resources/a-level-sociology-9699-intelligence-social-class-and-educational-attainment/) sets out the core studies, from Douglas and Bernstein to Ball, Hargreaves and Head Start. This page concentrates on the skills that separate good answers from excellent ones: measuring class carefully, showing how factors interact, using a wider range of evidence, and judging which explanation is strongest.

## First, what is being explained?

**Measuring class.** Studies usually classify pupils by parental occupation, parental education or a poverty marker such as eligibility for free school meals. Each measure has weaknesses: free meal eligibility divides pupils into two groups only, missing differences among the majority; occupation-based measures can misclassify families with one unemployed or two very differently employed parents. When you use evidence, note how class was measured.

**Patterns, not individuals.** Class gaps describe averages. Many working-class pupils achieve highly and some middle-class pupils fail. An explanation must account for the pattern while allowing for exceptions; this is a useful evaluation point against any explanation that sounds deterministic.

**When do gaps appear?** Leon Feinstein (2003), using a British birth cohort, reported that children from advantaged backgrounds with low early test scores tended to overtake children from disadvantaged backgrounds with high early scores during the primary years. This suggested that class shapes development from very early. Jerrim and Vignoles (2013) later argued that part of this pattern is a statistical artefact, regression to the mean, so the size of the effect is debated. Either way, the debate shows why explaining class gaps needs evidence from before, during and outside school.

## Structural and action explanations

A clear way to organise an answer:

| Type | Where the cause lies | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **External, material** | Family resources | Poverty, housing, diet, costs of schooling |
| **External, cultural** | Family culture and values | Parental attitudes, speech codes, cultural capital |
| **Internal, interactionist** | Processes within school | Labelling, setting, subcultures |
| **Policy** | State intervention | Compensatory education |

Structural theories (Marxist, many material accounts) stress constraints; interactionist accounts stress meanings and labels. The best answers show the levels working together.

## Material factors: going further

Beyond poverty and housing, three further points strengthen a material argument.

1. **Opportunity cost.** For a family on a low income, a teenager staying in education means lost wages. This is a material cause of a *choice*, which links to Boudon's secondary effects in [education and social mobility](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-education-and-social-mobility/).
2. **Money buys cultural capital.** Tutoring, books, trips and music lessons cost money, so material and cultural explanations overlap.
3. **Marketised choice.** Where school places depend on where families can afford to live, income shapes which school a child attends before any teacher meets them.

**Evaluation.** Material accounts are strong where poverty is severe, and many countries' compensatory policies assume them. But they cannot explain why pupils with similar incomes but different parental education achieve differently, which points to culture.

## Cultural explanations: deprivation, difference and capital

The syllabus names parental attitudes, values, speech codes and cultural capital. The key analytical move is to separate three positions:

- **Cultural deprivation:** the home lacks something schools need (Douglas, Sugarman). Criticism: it blames the victim.
- **Cultural difference:** working-class culture is different, not deficient; schools favour one culture (Keddie, Labov).
- **Cultural capital:** the advantage lies in a match between middle-class culture and school culture, which schools treat as merit (Bourdieu).

**Diane Reay**, in *Class Work* (1998), studied mothers' involvement in their children's primary schooling in London. Working-class mothers often cared just as much but had less confidence, time and educational knowledge to intervene, while middle-class mothers drew on cultural capital to challenge teachers and secure extra support. This is valuable evidence against the claim that working-class parents simply do not value education.

**Bourdieu's habitus** adds a further layer. Habitus is a set of dispositions learned in early life. When a pupil's habitus fits the school "field", they feel at ease; when it does not, they may feel education is "not for people like us" and withdraw. This explains self-exclusion without assuming that working-class pupils lack ability or values.

## In-school factors: beyond pro- and anti-school

Studies of subcultures are often reduced to a simple pro-school versus anti-school split. Peter Woods, in *The Divided School* (1979), identified a wider range of adaptations to school, including ingratiation, compliance, opportunism, ritualism, retreatism, colonisation, intransigence and rebellion. Pupils may comply without believing, or "colonise" school by doing just enough to get by. This range helps you avoid deterministic answers: a label does not produce one fixed response.

**How labelling, grouping and subcultures connect:**

1. A teacher judges a pupil against the "ideal pupil" image.
2. The judgement shapes placement in a set or stream.
3. Lower sets receive different knowledge and lower-tier exam entries.
4. Pupils respond — some reject the label, some accept it, some form anti-school groups.
5. Outcomes confirm the original judgement, so the process looks like it simply measured ability.

Writing this sequence out shows analysis (AO3), because it explains how a small interaction becomes a structural outcome.

**Evaluation.** Interactionist studies are usually small ethnographies of one or two schools, so generalisation is risky. They can also ignore why labels follow class lines in the first place, which material and cultural factors outside school explain.

## Compensatory education: beyond Head Start

The overview covers Head Start, Perry Preschool, Plowden, Education Action Zones, Sure Start and the Pupil Premium. Two further examples widen your application.

- **Education Maintenance Allowance (England).** Introduced nationally in 2004, it paid weekly allowances to young people from low-income families who stayed in post-16 education. It was abolished in England in 2011 but continued in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. It targeted the opportunity cost of staying on, a material barrier.
- **Conditional cash transfers.** Mexico's Progresa programme (1997, later Oportunidades) and Brazil's Bolsa Família (2003) paid poor families on condition that their children attended school, among other requirements. These tackle material causes of non-attendance at national scale.

**Evaluating compensatory education.** Programmes that target material barriers avoid the "deficit" criticism made of programmes that try to change family culture. But compensatory measures act on symptoms if the wider inequality that produces disadvantage remains. Marxists argue education cannot compensate for society; social democrats reply that well-targeted, long-term programmes can narrow gaps.

## Worked example: Question 3 style

*Quotation:* "Working-class pupils under-achieve because their parents do not value education." Give two arguments against this view.

> **Argument 1.** Reay (1998) found that working-class mothers cared deeply about their children's education but lacked the confidence, time and educational knowledge to intervene effectively. The difference lay in cultural capital, not in values. So the view mistakes a lack of resources for a lack of interest.
>
> **Argument 2.** In-school processes can create under-achievement regardless of parents' values. Ball found pupils from manual backgrounds less likely to be placed in top bands than similar-ability peers, and lower bands met lower expectations. The cause here is the school's decisions, so blaming parental values ignores what happens inside school.

## Worked essay plan

*"Factors inside the school are more important than factors outside it in explaining social class differences in attainment." Evaluate this view.*

1. Clarify internal and external; note how class is measured.
2. Internal: labelling, ideal pupil, setting (Ball), triage (Gillborn and Youdell), subcultures (Hargreaves, Lacey, Woods, Willis).
3. External, material: poverty, opportunity cost, housing and school choice.
4. External, cultural: deprivation versus difference versus capital; Reay; habitus.
5. Interaction: labels follow class lines because of external factors; early gaps (Feinstein, and the debate about it).
6. Policy evidence: compensatory programmes and what their success or failure suggests.
7. Judgement: external factors create the starting gap; internal factors widen or narrow it.

## Common mistakes

- Using "cultural deprivation" and "cultural capital" interchangeably.
- Assuming every working-class pupil underachieves.
- Describing studies without saying what they show about the question.
- Treating compensatory education as a separate topic rather than as evidence about causes.
