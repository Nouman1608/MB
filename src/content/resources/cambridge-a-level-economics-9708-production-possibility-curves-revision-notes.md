These notes condense sub-topic **1.5 Production possibility curves** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028 (AS Level). Full explanations and worked examples are in the [production possibility curves study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-production-possibility-curves/), and exam-style questions are in the [production possibility curves practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-production-possibility-curves-practice/). All data are invented.

## Syllabus points

| Point | Wording | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| 1.5.1 | Nature and meaning of a PPC | Maximum combinations of two goods with full, efficient use of resources |
| 1.5.2 | Shape of the PPC: constant and increasing opportunity costs | Straight line or concave to the origin |
| 1.5.3 | Causes and consequences of shifts in a PPC | Changes in the quantity or quality of resources, or technology |
| 1.5.4 | Significance of a position within a PPC | On, inside or outside the curve |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Production possibility curve | The maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with all resources fully and efficiently employed |
| Opportunity cost | The amount of one good given up to produce more of the other |
| Increasing opportunity cost | Each extra unit of a good costs more of the other good |
| Constant opportunity cost | Each extra unit costs the same amount of the other good |
| Concave to the origin | Bowed outwards, showing increasing opportunity cost |
| Outward shift | A rise in productive capacity |
| Inward shift | A fall in productive capacity |
| Pivot | A shift that changes the maximum of one good only |
| Capital goods | Goods used to produce other goods, such as machinery |
| Consumer goods | Goods bought by households for their own use |

## Reading opportunity cost from data

**Rule:** opportunity cost per unit of X = units of Y given up ÷ extra units of X.

**Quick example (Country Kesh: wheat in million tonnes, cloth in million metres).**

| Point | Wheat | Cloth | Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of wheat |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 0 | 120 | — |
| R | 5 | 114 | 6 ÷ 5 = 1.2 metres |
| S | 10 | 102 | 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4 |
| T | 15 | 84 | 18 ÷ 5 = 3.6 |
| V | 20 | 60 | 24 ÷ 5 = 4.8 |
| W | 25 | 0 | 60 ÷ 5 = 12.0 |

Rising opportunity cost, so the curve is concave. Reciprocal: between S and T, 1 metre of cloth costs 1 ÷ 3.6 = 0.28 tonnes of wheat.

## Shape

| Shape | Opportunity cost | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Concave to the origin | Increasing | Resources not equally suited to both goods; less suitable ones must be switched |
| Straight line | Constant | Resources equally suited to both goods |

## Shifts

| Cause | Direction |
|---|---|
| More labour (population growth, immigration) | Out |
| New natural resources discovered | Out |
| Investment in capital | Out |
| Education and training | Out |
| Better technology | Out (pivot if it helps one good only) |
| Natural disaster, war | In |
| Emigration of skilled workers | In |
| Depletion of resources | In |

**Consequences.**
- Outward shift: more of both goods possible; higher potential living standards.
- Pivot: the opportunity cost of the good that has not improved rises in terms of the one that has.
- Capital goods now: less consumption today, faster outward shift later.

**Quick example.** Capacity growing 3% a year for 10 years gives 1.03¹⁰ = 1.34 times the original level; 1% a year gives 1.10 times.

## Positions

| Position | Meaning |
|---|---|
| On | Full and efficient employment; trade-off between goods |
| Inside | Unemployed or inefficiently used resources; more of both possible at no opportunity cost |
| Outside | Unattainable with current resources and technology |

Moving from inside to the curve is **not** a shift: capacity is unchanged.

## Exam traps

- Axes labelled "price" and "quantity".
- "Diminishing returns" given as the reason for the concave shape.
- A fall in unemployment shown as an outward shift.
- A parallel shift drawn when only one good's technology improved.
- Opportunity cost read as total output instead of the amount given up per extra unit.
- Assuming a point on the curve is automatically the best point.

## Self-test

1. Define a production possibility curve.
2. What does a concave PPC show about opportunity cost?
3. Why is a PPC usually concave to the origin?
4. What shape is the PPC under constant opportunity cost?
5. Using the Kesh table, find the opportunity cost of 1 tonne of wheat between T and V.
6. Using the Kesh table, find the opportunity cost of the 6 million metres of cloth gained when moving from R to P.
7. Kesh produces 10 wheat and 90 cloth. Where is it, and what does this show?
8. Give two causes of an outward shift.
9. Give two causes of an inward shift.
10. A new machine raises cloth output only. What happens to the PPC?
11. Why might a country choose more capital goods and fewer consumer goods?
12. Why is a move from inside to the curve not a shift?

### Answers

1. The maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with all resources fully and efficiently employed, given technology.
2. It is increasing: each extra unit of one good costs more of the other.
3. Resources are not equally suited to both goods, so as production of one rises, less suitable resources must be switched.
4. A straight line.
5. 24 ÷ 5 = **4.8 million metres** of cloth.
6. **5 million tonnes** of wheat (wheat falls from 5 to 0).
7. Inside the curve (S gives 102 cloth with 10 wheat): resources are unemployed or used inefficiently; 12 more million metres of cloth could be produced at no opportunity cost.
8. Any two of: more labour; new resources; investment; education; better technology.
9. Any two of: natural disaster; war; emigration; resource depletion.
10. It pivots outwards along the cloth axis; the wheat maximum is unchanged.
11. More capital raises productive capacity, shifting the PPC further out in future.
12. Capacity has not changed; existing unemployed resources are simply being used.
