| Statement | What you must do |
|---|---|
| Explain what is meant by price stability and inflation, including the difference between real and nominal values | Define the terms and convert nominal figures to real ones |
| Explain how inflation is measured using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) | Describe the basket, weights, index and base year |
| Calculate the effect of inflation on prices | Apply one or more years of inflation to a price |
| Analyse recent and historical inflation figures | Describe trends and explain them |
| Evaluate the causes of inflation and the consequences for consumers, producers, savers and the government | Weigh effects for each named group |

## Price stability as an objective

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general level of prices. **Price stability** means inflation that is low and predictable, not zero. In the UK the government sets the Bank of England a target of **2% CPI inflation**.

Why not aim for zero? Because the CPI may slightly overstate inflation when quality improves, and because a target of zero risks tipping into **deflation**, a sustained fall in the general price level. Deflation sounds good for shoppers but can harm an economy: if people expect prices to fall, they delay buying, firms' sales fall, and firms cut output and jobs. Debts also become harder to repay, because incomes may fall while the amount owed does not.

## Price indices, step by step

A **price index** compares prices with a **base year**, set at 100.

Index = price in current year ÷ price in base year × 100

**Worked example 1.** A bus fare was £2.00 in the base year and is £2.30 now. Index = 2.30 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = **115**. The fare is 15% higher than in the base year.

The CPI does the same for a whole basket of goods and services a typical household buys, with each item **weighted** by its share of spending. A higher index means a higher price level; the **inflation rate** is the percentage change in the index from one year to the next.

## Which items drive inflation?

Each category's contribution to inflation = its weight × its price change.

**Worked example 2.** In Country H, energy prices rise sharply.

| Category | Weight | Price change | Contribution (percentage points) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Energy | 0.08 | +50% | 0.08 × 50 = 4.0 |
| Food | 0.12 | +10% | 0.12 × 10 = 1.2 |
| Everything else | 0.80 | +2% | 0.80 × 2 = 1.6 |
| **CPI inflation** | 1.00 | | **6.8%** |

Energy is only 8% of spending, but it causes more than half the inflation (4.0 of 6.8 points). This explains why a rise in oil or gas prices can push inflation far above target even when most prices are rising slowly. It is a clear case of **cost-push** inflation, because energy is also a cost for almost every firm.

## Real and nominal values

A **nominal** value is in money of the day. A **real** value is adjusted for inflation to show purchasing power.

**Worked example 3: real pay.** In Country H, pay rises 4% while inflation is 6.8%.

- Real pay change = 1.04 ÷ 1.068 − 1 = **−2.6%**.
- A quick estimate is 4 − 6.8 = −2.8%; the exact method divides the growth factors.

Workers receive more money but can buy about 2.6% less.

**Worked example 4: purchasing power over time.** Inflation runs at 5%, 8% and 4% in three successive years.

- Price level rises by 1.05 × 1.08 × 1.04 = 1.179, so a £50 basket costs **£58.97**.
- £100 held as cash at the start buys only 100 ÷ 1.179 = **£84.79** of goods at start prices by the end.

Cash and fixed incomes lose about 15% of their value over the three years.

## Analysing inflation figures

When you are given data:

1. Separate the **rate** from the **level**: falling inflation means prices are still rising, only more slowly.
2. Compare with the **2% target**: how far above or below, and for how long?
3. Look for **turning points** and match them to events in the source, such as an energy price rise.
4. Calculate something: change in percentage points, or the cumulative rise in prices.

**Historical context.** UK inflation was very high in the 1970s, when oil prices rose sharply, and rose above 10% in 2022 as energy and food prices surged, the highest rate for around forty years, before falling back. Both episodes were driven largely by cost-push pressures from energy. Use the figures in the exam source rather than memorised ones.

## Causes: evaluating which matters most

| Cause | Signs in a case study | How persistent? |
|---|---|---|
| Demand-pull | Strong spending, low unemployment, rising borrowing | Lasts while demand outruns supply |
| Cost-push | Higher energy, raw material or wage costs | May fade when the cost shock passes |
| Imported | Currency has fallen, import prices up | Fades once the currency stabilises |
| Expectations | Workers demand pay rises to match expected inflation | Can make inflation self-sustaining |

**Wage-price spiral.** If prices rise and workers win pay rises to keep up, firms' costs rise, so they raise prices again. Expectations turn a one-off cost shock into lasting inflation. This is why central banks act early.

## Consequences for the four named groups

| Group | Losers | Winners |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Those whose pay rises more slowly than prices; people on fixed incomes | Borrowers on fixed rates, whose debt falls in real terms |
| Producers | Firms whose costs rise faster than they can raise prices; exporters if domestic prices rise faster than abroad; menu costs of changing prices | Firms that can raise prices quickly may see revenue and profit rise |
| Savers | Those earning an interest rate below inflation lose purchasing power | Savers whose interest rate is above inflation still gain in real terms |
| Government | Pays more for wages, supplies and inflation-linked benefits | Tax revenue rises with prices and incomes; the real value of its debt falls |

**Evaluation.** The effects depend on how high inflation is, whether it was **expected** (contracts and pay can adjust), how long it lasts, and whether incomes and interest rates keep pace. Moderate, steady inflation does little harm; high or unpredictable inflation makes planning hard and deters investment.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the consequences of a period of high inflation for savers.*

"If the interest rate on savings is below inflation, savers lose purchasing power: £1,000 earning 3% when prices rise 7% buys about 4% less after a year. **[chain with figures]** Savers on fixed incomes, such as many retired people, are hit hardest because they rely on savings income. **[named subgroup]** However, central banks often raise interest rates to fight inflation, so savers' returns may rise and partly offset the loss. **[counter-argument]** Overall, savers usually lose during high inflation, because interest rates tend to rise later and by less than prices, but the loss depends on how quickly rates respond." **[judgement with condition]**

## Common mistakes

- Saying prices fell when the inflation rate fell.
- Calling price stability "no change in prices".
- Adding inflation rates over several years instead of multiplying.
- Ignoring weights when explaining why one item drives inflation.
- Writing about "people" in general when the question names savers, producers or the government.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: write the growth factor (1 + rate) and show each step; round at the end.
- **Analyse** data: quote figures, compare with the 2% target, and link changes to a cause in the source.
- **Evaluate**: take each group named, give a loser and a possible winner, and finish with a judgement on what the outcome depends on.
