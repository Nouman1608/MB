This guide covers section 2.1.3 a of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026: "the impact of policies and the trade-off between macroeconomic objectives". The specification names four pairs: unemployment and inflation, economic growth and inflation, economic growth and environmental protection, and inflation and the current account on the balance of payments. It sits in Paper 2, Macroeconomics and the Global Economy. All countries and figures below are invented.

The [study guide on supply-side policy and trade-offs](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-supply-side-policy-and-trade-offs-between-objectives/) explains the basic mechanism behind each of the four trade-offs. This page goes one step further: it takes each **policy** in turn and traces what it does to all four pairs at once, then shows how to judge which policy a government should choose. Condense it with the [revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-relationships-between-objectives-and-policies-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-relationships-between-objectives-and-policies-practice/).

## Why the objectives pull against each other

Most short-run policies work by changing **total spending** in the economy. Spending affects four things together:

- **Output** (growth), because firms produce more when they sell more.
- **Jobs** (unemployment), because firms need more workers to produce more.
- **Prices** (inflation), because spending can grow faster than firms can expand.
- **Imports** (the current account), because part of any extra spending goes on foreign goods.

More output also tends to mean more resource use and pollution, which links growth to the environment. So a single change in spending moves all of these at once. That is the root of every trade-off in 2.1.3: the policy cannot pick which effects it has.

| Policy direction | Growth | Unemployment | Inflation | Current account | Environment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expansionary (raise spending) | Rises | Falls | Rises | Worsens | More pressure |
| Contractionary (cut spending) | Slows | Rises | Falls | Improves | Less pressure |

Read the table as tendencies, not certainties. How big each effect is depends on the state of the economy, which is where your evaluation comes from.

## Fiscal policy and the trade-offs

**Expansionary fiscal policy** means higher government spending, lower taxes, or both. A tax cut raises households' disposable income; more spending raises demand for goods and services.

- Growth and unemployment improve as firms raise output and hire.
- If the economy is close to full capacity, the extra demand pulls prices up (demand-pull inflation).
- Some of the extra income is spent on imports, so the current account worsens.
- The fiscal deficit usually widens, because spending rises while tax revenue falls.

**Contractionary fiscal policy** does the reverse. It can cut inflation and improve the current account, but at the cost of slower growth and higher unemployment.

Fiscal policy also has a special link to the environment. An **environmental tax**, such as a tax on fuel or on polluting output, makes pollution more expensive, so firms and households cut it back. But the tax raises firms' costs. Some of that is passed on in prices, which is **cost-push inflation**, and higher costs can slow output. So one fiscal tool can improve the environment while worsening two other objectives.

**Worked example: an environmental tax and the price index (Country K, invented).** Fuel and energy make up 10% of the weights in Country K's consumer price index. A new tax raises energy prices by 8%. Other prices do not change.

```
Contribution to inflation = weight x price change
                          = 0.10 x 8%
                          = 0.8 percentage points
```

If inflation was 2.0% before, it becomes about 2.8% for that year. Emissions fall, but the inflation objective is pushed further from a 2% target. This is the growth-environment trade-off showing up as an inflation cost too.

## Monetary policy and the trade-offs

The central bank sets the **interest rate**. A rise makes borrowing dearer and saving more attractive; a cut does the reverse.

**Worked example: an interest rate rise (Country M, invented).** Inflation is 6%, well above the 2% target. The central bank raises the interest rate from 2% to 4%. One year later:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | 6.0% | 3.5% |
| Real GDP growth | 4.0% | 1.5% |
| Unemployment rate | 4.0% | 5.5% |
| Current account (% of GDP) | -3.0% | -2.0% |

Inflation fell by 2.5 percentage points, growth slowed by 2.5 percentage points and unemployment rose by 1.5 percentage points. The chain:

1. Loans and mortgages cost more, so households borrow and spend less; saving earns more.
2. Firms delay investment because borrowing is expensive.
3. Lower total spending reduces demand-pull pressure, so inflation falls.
4. Firms sell less, so they produce less and hire fewer workers.
5. Households buy fewer imports, so the current account deficit narrows.

Notice that the inflation-current account pair moved in the **same good direction** here: lower inflation and a smaller deficit. That is consistent with the trade-off in the specification, which runs the other way: higher inflation tends to **worsen** the current account, because exports become less price competitive. So cutting inflation can help both. The cost falls on growth and jobs.

There is one complication worth a sentence in a long answer. Higher interest rates can attract money from abroad and push up the exchange rate (section 2.2.3). An appreciation makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, which works **against** the current account improvement. Which effect is larger depends on how much the currency rises.

Monetary policy has little direct effect on the environment, except through output: slower growth usually means less pollution, which is no comfort to the unemployed.

**Asset purchasing** by a central bank (buying bonds to push more money into the economy) works like an interest rate cut, so it carries the same trade-offs as expansionary policy.

## Supply-side policy: easing the trade-offs

Supply-side policy raises the economy's **productive potential** rather than spending. If capacity grows alongside demand, output and jobs can rise without the same pressure on prices. Training, infrastructure and deregulation can also make exports more competitive, helping the current account. This is why supply-side policy is often described as the way to reduce several trade-offs together.

Its limits are what decide an evaluation:

- **Time lags.** Training takes years to raise productivity, and infrastructure takes years to build. It cannot cut inflation that is high now.
- **Cost.** Infrastructure and training use government funds, widening the fiscal deficit unless other spending is cut.
- **Some policies sharpen other trade-offs.** Deregulation of an industry can raise output but may relax environmental rules. Lower business taxes may raise investment, but the extra output may still be polluting.

## Government controls and the environment

Regulation, legislation, fines and pollution permits (section 2.1.2 d) target environmental protection directly. They show the growth-environment trade-off most clearly: a firm forced to fit cleaner equipment or buy permits faces higher costs, which may reduce output, hold back hiring or raise prices. Permits that can be traded allow cuts to happen where they are cheapest, which lowers the cost to growth, but the total limit still binds.

## Choosing a policy: the questions that decide

When a question asks which policy a government should use, judge it against these points:

1. **What is the main problem?** A government facing 9% inflation will accept higher unemployment more readily than one facing 12% unemployment and 1% inflation.
2. **What kind of inflation is it?** Contractionary policy deals well with demand-pull inflation. With cost-push inflation (for example, a rise in world oil prices), cutting demand lowers prices only slowly and costs many jobs.
3. **How much spare capacity is there?** In a recession, expansion raises output with little inflation. Near full employment, the same expansion is mostly inflation.
4. **How fast must it work?** Interest rate changes act within months to a couple of years; supply-side policies take years.
5. **Can policies be combined?** A **policy mix**, such as higher interest rates now plus training spending for the longer term, can control inflation while protecting future jobs.

## Common mistakes

- Treating each trade-off in isolation when the question names a policy: a policy affects all four pairs, and the best answers say so.
- Assuming lower inflation always means a worse current account. In the specification's trade-off, higher inflation worsens the current account, so cutting inflation tends to help it.
- Forgetting that environmental taxes and regulations raise costs, so they can add to inflation as well as slowing growth.
- Describing supply-side policy as a cure for every trade-off without mentioning time lags or cost.
- Comparing two rates with percentage change when percentage points are needed.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain:** one statement of fact plus two linked expansion points, in the context given. "A rise in interest rates makes borrowing dearer, so households spend less on credit, so firms sell less and hire fewer workers."
- **Analyse:** a developed chain from the policy to at least two objectives, showing the trade-off between them.
- **Assess:** use the data provided to weigh factors, for example the size of the fall in inflation against the rise in unemployment.
- **Evaluate:** reach a supported conclusion. Say which objective should take priority **in this case** and what the result depends on, such as spare capacity or the type of inflation.

Always quote the figures in the extract and state each change in percentage points when comparing rates.
