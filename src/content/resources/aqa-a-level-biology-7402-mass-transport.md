## Haemoglobin and the dissociation curve

Haemoglobin is a protein with quaternary structure: four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing an iron ion that binds one oxygen molecule. Inside red blood cells it **loads** oxygen where the partial pressure of oxygen is high (the lungs) and **unloads** it where the partial pressure is low (respiring tissues).

**Cooperative binding.** The first oxygen molecule binds with difficulty. Its binding changes the shape of the haemoglobin molecule, making it easier for the second and third to bind. This is why the dissociation curve is S-shaped: a small rise in partial pressure in the middle range causes a large rise in saturation.

**The Bohr effect.** Respiring tissues release carbon dioxide, which lowers the pH of the blood. This changes the shape of haemoglobin and lowers its affinity for oxygen. The curve shifts to the **right**: at any partial pressure, saturation is lower, so more oxygen is unloaded where it is needed. In the lungs, carbon dioxide diffuses out, affinity rises, and loading is easier.

**Different haemoglobins.** Questions give unfamiliar animals and ask you to reason from the curve:

- Curve to the **left** (higher affinity): loads oxygen at low partial pressures. Suits animals in low-oxygen environments, such as those living at high altitude or in burrows, and the fetus, which must load oxygen from the mother's blood.
- Curve to the **right** (lower affinity): unloads oxygen more readily. Suits active animals or small mammals with a high metabolic rate per gram.

**Worked example (invented data).** Blood leaves the lungs 97% saturated. At the partial pressure of resting muscle it is 75% saturated; in exercising muscle, with more carbon dioxide, it is 40% saturated.

```
Oxygen unloaded at rest       = 97 − 75 = 22% of capacity
Oxygen unloaded in exercise   = 97 − 40 = 57% of capacity
57 ÷ 22 = 2.6 times as much oxygen released per volume of blood
```

## The circulation and the heart

Mammals have a **double circulation**: blood passes through the heart twice per circuit, once to the lungs and once to the body.

| Organ | Vessel in | Vessel out |
|---|---|---|
| Heart | Vena cava (to right atrium); pulmonary vein (to left atrium) | Pulmonary artery (from right ventricle); aorta (from left ventricle) |
| Lungs | Pulmonary artery | Pulmonary vein |
| Kidneys | Renal artery | Renal vein |

The **coronary arteries** branch from the aorta and supply the heart muscle itself with oxygenated blood.

Gross structure: two atria above two ventricles; atrioventricular valves between each atrium and ventricle; semilunar valves at the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery. The left ventricle wall is thicker than the right because it pumps blood at higher pressure around the whole body.

## The cardiac cycle

Valves open and close only because of pressure differences. A valve opens when pressure behind it is higher than pressure in front, and closes when the difference reverses.

| Stage | What happens | Valves |
|---|---|---|
| Atrial systole | Atria contract; ventricles relaxed; blood pushed into ventricles | Atrioventricular open; semilunar closed |
| Ventricular systole | Ventricles contract; ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, then above arterial pressure | Atrioventricular close first; semilunar then open |
| Diastole | Atria and ventricles relax; ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure, then below atrial pressure | Semilunar close; atrioventricular open as atria refill |

**Reading a pressure graph.** Find where two lines cross. Where ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, the atrioventricular valve closes. Where it rises above aortic pressure, the semilunar valve opens. Where it falls below aortic pressure, the semilunar valve closes. Where it falls below atrial pressure, the atrioventricular valve opens. Between the two crossing points in each case, ventricular volume does not change, because both valves are shut.

To find heart rate from a trace, measure the time for one cycle: a cycle of 0.8 s gives 60 ÷ 0.8 = 75 beats per minute.

## Blood vessels

| Vessel | Structure | Link to function |
|---|---|---|
| Artery | Thick muscle and elastic tissue; folded endothelium; narrow lumen | Withstands high pressure; elastic recoil smooths flow |
| Arteriole | More muscle relative to size | Contracts or relaxes to control blood flow into capillary beds |
| Vein | Wide lumen; thin muscle and elastic layers; valves | Low pressure; valves prevent backflow |
| Capillary | Wall of endothelium one cell thick; narrow lumen; highly branched beds | Short diffusion distance; large surface area; slow flow for exchange |

## Tissue fluid

Tissue fluid bathes cells and is the route by which substances pass between blood and cells.

1. At the arteriole end of a capillary bed, hydrostatic pressure in the capillary is high. It is greater than the tendency of water to move in by osmosis, so water and small solutes are forced out through the capillary wall. Large plasma proteins stay behind.
2. Along the capillary, loss of fluid lowers hydrostatic pressure.
3. At the venule end, hydrostatic pressure is lower and the plasma proteins give the blood a lower water potential than the tissue fluid, so water returns into the capillary by osmosis.
4. Excess tissue fluid drains into the lymphatic system and returns to the blood.

High blood pressure increases fluid forced out; low plasma protein raises the water potential of blood so less returns. Both can cause tissue fluid to build up.

## Mass transport in plants

**Xylem: cohesion-tension.** Water evaporates from mesophyll cells into air spaces and diffuses out through stomata (transpiration). This lowers the water potential of the leaf cells, so water moves into them from the xylem. Water molecules are held together by hydrogen bonds (**cohesion**), so a continuous column is pulled up the xylem under **tension**. Adhesion to the xylem walls also helps. Evidence: tree trunks become slightly narrower during the day when transpiration and tension are highest; when a xylem vessel is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out.

**Phloem: mass flow hypothesis.**

1. At a **source** (for example a photosynthesising leaf), sucrose is actively loaded into sieve tube elements via companion cells.
2. This lowers the water potential of the sieve tube, so water enters by osmosis from the xylem, raising hydrostatic pressure.
3. At a **sink** (for example roots or growing tissue), sucrose is removed and used in respiration or stored, raising the water potential; water leaves by osmosis and pressure falls.
4. The pressure difference moves the solution from source to sink by mass flow.

**Tracer and ringing experiments.** Supplying a leaf with carbon dioxide containing a radioactive carbon isotope labels the sugars made in photosynthesis; an autoradiograph then shows the label in the phloem, and later in sinks. Removing a ring of bark, including the phloem but not the xylem, causes the stem to swell above the ring, and the fluid there contains sugars: evidence that the phloem carries sugars downwards.

| Evidence for mass flow | Evidence against, or not explained |
|---|---|
| Sap is released under pressure when phloem is pierced | Different solutes move at different speeds |
| Sucrose concentration is higher at sources than at sinks | Sucrose moves to different sinks in different directions at the same time |
| Metabolic inhibitors that stop ATP production stop translocation | Sieve plates would be expected to slow mass flow |

## Common mistakes

- Saying the Bohr effect shifts the curve to the left.
- Saying valves are opened by muscles: they move because of pressure differences.
- Saying "the left ventricle pumps more blood" when it pumps at a higher pressure.
- Forgetting that plasma proteins stay in the capillary and cause the lower water potential.
- Saying water is "sucked up" the xylem without cohesion and tension.
- Saying sucrose moves in the xylem.
