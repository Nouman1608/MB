## Section A: knowledge

**1.** Give two problems that surgeons faced before the introduction of anaesthetics in the 1840s. **[2]**

**2.** Name three diseases whose causes were identified by Koch or other microbe hunters in the nineteenth century. **[3]**

**3.** State two things that the 1875 Public Health Act required local councils to do. **[2]**

## Section B: utility, significance, comparison and factors

**Source A** *(invented)* -- A description of a cartoon imagined as published in a London magazine in July 1858. Members of Parliament lean out of the windows of the Houses of Parliament, holding handkerchiefs over their noses. Below them, on a filthy river full of dead fish and rubbish, a skeleton labelled "Cholera" rows a small boat. The caption reads: "The Members' new neighbour."

**4.** How useful is Source A to an historian studying public health problems in nineteenth-century London? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. **[8]**

**Source B** *(invented)* -- From a letter imagined as written by a London surgeon to a medical journal in 1870.
"Mr Lister would have us operate in a fog of carbolic acid, which stings the eyes and cracks the skin of every hand in the theatre. I have operated for thirty years with clean water and a quick knife. I see no proof of these invisible germs that cannot be explained by bad air and a poor constitution."

**5.** How useful is Source B to an historian studying opposition to antiseptic surgery? Explain your answer using Source B and your contextual knowledge. **[8]**

**6.** Explain the significance of Pasteur's germ theory for medicine in Britain. **[8]**

**7.** Explain the significance of the 1875 Public Health Act. **[8]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which the work of Louis Pasteur and the work of Robert Koch were similar. **[8]**

**9.** Explain two ways in which the 1848 and 1875 Public Health Acts were different. **[8]**

**10.** Explain two ways in which the reactions to anaesthetics in the 1840s and to antiseptics in the 1860s and 1870s were similar. **[8]**

**11.** "Science and technology have been the main factor in the development of surgery in Britain since c1000." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer with reference to science and technology and other factors. **[16 + 4 SPaG]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** Utility: content linked to the enquiry and supported by knowledge [4]; provenance evaluated for what it makes the source useful for [4]. Significance: significance at the time, explained with evidence [4]; significance over time [4]. Similarity or difference: each of two ways identified [1], supported with evidence from both [2] and explained [1]. Factor essay: the stated factor developed with evidence [6]; other factors developed [6]; a substantiated judgement [4]; spelling, punctuation and grammar [4].

**1.** Any two, [1] each: pain, so operations had to be very fast; infection after the operation; blood loss; patients dying of shock.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: anthrax; tuberculosis; cholera; typhoid; diphtheria; plague.

**3.** Any two, [1] each: provide clean water; provide drainage and sewage disposal; appoint a Medical Officer of Health.

**4.** Content and context [4]: it shows the filthy state of the Thames, used as an open sewer, and links it with cholera; the MPs' handkerchiefs refer to the Great Stink of summer 1858, when the smell disrupted Parliament. My knowledge confirms that London had cholera epidemics in 1848-49 and 1853-54 and that Snow had linked cholera to polluted water in 1854. The cartoon still reflects miasma ideas, implying that smell itself was dangerous. Provenance [4]: a London magazine in July 1858 was commenting at the height of the Great Stink, so it is useful for showing how public health had become a political issue that MPs could not ignore; Parliament soon backed Bazalgette's sewer scheme. As a cartoon it exaggerates for effect and shows only London's river, not housing or water supply in other industrial towns.

**5.** Content and context [4]: it shows practical objections to Lister's carbolic spray, that it stung the eyes and cracked the skin, and a refusal to believe in invisible germs, preferring miasma ("bad air"). This fits my knowledge that Lister published in 1867 and that many surgeons were slow to accept germ theory, which Pasteur had published only in 1861. Provenance [4]: written to a medical journal by an experienced surgeon, it is useful for how some respected professionals argued against antiseptics in public, and his "thirty years" shows that older surgeons trusted their own experience. It is less useful for how widespread this opposition was, since it gives one surgeon's view.

**6.** At the time [4]: Pasteur's germ theory (1861) disproved spontaneous generation and showed that microbes cause decay; it inspired Lister's antiseptic surgery from 1865 and Koch's identification of specific germs; Pasteur's vaccines for anthrax (1881) and rabies (1885) followed. But at first it changed little in everyday treatment in Britain, where many doctors doubted it. Over time [4]: it became the basis of modern medicine: it led to aseptic surgery, gave scientific support for clean water and sewers, and led to the search for magic bullets, starting with Salvarsan (1909), and later to antibiotics such as penicillin.

**7.** At the time [4]: it made it compulsory for councils to provide clean water, drainage and sewage disposal and to appoint Medical Officers of Health, unlike the permissive 1848 Act; this improved conditions in many towns in the following decades. Over time [4]: it marked a turning point in the role of government, moving away from *laissez-faire*, and paved the way for later state involvement in health, such as the Liberal reforms of the early twentieth century and eventually the NHS (1948).

**8.** Way one [4]: both were scientists working on the causes of disease [1]; Pasteur showed that microbes caused decay (1861) and Koch showed that specific microbes caused specific diseases such as anthrax (1876) [2]; so both helped replace miasma and spontaneous generation with germ theory [1]. Way two [4]: both led teams supported by their governments in national rivalry [1]; Pasteur's French team and Koch's German team competed after the war of 1870-71, for example over cholera in the 1880s [2]; so national pride and funding sped up both men's discoveries [1].

**9.** Way one [4]: permissive versus compulsory [1]; the 1848 Act allowed towns to set up local boards of health but required action only where the death rate exceeded 23 per 1,000, while the 1875 Act required all councils to provide clean water and sewage disposal [2]; so the 1875 Act had far greater effect across the country [1]. Way two [4]: different context and evidence [1]; the 1848 Act came after Chadwick's report and a cholera epidemic, when miasma was still widely believed, while the 1875 Act followed Snow's work, germ theory and the 1867 extension of the vote [2]; so in 1875 government had stronger scientific and political reasons to intervene [1].

**10.** Way one [4]: both faced practical problems [1]; chloroform's dose was hard to judge and Hannah Greener died in 1848, while Lister's carbolic spray irritated skin and slowed operations [2]; so surgeons had real reasons to be cautious about both [1]. Way two [4]: both faced resistance from attitudes and beliefs [1]; some objected that pain in childbirth was natural or God-given, while many surgeons doubted the germ theory on which antiseptics rested [2]; so acceptance depended on changing ideas as well as evidence, helped by Queen Victoria's use of chloroform (1853) and later the move to aseptic methods [1].

**11.** Science and technology [6]: chloroform (1847) and carbolic acid (1865) depended on chemistry; steam sterilisation made aseptic surgery possible; in the twentieth century X-rays, blood storage, transplant techniques, lasers and keyhole surgery transformed operations. Other factors [6]: **war** gave surgeons experience, from Paré's ligatures and ointments on the battlefield in the sixteenth century to plastic surgery by Harold Gillies and blood banks in the world wars; **individuals** such as Simpson and Lister drove change; **communication** spread ideas, from Islamic surgical texts in the Middle Ages to Lister reading Pasteur; **religion and superstition** slowed change, as in objections to anaesthetics. Judgement [4]: for example, science and technology were essential to the nineteenth- and twentieth-century breakthroughs, but war often provided the conditions and urgency that made new technology develop and spread; an answer must cover all four parts of the thematic study. SPaG [4]: accurate spelling of terms such as anaesthetic, antiseptic and chloroform.
