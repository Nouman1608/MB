## The specification points

1. Explain what is meant by 'product'.
2. Evaluate the product strategy of a business using the product life cycle, value analysis, Boston matrix, Ansoff's matrix and product portfolio analysis.
3. Explain what is meant by a unique selling point (USP), identify the USP of a business, and explain how and why it is used.
4. Explain what is meant by a 'brand' and 'branding', and evaluate the importance of a brand.
5. Evaluate the importance of product as an element of the marketing mix.
6. Evaluate the impact and importance of product differentiation.
7. Recommend and justify a product mix as part of a marketing strategy.

## Core recall

### What a product is

Anything offered to meet a need: core benefit, features, design, quality, packaging, brand and support (warranty, service). Customers buy a package of benefits.

### Tools

| Tool | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Product life cycle | Stage of each product; when to launch, support or withdraw |
| Value analysis | Cost of each feature against customer value |
| Boston matrix | Products by market growth and relative market share |
| Ansoff's matrix | Growth options: penetration, product development, market development, diversification |
| Portfolio analysis | Balance of cash earners and future earners |

### Boston matrix

Relative market share = own share ÷ largest rival's share.

| | High relative share (above 1) | Low relative share |
|---|---|---|
| High growth | Star: invest to keep the lead | Question mark: invest or drop |
| Low growth | Cash cow: milk for cash | Dog: sell, withdraw or keep if it supports other sales |

Limits: two measures only; the definition of the market changes results; ignores links between products; does not say how to improve.

### Value analysis

- List each feature's cost and the share of customers who value it.
- Cut or redesign features that cost a lot but are valued little.
- Check by testing: surveys may differ from behaviour; a feature may support the image.

### USP

- A feature or benefit that sets a product apart and gives a reason to choose it.
- Identify by asking customers what the business does better and whether rivals can copy it.
- Used in promotion, packaging, brand message; supports a premium price.
- Lasts only while rivals cannot copy it.

### Brand and branding

| Benefit | Limit |
|---|---|
| Premium price | Slow and costly to build |
| Loyalty and repeat purchase | One scandal damages all products under the name |
| Easier new launches | Loyalty falls if quality drops |
| Protection from price competition | Value depends on consistent delivery |
| Asset for shareholders | |

### Differentiation

Makes the product different in customers' eyes (quality, design, features, service, image). Reduces price competition and supports a higher price. Must be real or well communicated, and can be copied.

### Product mix

- Width = number of product lines. Depth = variants in each line.
- Recommend: keep, develop or drop products, link to objectives, justify with figures and state a risk.

## Quick worked reminder

Kestrel Foods (invented): relative share Oat Crunch 18 ÷ 9 = 2.0 with market growth 12%: star. Classic Muesli 30 ÷ 15 = 2.0 with growth 2%: cash cow. Fruit Bites 4 ÷ 20 = 0.2 with growth 9%: question mark. Rice Cakes 3 ÷ 25 = 0.12 with growth −1%: dog. Sales £12.5m, of which Classic Muesli is 48% and Oat Crunch 36%.

Aria kettle: price £50, cost £28. Removing a £6 app feature that 12% value: cost £22, margin 44% to 56%. Sales 20,000 fall 3% to 19,400. Contribution £440,000 becomes 19,400 × £28 = £543,200.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Product | A good or service offered to meet a need, with its features, brand and support |
| Relative market share | Own market share divided by the largest rival's share |
| Star | High growth, high relative share |
| Cash cow | Low growth, high relative share |
| Question mark | High growth, low relative share |
| Dog | Low growth, low relative share |
| USP | A feature that makes a product stand out from rivals |
| Brand | A name, symbol or reputation that identifies a product |
| Product mix | The full range of products a business sells |

## Exam traps

- Calculate relative share; do not just copy the labels.
- A dog may still earn cash or support other sales.
- A USP can be copied.
- Branding is the whole identity, not just advertising.
- State the objective before recommending a mix.

## Command words

- **Explain:** define the term (product, USP, brand) and give an example.
- **Evaluate:** apply the tool (Boston matrix, value analysis) with figures, then weigh its limits.
- **Recommend and justify:** state which products to keep, develop or drop, give figures and a risk.
- **Calculate:** show relative market share and contribution step by step.

## Self-test

1. Define a product.
2. Define relative market share.
3. Own share 12%, largest rival 24%. Find relative share.
4. Name the four Boston matrix categories.
5. What does value analysis compare?
6. Give one limit of the Boston matrix.
7. Define a USP.
8. Give two benefits of a strong brand.
9. A branded product sells at £4.50 and an own-label one at £3.00. Find the brand premium.
10. Explain product differentiation.
11. Explain the width of a product mix.
12. Why might a business keep a dog?

### Answers

1. A good or service, with its features, design, brand and support, offered to meet a customer need.
2. A business's share of the market divided by the share of the largest rival.
3. 12 ÷ 24 = **0.5**.
4. Star, cash cow, question mark and dog.
5. The cost of each feature with the value customers place on it.
6. Any one: only two measures; market definition changes the result; ignores links between products; does not say how to improve.
7. A feature or benefit that sets a product apart and gives customers a reason to choose it.
8. Any two: premium price; loyalty; easier launches; protection from price competition; asset value.
9. (4.50 ÷ 3.00 − 1) × 100 = **50%**.
10. Making a product different from rivals in customers' eyes through quality, design, features, service or image.
11. The number of product lines the business sells.
12. It may still make a contribution to fixed costs or bring customers who buy other products.
