These notes cover **section 3.2.4.1 Why countries trade and the importance of international trade to the UK** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: "benefits of trade to countries", "UK's exports and imports", "the importance of trade to economies" and "the advantages of trade and the consequences of global interdependence to the UK economy". They add specialisation calculations, a breakdown of UK service trade and the ways interdependence spreads shocks to the shorter [international trade and exchange rates revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-international-trade-and-exchange-rates-revision-notes/). Full explanations are in the [why countries trade study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-why-countries-trade-and-the-importance-of-international-trade/); test yourself with the [why countries trade practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-why-countries-trade-and-the-importance-of-international-trade-practice/). Calculation figures are invented.

## Condensed recall

- Countries differ in resources, climate, skills, capital and technology.
- **Specialisation** + **trade** → more total output from the same resources → higher living standards.
- Benefits: access to goods not available at home, lower prices, more choice, bigger markets and economies of scale, competition, spread of technology, export jobs.
- The UK is a major **exporter of services**; it imports more goods than it exports.
- **Global interdependence**: economies rely on each other for goods, inputs, markets and investment, so events abroad affect the UK.
- Trade has losers as well as winners: firms and workers that cannot compete with imports.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Specialisation | Concentrating on producing the goods and services a country produces best, and trading for others |
| Export | A good or service sold to buyers in another country |
| Import | A good or service bought from producers in another country |
| Supply chain | The sequence of firms, often in several countries, that turns materials into a finished product |
| Global interdependence | Economies relying on one another, so changes in one affect others |
| Economies of scale | Lower average costs as a firm produces more, which larger export markets make possible |
| Import penetration | The share of a home market supplied by imports |

## Gains from specialisation: method

1. Work out each country's output **without** trade (usually resources split evenly).
2. Work out output **with** specialisation (each country makes only the good it is better at).
3. Compare the **totals**: specialisation gives more of both goods, or more of one and no less of the other.
4. Show a swap that leaves **both** countries better off.

**Example.** Each country has 6 workers. One worker makes:

| | Bikes | Boats |
|---|---|---|
| Country C | 8 | 2 |
| Country D | 2 | 4 |

- Without trade (3 workers on each good): C makes 24 bikes and 6 boats; D makes 6 bikes and 12 boats. Total **30 bikes, 18 boats**.
- With specialisation: C makes 48 bikes; D makes 24 boats. Total **48 bikes, 24 boats**: 18 more bikes and 6 more boats.

## UK trade by type

| UK service exports | UK goods exports | Main UK imports |
|---|---|---|
| Financial services and insurance | Cars | Machinery and electrical equipment |
| Professional and business services | Machinery, including aircraft engines | Vehicles |
| Computer and telecommunications services | Medicines and chemicals | Fuels |
| Education and tourism (foreign students and visitors paying in the UK) | Fuels and precious metals | Food, clothing and consumer electronics |
| Royalties for intellectual property, such as music and film | | Travel services (UK residents holidaying abroad) |

## How interdependence spreads shocks

| Shock abroad | Route into the UK | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Recession in a trading partner | Fewer UK exports bought | Lower UK output and jobs |
| Supply-chain disruption | Components arrive late or not at all | UK production delayed; shortages; higher prices |
| Rise in world energy or food prices | Dearer imports | Higher UK inflation; higher costs for firms |
| Fast growth abroad | More UK exports bought | Higher UK output and jobs |

## Worked calculations

**1. Import penetration.** In an invented home market 2.4 million bikes are sold, of which 1.8 million are imported. Import penetration = 1.8 ÷ 2.4 × 100 = **75%**.

**2. Exports as a share of GDP.** An invented country exports 120 billion of goods and services and has GDP of 400 billion. Exports = 120 ÷ 400 × 100 = **30%** of GDP.

**3. Growth in exports.** Service exports rise from 30 billion to 36 billion: (36 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = **20%**.

## Exam traps

- Specialisation does not mean producing only one product; it means concentrating on what a country does best.
- Foreign students' fees and foreign tourists' spending in the UK are **exports** of services.
- Higher import penetration is not automatically bad: consumers gain cheaper goods, even if some domestic producers lose.
- "Interdependence" questions want both sides: gains and vulnerability.
- Show **totals** in specialisation questions; the gain is in combined output.

## Self-test

1. State what is meant by specialisation.
2. Give two benefits of trade for consumers.
3. Give one benefit of trade for firms.
4. Name two types of service the UK exports.
5. Name two types of good the UK imports.
6. Is an Indian student paying fees to a UK university a UK export or import?
7. What is a supply chain?
8. Explain how a recession in a major trading partner affects UK unemployment.
9. In a market, 900,000 of 1.5 million cars sold are imported. Find import penetration.
10. Two countries with 4 workers each: Country E's worker makes 6 hats or 2 coats; Country F's makes 1 hat or 3 coats. Find total output without trade (2 workers on each good) and with full specialisation.
11. Give one group in the UK that may lose from more imports.
12. Why is trade especially important for a small country?

### Answers

1. Concentrating on producing what a country produces best and trading for other goods and services.
2. Any two: lower prices, more choice, access to goods not produced at home, better quality through competition.
3. Any one: larger markets and economies of scale; cheaper inputs; access to new technology.
4. Any two: financial services, insurance, business and professional services, computer services, education, tourism, royalties.
5. Any two: machinery, vehicles, fuels, food, clothing, consumer electronics.
6. An export of services.
7. The sequence of firms, often in several countries, that turns materials into a finished product.
8. Incomes there fall, so they buy fewer UK exports; UK exporting firms cut output and jobs, so UK unemployment rises.
9. 0.9 ÷ 1.5 × 100 = 60%.
10. Without trade: E makes 12 hats and 4 coats; F makes 2 hats and 6 coats; total 14 hats and 10 coats. With specialisation: E makes 24 hats; F makes 12 coats; total 24 hats and 12 coats.
11. Workers and owners of UK firms that cannot compete with cheaper imports.
12. It cannot produce the full range of goods it needs, and its home market is too small for firms to gain economies of scale.
