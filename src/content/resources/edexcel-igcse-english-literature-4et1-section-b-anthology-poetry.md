This guide goes deeper on one part of Component 1 of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Literature (4ET1): **Section B, Anthology Poetry**. It sits between the unseen poem and the modern
prose essay on Paper 1. For the paper as a whole, start with the
[Poetry and Modern Prose study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-poetry-and-modern-prose/).

## What Section B covers

You study the sixteen poems in Part 3 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology,
listed in full on the Poetry and Modern Prose study guide. In the exam you answer **one question
from a choice of two**. Each asks you to **compare two poems**: often one question names both poems,
while the other names one poem and lets you choose the second from the anthology. The section is
worth **30 marks** and the paper recommends **40 minutes**. Marlbridge's Paper 1 pages describe the
anthology poems as provided in the exam, so check the front cover of the paper and your centre's
guidance for your own series.

The section is assessed on two objectives:

- **AO2**: analysis of language, form and structure and the meanings and effects they create, the
  same skill as Section A but applied to poems you know well.
- **AO3**: exploring links and connections between texts. In practice this means comparison, sustained
  through the whole essay.

The anthology list is set by the specification and changes only when Pearson revises it, not every
series. Confirm the list for your specification issue before you revise.

## Why comparison changes everything

An answer that analyses Poem 1 brilliantly, then Poem 2 brilliantly, has done the AO2 work but only
half of the task. AO3 credit comes from the **relationship** between the poems: what is similar, what
differs, and why each poet's choices produce a different effect. The examiner should never be able to
cut your essay into two separate halves.

Comparison works on three levels, and strong answers use all three:

1. **Ideas and attitudes**: what each poem says about the question's focus.
2. **Methods**: how each poet uses language, form and structure to say it.
3. **Effects**: how the reader's response differs as a result.

## Theme clusters across the sixteen poems

Revising in pairs and clusters prepares you for both question types. The groupings below are starting
points; most poems belong in more than one.

| Focus | Poems that work well together | A possible line of comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Parents and children | *Piano*, *Poem at Thirty-Nine*, *Do not go gentle into that good night*, *If*, *Prayer Before Birth* | Remembering a parent, pleading with one, advising a child, or a plea from an unborn child |
| Childhood | *Half-past Two*, *Hide and Seek*, *Piano* | A child's limited perspective presented by an adult voice |
| Love and loss | *Sonnet 116*, *Remember*, *La Belle Dame sans Merci* | Love as permanent, selfless or destructive |
| Power and control | *My Last Duchess*, *La Belle Dame sans Merci*, *The Tyger* | Who holds power, and how the form reveals it |
| Identity and language | *Search For My Tongue*, *Half-caste*, *Blessing* | Culture, belonging and a challenge to the reader |
| Death and suffering | *Do not go gentle into that good night*, *Remember*, *War Photographer* | Resisting, accepting or witnessing death |

For each pair you revise, write three lines: a shared idea, one key difference in attitude, and one key
difference in method.

## Form as a point of comparison

Several anthology poems use recognisable forms, which give you ready-made comparisons:

- **Sonnets**: *Sonnet 116* is a Shakespearean sonnet ending in a rhyming couplet; *Remember* is a
  Petrarchan sonnet whose turn comes after the eighth line.
- **Villanelle**: *Do not go gentle into that good night*, with two refrains repeated throughout.
- **Dramatic monologue**: *My Last Duchess*, in which the speaker reveals more than he intends.
- **Ballad**: *La Belle Dame sans Merci*, telling a story through question and answer.
- **Free verse and broken form**: *Half-caste* and *Search For My Tongue* challenge conventional
  layout, spelling or language as part of their meaning.

Saying "both are sonnets" is a start. Saying how each poet uses the sonnet's turn differently is
comparison.

## Worked example: a named-pair question

**Question:** Compare how the poets present lasting love in *Sonnet 116* and *Remember*. [30]

**Overview:** Both poems imagine love tested by time and death, but Shakespeare defines love as
unchanging, while Rossetti's speaker shows her love by letting go of the wish to be remembered.

**Plan (by point, not by poem):**

1. **Constancy versus release.** Shakespeare's metaphor of love as "an ever-fixed mark" presents it as
   immovable; Rossetti's speaker first pleads to be remembered, then gives that plea up.
2. **How each sonnet turns.** Shakespeare builds an argument that ends in the confident challenge of
   the couplet; Rossetti's volta after line 8 changes the poem's direction from request to permission.
3. **Time and death.** Both personify or confront time, but Shakespeare's love outlasts it, while
   Rossetti accepts that memory may fade and prioritises the beloved's happiness.
4. **Voice and the reader.** Shakespeare's speaker addresses the world with certainty; Rossetti's
   speaker addresses one person directly, which makes her selflessness feel intimate.

**Sample comparative sentence:** "Where Shakespeare's couplet stakes his whole credibility on love's
permanence, Rossetti's sestet quietly reverses her own request, so lasting love becomes not being
remembered but wanting the beloved to be happy."

Notice the shape: a comparative connective ("Where"), a method in each poem (couplet; sestet), and a
difference in effect.

## Worked example: choosing your own second poem

If a question names *Piano* and asks you to compare how memory is presented with a poem of your choice,
pick a poem that genuinely shares the focus **and** offers a contrast in method. *Poem at Thirty-Nine*
would allow you to compare two adult speakers remembering a parent; *Half-past Two* would let you
compare memories of childhood told through very different tones. Ask yourself:

- Does the second poem give me at least three comparable points?
- Does it differ enough in method (form, tone, voice) to give me something to analyse?
- Do I know it well enough to find short quotations quickly?

Choose in the first two minutes and do not change your mind.

## Writing the comparison

- **Open with a comparative overview**: one or two sentences covering both poems.
- **Use three or four paragraphs**, each built round one point of comparison and moving between both
  poems.
- **Use comparative connectives**: whereas, similarly, in contrast, both, however, unlike.
- **Keep quotations short**: a word or phrase from each poem is enough for a precise point.
- **Finish with a judgement**, not a summary: which presentation is more hopeful, more unsettling or
  more convincing, and why?

## Common mistakes

- Writing about the poems separately and adding a comparison only in the final paragraph.
- Comparing only content ("both are about love") without comparing methods.
- Choosing a second poem with a weak connection because it is a personal favourite.
- Retelling a poem's story, especially narrative poems such as *La Belle Dame sans Merci* or *My Last
  Duchess*.
- Treating poets' biographies or historical background as the main material: the objectives here are
  AO2 and AO3, so context should only support a point about the poems.
- Spending more than 40 minutes because the poems are familiar, leaving Section C short.

## Exam technique and command words

"**Compare**" means identify similarities and differences and keep them in view together. "**How**"
directs you to methods and effects. If the question names a focus (a relationship, a feeling, an idea),
make sure every paragraph addresses it. Spend about five minutes planning: overview, three or four
comparative points, and two or three short quotations from each poem per point.
