Section 1.5 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 is short in the syllabus but produces some of the longest answers in the theory paper. It asks about the "impact on everyday life" of emerging technologies, and names two: **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** and **extended reality**, which the syllabus splits into **virtual** and **augmented** reality. You are not expected to know how these technologies are built. You are expected to describe what they do for people, in specific areas of life, and to weigh the positive effects against the negative ones.

For condensed recall use the [emerging technologies revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-emerging-technologies-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [emerging technologies practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-emerging-technologies-practice/).

## What "impact on everyday life" means

An impact is an effect on people. A good impact point has three parts: **the technology**, **where it is used**, and **what changes for the person**. Compare:

- Weak: "AI is used in healthcare."
- Strong: "AI software can analyse medical scans and highlight possible signs of disease, so doctors can find problems earlier and treat patients sooner."

The strong version names the use and the effect. Every impact point you write should follow that pattern, and in discussion questions you need effects on both sides.

## Artificial Intelligence (AI)

**Artificial intelligence** is the ability of a computer system to carry out tasks that would normally need human intelligence, such as recognising speech and images, understanding and producing language, learning from data, and making decisions or predictions.

### Where AI affects everyday life

| Area | Example use | Effect on people |
|---|---|---|
| Home | Voice assistants answer questions, set reminders and control smart devices | Tasks done hands-free; helps people with limited mobility |
| Shopping and entertainment | Recommendations based on what a person has bought, watched or listened to | Finds relevant products and content faster; may narrow what people see |
| Communication | Translation software, predictive text and chatbots | People can communicate across languages; quick answers to customer queries at any hour |
| Health | Analysing scans, monitoring patients' readings for warning signs | Earlier detection; doctors' time freed for patients |
| Banking | Detecting unusual card payments that may be fraud | Fraud stopped quickly; occasional genuine payments blocked |
| Education | Systems that adapt practice questions to a learner's level; tools that generate text and images | Personalised learning; risk of work not being the student's own |
| Work | Software that sorts documents, schedules deliveries or answers routine enquiries | Routine tasks automated; some jobs change or are lost, new jobs created |

### Positive impacts of AI

- **Saves time** by automating repetitive tasks, such as sorting emails or answering common questions.
- **Available at all times**: a chatbot does not need breaks.
- **Can spot patterns** in very large amounts of data that people would miss, as in fraud detection or medical scans.
- **Improves accessibility**: speech recognition, captions and image descriptions help people with disabilities.
- **Personalises services**, such as learning apps that adjust to a student's progress.

### Negative impacts of AI

- **Jobs**: some roles, especially routine ones, may be reduced or replaced; workers may need retraining.
- **Privacy**: AI systems often depend on collecting large amounts of personal data about what people do, say and buy.
- **Bias**: if a system learns from data that is unfair or unrepresentative, its decisions can be unfair too.
- **Errors and over-reliance**: AI can give confident but wrong answers, and people may trust it without checking.
- **Misinformation**: AI can produce realistic fake images, video, audio and text, making it harder to know what is genuine.
- **Less human contact**, for example when customers can only reach a chatbot.

## Extended reality (XR)

**Extended reality** is the umbrella term for technologies that change how a person sees and experiences their surroundings using computer-generated content. The syllabus names two types.

### Virtual reality (VR)

**Virtual reality** places the user in a **completely computer-generated environment** that replaces their view of the real world. The user normally wears a **headset** with a screen in front of each eye, and sensors track head movement so that the view changes as they look around. Hand controllers or gloves may let them interact with objects.

Everyday uses:

- **Training** in situations that would be dangerous or costly to practise for real, such as flying an aircraft, operating on a patient or fighting a fire.
- **Education**: virtual visits to historic sites, the inside of the human body or the surface of another planet.
- **Gaming and entertainment**: fully immersive games and films.
- **Design and property**: walking through a building before it is built.

### Augmented reality (AR)

**Augmented reality** **adds computer-generated images or information on top of the user's view of the real world**. The real surroundings stay visible. AR usually runs on a smartphone or tablet using its camera and screen, or on smart glasses.

Everyday uses:

- **Shopping**: seeing how a piece of furniture would look in a room, or trying on glasses or clothes virtually.
- **Navigation**: arrows and directions shown over the camera's view of the street.
- **Education and museums**: labels and models appearing when a camera is pointed at an exhibit or a textbook page.
- **Maintenance and repair**: instructions overlaid on the machine being repaired.
- **Gaming**: characters that appear in the player's real surroundings.

### VR and AR compared

| | Virtual reality | Augmented reality |
|---|---|---|
| Real world visible? | No: replaced completely | Yes: digital content added to it |
| Typical hardware | Headset, sensors, controllers | Smartphone, tablet or smart glasses |
| Typical use | Immersive training, games, virtual visits | Information and objects shown in a real place |
| Where used | Usually in one safe, cleared space | Can be used while moving around |

### Positive impacts of extended reality

- **Safe practice** of dangerous or rare situations, with no risk to people or equipment.
- **Lower costs** of some training and travel, since virtual visits replace real ones.
- **Engaging learning** that makes abstract ideas visible, such as 3D models of molecules.
- **Better buying decisions**, because customers can see a product in their own home first.

### Negative impacts of extended reality

- **Health**: VR can cause motion sickness, headaches and eye strain if used for long periods.
- **Safety**: users of VR cannot see real obstacles; AR users may walk into danger while looking at a screen.
- **Cost**: headsets and powerful computers are expensive, so not everyone has access.
- **Isolation**: long periods in virtual environments may reduce face-to-face social contact.
- **Privacy**: AR devices use cameras that may record people nearby without their knowledge.

## Annotated model answer: a discuss question

*Discuss the impact of augmented reality on everyday life. [6]*

> Augmented reality lets shoppers use a phone camera to see how furniture would look in their own room (1), so they are less likely to buy something that does not fit and need to return it (1). In museums, pointing a tablet at an exhibit can display extra information and 3D models, which can make learning more engaging (1). Navigation apps can show directions over the view of the street, so people find their way more easily in unfamiliar places (1).
>
> However, people using AR while walking may stop paying attention to traffic and obstacles, which can cause accidents (1). AR glasses and phone cameras can record people nearby who have not given permission, raising privacy concerns (1). Devices that can run AR well can be expensive, so not everyone benefits (1).

The answer would reach the full 6 marks because it gives several specific uses with their effects, then several negative effects, rather than listing features. A one-sided answer would be limited however long it was.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up VR and AR. VR replaces the real world; AR adds to it.
- Writing "AI is a robot". AI is software; a robot may or may not use it.
- Giving features instead of impacts, such as "VR uses a headset" when the question asks for an effect on people.
- Writing only positive points in a discuss question.
- Vague negatives like "it is bad for you". Name the problem: motion sickness, eye strain, loss of privacy.
- Using brand names of devices or apps, which earn no marks.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an impact: use, then effect on people.
- **Explain**: add the reason with "so" or "because".
- **Discuss** or **evaluate**: give positives and negatives, ideally from different areas of life, and finish with a short judgement if the question asks for one.
- **Compare** VR and AR: use the "replace versus add" distinction and one hardware difference.
