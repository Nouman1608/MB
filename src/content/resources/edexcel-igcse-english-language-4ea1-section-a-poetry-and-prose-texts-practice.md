## Section A: short questions

Read the poem, then answer questions 1 to 4.

> **The Last Bus**
>
> It comes at ten, or says it does,  
> its windows lit like a row of teeth.  
> My mother waves. I do not wave.  
> I am fifteen and leaving for the city school.
>
> Behind me, the village folds itself  
> into the dark like a letter  
> nobody will read again. Ahead,  
> the road unspools its one grey thought.
>
> I tell myself I will come back.  
> I tell myself.
>
> *(An original poem written for this practice page.)*

**1.** Explain the effect of the simile "like a row of teeth" in line 2. **[2]**

**2.** Comment on the effect of the short sentences in line 3. **[3]**

**3.** Explain how the second stanza presents the speaker's feelings about leaving. Refer to **one**
image. **[3]**

**4.** Explain the effect of the final two-line stanza, including the change in its second line. **[3]**

Read the extract, then answer questions 5 to 8.

> The parcel arrived on a Tuesday, addressed in her grandmother's careful capitals. Mira did not open it.
> She set it on the kitchen table and made tea, and then made more tea, and the parcel sat there through
> both cups like a guest who had not been introduced.
>
> At six her father came home. He saw the parcel, saw her, and sat down without taking off his coat.
> "She posted it in March," he said. "Before the hospital."
>
> Mira cut the string. Inside, wrapped in newspaper, was the blue scarf she had refused, aged nine,
> because it was itchy and old-fashioned. A note was pinned to it. *For when you are cold enough.*
>
> *(An original story extract written for this practice page.)*

**5.** What can you infer about Mira's feelings from the first paragraph? Give **two** points with
evidence. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the effect of the simile "like a guest who had not been introduced". **[2]**

**7.** How does the writer use the father's dialogue to change the reader's understanding? **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the final sentence works as an ending. **[3]**

## Section B: essay questions

Answer each in about 45 minutes. Each is marked out of 30 (AO1 12, AO2 18). In each answer you should
consider the writer's use of language and structure and support your points with references to the
text.

**9.** Explore how Kate Chopin presents a moment of private freedom in "The Story of an Hour". **[30]**

**10.** Explore how Robert Frost presents the reactions of the adults in "Out, Out–". **[30]**

**11.** Explore how Maya Angelou presents defiance in "Still I Rise". **[30]**

**12.** Explore how Susan Hill creates fear in "Whistle and I'll Come to You". **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The windows are compared to teeth, making the bus seem like a mouth or a creature (1); this
suggests the speaker fears being swallowed or taken away, so the departure feels threatening (1).

**2.** The short, balanced sentences "My mother waves. I do not wave." create a stark contrast (1); the
repetition of "wave" highlights the speaker's refusal (1); the full stops create distance, suggesting
emotional withdrawal or the effort of holding back feeling (1).

**3.** One mark for identifying an image, up to two for explanation. For example: the village "folds
itself / into the dark like a letter / nobody will read again" (1) presents home as closing behind her
and becoming part of the past (1), with a sense of finality or loss suggesting she fears she will be
forgotten or will not return (1). Or "the road unspools its one grey thought" (1): the dull colour and
single "thought" suggest a monotonous, uncertain future (1) and her fixed, anxious mind (1).

**4.** The isolated stanza gives the speaker's promise emphasis (1). The second line repeats the first
but stops short, removing "I will come back" (1), which suggests doubt: the promise may be only
self-reassurance, and the ending is left unresolved (1).

**5.** Two points, 2 marks each (1 for inference, 1 for evidence): she is reluctant or afraid to face
what the parcel contains (1), shown by "Mira did not open it" (1); she is delaying or distracting
herself (1), shown by repeatedly making tea (1). Accept other supported inferences, such as unease
caused by her grandmother's handwriting. Total 4.

**6.** The parcel is personified as an awkward visitor (1), making its presence uncomfortable and
suggesting that something important and unspoken is waiting to be dealt with (1).

**7.** The father's brief, plain sentences reveal that the grandmother sent the parcel before going to
hospital (1), implying she is ill or has died (1); because the information comes through dialogue
rather than narration, the reader learns it at the same moment as Mira, increasing its emotional
impact (1).

**8.** The final sentence reveals the grandmother's note (1), whose wording reframes the old refusal:
the scarf becomes an act of patience and love, waiting for the time Mira would need comfort (1). The
italic, unexplained phrase leaves the reader to interpret "cold enough" as grief, so the ending is
moving but open (1).

### Section B: how to mark questions 9 to 12

Marked holistically. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not Pearson's published
mark scheme.

| AO1 (12) | Typical features | AO2 (18) | Typical features |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10–12 | Perceptive interpretation; discriminating references | 15–18 | Perceptive analysis of language and structure; subtle selection |
| 7–9 | Clear, relevant interpretation and references | 11–14 | Detailed exploration of effects |
| 4–6 | Some valid interpretation | 7–10 | Clear explanation of some effects |
| 1–3 | Basic understanding, often retelling | 1–6 | Identification of devices with limited comment |

**9. "The Story of an Hour": indicative content.**
- AO1: Louise's response to the news of her husband's death moves from grief to a secret sense of
  freedom; the story suggests the constraints of marriage at the time; the ending can be read as tragic
  irony.
- AO2: the open window and images of spring and new life outside; the repetition of "free" as her
  realisation grows; the contrast between the private room and the others outside the door; the
  compression of the plot into one hour; the final sentence, in which the doctors' explanation is
  ironic because the reader knows what she has lost.

**10. "Out, Out–": indicative content.**
- AO1: the adults are presented as practical and perhaps hardened; the narrator's wish that the work
  had stopped early hints at criticism; the final lines suggest life carries on after the death.
- AO2: personification of the saw with harsh sound words; the calm description of the landscape
  against the violence; the narrator's intervention ("I wish..."); the speed of the ending, with short
  statements; the possible reading of the adults' turning back to their affairs as necessity rather
  than coldness.

**11. "Still I Rise": indicative content.**
- AO1: the speaker answers oppression with confidence and pride; the poem moves from personal defiance
  to collective history.
- AO2: direct address to "you"; rhetorical questions that challenge the oppressor; similes drawn from
  nature (dust, air, moons and suns, tides) that make rising seem inevitable; the refrain gathering
  force; the change in form towards the end, where short repeated lines build to a climax.

**12. "Whistle and I'll Come to You": indicative content.**
- AO1: fear is created through isolation, uncertainty and the narrator's growing helplessness.
- AO2: first-person retrospective narration that hints at later knowledge; the isolated house and
  marsh as a Gothic setting; sound (the whistle) used before anything is seen, so the reader imagines
  the threat; the dog's reactions as a signal of danger; long sentences that build tension and short
  ones for shock; the narrator's physical reactions conveying terror.

**What separates stronger answers.** They select three or four moments, analyse how each is shaped,
and connect them in an argument about the focus; weaker answers move through the text retelling what
happens.
