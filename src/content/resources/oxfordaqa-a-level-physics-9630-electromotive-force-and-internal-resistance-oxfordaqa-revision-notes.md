## Equations

| Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ε = E/Q | emf = energy transferred to electrical per unit charge by the source |
| ε = I(R + r) | emf drives current through external and internal resistance |
| V = ε − Ir | terminal pd = emf − lost volts |
| I = ε/r | short-circuit current (R = 0) |
| εI = I²R + I²r | power supplied = useful power + power wasted in the source |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Emf (ε) | Energy transferred to electrical energy per unit charge passing through the source |
| Internal resistance (r) | Resistance of the materials inside the source |
| Terminal pd (V) | pd across the source's terminals; energy per coulomb delivered to the external circuit |
| Lost volts (Ir) | Energy per coulomb transferred to heating inside the source |
| Open circuit | No current: terminal pd = emf |

## Emf versus pd

| | Emf | pd |
|---|---|---|
| Energy transfer | Other forms → electrical | Electrical → other forms |
| Belongs to | A source | Any component, including r |
| Unit | V (J C⁻¹) | V (J C⁻¹) |

## Cell combinations (identical cells)

| Arrangement | emf | Internal resistance |
|---|---|---|
| n in series | nε | nr |
| n in parallel | ε | r/n |

## Required practical 3 graphs

| Plot | Straight-line form | Gradient | Intercept |
|---|---|---|---|
| V (vertical) against I | V = −rI + ε | −r | ε on V axis |
| R (vertical) against 1/I | R = ε(1/I) − r | ε | −r on R axis |

Method points: voltmeter across the cell; variable resistor and ammeter in series; small protective resistor; switch off between readings so the cell does not warm (changing r) or run down (lowering ε).

## Where low and high r matter

- **Low r wanted:** sources that must supply large currents, such as a car battery for a starter motor.
- **High r wanted:** EHT supplies, so the short-circuit current is too small to be dangerous.

## Must-know chain

Smaller external R → larger current → larger lost volts Ir → smaller terminal pd. This is why lamps dim when a large load is switched on across the same supply.

## Worked reminder

A cell gives a terminal pd of 1.40 V when the current is 0.20 A, and 1.10 V when the current is 0.80 A.

1. ε = 1.40 + 0.20r and ε = 1.10 + 0.80r.
2. Subtract: 0.30 = 0.60r, so r = 0.50 Ω.
3. ε = 1.40 + 0.20 × 0.50 = 1.50 V.

## Exam traps

- Emf defined as "voltage" or as a force.
- Internal resistance left out of the current calculation.
- Terminal pd stated to equal the emf while current flows.
- V-I gradient read as +r, or ε read from the current axis.
- Internal resistances added for parallel cells.
- No mention of switching off between readings in the practical.

## Quick self-test

1. Define emf.
2. State one difference between emf and pd.
3. A source (ε = 9.0 V, r = 0.50 Ω) is connected to a 4.0 Ω resistor. Find the current, terminal pd and lost volts.
4. What is the terminal pd of a cell when no current flows?
5. Find the short-circuit current of a cell with ε = 1.5 V and r = 0.25 Ω.
6. Three cells, each 1.5 V and 0.50 Ω, are in series with a 3.0 Ω resistor. Find the current.
7. State the emf and internal resistance of three cells, each 1.5 V and 0.50 Ω, in parallel.
8. A V-I graph has intercept 1.48 V and gradient −0.80 V A⁻¹. Find ε and r.
9. Why is the circuit switched off between readings in Required practical 3?
10. A 6.0 V source with r = 1.0 Ω drives a 5.0 Ω resistor. Find the power wasted inside the source and the useful power.
11. Why must a car battery have a very low internal resistance?
12. Why are EHT supplies given a very high internal resistance?

### Answers

1. The energy transferred to electrical energy per unit charge passing through a source.
2. Emf is a transfer into electrical energy by a source; pd is a transfer from electrical energy to other forms in a component.
3. I = 9.0 / 4.5 = 2.0 A; V = 2.0 × 4.0 = 8.0 V; lost volts = 2.0 × 0.50 = 1.0 V.
4. The emf.
5. I = 1.5 / 0.25 = 6.0 A.
6. ε = 4.5 V, r = 1.5 Ω; I = 4.5 / (3.0 + 1.5) = 1.0 A.
7. ε = 1.5 V; r = 0.50 / 3 = 0.17 Ω.
8. ε = 1.48 V; r = 0.80 Ω.
9. So the cell does not warm up (changing r) or run down (lowering ε) during the experiment.
10. I = 6.0 / 6.0 = 1.0 A; wasted I²r = 1.0 W; useful I²R = 5.0 W.
11. It must deliver a very large current to the starter motor; with a large r, the lost volts would be too large and little pd would remain across the motor.
12. So that the short-circuit current ε/r is small, making the supply safer if the terminals are touched.
