These questions cover section 1.6 Classification of goods and services. All places, people and figures are invented, and money is in currency units (cu). Revise first with the [classification of goods study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-classification-of-goods-and-services-cambridge-alevel-economics/) and the [classification of goods revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-classification-of-goods-and-services-cambridge-alevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Which good is most likely to be a public good?

A: A seat on a crowded train

B: A warning siren that alerts a whole town to storms

C: A place at a fee-paying school

D: A bottle of filtered water

**[1]**

**2.** A good is described as rival. What does this mean?

A: It is produced by more than one firm

B: Non-payers can be stopped from using it

C: One person's consumption reduces the amount available to others

D: It competes with substitutes for consumers' spending

**[1]**

**3.** Define a free good and give one example. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a good that is supplied at zero price by the government is not necessarily a free good. **[2]**

**5.** State the syllabus definition of a merit good. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a toll bridge is not a public good. **[2]**

**7.** A consumer buys a sugary drink for 2 cu, believing it is worth 3 cu to her. Fully informed, she would value it at 0.50 cu. Explain what this shows about the drink. **[3]**

---

## Section B

**8.** A coastal district of 250 households is considering a lighthouse to guide its fishing boats. It would cost 30,000 cu a year to run. Each household values it at 160 cu a year.

**(a)** Calculate the total value of the lighthouse to the district and the net benefit. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain, using both characteristics of a public good, why a private firm would not supply the lighthouse. **[4]**

**(c)** Calculate the annual tax per household needed to fund the lighthouse and the net gain to each household. **[2]**

**9.** In Country R, take-up of a free cervical cancer screening programme is low. A survey finds that many eligible people believe the test is unnecessary if they feel healthy.

**(a)** Explain why screening is a merit good, using evidence from the information above. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the screening is a private good even though it is supplied at no charge. **[3]**

**10.** Classify each of the following as a free good, a private good or a public good, giving a reason for each.

**(a)** Wind used to dry clothes on a washing line. **[2]**

**(b)** A firework display over a city harbour that anyone can watch. **[2]**

**(c)** A bicycle hired from a docking station. **[2]**

**11.** Explain the difference between the basis on which public goods are defined and the basis on which merit goods are defined. **[4]**

**12.** "Gambling is a demerit good." Explain this statement and comment on one reason why consumers might underestimate the harm of gambling. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. The siren protects everyone in range, so no one can be excluded, and one more listener does not reduce what others hear.

**2.** **C** [1]. Option B describes excludability, not rivalry.

**3.** A good with **no opportunity cost**, because it is so plentiful that using it means giving up nothing else [1]. Example: air for breathing, sunlight or sea water at the shore [1].

**4.** The good still **uses scarce resources**, such as labour and materials, to supply [1], so it has an **opportunity cost**: it is an economic good whose cost is paid through taxation rather than at the point of use [1].

**5.** A good that is **under-consumed** [1] **as a result of imperfect information in the market** [1].

**6.** The toll means non-payers can be **excluded**, so the bridge is **excludable** [1]. A public good must be non-excludable as well as non-rival, so the bridge does not meet the definition [1].

**7.** She buys because her perceived value (3 cu) is above the price (2 cu) [1]. A fully informed buyer would not buy, because 0.50 cu is below the price [1]. The drink is **over-consumed as a result of imperfect information**, so it is a **demerit good** [1].

**8. (a)** Total value = 250 x 160 = **40,000 cu** [1]. Net benefit = 40,000 - 30,000 = **10,000 cu** [1].

**(b)** The lighthouse is **non-excludable**: its light guides every boat in the area, including those whose owners do not pay [1]. This creates a **free-rider problem**, because each household can benefit without contributing, so a firm cannot raise enough revenue to cover the 30,000 cu cost [1]. It is also **non-rival**: one more boat seeing the light does not reduce its use to others [1]. So the market would **not supply it at all**, although its value exceeds its cost [1].

**(c)** Tax = 30,000 / 250 = **120 cu** per household [1]. Net gain = 160 - 120 = **40 cu** per household [1].

**9. (a)** A merit good is **under-consumed as a result of imperfect information** [1]. The survey shows that many people **underestimate the benefit** of screening because they feel healthy [1], so take-up is lower than it would be if they were fully informed of the benefit of early detection [1].

**(b)** Screening is **scarce**: it uses clinics, staff and laboratory time, so it has an opportunity cost [1]. It is **excludable**: access can be limited to those booked or eligible [1]. It is **rival**: an appointment used by one person cannot be used by another [1].

**10. (a)** **Free good** [1]: wind is so plentiful that one household's use gives up nothing else, so it has no opportunity cost [1].

**(b)** **Public good** [1]: people cannot be stopped from watching from around the harbour (non-excludable), and one more viewer does not reduce anyone else's view (non-rival) [1].

**(c)** **Private good** [1]: the hire charge excludes non-payers, and a bicycle used by one rider is not available to another (excludable and rival) [1].

**11.** Public goods are defined by the **characteristics of the good itself** [1]: non-excludability and non-rivalry [1]. Merit goods are defined by **consumers' information** [1]: they are under-consumed because consumers underestimate the benefit, whatever the good's excludability or rivalry [1].

**12.** A demerit good is **over-consumed as a result of imperfect information** in the market [1]. Gamblers may **underestimate the harm**, such as financial loss or dependency, so they gamble more than they would if fully informed [1]. One reason: the losses build up over time, while wins are immediate and memorable, so the gambler misjudges the expected outcome [1]. Comment: the strength of this reason varies between consumers; regular gamblers may be the least well informed about their own losses, so over-consumption is concentrated in that group [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming only one public-good property in Question 8(b) or Question 10(b).
- Writing "the government provides it" as a reason for calling something a public good.
- Defining merit goods without the words "imperfect information".
- Classifying a zero-price service as a free good.
