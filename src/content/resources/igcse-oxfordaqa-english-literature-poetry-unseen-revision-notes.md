## Anthology poetry: revise by theme, not by poem

*People and Places* is deliberately built as 20 thematically linked
poems. Revising them one at a time wastes that design. Instead, prepare
**two or three comparison pairings** across recurring themes (identity,
displacement, a sense of place, memory) so you walk in with ready-made
points of connection rather than isolated summaries.

For each pairing, fix in advance:

- One genuine **similarity** (shared theme, form, or technique).
- One genuine **difference** (how each poem treats that shared theme
differently).

A comparison answer built on a real point of contrast always
outperforms one that states "both poems are about ___" and stops there.

## Unseen poetry: the transferable skill

Unseen work has no biography or critical context to fall back on — the
entire mark is earned from what the poem or extract actually contains.
Train this directly: on a genuinely unfamiliar poem, work through
structure (stanza shape, line length, rhyme or its absence), language
(word choice, imagery, sound), and tone, and ask what effect each
choice creates, in that order, every time. A consistent method matters
more here than flair, because there is no prior knowledge to lean on.

## Unseen prose (Route A): working from the extract alone

Unseen prose tests the same discipline applied to narrative fiction:
analyse how viewpoint, structure and language create meaning **within
the given extract**, not what might be happening elsewhere in an
imagined wider novel. Resist the temptation to speculate about plot
beyond the extract — the assessment rewards close reading of what is
actually on the page.

## A shared analytical method for both unseen sections

| Step | Ask |
|---|---|
| 1. Structure | Where does the text open, shift, and end — and why there? |
| 2. Language | What specific words or images stand out, and what do they suggest? |
| 3. Effect | What is the reader meant to feel or understand as a result? |

Applying the same three-step method to both unseen poetry and unseen
prose builds consistency under exam pressure, rather than treating them
as unrelated skills to revise separately.

## Confirm your route before revising further

Route A (Paper 2a, covered here) includes unseen prose; Route B
substitutes a non-exam assessment on a whole prose text instead. Since
the two routes prepare genuinely different content, confirming which
route your centre follows is a prerequisite to sensible revision
planning, not an afterthought.

## Timing the paper across three demands

This topic sits alongside the anthology question, unseen poetry and
(Route A) unseen prose within a single paper, so time discipline across
all three matters as much as skill in any one. Allocate time in
proportion to marks available, and decide that allocation **before**
opening the paper rather than mid-exam — a common failure is spending
disproportionate time perfecting the anthology answer (which draws on
studied, prepared material and therefore feels safer) and then rushing
the unseen sections, which are equally weighted but feel less familiar
under pressure precisely because there is no prior preparation to fall
back on.

## Exam traps

- Summarising anthology poems individually rather than comparing them.
- Naming a poetic device without explaining the effect it creates.
- Speculating about an unseen prose extract's wider plot instead of
analysing what is actually given.
- Applying the same close-reading method inconsistently between unseen
poetry and unseen prose.
- Running out of time on the anthology question because time was not
planned proportionately across the paper.

## Comparison language that does real analytical work

When writing an anthology comparison, examiners reward precise
comparative connectives over vague linking phrases. "Similarly,"
"in contrast," "whereas," and "both poems" used to introduce a genuine
point of connection or difference show the comparison is structural,
not just two summaries placed next to each other. A paragraph that
discusses Poem A fully and then discusses Poem B fully, only
connecting them in a closing sentence, is weaker than one that moves
between both poems throughout, feature by feature — the same
point-by-point principle that applies to comparative writing across
this qualification's other components.

## Annotating an unseen text under exam conditions

Develop a fixed, brief annotation habit for unseen work: a first read
for overall sense, then a second read marking only what will actually
be used — a shift in tone, a repeated image, a structural break.
Annotating everything indiscriminately wastes time that should go into
writing; a focused, purposeful annotation pass is what timed unseen
practice should train.

## Self-test

1. Why does the anthology reward thematic revision over poem-by-poem
revision?
2. What is the core disadvantage of unseen texts compared with studied
texts, and how should revision compensate for it?
3. Name the three-step method that applies to both unseen poetry and
unseen prose.
4. Why should analysis of an unseen prose extract avoid speculating
about the wider novel?
5. What is the key content difference between Route A and Route B?

**Answers:** 1. Because the 20 poems are deliberately linked by theme,
so prepared comparison pairings are more exam-productive than isolated
poem-by-poem summaries. 2. There is no biography or critical context to
draw on; revision should build a consistent, repeatable close-reading
method (structure, language, effect) that works from the text alone.
3. Structure, then language, then effect. 4. Because the mark is earned
from analysis of what the given extract actually contains; speculation
beyond it is not evidenced by the text provided. 5. Route A includes an
examined unseen-prose section; Route B replaces it with a non-exam
assessment on a substantial whole prose fiction text chosen outside the
prescribed set texts.
