This guide goes deeper into **3.2.1.2 Physical properties of Period 3 elements** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification. The point covers "the trends in atomic radius, first ionisation energy and melting point of the elements Na–Ar" and "the reasons for these trends in terms of the structure of and bonding in the elements". You must be able to explain the trends in atomic radius and first ionisation energy, and explain the melting points of the elements in terms of their structure and bonding. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1 and available in the synoptic Unit 5.

The [periodicity study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-periodicity/) sets out each trend with the data. This page concentrates on **how to build a full-mark explanation**: which factors to name, in what order, and how to deal with the comparisons examiners like to set. Afterwards, use the [Period 3 properties revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-2-1-2-revision-notes/) and the [Period 3 properties practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-2-1-2-practice/). The classification of these elements into s and p blocks is in [classification (3.2.1.1)](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-2-1-1/).

## The data you will be given or asked to sketch

| | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First ionisation energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 496 | 738 | 578 | 787 | 1012 | 1000 | 1251 | 1521 |
| Melting point / K | 371 | 923 | 933 | 1687 | 317 | 388 | 172 | 84 |
| Structure | metallic | metallic | metallic | macromolecular | molecular P₄ | molecular S₈ | molecular Cl₂ | atoms |

You are not expected to memorise these numbers, but you should be able to sketch the **shape** of each trend: radius falling steadily from Na to Cl; ionisation energy rising with dips at Al and S; melting point rising to a peak at Si, collapsing at P, a small rise at S, then falling to Ar.

## The three factors for radius and ionisation energy

Every explanation of the radius and ionisation energy trends across a period rests on the same three factors. Name all three.

1. **Nuclear charge** increases by one proton at each step.
2. **Shielding** is similar: the outer electrons are all in the third shell, behind the same ten inner electrons (1s² 2s² 2p⁶).
3. **Attraction** between the nucleus and the outer electrons therefore increases.

For atomic radius, the conclusion is that the outer electrons are pulled closer, so **the radius decreases** from Na to Cl. For first ionisation energy, the conclusion is that the outer electron is held more strongly and is closer to the nucleus, so **more energy is needed** to remove it.

A frequent error is to say shielding "decreases" across the period. It does not: the number of inner electrons is the same. What changes is the charge pulling on the outer electrons.

## First ionisation energy: the two dips

The general rise is explained by the three factors. The two dips need a separate explanation each, and both are evidence for sub-shells.

### Magnesium to aluminium

```
Mg(g) → Mg⁺(g) + e⁻      electron removed from 3s
Al(g) → Al⁺(g) + e⁻      electron removed from 3p
```

Aluminium's outer electron is in a **3p** sub-shell, which is higher in energy than 3s and slightly further from the nucleus. It is also partly shielded by the 3s electrons. Less energy is needed to remove it, despite aluminium's extra proton.

### Phosphorus to sulfur

Phosphorus has three 3p electrons, one in each 3p orbital. Sulfur has four, so one 3p orbital contains a **pair** of electrons. The **repulsion** between the paired electrons makes one of them easier to remove, so sulfur's first ionisation energy is slightly lower than phosphorus's.

Both dips are small compared with the overall rise. Do not use either as evidence that the general trend reverses.

### Worked example 1: a two-part comparison

*Explain why the first ionisation energy of chlorine is greater than that of sulfur, and why that of sulfur is less than that of phosphorus.*

Model answer:

- Chlorine has one more proton than sulfur, and its outer electrons are in the same shell with similar shielding, so the nucleus attracts the outer electron of chlorine more strongly. (2 points: nuclear charge; similar shielding / same shell)
- In sulfur the electron removed is from a 3p orbital that holds two electrons. (1 point)
- Repulsion between the two paired electrons makes it easier to remove one of them than to remove an unpaired 3p electron from phosphorus. (1 point)

Notice that the answer says *which* electron is removed in each case. That is what turns a general statement into an explanation.

## Melting point: always name the structure first

The melting point depends on what has to be overcome. A full explanation identifies the structure, names the forces broken or overcome, and compares their strength.

### Sodium, magnesium, aluminium: metallic

Each is a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. From Na to Al:

- the ion charge increases (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺)
- the number of delocalised electrons per atom increases (1, 2, 3)
- the ions get smaller

so the attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons gets stronger and more energy is needed to melt the metal.

### Silicon: macromolecular

Silicon is a giant covalent lattice. Each atom is covalently bonded to four others. To melt it, **many strong covalent bonds must be broken**, so silicon has the highest melting point in the period.

### Phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine: molecular

These consist of P₄, S₈ and Cl₂ molecules. Melting overcomes the **van der Waals forces between molecules**; the covalent bonds within the molecules are not broken. Van der Waals forces are weak, so the melting points are low.

| Molecule | Electrons per molecule | Melting point / K |
|---|---|---|
| S₈ | 128 | 388 |
| P₄ | 60 | 317 |
| Cl₂ | 34 | 172 |

More electrons per molecule means larger temporary dipoles and stronger van der Waals forces. That explains the order S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂.

### Argon: single atoms

Argon exists as separate atoms with only 18 electrons each, so the van der Waals forces between atoms are the weakest in the period and its melting point is the lowest.

### Worked example 2: an extended response

*Explain the trend in the melting points of the Period 3 elements from silicon to argon.*

Model answer, with each creditworthy idea marked:

1. Silicon is macromolecular (giant covalent); melting it requires many strong covalent bonds to be broken, so its melting point is very high.
2. Phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine are simple molecular, made of P₄, S₈ and Cl₂ molecules.
3. When they melt, only the weak van der Waals forces between the molecules are overcome; the covalent bonds inside the molecules stay intact.
4. The strength of van der Waals forces increases with the number of electrons in the molecule.
5. S₈ has more electrons than P₄, which has more than Cl₂, so the melting points decrease in the order S > P > Cl.
6. Argon is monatomic with the fewest electrons, so it has the weakest van der Waals forces and the lowest melting point.

### Worked example 3: the magnesium–aluminium puzzle

*The melting point of aluminium is only 10 K higher than that of magnesium, but the melting point of magnesium is 552 K higher than that of sodium. Do the data support the idea that metallic bonding gets stronger from Na to Al?*

All three changes in ion charge, delocalised electrons and ion size point the same way, and the melting points do rise across Na, Mg and Al. The small step from Mg to Al shows that melting point is not a perfect measure of metallic bond strength; other factors, such as the way the ions are packed in the lattice, also affect it. The direction of the trend still supports the idea. An answer that notes the direction and the size of each step shows good use of data.

## Common mistakes

- Saying covalent bonds break when phosphorus, sulfur or chlorine melt.
- Describing silicon as having "strong intermolecular forces".
- Explaining the radius trend with "more shells", which applies down a group, not across a period.
- Saying shielding decreases across the period.
- Giving one explanation for both dips in ionisation energy.
- Writing ionisation equations without state symbols, or with X⁺(aq).

## Exam technique

- **"Explain the trend"**: describe it in one sentence first, then give the cause.
- **"Explain the difference between X and Y"**: name the structure of each and the force overcome in each.
- **"Sketch"**: plot points in order, show the dips or peaks clearly, and label axes with units.
- **"Use the data"**: quote numbers and differences, not just "higher" or "lower".
