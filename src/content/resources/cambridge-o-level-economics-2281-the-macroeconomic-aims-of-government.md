This guide covers section 4.2 **The macroeconomic aims of government** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. The syllabus lists the aims as "economic growth, full employment/low unemployment, stable prices/low inflation, balance of payments stability, redistribution of income", and asks for the "reasons behind the choice of aims and the criteria that governments set for each aim". It then names three "possible conflicts between aims: full employment versus stable prices; economic growth versus balance of payments stability; and full employment versus balance of payments stability".

The combined [role of government and macroeconomic aims guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-the-role-of-government-and-macroeconomic-aims/) works through a calculation for each aim and each conflict. This page concentrates on the reasoning around the aims: what goes wrong when each aim is missed (the reason governments choose it), how criteria are set and read, how to judge an economy against its criteria from a data table, how governments decide which aim comes first, and why the named conflicts are possible rather than certain. ## 4.2.1 The five aims and why governments choose them

The best way to explain why an aim is chosen is to show the cost of failing to meet it.

### Economic growth

**Meaning:** a sustained rise in real output (real GDP) over time.

**Why chosen:** growth raises incomes and living standards, creates jobs and raises tax revenue without raising tax rates, which pays for schools, hospitals and roads. Without growth, a rising population means falling output per person.

### Full employment / low unemployment

**Meaning:** nearly everyone who is willing and able to work has a job. It never means zero unemployment, because some people are always between jobs.

**Why chosen:** unemployment wastes resources, since the economy produces less than it could. The government loses income tax and pays more in benefits. Unemployed people lose income and skills, and long spells out of work are linked to poverty and social problems.

### Stable prices / low inflation

**Meaning:** a low and steady rate of increase in the general price level. Stable prices does not mean no price rises at all.

**Why chosen:** high or unpredictable inflation reduces the purchasing power of money, harms savers and people on fixed incomes, makes planning and investment harder because firms cannot predict costs and prices, and makes exports less competitive if prices rise faster than abroad.

### Balance of payments stability

**Meaning:** the current account (mainly trade in goods and services) does not show a large deficit or surplus for long periods.

**Why chosen:** a large, persistent deficit must be financed by borrowing from abroad or selling assets, and can put downward pressure on the currency. A large, persistent surplus means the country is producing goods that its own people do not consume, and can cause friction with trading partners.

### Redistribution of income

**Meaning:** reducing the gap between high and low incomes, usually through progressive taxes and benefits.

**Why chosen:** very unequal incomes mean some people cannot afford essentials, which leads to poverty, poor health and lost opportunities, and can cause social tension. Governments must balance this against the risk that very high taxes reduce incentives to work and invest.

## Criteria: turning aims into targets

A **criterion** is the measurable standard by which a government judges whether it has met an aim. A good criterion names the measure and the target.

| Aim | Measure | Example criterion (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | Annual percentage change in real GDP | 2% to 4% a year |
| Low unemployment | Unemployment rate: unemployed ÷ labour force × 100 | Below 5% |
| Stable prices | Annual percentage change in a consumer price index | 1% to 3% |
| Balance of payments stability | Current account balance as a percentage of GDP | Deficit no larger than 3% of GDP |
| Redistribution | Share of income received by the richest and poorest groups | Ratio of the top fifth's share to the bottom fifth's share falling |

Real examples exist: many central banks, including the Bank of England and the European Central Bank, aim for inflation of 2% a year. Criteria differ between countries because circumstances differ. A fast-growing developing economy may set a higher growth target than a mature one; a country with high unemployment may set a less ambitious unemployment target at first.

## Worked example 1: judging an economy against its criteria

Country V uses the criteria in the table above. Data for two years:

| Indicator | Year 1 | Year 2 | Criterion | Year 2 met? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth | 1.0% | 4.5% | 2% to 4% | No (above range) |
| Unemployment rate | 7.5% | 4.8% | Below 5% | Yes |
| Inflation | 1.5% | 4.2% | 1% to 3% | No |
| Current account | −1.0% of GDP | −3.6% of GDP | Deficit ≤ 3% | No |

Checking the Year 2 figures from raw data:

- Price index from 120.0 to 125.04: 5.04 ÷ 120 × 100 = 4.2%.
- Nominal GDP rose from 500 to 544.5 while prices rose by 4.2%, so real GDP is 544.5 ÷ 1.042 = 522.6 (in Year 1 prices), a real growth rate of 22.6 ÷ 500 × 100 = 4.5%.
- 0.6 million unemployed in a labour force of 12.5 million: 0.6 ÷ 12.5 × 100 = 4.8%.
- Current account deficit of 19.6 on nominal GDP of 544.5: 19.6 ÷ 544.5 × 100 = 3.6%.

**Interpretation.** In Year 1 the economy grew slowly with high unemployment but low inflation and a small deficit. In Year 2 strong growth cut unemployment, but inflation rose above its target and the deficit widened beyond its criterion. The data show two of the named conflicts at work: full employment versus stable prices, and growth versus balance of payments stability.

## Choosing priorities

A government cannot always meet every criterion at once, so it decides which aim matters most at the time. Reasons include:

- **The size of each problem.** In Year 1, Country V's main problem was unemployment; in Year 2 it was inflation.
- **Public opinion and elections.** Voters notice job losses and rising prices quickly.
- **The stage of development.** A low-income country may put growth first; a high-income country may put more weight on stability.
- **Commitments.** A government may have promised a target, or agreed one with an international lender.

## 4.2.2 Conflicts between aims

### Full employment versus stable prices

**Chain:** policies that raise spending → firms produce more and hire more workers → unemployment falls → as spare workers run out, firms compete for staff and wages rise → higher costs and strong demand push up prices → inflation rises.

**When it is likely:** when the economy is close to full capacity. **When it may not arise:** when there is plenty of spare capacity, so output can rise without pushing up prices, or when jobs come from better skills and productivity, which also lowers costs.

### Economic growth versus balance of payments stability

**Chain:** growth raises incomes → households and firms buy more, including more imports → if exports do not rise as fast, the current account deficit widens. Rising demand may also raise domestic prices and make exports less competitive.

**When it is likely:** when growth is led by domestic spending and the country imports much of what it consumes. **When it may not arise:** when growth is led by exports, or when imports are machinery that will raise future exports.

### Full employment versus balance of payments stability

**Chain:** more people in work → more income to spend → more spent on imports → current account deficit widens. If wages rise as labour becomes scarce, export prices may rise too.

**When it is likely:** when a large share of extra income is spent on imports. **When it may not arise:** when the extra jobs are in export industries.

### Aims that support each other

Not every pair conflicts. Growth usually helps employment, since firms need more workers to produce more. Low inflation supports the balance of payments by keeping exports competitive. Strong answers say when aims support each other as well as when they clash.

## Common mistakes

- Defining full employment as zero unemployment or stable prices as no price change.
- Stating an aim without a reason for choosing it.
- Giving a criterion with no measure or no number.
- Asserting a conflict without the chain of cause and effect.
- Treating conflicts as certain; the syllabus calls them possible conflicts.
- Judging growth on nominal rather than real GDP.

## Exam technique for 4.2

- **Identify** an aim from data or a description.
- **Explain** why an aim is chosen by showing the cost of missing it.
- **Calculate** rates from raw data and compare them with the criterion.
- **Analyse** a conflict as a chain, step by step.
- **Discuss** whether a government should prioritise one aim: compare the size of each problem, consider which conflicts are likely in the circumstances given, and reach a supported judgement.
