## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balance of payments | Record of all transactions between residents and the rest of the world over a period |
| Credit item | A transaction that brings money into the country |
| Debit item | A transaction that sends money out |
| Surplus on an account | Credits exceed debits |
| Deficit on an account | Debits exceed credits |
| Balance of an account | Credits minus debits |

## The three accounts

| Account | Items | Typical credit | Typical debit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current | Trade in goods, trade in services, income, current transfers | Export sale, tourist spending, profits received, remittances received | Import purchase, firm paying for foreign services, profits sent out, aid given |
| Capital | Capital transfers; non-produced, non-financial assets | Debt written off for the country; sale of a patent abroad | Debt the country writes off; purchase of a foreign licence |
| Financial | FDI, portfolio investment, reserve assets, official borrowing | Foreign firm builds a factory; foreign purchase of bonds; fall in reserves; new foreign loan to government | Domestic firm builds abroad; domestic purchase of foreign shares; rise in reserves; repaying a foreign loan |

Convention in this guide: a net inflow of financial capital is a credit, and a fall in reserves is a credit.

## Method: calculating from data

1. Work out each item's balance as credits minus debits.
2. Add the four current account items for the current account balance.
3. Add the two capital account items for the capital account balance.
4. Financial account = minus (current account + capital account).
5. Find any one missing financial item by subtracting the known ones from the financial account total.
6. State whether each balance is a surplus or a deficit, with units.

### Worked reminder (Pelmora, billions of pels)

| Item | Credits | Debits | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods | 180 | 205 | -25 |
| Services | 60 | 38 | +22 |
| Income | 25 | 31 | -6 |
| Current transfers | 9 | 14 | -5 |
| Current account | | | -14 |
| Capital account (+2 and -1) | | | +1 |

Financial account = -(-14 + 1) = **+13**. If FDI is +9, portfolio investment +5 and official borrowing -2, then reserve assets = 13 - (9 + 5 - 2) = **+1**, so reserves fell by 1.

## Why the total is zero

- Each transaction has two sides, so every debit has a matching credit.
- Current account + capital account + financial account = 0.
- A deficit on one account is matched by a surplus on another.
- "Deficit in the balance of payments" should always name the account.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| FDI and portfolio investment | FDI is a lasting stake such as a factory; portfolio investment is shares or bonds without control |
| Current transfers and capital transfers | Current transfers are one-way payments such as remittances and aid; capital transfers are one-way and linked to assets, such as debt write-offs |
| Reserve assets and official borrowing | Reserves are assets held by the central bank; official borrowing is government or central bank borrowing from abroad |
| Expenditure switching and expenditure reducing | Switching redirects spending to home output; reducing cuts total demand |

## HL only: currency market links

**Current account and exchange rate.** Foreign demand for exports creates demand for the currency, and imports create supply of it. A deficit means supply of the currency exceeds demand at the going rate, so it tends to fall. A depreciation then makes exports cheaper and imports dearer. A surplus has the opposite effect.

**Financial account and exchange rate.** Net inflows raise demand for the currency and cause appreciation. Net outflows raise supply and cause depreciation. A higher interest rate tends to attract portfolio inflows.

Diagram steps:

1. Axes: exchange rate on the vertical axis, quantity of the currency on the horizontal axis.
2. Draw a downward-sloping demand curve and an upward-sloping supply curve.
3. Mark the equilibrium rate.
4. Show the rate held above equilibrium to illustrate deficit pressure, or shift a curve to show a financial flow.
5. Label every curve and rate.

## HL only: persistent deficit

| Area | Link |
|---|---|
| Exchange rates | Downward pressure on the currency |
| Interest rates | May rise to attract inflows |
| Foreign ownership of assets | Inflows hand ownership abroad; future profits flow out |
| Debt | Borrowing builds up and interest must be paid |
| Credit ratings | Greater perceived risk, higher borrowing costs |
| Demand management | Expansion pulls in imports, so policy is constrained |
| Growth | Capital goods imports can help; consumption-led deficits do not |

**Evaluate** by size relative to GDP, duration, how it is financed and what imports are used for.

## HL only: correction methods

| Method | Examples | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expenditure switching | Tariffs, quotas, subsidies, depreciation | Acts on imports and exports quickly | Retaliation; higher import prices; depends on elasticities |
| Expenditure reducing | Higher taxes, lower government spending, higher interest rates | Cuts imports quickly | Lower output, jobs and growth |
| Supply-side policies | Education, infrastructure, research | Lasting competitiveness without sacrificing demand | Slow, costly, uncertain |

Match the method to the cause. Weak competitiveness points to supply-side policy; excess spending points to expenditure reducing.

## HL only: Marshall-Lerner and the J-curve

- **Marshall-Lerner condition:** a depreciation improves the current account only if the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports (ignoring signs) sum to more than 1.
- **J-curve:** demand is less elastic in the short run, so the balance first worsens, then improves as elasticities rise.

Reminder: exports 160, imports 200, balance -40. A 10% depreciation with export elasticity 0.5 and import elasticity 0.4 (sum 0.9):

```
Exports = 160 x (1 + 0.5 x 0.10) = 168.00
Imports = 200 x 1.10 x (1 - 0.4 x 0.10) = 211.20
New balance = 168.00 - 211.20 = -43.20
```

The deficit widens, as the sum is below 1.

## HL only: persistent surplus

| Area | Link |
|---|---|
| Domestic consumption and investment | Output sold abroad is not available at home |
| Exchange rates | Upward pressure on the currency |
| Inflation | Export demand adds to demand, but a stronger currency lowers import prices |
| Employment | Export jobs, but appreciation can cost some |
| Export competitiveness | Appreciation raises export prices abroad |

## Quick self-test

1. Define a debit item.
2. Name the three accounts of the balance of payments.
3. Is a fall in a central bank's reserves a credit or a debit in the financial account?
4. A country has goods exports of 90 and imports of 110, and services exports of 40 and imports of 25. Calculate the balance of trade in goods and in services.
5. The current account is -30 and the capital account is +4. Calculate the financial account balance.
6. Explain why the balance of payments as a whole sums to zero.
7. (HL only) In a floating system, which way does a current account deficit tend to push the exchange rate?
8. (HL only) State the Marshall-Lerner condition.
9. (HL only) Export elasticity is 0.6 and import elasticity is 0.3. Will a depreciation improve the current account?
10. (HL only) Give two expenditure reducing policies.
11. (HL only) State one effect of a persistent surplus on the exchange rate.

### Answers

1. A transaction that sends money out of the country, such as an import purchase.
2. Current, capital and financial accounts.
3. A credit.
4. Goods: 90 - 110 = -20 (deficit). Services: 40 - 25 = +15 (surplus).
5. -(-30 + 4) = **+26**.
6. Every transaction has a credit and a matching debit, so the accounts together sum to zero.
7. Downward, since supply of the currency exceeds demand.
8. A depreciation improves the current account only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than 1.
9. No. The sum is 0.9, below 1, so the balance would worsen.
10. Any two of higher income tax, lower government spending, higher interest rates.
11. Upward pressure, because foreign demand for the currency exceeds the supply of it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the balance of payments "in deficit" without naming an account.
- Reversing credits and debits, so balances take the wrong sign.
- Getting the sign of the financial account wrong when finding it from the other two accounts.
- Giving a figure with no units or no statement of surplus or deficit.
- Treating FDI and portfolio investment as the same thing.
- Drawing a currency diagram with unlabelled axes or curves.
- Claiming depreciation always helps, with no reference to elasticities or the time lag.
- Listing implications of a deficit with no judgement of size, cause or financing.
- Describing correction methods without saying which cause each one fits.
