This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.2.4 HIV as an example of a human disease caused by a virus**, in Unit 2, Biological systems and disease. Unit 2 is listed for both the International AS and the International A-level.

The broader [HIV and plant virus transmission guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-hiv-and-plant-virus-transmission-by-aphids/) lists the parts of the virus and the steps of the cycle. This page concentrates on the reasoning: why each part is needed, why each step happens the way it does, and how to work out a drug's effect from information you have never seen before, which is the skill the specification names. Afterwards, use the [HIV revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-hiv-as-an-example-of-a-human-disease-caused-by-a-virus-oxfordaqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) and the [HIV practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-hiv-as-an-example-of-a-human-disease-caused-by-a-virus-oxfordaqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.2.4.1 | HIV structure: RNA genetic material; a protein capsid enclosing the RNA and the enzymes reverse transcriptase, integrase and protease; an envelope from the host cell plasma membrane containing glycoproteins |
| 3.2.4.2 | HIV binds to receptors on CD4 helper T-cells; capsid contents released into the cell; viral RNA transcribed into double-strand DNA, then integrated into a host chromosome; viral DNA may be transcribed to give new viral RNA and proteins, which are packaged and released as new virus particles |
| Skill | Use knowledge of structure and replication to explain the effects of drugs used to treat HIV/AIDS when given appropriate information |

The Unit 2 biological principles add that viruses are acellular and non-living, and that after their nucleic acid enters, the infected host cell replicates the virus particles.

## Structure linked to function

Learn each component together with the step that needs it. This turns a list into an explanation.

| Component | Needed for | What would happen without it |
|---|---|---|
| Envelope glycoproteins | Binding to CD4 receptors on helper T-cells | The virus could not attach to or enter its host cell |
| Envelope (from host plasma membrane) | Fusing with the host cell surface membrane, so the contents enter | Contents could not be released into the cell |
| RNA | Carrying the genes for the virus's proteins | No viral proteins could be made |
| Reverse transcriptase | Making DNA from the viral RNA | No DNA copy, so nothing to integrate |
| Integrase | Inserting viral DNA into a host chromosome | Viral DNA would not become part of the host genome |
| Protease | Cutting long viral protein chains into working proteins | New particles would form but would not be infectious |
| Capsid | Enclosing and protecting the RNA and enzymes | Contents exposed and not packaged together |

The enzymes are carried inside the particle because the host cell does not supply them. A T-cell has its own ribosomes, nucleotides and ATP, but not the enzymes HIV needs to copy its RNA genome into DNA and insert that DNA into a chromosome.

## Why each step happens as it does

**Binding.** Glycoproteins on the envelope fit the CD4 receptor, a protein on the surface of helper T-cells. This specific fit is why HIV infects helper T-cells in particular.

**Release of the contents.** The envelope fuses with the cell surface membrane, and the capsid contents, including the RNA and the three enzymes, enter the cytoplasm.

**Reverse transcription.** The host cell can only transcribe genes from DNA, and only DNA can be inserted into a chromosome. So reverse transcriptase uses the viral RNA as a template to make a complementary DNA strand, and then a second strand, giving double-strand DNA.

**Integration.** Integrase inserts the double-strand viral DNA into a host chromosome. From now on, the viral genes are copied whenever the cell's DNA is replicated, and are passed to both daughter cells if the cell divides.

**Transcription and translation.** The specification says the viral DNA "may" be transcribed. When it is, the host's enzymes make viral RNA, some of which acts as the genome of new particles and some as mRNA. Host ribosomes translate the mRNA into viral proteins, including long chains that protease cuts into separate working proteins.

**Assembly and release.** New RNA, enzymes and capsid proteins are packaged together. The particles leave the cell, taking part of the host cell surface membrane, with viral glycoproteins inserted in it, as their envelope.

## The consequence: from HIV to AIDS

Helper T-cells initiate the body's response to infections. Each cycle of HIV replication damages or destroys infected helper T-cells, and their number falls over time. With too few helper T-cells, the immune system responds poorly to other pathogens, and the person develops infections that a healthy immune system would control. This stage is AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome). HIV itself does not directly cause the fatal illnesses; the weakened immune response allows other infections to do so.

## Reasoning about drugs from information given

The question will describe what a drug does, or what is observed in treated cells. You are not expected to know drug names. Use three steps:

1. **Locate the step.** What does the drug block, or what is missing?
2. **Name the consequence downstream.** Which later steps cannot happen?
3. **Link to the outcome.** Fewer, or non-infectious, new virus particles, so fewer helper T-cells infected.

**Worked example 1.** Drug A has a shape similar to a DNA nucleotide. Reverse transcriptase builds it into the new DNA strand, but no further nucleotide can then be added. Explain how drug A slows the progress of HIV infection.

- Step blocked: the DNA strand made by reverse transcriptase is left incomplete.
- Downstream: there is no complete double-strand DNA copy of the viral genome, so it cannot be integrated, and no viral RNA or proteins are made from it.
- Outcome: no new virus particles are produced from newly infected cells, so fewer helper T-cells are infected and destroyed.

**Worked example 2.** In cells treated with drug B, double-strand viral DNA is found in the cytoplasm and nucleus, but none is found inside the chromosomes. Which enzyme does drug B affect?

- DNA has been made, so binding, entry and reverse transcription all worked.
- It has not been inserted into a chromosome, which is the job of integrase.
- So drug B inhibits **integrase**.

**Why drugs can target the virus selectively.** Uninfected cells do not use the viral reverse transcriptase, integrase or protease. A drug that inhibits one of these viral enzymes can stop replication with less harm to the host's own metabolism. Antibiotics do not work, because viruses have no cell wall, ribosomes or metabolism of their own for them to act on.

## Interpreting treatment data

**Worked example 3 (illustrative data).** A patient took a combination of drugs for 12 months, then stopped.

| Time | Viral load (copies per mm³) | Helper T-cell count (cells per mm³) |
|---|---|---|
| Start | 120 000 | 210 |
| 12 months, on treatment | 400 | 520 |
| 3 months after stopping | 85 000 | 330 |

- On treatment, viral load fell by a factor of 120 000 ÷ 400 = 300, and the helper T-cell count rose by (520 − 210) ÷ 210 × 100 = 148%.
- After stopping, viral load rose sharply. Explanation: viral DNA had already been integrated into host chromosomes. The drugs stop new rounds of replication but do not remove integrated DNA. When treatment stopped, cells carrying integrated DNA began transcribing it again.
- This is why treatment must continue, and why it controls infection rather than curing it.

## Common mistakes

- Saying HIV "attacks the immune system" without naming CD4 receptors and helper T-cells.
- Saying the envelope is made by the virus. It comes from the host plasma membrane.
- Confusing reverse transcriptase (RNA → DNA) with integrase (DNA into chromosome).
- Saying viral RNA is integrated. Only DNA is integrated.
- Treating "may be transcribed" as "is always transcribed straight away".
- Explaining a drug by naming it, instead of saying which step is blocked and what follows.
- Saying HIV directly causes the infections seen in AIDS.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the cycle: use the specification's order and terms: binding to CD4, release of contents, reverse transcription to double-strand DNA, integration, transcription, packaging and release.
- **Explain** a drug's effect: the three-step method above, always ending with fewer infectious particles.
- **Suggest** why a drug is less harmful to host cells: the target is a viral enzyme that uninfected cells do not need.
- **Use the data**: quote values, calculate changes, and give a biological explanation for each trend.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does HIV need reverse transcriptase?**
Its genetic material is RNA, but only DNA can be integrated into a host chromosome and transcribed by the host cell. Reverse transcriptase makes double-strand DNA from the viral RNA.

**Why can't drugs cure HIV infection?**
Once viral DNA is integrated into host chromosomes, drugs that block replication steps cannot remove it. If treatment stops, the integrated DNA can be transcribed again and new particles are made.
