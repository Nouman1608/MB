These questions cover section 3.3.1, Water, carbon and life on earth (3.3.1.1 to 3.3.1.6), of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2. The section is Section A of Unit 3: Physical geography 2, a 1 hour 30 minute written exam with 40 marks in Section A and 40 in Section B, all questions compulsory. All places and data in Questions 5 to 8 are fictional; Question 7 uses illustrative figures of a realistic size.

## Questions

**1.** Outline what is meant by the cryosphere and name one process that transfers water out of it. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how rising air leads to cloud formation. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between infiltration-excess and saturation-excess overland flow. **[2]**

**4.** Outline two processes that transfer carbon from the atmosphere to the ocean. **[4]**

**5.** The fictional River Wenlow drains a catchment of 48 km². In one year, catchment-average precipitation was 1,150 mm and evapotranspiration was 430 mm. The gauging station at the basin outlet recorded 33.6 million m³ of runoff.

**(a)** Convert the runoff into millimetres of water over the catchment. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the water balance equation to calculate the change in storage, and state what it shows. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why the share of precipitation leaving as runoff might rise if forest in the upper catchment were cleared for grazing. **[4]**

**6.** Two fictional streams received the same 42 mm storm. Peak rainfall on both was at 10:00.

| | Fellside Beck | Ashby Brook |
|---|---|---|
| Area (km²) | 9 | 11 |
| Basin | Steep moorland, thin peat over impermeable rock, many drainage ditches | Gentle slopes, mixed woodland, permeable sandstone |
| Baseflow before storm (m³/s) | 1.2 | 1.5 |
| Peak discharge (m³/s) | 18.6 | 7.2 |
| Time of peak discharge | 14:00 | 21:00 |

**(a)** Calculate the lag time for each stream. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the data, explain the differences between the two hydrographs. **[6]**

**7.** Illustrative figures for one year: fossil fuel emissions 36.7 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide; land use change emissions 4.0 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide; ocean uptake 2.9 GtC; land uptake 3.3 GtC.

**(a)** Convert total emissions to GtC. Use 12/44. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the increase in the atmospheric store in GtC and in ppm (1 ppm = 2.12 GtC), and the share of emissions that stayed in the atmosphere. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why ocean uptake of carbon may weaken as the climate warms. **[2]**

**8.** A student measured infiltration rates (mm per hour) at five points on each of three surfaces in a fictional catchment.

| Surface | Readings (mm/h) |
|---|---|
| Woodland | 62, 75, 58, 81, 69 |
| Grazed pasture | 24, 31, 19, 28, 23 |
| Footpath | 4, 7, 3, 6, 5 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean and range for grazed pasture. **[2]**
**(b)** A storm brings rain at 30 mm per hour for one hour. Using the data, explain the likely effect on overland flow at each surface. **[4]**
**(c)** Suggest one limitation of the student's sampling and how to reduce it. **[2]**

**9.** Explain the relationship between the water cycle and the carbon cycle in the atmosphere. **[6]**

**10.** With reference to a local river catchment you have studied, analyse the impact of heavy precipitation on drainage basin stores and transfers. **[8]**

**11.** With reference to a tropical rainforest you have studied, assess the extent to which human activity has changed its water and carbon cycles. **[16]**

**12.** Evaluate the effectiveness of human interventions in the carbon cycle designed to mitigate climate change. **[16]**

## Answers

Questions 10 to 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; the [1]s below show indicative creditworthy points.

**1.** The cryosphere is the frozen part of Earth's water: ice sheets, glaciers, sea ice, snow and permafrost [1]. Any one transfer out: melting, sublimation or calving [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "ice" alone is too vague for the definition; list at least two named forms of frozen water.

**2.** Air rises by convection, over relief or at a front, and cools adiabatically as pressure falls [1]. When it cools to its dew point it is saturated [1], and water vapour condenses on condensation nuclei such as dust or salt to form cloud droplets [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the dew point and the nuclei are the two ideas most often left out; each is a separate mark.

**3.** Infiltration-excess flow happens when rain falls faster than the soil's infiltration rate [1]; saturation-excess flow happens when the soil is already full of water, so any further rain runs off [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "distinguish" needs both sides in one answer; defining only one type caps you at half marks.

**4.** Carbon dioxide dissolves in surface water, especially cold water [1], which sinks at high latitudes and carries it to the deep ocean (the physical pump) [1]. Phytoplankton take in carbon by photosynthesis [1], and their remains and shells sink to the deep ocean and sea-floor sediments (the biological pump) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each process has two marks, one for the uptake and one for where the carbon goes next.

**5. (a)** 33,600,000 m³ ÷ 48,000,000 m² = 0.7 m [1] = 700 mm [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** ΔS = 1,150 − 430 − 700 = +20 mm [1]. Storage rose slightly, so the catchment gained water in soil and groundwater that year [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Grazing removes the canopy, so interception and evapotranspiration fall and more water reaches the ground [1], leaving more to become runoff rather than return to the air [1]. Livestock compact the soil, cutting infiltration [1], so more rain moves quickly as overland flow to the channel [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* in (a), convert km² to m² before dividing; most lost marks here come from a missing factor of a million.

**6. (a)** Fellside Beck: 14:00 − 10:00 = 4 hours [1]. Ashby Brook: 21:00 − 10:00 = 11 hours [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Fellside's peak of 18.6 m³/s is about 2.6 times Ashby's 7.2 m³/s, despite its smaller area [1]. Steep slopes move water quickly downslope [1]. Thin peat over impermeable rock saturates fast, so rain becomes overland flow [1], and ditches speed water to the channel, giving a short 4-hour lag [1]. At Ashby, woodland intercepts rain and slows its arrival [1], and permeable sandstone lets water percolate to groundwater, released slowly as throughflow and baseflow, giving an 11-hour lag [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* quote figures from the table and pair each factor with the process it controls; a list of factors with no process gains little.

**7. (a)** 36.7 + 4.0 = 40.7 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide [1]; 40.7 x 12/44 = 11.1 GtC [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 11.1 − (2.9 + 3.3) = 4.9 GtC [1]; 4.9 ÷ 2.12 = 2.3 ppm [1]; 4.9 ÷ 11.1 = 44% stayed in the atmosphere [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Warmer water dissolves less carbon dioxide [1]. Warmer surface water is also less dense, so it mixes less with deep water and carries less carbon down [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* write the unit at every step; a correct number labelled as carbon dioxide when it is carbon loses the mark.

**8. (a)** Mean = 125 ÷ 5 = 25 mm/h [1]; range = 31 − 19 = 12 mm/h [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Woodland's mean of 69 mm/h is well above 30 mm/h, so almost all rain infiltrates and overland flow is minimal [1]. Pasture's mean of 25 mm/h is just below 30, so about 5 mm in the hour may run off as infiltration-excess flow, more where readings were as low as 19 [1]. The footpath's mean of 5 mm/h means about 25 mm of the 30 mm becomes overland flow [1]. Compaction by trampling and grazing has reduced pore space, which explains the pattern [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** Five readings per surface is a small sample, so one unusual point affects the mean [1]; take more readings using stratified or systematic sampling across each surface [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "using the data" in (b) means comparing each mean with the 30 mm rain rate, not just ranking the surfaces.

**9.** Water vapour and carbon dioxide are both greenhouse gases [1]. More carbon dioxide warms the atmosphere [1], which increases evaporation [1]. Warmer air holds more water vapour, which traps more heat, a positive feedback [1]. Plants link the cycles: stomata open to take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis and lose water by transpiration at the same time [1]. Carbon dioxide dissolves in rain to form weak carbonic acid, which weathers rock and removes carbon from the atmosphere over long timescales [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* the question asks for a relationship, so every point must link one cycle to the other rather than describe each separately.

**10.** Indicative content (River Eden, Cumbria):
- Heavy rain exceeds interception storage quickly, so throughfall and stemflow rise [1].
- Infiltration fills the soil water store; once full, saturation-excess overland flow dominates [1].
- In December 2015 (Storm Desmond), nearly 36 hours of continuous rain affected Carlisle [1], so soil stores filled and transfers to the channel were rapid [1].
- Channel storage rose until the Eden, Caldew and Petteril overtopped and defences in Carlisle were breached [1].
- In January 2005, up to 180 mm in a day upstream flooded about 2,700 Carlisle homes [1].
- Groundwater and reservoir stores, such as Haweswater, refill after heavy rain, which supports later water supply [1].
- Analysis: antecedent conditions matter as much as rainfall total, because a full soil store turns rain directly into runoff [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* name the stores and flows in order from canopy to channel, and back each step with a detail from your catchment.

**11.** Indicative content (the Amazon):
- Introduce the forest as a large water and carbon store: about 5.5 million km², about 110 billion tonnes of carbon [1].
- Natural cycles: high interception and evapotranspiration; about half of rainfall is recycled from the forest [1].
- Deforestation reached 27,772 km² in the Brazilian Amazon in 2004 [1], about 80% linked to cattle ranching [1].
- Water cycle change: less interception and evapotranspiration [1]; compacted soils increase overland flow and erosion [1]; less moisture for rain downwind [1].
- Carbon cycle change: burning releases biomass carbon to the atmosphere [1], and soil carbon is lost once the litter supply stops [1].
- Gatti and colleagues (2021) reported that the south-eastern Amazon had become a net carbon source [1].
- Feedback: a drier forest burns more easily, so the two cycles reinforce each other [1].
- By 2022 about 20% had been deforested [1], close to a suggested tipping point of 20 to 25% [1].
- Counter-evidence: deforestation fell to 4,571 km² in 2012, so change is not one-way [1].
- Much of the basin is still forested, and the effects vary from place to place [1].
- Judgement: human activity has changed both cycles greatly at regional scale, with the largest change where clearance is concentrated [1]. **[16]**
*Examiner insight:* higher-level answers judge "extent" with scale and place, rather than assuming the whole forest has changed equally.

**12.** Indicative content:
- Define mitigation as reducing carbon transfers to the atmosphere or increasing them out of it [1].
- Set criteria: scale of carbon removed, cost, permanence and side effects [1].
- Afforestation increases photosynthesis and biomass storage [1], but carbon is lost again if trees burn or are felled [1].
- Forest protection: Brazilian Amazon clearance fell from 27,772 km² in 2004 to 4,571 km² in 2012, alongside measures such as the Soy Moratorium and the Amazon Fund [1], but the fall was not sustained [1].
- Carbon capture and storage: Sleipner has injected about 1 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year since 1996 [1], but this is tiny beside global emissions of tens of billions of tonnes [1].
- Farming changes such as reduced tillage keep carbon in the soil [1] but can be reversed by later ploughing [1].
- Peatland restoration stops drained peat oxidising [1].
- Cutting fossil fuel use tackles the largest flow at its source [1], supported by agreements such as the Paris Agreement (2015) [1], though pledges depend on national action [1].
- Interventions work best together; none alone matches the size of fossil emissions [1].
- Judgement: emissions cuts are most effective; storage-based methods help but are slower, smaller or less permanent [1]. **[16]**
*Examiner insight:* evaluation needs criteria; judge each intervention against scale and permanence, not just describe it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculations with no working or no units, especially when converting km² to m².
- Mixing up GtC and gigatonnes of carbon dioxide.
- Lag time measured from the start of rain instead of peak rainfall.
- Hydrograph answers that list basin features without the process (infiltration, throughflow) they control.
- Data questions answered without quoting figures from the table.
- Catchment answers with general flood facts and no named stores and flows.
- Rainforest essays that describe deforestation but never measure its scale.
- Evaluation of interventions with no criteria and no overall judgement.
