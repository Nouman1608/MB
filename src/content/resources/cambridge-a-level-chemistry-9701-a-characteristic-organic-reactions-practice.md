## Section A

**1.** Define the term *electrophile*. **[1]**

**2.** State the type of mechanism for the reaction of propanoyl chloride with ethanol. **[1]**

**3.** State the type of mechanism for the reaction of methylbenzene with Br₂ in the presence of AlBr₃. **[1]**

**4.** Give the formula of the electrophile in the nitration of benzene. **[1]**

**5.** Identify the nucleophile in the reaction CH₃COCl + CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃ + HCl. **[1]**

**6.** State the two features of an acyl chloride molecule that allow it to react by addition–elimination. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Classify each reaction as electrophilic substitution, addition–elimination, electrophilic addition, nucleophilic addition or nucleophilic substitution.

**(a)** C₆H₆ + CH₃COCl → C₆H₅COCH₃ + HCl (AlCl₃, heat) **[1]**

**(b)** CH₃COCl + H₂O → CH₃COOH + HCl **[1]**

**(c)** CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + NaOH → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + NaBr **[1]**

**(d)** CH₃COCH₃ + HCN → (CH₃)₂C(OH)CN **[1]**

**(e)** C₆H₅OH + 3Br₂ → C₆H₂Br₃OH + 3HBr **[1]**

**8.** Benzene reacts with chloromethane in the presence of aluminium chloride when heated.

**(a)** Write an equation to show how the electrophile is formed. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe the mechanism of the reaction of the electrophile with benzene, using curly arrows. **[3]**

**(c)** Write an equation to show how the catalyst is regenerated. **[1]**

**(d)** Name the organic product. **[1]**

**9.** Ethanoyl chloride reacts with water at room temperature.

**(a)** Describe the mechanism of this reaction, using curly arrows, including the intermediate. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why this mechanism is called addition–elimination. **[2]**

**10.** A student says: "Bromoethane and ethanoyl chloride both react with ammonia by nucleophilic substitution, because in both the halogen is replaced by an NH₂ group."

**(a)** Explain why the student is correct about bromoethane. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the student is wrong about ethanoyl chloride. **[2]**

**11.** Benzenediazonium chloride reacts with phenol in aqueous sodium hydroxide below 10 °C to form a coloured azo compound.

**(a)** Identify the electrophile and the species it attacks. **[2]**

**(b)** State the type of mechanism. **[1]**

**(c)** State the ring position at which the new bond forms. **[1]**

**12.** Explain why benzene reacts with electrophiles by substitution, whereas cyclohexene reacts with electrophiles by addition. **[3]**

**13.** Phenyl benzoate, C₆H₅COOC₆H₅, can be made at room temperature from phenol and benzoyl chloride.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** State the type of mechanism and identify the nucleophile. **[2]**

**(c)** State the inorganic product and describe what would be seen if moist blue litmus paper were held above the mixture. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** An electron-pair acceptor [1].

**2.** Addition–elimination [1].

**3.** Electrophilic substitution [1].

**4.** NO₂⁺ [1].

**5.** Ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂ (its nitrogen lone pair) [1].

**6.** The carbonyl carbon is strongly δ+ because it is bonded to both O and Cl, so it attracts nucleophiles, and the C=O π bond can accept an electron pair [1]; Cl⁻ is a good leaving group, so it is eliminated as C=O re-forms [1].

**7. (a)** Electrophilic substitution [1].

**(b)** Addition–elimination [1].

**(c)** Nucleophilic substitution [1].

**(d)** Nucleophilic addition [1].

**(e)** Electrophilic substitution [1].

**8. (a)** CH₃Cl + AlCl₃ → CH₃⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ [1].

**(b)** Curly arrow from the delocalised π system of the ring to the C of CH₃⁺ [1]; intermediate drawn with a partial (horseshoe) ring and a positive charge, the attacked carbon carrying both H and CH₃ [1]; curly arrow from the C–H bond into the ring, H⁺ lost and the delocalised ring restored [1].

**(c)** H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → HCl + AlCl₃ [1].

**(d)** Methylbenzene [1].

**9. (a)** Curly arrow from a lone pair on the O of H₂O to the δ+ carbonyl carbon [1]; curly arrow from the C=O π bond to O, giving an intermediate with O⁻ and –OH₂⁺ on the same carbon as the Cl [1]; curly arrow from the O⁻ lone pair re-forming C=O, with a curly arrow from the C–Cl bond to Cl, so Cl⁻ leaves [1]; H⁺ lost from the –OH₂⁺ group, giving CH₃COOH and HCl [1].

**(b)** In the first step the water molecule adds to the C=O bond, forming an intermediate [1]; in the second step a group (Cl⁻, then H⁺: overall HCl) is eliminated and the C=O bond re-forms [1].

**10. (a)** Bromoethane has an sp³ carbon with no C=O; NH₃ attacks the δ+ carbon and Br⁻ leaves, a nucleophilic substitution [1].

**(b)** In ethanoyl chloride the nucleophile first adds to the C=O bond, forming an intermediate [1]; Cl⁻ is then eliminated as C=O re-forms, so the mechanism is addition–elimination, even though the overall change looks like a substitution [1].

**11. (a)** Electrophile: the benzenediazonium ion, C₆H₅N₂⁺ [1]; it attacks the ring of the phenoxide ion (phenol in alkali) [1].

**(b)** Electrophilic substitution [1].

**(c)** The 4-position relative to the –O⁻ (–OH) group [1].

**12.** Benzene has a delocalised π system that gives it extra stability [1]; addition would destroy this delocalisation, while substitution restores it after the intermediate loses H⁺ [1]. Cyclohexene has a localised π bond with no extra stability to lose, and addition converts the π bond into two stronger σ bonds, so addition is favoured [1].

**13. (a)** C₆H₅COCl + C₆H₅OH → C₆H₅COOC₆H₅ + HCl [1].

**(b)** Addition–elimination [1]; the nucleophile is phenol, through a lone pair on its oxygen atom [1].

**(c)** Hydrogen chloride, HCl [1]; steamy fumes turn the moist blue litmus paper red [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling acyl chloride reactions as nucleophilic substitution.
- Giving only one word of a two-word mechanism name.
- Leaving out the H⁺ loss in electrophilic substitution, or the C=O re-forming in addition–elimination.
- Starting curly arrows at atoms or positive charges.
- Forgetting the equation that generates the electrophile, or the one that regenerates the catalyst.
