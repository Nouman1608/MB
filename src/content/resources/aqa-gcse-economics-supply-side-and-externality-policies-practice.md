## Questions

**1.** Which of the following is a supply-side policy? **[1]**
A) Raising interest rates  B) Increasing benefits  C) Investment in training  D) Raising VAT

**2.** Define privatisation. **[1]**

**3.** Explain how a cut in corporation tax could increase economic growth. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **one** disadvantage of using lower income tax as a supply-side policy. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how investment in education and training could improve the balance of payments. **[3]**

**6.** Define a positive externality and give an example. **[2]**

**7.** A town's factory emits smoke that damages nearby residents' health. State **two** policies the government could use to reduce this externality. **[2]**

**8.** A government places a tax of 0.50 units per litre on fizzy drinks. Sales fall from 40 million litres to 31 million litres.
**(a)** Calculate the tax revenue raised. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in sales. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why a government might subsidise bus travel. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why a tax on a harmful good might conflict with the objective of reducing inequality. **[3]**

**11.** A government wants to cut carbon emissions from cars. It can either ban new petrol cars in ten years' time or give a subsidy to buyers of electric cars. Evaluate which policy is better. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The transfer of a state-owned firm or industry to private ownership [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Firms keep more of their profits after tax [1], so they have more funds and a stronger incentive to invest in machinery and new technology [1]; this raises productive capacity, so real GDP can grow [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Any one developed: it reduces tax revenue [1], so less is available for public services [1] unless the government borrows more [1]; or high earners gain most in cash terms [1], so inequality may widen [1], conflicting with another objective [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Workers become more skilled and productive [1], so domestic firms produce higher-quality goods at lower cost [1]; exports become more competitive, so export sales rise and the current account improves [1]. **[3]**

**6.** A benefit to a third party that is not reflected in the market price [1], e.g. others are protected when a person is vaccinated [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Any two: a tax on emissions [1]; regulation setting emission limits or fines [1]; providing information about the harm [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** 0.50 × 31 million = **15.5 million units** [1].
**(b)** (40 − 31) ÷ 40 × 100 [1] = **22.5%** [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Bus travel has positive externalities, such as less congestion and pollution when people switch from cars [1]; the market price ignores these benefits so too little bus travel is used [1]; a subsidy lowers fares, so more people use buses [1]. **[3]**

**10.** An indirect tax adds the same amount to the price for everyone [1], so it takes a larger share of income from low-income households [1]; the tax is regressive and so widens inequality [1]. **[3]**

**11.** *Ban:*
- Certain effect: no new petrol cars after the deadline [1], so emissions fall as old cars are replaced [1].
- Costs the government little directly [1].
- But it removes choice and may raise car prices, hurting low-income drivers [1].

*Subsidy:*
- Lowers the price of electric cars, so more people choose them now [1].
- But it costs the government money [1] and mainly benefits buyers who can afford new cars [1].
- The effect is less certain if people still prefer petrol cars [1].

*Judgement:* For example, the ban gives a more certain cut in emissions, but combining it with a subsidy would speed up the switch and soften the cost for consumers [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing supply-side policy with fiscal or monetary policy.
- Stating a policy without explaining how it increases productive capacity.
- Treating pollution as a private cost to the firm.
- Writing one-sided evaluations with no judgement.
