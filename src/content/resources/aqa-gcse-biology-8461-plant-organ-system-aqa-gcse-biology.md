## What the specification asks for

- "The roots, stem and leaves form a plant organ system for transport of substances around the plant."
- Explain how root hair cells, xylem and phloem are adapted to their functions.
- Describe transpiration and translocation, "including the structure and function of the stomata."
- Explain the effect of changing temperature, humidity, air movement and light intensity on the rate of transpiration.
- Use simple compound measures such as the rate of transpiration; translate between graphs and numbers; plot graphs with suitable scales; extract and interpret data (MS 1a, 1c, 2c, 4a, 4c).
- Practical skills: "Measure the rate of transpiration by the uptake of water"; "Investigate the distribution of stomata and guard cells"; process data to find means, understand sampling, and "calculate surface areas and volumes" (MS 2a, 2d, 5c).
- "Detailed structure of phloem tissue or the mechanism of transport is not required."

## One organ system, two transport routes

| | Transpiration stream (xylem) | Translocation (phloem) |
|---|---|---|
| Substances | Water and mineral ions | Dissolved sugars (food molecules) |
| From → to | Roots → stems → leaves | Leaves → rest of the plant, for immediate use or storage |
| Direction | Upwards | To wherever sugars are needed, up or down |
| Tissue structure | Hollow tubes strengthened by lignin | Tubes of elongated cells; cell sap moves through pores in the end walls |

## Root hair cells: adapted for uptake

The specification states: "Root hair cells are adapted for the efficient uptake of water by osmosis, and mineral ions by active transport."

- A long, thin projection gives a **large surface area**, so more water and ions are absorbed per second.
- Water moves in by **osmosis**, from the dilute soil water into the more concentrated cell sap.
- Mineral ions are usually more concentrated inside the cell than in the soil, so they are taken in by **active transport**, against the concentration gradient. This needs energy from respiration, so root hair cells contain **many mitochondria**.

## Transpiration as a process

1. Water evaporates from the surfaces of mesophyll cells into the air spaces of the leaf.
2. Water vapour diffuses out through open stomata into the air.
3. Water moves from the xylem in the leaf into the mesophyll cells to replace what is lost.
4. This pulls water up the xylem from the roots: the **transpiration stream**.
5. Root hair cells take in more water from the soil by osmosis.

## Stomata and guard cells

"The role of stomata and guard cells are to control gas exchange and water loss." Each stoma is a pore between two guard cells.

- When guard cells take in water and become **turgid**, they curve apart and the **stoma opens**.
- When they lose water and become **flaccid**, they close the **stoma**.
- Stomata usually open in light, when carbon dioxide is needed for photosynthesis, and close in the dark or when the plant is short of water, which reduces water loss.

This is a trade-off: open stomata allow carbon dioxide in but also let water vapour out.

## Four factors and how they combine

| Factor increased | Effect on rate | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Faster | Water molecules have more energy, so evaporation and diffusion are faster |
| Humidity | Slower | Less difference in water vapour concentration between the leaf and the air |
| Air movement | Faster | Water vapour is blown away from the leaf, keeping the concentration gradient steep |
| Light intensity | Faster | Stomata open wider for photosynthesis, so more water vapour can escape |

On a real day the factors act together. A hot, dry, windy, sunny afternoon gives a very high rate; a cool, humid, still night gives a very low one. If water is lost faster than the roots can take it up, guard cells lose water, the stomata close, and the rate falls even though conditions favour transpiration.

## Rate calculations

The rate of transpiration is a compound measure: an amount of water divided by time. Measuring water **uptake** with a potometer gives an estimate, because a little of the water taken up is used in the plant rather than lost.

### Worked example 1: repeat readings and percentage change

A potometer bubble was timed for 10 minutes, three times in still air and three times with a fan (invented data).

| Condition | Distance moved (mm) | Mean (mm) | Rate (mm/min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Still air | 12, 14, 13 | 13 | 1.3 |
| Fan | 31, 29, 33 | 31 | 3.1 |

```
Mean (fan) = (31 + 29 + 33) / 3 = 31 mm; rate = 31 / 10 = 3.1 mm/min
Percentage increase = (3.1 - 1.3) / 1.3 x 100 = 138% (to 3 significant figures)
```

The close repeat readings suggest the results are repeatable.

### Worked example 2: volume from bubble distance (MS 5c)

The capillary tube has a radius of 0.75 mm, and the bubble moves 24 mm.

```
Cross-sectional area = pi x r^2 = 3.14 x 0.75^2 = 1.766 mm^2
Volume = area x distance = 1.766 x 24 = 42.4 mm^3 (to 3 s.f.)
```

Keep the unrounded area until the final step; rounding to 1.77 first would give 42.5 mm³.

### Worked example 3: rate per unit leaf area

Bigger shoots lose more water simply because they have more leaf. To compare shoots fairly, divide by leaf area. A student draws round the leaves on 1 cm² grid paper: 38 whole squares plus 14 part squares, each counted as half.

```
Leaf area = 38 + 14 / 2 = 45 cm^2
Uptake: 0.42 cm^3 in 20 minutes = 0.021 cm^3/min = 1.26 cm^3 per hour
Rate per unit area = 1.26 / 45 = 0.028 cm^3 per cm^2 per hour
```

## Graph skills

- Put the independent variable (for example distance of the fan) on the x-axis and the rate on the y-axis.
- Choose scales that use more than half the grid, with even steps.
- Plot points accurately and draw a line or curve of best fit.
- Describe the trend with data, then explain it using the four-factor reasons.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** transpiration: evaporation, diffusion through stomata, replacement from xylem, transpiration stream.
- **Explain** an adaptation: feature, effect, function (root hair, xylem, phloem).
- **Explain** a factor: direction of change plus the reason in terms of evaporation, diffusion or stomata.
- **Calculate**: show working, carry units, round to the precision asked.

## Common errors

- Saying mineral ions enter by osmosis or diffusion; they enter by active transport.
- Saying phloem carries water or that xylem carries sugars.
- Saying transpiration is "water being lost through the roots".
- Saying higher humidity increases transpiration.
- Forgetting that a potometer measures uptake, not loss.
- Comparing shoots of different sizes without dividing by leaf area.
