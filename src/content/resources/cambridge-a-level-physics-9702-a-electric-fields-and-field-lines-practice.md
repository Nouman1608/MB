Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, mass of electron = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, mass of proton = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and g = 9.81 m s⁻².

## Section A

**1.** Explain why the definition of electric field strength refers to a **small positive** test charge. **[2]**

**2.** Express the unit of electric field strength, N C⁻¹, in SI base units. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the force on an electron in an electric field of strength 2.4 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹, and state its direction relative to the field. **[2]**

**4.** A charge of −6.0 nC experiences a force of 1.5 × 10⁻³ N to the left. Calculate the electric field strength and state its direction. **[2]**

**5.** State four rules that are followed when an electric field is represented by field lines. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why two field lines can never cross. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** An oil drop of mass 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg is held stationary in a vertical electric field of strength 6.54 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹. The field points downwards.

**(a)** State the direction of the electric force on the drop, and hence the sign of its charge. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the charge on the drop. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the number of excess electrons on the drop. **[1]**

**(d)** The direction of the field is suddenly reversed without changing its strength. Calculate the initial acceleration of the drop. **[2]**

**8.** Describe the field-line patterns for

**(a)** two equal positive point charges a short distance apart

**(b)** a positive and a negative point charge of equal magnitude a short distance apart.

For each, state where the field is strongest, and identify any point where the field is zero. **[5]**

**9.** A proton and an alpha particle (charge +2e, mass 4.0 u) are each placed in a uniform electric field of strength 5.0 × 10³ N C⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the force on each particle. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the acceleration of each particle. **[2]**

**10.** At a point P, the electric field due to one charge is 3.0 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹ due east, and the field due to a second charge is 4.0 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹ due north.

**(a)** Calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant field at P. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the magnitude of the force on a +2.0 nC charge placed at P. **[1]**

**11.** A small positively charged sphere is held near a large flat metal plate that has been earthed.

**(a)** Describe the field-line pattern between the sphere and the plate. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the field lines meet the plate at right angles. **[2]**

**12.** Calculate the ratio of the electric force to the gravitational force on an electron in a field of strength 1.0 N C⁻¹, and comment on your answer. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **Small** so that it does not disturb (move) the charges that produce the field, which would change the field being measured [1]. **Positive** because the direction of the field is defined as the direction of the force on a positive charge [1].

**2.** N C⁻¹ = (kg m s⁻²) ÷ (A s) [1] = **kg m s⁻³ A⁻¹** [1].

**3.** F = qE = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.4 × 10⁴ = **3.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ N** [1], **opposite** to the direction of the field [1].

**4.** E = F ÷ q = 1.5 × 10⁻³ ÷ 6.0 × 10⁻⁹ = **2.5 × 10⁵ N C⁻¹** [1], to the **right** (opposite to the force on a negative charge) [1].

**5.** Any four: lines show the direction of the force on a positive charge [1]; they start on positive and end on negative charges [1]; they never cross [1]; closer lines mean a stronger field [1]; they meet conductor surfaces at right angles.

**6.** At any point the field has only **one direction**, so the force on a positive charge there has one direction [1].

**7. (a)** **Upwards**, to balance the weight; the field points down, so the charge is **negative** [1].

**(b)** qE = mg, so q = (3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 9.81) ÷ 6.54 × 10⁴ [1] = **4.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ C** [1].

**(c)** 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **3** [1].

**(d)** Electric force now acts **downwards**, adding to the weight: resultant force = 2mg [1]; a = 2g = **19.6 m s⁻²** downwards [1].

**8. (a)** Lines leave both charges radially and **bend away from each other**; no lines join the two charges [1]. There is a **neutral point midway** between them where the field is zero [1].

**(b)** Lines leave the positive charge and **curve round to end on the negative** charge [1]. The field is strongest on the line between the charges, where the lines are closest [1]. There is **no** point between them where the field is zero (the two fields point the same way there) [1].

**9. (a)** Proton: F = eE = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 5.0 × 10³ = **8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ N** [1]. Alpha: F = 2eE = **1.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ N** [1].

**(b)** Proton: a = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ ÷ 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = **4.8 × 10¹¹ m s⁻²** [1]. Alpha: a = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ ÷ (4.0 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷) = **2.4 × 10¹¹ m s⁻²** [1].

**10. (a)** E = √((3.0 × 10⁴)² + (4.0 × 10⁴)²) [1] = **5.0 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹** [1], at tan⁻¹(4/3) = **53° north of east** [1].

**(b)** F = qE = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁴ = **1.0 × 10⁻⁴ N** [1].

**11. (a)** Lines leave the sphere **radially** near its surface [1] and **curve** to meet the plate, more densely on the part of the plate nearest the sphere [1].

**(b)** The plate is a conductor, so its charges are free to move [1]; if the field had a component along the surface, charges would move until that component was zero, so the field is perpendicular to the surface [1].

**12.** Electric force = eE = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ N; weight = mg = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 9.81 = 8.94 × 10⁻³⁰ N [1]. Ratio = **1.8 × 10¹⁰** [1]. Even in a very weak field, the gravitational force on an electron is **negligible** compared with the electric force [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "positive" out of explanations of the field direction.
- Drawing field-line patterns without arrows, or with lines crossing.
- Giving the force on an electron in the direction of the field.
- Adding perpendicular fields as if they were in a straight line.
- Forgetting that a balanced oil drop needs the electric force upwards.
- Ignoring gravity for a charged drop, where it matters, or including it for an electron, where it does not.
