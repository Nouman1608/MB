This guide teaches section **3.1.3.3 Metallic bonding** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The specification statement is short: metallic bonding involves attraction between delocalised electrons and positive ions arranged in a lattice. The marks come from explaining properties with it, so this page concentrates on describing the model precisely, predicting how strong the bonding is, and writing the explanations examiners reward. The [Bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-bonding/) puts metallic bonding alongside the other types. ## The model

A metal atom has few outer electrons and holds them loosely. In a solid metal each atom releases its outer electrons into a shared pool. What is left is a regular lattice of **positive ions** (cations). The outer electrons are **delocalised**: they are not fixed to one atom but are free to move throughout the structure.

The metallic bond is the **electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons**. It acts in all directions through the whole lattice, so the structure is a giant one.

Use these exact words in an answer:

- the particles in the lattice are **positive ions**, not atoms and not molecules;
- the electrons are **delocalised** (or "free to move");
- the force is **electrostatic attraction**.

The common phrase "sea of electrons" is a useful picture, but marks are given for the three points above.

### Worked example 1: describing magnesium

A student is asked to describe the bonding in magnesium.

Mg atoms each lose their two outer electrons. The structure is a lattice of Mg²⁺ ions surrounded by delocalised electrons. There is strong electrostatic attraction between the Mg²⁺ ions and the delocalised electrons throughout the lattice.

A diagram would show rows of circles labelled 2+ with small dots (or "e⁻") between them.

## What controls the strength

Three factors decide how strong the metallic bond is.

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Charge on the metal ion (equal to the number of delocalised electrons per atom) | A higher charge means more delocalised electrons and a stronger attraction |
| Radius of the metal ion | A smaller ion allows the delocalised electrons to get closer to the nucleus, so the attraction is stronger |
| Number of delocalised electrons per unit volume | More electrons packed into the same space give a greater attraction (the effect of charge and radius together is often called charge density) |

### Worked example 2: sodium, magnesium and aluminium

| Metal | Ion | Delocalised electrons per atom | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | Na⁺ | 1 | 98 |
| Magnesium | Mg²⁺ | 2 | 650 |
| Aluminium | Al³⁺ | 3 | 660 |

Explain the trend in melting point.

From Na to Al the ion has a bigger charge (1+, 2+, 3+) and so provides more delocalised electrons. The ions also get smaller as the nuclear charge rises across the period, so the delocalised electrons are closer to the positive ions. Both factors increase the electrostatic attraction, so more energy is needed to separate the ions from the delocalised electrons and the melting point rises. The rise from magnesium to aluminium is small because other factors, such as the structure of the lattice, also affect the melting point.

### Worked example 3: down a group

Explain why the melting point falls from lithium (181 °C) to potassium (63 °C).

The ions have the same charge, 1+, and one delocalised electron per atom. Going down the group the ions get larger (more electron shells), so the distance between the nuclei and the delocalised electrons increases. The electrostatic attraction weakens and less energy is needed to melt the metal.

## Properties explained by the model

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| High melting and boiling points | Strong electrostatic attraction between ions and delocalised electrons throughout the lattice needs much energy to overcome |
| Good electrical conductor (solid and liquid) | Delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge when a voltage is applied |
| Good thermal conductor | Delocalised electrons transfer kinetic energy through the lattice quickly; the ions also vibrate and pass on energy |
| Malleable and ductile | Layers of positive ions can slide over each other, and the delocalised electrons keep the ions bonded in the new position, so the structure does not shatter |
| Insoluble in water | No attraction strong enough to overcome the metallic bonding; the metal does not dissolve unless it reacts |

### Metals and ionic solids compared

This comparison is often asked.

| | Metal | Ionic solid |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile particles in the solid | Delocalised electrons | None |
| Conducts when solid | Yes | No |
| Conducts when molten | Yes | Yes, ions are free to move |
| When struck | Layers slide; the metal bends | Like charges align; the crystal shatters |

The key difference is that, in a metal, the electrons are mobile in the **solid** and the bonding is not broken when layers slide. In an ionic solid the ions are fixed and a shift of one layer places like charges together.

### Worked example 4: malleability

Explain why aluminium can be hammered into a sheet without breaking.

When a force is applied, layers of Al³⁺ ions slide past each other. The delocalised electrons move with them and remain between the ions, so the electrostatic attraction continues in the new positions. The lattice is distorted but not broken.

(A common mistake is to say the "bonds break and reform". The bonding is non-directional, so the same attraction is simply present in the new arrangement.)

### Worked example 5: conductivity

Explain why the electrical conductivity of aluminium is greater than that of sodium.

Aluminium has three delocalised electrons per atom and sodium has one, so aluminium has more charge carriers per unit volume. More electrons are available to carry the current.

## Exam technique

Always do three things in a metallic bonding answer: name the **particles** (positive ions and delocalised electrons), name the **force** (electrostatic attraction) and state the **consequence** (energy needed, or ability to move).

- **Describe** metallic bonding: lattice of positive ions, delocalised electrons, electrostatic attraction.
- **Explain** a melting point trend: compare charge, ion size and number of delocalised electrons.
- **Explain** conduction: say that the delocalised electrons are free to move, not that "electrons flow" or "the ions move".
- **Draw**: show at least three rows of ions with the charge written in each, and label the electrons.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "metal atoms" or "metal molecules" instead of positive ions in the lattice.
- Saying metals contain free ions. Only the electrons are mobile.
- Stating that metallic bonds are between pairs of atoms, rather than throughout a lattice.
- Writing that "the metal ions move" to explain conduction.
- Comparing melting points using only the number of outer electrons and ignoring ion size.
- Saying that melting a metal breaks covalent bonds.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are the delocalised electrons the same as the electrons in an ionic compound?**
No. In an ionic compound each electron belongs to a particular ion. In a metal they are shared by the whole structure.

**Why does a metal conduct heat so well?**
Delocalised electrons carry kinetic energy quickly through the lattice, and the vibrating ions also pass energy on.

**Do I need to know the melting points of metals?**
No. Data are given when needed. You must be able to explain a given trend.
