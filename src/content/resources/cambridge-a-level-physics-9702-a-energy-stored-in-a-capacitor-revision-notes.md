Subtopic 19.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) asks you to determine the electric potential energy stored in a capacitor from the area under the potential–charge graph, and to recall and use W = ½QV = ½CV². These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Energy stored in a capacitor study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-energy-stored-in-a-capacitor/); to apply the ideas, try the [Energy stored in a capacitor practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-energy-stored-in-a-capacitor-practice/).

## The argument in five steps

1. With charge Q on the capacitor, the p.d. is V = Q/C.
2. Adding a small charge ΔQ needs work ΔW = VΔQ: a thin strip under the V–Q graph.
3. Summing the strips from 0 to Q gives the total work done, which is the energy stored.
4. V ∝ Q, so the V–Q graph is a straight line through the origin (gradient 1/C).
5. Area of the triangle = ½QV.

The ½ arises because the charge is moved across a p.d. that rises steadily from 0 to V, so the average p.d. is V/2.

## The equations

```
W = ½QV = ½CV²          (both required by the syllabus)
W = Q²/(2C)             (follows from Q = CV; useful when Q is fixed)
ΔW = ½C(V₁² − V₂²)      (energy released between two p.d.s)
Work done by supply when charging to V:  QV  (half stored, half dissipated)
```

- C in **F**, Q in **C**, V in **V**, W in **J**.
- 1 μF = 10⁻⁶ F; 1 mC = 10⁻³ C; 1 mJ = 10⁻³ J.

## Reading a graph

| Graph given | Energy stored is | Capacitance is |
|---|---|---|
| V (y) against Q (x) | area between line and Q-axis (triangle under the line) | 1 ÷ gradient |
| Q (y) against V (x) | area between line and Q-axis (triangle to the left of the line) | gradient |
| Energy between two p.d.s | trapezium between the two charges | — |

## Scaling rules

| Change | Effect on W |
|---|---|
| V doubled, same C | × 4 |
| V halved, same C | × ¼ |
| C doubled, same V (still connected) | × 2 |
| C doubled, same Q (isolated) | × ½ |
| Q doubled, same C | × 4 |

## Charge sharing in three lines

```
Charge conserved:     Q_total = C₁V₁ + C₂V₂   (before connection)
Common p.d.:          V = Q_total / (C₁ + C₂)
Energy after:         ½(C₁ + C₂)V²  — always less than before
```

For a charged capacitor joined to an identical uncharged one, the p.d. halves and the stored energy halves. The missing energy is dissipated in the resistance of the connecting wires.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electric potential energy (of a capacitor) | Energy stored in the electric field between the plates; equal to the work done in charging |
| Potential–charge graph | Graph of p.d. V against charge Q; straight line through the origin for a capacitor |
| Area under the graph | Sum of VΔQ strips; equals the energy stored |
| Capacitance C | Charge stored per unit p.d., C = Q/V, unit farad (F) |
| Farad | One coulomb per volt |
| Charge sharing | Connecting a charged capacitor to another so charge redistributes until both have the same p.d. |

## Typical sizes

- 470 μF at 12 V stores about 0.034 J; 2200 μF at 9.0 V stores 0.089 J.
- Capacitors store little energy compared with cells but can release it in a very short time, so the power can be large.
- A result of kilojoules for a laboratory electrolytic capacitor at a few volts means a unit slip (μF treated as F).

## Exam traps

- Using W = QV for energy stored.
- Writing ½C(V₁ − V₂)² for an energy change.
- Leaving capacitance in μF or charge in mC.
- Taking the area on the wrong side of the line when the axes are swapped.
- Assuming energy is conserved when two capacitors share charge.
- Saying the "lost" energy is destroyed; it is transferred to internal energy in the wires (and some radiated).
- Forgetting that a larger charging resistor does not change the energy dissipated during charging, only how long charging takes.

## Self-test

1. What does the area under a graph of p.d. against charge for a capacitor represent?
2. Why is the energy stored ½QV and not QV?
3. Write W in terms of Q and C.
4. A 47 μF capacitor is charged to 15 V. Calculate the energy stored.
5. The p.d. across a capacitor is tripled. By what factor does the stored energy change?
6. A capacitor stores 0.50 J at 25 V. Calculate its capacitance.
7. A 330 μF capacitor discharges from 12 V to 6.0 V. Calculate the energy released.
8. What fraction of the stored energy remains when the p.d. has fallen to half its initial value?
9. A V–Q graph is a straight line through (4.0 mC, 8.0 V). Calculate the energy stored at 8.0 V.
10. A supply of e.m.f. 6.0 V charges a capacitor fully, moving 3.0 mC. How much energy is dissipated in the circuit resistance?
11. A 50 μF capacitor at 40 V is connected to an uncharged 50 μF capacitor. Find the common p.d. and the total energy stored afterwards.
12. An isolated charged capacitor has its capacitance halved while its charge stays the same. What happens to the stored energy?

**Answers:** 1. The energy stored (work done in charging the capacitor). 2. The p.d. rises from 0 to V while the charge is added, so the average p.d. is V/2; the area under the straight-line graph is a triangle, ½QV. 3. W = Q²/(2C). 4. ½ × 47 × 10⁻⁶ × 225 = 5.3 × 10⁻³ J. 5. × 9. 6. C = 2W/V² = 1.0/625 = 1.6 × 10⁻³ F. 7. ½ × 330 × 10⁻⁶ × (144 − 36) = 0.018 J. 8. One quarter. 9. ½ × 4.0 × 10⁻³ × 8.0 = 0.016 J. 10. Supply does QV = 0.018 J; stored ½QV = 0.009 J; dissipated 0.009 J (9.0 mJ). 11. Q = 2.0 × 10⁻³ C on 100 μF, so V = 20 V; W = ½ × 100 × 10⁻⁶ × 400 = 0.020 J (half the original 0.040 J). 12. W = Q²/(2C), so it doubles.
