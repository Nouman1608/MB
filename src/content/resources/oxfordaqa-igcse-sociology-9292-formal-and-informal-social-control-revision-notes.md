**These notes cover the social control content of 3.2.2.3** in OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology
(9292): "Formal and informal social control", "Sanctions, both positive and negative", "Consensus and
conflict views of social control", the agencies of informal and formal control, and "Sociological
perspectives on social control: functionalist; Marxist; feminist; social action/interactionist". They
condense the [formal and informal social control study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-formal-and-informal-social-control/)
and follow on from the [Socialisation revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-socialisation-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Social control | The ways a society encourages conformity to norms and discourages deviance |
| Informal social control | Unofficial pressure through everyday relationships, enforcing unwritten norms |
| Formal social control | Official enforcement of written rules by agencies with authority |
| Sanction | A reward or punishment that encourages conformity |
| Positive sanction | A reward for conforming |
| Negative sanction | A punishment for breaking a norm or rule |
| Consensus view | Social control protects shared values and benefits everyone |
| Conflict view | Social control serves the interests of powerful groups |
| Collective conscience | Durkheim's term for a society's shared values and beliefs |
| Repressive state apparatus | Althusser: agencies that control by force (police, courts, army) |
| Ideological state apparatus | Althusser: agencies that control by shaping beliefs (education, media, religion, family) |
| Moral entrepreneur | Becker: a person or group that campaigns to create or enforce a rule |

## The sanctions grid

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Informal** | Praise; popularity; a smile | Gossip; teasing; being ignored |
| **Formal** | Certificate; promotion; medal | Fine; detention; dismissal; prison |

## Agencies and how they control

| Agency | Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Families | Informal | Rewards and punishments; pressure over marriage, careers, reputation |
| Schools | Informal and formal within the school | Teachers' approval; hidden curriculum; written rules and detentions |
| Peers | Informal | Desire to belong; teasing and exclusion |
| Media | Informal | Defining normal and shameful; online shaming |
| Religion | Informal | Moral codes; community approval; beliefs about reward and punishment |
| Workplace | Informal and formal | Promotion and bonuses; colleagues' opinion; disciplinary procedures |
| Government and laws | Formal | Defines crimes and punishments |
| Police and enforcement agencies | Formal | Investigation, arrest, regulation |
| Criminal justice systems | Formal | Courts decide guilt and sentence; prisons and probation |

## Perspectives in one line each

- **Functionalist (consensus):** Durkheim: punishment reinforces the collective conscience and marks
  the boundaries of acceptable behaviour.
- **Marxist (conflict):** Althusser: repressive and ideological state apparatuses maintain ruling-class
  power; property laws are enforced more strictly than laws regulating the powerful.
- **Feminist (conflict):** Heidensohn, *Women and Crime* (1985): women are controlled at home, in
  public and at work, which helps explain their lower recorded crime.
- **Interactionist:** Becker, *Outsiders* (1963): deviance results from labelling; rules are created
  by moral entrepreneurs and enforced selectively.

## Evaluation points

- Functionalism ignores **whose values** become laws.
- Marxism underplays laws that **protect everyone** and inequalities other than class.
- Feminism must recognise **variation** between women and between societies.
- Interactionism explains **how** labels are applied, less **why** some groups have the power to
  apply them.

## Model paragraph: a discussion point

"From a consensus view, laws against violence protect everyone, which supports Durkheim's argument
that social control maintains shared values. However, Marxists such as Althusser argue that the
police and courts are repressive state apparatuses that protect ruling-class interests, for example
by enforcing laws on theft more strictly than laws on corporate wrongdoing. In [a named society], the
way laws on public protest are enforced could be used to judge which view is more convincing."

## Exam traps

- Mixing up the boxes of the sanctions grid.
- Calling schools purely informal agencies.
- Listing agencies without mechanisms.
- A perspective with no sociologist, or a sociologist with no argument.
- Confusing consensus and conflict (the overarching views) with the four perspectives.
- Using "social control" and "socialisation" as if they were the same.

## Self-test

1. Define social control.
2. What is the difference between formal and informal social control?
3. Give an example of an informal positive sanction and a formal negative sanction.
4. Name **three** agencies of informal social control.
5. Name **three** agencies of formal social control.
6. Explain why a school can be both an agency of formal and informal control.
7. What is the difference between the consensus and conflict views of social control?
8. What did Durkheim argue punishment does for society?
9. Distinguish Althusser's repressive and ideological state apparatuses.
10. What did Heidensohn argue about the control of women?
11. What is a moral entrepreneur?
12. Give one criticism of the functionalist view of social control.

**Answers:** 1. The ways a society encourages people to follow its norms and discourages deviance.
2. Formal control uses written rules enforced by official agencies; informal control uses unofficial
pressure from everyday relationships to enforce unwritten norms. 3. For example, praise from a
friend; a fine from a court. 4. Any three: families; schools; peers; media; religion; workplace.
5. Any three: government and laws; police; other enforcement agencies such as customs; courts;
prisons and probation. 6. It has written rules and official sanctions such as detentions, and also
everyday approval and peer pressure. 7. The consensus view sees control as protecting shared values
for everyone; conflict views see it as serving powerful groups. 8. It reinforces the collective
conscience and reminds everyone of the boundaries of acceptable behaviour. 9. Repressive apparatuses
control by force (police, courts, army); ideological apparatuses control by shaping beliefs
(education, media, religion, family). 10. That women are controlled more closely than men at home, in
public and at work, which helps explain their lower recorded crime. 11. Becker's term for a person or
group that campaigns to create or enforce a rule. 12. It ignores whose values become laws and assumes
a consensus that may not exist.
