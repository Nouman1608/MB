These notes condense section 14.1 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696), for examination in 2025 and 2026: the nature and roles of the four economic sectors; the nature, physical and human causes and distribution of global inequalities in wellbeing; and the critical evaluation of measures and indices of inequality. Full explanations and worked calculations are in the [14.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-national-development/). Exam-style questions are in the [14.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-national-development-practice/). The whole option is summarised in the [Topic 14 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-economic-transition-revision-notes/).

## Sectors in one table

| Sector | Examples | Typical share of jobs as a country develops |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Farming, fishing, mining, forestry | Falls from a majority to a few per cent |
| Secondary | Manufacturing, construction, processing | Rises during industrialisation, then falls (deindustrialisation) |
| Tertiary | Retail, transport, health, tourism | Rises steadily; largest sector in high-income countries |
| Quaternary | Research, ICT, finance, consultancy | Grows in the richest economies and world cities |

**Roles in development.** Primary: food and early exports, labour released by rising farm productivity. Secondary: value added, urban jobs, linkages to suppliers. Tertiary: supports other sectors, grows with income. Quaternary: innovation that raises productivity everywhere.

**Two sharpening points.**

- Employment share is not output share: farming may employ most people but produce a small share of output because productivity is low.
- Linkages decide how far a sector spreads benefits. Raw exports with no processing create few linkages.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Deindustrialisation | Decline in the share of jobs (and often output) in manufacturing |
| Informal sector | Work outside official regulation and records, such as street trading |
| Backward linkage | Demand a firm creates for its suppliers |
| Forward linkage | Activity a firm supports among the firms using its products |
| Wellbeing | Quality of life, both economic (income, jobs) and social (health, education, rights) |
| PPP (purchasing power parity) | Adjusts income for differences in prices between countries |
| Geometric mean | Cube root of the product of three values; used in the HDI |
| Lorenz curve | Graph of cumulative income share against cumulative population share |
| Gini coefficient | Area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve, divided by the total area under the line; 0 = equal, 1 = unequal |
| Composite index | A single number combining several indicators |

## Causes of inequality as chains

- **Landlocked** → transport through other countries → higher trade costs → less investment.
- **Disease burden** → missed school and work → lower productivity → lower income.
- **Climatic hazards** → repeated asset loss → recovery spending crowds out investment.
- **Single-commodity exports** → income follows world prices → unstable revenue and debt.
- **Conflict** → destroyed infrastructure and displacement → investment deterred.
- **Weak governance** → diverted funds and unreliable contracts → poor services.
- **Low human capital** → workforce cannot move into higher-value work.

Physical causes are seldom decisive alone; human responses such as irrigation or disease control change their impact.

## Distribution

- Highest wellbeing: North America, Western Europe, Japan, South Korea, Australasia; some Gulf states high on income.
- Lowest: much of sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia.
- The 1980 Brandt line no longer fits well because of East Asian growth and widening inequality within countries.

## Measures: construction and judgement

| Measure | Built from | Use it for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| GNI per capita (PPP) | Total income ÷ population, price-adjusted | Average income comparisons | Hides distribution and informal work |
| HDI | Geometric mean of health, education and income indices | Broad human development | National averages; three dimensions only |
| Gini coefficient | Lorenz curve areas | How unequally income is shared | Says nothing about absolute poverty |
| Multidimensional Poverty Index | Ten household indicators | Overlapping deprivation | Sensitive to cut-offs and weights |
| Gender Inequality Index | Reproductive health, empowerment, labour market | Gaps between women and men | Data gaps in many countries |

**Why the geometric mean matters.** Fictional indices 0.80, 0.60, 0.70 give an HDI of 0.695; 0.90, 0.40, 0.80 give 0.660. Both have an arithmetic mean of 0.700, but the uneven profile scores lower.

## Exam traps

- Treating sector models as fixed laws; countries can skip or shorten stages.
- Naming a cause without its mechanism.
- Saying the HDI uses adult literacy; it uses years of schooling.
- Calling the Gini a poverty measure.
- Evaluating with weaknesses only; give a strength and say when the measure is most useful.
- Describing a distribution with no exceptions or named regions.

## Self-test

1. Define the quaternary sector and give two examples.
2. Why might a country's primary sector employ 60% of workers but produce a much smaller share of output?
3. What is deindustrialisation?
4. Give the mechanism by which a landlocked location can reduce wellbeing.
5. Give one physical and one human cause of inequality, each with its effect.
6. Which three dimensions does the HDI combine?
7. Why does the HDI use a geometric rather than arithmetic mean?
8. What does a Gini coefficient of 0 mean?
9. Give one limitation of GNI per capita as a measure of wellbeing.
10. Which measure would you use to compare deprivation within households, and why?
11. Why is the Brandt line now a weak description of global inequality?
12. What is a backward linkage?

### Answers

1. Knowledge and information work, such as research and development, ICT or financial consultancy.
2. Output per farm worker is low (low productivity), so many workers produce relatively little value.
3. A decline in the share of employment (and often output) in manufacturing.
4. Exports and imports must cross other countries to reach ports, raising cost and delay, so trade and investment are lower.
5. Physical: drought destroys crops and livestock, cutting income. Human: conflict destroys infrastructure and deters investment (accept any valid pair).
6. Health (life expectancy), education (mean and expected years of schooling) and income (GNI per capita at PPP).
7. It penalises uneven development, so a high score in one dimension cannot fully offset a low score in another.
8. Perfect equality: everyone has the same income.
9. It is an average and hides how income is distributed; it also misses informal and unpaid work.
10. The Multidimensional Poverty Index, because it records overlapping deprivations in each household rather than national averages.
11. Many East Asian and some Gulf economies are now high income, and inequality within countries has grown.
12. Demand a firm creates for its suppliers, such as a car plant buying steel and components.
