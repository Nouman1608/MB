## Questions

**1.** Define "profit for the year" and state how it differs from gross profit. **[3]**

**2.** Skelmanby Bakery had revenue of £520,000, cost of sales of £301,600, operating expenses of £171,600 and finance costs of £7,800. Tax was 20% of profit before tax.

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit margin. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit for the year margin. **[3]**

**3.** Jessup Tools invests £120,000 in a new machine that is expected to add £15,600 to profit each year. Its long-term funding is £180,000 of loans and £420,000 of equity.

**(a)** Calculate the return on investment. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the proportion of long-term funding that is debt. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the owner might set a capital structure objective that limits debt. **[2]**

**4.** Crowcombe Garden Centre set a budget for one month and recorded the actual results below.

| Item | Budget (£) | Actual (£) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 36,000 | 38,700 |
| Materials | 14,400 | 16,200 |
| Wages | 9,000 | 9,450 |
| Overheads | 5,000 | 4,700 |

**(a)** Calculate the variance for each item and state whether it is adverse or favourable. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the variance in monthly profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one limitation of relying on variance analysis. **[2]**

**5.** Trelowan Surf School expects the following cash flows. It starts April with £1,500.

| | April | May | June |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash in (£) | 9,000 | 14,000 | 16,000 |
| Cash out (£) | 11,000 | 12,500 | 12,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the closing balance for each month. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how an overdraft could help in April and state one risk of relying on it. **[2]**

**6.** Belcaster Pottery sells mugs for £18 each. Variable cost is £7 per mug and fixed costs are £38,500 a year. It plans to make and sell 4,500 mugs.

**(a)** Calculate the contribution per mug. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate break-even output. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the margin of safety, in mugs. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the profit at planned output. **[2]**

**7.** Belcaster Pottery considers cutting the price to £16, with other figures unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate the new break-even output, in whole mugs. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new margin of safety at 4,500 mugs. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why a break-even forecast may be unreliable. **[2]**

**8.** Define "debt factoring" and state one advantage and one disadvantage of using it. **[4]**

**9.** Wrothamby Wholesale buys £31,500 of stock a month from suppliers on 30 days' credit and sells £48,000 of goods a month to customers on 60 days' credit. The business is profitable. Analyse why it may still face cash flow problems. **[5]**

**10.** Ambleforth Joinery has long-term funding of £150,000 of loans and £450,000 of equity. It needs £150,000 for a new workshop that should add £21,000 a year to operating profit. A bank offers a loan at 8% a year. Alternatively, a venture capital firm would buy new shares. Evaluate which source the owner should choose. **[9]**

**11.** Quarrenden Signs has revenue of £300,000 and cost of sales of £180,000, which varies with volume. The owner considers two ways to raise gross profit. Option A is a 10% price rise, which is expected to cut volume sold by 6%. Option B is a change of supplier that is expected to cut cost of sales by 5% with no change in volume. Evaluate which option the owner should choose. **[9]**

**12.** A start-up selling reusable lunch boxes online faces several established rivals with low prices. Analyse how technology and market conditions might influence its financial decisions. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Profit for the year is operating profit less finance costs (such as interest) and tax [1]. Gross profit is only revenue minus cost of sales [1], so it is calculated before operating expenses, finance costs and tax and is larger [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name what is deducted; "what is left over" alone does not separate the two measures.

**2. (a)** Gross profit = 520,000 − 301,600 = £218,400 [1]. Margin = 218,400 ÷ 520,000 × 100 = **42%** [1].
**(b)** Operating profit = 218,400 − 171,600 = £46,800; profit before tax = 46,800 − 7,800 = £39,000 [1]. Tax = 20% × 39,000 = £7,800, so profit for the year = **£31,200** [1]. Margin = 31,200 ÷ 520,000 × 100 = **6%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the profit line by line so a reader can see which stage went wrong.

**3. (a)** ROI = 15,600 ÷ 120,000 [1] × 100 = **13%** [1].
**(b)** Long-term funding = 180,000 + 420,000 = £600,000 [1]. Debt proportion = 180,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **30%** [1].
**(c)** Debt brings a fixed commitment to pay interest and repay [1], which can threaten cash flow if profits fall, so a limit protects the business (or keeps ownership with the owner) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing debt by equity alone gives a different ratio from the one asked for; use total long-term funding as the denominator.

**4. (a)** Revenue +£2,700, favourable [1]; materials +£1,800, adverse [1]; wages +£450, adverse [1]; overheads −£300, favourable [1].
**(b)** Budgeted profit = 36,000 − 14,400 − 9,000 − 5,000 = £7,600 [1]. Actual profit = 38,700 − 16,200 − 9,450 − 4,700 = £8,350, so variance **£750 favourable** [1].
**(c)** It shows what differed, not why [1], so managers may act on the wrong cause, for example when the budget itself was unrealistic [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The label depends on the effect on profit, so a higher cost is adverse even though the number is positive.

**5. (a)** April: 1,500 + 9,000 − 11,000 = **−£500** [1]. May: −500 + 14,000 − 12,500 = **£1,000** [1]. June: 1,000 + 16,000 − 12,000 = **£5,000** [1].
**(b)** An overdraft would cover the £500 shortfall so bills could be paid until May's receipts arrive [1]. Risk: interest is charged and the bank can withdraw or limit it [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the finance to the month in the table.

**6. (a)** 18 − 7 = **£11** [1].
**(b)** 38,500 ÷ 11 [1] = **3,500 mugs** [1].
**(c)** 4,500 − 3,500 = **1,000 mugs** [1].
**(d)** Total contribution = 4,500 × 11 = £49,500 [1]; profit = 49,500 − 38,500 = **£11,000** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use per-unit variable cost in the contribution step; total variable cost would give a wrong figure.

**7. (a)** Contribution = 16 − 7 = £9 [1]. Break-even = 38,500 ÷ 9 = 4,277.8, so **4,278 mugs** [1].
**(b)** 4,500 − 4,278 = **222 mugs** [1].
**(c)** Break-even assumes everything made is sold at one price and that costs behave in straight lines [1]; in practice demand may change when price changes or discounts are needed, so the figures may not hold [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Round break-even output up to a whole unit; the margin of safety then shows how thin the cushion has become.

**8.** Debt factoring is selling unpaid customer invoices (receivables) to a specialist firm for immediate cash [1], with the factor collecting the money later [1]. Advantage: releases cash quickly, improving cash flow [1]. Disadvantage: a fee is charged, which reduces income [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each advantage or disadvantage with a short reason; a bare "cheap" or "costly" is thin.

**9.** Wrothamby pays suppliers after 30 days but receives customers' money after 60 days [1], a mismatch of about a month [1]. It must pay £31,500 for stock before the related £48,000 arrives [1], so it may be unable to pay wages or bills despite earning a profit [1]. If it grows, it buys more stock before cash arrives, so the funding gap widens, forcing it to borrow or factor invoices [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Build the chain from timing, to the figures, to the effect on the business.

**10.** Loan: debt rises to £300,000 of £750,000 funding, a proportion of 40% (from 25%) [1]. Interest is 8% × 150,000 = £12,000, cutting the extra £21,000 to £9,000 [1]. Repayments are fixed even if the workshop under-performs [1], but the owner keeps full control [1]. Share issue: debt falls to £150,000 of £750,000, or 20% [1]; there is no interest or repayment to fund [1], but the venture capital firm takes a share of ownership and influence, and may want a share of profits [1]. Judgement: the loan suits an owner confident of the £21,000 and keen to keep control, because the net £9,000 gain is kept entirely and 40% is manageable [1]; the share issue suits an owner worried about sales risk, so the answer depends on confidence in the forecast [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation is stronger when the decision rests on a stated condition, such as confidence in the forecast.

**11.** Currently gross profit = 300,000 − 180,000 = £120,000 (40%) [1]. Option A: revenue = 300,000 × 1.10 × 0.94 = £310,200 and cost of sales = 180,000 × 0.94 = £169,200 [1], so gross profit = £141,000, a rise of £21,000 [1]. Option B: cost of sales = 180,000 × 0.95 = £171,000, so gross profit = £129,000, a rise of £9,000 [1]. Risks of A: if volume falls by more than 6%, or rivals hold prices, the gain shrinks [1]. Risks of B: a cheaper supplier may reduce quality or reliability, harming sales [1]. The figures are forecasts, and B is easier to reverse [1]. Judgement: A gives more than twice the gain on the forecasts, so it is preferable if demand is not very price sensitive [1]; test the volume assumption first, and prefer B if it looks doubtful [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculate both options before judging; a recommendation supported by the figures from the case is easier to credit than a preference stated first.

**12.** Technology: crowd funding lets the start-up raise small sums from many people online [1], which reduces dependence on bank loans and interest [1], but it takes marketing effort and fees may apply [1]. Market conditions: strong competition and low rival prices limit the price it can charge [1], so margins are thinner and it may set tighter cost objectives or need cash for promotion [1]. Judgement: fund growth cautiously until margins are proven [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each factor to a specific financial decision, such as the source of finance or the profit target, rather than describing the factor in general.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling variances by whether the number is positive or negative, not by the effect on profit.
- Using debt ÷ equity instead of debt ÷ total long-term funding.
- Giving break-even output as a decimal without rounding up to a whole unit.
- Mixing up margin of safety and break-even output.
- One-sided "evaluate" answers with no reasoned judgement.
