This guide covers one subtopic of AQA GCSE Physics (8463) in depth: **4.2.4 Energy transfers**, made up of
4.2.4.1 Power, 4.2.4.2 Energy transfers in everyday appliances and 4.2.4.3 The National Grid. It is assessed on
Paper 1 at both tiers. Four equations must be recalled, and you must be able to explain, not just calculate.

For quick recall, use the [energy transfers revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-energy-transfers-aqa-electricity-revision-notes/).
To test yourself, use the [energy transfers practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-energy-transfers-aqa-electricity-practice/).

## The four equations and what each one means

```
power = potential difference × current          P = V I
power = current² × resistance                   P = I² R
energy transferred = power × time               E = P t
energy transferred = charge flow × pd           E = Q V
P in W, V in V, I in A, R in Ω, E in J, t in s, Q in C
```

**Power** is the rate of energy transfer: 1 W is 1 J transferred each second. A device has a high power if it
has a large pd across it, a large current through it, or both.

**E = QV** describes what potential difference is: the energy transferred per coulomb of charge. A 12 V supply
transfers 12 J to every coulomb that passes through it, and the charge transfers that energy in the components.
**Work is done when charge flows** in a circuit, and the amount of work done is the energy transferred.

**P = I²R** is useful when you know the current and resistance but not the pd. It also shows that heating in a
resistor depends on the **square** of the current: double the current and the heating power is four times as
great.

### Choosing the right equation

| You know | You want | Use |
|---|---|---|
| V and I | P | P = VI |
| I and R | P | P = I²R |
| P and t | E | E = Pt |
| Q and V | E | E = QV |
| I and t, and V | E | Q = It first, then E = QV (or P = VI, then E = Pt) |

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 (power and current).** A 1.8 kW hairdryer runs on 230 V. Calculate the current.

```
P = 1.8 kW = 1800 W
I = P ÷ V = 1800 ÷ 230
I = 7.8 A  (2 s.f.)
```

**Worked example 2 (two routes to one answer).** A 6.0 V battery drives 0.50 A through a motor for 5.0 minutes.
Calculate the energy transferred.

```
t = 5.0 × 60 = 300 s
Route 1: P = V I = 6.0 × 0.50 = 3.0 W;   E = P t = 3.0 × 300 = 900 J
Route 2: Q = I t = 0.50 × 300 = 150 C;   E = Q V = 150 × 6.0 = 900 J
```

Getting the same answer both ways is a good check in multi-step questions.

**Worked example 3 (P = I²R and doubling).** A 20 Ω resistor carries 0.30 A. Calculate its power. Then find the
power if the current is doubled and the resistance stays the same.

```
P = I² R = 0.30² × 20 = 0.090 × 20 = 1.8 W
At 0.60 A: P = 0.60² × 20 = 0.36 × 20 = 7.2 W   (four times as much)
```

**Worked example 4 (rearranging E = Pt).** An 800 W microwave oven transfers 360 kJ. How long was it on?

```
E = 360 kJ = 360 000 J
t = E ÷ P = 360 000 ÷ 800
t = 450 s  (7.5 minutes)
```

## Energy transfers in everyday appliances

Appliances are designed to bring about energy transfers. The energy an appliance transfers depends on **how long
it is switched on** and its **power**.

| Appliance | Energy from | Useful transfer to |
|---|---|---|
| Electric fan | ac mains | Kinetic energy of the motor and blades |
| Kettle | ac mains | Thermal energy store of the water (heating device) |
| Battery-powered drill | Chemical store of the battery | Kinetic energy of the motor |
| Electric heater | ac mains | Thermal energy store of the room (heating device) |
| Hairdryer | ac mains | Kinetic energy (motor and fan) and thermal energy (heating element) |

**Power ratings and stored energy.** The power rating tells you how much energy the appliance transfers each
second. A 3.0 kW kettle increases the thermal energy store of its water by up to 3000 J every second, while a
1.5 kW kettle does it by up to 1500 J every second. With the same mass of water starting at the same temperature,
the 3.0 kW kettle reaches boiling in about half the time. In a battery appliance, a higher power means the chemical
store of the battery is emptied faster.

## The National Grid

The National Grid is a system of **cables and transformers** linking power stations to consumers.

1. Power stations generate electricity.
2. **Step-up transformers** increase the pd to a very high value for the transmission cables.
3. Transmission cables carry the power across the country.
4. **Step-down transformers** decrease the pd to a much lower value for domestic use.

### Why it is an efficient way to transfer energy

For a fixed power, P = VI, so a **higher pd means a smaller current**. The power wasted heating the cables is
I²R, which depends on the square of the current. A smaller current therefore wastes much less energy, so a larger
fraction of the power from the station reaches consumers. The pd is then stepped down because a very high pd would
be dangerous in homes.

**Worked example 5.** A power station sends 50 MW through cables of total resistance 2.0 Ω. Compare the heating
loss at 100 kV and at 400 kV.

```
At 100 kV: I = 50 000 000 ÷ 100 000 = 500 A
           loss = 500² × 2.0 = 500 000 W  (0.50 MW, 1% of the power)
At 400 kV: I = 50 000 000 ÷ 400 000 = 125 A
           loss = 125² × 2.0 = 31 250 W   (about 0.031 MW)
```

Four times the pd gives a quarter of the current and one sixteenth of the heating loss.

How transformers work is in 4.7.3, which is Higher tier only; for this subtopic you only need what they do to the
pd and why.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to square the current in P = I²R, or squaring the resistance.
- Leaving kW as kW or minutes as minutes in E = Pt.
- Saying step-up transformers increase the current.
- Explaining the National Grid with "less energy is lost" but no link to current and heating.
- Saying higher-power appliances "use more electricity" without referring to energy per second.
- Confusing charge (C) with energy (J) in E = QV.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** why the National Grid is efficient: higher pd → lower current for the same power → less heating
  in the cables (I²R) → less energy wasted. Four links, in order.
- **Describe** an energy transfer: name the source (battery or mains) and the store or motion it ends in.
- **Calculate** with multiple steps: write each intermediate value with its unit, so an early slip costs only
  one mark.
- **Show that** questions: work to one more significant figure than the value given, and state it at the end.
