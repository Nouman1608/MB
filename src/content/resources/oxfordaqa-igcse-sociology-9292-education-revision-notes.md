## Education and schooling (3.1.2.1)

- **Formal** (planned, in institutions) v **informal** (everyday learning).
- **Official curriculum** (subjects taught) v **hidden curriculum** (values taught through routines, such as punctuality and obedience).
- **Levels**: nursery or pre-school, primary, secondary, tertiary.
- **School types**: state or public, private, selective, non-selective, faith, international, boarding, progressive, vocational.
- **Online learning**: wider access but needs devices and connection.

## Perspectives (3.1.2.2)

| Perspective | Key ideas | Sociologists |
|---|---|---|
| Functionalism | Shared values; role allocation; human capital; bridge from particularistic to universalistic standards | Durkheim, Parsons, Davis and Moore |
| Marxism | Schooling differs by class; reproduces and legitimises class; prepares workers; myth of meritocracy | Bowles and Gintis, Bourdieu (cultural capital) |
| Radical critics | Schools create dependence; "banking" education | Illich, Freire |
| Feminisms | Schools shape gender roles and expectations | Sharpe |

## Relationships and processes (3.1.2.3)

- **Interactionism**: micro-level classroom interaction.
- **Labelling** → **self-fulfilling prophecy**. **Becker**: the **ideal pupil**. **Rosenthal and Jacobson**: "spurters" field experiment.
- **Stereotyping**; **halo effect** (one good impression colours all judgements).
- **Sanctions**: positive (praise, rewards) and negative (detention, exclusion).
- **Subcultures**: pro-school and anti-school (**Willis**, "the lads").
- **Ethos and curricula**: ethnocentrism, vocational routes, unequal access to knowledge; racism and sexism.

## Attainment (3.1.2.4)

- **Measuring**: exams, IQ tests (cultural bias). **Credentialism**: rising demand for qualifications.
- **Material**: money for resources, space, tuition.
- **Cultural**: cultural capital, language, attitudes.
- **Home and community**: parental support and expectations.
- **In-school**: labelling, setting, subcultures, curriculum.
- Class, gender and ethnicity **intersect**.

## Global education (3.1.2.5)

Literacy rates; differences in provision (free and compulsory years, class sizes, trained teachers, buildings); poverty (child labour, fees, uniforms); gender and other barriers (early marriage, disability, rural location, conflict); the digital divide. Example: Malala Yousafzai and girls' education in Pakistan.

## Evaluating the perspectives

Examiners reward points that weigh one view against another. Learn one strength and one criticism for each.

| Perspective | Strength | Criticism |
|---|---|---|
| Functionalism | Explains why every modern society invests in schooling: shared values, skills and sorting for jobs | Assumes selection is fair; Marxists and interactionists show class, labelling and cultural capital shape results, not ability alone |
| Marxism | Explains why working-class pupils on average achieve less, despite equal-sounding rules | Can be deterministic: Willis's lads actively rejected school rather than being passively shaped by it, and some working-class pupils succeed |
| Radical critics | Questions whether schools teach people to think for themselves | Deschooling offers few practical alternatives for mass education |
| Feminisms | Shows how subject choice, careers advice and expectations steer boys and girls differently | In many countries girls now do as well as or better than boys in school exams, so the focus has partly shifted to why some boys underachieve |
| Interactionism | Shows how inequality is made in everyday classroom judgements | Can ignore wider structures such as poverty and the type of school a pupil can attend |

**Linking the levels.** A strong answer connects the **macro** (structural) view of functionalism and Marxism with the **micro** view of interactionism. For example, Bourdieu's cultural capital (structure) helps explain why some pupils fit Becker's ideal pupil (interaction), which then feeds a self-fulfilling prophecy.

## Explaining differences in attainment

Use the four types of factor for each social group, and say that they combine.

- **Social class**: material deprivation (cramped housing, no quiet space, no tuition, need for part-time work); cultural capital and language that match or do not match the school's; parents' own education and confidence with the system; labelling and placement in lower sets.
- **Gender**: changing job opportunities and expectations for women; feminist challenges to gender stereotyping; teacher expectations and subject steering; boys' anti-school or "laddish" subcultures where effort is seen as uncool.
- **Ethnicity**: material disadvantage shared by some minority groups; language spoken at home; an ethnocentric curriculum that leaves some pupils feeling excluded; racism, stereotyping and harsher sanctions in some schools; strong family emphasis on education in some communities, which raises attainment.
- **Intersection**: class, gender and ethnicity act together, so state the combination you are explaining rather than treating each as separate.

**Evaluation point.** In-school explanations (labelling, setting) and out-of-school explanations (material, cultural, home) are not alternatives: the out-of-school factors can shape the labels pupils receive.

## Global education: building an answer

When a question asks about education in different societies:

1. **Name the barrier** (cost of fees and uniforms, child labour, early marriage, distance to school, disability, conflict, lack of trained teachers, no internet).
2. **Explain the mechanism**: who is kept out and why. For example, if a poor family can afford fees for only one child, sons may be chosen where boys are expected to be the main earners.
3. **Apply it** to a named society or a widely known case such as Malala Yousafzai's campaign for girls' schooling in Pakistan.
4. **Contrast** with a society where provision is free, compulsory and widely accessed.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name the point; no explanation needed.
- **Describe**: say what something is or what it involves, with detail.
- **Explain**: give the reason or process, using "because" and "so" to link cause to effect.
- **Discuss how far**: present arguments on more than one side, support them with sociologists and examples, and finish with a justified judgement.

## Model paragraph: a discussion point

*Topic: do schools provide equal opportunities?*

> Functionalists such as Davis and Moore argue that schools sort pupils fairly by ability through role allocation, so the most talented reach the most important jobs. However, Bourdieu argues that middle-class pupils arrive with cultural capital that schools reward, so their success looks like merit when it partly reflects their background. This supports the Marxist view that meritocracy is a myth. Interactionists add that teachers may label working-class pupils as less able, as Becker's ideal pupil suggests, which can lead to a self-fulfilling prophecy. Overall, schools may offer formally equal rules, but unequal starting points and classroom processes mean opportunities are not equal in practice.

Why it works: a view, a counter-view with a named sociologist, a linked micro-level point, and a judgement that answers the question.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Role allocation | Sorting people into jobs by ability |
| Meritocracy | A system where success depends on ability and effort |
| Cultural capital | Knowledge, language and tastes valued by schools |
| Ideal pupil | Teachers' image of the perfect pupil |
| Self-fulfilling prophecy | A prediction that comes true because it was made |
| Credentialism | Increasing demand for formal qualifications |
| Ethnocentric curriculum | One that centres one culture and marginalises others |
| Hidden curriculum | Values and attitudes taught through school routines and rules |
| Human capital | The skills and knowledge that make workers productive |
| Deschooling | Illich's call to replace compulsory schooling with freer learning |
| Banking education | Freire's term for teachers depositing facts in passive pupils |
| Halo effect | One positive impression colouring all of a teacher's judgements of a pupil |
| Anti-school subculture | A pupil group whose values reject the school's |
| Digital divide | Unequal access to devices, internet and digital skills |

## Exam traps

- Hidden curriculum ≠ informal education.
- Cultural capital is not money.
- Labels can be rejected, so the prophecy is not automatic.
- One-factor explanations of attainment gaps.
- Global education points with no example from a named society, when the question asks for one.
- Writing "Marxists think school is bad": say what it does (reproduces and legitimises class inequality, prepares a compliant workforce).
- Describing a study without saying what it shows about education.
- "Discuss" answers with only one side, or with no final judgement.
- Treating the halo effect and labelling as identical: the halo effect is one good impression spreading to other judgements.

1. What did Parsons mean by school as a "bridge"?
2. What is the myth of meritocracy?
3. What did Rosenthal and Jacobson find?
4. Why did Willis's lads reject school?
5. Give one material and one cultural explanation of class differences in attainment.
6. What is credentialism?
7. What is the difference between the official and the hidden curriculum?
8. What did Bowles and Gintis mean by a correspondence between school and work?
9. What is Freire's "banking" education?
10. Give one criticism of the Marxist view of education.
11. Give two barriers to girls' education in some low-income countries.
12. What does it mean to say class, gender and ethnicity intersect?

**Answers:** 1. School moves the child from the family's particularistic standards to society's universalistic standards. 2. The Marxist claim that success appears to be based on merit but is really shaped by class. 3. Pupils whom teachers expected to "spurt" often made greater gains, showing the power of expectations. 4. They saw schoolwork as pointless for the manual jobs they expected and valued having a laugh. 5. Material: lack of money for books, devices or study space. Cultural: less cultural capital valued by school. 6. The growing demand for formal qualifications for jobs. 7. The official curriculum is the subjects formally taught; the hidden curriculum is the values pupils learn through routines, such as punctuality and obedience. 8. School life mirrors work, for example obeying teachers prepares pupils to obey employers. 9. Teaching in which teachers deposit knowledge in passive pupils, rather than developing critical thinking. 10. It can be deterministic, ignoring pupils who resist or succeed (Willis's lads chose to reject school). 11. Any two: fees or uniform costs, early marriage, child labour or household work, distance and safety, conflict. 12. They combine, so a pupil may face several advantages or disadvantages at once and the groups cannot be explained separately.
