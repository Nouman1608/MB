This guide goes deeper on **3.2.2 Language, thought and communication**, the second topic in
**Paper 2: Social context and behaviour** of AQA GCSE Psychology (8182), first teaching 2017. The
paper lasts 1 hour 45 minutes, is worth 100 marks and counts for 50% of the GCSE. The
[Social Context and Behaviour study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-social-context-and-behaviour/)
gives the overview; this page adds the evidence, evaluation and application practice you need to
write full answers.

## What 3.2.2 covers

The topic has four parts:

1. **The possible relationship between language and thought** -- Piaget's theory (language depends
   on thought) versus the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (thinking depends on language), including
   variation in recall and in recognition of colours across cultures.
2. **Differences between human and animal communication** -- the limited functions of animal
   communication (survival, reproduction, territory, food) and Von Frisch's bee study.
3. **Non-verbal communication** -- eye contact, body language (including open and closed postures
   and postural echo), touch, and personal space with its cultural, status and gender differences.
4. **Explanations of non-verbal behaviour** -- Darwin's evolutionary theory versus evidence that
   non-verbal behaviour is learned, including Yuki's study of emoticons.

## Language and thought: two opposite claims

**Piaget's theory.** Piaget argued that thought comes first. Children develop concepts through
interacting with the world, and only later acquire words for them. A baby understands that objects
still exist when hidden (object permanence) before it can say "gone". So language **reflects**
thinking; it does not create it. Supporting evidence: Sinclair-de-Zwart (1969) taught children who
could not yet conserve the words used by children who could (such as "longer" and "shorter"), and
found this made little difference to their ability to conserve. Learning the words did not bring the
understanding with it.

**The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis.** Sapir and Whorf argued the reverse: the language you speak shapes
how you think. The **strong version** (linguistic determinism) says language determines thought, so
you cannot think about something your language has no words for. The **weak version** (linguistic
relativity) says language influences thought, making some ideas easier to notice and remember.

**Evidence on colour recognition.** Studies of the Berinmo people of Papua New Guinea, whose language
has fewer basic colour terms than English and divides the colour space differently, found that
speakers distinguished and remembered colours more easily when the colours fell on either side of a
boundary in their own language. English speakers showed the same advantage for English colour
boundaries. This supports the **weak** version: language affects how easily colours are recognised
and remembered.

**Evidence on recall.** In Carmichael and colleagues' classic study (1932), participants were shown
ambiguous drawings with one of two labels -- for example, the same shape described as "eyeglasses"
or "dumbbells". When they later drew the shapes from memory, their drawings tended to look more like
the label they had been given. Words shaped what was remembered. Across cultures, people also recall
events in ways that reflect how their language describes them.

**Evaluation.** The strong version is generally rejected: people can think about things they have no
words for (we can see shades of colour we cannot name), and new words are invented for new ideas,
which suggests thought can come first. The weak version has more support. Piaget's view fits
evidence from young children but may underplay the way that language, once learned, helps us
organise and remember ideas.

## Human and animal communication

Animal communication is mostly limited to four functions:

| Function | Example |
|---|---|
| Survival | Vervet monkeys give different alarm calls for different predators |
| Reproduction | Courtship displays and calls |
| Territory | Birdsong warning rivals away |
| Food | The honeybee dance |

**Von Frisch's bee study.** Von Frisch marked individual honeybees and placed feeding stations at
different distances and directions from the hive, then observed the bees that returned. He found two
dances:

- the **round dance**, used when food is close to the hive, telling other bees to search nearby;
- the **waggle dance**, used when food is further away. The angle of the straight "waggle run"
  relative to vertical on the honeycomb showed the direction of the food relative to the sun, and
  the length of the waggle showed the distance.

Other bees then flew to the correct location. **Evaluation:** this was careful, repeated
observation, and later research has supported it; however, bees cannot use the dance to
communicate anything other than the location of resources.

**How human language differs.** Human language has **displacement** (we can talk about the past,
the future, distant places and imaginary things), **creativity** or **productivity** (we can combine
a limited set of words into an unlimited number of new sentences), and can express abstract ideas
and plans. Note the subtlety: the bee dance does refer to food that is not present, so it shows a
limited form of displacement, but only about one thing.

## Non-verbal communication

- **Eye contact** regulates the flow of conversation (who speaks next), signals interest and
  attraction, and expresses emotion. Prolonged staring can signal hostility.
- **Body language**: **open posture** (arms and legs uncrossed) signals friendliness and acceptance;
  **closed posture** (arms folded) can signal defensiveness. **Postural echo** is unconsciously
  mirroring another person's posture, which signals rapport and tends to increase liking.
- **Touch** can signal support, affection or status. Higher-status people tend to initiate touch
  more freely with lower-status people than the other way round.
- **Personal space** is the area around us that we feel is ours. Hall described zones, from intimate
  distance (close friends and family) to public distance (strangers, speakers). It varies with
  **culture** (some cultures stand closer when talking than others), **status** (people keep more
  distance from those of higher status) and **gender**.

## Explanations of non-verbal behaviour

**Darwin's evolutionary theory.** Darwin argued that facial expressions and other signals are innate
and evolved because they helped survival -- for example, a fear expression warns others of danger,
and baring teeth signals threat. Supporting evidence: babies show facial expressions very early,
children born blind produce similar expressions to sighted children, and Ekman found that people in
an isolated culture in Papua New Guinea recognised the same basic emotional expressions as Western
participants.

**Evidence that it is learned.** **Yuki's study** (2007) compared Japanese and American participants
rating how happy or sad emoticons and faces looked, when the eyes and mouth were varied. Japanese
participants gave more weight to the **eyes**, American participants to the **mouth**. Yuki linked
this to cultural norms: in Japan, controlling emotional expression is valued, so people look to the
eyes, which are harder to control. Cultural differences in personal space and in gestures (the same
hand sign can be friendly in one country and offensive in another) also suggest learning.

**Evaluation.** The most balanced conclusion is that some basic expressions are innate but culture
shapes how and when they are displayed and how they are read.

## Worked example

*Question: Kai has moved from a country where people usually stand close when talking. His new
classmates keep stepping back from him. Using your knowledge of non-verbal communication, explain why.
[3]*

A strong answer: "Personal space varies with **culture** [1]. In Kai's home culture the normal
conversational distance is smaller [1], so when he stands at that distance his classmates feel
their personal space has been invaded and step back to restore their own comfortable distance [1]."

## Common mistakes

- Reversing Piaget and Sapir-Whorf. Piaget: thought then language. Sapir-Whorf: language then thought.
- Saying the waggle dance shows distance by its angle. **Angle = direction; length of waggle =
  distance.**
- Describing Yuki's study as evidence for Darwin. It is evidence that non-verbal behaviour is
  **learned**.
- Confusing postural echo (mirroring) with open posture.
- Describing body language without linking it to what it communicates.

## Exam technique

Questions on this topic often ask you to **outline** a theory, **explain** a difference or
**evaluate** an explanation. For "evaluate", use evidence: Sinclair-de-Zwart for Piaget, colour and
recall studies for Sapir-Whorf, blind-born children and Ekman for Darwin, Yuki for learning. For
application questions, name the non-verbal behaviour, say what it communicates and link it to the
scenario.
