## Section A

**1.** Give the meaning of the term "degraded environment". **[2]**

**2.** Explain why reduced river flow can make water pollution worse. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows faecal coliform counts (bacteria per 100 ml) at four sites along the fictional River Selna, from upstream to downstream. The safe bathing standard is 500 per 100 ml. A sewage programme began in the city in 2014.

| Site | Description | 2012 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | Upstream of the city | 400 | 350 |
| Q | City centre | 45 000 | 30 000 |
| R | Below an industrial zone and informal settlements | 60 000 | 52 000 |
| S | 30 km downstream | 12 000 | 6 000 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the count at site Q. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the pattern of faecal coliform counts along the river in 2024. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest why the improvement at site R was smaller than at site Q. **[3]**

**4.** A fictional city produces 900 million litres of sewage a day. Its treatment plants have a combined capacity of 600 million litres a day but operate, on average, at 70% of capacity.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the city's sewage that is not treated. Show your working. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest **two** reasons why the plants operate below capacity. **[4]**

**5.** Outline **two** reasons why sharing responsibility between several authorities can slow the improvement of a degraded environment. **[4]**

**6.** Distinguish between solutions that treat the causes and solutions that treat the symptoms of environmental degradation, giving an example of each. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** With reference to one degraded environment you have studied, explain the causes of its degradation. **[6]**

**8.** With reference to one degraded environment you have studied, describe the problems faced by people who live there or depend on it. **[6]**

**9.** Read the information about a fictional lake.

> Lake Marva is a shallow lake used for fishing and drinking water by 80 000 people. Algal blooms now cover it each summer. Studies show that 60% of the nutrients entering it come from fertiliser on surrounding farms and 40% from the untreated sewage of one town. Three proposals have been made: (A) a sewage treatment plant for the town, costing most of the regional budget for five years; (B) grass buffer strips along streams and limits on fertiliser use, with payments to farmers; (C) dredging the lake bed and removing algae each summer.

Assess which proposal, or combination, would be most effective in improving Lake Marva. **[8]**

**10.** "Attempts to improve degraded environments fail mainly because of a lack of money." With reference to one degraded environment you have studied, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** An environment whose quality has seriously declined [1] because of human activity such as pollution, over-use of water or land clearance, so that it no longer supports people or ecosystems as before [1]. **[2]**

**2.** River flow dilutes pollutants [1]. When dams, barrages or abstraction for irrigation reduce flow, the same amount of waste enters less water [1], so concentrations rise and oxygen levels fall further [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** (30 000 − 45 000) ÷ 45 000 × 100 [1] = −33.3%, a fall of one-third [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The count is lowest upstream at P (350), the only site below the bathing standard [1]. It rises sharply to 30 000 at Q and peaks at R (52 000) [1]. It falls downstream to 6 000 at S but remains twelve times the standard [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Informal settlements near R may have no sewer connections, so the city programme does not reach them [1]. Industrial effluent continues to add organic waste [1]. Water reaching R from the city centre is still heavily polluted, so R receives the city's remaining load plus its own [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Sewage treated = 600 × 0.70 = 420 million litres a day [1]; untreated = 900 − 420 = 480 million litres [1]; 480 ÷ 900 × 100 = 53.3% [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Some homes are not connected to sewers, so sewage does not reach the plants [1], which then receive less than their design volume [1]. Power cuts or lack of money for running costs and repairs [1] mean plants stop or run below their designed rate [1]. **[4]**

**5.** No single authority has full responsibility, so tasks are delayed or left undone [1], and each may wait for others to act first [1]. Authorities have different priorities and budgets [1], so measures are applied unevenly along a river or across a region [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Treating causes means reducing the source of degradation [1], for example building sewage treatment so waste no longer enters a river [1]. Treating symptoms means dealing with the effects after they appear [1], for example removing algae from a lake without reducing the nutrients that feed it [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Credit any degraded environment. Example for the River Ganga: towns and cities have grown faster than sewers and treatment works [1], so untreated sewage, the largest source, enters the river [1]. Tanneries around Kanpur and other industries discharge effluent containing chemicals and metals [1]. Fertilisers and pesticides wash in from intensively farmed land, a diffuse source [1]. Dams, barrages and canals reduce dry-season flow [1], so there is less water to dilute pollution [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Credit any degraded environment. Example for the River Ganga: high faecal bacteria make bathing and drinking water unsafe [1], spreading waterborne diseases [1]. Low dissolved oxygen downstream of cities reduces fish stocks [1], harming fishing livelihoods [1]. Metals such as chromium enter irrigation water and soils [1], and millions of pilgrims bathe in water that does not meet bathing standards [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Proposal A tackles 40% of the nutrient load [1] and also reduces disease risk for drinking water [1], but it uses most of the regional budget and leaves the larger farm source untouched [1]. Proposal B targets the largest source, 60% from fertiliser [1], and is cheaper, though it depends on farmers taking part and takes time to work [1]. Proposal C treats the symptoms, not the causes, so blooms would return each year [1]. The most effective approach combines A and B, tackling both sources [1], perhaps starting with B because it addresses the larger share at lower cost [1]. **[8]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define a degraded environment [1] and name the case study, such as the River Ganga [1].

*Agree:* treatment capacity has lagged behind sewage growth because building plants and sewers is costly [1]. Some plants have worked below capacity because cities lacked funds for running costs and repairs [1]. Poor households cannot pay for connections or services [1]. Programmes have grown with larger budgets, suggesting money was a limit [1].

*Disagree:* governance matters: responsibility is split between central government, states and cities [1], so even funded plans are applied unevenly [1]. Economic interests resist change, as tanneries provide jobs and exports [1]. Physical limits matter: low dry-season flow reduces dilution, whatever is spent on treatment [1]. Growth of cities outpaces any fixed investment [1]. Cultural practices need sensitive management, not only money [1]. In the Aral Sea, the South Aral was not saved because restoring it would require cutting irrigation across borders, not because a dike was unaffordable [1].

*Analysis:* money is necessary but not sufficient [1]. Spending on construction without operation and maintenance gives poor results [1]. The cause that limits success may change over time, from lack of plants to lack of connections and coordination [1].

*Use of case study:* detailed, named evidence for both sides [1], with judgement varied by stretch or area [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned conclusion on "how far", for example that money was the main barrier early on, but governance and growth now matter more [1]. A clear final view [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* the best essays identify which barrier matters most at different stages of the clean-up, using case-study evidence.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculations that apply 70% to the sewage volume instead of the capacity.
- Describing a pattern without values or reference to the standard.
- Confusing problems (effects) with issues (barriers to improvement).
- Choosing a proposal without weighing its cost and the share of the problem it addresses.
- Essays about degradation in general with no named place.
