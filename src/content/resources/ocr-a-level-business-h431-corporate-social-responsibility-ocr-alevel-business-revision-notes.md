## The six specification points

1. Explain what is meant by CSR.
2. Explain the potential conflict between CSR and profit and other objectives.
3. Evaluate the impact and importance of a CSR policy to a business and its stakeholders.
4. Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of a business's CSR profile to the business and its stakeholders.
5. Recommend and justify how a business could improve its CSR profile.
6. Evaluate the impact and importance of CSR to a business and its stakeholders.

## Core recall

### Meaning

- CSR: a business accepting responsibility for the effects of its activities on society, the environment and all stakeholders, beyond what the law requires.
- Voluntary: obeying the law is not CSR; choosing to do more is.
- Covers how the whole business operates (pay, sourcing, waste, marketing, community), not only donations.

### Four layers (a teaching model, not named in the specification)

| Layer | Required? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Yes, to survive | Make a profit, pay staff |
| Legal | Yes | Meet health and safety law |
| Ethical | No: CSR | Pay suppliers on time; refuse exploitative suppliers |
| Discretionary | No: CSR | Volunteering days, local sponsorship |

### Two views

- Shareholder view: business should maximise profit within the law (Milton Friedman, 1970). CSR must pay its way.
- Stakeholder view: business should balance the interests of all groups it depends on.

### Policy versus profile

- **Policy:** written commitments and how they will be delivered. Controlled by the business.
- **Profile:** how responsible the business is seen to be, judged on its record by customers, staff, media and investors.
- A gap between them is **greenwashing**: claims not matched by actions. It can leave the profile worse than before.

### The conflict with profit

- Costs come now (higher wages, dearer materials, new equipment); benefits are uncertain and later (loyalty, price premium, lower staff turnover, lower energy use, fewer fines).
- Conflict is usually sharpest in the short run and narrower in the long run.
- Quantify it: CSR cost as a percentage of profit; payback on a CSR investment; extra revenue needed to restore profit = profit gap ÷ contribution per £1 of revenue.

### Conflict with other objectives

| Objective | Why CSR can conflict |
|---|---|
| Growth | Supplier or market standards limit expansion options |
| Cost leadership | Extra costs undermine the lowest price |
| Survival | CSR is cut in a downturn, damaging the profile |
| Shareholder returns | Dividends fall in the short run |
| Market share | Higher prices lose price-sensitive buyers |

### Importance of CSR depends on

- Visibility to consumers (consumer brand or anonymous supplier).
- Industry risk (food, clothing, chemicals, transport are higher risk).
- Ownership (family owner or plc shareholders).
- What rivals do (an advantage, or just the minimum expected).
- Financial strength (margins and cash available).

### Improving a CSR profile: criteria for a recommendation

1. Does the action address the business's actual problem?
2. Is it credible and checkable (independent audit, published targets)?
3. Can the business afford it?
4. Which stakeholders benefit, and do they matter to the business?
5. How quickly will it show results?

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Corporate social responsibility | Voluntary acceptance of responsibility for the effects of business activity on society, the environment and stakeholders |
| CSR policy | A written statement of CSR commitments and how they will be met |
| CSR profile | The reputation a business has for responsible behaviour, based on its record |
| Greenwashing | Making environmental or social claims that actions do not support |
| Ethical sourcing | Buying from suppliers who meet standards on pay, conditions and environment |
| Social audit | An independent check of a business's social and environmental performance |
| Stakeholder | Any person or group with an interest in, or affected by, a business |

## Exam traps

- Legal compliance is not CSR.
- A policy is not the same as a good profile.
- Do not say CSR "always" cuts profit; use time period and customer response.
- A recommendation must link to the case: sponsorship does not fix a pollution problem.
- Weigh stakeholders; do not just list them.
- Use figures from the case to show the size of the conflict.

## Self-test

1. Define CSR.
2. Is paying the legal minimum wage an example of CSR? Explain.
3. Distinguish between a CSR policy and a CSR profile.
4. What is greenwashing, and why can it harm a business?
5. A firm has operating profit of £500,000 and a CSR plan costing £75,000 a year. What percentage of profit does the plan cost?
6. A £120,000 solar installation saves £20,000 a year. Calculate payback.
7. A firm needs £45,000 more profit. Each £1 of revenue brings 25p contribution. How much extra revenue is needed?
8. Give two reasons why CSR conflicts with profit in the short run more than in the long run.
9. Why might CSR matter more to a chocolate brand than to a firm making steel bolts?
10. Explain how CSR can conflict with a cost leadership strategy.
11. State three criteria for judging a recommendation to improve a CSR profile.
12. Name one stakeholder who may lose from a CSR policy, and explain why.

### Answers

1. A business voluntarily accepting responsibility for the effects of its activities on society, the environment and all stakeholders, beyond what the law requires.
2. No: it is a legal requirement. Paying above it by choice would be CSR.
3. The policy is the written commitment the business controls; the profile is how responsible it is seen to be, based on its record.
4. Claims of responsibility that actions do not support; when exposed, trust falls and the profile can end up worse than with no claims.
5. 75,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **15%**.
6. 120,000 ÷ 20,000 = **6 years**.
7. 45,000 ÷ 0.25 = **£180,000**.
8. Costs are paid immediately while benefits such as loyalty and lower staff turnover build slowly; savings such as lower energy bills accumulate over years.
9. The chocolate brand sells to consumers on trust and faces scrutiny of its supply chain; the bolt maker sells to businesses that may choose mainly on price and specification.
10. A cost leader needs the lowest costs to charge the lowest prices; CSR spending raises costs and squeezes the margin or forces a price rise.
11. Any three: addresses the actual problem; credible and checkable; affordable; benefits stakeholders that matter; results within a reasonable time.
12. For example shareholders, if dividends fall in the short run; or price-sensitive customers, if prices rise.
