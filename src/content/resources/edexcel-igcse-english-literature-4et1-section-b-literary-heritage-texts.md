This guide goes deeper on one part of Component 2 of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Literature (4ET1): **Section B, Literary Heritage Texts**. It is the second of the two 30-mark essays in
the 1 hour 30 minute open-book exam. For the whole paper, including Section A on modern drama, start
with the [Component 2 study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-component-2/).

## What Section B covers

You study one literary heritage text from the specification's list and answer **one essay question
from a choice of two** on it. Under the Issue 3 specification the list is *Romeo and Juliet*,
*Macbeth* and *The Merchant of Venice* (Shakespeare), *Pride and Prejudice* (Jane Austen), *Great
Expectations* (Charles Dickens) and *The Scarlet Letter* (Nathaniel Hawthorne). Set texts are reviewed
from time to time, so check the current specification for your exam series.

The 30 marks divide equally:

| AO | Marks | What it asks for in a heritage essay |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | 10 | Close knowledge of the whole text, a critical style and an informed personal response to the question |
| AO2 | 10 | Analysis of how language, form and structure create meanings and effects |
| AO4 | 10 | Understanding of the relationship between the text and the contexts in which it was written and received |

The important difference from Section A is AO4. A drama essay can score full marks without a word of
context; a heritage essay cannot.

## Key ideas

### 1. Context is a lens, not a topic

Examiners are not testing history. They want to see context **explaining a choice the writer made**
or **changing what a moment means**. A useful test for any contextual sentence: if you deleted it,
would your reading of the quotation become weaker? If not, it is decoration.

### 2. The five kinds of context

The specification names several kinds of context. Having one ready-made example of each for your
text gives you flexibility when the question is unfamiliar.

| Kind of context | Example of the kind of link to make |
|---|---|
| The writer's situation (only where it relates to the text) | Hawthorne writing about the Puritan society of his own ancestors |
| Historical setting, time and place | Venice as a trading city where Christian and Jewish merchants traded together but Jewish residents lived under legal restrictions |
| Social and cultural attitudes | Expectations that a daughter obeys her father's choice of husband in *Romeo and Juliet* |
| Literary context | *Great Expectations* as a novel of growing up, first published in weekly parts |
| Reception by different audiences | How a modern audience responds to the treatment of Shylock compared with an audience of the 1590s |

### 3. Writer, period and reader

Many heritage texts have **two or three time frames**: when the story is set, when it was written,
and when you are reading it. *The Scarlet Letter* is set in seventeenth-century Boston but was
published in 1850; *Great Expectations* was published in 1860–61 but looks back to the earlier part of
the century. Noticing the gap lets you ask why a writer looked backwards, which is often the most
interesting AO4 point available.

### 4. Open book changes the job

Your prescribed edition must be completely unmarked, but you can open it. That means quotations can
be accurate and well chosen, and the examiner will expect them to be. What you cannot look up in the
exam is context, so that is what you must carry in your head.

## Worked example 1: one paragraph, three objectives

**Question:** Explore how Austen presents attitudes to marriage in *Pride and Prejudice*.

A paragraph built on Charlotte Lucas's decision to marry Mr Collins:

> **Point (AO1):** Through Charlotte, Austen shows that marriage could be a practical bargain rather
> than a romantic choice, and she invites us to understand Charlotte even while Elizabeth is shocked.
> **Method (AO2):** Austen lets Charlotte explain herself in calm, reasonable statements, and the
> narrator presents her view without mockery, so the reader cannot dismiss her as foolish. The
> contrast with Elizabeth's reaction is structural: placing the two friends side by side makes the
> reader weigh love against security. **Context (AO4):** For a woman of Charlotte's class in the early
> nineteenth century, with little money of her own and no profession open to her, marriage was close
> to the only route to a home of her own. Austen's restraint suggests sympathy with a choice that
> society's rules made sensible, which complicates Elizabeth's judgement.

Notice that the context sentence explains *why* Charlotte's speech is so measured. It does not start a
new paragraph about the Regency period.

## Worked example 2: improving a bolt-on sentence

A student writing on *Macbeth* produces this:

> "Shakespeare uses dark imagery when Macbeth first thinks of murder. In Jacobean times people believed in
> the divine right of kings."

The two sentences sit next to each other but never meet. A stronger version:

> "Macbeth asks the stars to hide their light, wanting darkness to cover what he is about to do. For
> an audience who believed a king was chosen by God, killing one was a crime against heaven as well
> as against a man, so Macbeth's wish to hide from the sky shows he already knows the murder is
> sacrilege."

The context now explains the imagery (AO2) and supports an argument about Macbeth's awareness of
guilt (AO1).

## Planning a Section B answer in about 45 minutes

| Stage | Time | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Choose | 2 minutes | Read both questions; pick the one where you can name three moments and two contexts at once |
| Plan | 5 minutes | Write a one-line argument, then three or four paragraph headings, each with a moment and a context |
| Locate | 3 minutes | Find the passages in your unmarked copy and note page numbers on the plan |
| Write | 32 minutes | Roughly eight minutes per paragraph, plus a short introduction and conclusion |
| Check | 3 minutes | Make sure every paragraph contains at least one context link |

If Section A overran, shorten the plan rather than drop the context. AO4 is a third of this essay.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explore how / Explore the ways in which.** Range matters: look at more than one method (for
  example dialogue, narrative voice and structure) and more than one part of the text.
- **How does the writer present...** Keep the writer as the subject of your sentences: "Dickens
  makes...", "Shakespeare places...". This keeps your answer on AO2 rather than drifting into
  character gossip.
- **In what ways...** Usually invites a list of different aspects; organise paragraphs by aspect, not
  by chronology, so you do not slip into retelling.

Whatever the wording, open with a direct answer in one or two sentences. An introduction that only
gives the date and author is wasted time.

## Using the whole text

In an open-book essay you choose the evidence yourself, from anywhere in the text. Strong
answers usually draw from the **beginning, a turning point and the ending**, which shows structural
awareness. Weaker answers stay in the two or three scenes the student knows best.

Build a short navigation sheet in your study copy: for each act or group of chapters, note two key
moments and one context they connect to. Practise finding each moment in a clean copy in under a
minute.

## Common mistakes

- **The history opening.** A first paragraph of background facts earns little, because it is not
  linked to any moment in the text.
- **Context in the conclusion only.** One sentence at the end cannot show the "integrated" use of
  context the higher levels reward.
- **Biography without relevance.** Details of the writer's life count only where they shed light on
  the text.
- **Judging the past from the present without saying so.** "Capulet is a terrible father" is an
  opinion; "a modern audience may find Capulet's threats shocking, but an Elizabethan audience would
  recognise a father's legal authority" is an AO4 point about reception.
- **Over-quoting from the open book.** Long copied quotations waste time. Choose a few words and
  analyse them.
- **Treating the novel or play as real life.** Characters are constructed; ask why the writer made
  them behave that way.
