The aggregate demand curve itself, the multiplier, output gaps, the Phillips curve and the wider schools of thought are separate topics. Here AD is simply a downward-sloping curve that you can shift.

## 1. What aggregate supply is

**Aggregate supply (AS)** is the planned real output of the whole productive sector at each possible price level, over a stated period. The axes are the **price level** (a general price index, vertical) and **real output** (real GDP, horizontal).

The **short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)** curve slopes upwards. In the short run some costs, mainly nominal wages, are fixed by contracts. If the price level rises while costs stay put, profit per unit rises, so firms supply more. The reverse holds when the price level falls. Higher output also means overtime, less efficient machines and rising unit costs, which is why the curve steepens as capacity is approached.

The **long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)** curve shows the economy's productive capacity once all costs and prices have had time to adjust. Its position depends on the quantity and quality of factors of production and on technology, not on the price level.

## 2. Keynesian and neo-classical approaches

The two approaches disagree about the LRAS curve and about how quickly an economy returns to full employment.

| | Neo-classical | Keynesian |
|---|---|---|
| LRAS shape | Vertical at potential output | Can be horizontal, upward-sloping then vertical (a reverse-L shape) |
| Wages and prices | Flexible, so markets clear in the long run | Sticky downwards, so unemployment can last |
| Equilibrium | Economy gravitates to potential output | Equilibrium can sit below full employment for a long time |
| AD changes | Change only the price level in the long run | Change real output when there is spare capacity |

A sketch of the Keynesian curve (price level up, real output across):

```
P |                       |
  |                      /|  <- vertical: full capacity
  |                    /  |
  |  ---------------/     <- rising: bottlenecks
  |  flat: spare capacity
  +---------------------------- Real output
```

**Worked example 1 (invented Country B, Keynesian AS).** The AS curve is flat at a price level of 100 up to real output 400, then rises (real output = 4 × price level) up to output 500 at a price level of 125, then is vertical. AD starts as real output = 560 − 2 × price level and shifts right by 40, 120 and 80 in turn.

| AD curve | Equilibrium (price, output) | Change in output | Change in price |
|---|---|---|---|
| AD0: 560 − 2P | 100, 360 | | |
| AD1: 600 − 2P | 100, 400 | +11.1% | 0% |
| AD2: 720 − 2P | 120, 480 | +20.0% | +20.0% |
| AD3: 800 − 2P | 150, 500 | +4.2% | +25.0% |

Check AD2: 720 − 2P = 4P gives P = 120 and output 480. For AD3 the equilibrium is on the vertical section, so output is 500 and P = (800 − 500)/2 = 150. Demand boosts raise output only while there is spare capacity. Once the economy is at full capacity it raises only prices.

## 3. Why AS shifts

A **movement along** SRAS is caused by a change in the general price level. A **shift** comes from something else that changes costs or capacity.

| Shifts SRAS (short run) | Shifts LRAS (long run) |
|---|---|
| Wage costs and labour productivity changes | Size of the labour force, through migration or participation |
| Raw material and energy prices | Skills and education of the workforce |
| Exchange rate (changes the price of imported inputs) | Capital stock and infrastructure |
| Taxes on firms and subsidies | Technology and innovation |
| Supply shocks such as bad weather or disruption | Institutions, competition and regulation |

SRAS shifts right when costs fall and left when they rise. An LRAS shift is a change in potential output, so it also moves SRAS. A fall in the price of imported fuel shifts SRAS only. Investment in a new technology shifts both. Treat a change as long run only if it alters productive capacity.

## 4. Completing and reading an AD/AS diagram

A full diagram has labelled axes (price level, real output), downward-sloping AD, upward-sloping SRAS and a vertical LRAS at potential output (neo-classical view). **Macroeconomic equilibrium** is where AD meets SRAS: total planned spending equals the output firms will supply at that price level. **Long-run equilibrium** is where AD, SRAS and LRAS all meet.

**Worked example 2 (invented Country A).** AD0: output = 840 − 4P. SRAS0: output = 6P − 160. LRAS: 440.

| Price level | AD0 | SRAS0 |
|---|---|---|
| 80 | 520 | 320 |
| 90 | 480 | 380 |
| 100 | 440 | 440 |
| 110 | 400 | 500 |
| 120 | 360 | 560 |

Equilibrium: P = 100, real output 440, which is on LRAS. Now AD rises to AD1: output = 900 − 4P.

- Short run: 900 − 4P = 6P − 160, so P = 106 and output = 476. Real output rises by 36/440 = 8.18% and the price level by 6.0%. Output is 36 above potential.
- Long run: firms and workers face higher prices, so wages and costs rise and SRAS shifts left. The economy returns to output 440 on AD1, where P = (900 − 440)/4 = 115, which is 15.0% above the starting price level.

**Nominal and real.** Nominal GDP = price level × real output / 100. It was 440. In the short-run equilibrium it is 106 × 476 / 100 = 504.56, up 14.67%. That is (1.0818 × 1.06) − 1, so real growth plus inflation gives the nominal change.

## 5. Assumptions of the AD/AS model

- A single **price level** and a single **real output** represent millions of prices and products. The model hides which sectors or regions gain.
- Curves are drawn ceteris paribus. In practice AD and AS interact: higher investment raises AD now and AS later.
- SRAS is upward-sloping because input prices, especially wages, are sticky in the short run. In the neo-classical view they are flexible in the long run.
- Potential output is treated as known and stable. It is hard to measure.
- Expectations and policy are not modelled, and the model is static: it compares equilibria without showing the path or the timing between them.
- Choosing between a vertical and a Keynesian LRAS is itself an assumption about how flexible wages and prices are.

These limits matter when you evaluate. A conclusion drawn from the diagram is only as good as the assumptions behind the curves.

## 6. How equilibrium changes, and the effect on growth, unemployment and inflation

Unemployment is read from output: more output usually needs more labour, so unemployment tends to fall as output rises. The link is not exact because productivity, participation and the type of unemployment all matter.

| Change (starting at output 440, price 100) | New equilibrium | Growth | Inflation | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AD rises (example 2) | 106, 476 | +8.18% | +6.0% | Falls |
| SRAS shifts left, cost push: SRAS = 6P − 220 | 106, 416 | −5.45% | +6.0% | Rises |
| LRAS and SRAS shift right: SRAS = 6P − 100, LRAS 464 | 94, 464 | +5.45% | −6.0% | Falls (if workers are needed) |

Check the cost-push row: 840 − 4P = 6P − 220 gives P = 106, output 416, which is 24 (5.45%) below potential. This is **stagflation**: lower output and higher prices together. Check the last row: 840 − 4P = 6P − 100 gives P = 94 and output 464, equal to the new potential output (464/440 = +5.45%).

**Evaluation chain for any shift.** Say what shifts and why, show the new equilibrium, name the effect on growth, unemployment and inflation, then judge using:

- how much **spare capacity** there is (the flatter the AS, the less inflation);
- whether the shift is **temporary or permanent**;
- **time lags** before costs adjust;
- **size** of the shock and of the multiplier;
- **expectations**: if workers expect higher inflation, SRAS shifts left sooner;
- the **aim** of government. A policy that is good for growth may harm inflation or the balance of payments.

## 7. Using the model to judge demand-side and supply-side policies

Read the data first, then choose the policy.

| Invented economy | Output vs potential | Unemployment | Inflation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Country P | 4% below | 9.0% | 0.5% |
| Country Q | 3% above | 3.5% | 5.5% |
| Country R | 3% below | 8.0% | 6.0% |

- **Country P**: weak AD with spare capacity and little inflation risk. A rise in AD (lower interest rates, tax cuts, more public spending) moves output toward potential. This is a demand-side case.
- **Country Q**: AD is pushing the economy past capacity and prices are rising. Restraining AD (tighter policy) or raising capacity through supply-side measures reduces inflation.
- **Country R**: output low and inflation high suggests SRAS has shifted left. Raising AD would restore output at the price of still higher inflation (see practice question 10). Policies that cut costs or raise productivity attack the cause.
- **Sustained growth** needs LRAS to move right. Demand policy cannot do that alone, because past potential output extra AD produces only inflation. Supply-side policies (education, infrastructure, competition, tax incentives to invest) shift LRAS but act slowly and cost money.

Evaluation: demand-side policy is quick but depends on spare capacity and can be inflationary. Supply-side policy is durable but slow, uncertain and often costly. Many economies use both.

## Common errors

- Shifting AD or AS when only the price level has changed. A price change is a movement along.
- Drawing LRAS sloping upwards and calling it neo-classical. The neo-classical LRAS is vertical.
- Calling any cost fall long run. Only changes in capacity shift LRAS.
- Forgetting to label the new equilibrium on both axes, or to compare it with the old one.
- Writing "inflation falls" when only the rate of increase in prices has slowed.
- Using the model without naming an assumption when you evaluate.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the SRAS curve slope upwards?**
In the short run some costs, mainly nominal wages, are fixed by contracts. If the price level rises while costs stay put, profit per unit rises and firms supply more; higher output also brings overtime and rising unit costs, which steepens the curve near capacity.

**What determines the position of the LRAS curve?**
LRAS depends on the quantity and quality of factors of production and on technology, not on the price level. Changes in the labour force, skills, capital stock, infrastructure, technology and institutions shift it.

**What happens in the long run after a rise in AD in the neo-classical model?**
In the short run prices and output both rise and output exceeds potential. Tight input markets then push up wages and costs, shifting SRAS left until output returns to potential, leaving a higher price level and no lasting gain in output.

**Is aggregate supply AS-level or A-level content for OCR Economics?**
The OCR A Level Economics (H460) specification labels no content as AS-level or A-level only, so aggregate supply and the AD/AS model are treated as A Level content within Component 2, Macroeconomics.

**How do you choose between demand-side and supply-side policies using AD/AS?**
Read the data first. Low output, high unemployment and low inflation suggest weak AD and a demand-side case; low output with high inflation suggests SRAS has shifted left, so cost-cutting supply-side action attacks the cause; sustained growth needs LRAS to shift right.
