## Questions

**1.** Define supply-side policy. **[2]**

**2.** In Country P, nominal GDP is 2 000 in year 1 (price index 100) and 2 310 in year 2 (price index 110). Figures are in billions.

**(a)** Calculate real GDP in year 2. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the rate of economic growth between the two years. **[2]**

**3.** In Country E, real GDP is 2 000 billion in year 1 and 2 060 billion in year 2. Population is 50 million in year 1 and 52 million in year 2.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of economic growth. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate real GDP per head in each year. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in real GDP per head. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how government spending on training could raise a country's rate of economic growth. **[4]**

**5.** A worker in Country H is paid 15 an hour for a 40-hour week. Income tax is 20% of gross pay.

**(a)** Calculate weekly take-home pay. **[1]**
**(b)** The tax rate is cut to 15%. Calculate the new take-home pay and the percentage rise. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the tax cut might not lead the worker to work more hours. **[2]**

**6.** Real GDP in Country G, by quarter, is 400, 396, 392, 394 and 399.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in real GDP in quarters 2 and 3. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether Country G was in recession, giving a reason. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain whether output had returned to its level in quarter 1 by quarter 5. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how a recession affects an economy's position relative to its production possibility curve (PPC). **[4]**

**8.** Explain two possible causes of a recession. **[4]**

**9.** Explain one benefit and one drawback of privatising a state-owned water company. **[4]**

**10.** Discuss whether education and training is the best supply-side policy for a country with high unemployment and low productivity. **[8]**

**11.** In Country D, nominal GDP is 900 in year 1 (price index 100) and 945 in year 2 (price index 105). Population is 30 million in year 1 and 30.6 million in year 2.

**(a)** Calculate real GDP in year 2. **[2]**
**(b)** State the rate of real economic growth. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage change in real GDP per head. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain two reasons why GDP per head may not accurately show living standards. **[2]**

**12.** Explain how supply-side policy might help a government to reduce unemployment and control inflation. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Government action to raise the economy's productive capacity [1] and the efficiency of its markets, shifting the PPC outwards over time [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both ideas help: capacity or output potential, and a long-run effect. Saying only "increase supply" gives no sense of the whole economy.

**2. (a)** Real GDP = 2 310 ÷ 110 × 100 [1] = **2 100 billion** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Growth = (2 100 − 2 000) ÷ 2 000 × 100 [1] = **5.0%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The nominal rise is 15.5%, which is not the answer. A question about economic growth needs real GDP, so show the deflating step.

**3. (a)** (2 060 − 2 000) ÷ 2 000 × 100 [1] = **3.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Year 1: 2 000 billion ÷ 50 million = **40 000** [1]. Year 2: 2 060 billion ÷ 52 million = **39 615** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** (39 615 − 40 000) ÷ 40 000 × 100 [1] = **−1.0%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep billions and millions consistent. Write the unit change or the working so a slip can be traced.

**4.** Training raises the skills of workers [1]. Output per worker rises, so the same labour produces more [1]. Skilled workers are more mobile, so fewer are unemployed and more resources are used [1]. Potential output grows, shifting the PPC outwards, so growth in real GDP can rise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer runs from the measure to productivity to output to growth. A list of benefits without links earns little.

**5. (a)** 15 × 40 = 600 gross; 600 × 0.80 = **480** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 600 × 0.85 = **510** [1]. Rise = (510 − 480) ÷ 480 × 100 [1] = **6.25%** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** The worker can reach the same take-home pay in fewer hours, so may choose more leisure [1]. A worker already at full-time hours may have no extra work available [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the base of the percentage change (the old take-home pay). For (c), the point is that the worker's choice is uncertain, not that tax cuts are bad.

**6. (a)** Quarter 2: (396 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = **−1.0%** [1]. Quarter 3: (392 − 396) ÷ 396 × 100 = **−1.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Yes [1]. Real GDP fell for two consecutive quarters [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** No: quarter 5 is 399, below 400 in quarter 1 [1], so output was still 0.25% lower, even though growth had resumed [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use real GDP and consecutive falls. Saying "GDP fell" is weaker than naming two successive quarters.

**7.** A recession means real GDP falls [1]. Resources such as workers and machines become unemployed or under-used [1]. The economy moves from a point on the PPC to a point inside it [1]. The PPC has not moved, because the quantity and quality of resources have not changed in the short run [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention both the move inside the curve and that the curve stays put. Describing a recession as a shift of the PPC is a common slip.

**8.** Cause 1: a fall in consumer spending, for example from lower confidence [1], so firms sell less and cut output [1]. Cause 2: a fall in export demand when trading partners slow down [1], reducing output in export industries and incomes across the economy [1]. Other sound causes include higher interest rates, cuts in government spending, falling investment. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two developed causes beat four listed. Each needs the step from cause to lower output.

**9.** Benefit: the profit motive encourages the owners to cut waste and costs [1], which may lower prices or raise quality [1]. Drawback: a private water firm is likely to be a monopoly, since people cannot choose another supplier [1], so it can raise prices and charge more than a public body would [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A benefit and a drawback must both be explained to earn the full four. Pick a drawback that fits water, a natural monopoly, instead of a general one.

**10.** Support: training raises output per worker [1]; it improves job matching, cutting structural unemployment [1]; it helps both unemployment and low productivity [1]. Against: payoff is slow and costly [1]; trained workers may leave the country or move to rivals [1]; training needs vacancies, so weak demand limits the effect [1]. Alternatives: lower taxes or labour market reforms may work faster [1]. Judgement that depends on a condition: training is best if skills are the main cause of low productivity, but not if firms lack customers [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A conclusion that names a condition, such as the cause of the unemployment, shows evaluation. Repeating the benefits in the last line does not.

**11. (a)** 945 ÷ 105 × 100 [1] = **900 billion** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (900 − 900) ÷ 900 = **0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Year 1: 900 000 ÷ 30 = 30 000 [1]. Year 2: 900 000 ÷ 30.6 = 29 412 [1]. Change = **−2.0%** [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** It is an average that hides inequality [1]. It leaves out unpaid work or pollution and leisure [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A rise in nominal GDP of 5.0% here is entirely price rises. State that real output is unchanged before moving to per head.

**12.** Unemployment: training and labour market reforms match skills to jobs [1], reducing structural unemployment [1]. Lower direct taxes and better incentives mean more people accept jobs [1]. Inflation: higher productivity and more competition cut firms' costs per unit [1], so they can hold prices down [1], and higher output capacity reduces pressure on prices when demand rises [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both aims, with a chain for each. A supply-side effect on inflation works through costs and capacity, not through lower spending.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using nominal GDP when asked for growth.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100 when using the price index.
- Mixing billions and millions in GDP per head.
- Giving the percentage change against the wrong base year.
- Describing a recession as a shift of the PPC.
- Naming a supply-side measure with no chain to the aim.
- Treating lower taxes as having certain results.
- Writing a conclusion that repeats the argument.
