## Equations to know

| Equation | Use |
|---|---|
| E_k = 2Ze²/(4πε₀d) | Closest approach of an alpha particle (charge 2e) to a nucleus (charge Ze) |
| d = 2Ze²/(4πε₀E_k) | Rearranged; d ∝ Z/E_k |
| λ = hc/E | Wavelength of very high-energy electrons |
| sin θ ≈ 1.22λ/2R | First diffraction minimum (given when needed) |
| R = R₀A^(1/3), R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm | Radius from nucleon number |
| V = (4/3)πR₀³A | Nuclear volume, proportional to A |
| ρ = 3u/(4πR₀³) | Nuclear density, independent of A, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³ |

## Condensed recall

- At closest approach the alpha particle is momentarily at rest: all kinetic energy has become electric potential energy.
- Closest approach is an **upper limit** for the radius; the alpha particle never touches the nucleus.
- Assumptions: head-on approach, no recoil of the much heavier nucleus, only the electric force acts.
- Electrons are **leptons** and do not feel the strong interaction, so they give a cleaner measurement.
- Electron energies of hundreds of MeV are needed for wavelengths of a few fm.
- Intensity against angle: large central maximum, first minimum, then weaker maxima; minima do not fall to zero because the nucleus has no sharp edge.
- A smaller nucleus or a higher electron energy moves the first minimum to a larger angle.
- Typical nuclear radius: about 1 fm to 7 fm. Atomic radius: about 10⁻¹⁰ m.
- R = R₀A^(1/3) came from experimental data: lg R against lg A gives gradient 1/3 and intercept lg R₀.
- Volume ∝ A and mass ∝ A, so all nuclei have the same density. Nucleons are packed in contact, about 2 fm apart.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Distance of closest approach | Minimum separation of an alpha particle and a nucleus in a head-on approach |
| Electron diffraction | Spreading of an electron beam by nuclei, used to measure their size |
| Lepton | Particle such as the electron that does not feel the strong interaction |
| Nucleon number, A | Total number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus |
| R₀ | Constant in R = R₀A^(1/3), about 1.2 fm |
| Nuclear density | Mass per unit volume of nuclear matter, the same for all nuclei |

## The two methods side by side

| | Alpha closest approach | Electron diffraction |
|---|---|---|
| Result | Upper limit on R | Value of R from the first minimum |
| Interaction | Electric repulsion | Electric attraction only; no strong interaction |
| Beam energy | A few MeV | Hundreds of MeV |
| Weakness | Particle never reaches the nucleus | Needs very high energies; nuclear edge is not sharp |

## Proportional reasoning

| Change | Effect |
|---|---|
| Alpha energy doubled | Closest approach halved |
| Target Z doubled (same energy) | Closest approach doubled |
| A multiplied by 8 | R doubled |
| A multiplied by 27 | R tripled |
| R doubled | Volume × 8 |
| Electron energy increased | Wavelength shorter, first minimum at larger angle |

## Exam traps

- Alpha charge written as e instead of 2e.
- Energy left in MeV in the Coulomb equation.
- Closest approach called the actual radius.
- R = R₀A used instead of R₀A^(1/3).
- Density said to increase with A. It is constant.
- The minima on the intensity graph drawn reaching zero.
- Electrons chosen "because they are small": the reason is that they do not feel the strong interaction.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the typical range of nuclear radii.
2. Why is the distance of closest approach an upper limit for the nuclear radius?
3. Find the distance of closest approach of a 4.0 MeV alpha particle to a gold nucleus (Z = 79).
4. Find the radius of a nucleus with A = 27, using R₀ = 1.2 fm.
5. Find the ratio of the radii of nuclei with A = 216 and A = 8.
6. Give the main reason electrons are used to measure nuclear radii.
7. Find the wavelength of 500 MeV electrons.
8. What graph of the data gives a straight line of gradient 1/3?
9. Show that nuclear density does not depend on A.
10. How does increasing the electron energy affect the angle of the first diffraction minimum?
11. An alpha particle's energy is doubled. What happens to its closest approach to a given nucleus?
12. Find the volume of a nucleus with A = 64, using R₀ = 1.2 fm.

### Answers

1. About 1 fm to 7 fm (1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m to 7 × 10⁻¹⁵ m).
2. The alpha particle stops and turns back before reaching the nucleus, so the nucleus must be smaller than this distance.
3. d = (8.99 × 10⁹ × 2 × 79 × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)²)/(4.0 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³) = 5.7 × 10⁻¹⁴ m.
4. R = 1.2 × 27^(1/3) = 1.2 × 3 = 3.6 fm.
5. (216/8)^(1/3) = 27^(1/3) = 3.
6. Electrons are leptons and do not feel the strong interaction, so they are affected only by the charge of the nucleus.
7. λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(500 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³) = 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
8. lg R against lg A (the intercept is lg R₀).
9. Mass ≈ A × u and volume = (4/3)πR₀³A, so ρ = 3u/(4πR₀³): A cancels.
10. The wavelength becomes shorter, so the first minimum moves to a larger angle.
11. d ∝ 1/E_k, so the closest approach halves.
12. R = 1.2 × 4 = 4.8 fm; V = (4/3)π(4.8 × 10⁻¹⁵)³ = 4.6 × 10⁻⁴³ m³.
