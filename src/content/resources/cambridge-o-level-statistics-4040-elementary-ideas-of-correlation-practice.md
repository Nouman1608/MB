These questions cover section 9.1 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: elementary ideas of correlation, including the terms positive, negative, strong and weak correlation. Where a question asks you to plot, use graph paper. Recall the ideas from the [correlation study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-elementary-ideas-of-correlation/) or the [correlation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-elementary-ideas-of-correlation-revision-notes/). All data are invented for practice.

## Section A

**1.** State the type of correlation you would expect between each pair of variables.

**(a)** The outside temperature on winter days and the amount of gas used for heating a house. **[1]**

**(b)** The shoe size of an adult and the adult's score in a spelling test. **[1]**

**(c)** The length of a taxi journey and the fare paid. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the difference between strong positive correlation and weak positive correlation. **[2]**

**3.** A student says: "The points on my scatter diagram rise steeply, so the correlation must be strong." Explain why the student's reasoning is wrong. **[2]**

**4.** A survey of teenagers shows weak negative correlation between daily screen time and hours of sleep.

**(a)** Interpret this correlation in context. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain what the word "weak" tells you about the data. **[1]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by an outlier on a scatter diagram, and give two possible causes of one. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows the age and the selling price of eight cars of the same model.

| Age (years) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price ($ thousand) | 18.5 | 16.0 | 14.2 | 12.5 | 11.0 | 9.6 | 8.4 | 15.0 |

**(a)** State which variable should be plotted on the horizontal axis, giving a reason. **[2]**

**(b)** Plot the data and describe the correlation shown by the first seven cars. **[2]**

**(c)** Identify the outlier and suggest a reason for it. **[2]**

**(d)** Interpret the correlation in context. **[1]**

**7.** Two sets of data are shown.

Set P

| x | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 20 | 21 | 23 | 24 | 25 | 27 |

Set Q

| x | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 10 | 35 | 22 | 50 | 38 | 65 |

**(a)** Describe the correlation in each set. **[2]**

**(b)** The values of y in set Q increase by much more than those in set P. State which set shows the stronger correlation, and explain your answer. **[2]**

**8.** Over 30 days in a city, the number of umbrellas sold and the number of road accidents each day show strong positive correlation.

**(a)** State what this correlation means in context. **[1]**

**(b)** A newspaper suggests that banning umbrella sales would reduce road accidents. Comment on this suggestion. **[3]**

**9.** A driver records the fuel economy of a car at different steady speeds.

| Speed (km/h) | 30 | 50 | 70 | 90 | 110 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fuel economy (km per litre) | 14 | 18 | 19 | 17 | 13 |

**(a)** Describe the pattern these points would show on a scatter diagram. **[2]**

**(b)** A student says: "There is no correlation, so speed does not affect fuel economy." Comment on this statement. **[2]**

**10.** A researcher plots the heights and reading scores of a group of pupils aged 6 and a group aged 14 on one scatter diagram. The 14-year-olds are all taller and have higher scores. Within each age group the points show no clear pattern.

**(a)** Describe the correlation shown by the whole diagram. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why it would be misleading to conclude that taller pupils read better. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest how the researcher could analyse the data more fairly. **[1]**

**11.** A theme park records the hours of sunshine and the number of visitors on seven days.

| Sunshine (hours) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Visitors | 120 | 150 | 170 | 200 | 230 | 40 | 280 |

**(a)** Identify the outlier. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe the correlation shown by the other six days. **[2]**

**(c)** The park closed at midday on one of the days because of a power failure. Explain whether the outlier should be removed before the data are used to describe the relationship. **[2]**

**12.** For each statement, say whether it is true or false and give a reason.

**(a)** "Negative correlation means that y decreases every time x increases." **[2]**

**(b)** "Correlation can be strong even when no point lies exactly on a straight line." **[2]**

**(c)** "If two variables show no correlation, they are not related in any way." **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Negative** correlation: colder days need more heating [1].

**(b)** **No** correlation [1].

**(c)** **Positive** correlation, as longer journeys cost more [1]. **[3]**

**2.** In both, y tends to increase as x increases [1]. With strong correlation the points lie close to a straight line; with weak correlation they are widely scattered about it [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Strength depends on how closely the points lie to a straight line, not on its gradient [1]. A steep pattern can be widely scattered, and changing the axis scale changes the steepness without changing the correlation [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Teenagers with more screen time tend to sleep for fewer hours [1].

**(b)** The points are widely scattered, so many individuals do not follow the pattern [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Tend to" earns the interpretation mark; "more screen time means less sleep" overstates a weak correlation.

**5.** An outlier is a point that lies well away from the pattern of the other points [1]. Possible causes: a recording or measuring error [1]; a genuine but unusual individual [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** **Age** [1], because age may influence the price, not the other way round [1].

**(b)** Points plotted accurately with labelled axes [1]. The first seven show **strong negative correlation** [1].

**(c)** The **8-year-old car priced at $15 000** [1]. It may be in exceptional condition or have very low mileage, or the price may have been recorded wrongly [1].

**(d)** Older cars of this model tend to sell for lower prices [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* For (c), name the point by its values; "the last one" is acceptable only if it is clear which point is meant.

**7. (a)** Set P: **strong positive** correlation [1]. Set Q: **weak positive** correlation [1].

**(b)** **Set P** [1]. Its points lie close to a straight line, while set Q's points are widely scattered; the larger increase in Q is a steeper gradient, not a stronger correlation [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Days on which more umbrellas were sold tended to have more road accidents [1].

**(b)** The suggestion is not justified, because correlation does not show that umbrella sales cause accidents [1]. Rain is likely to increase both umbrella sales and accidents, through wet roads and poor visibility [1]. Banning umbrellas would not change the weather, so accidents would not fall [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Fuel economy rises from 14 to a peak of 19 at 70 km/h and then falls to 13 [1], so the points follow a curve with little or no linear correlation [1].

**(b)** The statement is wrong: speed clearly affects fuel economy [1], but the relationship is curved, not linear, so a lack of linear correlation does not mean there is no relationship [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** **Positive** correlation overall [1].

**(b)** The pattern comes from the two age groups: older pupils are both taller and better readers [1]. Within each age group height shows no relationship with reading score, so age is the third variable [1].

**(c)** Analyse each age group separately, for example with a separate scatter diagram for each [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** **(7, 40)** [1].

**(b)** **Strong positive** correlation [1]: days with more sunshine tended to have more visitors [1].

**(c)** It should be removed [1], because a half-day closure explains the low number of visitors, so that day is not comparable with the others [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The reason for removal must come from the context. Removing a point only because it does not fit is not acceptable.

**12. (a)** **False** [1]. Negative correlation is a general tendency; some pairs may have a higher x and a higher y than others [1].

**(b)** **True** [1]. Strong correlation means the points lie close to a line, not exactly on it [1].

**(c)** **False** [1]. They may have a curved relationship, as with speed and fuel economy [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving direction without strength when asked to describe correlation.
- Interpreting without context, or without "tend to".
- Treating a steep pattern as strong correlation.
- Accepting a cause claim, or rejecting it with no reason drawn from the context.
- Removing an outlier without a reason from the context.
- Calling a curved relationship "no relationship".
