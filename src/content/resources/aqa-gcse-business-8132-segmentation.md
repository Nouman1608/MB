This guide covers section 3.5.2 Segmentation of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017, in Topic 5, Marketing. The [Marketing study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-marketing/) gives one line on each type of segmentation; this page explains how a business uses each one, why it bothers, the drawbacks, and how to read segment data in an exam. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

| You must be able to | Covered below |
|---|---|
| Explain how businesses segment their market | How segmentation works |
| Explain why businesses segment their market | Why segment |
| Use the four named criteria: gender, age, location and income | The four criteria |

The specification names exactly four criteria. Other textbooks mention more, but in an AQA answer the four named ones are the safe ground.

## What segmentation is

A market is all the potential customers for a product. **Segmentation** means dividing that market into smaller groups, called **segments**, whose members share a characteristic and so tend to have similar needs. The business then chooses one or more segments as its **target market** and designs a marketing mix to suit them.

Segmentation follows directly from 3.5.1: customers do not all have the same needs, so treating them as one group means the product, price and promotion will suit nobody very well.

## How segmentation works: three steps

1. **Divide** the market using a criterion, such as age bands.
2. **Choose** the segment or segments to target, based on size, spending power, growth and competition.
3. **Design** a marketing mix for each chosen segment: product features, price, promotion methods and places to sell.

## The four criteria

### Gender

Some products are bought mainly by, or designed differently for, men or women: clothing, toiletries, some magazines. A business might design separate ranges, use different packaging and advertise in different places. Care is needed: many products appeal across genders, and assuming otherwise can miss customers or cause offence.

### Age

Needs change through life. Age bands such as under 16, 16 to 24, 25 to 44, 45 to 64 and 65 and over are common. A holiday company might sell lively group holidays to 18 to 30 year olds, family packages to parents, and quieter coach tours to retired customers. Age also shapes where people see promotion: younger customers are more likely to be reached through social media, while printed press may reach older readers.

### Location

Where customers live affects what they need and how they can be reached. Location can mean a country, a region, a town or urban against rural areas. A garden centre in a city may stock more balcony and patio products; one in a village may sell more lawnmowers and large plants. Location also affects place: a business may choose which towns to open in, or deliver only within a certain distance.

### Income

Customers with different incomes can afford different prices and look for different things. Higher-income customers may pay more for premium quality, design or a strong brand. Lower-income customers may look for value for money. Many businesses target several income segments with different ranges, such as a basic, a standard and a premium version of the same product.

### Combining criteria

Businesses often combine criteria to describe a target market precisely. "Women aged 25 to 44 with high incomes living in cities" is much more useful for planning promotion than any one criterion alone.

## Why businesses segment

| Benefit | Chain to write |
|---|---|
| Better-targeted marketing mix | Product, price and promotion fit the group's needs, so more of them buy |
| Less wasted spending | Promotion reaches likely buyers instead of everyone |
| Spotting market opportunities | A segment whose needs no rival meets is a gap the business can fill |
| Higher sales and loyalty | Customers who feel a product is made for them buy more and return |
| Pricing to suit the segment | A premium segment can be charged more; a value segment can be won with lower prices |

### Drawbacks

- **Cost.** Each segment may need its own product version and promotion, which raises costs.
- **A narrow target limits sales.** Aiming at one small segment leaves other customers to rivals.
- **Inaccurate assumptions.** Not every member of a segment behaves the same way, so stereotypes can lead to poor decisions.
- **Change over time.** Segments grow, shrink and change their needs, so research must be repeated.

## Worked example: reading segment data

Ellery Audio sells wireless headphones. Its research gives this information about last year's customers.

| Age group | Customers | Average spend per customer |
|---|---|---|
| 16 to 24 | 2,400 | £45 |
| 25 to 44 | 1,800 | £85 |
| 45 to 64 | 900 | £110 |
| 65 and over | 300 | £70 |

**Step 1: total customers.** 2,400 + 1,800 + 900 + 300 = 5,400.

**Step 2: share of customers.** 16 to 24: 2,400 ÷ 5,400 × 100 = about 44.4%. 45 to 64: 900 ÷ 5,400 × 100 = about 16.7%.

**Step 3: revenue from each segment.**

- 16 to 24: 2,400 × £45 = £108,000
- 25 to 44: 1,800 × £85 = £153,000
- 45 to 64: 900 × £110 = £99,000
- 65 and over: 300 × £70 = £21,000
- Total: £108,000 + £153,000 + £99,000 + £21,000 = £381,000

**Step 4: interpret.** The 16 to 24 segment has the most customers, but the 25 to 44 segment earns the most revenue: £153,000 ÷ £381,000 × 100 = about 40.2% of the total. The 45 to 64 segment is small but spends the most per person, so it may suit a premium range with noise-cancelling features. A judgement depends on the business's objective: to grow customer numbers, target 16 to 24 year olds with a low-priced model; to grow revenue, target 25 to 44 and 45 to 64 year olds with higher-priced products.

This is the kind of reasoning examiners reward: use the data, then link it to a marketing decision.

## Worked example: designing a mix for a segment

Bracklow Bikes decides to target commuters aged 25 to 44 living in a large city, with above-average incomes.

| Element | Decision | Reason from the segment |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Folding electric bike | City commuters need to carry it on trains and store it in flats |
| Price | Premium | Above-average incomes; quality matters more than lowest price |
| Promotion | Online adverts targeted by city and age | Reaches the segment without paying to reach everyone |
| Place | City-centre showroom plus online sales | Close to where the segment lives and works |

Every decision is justified by a feature of the segment. That is what "how businesses use segmentation" means in practice.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain one benefit to Sennow Swimwear of segmenting its market by age.*

"Segmenting by age lets Sennow Swimwear design ranges for different groups, such as bright, fashionable styles for teenagers and more supportive styles for older customers. *(benefit applied)* Each group is more likely to find a design it wants, so Sennen's sales should rise." *(consequence)*

## Common mistakes

- Confusing segmentation (dividing the market) with the target market (the segment chosen).
- Listing the four criteria with no explanation of how a business would use one.
- Using criteria the specification does not name instead of the four it does.
- Assuming all members of a segment behave identically.
- Reading only the largest number in a table: the biggest segment by customers may not earn the most revenue.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State or identify:** name a criterion, or the largest segment from data.
- **Explain:** how one criterion helps a business choose its marketing mix, with a consequence.
- **Analyse:** use segment data to show which group matters most and why.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the benefits of targeting a segment against its cost or narrowness, then decide.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the four ways of segmenting a market in AQA GCSE Business (8132)?**
Gender, age, location and income.

**What is the difference between segmentation and a target market?**
Segmentation divides the market into groups. The target market is the group, or groups, the business chooses to aim its product at.

**What are the drawbacks of segmentation?**
Designing different products and promotion for each segment costs more, a narrow target can limit sales, and assumptions about a segment may be wrong.
