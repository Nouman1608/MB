These notes condense **3.1.4.1 Enthalpy change** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. For explanations and worked examples, read the [enthalpy change study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-1/); for exam-style questions, use the [enthalpy change practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-1-practice/).

## Core ideas

- Reactions are **exothermic** (ΔH negative) or **endothermic** (ΔH positive).
- **ΔH** is the heat energy change measured at **constant pressure**.
- **Standard** enthalpy changes use **100 kPa** and a **stated temperature** (usually 298 K): ΔH°₂₉₈.
- Every substance must be in its **standard state**.
- You must define **ΔcH°** and **ΔfH°** and write the matching equations.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Enthalpy change, ΔH | Heat energy change measured under conditions of constant pressure |
| Exothermic | Heat transferred to the surroundings; ΔH negative; surroundings warm up |
| Endothermic | Heat taken in from the surroundings; ΔH positive; surroundings cool down |
| Standard conditions | 100 kPa and a stated temperature (usually 298 K) |
| Standard state | The physical state of a substance under standard conditions |
| ΔcH° | Enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen under standard conditions, all substances in standard states |
| ΔfH° | Enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements under standard conditions, all substances in standard states |

## Sign and scaling rules

| Change to the equation | Effect on ΔH |
|---|---|
| Multiply all amounts by 2 | ΔH × 2 |
| Halve all amounts | ΔH ÷ 2 |
| Reverse the equation | Change the sign |

ΔH is per mole **of the equation as written**, in kJ mol⁻¹.

## Standard states at 298 K

- Gases: H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, F₂, CO₂, CH₄.
- Liquids: Br₂, H₂O, C₂H₅OH, Hg.
- Solids: C (graphite), I₂, S, metals except mercury, ionic compounds such as NaCl and CaCO₃.

## Writing the equations

**Combustion (one mole of fuel; O₂ may be fractional)**

```
C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)
C₄H₁₀(g) + 6½O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l)
```

**Formation (one mole of product, from elements in standard states)**

```
½H₂(g) + ½Br₂(l) → HBr(g)
2C(s) + 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → CH₃COOH(l)
Mg(s) + ½O₂(g) → MgO(s)
```

## Useful facts

- ΔfH° of any **element in its standard state = 0**.
- ΔcH°(C) = ΔfH°(CO₂) = −394 kJ mol⁻¹.
- ΔcH°(H₂) = ΔfH°(H₂O(l)) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹.
- Combustion is always exothermic; formation can be either sign.

## Quick calculations with ΔH

- **Heat from an amount**: heat transferred = n × size of ΔH. Burning 0.200 mol of methane (ΔcH° = −890 kJ mol⁻¹) releases 0.200 × 890 = **178 kJ**.
- **Amount from heat**: n = heat ÷ size of ΔH. To release 445 kJ from methane needs 445 ÷ 890 = **0.500 mol** (8.00 g).
- **From a scaled equation**: if 2CO(g) + O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) has ΔH = −566 kJ mol⁻¹, then ΔcH°(CO) = −566 ÷ 2 = **−283 kJ mol⁻¹**.
- Heat released or absorbed is a positive quantity; the sign belongs to ΔH.

## What "constant pressure" means in practice

Reactions in open containers, such as a beaker or a calorimeter open to the air, take place at the constant pressure of the atmosphere. Any gas produced can escape, and the heat change measured under these conditions is the enthalpy change, ΔH.

## Enthalpy level diagrams

- Vertical axis: enthalpy.
- Exothermic: products below reactants; arrow down, labelled with the negative ΔH.
- Endothermic: products above reactants; arrow up, labelled with the positive ΔH.

## Exam traps

- "One mole" left out of a definition, or the wrong substance made one mole.
- 101 kPa or 1 atm instead of **100 kPa**.
- Water as H₂O(g) in a standard combustion equation.
- Carbon written as C(g) or diamond rather than graphite, C(s).
- Formation equations doubled to remove fractions.
- An enthalpy of formation equation that starts from a compound (for example CO) rather than elements.
- Heat released given as a negative number.

## Self-test

1. State the sign of ΔH for an endothermic reaction.
2. What does the specification say ΔH measures?
3. Give the standard pressure used in this specification.
4. What is the standard state of bromine at 298 K?
5. Define standard enthalpy of combustion.
6. Define standard enthalpy of formation.
7. Write the equation for ΔfH° of methanol, CH₃OH(l).
8. Write the equation for ΔcH° of propane, C₃H₈(g).
9. What is ΔfH° for N₂(g)? Explain.
10. ΔH for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) is −92 kJ mol⁻¹. What is ΔfH°(NH₃)?
11. ΔcH° for ethanol is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. How much heat is released when 2.30 g of ethanol burns completely?
12. Why does 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) not represent ΔfH° of water?

### Answers

1. Positive.
2. The heat energy change under conditions of constant pressure.
3. 100 kPa.
4. Liquid, Br₂(l).
5. The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen under standard conditions, with all reactants and products in their standard states.
6. The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements under standard conditions, with all reactants and products in their standard states.
7. C(s) + 2H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → CH₃OH(l).
8. C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l).
9. **Zero**: it is an element in its standard state, so no change occurs.
10. The equation forms two moles, so ΔfH° = −92 ÷ 2 = **−46 kJ mol⁻¹**.
11. n = 2.30 ÷ 46.0 = 0.0500 mol; heat = 0.0500 × 1367 = **68.4 kJ** released.
12. It forms **two** moles of water; the definition needs one mole.
