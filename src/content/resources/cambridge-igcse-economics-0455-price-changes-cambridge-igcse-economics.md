## 2.6.1 Causes: changing market conditions

The equilibrium price changes only when a **condition of demand** or a **condition of supply** changes. A change in the price itself is a result, not a cause.

### Turning an event into a shift

Use four questions for any event in a source.

1. **Which market?** Name the product whose price you are asked about.
2. **Which side?** Does the event change buyers' willingness or ability to buy (demand), or producers' costs or ability to sell (supply)?
3. **Which direction?** More at every price (right) or less at every price (left)?
4. **What follows?** At the old price there is now a shortage or surplus, so the price moves to a new equilibrium.

| Event (invented) | Market | Curve | Shift |
|---|---|---|---|
| A storm destroys a third of the mango crop | Mangoes | Supply | Left |
| A report says mangoes improve health | Mangoes | Demand | Right |
| Wages of fruit pickers rise | Mangoes | Supply | Left |
| A subsidy for mango growers | Mangoes | Supply | Right |
| The price of papaya, a substitute, falls | Mangoes | Demand | Left |
| Incomes rise in the main city | Mangoes | Demand | Right |
| Better refrigerated lorries cut transport costs | Mangoes | Supply | Right |

### The circular-reasoning trap

A common mistake runs: "Supply falls, so the price rises, so demand falls, so the price falls again." This confuses a contraction in quantity demanded (a movement along the unchanged demand curve) with a decrease in demand (a shift). When the price rises after a supply shift, buyers move up the demand curve. The demand curve itself does not move, and the price does not fall back.

## 2.6.2 Consequences for equilibrium price and sales

### The starting market

Weekly market for mangoes in the city of Pira (invented); price per kg, quantities in kg.

| Price | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 800 | 200 |
| 3 | 700 | 400 |
| 4 | 600 | 600 |
| 5 | 500 | 800 |
| 6 | 400 | 1,000 |

Equilibrium: price **4**, sales **600 kg**, total spending 4 × 600 = **2,400**.

### Worked example 1: a fall in supply

A storm cuts supply by 300 kg at every price.

1. New quantity supplied: at 4, 600 − 300 = 300; at 5, 800 − 300 = 500; at 6, 1,000 − 300 = 700.
2. At the old price of 4: demand 600, supply 300, a shortage of 300.
3. The price rises. At 5, quantity demanded is 500 and the new quantity supplied is 500.
4. New equilibrium: price **5**, sales **500 kg**.
5. Price rises by 1 ÷ 4 × 100 = **25%**; sales fall by 100 ÷ 600 × 100 = **16.7%**.
6. Total spending changes from 2,400 to 5 × 500 = **2,500**.

Diagram: the supply curve shifts left from S1 to S2. The new crossing point with the unchanged D is higher and further left. Label P1, P2, Q1 and Q2.

### Worked example 2: a rise in demand

Instead, a health report raises demand by 150 kg at every price.

1. New quantity demanded: at 4, 750; at 5, 650.
2. At the old price of 4: demand 750, supply 600, a shortage of 150.
3. Between 4 and 5 the gap closes: at 4 it is +150; at 5 it is 650 − 800 = −150. Halfway, at **4.50**, quantity demanded is 700 and quantity supplied is 700.
4. New equilibrium: price **4.50**, sales **700 kg**, total spending 4.50 × 700 = **3,150**.

Diagram: D shifts right to D2; price and sales both rise.

### Worked example 3: both shifts together

Suppose the storm and the health report happen in the same week: demand rises by 150 and supply falls by 300 at every price.

1. New demand at 5 is 650 and at 6 is 550. New supply at 5 is 500 and at 6 is 700.
2. The gap is +150 at 5 and −150 at 6, so equilibrium is halfway: price **5.50**.
3. At 5.50, quantity demanded = 650 − 50 = 600 and quantity supplied = 500 + 100 = 600. Sales: **600 kg**.

Both shifts push the price up, so the price is certain to rise (here to 5.50). Their effects on sales pull in opposite directions; in this case they cancel exactly, and sales stay at 600. With a bigger storm, sales would fall; with a bigger rise in demand, they would rise. When two curves shift, one result is certain and the other depends on the sizes of the shifts.

### Why the size of the change differs between markets

For the same shift, the change in price is larger when the other curve is steep. If supply cannot respond quickly to a rise in demand (a steep supply curve), most of the adjustment comes through price. If buyers can easily switch away when supply falls (a flat demand curve), most of the adjustment comes through quantity. This idea is developed as price elasticity in sections 2.7 and 2.8.

## Knock-on effects in related markets

A price change in one market changes the conditions in others.

**Worked example: a chain of markets.** Following the storm, mangoes rise from 4 to 5 per kg.

1. **Substitute market (papaya):** mangoes are now relatively dearer, so demand for papaya increases. The papaya demand curve shifts right, raising papaya prices and sales.
2. **A market that uses mangoes as an input (mango chutney):** chutney makers face a higher cost for their fruit, so the supply of chutney decreases; its price rises and its sales fall.
3. **Factor market (fruit pickers):** with less fruit to pick, demand for pickers falls, putting downward pressure on their wages.

Each step uses the same four questions: which market, which curve, which direction, what follows.

## Common mistakes

- Circular reasoning: treating the contraction in quantity demanded after a price rise as a second shift.
- Shifting the wrong curve, for example shifting demand for a rise in production costs.
- Drawing the diagram without labelling the original and new equilibrium.
- Stating only the direction of price when the question asks about price and sales.
- When both curves shift, stating a definite change for the variable that depends on the sizes of the shifts.

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Explain** (often "using a demand and supply diagram"): name the condition, the curve, the direction of shift, the shortage or surplus at the old price, and the new equilibrium price and sales.
- **Calculate:** new quantities from a percentage or absolute shift, the new equilibrium, percentage changes and total spending.
- **Analyse:** follow a change from one market into a substitute, complement or factor market.
- **Discuss:** weigh which of two simultaneous changes is likely to dominate, and conclude with the condition the result depends on.
