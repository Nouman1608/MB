These questions follow section 3.8.2 (Solar system and orbital motion) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The equation for centripetal force is not required; use speed = distance ÷ time, with the distance for one circular orbit equal to 2πr. Planetary data are approximate supplied values.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Put these in order of size, starting with the largest: asteroid, Earth, Earth's Moon, Jupiter, Sun. **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by a moon, and give one way in which the motion of a moon differs from the motion of a planet. **[2]**

**3.** Describe what the universe is made of, and state where the Sun is found within it. **[3]**

**4.** Give one difference in size or make-up, and one difference in motion, between a comet and an asteroid. **[2]**

**5.** A satellite moves in a circular orbit at constant speed. Which statement is correct?

A: The resultant force on the satellite is zero.

B: The velocity of the satellite is constant.

C: The satellite is accelerating towards the centre of its orbit.

D: Gravity acts on the satellite in its direction of motion. **[1]**

**6.** Name the force that provides the centripetal force in each case, and state the body it pulls towards.

**(a)** The Earth moving round the Sun. **[1]**

**(b)** A weather satellite moving round the Earth. **[1]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** The table gives approximate data for four planets.

| Planet | Orbital radius (million km) | Orbital speed (km/s) |
|---|---|---|
| Venus | 108 | 35.0 |
| Earth | 150 | 29.8 |
| Saturn | 1430 | 9.7 |
| Uranus | 2870 | 6.8 |

**(a)** Describe the relationship between orbital radius and orbital speed. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain this relationship using the idea of gravitational force. **[2]**

**(c)** Jupiter orbits at 779 million km. Suggest a range within which its orbital speed lies. **[1]**

**8.** Io is a moon of Jupiter. It orbits at a radius of 4.22 × 10⁸ m and takes 1.77 days for one orbit.

**(a)** Convert the period into seconds. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate Io's orbital speed. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**

**(c)** Europa, another moon of Jupiter, orbits at 6.71 × 10⁸ m. State and explain whether Europa's orbital speed is greater or smaller than Io's. **[2]**

**9.** A satellite orbits 800 km above the Earth's surface. The radius of the Earth is 6370 km. One orbit takes 6040 s.

**(a)** Calculate the radius of the orbit in metres. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the orbital speed of the satellite. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate how many orbits it completes in one day. **[1]**

**10.** A student says: "The Moon goes round the Earth at a steady speed, so the forces on it must be balanced." Explain why the student is wrong. **[3]**

**11.** A satellite is launched into a low orbit and later moved to a geostationary orbit, much further from the Earth. Explain what must happen to its orbital speed, and why the final speed is one particular value. **[4]**

**12.** A space agency needs two satellites: one to send television signals to fixed dishes in one country, and one to photograph the entire surface of the Earth for maps. For each satellite, choose geostationary or low polar orbit and give a reason. **[4]**

**13.** A ball on a string is whirled in a horizontal circle. The string suddenly breaks.

**(a)** Describe the motion of the ball just after the string breaks, ignoring gravity and air resistance. **[1]**

**(b)** Use this example to explain the role of gravity in keeping a planet in orbit. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Sun, Jupiter, Earth, Earth's Moon, asteroid. Sun and Jupiter first and second [1]; Earth, Moon, asteroid in the correct order [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The Sun is about 109 times Earth's diameter and Jupiter about 11 times, so these two are always first.

**2.** A moon is a natural satellite of a planet [1]. A moon orbits a planet, whereas a planet orbits the Sun [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "A moon is smaller" is a size point, not a motion point, so it does not answer the second part.

**3.** Thousands of millions of galaxies [1], each containing thousands of millions of stars [1]. The Sun is one of the stars in our galaxy, the Milky Way [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the Milky Way. "In a galaxy" alone loses the final mark.

**4.** Make-up or size: a comet is made of ice and dust, whereas an asteroid is rocky (or: comets and asteroids are both small, with a comet's nucleus usually only a few kilometres to tens of kilometres across) [1]. Motion: a comet follows a long, stretched orbit coming close to the Sun only briefly, whereas most asteroids orbit in a belt between Mars and Jupiter [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Make each point a comparison of both objects.

**5.** **C** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The direction changes, so the velocity changes, so there is an acceleration. A and B contradict this; D describes a force that would change the speed.

**6. (a)** Gravity, pulling the Earth towards the Sun [1].

**(b)** Gravity, pulling the satellite towards the Earth (the centre of its orbit) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Centripetal force" is not the name of a separate force. Name gravity and its direction.

**7. (a)** As orbital radius increases, orbital speed decreases [1].

**(b)** The gravitational force decreases as the separation increases [1], so a smaller centripetal force can only keep the planet in orbit at a lower speed [1].

**(c)** Between 9.7 and 29.8 km/s (any range inside these values, for example 10 to 25 km/s) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c) use the neighbouring data: 779 million km lies between Earth and Saturn.

**8. (a)** 1.77 × 86 400 = **1.53 × 10⁵ s** (152 928 s) [1].

**(b)** Distance = 2π × 4.22 × 10⁸ = 2.65 × 10⁹ m [1]; speed = 2.65 × 10⁹ ÷ 152 928 = **1.73 × 10⁴ m/s** [1].

**(c)** Smaller [1], because Europa is further from Jupiter, so the gravitational force on it is weaker and its orbital speed is lower [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the unrounded time in your calculator for (b). The answer is about 17.3 km/s.

**9. (a)** 6370 + 800 = 7170 km = **7.17 × 10⁶ m** [1].

**(b)** 2π × 7.17 × 10⁶ = 4.51 × 10⁷ m [1]; 4.51 × 10⁷ ÷ 6040 = **7.46 × 10³ m/s** [1].

**(c)** 86 400 ÷ 6040 = 14.3, so **14** complete orbits [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Using 800 km as the radius is the commonest error. The orbit is centred on the Earth's centre.

**10.** The Moon moves in a circle, so its direction of motion is always changing [1]. Its velocity is therefore changing, so it is accelerating [1]. An acceleration needs an unbalanced (resultant) force; this is gravity, acting towards the Earth [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Steady speed" is correct; "balanced forces" is not. Separate speed from velocity.

**11.** The satellite's orbital speed must decrease [1], because gravity is weaker at the larger radius, so a lower speed is needed for a stable orbit there [1]. At each radius there is only one speed that gives a stable orbit [1], so at the geostationary radius the satellite must be given exactly that speed, or its orbital radius would change [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* This is the specification's "speed will determine the radius" idea. State it directly.

**12.** Television: geostationary [1], because the satellite stays above one point on the equator, so fixed dishes can always point at it [1]. Mapping: low polar [1], because it passes over the poles and the Earth turns beneath it, so over many orbits it passes over the whole surface [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason must link a property of the orbit to the job, not just restate the name of the orbit.

**13. (a)** It moves off in a straight line (at a tangent to the circle) at constant speed [1].

**(b)** The string provided the inward force that kept the ball moving in a circle [1]. For a planet, gravity towards the Sun provides this centripetal force; without it the planet would move off in a straight line [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "It flies outwards" suggests an outward force. The ball continues in the direction it was already moving.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating constant speed as zero acceleration.
- Using height above the surface instead of the radius from the centre.
- Leaving a period in days or hours before dividing.
- Explaining slower outer orbits with "longer distance" rather than weaker gravity.
- Giving only one side of a comparison.
- Naming "centripetal force" as if it were a separate force rather than saying gravity provides it.

**Are these OxfordAQA past paper questions?**
No. They are original questions for revising section 3.8.2 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203). Use them alongside the board's official past papers.

**Which radius do I use in orbit calculations?**
Use the distance from the centre of the body being orbited. If you are given a height above the surface, add the radius of that body.
