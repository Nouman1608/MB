## Data for questions 6 to 11

**Data set A (original, invented).** Titles of eight picture books for children aged 3 to 6.

1. Max and the Muddy Boots
2. The Dragon Who Hated Homework
3. Zara Zooms to the Moon
4. Little Owl Learns to Share
5. Grandpa's Great Big Garden
6. Captain Crumb and the Cake Caper
7. Why Is the Sky Sleepy?
8. Detective Dot and the Missing Mittens

**Data set B (original, invented).** A parent reads a picture book with a child aged four. A full stop in brackets (.) is a short pause.

```
PARENT: look (.) it's Max (.) and what's he wearing
CHILD:  boots
PARENT: boots (.) yes (.) muddy boots (.) and where do you think he's going
CHILD:  puddle
PARENT: puddle (.) good guess (.) shall we see
```

## Questions

### Section A: knowledge of the unit

**1.** How long is the written route of Unit 4, and how many marks is it worth? **[2]**

**2.** State how many questions a student answers in the written unit, and what the student must produce. **[2]**

**3.** What is the maximum number of texts or data sets in any task? **[1]**

**4.** Name the four parts of the report structure suggested in the specification. **[2]**

**5.** Which assessment objective rewards the organisation, register and accuracy of the report? **[1]**

### Section B: working with the data

**6.** Write one research question that could be answered using data set A and data set B. Explain why the data suit the question. **[3]**

**7.** Write a Method paragraph for an investigation into data set A only. Explain which data have been selected and how they will be analysed. **[4]**

**8.** Count how many of the eight titles contain at least two words that begin with the same sound. Calculate the percentage, and the mean number of words in a title. **[3]**

**9.** Write one analysis paragraph (about 120 words) on the language of the titles in data set A. Use at least two language levels and link the points to an aim of finding out how the titles are designed to appeal to young children and their parents. **[8]**

**10.** In data set B, the parent repeats the child's words. Explain how this repetition could be analysed and what it might show about the interaction. **[4]**

**11.** Write a conclusion of about 60 words to an investigation that used both data sets. State one limitation. **[4]**

### Section C: evaluating a report extract

**12.** A student wrote this opening to a report.

> *Aim:* I will look at the data and talk about the language. *Method:* I will read the titles and the conversation and write about them. They are about books so it is about reading.

**(a)** Identify two weaknesses in the extract. **[2]**

**(b)** Rewrite the aim so that it is suitable. **[2]**

## Answers

Marking guidance is Marlbridge's own. Award marks for the points listed or for equally valid alternatives.

**1.** 2 hours 30 minutes [1] and 50 marks [1]. **[2]**

**2.** One question [1], from a choice of two. The student writes a report on an investigation of the data supplied [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Three [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Aim(s), Method, Analysis and Conclusion [2 for all four; 1 if two or three are correct]. **[2]**

**5.** AO3 [1]. **[1]**

**6.** A suitable question, for example: "How do picture book titles and parent-child reading talk use language to engage young children?" [1]. Both data sets concern language aimed at young children [1], and both can be analysed for features that capture attention, so they can be compared [1]. **[3]**
*Insight:* A question that needs only one data set, or data that are not supplied, would not meet the task.

**7.** The titles were chosen because they are a coherent set of eight with the same audience and purpose, and a list can be analysed in detail in the time available [1]. Data set B was left out because it is spoken interaction and would need a different method [1]. Each title will be analysed for lexis and semantics (character names, adjectives, sound patterns) [1] and for grammar (title length, questions, sentence type) and graphology (capitalisation, punctuation), and the patterns will be counted and compared [1]. **[4]**

**8.** All eight titles have at least two words that begin with the same sound [1; for example Muddy and Max, Zara and Zooms, Sky and Sleepy]. 8 / 8 x 100 = 100 % [1]. The titles total 41 words, so the mean is 41 / 8 = 5.1 words (5.125) [1]. **[3]**
*Insight:* A check with a clear rule for "same sound" (here, the same initial consonant) is part of a sound method.

**9.** Award up to 8 using the guidance below. Marks: a clear claim that links to the aim [1]; accurate examples quoted from the data [2]; terms from at least two levels, such as alliteration and phonology, semantics of character names, graphology of question marks [2]; explanation of the effect on children, such as memorability, rhythm and sound play, and on parents, such as humour or reassurance [2]; a comparison or a link back to the aim, for example that the same strategies appear across all eight titles [1]. A model answer: "All eight titles use alliteration, for example *Max and the Muddy Boots* and *Captain Crumb and the Cake Caper*. The repeated initial sounds create rhythm and make the titles easy to say and remember, which suits young listeners who learn by repetition. The titles also name a character (*Zara*, *Little Owl*, *Detective Dot*), so the child can identify with a figure. The lexis is concrete and familiar, such as *boots*, *garden* and *cake*, which parents can relate to everyday experience. These choices show that the titles are designed to be read aloud, which supports the aim of appealing to both children and the adults who choose and read the books." **[8]**

**10.** Method: count repeated child utterances (2 of the child's 2 turns are repeated by the parent) [1]. Analyse as a discourse feature of parent-child talk: the repetition confirms what the child said [1], gives positive feedback ("yes", "good guess") [1], and keeps the child's words in the conversation, which may model correct vocabulary and invite more talk [1]. **[4]**

**11.** A conclusion links to the aim and states findings [1]; for example: both the titles and the reading talk use repetition and sound play to engage children [1]; the sample is very small, with eight titles and one short conversation [1], so the findings cannot be generalised to all picture books or all parents [1]. **[4]**

**12. (a)** The aim is vague and has no research question [1]; the method does not say which data are chosen and why or which frameworks will be used [1]. (Also accept: unsuitable register or "it is about reading" is a content statement, not an analysis plan.) **[2]**

**(b)** A suitable rewrite states the focus and the data [1] and links to a language feature, for example: "The aim is to investigate how the titles in data set A and the reading talk in data set B use language, such as repetition and sound patterns, to engage young children" [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- A research question that cannot be answered by the data given.
- Using only one of the data sets.
- Describing the content instead of analysing the language.
- Features named without an effect or a link to the aim.
- A conclusion that claims more than the data can show.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these OxfordAQA past paper questions?**
No. They are original practice tasks with invented data for section 3.4.1 of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level English Language (9670). Use them with the official past papers.

**Why are the tasks short when the real task is a full report?**
Each task practises one part of the report, so that you can build skills before writing a whole report in timed conditions.
