## What the syllabus lists

| Section | Content as printed |
|---|---|
| 9.1 Tectonic hazards | Distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes related to plate tectonics; earthquake hazards (shaking, landslides, soil liquefaction, tsunami); eruption types and products (nuées ardentes, lava flows, lahars, volcanic landslides, pyroclastic flows, ash fallout); primary and secondary impacts; prediction, hazard mapping, preparedness, monitoring, perception of risk |
| 9.2 Mass movements | Nature and causes; impacts on lives and property; prediction, hazard mapping, preparedness, monitoring, perception of risk |
| 9.3 Atmospheric disturbances | Areas most at risk from cyclones, hurricanes, typhoons and tornadoes; their formation; large-scale hazards (storm surge, coastal flooding, intense rain leading to river floods and mass movement, high winds); small-scale hazards (rain and hail, high winds, pressure imbalances); impacts; prediction, preparedness, monitoring, perception of risk |
| 9.4 Sustainable management | Case study of the problems of sustainably managing a hazardous environment, evaluating attempted or possible solutions |

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Hazard | A natural event that threatens life and property |
| Disaster | A hazard event that overwhelms a community's ability to cope |
| Vulnerability | How easily people or property are harmed |
| Risk | The likelihood of harm; often summarised as hazard x vulnerability |
| Moment magnitude | A measure of the energy released by an earthquake |
| Intensity | The strength of shaking and damage at a particular place |
| Soil liquefaction | Saturated sand or silt loses strength during shaking and behaves like a liquid |
| Tsunami | Sea waves caused by sudden displacement of the water column, often by sea-floor uplift |
| Nuée ardente | A glowing cloud of hot gas and ash moving rapidly downslope |
| Pyroclastic flow | A fast, ground-hugging current of hot gas, ash and rock |
| Lahar | A volcanic mudflow of ash and water |
| Volcanic landslide | The collapse of an unstable volcano flank |
| VEI | Volcanic Explosivity Index, from 0 to 8 |
| Shear stress / shear strength | Forces pushing material downslope / resistance from friction and cohesion |
| Pore-water pressure | Pressure of water in the spaces in soil; when high, it reduces shear strength |
| Storm surge | A rise in sea level caused by low pressure and onshore winds |
| Mesocyclone | A rotating updraught inside a supercell thunderstorm |
| Primary / secondary impact | Caused directly by the event / caused by the primary impacts |
| Hazard mapping | Zoning land by level of danger to guide planning and evacuation |

## Case studies at a glance

### 9.1 Tectonic

| Case | What | Key facts | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tohoku, Japan, 11 March 2011 | Mw 9.0-9.1 earthquake and tsunami | Run-up of 38.9 m at Miyako; 19,759 deaths; Fukushima Daiichi meltdowns | A prepared, wealthy country was overwhelmed by an event larger than planned for |
| Christchurch, NZ, 22 February 2011 | Mw 6.2 earthquake | 185 deaths; around 400,000 tonnes of liquefied silt; over 8,000 red-zone homes removed | Moderate magnitude, high impact: shallow focus close to a city |
| Gorkha, Nepal, 25 April 2015 | Mw 7.8 earthquake | Nearly 9,000 deaths; Langtang avalanche and landslide left over 300 missing | Shows earthquake-triggered mass movement in mountains |
| Mont Pelée, Martinique, 8 May 1902 | Nuée ardente | About 28,000 deaths in Saint-Pierre | Older than 1980, but the event that defined the term |
| Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia, 13 November 1985 | Lahars | Over 20,000 deaths in Armero; a newspaper version of the hazard map had no key | Failure of communication, not of science |
| Mount St Helens, USA, 18 May 1980 | Volcanic landslide and lateral blast | Largest landslide on land in recorded history; about 57 deaths; flank bulged 1.5-1.8 m a day | Monitoring led to a "red zone" before the eruption |
| Pinatubo, Philippines, 15 June 1991 | VEI 6 eruption | About 60,000 evacuated; 847 deaths, mostly from roofs under wet ash during Typhoon Yunya | Credited with saving tens of thousands of lives |

### 9.2 Mass movements

| Case | What | Key facts | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freetown, Sierra Leone, 14 August 2017 | Mudslides | 1,141 dead or missing; about 1,000 mm of rain since 1 July | Deforestation and informal housing turned heavy rain into disaster |
| Oso, USA, 22 March 2014 | Landslide | 43 deaths; 49 homes and structures destroyed; earlier slide in 2006 | Risk was known but under-perceived |

### 9.3 Atmospheric

| Case | What | Key facts | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typhoon Haiyan, Philippines, 8 November 2013 | Storm surge and wind | About 5 m surge at Tacloban airport; about 6,300 deaths | Surge, not wind, drove most deaths |
| Cyclone Idai, Mozambique, 15 March 2019 | Flooding from intense rain | About 1,600 deaths in three countries; over 4,000 cholera cases | Secondary impacts lasted weeks |
| Joplin, USA, 22 May 2011 | EF5 tornado | 158 deaths; 17 minutes' warning | Warnings were given but many did not act |
| Daulatpur-Saturia, Bangladesh, 26 April 1989 | Tornado | About 1,300 deaths | Tornado risk is not only a US issue |

### 9.4 Sustainable management: coastal Bangladesh

- Cyclone Preparedness Programme set up in the early 1970s, after the 1970 cyclone, by the government and the Bangladesh Red Crescent Society.
- Deaths: 300,000-500,000 (1970), about 138,000 (1991), at least 3,447 (Sidr, 2007), when about 2 million people went to shelters.
- 139 polders built from the 1960s; the Sundarbans mangroves act as a buffer but face shrimp farming, salinity and sea-level rise.
- Evaluation: big gains in lives saved; weaker gains in livelihoods and the environment.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Magnitude vs intensity:** one number per earthquake vs many values across places.
- **Lahar vs pyroclastic flow:** cold or warm water-rich mud vs hot gas and rock.
- **Nuée ardente vs ash fallout:** a glowing flow down the slope vs ash falling from the air over a wide area.
- **Effusive vs explosive eruptions:** runny basaltic magma vs viscous, gas-rich magma.
- **Prediction vs forecasting:** an exact time and place vs a probability over years.
- **Hazard vs disaster:** the event vs the event plus human vulnerability.
- **Large-scale vs small-scale atmospheric disturbances:** cyclones hundreds of kilometres wide lasting days vs tornadoes a few hundred metres wide lasting minutes.
- **Tropical cyclone vs tornado formation:** warm ocean and latent heat vs wind shear in a supercell over land.
- **Primary vs secondary impacts:** collapse and drowning vs disease, fire and lost income.

## Mini essay plan

**Question:** "Assess the extent to which the impacts of tectonic hazards depend more on human factors than on physical factors." [20]

1. **Introduction.** Define hazard, vulnerability and risk. State your line: physical factors set the potential scale; human factors decide how much of it becomes disaster.
2. **Physical factors.** Magnitude and depth (Tohoku Mw 9.0-9.1); ground conditions (Christchurch liquefaction); type of hazard (pyroclastic flows give no escape).
3. **Human factors.** Communication and trust (Armero's hazard map with no key); monitoring and evacuation (Pinatubo); building quality and land use (Christchurch red zone).
4. **Interaction.** Pinatubo's deaths were raised by a typhoon (physical) acting on weak roofs (human).
5. **Counter-argument.** At the extreme end, physical scale wins: Tohoku's tsunami overwhelmed a wealthy, prepared country.
6. **Judgement.** Human factors explain most differences between places; physical factors dominate only for the very largest events.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four earthquake hazards listed in 9.1.
2. What raises pore-water pressure during liquefaction?
3. Give two differences between a lahar and a pyroclastic flow.
4. Why did roofs collapse at Pinatubo in 1991?
5. What went wrong with the Armero hazard map?
6. State two human causes of mass movement.
7. Give the sea surface temperature needed for a tropical cyclone.
8. Why do tropical cyclones not form on the equator?
9. What is a mesocyclone?
10. Name two hazards from a small-scale atmospheric disturbance.
11. How many deaths did Cyclone Sidr cause, and how many people were evacuated?
12. Give one problem with relying on embankments in coastal Bangladesh.

### Answers

1. Shaking, landslides, soil liquefaction and tsunami.
2. Shaking compacts saturated grains, so water pressure between them rises and grain contact is lost.
3. Lahars are water-rich mudflows; pyroclastic flows are hot gas and rock. Lahars can happen years later with rain.
4. Wet ash from Typhoon Yunya's rain made the ash fallout heavy.
5. It was poorly distributed, and a newspaper version had no key and showed Armero in a green zone read as safe.
6. Any two: deforestation, cutting slopes for roads, building on slopes, poor drainage.
7. At least 26.5 °C, to a depth of about 50 m.
8. The Coriolis force is too weak there to start rotation.
9. A rotating updraught within a supercell thunderstorm.
10. Any two: intense rain, hail, high winds, pressure imbalances.
11. At least 3,447 deaths; about 2 million people were evacuated to shelters.
12. Any one: costly to maintain; can be overtopped or breached; may give a false sense of safety.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing hazards from the syllabus without explaining the process (for example, liquefaction without pore-water pressure).
- Mixing up lahars, pyroclastic flows and nuées ardentes.
- Using case studies as stories instead of as evidence for a point.
- Writing "earthquakes can be predicted" without saying only probabilities are possible.
- Giving cyclone formation conditions as a list, with no link to latent heat and falling pressure.
- Forgetting the small-scale hazards: hail and pressure imbalances are named in the syllabus.
- In 9.4, describing measures without judging them against social, economic and environmental sustainability.
- Ending an essay without a clear judgement on the question's "extent" or "how far".
