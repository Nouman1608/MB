## Questions

**1.** State three functions of a central bank. **[3]**

**2.** In Country A, the CPI index rises from 98.0 to 101.4 over a year. The inflation target is 2%.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of inflation, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** State the gap between inflation and the target, and say which way the MPC is likely to move bank rate, with one reason. **[2]**

**3.** A bank pays 6% on savings while inflation is 4.5%. Later the bank pays 2% and inflation is 3.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the approximate real interest rate in each case. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the second result implies for saving and spending. **[1]**

**4.** A household has a variable-rate, interest-only mortgage of £90,000. The rate rises from 2.5% to 4.0%.

**(a)** Work out how much more the household pays in interest each year, and the monthly equivalent. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why a rise in bank rate might reduce total consumption by less than this suggests. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how a rise in bank rate can reduce inflation. **[6]**

**6.** The exchange rate falls from £1 = €1.25 to £1 = €1.10.

**(a)** A UK firm sells a machine for £80. Find its euro price before and after, and the percentage change in that price. **[3]**
**(b)** A UK importer buys goods priced at €55. Calculate the sterling cost before and after. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how an appreciation of the pound affects AD and two macroeconomic objectives. **[6]**

**8.** The Bank carries out quantitative easing by buying £25bn of gilts from a pension fund.

**(a)** Explain what happens in this purchase, including the effect on bank reserves. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how QE may raise AD. **[3]**

**9.** Explain what is meant by (a) Funding for Lending and (b) forward guidance. **[4]**

**10.** Country A's data before an MPC meeting: forecast inflation 3.6% (target 2%); unemployment 3.8%; wage growth 5.5% against productivity growth of 1.0%; output gap +1.5% of potential output; exchange rate index down 8% over the year; consumer confidence weak.

Using the data, explain two reasons why members may vote to raise bank rate, and one reason for caution. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that raising bank rate is the best way for a central bank to reduce inflation. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three of: issues banknotes [1]; banker to the government [1]; banker to commercial banks, holding their reserve accounts [1]; lender of last resort; carries out monetary policy (each worth a mark, up to three in all). **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name distinct functions; "controls the economy" is too vague to be a function.

**2. (a)** (101.4 - 98.0) / 98.0 x 100 [1] = 3.469..., so **3.5%** [1]
**(b)** Inflation is 3.5 - 2.0 = **1.5 percentage points above target** [1]; a rise in bank rate is likely, to slow AD and bring inflation back toward 2% [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula before the answer, and give the gap in percentage points, not percent.

**3. (a)** First case 6 - 4.5 = **1.5%** [1]; second case 2 - 3.5 = **-1.5%** [1]
**(b)** A negative real return discourages saving and encourages spending and borrowing, so AD tends to rise [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative sign on the real rate carries the meaning; saying "lower" loses it.

**4. (a)** Before: 90,000 x 0.025 = £2,250 [1]. After: 90,000 x 0.04 = £3,600 [1]. Increase **£1,350 a year, £112.50 a month** [1]
**(b)** Savers receive higher interest, for example £20,000 moving from 1% to 2.5% gains £300 a year [1]; households on fixed rates do not feel the rise yet, so total consumption changes by less [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason for a smaller effect must be developed. Naming "fixed rates" without saying what they do to spending gains less.

**5.** Bank rate rises, so banks raise lending and savings rates [1]. Consumption falls because borrowing is dearer, saving pays more and variable-rate borrowers have less disposable income [1]. Investment falls because the cost of finance rises and fewer projects are profitable [1]. Higher interest rates attract foreign financial inflows, so the currency appreciates [1]. Foreign buyers face higher export prices and imports cost less, so net exports fall [1]. AD shifts left, which reduces demand-pull pressure, and cheaper imports reduce cost pressure, so inflation slows [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Completing the chain to inflation matters. Stopping at "AD falls" leaves the question half answered.

**6. (a)** Before: 80 x 1.25 = **€100** [1]. After: 80 x 1.10 = **€88** [1]. Change **-12%** [1]
**(b)** Before: 55 / 1.25 = **£44** [1]. After: 55 / 1.10 = **£50** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the direction of the division. Multiply when converting pounds to euros, divide when converting euros to pounds.

**7.** Foreign buyers pay more for UK exports, while UK buyers pay less for imports [1], so net exports and AD fall [1]. Inflation: cheaper imported raw materials and goods lower costs and prices, and lower AD reduces demand pressure [2]. Growth and employment: lower AD slows growth, and export-led firms may cut output and jobs [2]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Pick two objectives and give each its own reasoning. A list of three or four effects with no development gains less than two explained effects.

**8. (a)** The Bank creates new electronic reserves and uses them to buy gilts [1]. The pension fund gives up £25bn of gilts and receives a £25bn deposit at its bank [1]; the bank's reserves at the Bank rise by £25bn [1].
**(b)** Gilt prices rise and yields fall, lowering longer-term borrowing costs and encouraging spending [1]. Higher asset prices create a wealth effect on consumption [1]. Banks with extra reserves and deposits may lend more, raising credit [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Say that QE creates reserves electronically; "printing money" invites confusion with banknotes.

**9. (a)** Funding for Lending: cheap funding for banks, linked to their lending to households and firms [1], to lower loan rates and increase the supply of credit [1].
**(b)** Forward guidance: the central bank signals the likely future path of policy [1], which shapes expectations so that borrowing and spending adjust today [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Define the tool, then give its purpose. A definition alone is one step.

**10.** Raise: forecast inflation of 3.6% is above the 2% target [1], so tighter policy is needed to slow AD [1]. Wage growth of 5.5% far exceeds productivity growth of 1.0%, a source of cost pressure [1]; with a positive output gap and low unemployment, spare capacity is small and demand-pull pressure is likely to continue [1]. Caution: weak consumer confidence suggests demand may already be slowing, and with policy lags a rise could overshoot and cut output [2]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures, and compare them with something, such as the target or productivity growth. Quoting a figure alone adds nothing.

**11.** Definition and mechanism: bank rate works through borrowing costs, saving, wealth and the exchange rate to reduce AD [1]; effective against demand-pull inflation, because it removes excess demand [1]; the MPC is independent of government and rate changes are credible, which anchors expectations [1]; it is flexible and can be reversed quickly [1]. Limits: time lags of a year or more, so decisions rest on uncertain forecasts [1]; against cost-push inflation from energy or import prices, a rise cuts output and raises unemployment without addressing the cause [1]; effects are uneven, hitting borrowers and exporters more than savers [1]; effects depend on how interest-sensitive spending is and on household debt [1]. Alternatives: fiscal policy and supply-side policy tackle different causes, and QE or forward guidance may help when rates are low [1]; the exchange rate and global factors can reinforce or weaken the effect [1]. Judgement: bank rate is the main tool for demand-pull inflation but not the only tool, and its success depends on the source of inflation, the lags and the state of confidence [2]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation is strongest when tied to a condition, such as the type of inflation or the size of the lag, and ends with a judgement that follows from it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong base for an index or growth calculation.
- Giving a gap in percent instead of percentage points.
- Dividing and multiplying the wrong way in exchange rate conversions.
- Describing QE as printing banknotes, or forgetting the effect on reserves and deposits.
- Stopping the transmission chain before reaching inflation.
- Treating an appreciation as good or bad without naming which objective.
- Quoting data without comparing it to a target or another figure.
- Writing a one-sided answer to an evaluation question, or ending without a judgement.
