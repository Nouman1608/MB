## Questions

**1.** For each transaction in Country M, name the account and, if it is in the financial account, the category. State whether it is a credit or a debit. (i) A foreign firm buys a 70% stake in a Country M company. (ii) A foreign government cancels part of Country M's debt. (iii) Country M's central bank sells foreign currency reserves. (iv) A Country M pension fund buys bonds issued abroad. **[4]**

**2.** Country M, one illustrative year. Goods: credits 120, debits 134. Services: 70 and 52. Primary income: 18 and 25. Secondary income: 9 and 12. Capital account: credits 5, debits 1. Financial account: direct investment +6, portfolio investment −3, other investment +2, reserves unchanged. GDP is 400.

**(a)** Find the combined trade balance for goods and services. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the current account balance. **[2]**
**(c)** Show the current account balance as a share of GDP, in percent. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate net errors and omissions. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how a current account deficit is paid for. **[4]**

**4.** In Country M the marginal propensity to save is 0.12, to be taxed 0.18 and to import 0.20. The government cuts spending by 5 to reduce the deficit.

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the fall in national income and the fall in imports. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one cost of using this policy. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a rise in the interest rate may leave the current account worse. **[4]**

**6.** In Country M wages rise 3% and output per worker rises 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the change in unit labour cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Export price elasticity of demand is 1.5 and the cost fall is passed on fully. Calculate the change in export volume. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why supply-side policy improves the current account slowly. **[2]**

**7.** Country M imports 2,000 units of a good at 35 mu each. It imposes a 20% tariff. Price elasticity of demand is −1.25.

**(a)** Calculate the new price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Find how much the payments made to foreign suppliers change. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the tariff revenue. **[1]**
**(d)** State one risk of using the tariff. **[1]**

**8.** Distinguish between expenditure-reducing and expenditure-switching policies, with one example of each. **[4]**

**9.** The currency of Country M falls 8%. Goods exports are 150 and imports are 180. Export prices in mu are unchanged and import prices in mu rise 8%. Export price elasticity of demand is 0.9 and import price elasticity of demand is 0.7.

**(a)** Calculate the new value of exports. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the new value of imports. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the new goods balance and its change from the original. **[2]**

**10.** Country N has a current account deficit caused mainly by rising costs and poor quality in its manufactured exports. The government is choosing between a devaluation and spending cuts. Evaluate the two policies. **[10]**

**11.** Country P's current account deficit is financed mainly by short-term portfolio inflows. Evaluate whether the government should treat this as a serious problem. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) Financial account, direct investment, credit [1]. (ii) Capital account, credit [1]. (iii) Financial account, reserve assets, credit [1]. (iv) Financial account, portfolio investment, debit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the account, the category and the direction each time. A fall in reserves is a source of funds, which is why it is a credit here.

**2. (a)** Goods −14 and services +18, so **+4** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Primary −7 and secondary −3 [1]. Current account = −14 + 18 − 7 − 3 = **−6** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 6 ÷ 400 × 100 = **−1.5% of GDP** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Capital account +4 and financial account 6 − 3 + 2 = +5 [1]. Errors = −(−6 + 4 + 5) = **−3** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Write out every line, with its sign, before you add. The identity (all accounts sum to zero) is a quick check on your balancing item.

**3.** The accounts sum to zero [1], so a current account deficit is matched by net inflows on the capital and financial accounts [1]. Inflows include foreign direct investment, portfolio investment and borrowing from abroad [1], or the central bank may draw down reserves [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the financing routes. An answer that only says "borrowing" misses most of the financial account.

**4. (a)** Withdrawals = 0.12 + 0.18 + 0.20 = 0.50 [1]. Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.5 = **2** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Income falls 5 × 2 = **10** [1]. Imports fall 0.2 × 10 = **2.0** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Output falls by 10 to save 2 of imports, so growth and jobs suffer [1]. If the deficit comes from weak competitiveness, the cause is untouched and imports rise again in recovery [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the cost to numbers from your own working. Referring to the 10 against the 2 shows the cost rather than asserting it.

**5.** A higher rate attracts hot money [1], raising demand for the currency and its exchange rate [1]. Exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper [1], so if demand is elastic export volume falls and import volume rises, which can outweigh the fall in import spending from lower income [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Take the chain to the current account. Stopping at "the currency rises" misses the final link.

**6. (a)** 1.03 ÷ 1.05 = 0.981 [1]. Unit labour cost falls **1.9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 1.5 × 1.9 = **about 2.9%** rise in export volume [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Training, infrastructure and research take years to build [1], and it is uncertain how far they raise productivity, so costs and quality change gradually [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the rounded or unrounded figure consistently and say "about" for a rounded elasticity result.

**7. (a)** Price = 35 × 1.2 = **42 mu** [1]. Quantity falls 1.25 × 20 = 25%, to **1,500** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 2,000 × 35 = 70,000. After: 1,500 × 35 = 52,500 [1]. Payments fall by **17,500 mu** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 1,500 × 7 = **10,500 mu** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Trading partners may retaliate with their own tariffs, cutting exports (or buyers face higher prices) [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the pre-tariff price for payments to foreign suppliers. Using 42 would count the tariff as money sent abroad.

**8.** Expenditure-reducing policies cut total spending in the economy, including spending on imports [1]. Example: contractionary fiscal policy or a higher interest rate [1]. Expenditure-switching policies change relative prices so that spending moves from imports to home goods [1]. Example: devaluation or a tariff [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition and an example are separate points. A tariff is switching because it redirects spending, not reduces it.

**9. (a)** Export volume rises 0.9 × 8 = 7.2% [1]. Exports = 150 × 1.072 = **160.8** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Import volume falls 0.7 × 8 = 5.6% [1]. Imports = 180 × 1.08 × 0.944 = **183.5** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Balance = 160.8 − 183.5 = **−22.7** [1]. Original −30, so the deficit narrows by **7.3** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the price rise and the volume change to imports together. Using only the volume change understates the value.

**10.** A devaluation makes exports cheaper to foreign buyers and imports dearer to home buyers [1], switching spending to home goods [1]. It works if the sum of the elasticities is high enough [1]. But the deficit comes from costs and quality, and weak quality makes demand less responsive to price [1]. Dearer imports can add to inflation, and the J curve delays any gain [1]. Spending cuts reduce income and so imports through the mpm [1], at a cost in output and jobs [1], and they do not improve competitiveness, so the cause remains [1]. Supply-side policy on quality and productivity is needed with either [1]. Judgement: devaluation fits the cause better, if there is spare capacity and the elasticities are high enough, while cuts suit only a deficit from excess demand [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Match each policy to the stated cause and finish with a judgement that names a condition. A comparison of two lists without a verdict leaves the question unanswered.

**11.** The deficit is matched by net financial inflows, so the accounts balance [1]. Portfolio inflows are mobile and can reverse quickly [1]. A reversal can cause a sharp fall in the currency, a drain on reserves or a forced rise in interest rates [1]. A fall in the currency raises import prices and makes foreign debt dearer to repay [1]. On the other hand, a small deficit, or inflows that fund productive investment, may be sustainable [1]. Ample reserves or a flexible exchange rate can absorb shocks [1]. The seriousness depends on the size, the cause and how long it lasts [1]. Judgement: a concern if it is large and rests on volatile funds, with the condition stated [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the type of funding from the size of the deficit. A judgement that names the condition on which it rests is stronger than a flat yes or no.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a financial account item the wrong direction, especially a fall in reserves.
- Dropping the minus sign on a deficit or on net errors and omissions.
- Calling the current account "the balance of payments" when the whole account sums to zero.
- Counting tariff revenue as payment to foreign suppliers.
- Naming a policy without linking it to the cause of the deficit.
- Calling a tariff expenditure-reducing.
- Saying a devaluation always improves the current account with no mention of elasticities or the J curve.
- Stopping an evaluation at a list of points with no judgement.
