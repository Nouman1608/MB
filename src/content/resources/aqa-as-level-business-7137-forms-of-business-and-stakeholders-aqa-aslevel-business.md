This guide goes deeper into section **3.1.2 Forms of business and stakeholders** of AQA AS Business (7137), part of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (Version 1.1, November 2025) for first teaching from September 2026. The specification lists:

- **Different forms of business**: sole traders; private limited companies (Ltd); public limited companies (plc); co-operatives (worker, consumer and producer); social enterprises.
- **Issues to consider when comparing forms**: control; objectives; sources of finance; distribution of profits; unlimited and limited liability; reasons to buy shares; influences on share price and the significance of share price changes.
- **Calculate, interpret and analyse**: market capitalisation; dividends per share; dividend yield.
- **Stakeholders**: internal stakeholders (employees, owners/shareholders); external stakeholders (customers, suppliers, other creditors, community, government); the impact of business activity on stakeholders; ethical responsibilities to stakeholders.

The specification places this section in Unit 3.1 for AS Paper 1, and states that AS Paper 2 also draws on it. The broader [AQA AS What is business? guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-business-what-is-business/) gives an overview. This page compares the forms issue by issue, adds the three types of co-operative, interprets the share calculations and applies stakeholder analysis to a decision. ## Comparing the forms, issue by issue

The specification asks you to compare forms using named issues, so learn them as a grid.

| Issue | Sole trader | Ltd | plc | Co-operative | Social enterprise |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Control | One owner decides | Shareholders, usually a small group; directors run it | Shareholders vote; directors run it, often separate from owners | Members, usually one member one vote | Depends on its legal form; social aim guides decisions |
| Objectives | Owner's choice, often independence or satisfactory profit | Often long-term growth and control | Often profit, dividends and share price | Benefit to members | A social or environmental aim |
| Sources of finance | Owner's savings, loans, retained profit | Shares sold privately, loans, retained profit | Shares sold to the public, loans, retained profit | Members' contributions, loans, retained surplus | Trading income, social investors, grants, loans |
| Distribution of profits | All to the owner | Dividends to shareholders | Dividends to shareholders | Shared among members, often according to how much they use or work in the co-operative | Mainly reinvested in the social aim |
| Liability | Unlimited | Limited | Limited | Usually limited if registered as a society or company | Usually limited if incorporated |

### Co-operatives: three types

A co-operative is owned by its members, who share the benefits and usually have one vote each regardless of how much they have put in.

- **Worker co-operative**: owned and run by its employees, who share decisions and the surplus. Motivation can be high because staff own the business, but decisions can be slow and raising large capital is hard.
- **Consumer co-operative**: owned by its customers, who become members. Surplus may be returned to members in proportion to their purchases, or used to lower prices and improve services.
- **Producer co-operative**: independent producers, such as farmers, join together to buy inputs, process or market their output. They gain bargaining power and economies of scale they could not achieve alone.

The one-member-one-vote principle protects members' control but makes co-operatives less attractive to outside investors, because buying more shares does not buy more say.

### Social enterprises

A social enterprise trades like a business but exists mainly to achieve a social or environmental aim, reinvesting most of its surplus. Examples of aims include employing people who struggle to find work, or reducing waste. It can attract customers and grants because of its aim, but it may find it harder to raise finance from investors who want high returns.

## Unlimited and limited liability

**Unlimited liability**: the owner is personally responsible for all the business's debts, so personal possessions can be taken to pay them. **Limited liability**: shareholders can lose only what they have invested, because the company is a separate legal entity.

Limited liability encourages investment and risk-taking. Its drawback for others is that suppliers and lenders bear more of the risk, so they may ask for personal guarantees from the owners of small companies.

## Shares: why buy, and what moves the price

**Reasons to buy shares:** dividend income; capital growth if the share price rises; a say in the company through voting; personal or ethical reasons such as supporting a business whose aims the investor shares.

**Influences on share price:** company results and forecasts; dividend announcements; takeover rumours; news about the industry; the state of the economy and interest rates; investor confidence.

**Significance of changes:** a rising price increases shareholders' wealth and makes it easier to raise money through new shares; a falling price reduces confidence, makes a takeover cheaper and may put pressure on directors. A share price change does not, in itself, change the company's cash.

## The three calculations

The specification's annex of formulae gives:

- **Market capitalisation** = number of issued shares × current share price
- **Dividend per share** = total dividend paid ÷ number of ordinary shares in issue
- **Dividend yield (%)** = dividend per share (pence) × 100 ÷ share price (pence)

### Worked example: interpreting the figures

Ardmore Retail plc has 25 million ordinary shares, trading at 320p. It pays a total dividend of £2 million.

- Dividend per share = £2,000,000 ÷ 25,000,000 = **8p**.
- Dividend yield = 8 × 100 ÷ 320 = **2.5%**.
- Market capitalisation = 25,000,000 × £3.20 = **£80 million**.

The share price then falls to 250p after weak results, while the dividend stays at 8p.

- New dividend yield = 8 × 100 ÷ 250 = **3.2%**.
- New market capitalisation = 25,000,000 × £2.50 = **£62.5 million**.

**Interpretation.** The yield has risen, but not because Ardmore is paying more: the price has fallen. A high yield can signal that investors doubt the dividend will last. Comparing the yield with the interest available on savings, and with similar companies, helps an investor judge whether the share is good value. Existing shareholders have lost £17.5 million of value in total, and the lower market capitalisation leaves Ardmore more exposed to a takeover.

## Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities of a business.

| Internal | External |
|---|---|
| Employees | Customers |
| Owners/shareholders | Suppliers |
| | Other creditors, such as banks |
| | Community |
| | Government |

### Impact of business activity on stakeholders

Business decisions can help some stakeholders and harm others. The skill is to trace one decision through several groups.

**Worked example: Penwith Packaging automates its warehouse.**

| Stakeholder | Likely impact |
|---|---|
| Shareholders | Lower costs could raise profit and dividends, after the cost of the machines |
| Employees | Some jobs lost; remaining staff may need retraining but could gain higher-skilled roles |
| Customers | Faster, more accurate deliveries, perhaps lower prices |
| Suppliers | The machine supplier gains an order; others are unaffected |
| Bank (creditor) | If the machines are financed by a loan, the bank gains interest but takes on risk |
| Community | Fewer local jobs, less spending in local shops |
| Government | Possible higher tax from profits; possible extra spending if redundant staff claim support |

An analysis answer would pick the two most affected groups (here shareholders and employees), explain the conflict and say how the business could reduce the harm, for example through retraining or redeployment.

### Ethical responsibilities to stakeholders

Beyond what the law requires, businesses have ethical responsibilities such as treating employees fairly, being honest with customers, paying suppliers and creditors on time, limiting harm to the community and paying taxes properly. Meeting them can cost money, but it builds trust, which can bring loyalty, better staff and easier access to finance.

## Exam technique: AQA's command words

The specification lists **Define** (2 marks), **Explain** (4), **Analyse** (6), **Assess** (9) and **Evaluate** (15) for AS. For "Explain" use the case; for "Analyse" build a chain of consequences; for "Assess" weigh both sides and judge; for "Evaluate" compare two options and recommend one, saying what the recommendation depends on. For calculations, show the formula and state the units (pence, pounds or per cent).

## Common mistakes

- Using the price at which shares were first issued, rather than the current price, for market capitalisation.
- Mixing pounds and pence in the dividend yield.
- Saying a higher dividend yield always means a better investment.
- Treating co-operatives as one type, or forgetting one member one vote.
- Listing stakeholders without analysing the impact of the decision on them.
- Calling shareholders external stakeholders; the specification classes owners/shareholders as internal.
