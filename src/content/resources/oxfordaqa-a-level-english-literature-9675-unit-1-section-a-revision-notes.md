method and a worked model paragraph, use the
[Unit 1 Section A study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-1-section-a/).

## The question in one box

- **One** passage-based question on your chosen play: **25 marks**, half of Unit 1.
- The passage is printed; the rest of the play comes **from memory** (closed book).
- AO1, AO2 and AO3 carry **equal weight**: contexts, genre and interpretations (AO1), authorial
  methods (AO2), and an argued response in accurate writing (AO3).
- Unit 1 is 2 hours for two answers, so allow about **an hour** for Section A.

## Aspects of tragedy a passage can show

The specification lists areas to explore rather than a closed list:

- type of tragedy: **classical and public** or **domestic and ordinary**;
- **settings**, both place and time;
- the hero's **journey towards death**: flaws, pride, folly, blindness, insight, discovery, values;
- the **villain or opponent** and the contest of power;
- **fate** and inevitability;
- how the hero's behaviour **affects the world** around them;
- **violence and revenge**;
- **structure**: complication to catastrophe, order to disorder, prosperity to the tragic end;
- **plots and sub-plots**;
- **dramatic language** heightening the tragedy;
- the **audience**: pity, fear and understanding of the human condition.

Remember: the **absence** of an aspect can be as significant as its presence.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Use it to say |
|---|---|---|
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter | When a character's control of verse holds or breaks |
| Prose | Unmetred speech | A drop in status, madness, intimacy or plotting |
| Shared line | One verse line split between speakers | Speed, tension, one character overriding another |
| Soliloquy | Speech alone on stage | Private thought; the audience as confidant |
| Aside | Brief remark others on stage do not hear | Dramatic irony and complicity |
| Embedded stage direction | Action implied in the dialogue | What the audience actually sees |
| Dramatic irony | Audience knows more than a character | Dread, pity and suspense |
| Anagnorisis | Recognition or discovery | The moment of insight, often too late |
| Peripeteia | Reversal of fortune | A turning point in the structure |
| Catharsis | Release of pity and fear | The audience's response at the end |
| Machiavel | Scheming stage villain | Iago, Edmund and the Cardinal as types |
| Revenge tragedy | Genre of wrong, delay and bloody revenge | *Hamlet*, *The Duchess of Malfi* |

The Greek terms are useful shorthand; the specification itself describes these ideas in plain
English, so either is acceptable when used accurately.

## Method in four words: place, read, name, reach

1. **Place** the passage in the arc and note the dramatic irony at that point.
2. **Read** the method closely: verse, prose, imagery, rhetoric, stage directions.
3. **Name** the tragic aspect each point illuminates.
4. **Reach** out to two other moments from memory: one before, one after.

## Context that earns its place

- **Revenge tragedy**: the ghost, delay, feigned madness, the violent final act.
- **Morality tradition** in *Faustus*: Good and Evil Angels; the Seven Deadly Sins.
- **Order and hierarchy**: kingship, inheritance, family and gender roles.
- **Staging**: a bare stage, boy actors in female roles, language creating night and storm.

Each contextual point should change how a specific line or moment reads.

## Exam traps

- A general essay on the play with the passage barely used.
- Close analysis of the passage with no reference to the rest of the play.
- Naming a convention ("this is hamartia") without arguing how the play uses it.
- Quoting from memory inaccurately: shorter, accurate quotations are safer than long, wrong ones.
- Forgetting the audience: a play is seen and heard, so write about staging and sound.
- Running out of time for Section B: Section A is half the unit, not more.

## Self-test

1. How many marks is Unit 1 Section A worth, and what is printed on the paper?
2. Which three assessment objectives are tested, and how are they weighted?
3. Which two early versions of *Doctor Faustus* exist, and which is set?
4. What does the specification say about the absence of an aspect of tragedy?
5. Give two features of a revenge tragedy.
6. What is an embedded stage direction, and why does it matter for these plays?
7. What can a shift from verse into prose suggest?
8. Define anagnorisis.
9. Name the four steps of the passage method in these notes.
10. Which play's sub-plot follows a parallel fall of a father deceived by a child?
11. Why might a writer place a comic or low-status moment just before a catastrophe?
12. Roughly how long should you spend on Section A?

## Answers

1. 25 marks, half of the 50-mark unit; a passage from the set play is printed.
2. AO1 (genre, contexts, interpretations), AO2 (authorial methods), AO3 (argued response in
   accurate writing); all three are weighted equally.
3. The A text (printed 1604) and the B text (printed 1616); the B text is set.
4. That the absence of an aspect can be as significant as its presence.
5. Any two: a wrong demanding revenge; delay; a ghost or haunting past; feigned or real madness;
   a violent final act.
6. An action implied within the dialogue; early texts print few stage directions, so these lines
   are the best evidence of what the audience sees.
7. Any one: a fall in status or control; madness; intimacy; plotting or deception.
8. Recognition or discovery: the moment a character sees the truth, often too late.
9. Place, read, name, reach.
10. *King Lear*: Gloucester is deceived by Edmund about Edgar.
11. Contrast heightens the shock and pity of the catastrophe, and can comment ironically on it.
12. About an hour, since Unit 1 lasts 2 hours for two answers of equal value.
