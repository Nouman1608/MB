Condensed recall for **4.2 Demographic transition** (the model, its stages and its limits). For full explanations, read the [Core Human Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-human-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demographic transition | Shift from high to low birth and death rates as a country develops |
| Demographic transition model (DTM) | A generalised graph of birth rate, death rate and total population through five stages |
| Youthful population | Large share of children |
| Ageing population | Large share of elderly people |
| Life expectancy | Average expected years of life |
| Natural decrease | Deaths exceed births |
| Epidemiological transition | Shift in the main causes of death from infectious disease to chronic, age-related disease |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths under one year per 1000 live births |

## The five stages

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Natural increase | Population |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | High | High | Low | Small, stable or fluctuating |
| 2 | High | Falls rapidly | Rising to high | Grows quickly, youthful |
| 3 | Falls | Low, falling | Slowing | Still growing |
| 4 | Low | Low | Low | Large, stable |
| 5 | Very low | Low | Zero or negative | Ageing, may shrink |

**Pyramid shapes by stage:** stage 2 has a very wide base and concave sides; stage 3 a base beginning to narrow; stage 4 near-vertical sides; stage 5 a base narrower than the middle (a "beehive" or inverted shape).

## Reasons for change

- **Death rate falls:** better food supply, clean water, sanitation, medicine and vaccination, public health.
- **Birth rate falls:** lower infant mortality, education and employment of women, contraception, urbanisation, rising cost of children.
- **Stage 5:** low fertility, high old-age dependency, possible reliance on immigration.

**Why the birth rate lags behind the death rate:** fertility depends on attitudes, customs and economic needs that change slowly. Parents only reduce family size once they are confident children will survive, once children stop being a source of farm labour, and once women gain education and paid work. This time lag is what produces the rapid growth of stage 2 and early stage 3.

## Illustrative countries

Placing countries on the model is a generalisation and changes over time, so use a recent, dated source:

| Stage | Typical examples | Signs |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No countries today; some isolated groups | High, fluctuating birth and death rates |
| 2 | Some of the least developed countries, such as Niger | Very high fertility, falling death rate |
| 3 | Many MICs, such as India | Falling fertility, still growing population |
| 4 | Many HICs, such as the UK or the USA | Low, stable rates |
| 5 | Japan, Italy, Germany | Natural decrease without immigration |

Japan is a widely cited stage 5 example: its population peaked in the late 2000s and has since been falling, with one of the oldest age structures in the world.

## Links with development

As infant mortality falls and life expectancy rises, a country moves along the model. These changes accompany economic and social development, though the relationship is not exact.

Infant mortality is a sensitive indicator because it responds quickly to clean water, sanitation, vaccination and maternal education. Falling infant mortality is also a **cause** of later falls in fertility, so it links the death rate side of the model to the birth rate side. Rising life expectancy reflects both lower infant deaths and longer adult lives, and goes with the **epidemiological transition** from infectious to chronic disease.

## Critical appreciation

- Based on European experience; **later transitions** (many LICs) can be faster, because medical technology arrives from outside.
- Does not include migration.
- Assumes a single path; some countries show fertility falling before economic growth, others stall.
- Stage 5 is a later addition, not part of the original four-stage model.
- Government policy and HIV/AIDS and conflict can distort the path.

More points to use in evaluation:

- **Timing:** the transition in the UK took well over a century; in parts of East Asia fertility fell to low levels within a few decades.
- **Policy:** China's birth limits pushed fertility down faster than development alone might have (see 4.4).
- **Culture and religion** can keep fertility higher than income levels would predict.
- **Usefulness:** the model is still a valuable framework for comparing countries and predicting future pressures, such as ageing, as long as it is not treated as inevitable.

The model was developed from work by Warren Thompson (1929) and Frank Notestein (1945), using the experience of Europe and North America.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Assess the usefulness of the demographic transition model."*

> The model fits the European experience well and helps explain why stage 2 countries grow rapidly (**strength**). But many LICs had faster death rate falls because of imported medicine, and some have stalled in stage 3 (**limitation**). It ignores migration, which shapes many populations (**limitation**). It is useful as a framework but not as a forecast (**judgement**).

## Exam traps

- In stage 2 the death rate falls first; the birth rate stays high.
- Natural increase is highest in stage 2, not stage 3, but population keeps growing in stage 3.
- Do not call the model a prediction; it is a generalisation.
- Explain the time lag between death rate and birth rate falls; it is the core of the model.
- Back a stage placement with evidence, such as a fertility rate or a pyramid shape.

## Self-test

1. Which stage has the highest natural increase?
2. Why does the death rate fall in stage 2?
3. Describe stage 4.
4. What is distinctive about stage 5?
5. Give one limitation of the model.
6. Why do many LICs transition faster?
7. Why does the birth rate fall later than the death rate?
8. Describe the pyramid shape typical of stage 5.
9. Name one country often placed in stage 5 and give evidence.
10. What is the epidemiological transition?
11. Why is infant mortality a useful link between the two sides of the model?

**Answers:** 1. Stage 2. 2. Better food, sanitation, medicine and public health. 3. Low birth and death rates, large stable population. 4. Birth rate below death rate, ageing population, natural decrease. 5. European basis; ignores migration; assumes one path. 6. Imported medical technology cut death rates quickly. 7. Fertility depends on slowly changing attitudes and needs, and parents wait until children are sure to survive. 8. A base narrower than the middle, with a large elderly share. 9. Japan: falling population and a very old age structure. 10. The shift from infectious to chronic, age-related disease as the main causes of death. 11. Falling infant deaths lower the death rate and later encourage parents to have fewer children.
