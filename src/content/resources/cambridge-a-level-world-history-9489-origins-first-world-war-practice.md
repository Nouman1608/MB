## Section A

**1.** Explain how Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles shaped writing about the origins of the war in the 1920s. **[3]**

**2.** Identify two reasons why the idea of shared responsibility became widely accepted between the wars. **[2]**

**3.** State three pieces of evidence Fritz Fischer used to argue that Germany sought world power. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why Fischer's work caused such controversy in West Germany. **[3]**

**5.** Identify two criticisms made of the Fischer thesis. **[2]**

**6.** Give three examples of historians who have emphasised the responsibility of states other than Germany, and the state each emphasised. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how the questions a historian asks can shape their interpretation of 1914. **[2]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which new evidence has changed interpretations of the origins of the war. **[4]**

## Section B

**9.** *Constructed extract (Marlbridge).*

> Europe in 1914 was a machine built for war, though no engineer intended it. Two armed camps faced each other, each convinced that it was defending itself and that the other was preparing to attack. Every crisis since 1905 had been settled, but each settlement left the losers resentful and the winners convinced that firmness worked. General staffs drew up plans that depended on speed, and once mobilisation began, diplomacy had hours rather than weeks. The men who led Europe shared an outlook that is hard now to recover: that war was a test of a nation's vitality, that the next one would be short, and that a great power which backed down would cease to be great. In July 1914 they did not choose war so much as fail to imagine any alternative to the moves their alliances and plans demanded. To ask which capital was guilty is to ask the wrong question. The guilt lay in a system that all of them had built and none of them could control.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer. **[40]**

**10.** *Constructed extract (Marlbridge).*

> The road to the general war ran through St Petersburg. Russian ministers had long wanted control of the Straits, and they saw in the Balkan crisis a chance to secure Russia's position before Germany's influence in Constantinople grew any further. Serbia was encouraged to believe that Russia would stand behind it. When the Austrian ultimatum arrived, the Russian government did not wait for Vienna to act; it began military preparations within days, and on 30 July it ordered general mobilisation, knowing that Germany's plans made a German response unavoidable. France, whose President had been in the Russian capital only a week before, did nothing to restrain its ally. Berlin's blank cheque was reckless, but it was a promise to support an ally in a Balkan quarrel. It was Russia that made the Balkan quarrel a European war.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer. **[40]**

**11.** *Constructed extract (Marlbridge).*

> To understand Germany's decisions in July 1914 one must look not at the Foreign Office but at the Reichstag. The elections of 1912 made the Social Democrats the largest party. The landowners, industrialists and officers who dominated the Empire faced a choice between reform, which would end their power, and a policy that would unite the nation behind the throne. Naval building and colonial agitation had served that purpose before; by 1914 they no longer did. A successful war offered what domestic politics could not: a surge of patriotism, the discrediting of socialism and the preservation of the existing order. The diplomats' notes of July are the surface of the story. Beneath them lay a ruling class that had come to see war as a solution to problems at home.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer. **[40]**

## Answers

**1.** 1 mark each: Article 231 assigned responsibility for loss and damage to Germany and its allies (1); Germans treated it as a verdict of "war guilt" that justified the reparations and had to be refuted (1); the German government funded publication of selected documents and research to prove its innocence, and other governments replied with their own collections, so the debate was conducted with politically selected evidence (1). **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: horror at the scale of the slaughter made many reject the idea that one nation alone was guilty (1); the Versailles settlement came to be seen as harsh, and revising war guilt supported revising the treaty (1); published diplomatic documents showed mistakes by all governments (1); reconciliation with Germany was a political aim in the late 1920s (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: the September Programme of 1914 listing annexations and a German-led central Europe (1); the "blank cheque" to Austria-Hungary and German pressure on Vienna to act quickly (1); the War Council of December 1912 (1); government files showing leaders' expectations of Russian intervention (1); evidence of pre-war ambitions for *Weltpolitik* and economic dominance (1). **[3]**

**4.** 1 mark each: it challenged the post-war consensus that Germany shared responsibility with others (1); it suggested continuity between the aims of Imperial Germany and those of Hitler, implicating the old elites whom many saw as respectable (1); it came from a German professor using German archives, so it could not be dismissed as Allied propaganda (1). **[3]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: he read war aims drawn up after war began back into the decisions of July 1914 (1); he concentrated on German archives and neglected the decisions of other powers (1); the War Council of December 1912 did not lead to a concrete plan for war (1); his emphasis on domestic motives was not proven by the documents of July (1). **[2]**

**6.** Any three, 1 mark each: Sean McMeekin -- Russia (1); Samuel Williamson -- Austria-Hungary (1); Niall Ferguson -- Britain, for turning a continental war into a world war (1); Christopher Clark -- responsibility spread across several capitals including Serbia and Russia (1); 1920s writers such as Barnes -- France and Russia (1). **[3]**

**7.** 1 mark each: a historian who asks "why" looks for long-term causes and tends to assign blame (1); a historian who asks "how" studies decisions in sequence and tends to stress contingency and shared responsibility, as Clark does (1). **[2]**

**8.** Two ways, 2 marks each: German archives opened after 1945 allowed Fischer to argue for deliberate German policy (2); work using Russian and Serbian material gave more weight to decisions in St Petersburg and Belgrade (2); research on German military planning, including Zuber's questioning of the Schlieffen Plan and Mombauer's work on Moltke, changed how war plans are weighed (2). **[4]**

**9.** Indicative content. **Main interpretation:** responsibility lay with the system -- alliances, war plans and a shared mentality -- rather than with any one government; the question of national guilt is "the wrong question". **Approach:** structural, focused on long-term conditions and collective attitudes (close to arguments about "unspoken assumptions" and "war by timetable"); it plays down individual decision and motive. **Knowledge to support:** the alliance blocs of 1882, 1894, 1904 and 1907; the naval race and army laws of 1912-13; Moroccan and Bosnian crises hardening attitudes; the Schlieffen Plan's dependence on rapid mobilisation; widespread belief in a short war and Social Darwinist ideas. **Knowledge to challenge:** Anglo-German détente and co-operation in the Balkan Wars show the system could restrain conflict; specific choices -- the blank cheque, the ultimatum, Russian mobilisation -- were made by individuals who had alternatives; Fischer's evidence of German aims and Clark's evidence of particular decisions both reject the idea that "none of them could control" events. **Avoid:** treating the comment on war plans or crisis settlements as the main message; they support the overall structural claim. **[40]**

**10.** Indicative content. **Main interpretation:** Russia bore the main responsibility for turning a Balkan dispute into a general war, with France failing to restrain it; German responsibility is acknowledged but minimised. **Approach:** focuses on Russian aims and military decisions, close to work that emphasises Russian responsibility (such as McMeekin's), and on the sequence of decisions in the July crisis rather than structure. **Knowledge to support:** Russian interest in the Straits and in Balkan influence; Russian assurances to Serbia; the decision for pre-mobilisation measures on 25-26 July; general mobilisation on 30 July before Germany mobilised; Poincaré's visit of 20-23 July and the absence of French restraint. **Knowledge to challenge:** Germany issued the blank cheque knowing a strike on Serbia might bring in Russia, and urged Vienna to act fast; the Austrian ultimatum was designed to be rejected; German war plans required an attack on France and Belgium whatever Russia intended, which brought in Britain; Fischer, Röhl and Mombauer place the main responsibility on Berlin. **Avoid:** writing mainly about France, which is a sub-message. **[40]**

**11.** Indicative content. **Main interpretation:** Germany's ruling elites deliberately accepted war in 1914 to solve domestic political problems and preserve their power against socialism; diplomacy is "the surface of the story". **Approach:** the primacy of domestic politics -- explaining foreign policy by social and political pressures at home, an approach associated with Fischer's *War of Illusions* and with Wehler and Berghahn; it reflects interest in social structure and the ruling class, often from a left-liberal standpoint. **Knowledge to support:** the 1912 Reichstag elections made the SPD the largest party; Weltpolitik and the navy had been used to rally nationalist support; the War Council of December 1912; the political truce (*Burgfrieden*) of August 1914, when the SPD voted for war credits, showed war could unite the nation. **Knowledge to challenge:** the documents of July show leaders preoccupied with Russia's growing military power and with Austria-Hungary's survival, not with domestic politics; domestic pressures existed in every power, including Russia and Austria-Hungary; other powers' decisions (the ultimatum, Russian mobilisation) mattered; critics argue the domestic argument rests on inference rather than direct evidence. **Avoid:** treating the naval and colonial point as the main message. **[40]**
