This set practises key question 2.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What influences our social
identity?* Parts (a) to (f) follow the six-part shape the syllabus describes for Paper 1 Question 2;
parts (g) to (k) give extra practice across the four syllabus content points.

## Questions

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *cultural defence* **[2]**
- (ii) *homogenisation of identities* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of virtual communities. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways age can influence a person's identity in different societies. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** positive impacts of social networks on identity. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the impact of gender on social identity in different societies. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that globalisation is creating a single global culture. **[14]**

### Extra practice (2.3.1 to 2.3.4)

**(g)** Explain **two** ways ageism may affect the identity of older people. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain how consumption patterns can be linked to identity in a "pick and mix" society.
**[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** criticisms of the view that social class no longer shapes identity. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** negative impacts of cultural diversity on identity. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain what is meant by a *digital identity* and **one** way it may differ from a person's
offline identity. **[4]**

## Answers and mark guidance

**(a)(i) [2]** Strengthening a traditional, national or religious identity (1) when a group feels its
culture is threatened, for example by globalisation or by a dominant outside power (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** The process by which identities in different societies become increasingly similar
(1), for example young people worldwide wearing the same global brands and following the same media
(1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: online gaming communities; fan forums; support groups for people
with a health condition; language-learning communities; hobby groups on social media platforms.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each (identify + explain with reference to variation):

- **Expected behaviour at each life stage** — what is seen as suitable for a "child" or an "adult"
  varies, for example in some societies teenagers take on paid work and marriage early, while in
  others long education extends youth into the twenties.
- **Status of older people** — in some cultures elders are respected as wise and hold authority in the
  family; in others they are stereotyped as dependent, which can lower their self-esteem.
- **Generational identity** — people of the same age share experiences, music and technology, which
  shapes a sense of belonging to a generation.
- **Legal age markers** — ages for voting, driving or leaving school differ between countries and give
  formal recognition of adulthood.

**(d) [6]** Three impacts, 2 marks each:

- **Self-expression** — people can explore and present aspects of identity, such as creative work or
  beliefs, that they may not share offline.
- **Support and belonging** — minorities or people with rare interests or conditions can find others
  like them, strengthening identity.
- **Maintaining family ties** — migrants and their families keep in contact across distance,
  sustaining cultural identity.
- **Activism** — social networks give a voice to groups that are under-represented in traditional
  media.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 a clear, developed discussion with variation between societies and some
evaluation; 4–6 some explanation; 1–3 basic points.

- Gender identity is **learned** through socialisation, e.g. canalisation (Oakley, 1972), so it differs
  between societies.
- Gender shapes **appearance, interests and emotional expression**, such as norms that men should be
  tough and women caring.
- It shapes **roles and opportunities** — expectations about paid work, domestic labour and education
  differ widely between societies.
- **Change:** in many societies women's education and employment have grown and norms of masculinity
  are broadening, though domestic expectations change more slowly.
- **Evaluation:** gender intersects with class, ethnicity and religion; some people resist or
  redefine gender norms, so gender is an influence rather than a fixed outcome.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed and evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

- *For:* global brands, fast food and platforms spread similar products and habits (Ritzer's
  "McDonaldization", 1993); global media and streaming give young people shared music, films and
  fashions; English spreads as a global language; global youth culture links teenagers across
  continents.
- *Against:* **hybrid identities** — people mix global and local culture rather than replacing one with
  the other; **cultural defence** — religion and tradition may be strengthened in response
  (Bruce); global culture is not only Western, as the worldwide success of South Korean pop music
  shows; global products are adapted to local tastes; class and income limit access to global culture.
- *Conclusion:* globalisation is spreading shared elements, but the outcome is more often a mix of
  global and local identities than one single culture. Credit a reasoned judgement.

**(g) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each:

- **Lower self-esteem** — being stereotyped as slow, dependent or out of date may lead older people to
  see themselves that way (1 + 1).
- **Exclusion** — age discrimination in work or in the design of technology may reduce older people's
  participation, weakening a working or active identity (1 + 1).
- **Resistance** — some older people reject the stereotype, building identities around activity,
  learning or volunteering (1 + 1).

**(h) [4]** Postmodernists argue that people build identity from what they buy and do (1) rather than
inheriting it from class or religion (1). For example, choosing particular brands, music, diets or
fitness activities signals who a person is (1), and people can change these choices, so identity is
fluid and chosen (1).

**(i) [4]** Two criticisms, 2 marks each:

- **Life chances** — income and wealth still shape education, health and housing, which in turn shape
  how people see themselves (1 + 1).
- **Cultural capital** — tastes, accent and confidence passed on in families continue to mark class
  differences (Bourdieu) (1 + 1).
- **Limits on choice** — the "pick and mix" lifestyle depends on money, so poorer people have less
  freedom to choose an identity (1 + 1).

**(j) [4]** Two impacts, 2 marks each:

- **Prejudice and conflict** — contact between groups can produce stereotyping and hostility, leading
  some people to feel rejected (1 + 1).
- **Feeling threatened** — majority or minority groups may feel their culture is being lost, which can
  lead to defensive or exclusive identities (1 + 1).
- **Pressure on young people** — children of migrants may feel torn between their family's culture and
  that of their peers (1 + 1).

**(k) [4]** A **digital identity** is the identity a person creates and presents online (1), through
profiles, posts, images and interactions (1). It may differ because people can **edit and select**
what they show, presenting an idealised or experimental self (1), or because anonymity allows them to
express aspects of identity they hide offline (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Must I refer to "different societies" in (c) and (e)?** Yes — the syllabus asks for the impact of
each aspect "in different societies", so show variation between them.

**Is Westernisation the same as homogenisation?** It is one form of it; homogenisation can also spread
non-Western culture.
