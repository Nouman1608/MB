## Questions

**1.** State **three** factors that would increase the power of suppliers over a business. **[3]**

**2.** Tidewick Ferries runs car ferries across a sea strait. Explain what is meant by "substitute threat", using an example from Tidewick's industry. **[2]**

**3.** Last year 4.8 million passengers crossed the strait by ferry. Tidewick carried 1.68 million. The next three operators carried 1.2 million, 0.72 million and 0.48 million.

**(a)** Calculate Tidewick's market share. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the four firm concentration ratio. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why rivalry could still be intense despite your answer to (b). **[2]**

**4.** Pellwick Flour Mills sells flour at £520 per tonne. The wheat used in one tonne of flour costs £310. Wheat suppliers raise prices by 12%. Pellwick produces 24,000 tonnes a year and last year made a profit of £1.5 million.

**(a)** Calculate the rise in Pellwick's annual wheat cost. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate this rise as a percentage of last year's profit. **[2]**

**5.** Kiverton Components makes seat frames for car makers. Its sales last year were £64 million. Its largest customer bought £20.8 million and its three largest customers together bought £44.8 million.

**(a)** Calculate the share of Kiverton's sales taken by its largest customer and by its three largest customers. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **two** ways Kiverton might reduce the power of its buyers. **[4]**

**6.** Thistlemoor Fitness plans to open a boutique fitness studio. Explain **two** influences on the strength of the entry threat in the boutique fitness industry. **[4]**

**7.** Using question 4, analyse how the rise in wheat prices might affect **two** functional areas of Pellwick Flour Mills. **[6]**

**8.** Brindlecote Cabs is a radio taxi firm in a market town. A ride-hailing app has launched in the town with fares about 20% below Brindlecote's. Analyse **two** ways Brindlecote might respond to this change in the forces it faces. **[8]**

**9.** Thistlemoor Fitness has gathered this information about boutique fitness in its city:

- 14 studios operate; none has more than 10% of the market. The market is growing by about 12% a year.
- Fitting out a studio costs about £60,000, and short leases are easy to find.
- Members pay monthly and can leave with one month's notice.
- Skilled instructors are in short supply and work freelance for several studios.
- Large gym chains charge lower monthly fees; home workout apps cost even less.

Using Porter's five forces, evaluate whether boutique fitness in the city is an attractive industry for Thistlemoor to enter. **[12]**

**10.** Calderine Paints sells decorative paint. Total sales are £18 million and profit is £1.6 million. Two DIY retail chains that together buy £7.2 million of Calderine's paint are merging. The merged chain has asked for a 6% price cut on everything it buys from Calderine.

**(a)** Calculate the share of Calderine's sales going to the merged chain, and the cost of the price cut as a percentage of profit. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Calderine should accept the price cut. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three: few suppliers of the input [1]; the input is unique or has no substitute [1]; switching to another supplier is costly [1]. Also creditworthy: the business is a minor customer for the supplier; the supplier could enter the business's industry itself. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "State" questions reward brief, separate points; three versions of "few suppliers" tend to gain credit only once.

**2.** Substitute threat is the risk that customers meet the same need with a different kind of product [1], such as crossing the strait by air or through a new tunnel or bridge rather than by ferry [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A rival ferry operator is not a substitute; using one as the example is likely to lose the application mark.

**3. (a)** 1.68 ÷ 4.8 × 100 [1] = **35%** [1].
**(b)** (1.68 + 1.2 + 0.72 + 0.48) ÷ 4.8 × 100 [1] = **85%** [1].
**(c)** Ferry crossings are a fairly standard service, so customers choose mainly on price [1]; ferries have high fixed costs, so operators cut fares to fill spare capacity [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A high concentration ratio shows few large firms, not low rivalry; answers that treat it as proof either way tend to lose the explanation marks.

**4. (a)** New wheat cost = 310 × 1.12 = £347.20 [1]; rise per tonne = £37.20 [1]; annual rise = 37.20 × 24,000 = **£892,800** [1].
**(b)** 892,800 ÷ 1,500,000 × 100 [1] = **59.5%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each stage of working; a wrong final figure with a clear method can still earn the method credit.

**5. (a)** Largest customer: 20.8 ÷ 64 × 100 = **32.5%** [1]. Top three: 44.8 ÷ 64 × 100 [1] = **70%** [1].
**(b)** Win customers in other industries, such as bus or rail seat makers [1], so no single car maker can threaten a large share of sales and Kiverton can resist price cuts [1]. Design frames around Kiverton's own patented fittings [1], which raises the cost to car makers of switching supplier and so weakens their bargaining power [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Each "way" needs an action and a reason it weakens buyer power; naming the action alone gains about half the credit.

**6.** Set-up costs are low: about £60,000 to fit out, with easy short leases [1], so many new studios can open, which makes the entry threat strong [1]. Customer loyalty is weak: members can leave on one month's notice [1], so a newcomer can win members quickly, again strengthening the threat [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Influences gain more credit when tied to the barriers to entry in the specification and to evidence in the case.

**7.** Finance: costs rise by £892,800, cutting profit by 59.5% [1]. This reduces retained profit, so Pellwick may delay investment in new milling equipment [1], and lower profit may make lenders less willing to lend [1]. Marketing: Pellwick may need to raise flour prices by £37.20 per tonne (7.2%) to protect its margin [1]. Its buyers, bakeries and supermarkets, may switch to rival mills if flour is a standard product [1], so marketing may focus on long-term contracts or quality to justify the price [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis credit comes from a chain of cause and effect using case figures; two separate one-line statements gain limited credit.

**8.** The app is a new entrant that increases rivalry and offers customers a cheaper option [1]. Response 1: cut fares to match [1]. This keeps price-sensitive customers [1], but with fares 20% lower Brindlecote's margin may disappear, and the app may be able to absorb losses for longer [1]. Response 2: target segments the app serves poorly, such as pre-booked airport runs, school contracts and older customers who phone rather than use apps [1]. These customers value reliability and a known driver more than price [1], so Brindlecote faces less direct rivalry [1]. However, the segment may be too small to replace lost volume, so Brindlecote may also need its own booking app [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Each response should have a consequence and a limitation; one-sided responses tend to stay in the lower analysis levels.

**9.** Rivalry is high: 14 studios with none above 10% means many similar firms [1], although 12% market growth eases rivalry because firms can grow without taking members from each other [1]. Entry threat is high: £60,000 set-up cost and short leases are low barriers [1], so even if Thistlemoor succeeds, new studios can copy it quickly [1]. Buyer power is fairly high: members can leave on one month's notice [1], so prices must stay competitive [1]. Supplier power is high for instructors: skilled instructors are scarce and freelance [1], so they can demand higher fees, raising Thistlemoor's main cost [1]. Substitute threat is high: gym chains and apps meet the same fitness need more cheaply [1]. Judgement: four of the five forces are strong, so margins are likely to be thin [1]. Growth is the main attraction, and the low entry cost limits Thistlemoor's risk if it fails [1]. The industry is attractive only if Thistlemoor can differentiate, for example by tying top instructors to exclusive contracts; otherwise it is unattractive [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers rate each force with case evidence and finish with a judgement on overall attractiveness; five descriptions with no conclusion tend to be held back.

**10. (a)** 7.2 ÷ 18 × 100 = **40%** [1]. Cost of cut = 7.2 × 0.06 = £0.432 million [1]. 0.432 ÷ 1.6 × 100 [1] = **27%** of profit [1].
**(b)** The merger raises buyer power: one buyer will take 40% of sales [1], so losing it would remove a large share of volume and contribution [1]. For accepting: Calderine keeps its biggest outlet and protects factory utilisation [1], and the 27% fall in profit may be smaller than the loss if the chain stocked a rival brand [1]. Against: accepting signals weakness, and the chain may demand further cuts in future [1]. Rivals may face the same demand, but if they refuse, the chain may still need Calderine's paint [1]. Alternatives: build sales to trade decorators and online [1], which spreads buyer power over many smaller customers [1]; or strengthen the brand so shoppers expect to find Calderine on the shelf [1], which weakens the chain's ability to delist it [1]. Judgement: Calderine should negotiate rather than accept 6% outright, perhaps a smaller cut tied to a longer contract [1], while growing other channels so that within a few years no buyer takes such a large share; the decision depends on how easily the chain could switch to a rival brand [1]. **[16]**
*Examiner insight:* In evaluation questions, a supported recommendation that uses the calculated figures and says what it depends on tends to gain more credit than a balanced list.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Rating a force without evidence from the case.
- Using a rival product as an example of a substitute.
- Treating the concentration ratio as proof of high or low rivalry on its own.
- Forgetting to multiply a per-unit cost change by annual volume.
- Dividing by sales instead of profit when asked for a percentage of profit.
- Naming a way to influence a force without explaining why it weakens that force.
- Analysing the firm's internal strengths instead of the industry's structure.
- Ending a five forces evaluation without a view on overall attractiveness or a clear recommendation.
