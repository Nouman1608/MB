## Structure terms

An **organisation structure** shows how a business is arranged: who does what, who reports to whom and how decisions flow.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Line relationship | Direct reporting link between a manager and the staff below | A shop manager and the sales assistants |
| Staff relationship | Advice or support link from a specialist who has no direct authority over line staff | An HR adviser advising the shop manager |
| Chain of command | The route of authority from top to bottom | Director, regional manager, shop manager, assistant |
| Span of control | Number of people a manager directly supervises | A supervisor with 8 staff |
| Authority | The right to make decisions and give instructions | A manager approving overtime |
| Responsibility | The duty to carry out a task and answer for the result | A supervisor accountable for the weekly rota |
| Centralisation | Decisions taken at the top | Head office sets all prices |
| Decentralisation | Decisions passed down to lower levels | Shop managers set local prices |
| Delegation | Passing a task, with the authority to do it, to a subordinate, while the manager stays accountable | A manager asks an assistant manager to run the stock count |
| Delayering | Removing one or more levels of management | Removing middle managers between directors and supervisors |

Two points are often confused. **Authority can be delegated; ultimate accountability cannot.** And **delegation** is the passing down of a task for one occasion, whereas **decentralisation** is a standing policy of taking decisions lower down.

## Models of structure

- **Hierarchy.** A pyramid with a chain of command from top to bottom. Clear lines of authority, but slow communication and little flexibility.
- **By function.** Departments by activity, such as finance, marketing, operations and HR. It builds expertise and avoids duplication, but departments can focus on their own goals and work poorly across departments.
- **By product.** Teams or divisions for each product or product line. Each can focus on its own market, but resources may be duplicated and standards may differ.
- **Division (by region or business unit).** Semi-independent units, often by region or brand, each with its own managers and sometimes its own functions. It suits large, varied businesses and gives local responsiveness, but can duplicate costs and weaken central control.
- **Matrix.** Staff report to both a functional manager and a project or product manager. It makes good use of specialists and encourages teamwork, but staff have two bosses, which can cause conflict and confusion.
- **System.** A structure arranged around the business's processes or systems rather than around departments, so that work flows across functions to the customer. The specification lists it without further description, so use your teacher's definition and the case.

The best structure depends on size, products, markets, the pace of change and the skills of staff.

## Tall and flat structures

A **tall** structure has many layers and a narrow span of control. A **flat** structure has few layers and a wide span.

| | Tall | Flat |
|---|---|---|
| Span of control | Narrow (for example 3 to 5) | Wide (for example 8 to 12) |
| Layers | Many | Few |
| Communication | Slow; messages may be distorted | Faster, more direct |
| Supervision | Close | Looser; staff have more freedom |
| Promotion | Many steps | Few steps |
| Cost | More managers to pay | Fewer managers |
| Decision making | Often centralised | Often decentralised |

The span of control and the number of layers are linked: if you hold the number of employees fixed, a wider span means fewer layers.

### Worked example 1: span of control and delayering

Hartwell Insurance (invented) has 240 front-line staff. The current structure has a span of control of 6 at every level. The directors propose widening the span to 10.

With a span of 6:

- first-line managers needed = 240 ÷ 6 = 40;
- managers above them = 40 ÷ 6 = 6.67, so 7;
- next level = 7 ÷ 6 = 1.17, so 2;
- top = 1.

Total managers = 40 + 7 + 2 + 1 = **50**, in **4** layers.

With a span of 10:

- 240 ÷ 10 = 24;
- 24 ÷ 10 = 2.4, so 3;
- top = 1.

Total managers = 24 + 3 + 1 = **28**, in **3** layers.

Delayering removes one layer and **22** management posts. If each manager costs £45,000 a year, the saving is 22 × £45,000 = **£990,000**. Redundancy costs of £25,000 a post are 22 × £25,000 = £550,000, so the redundancy costs are recovered in £550,000 ÷ £990,000 = **0.56 years**, about 7 months.

Evaluation:

- The saving is large, but it assumes that front-line staff can be supervised just as well by a manager with 10 people as by one with 6. If supervision falls, errors, absence and complaints may rise.
- Decisions that used to be made by middle managers pass to staff or higher up. Without training, this could slow decisions.
- Communication may be faster, as there are fewer layers. Staff may feel more trusted and motivated, or overworked.
- Redundancy damages morale among the survivors, and the business may lose experience.
- The promotion route shortens, which may disappoint ambitious staff.

**Judgement.** Delayering is likely to be worthwhile for Hartwell if the work is routine and staff are experienced, and if the redundancies are managed fairly. It would be risky where tasks are complex and need close guidance. It should be introduced with training and a review of results.

## Evaluating structures

Consider the following when asked to judge a structure.

1. **Fit with size and activity.** A small firm needs few layers; a large multi-product firm may need divisions.
2. **Speed of communication and decisions.** Fewer layers mean faster flow.
3. **Cost.** More managers means higher wage cost.
4. **Control.** Centralised, tall structures give tight control; flat, decentralised ones give flexibility.
5. **Staff.** Experienced staff do well with delegation; new staff need guidance. Motivation is affected by trust and promotion routes.
6. **Customers.** Structures that bring decisions close to the customer give faster service.
7. **Change.** Restructuring costs money and causes uncertainty.

Stakeholders: owners want efficiency and control; managers care about status and promotion; employees about job security, workload and say; customers about speed and quality; suppliers about who makes decisions.

## Organisational culture

**Organisational culture** is the shared values, beliefs, attitudes and habits that shape "how things are done here". It shows in how people dress, speak, treat customers, take risks, handle mistakes and make decisions.

Factors affecting culture:

- **Leadership.** The values and behaviour of the founders and senior managers set an example.
- **History and age.** Long-established firms often have settled traditions.
- **Size and structure.** A small firm tends to be informal; a large hierarchy more formal.
- **The type of business and sector.** A hospital values safety and procedure; a design studio values creativity.
- **Staff.** Who is recruited, their ages, backgrounds and attitudes.
- **Reward and recognition systems.** What is rewarded is what is repeated.
- **Technology and working methods.** Remote working or shift work change how people relate.
- **The external environment.** Law, competition and national culture.
- **Mergers and takeovers.** Different cultures can clash.

Impact and importance. A strong, positive culture can raise motivation, loyalty and productivity, reduce turnover and attract good staff. It can support the strategy (for example a culture of service in a hotel). A poor culture can cause conflict, absence, resistance to change and bad customer service. Culture is hard to change, because it develops over years and is held by people. When a business changes its structure, for example by delayering, the culture may need to change as well.

Writers such as Charles Handy describe types of culture (power, role, task and person). The specification does not name them, so use them only if you can apply them to the case.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a long list of terms as a definition of a structure. Show how the terms combine in the case.
- Confusing delegation with decentralisation.
- Assuming flat is always better. It depends on the staff, tasks and need for control.
- Calculating managers without rounding up. Each level needs a whole number of managers.
- Writing about culture without linking it to a factor or an effect on performance.

## Exam technique

For "distinguish", use "whereas" and give both sides. For calculations on span and layers, show the division at each level and round up. For evaluation, weigh at least two stakeholder effects, then make a judgement tied to the case.
