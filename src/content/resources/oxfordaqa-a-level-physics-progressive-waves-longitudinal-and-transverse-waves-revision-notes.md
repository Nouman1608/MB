## Key definitions

- **Progressive wave:** a wave that carries energy onward while the medium itself stays in place.
- **Displacement:** distance of a particle from its equilibrium position, in a stated direction.
- **Amplitude (A):** maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.
- **Wavelength (λ):** distance between two adjacent points oscillating in phase.
- **Period (T):** time for one complete oscillation of a particle.
- **Frequency (f):** number of complete oscillations per second, in hertz.
- **Wave speed (c):** distance moved by the wave pattern per second.
- **Phase difference:** the gap in stage between two oscillations, measured as an angle or a fraction of a cycle.
- **In phase:** phase difference of 0, 2π, 4π and so on. **Antiphase:** phase difference of π, 3π and so on.

## Equations

| Equation | Use |
|---|---|
| f = 1/T | Frequency and period |
| c = fλ | Wave speed, frequency and wavelength (any wave) |
| phase difference (cycles) = separation / λ | Two points on the same wave at the same instant |
| phase difference (rad) = 2π × separation / λ | The same, in radians |
| phase difference (cycles) = Δt / T | One point at two instants |
| depth = (speed × delay) / 2 | Ultrasound pulse-echo |

One cycle = 360° = 2π rad. Half a cycle = 180° = π rad. A quarter cycle = 90° = π/2 rad.

## Method: phase difference in four steps

1. Convert the separation (or time gap) to metres (or seconds).
2. Divide by λ (or by T) to get a fraction of a cycle.
3. Multiply by 360 for degrees, or by 2π for radians.
4. State which point lags. The point further along the direction of travel lags.

Worked reminder: points 0.45 m apart on a wave of wavelength 0.60 m.

```
0.45 / 0.60 = 0.75 cycle = 270° = 3π/2 rad
```

## Two graphs, two jobs

| Graph | Read off |
|---|---|
| Displacement against distance (snapshot) | Wavelength, amplitude |
| Displacement against time (one particle) | Period, amplitude |

Frequency comes from 1/T. Speed comes from fλ, or from λ/T using one reading from each graph.

## Which way is a particle moving?

For a transverse wave travelling to the right, a particle just to the right of a crest is on a downward slope. The wave is about to carry the crest to it, so it is moving up. A particle just to the left of a crest is moving down. A particle at a crest or trough is momentarily at rest, and a particle at equilibrium moves fastest. To predict the motion a short time later, slide the whole snapshot a little to the right and see what each particle's new displacement would be.

## Transverse and longitudinal waves

| | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| Oscillation compared with energy travel | At 90° | Along the same line |
| Features | Crests and troughs | Compressions and rarefactions |
| Examples | String waves, all electromagnetic waves | Sound |
| Polarisation | Possible | Not possible |

Must-know details:

- Electromagnetic waves are oscillating electric and magnetic fields, perpendicular to each other and to the energy flow. No medium is needed.
- In a vacuum every electromagnetic wave moves at 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Only frequency and wavelength differ.
- Sound is carried by particles, so it cannot cross a vacuum. Air particles oscillate along the direction of travel.
- At the centre of a compression the particles are momentarily moving forwards, with zero displacement.
- Waves on a string: each piece of string moves at right angles to the string's length while the pulse moves along it.

## Ultrasound in medicine

- Ultrasound is sound above the range of human hearing, above about 20 kHz. Imaging uses a few megahertz.
- A probe emits short pulses and detects reflections from boundaries between different tissues.
- Gel between probe and skin removes air. Without it most of the ultrasound would be reflected at the air-skin boundary.
- The time delay of each echo gives the depth of the boundary: depth = (speed × delay) / 2. The factor of 2 is there because the pulse travels there and back.
- Higher frequency gives shorter wavelength and finer detail but weaker penetration, because tissue absorbs it more.

Worked reminder: an echo returns 84 μs after the pulse, with speed 1540 m s⁻¹ in tissue.

```
depth = 1540 × 84 × 10⁻⁶ / 2 = 0.0647 m = 6.5 cm
```

## Polarisation

- **Plane-polarised:** oscillation (displacement or electric field) confined to a single plane that contains the direction of travel.
- A **Polaroid** transmits the component of the oscillation along its transmission axis and absorbs the perpendicular component.
- Two Polaroids with axes parallel pass the light. With axes at 90° (crossed) almost no light passes.
- Rotating the second Polaroid through 360° gives two maxima and two minima.
- Only transverse waves can be polarised. This is the evidence that light and radio waves are transverse. Sound cannot be polarised.
- **Aerials:** a rod aerial responds to the component of the electric field along its length. Align the receiving aerial parallel to the transmitting one for the strongest signal. At 90° the signal is nearly zero.

Malus's law is not expected, so you do not need to calculate transmitted intensity from the angle.

## Must-know distinctions

- Frequency (set by the source, unchanged when the wave enters a new medium) against wavelength and speed (change with the medium).
- Particle speed (changing as the particle oscillates) against wave speed (constant in a given medium).
- Period (one particle, time) against wavelength (the wave pattern, distance).
- Unpolarised (many directions) against plane-polarised (one direction).
- Reduced intensity against polarised. A Polaroid absorbs part of unpolarised light, and the light that gets through is plane-polarised.

## Quick self-test

1. The oscillation of a particle in a wave has period 0.025 s. Find its frequency.
2. A sound wave has frequency 1.2 kHz and wavelength 0.28 m. Find the speed.
3. Two points 0.45 m apart lie on a wave of wavelength 0.60 m. State the phase difference in degrees and radians.
4. A particle has period 16 ms. Find the phase difference between its motion at two instants 6.0 ms apart, in degrees and radians.
5. An echo returns 68 μs after a pulse, and sound travels at 1540 m s⁻¹ in the tissue. Find the depth of the reflecting boundary.
6. A wireless router transmits at 2.4 GHz. Find the wavelength in a vacuum, taking c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
7. A radio pulse from a lunar orbiter is received 3.9 × 10⁸ m away. Find the travel time.
8. Sound, microwaves, ultrasound and X-rays. Which can be plane-polarised?
9. State the direction of the oscillation of air particles in a sound wave relative to the direction of travel.
10. A transmitting aerial is horizontal. How should the receiving rod aerial be oriented for the strongest signal, and what happens at 90° to this?

### Answers

1. f = 1/0.025 = **40 Hz**.
2. c = fλ = 1200 × 0.28 = **336 m s⁻¹**.
3. 0.45/0.60 = 0.75 cycle: **270°, or 3π/2 rad (4.71 rad)**.
4. 6.0/16 = 0.375 cycle: **135°, or 3π/4 rad (2.36 rad)**.
5. d = 1540 × 68 × 10⁻⁶ / 2 = **0.052 m (5.2 cm)**.
6. λ = c/f = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 2.4 × 10⁹ = **0.125 m**.
7. t = d/c = 3.9 × 10⁸ / 3.00 × 10⁸ = **1.3 s**.
8. **Microwaves and X-rays** (transverse). Sound and ultrasound are longitudinal.
9. **Parallel** to the direction of travel (along the same line).
10. **Horizontal**, parallel to the transmitter. At 90° the signal is close to zero, because the rod is at right angles to the electric field oscillation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that the particles "move along with the wave" instead of oscillating about fixed positions.
- Defining wavelength as "the length of a wave" without saying it is the distance between adjacent points in phase.
- Using the whole separation without dividing by λ, or forgetting to say which way round a phase difference goes.
- Mixing up the graphs: reading T from a displacement-distance graph.
- Leaving out the factor of 2 in ultrasound depth calculations.
- Not converting MHz, GHz, μs or nm before substituting.
- Saying polarisation shows light is "a wave" rather than a transverse wave.
- Describing a longitudinal wave with a drawing of crests and troughs. Use compressions and rarefactions.
- Describing polarisation without mentioning the transmission axis or the direction of oscillation.
