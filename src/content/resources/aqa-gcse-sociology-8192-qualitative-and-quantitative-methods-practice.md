These questions cover the "qualitative and quantitative methods" row of section 3.7 in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification: questionnaires, interviews, observations and mixed methods. Methods can be assessed in Paper 1 or Paper 2. All people, places and data are fictional.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** In which type of interview are the same questions asked in the same order to every respondent?

A. Unstructured
B. Semi-structured
C. Structured
D. Focus group

**[1]**

**2.** A researcher joins a youth football club as a volunteer coach without telling anyone she is studying them. This is:

A. Overt participant observation
B. Covert participant observation
C. Overt non-participant observation
D. Covert non-participant observation

**[1]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a leading question, using an example. **[2]**

**4.** Define the term "going native". **[2]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** reason why unstructured interviews produce valid data. **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** weakness of using closed questions to study attitudes to marriage. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** A researcher sent an online questionnaire about leisure to 800 residents of Marsh End (a fictional town). The data are fictional.

| Age group | Questionnaires sent | Questionnaires returned |
|---|---|---|
| 16 to 39 | 400 | 60 |
| 40 and over | 400 | 140 |

**(a)** Calculate the overall response rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how the pattern of returns could affect the representativeness of the findings. **[3]**

**8.** A sociologist wants to study how teachers talk to boys and girls in primary school classrooms. Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of using non-participant observation for this research. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which the interviewer effect could affect a study of young people's experiences of being stopped by the police. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why a researcher might use mixed methods to study why some pupils are excluded from school. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of participant observation for studying pupil subcultures in schools. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far questionnaires are more useful than interviews for studying the division of housework in families. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** C. Structured [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* fixed questions in a fixed order mark a structured interview; it produces mainly quantitative data.

**2.** B. Covert participant observation [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* she joins in (participant) and keeps the research secret (covert).

**3.** A question worded so that it suggests the answer the researcher expects [1], for example "Don't you agree that parents should do more to stop truancy?" [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the example must actually push towards one answer; a neutral question earns nothing.

**4.** When a researcher becomes so involved with the group being studied [1] that they lose objectivity and start to see things only from the group's point of view [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* link it to participant observation, where long involvement makes it most likely.

**5.** Unstructured interviews allow the interviewer to build rapport with the respondent [1]. This trust encourages people to talk openly and explain their views in their own words [1], so the data are more likely to give a true picture of their experiences rather than answers squeezed into the researcher's categories [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "valid" must be explained as giving a true picture, not just stated.

**6.** Closed questions force respondents to choose from fixed options [1], such as "agree" or "disagree" with "marriage is important" [1]. This hides the reasons and the mixed feelings people have about marriage, so the data may lack validity even if they are easy to count [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* apply the weakness to marriage; a generic weakness scores less.

**7. (a)** 60 + 140 = 200 returned; 200 ÷ 800 × 100 = 25% [1]
**(b)** Only 15% of 16 to 39-year-olds returned the questionnaire (60 ÷ 400), compared with 35% of those aged 40 and over (140 ÷ 400) [1]. Older residents are therefore over-represented among the replies [1]. If younger people have different leisure habits, the findings will mainly reflect older people's views and cannot be generalised to the whole town [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* calculate group response rates; the overall rate alone does not show the bias.

**8.** Advantage: the researcher records what teachers actually say and do [1], which may differ from what they would report in an interview, so the data on how boys and girls are treated are more valid [1]. Disadvantage: teachers who know they are being watched may change their behaviour, for example treating boys and girls more equally than usual [1], so the observer effect reduces validity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* a checklist of behaviours is a useful detail that also raises reliability.

**9.** First, an interviewer who looks like a police officer or authority figure, for example older and formally dressed [1], may make young people wary, so they may play down or hide their experiences [1]. Second, the interviewer's ethnicity could matter [1]: young people may be more willing to describe experiences they see as racially unfair to an interviewer they think will understand them [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* name a specific characteristic each time and say how it changes the answers.

**10.** First, a questionnaire to pupils or school records could show how common exclusion is in different groups [1], giving quantitative data on patterns by gender, class or ethnicity [1]. Second, unstructured interviews with excluded pupils could explain the reasons behind those patterns [1], giving qualitative depth about their experiences, and the two sets of findings could be triangulated [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the reasons must show why combining methods helps, not just describe two methods.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Participant observation means the researcher joins the group being studied [1].
- Pupil subcultures, such as anti-school groups, involve behaviour pupils may hide from adults [1].
- It can give high validity, recording what pupils actually do and say in context [1].
- Willis's study of the lads used observation and participant observation to see school from the pupils' view [1].
- Ball's three years at Beachside allowed him to see how banding shaped attitudes [1].
- The researcher may discover issues they had not thought to ask about [1].
- Against: an adult cannot easily join a pupil group, so covert observation is rarely possible [1].
- In overt observation, pupils may behave differently: the observer effect [1].
- Access needs the head teacher's agreement, and parental consent is normally needed for pupils [1].
- Time and cost are high; studies of one school have low reliability and representativeness [1].
- Ethical risks: recording rule-breaking raises confidentiality and harm issues [1].
- Judgement: it is highly useful for understanding meanings and behaviour within subcultures, but is best combined with other methods to judge how widespread they are [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "evaluate" means weighing usefulness, not just listing strengths and weaknesses; finish with a reasoned verdict.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Questionnaires can reach a large sample and give comparable data on who does which tasks [1].
- Closed questions make it easy to calculate patterns, such as hours of housework by gender [1].
- They are reliable because every respondent answers the same questions [1].
- Anonymity may encourage honesty about private family life [1].
- Against: respondents may give socially desirable answers, such as exaggerating men's share [1].
- Partners may disagree about who does what, and a questionnaire cannot explore this [1].
- Closed questions impose the researcher's categories and miss meanings, such as who takes responsibility for organising tasks [1].
- Interviews allow probing and clarification, increasing validity [1].
- Young and Willmott's large survey of nearly 2,000 people used interviews to study the symmetrical family [1].
- Interviews cost more and the interviewer effect may influence answers [1].
- Mixed methods could combine questionnaire patterns with interview explanations [1].
- Judgement: questionnaires are more useful for measuring how common unequal housework is, but interviews are more useful for understanding why, so the answer depends on the research aim [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* compare the two methods directly throughout rather than writing about each in turn.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "interviews" without saying which type.
- Strengths and weaknesses with no link to the topic.
- Calculating an overall response rate but not group rates.
- Confusing the observer effect with going native.
- Listing two methods for mixed methods without explaining the benefit of combining them.
