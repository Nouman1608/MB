## 10.21 Mass spectra: key definitions

- **Molecular ion, M⁺**: the ion formed when a whole molecule loses one electron. M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻.
- **m/z**: mass-to-charge ratio. For a 1+ ion, m/z is simply the ion's mass.
- **Molecular ion peak**: found at the largest m/z value, if you ignore the tiny M+1 peak from carbon-13. Its m/z gives Mr.
- **Fragmentation**: M⁺ splits into a positive ion and a radical. M⁺ → X⁺ + Y•. Only X⁺ is detected.
- **Base peak**: the tallest peak, given 100% relative abundance. It is often a fragment.

### Fragment ions to recognise

| m/z | Ion (or ions) |
|---|---|
| 15 | CH₃⁺ |
| 29 | C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺ |
| 31 | CH₂OH⁺ |
| 43 | C₃H₇⁺ or CH₃CO⁺ |
| 45 | COOH⁺, or CH₃CHOH⁺ from a secondary alcohol |
| 57 | C₄H₉⁺ or C₂H₅CO⁺ |

### Gaps between peaks

| Gap from M⁺ | Radical lost |
|---|---|
| 15 | CH₃• |
| 17 | OH• |
| 29 | C₂H₅• or CHO• |
| 45 | COOH• |

### Halogen patterns

| Halogen | Isotopes | M⁺ pattern |
|---|---|---|
| one Cl | ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl, about 3 : 1 | two peaks 2 apart, heights about 3 : 1 |
| one Br | ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br, about 1 : 1 | two peaks 2 apart, heights about 1 : 1 |

Any fragment that keeps the halogen also shows the pair. A fragment that has lost it is a single peak.

### Method in steps: suggesting a structure from a mass spectrum

1. Find M⁺ (highest m/z, ignoring M+1). Write down Mr.
2. Check for a pair of peaks two units apart (Cl or Br).
3. Use any functional group information (from IR or the question) to write a general formula. Solve for the number of carbons.
4. List the main fragment peaks. For each, work out the ion and the mass lost.
5. Draw the isomers that fit the formula. Ask which bonds each one can break and which ions that would give.
6. Pick the isomer whose expected fragments match. Write "suggest" and give the reason.

**Small worked reminder.** M⁺ = 86 with a peak at 57: 86 − 57 = 29, so C₂H₅• or CHO• has been lost.

## 10.22 Infrared spectra: key ideas

- Bonds absorb IR radiation at the frequency at which they stretch. Each bond type has a typical range.
- The x-axis is wavenumber in cm⁻¹; absorptions are dips in transmittance.
- Hydrogen bonding makes O–H absorptions broad.
- Below about 1500 cm⁻¹ lies the fingerprint region, used to match a whole spectrum to a known compound.

### IR ranges used in these notes

Questions supply wavenumber data; use the values given there. These are the typical values used across this unit's pages:

| Bond and compound type | Range (cm⁻¹) | Look for |
|---|---|---|
| C–H, alkanes | 2962–2853 | present in almost everything |
| =C–H, alkenes | 3095–3010 | just above 3000 |
| C–H, aldehydes | 2900–2820 and 2775–2700 | the 2775–2700 band |
| C=C, alkenes | 1669–1645 | weak to medium |
| O–H, alcohols | 3550–3230 | broad |
| O–H, carboxylic acids | 3300–2500 | very broad |
| C=O, aldehydes | 1740–1720 | strong |
| C=O, ketones | 1720–1700 | strong |
| C=O, carboxylic acids | 1725–1700 | strong |
| C–Cl | 800–600 | low end |
| C–Br | 600–500 | low end |
| C–I | about 500 | low end |
| N–H, amines | 3500–3300 | sharper than O–H |

### Method in steps: reading an IR spectrum

1. Strong peak at 1750–1680 cm⁻¹? Then C=O is present.
2. Very broad 3300–2500 band with C=O? Carboxylic acid.
3. C=O, no O–H, but a peak at 2775–2700? Aldehyde. No such peak? Ketone.
4. Broad 3550–3230 band with no C=O? Alcohol.
5. Peaks at 1669–1645 and 3095–3010? Alkene.
6. Sharper peak at 3500–3300 with no C=O? Consider an amine.
7. Peaks below 800 cm⁻¹ that fit the C–X ranges? Possible halogenoalkane; confirm with a mass spectrum or a silver nitrate test.

**Small worked reminder.** Propan-1-amine, CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂, should show N–H at 3500–3300 and C–H at 2962–2853 cm⁻¹, with no C=O and no broad O–H.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Molecular ion peak vs base peak.** M⁺ is at the highest m/z and gives Mr. The base peak is the tallest and is often a fragment.
- **Alcohol O–H vs acid O–H.** Alcohol: broad, 3550–3230. Acid: very broad, 3300–2500, running into the C–H peaks, and always with C=O.
- **Aldehyde vs ketone.** Both have C=O near 1700–1740. Only the aldehyde has C–H at 2775–2700. In a mass spectrum, CHO⁺ at 29 fits an aldehyde and CH₃CO⁺ at 43 fits a methyl ketone.
- **Chlorine vs bromine.** Both give peaks two apart. Chlorine is about 3 : 1; bromine is about 1 : 1.
- **Primary vs secondary alcohol in a mass spectrum.** CH₂OH⁺ at 31 suggests a primary alcohol. CH₃CHOH⁺ at 45 suggests a secondary alcohol with an OH on carbon 2.
- **IR vs MS.** IR identifies bonds and so functional groups. MS gives Mr and the carbon skeleton. Isomers with the same groups often need MS; isomers with different groups are easier by IR.

## Quick self-test

**1.** Chloromethane, CH₃Cl, is analysed. Give the m/z values of its two molecular ion peaks and their approximate height ratio.

**2.** Give two different ions that could cause a peak at m/z 29.

**3.** An IR spectrum shows a strong peak at 1715 cm⁻¹ and a very broad band from 3300 to 2500 cm⁻¹. Name the type of compound.

**4.** A compound has M⁺ at 86 and a large peak at 57. State the mass lost and suggest what it was.

**5.** Which bond absorbs at 3095–3010 cm⁻¹?

**6.** Explain why the O–H absorption of an alcohol is broad.

**7.** Bromoethane, C₂H₅Br, is analysed. Give the m/z values of its molecular ion peaks and describe their relative heights.

**8.** An IR spectrum shows peaks at 1730 and 2720 cm⁻¹ but nothing broad above 3000 cm⁻¹. Name the type of compound.

**9.** Hexan-1-ol and hexan-2-ol give similar IR spectra. Suggest one mass spectrum peak that would distinguish them.

**10.** Write an equation for the formation of the molecular ion of methanol.

### Answers

**1.** m/z 50 (CH₃³⁵Cl) and m/z 52 (CH₃³⁷Cl), in a ratio of about 3 : 1.

**2.** C₂H₅⁺ and CHO⁺.

**3.** A carboxylic acid (C=O plus the very broad acid O–H).

**4.** 86 − 57 = 29, so C₂H₅• or CHO• was lost.

**5.** The C–H bond in an alkene (=C–H).

**6.** Hydrogen bonding between alcohol molecules means O–H bonds in slightly different surroundings absorb at a spread of wavenumbers.

**7.** m/z 108 (C₂H₅⁷⁹Br) and m/z 110 (C₂H₅⁸¹Br), of almost equal height.

**8.** An aldehyde: C=O at 1730 and aldehyde C–H at 2720, with no O–H.

**9.** Hexan-1-ol can give CH₂OH⁺ at m/z 31; hexan-2-ol can give CH₃CHOH⁺ at m/z 45.

**10.** CH₃OH(g) → CH₃OH⁺(g) + e⁻

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the tallest peak the molecular ion when it is the base peak.
- Giving a fragment with no positive charge, or giving the radical a charge.
- Writing a fragmentation equation that does not balance in mass. The ion plus the radical must add up to M⁺.
- Stating "a peak at 1700 shows a ketone" without ruling out an aldehyde (2775–2700) and an acid (3300–2500).
- Confusing the alcohol O–H range with the acid O–H range.
- Quoting a wavenumber without naming the bond responsible, or naming a functional group without quoting the evidence.
- Missing the two-unit pair that shows Cl or Br, or swapping the 3 : 1 and 1 : 1 ratios.
- Giving one structure when the data fit more than one isomer, instead of saying which isomers fit and what would separate them.
