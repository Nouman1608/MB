These questions practise key question 4.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What is the role of the family for the individual and society?* Section A mirrors the short parts (a) and (b) of a Paper 2 question; Section B mirrors parts (c) to (f).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *warm bath theory*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *dual burden*. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** of the functions of the family identified by Murdock. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** ways in which Marxists argue the family benefits capitalism. **[2]**

**5.** Define the term *segregated conjugal roles*. **[2]**

**6.** Give **two** examples of gendered socialisation within the family. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain **three** functions the family has lost, or shared, with other institutions since industrialisation. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **three** ways in which feminists argue the family benefits men. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **three** limitations of the Marxist view of the family. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss the functionalist view that the nuclear family is important for modern industrial society.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss how the family may act as an agent of ideological control.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that family life is mainly positive for its members.

Your answer should include:
- at least three arguments for the view and three arguments against the view
- a conclusion. **[14]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** Parsons' idea that the family stabilises adult personalities (1) by giving stressed workers, traditionally the husband, a place to relax and release tension, so that it does not disrupt society (1).

**2. [2]** When a woman does paid work (1) and also carries out most of the unpaid housework and childcare at home (1).

**3. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: sexual; reproductive; economic; educational (socialisation).

**4. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: passing on wealth through inheritance, reproducing class inequality; acting as a unit of consumption; socialising children to accept authority (ideological control); reproducing the workforce through women's unpaid labour; acting as a refuge that makes alienating work bearable.

**5. [2]** Conjugal roles in which husband and wife have clearly separate tasks (1), for example a male breadwinner and a female housewife, and usually separate leisure activities (1). Credit Bott (1957) as a source.

**6. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: buying gender-typed toys (canalisation); praising girls for being gentle and boys for being tough (manipulation); giving girls more household chores; using different language, such as "brave boy" or "pretty girl"; parents modelling traditional roles.

### Section B

**7. [6]** Three functions, 1 for identifying and 1 for developing each:
- **Production (economic)**: before industrialisation the family worked together on farms or in workshops; factories and offices took over production, so the family became a unit of consumption.
- **Education**: compulsory schooling now provides formal education and secondary socialisation, though families still support learning.
- **Health care**: hospitals and doctors provide care once given at home.
- **Welfare**: state pensions and benefits reduce reliance on kin for support in old age, sickness or unemployment.
- **Social control and religion**: police, courts and other institutions share functions once centred on the family.
Credit Parsons' view that the family became specialised, or Fletcher's counter-argument, as development.

**8. [6]** Three ways, 1 + 1 each:
- **Unpaid domestic labour**: women do most housework and childcare, freeing men's time for paid work and leisure (Oakley).
- **Dual burden and triple shift**: women in paid work still do most domestic and emotion work (Duncombe and Marsden).
- **Decision-making power**: men tend to dominate major decisions such as moving house (Edgell, 1980).
- **Domestic violence and abuse**: violence is used to assert male authority and control (Dobash and Dobash, 1979).
- **Gendered socialisation**: children learn that women care and men lead, reproducing patriarchy in the next generation.

**9. [6]** Three limitations, 1 + 1 each:
- **Ignores positive aspects**: families give love, care and support, not only service to capitalism.
- **Ignores diversity**: it assumes one family type serving the ruling class, overlooking lone-parent, reconstituted and other families.
- **Neglects gender**: feminists argue men, not just capitalism, benefit from women's unpaid work.
- **Treats people as passive**: family members make choices and may resist consumer pressure.
- **Hard to test**: families in non-capitalist or socialist societies have performed similar roles, suggesting the family is not simply a product of capitalism.

**10. [8]** Levels: 7–8 good knowledge, three developed points with evidence, terms used well; 4–6 some knowledge, most points supported, some undeveloped; 1–3 limited, descriptive.
Indicative content:
- Parsons argued the isolated nuclear family **fits** industrial society: small enough to move where work is (geographical mobility).
- Status in industrial society is achieved through work, so a small family without wide kin obligations avoids conflict over inherited status.
- It performs the two irreducible functions of primary socialisation and stabilising adult personalities, producing well-adjusted workers.
- Murdock saw the nuclear family as universal because it meets sexual, reproductive, economic and educational needs.
- Evaluation: historians and sociologists found extended kin remained important during industrialisation; family diversity challenges the claim that the nuclear family is needed; feminists say the model depends on women's unpaid work.

**11. [8]** Levels as Question 10. Indicative content:
- **Obedience to authority**: children learn to accept the parent's authority, preparing them to accept bosses and a hierarchical society (Marxist).
- **Making inequality seem natural**: families pass on the idea that wealth and success depend on effort, hiding class inequality.
- **Consumerism**: families learn to find happiness through buying goods, which supports capitalism.
- **Refuge from alienation**: Zaretsky argued the private family makes exploitation at work bearable, reducing pressure for change.
- **Patriarchal ideology** (feminist): girls and boys learn traditional gender roles as normal.
- Evaluation: children may reject parental values; schools, media and peers also shape ideas; functionalists see value consensus, not control.

**12. [14]** Levels: 12–14 excellent knowledge, three developed and evidenced points on each side, balanced, with a conclusion; 8–11 good knowledge, developed points on both sides but less balance; 4–7 some knowledge, simple two-sided or one-sided answer; 1–3 limited and descriptive. A one-sided answer cannot score more than 6.
*For the view:*
- Functionalists: the family provides primary socialisation and emotional security (Parsons' warm bath).
- Practical and financial support across generations, such as grandparents providing childcare.
- Care for vulnerable members: children, the sick and the elderly.
- Postmodernists: people now choose relationships that suit them, so family life is more likely to be satisfying.
- Movement towards joint conjugal roles has made many families more equal.
*Against the view:*
- Feminists: unequal division of labour, the dual burden and the triple shift.
- Domestic violence and abuse, including child and elder abuse, mostly hidden in private homes (Dobash and Dobash).
- Leach (1967): the inward-looking nuclear family creates tension and discontent.
- Marxists: the family serves capitalism and passes on class inequality, not members' interests.
- Sandwich-generation carers face stress and financial pressure.
*Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that family life is positive for many but the experience varies by gender, age and class, so "mainly positive" is true for some members more than others.
