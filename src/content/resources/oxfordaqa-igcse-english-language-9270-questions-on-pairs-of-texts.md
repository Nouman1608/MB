**This guide covers one requirement of Paper 2 Section A in OxfordAQA International GCSE English
Language (9270).** The specification says the stimulus material "will consist of 5–6 texts.
Questions will be asked on individual texts and pairs of texts. At least one question will require
students to consider two texts." The reading objective behind these questions is R2: "collate and
synthesise detail from one or more sources, making cross-references and comparisons as appropriate".
The [Paper 2 Section A reading study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-a-reading/)
introduces synthesis and comparison with short examples; this page explains how to choose what to
compare, how to keep two texts in balance, and how to handle pairs of very different texts.

## Three jobs a pair question can ask for

Read the question's verb and focus carefully. Most pair questions ask for one of three jobs.

| Job | Typical wording | What you produce |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesis** | "Using both texts, summarise…", "What do the two texts tell you about…?" | A combined picture drawn from both |
| **Comparison of ideas or perspectives** | "Compare the writers' attitudes to…" | Similarities and differences in viewpoint |
| **Comparison of methods** | "Compare how the writers present…" | Similarities and differences in language, structure and presentation, with effects |

Some questions combine jobs: "Compare the writers' views and how they present them" needs both
perspectives and methods. Underline each part of the question so that neither is forgotten.

## Choosing what to compare: comparison axes

A comparison is only as good as the points it sets side by side. Before writing, choose **axes**:
aspects on which both texts can be measured.

- **Purpose**: to inform, persuade, entertain, warn, complain.
- **Audience**: who each text is written for.
- **Perspective**: each writer's attitude to the issue.
- **Tone**: angry, playful, neutral-seeming, nostalgic.
- **Form**: article, blog, story extract, poster, speech.
- **Evidence**: facts, personal anecdote, expert voice, imagery.
- **Structure**: how each text opens, develops and ends.

Pick two or three axes that the question invites and that show real differences or surprising
similarities. A comparison on an axis where the texts are identical has little to say.

## The comparison grid

During your first survey of the pack, a grid makes pair questions faster.

```
               Text 1                    Text 2
Perspective    ...                       ...
Method 1       ... + quotation           ... + quotation
Method 2       ... + quotation           ... + quotation
Effect         ...                       ...
```

Fill only what the question needs. When you write, each row becomes one integrated paragraph.

## Keeping the texts in balance

The most common weakness in pair answers is imbalance: a long, detailed account of one text and a
sentence on the other. Three habits prevent it.

1. **Name both texts in every topic sentence.** "Both writers see the scheme as a change, but they
   disagree about who it serves."
2. **Give each text its own evidence in every paragraph.** A short quotation or precise reference for
   each.
3. **Finish each paragraph with what the comparison shows**, not with the second text's quotation.

## Pairs of different kinds of text

Because the pack can include fiction and non-fiction, and some texts "may be brief or solely
visual", a pair question may set very different texts side by side. That is an opportunity.

- **Fiction and non-fiction.** A report states a problem; a story extract makes the reader live it.
  Compare how each creates understanding: facts and explanation against character and situation.
- **Visual and written.** An image persuades instantly; an article persuades step by step. Compare
  the speed and kind of effect.
- **Brief and long.** A slogan compresses an argument the article develops. Compare what each
  includes and leaves out.

In these pairs, comparing **methods** is often more revealing than comparing ideas, because the
forms work so differently.

## Synthesis: making a combined picture

For a synthesis question, do not compare; **combine**. Group details from both texts under shared
headings and present them as one account.

> Both texts suggest that the heatwave affects people unequally. The council report notes that
> elderly residents in top-floor flats are most at risk, while the story shows a delivery rider who
> cannot stop working in the afternoon heat; together they suggest that age, housing and work all
> decide who suffers most.

The final clause is what turns two details into synthesis: it says what they show together.

## Worked example: comparing methods

Two **original sources written for this guide**:

> **Source 1 (council report):** *"During the five-day heatwave, calls to the emergency helpline
> increased. Residents are advised to keep curtains closed during the day, drink water regularly
> and check on vulnerable neighbours."*

> **Source 2 (story extract):** *"The fan in Mrs Osei's flat had stopped on the second day. By the
> fourth, she had moved her chair to the stairwell, where the concrete was cool, and she sat there
> with her eyes closed, listening for her neighbour's door, which did not open."*

**Question:** Compare how the two writers present the effects of the heatwave.

**Model answer (extract):**
Both writers present the heatwave as a danger to vulnerable people, but the report presents it as a
problem to be managed, while the story presents it as an experience to be felt. The report uses
impersonal, general language: "residents" and "vulnerable neighbours" are categories, and the list
of imperatives, "keep curtains closed… drink water… check on", turns the danger into a set of
practical steps. The story, by contrast, focuses on one named person, and its structure follows the
days, "the second day… By the fourth", so the reader feels the heat building through time. The
image of Mrs Osei moving her chair to the stairwell "where the concrete was cool" shows her
improvising alone, and the final detail of the neighbour's door "which did not open" quietly
answers the report's advice: no one checked on her. The report tells readers what should happen;
the story shows what happens when it does not.

*Why it works:* every point names both texts; methods (register, imperatives, structure, imagery)
are compared with effects; the final sentence draws out what the comparison reveals.

## Writing about evaluation in a pair question

R4 includes "evaluate". If a question asks which text is more effective, or how successfully each
achieves its purpose, judge each text **against its own purpose** before comparing. A report may
be more useful; a story may be more moving. Say so, and explain why.

## Common mistakes

- **Two separate mini-answers** joined by "Text 2, on the other hand…".
- **Imbalance**, with one text dominating.
- **Comparing on trivial axes** ("both use full stops").
- **Synthesising when asked to compare**, or comparing when asked to synthesise.
- **Using the wrong pair**, or bringing in a third text the question does not name.
- **Ignoring form** when the texts are of different kinds.

## Exam technique

Box the two texts the question names. Spend a minute choosing two or three axes and filling the
relevant rows of your grid. Write integrated paragraphs, each naming both texts, and finish with a
sentence that says what the comparison reveals. Let the marks shown guide length.
