This guide goes deeper into **3.3.12.2 Biodegradability and disposal of polymers**, the second subtopic of section 3.3.12 Polymers in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5. The subtopic mixes a precise chemical explanation (why some polymers hydrolyse and others do not) with an extended evaluation of disposal methods, and the two are often asked together.

Condense the ideas with the [biodegradability and disposal revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-12-2-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [biodegradability and disposal practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-12-2-practice/). ## What the specification asks for

- **Polyalkenes are chemically inert and non-biodegradable.**
- **Polyesters and polyamides can be broken down by hydrolysis and are biodegradable.**
- **The advantages and disadvantages of different methods of disposal of polymers, including recycling.**
- Explain **why polyesters and polyamides can be hydrolysed but polyalkenes cannot**.

The specification covers both addition polymers (polyalkenes, from section 3.3.4) and the condensation polymers of section 3.3.12.1.

## Why polyalkenes resist attack

A polyalkene chain, such as poly(ethene) or poly(propene), contains only C–C and C–H bonds.

- **C–C bonds** are non-polar, and **C–H bonds** are almost non-polar, because carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativities.
- With no significant partial charges, there is no δ+ carbon for a nucleophile such as H₂O or OH⁻ to attack, and no δ− site to attract an electrophile.
- The bonds are also strong, so breaking them needs a lot of energy.

So polyalkenes are **chemically inert**. Micro-organisms cannot break them down either, so they are **non-biodegradable** and persist in the environment for a very long time. This is the same reason alkanes are unreactive; the polymer is effectively a very long alkane.

## Why polyesters and polyamides hydrolyse

Both ester links and amide links contain a **polar C=O bond**. Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so the carbonyl carbon is **δ+**. It is also bonded to a second electronegative atom (O in an ester, N in an amide), which adds to the partial positive charge.

- A **nucleophile**, water or hydroxide ion, attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon.
- The C–O bond of the ester link, or the C–N bond of the amide link, breaks.
- The reaction is **hydrolysis**: the reverse of the condensation step that made the polymer.

Hydrolysis is slow with water alone at room temperature, which is why these polymers are useful materials. It is much faster when heated with acid or alkali. In the environment it happens slowly, so these polymers break down over time and are described as **biodegradable**.

### Worked example 1: a model explanation

*Question: Explain why nylon 6,6 can be hydrolysed but poly(propene) cannot. [4]*

1. "Nylon 6,6 contains amide links, which contain a polar C=O bond, so the carbonyl carbon is δ+." (identifies the polar group)
2. "Water or hydroxide ions act as nucleophiles and attack this δ+ carbon, breaking the C–N bond." (describes the attack)
3. "Poly(propene) contains only C–C and C–H bonds, which are non-polar." (identifies the absence of polarity)
4. "So there is no δ+ carbon for nucleophiles to attack, and the polymer is not hydrolysed." (links back)

"Poly(propene) has strong bonds" alone does not answer the question; polarity is the key difference.

## Predicting hydrolysis products

The products depend on the conditions, just as for simple esters and amides:

- In **acid**, –COOH groups stay as –COOH and amine groups are protonated to –NH₃⁺.
- In **alkali**, –COOH groups become carboxylate ions, –COO⁻, and amine groups stay as –NH₂.
- Diol (or alcohol) products are the same in both conditions.

### Worked example 2: Terylene and Kevlar

Give the organic products of (a) Terylene heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide and (b) Kevlar heated with concentrated hydrochloric acid.

- **(a)** Terylene is a polyester of benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and ethane-1,2-diol. In alkali: **sodium benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate**, NaOOC–C₆H₄–COONa, and **ethane-1,2-diol**, HO–CH₂CH₂–OH.
- **(b)** Kevlar is a polyamide of benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and benzene-1,4-diamine. In acid: **benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid**, HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH, and the **benzene-1,4-diammonium** ion, ⁺H₃N–C₆H₄–NH₃⁺ (as its chloride salt).

### Worked example 3: chemical recycling by hydrolysis

1.00 kg of Terylene (repeating unit C₁₀H₈O₄, Mr 192.0) is completely hydrolysed back to its monomers. Calculate the masses of benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (Mr 166.0) and ethane-1,2-diol (Mr 62.0) formed.

1. n(repeating units) = 1000 ÷ 192.0 = 5.21 mol.
2. Each unit gives one molecule of each monomer.
3. Benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid: 5.21 × 166.0 = **865 g**. Ethane-1,2-diol: 5.21 × 62.0 = **323 g**.
4. Check: 865 + 323 = 1188 g, which is 1000 g of polymer plus 188 g of water (2 × 5.21 mol × 18.0).

These recovered monomers can be polymerised again, which is the basis of chemical recycling for condensation polymers.

## Disposal methods

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Landfill** | Cheap and simple; no sorting needed | Uses large areas of land; polyalkenes persist for a very long time; space is limited |
| **Incineration** | Greatly reduces the volume of waste; heat released can generate electricity | Produces CO₂, a greenhouse gas; polymers containing chlorine, such as PVC, give toxic HCl, which must be removed from waste gases; the polymer is not reused |
| **Mechanical recycling** (melt and remould) | Conserves crude oil and the energy used to make new polymer; less waste to landfill | Waste must be collected, sorted by polymer type and cleaned; quality of the polymer often falls with each cycle |
| **Chemical recycling** (break down to monomers or feedstock) | Gives raw materials for new, good-quality polymer; condensation polymers can be hydrolysed to their monomers | Needs energy and specialised plant; can be costly |

### Worked example 4: quantities from incineration

Calculate the mass of CO₂ from incinerating 1.00 kg of poly(ethene), and the mass of HCl from 1.00 kg of poly(chloroethene), PVC. (Mr: CH₂ unit 14.0, CH₂CHCl unit 62.5, CO₂ 44.0, HCl 36.5)

1. Poly(ethene): each CH₂ unit gives one CO₂. n = 1000 ÷ 14.0 = 71.4 mol; mass of CO₂ = 71.4 × 44.0 = **3.14 kg**. The CO₂ produced weighs more than three times the polymer burned.
2. PVC: each CH₂CHCl unit contains one Cl, which leaves as HCl. n = 1000 ÷ 62.5 = 16.0 mol; mass of HCl = 16.0 × 36.5 = **584 g**.

Numbers like these make an evaluation answer more convincing: they show why incineration needs gas treatment and why it adds to CO₂ emissions.

## Writing an evaluation

A disposal question worth several marks usually rewards balanced points linked to chemistry. A reliable structure:

1. For each method, give **one advantage and one disadvantage**.
2. **Link each to the chemistry**: inertness and non-biodegradability for landfill, combustion products for incineration, the need for sorting because different polymers have different properties for recycling.
3. **Apply it to the polymer in the question**: a polyalkene persists in landfill; a polyester can be chemically recycled by hydrolysis; a chlorine-containing polymer releases HCl when burned.
4. **Reach a judgement**, for example that recycling is preferable where waste can be collected and sorted, with energy-recovering incineration for mixed or contaminated waste.

## Common mistakes

- Saying polyalkenes resist hydrolysis because their bonds are "weak"; they are non-polar and strong.
- Saying polyalkenes are "unreactive because they are saturated" without the polarity argument.
- Giving the free acid as a product of alkaline hydrolysis, or the free diamine in acid.
- Listing disposal methods without advantages and disadvantages linked to chemistry.
- Saying incineration makes the waste "disappear"; it converts the polymer to CO₂ and H₂O and, for PVC, HCl as well.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** hydrolysis: polar C=O, δ+ carbon, nucleophile attack, bond in the linkage breaks.
- **Explain** inertness: only C–C and C–H, non-polar, no δ+ site.
- **Give the products**: decide acid or alkali first, then protonate amines in acid and deprotonate acids in alkali.
- **Evaluate**: pair advantages and disadvantages, use chemistry, and finish with a judgement.
