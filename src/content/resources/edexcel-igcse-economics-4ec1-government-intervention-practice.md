These questions cover section 1.2.6, Government intervention, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. There is a single tier of entry. All countries, firms and figures are invented. Read the [government intervention study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-government-intervention/) and the [government intervention revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-government-intervention-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which policy would a government most likely use to increase consumption of a good with external benefits?

A: an indirect tax

B: a fine

C: a subsidy

D: pollution permits

**[1]**

**2.** State **two** aims of government regulation of competition. **[2]**

**3.** What is meant by a minimum wage? **[2]**

**4.** State **one** disadvantage of using regulation to reduce pollution. **[1]**

**5.** Two airlines with market shares of 34% and 21% propose to merge. Calculate their combined market share. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Country W places a tax of 0.60 on each litre of a polluting fuel additive. Before the tax, 8 million litres a month were sold at 2.40. After the tax, 7 million litres are sold at 2.85.

**(a)** Calculate the tax revenue each month. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate how much of the tax per litre is paid by consumers and how much by producers. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage fall in sales. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain **one** disadvantage of using a tax to reduce pollution. **[3]**

**7.** A factory saves 90 000 a year by dumping waste in a river. Inspectors catch 1 in 5 offending firms, and the fine is 300 000.

**(a)** Calculate the expected cost of the fine to the factory. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain whether the fine is likely to stop the dumping. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** way the government could make the fine more effective. **[2]**

**8.** Four power stations each emit 250 tonnes of pollution a year. The government issues 800 tradable pollution permits, each allowing 1 tonne.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage reduction in total pollution. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** advantage of pollution permits compared with regulation. **[3]**

**9.** The table shows an invented labour market for hotel cleaners.

| Wage per hour | Cleaners demanded | Cleaners supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 3 000 | 2 200 |
| 9 | 2 700 | 2 700 |
| 10 | 2 400 | 3 200 |
| 11 | 2 100 | 3 700 |

**(a)** Identify the equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**

**(b)** A minimum wage of 10 is introduced. Calculate the change in employment and the surplus of labour. **[2]**

**(c)** The minimum wage is raised to 11. Describe how the diagram would change. **[3]**

**10.** Explain **one** way the government protects consumer interests. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a government should use a subsidy to increase vaccination rates. **[6]**

**12.** Assess the impact of introducing a minimum wage on workers and businesses. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Any two: promote competition [1]; limit monopoly power [1]; (also accept protect consumer interests, control mergers and takeovers). **[2]**

**3.** The lowest wage per hour [1]; that employers are legally allowed to pay [1]. **[2]**

**4.** It must be enforced by inspectors, which is costly (accept: no incentive to cut pollution below the limit; raises firms' costs) [1]. **[1]**

**5.** 34 + 21 = **55%** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 0.60 × 7 million = **4.2 million** [1].

**(b)** Consumers: 2.85 − 2.40 = **0.45** [1]; producers: 0.60 − 0.45 = **0.15** [1].

**(c)** (7 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 [1] = **−12.5%** [1].

**(d)** Demand may be price inelastic [1]; so the quantity bought falls only a little [1]; so pollution is reduced only slightly, even though revenue is raised (accept: hard to set the tax at the right level; may hit poorer households) [1]. **[8]**

**7. (a)** 300 000 ÷ 5 = **60 000** [1].

**(b)** The expected cost (60 000) is less than the money saved (90 000) [1]; so the factory may continue dumping [1].

**(c)** Increase inspections, so the chance of being caught rises [1]; which raises the expected cost above the 90 000 saving (accept: raise the fine above 450 000) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* For the fine alone to deter, it must exceed 90 000 × 5 = 450 000 at the current rate of inspection.

**8. (a)** Total before = 4 × 250 = 1 000 tonnes [1]; (1 000 − 800) ÷ 1 000 × 100 = **20%** [1].

**(b)** Firms that can cut pollution cheaply can sell spare permits [1]; so the reductions are made where they cost least [1]; whereas regulation makes every firm meet the same limit, whatever the cost, and gives no reward for going further [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** Wage **9** [1]; employment **2 700** [1].

**(b)** Employment falls from 2 700 to 2 400, a fall of **300** [1]; surplus = 3 200 − 2 400 = **800** [1].

**(c)** The minimum wage line moves up to 11 [1]; quantity demanded falls to 2 100 and quantity supplied rises to 3 700 [1]; so the surplus of labour widens to **1 600** [1]. **[7]**

**10.** Laws require honest advertising and accurate labelling [1]; so consumers know what they are buying [1]; which protects them from being misled by sellers who know more about the product (accept: product safety laws; rights to refunds) [1]. **[3]**

**11.** For: vaccination has external benefits, so it is under-consumed [1]; a subsidy lowers the price, so more people are vaccinated [1]; others benefit because disease spreads less [1]. Against: the subsidy costs taxpayers and has an opportunity cost, such as less spending on schools [1]; some people may still not be vaccinated even at a lower price [1]. Judgement: worthwhile if the external benefit is large compared with the cost, perhaps combined with information campaigns; a supported conclusion [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Workers, gains: low-paid workers who keep their jobs earn more [1]; which reduces poverty and inequality [1]. Workers, losses: if set above equilibrium, fewer workers are demanded [1]; so some lose jobs or cannot find work [1]. Businesses, losses: wage costs rise [1]; so firms may raise prices, accept lower profit or replace workers with machines [1]. Businesses, gains: higher pay may raise motivation and productivity, offsetting some of the cost [1]. Judgement: the impact depends on how far above equilibrium the minimum wage is set [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Assess" requires both groups and a judgement that names what the impact depends on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a tax or fine where a subsidy is needed, or the reverse.
- Forgetting to multiply the tax by the quantity sold after the tax.
- Comparing the fine itself, not the expected fine, with the money saved.
- Minimum wage answers that do not read both demand and supply at the new wage.
- Evaluations with no deciding factor.
