## Cell structure (3.1.1)

**Most animal cells** (eukaryotic) have:

- **Nucleus** — controls the activities of the cell.
- **Cytoplasm** — where most of the chemical reactions take place.
- **Cell membrane** — controls the passage of substances into and out of the cell.
- **Mitochondria** — where most energy is released in respiration.
- **Ribosomes** — where protein synthesis occurs.

**Plant cells** (eukaryotic) often also have **chloroplasts** (absorb light energy to make food) and a **permanent vacuole** filled with cell sap. Plant and algal cells have a **cell wall made of cellulose**, which strengthens the cell.

**Bacterial cells** (prokaryotic) have cytoplasm and a membrane surrounded by a cell wall; the genes are **not in a distinct nucleus**, and some genes are in small circular structures called **plasmids**.

Cells may be **specialised** for a particular function: be ready to link a named cell's structure to its job in a tissue, organ or organism.

## Levels of organisation (3.1.2)

```
cell  ->  tissue  ->  organ  ->  organ system  ->  organism
```

During the development of a multicellular organism, cells **differentiate** so that they can perform different functions.

- **Tissue** — a group of cells with **similar** structure and function.
- **Organ** — made of tissues; one organ may contain **several different tissues**.
- **Organ system** — a group of organs that perform a particular function.

## Animal tissues, organs and systems (3.1.3)

| Tissue | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Muscular** | Contracts to bring about movement |
| **Glandular** | Produces substances such as enzymes and hormones |
| **Epithelial** | Covers some parts of the body |

The **stomach** is the standard example of an organ: **muscular tissue** moves the contents through the digestive system, **glandular tissue** produces digestive juices, and **epithelial tissue** covers the outside and the inside.

The **digestive system** is the standard example of an organ system:

- **Glands** (pancreas, salivary glands) produce digestive juices.
- **Stomach and small intestine** — where digestion occurs.
- **Liver** — produces bile.
- **Small intestine** — where soluble food is absorbed.
- **Large intestine** — where water is absorbed from the undigested food, producing faeces.

## Transport in cells (3.1.5)

| Process | What moves | Direction | Energy from respiration? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diffusion** | Particles in solution or of a gas | Higher → lower concentration (net movement) | No |
| **Osmosis** | Water, through a partially permeable membrane | Dilute → more concentrated solution | No |
| **Active transport** | Substances such as mineral ions or sugar | **Against** a concentration gradient | **Yes** |

- The **greater the concentration difference**, the faster the rate of diffusion. Oxygen for respiration enters cells by diffusion.
- Osmosis terms: a solution that is **isotonic** with the cell contents causes no net movement of water; in a **hypotonic** (more dilute) solution water enters the cell; in a **hypertonic** (more concentrated) solution water leaves. A plant cell that has taken in water presses its contents against the cell wall (**turgor**); one that loses a lot of water shrinks away from its cell wall (**plasmolysis**).
- Active transport lets **root hair cells** absorb ions from very dilute soil solutions, and lets sugar be absorbed from low concentrations in the **intestine** and the **kidney tubules**.
- **Required practical** (3.1.5): investigate the effect of different concentrations of solutions separated by a partially permeable membrane (for example, potato pieces in sugar solutions; the concentration giving no change in mass is isotonic with the cells).

**Surface area to volume ratio.** A single-celled organism has a relatively large surface area to volume ratio, so all its exchanges happen across its surface membrane. Larger, more complex organisms need specialised exchange surfaces, which work better when they have:

1. a **large surface area** that is **thin**, giving a short diffusion path;
2. (in animals) an **efficient blood supply**;
3. (in animals, for gas exchange) **ventilation**.

| Exchange surface | Adaptations |
|---|---|
| **Small intestine** | Long and folded, covered in **villi and microvilli** (large surface area); thin wall one cell thick (short diffusion path); rich blood supply (keeps the concentration gradient steep) |
| **Lungs** | Millions of **alveoli** (very large surface area); walls one cell thick (short diffusion path); dense capillary network; ventilated, so fresh air keeps the gradient steep |
| **Roots** | **Root hair cells** greatly increase the surface area for absorbing water and mineral ions |
| **Leaves** | Flat, thin shape and air spaces inside (large surface area, short diffusion path for gases) |

**Worked example.** Explain why the small intestine is not ventilated but the lungs are. Both are exchange surfaces with a large surface area, a short diffusion path and a rich blood supply. The lungs exchange **gases with the air**, so fresh air must keep arriving or the air in the alveoli would reach the same concentrations as the blood and diffusion would stop; the small intestine exchanges **dissolved substances** with the blood, so blood flow alone keeps its gradient.

## Linked Topic 2 notes: enzymes, digestion, the heart and the lungs

The 9201 specification places the next three sections in **Topic 2 Bioenergetics** (3.2.4 Digestion, 3.2.3 Circulation in humans, 3.2.5 Breathing), not in Topic 1. They are kept here as linked recall because Topic 1 names the digestive system and uses the small intestine and lungs as exchange surfaces. For the full Topic 2 coverage, use the [Bioenergetics study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-biology-bioenergetics/).

### Enzymes and digestion (Topic 2, 3.2.4)

Enzymes are large proteins that act as biological **catalysts**, speeding up reactions without being used up.

The active site has a shape **complementary** to the substrate, so each enzyme is **specific** to its substrate.

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Rate rises to an optimum (~37 °C in humans), then falls sharply as the enzyme **denatures** |
| **pH** | Optimum varies by enzyme; away from it the active site changes shape |

**Denaturation** is the change in shape of the active site so the substrate no longer fits. Enzymes are **not alive** and are not "killed".

| Enzyme | Substrate | Products | Made in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Amylase** | Starch | Sugars (maltose) | Salivary glands, pancreas, small intestine |
| **Protease** | Protein | Amino acids | Stomach, pancreas, small intestine |
| **Lipase** | Lipids | **Fatty acids + glycerol** | Pancreas, small intestine |

The **stomach** produces hydrochloric acid; the enzymes in the stomach work best in acid conditions.

**Bile** is made in the **liver**, stored in the **gall bladder**, released into the small intestine, and does two things:

1. **Neutralises stomach acid**, giving the alkaline conditions in which the enzymes in the small intestine work best.
2. **Emulsifies fats** — breaks large drops into smaller droplets, increasing the **surface area** for lipase.

Bile is **not an enzyme**. Saying it digests fat loses the mark; it increases surface area so lipase can work faster.

**Worked example.** Explain why boiling amylase before adding starch means no sugar is produced. The high temperature changes the shape of the enzyme's active site permanently; starch can no longer fit, so it is not broken down; the enzyme is **denatured**. Describing the enzyme as "killed" rather than denatured scores nothing on this kind of question.

### The heart and blood vessels (Topic 2, 3.2.3)

The heart has **four chambers** (right and left atria, right and left ventricles). Blood enters the atria; the atria contract and force blood into the ventricles; the ventricles contract and force blood out of the heart. **Valves** make sure blood flows in the correct direction.

**Double circulation:** one circuit to the lungs and one to all the other organs, so blood passes through the heart **twice** on each complete journey round the body. The **left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall** because it pumps blood around the whole body, while the right ventricle pumps only to the nearby lungs.

| Vessel | Wall | Lumen | Valves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Artery** | Thick, with muscle and elastic fibres | Narrow | No |
| **Vein** | Thinner | Wide | Often |
| **Capillary** | **One cell thick** | Very narrow | No |

Arteries carry blood **away** from the heart, veins **towards** it; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood, so "arteries carry oxygenated blood" is wrong.

### The lungs (Topic 2, 3.2.5)

Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli.

- **Inhaling:** intercostal muscles contract and pull the ribcage up; the diaphragm contracts and flattens; the volume of the thorax increases, the pressure falls below atmospheric pressure, and air enters the lungs.
- **Exhaling:** intercostal muscles and diaphragm relax; the volume of the thorax decreases, the pressure rises, and air leaves the lungs.

The alveoli provide a very large surface area, richly supplied with blood capillaries, so gases readily diffuse into and out of the blood.

## Exam traps

**Topic 1**

- Confusing tissue with organ, or giving a definition without a named example.
- Saying all cells have a cell wall (animal cells do not), or that a bacterial cell has a nucleus.
- Describing osmosis as "diffusion of water" without the partially permeable membrane or the direction (dilute to more concentrated).
- Saying diffusion or osmosis needs energy; only active transport uses energy from respiration.
- Naming an exchange-surface adaptation without saying what it increases or shortens.

**Linked Topic 2**

- Calling bile an enzyme.
- Saying enzymes are killed rather than denatured.
- Giving "glycerol and fatty acid" as one product rather than two.
- Saying all arteries carry oxygenated blood.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces?**
A single-celled organism has a large surface area to volume ratio, so it can exchange materials across its surface membrane. As organisms get larger the ratio falls, so they need specialised exchange surfaces that are large and thin, with an efficient blood supply and, for gas exchange in animals, ventilation.

**Is bile an enzyme?**
No. Bile is made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder. It neutralises stomach acid, giving alkaline conditions for enzymes in the small intestine, and emulsifies fats to increase their surface area, but it does not digest fat itself.

**Where are enzymes, the heart and the lungs in the OxfordAQA 9201 specification?**
They are in Topic 2, Bioenergetics: digestion and enzymes in 3.2.4, the heart and blood vessels in 3.2.3, and the lungs in 3.2.5. These notes link them to Topic 1 because they build on cells, tissues and organs.

**What does it mean when cells differentiate?**
During the development of a multicellular organism, cells differentiate so that they can perform different functions. Differentiated cells then form tissues, organs and organ systems.
