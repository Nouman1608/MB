## Solubility rules (learn exactly)

| Salts | Rule | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium, potassium, ammonium | Soluble | None |
| Nitrates | Soluble | None |
| Chlorides | Soluble | Lead, silver |
| Sulfates | Soluble | Barium, calcium, lead |
| Carbonates | Insoluble | Sodium, potassium, ammonium (soluble) |
| Hydroxides | Insoluble | Sodium, potassium, ammonium (soluble); calcium (partially) |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Salt | Compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal or ammonium ion |
| Precipitation | Formation of an insoluble solid by mixing two solutions |
| Filtrate | Liquid that passes through filter paper |
| Residue | Solid left on the filter paper |
| Saturated solution | Contains as much dissolved solute as possible at that temperature |
| Point of crystallisation | When crystals start to form, e.g. on a cooled glass rod |
| Hydrated substance | Chemically combined with water |
| Anhydrous substance | Contains no water |
| Water of crystallisation | Water molecules present in hydrated crystals, e.g. CuSO₄•5H₂O, CoCl₂•6H₂O |

## Choosing a method

```
Is the salt insoluble?  → PRECIPITATION (two soluble solutions; filter, wash, dry)
Is it a Na, K or NH₄ salt? → TITRATION (acid + alkali; repeat without indicator)
Otherwise, soluble salt   → acid + EXCESS solid:
                            metal (not Na, K; not Cu), or
                            insoluble base (oxide / hydroxide), or
                            insoluble carbonate
```

Check the product too: if it is insoluble (e.g. CaSO₄ from CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄), it coats the solid and stops the reaction.

## Excess-solid method: steps and reasons

1. Warm acid → faster reaction.
2. Add solid until in excess / fizzing stops → all acid used up.
3. Filter → remove excess solid.
4. Evaporate to the point of crystallisation → saturated solution.
5. Cool → crystals form (less soluble when cold).
6. Filter, wash with a little cold distilled water, dry between filter papers → remove soluble impurities without dissolving crystals.

Do not evaporate to dryness: it removes water of crystallisation.

## Titration method: key points

- Measure alkali with a pipette; add indicator; add acid from a burette to the end-point.
- Repeat with the same volumes **without indicator** so the salt is pure.
- Then crystallise as above.

## Precipitation method: key points

- Choose two **soluble** reactants: one supplies the cation, the other the anion. Nitrates and sodium salts are ideal.
- Filter, **wash with distilled water**, dry. No evaporation.
- Example ionic equations: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄; Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl; Pb²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → PbCl₂; Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃.

## Worked choices

| Salt wanted | Soluble? | Method | Suitable reagents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ammonium sulfate | Yes (ammonium) | Titration | Aqueous ammonia + dilute sulfuric acid |
| Iron(II) sulfate | Yes | Excess metal | Iron + dilute sulfuric acid |
| Copper(II) nitrate | Yes | Excess insoluble base or carbonate | Copper(II) oxide or carbonate + dilute nitric acid |
| Lead(II) sulfate | No | Precipitation | Lead(II) nitrate(aq) + sodium sulfate(aq) |
| Silver chloride | No | Precipitation | Silver nitrate(aq) + sodium chloride(aq) |
| Calcium carbonate | No | Precipitation | Calcium chloride(aq) + sodium carbonate(aq) |

## Water of crystallisation calculation

1. Mass of water = hydrated mass − anhydrous mass.
2. Moles of anhydrous salt = mass ÷ Mr.
3. Moles of water = mass ÷ 18.
4. x = moles of water ÷ moles of anhydrous salt.

Heat to constant mass to remove all the water.

## Exam traps

- Copper does not react with dilute acids: use CuO, Cu(OH)₂ or CuCO₃.
- Sodium and potassium are too reactive to add to acid.
- Lead is an exception for **both** chlorides and sulfates.
- Give the **reason** for excess: all the acid reacts.
- Precipitates must be **washed**; crystals are washed with only a **little cold** water.
- "Evaporate to dryness" loses the mark for a hydrated salt.
- In titration, repeating without indicator is a separate marking point.
- Both reagents for precipitation must be soluble: you cannot start from silver chloride.
- With a metal or carbonate, "until fizzing stops" is the observation that shows the acid has been used up.

## Self-test

1. Is lead(II) sulfate soluble or insoluble?
2. Name **two** insoluble chlorides.
3. Which method makes potassium sulfate? Why?
4. Which method makes silver chloride? Name two suitable reagents.
5. Why is excess copper(II) oxide added when making copper(II) sulfate?
6. Why is the excess copper(II) oxide then filtered off?
7. Why is the filtrate not evaporated to dryness?
8. Why is the titration repeated without indicator?
9. Why can't copper(II) nitrate be made from copper and dilute nitric acid?
10. Define water of crystallisation.
11. 4.92 g of hydrated magnesium sulfate, MgSO₄•xH₂O, gives 2.40 g of anhydrous MgSO₄ on heating. Find x. (Mr MgSO₄ = 120)
12. Write the ionic equation for the precipitation of calcium carbonate.

**Answers**

1. Insoluble.
2. Lead(II) chloride and silver chloride.
3. Titration: both potassium hydroxide and sulfuric acid are soluble, and potassium is too reactive to add as a metal.
4. Precipitation: e.g. aqueous silver nitrate and aqueous sodium chloride.
5. To make sure all the acid reacts.
6. It is insoluble and would contaminate the salt.
7. Heating to dryness would remove the water of crystallisation; hydrated crystals form only from a saturated solution left to cool.
8. The indicator would contaminate the salt.
9. Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so it does not react with dilute acids.
10. The water molecules present in hydrated crystals.
11. Water = 2.52 g = 0.140 mol; MgSO₄ = 2.40 ÷ 120 = 0.0200 mol; x = 0.140 ÷ 0.0200 = 7.
12. Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s).
