This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.2.9 The role of aphids in spreading plant viruses**, in Unit 2, Biological systems and disease. Unit 2 is listed for both the International AS and the International A-level.

The broader [HIV and plant virus transmission guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-hiv-and-plant-virus-transmission-by-aphids/) states the facts about symptoms, stylets and winged females. This page explains the biology behind them: why symptoms reduce yield, why plant viruses need vectors, why phloem sap is under pressure, how a virus travels from one plant to another, and how to interpret field and experimental data. ## What the specification asks

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.2.9.1 | Many important plant diseases are caused by viruses, causing losses in crop production and quality worldwide; symptoms such as yellowing of leaves, leaf distortion or abnormalities of other parts; because plants are immobile, transmission usually involves vectors such as aphids |
| 3.2.9.2 | Aphids have sucking mouthparts called stylets, inserted into phloem vessels; once a phloem vessel is punctured, sap under high pressure is forced into the aphid's gut; some species produce winged females that migrate to start colonies on new host plants |

This section links closely to 3.2.8.2 (phloem and the mass flow hypothesis) and to the Unit 2 principle that viruses are acellular and are replicated by the host cell after their nucleic acid enters it.

## Why symptoms reduce yield and quality

The specification names three kinds of symptom. Explaining their effect on a crop is a common application question.

| Symptom | Effect on the plant | Effect on the crop |
|---|---|---|
| Yellowing of leaves | Less chlorophyll, so less light is absorbed and the rate of photosynthesis falls | Less sugar is made and translocated to growing and storage organs, so yield falls |
| Leaf distortion (curling, crinkling) | Smaller effective leaf area facing the light; less photosynthesis | Lower yield |
| Abnormalities of other parts | Stunted growth, misshapen fruit or storage organs | Lower yield and lower quality, so produce may be worth less or unsellable |

"Production" means quantity and "quality" means value or appearance. A full answer to "explain why plant viruses cause economic losses" covers both.

## Why plant viruses need vectors

A virus cannot move by itself; it is acellular and has no means of locomotion. Between animals, viruses can spread by contact, droplets or body fluids as hosts move and meet. Plants are immobile, so infected and healthy plants do not come into contact in that way. Plant cells are also surrounded by cell walls, so a virus particle landing on a leaf cannot simply enter a cell.

A vector solves both problems. An aphid moves between plants, and its stylets pierce the plant tissue and deliver virus particles directly into the phloem. Inside the plant, the virus's nucleic acid is replicated by the host cells.

## How an aphid feeds

1. The aphid lands on a plant and pushes its stylets, thin needle-like mouthparts, into a stem or leaf.
2. The stylets work their way through the tissues until they reach a phloem vessel and puncture it.
3. Phloem sap is under high pressure, so it is forced up the stylets into the aphid's gut. The aphid does not need to suck hard.

**Why is phloem sap under pressure? (link to 3.2.8.2).** In the mass flow hypothesis, sucrose is loaded into the phloem at a source, such as a photosynthesising leaf. This lowers the water potential inside the phloem, so water enters by osmosis from the surrounding cells and the xylem. The volume of liquid in the phloem increases, creating a high hydrostatic pressure, which drives the sap towards sinks where sucrose is removed. Puncturing a phloem vessel releases this pressure into the aphid.

## From one plant to the next

1. An aphid feeds on an infected plant. Virus particles in the phloem sap enter its stylets and gut.
2. The aphid moves to a healthy plant, by walking, or by flying if it is a winged female.
3. It inserts its stylets into the phloem of the healthy plant, and virus particles are introduced.
4. The virus is carried in the phloem sap to other parts of the plant, such as growing shoots, roots and storage organs.
5. Host cells replicate the virus, and symptoms appear.

**Winged females and spread over distance.** Some aphid species produce winged females, which migrate to new host plants and start colonies there. Wingless aphids spread a virus within a plant or to neighbouring plants; winged females can carry it to plants in other parts of a field or to other fields. Each new colony can then spread the virus to the plants around it.

## Experimental evidence: aphids as phloem samplers

The specification asks you to analyse and interpret evidence from experiments on the movement of organic substances in the phloem. Aphids provide a classic technique.

**The method.** An aphid is allowed to insert its stylets into a phloem vessel. The aphid is then anaesthetised and its body is cut away, leaving the stylet in place. Sap continues to flow out of the cut stylet.

**What it shows:**

- Sap flows out on its own for some time, so the phloem contents are under pressure. This supports the mass flow hypothesis.
- The sap can be collected and analysed. It contains sucrose and other organic substances, showing what the phloem carries.
- Comparing the rate of flow at different points on a stem, or at different times, gives information about the direction and rate of translocation.

**Worked example 1 (illustrative data).** Sap was collected from stylets left in the stem of a plant at two heights.

| Stylet position | Sucrose concentration in sap (mmol dm⁻³) |
|---|---|
| Near the leaves (upper stem) | 550 |
| Near the roots (lower stem) | 420 |

- The concentration falls from 550 to 420 mmol dm⁻³, a fall of (550 − 420) ÷ 550 × 100 = 23.6%.
- A concentration gradient from the leaves (source) to the roots (sink) is consistent with mass flow from source to sink.

## Interpreting field data

**Worked example 2 (illustrative data).** In a field of 600 lettuce plants, winged aphids were trapped each week and infected plants were counted.

| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Winged aphids trapped | 3 | 20 | 75 | 40 | 12 | 5 |
| Infected plants (total) | 0 | 1 | 6 | 30 | 72 | 90 |

1. Infected plants at week 6: 90 ÷ 600 × 100 = 15%.
2. The trap catch peaks at week 3, but new infections per week are 1, 5, 24, 42, 18 from week 2 onwards, peaking at week 5.
3. The two-week delay is consistent with the time a virus takes to multiply and produce visible symptoms after an aphid introduces it.
4. The rise in infections follows the rise in aphids, so the data are consistent with aphids spreading the virus. But this is a correlation. Stronger evidence would come from testing trapped aphids for the virus, or from comparing plants protected from aphids with unprotected plants.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain how an aphid could spread a virus from an infected plant in one field to healthy plants in another field. [5 marks]

**Answer:** The aphid inserts its stylets into a phloem vessel of the infected plant (1). Phloem sap, which is under high pressure, is forced into its gut, carrying virus particles (1). The aphid is a winged female, which migrates to a plant in another field (1). It feeds by inserting its stylets into the phloem of the new plant, introducing virus particles (1). It starts a colony on the new host, and the colony can spread the virus to neighbouring plants (1).

*Why it scores:* it covers stylets and phloem, pressure, the role of winged females in distance, delivery into the new plant, and colony formation. Answers that say "the aphid bites the plant" lose the stylet and phloem marks.

## Common mistakes

- Saying aphids chew or bite leaves. They insert stylets into the phloem.
- Saying aphids suck sap up by muscle effort. The high pressure in the phloem forces sap into the gut.
- Saying the virus enters through xylem. Aphids feed on phloem.
- Saying plants pass viruses to each other directly. Plants are immobile; vectors are needed.
- Describing symptoms without linking them to photosynthesis or yield when the question asks for effects on a crop.
- Treating correlation in field data as proof that aphids carried the virus.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** feeding: stylets, phloem, pressure, gut.
- **Explain** why vectors are needed: immobile plants, cell walls, acellular virus.
- **Explain** spread: winged females migrate and start colonies.
- **Analyse** data: calculate percentages and rates of new infection, look for time lags, and say what the data do and do not show.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is phloem sap under high pressure?**
Sucrose loaded into the phloem at a source lowers the water potential, so water enters by osmosis and raises the hydrostatic pressure. This is the basis of the mass flow hypothesis.

**What is the role of winged female aphids?**
They migrate to new host plants and start colonies there, so they can carry a virus to plants that are far from the original infected plant.
