## The three functions of prices

In a market economy no one plans the whole system. Prices pass information and push decisions into line.

- **Rationing:** when demand exceeds supply, a higher price discourages some buyers, so the limited stock goes to those willing and able to pay.
- **Incentive:** a higher price raises the reward to producers, so they supply more. Higher profits also attract new firms.
- **Signalling:** a price change tells buyers and sellers that conditions have changed, without anyone explaining why.

**Worked example 1 (invented market for sourdough loaves in Town Z).** Qd = 600 − 50P and Qs = 100P, where P is in pounds.

1. Equilibrium: 600 − 50P = 100P, so 150P = 600, P = £4 and Q = 400 loaves.
2. Demand rises by 150 at every price (a new fashion for sourdough): Qd = 750 − 50P.
3. At the old price of £4: Qd = 750 − 200 = 550 and Qs = 400. There is a **shortage of 150**.
4. The new equilibrium: 750 − 50P = 100P, so P = £5 and Q = 500.

Read the chain. The shortage lets bakers raise price (**signal**). Buyers on the margin drop out as quantity demanded falls from 550 to 500 (**rationing**). The extra £1 per loaf pulls quantity supplied up from 400 to 500 (**incentive**). If demand had instead fallen to Qd = 450 − 50P, the equilibrium would be £3 and 300 loaves. At £4 there would be a **surplus of 150** (Qd = 250, Qs = 400) and the falling price would tell bakers to cut back.

### What, how and for whom

- **What:** resources move towards goods whose prices and profits are rising, and away from goods whose prices fall.
- **How:** firms choose the cheapest method at current input prices.
- **For whom:** goods go to those with the ability, as well as the willingness, to pay. Income decides the share each person gets.

**Worked example 2 (how).** A firm can make the same output by method L (8 workers, 1 machine per day) or method C (3 workers, 3 machines). A machine costs £200 a day.

- Wage £120: L = 8 × 120 + 200 = £1,160; C = 3 × 120 + 600 = £960. Choose **C**.
- Wage £60: L = 8 × 60 + 200 = £680; C = 3 × 60 + 600 = £780. Choose **L**.

The relative price of inputs changed the method. No instruction was needed.

## Advantages and disadvantages of the price mechanism

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Decisions are decentralised, so no planner needs all the information | Ignores people without purchasing power |
| Profit and prices give firms an incentive to be efficient and respond quickly | Prices may leave out external costs and benefits |
| Consumers' choices direct production (consumer sovereignty) | No provision of public goods |
| Competition tends to hold prices down and encourage innovation | Monopoly power can distort prices |
| Low administration cost compared with central allocation | Merit goods may be under-consumed and demerit goods over-consumed |

### Assessing the view that the price mechanism is impersonal

Price allocation does not ask who you are, why you want the good or how badly you need it. That is a strength and a weakness.

- **For:** it treats buyers equally, avoids favouritism and bribery and needs no official to judge need.
- **Against:** it responds to purchasing power, not need.

**Worked example 3 (invented).** Two units of a scarce good are on sale. Four households have these maximum prices and monthly incomes.

| Household | Willingness to pay (£) | Monthly income (£) |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | 40 | 3,000 |
| H2 | 34 | 1,200 |
| H3 | 26 | 1,500 |
| H4 | 15 | 600 |

A price of £30 clears the market. H1 and H2 buy; revenue is £60 and consumer surplus is (40 − 30) + (34 − 30) = £14. The price is 1% of H1's income but 5% of H4's. H4's low willingness to pay may reflect low income, not low need. So the market allocates **efficiently in its own terms** but not necessarily **fairly**.

Evaluation: the criticism is strongest for necessities with no substitutes, and weaker where income differences are small or the government can adjust incomes. Whether a result is "unfair" is a normative judgement.

## Extending the price mechanism into new areas

Some activities have traditionally relied on gifts, queues or public provision. Arguments for extending the market are better supply, shorter queues and a clear price signal. Arguments against are that payment can change motives and the meaning of the activity.

**Worked example 4 (a market for blood, invented Country B).** Voluntary donors: 10,000, with 2% of donations rejected. Payment of £20 is introduced. Donors rise to 12,000 but rejections rise to 6%, because some donors now hide risks. Screening costs £15 per donor.

- Usable units before: 10,000 × 0.98 = 9,800. After: 12,000 × 0.94 = 11,280, a **15.1% rise**.
- Cost before: 10,000 × 15 = £150,000, or £15.31 per usable unit.
- Cost after: 12,000 × (15 + 20) = £420,000, or £37.23 per usable unit.

The market raises quantity but cost per usable unit more than doubles. The specification point is that **payment changes the nature of the transaction and the incentives**. People who gave from a sense of duty may stop when giving becomes a sale (crowding out of intrinsic motives), and extrinsic rewards may attract donors with the most reason to hide their health. Judge by weighing the extra supply against quality, cost and the meaning attached to giving. Other examples include charging for road space or charging for school places.

## The meaning of market failure

**Market failure occurs whenever a market leads to a misallocation of resources.** The market outcome differs from the one that would maximise social welfare. Compare with the ideal where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost.

### Complete and partial failure

- **Complete failure (missing market):** the market does not exist at all, so nothing is produced although it would be worthwhile.
- **Partial failure:** the market exists but produces the wrong quantity or price.

**Worked example 5 (missing market, invented).** Three neighbours would pay £10, £8 and £6 a month for a street light. It costs £15 a month.

1. Total willingness to pay is 10 + 8 + 6 = £24, which exceeds the cost of £15.
2. Net benefit if provided = 24 − 15 = **£9**.
3. If the light is provided, nobody can be stopped from using it, so each neighbour hopes the others will pay. A private firm cannot collect revenue, so no market forms and the £9 of net benefit is lost.

**Worked example 6 (partial failure, invented).** Marginal private benefit is 20 − 0.05Q. Marginal private cost is 4 + 0.03Q. Each unit also imposes an external cost of £2, so marginal social cost is 6 + 0.03Q.

1. Market: 20 − 0.05Q = 4 + 0.03Q, so Q = 200 and P = £10.
2. Social optimum: 20 − 0.05Q = 6 + 0.03Q, so Q = 175 and P = £11.25.
3. The market overproduces by 25 units. The welfare loss is a triangle: ½ × £2 × 25 = **£25**.

The market exists, but it misallocates. Detailed treatment is left to the guides on public goods, externalities and policy.

### Overview of causes

| Cause | Why resources are misallocated | Type | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public goods | Non-excludability means no payment | Complete | Street lighting |
| Negative externality | Third-party costs ignored | Partial | Factory smoke |
| Positive externality | Third-party benefits ignored | Partial | Vaccination |
| Merit goods | Benefits underestimated | Partial | Education |
| Demerit goods | Harms underestimated | Partial | Tobacco |
| Monopoly and other imperfections | Price above marginal cost, immobile resources, poor information | Partial | A sole supplier |
| Inequality | Allocation follows income, not need | Partial | Unaffordable essentials |

## Common errors

- Writing "price rises, demand falls" with no link to rationing or signalling. Name the function and give the chain.
- Calling any unfairness market failure. Show that resources are misallocated or that society would gain from change.
- Mixing up a missing market with a market that is merely small.
- Listing disadvantages of the price mechanism without examples.
- Treating a blood market as good or bad without weighing motives, safety and cost.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three functions of the price mechanism?**
Rationing, where a higher price limits demand to the available supply; incentive, where higher prices and profits reward producers for supplying more; and signalling, where price changes tell buyers and sellers that conditions have changed.

**How does the price mechanism decide what, how and for whom to produce?**
Resources move towards goods with rising prices and profits, firms choose the cheapest method at current input prices, and goods go to those willing and able to pay.

**What is the difference between complete and partial market failure?**
Complete market failure is a missing market, where nothing is produced, as with a pure public good. Partial failure is when a market exists but produces the wrong quantity or price.

**Is the price mechanism impersonal?**
Yes, in that it allocates by willingness and ability to pay without favouritism. Critics argue this means it responds to purchasing power, not need, especially for necessities.

**What are the causes of market failure in AQA A-level Economics?**
Public goods, positive and negative externalities, merit and demerit goods, monopoly and other market imperfections, and inequality in the distribution of income and wealth.
