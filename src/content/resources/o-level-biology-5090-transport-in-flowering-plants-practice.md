These questions cover Topic 7, Transport in flowering plants, of the Cambridge O Level Biology 5090 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028 (version 4): section 7.1 Uptake and transport of water and ions and section 7.2 Transpiration and translocation. The syllabus is not tiered. Questions 3, 4, 11 and 12 use practical contexts; a calculator is useful for questions 4 and 11.

Learn the content first with the [study guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-transport-in-flowering-plants/) and [revision notes](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-transport-in-flowering-plants-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Describe what is meant by *transpiration*. **[2]**

**2.** Water moves from the soil to the leaves of a plant.

**(a)** State **one** feature of a root hair cell that increases water uptake, and explain how it does so. **[2]**
**(b)** Outline the pathway taken by water from a root hair cell to the cells inside a leaf. **[3]**

**3.** A student wants to find which tissue carries water up a stem.

**(a)** Describe a method the student could use, including a suitable stain. **[4]**
**(b)** State where the stain would appear in a transverse section of the stem. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why the stem should be cut under water before it is placed in the stain. **[1]**

**4.** (calculator allowed) A potometer was used to measure water uptake by a leafy shoot at three temperatures. The bubble moved along a capillary tube with a cross-sectional area of 0.8 mm². Each reading was taken over 4 minutes.

| Temperature / °C | Distance moved by bubble in 4 min / mm |
|---|---|
| 10 | 12 |
| 20 | 22 |
| 30 | 38 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate of bubble movement, in mm per minute, at 10 °C and at 30 °C. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of water taken up per minute at 30 °C. Give your units. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the effect of temperature on the rate of transpiration. **[2]**
**(d)** State **one** variable, other than the shoot, that should be kept constant. **[1]**

**5.** Transpiration rate changes with the weather.

**(a)** Explain why transpiration is slower when the air is humid. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why transpiration is faster on a windy day. **[2]**

**6.** A pot plant is left on a sunny windowsill on a hot day without being watered. By the afternoon its leaves are drooping. Explain how this wilting occurs. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the mechanism by which water moves upwards in the xylem of a tall plant. **[4]**

**8.** Potato plants store starch in tubers underground.

**(a)** Describe translocation. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify whether a potato tuber is a source or a sink (i) in summer, when the leaves are photosynthesising, and (ii) in spring, when new shoots are growing from it. Give a reason for each answer. **[4]**

**9.** A transverse section of a non-woody dicotyledonous organ shows a ring of vascular bundles near the outside, with a band of cells outside them. In each bundle, tissue X faces the centre and tissue Y faces the outside.

**(a)** State whether this is a root or a stem, and give a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify tissue X and tissue Y. **[1]**
**(c)** Name the band of cells between the bundles and the outer surface. **[1]**

**10.** Xylem vessels are adapted to their functions.

**(a)** State **two** functions of xylem. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how each of these features adapts xylem vessels for their functions: (i) walls thickened with lignin; (ii) cells joined end-to-end with no cross walls. **[4]**

**11.** (calculator allowed) Four similar leaves were treated with grease (which waterproofs a surface) and hung up in the same room for 48 hours.

| Leaf | Surface greased | Mass at start / g | Mass after 48 h / g | Percentage loss in mass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | none | 2.50 | 1.75 | 30.0 |
| B | upper only | 2.40 | 1.74 | 27.5 |
| C | lower only | 2.60 | 2.47 | |
| D | both | 2.50 | 2.43 | |

**(a)** Explain why the results are compared as percentage loss in mass, not mass lost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage loss in mass for leaves C and D. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how leaf A lost mass. **[2]**
**(d)** What do the results show about the distribution of stomata? Use data in your answer. **[2]**
**(e)** Suggest why leaf D still lost some mass. **[1]**

**12.** Plan an investigation, using a potometer, into the effect of light intensity on the rate of transpiration of a leafy shoot. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** The loss of water vapour [1] from the leaves of a plant (through the stomata) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "loss of water" alone is unlikely to score; the word vapour and the link to leaves are what the marks look for.

**2. (a)** Long, thin extension (hair) [1], which gives a large surface area for water to enter by osmosis [1]. (Accept: cell sap with a lower water potential than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis.)
**(b)** From root hair cells through the **root cortex cells** [1], into the **xylem**, which carries water up the root and stem to the leaf veins [1], then into the **mesophyll cells** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* the pathway is marked as a sequence, so a correct list in the wrong order, or one that skips the cortex, loses credit.

**3. (a)** Stand a leafy stem (e.g. celery) in a coloured stain such as eosin [1]. Leave it for a few hours in warm, bright conditions so water is drawn up [1]. Cut thin transverse sections at several heights with a sharp blade [1]. Examine the sections with a hand lens or microscope to see which tissue is stained [1].
**(b)** In the **xylem** of the vascular bundles [1].
**(c)** To stop air entering the xylem and blocking the flow of water [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* a method answer needs the stain named and the sectioning step; "put it in dye and look" covers only one point.

**4. (a)** 10 °C: 12 ÷ 4 = **3.0 mm per minute** [1]. 30 °C: 38 ÷ 4 = **9.5 mm per minute** [1].
**(b)** Volume per minute = 9.5 × 0.8 [1] = **7.6 mm³ per minute** [1].
**(c)** As temperature rises the rate increases [1], because water molecules have more kinetic energy, so they evaporate from mesophyll cell surfaces and diffuse out of the stomata faster [1].
**(d)** Any one: light intensity; wind speed (air movement); humidity; time allowed for the shoot to settle [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* a volume needs mm³ as the unit, and a rate needs "per minute"; an answer with no units is usually not given the accuracy mark.

**5. (a)** Humid air already contains more water vapour [1], so the concentration gradient between the air spaces in the leaf and the outside air is smaller and water vapour diffuses out more slowly [1].
**(b)** Wind blows away water vapour from around the leaf surface [1], keeping the concentration gradient steep, so diffusion out through the stomata is faster [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each part needs the cause and the gradient link; "less diffusion" alone gives half the credit at most.

**6.** High temperature and bright light increase transpiration [1]. Water is lost from the leaves faster than it is taken up from the dry soil [1]. The cells lose water and become flaccid / lose turgor pressure [1]. They no longer press against each other to support the leaves and soft stem, so these droop [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* use flaccid or turgor; "the plant runs out of water" is too vague for the cell-level marks.

**7.** Water evaporates from the mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata (transpiration) [1]. Water moves from the xylem into the mesophyll cells to replace it, creating a transpiration pull [1]. Water molecules are held together by forces of attraction between them [1], so a continuous column of water is drawn up the xylem from the roots [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the pull starts at the leaves; "sucked up by the roots" does not match the mechanism asked for.

**8. (a)** The movement of sucrose and amino acids in the phloem [1], from sources, where they are produced or released, to sinks, where they are used or stored [1].
**(b) (i)** Sink [1]: it receives sucrose from the leaves and stores it (as starch) [1].
**(ii)** Source [1]: it releases stored food, carried to the growing shoots where it is used [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* each identification needs its reason to gain the second mark; "sink because it is underground" is not a reason.

**9. (a)** Stem [1], because the vascular bundles are in a ring near the outside, not in the centre [1].
**(b)** X is xylem and Y is phloem [1].
**(c)** Cortex [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* both tissues must be correct for (b); swapping xylem and phloem in a stem bundle loses the mark.

**10. (a)** Transport of water and mineral ions [1]; support [1].
**(b) (i)** Lignin makes the walls strong and rigid [1], so the vessels support the plant and do not collapse as water is pulled up [1].
**(ii)** There are no cross walls, so the cells form a long continuous tube [1] and water can flow in an unbroken column from root to leaf without obstruction [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* name the feature and then the function it serves; a feature with no "so that" link is unlikely to gain the second mark in each pair.

**11. (a)** The leaves had different starting masses, so percentage loss allows a fair comparison [1].
**(b)** C: (2.60 − 2.47) ÷ 2.60 × 100 = **5.0%** [1]. D: (2.50 − 2.43) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = **2.8%** [1].
**(c)** Water evaporated from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces [1], and the water vapour diffused out through the stomata [1].
**(d)** Most stomata are on the lower surface [1]: greasing the lower surface cut the loss from 30.0% to 5.0%, but greasing the upper surface only reduced it to 27.5% [1].
**(e)** Any one: the grease did not seal the surface completely; water was lost from the cut leaf stalk [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "use data" means quoting figures; a conclusion with no numbers is likely to gain only one mark.

**12.** Fit a leafy shoot into the potometer under water, with airtight joints [1]. Place a lamp 10 cm from the shoot, with a beaker of water between as a heat filter [1]. After a few minutes to settle, measure the distance the bubble moves in 5 minutes [1]. Repeat at 20, 30 and 40 cm with the same shoot [1]. Keep temperature, wind speed and humidity constant (same room, no fan) [1]. Take three readings at each distance and calculate a mean [1]. Calculate rate as distance ÷ time and plot rate against lamp distance [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* a plan is credited for the variable changed, what is measured and what is controlled; a lamp also heats the shoot, so the heat filter matters.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that water evaporates from the stomata instead of from mesophyll cell surfaces.
- Giving the effect of a factor without the reason (concentration gradient or stomatal opening).
- Missing out root cortex cells or mesophyll cells in the water pathway.
- Describing a wilted plant without flaccid or turgor.
- Placing phloem on the inside of a stem vascular bundle.
- Forgetting the heat filter or the settling time in a potometer plan.
