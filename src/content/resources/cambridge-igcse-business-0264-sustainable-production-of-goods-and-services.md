This guide goes deeper on section 4.3 (Sustainable production of goods and services) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Its one content statement, 4.3.1 Methods of sustainable production of goods and services, lists "how businesses can be more sustainable: using renewable energy, using fewer resources / less waste, reusing, recycling, developing environmentally friendly products, using environmentally friendly packaging" and the "advantages and disadvantages of businesses becoming more sustainable". The broader [technology and production guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-technology-and-production-of-goods-and-services-and-related-topics/) defines the six methods with a soap-maker example. This page applies them to a service business, compares several options at once, and shows how to reach a justified recommendation. Revise with the [sustainable production revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-sustainable-production-of-goods-and-services-revision-notes/) and practise with the [sustainable production practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-sustainable-production-of-goods-and-services-practice/).

All businesses and figures below are invented. Money is in dollars.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Point in 4.3.1 | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Six ways to be more sustainable | Recognise each method in a case and suggest a suitable one for a given business |
| Advantages and disadvantages | Weigh the benefits against the costs for the business in the question, then judge |

Section 4.3 is part of operations management: it is about *how goods and services are produced*. Section 6.3, Business and the environment, looks at the wider effects of business activity and the laws that control them. A question on 4.3 wants practical changes to production.

## The six methods applied to goods and to services

Students often find it easy to apply the methods to a factory and hard to apply them to a service. The table does both.

| Method | Manufacturer example: a furniture maker | Service example: a hotel |
|---|---|---|
| Using renewable energy | Solar panels on the workshop roof power the saws | Solar water heating for guest showers |
| Using fewer resources / less waste | Computer-planned cutting gets more parts from each sheet of wood | Guests choose whether towels are washed daily, cutting water and energy use |
| Reusing | Delivery blankets used again instead of throwaway wrapping | Refillable shampoo dispensers instead of small bottles |
| Recycling | Sawdust sold to be pressed into fuel pellets | Separate bins so glass, paper and cans go for recycling |
| Environmentally friendly products | Furniture made from timber grown in managed forests, designed to be repaired | Meals made with local, seasonal food to reduce transport |
| Environmentally friendly packaging | Recycled cardboard corners instead of plastic foam | Card key wallets and toiletry wrappers made from recycled paper |

Two distinctions earn marks.

- **Reusing** keeps the item in use as it is (a refilled dispenser). **Recycling** breaks it down into material for something new (crushed glass made into new bottles). Reusing usually saves more, because no energy is needed to reprocess the material.
- **Fewer resources** is about using less in the first place. Recycling deals with waste after it is created. Using fewer resources avoids creating it.

## Worked example: Ellery Hotel compares three options

Ellery Hotel has 60 rooms. The manager has a budget of $50,000 this year to make the hotel more sustainable and has three proposals.

| Option | Method | Cost to introduce | Yearly saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: refillable dispensers in every bathroom | Reusing; less packaging waste | $4,800 | $6,000 on small bottles, less $2,400 on bulk refills = $3,600 |
| B: solar water heating | Renewable energy | $45,000 | $7,500 on electricity |
| C: towel reuse scheme with signs in every room | Fewer resources | $500 | $4,000 on laundry |

**How long until savings cover the cost?**

```
Option A: 4,800 / 3,600 = 1.33 years (about 16 months)
Option B: 45,000 / 7,500 = 6 years
Option C: 500 / 4,000 = 0.125 years (about 6 weeks)
```

**Can it afford them together?** A + C = $5,300. A + B = $49,800. All three = $50,300, just over budget.

**Factors the numbers leave out**

- Option B cuts the hotel's use of electricity from fossil fuels the most, and the saving continues long after six years. It also gives a visible message to guests. But it uses almost all the budget, and the saving depends on sunny weather and electricity prices.
- Option A removes thousands of small plastic bottles a year. Some guests in a luxury hotel may see dispensers as cheap, so it suits Ellery only if its guests accept them.
- Option C costs almost nothing and saves quickly, but depends on guests taking part.

**Judgement.** Ellery should introduce A and B this year (total $49,800, within budget), because A recovers its cost quickly and B has the largest long-term effect on energy use. Option C costs so little that the manager could find the extra $500 elsewhere, such as from the first months of savings from Option A. If the hotel's guests are mainly price-conscious families, they are unlikely to object to dispensers, which supports Option A.

## Worked example: a packaging decision

Brackwell Crisps sells 2,000,000 bags a year in plastic film costing $0.04 a bag. Compostable film costs $0.07 a bag.

```
Extra cost per bag  = 0.07 - 0.04 = $0.03
Extra cost per year = 0.03 x 2,000,000 = $60,000
```

Brackwell can either absorb the $60,000 (lower profit) or raise its price. A price rise of $0.05 a bag would more than cover the extra cost if sales held up, but price-sensitive customers may switch to rivals. The decision depends on how much Brackwell's customers value environmentally friendly packaging. Market research (section 3.2) would help it find out before making the change.

## Advantages and disadvantages in context

Generic lists lose marks. Tie each point to the kind of business.

| | Strongest when | Weakest when |
|---|---|---|
| Lower running costs from less energy and waste | The business uses a lot of energy or materials (a bakery, a hotel, a factory) | Its main cost is labour, such as a consultancy |
| Better reputation and higher sales | Customers can see the change and care about it | Customers mainly buy on price |
| Easier to meet laws and avoid fines | Rules in its industry are tightening | Rules are unlikely to change |
| Motivated employees | Staff are proud of the firm's values | Staff see it as extra work with no explanation |
| High start-up costs | The business is short of cash or finance | It has savings or easy access to loans |
| Higher input costs | Competitors with cheaper methods win on price | Customers will pay more for a sustainable product |

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **calculate**, **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Identify:** name the method exactly as the syllabus does, such as "using renewable energy".
- **Explain:** name the method, show how the business would use it, then give the effect on costs, sales or reputation.
- **Calculate:** extra cost per unit times units, or cost divided by yearly saving. Show the working and the units ("years", "$ a year").
- **Consider / justify:** compare options, use the figures *and* the non-financial factors, then recommend one and give the deciding reason.

## Common errors

- Treating reusing and recycling as the same thing.
- Giving only manufacturing examples when the business is a service.
- Saying sustainability "always saves money". Many changes cost more at first, and some raise costs permanently.
- Recommending the option with the quickest saving without considering its size or its effect on the environment.
- Writing about pollution laws and government fines in detail when the question is about methods of production.
