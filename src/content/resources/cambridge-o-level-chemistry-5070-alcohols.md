Section 11.6 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has three learning outcomes. You must be able to describe the manufacture of ethanol by fermentation "of aqueous glucose at 25–35 °C in the presence of yeast and in the absence of oxygen" and by "catalytic addition of steam to ethene at 300 °C and 6000 kPa / 60 atm in the presence of an acid catalyst", including "a comparison of the advantages and disadvantages of the two methods"; describe the combustion of alcohols; and state the uses of ethanol as a solvent and a fuel. 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so every outcome is examined for every candidate.

The broader [Alcohols and Carboxylic Acids guide](/resources/alcohols-and-carboxylic-acids/) summarises both manufacturing routes. This guide explains why each condition is chosen, builds a comparison you can apply to unfamiliar scenarios, and practises balanced combustion equations for alcohols other than ethanol, since the syllabus says "alcohols", not only ethanol.

## Route 1: fermentation

Yeast converts aqueous glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide:

```
glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide
C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2
```

Each condition has a reason, and "explain" questions ask for it.

| Condition | Reason |
|---|---|
| Aqueous glucose | the glucose must be in solution for the yeast to use it |
| Yeast | contains the enzymes that catalyse the reaction |
| 25–35 °C | warm enough for a reasonable rate; higher temperatures denature the enzymes in the yeast and the reaction stops |
| Absence of oxygen | with oxygen present, the yeast respires aerobically, producing carbon dioxide and water instead of ethanol, and the ethanol can be oxidised to ethanoic acid |

The product is a **dilute aqueous solution** of ethanol mixed with yeast and other substances. Ethanol is obtained from it by filtration to remove the yeast, followed by **fractional distillation**.

**Worked example 1.** Calculate the maximum mass of ethanol that can be made by fermenting 90 g of glucose. (Mr: C₆H₁₂O₆ = 180, C₂H₅OH = 46)

*Answer.* Moles of glucose = 90 ÷ 180 = 0.50 mol [1]. From the equation, 1 mol glucose gives 2 mol ethanol, so 1.0 mol ethanol [1]. Mass = 1.0 × 46 = **46 g** [1].

## Route 2: catalytic addition of steam to ethene

Ethene (from cracking, section 11.5) reacts with steam:

```
ethene + steam → ethanol
C2H4 + H2O → C2H5OH
```

Conditions: **300 °C**, **6000 kPa (60 atm)** and an **acid catalyst**. This is an addition reaction, so ethanol is the only product. The process runs **continuously**: ethene and steam are fed in and ethanol is removed without stopping.

## Comparing the two methods

| Factor | Fermentation | Steam + ethene |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material | glucose from plants: **renewable** | ethene from petroleum: **non-renewable** |
| Rate | slow | fast |
| Product | dilute, impure solution; needs fractional distillation | pure ethanol |
| Conditions | low temperature, atmospheric pressure: low energy cost | high temperature and pressure: high energy cost, more expensive plant |
| How it runs | in tanks, stopped and restarted for each load | continuous |
| Other products | carbon dioxide | none (addition) |

### Applying the comparison

Questions often describe a country or company and ask which method suits it. Match the scenario to the table.

**Worked example 2.** Country X grows large amounts of sugar cane but has no petroleum reserves. Country Y has a large oil refinery that cracks naphtha. Suggest which method each country should use. Explain your answers. [4]

*Annotated answer.* "Country X should use fermentation [1], because it has a plentiful supply of renewable plant material to provide glucose and has no petroleum to make ethene [1]. Country Y should use steam and ethene [1], because cracking at its refinery supplies ethene, and this method produces pure ethanol quickly and continuously [1]."

Each mark ties a method to a reason **from the scenario**. Listing every advantage of each method without reference to X and Y scores less.

## Combustion of alcohols

Alcohols burn in plenty of air to form **carbon dioxide and water**, releasing energy. The syllabus refers to the combustion of alcohols in general, so be ready to balance equations for alcohols other than ethanol.

| Alcohol | Balanced equation for complete combustion |
|---|---|
| Methanol | 2CH₃OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 4H₂O |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O |
| Propan-1-ol | 2C₃H₇OH + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 8H₂O |
| Butan-1-ol | C₄H₉OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O |

**Worked example 3.** Balance the equation for the complete combustion of propan-1-ol, C₃H₇OH.

1. Carbon: 3 C → 3CO₂.
2. Hydrogen: 8 H in total (7 + 1) → 4H₂O.
3. Oxygen needed on the right: (3 × 2) + 4 = 10 atoms. The alcohol already supplies **1**, so 9 more are needed: 4½O₂.
4. Double: 2C₃H₇OH + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 8H₂O.

Propan-2-ol has the same molecular formula, so its combustion equation is identical; only the arrangement of atoms differs, not the numbers.

With a limited supply of air, alcohols undergo **incomplete combustion**, like any carbon-containing fuel, and toxic carbon monoxide forms:

```
C2H5OH + 2O2 → 2CO + 3H2O
```

The most common error is forgetting the oxygen atom already in the alcohol, which gives too much O₂. A second error is counting only 7 hydrogens: the H in –OH counts too.

## Uses of ethanol

| Use | Property behind it |
|---|---|
| Solvent | dissolves many substances that do not dissolve in water, and evaporates easily |
| Fuel | burns readily, releasing a lot of energy; when made by fermentation it comes from a renewable source |

When ethanol made by fermentation is burned, the carbon dioxide released was originally taken in by the plants during photosynthesis. That is one reason ethanol from crops is promoted as a fuel.

## Common mistakes

- Giving fermentation temperatures such as 37 °C or 100 °C. Learn **25–35 °C**.
- Writing "in the presence of oxygen" for fermentation.
- Forgetting the acid catalyst or giving the pressure as 6000 atm. It is **6000 kPa** or **60 atm**.
- Saying fermentation produces pure ethanol.
- Listing carbon dioxide as a product of the steam and ethene route. Carbon dioxide forms only in fermentation; addition gives ethanol alone.
- Calling the ethene route renewable. Ethene comes from petroleum.
- Forgetting the oxygen in the alcohol when balancing a combustion equation.
- Giving uses of ethanol outside the syllabus list instead of **solvent** and **fuel**.

## Exam technique: the command words

- **Describe** (manufacture): reactants, all conditions with numbers, and the product; include the equation if space allows.
- **Explain** (a condition): link it to the yeast, rate or purity.
- **Compare** / **discuss**: give advantages and disadvantages of **both** methods, matched factor by factor.
- **Suggest**: apply the comparison to the scenario given.
