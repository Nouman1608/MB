## Section A

**1.** Define the term watershed. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between throughflow and percolation. **[2]**

**3.** A river channel is 6 m wide with a mean depth of 0.8 m. Its mean velocity is 1.5 m per second. Calculate the discharge, giving units. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between the competence and the capacity of a river. **[2]**

**5.** Outline why a basin on permeable chalk keeps a higher baseflow in a dry summer than a basin on impermeable clay. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A rectangular channel has a cross-sectional area of 18 m squared.

**(a)** Calculate the hydraulic radius if the channel is 9 m wide and 2 m deep. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the hydraulic radius if the channel is 18 m wide and 1 m deep. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain which channel is likely to flow faster for the same gradient. **[2]**

**7.** The table shows an illustrative soil water budget. The soil holds 120 mm when full and is full at the start of Month 1.

| Month | P (mm) | PET (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90 | 40 |
| 2 | 60 | 100 |
| 3 | 30 | 150 |
| 4 | 80 | 50 |

**(a)** State the soil moisture store at the end of Month 2. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the deficit in Month 3. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what happens to river discharge in Month 4 compared with Month 1. **[3]**

**8.** Two storms each deliver 36 mm of rain on the same basin. Storm X lasts 2 hours; Storm Y lasts 18 hours. The soil's infiltration capacity is 10 mm per hour.

**(a)** Calculate the mean rainfall intensity of each storm. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why Storm X is more likely to produce a flashy hydrograph. **[3]**

## Section C

**9.** Explain the formation of a delta, referring to changes in velocity and load. **[6]**

**10.** Examine the factors that affect the movement of water through the stores of a drainage basin. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The boundary of a drainage basin, usually a ridge of high land, dividing it from neighbouring basins [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Throughflow is the lateral movement of water through the soil towards the channel [1]; percolation is the downward movement of water from the soil into the underlying rock, recharging groundwater [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Area = 6 x 0.8 = 4.8 m squared [1]. Discharge = 4.8 x 1.5 = **7.2 cubic metres per second (cumecs)** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Competence is the largest particle size a river can carry at a given velocity [1]; capacity is the total quantity of load it can carry [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Chalk is permeable, so rain percolates into large groundwater stores [1], which release water slowly into the river as baseflow for months after rain, whereas clay lets little water percolate, so there is little stored groundwater to sustain flow [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Wetted perimeter = 2 + 9 + 2 = 13 m [1]. Hydraulic radius = 18 / 13 = **1.38 m** [1].

**(b)** Wetted perimeter = 1 + 18 + 1 = 20 m, so hydraulic radius = 18 / 20 = **0.9 m** [1].

**(c)** The 9 m by 2 m channel [1], because its larger hydraulic radius means proportionally less water is in contact with the bed and banks, so less energy is lost to friction and more goes into flow [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Month 1: P - PET = +50 mm, but the store is already full, so it stays at 120 mm. Month 2: P - PET = -40 mm, so the store falls to **80 mm** [1].

**(b)** Month 3: demand exceeds rain by 150 - 30 = 120 mm [1]. The store supplies its remaining 80 mm, leaving a deficit of 120 - 80 = **40 mm** [1].

**(c)** In Month 1 the 50 mm surplus drains from a full soil to the river, so discharge is high [1]. In Month 4 there is a 30 mm surplus, but the soil store is empty after Month 3, so the water goes into recharging the soil rather than draining [1]. Discharge therefore stays low in Month 4, fed mainly by baseflow, until the store is refilled [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Storm X: 36 / 2 = **18 mm per hour** [1]. Storm Y: 36 / 18 = **2 mm per hour** [1].

**(b)** Storm X's intensity of 18 mm per hour exceeds the infiltration capacity of 10 mm per hour [1], so about 8 mm per hour cannot infiltrate and becomes infiltration-excess overland flow [1]. Overland flow reaches the channel quickly, giving a shorter lag time and higher peak; Storm Y's rain can all infiltrate and travel slowly as throughflow and groundwater flow [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 6:

- Where the river enters a sea or lake, it meets standing water, so its velocity falls sharply [1].
- Falling velocity reduces competence and capacity, so the load is deposited [1].
- Coarser bedload is dropped first, nearest the mouth; finer suspended load is carried further out [1].
- In salt water, fine clay particles clump together (flocculation) and settle [1].
- A delta forms only where sediment is supplied faster than waves, tides and currents can remove it, so low-energy coasts favour deltas [1].
- Deposition blocks the channel, which splits into distributaries; repeated splitting and deposition build the delta outwards, with shapes such as arcuate or bird's foot depending on the balance of river and marine energy [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content. A strong answer organises factors and examines how they interact.

- **Precipitation characteristics:** intensity relative to infiltration capacity; snow acting as a store; duration and antecedent moisture deciding whether soils saturate [1] [1].
- **Vegetation:** interception delays and reduces water reaching the ground; roots raise infiltration; transpiration lowers soil moisture between storms [1].
- **Soil and geology:** permeable rock and deep porous soils favour percolation and groundwater storage; impermeable rock and thin or compacted soils favour overland flow [1] [1].
- **Relief and basin shape:** steep slopes speed overland flow; circular basins concentrate flow; high drainage density shortens travel to the channel [1].
- **Human activity:** urban surfaces and drains, deforestation, ploughing and groundwater abstraction alter stores and flows [1].
- **Examination of interaction:** for example, forest reduces runoff in moderate storms but has less effect once soils are saturated in prolonged rain [1]; geology sets long-term storage while storm intensity controls the short-term pathway [1].
- **Conclusion:** a supported judgement, such as that no single factor dominates and the relative importance depends on the scale of the storm and the time period considered [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Examine" rewards showing how factors interact and when each matters most, not a list. Located examples and precise flow names (infiltration-excess, saturation-excess, baseflow) lift an answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving discharge without units, or forgetting to multiply width by depth first.
- Forgetting that a full soil cannot store a surplus, or that an empty soil must recharge before drainage resumes.
- Writing "more runoff" without naming the flow and its speed.
- Explaining a delta without linking deposition to the fall in velocity and competence.
