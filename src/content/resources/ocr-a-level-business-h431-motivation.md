## What motivation is

**Motivation** is the desire to work towards a goal: the reason why a person puts in effort. Motivation is not the same as satisfaction. A person can be satisfied with a comfortable job and still put in little effort. Businesses want motivation because effort affects output, quality, absence and the cost of replacing staff who leave.

## The theories

### Content theories: what motivates people

**Maslow's hierarchy of needs** says people have five levels of need and move up only when the lower level is mostly met:

1. Physiological (pay enough to eat, rest breaks).
2. Safety (job security, safe conditions).
3. Social (belonging, friendship at work).
4. Esteem (status, recognition, respect).
5. Self-actualisation (reaching one's potential).

The business lesson is to find which level a worker is at. A pay rise may do little for a person who already has security but craves recognition.

**Herzberg's two-factor theory** separates:

- **Hygiene factors** (pay, conditions, supervision, company policy, job security). If poor, they cause dissatisfaction. If good, they only remove dissatisfaction; they do not motivate.
- **Motivators** (achievement, recognition, interesting work, responsibility, promotion). These raise motivation.

The lesson is to fix hygiene factors first, then add motivators through job enrichment.

**McClelland's theory of needs** says that people are driven by one of three learned needs:

- **Achievement:** wants challenging goals and feedback.
- **Power:** wants to influence others.
- **Affiliation:** wants to belong and be liked.

The lesson is to match tasks to needs: stretch targets for the achiever, a leadership role for the power-seeker, team projects for the affiliator.

### Expectancy theory: Vroom

Vroom says that people ask three questions before making an effort:

- **Expectancy (E):** will my effort lead to good performance? (a probability from 0 to 1)
- **Instrumentality (I):** will good performance lead to a reward? (0 to 1)
- **Valence (V):** how much do I value the reward? (a score, here 0 to 10)

**Motivational force = E × I × V.** If any one factor is zero, the force is zero.

### Goal-setting theory: Locke and Drucker

**Locke** found that specific, challenging goals, which the person accepts and receives feedback on, lead to higher performance than vague goals such as "do your best". **Drucker's management by objectives (MBO)** applies this: manager and employee agree objectives together, review progress and discuss results. MBO links individual goals to business goals and gives staff a say.

### Excellence theory: Peters

Peters argued that the most successful organisations treat staff as their main source of quality and productivity. His ideas include giving autonomy, encouraging initiative, staying close to customers and valuing people. Motivation comes from trust and a sense of ownership rather than detailed control.

## Methods of motivation

| Type | Examples | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monetary | Pay, bonus, commission, profit share, share options | Works fast; easy to understand | Cost; effect fades; may encourage short-term focus |
| Non-monetary | Recognition, job enrichment, training, flexible hours, team projects | Cheaper; may last longer | Slower; depends on managers; not every worker values it |

**Job enrichment** gives more depth (planning, deciding) and so matches Herzberg's motivators. **Job enlargement** adds more tasks at the same level and may not motivate. **Job rotation** moves staff between tasks to reduce boredom.

## Worked example 1: applying Vroom

Maple Contact Centre employs 40 agents. The manager is deciding between three rewards. Estimates from a staff survey (invented):

| Option | E | I | V | Force = E × I × V |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: pooled team bonus | 0.8 | 0.5 | 6 | 2.4 |
| B: individual bonus | 0.8 | 0.9 | 6 | 4.32 |
| C: extra day of leave | 0.8 | 0.9 | 8 | 5.76 |

Working for C: 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72; 0.72 × 8 = **5.76**.

Reading the table: A scores low because agents doubt that their own efforts affect a pooled bonus (I is only 0.5). Option B raises I to 0.9, which lifts the force by 80% (4.32 ÷ 2.4 = 1.8). Option C is valued most (V = 8), so it has the highest force.

Limits: the scores are guesses, and the product treats all three factors as equal in weight. Vroom's value is in showing *which* link to repair: if agents do not believe effort leads to reward, a larger bonus will not help.

## Worked example 2: cost and benefit of a bonus

The manager decides to test option B: a £150 monthly bonus for each agent who reaches target. Before the scheme, an agent handles an average of 60 calls a day; after it, 66. There are 40 agents, 22 working days a month and each call is worth £2.50 in revenue.

- Extra calls per month = (66 − 60) × 40 × 22 = **5,280**.
- Extra revenue = 5,280 × £2.50 = **£13,200**.
- Bonus cost, if all 40 agents qualify = 40 × £150 = **£6,000**.
- Net gain = £13,200 − £6,000 = **£7,200** a month.

Evaluation. The scheme pays for itself on these figures, with a 10% rise in calls per agent (6 ÷ 60). But: the improvement may fall as agents get used to the bonus; the pressure to reach target may lower call quality or raise stress; and the revenue per call may fall if calls are rushed. A sensible manager would track customer satisfaction and sickness absence as well as call numbers. Combined with non-monetary methods such as recognition and coaching, the effect is more likely to last.

## Evaluating the theories

| Theory | Useful for | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Maslow | Identifying unmet needs | People do not always climb the levels in order |
| Herzberg | Job design and the pay debate | Based on a limited sample; some staff value pay highly |
| McClelland | Matching jobs to people | Needs are hard to measure |
| Vroom | Explaining why rewards fail | Scores are subjective |
| Locke and Drucker | Targets and appraisals | Targets can narrow focus or be set too high |
| Peters | Culture and empowerment | Hard to measure; needs trusted staff |

A good answer compares theories for the case: Herzberg suits a business with a pay dispute, Vroom suits one whose bonus has not worked, Locke and Drucker suit a business setting targets, and Peters suits a business with skilled staff and a culture of trust.

## Impact and importance

Motivated staff usually mean higher productivity, better quality, lower absence and lower turnover. In a service business such as a restaurant or a call centre, motivation also shows up in how customers are treated. Importance is greatest where staff have discretion, where labour is a large share of cost and where skills are scarce. It is less critical in a fully automated process, although even there the staff who maintain machines need motivation.

For stakeholders: owners gain from profit; managers from easier supervision; customers from service; staff from pay, security and satisfaction; and the community from stable employment.

## Recommending methods

To recommend and justify, tie the method to the diagnosis. Example: a hotel group with high turnover among housekeeping staff on minimum pay.

- Diagnosis (Herzberg): hygiene factors, pay and shift patterns, are causing dissatisfaction.
- Recommendation: first raise pay slightly above the local going rate and give predictable rosters, then add recognition and a path to supervisor roles as motivators.
- Risk: the pay rise raises costs and its effect will fade if only pay changes.
- Judgement: both parts are needed. Pay removes the main cause of leaving; recognition and progression make staying attractive.

## Common mistakes

- Treating pay as the only motivator.
- Confusing motivation with satisfaction.
- Describing a theory without applying it to the case.
- Using the same theory for every case; choose the one that fits the problem.
- Forgetting the cost of a method when recommending it.

## Exam technique

"Explain" needs the meaning and a link to the case. "Distinguish" between monetary and non-monetary needs the difference and an example of each. "Evaluate" needs both sides and a judgement. "Recommend and justify" needs a clear choice, reasons and a risk.
