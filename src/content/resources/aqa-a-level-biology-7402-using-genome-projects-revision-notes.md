These notes condense section 3.8.3 of the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), for first teaching 2015: *Using genome projects* (A-level only). For full explanations and worked examples, use the [study guide on using genome projects](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-using-genome-projects/). Then try the [practice questions on using genome projects](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-using-genome-projects-practice/).

## The four statements

1. Sequencing projects have read the genomes of a wide range of organisms, including humans.
2. In simpler organisms, the genome lets the proteome be determined, with applications such as finding potential antigens for vaccines.
3. In more complex organisms, non-coding DNA and regulatory genes mean the genome cannot easily be translated into the proteome.
4. Sequencing methods are continuously updated and have become automated.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Genome | The complete set of genes in a cell |
| Proteome | The full range of proteins that a cell is able to produce |
| Genome project | Sequencing the order of bases in all the DNA of an organism |
| Non-coding DNA | DNA that does not code for polypeptides, including introns and repeated sequences between genes |
| Intron | Non-coding section within a eukaryotic gene, removed from pre-mRNA by splicing |
| Exon | Section of a gene that codes for amino acids |
| Regulatory gene | Gene whose product controls the expression of other genes |
| Antigen | A molecule, often a protein, that triggers an immune response |
| Strain | A genetically distinct variety of a pathogen species |

## Simple versus complex genomes

| Feature | Simpler organism (e.g. bacterium) | Complex organism (e.g. mammal) |
|---|---|---|
| Proportion of genome coding for protein | High | Low |
| Introns | None | Present in most genes |
| Regulatory genes | Few | Many |
| Proteome predictable from genome? | Yes, fairly directly | Not easily |
| Proteins made in each cell | Similar in all cells of a culture | Differ between cell types |

## Reading a coding sequence

- Find the start triplet and the stop triplet in the same reading frame.
- Number of amino acids = (bases from start to stop inclusive ÷ 3) − 1.
- Worked reminder: a bacterial gene has 1,500 bases from start to stop inclusive. 1,500 ÷ 3 = 500 triplets; 500 − 1 = **499 amino acids**.
- In a eukaryotic gene, introns must be removed first or the answer is too large.

## Vaccines from genomes: the steps

1. Sequence the pathogen genome.
2. Predict the proteome.
3. Pick proteins likely to be on the pathogen's surface (reachable by antibodies).
4. Keep those with the same sequence in all strains.
5. Produce the candidates and test them for an immune response.

Worked reminder (invented): 3,200 predicted proteins; 240 likely surface proteins; 60 identical in every strain. Candidates as a percentage of the proteome = 60 ÷ 3,200 × 100 = **1.9%** (2 significant figures).

Why "potential" antigens? A predicted protein may not be made in large amounts, may not be exposed, or may not trigger a protective response. It must be tested.

## Why the genome does not give the proteome in complex organisms

- Most DNA is non-coding, so genes are hard to locate.
- Introns break up genes; exons must be identified.
- Regulatory genes decide whether, when and where genes are expressed; the base sequence alone does not say.
- Different cell types translate different parts of the same genome.
- After transcription, pre-mRNA is spliced, and many proteins are modified after translation.

## Automated sequencing: what to say

- Early methods were manual, slow and read short sections.
- Methods are continuously updated.
- Modern sequencing is automated: computers read fluorescent signals and assemble overlapping fragments.
- Time and cost per genome have fallen hugely, so many organisms and many individuals can be sequenced and compared.
- You do not need the details of any one method.

## Data skills

- **Coding DNA** = genome size × percentage coding ÷ 100. Example (invented): a genome of 2.0 million base pairs, 90% coding, has 1.8 million coding base pairs.
- **Genes per million base pairs** = number of genes ÷ genome size in millions.
- **Ratio of genome sizes**: divide the larger by the smaller and give a sensible number of significant figures.
- Comment on what a low coding percentage means: much non-coding DNA, so the proteome is hard to predict.

## Exam traps

- "Proteome = proteins being made right now". It is the proteins the cell is *able* to make.
- Forgetting the stop triplet in amino acid counts.
- Saying a larger genome must mean more genes or a more complex organism.
- Saying a genome project produces a vaccine. It identifies potential antigens; the vaccine still has to be made and tested.
- Saying a eukaryote's proteome is impossible to find. It is hard to predict from the genome, not impossible to study.
- Mixing up regulatory genes with non-coding DNA. Regulatory genes code for proteins (such as transcription factors) that control other genes.

## Quick self-test

1. Define genome.
2. Define proteome.
3. Give **two** features of a bacterial genome that make its proteome easy to predict.
4. Name the application of determining a simple organism's proteome given in the specification.
5. Why are surface proteins chosen as potential antigens?
6. Why compare the genomes of several strains of a pathogen before choosing an antigen?
7. A gene from a bacterium is 2,700 bases long from start to stop triplet inclusive. How many amino acids are in the polypeptide?
8. Give **two** reasons why the human genome cannot easily be translated into the human proteome.
9. What is a regulatory gene?
10. Give **two** ways sequencing methods have changed.
11. A genome is 5.0 million base pairs and 86% codes for protein. How many base pairs are non-coding?
12. Why might a vaccine designed from one strain's genome fail to protect against a new strain?

### Answers

1. The complete set of genes in a cell.
2. The full range of proteins that a cell is able to produce.
3. Any two: little non-coding DNA; no introns; few regulatory genes.
4. Identifying potential antigens for use in vaccine production.
5. They are exposed on the pathogen, so antibodies and immune cells can bind to them.
6. To find antigens with the same sequence in all strains, so the vaccine protects against every strain.
7. 2,700 ÷ 3 = 900 triplets; 900 − 1 = 899 amino acids.
8. Any two: most of the genome is non-coding DNA; genes contain introns; regulatory genes control expression; different cells express different genes.
9. A gene whose product controls the expression of other genes.
10. Any two: continuously updated; automated; faster; cheaper; whole genomes read and compared.
11. 14% of 5.0 million = 700,000 base pairs.
12. The new strain may have a mutation that changes the antigen's amino acid sequence and shape, so antibodies from the vaccine no longer bind.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does "proteome" mean in AQA A-level Biology?**
The full range of proteins that a cell is able to produce. It is defined in section 3.4.2 and used again in section 3.8.3.

**Do I need to learn a sequencing method?**
No. The specification only says that sequencing methods are continuously updated and have become automated.
