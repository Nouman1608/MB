## What the specification lists

- Explain and calculate costs, revenue, profit, total cost, average cost, fixed cost, variable cost, total revenue, price, average revenue, and direct, indirect and overhead costs.
- Explain the importance of direct, indirect and overhead costs.
- Evaluate the impact of costs and revenue, and of cost changes.
- Evaluate cost centres and profit centres.
- Calculate and interpret full, absorption and marginal costing; evaluate their usefulness and the effects of allocation.
- Evaluate the profit level of a business.

## Core recall

### Formulas

| Term | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total variable cost | Variable cost per unit × output |
| Total cost | Fixed cost + total variable cost |
| Average cost | Total cost ÷ output |
| Total revenue | Price × quantity sold |
| Average revenue | Total revenue ÷ quantity (= price) |
| Profit | Total revenue − total cost |
| Profit margin | Profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| Contribution per unit | Price − variable cost per unit |

### Types of cost

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Does not change with output (short run) | Rent, salaries |
| Variable | Changes with output | Materials |
| Direct | Traced to a product | Oak for a table |
| Indirect | Shared by products | Supervisor's salary |
| Overhead | General running costs of the whole business | Head office rent, administration |

### Why direct, indirect and overhead costs matter

- Pricing must cover all three and leave profit.
- Direct costs are controlled per unit; overheads as a budget.
- A product that covers its direct costs may be worth making short term, even if it does not cover its overheads.
- Separating costs shows which products make money.

### Cost and profit centres

| | Cost centre | Profit centre |
|---|---|---|
| Records | Costs | Revenue and costs |
| Benefit | Control and accountability | Profit focus and comparison |
| Risk | Cutting service to meet budget | Local profit harms the whole; arguments over shared costs |

### Costing methods

- **Full costing:** all costs assigned to products.
- **Absorption costing:** overheads added by a rate (per labour or machine hour); rate = overheads ÷ total hours.
- **Marginal costing:** only variable costs per unit; contribution = price − variable cost; fixed costs deducted in total.

| | Absorption | Marginal |
|---|---|---|
| Use | Pricing, long-run | Short-run, special orders, break-even |
| Weakness | Arbitrary allocation | May lead to prices that never cover overheads |

### Evaluating profit level

Size, margin, trend, comparison with rivals and targets, quality (one-off or regular), purpose (growth, owners).

## Quick worked reminder

Tarn Furniture (invented): fixed £60,000, variable £140 a table, price £300, output 900. Total cost £186,000; average cost £206.67; revenue £270,000; profit £84,000. Ashby Joinery: overheads £58,500 over 3,900 labour hours give £15 per hour. A table costs £185 (profit £115) and a chair £45.50 (profit £24.50); total profit £162,300. Marginal contribution: tables £160, chairs £32. A £50 special order for chairs gives contribution £12 each, but only £4.50 profit under absorption.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fixed cost | A cost that does not vary with output in the short run |
| Variable cost | A cost that varies with output |
| Direct cost | A cost traced to a particular product |
| Overhead | A general running cost of the business |
| Cost centre | A unit to which costs are charged |
| Profit centre | A unit for which revenue and costs are recorded |
| Absorption rate | The overhead charged per hour or unit of output |
| Contribution | Price minus variable cost per unit |
| Marginal cost | The extra cost of one more unit |

## Exam traps

- Average revenue equals price.
- Divide total cost, not fixed cost, by output to get average cost.
- Absorption rate = overheads ÷ total hours, not output.
- Total profit is the same under absorption and marginal costing (if all output is sold); product profit differs.
- Evaluate: weigh both methods and link to the decision.

## Self-test

1. Define a variable cost and give an example.
2. Fixed costs are £18,000, variable cost £2.20 a unit, output 8,000. Calculate total cost.
3. Calculate average cost.
4. The price is £5. Calculate total revenue and profit.
5. Calculate contribution per unit.
6. Distinguish between a direct cost and an overhead.
7. Give one strength of a profit centre.
8. Overheads £27,000; machine hours 4,500. Calculate the absorption rate.
9. A product uses 3 hours. What overhead is absorbed per unit?
10. Which costing method is better for a one-off special order?
11. Why can absorption costing make a product look loss-making?
12. Profit is £4,400 and revenue £40,000. Calculate the profit margin.

### Answers

1. A cost that changes with output, such as materials.
2. 8,000 × £2.20 = £17,600; + £18,000 = **£35,600**.
3. £35,600 ÷ 8,000 = **£4.45**.
4. Revenue = 8,000 × £5 = £40,000; profit = £40,000 − £35,600 = **£4,400**.
5. £5 − £2.20 = **£2.80**.
6. A direct cost can be traced to a product (materials); an overhead is a general running cost of the whole business (head office rent).
7. Managers focus on profit, and units can be compared.
8. £27,000 ÷ 4,500 = **£6 per hour**.
9. 3 × £6 = **£18**.
10. Marginal costing, because it shows the contribution, assuming fixed costs are already covered.
11. A share of overheads is added, which can push full cost above price even though the product earns a positive contribution.
12. £4,400 ÷ £40,000 × 100 = **11%**.
