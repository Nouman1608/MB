This guide covers **3.2.1 The measurement of macroeconomic performance** in AQA A-level Economics (7136), the
first sub-section of 3.2 The national and international economy. Its four parts are 3.2.1.1 The objectives of
government economic policy, 3.2.1.2 Macroeconomic indicators, 3.2.1.3 Uses of index numbers and 3.2.1.4 Uses of
national income data.

The site's [measurement of macroeconomic performance guide](/resources/a-level-aqa-economics-measurement-of-macroeconomic-performance/)
introduces the objectives, indicators and the ideas behind index numbers and PPP. This page concentrates on the
**quantitative skills** this sub-section tests: calculating and rebasing index numbers, converting nominal
figures to real ones, reading unemployment and current account data, and making fair international
comparisons. All countries and figures are invented. Revise with the
[revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-the-measurement-of-macroeconomic-performance-aqa-alevel-economics-revision-notes/)
and practise with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-the-measurement-of-macroeconomic-performance-aqa-alevel-economics-practice/).

## Objectives and the indicators that track them

The main objectives of government macroeconomic policy named in the specification are **economic growth**,
**price stability**, **minimising unemployment** and **a stable balance of payments on current account**.
Governments may also aim to balance the budget and achieve an equitable distribution of income.

The specification adds that "the importance attached to the different objectives changes over time". In the UK,
for example, governments from 1992 set an explicit target for inflation, giving price stability a central place
in policy. A data question may ask you to judge performance against whichever objectives the extract stresses.

| Objective | Main indicators |
|---|---|
| Economic growth | real GDP; real GDP per capita |
| Price stability | Consumer Prices Index (CPI); Retail Prices Index (RPI) |
| Minimising unemployment | measures of unemployment; employment rate |
| Stable current account | balance of payments on current account, often as % of GDP |
| Supporting all of them | productivity (output per worker or per hour) |

## Index numbers: calculating with weights

An index number expresses a value relative to a **base year** set at 100. A price index such as the CPI tracks
the cost of a **basket of goods and services** bought by a typical household (the "average family"), with each
item **weighted** by its share of spending.

**Worked example (invented).** Country G's basket has four groups:

| Group | Weight | Price index this year (base year = 100) | Weight × index |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 30 | 108 | 3,240 |
| Housing | 40 | 104 | 4,160 |
| Transport | 20 | 95 | 1,900 |
| Leisure | 10 | 110 | 1,100 |
| **Total** | **100** | | **10,400** |

Weighted index = 10,400 ÷ 100 = **104.0**. Prices on average are 4% higher than in the base year. Leisure prices
rose most (10%) but carry only a small weight, so they add little to the overall figure; housing rose less (4%) but
has the largest weight.

## Index numbers: rebasing and comparing years

The percentage change between two years that are **not** the base year is not the difference in index points.

**Worked example (invented).** A price index with 2020 = 100 reads 104 in 2021, 112 in 2022 and 117 in 2023.

- Change from 2022 to 2023 = (117 − 112) ÷ 112 × 100 = **4.5%**, not 5%.
- **Rebasing to 2022 = 100:** divide each value by 112 and multiply by 100. The series becomes 89.3 (2020), 92.9
  (2021), 100 (2022) and 104.5 (2023).

Rebasing is useful when you compare two series with different base years, or when a recent year is a more
relevant reference point.

**Rate of inflation versus price level.** If inflation falls from 6% to 3%, prices are **still rising**, just more
slowly: this is disinflation, not deflation. Students often misread a falling inflation line on a graph as falling
prices.

## From nominal to real

Nominal values are at current prices. To find real values, remove the effect of price changes.

**Worked example (invented).** Country G's nominal GDP is 2,400 billion in Year 1 and 2,650 billion in Year 2. The
price index (Year 1 = 100) rises to 106.

- Real GDP in Year 2, at Year 1 prices = 2,650 ÷ 1.06 = **2,500 billion**.
- Real growth = (2,500 − 2,400) ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **4.2%**, while nominal growth was **10.4%**.
- Population rises from 50 million to 50.5 million. Real GDP per capita rises from 48,000 to about 49,505, a
  growth rate of **3.1%**.

Real GDP per capita grew more slowly than real GDP because the population grew by 1%. For living standards, the
per capita figure is the more relevant one.

## Unemployment and productivity data

**Unemployment rate** = number unemployed ÷ economically active population (the labour force) × 100.

**Worked example (invented).** The labour force is 34 million. Unemployment rises from 1.36 million to 1.53
million.

- Rate rises from 4.0% to 4.5%: a rise of **0.5 percentage points**.
- The number unemployed rises by **12.5%**.

Both statements are correct, but they are different measures. Use "percentage points" for the change in a rate,
and "per cent" for the proportional change in a quantity.

**Productivity.** If output rises 2% while total hours worked also rise 2%, **output per hour is unchanged**.
Growth that comes only from working more hours does not raise productivity.

## The current account

The current account records trade in goods, trade in services, primary income (such as investment income and
wages earned abroad) and secondary income (transfers such as foreign aid and remittances).

**Worked example (invented, billions).** Trade in goods −40; trade in services +25; primary income −5;
secondary income −10. Current account balance = **−30**, a deficit. With GDP of 2,500, the deficit is
**1.2% of GDP**. Expressing the balance as a share of GDP allows comparison across years and countries of
different sizes.

## National income data and living standards

National income data are used to compare living standards **over time** and **between countries**. Their
limitations matter for evaluation:

- **Distribution:** average income per head says nothing about how income is shared.
- **Non-marketed and informal output:** unpaid work and unrecorded activity are left out.
- **Quality of life:** leisure, working hours, health and the environment are not measured.
- **Quality and new products:** better goods at the same price may not be fully captured.
- **Composition:** a rise in defence spending and a rise in health spending add the same amount to GDP.

**Comparing countries: purchasing power parity.** Market exchange rates do not reflect what money buys locally.
Suppose Country L's GDP per capita is 600,000 local units. At the market rate of 40 local units per US dollar it
is worth **15,000 dollars**. If the same basket of goods that costs 1 dollar in the USA costs 20 local units in
Country L, the PPP rate is 20 local units per dollar, and income per head is **30,000 dollars** at PPP. The PPP
figure is the better guide to living standards, because it adjusts for differences in the cost of living.

## Common errors

- Subtracting index points to find a percentage change between non-base years.
- Treating a fall in the inflation rate as a fall in prices.
- Using nominal GDP growth as a measure of economic growth.
- Confusing a rise of 0.5 percentage points with a rise of 0.5%.
- Treating a current account deficit as the same thing as a budget deficit.
- Comparing countries' incomes at market exchange rates without comment.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show the formula, the substitution and the unit (per cent, percentage points, billions).
- **Using the data, explain:** quote the specific figures and state what they show about the objective.
- **Assess:** weigh several indicators together; a country can grow quickly while running a widening current
  account deficit or experiencing rising inflation.
