This guide covers subtopic **33.3, Acyl chlorides**, from Topic 33, Carboxylic acids and
derivatives, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level
content. The broader [Carboxylic Acids and Acyl Chlorides study guide](/resources/a-carboxylic-acids-and-acyl-chlorides/)
introduces the reactions; this page adds full equations, by-products, the curly-arrow detail
examiners credit, and a structured way to answer the hydrolysis comparison.

## What the syllabus asks for

Subtopic 33.3 has four learning outcomes:

1. recall the production of acyl chlorides from carboxylic acids with "PCl₃ and heat, PCl₅ or SOCl₂"
2. describe five reactions of acyl chlorides, all at room temperature: hydrolysis with water;
   reaction with an alcohol and with phenol to give an ester and HCl; reaction with ammonia to give
   an amide and HCl; reaction with a primary or secondary amine to give an amide and HCl
3. describe the addition–elimination mechanism for those five reactions
4. explain "the relative ease of hydrolysis of acyl chlorides, alkyl chlorides and halogenoarenes
   (aryl chlorides)"

## Making acyl chlorides

Each reagent swaps the –OH of the carboxylic acid for –Cl. Learn each equation with its
by-products, because questions often ask how the acyl chloride is separated.

| Reagent | Conditions | Equation (ethanoic acid) | By-products |
|---|---|---|---|
| PCl₅ (solid) | room temperature | CH₃COOH + PCl₅ → CH₃COCl + POCl₃ + HCl | POCl₃ (liquid), HCl (steamy fumes) |
| PCl₃ (liquid) | heat | 3CH₃COOH + PCl₃ → 3CH₃COCl + H₃PO₃ | H₃PO₃ |
| SOCl₂ (liquid) | room temperature or warm | CH₃COOH + SOCl₂ → CH₃COCl + SO₂ + HCl | SO₂ and HCl, both gases |

SOCl₂ is often preferred because both by-products leave as gases, so the acyl chloride is easier to
purify. With PCl₅ and PCl₃ a phosphorus compound has to be separated, usually by fractional
distillation. All three reagents, and the acyl chloride, react with water, so the apparatus must be
dry.

## The five reactions

All five happen at room temperature and release HCl. Use ethanoyl chloride as your model:

| Nucleophile | Equation | Organic product |
|---|---|---|
| water | CH₃COCl + H₂O → CH₃COOH + HCl | ethanoic acid |
| ethanol | CH₃COCl + C₂H₅OH → CH₃COOC₂H₅ + HCl | ethyl ethanoate |
| phenol | CH₃COCl + C₆H₅OH → CH₃COOC₆H₅ + HCl | phenyl ethanoate |
| ammonia | CH₃COCl + NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + HCl | ethanamide |
| methylamine (primary) | CH₃COCl + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₃ + HCl | N-methylethanamide |
| dimethylamine (secondary) | CH₃COCl + (CH₃)₂NH → CH₃CON(CH₃)₂ + HCl | N,N-dimethylethanamide |

Two details are worth adding when the question allows:

- **Ammonia and amines are bases.** If they are in excess, the HCl formed reacts with them, so the
  inorganic product is an ammonium or alkylammonium chloride rather than HCl gas:
  CH₃COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + NH₄Cl. Either form is accepted when the question does not specify
  quantities.
- **Each nucleophile replaces Cl with its own group, minus one H.** Water gives –OH, an alcohol
  gives –OR, ammonia gives –NH₂, a primary amine gives –NHR and a secondary amine gives –NR₂.
  The H lost combines with Cl to make HCl.

## The addition–elimination mechanism

Use the reaction with ammonia as the worked case; every other nucleophile follows the same steps.

**Why the carbonyl carbon is attacked.** Both oxygen and chlorine are more electronegative than
carbon, so the carbonyl carbon carries a large δ+ charge. It is also sp² hybridised and planar,
leaving it open to attack from above or below the plane.

**Step 1, addition.**

- Curly arrow from the **lone pair on nitrogen** of NH₃ to the **carbonyl carbon**.
- Curly arrow from the **C=O π bond** to the **oxygen**.
- The result is a **tetrahedral intermediate**: the carbon now has four groups (CH₃, O⁻, Cl and
  ⁺NH₃), with a negative charge on oxygen and a positive charge on nitrogen.

**Step 2, elimination.**

- Curly arrow from a **lone pair on O⁻** back down to re-form the **C=O** bond.
- Curly arrow from the **C–Cl bond** to the **chlorine**, which leaves as **Cl⁻**.
- A proton is lost from the ⁺NH₃ group (to Cl⁻ or another NH₃), giving neutral ethanamide and HCl
  (or NH₄Cl).

The overall change is substitution of Cl by NH₂, but it happens by addition followed by
elimination, not in one step. That is the difference from nucleophilic substitution in
halogenoalkanes, and from simple nucleophilic addition to aldehydes and ketones, where no group
leaves.

## Relative ease of hydrolysis

The three types of chloride can be compared by adding each to water containing aqueous silver
nitrate (with ethanol as a common solvent). The chloride ions released form a white precipitate of
silver chloride, and the time taken shows the relative rate.

| Compound | Example | Hydrolysis | Observation with AgNO₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| acyl chloride | CH₃COCl | fast at room temperature, even with cold water | white precipitate at once; steamy fumes |
| alkyl chloride | CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl | slow; needs warming | white precipitate forms slowly on warming |
| aryl chloride | C₆H₅Cl | no hydrolysis under these conditions | no precipitate |

**Order of ease of hydrolysis: acyl chloride > alkyl chloride > aryl chloride.**

The explanation has two parts for each compound: how electrophilic the carbon is, and how easily the
C–Cl bond is broken.

- **Acyl chloride.** The carbon is bonded to both O and Cl, so it is strongly δ+. It is planar and
  the C=O π bond can accept the incoming electron pair, so the nucleophile adds easily before Cl⁻
  leaves. The activation energy is low.
- **Alkyl chloride.** The carbon is δ+ only because of chlorine. It is sp³ and tetrahedral, with no
  π bond to accept electrons, so the nucleophile has to displace Cl⁻ directly (SN1 or SN2). The
  activation energy is higher.
- **Aryl chloride.** One chlorine lone pair is drawn into the delocalised π system of the ring. The
  C–Cl bond therefore has some double-bond character and needs more energy to break. The cloud of
  π electrons also repels an approaching nucleophile. Hydrolysis does not happen under these
  conditions.

## Worked example 1: an annotated mechanism answer

**Question.** Describe the mechanism for the reaction of propanoyl chloride with methylamine. Name
the organic product. **[5]**

**Model answer.**

1. The carbonyl carbon of CH₃CH₂COCl is δ+ because O and Cl are electronegative. *(sets up the
   attack; often not credited alone but makes the arrows logical)*
2. A curly arrow goes from the **lone pair on N** of CH₃NH₂ to the carbonyl carbon, and a curly arrow
   goes from the C=O π bond onto O. **[1]**
3. The tetrahedral intermediate, CH₃CH₂C(O⁻)(Cl)(N⁺H₂CH₃), is drawn with both charges. **[1]**
4. A curly arrow from O⁻ re-forms C=O, and a curly arrow from the C–Cl bond to Cl releases Cl⁻.
   **[1]**
5. H⁺ is lost from nitrogen, giving HCl. **[1]**
6. The product is **N-methylpropanamide, CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃**. **[1]**

*Why answers lose marks:* arrows starting from the nitrogen atom rather than its lone pair, a missing
charge on the intermediate, or showing Cl⁻ leaving in the same step as the attack.

## Worked example 2: predicting products from an unfamiliar reagent

**Question.** Benzoyl chloride reacts with diethylamine, (CH₃CH₂)₂NH, at room temperature. Give the
structural formula of the organic product and classify the amine.

**Answer.** Diethylamine has one N–H, so it is a **secondary amine**. The N replaces Cl and loses its
one H:

    C₆H₅COCl + (CH₃CH₂)₂NH → C₆H₅CON(CH₂CH₃)₂ + HCl

The product is **N,N-diethylbenzamide**, an amide with no N–H bond. A tertiary amine has no N–H, so
it cannot form an amide this way: there is no hydrogen to lose.

## Common mistakes

- **Giving heat for the five reactions.** They all occur at room temperature; only PCl₃ needs heat,
  and that is in the preparation.
- **Forgetting the by-products** of PCl₅ (POCl₃ and HCl) or SOCl₂ (SO₂ and HCl).
- **Calling the mechanism nucleophilic substitution** or **nucleophilic addition**. Use
  addition–elimination.
- **Leaving the charges off** the tetrahedral intermediate.
- **Explaining the hydrolysis order only by bond strength.** For the acyl chloride, the key point is
  the highly δ+, planar carbon with a π bond that can accept electrons.
- **Saying chlorobenzene reacts slowly.** Under these conditions it does not react at all.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Recall** the reagents: give formulae and the one condition (heat for PCl₃ only).
- **Describe the mechanism:** draw or describe every curly arrow, starting from a lone pair or a
  bond; show the intermediate with charges; show the leaving group and the proton lost.
- **Explain the relative ease of hydrolysis:** state the order first, then give a reason for each
  compound. A comparison that only explains the fastest one earns partial credit.
- **Suggest** an observation: steamy fumes of HCl for the reactions; a white precipitate with
  AgNO₃ for the hydrolysis comparison.
