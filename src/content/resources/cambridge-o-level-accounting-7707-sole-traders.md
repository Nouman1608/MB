## The sole trader

A **sole trader** is a business owned by one person, who may employ others.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Owner makes all decisions quickly | Unlimited liability: the owner's private possessions can be used to pay business debts |
| Owner keeps all the profit | Capital is limited to the owner's savings and what lenders will provide |
| Easy and cheap to set up | Heavy workload; illness or absence can stop the business |
| Financial statements are private | Fewer skills than a team of owners |
| Close contact with customers | The business may not continue if the owner dies or retires |

## Types of business and their statements

- A **trading** business buys and sells goods. Its statement of profit or
loss has a **cost of sales** section and a **gross profit**.
- A **service** business sells skills or time (a hairdresser, an
accountant). It has no cost of sales: its fees are income, and expenses are
deducted from them.
- A **manufacturing** business makes the goods it sells. It prepares a
manufacturing account first to find the cost of production, which replaces
purchases in cost of sales (see
[manufacturing accounts](/resources/igcse-accounting-manufacturing-accounts/)).
- A **combination**, such as a shop that also repairs goods, shows trading
income in the gross profit section and service income as other income.

## Why the statements matter

The **statement of profit or loss** shows whether the business made a profit
or loss in the period, so the owner can judge performance, set drawings,
plan and compare years. The **statement of financial position** records
the assets, liabilities and capital **on a specified date**, showing what
the business owns and owes, its liquidity and the owner's stake. Lenders,
tax authorities and potential buyers rely on both.

## Content of the statement of financial position

| Heading | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | Assets held for use in the business for more than a year | Premises, fixtures, vehicles (at net book value) |
| Intangible assets | Non-current assets with no physical form | Goodwill |
| Current assets | Assets expected to be turned into cash within a year | Inventory, trade receivables less allowance, other receivables, bank, cash |
| Current liabilities | Amounts due within a year | Trade payables, other payables, bank overdraft |
| Non-current liabilities | Amounts due after more than a year | Long-term bank loan |
| Capital | The owner's claim | Opening capital + capital introduced + profit − drawings |

## Adjustments and where they go

| Adjustment | Statement of profit or loss | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation | Expense | Deducted from cost to give net book value |
| Accrued expense | Added to the expense | Other payables |
| Prepaid expense | Deducted from the expense | Other receivables |
| Accrued income | Added to the income | Other receivables |
| Income received in advance | Deducted from the income | Other payables |
| Irrecoverable debt | Expense | Deducted from trade receivables |
| Allowance change | Increase = expense; decrease = income | Full allowance deducted from trade receivables |
| Goods taken by owner | Deducted from purchases | Added to drawings |
| Private expense paid by the business | Removed from the expense | Added to drawings |

Additional information at 31 December 2027:

1. Closing inventory was $10,200.
2. Rashid took cycles costing $900 for his own use. This has not been
recorded.
3. General expenses include $340 for Rashid's private telephone.
4. Wages of $600 were owing.
5. Insurance of $260 was prepaid.
6. Repair work of $450 completed in December had not been billed or
received.
7. Shop fittings are depreciated at 10% per year on cost.
8. A debt of $300 is to be written off. The allowance is to be 4% of the
remaining trade receivables.

**Workings**

- Net purchases: $71,600 − $800 − $900 = $69,900.
- Repair fees: $9,800 + $450 = $10,250.
- Wages: $22,300 + $600 = $22,900. Insurance: $1,560 − $260 = $1,300.
General expenses: $2,740 − $340 = $2,400.
- Depreciation: 10% × $24,000 = $2,400. Accumulated: $7,200 + $2,400 =
$9,600.
- Receivables: $6,300 − $300 = $6,000. Allowance: 4% × $6,000 = $240,
an increase of $60.
- Drawings: $14,200 + $900 + $340 = $15,440.

## The effect of a change in one balance

Every adjustment moves at least two figures. Suppose the wages owing had
been $900 instead of $600:

- wages expense rises by $300, so profit for the year falls by $300
- other payables (current liabilities) rise by $300
- closing capital falls by $300.

Total assets are unchanged, and capital plus liabilities is unchanged
(−$300 + $300), so the statement still balances. Testing a change this way
is a quick check on any answer that asks "what would be the effect if...".

## Common mistakes

- Deducting goods taken by the owner from revenue instead of purchases.
- Leaving the owner's private expenses in business expenses.
- Charging the full allowance instead of the change.
- Putting carriage inwards in expenses instead of cost of sales.
- Showing goodwill as a current asset, or omitting the intangible label.
- Placing a long-term loan in current liabilities.
- Heading the statement of financial position "for the year ended".

## Exam technique

- **Prepare**: full heading with business name, statement name and date
wording ("for the year ended" for the statement of profit or loss, "at"
for the statement of financial position).
- Show workings in brackets beside each adjusted figure, as above, so each
adjustment can earn its mark.
- **Explain** advantages or disadvantages in the context of the business in
the question, for example a sole trader needing capital to open a second
shop.
