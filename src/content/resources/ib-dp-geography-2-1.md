## Why the ocean moderates climate

Water has a **high specific heat capacity**: it takes far more energy to warm a kilogram of water by one degree than a kilogram of rock or soil. Three further properties add to this:

- **Mixing.** Waves and currents spread heat through the upper tens of metres, so a large volume warms slowly.
- **Transparency.** Sunlight penetrates water, warming a layer rather than a thin surface skin.
- **Evaporation.** Some energy goes into evaporating water rather than raising temperature.

As a result, coastal places have smaller annual and daily temperature ranges than places deep inside continents: a **maritime** climate rather than a **continental** one.

**Worked example (invented stations at the same latitude).**

| | Coastal station | Interior station, 1,500 km inland |
|---|---|---|
| Mean temperature of warmest month | 18 degrees Celsius | 24 degrees Celsius |
| Mean temperature of coldest month | 6 degrees Celsius | -14 degrees Celsius |
| Annual range | 18 - 6 = 12 degrees | 24 - (-14) = 38 degrees |

The interior range is more than three times the coastal one. Note the subtraction with a negative number: 24 minus -14 is 38, not 10. Where a warm current such as the North Atlantic Drift flows offshore, the coastal winter is milder still, which is why currents and heat capacity together explain north-west Europe's climate.

The ocean also stores most of the extra energy trapped by greenhouse gases. Because it warms slowly, part of the warming already caused by past emissions is still to appear at the surface, a delay known as **thermal inertia**.

## How ENSO is measured

El Nino and La Nina are not declared by watching for floods in Peru. Scientists use two indicators.

- **Sea surface temperature anomaly** in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific, the difference between the observed temperature and the long-term average for that time of year. The United States National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) uses an index based on a region called Nino 3.4. El Nino conditions are recognised when the three-month average anomaly is at least +0.5 degrees Celsius for five consecutive overlapping three-month periods; La Nina when it is -0.5 degrees or lower for the same length of time.
- **The Southern Oscillation Index**, which compares air pressure at Tahiti, in the central Pacific, with air pressure at Darwin, in northern Australia. A strongly negative index (lower than usual pressure at Tahiti relative to Darwin) goes with weaker trade winds and El Nino; a strongly positive index goes with La Nina.

The two together show the **coupling**: the ocean (temperature) and atmosphere (pressure and winds) change together and reinforce each other. Weaker trade winds let warm water spread east, which warms the eastern Pacific, which reduces the pressure difference that drives the trade winds. This positive feedback is why an El Nino, once established, tends to persist for months.

**Worked example: classifying an episode (invented anomalies, degrees Celsius).**

| Three-month period | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anomaly | +0.3 | +0.6 | +0.8 | +1.1 | +1.0 | +0.7 | +0.4 |

Periods 2 to 6 are all at least +0.5, giving five in a row, so this counts as an El Nino episode. If period 6 had been +0.4, there would be only four, and the episode would not qualify on this definition, even though the peak of +1.1 is well above the threshold. Duration matters as much as size.

## Two ways the ocean takes up carbon

The ocean holds far more carbon than the atmosphere, and exchanges carbon dioxide with the air at the surface.

- **The solubility pump.** Carbon dioxide dissolves more readily in cold water. Cold, dense surface water at high latitudes absorbs carbon dioxide and then sinks as part of thermohaline circulation, carrying dissolved carbon to the deep ocean, where it can remain for centuries.
- **The biological pump.** Phytoplankton take up carbon dioxide in photosynthesis. When they and the animals that eat them die, some of their remains sink, transferring carbon to deep water and sediments.

**Why this matters for the future.** A warmer ocean dissolves less carbon dioxide, and stronger warming of the surface makes the water column more stratified, reducing the mixing that carries carbon down and brings nutrients up. Both effects could weaken the ocean sink, leaving more carbon dioxide in the atmosphere: a positive feedback on warming.

## The pH scale and acidification

The pH scale is **logarithmic**: each fall of one pH unit means ten times as many hydrogen ions. A fall of 0.1 therefore means a rise in hydrogen ion concentration by a factor of 10 to the power 0.1.

```
10 to the power 0.1 = 1.26
Increase in hydrogen ion concentration = (1.26 - 1) x 100 = 26%, about a quarter
```

So a change that sounds tiny is chemically large. Seawater remains alkaline (above pH 7); acidification means it is becoming less alkaline. The extra hydrogen ions combine with carbonate ions, reducing the carbonate available to corals, shellfish and some plankton to build their skeletons and shells.

## Oceans and tropical cyclones

Tropical cyclones draw energy from warm surface water through evaporation: water vapour rising in the storm condenses and releases **latent heat**, which powers the rising air. The interaction runs both ways:

- A warm, deep upper layer can fuel rapid intensification, because the storm cannot easily churn up cold water.
- Strong winds mix the upper ocean, bringing cooler water to the surface and leaving a **cold wake** behind the storm. A slow-moving cyclone over a shallow warm layer can weaken itself in this way.
- Over land, the energy supply is cut off and the storm weakens, though rainfall can remain extreme.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain how ENSO demonstrates interaction between the ocean and the atmosphere.* **(sample paragraph)**

"In normal years, strong trade winds pile warm water in the western Pacific and allow cold water to upwell off South America, so the pressure difference between Tahiti and Darwin stays high **[ocean and atmosphere linked in the normal state]**. During El Nino, the trade winds weaken, warm water spreads east, and sea surface temperatures in the central Pacific rise by at least half a degree above average for several months **[measured change]**. Warmer eastern water reduces the pressure difference that drives the trade winds, so they weaken further **[feedback shows coupling]**. The change in the ocean therefore alters the atmosphere, which in turn alters the ocean, which is why ENSO is described as a coupled system **[concept stated explicitly]**."

## Common mistakes

- Explaining maritime climates by currents alone and forgetting heat capacity.
- Subtracting negative temperatures incorrectly when calculating ranges.
- Defining El Nino by its impacts rather than by measured ocean and pressure conditions.
- Treating the ocean carbon sink as fixed, without the warming feedback.
- Saying a pH fall of 0.1 is a 10 per cent change.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a process with a chain and a feedback where relevant.
- **Describe** data such as an anomaly chart with values, duration and pattern.
- **Examine** the ocean's role in climate by weighing heat transfer, carbon uptake and their limits.
- Use precise terms: anomaly, coupling, solubility pump, latent heat, thermal inertia.
