## Progressive waves

A **progressive wave** transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. The particles of the medium oscillate about fixed positions.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Displacement | Distance and direction of a point from its equilibrium position |
| Amplitude | Maximum displacement |
| Wavelength λ | Distance between two adjacent points oscillating in phase, such as two crests |
| Period T | Time for one complete oscillation |
| Frequency f | Number of oscillations per second; f = 1/T |
| Wave speed c | Distance a wavefront travels per second; c = fλ |
| Phase difference | How far one oscillation is ahead of another, as a fraction of a cycle |

### Phase difference

A whole cycle is 2π radians or 360°. Two points a distance x apart along a wave differ in phase by **(x/λ) × 2π**. Two points on the same oscillation at times t apart differ by **(t/T) × 2π**. Points one wavelength apart are **in phase**; points half a wavelength apart are in **antiphase** (π rad, 180°).

**Worked example 1.** A wave on a rope has frequency 50 Hz and speed 12 m s⁻¹. Find the phase difference between two points 0.18 m apart.

```
λ = c/f = 12 / 50 = 0.24 m
x/λ = 0.18 / 0.24 = 0.75 of a cycle
phase difference = 0.75 × 2π = 3π/2 rad = 270°
```

A phase difference of 270° is the same as the second point being 90° *ahead* rather than 270° behind; either description is acceptable if it is stated clearly.

## Transverse and longitudinal waves

| | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| Oscillation | Perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer | Parallel to the direction of energy transfer |
| Examples | Electromagnetic waves, waves on a string | Sound, compression waves in a spring |
| Features | Crests and troughs | Compressions and rarefactions |
| Can be polarised? | Yes | No |

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum, 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

### Polarisation

A polarised wave oscillates in **one plane** only. Polarisation is evidence that a wave is transverse, because a longitudinal wave has only one direction of oscillation (along its travel) and so cannot be restricted to a plane.

When unpolarised light passes through a Polaroid filter, it becomes plane-polarised. A second filter (the analyser) is then rotated:

- transmission axes **parallel**: maximum intensity passes;
- rotated by **90°**: no light passes;
- rotated by **180°**: maximum again.

So the intensity falls to zero twice in each full rotation of the analyser. Applications: Polaroid sunglasses reduce glare from light reflected off water and roads, which is partly polarised; television and radio aerials are aligned with the plane of polarisation of the transmitted signal for the strongest reception.

## Superposition and stationary waves

The **principle of superposition**: when two waves meet, the resultant displacement at any point is the vector sum of the individual displacements.

A **stationary wave** forms when two progressive waves of the same frequency (and similar amplitude) travel in opposite directions and superpose, usually a wave and its reflection.

- **Nodes**: points of zero amplitude, where the two waves always cancel.
- **Antinodes**: points of maximum amplitude, where they reinforce.
- Adjacent nodes, and adjacent antinodes, are **λ/2** apart; a node and the next antinode are λ/4 apart.

| Property | Progressive wave | Stationary wave |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Transferred | Not transferred (stored) |
| Amplitude | Same for all points (if undamped) | Varies from zero at nodes to maximum at antinodes |
| Phase | Varies continuously along the wave | All points between two adjacent nodes in phase; antiphase across a node |
| Wavelength | Distance between adjacent points in phase | Twice the distance between adjacent nodes |

## Harmonics on a string

A string fixed at both ends must have a node at each end. The simplest pattern is one loop, the **first harmonic**: l = λ/2, so

**f = (1/2l)√(T/μ)**

where T is the tension and μ the mass per unit length. The nth harmonic has n loops and frequency n × f₁.

**Worked example 2.** A string 0.65 m long has mass per unit length 1.2 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹ and tension 75 N.

```
wave speed = √(T/μ) = √(75 / 1.2 × 10⁻³) = √62 500 = 250 m s⁻¹
first harmonic f₁ = 250 / (2 × 0.65) = 192 Hz
third harmonic f₃ = 3 × 192.3 = 577 Hz
third-harmonic wavelength = 2l/3 = 0.433 m; nodes 0.217 m apart
```

To double f₁, the tension must be multiplied by 4 (to 300 N), because f ∝ √T. Halving the length also doubles f₁, because f ∝ 1/l.

## Stationary waves with microwaves and sound

A microwave transmitter facing a metal plate, or a loudspeaker facing a hard wall, sets up a stationary wave between source and reflector. A detector (a microwave probe or a microphone with an oscilloscope) moved along the line finds alternating maxima and minima.

**Worked example 3.** A loudspeaker emits 1.7 kHz sound towards a wall. Successive minima are 10 cm apart. The minima are nodes, so λ/2 = 0.10 m and λ = 0.20 m. The speed of sound is c = fλ = 1700 × 0.20 = **340 m s⁻¹**. Measuring across several nodes and dividing reduces the percentage uncertainty.

## Required practical 1

**Investigation into the variation of the frequency of stationary waves on a string with length, tension and mass per unit length of the string.**

- A string passes over a pulley to a mass hanger, so T = mg, and is driven by a vibration generator connected to a signal generator.
- Adjust the frequency until the first harmonic (one clear loop) appears; read f from the signal generator.
- Vary one factor at a time: length (move the bridge), tension (change the hanging mass), or mass per unit length (change the string).
- Find μ by weighing a measured length of the string.

**Worked example 4: analysing results.** With a 0.50 kg hanging mass (T = 0.50 × 9.81 = 4.91 N), a graph of f against 1/l is a straight line through the origin with gradient 45 Hz m. The gradient equals ½√(T/μ):

```
√(T/μ) = 2 × 45 = 90 m s⁻¹
μ = T / 90² = 4.91 / 8100 = 6.1 × 10⁻⁴ kg m⁻¹
```

A straight line through the origin confirms f ∝ 1/l. Plotting f against √T at fixed length tests the tension dependence the same way.

Safety: keep the hanging masses low over the floor and keep feet clear.

## Common mistakes

- Using the distance between nodes as the wavelength (it is λ/2).
- Writing phase difference in degrees when the question asks for radians, or the reverse.
- Claiming sound can be polarised.
- Saying a stationary wave transfers energy.
- Saying all points on a stationary wave have the same amplitude.
- Using the mass of the hanging load as T without multiplying by g.
- Forgetting that f ∝ √T, so doubling T does not double f.

## Exam technique

"Explain how a stationary wave is formed" needs three points: two waves travelling in opposite directions, of the same frequency, superposing. Add where nodes and antinodes form for a full answer. For "describe" questions on Required practical 1, name the apparatus, how the first harmonic is identified, which variable is changed and controlled, and the graph you would plot.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is polarisation evidence that light is transverse?**
A polarised wave oscillates in one plane. Only transverse waves have oscillations perpendicular to their direction of travel that can be restricted to one plane, so the fact that light can be polarised shows it is transverse.

**What is the phase relationship between points on a stationary wave?**
All points between two adjacent nodes oscillate in phase. Points on opposite sides of a node are in antiphase.

**How does the first-harmonic frequency depend on tension?**
It is proportional to the square root of the tension, so increasing the tension by a factor of four doubles the frequency.
