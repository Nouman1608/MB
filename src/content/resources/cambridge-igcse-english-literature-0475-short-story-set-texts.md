**This guide covers one kind of prose set text in Section B of Paper 1: a selection of short
stories.** For the 2026 series the syllabus lists, alongside the novels, a set of stories "From
Stories of Ourselves Volume 2, the following 10 stories", and states that "passages from these
stories in the paper will be printed as in this text". You answer on one set text in Section B, so
a candidate who chooses the stories is answering on that whole selection, not on a single novel.
The [Section B: Prose study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-section-b-prose/)
explains the general method for prose; this page deals with what changes when the set text is a
group of short stories. Set texts rotate, so check the list for your own examination year.

## Why a short story needs its own approach

A novel gives you hundreds of pages to trace a character's development. A short story works in a
few thousand words, so every choice carries more weight. Three features follow from this.

- **Compression.** A story usually centres on one situation, a small cast and a short span of time.
  Details that a novel might repeat are given once, so a single image may have to carry the whole
  story's meaning.
- **A turning point.** Many stories build towards a moment of change: a decision, a discovery, or a
  realisation in which a character suddenly sees their situation differently. Locating that moment
  is the key to explaining what the story is about.
- **The ending as the key to the whole.** Because the story is short, the ending shapes how the reader
  understands everything before it. Endings can resolve the situation, leave it open, reverse what we
  expected, or return to an image from the opening with its meaning changed.

When you write about a printed passage from a story, you can therefore place it more precisely than
you could place a page of a novel: you know the whole story, and you can say how this moment prepares
for, or follows from, its turning point and ending.

## How should you organise your knowledge of ten stories?

The biggest risk with this set text is confusion: characters, settings and endings blur together
when ten stories are revised as one block. Keep a separate story card for each story with:

1. **Narrative voice:** first person or third person; whether the narrator is a child, an adult
   looking back, or an outside observer; whether we trust them.
2. **Situation in one sentence:** who wants what, and what stands in the way.
3. **Structure:** where the story opens (often in the middle of events), where the turning point
   falls, and how it ends.
4. **Setting and atmosphere:** place and time, and how they reflect the characters' feelings.
5. **Central idea:** a sentence beginning "The writer shows that…".
6. **Five short quotations:** one from the opening, one from the turning point, one from the ending
   and two that show the narrator's characteristic style.

The question on a short-story text is set on the stories in the selection, so read its wording
carefully to see which story or stories it names, and keep the answer on that story rather than
drifting into others you know better.

## Methods that matter most in short stories

**Openings.** Writers of short fiction often start without explanation: a line of dialogue, a strange
detail, or a character already in trouble. Ask what the reader is made to wonder about, and when the
story answers it.

**Titles.** A story's title is often an object, a place or a phrase whose meaning changes by the end.
Comment on the title when the question allows; it frequently names the story's central symbol.

**Symbol and motif.** With few pages to work in, writers attach meaning to objects that recur: a
letter, a gift, a door. Track how an object's significance shifts between its appearances.

**Withholding.** Short stories often leave things unsaid. The reader is trusted to infer a past
event or an unspoken feeling from small hints. Strong answers explain what is implied and how the
writer signals it.

**Pace.** Watch for moments where the writer slows down, giving many details for a few seconds of
action. That slowing marks the moment as important, often the turning point itself.

## Worked example: an original passage

The passage below was written for this guide; it is not from a set text. Imagine it comes from just
before the turning point of a story called "The Ladder".

> **From "The Ladder"** *(original passage)*
>
> Every evening that summer, Joseph carried the ladder out to the plum tree, although the plums had
> been picked weeks ago. His father had built it from the frame of an old bed, and the third rung
> still creaked when anyone heavier than a child stood on it. Joseph was no longer a child. He
> stepped onto the third rung anyway and listened.
>
> From the top he could see over the wall into the lane, where the bus from the city stopped at six.
> Today, as every day, it slowed, sighed and pulled away again. No one got off. Joseph counted to a
> hundred, then climbed down, one rung at a time, and laid the ladder in the long grass where his
> father had always left it, as if someone might need it in the morning.

**Question:** How does the writer make this such a moving moment in the story?

**What a strong candidate notices:**
- *Repetition of routine:* "Every evening", "as every day": the waiting has become a ritual, which
  suggests how long Joseph has hoped.
- *The ladder as symbol:* it was built by the father and is left "where his father had always left
  it", so it stands for the absent father; the useless trip to a tree with no plums shows the ladder
  is really for watching, not picking.
- *The creaking rung:* "Joseph was no longer a child. He stepped onto the third rung anyway." The
  short sentences mark his growing up, while his choice to stand on the creaking rung keeps him
  close to his childhood and his father.
- *Personification of the bus:* it "slowed, sighed and pulled away", so even the bus seems to share
  his disappointment; the three verbs build and then deflate hope.
- *Withholding:* the writer never says who Joseph is waiting for or why; the reader infers it from
  the father's ladder and the bus "from the city".
- *Ending of the passage:* "as if someone might need it in the morning" keeps hope alive in a final
  gesture, which makes the moment moving rather than simply sad.

**Model paragraph:**

> The writer makes Joseph's waiting moving by never naming what he waits for. We learn only that the
> ladder was built by "his father" and that the bus comes "from the city", and the reader must put
> these details together, just as Joseph must keep hoping without being told anything. The bus
> "slowed, sighed and pulled away": the first two verbs raise our hope that it will stop, and the
> third takes that hope away, so the sentence enacts his daily disappointment. Most moving for me is
> the final gesture, laying the ladder down "as if someone might need it in the morning", because
> it shows that Joseph keeps his father's habits ready for a return that the story has given us no
> reason to expect.

*Why it works:* every point serves the key word "moving"; the analysis focuses on implication, which
is central to short fiction; and the paragraph shows awareness of where the moment sits in the
story's structure.

## Writing about the wider story

When a question asks about a story more widely rather than a printed passage, use the story's shape
as your structure: the situation at the opening, the build-up, the turning point, the ending. Because
the story is short, you can and should refer to its ending in any answer, explaining how it changes
the meaning of earlier moments. Quote briefly from memory; the text is not available in the exam.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up stories.** Attributing a character or quotation to the wrong story undermines AO1.
- **Retelling the whole story.** A story is short enough to summarise in full, which tempts
  candidates to do so; summary earns little.
- **Ignoring the narrator.** Many stories depend on who tells them; say how the voice shapes our view.
- **Forgetting implication.** Explaining only what is stated misses the method short stories rely on.
- **Treating the ending as an afterthought.** In a short story the ending is often the main point.

## Exam technique for "Explore" and "How does the writer…?"

The syllabus defines "Explore" as "write in detail about particular aspects". For a short-story
question, choose four or five aspects (voice, a symbol, the turning point, pace, the ending) and
analyse each closely rather than surveying the whole story thinly. When the question asks directly
for your view ("What do you think…?"), state it in your opening sentence and return to it as you
analyse.
