This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 7.3 of Option G, Urban environments, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): urban environmental and social stresses. It is examined in Paper 1 if your school teaches this option. The [7.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-urban-environmental-and-social-stresses-revision-notes/) already explain the urban heat island, primary and secondary pollutants, temperature inversions, the cycle of deprivation, and pollution and congestion policies in London, Singapore, Beijing, Mexico City and Delhi. This page adds what those notes leave short: how to measure segregation, heat island intensity and congestion, why stresses fall unevenly on different groups, how urban design relates to crime, and how informal settlement upgrading compares with clearance. Test yourself with the [7.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-7-3-practice/); the [Urban environments option study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-urban-environments-study-guide/) gives the overview of all four sub-topics.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the causes and consequences of environmental stresses in cities.
- Explain the causes and consequences of social stresses, including deprivation, segregation and crime.
- Explain how these stresses vary within cities and between groups.
- Evaluate responses to them.

## Measuring segregation

Segregation is the uneven distribution of different groups across a city. It is measured with the **index of dissimilarity (D)**:

**D = 1/2 x the sum of |a/A - b/B| across all districts**

where a and b are the numbers of each group in a district, and A and B are each group's city totals. D runs from 0 (identical distributions) to 1 (complete separation). It can be read as the share of either group that would have to move for the two distributions to match.

### Worked example 1: four districts

An invented city has two income groups.

| District | Higher-income households (a) | a/A | Lower-income households (b) | b/B | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| North | 4,000 | 0.40 | 500 | 0.10 | 0.30 |
| East | 3,000 | 0.30 | 500 | 0.10 | 0.20 |
| South | 2,000 | 0.20 | 1,500 | 0.30 | 0.10 |
| West | 1,000 | 0.10 | 2,500 | 0.50 | 0.40 |
| Total | 10,000 | | 5,000 | | 1.00 |

D = 1/2 x 1.00 = **0.50**. Half of either group would need to move to another district to produce an even distribution. Tracking D over time shows whether segregation is increasing, for instance after gated developments are built or as gentrification changes a district. Its limits: the result depends on how district boundaries are drawn, and it says nothing about whether districts are next to each other.

## Measuring heat island intensity and congestion

**Urban heat island intensity** is the temperature difference between the city and its rural surroundings at the same time. Invented readings taken at 02:00 on a calm, clear night:

| Site | Temperature (degrees C) |
|---|---|
| Rural field | 14.0 |
| Suburban park | 15.5 |
| Dense housing | 18.0 |
| City centre | 19.0 |

Intensity = 19.0 - 14.0 = **5.0 degrees C**. The suburban park is only 1.5 degrees warmer than the rural site, showing the cooling effect of vegetation even within the city. Measuring at the same time on a calm, clear night matters, because wind and cloud reduce the difference.

**Congestion** can be measured with a **travel time index**: peak-hour travel time divided by travel time on empty roads. If a 12 km journey takes 18 minutes in free-flowing traffic and 45 minutes in the morning peak, the index is 45 / 18 = **2.5**: the peak journey takes two and a half times as long. Indices like this allow comparison between roads, times of day and cities.

## Environmental justice: who bears the stress?

Urban stresses do not fall evenly. **Environmental justice** is the principle that no group should bear a disproportionate share of environmental harm. In many cities:

- lower-income households live closer to main roads, industrial sites and waste facilities, where air pollution and noise are highest, because land and rents there are cheapest;
- informal settlements occupy floodplains and steep slopes that formal developers avoid;
- older and isolated people in poorly ventilated housing are most at risk in heatwaves. In the August 2003 European heatwave, thousands of excess deaths were recorded in France, many among older people living alone;
- districts with the least green space are often the poorest, so they also have the strongest heat island effects.

These overlaps produce **compound stress**: the same households face pollution, heat, flood risk and poor services together. Answers that show these links score higher than lists of separate stresses.

## Urban design and crime

Crime varies within cities, and design affects opportunity. Oscar Newman's idea of **defensible space** (1972) argued that layouts in which residents can see and feel ownership of shared spaces deter crime, while anonymous corridors and walkways encourage it. The wider approach, **crime prevention through environmental design**, includes:

- natural surveillance: windows overlooking streets, good lighting;
- clear boundaries between public and private space;
- maintenance, so that areas do not look abandoned;
- mixed land use, so that streets are busy at different times of day.

Design is not the whole answer. Crime is also linked to unemployment, inequality and the policing a district receives, so design measures work best alongside social programmes. Gated communities may reduce crime inside their walls while displacing it and increasing segregation outside.

## Informal settlements: clearance or upgrading?

Governments have responded to informal settlements in two broad ways.

**Clearance (eviction and demolition)** removes unsafe or unauthorised housing, often to free land for development. It destroys homes, livelihoods and social networks, and residents frequently rebuild informal housing elsewhere, sometimes on even riskier land.

**In-situ upgrading** improves the settlement where it is: paved paths, drainage, water, sanitation, electricity and, ideally, secure tenure. Indonesia's Kampung Improvement Programme, begun in Jakarta in 1969, is a long-running example of upgrading existing neighbourhoods rather than clearing them. Upgrading keeps communities and workplaces intact and costs much less per household than new housing, but it may legitimise building on hazardous land and depends on long-term maintenance.

**Sites-and-services** schemes sit between the two: the government provides serviced plots with water and roads, and households build their own homes. They can house many people cheaply but are often located far from jobs.

### Worked example 2: comparing costs (invented)

| Approach | Cost per household | Households helped with a budget of 60 million |
|---|---|---|
| New apartment blocks | 30,000 | 2,000 |
| In-situ upgrading | 4,000 | 15,000 |
| Sites-and-services | 6,000 | 10,000 |

Upgrading reaches seven and a half times as many households as new apartments for the same budget (15,000 / 2,000 = 7.5). The judgement still depends on safety: upgrading a settlement on an active landslide slope may be worse than relocation, whatever the cost.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine why the impacts of urban environmental stress vary between social groups.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Lower-income households often live near main roads and industrial sites because rents there are lowest, so they breathe the most polluted air **[mechanism linking income and exposure]**. Informal settlements on floodplains face flood risk that formal developers avoid **[second stress, same group]**. Age adds a further layer: in heatwaves, older people living alone in poorly ventilated flats face the greatest danger **[different dimension of vulnerability]**. Because these stresses overlap in the same districts, they compound one another, so inequality in exposure is greater than any one measure suggests **[synthesis and judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Listing stresses without linking them to the groups affected.
- Measuring a heat island with readings taken at different times or in windy weather.
- Treating segregation as only negative or only voluntary.
- Claiming that design alone can prevent crime.
- Assuming clearance solves informal housing rather than moving it.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a stress with its causes, consequences and the groups most affected.
- **Analyse** data by calculating indices such as D or a travel time index, then interpreting them.
- **Evaluate** a response by asking who benefits, whether it tackles causes or symptoms, and whether it lasts.
- **Examine** questions reward links between stresses and between scales.
