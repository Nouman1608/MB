## Questions

### Passage A -- prose fiction (original text written for Marlbridge)

> The key still stuck. Nadia had to lift the door by its handle and lean, the way her father always
> had, before the lock gave. Inside, the hall smelled of dust and lemon polish.
>
> She had meant to start in the kitchen. Instead she found herself on the third stair, the one that
> creaked. Twenty years ago she had sat exactly here, shoes already on, listening to him argue with
> the man from the bank. She had not understood the words. She had understood the silence
> afterwards, and the way he came out and asked, too brightly, who wanted pancakes.
>
> Well. There were no pancakes now. There were forty years of receipts in shoeboxes, and a coat on
> the peg that still held the shape of his shoulders. She would be practical. She would start with
> the coat.
>
> She did not start with the coat.

### Passage B -- drama (original text written for Marlbridge)

> *A front room, late afternoon. Cardboard boxes, half-packed. On the mantelpiece, a clock that has
> stopped. TOMAS, forties, black suit, tie loosened, sits on a box. DANIEL, younger, still in his
> overcoat, stands by the door.*
>
> DANIEL: You've started, then.
> TOMAS: Someone had to.
> DANIEL: I said I'd come Saturday.
> TOMAS: You said that in March.
> *(Pause. DANIEL picks up the clock and turns it over.)*
> DANIEL: This was Grandad's.
> TOMAS: It doesn't go.
> DANIEL: It went when he --
> TOMAS: It doesn't go, Dan. *(He holds out his hand for it.)* Box.
> *(DANIEL does not give it to him. A long pause. Somewhere upstairs, a door bangs in the wind. Both
> look up.)*
> DANIEL: *(quietly)* I'll take it.
> TOMAS: *(beat)* Course you will.

### Passage C -- drama, pre-1929

William Shakespeare, *Macbeth* (first performed c. 1606; published 1623), Act 2, Scene 1. Macbeth
is alone on stage.

> Is this a dagger which I see before me,
> The handle toward my hand? Come, let me clutch thee:
> I have thee not, and yet I see thee still.
> Art thou not, fatal vision, sensible
> To feeling as to sight? or art thou but
> A dagger of the mind, a false creation,
> Proceeding from the heat-oppressed brain?

**1.** Passage A. Identify the narrative perspective and give a short quotation showing whose eyes
we see through. **[2]**

**2.** Passage A. Explain how the paragraph beginning "Well." uses free indirect style to reveal
Nadia's state of mind. **[3]**

**3.** Passage A. The second paragraph moves back twenty years. Name this technique and explain its
effect on how the reader understands the present scene. **[3]**

**4.** Passage A. Explain the effect of the final one-sentence paragraph. **[3]**

**5.** Passage B. Explain how the opening stage directions establish the relationship between the
brothers before either speaks. **[3]**

**6.** Passage B.

**(a)** Identify one example of an unfinished line and state what it leaves unsaid. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the stopped clock works as a prop in the scene. **[3]**

**7.** Passage B. Explain the effect of the off-stage sound and the final exchange ("I'll take it."
/ "Course you will."). **[3]**

**8.** Passage C. Explain how Shakespeare uses the soliloquy convention and the language of the
speech to present Macbeth's state of mind. **[4]**

**9.** Extended task (Paper 1-style practice). Plan a guided analysis of Passage B in response to
this guiding question: **How does the playwright use staging and dialogue to show what the brothers
cannot say to each other?** Give a thesis and a paragraph plan with evidence. **[8]**

**10.** Extended task (comparative practice). Plan a comparison of Passages A and B in response to
this question: **How do the two texts use objects left behind to present grief?** Give a thesis, a
paragraph plan that compares throughout, and one model comparative paragraph. **[10]** (Paper 2 is an
essay on two literary works; this practises the structure.)

## Answers

**1.** Third-person limited narration [1], focalised through Nadia, for example "She had meant to
start in the kitchen", which gives her intentions from the inside. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Name the focaliser; "third person" alone does not show whose view shapes the
passage.

**2.** "Well." is a one-word sentence in Nadia's own voice, a verbal shrug, with no "she thought".
[1] "She would be practical." gives her self-instruction in short, firm sentences, as if talking
herself into control. [1] The blend of voices shows grief held down by effort, preparing for her
failure in the next line. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers show *why* the thought is unmarked; weak ones only define it.

**3.** Analepsis (flashback). [1] The memory of the argument with "the man from the bank" and the
father asking "too brightly" about pancakes shows a man hiding trouble from his child. [1] The past
reframes the present: the adult now understands what the child only felt. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Credit comes from linking the flashback to the present, not retelling it.

**4.** It flatly contradicts the previous sentence ("She would start with the coat."), repeating
its words with "did not". [1] Set alone, it creates a pause on the page that mirrors her pause in
the hall. [1] The quiet reversal shows that grief defeats practical plans, without the narrator
ever naming the feeling. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Comment on layout and repetition together; white space is part of the effect.

**5.** Tomas is seated on a box in a "black suit, tie loosened", which suggests he has come from the
funeral and gone straight to work. [1] Daniel is "still in his overcoat" and "by the door", ready to leave and
not yet part of the task. [1] The stage picture sets up the conflict before a word is spoken. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers read position and costume as choices an audience sees.

**6. (a)** "It went when he --" [1] leaves unsaid "when he was alive" or "when he died", the death
neither brother can name. [1]
**(b)** A stopped clock suggests time halted by the death. [1] Tomas repeats "It doesn't go" and
wants it boxed, treating it as a fact to be packed. [1] Daniel holds on to it, treating it as
memory, so the one prop carries their two ways of grieving. [1]
*Examiner insight:* For a prop, show how characters handle it differently.

**7.** The door banging upstairs makes both men look up, as if the absent person were still in the
house. [1] Daniel then speaks "quietly": the sound softens him and he claims the clock. [1] Tomas's
"*(beat)* Course you will." is subtext: agreement on the surface, resentment underneath that
Daniel takes the keepsake without the work. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Stage directions such as "beat" and "quietly" shape delivery; quote them as
evidence.

**8.** The soliloquy gives the audience alone access to Macbeth's mind just before the murder. [1] The rhetorical questions ("Is this a dagger...?", "art
thou but / A dagger of the mind") show him unable to trust his own senses. [1] He addresses the
dagger directly ("Come, let me clutch thee"), and the contrast "I have thee not, and yet I see thee
still" shows sight and touch at odds. [1] The phrase "heat-oppressed brain" names his own disturbed state, so
the audience watches a mind divided between the act and its horror. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Explain the convention's effect on the audience, not only the imagery.

**9.** The real Paper 1 is marked holistically against the IB criteria, not point by point;
these are indicative points.

Thesis: the brothers never mention their loss directly; the playwright places it in what they do
with objects, in pauses and in a sound from an empty room. [1]

- P1: the opening stage picture sets Tomas (seated, working) against Daniel (coat on, by the
  door). [1]
- P2: clipped lines ("I said I'd come Saturday." / "You said that in March.") turn small talk into
  accusation. [1]
- P3: the unfinished line "It went when he --" and Tomas's cut-in show a death neither can name.
  [1]
- P4: the clock, handled differently by each brother, shows two ways of grieving. [1]
- P5: the pauses and the refusal to hand over the clock let silence carry the conflict. [1]
- P6: the off-stage door bang draws both men's eyes upwards, the absent person made audible. [1]
- Ending: "Course you will." leaves the conflict open; the audience hears what Tomas does not say.
  [1]

*Examiner insight:* Strong plans are organised by argument; weak plans go line by line into
summary.

**10.** The real exam is marked holistically against the IB criteria; these are indicative points.

Thesis: both texts present grief through objects the dead leave behind, but the prose enters one
mind through narration, while the play shows grief outwardly, through props, silence and
conflict. [1]

- P1 (method): Passage A uses free indirect style to show Nadia's inner struggle; Passage B has no
  narrator, so grief appears in how the brothers handle the clock. [1]
- P2 (objects): the coat that "still held the shape of his shoulders" and the stopped clock both
  keep the dead present; one is described to a reader, the other held on stage. [1]
- P3 (time): Passage A reaches back twenty years through analepsis; Passage B gives the past only
  through dialogue ("You said that in March", "This was Grandad's"). [1]
- P4 (silence): the white space before "She did not start with the coat." and the long pause in
  Passage B both let absence speak. [1]
- P5 (difference): Nadia grieves alone; the brothers grieve against each other, so the play adds
  conflict, and form decides whether grief is shown inwardly or outwardly. [1]

Model comparative paragraph:

> Both writers make an object stand in for the dead, but the forms handle it differently. Nadia's
> father survives in a coat that "still held the shape of his shoulders" [1], an image the narrator
> can linger over, because prose can pause on a detail. [1] Passage B cannot describe the clock at
> that length; the audience watches Daniel turn it over and refuse to hand it back, so its meaning
> comes from action. [1]
> Where Nadia's object stops her acting, the brothers' object starts an argument, so the play
> makes grief a conflict between people rather than a private paralysis. [1]

*Examiner insight:* Strong comparisons link the texts in every paragraph; two separate blocks,
one per text, read as two single-text essays placed side by side.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a technique (analepsis, soliloquy) without explaining its effect here.
- Ignoring stage directions in Passage B or quoting them without saying what the audience sees.
- Retelling the flashback in Passage A instead of linking it to the present scene.
- Dropping long quotations in without comment rather than embedding a few precise words.
- Writing "this creates tension" without naming what creates it.
- In the comparison, describing each passage separately with no linking sentence.
