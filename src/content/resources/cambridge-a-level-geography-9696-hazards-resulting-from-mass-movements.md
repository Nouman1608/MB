## Nature: a classification of mass movements

Mass movement is the downslope movement of rock, regolith or soil under gravity. It is classified by the **type of movement**, the **material** and the **speed and water content**.

| Type | Movement | Speed | Typical setting and hazard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rockfall | Free fall of detached blocks | Very rapid | Steep jointed cliffs, road cuttings; blocks hit vehicles and buildings |
| Topple | Blocks rotate forward about a base | Rapid | Steep slopes with vertical joints |
| Translational slide | Mass moves along a planar surface such as a bedding plane | Slow to very rapid | Beds dipping parallel to the slope |
| Rotational slide (slump) | Mass rotates on a curved failure surface | Slow to rapid | Clay and weak rock, often undercut at the base |
| Debris flow | Mixed rock, soil and water flowing like wet concrete | Rapid to very rapid | Steep channels after intense rain |
| Mudflow | Fine-grained, very wet flow | Rapid | Deforested or volcanic slopes |
| Earthflow | Slow flow of saturated fine soil | Slow | Gentle clay slopes |
| Soil creep | Gradual movement of soil particles | Extremely slow | Most slopes; damages walls and pipes over years |

The most dangerous hazards to life are the **rapid** ones: falls, rapid slides and flows give little or no time to escape. Slow movements mainly damage property and infrastructure.

## Causes: the balance of forces

Slope stability depends on two sets of forces:

- **Shear stress** (driving forces): the component of the weight of material acting down the slope. It increases with slope angle and with added weight, such as water, buildings or fill.
- **Shear strength** (resisting forces): friction between particles and cohesion that binds them. It decreases with weathering, with fewer roots, and above all with **rising pore-water pressure**, which pushes particles apart and reduces friction.

The ratio of resisting to driving forces is the **factor of safety**. A slope with a factor of safety above 1 is stable; at or below 1 it fails.

**Causes can be grouped as follows.**

1. **Preparatory factors** (slowly lower the factor of safety): weathering, steepening by river or wave undercutting, removal of vegetation, and long-term rainfall that raises the water table.
2. **Triggers** (cause failure at a particular moment): intense or prolonged rain, rapid snowmelt, earthquakes, and sudden human actions such as cutting the base of a slope.

**Human causes** include deforestation (losing root cohesion and interception), road cuttings that steepen and undercut slopes, adding weight with buildings or waste tips, leaking water pipes and poor drainage, quarrying and mining, and reservoir filling, which raises pore-water pressure in valley sides.

## Impacts on lives and property

**Primary impacts** include deaths and injuries from burial or impact, destruction of homes, roads, railways and pipelines, and loss of farmland. **Secondary impacts** include blocked rivers forming landslide-dammed lakes that later burst and cause floods, loss of access for rescue and supplies, disease and homelessness, and economic losses from damaged transport routes and tourism.

### Examples of different types

- **Aberfan, Wales, 21 October 1966:** a colliery spoil tip on a hillside above the village, built over springs, became saturated and flowed downhill into the village. It killed 144 people, 116 of them children in Pantglas Junior School. The disaster shows a human-made slope and a human-caused hazard, and it led to new UK laws on the safety of tips.
- **Vajont, Italy, 9 October 1963:** a huge landslide fell into the reservoir behind the Vajont Dam. The displaced water overtopped the dam, which itself survived, and flooded the valley below, destroying the town of Longarone and killing around 2,000 people. Filling the reservoir had raised pore-water pressure in the weak valley side.
- **Vargas, Venezuela, December 1999:** days of exceptional rain on steep coastal mountains triggered debris flows and flash floods that swept through towns built on alluvial fans at the foot of the slopes. Estimates of deaths range widely, into the tens of thousands, because many bodies were never recovered.

These examples show three routes to disaster: building on unstable human-made material, altering hydrology through engineering, and settling on the depositional zones of past flows.

## Prediction and monitoring

Exact timing cannot be predicted, but **likelihood** can be estimated.

- **Rainfall thresholds:** records of past landslides are compared with rainfall intensity and duration, giving a threshold above which landslides are likely. Warnings are issued when forecast or measured rain exceeds it.
- **Ground monitoring:** inclinometers in boreholes detect movement at depth; extensometers measure cracks opening; GPS and satellite radar detect small surface movements; piezometers measure pore-water pressure.
- **Accelerating movement:** a slope that creeps faster and faster can signal approaching failure, so frequent readings can give warning of collapse.

## Hazard mapping

Landslide susceptibility maps combine slope angle, geology, soil type, drainage, vegetation, land use and the inventory of past landslides. Each area is ranked, for example low, moderate or high susceptibility. Maps support **land-use zoning** (no new building on high-risk slopes), insurance, and the targeting of engineering works.

## Preparedness and slope management

**Hong Kong** offers a long-running example. After deadly landslides in the 1970s, including the collapse at Po Shan Road in 1972, the government set up the Geotechnical Control Office in 1977 (now the Geotechnical Engineering Office). It registers and checks man-made slopes, sets standards for slope design, upgrades old slopes, issues landslip warnings during heavy rain, and runs public education. The approach shows that a dense city on steep slopes can reduce risk through sustained investment and regulation.

Common engineering measures include:

- **drainage** (surface channels and horizontal drains) to lower pore-water pressure;
- **regrading** to reduce slope angle;
- **retaining walls**, **soil nails** and **rock bolts** to add resisting force;
- **netting and catch fences** to stop rockfall;
- **afforestation** to add root cohesion and interception;
- **debris basins** and check dams to trap flows in channels.

## Perception of risk

People may underestimate mass movement risk because:

- slow movements seem harmless until a sudden failure;
- landslides affect small areas, so few people have experience of them;
- land on or below slopes may be the only affordable land, especially in informal settlements;
- warnings may come too often without an event, reducing trust;
- hazards from human-made slopes, such as tips, may not be seen as natural hazards at all.

## Worked example

*Explain how heavy rainfall can trigger a rotational slide. [5]*

> Rain infiltrates and raises the water table in the slope [1]. Water adds weight, increasing shear stress [1]. Pore-water pressure rises, pushing particles apart and reducing friction, so shear strength falls [1]. When shear stress exceeds shear strength (factor of safety below 1), the mass fails along a curved surface [1], rotating backward as it slips and leaving a scar at the top and a bulging toe at the base [1].

## Common mistakes

- Using "landslide" for every mass movement; name the type.
- Explaining failure without the balance of shear stress and shear strength.
- Treating a trigger as the only cause and ignoring preparatory factors.
- Saying mass movements cannot be monitored; they can, though timing is uncertain.
- Forgetting secondary impacts such as landslide-dammed lakes.

## Exam technique

**Describe** the nature of a movement by type, speed and material. **Explain** causes as a chain linking factors to shear stress or shear strength. **Assess** management by comparing engineering, mapping and warnings, and by asking who can afford them.
