Health is the state of physical and mental well-being, and the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification asks you to
explain how disease, lifestyle and other factors affect it, how risk factors are linked to non-communicable
diseases, and what cancer is. In the exam you must also **handle data**: work out disease rates, read charts and
scatter diagrams, and judge whether a study's sample and evidence support its conclusion.

This guide teaches three linked sections of Topic 4.2 Organisation: **4.2.2.5 Health issues**, **4.2.2.6 The
effect of lifestyle on some non-communicable diseases** and **4.2.2.7 Cancer** (specification Version 1.0, for
teaching from September 2016 and exams from 2018). All three are assessed on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher
Tier, and none of the content is marked (HT only) or (biology only), so everything here applies to every student.

## What is the difference between health and disease?

The specification defines **health** as *the state of physical and mental well-being*. Two things follow from that
wording. First, health includes mental well-being, so an answer that only talks about the body is incomplete.
Second, health is not simply "not being ill": a person can have no diagnosed disease and still be in poor health
because of stress or poor diet.

**Diseases** are major causes of ill health. They come in two kinds:

| Type | Caused by a pathogen? | Can spread between people? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communicable | Yes (virus, bacterium, fungus or protist) | Yes | Measles, salmonella food poisoning |
| Non-communicable | No | No | Coronary heart disease, Type 2 diabetes, most cancers |

The specification also names **other factors** that can have a profound effect on both physical and mental
health: **diet**, **stress** and **life situations**. Life situations cover things such as where you live, your
income and your access to healthcare. Learn these three by name; a 2-mark "give two factors" question expects them.

## How can different types of disease interact?

Diseases do not happen in isolation. The specification lists four interactions, and you should be able to give
each with an example.

1. **Defects in the immune system** make a person more likely to suffer from infectious diseases. HIV, for
   example, damages white blood cells, so infections that a healthy immune system would control can take hold.
2. **Viruses living in cells can trigger cancers.** Infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) can lead to cervical
   cancer, which is why the HPV vaccine is offered to young people in the UK.
3. **Immune reactions first caused by a pathogen can trigger allergies**, such as skin rashes and asthma.
4. **Severe physical ill health can lead to depression** and other mental illness.

In an "explain" question, link cause and effect: "HIV destroys white blood cells, so the body is less able to
destroy pathogens, so the person is more likely to get other infections."

## How do you handle disease incidence data?

**Incidence** means the number of new cases of a disease in a population over a stated time. Because populations
differ in size, you compare **rates**, not raw numbers.

```
rate per 100 000 people = (number of new cases / population) x 100 000
```

**Worked example 1.** Town A (population 600 000) records 1 020 new cases of a disease in a year. Town B
(population 150 000) records 330.

- Town A: 1 020 / 600 000 x 100 000 = **170 per 100 000**
- Town B: 330 / 150 000 x 100 000 = **220 per 100 000**

Town A has more cases, but Town B has the higher rate, so a person in Town B is more likely to develop the disease.

### Which chart should you draw?

| Data | Chart |
|---|---|
| Separate categories, such as cases in different countries | Bar chart with gaps between bars |
| Continuous data grouped into classes, such as cases by age group | Histogram with no gaps |
| Two variables measured for each person or place | Scatter diagram |
| Raw counts sorted into groups | Frequency table first, then a bar chart or histogram |

**Worked example 2 (histogram with unequal classes).** In a survey, 180 people aged 20 to under 40 had a condition.
The class width is 20 years, so the bar height is frequency density = 180 / 20 = **9 people per year of age**. With
unequal class widths, the **area** of each bar, not its height, shows the frequency.

### What does a scatter diagram show?

If the points rise from left to right there is a **positive correlation**; if they fall, a **negative
correlation**; if they show no pattern, **no correlation**. A correlation shows that two variables are linked. It
does not prove that one causes the other. That distinction is behind most of the marks in this section.

## What are the principles of sampling for epidemiological data?

Epidemiology is the study of patterns of disease in populations. Scientists can rarely study every person, so they
study a **sample**. A sample gives trustworthy results when it is:

- **large**, so that a few unusual individuals do not distort the pattern;
- **representative** of the population (similar ages, sexes and backgrounds);
- chosen **at random**, so the researchers do not bias it.

Confidence grows when **other independent studies** find the same pattern. When you evaluate a study, comment on
each of these points using the information given in the question.

## What is a risk factor?

A **risk factor** is something linked to an **increased rate** of a disease. The specification gives two kinds:

- **aspects of a person's lifestyle**, such as smoking, diet, exercise and alcohol;
- **substances in the person's body or environment**, such as high blood cholesterol or ionising radiation.

For some risk factors a **causal mechanism** has been proven, meaning scientists can explain how the factor causes
the disease. For others there is a correlation but no proven mechanism yet. **Many diseases are caused by the
interaction of a number of factors**, which is why one heavy smoker may never get lung cancer and a non-smoker
may.

## Which lifestyle links does AQA specify?

| Risk factor | Disease or effect | How it works (at GCSE depth) |
|---|---|---|
| Diet, smoking and lack of exercise | Cardiovascular disease | A diet high in saturated fat raises blood cholesterol; smoking damages artery walls; both lead to fatty deposits that narrow arteries. Regular exercise lowers the risk |
| Obesity | Type 2 diabetes | Body cells no longer respond properly to insulin |
| Alcohol | Liver and brain function | Alcohol is broken down in the liver; long-term heavy drinking damages liver cells (for example cirrhosis). Alcohol also slows reactions and can damage brain tissue over time |
| Smoking | Lung disease and lung cancer | Tar damages the airways and alveoli and contains carcinogens |
| Smoking and alcohol in pregnancy | Harm to the unborn baby | Carbon monoxide in smoke reduces the oxygen the blood carries, linked to low birth weight and premature birth; alcohol crossing the placenta can damage the baby's development, including the brain |
| Carcinogens, including ionising radiation | Cancer | Carcinogens damage DNA, so cells may begin to divide uncontrollably |

### How does lifestyle change incidence at different levels?

The incidence of non-communicable diseases follows lifestyle in a population. **Locally**, an area where more people
smoke tends to have more lung cancer. **Nationally**, rising obesity has been accompanied by rising Type 2
diabetes. **Globally**, as diets and habits change in many lower- and middle-income countries, non-communicable
diseases are becoming more common there too. When asked to "explain", name the factor, the disease and the
direction of the change.

### What are the human and financial costs?

| Level | Human cost | Financial cost |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Pain, reduced quality of life, shorter life expectancy | Lost income; care costs |
| Local community | Families become carers; people leave work | Pressure on local health and care services |
| Nation | Fewer healthy people in work | Cost of treatment (in the UK mostly through the NHS); lost productivity |
| Global | Many early deaths | Large health spending; slower economic development |

A "discuss" question wants both human **and** financial costs, at more than one level.

**Worked example 3 (interpreting risk data).** In a study, 45 in every 1 000 smokers and 9 in every 1 000
non-smokers developed a lung disease. 45 / 9 = 5, so smokers in this sample were **5 times** as likely to develop
the disease. To conclude that smoking causes it, you would also need a known mechanism (such as tar damaging the
alveoli), a large representative sample, and other studies that agree.

## What is cancer?

Cancer is **the result of changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled growth and division**. Normally, cell division
by mitosis is controlled. When those controls are lost, cells keep dividing and form a mass called a **tumour**.

| | Benign tumour | Malignant tumour |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A growth of abnormal cells | Cancer cells |
| Where it stays | Contained in one area, usually within a membrane | Invades neighbouring tissues |
| Does it spread? | No, it does not invade other parts of the body | Yes: cells break off and travel in the **blood** to other parts of the body |
| What forms elsewhere | Nothing | **Secondary tumours** |

A benign tumour can still be harmful if it presses on an organ, but it is not cancer. The word to remember for
malignant tumours is **spread**: they invade, travel in the blood and form secondary tumours.

### What causes cancer?

Scientists have identified **lifestyle risk factors** for various types of cancer: for example, smoking and lung
cancer, and exposure to carcinogens and ionising radiation (such as X-rays and gamma rays) more generally. There are
also **genetic risk factors** for some cancers: some people inherit gene variants that raise their risk (for
example, inherited faulty BRCA1 or BRCA2 genes raise the risk of breast cancer). The link to 4.2.2.5 is also
examinable: viruses living in cells, such as HPV, can trigger cancer.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a risk factor the same as a cause?**
No. A risk factor is linked to an increased rate of a disease. It becomes a cause only when a causal mechanism has
been shown, and even then it does not guarantee that a particular person will get the disease.

**Why do studies compare rates rather than numbers of cases?**
Because populations are different sizes. A rate per 100 000 people puts every population on the same scale, so you
can see where a person is genuinely more likely to become ill.

**Can a benign tumour turn into cancer?**
The specification only asks you to distinguish benign tumours (contained, not invading) from malignant tumours
(invading and spreading in the blood to form secondary tumours). Answer exam questions with those definitions.

**Is Type 2 diabetes a communicable disease?**
No. It is non-communicable: it is not caused by a pathogen and cannot be passed from person to person. Obesity is
the risk factor the specification names.

**Does a scatter diagram showing a correlation prove the link?**
No. It shows the two variables change together. Other factors could explain the pattern, so you need a mechanism
and repeated, well-sampled studies before claiming cause.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that smoking "causes" a disease when the data only shows a correlation; say it **increases the risk** or
  rate unless the question gives a mechanism.
- Leaving mental well-being out of the definition of health.
- Comparing raw numbers of cases between populations of different sizes.
- Saying malignant tumours spread "through the body" without saying **in the blood** and naming **secondary
  tumours**.
- Describing a benign tumour as "harmless" or "not made of abnormal cells".
- Describing a trend on a graph without quoting any figures.
