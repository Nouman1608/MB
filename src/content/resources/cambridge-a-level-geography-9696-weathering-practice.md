## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between weathering and erosion. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how pressure release (dilatation) weathers rock. **[3]**

**3.** Name the mineral in granite that is altered by hydrolysis, and the clay mineral that is formed. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why carbonation can be effective in cool climates. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how salt crystal growth breaks down rock. **[3]**

**6.** Name the **two** climatic variables plotted on a Peltier diagram. **[2]**

## Section B

## Answers

**1.** Weathering is the breakdown of rock in situ, at or near the surface [1]. Erosion is the wearing away and removal of material by an agent such as water, ice or wind [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Rock formed at depth is under great pressure from the rock above [1]. When the overlying rock is removed by erosion, the pressure is released and the rock expands [1]. Cracks form parallel to the surface, so layers or sheets of rock break away [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Feldspar [1] is altered to kaolinite (china clay) [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Cold water can hold more dissolved carbon dioxide than warm water [1], so rainwater forms more carbonic acid, which reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone and chalk [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Saline water enters pores and cracks in the rock [1]. As the water evaporates, salt crystals grow [1], exerting pressure on the pore walls that loosens grains and widens cracks [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Mean annual temperature [1] and mean annual rainfall [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Site B [1]. It has by far the most freeze-thaw cycles, 110 a year [1], so water in joints repeatedly freezes, expands and thaws, widening cracks [1].

**(b)** At site A, temperatures stay below 0°C for most of the year [1], so water in joints remains frozen and rarely thaws and refreezes [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** Station L: strong chemical weathering [1]. Station M: moderate chemical weathering, with some frost action [1].

**(b)** Rainfall is very low, so little water is available for either chemical processes or freeze-thaw [1], and low temperatures slow chemical reactions [1].

**(c)** Annual means hide the number of freeze-thaw cycles and the diurnal range [1]; the diagram ignores rock type and structure, which strongly affect weathering [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Roots grow into joints and widen them as they thicken, splitting rock [1]. Roots also open pathways that let water reach rock surfaces [1]. Decaying vegetation releases carbon dioxide into the soil, making soil water more acidic [1], which speeds carbonation and other chemical weathering [1]. A vegetation and soil cover holds moisture against the rock and moderates temperature changes, which reduces heating/cooling and freeze-thaw at the rock surface [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Limestone is mainly calcium carbonate, so it is weathered by carbonation [1], as rainwater containing carbonic acid forms soluble calcium bicarbonate, which is removed in solution [1]. Weathering concentrates along joints and bedding planes, widening them into grikes and leaving blocks between [1]. Granite is weathered mainly by hydrolysis of feldspar to clay [1], which leaves loose quartz grains and causes the rock to crumble [1]. Granite's joints and its expansion when pressure is released also control where weathering is deepest [1]. **[6]**

**11.** On steep slopes, weathered material is removed quickly by mass movement and runoff [1], exposing fresh rock to further weathering [1]. On gentle slopes and valley floors, deep regolith accumulates and keeps water in contact with rock, favouring chemical weathering [1]. Altitude and aspect affect temperature, so high or shaded slopes may have more freeze-thaw cycles [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For the view: temperature controls the rate of chemical reactions, so chemical weathering is fastest in hot climates [1]. Moisture is needed for almost all processes, so dry climates have weak weathering [1]. Freeze-thaw depends on temperatures fluctuating around 0°C [1]. The Peltier diagram shows clear climatic zones of weathering type [1].

Against the view: rock type determines which processes can act, such as carbonation on limestone or hydrolysis on granite [1]. Rock structure, including joints and porosity, controls how far water can penetrate [1]. Relief and vegetation change local conditions within one climatic zone [1].

Judgement: climate sets the broad type and rate of weathering at a global scale, but at a local scale rock type and structure often explain why neighbouring rocks weather differently in the same climate [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing the coldest site for freeze-thaw instead of the one with the most cycles.
- Describing chemical weathering without naming the mineral or rock affected.
- Writing "acid rain" as the cause of carbonation; natural rainwater is already weakly acidic.
- Using the Peltier diagram without saying which zone each place falls in.
- Ignoring rock type and structure in a discussion of climate.
