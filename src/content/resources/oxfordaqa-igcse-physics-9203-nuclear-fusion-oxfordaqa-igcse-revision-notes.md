## The four statements in one line each

- **3.7.4 a:** fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus.
- **3.7.4 b:** some of the mass of the smaller nuclei is converted into energy.
- **3.7.4 c:** the repulsion between the two positive nuclei must be overcome, which happens at very high temperatures and pressures.
- **3.7.4 d:** fusion is the process by which energy is released in stars.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Nuclear fusion | Two light nuclei join to form one heavier nucleus |
| Light nucleus | A nucleus with a small mass number, such as hydrogen-1, hydrogen-2, hydrogen-3 or helium-3 |
| Mass number | Total number of protons and neutrons; conserved in a fusion equation |
| Atomic number | Number of protons; conserved in a fusion equation |
| Repulsion | The push apart between two positively charged nuclei; stronger the closer they are |
| Core of a star | The centre, where temperature and density are greatest and most fusion takes place |
| Main sequence | The stable stage of a star's life when hydrogen nuclei fuse to make helium nuclei in the core |

## Particles you will meet in fusion equations

| Particle | Symbol | Mass number | Atomic number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton (hydrogen-1 nucleus) | ¹₁H | 1 | 1 |
| Neutron | ¹₀n | 1 | 0 |
| Hydrogen-2 nucleus | ²₁H | 2 | 1 |
| Hydrogen-3 nucleus | ³₁H | 3 | 1 |
| Helium-3 nucleus | ³₂He | 3 | 2 |
| Helium-4 nucleus | ⁴₂He | 4 | 2 |

## Balancing method

1. Add the mass numbers on the left. Subtract the known mass numbers on the right.
2. Do the same with the atomic numbers.
3. If the equation shows "2X", divide the leftover numbers by two.
4. Identify the particle from its pair of numbers: (1, 1) proton, (1, 0) neutron, (4, 2) helium-4.
5. Check both totals again.

**Worked reminders.**

- ¹₁H + ²₁H → ᴬ_Z X: A = 1 + 2 = 3, Z = 1 + 1 = 2, so X is ³₂He.
- ²₁H + ²₁H → ³₁H + Y: A = 4 − 3 = 1, Z = 2 − 1 = 1, so Y is a proton.
- ³₂He + ³₂He → ⁴₂He + 2X: 6 − 4 = 2 and 4 − 2 = 2, shared between two particles, so each X is ¹₁H.

## Mass and energy: the key distinction

| | Before fusion | After fusion |
|---|---|---|
| Total mass number | Same | Same |
| Total atomic number | Same | Same |
| Total actual mass | Slightly larger | Slightly smaller |

The small decrease in mass is converted into energy, carried away mainly as kinetic energy of the products. With supplied mass data, subtract the total after from the total before; a percentage is that decrease divided by the total before, times 100. For hydrogen-2 + hydrogen-3 → helium-4 + neutron the decrease is under half of one per cent of the starting mass.

## The conditions chain

Learn it as a sequence you can write out in order:

1. All nuclei contain protons, so they are positive.
2. Two positive nuclei repel; the repulsion grows as they approach.
3. Fusion needs the nuclei to get very close, so the repulsion must be overcome.
4. **Very high temperature:** nuclei move very fast and have enough kinetic energy to get close.
5. **Very high pressure:** nuclei are packed closely, so close collisions happen often.

Extra link: nuclei with more protons repel more strongly, so fusing them needs a higher temperature. Helium nuclei (+2) need a hotter core than hydrogen nuclei (+1). This is why more massive stars, with hotter cores, can build heavier nuclei.

## Fusion in stars

- Gravity pulls a star's material inwards, producing extreme temperature and density in the core.
- Main sequence: hydrogen nuclei fuse to make helium nuclei in the core.
- The energy released keeps the core hot and leaves the star as light and heat.
- Larger stars later fuse helium into carbon, then build heavier nuclei such as nitrogen and oxygen.
- Fusion in stars produces the naturally occurring elements.

## Fission against fusion

| | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclei | One large unstable nucleus | Two light nuclei |
| Change | Splits into two smaller nuclei | Join into one heavier nucleus |
| Trigger | Absorbs a neutron | Very high temperature and pressure |
| Typical setting in 9203 | Nuclear reactors | Stars |
| Energy source | Products have slightly less mass | Products have slightly less mass |

## Exam traps

- "Atoms fuse." Write **nuclei**; electrons are not involved.
- "The mass number goes down." It balances. The **mass** goes down slightly.
- "High temperature gives the nuclei energy" with nothing more. Say the energy lets them get close **despite the repulsion**.
- Giving temperature but not pressure, or pressure but not temperature, when both are asked for.
- Missing the factor of two in an equation with "2X".
- "Stars burn hydrogen." Burning is chemical; stars release energy by **nuclear fusion**.

## Quick self-test

1. Define nuclear fusion.
2. Why must the nuclei in fusion be "light"?
3. Complete: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ?
4. Complete: ¹₁H + ²₁H → ᴬ_Z X.
5. ³₂He + ³₂He → ⁴₂He + 2X. Identify X.
6. In fusion, which is conserved: mass number, or actual mass?
7. Where does the energy released by fusion come from?
8. Why do two nuclei repel each other?
9. Explain why a very high temperature is needed for fusion.
10. Explain why a very high pressure is also needed.
11. Why does fusing helium nuclei need a higher temperature than fusing hydrogen nuclei?
12. Name the process that releases energy in a main sequence star, and say where in the star it mainly happens.

### Answers

1. Two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus.
2. The specification defines fusion as joining light nuclei; the lightest nuclei carry the least charge and repel least, so they fuse most readily.
3. A neutron, ¹₀n (5 − 4 = 1; 2 − 2 = 0).
4. ³₂He (A = 3, Z = 2).
5. A proton, ¹₁H: the leftover 2 and 2 are shared between two particles.
6. Mass number is conserved. The actual mass decreases slightly.
7. Some of the mass of the smaller nuclei is converted into energy.
8. Both contain protons, so both are positively charged, and like charges repel.
9. At a very high temperature the nuclei move very fast, so they have enough kinetic energy to get close enough to fuse despite the repulsion.
10. It packs the nuclei close together, so close collisions are frequent and fusion happens at a high enough rate.
11. A helium nucleus has a charge of +2, so two helium nuclei repel more strongly than two hydrogen nuclei (+1 each); more kinetic energy is needed to overcome this.
12. Nuclear fusion of hydrogen nuclei into helium nuclei, mainly in the core, where temperature and density are greatest.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between nuclear fusion and nuclear fission?**
Fusion joins two light nuclei into one heavier nucleus and needs very high temperature and pressure. Fission splits one large unstable nucleus into two smaller ones after it absorbs a neutron.

**Why is nuclear fusion difficult to achieve?**
Nuclei are positively charged and repel each other. Only at very high temperatures and pressures do they move fast enough, and meet often enough, to get close and fuse.
