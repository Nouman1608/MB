## What the specification lists

- Explain break-even, contribution, margin of safety, target level of profit and a stepped fixed cost.
- Construct and interpret a break-even graph.
- Calculate and interpret contribution, break-even, margin of safety and target profit, and the effect of changes in costs and revenue.
- Evaluate the impact of changes, and the impact and usefulness of break-even analysis.
- Recommend and justify how to lower break-even.
- Evaluate special order decisions.

## Core recall

### Formulae

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Contribution per unit | Price − variable cost per unit |
| Break-even output | Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit |
| Break-even revenue | Break-even output × price |
| Margin of safety (units) | Actual output − break-even output |
| Margin of safety (%) | Margin of safety ÷ actual output × 100 |
| Target output | (Fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit |
| Profit | (Contribution per unit × output) − fixed costs |

Round break-even output up to the next whole unit.

### Constructing a graph

1. Axes: output (horizontal), £ (vertical).
2. Fixed cost line: horizontal.
3. Total cost line: starts at fixed costs, rises by variable cost per unit.
4. Total revenue line: starts at the origin, rises by price per unit.
5. Mark break-even where revenue and total cost cross.
6. Mark loss (left) and profit (right) areas, and the margin of safety.

### Effects of changes

| Change | Break-even | Margin of safety |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs rise | Rises | Falls |
| Variable cost rises | Rises | Falls |
| Price rises (demand holds) | Falls | Rises |
| Price falls | Rises | Falls |
| Output plan rises | No change | Rises |

### Stepped fixed cost

- Fixed cost that stays constant over a range of output and then jumps to a higher level when capacity is passed.
- The model is only valid within one step.

### Special orders

- Accept if: spare capacity, no extra fixed costs, price above variable cost (positive contribution), and no knock-on effect on regular prices.
- Refuse or negotiate if: regular customers would demand the same price, or capacity could earn more elsewhere.
- Short-run decision only.

### Ways to lower break-even

Raise price, cut variable cost, cut fixed cost, change the product mix. Each has a risk to demand or quality.

### Usefulness

- Strengths: simple; shows risk; helps pricing and loan applications; "what if" tests.
- Limits: one product, constant price and variable cost; assumes all output is sold; ignores stepped costs; depends on forecasts; says nothing about demand.

## Quick worked reminder

Cobb Candles (invented): price £12, variable cost £5, fixed costs £42,000, planned sales 8,000. Contribution £7; break-even 6,000 candles (£72,000 revenue); margin of safety 2,000 (25%); profit £14,000; target profit £21,000 needs 9,000 candles. Fixed costs up to £49,000 give break-even 7,000. A special order of 1,500 candles at £8 adds 1,500 × £3 = £4,500 of contribution.

## Exam prompts and how to approach them

- **"Calculate the margin of safety":** state break-even output, subtract it from actual output, and give the result in units and as a percentage.
- **"Evaluate a price rise using break-even":** show the new break-even, then test whether demand could fall enough to cancel the benefit.
- **"Evaluate a special order":** work out contribution, check spare capacity and fixed costs, and comment on the effect on regular prices.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Contribution | Price minus variable cost per unit |
| Break-even point | Output at which total revenue equals total cost |
| Margin of safety | How far sales can fall before a loss is made |
| Target profit | A planned profit used to find the output needed |
| Stepped fixed cost | A fixed cost that jumps when capacity is exceeded |
| Special order | A one-off order, often at a lower price |
| Total cost line | Fixed plus variable costs at each output |

## Exam traps

- Divide fixed costs by contribution, not by price.
- The total cost line starts at the fixed cost level.
- Rounding: break-even is rounded up.
- A margin of safety is relative to actual or planned output, not to break-even.
- A special order is judged on contribution, with a comment on the regular price.

## Self-test

1. Define contribution per unit.
2. Price £25, variable cost £10, fixed costs £60,000. Calculate break-even output.
3. Calculate break-even revenue.
4. Planned output is 5,000. Calculate the margin of safety in units and as a percentage.
5. Calculate the profit at 5,000 units.
6. Calculate the output needed for a profit of £30,000.
7. Fixed costs rise to £66,000. State the new break-even output.
8. Name one effect of a price rise on break-even.
9. What is a stepped fixed cost?
10. 600 units are offered at £16 each. Calculate the contribution from the order.
11. Give one condition for accepting a special order.
12. Give one limitation of break-even analysis.

### Answers

1. Price minus variable cost per unit.
2. Contribution £15; £60,000 ÷ £15 = **4,000 units**.
3. 4,000 × £25 = **£100,000**.
4. 5,000 − 4,000 = **1,000 units**; 1,000 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **20%**.
5. (£15 × 5,000) − £60,000 = **£15,000**.
6. (£60,000 + £30,000) ÷ £15 = **6,000 units**.
7. £66,000 ÷ £15 = **4,400 units**.
8. It raises the contribution, so break-even output falls (if demand holds).
9. A fixed cost that stays constant up to a level of output and then jumps to a higher level.
10. £16 − £10 = £6 per unit; 600 × £6 = **£3,600**.
11. Any one: spare capacity; no rise in fixed costs; no effect on regular prices; positive contribution.
12. Any one: assumes all output is sold; assumes constant price and variable cost; ignores demand.
