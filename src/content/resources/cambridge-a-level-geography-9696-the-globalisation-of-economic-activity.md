## Global patterns of resources, production and markets

Think of the global economy as three overlapping maps:

- **Resources**: where raw materials, energy and labour are found. Oil and gas are concentrated in the Middle East, Russia and the Americas; many metal ores in Australia, Latin America and Africa; large pools of lower-cost labour in South and South-East Asia and Africa.
- **Production**: where goods are made. Manufacturing has shifted towards East and South-East Asia since the 1970s, while high-value design and research remain concentrated in high-income countries.
- **Markets**: where goods and services are bought. Purchasing power is still greatest in North America, Europe and East Asia, but middle-income markets in Asia and Latin America are growing fastest.

Globalisation is the increasing connection of these three maps through trade, investment and information flows. A single product may use minerals from one continent, be assembled on a second and sold on a third. Questions on patterns reward answers that describe where each element is concentrated and explain the flows that link them.

## Kinds of foreign direct investment

FDI is investment that gives a firm lasting control over a productive asset abroad. Distinguish it by **how** it enters a country and **why**.

| Type | Meaning | Example of the idea |
|---|---|---|
| Greenfield | Building new facilities from scratch | A carmaker building a new assembly plant on an empty site |
| Brownfield (merger or acquisition) | Buying or merging with an existing foreign firm | Tata Motors of India buying Jaguar Land Rover in 2008 |
| Horizontal | Doing the same activity abroad as at home | A retailer opening stores in another country |
| Vertical | Controlling a different stage of the supply chain abroad | A food firm buying plantations that supply it |

Motives are often grouped as **resource-seeking** (access to minerals or crops), **market-seeking** (selling inside a large market and avoiding tariffs) and **efficiency-seeking** (lower costs of labour, land or tax).

FDI is no longer only from high-income to lower-income countries. Firms from emerging economies now invest abroad, including in high-income countries: Lenovo of China bought IBM's personal computer business in 2005. This reverse flow is one of the main limitations of the original NIDL model.

## Factors in TNC growth and spatial structure

A TNC's spatial structure is the pattern of where its different functions are located. Common structures:

- **Hierarchical**: headquarters and research in the home country, regional headquarters in world cities, production and routine services in lower-cost locations. This is the classic NIDL pattern.
- **Regional production**: plants within each major market region, each serving that region. This reduces transport costs and trade barriers and allows products to be adapted to local tastes, a strategy often called **glocalisation**.
- **Networked or contract-based**: the TNC controls design, branding and logistics but most production is done by independent contractors. Nike's model is of this type.

Factors that change the structure include transport and ICT costs, trade agreements and tariffs, wage differences, government incentives, exchange rates, and risks such as natural hazards, political instability or supply-chain disruption.

**Contrast: Toyota.** Toyota's headquarters is in Toyota City, Aichi Prefecture, Japan. Unlike Nike, it owns many of its overseas plants, which is greenfield FDI. Its first wholly owned US plant at Georgetown, Kentucky began production in 1988, and its UK plant at Burnaston, Derbyshire in 1992. These locations were market-seeking: building inside North America and the European market reduced exposure to trade barriers and exchange-rate swings. Its just-in-time system depends on suppliers clustering close to assembly plants. Comparing Toyota with Nike shows that TNCs in different industries choose very different spatial structures.

## A template for the TNC case study

The syllabus asks for "global spatial organisation and operation". Organise your chosen TNC under these headings:

1. **Origin and headquarters**: home country, date and city, what functions stay there.
2. **Research, design and management**: where knowledge work is located and why.
3. **Production**: owned plants or contractors, which countries, how this has shifted.
4. **Markets and distribution**: main sales regions, distribution hubs, any adaptation to local markets.
5. **Operation**: supply-chain methods, how decisions flow, labour and environmental standards.
6. **Change over time**: relocations, acquisitions, closures, reshoring.
7. **Evaluation**: impacts on home and host countries, and how well the case fits the NIDL.

A case study with all seven headings and specific places, dates and figures can answer any 14.2 TNC question.

## The emergence and growth of NICs

NICs are often grouped in generations:

- **First generation** (from the 1960s): Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea and Taiwan.
- **Second generation** (from the 1970s and 1980s): Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and the Philippines.
- **Later industrialisers**: China from the 1980s, and more recently Vietnam.

**Factors**, grouped for essays:

| Group | Factors |
|---|---|
| State policy | Export-oriented strategy, export processing zones, tax breaks, planning of strategic industries |
| Labour and skills | Lower wages at first, then investment in education to move into higher-value work |
| Capital | Inward FDI, high domestic savings, state-directed finance |
| Location and access | Coastal ports, position on shipping routes, access to large markets |
| Global context | Falling transport costs, TNCs seeking low-cost locations, open markets in high-income countries |

**Second-generation example: Malaysia.** A free trade zone in Penang in the early 1970s attracted electronics assembly by US firms; Intel opened its first assembly plant outside the United States there in 1972. Electronics became a leading export. This shows the second generation relying more on inward FDI than South Korea, which built domestic conglomerates. Evaluate the two routes: FDI brought jobs and technology quickly but left much design and profit with foreign firms.

## Changes in the location of economic activity

Two questions define any relocation: **who** does the work, and **where**?

| | Same firm | Another firm (outsourced) |
|---|---|---|
| **Same country** | In-house, domestic | Domestic outsourcing |
| **Another country (offshored)** | Captive offshoring: the firm's own subsidiary abroad | Offshore outsourcing: a contractor abroad |

The syllabus examples fit this matrix: **outsourcing of manufacturing** (a brand contracting factories abroad) and **offshoring of services** (call centres, software or accounting moved abroad, either to the firm's own subsidiary or to a contractor).

**Causes:** cost differences, especially wages; ICT that allows services to be delivered remotely; containerisation; skilled, English-speaking graduates in some host countries; government incentives; and the wish to focus on core activities.

**Impacts:**

- *Host country*: jobs, skills, export earnings and urban growth; but low wages in some sectors, dependence on foreign decisions, and concentration in a few cities.
- *Source country*: cheaper goods and services and higher profits for firms; but job losses in manufacturing regions and some service jobs, contributing to regional decline.

**Recent trends.** Some firms now **reshore** (bring work back home) or **nearshore** (move it closer to the home market, such as US firms using Mexico), because of rising wages in former low-cost locations, trade tensions, supply-chain shocks and automation. This suggests relocation is not a one-way process.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all TNC expansion as FDI; contracting is not FDI.
- Describing a TNC's locations without explaining why each function is there.
- Using only one generation of NICs, so factors appear uniform.
- Confusing outsourcing (who) with offshoring (where); use the matrix.
- Giving impacts only for host countries; source-country impacts are part of the syllabus wording.
- Writing about the NIDL without evaluating its fit with today's patterns.

## Command words

- **Describe** a TNC's spatial organisation: functions, places and hierarchy.
- **Explain** NIC growth: link each factor to how it raised output or exports.
- **Assess** impacts: weigh benefits against costs for named groups and reach a judgement.
