## Questions

**1.** Define the term *activation energy*. **[1]**

**2.** Explain why most collisions between reactant particles do not lead to a reaction. **[2]**

**3.** State three features of a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution curve for a sample of gas at a fixed
temperature. **[3]**

**4.** A sealed sample of a gas mixture is warmed from 300 K to 310 K.

**(a)** Describe how the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution at 310 K differs from the one at 300 K. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why this small rise in temperature causes a large increase in the rate of reaction. **[2]**

**5.** In the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, the concentration of H₂O₂ falls from 1.20 mol dm⁻³ to
0.84 mol dm⁻³ in 90 s. Calculate the mean rate of reaction over this time. Give the units. **[2]**

**6.** A student investigates how temperature affects the rate of reaction between sodium thiosulfate and
hydrochloric acid. She times how long it takes for a cross under the flask to disappear.

| Temperature / °C | 25 | 35 | 45 | 55 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time, t / s | 96 | 50 | 26 | 14 |

**(a)** Calculate 1/t for each temperature, to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times faster the reaction is at 55 °C than at 25 °C. **[1]**
**(c)** State two variables she must keep the same. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why 1/t can be used as a measure of the rate. **[1]**
**(e)** Suggest why both solutions are warmed separately before they are mixed. **[1]**
**(f)** Identify one hazard in this experiment and a precaution to reduce the risk. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how increasing the concentration of hydrochloric acid affects the rate of its reaction with
magnesium. **[3]**

**8.** Excess calcium carbonate powder reacts with 25 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid. The carbon dioxide is
collected in a gas syringe.

| Time / s | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 | 120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of CO₂ / cm³ | 0 | 36 | 60 | 76 | 86 | 90 | 90 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of reaction between 0 and 40 s. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean rate of reaction between 40 and 80 s. **[1]**
**(c)** A tangent drawn to the curve at t = 0 passes through (0, 0) and (15 s, 40 cm³). Calculate the initial
rate. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the rate decreases as the reaction proceeds. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why the volume stops changing after 100 s. **[1]**

**9.** Use a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to explain how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction
between gases at constant temperature. **[4]**

**10.** A 2.00 g sample of calcium carbonate reacts with excess hydrochloric acid in a flask on a balance.
After 50 s the mass has fallen by 0.55 g. Mr of CO₂ = 44.0; Mr of CaCO₃ = 100.1.

```
CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
```

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in moles, of CO₂ released in the first 50 s. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean rate of CO₂ release in mol s⁻¹ over this time. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the maximum mass of CO₂ that the sample can release. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage of the calcium carbonate that has reacted after 50 s. **[1]**

**11.** A reaction between two gases is carried out in a closed reactor. Explain, in terms of collisions and
the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, the effect on the rate of each of these changes made separately:
increasing the pressure at constant temperature; increasing the temperature at constant pressure; adding
a catalyst. **[6]**

**12.** A student writes: "Doubling the concentration of a reactant and adding a catalyst both increase the
rate for the same reason: each one increases the proportion of particles with energy above the activation
energy." Evaluate this statement. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The minimum energy that particles must have when they collide for a reaction to occur [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "Minimum" is the key word; "the energy needed to start a reaction" usually earns nothing.

**2.** Most particles have energy less than the activation energy [1]; so when they collide no bonds break and
the particles move apart unchanged, an unsuccessful collision [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer about collision frequency does not score here; the mark depends on comparing
particle energies with Ea.

**3.** Any three: the curve starts at the origin (no molecule has zero energy) [1]; it has a single peak at
the most probable energy, with the mean energy to its right [1]; it approaches but never meets the
energy axis, as there is no maximum energy [1]. Also allow: the area under the curve equals the total number
of molecules. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Each feature must be a separate point for its own mark; "it is a curve with a peak" alone
is too vague for credit.

**4. (a)** The peak moves to higher energy (to the right) [1]; the peak is lower [1]; the area under the curve
is unchanged because the number of molecules is the same [1].
**(b)** Many more molecules now have energy equal to or greater than Ea [1]; so the frequency of successful
collisions rises sharply, by much more than the small rise in collision frequency [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), "particles move faster and collide more often" alone gets at most one mark; the
proportion with E ≥ Ea must be the main reason.

**5.** Change in concentration = 1.20 − 0.84 = 0.36 mol dm⁻³; rate = 0.36 ÷ 90 [1] = **0.0040 mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**
(4.0 × 10⁻³) with units [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The units mark is lost for mol dm⁻³ alone; rate units must include per unit time (s⁻¹).

**6. (a)** 1/96 = **0.0104 s⁻¹**; 1/50 = **0.0200 s⁻¹** [1]; 1/26 = **0.0385 s⁻¹**; 1/14 = **0.0714 s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** 96 ÷ 14 = **6.86 times** faster [1].
**(c)** Any two: volume and concentration of the thiosulfate solution [1]; volume and concentration of the
acid [1]; also allow the same cross, flask or total volume.
**(d)** The same amount of sulfur forms each time before the cross is hidden, so rate is proportional to
1/t [1].
**(e)** So the mixture is at the recorded temperature from the moment the reaction starts [1].
**(f)** Sulfur dioxide is produced, which is toxic and an irritant [1]; work in a well-ventilated room (or fume
cupboard) and use small volumes [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* For (c), "the amount of solution" is too vague; a variable needs a named quantity such as
volume or concentration.

**7.** At higher concentration there are more acid particles in the same volume [1]; so collisions between acid
particles and the magnesium surface are more frequent [1]; so there are more collisions with energy ≥ Ea per
unit time and the rate increases [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing that the particles "have more energy" at higher concentration contradicts the
answer and can cost the final mark.

**8. (a)** 60 ÷ 40 = **1.5 cm³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** (86 − 60) ÷ 40 = 26 ÷ 40 = **0.65 cm³ s⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Gradient = 40 ÷ 15 [1] = **2.7 cm³ s⁻¹** (2.67) [1].
**(d)** The acid is used up so its concentration falls [1]; so collisions become less frequent and there are
fewer successful collisions per second [1].
**(e)** All the hydrochloric acid has reacted (it is the limiting reagent) [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Allow error carried forward in (c) from a tangent you have drawn yourself, provided the
gradient is read from two well-separated points on the tangent.

**9.** A catalyst provides an alternative reaction route with a lower activation energy [1]; the distribution
curve itself does not change, as the temperature is constant [1]; with Ea(cat) to the left of Ea, a greater
proportion of molecules have energy ≥ the activation energy [1]; so there are more successful collisions per
unit time and the rate increases [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A diagram that shifts or reshapes the curve for the catalysed reaction loses the "curve
unchanged" mark even if the words are right.

**10. (a)** 0.55 ÷ 44.0 = **0.0125 mol** [1].
**(b)** 0.0125 ÷ 50 = **2.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol s⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Amount of CaCO₃ = 2.00 ÷ 100.1 = 0.01998 mol, which gives 0.01998 mol CO₂ (1 : 1) [1];
mass = 0.01998 × 44.0 = **0.879 g** [1].
**(d)** 0.55 ÷ 0.879 × 100 = **62.6 %** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Error carried forward is normally allowed from (a) into (b) and from (c) into (d), so show
each value you use.

**11.** Pressure: more molecules per unit volume [1]; so collisions are more frequent, more successful
collisions per second, rate increases; the proportion with E ≥ Ea is unchanged [1].
Temperature: the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve shifts to higher energy, so a much greater proportion of molecules
have E ≥ Ea [1]; so many more collisions are successful per second and the rate increases greatly [1].
Catalyst: provides an alternative route of lower activation energy [1]; the curve is unchanged but more
molecules have E ≥ the lower Ea, so more collisions succeed per second and the rate increases [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* This is marked as two points for each change; an answer that explains two changes well
but skips the third cannot reach full marks.

**12.** The statement is partly correct: for a catalyst, a greater proportion of particles have energy above the
(lower) activation energy of the alternative route [1]. It is wrong for concentration: doubling the
concentration increases the frequency of collisions, not the proportion of particles with E ≥ Ea [1]. So the
two changes increase the rate for different reasons, and the statement is not valid [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a judgement; listing facts about each change without concluding whether
the statement is right usually loses the last mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "minimum" out of the activation energy definition.
- Drawing Maxwell–Boltzmann curves that touch the energy axis, start above the origin, or have different areas.
- Making the higher-temperature curve taller instead of lower.
- Explaining temperature only through collision frequency.
- Claiming that higher concentration or pressure gives particles more energy.
- Moving the distribution curve for a catalyst, or omitting "alternative route".
- Using t instead of 1/t for rate in a disappearing-cross experiment.
- Leaving out rate units, or giving mol dm⁻³ with no s⁻¹.
- Reading a tangent gradient from two points too close together.
- Naming a vague control variable such as "amount" without saying volume or concentration.
