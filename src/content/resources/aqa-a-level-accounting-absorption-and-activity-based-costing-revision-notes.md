## 3.12 Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Allocation | Charging a whole cost to the one product or cost centre that caused it (direct costs to products; a department's own supervisor to that department) |
| Apportionment | Sharing an indirect cost between cost centres on a fair basis |
| Absorption | Charging cost-centre overheads to products using an overhead absorption rate (OAR) |
| Under-absorption | Overheads absorbed < actual overheads; deduct the difference from profit |
| Over-absorption | Overheads absorbed > actual overheads; add the difference to profit |
| Cost pool | A group of overheads caused by one activity (set-ups, inspections, ordering) |
| Cost driver | The factor that makes a pool's cost change (number of set-ups, inspections, orders) |
| Attribution | Charging each pool to products by their use of its cost driver |

**Allocation vs apportionment.** Allocation needs no sharing: the cost belongs to one place.
Apportionment always needs a basis because the cost is shared.

## Apportionment bases

| Overhead | Basis |
|---|---|
| Rent, rates, heating, lighting, building insurance | Floor area |
| Machinery depreciation and insurance | Cost or value of machinery |
| Machine power | Machine hours |
| Canteen, welfare, personnel | Number of employees |

Apportion with: share = total overhead × (department's basis ÷ total basis). Always check that the
shares add back to the total.

## Formulas

| What you need | Formula |
|---|---|
| OAR | Budgeted cost-centre overheads ÷ budgeted activity (machine hours, labour hours or units) |
| Overheads absorbed by a product | OAR × hours (or units) the product uses in that cost centre |
| Total cost per unit | Direct materials + direct labour + direct expenses + overheads absorbed |
| Overheads absorbed in the period | OAR × **actual** activity |
| Under/(over)-absorption | Actual overheads − overheads absorbed |
| Cost driver rate (ABC) | Cost of pool ÷ total quantity of driver |
| Overheads attributed (ABC) | Driver rate × driver quantity used by the product |
| Price with mark-up | Total cost × (1 + mark-up %) |
| Price with margin | Total cost ÷ (1 − margin %) |
| Profit difference (absorption vs marginal) | Change in inventory units × fixed production overhead per unit |

## Method in steps

### Absorption costing

1. Allocate overheads that belong to one department.
2. Apportion shared overheads on suitable bases.
3. Total each production department and check the grand total.
4. OAR: machine hours for machine-intensive departments; labour hours for labour-intensive ones.
5. Product cost = direct costs + (OAR × hours used) in each department.

### Activity based costing

1. Allocate direct costs to products.
2. Group overheads into cost pools; pick a cost driver for each.
3. Driver rate = pool ÷ total driver quantity.
4. Attribute: rate × each product's driver quantity.
5. Total each product's overheads, divide by units, add to direct costs.

## Worked reminders

**Pell Printing, Finishing department (absorption):**

```
Budget: overheads £54 000, 4 500 direct labour hours
OAR = 54 000 ÷ 4 500 = £12 per direct labour hour
Actual: 4 300 hours, overheads £53 100
Absorbed = 4 300 × 12 = £51 600
51 600 − 53 100 = −1 500  → under-absorbed £1 500 (reduce profit)
```

**Wren Bakery, deliveries cost pool (ABC):**

```
Pool £18 000; driver = number of deliveries; 600 deliveries in total
Driver rate = 18 000 ÷ 600 = £30 per delivery
Cakes use 420 deliveries: 420 × 30 = £12 600
Cakes made 9 000 → 12 600 ÷ 9 000 = £1.40 per cake
```

**Why under- or over-absorption happens:**

- actual activity differs from budgeted activity (more hours absorb more overhead)
- actual overhead spending differs from the budget
- both at once, sometimes partly cancelling out.

## When ABC changes the answer most

ABC gives very different costs from absorption when:

- products differ a lot in batch size, so set-ups and orders are not linked to volume
- overheads are a large share of total cost
- a low-volume, complex product shares a factory with a high-volume, simple one.

In that case a labour-hour or machine-hour rate under-costs the low-volume product and over-costs the
high-volume one. Prices set from absorption cost can then sell the complex product at a loss.

## Inventory and profit: the three methods

| | Marginal | Absorption | ABC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inventory valued at | Variable production cost | Full production cost (OAR) | Full production cost (driver rates) |
| Fixed production overheads | All charged in the period | Part carried forward in inventory | Part carried forward in inventory |
| Inventory rising | Lowest profit | Higher profit | Higher profit (amount depends on which products are in inventory) |
| Inventory falling | Highest profit | Lower profit | Lower profit |
| Production = sales | Same profit | Same profit | Same profit |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Mark-up vs margin**: mark-up is a % of cost; margin is a % of selling price. A 25% mark-up and a
  20% margin give the same price.
- **Budgeted vs actual activity**: budgeted activity sets the OAR; actual activity gives overheads
  absorbed.
- **Under vs over**: under-absorbed means products were charged too little, so profit falls when it is
  corrected.
- **Absorption vs ABC**: absorption uses one volume-based rate per department; ABC uses several rates,
  one per activity, many not linked to volume.
- **Full cost vs marginal cost**: full cost suits long-run pricing and inventory valuation; marginal
  cost suits short-term decisions.

## Benefits and limitations in brief

| Method | Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Absorption | Prices aim to cover all production costs; fixed production costs matched with the inventory they made; simple | Arbitrary apportionment; volume-based rates distort costs; producing for inventory raises profit; under/over-absorption |
| ABC | More accurate product costs and prices; shows which products really earn profit; drivers can be managed | Costly to set up and maintain; judgement in choosing pools and drivers; some costs have no clear driver |
| Marginal | No apportionment; profit follows sales; contribution suits short-term decisions | Inventory excludes fixed production overheads; prices on marginal cost may not cover fixed costs; cost splitting needs estimates |

## Quick self-test

1. Define apportionment.
2. Rent of £48 000 is shared between departments X (600 m²), Y (1 000 m²) and Z (400 m²). Apportion it.
3. A department has budgeted overheads of £87 500 and 12 500 budgeted machine hours. Calculate the OAR.
4. Using question 3, actual machine hours were 13 000 and actual overheads £89 900. Calculate the
   under- or over-absorption and its effect on profit.
5. A unit uses £22 of materials, £18 of labour and 1.5 machine hours at the OAR in question 3.
   Calculate its total cost and the price at a 30% mark-up.
6. Total cost is £64. Calculate the price for a 20% margin.
7. A purchasing pool costs £36 000 for 240 orders. Product K uses 90 orders and 3 000 units are made.
   Calculate the purchasing cost per unit of K.
8. Suggest a cost driver for (a) machine set-up costs (b) despatch costs.
9. Production 6 000 units, sales 5 200, no opening inventory, fixed production overheads absorbed at
   £5 per unit. By how much does absorption profit differ from marginal profit?
10. Which method gives the lowest closing inventory value, and why?
11. Give two reasons why overheads may be under-absorbed.
12. A product's ABC cost is £42 and its absorption cost £32. It is priced at a 25% mark-up on
    absorption cost. Calculate the profit or loss per unit measured against ABC cost.

### Answers

1. Sharing an indirect cost between cost centres on a fair basis.
2. X £14 400; Y £24 000; Z £9 600 (total £48 000).
3. 87 500 ÷ 12 500 = **£7.00 per machine hour**.
4. Absorbed 13 000 × 7 = £91 000; 91 000 − 89 900 = **£1 100 over-absorbed**; profit rises by £1 100.
5. 22 + 18 + (1.5 × 7) = **£50.50**; × 1.3 = **£65.65**.
6. 64 ÷ 0.8 = **£80**.
7. Rate 36 000 ÷ 240 = £150; 90 × 150 = £13 500; ÷ 3 000 = **£4.50 per unit**.
8. (a) Number of set-ups. (b) Number of despatches (deliveries).
9. Inventory rises by 800 units; 800 × 5 = **£4 000 higher** under absorption.
10. Marginal costing, because its inventory excludes fixed production overheads.
11. Actual activity below budget; actual overhead spending above budget.
12. Price 32 × 1.25 = £40; 40 − 42 = **loss of £2 per unit**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using budgeted activity, not actual activity, to find overheads absorbed.
- Treating under-absorption as extra profit: it reduces profit.
- Applying a mark-up when the question gives a margin, or the reverse.
- Dividing an ABC pool by the units made instead of by the total driver quantity.
- Stopping at total attributed overheads and not dividing by units.
- Not checking that apportioned shares add back to the original overhead.
- Choosing labour hours as the OAR for a clearly machine-intensive department without explanation.
- Stating that absorption profit is always higher than marginal profit; it depends on the change in
  inventory.
- Listing benefits of ABC without applying them to the business in the question.
