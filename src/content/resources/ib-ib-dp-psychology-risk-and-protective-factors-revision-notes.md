These notes cover **"risk and protective factors"**, a content point in the "determinants of health" topic of the health psychology option (IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2019). The rest of the option is in the [health psychology revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-health-psychology-revision-notes/); full study descriptions and an essay plan are in the [risk and protective factors study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-risk-and-protective-factors/).

## Recall in one minute

- **Risk factor:** raises the likelihood of a health problem.
- **Protective factor:** lowers the likelihood, or reduces the harm done by a risk factor.
- Both work at group level and in probabilities, not certainties.
- They act through a **behavioural** pathway (what people do) and a **physiological** pathway (what happens in the body).
- Protection often matters most when risk is high: the **buffering hypothesis**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Risk factor | a characteristic or experience that increases the likelihood of a health problem |
| Protective factor | a characteristic or experience that decreases that likelihood or weakens a risk factor's effect |
| Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) | abuse and household dysfunction in childhood, such as violence or a parent's substance misuse |
| Graded (dose-response) relationship | the more of a factor, the greater the effect |
| Behavioural pathway | a factor affects health by changing behaviour, such as smoking or seeking care |
| Physiological pathway | a factor affects health directly through body systems, such as the HPA axis and immune function |
| Social integration | the number and variety of a person's social ties |
| Perceived support | believing that others would help if needed |
| Main effect of support | benefit at all levels of stress |
| Buffering effect | benefit mainly under high stress |
| Prospective design | measuring the factor first and following people forward to see who becomes ill |

## Studies at a glance

| Study | Factor | Design | Finding | Key evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Felitti et al. (1998) | ACEs (risk) | questionnaire at a San Diego health-appraisal clinic, compared with health records | graded relationship between number of ACE categories and adult risk behaviours and diseases | correlational; retrospective recall; insured sample in one city |
| Cohen, Tyrrell and Smith (1991) | psychological stress (risk) | stress index, then controlled exposure to a cold virus in quarantine | infection and cold rates rose with stress, after controlling for health behaviours | exposure controlled; stress not manipulated; ethics of infection |
| Berkman and Syme (1979) | social ties (protective) | Alameda County survey; deaths over nine years | fewest ties linked to higher mortality, after controlling for baseline health, SES and health practices | prospective; correlational; ties counted, quality not measured |
| Cohen et al. (1997) | social network diversity (protective) | network questionnaire, then viral exposure | more diverse networks, lower susceptibility to colds | combines controlled exposure with a naturally varying factor |
| Cohen and Wills (1985) | social support | review | integration has a main effect; perceived support buffers under stress | theoretical framework rather than a single test |

## Evaluation checklist

- **Correlation and causation:** most factors cannot be manipulated; say what else could explain the link.
- **Confounding variables:** socio-economic status often affects both stress and health.
- **Direction:** ill people may lose social ties; prospective designs reduce this problem.
- **Measurement:** self-report and retrospective recall can be inaccurate.
- **Mechanism:** does the study show the behavioural route, the physiological route or neither?
- **Ethics:** viral challenge studies need informed consent, medical supervision and a mild illness.
- **Culture:** what counts as support, and how adversity is reported, varies between cultures.

## Model paragraph

> Social ties appear to protect health. Berkman and Syme (1979) surveyed adults in Alameda County and
> recorded deaths over nine years; those with the fewest social ties had higher mortality, even when
> baseline health, income and health practices were taken into account. Because ties were measured
> before deaths occurred, it is unlikely that illness caused the isolation. However, the data remain
> correlational, and the study counted relationships without measuring their quality, so it cannot
> show whether support itself, or something associated with being well connected, is protective.

## Exam traps

- **"Stress causes colds."** Say stress was associated with greater susceptibility when exposure was controlled.
- **No pathway.** Explain how the factor reaches the body.
- **Lists without research.** Each factor needs a study.
- **Protective factors as opposites.** A protective factor can reduce the effect of a risk, not only be its absence.
- **No health problem named** when the question asks for one.

## Self-test

1. Define a risk factor and a protective factor.
2. Distinguish the behavioural and physiological pathways.
3. What is a graded relationship, and which study showed one?
4. Give two ACE categories.
5. Why is the ACE study's evidence about childhood open to question?
6. How did Cohen, Tyrrell and Smith (1991) control exposure to infection?
7. Which pathway does their study support, and why?
8. What was the follow-up period in Berkman and Syme (1979)?
9. Why is a prospective design a strength in that study?
10. What did Cohen et al. (1997) find?
11. Distinguish the main effect and buffering effect of social support.
12. Give one ethical issue in viral-challenge research.

**Answers**

1. A risk factor increases the likelihood of a health problem; a protective factor decreases it or reduces a risk factor's effect.
2. The behavioural pathway works by changing behaviour, such as smoking; the physiological pathway works directly through body systems, such as stress hormones and immune function.
3. The more of a factor, the stronger the effect; Felitti et al. (1998), for the number of ACE categories.
4. Any two: psychological, physical or sexual abuse; living with someone who misused substances, had a mental illness, was imprisoned or was violent towards the mother.
5. It relied on adults' retrospective recall of childhood, which may be inaccurate.
6. Participants were given nasal drops containing a virus (or salt water) and kept in quarantine.
7. The physiological pathway, because health behaviours such as smoking and alcohol were controlled.
8. Nine years.
9. Social ties were measured before deaths, so illness is less likely to have caused isolation.
10. People with more diverse social networks were less susceptible to colds after viral exposure.
11. A main effect benefits health at all levels of stress; a buffering effect protects mainly when stress is high.
12. For example: deliberately causing illness; the need for full informed consent, medical supervision and the right to withdraw.
