This guide covers one subtopic in depth: **3.6.4 The motor effect**, statements a to d of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered. The specification says you should be able to **identify the direction of the force using Fleming's left-hand rule**, so most of this page is practice in applying the rule correctly, plus explaining why a coil turns. The subtopic is qualitative: the specification lists factors that increase the force but gives no equation for its size.

For a combined overview with electromagnetic induction, see the [electromagnetic induction, generators and the motor effect guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-electromagnetic-induction-generators-and-the-motor/). Afterwards, use the [motor effect revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-the-motor-effect-oxfordaqa-igcse-revision-notes/) and the [motor effect practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-the-motor-effect-oxfordaqa-igcse-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | A current-carrying conductor has a magnetic field around it. Placed in a magnetic field so that it cuts lines of magnetic force, the magnet and conductor exert a force on each other: the motor effect. No force if the conductor is parallel to the field |
| b | The force increases with a stronger magnetic field, a larger current, and a greater length of conductor in the field |
| c | Reversing either the current or the field reverses the force; use Fleming's left-hand rule |
| d | A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field tends to rotate: the basis of an electric motor |

## Why there is a force (statement a)

A current in a wire creates its own circular magnetic field around the wire. When the wire sits in the field of a magnet, the two fields interact. On one side of the wire they point the same way and combine to a stronger field; on the other side they point opposite ways and partly cancel. The wire is pushed from the stronger-field side towards the weaker-field side.

Notice the specification's wording: **the magnet and the conductor exert a force on each other**. The force is a pair. If the wire is pushed upwards, the magnet is pushed downwards with an equal force. This is why, in a demonstration where a wire is fixed and the magnet sits on a balance, the balance reading changes when the current is switched on.

**Angle matters.** The force is greatest when the wire is at 90° to the field lines. When the wire runs **parallel** to the field lines, it does not cut them and there is **no force**, however large the current.

## Increasing the force (statement b)

| Change | Effect on the force |
|---|---|
| Stronger magnetic field (stronger magnet, poles closer together) | Larger |
| Larger current (higher pd, lower resistance) | Larger |
| Longer length of wire inside the field (wider magnets, more turns of a coil) | Larger |

In a "suggest" question, name the change precisely. "Use a stronger magnet" and "increase the current" are both creditworthy; "add more power" or "a bigger magnet" (without saying stronger) may not be.

## Fleming's left-hand rule (statement c)

Hold the thumb and first two fingers of your **left** hand at right angles to each other.

- **F**irst finger: **F**ield, pointing from north to south.
- Se**c**ond finger: **C**urrent, pointing in the direction of conventional current (positive to negative).
- Thu**m**b: **M**otion, the direction of the force on the wire.

**Diagram conventions.** A wire coming out of the page is drawn as a dot (⊙). A wire going into the page is drawn as a cross (⊗). Field lines run from N to S.

## Worked example 1: three directions

In each case the field runs from left to right across the page (N on the left, S on the right).

```
(a) Current into the page    →  force downwards
(b) Current out of the page  →  force upwards
(c) Current up the page      →  force into the page
```

For (a), point your first finger to the right, turn your hand until your second finger points into the page, and your thumb points down. For (c), the current is in the plane of the page; your thumb ends up pointing into the paper. Always set the first finger first, then rotate the hand around it until the second finger matches the current.

## Worked example 2: reversing

A wire carries current into the page in a field pointing to the right, so the force is downwards. What happens if (i) the current is reversed, (ii) the magnet is turned round, (iii) both are reversed?

```
(i)   current out of page, field right  →  force upwards
(ii)  current into page, field left     →  force upwards
(iii) current out of page, field left   →  force downwards (unchanged)
```

Reversing **one** quantity reverses the force; reversing **both** leaves it unchanged. Exam questions use this to check you are not simply guessing "opposite".

## Coils and motors (statement d)

Place a rectangular coil between two magnet poles, with N on the left and S on the right, so the field runs to the right. Suppose the coil lies flat, parallel to the field, and current flows **into** the page along its left side and **out** of the page along its right side.

```
Left side:  current into page, field right   →  force downwards
Right side: current out of page, field right →  force upwards
```

The two forces are equal in size, opposite in direction and act on opposite sides of the axle, so they produce a turning effect: the coil rotates (anticlockwise as drawn). The two short sides of the coil run parallel to the field in this position, so they feel no force.

**The turning effect changes as the coil turns.** It is largest when the coil's plane is parallel to the field. After a quarter turn the forces still act up and down, but they now pull along the plane of the coil, through the axle, so there is no turning effect. The coil would rock back and settle there. To keep a motor turning, the current in the coil must be reversed every half turn, so the forces keep turning the coil the same way. (The generators subtopic states that detailed knowledge of split rings is not required, but you should know that this reversal is what keeps the rotation going.)

**Making a motor turn faster or with more force:** more turns of wire on the coil, a larger current, or a stronger magnetic field.

## Common mistakes

- Using the right hand. Fleming's left-hand rule is for the force on a current (motors).
- Using electron flow instead of conventional current for the second finger.
- Pointing the field from S to N.
- Claiming there is a force on a wire parallel to the field.
- Saying reversing both current and field reverses the force.
- Forgetting that the magnet feels an equal and opposite force.

## Exam technique

- **State** the motor effect: current-carrying conductor, in a magnetic field, cutting the field lines, force on the conductor (and on the magnet).
- **Identify** a direction: state the rule used and give the direction in words (up, down, into or out of the page, left or right).
- **Explain** why a coil rotates: opposite currents in the two sides, so opposite forces, giving a turning effect about the axle.
- **Suggest** ways to increase speed or force: use the specification's three factors, plus more turns for a coil.
