**This practice set covers topic 2.1, the physical environment, in Specialist Option 2, Consumer Psychology, of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style questions on the key study, research methods and planning. All shops and people in the scenarios are fictional.

## Answers

**1.** 1 mark each. Strength: it uses space efficiently and makes staple items easy to find and restock (1). Weakness: shoppers move through quickly in straight lines, so there is less browsing and impulse buying (1).

**2.** 1 mark each for two features described: storefront, the entrance, signage and materials that tell passers-by what kind of shop it is (1); window displays, arrangements of products or themes to attract attention (1); landscaping, plants and outdoor features that make the exterior inviting.

**3.** 2 marks for each reason developed. Attention: noise draws attention away from the taste of food, so flavours such as sweetness seem weaker (1), as Woods et al. (2011) found for sweet and salty ratings (1). Masking or arousal: loud noise may mask the sounds of eating or raise arousal and discomfort (1), changing perceived crunchiness and making the meal less enjoyable (1).

**4.** Up to 3 marks. Crowding, whether too many people or too little space, makes shoppers feel less in control, reducing dominance (1). It may also reduce pleasure and raise arousal to an unpleasant level (1). These states lead to avoidance behaviours, such as leaving early or not returning, although the effect depends on expectations and tolerance, as Machleit et al. (2000) found (1).

**5. (a)** Up to 6 marks. Mehrabian and Russell proposed that environmental stimuli produce emotional states (1) of pleasure, arousal and dominance (1). These lead to approach behaviours, such as staying and spending, or avoidance behaviours, such as leaving (1). Odour is one such stimulus (1). Chebat and Michon (2003) studied a pleasant ambient scent in a shopping mall using questionnaires with shoppers (1), and found it improved perceptions of the mall environment and of product quality, which were linked to spending (1).

**5. (b)** Level-based: 9–10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation with cultural differences developed and a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Cultural differences*: pleasant scents, acceptable crowd levels and the meaning of music differ between cultures, so findings from one country may not generalise; a busy market may be expected and enjoyed in one culture and avoided in another.
- *Validity*: field studies in real malls and restaurants have high ecological validity, but many factors vary at once, so it is hard to isolate the effect of one stimulus.
- *Subjective data*: questionnaires about mood and perceptions are open to bias; spending data are more objective.
- *Individual differences*: Machleit et al. show crowding effects depend on expectations and tolerance, so situational explanations are incomplete.
- *Application and ethics*: findings help retailers design pleasant spaces, but influencing shoppers without awareness raises questions of manipulation.
- *Conclusion*: atmospherics do influence shoppers, but effects are modest and depend on person and culture.

**6. (a)** 1 mark each. IV: the style of background music (classical, pop or none) (1). DV: the amount spent per head, on each course and in total (1).

**6. (b)** Up to 2 marks. The IV (music style) was manipulated by the researchers (1), but the study took place in a real restaurant with ordinary diners in their normal setting (1).

**6. (c)** Up to 2 marks. Diners were not randomly allocated to evenings, so differences such as the day of the week or the kind of customers on each evening could explain the spending differences (1), meaning the study may not have measured the effect of music alone (1).

**7.** Up to 2 marks. Areni and Kim found that classical music led customers to choose more expensive wine (1), which supports North et al.'s conclusion that classical music primes an upmarket atmosphere that leads to higher spending (1).

**8.** **(a)** Up to 2 marks. For example: reduce the amount of stock on display and widen the aisles (1), so customers feel less spatially crowded and more in control (higher dominance), making them more likely to stay (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. For example: add plants or flower boxes outside the shop (landscaping) (1), because Mower et al. (2012) suggest landscaping increases liking for a store, which is linked to intention to shop there (1). Or: a clear, uncluttered window display showing a few items.

**9.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks. IV: slow-tempo music (for example below 70 beats per minute) or fast-tempo music (for example above 100 beats per minute) (1), played at the same volume on alternate days (1). DV: the time each shopper spends in the store (1), measured from the time stamp of entering through the door sensor to the time stamp on their till receipt (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. For example: keep the volume constant across conditions (1), because louder music could itself change arousal and how long people stay, confounding the effect of tempo (1).

**10.** 2 marks each. Strength: questionnaires can be given to many shoppers quickly and can measure perceptions and emotions that cannot be observed, such as pleasure (1), giving quantitative data that can be compared between scented and unscented conditions (1). Weakness: shoppers may not be aware of the scent's effect or may give socially desirable answers (1), so self-reports may not reflect how the scent really influenced their behaviour (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need details of the suggested studies such as Woods et al.?**
You need one study for each point marked "including a study". A clear outline of the method and main finding is enough; the key study (North et al.) needs full detail.

**Can I use the PAD model to explain music effects?**
Yes. Music can change pleasure and arousal, which is a good way to link 2.1.2 and 2.1.3 in an essay.
