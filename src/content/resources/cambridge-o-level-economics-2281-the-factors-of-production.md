## 1.2.1 The four factors and their rewards

**Factors of production** are the resources used to produce goods and services.

| Factor | Definition | Examples | Reward |
|---|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources: everything provided by nature that is used in production | Fields, forests, minerals, fish in the sea, rivers, oil | Rent |
| Labour | Human effort, physical and mental, used in production | A bus driver, a nurse, a software designer | Wages |
| Capital | Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services | Machines, tools, factories, delivery vans, computers in an office | Interest |
| Enterprise | The organising of the other factors and the taking of risks in production | The owner who starts a business and makes its key decisions | Profit |

### The nature of each factor

**Land** is limited in total amount, though its quality can be improved, for example by irrigation or fertilisers. Some land resources are renewable, such as forests and fish if they are not over-used; others, such as oil and minerals, are non-renewable and run out.

**Labour** has two dimensions. Its **quantity** is the number of workers and the hours they work. Its **quality** is the skill, education, experience and health of those workers, which decides how much each worker can produce.

**Capital** is defined by its **use**, not by what it is. A car owned by a family for holidays is a consumer good. The same model of car used as a taxi is capital, because it is used to produce a service. Capital wears out over time and has to be replaced, and making it requires using resources today to raise production in the future.

**Enterprise** is what turns the other three into a business. The entrepreneur decides what to make and how, combines the factors, and bears the risk that the business makes a loss. Profit is the reward for that risk; if the business fails, the entrepreneur may receive nothing.

### Worked example 1: classifying the resources of a firm

Bay Catch is an invented fishing business.

| Resource | Factor | Reward |
|---|---|---|
| The fish stocks in the bay | Land | Rent (paid, for example, for a licence to fish there) |
| Eight crew members | Labour | Wages |
| Two boats, nets and a cold store | Capital | Interest |
| The owner, who borrowed to buy the boats and decides where and when to fish | Enterprise | Profit |

The boats are capital because they are man-made and used to catch fish. The fish are land because they are a natural resource. The owner provides enterprise because she organises the business and bears the risk.

## 1.2.2 Mobility of the factors of production

**Factor mobility** is how easily a factor can move. There are two kinds.

- **Occupational mobility:** how easily a factor can move from one use (or job) to another.
- **Geographical mobility:** how easily a factor can move from one place to another.

### Labour

| Influences on geographical mobility | Influences on occupational mobility |
|---|---|
| Cost and availability of housing in the new area | Skills and qualifications needed for the new job |
| Family and social ties | Time and cost of training |
| The cost of moving | Natural ability and aptitude |
| Information about jobs elsewhere | Information about other jobs |
| Differences in language or culture, and rules on working in another country | Rules requiring licences or professional membership |

**Worked example 2.** A factory closes in a northern town. Its machine operators struggle to move to software jobs in the capital. They face **occupational immobility** (they lack programming skills, and training takes time and money) and **geographical immobility** (housing in the capital is expensive and their families live locally). Both kinds together explain why unemployment can stay high in one area even when there are vacancies elsewhere.

### Land

Land is **geographically immobile**: a field or a mineral deposit cannot be moved. But land can be **occupationally mobile**: a field can be switched from growing wheat to grazing animals, or sold for housing. Its occupational mobility is limited by its quality (rocky land cannot easily be farmed), by planning rules, and by the time it takes to convert it.

### Capital

Mobility depends on the type of capital.

- Some capital is **geographically mobile**: laptops, delivery vans and portable tools can be moved.
- Some is **geographically immobile**: a dam, a port or a factory building cannot.
- **Occupational mobility** is high for general-purpose capital (a van can carry many kinds of goods) and low for specialised capital (a machine that only bottles one type of drink).

### Enterprise

Enterprise tends to be **occupationally mobile**, because organising and risk-taking skills can be used in many industries. Its geographical mobility depends on the same influences as labour, plus factors such as differences in rules and taxes between countries.

## 1.2.3 Changes in the quantity and quality of factors

| Factor | Causes of a change in quantity | Causes of a change in quality |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Discovery of new resources; land reclamation; depletion of non-renewable resources; erosion or flooding | Irrigation, fertilisers and drainage improve it; over-farming and pollution damage it |
| Labour | Population size; age structure; changes in retirement or school-leaving age; migration; how many people choose to work | Education and training; healthcare and nutrition; work experience |
| Capital | Investment adds to it; depreciation (wearing out) reduces it | New technology makes capital more productive |
| Enterprise | Ease of starting a business; government support; taxes on profit; attitudes to risk | Education in business skills; experience; access to advice |

**Worked example 3: the quantity of labour (invented).** A country has 10 million people of working age, and 62% are in the labour force, so the labour force is 6.2 million. A policy encourages more people to work and the proportion rises to 65%. The labour force becomes 6.5 million: an increase of 0.3 million, or 4.8% (0.3 ÷ 6.2 × 100). The population has not changed, but the quantity of labour has.

**Worked example 4: quantity versus quality.** A government extends free vocational training. This does not increase the **number** of workers, but it raises their **quality**, so each worker can produce more. A new mine opening increases the **quantity** of land in use. A firm replacing old machines with faster ones (same number of machines) improves the **quality** of capital.

## Common errors

- Pairing capital with profit or enterprise with interest. Capital earns interest; enterprise earns profit.
- Calling money "capital". In this section, capital means man-made goods used in production.
- Saying all factors are immobile because land is. Land is geographically immobile but can change use.
- Giving only labour examples when a question asks about "the factors of production".
- Mixing up quantity and quality: more workers is quantity; better-trained workers is quality.

## Exam technique

- **Define** each factor precisely and add an example.
- **Identify** the factor in a case study by asking: natural, human effort, man-made, or organising and risk-taking?
- **Explain** a change in mobility or quality with a chain: cause, effect on the factor, effect on what the economy can produce.
- **Analyse** a case with more than one influence, as in worked example 2, and say which matters most for the people described.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is money a factor of production?**
No. Money is used to buy factors of production. Capital is the machines, tools and buildings bought with it.

**Can land be mobile?**
Land is geographically immobile but can be occupationally mobile, because its use can change, for example from farming to housing.
