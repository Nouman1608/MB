## 3.2.1 Water as a solvent

Many substances dissolve in water, and this matters in three places named by the syllabus.

- **Digestion:** enzymes work on food in solution, and the small, soluble products, such as glucose and amino acids, dissolve in water so they can be absorbed into the blood.
- **Excretion:** waste such as urea dissolves in water and is removed from the body in urine.
- **Transport:** substances are carried dissolved in water: glucose, amino acids, ions and carbon dioxide in blood plasma, mineral ions in the xylem of plants, and sucrose in the phloem.

## 3.2.2 and 3.2.3 Osmosis at Core level

Water **diffuses through partially permeable membranes** by osmosis, and water moves **into and out of cells** by osmosis through the **cell membrane**. A partially permeable membrane lets small molecules such as water pass but stops larger ones, such as sucrose or starch.

At Core level, describe the direction using **dilute** and **concentrated** solutions: water moves by osmosis from a dilute solution (more water) to a more concentrated solution (less water) across the membrane.

## 3.2.4 Investigating osmosis with dialysis tubing

Dialysis (Visking) tubing is partially permeable, so it behaves like a cell membrane.

1. Tie one end of a length of tubing, fill it with concentrated sucrose solution, and tie the other end (or attach a capillary tube).
2. Blot and weigh the tubing, then place it in a beaker of distilled water.
3. After a set time, such as 30 minutes, remove, blot dry and reweigh, or read the height of liquid in the capillary tube.
4. Set up a control: tubing filled with distilled water in distilled water.

**Result:** the sucrose-filled tubing gains mass and becomes firm, because water moves in by osmosis. Sucrose molecules are too large to pass out. The control shows little change.

### Worked example 1: percentage change in mass (illustrative)

The tubing's mass rose from 10.0 g to 11.6 g.

```
change = 11.6 − 10.0 = 1.6 g
percentage change = 1.6 ÷ 10.0 × 100 = 16%
```

Percentage change is used because pieces rarely start at exactly the same mass.

## 3.2.5 Plant tissue in solutions of different concentrations

### Method

1. Use a cork borer to cut potato cylinders of equal diameter; trim them to the same length.
2. Blot and weigh each one.
3. Place each in the same volume of a different sucrose concentration, from distilled water upwards.
4. Leave for the same time at the same temperature.
5. Blot and reweigh; calculate percentage change in mass.
6. Repeat for each concentration.

### Worked example 2: analysing results (illustrative data)

| Sucrose concentration / mol/dm³ | Start mass / g | End mass / g | Change / g | Percentage change / % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 2.50 | 2.80 | +0.30 | +12.0 |
| 0.2 | 2.40 | 2.52 | +0.12 | +5.0 |
| 0.4 | 2.60 | 2.55 | −0.05 | −1.9 |
| 0.6 | 2.50 | 2.30 | −0.20 | −8.0 |
| 0.8 | 2.45 | 2.18 | −0.27 | −11.0 |

- **Describe:** as concentration increases, the percentage change falls from +12.0% to −11.0%; tissue gains mass in dilute solutions and loses mass in concentrated ones.
- **Explain (Core):** in water and dilute solutions, water moves into the cells by osmosis; in concentrated solutions, water moves out.
- **No change:** the line crosses 0% between 0.2 and 0.4 mol/dm³. Reading a graph, or estimating in proportion, gives about **0.34 mol/dm³**. At this concentration there is no net movement of water, so it estimates the concentration of the potato cell contents.

**Why blot?** Surface water would add mass that did not enter by osmosis. Blot every cylinder the same way.

## 3.2.6 How water supports plants

Plants are supported by the **pressure of water inside the cells pressing outwards on the cell wall**. When a plant has enough water, its cells are firm, so stems and leaves stay upright. When it lacks water, the pressure falls and the plant wilts. Non-woody plants rely on this support.

## 3.2.7 to 3.2.9 Water potential -- Extended only

### The Extended definition

**Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane.**

**Water potential** describes the tendency of water to move out of a solution. Pure water has the highest water potential. Adding solute lowers it. So water always moves **down a water potential gradient**, from high to low.

### The four terms

| Term | Meaning | When it happens |
|---|---|---|
| Turgid | The cell is swollen and firm | Water has entered the vacuole by osmosis from a solution of higher water potential |
| Turgor pressure | The pressure of the cell contents pushing outwards against the cell wall | In a turgid cell; it supports the plant |
| Flaccid | The cell is soft, not firm | Water has left, or turgor pressure has fallen |
| Plasmolysis | The cell membrane and cytoplasm pull away from the cell wall | A lot of water has left, in a solution of much lower water potential |

**Model explanation (concentrated solution):** the solution outside has a lower water potential than the cell sap, so water leaves the vacuole by osmosis through the partially permeable cell membrane. The vacuole shrinks, turgor pressure falls and the cell becomes flaccid. If water loss continues, the membrane pulls away from the wall: the cell is **plasmolysed**.

**In water:** water enters by osmosis; the cell becomes **turgid**. The cell wall stops it bursting.

### Importance in uptake and loss of water

- **Root hair cells:** soil water usually has a higher water potential than the cell sap, so water enters by osmosis.
- **Leaf cells and wilting:** when water is lost faster than it is taken up, cells become flaccid and the plant wilts.
- **Animal cells** have no cell wall. In a solution of higher water potential, such as pure water, red blood cells take in water and **burst**; in a solution of lower water potential, they lose water and **shrink**. This is why the water potential of blood plasma must be kept steady.

## Common errors

- Writing that water moves "from a high concentration to a low concentration" without saying it is the **water** concentration; examiners prefer dilute/concentrated (Core) or water potential (Extended).
- Saying sucrose moves through the membrane.
- Describing plasmolysis in animal cells. Animal cells burst or shrink; plasmolysis needs a cell wall.
- Saying a turgid cell bursts. The wall prevents it.
- Leaving out "partially permeable membrane" from the definition.
- Comparing masses instead of percentage changes.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **State:** short Core facts, such as "water moves into and out of cells by osmosis through the cell membrane".
- **Describe** (results): trend plus values with units, including the change from gain to loss.
- **Explain** (Extended): name the higher and lower water potential, the direction of water movement, the membrane, and the effect on the cell using the named term.
- **Investigate:** variables, blotting, repeats, percentage change, control.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do Core candidates need water potential?** No. Core candidates describe osmosis using dilute and concentrated solutions; water potential is Extended.

**Why does a plant cell not burst in pure water?** The cell wall resists the pressure of the swelling vacuole, so the cell becomes turgid instead.

**Why use percentage change in mass?** Pieces start at different masses, so percentage change makes the results comparable.
