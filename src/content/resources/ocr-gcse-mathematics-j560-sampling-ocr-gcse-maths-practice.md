These questions follow OCR GCSE (9-1) Mathematics J560, Version 2.1 (August 2026), section 12.01, Sampling (12.01a Populations and samples). Every question is for **both tiers**. Section A has short questions; Section B asks you to estimate, compare and evaluate. In written answers, always refer to the context of the question. All people, places and figures are invented.

Learn the methods first in the [12.01 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-mathematics-j560-sampling-ocr-gcse-maths/) or the [12.01 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-mathematics-j560-sampling-ocr-gcse-maths-revision-notes/). ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** A garden centre wants to know the mean height of its 500 rose bushes. It measures 25 of them.

**(a)** State the population. **[1]**

**(b)** State the sample. **[1]**

**2.** A fireworks maker checks that its rockets work by lighting them. Give two reasons why it tests only a sample. **[2]**

**3.** In a sample of 120 students at a college, 42 walk to college. The college has 1800 students. Estimate how many students at the college walk. **[2]**

**4.** A club's 250 members are numbered 001 to 250. A random number generator gives:

317, 042, 198, 042, 250, 000, 006, 133

Use the numbers in order to choose a simple random sample of four members. **[2]**

**5.** Kai says, "My survey asked 5000 people, so it cannot be biased." Explain why Kai may be wrong. **[2]**

**6.** A survey contains the question: "Do you agree that the new car park is a waste of money?"

**(a)** Explain why this question may produce biased results. **[1]**

**(b)** Write a better version of the question. **[1]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** A cinema had 3600 customers in one week. A sample of 90 customers gave the type of film they watched.

| Film type | Action | Comedy | Drama | Animation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 34 | 25 | 18 | 13 |

**(a)** Estimate the number of customers that week who watched each type of film. **[3]**

**(b)** Give one reason why your estimates may not equal the true numbers. **[1]**

**8.** A farmer harvests 12 000 potatoes. A sample of 40 potatoes has a total mass of 7.6 kg.

**(a)** Find the mean mass of a potato in the sample, in grams. **[1]**

**(b)** Estimate the total mass of the harvest in kilograms. **[2]**

**(c)** The farmer sells potatoes at 45p per kg. Estimate the value of the harvest. **[1]**

**9.** A school has 900 students. Mia asks a random sample of 50 students and 14 say they play a musical instrument. Leo asks a random sample of 150 students and 34 say they play one.

**(a)** Use each sample to estimate the number of students at the school who play an instrument. **[2]**

**(b)** Which estimate is likely to be more reliable? Give a reason. **[1]**

**(c)** Combine the two samples to make a single estimate. **[2]**

**10.** A gym owner wants to know whether members want longer opening hours. She emails a questionnaire to every member and uses the first 60 replies she receives.

**(a)** Give two reasons why her sample may be biased. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe a better way to choose a sample of 60 members. **[2]**

**11.** A company has 480 employees. Describe in detail how the manager could take a simple random sample of 30 employees. **[3]**

**12.** A council wants to estimate how many of the 8400 households in a town have solar panels. A researcher stands outside a hardware shop one Saturday and asks 60 passers-by whether their household has solar panels. 18 say yes.

**(a)** Use these results to estimate the number of households in the town with solar panels. **[2]**

**(b)** Give two reasons why this estimate may be unreliable. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one improvement to the method. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **All 500 rose bushes** at the garden centre [1]

**(b)** **The 25 rose bushes** that are measured [1]

Total: 2

*Examiner insight:* The population is set by the question (all the roses), not by who was measured.

**2.** Testing destroys the rocket, so testing every one would leave none to sell [1]. Testing every rocket would also take too long or cost too much [1]

Total: 2

**3.** 42/120 = 0.35 [1]; 0.35 × 1800 = **about 630 students** [1]

Total: 2

**4.** Ignore 317 (out of range), the repeated 042 and 000 [1]; sample: **042, 198, 250, 006** [1]

Total: 2

*Examiner insight:* 250 is a member's number, so it is kept. 000 matches no member. Stop once four members are chosen: 133 is not needed.

**5.** Bias comes from how a sample is chosen, not from its size [1]. If the 5000 people were chosen in a way that favours some groups, for example volunteers or people in one place, the results would still lean one way [1]

Total: 2

**6. (a)** It is a leading question: the wording suggests the answer "yes", so people are pushed to agree [1]

**(b)** For example: "What is your opinion of the new car park?" with balanced options such as good value / neither / poor value [1]

Total: 2

**7. (a)** Scale factor 3600 ÷ 90 = 40 [1]; Action 34 × 40 = **1360**, Comedy 25 × 40 = **1000** [1]; Drama 18 × 40 = **720**, Animation 13 × 40 = **520** [1]

**(b)** For example: a different sample of 90 would probably give different frequencies; or the sample may not be representative of all customers that week [1]

Total: 4

*Examiner insight:* Check that the estimates add up to 3600: 1360 + 1000 + 720 + 520 = 3600.

**8. (a)** 7.6 kg = 7600 g; 7600 ÷ 40 = **190 g** [1]

**(b)** 190 × 12 000 = 2 280 000 g [1]; = **2280 kg** [1]

**(c)** 2280 × 0.45 = **£1026** [1]

Total: 4

*Examiner insight:* Treat the answers as estimates. The sample mean stands in for the mean of all 12 000 potatoes, which is unknown.

**9. (a)** Mia: 14/50 × 900 = **252**; Leo: 34/150 × 900 = **204** [1] [1]

**(b)** **Leo's**, because his sample is larger (150 compared with 50) and both samples are random [1]

**(c)** combined proportion = (14 + 34)/(50 + 150) = 48/200 = 0.24 [1]; 0.24 × 900 = **about 216 students** [1]

Total: 5

*Examiner insight:* Averaging 252 and 204 gives 228, which gives Mia's small sample as much weight as Leo's large one. Combining the counts weights each student equally.

**10. (a)** Any two: members who do not use email cannot reply [1]; the first 60 replies come from members who are keen or have strong views, so they are over-represented [1]; members who reply later are never included; members who already use the gym often may want longer hours more than others.

**(b)** Use the full membership list and number every member [1]; use random numbers to choose 60 members and follow up any who do not reply, for example by phone or in person [1]

Total: 4

*Examiner insight:* Each criticism must say who is left out or over-represented. "It is not fair" alone does not earn the mark.

**11.** Obtain a list of all 480 employees and number them 001 to 480 [1]. Use a random number generator to produce three-digit numbers [1]. Ignore numbers above 480, 000 and repeats, and continue until 30 different employees are chosen [1]

Total: 3

**12. (a)** 18/60 = 0.3 [1]; 0.3 × 8400 = **about 2520 households** [1]

**(b)** Any two: people at a hardware shop may be more likely to own or be interested in solar panels [1]; only people out on a Saturday are asked [1]; passers-by may not live in the town; two people from the same household could both be counted; 60 is a small sample of 8400 households.

**(c)** For example: number every household address in the town and choose a random sample, larger than 60, to survey [1]

Total: 5

*Examiner insight:* Part (b) asks about reliability, so both the method (bias) and the small size are valid points. Make each one specific to this survey.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the sample when asked for the population.
- Claiming a large sample cannot be biased.
- Averaging two estimates instead of combining the counts.
- Keeping out-of-range or repeated random numbers.
- Criticisms with no reference to the context, such as "it is not random".
- Stating an estimate as an exact figure.
