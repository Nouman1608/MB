This guide goes deeper into outcomes **1.1.13** to **1.1.20** of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11) specification, Issue 2, September 2018: the causes of and reasons for depreciation; the reducing balance, straight line and revaluation methods; ledger accounts for non-current assets and provisions for depreciation; the effect on profit of different methods and of a change of method; disposal accounts; and the schedule of non-current assets. This is Unit 1, International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business named is invented.

## Causes and reasons in one view

| Causes (1.1.13): why value is used up | Reasons for charging (1.1.14) |
|---|---|
| Wear and tear through use | Accruals: match the asset's cost with the revenue it helps earn |
| Passage of time | Fairer profit each year, rather than all cost in the year of purchase |
| Obsolescence: newer technology or changed demand | Carrying amount closer to the remaining usefulness of the asset |
| Depletion of a natural resource | Prudence: assets not overstated |
| Inadequacy: the business outgrows the asset | Consistency of profit measurement over the life |

Depreciation is an **allocation of cost**, a non-cash expense. It does not set cash aside for replacement and it does not measure market value.

## Reading the depreciation policy for part years

The method gives the annual figure; the **policy** decides how much of it falls in a part year. Questions state the policy, and the wrong policy costs marks on every later figure.

### Worked example 1

A machine is bought on 1 April for $18,000. Residual value $2,000, useful life 8 years; the year ends on 31 December.

```
Straight line annual charge = (18,000 − 2,000) ÷ 8 = 2,000

Policy A: charge by months of ownership
  Year 1: 2,000 × 9/12 = 1,500
Policy B: full year in the year of purchase, none in the year of disposal
  Year 1: 2,000

Reducing balance at 25%, by months
  Year 1: 18,000 × 25% × 9/12 = 3,375
  Year 2: (18,000 − 3,375) × 25% = 3,656 (to the nearest dollar)
```

Under reducing balance the residual value is not used in the calculation; the rate is chosen so that the carrying amount approaches it.

## The revaluation method

The revaluation method suits groups of small, similar items, such as loose tools, crockery or containers, where recording each item is impractical. The items are valued at the end of each year.

```
Depreciation = opening valuation + purchases in the year − closing valuation
(less any proceeds from items sold during the year, if there are any)
```

### Worked example 2

Pell Joinery's loose tools were valued at $3,800 on 1 January. Tools costing $1,450 were bought during the year. At 31 December they are valued at $3,950.

Depreciation = 3,800 + 1,450 − 3,950 = **$1,300**.

**Loose tools account**

| | $ | | $ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Jan Balance b/d | 3,800 | 31 Dec Statement of profit or loss (depreciation) | 1,300 |
| Bank | 1,450 | 31 Dec Balance c/d | 3,950 |
| | 5,250 | | 5,250 |

There is no provision for depreciation account; the asset account itself carries the valuation. The method is simple but subjective, because the valuation is a judgement, and depreciation can vary sharply from year to year.

## Effect on profit of the choice of method (1.1.17)

| Method | Pattern of charge | Effect on profit |
|---|---|---|
| Straight line | Equal each year | Even effect over the life |
| Reducing balance | High early, falling later | Lower profit early, higher later |
| Revaluation | Depends on each valuation | Can fluctuate from year to year |

Total depreciation over an asset's whole life is broadly the same whichever method is used (cost less what remains at the end), so the methods mainly change **when** profit is reported. A good answer also says which method fits the asset: reducing balance for vehicles and computers that lose value fastest when new, straight line for assets used evenly, revaluation for small items.

## A change of method (1.1.18)

### Worked example 3

Pell Joinery bought equipment for $40,000 at the start of Year 1 and used reducing balance at 30%. At the start of Year 3 it decides that straight line better reflects how the equipment is used, over the remaining 4 years, with a residual value of $3,600.

```
Year 1: 40,000 × 30% = 12,000      carrying amount 28,000
Year 2: 28,000 × 30% = 8,400       carrying amount 19,600
Year 3 (new method): (19,600 − 3,600) ÷ 4 = 4,000
Year 3 (if unchanged): 19,600 × 30% = 5,880
```

Profit for Year 3 is **$1,880 higher** than it would have been without the change, and the charge is $4,000 in each of Years 3 to 6. Earlier years are not restated: the new method is applied to the carrying amount from the date of the change. Because the change breaks consistency, the reason and the effect on profit should be disclosed.

## Disposal by part exchange (1.1.19)

When an old asset is traded in against a new one, the **part-exchange allowance** acts as the sale proceeds of the old asset and as part of the payment for the new one.

### Worked example 4

Pell Joinery's old van cost $30,000. The policy is 20% of cost a year, straight line, a full year in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal. After three full years' charges its provision is $18,000. In Year 4 it is traded in for a new van priced at $36,000; the dealer allows $8,000 for it and the balance of $28,000 is paid from the bank.

**Disposal account: old van**

| | $ | | $ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vans (cost of old van) | 30,000 | Provision for depreciation | 18,000 |
| | | Vans (part-exchange allowance) | 8,000 |
| | | Statement of profit or loss (loss on disposal) | 4,000 |
| | 30,000 | | 30,000 |

Carrying amount 30,000 − 18,000 = 12,000; allowance 8,000; loss **$4,000**.

**Vans account**

| | $ | | $ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance b/d (old van) | 30,000 | Disposal | 30,000 |
| Disposal (part-exchange allowance) | 8,000 | Balance c/d | 36,000 |
| Bank | 28,000 | | |
| | 66,000 | | 66,000 |

The new van is recorded at its **full price of $36,000**, not just the $28,000 paid in cash. Recording only the cash understates the asset and the later depreciation.

A large loss on disposal suggests the old van was under-depreciated; a large profit suggests over-depreciation. Either way, the profit or loss on disposal is really an adjustment to the depreciation charged in earlier years.

## The schedule of non-current assets (1.1.20)

The schedule summarises the year's movements. Continuing Worked example 4, the new van is charged a full year at 20% of $36,000 = $7,200.

| Vans, Year 4 | $ |
|---|---|
| **Cost** at start | 30,000 |
| Additions | 36,000 |
| Disposals | (30,000) |
| Cost at end | 36,000 |
| **Accumulated depreciation** at start | 18,000 |
| Charge for the year | 7,200 |
| On disposals | (18,000) |
| Accumulated depreciation at end | 7,200 |
| **Carrying amount** at end | 28,800 |
| Carrying amount at start | 12,000 |

Check: carrying amount at end = cost at end − accumulated depreciation at end. The same totals appear in the statement of financial position.

## Common mistakes

- Using the wrong part-year policy, or mixing two policies in one answer.
- Deducting residual value before applying a reducing balance rate.
- Showing a provision account for assets on the revaluation method.
- Restating earlier years after a change of method, or not disclosing the effect.
- Recording a new asset at the cash paid instead of its full price in a part exchange.
- Leaving the disposed asset's depreciation in the provision account.
- Forgetting the charge for the part year before disposal when the policy is "by months".
- A schedule whose closing carrying amount does not equal closing cost less closing depreciation.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate** depreciation: state the policy you are applying and show the fraction of the year.
- **Prepare** ledger accounts: dates, narratives and balances brought down; disposal account balanced to a profit or loss.
- **Explain** the effect of a method or a change of method: give the figures for the year and the direction of the effect on profit and on the carrying amount.
- **Evaluate** or **Recommend** a method: link it to how the asset loses value, its effect on profit, and consistency, then conclude.
