This guide goes deeper on point **3.1.6.5 Catalysts** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. The specification defines a catalyst as "a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being changed in chemical composition or amount", states that "catalysts work by providing an alternative reaction route of lower activation energy", and asks students to "use a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to help explain how a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction involving a gas". It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2.

The [kinetics study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-kinetics/) introduces the definition and the basic sketch. This page explains what "alternative route" really means, how to read and draw enthalpy profiles for catalysed reactions, and how to write full-mark explanations. Condense it with the [3.1.6.5 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-5-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1.6.5 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-5-practice/).

## Reading the definition word by word

- **"increases the rate"**: a catalyst speeds a reaction up. It does not make an impossible reaction happen and does not change how much product an equilibrium gives.
- **"without being changed in chemical composition"**: at the end, the catalyst is the same substance it was at the start.
- **"or amount"**: the same quantity is left at the end, which is why a small amount of catalyst can convert a large amount of reactant.

The definition does **not** say the catalyst takes no part. It usually does take part: it is used in one step and re-formed in a later step, so overall it is not consumed. The physical form of a solid catalyst, such as the texture of its surface, can change during use even though its composition and amount do not.

## What an "alternative route" means

Without a catalyst, reactants turn into products by one route with activation energy Ea. A catalyst opens a **different** route, usually involving an intermediate formed between the catalyst and a reactant. The highest energy barrier on this new route is lower than Ea.

### Worked example 1: a catalyst used and regenerated

You meet acid-catalysed reactions in the AS organic content of the course, such as making an alkene from an alcohol using phosphoric acid (see the [alcohols study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-alcohols/)). In outline:

```
Step 1:   alcohol + H⁺ → protonated alcohol          (H⁺ used)
Step 2:   protonated alcohol → carbocation + H₂O
Step 3:   carbocation → alkene + H⁺                  (H⁺ released)
Overall:  alcohol → alkene + H₂O                     (no H⁺ in the overall equation)
```

H⁺ appears as a reactant in step 1 and a product in step 3, so it cancels from the overall equation. This is exactly what the specification's definition requires: the catalyst takes part but finishes unchanged in composition and amount. The route through the protonated alcohol has a lower activation energy than any route without acid.

## Enthalpy profiles for catalysed reactions

An enthalpy profile plots enthalpy against the progress of the reaction.

- The **reactant** and **product** levels are the same with and without a catalyst, so **ΔH is unchanged**.
- The catalysed route has a **lower maximum**, so its activation energy is smaller.
- If the catalysed route passes through an intermediate, its profile has **two (or more) humps** with a dip between them. Each hump must be lower than the single uncatalysed hump. The activation energy that controls the rate is measured from the reactants to the **highest** point of the catalysed route.

### Worked example 2: forward and reverse barriers

All values are invented for practice. For a reaction A ⇌ B, ΔH = −40 kJ mol⁻¹ and the uncatalysed forward activation energy is +180 kJ mol⁻¹. A catalyst gives a route with forward activation energy +120 kJ mol⁻¹. Find both reverse activation energies.

```
Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH
uncatalysed: 180 − (−40) = +220 kJ mol⁻¹
catalysed:   120 − (−40) = +160 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The catalyst lowers the forward **and** reverse barriers by the same 60 kJ mol⁻¹. That is why a catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions of an equilibrium and does not change its position: equilibrium is reached sooner, with the same composition (3.1.7.1). Our [Le Chatelier's principle guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-7-1/) develops this.

## Using the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

For a reaction involving a gas at a fixed temperature:

1. Draw **one** curve: the temperature is unchanged, so the energy distribution is unchanged. Start at the origin, one peak, a long tail approaching but never meeting the energy axis.
2. Mark **Ea** for the uncatalysed route far out in the tail.
3. Mark **Ea(cat)** to the **left** of it, nearer the peak.
4. The area to the right of Ea(cat) is larger than the area to the right of Ea. Shade or label the extra area: these molecules have enough energy to react by the catalysed route but not by the uncatalysed one.

### Worked example 3: catalyst or temperature?

The figures in this example are invented for practice. For a gas reaction at 300 K, 0.5% of molecules have energy at least the uncatalysed Ea, and 6.0% have energy at least Ea(cat). Raising the temperature to 320 K without a catalyst raises the uncatalysed figure to 1.6%.

- The catalyst multiplies the proportion able to react by 6.0 ÷ 0.5 = **12**, with no change to the curve.
- The temperature rise multiplies it by 1.6 ÷ 0.5 = **3.2**, by moving the whole curve to the right (peak lower and further right, same area).

Both effects come from the same idea, the area beyond the activation energy, but by different routes: the catalyst moves the **line**, temperature moves the **curve**. Saying which moved is the key to many marks.

### Worked example 4: an annotated model answer

**Question.** Use a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to explain how a catalyst increases the rate of a gas-phase reaction. **[3]**

**Model answer.**

- The catalyst provides an alternative route with a lower activation energy. *(mechanism of action)*
- On the distribution, Ea(cat) lies to the left of Ea, so a greater proportion of molecules have energy at least the activation energy; the curve itself is unchanged. *(use of the distribution)*
- So more collisions are successful per unit time, and the rate increases. *(link to rate)*

**What would lose marks:** "the catalyst lowers the activation energy" with no mention of an alternative route; drawing a second, shifted curve; or "the catalyst gives the molecules more energy".

## Why catalysts matter

- A catalyst lets a reaction run at a useful rate at a **lower temperature**, saving the energy needed for heating and so reducing fuel costs and the carbon dioxide released from burning fuels.
- In a reversible industrial reaction, a catalyst allows equilibrium to be reached quickly at a temperature low enough to give an acceptable yield. This is part of the compromise in 3.1.7.1.
- The catalyst is not used up, so a small amount works for a long time.

Heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysis, with the Contact process and Haber process catalysts, are International A2 content in 3.2.5.6: see the [transition metals guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-transition-metals/).

## Common mistakes

- "A catalyst lowers the activation energy." Incomplete: it provides an **alternative route** with lower activation energy.
- "A catalyst is not involved in the reaction." It usually is; it is re-formed.
- Drawing the catalysed enthalpy profile with different reactant or product levels, changing ΔH.
- Drawing a second Maxwell–Boltzmann curve for a catalyst.
- Saying a catalyst increases the yield of an equilibrium.
- Leaving out the link from "more molecules with E ≥ Ea" to "more successful collisions per unit time".

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define**: quote the specification's wording; include "without being changed in chemical composition or amount".
- **Explain** how a catalyst works: alternative route + lower Ea + larger proportion of collisions succeed + rate.
- **Sketch** a distribution: axes labelled (energy; number of molecules), one curve from the origin, two Ea lines, and the extra region identified.
- **Draw** an enthalpy profile: same start and end levels, lower peak (or peaks) for the catalysed route, ΔH and both activation energies labelled with arrows from the reactant level.
