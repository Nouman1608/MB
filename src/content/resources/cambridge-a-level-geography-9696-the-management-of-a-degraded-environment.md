This guide covers section 12.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. It is a case study: candidates "must study one degraded environment, showing the causes of its degradation, problems faced, issues in attempts to improve the environment and evaluate the attempted solutions". It is part of Topic 12, Environmental management, in Paper 4.

The [Topic 12 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-environmental-management/) uses the Aral Sea as its case study. This page shows how to build any 12.4 case study around the four syllabus phrases, works through the River Ganga in India as a second example, and gives a framework for evaluation. Condense it with the [12.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-a-degraded-environment-revision-notes/) and test it with the [12.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-a-degraded-environment-practice/).

## Unpacking the syllabus wording

| Phrase | What your case study must show |
|---|---|
| "one degraded environment" | A single, named, bounded place: a river, lake, wetland, city district, mining area or region |
| "causes of its degradation" | Human and physical causes, ideally ranked |
| "problems faced" | The consequences for people, the economy and ecosystems |
| "issues in attempts to improve" | What made improvement difficult: cost, conflict, governance, physical limits |
| "evaluate the attempted solutions" | What worked, for whom, at what scale, and for how long |

The 12.3 ideas (pollution types, rural and urban factors, constraints) are the tools you apply here. 12.4 tests whether you can use them on one real place.

## Choosing a good case study

A strong 12.4 example has clear human causes, several attempted solutions over time, and enough evidence to judge outcomes. Possible types:

| Type | What makes it useful |
|---|---|
| A polluted river | Point and diffuse sources, many stakeholders, long clean-up history |
| A shrinking lake | Clear cause from water abstraction; measurable change |
| A degraded urban district | Links to urbanisation, waste and industry |
| A mining landscape | Contaminated land and water; remediation |
| A deforested or eroded upland | Rural factors and land management |

## Worked case study: the River Ganga, India

### The environment

The Ganga flows for about 2 500 km from the Himalayas to the Bay of Bengal. Its basin supports several hundred million people and many of India's largest cities, including Kanpur, Prayagraj, Varanasi, Patna and Kolkata. The river is sacred in Hinduism, so millions bathe in it. India declared it the national river in 2008.

### Causes of degradation

- **Untreated sewage:** towns and cities along the river have grown faster than sewers and treatment works, so much domestic waste water enters the river untreated. This is the largest source of pollution.
- **Industrial effluent:** tanneries around Kanpur, together with sugar mills, distilleries, paper mills and other industries, discharge chemicals and organic waste.
- **Agricultural run-off:** fertilisers and pesticides wash in from the intensively farmed Gangetic plain, a diffuse source.
- **Reduced flow:** dams, barrages and canals take water for irrigation and cities, especially in the dry season, so there is less water to dilute pollution.
- **Solid waste and religious practices:** rubbish, offerings and incompletely burnt remains from riverside cremations add to the load in places.

### Problems faced

- High levels of faecal bacteria in many stretches, making bathing and drinking unsafe and spreading waterborne disease.
- Low dissolved oxygen downstream of large cities, harming fish and the people who depend on fishing.
- Threats to wildlife, including the Ganges river dolphin, which India named its national aquatic animal in 2009.
- Toxic metals such as chromium from tanneries in sediments and irrigation water.

### Attempts to improve

1. **Ganga Action Plan (from the mid-1980s):** built sewage treatment plants in major towns and intercepted drains before they reached the river.
2. **National Ganga River Basin Authority (2009):** set up to manage the whole basin rather than individual towns.
3. **Namami Gange programme (from 2014):** a larger national programme funding treatment plants and sewer networks, riverfront and ghat improvements, electric and improved crematoria, monitoring of industrial discharges, and tree planting along the river.
4. **Regulation of industry:** pollution control boards and courts have ordered polluting units, including tanneries, to install effluent treatment or close.

### Issues in attempts to improve

- **Capacity gap:** treatment capacity has repeatedly lagged behind the volume of sewage produced by growing cities.
- **Operation and maintenance:** some plants have worked below capacity because of power cuts, missing sewer connections or lack of funds to run them.
- **Divided responsibility:** central government, five main riverside states and many city authorities share responsibility, so coordination is difficult.
- **Economic conflict:** tanneries and other industries provide jobs and exports, so closures face resistance.
- **Flow:** cleaning effluent does less good if abstraction leaves too little water to dilute what remains.
- **Cultural sensitivity:** measures that affect bathing, cremation and offerings must respect religious practice.

### Evaluation

- **Effectiveness:** treatment capacity and sewer coverage have increased considerably, and water quality has improved in some stretches. But many sections downstream of large cities still fail bathing standards.
- **Durability:** early plants built under the Action Plan did not always keep working, which shows that building infrastructure is easier than running it.
- **Scale:** basin-wide authorities recognise that the problem crosses state borders, but local implementation still varies widely.
- **Equity:** industrial controls protect downstream users, but can cost low-paid tannery workers their jobs.
- **Judgement:** partial success. Each programme has improved on the last, but improvement has been slower than urban growth, so the river remains degraded in its most populated stretches.

## Comparing with the Aral Sea

| | Aral Sea | River Ganga |
|---|---|---|
| Main cause | Abstraction for irrigation | Untreated sewage and effluent |
| Type | Loss of water quantity | Loss of water quality |
| Most successful solution | One local dike, in one country | Expansion of treatment capacity |
| Main barrier | Economic dependence on irrigation across borders | Pace of urban growth, governance and maintenance |

The comparison suggests that solutions succeed where the cause is narrow and the responsible authority is single, and struggle where causes are diffuse and authority is shared.

## Evaluation framework

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Did measurable indicators improve? |
| Durability | Did improvements last after funding ended? |
| Cost | Was it affordable, and who paid? |
| Equity | Who gained, who lost? |
| Scale | Did it address causes at the right scale? |
| Root causes | Did it treat causes or only symptoms? |

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question: Assess the issues faced in attempts to improve one degraded environment.*

> On the River Ganga, the biggest issue has been that sewage treatment capacity has lagged behind urban growth (**main issue, ranked first**). Even when new plants are built, some work below capacity because of missing sewer connections or power cuts (**evidence of a second issue**). Responsibility is split between central government, several states and many cities (**governance**), so a basin-wide plan is applied unevenly. These issues matter more than cost, because funding has increased under successive programmes without a matching improvement downstream of the largest cities (**judgement with reason**).

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a place too large to analyse, such as "pollution in China".
- Describing causes without problems, or problems without attempted solutions.
- Treating "issues" as a repeat of "problems": issues are difficulties in improving.
- Giving a single, unqualified verdict: success varies by stretch, group and time.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the causes**: rank them and say which is largest and why.
- **Assess the issues**: decide which barrier matters most and justify it.
- **Evaluate**: use at least three criteria from the framework and finish with a clear judgement.
