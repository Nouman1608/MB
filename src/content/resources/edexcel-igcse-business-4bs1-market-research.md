This guide goes deeper into section 4.1 Market research of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Point 4.1.1 is "The purpose of market research": to identify and understand customer needs, to identify gaps in the market, to reduce risk and to inform business decisions. Point 4.1.2 is "Methods of market research": primary research (survey, questionnaire, focus group, observation, test marketing) and secondary research (internet, market reports, government reports). Point 4.1.3 is "The use of data in market research": qualitative and quantitative data, the role of social media in collecting market research data, and the importance of the reliability of market research data.

## The four purposes as decisions

Each purpose in 4.1.1 is useful in an answer only when it is tied to a decision the business faces.

| Purpose | Decision it supports | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Identify and understand customer needs | What to sell, at what price, in what form | A gym learns members want early-morning classes |
| Identify gaps in the market | Whether to launch something new | A town has no shop repairing phones on the same day |
| Reduce risk | Whether to commit money | A bakery tests demand before buying a second oven |
| Inform business decisions | Pricing, location, stock levels, promotion | A retailer checks which products to stock for a new branch |

Research **reduces** risk; it never removes it. Customers can say one thing and do another, and markets change after the research is done.

## Primary research in more depth

Primary research is collected first-hand by or for the business, for its own purpose.

### Surveys and questionnaires

A **survey** is the exercise of asking a group of people for information; a **questionnaire** is the written set of questions used. The quality of the results depends on question design.

- **Closed questions** give fixed answers to choose from ("Would you buy this? Yes / No / Not sure"). They produce **quantitative** data that is quick to count.
- **Open questions** let people answer in their own words ("What would make you visit more often?"). They produce **qualitative** data that explains reasons.
- **Leading questions** push people towards an answer ("You'd agree our prices are fair, wouldn't you?"). They make the data unreliable and should be avoided.

A well-designed questionnaire is short, uses clear wording, asks one thing per question and mixes mainly closed questions with one or two open ones.

### Focus groups

A small group discusses a product, advert or idea with a leader. Strength: rich qualitative insight into *why* people feel as they do. Weaknesses: a small group may not represent all customers, and one confident voice can sway the rest.

### Observation

Watching what people actually do, such as how many people pass a site, or which shelves shoppers stop at. Strength: records real behaviour rather than what people claim. Weakness: it shows *what* happens but not *why*.

### Test marketing

Selling a product in a limited area or for a limited time before a full launch. Strength: customers spend real money, which is the strongest evidence of demand. Weaknesses: it takes time and money, and competitors can see the product before the launch.

## Secondary research in more depth

| Source | Useful for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Internet | Competitors' prices and products, online reviews, trends | Quality varies; information may be out of date or biased |
| Market reports | Size and growth of a market, main competitors | Often expensive; may not cover a local area or niche |
| Government reports | Population, age structure, incomes, spending patterns | Can be too general, and may be some years old |

Secondary research is usually a first step: it is cheaper and faster, and it can show whether primary research is worth paying for.

## Choosing methods: a decision process

1. **What exactly must be found out?** Demand, price, reasons, or the size of the market?
2. **How much money and time is available?** A small business with little cash leans towards questionnaires, observation and free secondary sources.
3. **Is numerical or explanatory evidence needed?** Numbers suit closed questionnaires; reasons suit focus groups and open questions.
4. **How costly is a wrong decision?** A big investment justifies test marketing; a small change may not.

In Paper 1 (a small business, up to 49 employees), budget and time usually decide. In Paper 2 (a large business, more than 250 employees), the business may afford market reports and test marketing across regions.

## Analysing research data

The specification's quantitative skills appendix includes percentages, averages and the interpretation of market research data.

### Worked example 1: questionnaire results

Brayford Juice Bar asks 400 people whether they would buy a new protein smoothie. 172 say yes.

- Percentage yes = 172 ÷ 400 × 100 = **43%**.
- The 172 people who said yes estimate their weekly spending on it at a total of 1,032. Average = 1,032 ÷ 172 = **6.00** per person per week.

A tempting next step is to scale up: if the town has 9,000 people in the target market, 9,000 × 0.43 × 6.00 = 23,220 a week. Treat that figure with great caution. People often overstate what they will buy, and the 400 respondents may not represent the 9,000.

### Worked example 2: test marketing results

A drinks maker test markets a new flavour in 3 of its 20 shops for 4 weeks and sells 1,260 bottles.

- Sales per shop per week = 1,260 ÷ 3 ÷ 4 = **105**.
- Estimate across all 20 shops: 105 × 20 = **2,100** a week.

The estimate is only as good as the test shops are typical. If they were chosen in busy city centres, sales elsewhere may be lower. Early sales may also include curious first-time buyers who do not return.

## Qualitative and quantitative data together

| | Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Numbers, percentages, averages | Words, opinions, reasons |
| Typical source | Closed questions, sales figures, observation counts | Focus groups, open questions, reviews |
| Strength | Easy to compare and present | Explains why customers behave as they do |
| Limitation | Does not explain reasons | Hard to summarise; small samples |

Strong research uses both: numbers to show how many, and comments to show why.

## The role of social media

Social media lets a business collect research data cheaply and quickly:

- running polls or short surveys for followers;
- reading comments, reviews and messages;
- seeing which posts or product images get the most reactions;
- watching what customers say about competitors.

Limits: followers are already interested in the business, so they may not represent the wider market; some responses come from people who will never buy; and results can be skewed by a few very active users.

## Reliability of market research data

Data is reliable when it gives a true picture and similar research would give similar results. Check:

- **Sample size**: is it large enough to be meaningful?
- **Representativeness**: do respondents match the target market by age, location, income and lifestyle?
- **Bias**: were questions leading, or were only friends and loyal customers asked?
- **Age**: is the data recent enough?
- **Source**: is the publisher trustworthy and independent?

### Worked example 3: which survey to trust?

A cinema asks about a new late-night showing. Survey A: 120 replies from a social media poll, 48 interested (**40%**). Survey B: 120 face-to-face interviews with people leaving shows at different times on different days, 30 interested (**25%**). Survey B is more reliable because it reaches actual cinema-goers across the week, while Survey A reaches followers who are already keen and may never attend late at night.

## Annotated model answer

*Analyse one reason why Brayford Juice Bar should be careful about relying on its questionnaire results.*

> Brayford asked 400 people, but if they were all asked inside the juice bar they are already customers who like its drinks. They are more likely to say yes than the wider public, so the 43% figure may overstate demand. If Brayford buys stock and equipment based on that figure, it could be left with unsold smoothies and wasted spending, which defeats the purpose of research, reducing risk.

Why it works: one reason (an unrepresentative sample), applied to the case, followed through to a consequence.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a government report primary research.
- Saying a focus group produces mainly quantitative data.
- Treating research as removing risk entirely.
- Scaling up a small sample without commenting on reliability.
- Writing "social media is cheap" without saying what data it collects and how reliable it is.
