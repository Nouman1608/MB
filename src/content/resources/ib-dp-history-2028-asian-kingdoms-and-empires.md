This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. The regional study of Asian kingdoms and empires (750 CE to 1500 CE) is examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. It is the first of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028.

The guide says that every examination question will require you to refer to **one** kingdom or empire from the region. Suggested examples are the Shogunate, the Mongol Empire, the Angkor Empire, the Trần Dynasty of Vietnam and Srivijaya. You choose your own example, so this guide builds its main case around the **Mongol Empire**, with **Angkor** and the **Kamakura shogunate** of Japan as shorter comparisons. Use whichever one you know best, and use the others only to sharpen a point.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-asian-kingdoms-and-empires-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-asian-kingdoms-and-empires-practice/).

## Reasons for emergence

**Military factors.** The Mongol Empire grew from a federation of steppe peoples united by Temüjin, who was proclaimed Chinggis Khan at a gathering (a *quriltai*) in 1206. His army was organised on a decimal system, in units of ten, a hundred, a thousand and ten thousand, and it promoted men on merit and loyalty rather than clan rank. Its horse archers were fast and disciplined. Later campaigns added siege engineers from China and Persia. The war against the Khwarazmian Empire (1219 to 1221), which began after Mongol merchants were killed at Otrar, brought the conquest of Central Asia.

**Economic factors.** Steppe herding gave the Mongols horses and mobility, but the steppe could not supply everything they wanted. Raiding and tribute paid for the loyalty of followers, and control of the overland trade routes later paid for the state. Silk, silver, horses and luxury goods moved along them.

**Political factors.** Chinggis Khan broke older tribal loyalties by mixing groups in his armies and by relying on a personal guard (the *keshig*). Alliances, marriage ties and the defeat of rivals such as the Tatars and Naimans made one steppe state possible. The empire was never a simple "invasion from outside": local rulers who submitted early were often left in place.

**Environmental factors.** Historians argue about climate. Some point to a favourable run of wet years on the steppe in the early thirteenth century that supported larger herds and armies. Treat this as a hypothesis, not a settled cause, and say so.

Compare Angkor. It emerged when Jayavarman II established a kingdom around 802, and its power rested on rice agriculture supported by large reservoirs (*barays*) and canals, an environmental and economic base very unlike the steppe.

## Importance of leaders

Use the four stages in the guide.

| Stage | Mongol example |
|---|---|
| Establishment | Chinggis Khan united the steppe tribes by 1206 |
| Consolidation | Chinggis and his successors built the *yam* relay post, a law code tradition (the *yasa*) and a record-keeping bureaucracy staffed by Uyghur, Chinese and Persian officials |
| Domestic and foreign success | Ögedei destroyed the Jin dynasty in 1234; Hülegü took Baghdad in 1258; Kubilai completed the conquest of Song China in 1279 |
| Responding to challenges | Succession disputes after Möngke's death in 1259, the rivalry of Kubilai and Ariq Böke, and defeats at Ain Jalut (1260) and in Japan and Java |

Chinggis Khan was a brilliant organiser, but do not make him the only explanation. His sons and grandsons enlarged the empire and then divided it. At Angkor, Suryavarman II (who built Angkor Wat in the early twelfth century) and Jayavarman VII (who ruled from about 1181 after the Cham sacked the capital in 1177) show leaders responding to a crisis.

## Domestic developments

**Social structures.** Mongol society was hierarchical, but it gave unusual prominence to women of the ruling family, who sometimes acted as regents. In the Yuan dynasty of China, Kubilai divided subjects into groups with different legal status, with Mongols and their allies above northern Chinese and southern Chinese. At Angkor, society was ranked under a god-king, with large labouring populations attached to temples.

**Culture and the arts.** The Mongols sponsored artisans, historians and painters. Persian historians such as Rashid al-Din wrote in the Ilkhanate. Angkor's temples, including the Bayon with its carved faces, expressed royal power in stone.

**Religious and spiritual beliefs.** The Mongols generally tolerated religions. They honoured the sky god Tengri and also patronised Buddhist, Muslim, Christian and Daoist leaders. At Angkor, Hindu devotion to Shiva and Vishnu under earlier kings gave way to Mahayana Buddhism under Jayavarman VII.

**Science and technology.** Gunpowder weapons, paper money, astronomy and medicine travelled through Mongol networks. Kubilai's court employed Muslim astronomers. Take care: say that the Mongols helped to spread these things, not that they invented them.

## Role of foreign relations

**Alliances and diplomacy.** The Mongols demanded submission and often offered terms. Marriage alliances tied vassals, such as Korea's kings, to the Yuan court.

**Exchange of ideas.** The *Pax Mongolica* (a century or so of safer overland travel across Eurasia) allowed envoys, merchants and missionaries to cross continents. Marco Polo's account is famous but should be used with care, because historians question how much he saw himself.

**Economic relations.** Overland routes across Central Asia and sea routes through the Indian Ocean both flourished. The *yam* post stations gave travellers with an official tablet (the *paiza*) food, horses and shelter.

**Conflict.** Not every campaign succeeded. The Mamluks defeated a Mongol army at Ain Jalut in 1260. Two invasions of Japan (1274 and 1281) failed, partly because of storms, and the Mongols also failed in Java (1293). Đại Việt (Vietnam) under the Trần repelled invasions in 1258, 1285 and 1287 to 1288. These failures show the limits of an empire based on steppe cavalry when faced with sea, jungle and determined defenders.

## Maintenance of power

- **Political.** Use of local officials, the *yam*, a census and taxation, and a strong ruling lineage.
- **Social.** Privileges for loyal groups, and tolerance of local customs, kept most subjects quiet.
- **Military.** Terror was a tool: cities that resisted were sometimes destroyed, while those that submitted were spared. This reputation made later surrenders easier.
- **Belief.** The idea of a mandate from Eternal Heaven gave the khans a claim to universal rule. Kubilai also adopted the Chinese language of dynastic legitimacy when he declared the Yuan dynasty in 1271.

## Challenges and decline

- **Political.** The empire divided into four khanates (the Yuan, the Chagatai, the Ilkhanate and the Golden Horde), and they sometimes fought each other. Rivalry between princes weakened central authority.
- **Social and economic.** Heavy taxation, over-issue of paper money, and resentment among Chinese subjects of Mongol privilege strained the Yuan.
- **Conflict and rebellions.** Rebel movements, including the Red Turban rebels, grew in the 1350s. Zhu Yuanzhang took Beijing in 1368 and founded the Ming dynasty.
- **Environmental.** Famine, floods on the Yellow River and the plague pandemic of the fourteenth century all hurt the Yuan and other khanates. The Black Death spread along trade routes the empire had made safer. At Angkor, historians use tree-ring evidence of drought and heavy monsoon years in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries to argue that the water system came under strain, alongside the shift to Buddhism, pressure from Ayutthaya (which captured Angkor in 1431) and the rise of sea trade.

## The Kamakura shogunate: a short comparison

After the Genpei War (1180 to 1185), Minamoto no Yoritomo set up a warrior government at Kamakura, and in 1192 he took the title of shogun. After his death the Hōjō family ruled as regents. The emperor and court in Kyoto kept symbolic authority. The shogunate defeated an attempt by the retired emperor to restore court power (the Jōkyū War of 1221). The Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281 were repelled, but the defence brought no new land to reward warriors. Discontent among the *gokenin* (vassals) helped bring down the shogunate in 1333. This is a good example of power based on a warrior class and land grants, set against the Mongols' use of a bureaucracy and trade.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Older European views stressed Mongol destruction, relying on the accounts of victims. Persian and Chinese sources also recorded massacres, and some numbers in them are probably exaggerated.
- More recent historians emphasise the Mongols' role in connecting Eurasia, in trade, diplomacy and the movement of people. Be ready to explain why the same event can look like destruction or like connection.
- For Angkor, older accounts blamed Siamese conquest for the "fall". Many scholars now see a longer, more complex change in which the centre of power moved south.

## A worked example

**Question: "Evaluate the importance of leaders in the emergence of one Asian kingdom or empire."**

1. **Define the line of argument.** Say whether leaders were the main cause or only one cause.
2. **Paragraph on leaders.** Chinggis Khan united the tribes in 1206, created the army system and the *keshig*, and set the pattern of conquest.
3. **Paragraph on other factors.** Steppe mobility and horse archery, the divisions among neighbours such as the Jin and the Khwarazmians, and the use of foreign engineers and officials.
4. **Perspectives.** Note the view that conditions of the steppe produced a leader like Chinggis, not the reverse.
5. **Judgement.** For example: leadership was necessary because without a unifier there was no empire, but not sufficient on its own, since military organisation and weak neighbours turned unity into conquest.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a history of the Mongols with no link to the question's focus.
- Treating "the Mongols" as one unchanging force across 150 years.
- Using terror as the only explanation for control.
- Quoting Marco Polo as if his account were certain.
- Giving exact casualty figures that cannot be supported.

## How Paper 3 questions look

Questions use command terms such as *evaluate*, *to what extent*, *discuss*, *examine* and *analyse*. The essay is worth 15 marks, and the markbands reward an in-depth understanding of the argument in the question, a balanced evaluation of a range of perspectives and a substantiated judgement. Keep your single example in view and test each paragraph against the question.
