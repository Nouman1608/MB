Ravensholt Bay, Pellin Point and all data about them are fictional.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define fetch and explain how it affects wave energy. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between wave quarrying and cavitation. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how wave refraction concentrates wave energy on headlands. **[3]**

**4.** Students measured the mean long axis of pebbles at five sites, numbered west to east, along Ravensholt Bay.

| Site | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean long axis (mm) | 82 | 71 | 64 | 55 | 47 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage decrease in mean pebble size from Site 1 to Site 5. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest how these data support the idea of longshore drift from west to east. **[2]**

**5.** A sediment cell at Pellin Point gains 8,000 m^3 a year from cliff erosion and 1,500 m^3 from a river. It loses 6,000 m^3 a year by longshore drift and 2,000 m^3 offshore.

**(a)** Calculate the sediment budget. **[1]**
**(b)** A sea wall reduces cliff erosion input by 75%. Calculate the new budget. Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the likely effect of the new budget on beaches within the cell. **[2]**

**6.** Outline **one** difference between a ria and a fjord. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Explain the development of an estuarine saltmarsh. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how a coastline of emergence forms and describe **one** landform associated with it. **[4]**

**9.** Students counted waves breaking per minute on two beaches: Beach A recorded 6 per minute and Beach B recorded 13 per minute.

**(a)** Identify the likely wave type on each beach. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the likely effect of each wave type on the beach profile. **[4]**

**10.** With reference to a contrasting coastal landscape you have studied, evaluate human responses of resilience, mitigation and adaptation to the risks it presents. **[9]**

**11.** Assess the extent to which sea level change explains the landforms of a coastline you have studied. **[9]**

**12.** "Integrated coastal zone management is the only sustainable way to manage coastlines." To what extent do you agree with this view? **[12]**

## Answers

Questions 10 to 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks these with levels of response, not point by point; the [1] points show the kind of content that earns credit.

**1.** Fetch is the distance of open water over which the wind blows to generate waves [1]. A longer fetch allows waves to grow larger, so they carry more energy when they reach the coast [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Wave quarrying is the removal by waves of blocks of rock already loosened along joints and bedding planes [1]. Cavitation is the collapse of air bubbles in breaking waves, which releases small shock waves that weaken the rock surface [1]. **[2]**

**3.** As waves approach an indented coast, water is shallower off the headland, so that part of the wave front slows first [1]. The wave front bends so that it becomes more parallel to the shore [1], causing wave orthogonals (lines of energy) to converge on the headland and diverge in the bays, concentrating erosion on the headland [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** (82 - 47) / 82 x 100 [1] = 42.7% decrease [1].
**(b)** Pebbles become smaller towards the east [1], which suggests they have been worn by attrition as they were transported eastwards along the shore by longshore drift [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* for (b), a trend in size alone is not proof; mention that roundness data or tracer pebbles would strengthen the conclusion.

**5. (a)** (8,000 + 1,500) - (6,000 + 2,000) = +1,500 m^3 a year [1].
**(b)** New cliff input = 8,000 x 0.25 = 2,000 m^3 [1]. New budget = (2,000 + 1,500) - 8,000 = -4,500 m^3 a year [1].
**(c)** The budget becomes negative, so more sediment leaves the cell than enters it [1]. Beaches are likely to narrow and lower, giving less protection to the cliffs and coast further along the drift direction [1]. **[5]**

**6.** A ria is a drowned river valley with a V-shaped cross-section that becomes shallower inland [1], whereas a fjord is a drowned glacial trough with a U-shaped cross-section, steep sides and often a shallow threshold near its mouth [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Saltmarsh forms in sheltered, low-energy settings such as estuaries or behind spits [1]. Fine silt and clay settle out at slack water around high tide [1], helped by flocculation, where clay particles clump together in salt water and sink [1]. Mudflats build up and are colonised by salt-tolerant pioneer plants such as glasswort and cordgrass [1]. The plants slow water and trap more sediment, so the surface rises [1]. As it is flooded less often, less salt-tolerant species replace the pioneers, a succession towards a mature marsh [1]. **[6]**

**8.** A coastline of emergence forms where land rises relative to the sea, often through isostatic rebound after ice sheets melt [1], or through tectonic uplift [1]. A raised beach is an old beach, sometimes with rounded pebbles, now above the reach of waves [1], often backed by a former cliff with caves or notches [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Beach A: constructive waves [1]. Beach B: destructive waves [1].
**(b)** Constructive waves have a strong swash and weak backwash [1], so they push sediment up the beach, building a wider, gentler profile with berms [1]. Destructive waves have a weak swash and strong backwash [1], so they drag sediment offshore and steepen or lower the beach, sometimes forming offshore bars [1]. **[6]**

**10.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Introduce a landscape such as the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta and Sundarbans [1]. Opportunities: fertile land, fishing and mangrove resources [1]. Risks: tropical cyclones, storm surges, river flooding and sea level rise [1]. Resilience: volunteer cyclone warning networks and community preparedness [1]. Mitigation: raised cyclone shelters and embankments around polders [1]. Adaptation: protection and replanting of mangroves to absorb wave energy [1]. Evidence: deaths in later cyclones have been far lower than in 1970 [1]. Limits: embankments can trap water and sediment, and the poorest remain highly exposed [1]. Judgement: responses have greatly reduced deaths but have not removed long-term risks from sea level rise [1]. **[9]**

**11.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Identify the coastline and its main landforms [1]. Sea level rise after the last glacial period drowned river valleys, forming rias [1]. Isostatic rebound in formerly glaciated areas left raised beaches [1]. Sea level change sets the height at which waves now attack, shaping present cliffs [1]. Other factors: rock type and structure control headlands, bays and cliff profiles [1]. Wave energy and fetch control erosion rates [1]. Sediment supply and longshore drift explain spits and beaches [1]. Human intervention modifies some landforms [1]. Judgement: sea level change explains the large-scale form, while present processes and geology explain the detail [1]. **[9]**

**12.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Define ICZM and sustainability [1]. For: ICZM considers whole coastlines, so knock-on effects in the sediment cell are anticipated [1]. It balances fishing, tourism, ports, conservation and housing [1]. It plans for long timescales, including sea level rise [1]. Shoreline management plans apply similar cell-based thinking [1]. Against: coordination across agencies and borders is slow and difficult [1]. Local communities may oppose decisions such as managed realignment [1]. Soft engineering such as beach nourishment can be sustainable within any framework [1]. Hard engineering may still be justified for high-value urban areas [1]. Funding limits what any approach can achieve [1]. Example: the Sand Motor shows a sustainable scheme working with natural processes [1]. Judgement: integrated approaches are the most sustainable framework, but the specific methods inside them matter just as much [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculations without working, or percentages of the wrong base value.
- Wave type identified without any link to swash and backwash.
- Landform explanations that skip the sequence of processes.
- Management evaluated for one beach without the rest of the cell.
- Extended answers using only UK examples or ending without a judgement.
