## Section A: Short questions

**1.** The inflation rate in an economy falls from 3.5% to 1.2% over a year. Which **one** of the following describes this change? **[1]**

A: Deflation

B: Disinflation

C: Hyperinflation

D: A fall in the general price level

**2.** A price index is 132.0 in one year and 135.3 the following year. Which **one** is the rate of inflation? **[1]**

A: 2.5%

B: 3.3%

C: 35.3%

D: 3.3 index points

**3.** State **two** items that are included in the RPI but excluded from the CPI. **[2]**

**4.** A saver earns a nominal interest rate of 2.5% when inflation is 4.0%. Calculate the real interest rate, using the exact method, to two decimal places. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the UK inflation target is described as symmetric. **[2]**

**6.** A price index for Country F is 250 in year 4 and 270 in year 7. Rebase the index so that year 4 = 100, and state the year 7 value. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** The table shows an invented consumer price index for Country G.

| Category | Weight | Price index, year 1 | Price index, year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 25 | 100 | 106 |
| Energy | 15 | 100 | 120 |
| Transport | 20 | 100 | 98 |
| Services | 40 | 100 | 103 |

**(a)** Calculate the weighted price index for year 2 and state the inflation rate. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the contribution of energy to the inflation rate in percentage points. **[1]**

**(c)** A pensioner household spends 30% of its budget on energy. Explain why its experience of inflation may differ from the index. **[2]**

**8.** Nominal average earnings in Country H rise from 30,000 to 31,200 currency units. Over the same year the price index rises from 115 to 121.9.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in nominal earnings and the inflation rate. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate real earnings in each year in base-year prices, and the percentage change in real earnings. **[3]**

**9.** Explain, with the aid of an AD/AS diagram, how a sharp rise in world energy prices could cause inflation in an economy. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why a central bank may treat inflation persistently below its target as a problem. **[4]**

**11.** A government freezes income tax thresholds for four years while nominal incomes rise by about 5% a year. Explain how this policy raises tax revenue without any change in tax rates. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that inflation is always more harmful to an economy than deflation. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. The rate falls but stays positive, so prices still rise. **[1]**

**2.** A [1]: (135.3 - 132.0) / 132.0 x 100 = 2.5%. Option D gives the change in index points, which is 3.3, but that is not a rate. **[1]**

**3.** Mortgage interest payments [1]; council tax [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 1.025 / 1.040 - 1 [1] = **-1.44%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The shortcut 2.5 - 4.0 = -1.5% is close but the question asks for the exact method.

**5.** The Bank of England must respond to inflation below the target as well as above it [1], because deviations in either direction count as a failure to meet the 2% target [1]. **[2]**

**6.** 270 / 250 x 100 [1] = **108** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** (25 x 106 + 15 x 120 + 20 x 98 + 40 x 103) / 100 [1] = (2,650 + 1,800 + 1,960 + 4,120) / 100 = **105.3** [1]. Inflation = **5.3%** [1].

**(b)** 15 x 20 / 100 = **3.0 percentage points** [1].

**(c)** The index uses average spending weights, with energy at 15% [1]. Energy prices rose fastest (20%), so a household spending 30% on energy faces a personal inflation rate above 5.3% [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figure from the table (20%) rather than saying "energy is expensive".

**8. (a)** Nominal earnings: (31,200 - 30,000) / 30,000 = **4.0%** [1]. Inflation: (121.9 - 115) / 115 = **6.0%** [1].

**(b)** Year 1: 30,000 / 1.15 = **26,087** [1]. Year 2: 31,200 / 1.219 = **25,595** [1]. Change: 25,595 / 26,087 - 1 = **-1.9%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Check with 1.04 / 1.06 - 1 = -1.89%. A rise in nominal pay can hide a fall in purchasing power.

**9.** Energy is an input for most firms, so their costs of production rise [1]. The SRAS curve shifts to the left [1]. The diagram shows the price level rising from P1 to P2 and real output falling from Y1 to Y2 [1]. This is cost-push inflation, and the fall in output alongside rising prices risks stagflation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Label the axes "price level" and "real output"; "price" and "quantity" belong to micro diagrams.

**10.** Reason 1: low inflation can tip into deflation if demand falls further [1], and deflation can lead households to delay spending, cutting AD [1]. Reason 2: very low inflation makes it harder for real wages to fall in struggling sectors without cuts in money wages [1], so adjustment happens through job losses instead [1]. Other valid reasons: low inflation limits how far real interest rates can be cut; it raises the real burden of debt. **[4]**

**11.** As nominal incomes rise, more of each person's income falls above the frozen thresholds [1]. More people move into higher tax bands, and each taxpayer pays a higher proportion of income in tax [1]. Revenue rises faster than incomes even though rates are unchanged; this is fiscal drag [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Inflation harms when it is high or unanticipated: it redistributes from savers and fixed-income groups to borrowers [1] and creates uncertainty that can reduce investment [1].
- Inflation above that of trading partners can reduce export competitiveness and worsen the current account [1].
- Deflation caused by falling AD encourages households to delay spending, deepening a downturn [1].
- Deflation raises the real burden of debt and real interest rates, limiting monetary policy [1].
- Not all deflation is harmful: deflation from productivity gains comes with rising output and real incomes [1].
- Moderate, stable inflation (around 2%) has small costs and allows real wages to adjust [1].
- Hyperinflation is extremely damaging, so the size of the inflation matters more than its sign [1].
- Judgement: neither is always worse; low, stable inflation is less harmful than demand-led deflation, but high or volatile inflation can be worse than mild productivity-led deflation [1]. A conclusion that names the conditions under which each is worse earns the final mark [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* The best answers distinguish types of deflation and sizes of inflation instead of comparing "inflation" with "deflation" in general.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving index-point differences as percentages.
- Measuring this year's inflation from the base year.
- Omitting the weights when calculating an index.
- Treating falling inflation as falling prices.
- Drawing a micro supply-and-demand diagram for cost-push inflation.
- Evaluating consequences without asking whether inflation was anticipated.
