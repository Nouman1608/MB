Condensed notes on Article 2, the right to life. For full explanation and a worked scenario, use the
[Article 2 study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-article-2-of-the-european-convention-on-human-rights-1953/).

## The structure in one line

**2.1 right to life "protected by law"** → which **obligation**? (negative, framework, operational,
procedural) → if the state used force: **2.2(a), (b) or (c)** and **"no more than absolutely
necessary"** → **planning and control** → **investigation** → **remedy**.

Article 2 is **limited**: the exceptions in 2.2 are exhaustive. **No derogation** under Article 15,
except deaths from lawful acts of war.

## Article 2.1

- Death penalty exception superseded by **Protocols 6 and 13** (both ratified by the UK).
- "Protected by law" grounds the **positive obligations**.

| Obligation | Test | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | No intentional or unintended killing by state agents outside 2.2 | McCann v UK (1995) |
| Framework | Effective criminal law and regulation of dangerous activities | Öneryildiz v Turkey (2004): methane explosion at a rubbish tip |
| Operational | Knew or ought to have known of a **real and immediate risk** to an **identified individual**; failed to take reasonable measures | Osman v UK (1998) |
| Procedural | Effective official investigation of deaths involving the state | Jordan v UK (2001) |

**Operational duty cases**

| Case | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Osman v UK (1998) | No breach: no decisive point at which police knew of a real and immediate risk |
| Edwards v UK (2002) | Breach: prisoner killed by a mentally ill cellmate; information not passed on |
| Van Colle v CC Hertfordshire (2008) | No breach: threats to a witness did not show a real and immediate risk |
| Rabone v Pennine Care (2012) | Breach: duty owed to a voluntary psychiatric patient at real and immediate risk of suicide |

**Scope**

- **Vo v France (2004)**: status of the unborn left within the margin of appreciation.
- **Evans v UK (2007)**: destroying embryos after consent withdrawn did not breach Article 2.
- **Pretty v UK (2002)**: no right to die.

## Article 2.2

Force **no more than absolutely necessary**:

- (a) defence of any person from unlawful violence;
- (b) effecting a lawful arrest or preventing the escape of a person lawfully detained;
- (c) action lawfully taken to quell a riot or insurrection.

- **"Absolutely necessary"** is stricter than "necessary in a democratic society": force must be
  **strictly proportionate**.
- Covers **unintended** deaths from the use of force.

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| McCann v UK (1995) | Soldiers' honest belief excused them, but **planning and control** of the operation breached Article 2 |
| Da Silva v UK (2016) | Honest belief, **perceived for good reasons**, valid at the time, even if mistaken |
| Stewart v UK (1984) | Commission: plastic baton round in a riot within 2.2(c); no breach |
| Güleç v Turkey (1998) | Live fire on demonstrators not absolutely necessary; no less lethal equipment; breach |

## Procedural duty (Jordan v UK, 2001)

Investigation must be: **independent** · **effective** (able to establish facts and identify those
responsible) · **prompt** · open to **public scrutiny** · involve the **family**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Limited right | Exceptions only as listed in the Article |
| Positive obligation | Duty to take steps to protect life |
| Operational duty | Duty to act on a known real and immediate risk to an identified person |
| Real and immediate risk | Objectively verified and present and continuing, not remote |
| Absolutely necessary | Force strictly proportionate to a 2.2 aim |
| Planning and control | The state's responsibility for how an operation is organised |
| Procedural obligation | Duty to investigate deaths effectively |

## Exam traps

- Applying the qualified-rights test to Article 2.
- Missing the planning and control point after finding the officer's belief honest.
- Treating Osman as a breach; it was not.
- Forgetting that unintended killings by force are within 2.2.
- Omitting the investigation duty, which can be breached on its own.
- Claiming Article 2 includes a right to die.

## Self-test

1. Why is Article 2 described as a limited right?
2. Can a state derogate from Article 2?
3. What is the effect of Protocols 6 and 13?
4. State the Osman test for the operational duty.
5. Why was there a breach in Edwards v UK?
6. Why was there no breach in Van Colle?
7. What did Rabone v Pennine Care decide?
8. List the three aims in Article 2.2.
9. How does "absolutely necessary" differ from "necessary in a democratic society"?
10. Why did the Court find a breach in McCann v UK?
11. What test did the Grand Chamber apply in Da Silva v UK?
12. What are the requirements of an effective investigation under Jordan v UK?

**Answers:** 1. Its only exceptions are those listed in Article 2.2. 2. No, except for deaths resulting
from lawful acts of war. 3. They abolish the death penalty, in peacetime and in all circumstances. 4. The
authorities knew or ought to have known of a real and immediate risk to the life of an identified
individual from a third party's criminal acts, and failed to take reasonable measures within their powers
to avoid it. 5. Information about the cellmate's dangerousness was not passed on, and a prisoner was
killed. 6. The earlier threats did not show a real and immediate risk to life. 7. A hospital owed the
operational duty to a voluntary psychiatric patient at real and immediate risk of suicide and breached it.
8. Defence of any person from unlawful violence; effecting a lawful arrest or preventing escape; quelling
a riot or insurrection. 9. It is stricter: force must be strictly proportionate to the aim. 10. The
planning and control of the operation did not minimise the risk to life. 11. An honest belief,
perceived for good reasons to be valid at the time, even if mistaken. 12. Independent, effective, prompt,
open to public scrutiny and involving the family.
