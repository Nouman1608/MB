These questions cover sub-topic **11.2 Antibodies and vaccination** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027 (AS Level, Paper 1 and Paper 2 style). The number in square brackets after the last part of each question is the total for the whole question. All data are invented for practice. Calculators are allowed.

## Section A

**1.** A baby receives antibodies in its mother's breast milk. Which type of immunity is this?

A: artificial active

B: artificial passive

C: natural active

D: natural passive **[1]**

**2.** Which part of an antibody differs between antibodies that bind different antigens?

A: constant region

B: disulfide bond

C: hinge region

D: variable region **[1]**

**3.** State what a vaccine contains, and explain why it does not cause the disease. **[2]**

**4.** Define the term *monoclonal antibody*. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** differences between active and passive immunity. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Relate the structure of an antibody molecule to its functions. **[6]**

**7.** Outline the hybridoma method for producing monoclonal antibodies. **[5]**

**8.** A test strip uses monoclonal antibodies to detect a protein made by a pathogen in a drop of blood. Mobile antibodies carrying coloured particles sit near the sample end. A test line and a control line further along carry fixed antibodies.

**(a)** Explain why a coloured test line appears only if the pathogen protein is present.

**(b)** Explain why a coloured control line should appear whether or not the protein is present.

**(c)** Explain why only monoclonal antibodies are suitable for this test. **[5]**

**9.** Outline the principles of using monoclonal antibodies to treat a cancer, and explain why the antibodies are humanised. **[4]**

**10.** The table shows the concentration of antibody against a toxin in a child's blood after vaccination with a toxoid.

| Week | 0 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 13 | 14 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody / arbitrary units | 0 | 15 | 30 | 18 | 10 | 95 | 240 | 220 |

A booster was given at week 12.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in antibody concentration between week 12 and week 14.

**(b)** Explain why the response after week 12 differs from that after week 0.

**(c)** Explain why the toxoid does not cause the disease but does give long-term immunity. **[7]**

**11.** Explain how a vaccination programme can reduce the spread of a disease, even though not every person in the population is vaccinated. **[3]**

**12.** Smallpox was eradicated by vaccination, but there is no vaccine that has eradicated malaria. Suggest and explain **two** reasons for this difference. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **D** [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**2.** **D** [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**3.** Antigens (e.g. attenuated or killed pathogen, toxoid or isolated antigens) [1]; the pathogen is weakened, killed or absent, so it cannot reproduce or cause harm, but the antigens still stimulate an immune response [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**4.** A single type of antibody that binds one specific antigen [1]; produced by a clone of genetically identical cells (hybridomas) [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**5.** Any two from: in active immunity the body makes its own antibodies, in passive they are supplied; active produces memory cells, passive does not; active takes time to develop, passive is immediate; active is long-term, passive is short-term [2]. Total: 2 marks.

**6.** Any six from: four polypeptide chains (two heavy, two light) held by disulfide bonds, giving a stable molecule; variable region has a specific amino acid sequence/shape; forming an antigen-binding site complementary to one antigen; two binding sites per antibody, allowing agglutination; hinge region gives flexibility, so arms bind antigens at different distances; constant region binds receptors on phagocytes, aiding phagocytosis; binding to toxins neutralises them; binding to viral attachment sites prevents entry to cells [6]. Total: 6 marks.

**7.** Mouse injected with the antigen, so it makes B-lymphocytes producing the antibody [1]; B-lymphocytes removed from the spleen [1]; fused with myeloma cells to form hybridoma cells, which make antibody and divide indefinitely [1]; hybridomas screened and those making the required antibody selected [1]; selected hybridoma cloned and cultured, and the antibody collected [1]. Total: 5 marks.

**8. (a)** The protein binds to the mobile antibodies with coloured particles [1]; fixed antibodies at the test line bind the protein and hold the complex there, so colour builds up [1]; without the protein, the mobile antibodies are not captured at the test line [1].

**(b)** Fixed antibodies at the control line bind the mobile antibodies themselves, so it shows the sample has moved along the strip and the test has worked [1].

**(c)** All the antibodies bind only the one specific antigen, so the test gives no false positives from other proteins [1]. Total: 5 marks.

**9.** Antibodies are made that are complementary to an antigen found on the cancer cells [1]; they bind only to those cells, either blocking a receptor or marking the cells for destruction by the immune system [1]; or they carry an attached drug or toxin directly to the cancer cells, so fewer healthy cells are harmed [1]; mouse antibodies are non-self and would trigger an immune response, so they are humanised to reduce this [1]. Total: 4 marks.

**10. (a)** (240 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 [1] = **2300 %** [1].

**(b)** The booster triggers a secondary response [1]; memory cells formed after the first dose [1]; quickly divide into plasma cells, so antibody rises faster and to a higher level [1].

**(c)** The toxoid is a harmless form of the toxin, so it cannot damage cells [1]; but it carries the same antigens, so memory cells form that give long-term protection against the real toxin [1]. Total: 7 marks.

**11.** When a large proportion of people are immune, an infected person is unlikely to meet a susceptible person [1]; so chains of transmission are broken and the pathogen cannot spread [1]; this protects unvaccinated people too (herd immunity) [1]. Total: 3 marks.

**12.** Any two reasons, each with its explanation [4]: smallpox virus had stable antigens, so one vaccine always matched, whereas *Plasmodium* has many forms and changing antigens; *Plasmodium* spends much of its time inside liver cells and red blood cells, hidden from antibodies; smallpox infected only humans, whereas malaria is also maintained in a vector, the mosquito; smallpox cases were easy to recognise, allowing ring vaccination, whereas malaria may be present without obvious symptoms. Total: 4 marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a vaccine contains antibodies.
- Calling maternal antibodies artificial.
- Leaving out why myeloma cells are used.
- Describing antibody parts without their functions.
- Explaining herd immunity without transmission being interrupted.
