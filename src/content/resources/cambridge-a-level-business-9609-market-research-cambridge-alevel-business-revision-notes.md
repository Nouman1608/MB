Condensed notes for **3.2 Market research** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, 2026–2028). Worked data analysis and a research plan are in the [Market research study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-market-research-cambridge-alevel-business/); exam-style questions are in the [Market research practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-market-research-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

## What 3.2 asks you to handle

- **3.2.1** Purposes: market size, growth, competitors; customer and consumer characteristics, profiles, wants and needs.
- **3.2.2** Primary and secondary research: features and usefulness.
- **3.2.3** Sampling: need and limitations.
- **3.2.4** Data: reliability; quantitative and qualitative analysis; tables, charts and graphs.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market research | Collecting and analysing information about a market and its customers |
| Customer | The person or organisation that buys the product |
| Consumer | The person who uses the product |
| Primary research | New data collected first-hand for a specific purpose |
| Secondary research | Data that already exists, collected for another purpose |
| Sample | A smaller group chosen to represent the population |
| Population | The whole group the business wants to learn about |
| Reliability | How far the findings can be trusted |
| Quantitative data | Numerical data |
| Qualitative data | Descriptive data about opinions, reasons and feelings |
| Bias | A systematic error that pushes results in one direction |

## Research plan

Objective → secondary data first → primary methods for gaps → sample → analyse, judge reliability, act.

## Primary methods at a glance

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire | Many responses, quantitative | Shallow; low online response |
| Interview | Depth | Costly; interviewer bias |
| Focus group | Reactions to ideas | Small; dominant voices |
| Observation | Real behaviour | No reasons |
| Test marketing | Real sales | Costly; alerts rivals |

## Question design

- Closed → easy to analyse, no reasons.
- Open → reasons, hard to summarise.
- Avoid **leading** ("Don't you agree…") and **ambiguous** ("regularly") wording.
- Use rating scales and precise time periods.

## Secondary sources

- **Internal**: sales records, loyalty-card data, complaints.
- **External**: government statistics, trade associations, published accounts, media, commercial reports.
- Check: relevance, age, who produced it and why, cost.

## Primary against secondary

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Higher | Lower |
| Speed | Slower | Faster |
| Relevance | Exactly fits the question | May not fit |
| Up to date | Yes | May be old |
| Exclusive | Yes | Rivals can use it too |

## Sampling

- **Need**: whole population too costly and slow.
- **Limitations**: unrepresentative samples, small samples, non-response, cost trade-off.
- Response rate = replies ÷ number asked × 100.

## Types of bias

Sample bias · question bias · interviewer bias · respondent bias · out-of-date data.

## Analysing data

- **Quantitative**: percentages, percentage-point differences, means, changes over time.
- **Qualitative**: group comments into themes, count frequency, look for suggested solutions.
- Method: **quote** a figure → **manipulate** it → **interpret** it for the decision.

## Worked reminder: quote, manipulate, interpret

A gym asks 300 people about early-morning opening: 84 would use it weekly, 126 occasionally, 90 never.

- **Quote**: 84 would use it weekly.
- **Manipulate**: 84 ÷ 300 = 28% weekly; 126 ÷ 300 = 42% occasionally; 70% would use it at least sometimes.
- **Interpret**: demand looks strong, but "occasionally" is vague and people often overstate future use (respondent bias). If all 300 were current members, non-members who might join for early opening were missed (sample bias).

## Reading charts

- Bar chart → check the axis starts at zero.
- Line graph → direction, rate of change, turning points.
- Pie chart → check the total it represents.
- Table → calculate, do not just copy.

## When research is most worthwhile

Large launch, unfamiliar market, high cost of failure. **Less** worthwhile: small change, customers already well known, delay would let a rival in.

## Exam traps

- Reliability (trustworthiness) ≠ usefulness (relevance to the decision).
- Surveying existing customers tells you little about non-customers.
- A small business may not afford extensive primary research; justify the choice.
- A high response rate does not cure a badly chosen sample.
- Secondary data is not "unreliable" by default; judge the source.
- Observation shows what people do, not why; pair it with questions if reasons matter.
- Test marketing gives real sales data but warns competitors.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between a customer and a consumer.
2. Give two purposes of market research.
3. State one advantage of secondary research over primary research.
4. Rewrite the leading question "Wouldn't you prefer our cheaper range?" in a neutral form.
5. Why might a focus group give unreliable results?
6. A business sends 1 500 surveys and gets 180 replies. What is the response rate?
7. Give two limitations of sampling.
8. Which type of data is "70% of shoppers prefer recyclable packaging"?
9. How can qualitative comments be analysed?
10. Give one thing to check when reading a bar chart.
11. Name one type of bias other than sample bias.
12. Give one reason why test marketing might be avoided.

**Answers**

1. The customer buys the product; the consumer uses it.
2. Any two: identifying market size, growth or competitors; identifying customer profiles, wants and needs.
3. For example, it is cheaper or quicker to obtain.
4. For example: "Which of our ranges would you choose: standard, premium, or neither?"
5. It involves a few people who may not represent the market, and one strong voice may influence the others.
6. 180 ÷ 1 500 × 100 = 12%.
7. Any two: may be unrepresentative, small samples give unreliable results, non-response, larger samples cost more.
8. Quantitative.
9. Group the comments into themes and count how often each theme appears.
10. Whether the scale starts at zero.
11. Any one: question bias, interviewer bias, respondent bias.
12. For example, it is costly, or it shows the product to rivals before the full launch.
