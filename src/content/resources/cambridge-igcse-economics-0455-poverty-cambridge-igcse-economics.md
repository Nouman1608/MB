## 5.2.1 Two definitions, two different questions

**Absolute poverty** is having too little income to buy the basic necessities of life: enough food, clean water, shelter and clothing. It asks: *can this household survive in reasonable health?*

**Relative poverty** is having income well below that of most others in the same society, so that a household cannot afford what is normal there. It asks: *how far is this household behind everyone else?* It is often measured as income below a fixed percentage of median income, such as 50% or 60%.

| | Absolute poverty | Relative poverty |
|---|---|---|
| Poverty line | Cost of basic necessities | A percentage of median income |
| When average incomes rise | Line stays fixed (adjusted only for price rises) | Line rises |
| Can it be eliminated by growth? | Yes, in principle | Only if the poorest catch up with the middle |
| Most relevant in | Low-income countries | All countries, including high-income ones |

### Household size matters

A household's need depends on how many people it supports. A basic basket costs 2,000 a year per person in Country A (invented). Household M has 5 members and an income of 9,000.

Basic needs = 5 × 2,000 = 10,000. Income of 9,000 is 1,000 short, so the household is in **absolute poverty**, even though a single person on 9,000 would not be.

### Worked example: growth can raise relative poverty

Ten households in Country B (invented) earn, in order: 3,000; 4,500; 6,500; 8,000; 11,000; 14,000; 18,000; 22,000; 30,000; 50,000.

1. Median = average of the 5th and 6th incomes = (11,000 + 14,000) ÷ 2 = 12,500.
2. Relative line (50% of median) = 6,250. Two households are below it: **20%**.
3. Absolute line (cost of basics) = 4,000. One household is below it: **10%**.

Now the top five incomes rise by 20% and the bottom five do not change. The 6th income becomes 14,000 × 1.2 = 16,800.

4. New median = (11,000 + 16,800) ÷ 2 = 13,900. New relative line = 6,950.
5. Households below the relative line: 3,000, 4,500 and 6,500, so **30%**.
6. Absolute poverty is still **10%**.

Nobody's income fell, yet relative poverty rose by half. Growth that reaches only the better-off widens the gap.

## 5.2.2 Causes of poverty

Each cause should be written as a chain that ends in too little income for needs.

| Cause | Chain |
|---|---|
| Unemployment | Job lost → wage stops → income falls to savings or benefits, if any → long spells reduce skills and confidence, so finding work gets harder |
| Low wages | Low skills or weak bargaining power → low hourly pay, or only part-time work → income below needs, especially with dependants |
| Illness | Sickness or disability → fewer hours or no work, and treatment costs → income falls while spending rises |
| Age | Children cannot earn and depend on adults; elderly people may have no pension or a small one → large families and old people are at higher risk |

Causes interact. An illness can lead to job loss; a long spell out of work lowers future wages; elderly people who earned low wages saved little. This is why some households stay poor for years and why children of poor families can remain poor as adults if they leave school early.

## 5.2.3 Policies: match the policy to the cause

| Policy | How it works | Cause it best tackles | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Promoting economic growth | More output creates jobs and raises incomes and tax revenue | Unemployment | Gains may go mainly to the better-off; does little for those who cannot work |
| Improved education | Raises skills and productivity, so better-paid work | Low wages, unemployment | Slow, often a decade or more; costly; poorest children may not attend |
| More generous state benefits | Cash or support paid directly to poor households | Illness, age, unemployment | Cost to taxpayers; may reduce the incentive to work if withdrawn sharply as earnings rise |
| Progressive taxation | Higher-income people pay a higher proportion of income in tax | Inequality; funds other policies | Does not raise the income of the poor unless the revenue is spent on them; may reduce effort or encourage avoidance |
| National minimum wage | Legal minimum hourly pay | Low wages | No help to the unemployed, sick or elderly; may reduce jobs if set well above the market wage |

### Worked example: tax and benefits together

Country C (invented) taxes income at 0% up to 10,000 and 30% on income above 10,000. It pays a benefit of 4,000 to every household earning under 10,000.

| | Household X | Household Y |
|---|---|---|
| Income before tax | 80,000 | 8,000 |
| Tax | 0.3 × (80,000 − 10,000) = 21,000 | 0 |
| Benefit | 0 | 4,000 |
| Income after tax and benefit | 59,000 | 12,000 |
| Ratio X : Y | 80,000 ÷ 8,000 = 10 times | 59,000 ÷ 12,000 = 4.9 times |

The tax narrowed the gap, but only the benefit lifted Y's income. If the basic needs line is 11,000, Y has moved out of absolute poverty because of the spending side, not the tax side.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Discuss whether a national minimum wage is the best way to reduce poverty. **[8]**

> A minimum wage raises the pay of the lowest-paid workers. A full-time worker whose wage rises from 5 to 7 an hour earns 40% more, which can lift a household above the poverty line without any government spending. *(Mechanism and a precise effect.)*
>
> It may also encourage some unemployed people to look for work, because work now pays more than benefits. *(A second benefit, developed.)*
>
> However, it only helps people in work. Many poor people are unemployed, too ill to work, children or retired, so a minimum wage does nothing for them. *(Matches the policy to the causes it misses.)*
>
> If it is set well above the wage firms would otherwise pay, firms may employ fewer workers, so some low-paid workers lose their jobs and fall into poverty. *(A risk, with its condition.)*
>
> More generous state benefits reach those who cannot work, and education tackles low wages at their source, although more slowly. *(Compares with alternatives.)*
>
> A minimum wage is effective where poverty is caused mainly by low pay and it is set close to the market wage. It is not the best single policy, because poverty has several causes. A mix, such as a moderate minimum wage with benefits for those unable to work, is more likely to succeed. *(Judgement with conditions.)*

## Exam technique

- **Define** absolute and relative poverty separately; do not say one is "worse".
- **Explain** a cause by linking it to income, then to needs.
- **Analyse** a policy as a chain of steps to higher income for the poor.
- **Discuss** by naming which poor groups a policy reaches and misses, then judge.
- **Calculate** poverty lines and medians in clear steps.

## Common mistakes

- Saying relative poverty "does not exist in rich countries". It exists in every country.
- Treating progressive taxation as raising the incomes of the poor by itself.
- Assuming a minimum wage helps all poor people.
- Writing "education reduces poverty" without the chain through skills and wages, or without mentioning the time it takes.
- Forgetting that growth can leave relative poverty unchanged or higher.
