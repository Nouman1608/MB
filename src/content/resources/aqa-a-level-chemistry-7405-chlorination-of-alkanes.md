## Why alkanes are unreactive, and why chlorine reacts with them

Alkanes contain only C–C and C–H bonds. These bonds are strong and almost non-polar, because carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativities. There are no regions of positive or negative charge for ions or polar reagents to attack. So alkanes do not react with most reagents. Chlorine, however, reacts with methane when ultraviolet (UV) light provides enough energy to break the Cl–Cl bond into radicals. Radicals are very reactive and can attack the non-polar C–H bond.

## The overall reaction

```
CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl
```

The conditions are **ultraviolet light** (or sunlight). It is a **substitution** reaction because a hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom. The products are chloromethane and hydrogen chloride. It is a **free-radical** reaction because the mechanism involves radicals.

## The mechanism

A radical is a species with an unpaired electron, shown by a dot. The mechanism has three stages.

### Initiation

The Cl–Cl bond breaks by **homolytic fission**, with each chlorine atom taking one electron. UV light supplies the energy.

```
Cl₂ → 2Cl•
```

### Propagation

Two steps repeat in a chain. In each step, a radical reacts with a molecule and a new radical is formed.

```
Step 1:  Cl• + CH₄ → •CH₃ + HCl
Step 2:  •CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•
```

The chlorine radical used in step 1 is regenerated in step 2, so it can start the cycle again. This is why the process is a **chain reaction**: one photon can lead to thousands of molecules reacting. Adding steps 1 and 2 gives the overall equation CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl.

### Termination

Two radicals combine to form a molecule, removing radicals from the system and ending the chain.

```
Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
•CH₃ + Cl• → CH₃Cl
•CH₃ + •CH₃ → C₂H₆
```

The last equation shows how a trace of **ethane** can form as an impurity in the products. Termination steps are rare compared with propagation, because radicals are present in low concentration and so seldom meet one another.

### How to write the equations correctly

1. Show the dot on the radical on the atom that carries the unpaired electron (Cl•, •CH₃).
2. Check that atoms balance in each step.
3. In **initiation**, one molecule gives two radicals.
4. In **propagation**, each step has one radical on each side.
5. In **termination**, no radical appears on the right.
6. Label each step.

## Further substitution

The chloromethane formed still contains C–H bonds, so chlorine radicals can attack it and replace more hydrogen atoms. This produces a mixture:

```
CH₃Cl + Cl₂ → CH₂Cl₂ + HCl      dichloromethane
CH₂Cl₂ + Cl₂ → CHCl₃ + HCl      trichloromethane
CHCl₃ + Cl₂ → CCl₄ + HCl        tetrachloromethane
```

The propagation steps for the second substitution are Cl• + CH₃Cl → •CH₂Cl + HCl and •CH₂Cl + Cl₂ → CH₂Cl₂ + Cl•. This is a drawback of the reaction: it gives a mixture of products that has to be separated, and a lower yield of the desired product.

To favour the formation of chloromethane, use an **excess of methane** so that a chlorine radical is more likely to collide with a methane molecule than with a molecule of chloromethane.

## Other alkanes and halogens

The same type of mechanism applies to other alkanes (for example, ethane gives chloroethane) and other halogens (bromine with UV light). With a longer chain, different hydrogen atoms can be replaced, so isomers can form: propane gives both 1-chloropropane and 2-chloropropane.

### Worked example 1: the mechanism for ethane

Write the propagation steps for the reaction of ethane with chlorine.

```
Cl• + C₂H₆ → •C₂H₅ + HCl
•C₂H₅ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + Cl•
```

A termination step: •C₂H₅ + •C₂H₅ → C₄H₁₀.

### Worked example 2: explain the by-product

A small amount of ethane is found in the products from the reaction of methane with chlorine. Explain how it forms.

Two methyl radicals combine in a termination step: •CH₃ + •CH₃ → C₂H₆.

### Worked example 3: write the equation for a fully substituted product

Write the overall equation for the reaction of methane with excess chlorine to form tetrachloromethane.

CH₄ + 4Cl₂ → CCl₄ + 4HCl. Four hydrogen atoms are replaced, so four chlorine molecules are needed and four HCl are formed.

## Calculations

### Worked example 4: atom economy

Calculate the atom economy for making chloromethane by CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (Mᵣ: CH₄ = 16.0, Cl₂ = 71.0, CH₃Cl = 50.5).

```
atom economy = 50.5 ÷ (16.0 + 71.0) × 100 = 50.5 ÷ 87.0 × 100 = 58.0 %
```

The atom economy is below 100% because hydrogen chloride is also formed.

### Worked example 5: percentage yield

8.00 g of methane reacts with excess chlorine in UV light, and 17.5 g of chloromethane is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield.

```
n(CH₄) = 8.00 ÷ 16.0 = 0.500 mol
theoretical n(CH₃Cl) = 0.500 mol
theoretical mass = 0.500 × 50.5 = 25.25 g
percentage yield = 17.5 ÷ 25.25 × 100 = 69.3 %
```

### Worked example 6: moles of chlorine

How many moles of chlorine are needed to convert 0.500 mol of methane completely into tetrachloromethane, and what is the mass of CCl₄ (Mᵣ = 154.0) formed?

```
CH₄ + 4Cl₂ → CCl₄ + 4HCl
n(Cl₂) = 4 × 0.500 = 2.00 mol
mass CCl₄ = 0.500 × 154.0 = 77.0 g
```

## Comparing the mechanism with a polar reaction

The chlorination of methane is a good example of how a mechanism depends on the reagent and the bond. Chlorine reacts with methane through radicals because the C–H bond is non-polar and UV light is available. In contrast, a halogenoalkane reacts with hydroxide through a nucleophilic attack on a polar C–Hal bond, which needs curly arrows. In an exam, decide first whether the bond is polar or the conditions include UV light, and then choose the mechanism.

| Question clue | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Alkane plus halogen, UV light | Free-radical substitution |
| Halogenoalkane plus aqueous hydroxide | Nucleophilic substitution |
| Alkene plus hydrogen halide | Electrophilic addition |

## Reading questions on this topic

Questions often give the overall equation and ask for steps. Start by finding the species that appear on both sides across the propagation steps: these are the radicals. A species that appears on the left in one step and the right in the next is a radical carrier, and the others are reactants or products. Practice by writing the three stages for different alkane and halogen pairs until the pattern becomes automatic.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting the dot on a radical, or placing it on the wrong atom.
- Writing a propagation step that has two radicals on one side, or no radical on the product side.
- Writing a termination step with a radical on the right.
- Writing Cl₂ → Cl⁻ + Cl⁺ for initiation. The fission is homolytic and gives radicals.
- Omitting UV light from the conditions.
- Using curly arrows in a radical mechanism. They are not required.
- Calling the reaction addition. It is substitution.
- Forgetting that more than one hydrogen can be replaced.
- Describing the reaction as nucleophilic or electrophilic substitution.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain the mechanism**: name the type (free-radical substitution) and give three labelled stages with equations.
- **State the conditions**: ultraviolet light.
- **Explain why a mixture of products forms**: further substitution of the chloromethane, and termination steps.
- **Explain how to improve the yield of the monosubstituted product**: use excess methane.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is UV light needed and not just heat?**
The energy of ultraviolet photons is enough to break the Cl–Cl bond. The C–H and C–C bonds are not broken because they are stronger.

**Why does the reaction stop after a while?**
Reactants are used up, and radicals are removed by termination steps.

**Does the mechanism need curly arrows?**
No. The specification does not require curly arrows for free-radical mechanisms, just balanced equations with dots.
