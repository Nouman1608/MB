Condensed for the final weeks. For the worked poem, use the
[Unseen poetry study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-9275-unseen-poetry/).
These notes serve **Route A (3.2.2)** and **Route B (3.3.2)**.

## At a glance

| | Route A | Route B |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Paper 2a, Section B | Paper 2b, Section B |
| Task | One question on one unseen poem | One question on one unseen poem |
| Marks | 30 | 30 |
| Time | About 45 minutes | About 45 minutes |
| Next on the paper | Unseen prose (Section C) | Nothing: anthology and unseen only |

No prior knowledge of the poem or poet is expected. Everything comes from the page.

## What the marks reward

| AO | What it means for one unseen poem | How to show it |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Understanding of the poem's ideas and themes, shown through a clear interpretation | An opening sentence that answers the question and names how the poem moves |
| AO2 | Analysis of how the poet creates meanings and effects through language, form and structure | A method and its effect at a precise moment, in every paragraph |
| AO3 | An informed personal response, with appropriate terminology and accurate, organised writing | Terms used correctly, a developing argument, and an alternative reading where one fits |

All three objectives are met in the same paragraphs: there is no separate "AO3 section".

## Reading the question

The question usually asks **how the poet presents** a feeling, a relationship, a place or an
experience. Underline that focus and return to it in the first sentence of every paragraph. A
brilliant comment on a feature that has nothing to do with the focus earns far less than a modest
comment that does.

## The five-step method

1. **Sense:** read once; who speaks, about what? One-sentence summary.
2. **Shape:** stanzas, line length, rhyme, punctuation, where the poem turns.
3. **Detail:** underline five or six images or words you will use.
4. **Plan:** a one-sentence interpretation plus three or four points in poem order.
5. **Write:** introduction, developed paragraphs, conclusion on the ending.

A workable split of the 45 minutes: about 3 minutes for sense, 3 for shape, 4 for detail, 3 to 5
to plan and about 30 to write.

## Key terms

| Term | Use it when... |
|---|---|
| Speaker | Discussing the voice; do not assume it is the poet |
| Enjambment | A line runs on, often creating suspense or emphasis at the break |
| End-stopped line | A line closes with punctuation, creating a firm pause |
| Caesura | A mid-line pause slows or breaks the rhythm |
| Volta / turn | The poem changes direction or tone |
| Metaphor / simile | A comparison shapes how the subject is seen |
| Personification | Something non-human is given human qualities |
| Sibilance / assonance | Sound patterns clearly create a mood |
| Juxtaposition | Contrasting images are placed close together |
| Tone | The attitude of the voice: tender, bitter, ironic, uncertain |
| Cyclical structure | The ending returns to the opening |
| Persona | The poet adopts a character's voice, which the reader may judge |
| Half-rhyme | Sounds nearly but not fully rhyme, which can suggest unease or something unresolved |
| Semantic field | A group of words from one area of life (work, illness, weather) that colours the whole poem |
| Oxymoron | Two contradictory words joined, capturing a mixed feeling |
| Refrain | A repeated line or phrase that builds emphasis or obsession |

## Worked mini-example

These lines are original, written for these notes, and are not by a published poet.

> **Deep End**
>
> *My father's hands are under me, then not.*
> *He does not say when he lets go.*
> *The water holds what he was holding. I*
> *kick, and the tiles beneath me start to move.*
>
> *Now my daughter floats across my palms,*
> *her face fierce with trust. I count to three*
> *out loud, and do not stop at three.*
> *She kicks. My hands stay open in the water.*

| Detail | What you might say |
|---|---|
| Title | A swimming term that also suggests risk and being out of one's depth |
| "under me, then not" | The comma creates a caesura, so support vanishes mid-line, as it does for the child |
| "He does not say" | Trust is built on a loving silence: the father lets go without warning |
| "I / kick" | Enjambment leaves "I" alone at the line's edge, then the next line moves |
| "the tiles beneath me start to move" | The child feels the world moving rather than the body, a sign of first success |
| Stanza break | The poem jumps a generation: the speaker now plays the father's role |
| "do not stop at three" | A gentle deception that echoes the father's silence |
| Final line | Open hands become an image of love as letting go; the structure is cyclical |

**Annotated model paragraph** (question: how does the poet present the relationship between parent
and child?):

> The poet presents a parent's love as the courage to let go, a lesson passed from one generation
> to the next. **[AO1: answers the question and names the movement]** In the first stanza the
> father's support disappears inside a single line, "under me, then not", and the caesura makes his
> absence as sudden for the reader as for the child. **[short quotation; AO2: structure within the
> line]** Because he "does not say" when he lets go, his silence could seem like abandonment, yet
> the child's discovery that the water "holds what he was holding" suggests the trust he has given
> the child remains. **[AO2: language; AO3: weighs two readings]** The second stanza repeats the pattern
> with the speaker as parent: counting past three is a small, loving deception that echoes the
> father, and the final image of hands that "stay open" turns letting go into an act of care.
> **[AO2: cyclical structure and the ending; AO3: organised argument]**

## Paragraph pattern

**Point about the question → short quotation → method → effect → link to the poem's development.**

Tentative phrasing ("perhaps", "this could suggest") shows you are exploring, which the higher levels
reward.

## Always cover

- The **title**: it often frames the poem.
- The **turn**: what changes, and why there.
- The **ending**: how it resolves, or refuses to resolve, the poem.
- At least one **structural** point, not only language.

## Exam traps

- Hunting for hidden meanings before grasping the surface.
- Naming devices without effects.
- Spending the whole answer on the first stanza.
- Guessing the poet's biography.
- Line-by-line paraphrase.
- Route A: overrunning and leaving too little time for unseen prose.
- Comparing the poem with an anthology poem when the question asks only about the unseen poem.
- "The enjambment makes it flow": say what the run-on does to meaning at that point.
- Treating every rhyme as significant; comment on sound only where it clearly shapes the mood.
- Drifting from the question's focus into everything you noticed.

## Self-test

1. Which sections of which papers assess unseen poetry on each route?
2. What should Step 1 produce?
3. Why plan points in the order of the poem?
4. What is the difference between enjambment and an end-stopped line?
5. Name three features you should always cover.
6. Why is biography not useful in this question?
7. Which three assessment objectives apply, and which one covers terminology and organisation?
8. What is a semantic field? Give an example of how one could shape a poem.
9. In *Deep End*, what does the stanza break contribute?
10. Improve this sentence: "The poet uses enjambment to make it flow."
11. Roughly how should the 45 minutes be divided?
12. Where in a poem should your final paragraph usually focus, and why?

**Answers:** 1. Route A: Paper 2a, Section B. Route B: Paper 2b, Section B. 2. A one-sentence summary
of who is speaking and what the poem is about. 3. It shows the poem's development and makes sure you
reach the ending. 4. Enjambment runs a sentence over the line break; an end-stopped line closes with
punctuation. 5. Any three of: the title, the turn, the ending, a structural point. 6. The poem is
unseen, so you are not expected to know the poet; marks come from analysing what is on the page.
7. AO1, AO2 and AO3; AO3 covers the informed personal response, terminology and accurate, organised
writing. 8. A group of words from one area of life; for example, words of illness scattered through a
love poem could suggest the relationship is damaging. 9. It moves the poem on a generation, so the
speaker now repeats the father's role and the structure becomes cyclical. 10. For example: "The run-on line carries
the sentence past the break, so the speaker's thought spills over, as if the feeling cannot be contained." 11. About
10 minutes reading for sense, shape and detail, 3 to 5 planning and about 30 writing. 12. The ending,
because it shows how the poem resolves or refuses to resolve its ideas, and it is often where the
meaning shifts.
