In the AQA A-level English Language (7702) non-exam assessment, *Language in Action*, methods of language analysis
are the tools you use to study your own data in the Language Investigation and to reflect on your own writing in
the commentary. The specification says each task will have "a distinctive approach to analysis" shaped by the
research pathway or theme you choose, but that every analysis will cover relevant aspects of six language levels:
phonetics, phonology and prosodics; graphology; lexis and semantics; grammar, including morphology; pragmatics; and
discourse.

This guide teaches section 4.3.3 Methods of language analysis of the A-level subject content in the AQA AS and
A-level English Language specification (7701/7702), Version 1.2 (14 October 2021). The NEA is A-level only: it has a
word count of 3,500 (a 2,000-word investigation excluding data, and a piece of original writing and a commentary of
750 words each), is worth 100 marks and 20% of the A-level, and is assessed by teachers and moderated by AQA. For the
tasks themselves, see the [Language in Action NEA study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-language-nea-language-in-action/),
the [NEA revision notes](/resources/aqa-english-language-nea-revision-notes/) and the
[NEA practice tasks](/resources/aqa-english-language-nea-practice/).

## What does the specification say?

| Specification point (4.3.3) | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| Both the investigation and original writing have a distinctive approach to analysis, shaped by the research pathway or theme | Choose the levels that fit your research questions or writing purpose; you do not need to apply all six equally |
| Underlying any analysis will be coverage of some relevant aspects of the language levels | Your analysis must still be systematic and use the levels' terminology |
| The six levels: phonetics, phonology and prosodics; graphology; lexis and semantics; grammar, including morphology; pragmatics; discourse | These are the frameworks the moderator expects to see applied accurately |

The preparation list in the specification also asks you to "transcribe spoken data where appropriate", "use language
concepts and ideas", "work in greater depth and with greater range" and "reflect on the writing process using methods
of language analysis". Those are the skills this guide builds.

## How do you choose methods for your investigation?

Start from your **research question**, not from the list of levels. Ask which levels will give the most revealing
evidence for that question.

| Investigation focus (examples from the specification) | Most useful levels | What you might count or examine |
|---|---|---|
| Features of a regional dialect, based on a survey | Phonology; lexis and semantics; grammar | Pronunciation variants; dialect words; non-standard verb forms |
| Gendered talk | Pragmatics; discourse; grammar | Interruptions and overlaps; hedges; tag questions; topic control |
| The language of sports commentary | Grammar; lexis and semantics; prosodics | Ellipsis and present-tense verbs; specialist lexis; pitch and pace |
| How travel guides represent a community | Lexis and semantics; grammar; discourse | Evaluative adjectives; agency in clauses; how sections are ordered |
| The writing of two children aged 8 | Grammar, including morphology; orthography (graphology); discourse | Clause types and connectives; spelling patterns; narrative structure |
| How turn-taking works in real-time writing online | Discourse; pragmatics; graphology | Adjacency pairs; repair; emoji and punctuation used for tone |

The specification's four kinds of question help too: a **genre-based** investigation (what are the distinctive
features?), a **function-based** one (what is the language used to do?), an **attitudes-based** one (how do people
feel about it?) and a **user-based** one (who uses it?). Each suggests different methods. An attitudes-based study
may analyse questionnaire responses; a genre-based study may compare features across several texts.

## How do you apply each level well?

| Level | Precise terminology to use | Pitfall to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Phonetics, phonology and prosodics | Phonemes, elision, assimilation, glottal stop, intonation, stress, pause | Describing sounds with spelling alone; use a consistent transcription |
| Graphology | Typeface, layout, colour, image, positioning, white space | Describing visuals without linking them to meaning |
| Lexis and semantics | Semantic field, connotation, register, jargon, neologism, collocation | Listing words with no pattern or count |
| Grammar, including morphology | Clause types, sentence functions, modal verbs, tense and aspect, voice, affixes, compounds | Naming only word classes; look at phrase and clause structure |
| Pragmatics | Implicature, politeness and face, presupposition, speech acts | Guessing at intentions without evidence from context |
| Discourse | Structure, cohesion, turn-taking, adjacency pairs, openings and closings | Ignoring how the whole text or conversation is organised |

## How do you make analysis systematic?

Moderators reward analysis that is **methodical** and **linked to context**. Three habits help.

1. **Code your data.** Decide categories in advance (for example "hedges", "tag questions", "interruptions") and
   mark every instance in your transcript or texts. Keep clean copies of data in the appendices, as the
   specification requires.
2. **Combine quantitative and qualitative evidence.** Counts and percentages show patterns; close analysis of
   examples explains them. The appendices should hold evidence that supports any quantitative approach.
3. **Relate features to context.** Explain how audience, purpose, genre, mode and the relationship between
   participants shape the features you find.

**Worked example (quantitative to qualitative).** In 20 minutes of transcribed group talk, Speaker A produces 14 hedges
("sort of", "I think") in 52 turns and Speaker B 4 hedges in 48 turns. As percentages of turns: A = 14 / 52 x 100 =
**26.9%**, B = 4 / 48 x 100 = **8.3%**. The numbers show a difference; the analysis must then explain it, for example by
examining whether A's hedges soften disagreement (a politeness strategy) or express uncertainty, and whether the
roles of the speakers (for example, who is chairing) explain the pattern. The conclusion should be cautious, as a
small sample cannot support broad claims about all speakers.

## How do methods apply to the original writing commentary?

In the commentary you use the same levels to **reflect on your own writing and your style model**. The
specification says the commentary lets you "consider and evaluate the style model, the writing process and the
effectiveness of the final piece of writing".

- Identify features of the **style model** at several levels (for example the short declaratives and second-person
  address of a persuasive speech).
- Explain the **choices** you made in your own piece, using terminology: why you chose a particular sentence type,
  lexical field or structure, for your audience and purpose.
- **Evaluate** effectiveness honestly: what worked, what you redrafted and why.

**Worked example.** "In my travel article I followed the style model's use of present-tense narration ('The market
wakes slowly') to create immediacy, and I chose concrete sensory lexis from the semantic field of food to help a
non-specialist reader picture the scene. In my second draft I replaced several adjectives with verbs, because the
style model relied on dynamic verbs for pace."

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to use all six language levels in my investigation?**
No. The specification says the approach is shaped by your research pathway, with coverage of *relevant* aspects of
the levels. Choose those that best answer your research questions, and apply them in depth.

**Should my investigation use numbers?**
Quantitative evidence is often useful, and the specification expects appendices to contain evidence supporting
quantitative approaches. But numbers must be interpreted with qualitative analysis and linked to context.

**Do I need to transcribe spoken data?**
Where your data is spoken, yes: the specification lists transcribing spoken data where appropriate. Use a
consistent set of conventions and include a key.

**Is the commentary just a description of my writing?**
No. It should analyse the style model and your choices using methods of language analysis, and evaluate how well
your writing achieves its purpose.

## Common mistakes

- Applying every level superficially instead of the most relevant levels in depth.
- Counting features without explaining what the pattern means.
- Making claims bigger than the data supports.
- Using terminology loosely (for example calling any short word a "monosyllable" without purpose).
- Writing a commentary that narrates the writing process with no linguistic analysis.
- Leaving out clean copies of data or evidence for quantitative claims.
