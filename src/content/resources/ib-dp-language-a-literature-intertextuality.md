This guide covers **Intertextuality: Connecting Texts**, one of three
areas of exploration for IB Diploma Programme Language A: Literature,
first assessment 2021. The [full syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-syllabus-guide/)
confirms this exclusively literary course draws comparisons across a
wide span of literary history and form under this component, alongside
[Readers, Writers and
Texts](/resources/ib-dp-language-a-literature-readers-writers-texts/).

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Intertextuality is where this course's exclusively literary scope is
put to direct use: rather than analysing a single text in isolation,
this component asks how one literary work relates to another --
echoing, responding to, or subverting it. Because Paper 2's
comparative essay draws directly on this component's skills, it should
not be treated as a secondary or optional part of preparation relative
to close reading of individual texts.

## What "intertextuality" actually means

At its simplest, intertextuality is the idea that no literary work
exists in isolation -- every text is written and read against a
backdrop of other texts, whether the writer is deliberately echoing,
responding to, subverting, or unconsciously working within a tradition
established by earlier works. These relationships are distinct and
worth naming precisely: an **echo** or **homage** borrows form or
content admiringly; a **response** engages critically or in dialogue
with an earlier work's ideas; a **subversion** deliberately overturns
or undermines the conventions or assumptions of an earlier work or
genre.

## Building a genuine intertextual comparison

A weak comparative answer places two works side by side and describes
each separately. A strong one identifies a specific, genuine point of
connection -- shared theme, shared form, one work deliberately
referencing or reacting against the other -- and uses that connection
to say something neither work would reveal fully on its own. Structure
comparative practice around three questions: what specifically
connects these two works, not just "both deal with loss" but a more
precise shared concern or technique; how does each work treat that
shared element differently; and what does comparing them reveal that
reading either work alone would not?

## Worked example: naming the specific relationship

Consider comparing a modern novel that reimagines events from a much
older epic poem's minor character's point of view. A surface-level
comparison might simply note that "both works deal with war and its
aftermath." A stronger intertextual comparison identifies the specific
relationship: the modern novel is a deliberate **response** to the
epic, deliberately subverting whose perspective is centred -- shifting
attention from the epic's celebrated hero to a figure the original
text treats as peripheral. This shift is not just thematic overlap; it
is the modern work actively arguing with the older one about whose
experience of war deserves narrative attention. A comparative essay
that names this specific relationship, rather than a generic shared
topic, demonstrates exactly the kind of analysis this component and
Paper 2 both reward.

## Working across literary traditions and periods

Because this course draws from a genuinely international range of
literary traditions, and often includes works originally written in
languages other than the one being studied (read in translation),
intertextual comparison here can span very different historical
periods and cultural contexts -- a modern novel read alongside a much
older play, for instance. Account for context explicitly in a
comparison: two works can share a theme while treating it very
differently because of when, where and in what literary tradition each
was written, and a strong comparative answer accounts for that
difference rather than treating both works as though they were
produced in the same cultural moment.

## Connecting this component to Time and Space

Intertextuality and Time and Space are not separate skills to prepare
in isolation -- understanding a work's own historical and cultural
context is often the key to explaining why it echoes, responds to or
subverts another work in the specific way it does. Preparing these two
components together, rather than as unconnected blocks, produces
stronger comparative essays.

## How this feeds Paper 2 and the individual oral

Paper 2's comparative essay draws directly on the skill this component
develops -- structured, evidence-based comparison across two studied
literary works. Practising timed comparative writing specifically, not
just single-text close reading, is essential preparation, since
comparing under time pressure is a distinct skill. For the individual
oral, which pairs a work originally written in the studied language
with a work studied in translation, this component's comparative
habits transfer directly -- the oral itself is, in effect, a spoken
intertextual and contextual analysis of two works connected around a
common global issue.

## Common exam pitfalls

Describing two works separately and stopping, without ever stating
the specific relationship between them, is the most common way marks
are lost on this component -- a comparative essay is not two single-
text essays placed side by side. Naming only a broad shared theme
("both are about love," "both are about power") without identifying
how each work treats that theme differently, or why the difference
matters, is a second common gap. A third is treating context as
irrelevant to the comparison: assuming two works can be compared as
though produced in the same moment, when in fact part of what makes
the comparison interesting is that they were not.

## How to approach it

Practise naming the specific relationship between two works -- echo,
response, or subversion -- rather than settling for a generic shared
theme. Build comparative practice around the three-question structure
(what connects them specifically, how each treats it differently, what
comparing them reveals) under timed conditions, since Paper 2 rewards
fluency in this structure over recalling a memorised comparison of two
particular works. Keep the individual oral's paired-text comparison in
mind while preparing this component, since the two are the same
analytical skill applied in different formats.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a broad shared theme not enough for a comparison?**
Saying "both are about love" or "both are about power" does not show how each work treats the theme differently or why that matters. Name the specific relationship between the works instead.

**Should you memorise a comparison of two works for Paper 2?**
Practise the three-question structure under timed conditions instead. In IB DP Language A: Literature, Paper 2 rewards fluency in building a comparison over recalling a memorised comparison of two particular works.

**How does intertextuality connect to the individual oral?**
The oral pairs a work originally written in the language studied with a work studied in translation around a common global issue, so it is a spoken version of the same context-aware comparison.
