## Section A -- short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by "actionable per se" and why it matters in trespass to the person. **[2]**

**2.** Define assault and explain the requirement of immediacy, with an authority. **[4]**

**3.** Explain how words can both create and negate an assault. **[3]**

**4.** Define battery and explain the meaning of force. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the debate over whether a battery requires hostility. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how consent operates as a defence in medical treatment. **[3]**

**7.** Explain the requirement of total restraint in false imprisonment, with an authority. **[2]**

**8.** Explain whether a claimant must know of their restraint to sue for false imprisonment. **[3]**

## Section B -- scenario and essay questions

**9.** *Original scenario.* During an amateur football match, Kofi tackles Sean within the rules and Sean's ankle is broken. Later, after the referee has stopped play, Kofi deliberately kicks Sean. Sean's friend Liam runs over and pushes Kofi away. Kofi, angry, punches Liam in the face, breaking his nose. Advise Kofi on his liability in trespass to the person and any defences. **[12]**

**10.** *Original scenario.* Bea attends a music festival. Security staff, mistakenly thinking she has entered without a ticket, take her to a tent and tell her she must stay until her ticket is checked. The tent has an open flap at the back, which she does not notice. After two hours she is released when her ticket is found to be valid. The festival's terms say anyone leaving the site must pass through the exit gate. Advise Bea. **[10]**

**11.** *Original scenario.* Doctor Wong explains to Gemma, an adult, the nature of a knee operation but not a small risk of nerve damage, which then occurs. During the operation, Doctor Wong also removes a harmless mole from Gemma's leg, thinking it best. Advise Gemma whether she has a claim in battery. **[8]**

**12.** "The trespass torts give effective protection to personal liberty and bodily integrity." Evaluate this statement. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** It means the tort can be sued on without proof of damage (1). This matters because the trespass torts protect the right to bodily integrity and liberty itself, so a claimant can recover at least nominal damages even where there is no injury (1). **[2]**

**2.** Assault is an act that causes the claimant reasonably to apprehend the immediate infliction of unlawful force (1). The threatened force must be immediate, so the defendant must appear able to carry it out at once (1). In Stephens v Myers (1830) a man advancing with a clenched fist was liable although stopped before reaching the claimant (1), whereas in Thomas v NUM (1985) pickets held back by police could not cause apprehension of immediate force (1). **[4]**

**3.** Words, such as threats accompanied by an approach, can cause reasonable apprehension of immediate force, and silence may do so (R v Ireland, 1998) (1). Words can negate an assault by showing that force will not be used (1), as in Tuberville v Savage (1669), where the defendant said he would not take such language "if it were not assize-time" (1). **[3]**

**4.** Battery is the intentional and direct application of unlawful force to another person (1). Force means any physical contact, however slight, without the need for injury (Cole v Turner, 1704) (1), and may be applied through an object or as a continuing act (Fagan v MPC, 1969) (1). **[3]**

**5.** In Wilson v Pringle (1987) the Court of Appeal held that the touching must be hostile (1). In F v West Berkshire Health Authority (1989) Lord Goff rejected this, because a well-meant but unwanted touching, such as an operation without consent, should still be a battery (1). Collins v Wilcock (1984) offers an alternative test: whether the contact goes beyond what is generally acceptable in everyday life (1). The modern view favours this test, since requiring hostility could leave unwanted but friendly touching unremedied (1). **[4]**

**6.** Consent makes the contact lawful, so there is no battery (1). Consent is real if the patient was informed in broad terms of the nature of the procedure (Chatterton v Gerson, 1981) (1); failure to explain risks is a matter for negligence, and a competent adult may refuse treatment altogether (Re T, 1992) (1). **[3]**

**7.** The restraint must be complete, preventing movement in every direction (1). In Bird v Jones (1845) the claimant could go back the way he came, so there was no false imprisonment (1). **[2]**

**8.** Knowledge is not required (1). In Meering v Grahame-White Aviation (1920) a man unaware he was being watched to prevent him leaving was imprisoned (1), and in Murray v Ministry of Defence (1988) the House of Lords agreed, although damages may be nominal where the claimant was unaware and unharmed (1). **[3]**

**9.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- The first tackle is a direct, intentional contact (1), but players impliedly consent to contact within the rules and spirit of the game (1), so there is no battery for the broken ankle (1).
- The kick after play has stopped is outside the rules and spirit of the game, so consent does not cover it (1); it is a battery (1).
- Liam's push is a direct application of force to Kofi (1), but Liam may rely on defence of another, since reasonable force to protect Sean is justified (1).
- Kofi's punch is a battery against Liam (1).
- Kofi may claim self-defence (1), but he must honestly and reasonably believe force was necessary (Ashley) (1), and a punch breaking the nose in response to a push is disproportionate (Lane v Holloway) (1).
- Conclusion: Kofi is liable to Sean for the kick and to Liam for the punch (1). **[12]**

**10.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- False imprisonment is the unlawful total restraint of liberty (1).
- Telling Bea she must stay, backed by staff authority, can amount to restraint without physical force (1).
- The open flap: a means of escape defeats total restraint only if it is reasonable and known (1); Bea did not notice it, and leaving that way would breach the instruction to stay (1).
- Lack of knowledge of escape does not prevent the restraint being total, and knowledge of the restraint itself is not required (Meering) (1).
- Reasonable conditions on leaving the site: requiring use of the exit gate is reasonable (Robinson v Balmain) (1), but it does not justify detaining her in the tent (1).
- Lawful restraint: the staff's mistaken belief does not give lawful authority (1); a citizen's arrest requires strict conditions which detention for a ticket check does not meet (1).
- Conclusion: Bea is likely to succeed and may recover damages for two hours' detention (1). **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Battery requires direct, intentional force without consent (1).
- Knee operation: Gemma was informed in broad terms of its nature, so her consent is real (Chatterton v Gerson) (1); failure to mention the risk of nerve damage is not battery (1) but may be negligence (1).
- Mole removal: Gemma did not consent to this procedure (1), so it is a battery even if well-meant (F v West Berkshire HA; hostility not needed) (1).
- Necessity does not apply because there was no emergency and Gemma could have been asked later (1).
- Conclusion: battery for the mole removal only (1). **[8]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- Knowledge (up to 6): actionable per se; elements of assault (Stephens v Myers; Thomas v NUM; Tuberville v Savage), battery (Collins v Wilcock; Wilson v Pringle; F v West Berkshire), false imprisonment (Bird v Jones; Meering; Murray; Evans; Austin); defences.
- Analysis (up to 3): explain how actionable per se and the absence of a hostility requirement protect the right itself.
- Evaluation (up to 6): for the statement, no damage need be proved, unknowing detention is actionable, and good faith is no defence (Evans). Against, the total-restraint rule leaves partial restrictions unremedied (Bird v Jones); damages for unaware claimants may be nominal (Murray); Austin allowed long containment for public order; immediacy can leave threatening behaviour outside assault. A reasoned conclusion. **[15]**
