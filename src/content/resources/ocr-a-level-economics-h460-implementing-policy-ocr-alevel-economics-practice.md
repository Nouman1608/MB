## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following describes a regressive tax? **[1]**

A: A tax whose average rate rises as income rises

B: A tax whose average rate falls as income rises

C: A tax charged at the same percentage on all incomes

D: A tax on income rather than on spending

**2.** Which **one** of the following is an automatic stabiliser? **[1]**

A: A one-off increase in road-building announced in a budget

B: A cut in the central bank's interest rate

C: Unemployment benefit payments that rise as unemployment rises

D: Privatisation of a state-owned railway

**3.** Under quantitative easing, the central bank: **[1]**

A: Raises the reserve requirement for commercial banks

B: Creates money to buy financial assets such as government bonds

C: Sells government bonds to reduce the money supply

D: Cuts government spending to reduce borrowing

**4.** An invented income tax charges 0% on the first 15,000 cu, 25% on income from 15,001 to 60,000 cu and 45% on income above 60,000 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the tax paid and the average tax rate on an income of 75,000 cu. **[3]**

**(b)** State the marginal tax rate at this income. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain whether this tax is progressive. **[2]**

**5.** Country Corra's budget deficit is 7% of GDP. Its output gap is −4%, and each 1% of negative output gap adds 0.5% of GDP to the deficit.

**(a)** Calculate the cyclical and structural deficits. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the distinction matters for the government. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**6.** Distinguish between discretionary fiscal policy and automatic stabilisers, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**7.** With the aid of an AD/AS diagram, explain how a cut in the central bank's interest rate is likely to affect real output and the price level. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how quantitative easing is intended to increase aggregate demand. **[4]**

**9.** Using the Laffer curve, explain why raising a tax rate might reduce tax revenue. **[3]**

**10.** With the aid of a diagram, explain **two** supply-side policies a government could use to raise labour productivity. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate whether fiscal policy or monetary policy is more effective in reducing unemployment during a recession. **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that supply-side policies are the most effective way to achieve the government's macroeconomic objectives. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. A is progressive; C is proportional; D defines a direct tax. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. It changes with the cycle without a new decision. A is discretionary fiscal policy; B is monetary policy; D is supply-side policy. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. Option C is the reverse process. **[1]**

**4. (a)** Tax = 0.25 × 45,000 + 0.45 × 15,000 [1] = 11,250 + 6,750 = **18,000 cu** [1]. Average rate = 18,000 ÷ 75,000 × 100 = **24%** [1].

**(b)** Marginal rate = **45%** [1].

**(c)** The tax is progressive [1] because the average rate rises with income: for example, on 30,000 cu tax is 3,750, an average rate of 12.5%, below the 24% paid on 75,000 cu [1]. **[6]**

**5. (a)** Cyclical = 4 × 0.5 = **2% of GDP** [1]. Structural = 7 − 2 = **5% of GDP** [1].

**(b)** The cyclical part will disappear as the economy recovers, through automatic stabilisers [1]. The structural part will remain even at trend output, so it requires discretionary tax rises or spending cuts if the government wants to reduce borrowing [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Discretionary fiscal policy is a deliberate decision to change tax rates or government spending [1], for example raising capital spending on hospitals in a budget [1]. Automatic stabilisers are taxes and benefits that change with the economic cycle without any new decision [1], for example income tax revenue falling and benefit payments rising in a recession [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Lower interest rates make borrowing cheaper and saving less attractive, so consumption and investment rise [1]. The currency may also depreciate, raising net exports [1]. AD shifts to the right on a diagram with the price level and real output on the axes [1]. Real output rises and the price level rises; the split depends on spare capacity, with output rising more if there is a large negative output gap [1]. **[4]**

**8.** The central bank creates new money electronically and buys government bonds and other assets from financial institutions [1]. Higher demand for bonds raises their prices and lowers their yields, reducing long-term interest rates [1]. Sellers of bonds now hold more money, which they may lend or invest in other assets, raising asset prices and wealth [1]. Cheaper borrowing and higher wealth encourage consumption and investment, raising AD [1]. **[4]**

**9.** At low tax rates, raising the rate increases revenue because the tax base changes little [1]. Beyond some rate, the higher rate reduces the incentive to work, invest or declare income, so the tax base shrinks [1]. Revenue then falls, because the fall in the base outweighs the higher rate on each unit taxed [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Policy 1: investment in education and training [1] raises workers' skills, so each worker produces more per hour [1]. Policy 2: subsidies for research and development [1] encourage firms to develop new technology and methods that raise output per worker [1]. Diagram: LRAS shifts to the right with the price level and real output on the axes [1]; real output rises and, with AD unchanged, the price level falls [1]. Other valid policies: infrastructure investment; competition policy. **[6]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- In a recession, unemployment is mainly cyclical, caused by weak AD [1].
- Fiscal policy: higher spending or tax cuts raise AD directly, magnified by the multiplier [1].
- Fiscal policy can target regions or groups with high unemployment [1].
- But fiscal policy faces decision and implementation lags, and adds to government debt [1].
- Crowding out is limited in a recession because there is spare capacity [1].
- Monetary policy: interest rates can be cut quickly by the central bank [1].
- But in a recession confidence may be so low that households and firms do not borrow, however cheap credit is [1].
- If interest rates are already near zero, there is little room to cut; QE may help but its effect is uncertain [1].
- The two can be combined: monetary loosening keeps borrowing costs low while fiscal expansion adds demand [1].
- Judgement: in a deep recession with low confidence, fiscal policy is likely to be more effective, with a reasoned conclusion naming the deciding factor [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Supply-side policies aim to raise productive capacity, shifting LRAS right [1].
- They allow growth without inflation, easing the conflict between output and prices [1].
- They can improve competitiveness and so the current account [1].
- They can reduce structural unemployment through training and labour mobility [1].
- But they are slow, often taking years to have an effect [1].
- Their effect is uncertain and hard to measure [1].
- Interventionist measures cost public money; some market-based measures widen inequality [1].
- They cannot deal quickly with cyclical unemployment in a recession, which needs demand-side policy [1].
- The best approach depends on the cause of the problem: demand-side for cyclical, supply-side for structural [1].
- Judgement: supply-side policy is essential for long-run objectives but not sufficient on its own, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong conclusion separates short-run stabilisation from long-run capacity, rather than declaring one policy best in all cases.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing average and marginal tax rates.
- Treating the whole deficit as structural.
- Describing QE without the bond-buying and long-term interest rate mechanism.
- Shifting the wrong curve on the diagram for supply-side policy.
- Ignoring time lags and confidence when evaluating monetary policy.
- Concluding without naming what the verdict depends on.
