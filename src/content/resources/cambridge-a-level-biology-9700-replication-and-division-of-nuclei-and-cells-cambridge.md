## 5.1.1 Chromosome structure

The syllabus limits the description to five features.

| Feature | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **DNA** | One very long molecule per chromatid | Carries the genes |
| **Histone proteins** | Proteins around which DNA is wound | Package the DNA; allow tight coiling (condensation) before division, so chromosomes can be moved without tangling or breaking |
| **Sister chromatids** | The two identical copies present after S phase | One goes to each daughter nucleus |
| **Centromere** | The region joining sister chromatids | Where spindle microtubules attach |
| **Telomeres** | Repeated non-coding sequences at each end | Protect genes near the ends during replication |

Between divisions, DNA and histones are spread out through the nucleus as **chromatin**, which allows genes to be transcribed. Before mitosis, the chromatin coils tightly so that individual chromosomes become visible with a light microscope.

### Counting through one cycle

The words chromosome, chromatid and DNA molecule are easily confused. A useful rule: **count chromosomes by centromeres; count DNA molecules by chromatids.**

**Worked example 1: a human body cell (46 chromosomes).**

| Stage | Chromosomes | Chromatids per chromosome | DNA molecules in the cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | 46 | 1 | 46 |
| After S (G2, prophase, metaphase) | 46 | 2 | 92 |
| Anaphase (after centromeres divide) | 92, moving as two sets of 46 | 1 | 92 |
| Each daughter cell after cytokinesis | 46 | 1 | 46 |

The number of chromosomes per cell is the same in the parent and each daughter. S phase doubles the DNA without changing the chromosome number, and anaphase separates the copies.

## 5.1.2 Why genetic identity matters

Mitosis produces daughter nuclei with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent, because each chromosome is copied exactly in S phase and one copy of each goes to each nucleus. Identical cells carry the same genes and so can do the same job as the cells they join or replace.

| Purpose | Example | Why identical cells are needed |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Cells in a shoot tip dividing as a plant grows taller | Every cell in the organism needs the full, correct set of genes |
| Replacement | Cells lining the small intestine, which are constantly worn away | New cells must function exactly like the lost ones |
| Repair | New bone cells forming at a fracture | Repaired tissue must have the same properties as the original |
| Asexual reproduction | *Hydra* producing a bud that becomes a new individual | Offspring well adapted to a stable environment keep the same successful genes |

## 5.1.3 The cell cycle

| Phase | Main events |
|---|---|
| **G1** | Cell grows; makes proteins, including enzymes needed for DNA replication; organelles increase in number |
| **S** | DNA replicates; each chromosome becomes two sister chromatids |
| **G2** | Cell continues to grow; makes proteins needed for division, such as those forming spindle microtubules; energy stores increase |
| **Mitosis** | Nucleus divides into two genetically identical nuclei |
| **Cytokinesis** | Cytoplasm divides, forming two cells |

Interphase (G1, S and G2) is usually the longest part. It is a period of intense activity, not rest.

### Worked example 2: DNA content in a population of cells

A sample of 2 000 dividing cells is analysed for DNA content (invented data). G1 cells have 1 unit of DNA. G2 and mitotic cells have 2 units. Cells part-way through S have an amount between the two.

| DNA content | Number of cells | Phase(s) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 unit | 1 200 | G1 |
| Between 1 and 2 units | 300 | S |
| 2 units | 500 | G2 and mitosis |

If cells are dividing at random times, the proportion of cells in a phase equals the proportion of the cycle spent in it. The cycle lasts 20 hours.

```
G1: 1 200 / 2 000 = 0.60 -> 0.60 x 20 = 12 h
S:    300 / 2 000 = 0.15 -> 0.15 x 20 = 3 h
G2 + mitosis: 500 / 2 000 = 0.25 -> 0.25 x 20 = 5 h
```

Cells in G2 and mitosis cannot be separated by DNA content alone, because both have doubled DNA; looking at chromosomes under a microscope would separate them.

## 5.1.4 Telomeres

DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to the 3′ end of a strand, so the very end of each new strand cannot be completely copied. A short length is lost from the end of every DNA molecule in every round of replication.

Telomeres are long sequences of repeated bases at the ends of each chromosome that do not code for polypeptides. The DNA lost in each round comes from the telomere, not from a gene. Telomeres therefore **prevent the loss of genes** from the ends of chromosomes. Each division shortens the telomeres of most body cells a little.

## 5.1.5 Stem cells

A **stem cell** is an unspecialised cell that can divide by mitosis repeatedly and whose daughter cells can **differentiate** into specialised cells. When a stem cell divides, one daughter can remain a stem cell while the other differentiates, so the stem cell population is maintained while new specialised cells are produced.

- **Cell replacement:** stem cells in bone marrow divide continually to replace red blood cells, which have no nucleus and so cannot divide themselves.
- **Tissue repair:** stem cells in the lining of the small intestine divide to replace cells lost from the surface; after a cut, stem cells in the skin divide to fill the wound.

## 5.1.6 How a tumour forms

The cell cycle is controlled by genes that code for proteins which start or stop division. A **mutation** in such a gene can mean a cell divides when it should not, or ignores signals that would normally stop it dividing.

1. A mutation occurs in a gene controlling the cell cycle.
2. The cell divides by mitosis repeatedly, without control.
3. Every daughter cell inherits the mutation, so they also divide without control.
4. A mass of abnormal cells forms: a **tumour**.

Further mutations may allow tumour cells to invade surrounding tissues, which makes the tumour a cancer. Factors that increase the rate of mutation, such as ultraviolet radiation, ionising radiation and some chemicals, increase the risk.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Explain how uncontrolled cell division can result in the formation of a tumour. **[4]**

> A mutation occurs in a gene that controls the cell cycle. *(Names the starting point.)*
>
> The cell no longer responds to signals that normally stop division, so it divides by mitosis repeatedly. *(Links mutation to uncontrolled division.)*
>
> Because mitosis produces genetically identical cells, every daughter cell carries the mutation and also divides without control. *(Uses the key property of mitosis.)*
>
> The result is a mass of cells, called a tumour. *(Completes the chain.)*

## Exam technique

- **Describe** chromosome structure using only the five named features, each with what it is.
- **Explain** the importance of mitosis by linking "genetically identical" to each purpose.
- **Outline** the cycle in order, naming what happens in G1, S and G2.
- **Calculate** time in a phase as proportion of cells × cycle length; state the assumption.

## Common mistakes

- Saying DNA replicates in G2 or during mitosis. It is S phase.
- Counting sister chromatids as two chromosomes before anaphase.
- Saying telomeres stop DNA from being lost. DNA is lost; telomeres stop genes being lost.
- Defining a stem cell only as "unspecialised" without its ability to divide and differentiate.
- Describing a tumour without the mutation or the inheritance of the mutation by daughter cells.
