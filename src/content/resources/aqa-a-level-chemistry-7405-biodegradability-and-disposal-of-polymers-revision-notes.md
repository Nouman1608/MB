These notes condense section **3.3.12.2 Biodegradability and disposal of polymers** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3. ## What you must be able to do

- State that **polyalkenes** are chemically inert and **non-biodegradable**.
- State that **polyesters and polyamides** can be broken down by **hydrolysis** and are **biodegradable**.
- Give **advantages and disadvantages** of methods of disposal, including **recycling**.
- **Explain** why polyesters and polyamides can be hydrolysed but polyalkenes cannot.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Biodegradable | Can be broken down by living organisms, mainly microorganisms |
| Hydrolysis | Breaking a bond by reaction with water (or acid or alkali) |
| Inert | Chemically unreactive |
| Landfill | Burying waste in the ground |
| Incineration | Burning waste, often to release energy |
| Recycling | Reprocessing waste material into new products |
| Feedstock recycling | Chemically breaking polymers into small molecules for reuse |

## Why polyalkenes do not break down

- Chain of strong **C–C and C–H** bonds.
- Bonds are **non-polar**, so no δ+ atom for a nucleophile such as water to attack.
- Saturated, with no functional group.
- Enzymes of microorganisms cannot attack them.

## Why polyesters and polyamides break down

- Ester link –CO–O– and amide link –CO–NH– are **polar**.
- The carbonyl carbon is **δ+**.
- **Nucleophiles** (water, hydroxide) attack it, so the link breaks: **hydrolysis**.
- Acid, alkali or enzymes speed this up.

```
–CO–O–  + H₂O → –COOH + HO–
–CO–NH– + H₂O → –COOH + H₂N–
```

| Link | Acid hydrolysis | Alkaline hydrolysis |
|---|---|---|
| Ester | Dicarboxylic acid + diol | Dicarboxylate salt + diol |
| Amide | Dicarboxylic acid + diammonium salt | Dicarboxylate salt + diamine |

## One-sentence explanation to learn

"Polyesters and polyamides have polar links with a δ+ carbon that can be attacked by nucleophiles such as water, whereas polyalkenes have only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so there is nothing for a nucleophile to attack."

## Methods of disposal

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Landfill | Cheap, simple, little sorting | Uses land; polyalkenes persist; additives may leach; wastes raw material |
| Incineration | Reduces volume; energy can be used | CO₂ released; toxic gases such as HCl from PVC; scrubbers needed |
| Mechanical recycling | Saves crude oil; less waste; less energy than new | Needs sorting by type; lower quality; collection energy |
| Feedstock recycling | Handles mixed or dirty plastic; gives monomers | More energy needed; complex plant |
| Biodegradable polymers | Break down naturally | May be weaker or costlier; need the right conditions |

## Evaluating: a structure

1. State the polymer type and the bond types.
2. Link to biodegradability.
3. Give advantages and disadvantages of at least two methods.
4. Reach a justified conclusion.

## Worked examples to copy

**Example 1.** Explain why nylon 6,6 is biodegradable but poly(ethene) is not.

1. Nylon 6,6 has amide links, –CO–NH–, which are polar.
2. The carbon of C=O is δ+, so a nucleophile such as water can attack it and the link breaks (hydrolysis).
3. Poly(ethene) has only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so there is no site for attack.

**Example 2.** Name the products when a polyester made from ethane-1,2-diol and butanedioic acid is hydrolysed with dilute acid.

1. Acid hydrolysis breaks each ester link.
2. The –CO– end becomes –COOH: butanedioic acid.
3. The –O– end becomes –OH: ethane-1,2-diol.

## Quick calculations

- Complete hydrolysis of a polyester: n(diol) = n(diacid) = n(repeating units).
- Mass of HCl from burning PVC = (mass ÷ 62.5) × 36.5.
- Mass of CO₂ from burning poly(ethene) = (mass ÷ 14.0) × 44.0.

Example: 5.00 kg of PVC burns completely, all the Cl forming HCl. n(units) = 5000 ÷ 62.5 = 80.0 mol, so HCl = 80.0 × 36.5 = 2920 g.

## Exam traps

- "Polyalkenes are plastic" instead of "non-polar strong bonds, no δ+ site".
- Biodegradability explained by "natural" origin.
- Recycling described as simple.
- One-sided evaluations.
- Burning poly(ethene) said to give HCl.
- Ammonium salt omitted from amide hydrolysis in acid.
- Hydrolysis and condensation confused.
- Enzymes mentioned with no bond or nucleophile.

## Self-test

1. What does biodegradable mean?
2. Why are polyalkenes not biodegradable?
3. What is the charge on the carbonyl carbon in an ester link?
4. What type of reagent attacks that carbon?
5. Write the general equation for the hydrolysis of an ester link.
6. Name the products of acid hydrolysis of an ester link.
7. Give one advantage of landfill.
8. Give one disadvantage of burning PVC.
9. Give two advantages of recycling.
10. What is feedstock recycling?
11. Why does recycling require sorting?
12. How many moles of repeating units are in 960 g of poly(ethylene terephthalate) (Mᵣ of unit 192.0)?

### Answers

1. It can be broken down by living organisms such as microorganisms.
2. They have strong non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, with no δ+ site for attack.
3. Partial positive (δ+).
4. A nucleophile, such as water or hydroxide.
5. –CO–O– + H₂O → –COOH + HO–.
6. A carboxylic acid and an alcohol.
7. It is cheap and simple (needs little sorting).
8. It gives toxic hydrogen chloride (or CO₂, a greenhouse gas).
9. Saves crude oil; reduces waste to landfill; uses less energy than making new polymer.
10. Chemically breaking a polymer into small molecules or monomers that are reused.
11. Different polymers have different properties and cannot be reprocessed together.
12. 960 ÷ 192.0 = 5.00 mol.
