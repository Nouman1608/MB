These questions cover Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4, heading 8
*Contemporary issues* (sections 8.1, 8.2 and 8.3). Paper 4 essays are worth 35 marks and must
explore contrasting views or evidence; warm-up questions check the concepts first.

## Warm-up questions

**W1.** Define *net migration*. **[2]**

**W2.** Identify **two** push factors and **two** pull factors in migration. **[4]**

**W3.** Explain what is meant by *corporate crime*, with an example. **[3]**

**W4.** Name **two** transnational organisations involved in tackling global inequality. **[2]**

### Warm-up answers

- **W1.** The difference between the number of people entering a country (immigration) and the
  number leaving it (emigration) over a period. 2 marks for a precise definition.
- **W2.** Push: poverty, unemployment, conflict, persecution, natural disaster. Pull: jobs, higher
  wages, safety, family reunion, education. 1 mark each.
- **W3.** Crime committed by a company or by employees on its behalf, for profit — for example
  breaches of safety law causing disasters such as the Bhopal gas leak (1984), or pollution
  offences. 2 marks for meaning, 1 for example.
- **W4.** Any two: World Bank, International Monetary Fund, World Health Organization, UNICEF, an
  international NGO.

## Essay 1 (8.1)

**"Globalisation has improved life chances in developing countries." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**For:**
- Modernisation and neo-liberal views (Rostow, 1960): investment and trade raise incomes.
- Spread of mobile technology, medicines and vaccines; global goals (MDGs 2000–2015, SDGs
  2015–2030) increased primary school enrolment and reduced child mortality in many countries.
- Remittances from migrants support education and health spending.

**Against:**
- Dependency theory (Frank, 1966): integration on unequal terms keeps countries poor.
- Low-wage, unsafe export work (Rana Plaza, Bangladesh, 2013).
- Debt and loan conditions linked to cuts in public services.
- Brain drain of health workers; environmental damage from relocated industry.
- Feminist view: women's life chances lag because of patriarchy (Mies, 1986).

**Evaluation:** outcomes differ by country, class and gender; distinguish averages from
distribution; conclude on balance with reference to education, income and health separately.

## Essay 2 (8.1)

**"Transnational organisations have failed to reduce global inequality." Evaluate this view.**
**[35]**

### Indicative content

- **Failure arguments:** Moyo's *Dead Aid* (2009) — aid fuels corruption and dependency; Marxist
  critique of IMF and World Bank conditions; Western dominance of decision-making; inequality
  between and within countries remains high.
- **Success arguments:** Sachs (2005) — targeted aid breaks poverty traps; disease eradication and
  vaccination campaigns; disaster relief; NGO projects in water, sanitation and girls' education.
- **Evaluation:** distinguish between types of organisation; consider whether globalisation itself
  creates the inequality these organisations try to reduce; judge partial success.

## Essay 3 (8.2)

**"Migration benefits receiving countries more than migrants themselves." Evaluate this view.**
**[35]**

### Indicative content

**For the view:**
- Receiving economies gain workers for care, construction and technology, often trained at the
  sending country's expense.
- Marxist view: migrants form a reserve army of labour, accepting low pay and poor conditions.
- Irregular migrants face exploitation and lack legal rights.
- Global care chains (Hochschild): migrant women care for richer families at emotional cost to their
  own.

**Against the view:**
- Migrants usually earn far more than at home and send remittances that support families and
  communities.
- Some receiving societies experience concern over scarce resources and social tension, reducing
  their gains.
- Sending countries can gain from returning migrants' skills and investment.
- Castles and Miller (1993): outcomes depend on migration type (skilled, refugee, temporary).

**Evaluation:** judge by type of migrant, gender and legal status; avoid treating migrants as one
group.

## Essay 4 (8.3)

**"Globalisation has made crime harder to police." Evaluate this view.** **[35]**

### Indicative content

**For:**
- Crime crosses jurisdictions with different laws; extradition is slow.
- Encryption, the dark web and cryptocurrencies hide criminal activity.
- Corporations can relocate to weakly regulated states; corporate and green crime are rarely
  prosecuted.
- Castells (1998): networked global criminal economy; Glenny's *McMafia* (2008).

**Against:**
- International cooperation: Interpol, Europol, the International Criminal Court (2002), joint
  investigations.
- New technology helps police: data sharing, surveillance, financial tracking, digital forensics.
- International agreements such as the UN Palermo Protocol (2000) on trafficking create common
  definitions.

**Theory:** Marxists argue policing targets the powerless while powerful offenders escape;
feminists highlight under-protection of trafficked women.

**Evaluation:** policing capacity has grown, but so has the scale of crime; judgement may differ by
crime type.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should every essay use Marxist and feminist views?** For 8.3 the syllabus explicitly names them,
so yes; for 8.1 and 8.2 they are strong critical perspectives to set against optimistic views.

**How much description of a case study is enough?** Two or three sentences, followed by analysis of
what it shows about the question.

**Can I combine 7.1 perspectives with 8.1 content?** Yes — globalist, sceptic and
transformationalist views give useful framing for life-chances essays.
