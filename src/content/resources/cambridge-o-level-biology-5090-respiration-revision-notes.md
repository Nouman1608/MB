These notes condense subtopic **10.1 Respiration** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series: the definition of respiration, the uses of the energy it releases, and the investigation into the effect of temperature on respiration in yeast. The full explanations and the worked data are in the [10.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-respiration/). Then try the [10.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-respiration-practice/).

## Definition

**Respiration is the chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from glucose.**

Marking points: **chemical reactions**; **all living cells** (including plants, all the time); **release** energy (never "make"); from **glucose**.

## Respiration is not breathing

| | Respiration | Breathing |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Chemical reactions | Movement of air in and out of the lungs |
| Site | Cells | Lungs and thorax |
| Result | Energy released | Oxygen supplied; carbon dioxide removed |

## Uses of energy

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Muscle contraction | Running; heartbeat |
| Protein synthesis | Making enzymes |
| Cell division | Growth and repair of skin |
| Active transport | Ion uptake by root hair cells |
| Growth | Building new cells and tissue |
| Passage of electrical impulses along neurones | Sending nerve signals |
| Maintenance of a constant body temperature | Warm-blooded animals release heat |

## Yeast and temperature: the practical

1. Mix yeast with glucose solution; use the same amounts each time.
2. Place the tube in a water bath at a set temperature (for example 10 to 60 degrees Celsius).
3. Measure carbon dioxide produced: count bubbles per minute, collect the gas, or time the colour change of hydrogencarbonate indicator (red to yellow).
4. Repeat and calculate a mean.

| Variable | In this practical |
|---|---|
| Independent | Temperature |
| Dependent | Rate of carbon dioxide production |
| Control | Glucose concentration and volume, mass of yeast, time, same method |

**Typical result:** the rate rises with temperature to an optimum (about 40 degrees Celsius), then falls sharply, because the enzymes are denatured.

### Worked data

| Temperature (degrees Celsius) | Bubbles per minute |
|---|---|
| 10 | 6 |
| 20 | 14 |
| 30 | 29 |
| 40 | 44 |
| 50 | 8 |

- Percentage increase from 10 to 20: (14 - 6) divided by 6 x 100 = 133 per cent.
- From 20 to 30: 29 divided by 14 = about 2.1 times as great.

## Explaining the graph

| Part of graph | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Rising | Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy; more collisions between enzymes and substrate; faster reaction |
| Peak (optimum) | Fastest rate of enzyme activity |
| Falling | Enzymes denatured; active site shape changes; substrate no longer fits |

## Improving the investigation

- Use a mean of repeats.
- Use smaller temperature steps near the optimum.
- Use a thermostatic water bath.
- Measure gas volume rather than counting bubbles of different size.

## Where does the energy go?

- Some is used for the cell's work (the seven uses above).
- A large share is released as **heat**.
- In mammals and birds the heat helps to keep the body at a constant temperature.
- Heat from respiration warms compost heaps and germinating seeds in a closed container.

## Respiration in different organisms

| Organism | Note |
|---|---|
| Animals | Glucose comes from digested carbohydrate |
| Plants | Glucose comes from photosynthesis; plants respire day and night |
| Yeast | Single-celled fungus; releases carbon dioxide |
| Seeds | Respire quickly while germinating |

## Short model answers

**Describe respiration.** The chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from glucose.

**Explain why a person's breathing rate increases during exercise.** Muscle cells respire faster, using more oxygen and producing more carbon dioxide, so breathing increases to supply oxygen and remove carbon dioxide.

**Explain why respiration in yeast stops at 60 degrees Celsius.** The enzymes are denatured; their shape changes so substrates no longer fit the active sites.

**State two uses of energy in a plant.** Any two of: active transport, growth, cell division, protein synthesis.

## Respiration vocabulary check

| Word | Use it for |
|---|---|
| Release | Energy from glucose in respiration |
| Use | Energy in muscle contraction, active transport and so on |
| Breathing | Moving air in and out of the lungs |
| Denatured | Enzyme that has changed shape at high temperature |

## Exam traps

- Respiration is not breathing.
- Respiration **releases** energy; it does not make it.
- All living cells respire, including plants, all the time.
- Enzymes are **denatured**, not "killed".
- The yeast rate **falls** above the optimum; it is not a straight line.
- Temperature is the independent variable, not the measurement.
- Give the energy use, not "for energy".

## Self-test

1. Define respiration.
2. Why is "respiration is breathing" wrong?
3. State three uses of energy in a mammal.
4. Which use of energy involves ion uptake in roots?
5. Why does a mammal need energy to keep a constant body temperature?
6. What gas is produced when yeast respires?
7. Name two ways of measuring the rate of respiration in yeast.
8. State two variables to keep constant.
9. Describe the shape of the graph of rate against temperature.
10. Explain why the rate falls above the optimum temperature.
11. A yeast gives 14 bubbles per minute at 20 degrees Celsius and 29 at 30 degrees Celsius. What is the percentage increase?
12. Suggest one way to improve the reliability of the results.

### Answers

1. The chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from glucose.
2. Breathing is moving air in and out of the lungs; respiration is chemical reactions in cells.
3. Any three: muscle contraction; protein synthesis; cell division; active transport; growth; nerve impulses; constant body temperature.
4. Active transport.
5. Respiration releases heat that replaces heat lost, so the body stays warm.
6. Carbon dioxide.
7. Any two: counting bubbles; collecting and measuring gas volume; timing the colour change of hydrogencarbonate indicator.
8. Any two: volume and concentration of glucose; mass of yeast; time; the same method of measuring.
9. It rises to a peak and then falls sharply.
10. The enzymes are denatured, so their active sites no longer fit the substrate.
11. (29 - 14) divided by 14 x 100 = 107 per cent.
12. Repeat each temperature and use a mean (or use smaller steps and a thermostatic water bath).
