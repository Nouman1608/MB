This guide goes deeper into section **3.7.6 Analysing the external environment to assess opportunities and threats: social and technological** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **the impact of the social and technological environment on strategic and functional decision making**
- **social changes**, including demographic changes and population movements such as migration, changes in consumer lifestyle and buying behaviour, and the growth of online businesses
- **the social environment**, including Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR): the reasons for and against CSR, the difference between the stakeholder and shareholder concept, and Carroll's Corporate Social Responsibility pyramid
- **technological change**, including its impact on functional areas and strategy
- **the pressures for socially responsible behaviour**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## Social change

### Demographic change

**Demography** is the study of the size and structure of a population. Key trends include:

- **an ageing population**, with a larger share aged over 65 because people live longer and birth rates are low
- **changes in the birth rate**, which alters the number of children and, later, workers
- **changes in household size**, with more people living alone
- **ethnic and cultural mix**, which affects tastes, religious needs and language.

**Worked example.** A pharmacy chain serves a town of 60,000 people where the over-65 share rises from 18% to 22% over ten years.

- Over-65s: 60,000 × 18% = **10,800**, then 60,000 × 22% = **13,200**
- An increase of 2,400, which is **22%** more customers in this age group.

**Implications.** Higher demand for prescriptions, mobility aids and home delivery (marketing and operations); more staff with skills for older customers (human resources); and a labour supply problem, because fewer young people are available to recruit. The ageing trend is also a threat to a business selling mainly to teenagers.

### Population movements and migration

Migration changes the size and composition of local markets and the labour supply.

- **Opportunities:** a bigger labour pool, new skills, new customers, and demand for specialist goods such as international foods or money transfer services.
- **Threats:** labour shortages if migration falls; pressure on housing and local services; and, for some businesses, a need to adapt products and communications to different languages and cultures.
- Movement of people within a country, such as people leaving rural areas, can leave shops and services with falling demand.

### Changes in consumer lifestyle and buying behaviour

- **Health and wellbeing:** demand for low-sugar foods, gyms and fitness products.
- **Convenience:** ready meals, delivery services and click-and-collect.
- **Ethical and sustainable consumption:** fair-trade, recycled packaging, second-hand clothing.
- **Time pressure and flexible working:** home working changes demand for city-centre lunches and office space.
- **Customer expectations:** instant responses, personalised offers and reviews that shape reputation.

These shifts create opportunities for flexible, fast-moving businesses and threats for those tied to older habits.

### The growth of online businesses

Online shopping and services have changed how people find and buy products.

| Effect | Example |
|---|---|
| Wider market | A small producer sells nationally and abroad |
| Lower overheads | Fewer shops, but costs of warehouses, delivery and returns |
| More price comparison | Greater price competition |
| Data on customers | Targeted marketing |
| Pressure on high-street stores | Closures or conversion to showrooms and collection points |

For a traditional retailer, the strategic question is whether to compete online, merge channels or focus on the in-store experience.

## Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

**CSR** is the idea that a business has responsibilities beyond making a profit: to its employees, customers, communities and the environment.

### Reasons for CSR

- **Reputation and brand:** customers increasingly prefer businesses seen as ethical.
- **Recruitment and retention:** staff want to work for responsible employers.
- **Avoiding trouble:** responsible conduct reduces the risk of fines, boycotts and bad publicity.
- **Long-term profit:** good relationships with suppliers and communities keep the business secure.
- **Investor interest:** some investors favour companies with good environmental and social records.
- **Legal and regulatory pressure.**

### Reasons against CSR (or for limits)

- **Cost:** higher wages, greener materials and donations reduce short-term profit.
- **Shareholder duty:** managers are agents of the owners, and may be accused of spending shareholders' money on causes they have not chosen.
- **Competitive disadvantage:** rivals who ignore CSR may sell more cheaply.
- **Greenwashing risk:** a gap between claims and reality can damage reputation more.
- **Difficulty of measuring benefits.**

### Shareholder versus stakeholder concept

| | Shareholder concept | Stakeholder concept |
|---|---|---|
| Main duty | To maximise returns to shareholders | To balance the interests of all stakeholders: employees, customers, suppliers, community, owners |
| View of CSR | Acceptable only if it increases profit | A core responsibility |
| Typical measure | Profit, dividends, share price | Wider measures such as the Triple Bottom Line |
| Strength | Clear objective | Recognises that success depends on relationships |
| Weakness | Can encourage short-termism and ignore harm to others | Hard to balance conflicting interests; unclear objective |

### Carroll's CSR pyramid

Archie Carroll described four layers of responsibility, with the economic base supporting the rest:

1. **Economic:** be profitable, the foundation on which the rest depends.
2. **Legal:** obey the law.
3. **Ethical:** do what is right and fair, even where the law does not require it.
4. **Philanthropic:** be a good corporate citizen by contributing to society, for example through donations or volunteering.

**Using the pyramid.** It helps judge how far a business is going. A company that pays its staff only what the law requires meets the legal layer; one that pays a living wage beyond the minimum is acting ethically. Evaluation points: it can be criticised for putting economic responsibilities first, and the layers often overlap in practice.

### Pressures for socially responsible behaviour

Pressures come from **customers** (boycotts, buying choices), **employees**, **pressure groups** and **social media**, **investors**, **government and regulation**, and **competitors** who market their ethical credentials. The strength of the pressure depends on how visible the business is, how easily customers can switch, and how serious the issue is.

## Technological change

Technology affects every function and the strategy itself.

| Function | Impact |
|---|---|
| Operations | Automation, robotics and data analytics improve productivity and quality; lower unit costs; fewer workers needed; the need to maintain and upgrade equipment |
| Marketing | Online advertising, social media, personalisation, e-commerce; data from customers |
| Human resources | New skills needed; training; remote working; fewer routine jobs; recruitment through online platforms |
| Finance | Digital payments, faster accounting, real-time information; costs of investment |
| Strategy | New markets, new products, new competitors, new business models |

### Worked example: automation

Hartley Packaging buys a machine for £240,000 that replaces 6 workers, each costing £28,000 a year, and has running costs of £30,000 a year.

- Labour saved: 6 × £28,000 = **£168,000**
- Net annual saving: £168,000 − £30,000 = **£138,000**
- Payback: £240,000 ÷ £138,000 = **1.74 years**, about 21 months.

**Analysis.** The saving is large relative to the cost, so the investment appears attractive. But the figures leave out redundancy costs, retraining, the risk of breakdown, and the effect on morale. **Judgement.** Automation is likely to be a strong opportunity if demand is stable enough to keep the machine busy; the business should plan for retraining and consider redeploying staff.

### Strategic effects

- Technology can lower barriers to entry (a start-up can sell online) and so increase competition.
- It can create competitive advantage through speed, quality or low cost, but advantage is often short-lived because rivals copy.
- It can make existing assets obsolete.
- Rapid change makes forecasting difficult, so flexibility matters.

## Linking social and technological change

The two often work together. Social change creates demand, and technology makes it possible to meet it. Customers who want convenience (social) use apps and delivery services (technological). A business that sees only one half may misjudge the opportunity.

| Social change | Technological response | Decision for the business |
|---|---|---|
| Time-pressed households | Online ordering, apps, delivery tracking | Invest in e-commerce and logistics |
| Ageing population | Telehealth, voice assistants | Design simple, accessible products |
| Demand for ethical products | Supply chain tracking | Prove sourcing claims |
| Home and flexible working | Collaboration software | Change office space and HR policies |

## Evaluating the impact

Judge the impact by asking how **large** the change is for this business, how **fast** it is happening, whether it affects **demand** or **costs** or both, and whether the business has the **flexibility** to respond. A small business may adapt faster than a large one with fixed assets, while a large business may have the resources to invest.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** pick one change, say how it affects customers, costs or staff, and then how that affects a decision.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the size of the change and the business's ability to adapt. For CSR, use Carroll or the shareholder/stakeholder contrast to give structure.
- Do not describe technology in general terms. Name the function and the effect.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that CSR always raises profit, or always reduces it.
- Mixing up the shareholder and stakeholder concepts.
- Listing Carroll's four layers with no application.
- Treating technological change as only an opportunity, ignoring cost and disruption.
- Describing demographic change without linking it to this business's customers or workforce.
