## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by *cleaning of tanks at sea* as a cause of oil pollution. **[1]**

**2.** Explain why most crude oil spreads across the sea surface after a spill. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** ways in which pipelines can cause oil pollution. **[2]**

**4.** Describe what a sorbent is and how it is used. **[2]**

**5.** State **one** reason why regular maintenance of oil platforms reduces oil pollution. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe the impacts of an oil spill on **(a)** marine mammals and **(b)** crustaceans. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why an oil spill near a coral reef can affect many species, not only the coral. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how a fishing community may be affected by an oil spill, even if few people are directly exposed to the oil. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how **(a)** MARPOL and **(b)** risk assessments help to prevent oil pollution. **[4]**

**10.** The table shows the oil recovered after a 5,000-tonne spill.

| Method | Oil recovered (tonnes) |
|---|---|
| Skimmers | 1,150 |
| Sorbents | 350 |
| Controlled burning | 500 |

(a) Calculate the percentage of the spill that was recovered.
(b) Suggest **two** things that may have happened to the oil that was not recovered. **[4]**

**11.** An oil platform 20 km offshore leaks during calm weather. The slick is drifting towards a beach used by tourists. Describe and explain **two** strategies that could be used to minimise the impacts, and why they suit these conditions. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of detergent sprays and controlled burning for dealing with an oil spill. **[6]**

**13.** Explain why requiring all oil tankers to be double-hulled reduces, but does not remove, the risk of oil pollution from shipping. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Washing out a tanker's cargo tanks and pumping the oily wash water into the sea instead of keeping it for disposal at port [1].

**2.** Oil is less dense than water, so it floats [1]; it does not mix with water and is spread out by wind and currents [1].

**3.** Any two, 1 mark each: corrosion of the pipe allows leaks [1]; damage, for example by ship anchors or digging, breaks the pipe [1]. Also accept: poor maintenance, faulty valves or joints.

**4.** A material that soaks up or absorbs oil [1]; it is spread on the slick or along the shore and then collected with the oil [1].

**5.** Corrosion, worn parts or faulty valves are found and repaired before they fail and leak [1].

**6. (a)** Oil coats fur, so it loses its insulation and the animal may become cold [1]; mammals inhale toxic fumes when they surface to breathe, or are poisoned by eating contaminated prey [1]. **(b)** Crustaceans on the shore and sea bed are smothered by stranded or sinking oil [1]; they absorb toxic compounds and may die or become unsafe to eat [1].

**7.** Oil smothers coral and blocks light to the algae in the coral, so coral dies [1]; reefs provide food and shelter for a very large number of species [1]; when the coral dies, the organisms that depend on it lose habitat and food, so the whole reef food web is disrupted [1].

**8.** Fish and shellfish may be killed, or contaminated and unsafe to eat [1]; fishing areas may be closed, so catches and income fall [1]; buyers may avoid seafood from the area, reducing sales even after the clean-up [1].

**9. (a)** MARPOL is an international convention that limits the oil ships may discharge at sea [1] and requires oily waste to be kept on board and unloaded at port, with records that inspectors can check [1]. **(b)** A risk assessment identifies what could go wrong, how likely it is and how serious it would be [1], so precautions, such as safer shipping routes or emergency equipment, are put in place before work starts [1].

**10. (a)** Total = 1,150 + 350 + 500 = 2,000 tonnes [1]; 2,000 / 5,000 × 100 = 40% [1].
**(b)** Any two, 1 mark each: evaporated into the air [1]; washed ashore onto beaches and rocks [1]. Also accept: dispersed into the water as droplets, sank to the sea bed, carried away by currents.

**11.** Two strategies, 2 marks each. Booms placed around the slick or across the approach to the beach [1] contain or divert the oil, and they work well because the sea is calm [1]. Skimmers [1] can then lift the contained oil off the surface, which is effective in calm water [1]. Also accept: controlled burning of fresh, thick oil far from the beach, possible in calm conditions; sorbents along the shoreline.

**12.** Detergent sprays: benefit, break the slick into droplets so less oil reaches the shore and coats birds [1]; can be used when the sea is too rough for booms [1]; limitation, the detergents are toxic and the oil is spread into the water, harming fish and sea-bed life [1]. Controlled burning: benefit, removes a large amount of oil quickly from the surface [1]; limitation, produces smoke and carbon dioxide, causing air pollution, and only works on fresh, thick oil in calm conditions [1]. Judgement: neither removes all impacts; the choice depends on weather and how close sensitive habitats are [1].

**13.** If the outer hull is breached, the inner hull keeps the oil inside, so less oil escapes [1]; however, a severe collision or grounding can break both hulls [1]; double hulls also do nothing about other shipping sources such as deliberate tank cleaning at sea or fuel leaks [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "oil kills animals" without explaining insulation, buoyancy, smothering or poisoning.
- Treating MARPOL as a company or ship rather than an international convention.
- Choosing a strategy without using the weather or location details in the question.
- Adding the recovered amounts but forgetting to divide by the total spill.
- Listing benefits of both methods in a "discuss" question with no limitations or judgement.
