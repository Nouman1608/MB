## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by saying that labour is a derived demand. **[2]**

**2.** A packing firm pays 30 an hour. Each worker packs 12 boxes an hour and boxes sell at 3.

**(a)** Calculate the extra revenue from one worker for an hour. **[1]**
**(b)** State whether hiring is worthwhile. **[1]**
**(c)** The price falls to 2.40. Calculate the new figure and explain the effect on labour demand. **[2]**

**3.** A firm raises its hourly wage from 15 to 16.5 and cuts employment from 400 to 376.

**(a)** Calculate the elasticity of demand for labour. **[2]**
**(b)** Interpret your answer and calculate the effect on the wage bill. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two factors that would make the demand for labour at a car-wash firm more elastic. **[4]**

**5.** A bottling line can use workers or a machine. A worker costs 18 an hour and fills 9 crates; the machine costs 36 an hour and fills 16 crates.

**(a)** Calculate the labour cost and the machine cost per crate. **[2]**
**(b)** The wage rises to 21. Calculate the new labour cost per crate and state what the firm should do. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the wage at which the two methods cost the same. **[1]**

**6.** In Country A the income tax rate on a job paying 12 an hour for 35 hours a week falls from 25% to 20%.

**(a)** Calculate weekly take-home pay before and after the cut. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why labour supply may rise by less than the pay rise suggests. **[2]**

**7.** In a skilled trade the wage rises 8% and the number of workers rises from 250 to 255.

**(a)** Calculate the elasticity of supply of labour. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why supply is inelastic in this trade. **[4]**

**8.** In Town B, labour demand is QD = 700 - 20W and supply is QS = -100 + 30W, where W is the hourly wage.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium wage and employment. **[3]**
**(b)** Net migration raises supply by 50 workers at each wage. Calculate the new equilibrium. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the wage falls and employment rises. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how a trade union could change the supply of labour to an occupation and the wage. **[4]**

**10.** A public service has a pay budget of 2,400,000 and average pay of 32,000.

**(a)** Calculate the number of staff. **[1]**
**(b)** Pay rises 4% with the same budget. Calculate the number of staff that can be paid, as a whole number. **[2]**
**(c)** Inflation is 6%. Calculate the change in real pay to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain two considerations other than the budget in setting public sector pay. **[3]**

**11.** In a market for care workers (invented), demand is QD = 800 - 20W and supply is QS = -200 + 30W.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium, then the new equilibrium after demand rises by 120 at each wage. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the wage and in employment. **[2]**
**(c)** Net migration then raises supply by 90 at each wage. Calculate the new equilibrium and evaluate whether the wage rise will last. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Firms hire workers only to make goods or services [1]. So the demand for workers depends on demand for the product, for example fewer cars sold means fewer assembly workers hired [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An example that shows the link from product to worker makes the point; the phrase "derived" alone is just the term repeated.

**2. (a)** 12 x 3 = **36** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** 36 > 30, so **yes**, hire [1] **[1]**
**(c)** 12 x 2.40 = **28.80**, below 30 [1]. Revenue per worker is now less than the wage, so the firm hires fewer and demand shifts left [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the figure with the wage in words; a bare number leaves the decision unstated.

**3. (a)** Wage +10% [1]; employment -6%; elasticity = -6/10 = **-0.6** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Inelastic as the figure is below 1 in size [1]. Wage bill before 15 x 400 = 6,000 [1]; after 16.5 x 376 = 6,204, a **rise of 3.4%** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Find the two percentage changes separately before dividing, and state that the answer is inelastic as well as its value.

**4.** First factor identified [1] and explained [1]; second factor identified [1] and explained [1]. Options: easy substitution: if machines can replace workers, a wage rise makes firms switch, so employment falls by a larger percentage. Customers elastic: a wage rise lifts prices, customers go elsewhere and output and labour fall more. Labour a large share of costs: a wage rise raises total costs sharply. Longer time: more time to adjust technology **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the factor and then say why it makes employment fall by a larger percentage than the wage rises.

**5. (a)** Labour 18/9 = **2.00** [1]; machine 36/16 = **2.25** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** 21/9 = **2.33** [1], above 2.25, so the firm should **switch to the machine** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** 2.25 x 9 = **20.25** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Cost per unit, not total hourly cost, is the fair comparison because the outputs differ.

**6. (a)** Before 420 x 0.75 = **315** [1]; after 420 x 0.80 = **336** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Many workers cannot change hours, so extra pay does not raise their supply [1]. Higher pay can let some people work fewer hours for the same income [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the tax cut to take-home pay and then to the decision to work earns more than saying "more incentive".

**7. (a)** Workers +5/250 = +2% [1]; 2/8 = **0.25** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Long training: new workers cannot enter quickly [1][1]. Few spare workers: it is hard to attract people from other jobs without the skills [1][1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs its consequence for supply, so say what stops quantity rising.

**8. (a)** 700 - 20W = -100 + 30W [1]; 800 = 50W, so W = **16** [1]; Q = 700 - 320 = **380** [1] **[3]**
**(b)** New supply -50 + 30W [1]; 700 - 20W = -50 + 30W gives W = **15** [1]; Q = **400** [1] **[3]**
**(c)** More workers are willing to work at each wage, creating a surplus at 16 [1]; employers can fill jobs at lower pay, so the wage falls and more jobs are filled [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the new supply equation first; most slips come from changing the wrong coefficient.

**9.** A union may restrict entry through training or membership rules, so supply shifts left [1], and bargain for higher pay [1]. Fewer workers at each wage means a shortage at the old wage, so the wage rises [1] and employment falls [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A direction for the supply shift and a result for both wage and employment are needed; "unions raise wages" alone is thin.

**10. (a)** 2,400,000/32,000 = **75** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** 32,000 x 1.04 = 33,280 [1]; 2,400,000/33,280 = 72.1, so **72** staff [1] **[2]**
**(c)** 1.04/1.06 - 1 [1] = **-1.9%** [1] **[2]**
**(d)** Any two considerations, each identified [1][1]: recruiting and keeping staff against private sector pay; inflation and the cost of living; bargaining or national pay scales; public opinion of the service. One developed with a reason [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Round only at the end, and interpret the staff figure: 72.1 means 72 can be paid, not 73.

**11. (a)** Original: 800 - 20W = -200 + 30W gives W = **20**, Q = **400** [1]. New demand 920 - 20W [1]; 920 - 20W = -200 + 30W gives W = **22.4** [1], Q = **472** [1] **[4]**
**(b)** Wage (22.4 - 20)/20 = **+12%** [1]; employment 72/400 = **+18%** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** New supply -110 + 30W: 920 - 20W = -110 + 30W gives W = **20.6**, Q = **508** [1][1]. The wage rise shrinks from 12% to 3% [1]. Supply may grow further as people train, since supply is more elastic over time [1]. If pay is set by government, the wage may not follow the market [1]. Judgement: the wage rise probably will not last unless demand keeps rising faster than supply, so it depends on migration and training [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the final figure with the starting point and finish with a verdict that rests on a factor you name.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a wage change as a shift of the labour demand curve.
- Not linking labour demand to demand for the product.
- Dividing the wage change by the quantity change.
- Stating inelastic or elastic with no figure to support it.
- Comparing total costs when outputs differ, instead of cost per unit.
- Moving the wrong curve, or the wrong coefficient, in a supply shift.
- Rounding too early in the staff calculation.
- Giving a list of public sector pay factors with no explanation.
