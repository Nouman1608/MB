## Market orientation versus product orientation

### Spotting the orientation in a case

Case studies rarely state the orientation. Look for evidence of where decisions start.

| Evidence in the case | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Customer surveys, focus groups or sales data used before design | Market orientation |
| Product range changed after customer feedback | Market orientation |
| Founder or engineers decide features; sales team asked to "find buyers" | Product orientation |
| Pride in technical excellence; little research | Product orientation |
| Highly specialised or patented technology with few rivals | Product orientation may work |

### When each makes sense

Product orientation is not simply "wrong". It can succeed where:

* customers cannot describe a need for something they have never seen, so research gives weak guidance
* the business has a technical lead that rivals cannot easily copy
* quality or safety standards dominate buying decisions, as with some specialist equipment

Market orientation suits markets where tastes change quickly and rivals are many, such as food, fashion or consumer apps. Its cost is the research itself, and the risk of following customers so closely that the business never creates anything new. Most real businesses combine the two, and a balanced answer says which way the case business leans and whether that suits its market.

## Market share: value and volume

Market share can be measured by **value** (sales revenue) or by **volume** (units sold). The standard forms are:

```
Market share by value (%) = business's sales revenue / total market sales revenue x 100
Market share by volume (%) = business's units sold / total market units sold x 100
```

The two can differ a great deal, and the difference tells you about price.

### Worked example: Quillon Scooters

Quillon Scooters is an invented maker of electric scooters. All figures are invented and in dollars.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Market revenue | 250 million | 275 million |
| Market units | 500,000 | 520,000 |
| Quillon revenue | 30 million | 30.8 million |
| Quillon units | 75,000 | 77,000 |

**Shares in Year 1**

* By value: 30 ÷ 250 × 100 = **12.0%**.
* By volume: 75,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **15.0%**.

Quillon's share by volume is higher than its share by value, so its scooters are cheaper than the market average. The market's average price is 250 million ÷ 500,000 = 500; Quillon's is 30 million ÷ 75,000 = 400.

**Market growth from Year 1 to Year 2**

* By value: (275 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = **10.0%**.
* By volume: (520,000 − 500,000) ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **4.0%**.

Revenue in the market is growing faster than units, so customers are paying more per scooter on average (about 529 in Year 2).

**Shares in Year 2**

* By value: 30.8 ÷ 275 × 100 = **11.2%**, down 0.8 percentage points.
* By volume: 77,000 ÷ 520,000 × 100 = **14.8%** (to one decimal place), down about 0.2 percentage points.

**Interpretation.** Quillon sold more scooters, but its revenue grew only 2.7% while the market grew 10% by value, so its value share fell. The market is moving towards more expensive models, while Quillon's price has stayed at 400. A good comment links the numbers to a decision: Quillon must either add a higher-priced range or accept a narrower, budget position in a market that is moving upmarket.

## Reading share against growth

Share alone does not show strength. Read it with market growth.

| | Market growing | Market shrinking |
|---|---|---|
| **Share rising** | Strong: the business is outpacing a growing market | Mixed: winning a bigger slice of a smaller total |
| **Share falling** | Mixed: sales may still rise, but rivals are growing faster | Weak: losing ground in a declining market |

Quillon sits in the "share falling, market growing" box. Its sales rose, but rivals captured more of the growth. This is a warning sign rather than a crisis.

## HL only: the importance of market share and market leadership

The market leader is the business with the largest share. The guide asks HL candidates to judge why share and leadership matter, which means weighing benefits against costs and limits.

### Why share and leadership can matter

* **Lower unit costs.** High volume spreads fixed costs and gives buying power with suppliers.
* **Influence with retailers and distributors.** Shops give more and better shelf space to brands customers ask for.
* **Brand recognition and trust.** Buyers often see the leader as the safe choice, which can support price.
* **Price influence.** A leader may be able to set the reference price that rivals follow.
* **Attractiveness to investors and staff.** A leading position signals stability and success.

### Why share may matter less than it seems

* **Profit is not share.** Winning share through discounts or heavy promotion can reduce profit.
* **Market definition.** A business can be the leader of a narrowly defined segment but small in the wider market.
* **Declining markets.** Leadership of a shrinking market may be a weak position.
* **Customer taste.** In markets driven by individuality, such as craft products, buyers may avoid the biggest brand.
* **Regulation.** Very large shares can attract attention from competition authorities.

### Annotated HL judgement

*Question: Discuss the importance to Quillon Scooters of becoming market leader.*

> Leadership would give Quillon higher volumes, which would lower its unit costs and strengthen its bargaining power with battery suppliers and retailers. **[benefit applied]** However, Quillon currently competes on price, and the market is moving upmarket; chasing volume with low prices could win share by volume while value share and profit fall further. **[limit using case data]** Leadership by value would require higher-priced models, which needs investment and a stronger brand. **[what it would take]** On balance, profitable growth in a chosen segment matters more to Quillon than overall leadership. Leadership becomes more important if unit costs fall steeply with volume, for example if battery prices depend heavily on order size. **[judgement with condition]**

## Common mistakes

* Mixing value and volume in the same calculation.
* Saying "share fell by 0.8%" when it fell by 0.8 percentage points.
* Treating rising sales as proof of rising share.
* Treating market growth as the business's growth.
* Calling product orientation always wrong.
* Praising leadership without any cost or limit in an HL answer.

## Exam technique

* **Calculate**: state the formula, substitute and say whether you used value or volume.
* **Comment** or **analyse**: compare share with market growth and explain the cause.
* **Discuss** or **evaluate** (HL leadership): weigh benefits against limits in the case and judge, with a condition.
