Writing for impact is the second strand of 3.1.2 Writing in AQA GCSE English Language (8700). Where clear and coherent
writing is about control, writing for impact is about effect: making a reader feel, remember and agree. The
specification lists four parts: "selecting, organising and emphasising facts, ideas and key points; citing evidence
and quotation effectively and pertinently to support views; creating emotional impact; using language creatively,
imaginatively and persuasively, including rhetorical devices (such as rhetorical questions, antithesis,
parenthesis)". It is assessed by **AO5** with **AO6** in Section B of both papers. For recall, use the
[writing for impact revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-writing-for-impact-revision-notes/), and
to practise, use the [writing for impact practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-writing-for-impact-practice/).

## Selecting, organising and emphasising

Impact starts before you write a sentence: it depends on what you choose to include and where you put it.

- **Select:** three strong, distinct ideas beat six thin ones. Ask of each point, "Would my reader care about this?"
- **Organise:** order points for effect. A **climactic** order saves the most powerful point for last; a **strongest
  first** order wins attention immediately. Either is defensible if it is deliberate.
- **Emphasise:** position is emphasis. The end of a sentence, the end of a paragraph and a one-sentence paragraph all
  give weight. A short sentence after long ones lands hard. Repetition of a key word can make it the idea the reader
  takes away.

In narrative and description (Paper 1), the same principle applies to detail: choose the one image that carries the
mood, and place it where the reader will feel it most.

## Citing evidence and quotation pertinently

"Pertinently" means relevant and well-timed. Evidence persuades when it is specific and when the writer explains why
it matters.

| Kind of evidence | Example (original) | How to make it pertinent |
|---|---|---|
| Example | "the bus that stopped running last spring" | Concrete and local, so the reader recognises it |
| Anecdote | A short story of one person affected | Keep it brief, then draw the general point |
| Expert view | "Doctors have long warned that..." | Use it to support, not replace, your argument |
| Quotation | A well-known saying, or a phrase from the Paper 2 sources | Short, integrated, followed by your comment |

On Paper 2, ideas and details from the Section A sources can feed your argument in Section B, because the two
sections share a theme. Use them as springboards, rephrased in your own words, not as copied passages.

## Creating emotional impact

Emotional impact comes from making the abstract concrete. "Loneliness affects older people" informs; "Mrs Patel has not
spoken to anyone since Tuesday" makes the reader feel. Useful methods:

- a **specific, human detail** in place of a generalisation;
- **emotive vocabulary**, chosen precisely rather than piled up;
- **direct address**, making the reader part of the situation ("Imagine you...");
- a **shift in tone**, for example from humour to seriousness, which can make the serious moment hit harder;
- **restraint**: understatement can be more moving than exclamation marks.

Match the emotion to the task. An assembly speech can be passionate; a letter to a council officer is more convincing
when its feeling is controlled.

## Rhetorical devices

The specification names three devices. Know them precisely:

- **Rhetorical question:** a question asked for effect, where the answer is implied. *"Is a few minutes' convenience
  really worth a lifetime of litter?"* Use one or two at moments of challenge, not one per paragraph.
- **Antithesis:** contrasting ideas placed in a balanced structure. *"We built the roads for cars; we must now rebuild
  them for people."* The balance makes the contrast memorable and quotable.
- **Parenthesis:** an extra word, phrase or clause inserted into a sentence and marked off by brackets, dashes or
  commas. *"The council's plan, announced without any public meeting, will close the pool in June."* Parenthesis lets
  you add a pointed aside, a qualification or a note of irony without breaking the sentence.

Other devices often used for impact:

| Device | Example (original) |
|---|---|
| Tricolon (rule of three) | "It is cheaper, cleaner and kinder." |
| Anaphora | "We need trees. We need shade. We need somewhere to breathe." |
| Hyperbole | "The queue stretched halfway to the coast." |
| Irony | "What a triumph of planning: a cycle lane that ends in a wall." |
| Imperative | "Picture it. Now picture it gone." |
| Inclusive pronouns | "Together, we can keep it open." |

## Using language creatively and imaginatively

For Paper 1, impact comes from imaginative language and controlled structure: an extended metaphor that develops
across a description, a motif that returns at the end, sentence variety that matches pace to action. Originality
matters more than quantity: one fresh image ("the sky was the colour of a bruise healing") is worth more than three
familiar ones.

## Worked example: from plain to powerful

**Task (original):** write the opening of an article arguing that the town's library should stay open.

**Plain draft:**

> The library should stay open. It is useful for lots of people. Closing it would be bad. People need it.

**Revised opening (annotated):**

> Every weekday at nine, a queue forms outside Hartley Road Library **[concrete detail, emotional impact]**. It is not
> a queue for bargains or concert tickets; it is a queue for warmth, for help with a job application, for a quiet
> table to do homework **[antithesis, tricolon]**. The council calls the library "non-essential" **[pertinent
> quotation of the opposing view]**. Ask the pensioner who reads the paper there because she cannot afford her
> own. Ask the boy whose home has no desk **[anaphora, specific people]**. The building, which has
> stood for a hundred years, could be gone by spring **[parenthesis adds weight; end focus on loss]**. Is that really a
> saving worth making? **[a single, well-placed rhetorical question]**

The revision selects a striking opening image, emphasises loss by placing it last, uses each named device once and
for a reason, and keeps the register suitable for a public article.

## Common mistakes

- **Device-stuffing:** a rhetorical question in every paragraph loses force.
- **Melodrama:** exaggerated emotion that the topic or audience does not support.
- **Vague evidence:** "studies show" with no detail and no explanation of why it matters.
- **Hyperbole that undermines credibility** in a formal letter.
- **Misusing parenthesis**, for example opening a dash and never closing it, which damages AO6 too.
- **Saving nothing for the ending**, so the piece fades instead of landing.

## Exam technique

- In the plan, mark where your **strongest point** and your **most emotional moment** will go.
- Choose **two or three devices** in advance and decide where each will do most work.
- End with a deliberate final line: an echo of the opening, a call to action or a resonant image.
- When checking, read your first and last sentences aloud in your head: they carry the most impact.
