## Set 1

**1(a)** Explain **two** ways in which women's political rights in 1914 were different from their political
rights in 1928. **[6]**

**1(b)** Explain **two** causes of Commonwealth immigration to Britain in the years 1948–62. **[8]**

**1(c)** "Protest and pressure groups were the main reason for improvements in racial equality in Britain in
the years 1948–2010." How far do you agree? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- the Bristol Bus Boycott (1963)
- the Race Relations Act (1965)

You **must** also use information of your own.

## Set 2

**2(a)** Explain **two** ways in which opportunities for disabled people in 1914 were different from their
opportunities in 1970. **[6]**

**2(b)** Explain **two** causes of the Equal Pay Act (1970). **[8]**

**2(c)** "The two world wars did more to change opportunities for women than any other factor in the years
1914–75." How far do you agree? **[16]**

You may use the following in your answer:
- women's war work
- the Dagenham strike (1968)

You **must** also use information of your own.

## Set 3 — extra practice

**3(a)** Explain **two** ways in which the legal position of gay men in 1957 was different from their
position in 2010. **[6]**

**3(b)** Explain **two** causes of the Equality Act (2010). **[8]**

## Answers and mark guidance

### 1(a) [6]

- **Voting:** in 1914 no woman could vote in parliamentary elections; by 1928 women voted on the same terms as
  men from age 21.
- **Office and professions:** in 1914 women could not be MPs; from 1918 they could stand for Parliament (Nancy
  Astor took her seat in 1919), and the 1919 Act opened professions and juries to them.

### 1(b) [8]

- **Labour shortages after the war:** Britain needed workers for transport, the new NHS and industry, and
  organisations such as London Transport recruited in the Caribbean.
- **Citizenship and conditions at home:** the British Nationality Act (1948) confirmed the right of
  Commonwealth citizens to settle, while unemployment and poverty in the Caribbean and South Asia — and
  partition in 1947 — encouraged migration.

### 1(c) [16]

**Protest:** Claudia Jones and the *West Indian Gazette*; the Bristol Bus Boycott (1963); campaigns that led to
the Race Relations Acts; the Stephen Lawrence campaign and the Macpherson Report (1999); diversity in culture,
such as the Notting Hill Carnival.
**Government:** Race Relations Acts (1965, 1968, 1976); Equality Act (2010) — but also restrictions (1962, 1971).
**Other factors:** the courts (Constantine's case), changing attitudes in a more diverse society, education.
**Judgement:** protest exposed injustice and pushed governments; legislation made change enforceable. Strong
answers show the link.

### 2(a) [6]

- **Support:** in 1914 disabled people relied mainly on families and charities; by 1970 the state required
  councils to provide services (Chronically Sick and Disabled Persons Act).
- **Work and sport:** in 1914 there was no employment protection; by 1970 the 1944 quota scheme existed and the
  Paralympic movement, begun by Guttmann in 1948, had raised the profile of disabled athletes.

### 2(b) [8]

- **The Dagenham strike (1968):** women machinists at Ford stopped production, winning public attention and
  meeting Barbara Castle, then Employment Secretary.
- **Wider pressure:** growing feminist campaigns, trade union support and the UK's interest in joining the
  European Economic Community, whose treaty required equal pay.

### 2(c) [16]

**Wars:** First World War war work and the 1918 vote; Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act; the Second World War's
conscription of women, wider roles in industry and the services.
**Other factors:** suffrage campaigns before 1914; education (1944); feminist campaigns and the Dagenham strike;
laws of 1970 and 1975; changes in family life and employment. Limits: women were pushed out of jobs after both
wars.
**Judgement:** the wars opened doors but often temporarily; lasting change came from campaigns and laws in the
1960s and 1970s.

### 3(a) [6]

- In 1957 sex between men was a criminal offence; by 2010 it was legal and the age of consent equal.
- In 1957 there was no legal protection from discrimination; by 2010 the Equality Act protected sexual
  orientation (and civil partnerships existed from 2005).

### 3(b) [8]

- **Complex earlier law:** many separate Acts (1970, 1975, 1976, 1995 and others) made rights hard to enforce, so
  the government wanted one framework.
- **Campaigns and evidence of continuing inequality:** pressure groups for women, ethnic minorities, disabled
  people and LGBT people, and reports such as Macpherson, showed discrimination persisted.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying all women gained the vote in 1918.
- Writing about one group when the question asks about change across the period.
- Ignoring laws that restricted rights.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is the Paper 2 breadth study question structured?**
Part (a) asks you to explain two ways something was different at two dates (6 marks), part (b) to explain two causes (8 marks) and part (c) is a 16-mark essay on change, continuity or significance across the period.

**Did all women get the vote in 1918?**
No. In 1918 only women over 30 who met a property qualification could vote; women voted on the same terms as men, from 21, only from 1928.

**What is the most common mistake on these questions?**
Writing about one group or one date when the question asks about change across the period.
