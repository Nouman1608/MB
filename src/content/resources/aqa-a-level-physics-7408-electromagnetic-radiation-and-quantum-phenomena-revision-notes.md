## Equations

| Equation | Use | Units |
|---|---|---|
| E = hf = hc/λ | Photon energy | J |
| hf = ϕ + Ek(max) | Photoelectric equation | J or eV (be consistent) |
| f₀ = ϕ/h | Threshold frequency | Hz |
| eVs = Ek(max) | Stopping potential | V |
| hf = E₁ − E₂ | Photon from a level transition | J |
| λ = h/mv | de Broglie wavelength | m |
| 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J | Energy conversion | — |

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Photon | A quantum of electromagnetic radiation, energy hf |
| Threshold frequency f₀ | Minimum frequency that releases electrons from a given metal |
| Work function ϕ | Minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the metal surface |
| Stopping potential Vs | The pd that just stops the fastest photoelectrons |
| Electronvolt | Energy transferred when an electron moves through a pd of 1 V |
| Excitation | An atomic electron moves to a higher energy level |
| Ionisation | An atomic electron is removed from the atom |
| Ground state | The lowest energy level of an atom |
| Line spectrum | Discrete wavelengths emitted or absorbed by an element |
| de Broglie wavelength | The wavelength of a moving particle, h/mv |

## Photoelectric effect: what the wave model cannot explain

| Observation | Photon explanation |
|---|---|
| No emission below f₀, however intense | One photon frees one electron; below f₀, hf < ϕ |
| Ek(max) depends on f, not intensity | Photon energy depends only on f |
| More intense light gives more electrons | More photons per second |
| Emission is instantaneous | Each photon transfers its energy at once |

Graph of Ek(max) against f: straight line, gradient h, f-intercept f₀, extrapolated energy-axis intercept −ϕ. Graph of Vs against f: gradient h/e.

**Method for a photoelectric calculation**
1. Find the photon energy: E = hc/λ (in J).
2. Convert ϕ to J if it is given in eV (or convert E to eV).
3. Ek(max) = E − ϕ. A negative result means no emission.
4. Vs = Ek(max)/e.

Worked reminder: 220 nm light on a metal with ϕ = 4.10 eV. E = 9.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 5.65 eV, so Ek(max) = **1.55 eV** and Vs = **1.55 V**.

## Collisions and excitation

- An electron with kinetic energy K can excite an atom if K ≥ the level gap; it keeps K − gap.
- A photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals a level gap (or exceeds the ionisation energy).
- Hydrogen levels: −13.6, −3.40, −1.51, −0.85 eV. Ionisation from the ground state: 13.6 eV.

**Fluorescent tube in order**: high pd accelerates electrons → collisions excite mercury atoms → de-excitation emits UV → phosphor absorbs UV → phosphor de-excites in smaller steps → visible light.

## Energy levels

- Number of possible lines from level n down to the ground state: n(n − 1)/2. From n = 4: 6 lines.
- Larger gap → higher f → shorter λ.
- Line spectra are the evidence that energy levels are discrete.

Worked reminder: hydrogen 4 → 2: ΔE = 2.55 eV = 4.08 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so λ = hc/ΔE = **488 nm**.

## Wave-particle duality

| Evidence | Shows |
|---|---|
| Photoelectric effect | Light behaves as particles (photons) |
| Electron diffraction through graphite | Electrons behave as waves |

- λ = h/mv: greater momentum → shorter wavelength → less diffraction → smaller rings.
- For an electron accelerated through pd V: Ek = eV, mv = √(2meV), so λ = h/√(2meV) and λ ∝ 1/√V.
- Diffraction is noticeable when λ is comparable to the atomic spacing, about 10⁻¹⁰ m.

## Exam traps

- "Higher intensity gives faster electrons": it gives more electrons, not faster ones.
- Using ϕ in eV with E in J in the same subtraction.
- Writing "the electron absorbs part of the photon".
- Forgetting that Ek is a maximum: many electrons leave with less.
- Using a level value (−3.40 eV) instead of a level difference in hf = E₁ − E₂.
- Giving λ = h/mv with v found from Ek = ½mv² but the mass of the wrong particle.
- Stating that rings get bigger when the accelerating pd is increased.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Threshold frequency vs work function**: f₀ = ϕ/h; one is a frequency, the other an energy.
- **Excitation vs ionisation**: move up a level vs leave the atom.
- **Electron collision vs photon absorption**: part of the energy vs all or nothing.

## Quick self-test

1. Define work function.
2. Calculate the energy of a photon of wavelength 500 nm, in J and eV.
3. Light of photon energy 3.0 eV falls on a metal with ϕ = 2.2 eV. Find Ek(max) and Vs.
4. The metal in question 3 is lit by brighter light of the same frequency. What happens to Ek(max) and the number of photoelectrons per second?
5. Find the threshold frequency of a metal with ϕ = 3.6 eV.
6. What does the gradient of an Ek(max) against f graph represent?
7. Convert 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ J into eV.
8. An electron of kinetic energy 11.0 eV hits a hydrogen atom in its ground state. Which excitation can occur and what energy does the electron keep?
9. How many spectral lines can arise from hydrogen atoms excited to n = 3?
10. Find the wavelength of the photon from the hydrogen transition −0.85 eV to −1.51 eV.
11. Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving at 4.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹.
12. What happens to electron diffraction rings when the accelerating pd is increased?

### Answers

1. The **minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface** of the metal.
2. E = hc/λ = **3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 2.49 eV**.
3. Ek(max) = **0.8 eV**; Vs = **0.8 V**.
4. Ek(max) is **unchanged**; the number per second **increases**.
5. f₀ = (3.6 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) = **8.7 × 10¹⁴ Hz**.
6. The **Planck constant h**.
7. **4.0 eV**.
8. To n = 2 (needs 10.2 eV); it keeps **0.8 eV**. It cannot reach n = 3 (12.09 eV).
9. **3** (3→2, 3→1, 2→1).
10. ΔE = 0.66 eV = 1.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so λ = **1.88 × 10⁻⁶ m** (infrared).
11. λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 4.0 × 10⁶) = **1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ m**.
12. The electrons' momentum rises, λ falls, so the rings get **smaller**.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Ek(max) a maximum rather than the kinetic energy of every photoelectron?**
The work function is the energy needed for electrons at the surface. Electrons from deeper in the metal lose extra energy on the way out, so they leave with less than the maximum.

**Do I need to know how stopping potential is measured?**
No. The specification states that the experimental determination of stopping potential is not required, but you must use eVs = Ek(max).
