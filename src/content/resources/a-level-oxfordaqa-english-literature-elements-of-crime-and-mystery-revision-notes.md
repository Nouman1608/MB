Unit 3 (Elements of crime and mystery) of OxfordAQA International A-level English Literature (9675) is a
**two-hour, closed-book exam** worth 50 marks and 30% of the International A-level. You study **two set
texts** from the crime and mystery list and write **two essays of 25 marks each**, one on each text. The
texts are connected by genre: in each, a significant crime drives the narrative and shapes its structure.
For the full explanation, use the
[Unit 3 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-unit-3-elements-of-crime-and-mystery-study-guide/).

## Unit 3 at a glance

| Detail | Specification (Version 5.3) |
|---|---|
| Exam | 2 hours, closed book |
| Marks | 50: two essay questions, 25 marks each |
| Weighting | 30% of the International A-level (A2 only) |
| Texts | Two from the set list |
| AOs | AO1, AO2, AO3, equally weighted in all questions |

Closed book means you cannot take the texts into the exam, so learn short quotations and precise
references.

## The genre elements (the specification's list)

All set texts "focus on transgressions against established order" and the breaking of national, social,
religious or moral laws. Elements to explore, depending on the text:

| Element | Example question to ask of your text |
|---|---|
| Mystery to be unravelled | What is withheld, and when is it revealed? |
| Settings for crime and pursuit | How do places and times create menace? |
| Crimes, criminals, motives | Is the criminal monstrous, sympathetic or both? |
| Detectives and detection | Who investigates, and how reliable are they? |
| Investigation leading to capture or punishment | Is the criminal caught? |
| Moral purpose and restoration of order | Is order restored, and is it convincing? |
| Guilt, remorse, confession, forgiveness | Who confesses, and to whom? |
| Victims and suffering | Are victims given a voice? |
| Motifs: love, money, power, danger, death | Which dominate your text? |
| Punishment, justice, retribution, the legal system | Is justice legal, divine or poetic? |
| Structural patterning through crises to order | How does the plot move? |
| Language of the created world | What register or idiom defines it? |
| Crime writing as social comment | What does it say about its society and period? |
| Effects on readers | Suspense, repugnance, excitement, relief |

## Assessment objectives (equal weighting)

| AO | Wording | In a Unit 3 essay |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Demonstrate understanding of the ways in which readers find meanings in texts, showing an understanding of genre, the significance of contexts, and the ways in which different interpretations can be found | Argue about the text **as crime writing**; consider contexts and alternative readings |
| AO2 | Analyse ways in which meanings are shaped in literary texts with close attention to authorial methods | Narrative voice, structure, form, imagery, dramatic or poetic methods |
| AO3 | Express informed, personal and argued responses, using appropriate concepts and terminology, and coherent, accurate writing | A clear thesis, critical vocabulary, accurate expression |

Each of Units 3 and 4 contributes 10% of each AO to the International A-level.

### "Significance"

The specification gives the word "significance" a special meaning drawn from semiotics: weighing up all
the contributions to meaning, namely how the text is constructed and written, relevant text-specific
contexts, and potential interpretations. When a question asks about the "significance" of an element,
cover all three.

## Text-specific prompts

| Text | Crime elements worth knowing |
|---|---|
| *Macbeth* | Regicide; guilt and hallucination; the sleepwalking scene as confession; Macduff as avenger; order restored under Malcolm |
| *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* | The shooting of the albatross as transgression; supernatural punishment; penance and compulsive confession to the Wedding-Guest |
| Browning | Dramatic monologues voiced by murderers or would-be poisoners ("My Last Duchess", "Porphyria's Lover", "The Laboratory"); no detective; the reader as judge |
| *Oliver Twist* | Fagin's gang; Sikes's murder of Nancy and his flight; social criticism of the workhouse and the criminal underworld |
| Poe | Dupin as rational detective ("Rue Morgue", "The Purloined Letter"); unreliable confessing narrators ("The Tell-Tale Heart", "The Black Cat") |
| Stevenson | Respectability and hidden crime; Utterson as investigator; "Markheim" and conscience |
| Christie | The narrator as murderer; the "fair-play" puzzle and its subversion; Poirot's restoration of order |
| Qiu Xiaolong | Inspector Chen in 1990s Shanghai; crime investigation entangled with politics |
| Atkinson | Multiple viewpoints; crimes and their long aftermath; Jackson Brodie as detective |

## Worked example: an argued paragraph on Poe

Question: "In crime writing the most important mystery is the criminal's mind." Discuss with reference
to your chosen text.

"Poe's confessing narrators make the criminal mind the mystery and the evidence at once. In 'The
Tell-Tale Heart' the narrator insists on his sanity while describing the murder in feverish detail, so
the reader becomes the detective, reading the confession against itself. There is no investigator of
Dupin's kind here: the police are almost incidental, and the crime is solved by the narrator's own
guilt, heard as the beating heart. Yet Poe also wrote 'The Murders in the Rue Morgue', where the mind
that matters is the detective's, which suggests that across his stories the real subject is the mind's
capacity for both order and disorder."

## Common mistakes / exam traps

- **Plot summary of the crime** instead of analysis of how the text constructs it.
- **Ignoring genre**: AO1 asks for understanding of genre; frame your argument around crime elements.
- **Forgetting it is closed book**: vague references weaken AO2.
- **One reading only**: AO1 includes "the ways in which different interpretations can be found".
- **Treating poems as stories**: in Browning and Coleridge, analyse form, voice and sound.
- **Context as background**: use it to explain significance, for example Victorian anxieties about
  respectability in Stevenson.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I compare my two texts in Unit 3?**
The specification describes two essay questions, one on each text, so each answer focuses on one text.
Knowledge of the genre links them, but comparison is not required.

**Is Unit 3 open book?**
No. The specification states that Unit 3 is a closed-book examination.

**Which AO matters most?**
None. All three assessment objectives are weighted equally in every question.

**Why is *Macbeth* on a crime list?**
The specification connects the Unit 3 texts through a genre in which "a significant crime drives the
narrative". In *Macbeth* that crime is regicide, and the play's guilt, retribution and restoration of
order are all genre elements the specification names.

## Self-test

1. How long is the Unit 3 exam, and is it open or closed book? (2 hours; closed book.)
2. How many essays do you write, and for how many marks each? (Two; 25 each.)
3. Name two genre elements from the specification. (For example, detectives and detection; guilt and remorse.)
4. Which Poe story features Dupin? ("The Murders in the Rue Morgue" or "The Purloined Letter".)
5. What three things does "significance" involve? (Construction and writing; contexts; interpretations.)
