> **Original questions written for Marlbridge.** They are not past-paper questions and do not copy the real exam's structure or mark tariffs. Answers use Catherine II of Russia as the model despot; adapt them if you studied another.

**HL only.** These questions practise Regional study 3 in the Europe option (HL Paper 3). Revise first with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-enlightenment-absolutism-revision-notes/) and the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-enlightenment-absolutism/).

## Questions

**1.** State two ideas associated with Montesquieu or Locke. **[2]**

**2.** Explain two ways in which the Scientific Revolution influenced Enlightenment thinkers. **[4]**

**3.** Read this short extract, constructed for practice.

> "The sovereign must not rule for his own glory. He is the first servant of the people, and the laws must bind him as they bind the humblest peasant. Yet the peasant must obey, for disorder is worse than any tyrant." (Constructed for practice, in the voice of an eighteenth-century ruler.)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about the idea of enlightened despotism. **[3]**

**(b)** Analyse one reason why a ruler's own statements may not show what they actually did. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why legitimacy was important for the ruler you have studied. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the methods an enlightened despot used to centralize power. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the impact of Enlightenment ideas on women's rights in the eighteenth century. **[6]**

**7.** "The domestic reforms of an enlightened despot benefited the ruler more than the people." To what extent do you agree? Plan an answer: state your argument, three themes and a counter-argument. **[15]**

**8.** Compare the foreign policy aims of two enlightened despots, or explain how the foreign policy of your chosen despot differed from the ideas of Enlightenment writers. **[4]**

**9.** Discuss how historians disagree about whether "enlightened despotism" is a helpful term. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: Montesquieu, the separation of powers; Montesquieu, laws should suit a society's conditions; Locke, government rests on consent; Locke, natural rights of life, liberty and property; Locke, toleration. One mark each. **[2]**

**2.** Up to 4. Newton and others showed that nature followed discoverable laws, so thinkers hoped society could also be understood by reason (2). Experiment and evidence, urged by Bacon, became a model for questioning tradition and authority (2). Accept: scientific societies created a network for exchanging ideas. **[4]**

**3. (a)** Up to 3: the ruler presents himself as a servant of the people, linking power to the common good (1); he accepts that laws should bind rulers (1); the call for obedience and fear of disorder shows he keeps absolute authority, so reform is from above (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Up to 3: rulers write to win praise and legitimacy, so statements are shaped by purpose (1); policy may be limited by nobles, finances or revolt (1); a developed example, such as Catherine's *Nakaz*, which praised reform but was not enacted as law, or serfdom becoming harsher (1). **[3]**

**4.** Up to 4. For Catherine: she was a foreign-born woman who took power by a coup in 1762, so her claim was weak (1); she needed support from the nobility and guards regiments (1); association with Voltaire, Diderot and Enlightenment ideals gave her prestige at home and abroad (1); the Pugachev revolt, led by a man claiming to be Peter III, showed legitimacy remained fragile (1). Accept equivalent points for another ruler. **[4]**

**5.** Up to 6 using a markband approach. Look for: administrative reform, such as Catherine's Provincial Reform of 1775 (1); more officials and local courts under central control (1); taking church lands, as in 1764, reduced a rival power (1); favour to the nobility, tying elites to the crown (1); use of force and censorship, as against Pugachev and Radishchev (1); analysis of which method mattered most, or how they worked together (1). **[6]**

**6.** Up to 6: women's roles in salons as hosts, patrons and readers (1); a few writers argued for equal education and rights, such as Wollstonecraft in 1792 and de Gouges in 1791 (2); many leading thinkers, including Rousseau, still saw women's place in the home (1); legal and political rights did not change much in the eighteenth century (1); judgement that impact was real in culture but limited in law (1). **[6]**

**7.** Credit a plan with these features, 3 marks each, 15 in all. Understanding of the claim, with named reforms (3). Evidence the ruler gained: stronger administration, noble loyalty (Charter to the Nobility, 1785), secularized church wealth, a progressive image abroad (3). Evidence the people gained: education (Smolny), tolerance of religions, trade and town growth, but serfdom grew harsher so the peasant majority gained little (3). Perspectives: a historian's view, such as scepticism about enlightened despotism as state-building, against a view that reforms were sincere (3). Counter-argument and judgement: for example that the ruler gained most, but some groups such as nobles and townspeople benefited too (3). **[15]**

**8.** Up to 4. Comparison: Catherine and Frederick both expanded their states and joined in the partitions of Poland (1); both used war and diplomacy to gain territory (1); Enlightenment writers condemned aggressive war and urged peace, so conquest sat uneasily with the ideals (1); a judgement that foreign policy followed state interest more than principle (1). **[4]**

**9.** Up to 6: states one view that the term is useful, because rulers such as Joseph II carried out real reforms in law and religion (2); states the opposing view that reforms served the state and the ideas were decoration (2); explains why they differ, for example by which rulers and policies are emphasised (1); a judgement on whether the term helps (1). **[6]**
