This guide covers **4.4.3 Electrolysis** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from
September 2016. It sits in topic 4.4, Chemical changes, and is assessed on Paper 1. Sections 4.4.3.1 to 4.4.3.4
are for both tiers. Section 4.4.3.5, writing half equations for the electrode reactions, is **Higher tier
only**, and the specification says Higher tier students should be able to write half equations throughout
4.4.3. Required practical 3 (electrolysis of aqueous solutions with inert electrodes) belongs here.

Use the [electrolysis revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-electrolysis-revision-notes/) for
quick recall and the [electrolysis practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-electrolysis-practice/)
to test yourself. The [chemical changes study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-changes/)

## 4.4.3.1 The process of electrolysis

An ionic compound only conducts electricity when its ions are **free to move**. That happens when the compound
is **melted** or **dissolved in water**. These liquids and solutions are called **electrolytes**. A solid ionic
compound does not conduct, because its ions are held in a lattice.

When a direct current passes through an electrolyte:

- **positive ions** (cations) move to the **negative electrode**, the **cathode**
- **negative ions** (anions) move to the **positive electrode**, the **anode**
- the ions are **discharged** at the electrodes, producing **elements**.

This process is **electrolysis**. A way to remember which electrode is which: **PANIC**, Positive Anode,
Negative Is Cathode. Opposites attract, so cations go to the cathode.

**Inert electrodes** (graphite or platinum) do not take part in the reaction. They simply carry the current.

## 4.4.3.2 Electrolysis of molten ionic compounds

When a simple (binary) molten ionic compound is electrolysed with inert electrodes:

- the **metal** forms at the **cathode**
- the **non-metal** forms at the **anode**.

Binary means two elements, such as lead bromide, PbBr₂.

| Molten compound | Cathode product | Anode product |
|---|---|---|
| Lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂ | Lead | Bromine |
| Sodium chloride, NaCl | Sodium | Chlorine |
| Zinc chloride, ZnCl₂ | Zinc | Chlorine |
| Potassium iodide, KI | Potassium | Iodine |
| Magnesium oxide, MgO | Magnesium | Oxygen |

### Worked example 1: predicting products

*Predict the products when molten calcium bromide is electrolysed with inert electrodes.*

1. Calcium bromide is CaBr₂, a binary ionic compound, and it is molten.
2. The cation is Ca²⁺, which goes to the cathode and becomes calcium metal.
3. The anion is Br⁻, which goes to the anode and becomes bromine.

Cathode: calcium. Anode: bromine. Both can be seen: a silvery-grey metal at the bottom and brown bromine fumes
at the top (bromine is hazardous, so a fume cupboard is used with lead bromide).

## 4.4.3.3 Using electrolysis to extract metals

Metals above carbon in the reactivity series, and metals that react with carbon, **cannot be extracted by
reduction with carbon**. They are extracted by electrolysis of their molten compounds. The process uses
**large amounts of energy**, both to melt the compound and to supply the electric current, which makes it
expensive.

### Aluminium

Aluminium is made by electrolysing a **molten mixture of aluminium oxide and cryolite**, with **carbon
(graphite) as the electrodes**.

- **Why a mixture?** Aluminium oxide has a very high melting point, over 2000 °C. Dissolving it in molten
  cryolite gives an electrolyte that melts at a much **lower temperature**. This **saves a large amount of
  energy** and makes the process cheaper.
- **Cathode:** aluminium ions gain electrons. Molten aluminium collects at the bottom of the cell and is tapped
  off.
- **Anode:** oxide ions lose electrons and form oxygen.
- **Why the carbon anodes must be replaced:** at the high temperature, the oxygen formed **reacts with the
  carbon** to make **carbon dioxide**, so the anodes burn away and have to be replaced regularly.

C + O₂ → CO₂

## 4.4.3.4 Electrolysis of aqueous solutions

An aqueous solution has **two** sources of ions: the dissolved compound and **water**. Water ionises slightly:
H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. So there are two possible products at each electrode. The rules are:

**At the cathode (negative):** hydrogen is produced if the metal is **more reactive than hydrogen**. If the
metal is less reactive than hydrogen, the metal is produced.

**At the anode (positive):** oxygen is produced **unless the solution contains halide ions** (chloride,
bromide or iodide), in which case the **halogen** is produced.

| Solution | Cathode product | Anode product | Left in solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium chloride | Hydrogen | Chlorine | Sodium hydroxide |
| Copper(II) sulfate | Copper | Oxygen | Sulfuric acid |
| Dilute sulfuric acid | Hydrogen | Oxygen | Sulfuric acid (more concentrated) |
| Potassium iodide | Hydrogen | Iodine | Potassium hydroxide |
| Silver nitrate | Silver | Oxygen | Nitric acid |

### Worked example 2: aqueous sodium sulfate

*Predict the products when aqueous sodium sulfate is electrolysed with inert electrodes.*

1. Ions present: Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ from the compound, H⁺ and OH⁻ from water.
2. Cathode: sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so **hydrogen** is produced.
3. Anode: there are no halide ions, so **oxygen** is produced.

### Worked example 3: aqueous copper(II) chloride

1. Ions present: Cu²⁺ and Cl⁻, plus H⁺ and OH⁻.
2. Cathode: copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so **copper** forms (pink-brown solid on the electrode).
3. Anode: chloride is a halide ion, so **chlorine** forms (bubbles, bleaches damp litmus paper).

### Required practical 3

You are asked to investigate what happens when aqueous solutions are electrolysed with inert electrodes. The
usual approach is to form a **hypothesis** about the products, such as "the metal forms only when it is less
reactive than hydrogen". Test several solutions in a beaker or U-tube with graphite electrodes. Collect the
gases in inverted test tubes, if possible. Identify them with these tests:

- hydrogen: lit splint gives a squeaky pop
- oxygen: glowing splint relights
- chlorine: damp blue litmus paper turns red then bleaches white
- a metal: a coating on the cathode.

## 4.4.3.5 Half equations (Higher tier only)

At the **cathode**, positive ions **gain electrons**: this is **reduction**. At the **anode**, negative ions
**lose electrons**: this is **oxidation**. A **half equation** shows what happens at one electrode, with
electrons written in.

| Electrode reaction | Half equation |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen at the cathode | 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ |
| Copper at the cathode | Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu |
| Lead at the cathode | Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb |
| Aluminium at the cathode | Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al |
| Chlorine at the anode | 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ |
| Bromine at the anode | 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ |
| Oxygen from molten oxide | 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ |
| Oxygen from aqueous solution | 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ |

The specification gives the last one in two forms: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻, or 4OH⁻ – 4e⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O. Both
are accepted.

### Worked example 4: balancing electrons for the overall reaction

*Write the overall equation for the electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide.*

1. Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻.
2. Electrons gained must equal electrons lost. The lowest common multiple of 3 and 4 is 12.
3. Multiply the cathode half equation by 4: 4Al³⁺ + 12e⁻ → 4Al.
4. Multiply the anode half equation by 3: 6O²⁻ → 3O₂ + 12e⁻.
5. Add them: 4Al³⁺ + 6O²⁻ → 4Al + 3O₂, which is the same as 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂.

Check charge: each half equation must balance both **atoms** and **charge**. In 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ the left has
a charge of 2− and the right has 2 electrons, also 2−.

## Exam technique

- Always name the **electrode** with the product: "chlorine at the anode".
- For aqueous solutions, write out the ions from **both** the compound and water, then apply the two rules.
- Give the reason for hydrogen: "sodium is more reactive than hydrogen".
- For the aluminium cell, give both reasons: lower melting point of the mixture, and carbon anode oxidised to
  carbon dioxide.
- For half equations, check the number of electrons and put them on the correct side: on the right for
  oxidation at the anode, on the left for reduction at the cathode.

## Common mistakes

- Swapping the electrodes: positive ions go to the **negative** cathode, not the anode.
- Forgetting that molten and aqueous electrolysis give different cathode products for reactive metals.
- Writing that water is electrolysed to oxygen and hydrogen "in every case" rather than only when the metal is
  more reactive than hydrogen.
- Saying cryolite "reacts" with aluminium oxide. It is a solvent that lowers the melting point.
- Writing oxygen as O or chlorine as Cl in half equations. They are diatomic: O₂ and Cl₂.
