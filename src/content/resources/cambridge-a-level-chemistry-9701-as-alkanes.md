This guide covers subtopic **14.1, Alkanes**, from Topic 14, Hydrocarbons, of Cambridge International AS
& A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The
[Hydrocarbons: Alkanes and Alkenes study guide](/resources/as-hydrocarbons-alkanes-and-alkenes/) sets out
the free-radical mechanism with ethane and chlorine; this page goes further on balancing combustion and
cracking equations, the bromination of ethane, predicting the products of substitution, and the
chemistry of exhaust pollutants.

## What the syllabus asks for

The six learning outcomes in 14.1 are:

1. recall how alkanes are made: (a) **hydrogenation** of an alkene, H₂(g) with a Pt or Ni catalyst and
   heat; (b) **cracking** of a longer-chain alkane, heat with Al₂O₃
2. describe (a) complete and incomplete **combustion**; (b) **free-radical substitution** by Cl₂ or Br₂
   in ultraviolet light, as exemplified by ethane
3. describe the free-radical mechanism: **initiation, propagation, termination**
4. suggest how cracking turns heavy crude-oil fractions into more useful alkanes and alkenes of lower Mr
5. understand the general **unreactivity** of alkanes, including towards polar reagents, in terms of
   strong, non-polar C–H bonds
6. recognise the environmental consequences of **CO, oxides of nitrogen and unburnt hydrocarbons**
   from engines, and of their catalytic removal

## Making alkanes

**Hydrogenation.** Hydrogen adds across the C=C of an alkene, using a platinum or nickel catalyst and
heat:

    CH₃CH=CHCH₃ + H₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃      (but-2-ene to butane)
    C₆H₁₀ + H₂ → C₆H₁₂                   (cyclohexene to cyclohexane)

Each C=C takes one H₂. A compound with two C=C bonds, such as buta-1,3-diene, needs two moles of H₂ per
mole.

**Cracking.** Heating a long-chain alkane with an aluminium oxide catalyst breaks C–C bonds. The products
in exam equations are a shorter alkane plus at least one alkene: there are not enough hydrogen atoms for
every fragment to be saturated.

    C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₈H₁₆
    C₁₀H₂₂ → C₇H₁₆ + C₃H₆

To balance a cracking equation, check that both carbon and hydrogen totals match on each side. If the
question gives only some products, find the missing one by subtraction: C₁₄H₃₀ → C₁₀H₂₂ + ? leaves C₄H₈,
which could be one butene or two molecules of ethene, C₂H₄ (written as 2C₂H₄).

**Why cracking is useful.** Fractional distillation of crude oil gives more of the heavy, long-chain
fractions than are needed, and less of the lighter fractions, such as petrol, that are in high demand.
Cracking converts the surplus heavy molecules into shorter alkanes of lower Mr for fuels, and alkenes
that are used to make polymers and other chemicals.

## Combustion

**Complete combustion**, in plenty of oxygen, gives carbon dioxide and water. Balance C first, then H,
then O, using halves if needed and doubling at the end:

    C₈H₁₈ + 12½O₂ → 8CO₂ + 9H₂O
    2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O

**Incomplete combustion**, in a limited supply of oxygen, gives carbon monoxide or carbon (soot) instead
of, or as well as, carbon dioxide:

    C₈H₁₈ + 8½O₂ → 8CO + 9H₂O
    C₈H₁₈ + 4½O₂ → 8C + 9H₂O

**Worked example: gas volumes.** 20 cm³ of propane is burned completely in excess oxygen. Calculate the
volume of oxygen used and of carbon dioxide formed, all volumes measured at room temperature and
pressure.

    C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Gas volumes are in the same ratio as moles, so oxygen used = 5 × 20 = **100 cm³** and carbon dioxide
formed = 3 × 20 = **60 cm³**. At room temperature the water is a liquid and its volume is negligible.

## Why alkanes are unreactive

The data booklet gives an average C–H bond energy of 410 kJ mol⁻¹ and a C–C bond energy of
350 kJ mol⁻¹, so a lot of energy is needed to break either. The Pauling electronegativities of carbon
(2.5) and hydrogen (2.1) are close, so C–H bonds are almost non-polar. Polar reagents such as
nucleophiles (OH⁻, CN⁻) and electrophiles (H⁺, NO₂⁺) need a δ+ or δ− site to attack; alkanes offer
neither. Alkanes also have no π electrons and no lone pairs. They therefore react only with reagents that
can break strong, non-polar bonds: oxygen in combustion, and halogen radicals made by ultraviolet light.

## Free-radical substitution: bromine and ethane

Ultraviolet light supplies the energy to split the weak Br–Br bond (193 kJ mol⁻¹ in the data booklet)
by **homolytic fission**: each atom keeps one electron of the bonding pair, forming two **free radicals**
(species with an unpaired electron).

**Initiation:**

    Br₂ → 2Br•

**Propagation** (each step uses one radical and makes another):

    Br• + CH₃CH₃ → CH₃CH₂• + HBr
    CH₃CH₂• + Br₂ → CH₃CH₂Br + Br•

**Termination** (two radicals combine, removing both):

    Br• + Br• → Br₂
    CH₃CH₂• + Br• → CH₃CH₂Br
    CH₃CH₂• + CH₃CH₂• → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃

Overall: CH₃CH₃ + Br₂ → CH₃CH₂Br + HBr. With chlorine the steps are identical, with Cl• in place of Br•.

**Why the product is a mixture.** The radicals do not choose which molecule to attack. Once some
bromoethane has formed, Br• can remove a hydrogen from it instead, leading to dibromoethanes and further
substituted products. Termination also makes butane. Using an excess of the alkane reduces further
substitution, because a radical is then more likely to meet an alkane molecule than a product molecule.

**Predicting isomeric products.** Propane, CH₃CH₂CH₃, has two kinds of hydrogen: six on the end carbons
and two on the middle carbon. Monochlorination therefore gives two structural isomers, 1-chloropropane
and 2-chloropropane. Count the distinct hydrogen environments to find how many monosubstituted products
are possible: 2-methylpropane gives two (1-chloro-2-methylpropane and 2-chloro-2-methylpropane).

## Pollutants from the internal combustion engine

| Pollutant | How it forms in an engine | Environmental consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon monoxide, CO | incomplete combustion of the fuel | toxic: it binds to haemoglobin and reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen |
| Oxides of nitrogen, NO and NO₂ | N₂ and O₂ from the air react at the high temperature in the engine: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO | acid rain; contribute to photochemical smog |
| Unburnt hydrocarbons | fuel that passes through without burning | contribute to photochemical smog |

A **catalytic converter** in the exhaust uses metals such as platinum and rhodium on a ceramic support to
convert these pollutants into less harmful gases:

    2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂
    C₈H₁₈ + 12½O₂ → 8CO₂ + 9H₂O     (unburnt fuel oxidised)

Carbon dioxide is still a greenhouse gas, so a converter removes toxic pollutants but does not reduce
CO₂ emissions.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a cracking equation that balances for carbon but not for hydrogen.** Check both totals.
- **Leaving out UV light** for free-radical substitution, or adding a catalyst that is not used.
- **Writing H• in a propagation step.** The halogen radical removes a hydrogen atom to form HBr or HCl;
  free hydrogen atoms are not formed.
- **Forgetting the dot** on radicals, or putting a dot on a product molecule: CH₃CH₂• is a radical,
  CH₃CH₂Br is not.
- **Saying alkanes are unreactive because the bonds are "stable".** Give both reasons: strong bonds and
  very low polarity.
- **Claiming NOx comes from nitrogen in the fuel.** It comes from atmospheric nitrogen at high
  temperature.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** the mechanism: name each stage and write an equation for it, with radical dots.
- **Explain** unreactivity: bond strength plus polarity, with the electronegativity comparison.
- **Suggest** why cracking is useful: link supply and demand of fractions to the products' uses.
- **Give** or **State** an equation: balance every element; fractional O₂ coefficients are acceptable
  in combustion equations.
