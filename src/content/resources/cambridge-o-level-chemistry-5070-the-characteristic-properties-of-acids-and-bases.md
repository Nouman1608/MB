They range from stating that "aqueous solutions of acids contain H⁺ ions and aqueous solutions of alkalis contain OH⁻ ions", through the reactions of acids and bases and three indicators, to constructing "the symbol equations to show their complete dissociation" for strong acids and partial dissociation for weak ones.

## Two ways to describe acids and bases

**In terms of ions in water (outcome 1).** Aqueous acids contain **H⁺ ions**; aqueous alkalis contain **OH⁻ ions**.

**In terms of protons (outcome 2).** A hydrogen ion, H⁺, is just a proton. So an **acid is a proton donor** and a **base is a proton acceptor**.

The proton definition explains reactions that do not obviously involve H⁺ and OH⁻. When ammonia gas meets hydrogen chloride gas, white solid ammonium chloride forms:

NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

HCl donates a proton (acid) and NH₃ accepts it, becoming NH₄⁺ (base). No water is involved, yet it is still an acid–base reaction.

## What counts as a base

Outcome 3: **bases are oxides or hydroxides of metals**, and **alkalis are soluble bases**. So:

- copper(II) oxide and iron(III) hydroxide are bases but **not** alkalis, because they are insoluble
- sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide and calcium hydroxide are alkalis, because they dissolve, releasing OH⁻ ions
- aqueous ammonia is also an alkali, because it produces OH⁻ ions in water

## Reactions of acids

Outcome 4 names three reactions. Each one can be written as a full equation and as an ionic equation, which shows what the acid's H⁺ ions actually do.

| Reaction | Example equation | Ionic equation | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen | Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂ | Zn + 2H⁺ → Zn²⁺ + H₂ | Fizzing; metal dissolves; gas pops with a lighted splint |
| Acid + base → salt + water | MgO + 2HNO₃ → Mg(NO₃)₂ + H₂O | MgO + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂O | Solid base dissolves; solution warms |
| Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide | Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ | CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂ | Fizzing; gas turns limewater milky |

Only metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series react with dilute acids to give hydrogen; copper does not.

## Reactions of bases

Outcome 5 names two reactions.

- **Base + acid → salt + water**: neutralisation, as above.
- **Base + ammonium salt → salt + ammonia + water**, on warming:

2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃ + 2H₂O

Ionic equation: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O. This is itself a proton transfer: the ammonium ion donates a proton to the hydroxide ion. Ammonia is detected because it turns damp red litmus paper blue. This reaction is the basis of the syllabus test for ammonium ions: warming the unknown with aqueous sodium hydroxide releases ammonia only if NH₄⁺ ions are present.

## Neutralisation

Outcome 6: neutralisation occurs between an **acid and a base**. Outcome 7: between an acid and an **alkali**, the reaction is:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

Every acid–alkali neutralisation reduces to this equation once the **spectator ions** are removed. In KOH + HNO₃ → KNO₃ + H₂O, the K⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions are in solution before and after; only H⁺ and OH⁻ change.

## Indicators and pH

Outcome 8 names three indicators:

| Indicator | Acid | Alkali |
|---|---|---|
| Litmus | Red | Blue |
| Thymolphthalein | Colourless | Blue |
| Methyl orange | Red | Yellow |

Outcome 12 adds **universal indicator paper**, which gives a range of colours matched to pH. pH 7 is neutral (green). Below 7 is acidic: the lower the pH, the higher the H⁺ concentration and the more acidic the solution (red at the lowest values). Above 7 is alkaline: the higher the pH, the more alkaline (blue to purple).

A single indicator such as litmus only tells you acid or alkali; universal indicator lets you **compare** solutions, which is what outcome 12 asks for.

## Strong and weak acids

Outcome 9 defines a **strong acid** as one that is **completely dissociated** in aqueous solution, and a **weak acid** as one that is **partially dissociated**.

Outcome 10 asks for dissociation equations for strong acids, using a single arrow:

```
HCl(aq)   → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
HNO₃(aq)  → H⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)
H₂SO₄(aq) → 2H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq)
```

Outcome 11 asks for weak acids, which include **carboxylic acids**, using the reversible arrow:

```
CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq)       ethanoic acid
HCOOH(aq)   ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCOO⁻(aq)         methanoic acid
```

Only the H in the –COOH group is released; the hydrogen atoms bonded to carbon are not.

### Worked example: comparing a strong and a weak acid

**Question.** Hydrochloric acid and ethanoic acid of the same concentration are compared. Predict and explain the differences in (a) pH and (b) the reaction with magnesium ribbon. **[4]**

**Model answer.**

(a) Hydrochloric acid has the **lower pH** [1], because it is completely dissociated, so its H⁺ concentration is higher than that of the partially dissociated ethanoic acid [1].

(b) Magnesium **fizzes faster** in hydrochloric acid [1], because the higher H⁺ concentration gives more frequent collisions between H⁺ ions and the magnesium surface [1].

With excess magnesium and equal volumes, both acids eventually give the **same volume** of hydrogen, because they contain the same amount of acid. As ethanoic acid's H⁺ ions are used up, more molecules dissociate to replace them.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling every base an alkali.** Only soluble bases are alkalis.
- **Using a reversible arrow for HCl**, or a single arrow for ethanoic acid.
- **Writing H₂SO₄ → H⁺ + SO₄²⁻**, which does not balance in atoms or charge.
- **Missing carbon dioxide** from the acid + carbonate products.
- **Confusing strong with concentrated.** Strength is about dissociation; concentration is about the amount dissolved per dm³.
- **Saying a weak acid "has fewer hydrogen atoms".** It has the same number; fewer are released as ions.
- **Reversing indicator colours**, especially thymolphthalein (colourless in acid, blue in alkali).

## Command words

- **State:** a fact such as "acids are proton donors".
- **Describe** (reactions): give reactants, products and what is seen.
- **Construct:** a balanced symbol equation with state symbols and the correct arrow.
- **Explain** (strong and weak): link the observation to the degree of dissociation and the H⁺ concentration.
