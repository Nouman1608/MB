## What the syllabus asks for

| Outcome | Wording in the syllabus |
|---|---|
| 4.2.1 | circular flow of income in a closed economy and an open economy: the flow of income between households, firms and government and the international economy |
| 4.2.2 | injections and leakages (multiplier not required) |
| 4.2.3 | equilibrium and disequilibrium (marginal and average propensities not required) |

The two exclusions matter. At AS you explain the **direction** of change in national income; you are not asked to calculate how far it changes or to use propensities to consume or save. Those ideas return at A Level in section 9.1.

## Real flows and money flows

The model tracks two kinds of flow moving in opposite directions.

- **Real flows:** households supply factor services (labour, land, capital, enterprise) to firms; firms supply goods and services to households.
- **Money flows:** firms pay factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) to households; households spend money on goods and services from firms.

The money flow is the one the model is named after. In the simplest economy, every unit of income firms pay out returns to them as spending, so income goes round in a circle and stays the same size.

## Building the model

**Two sectors (households and firms).** All income is either spent or saved. Saving is the only leakage and investment the only injection.

**Closed economy with government.** Households and firms pay **taxes**, a leakage. Government buys goods and services and employs workers: **government spending** is an injection. The economy is still closed: no trade with other countries.

**Open economy.** The **international economy** joins. Spending on **imports** sends income to foreign producers, a leakage. Foreign spending on **exports** brings income to domestic firms, an injection.

| Leakages (withdrawals) | Injections |
|---|---|
| Saving (S) | Investment (I) |
| Taxation (T) | Government spending (G) |
| Imports (M) | Exports (X) |

A **leakage** is income that is not passed on as spending on domestic output. An **injection** is spending on domestic output that does not come from households' current income.

## Classifying transactions

Applied questions often describe a transaction and ask how it enters the flow. Ask: does money **leave** the circle of spending on domestic output, **enter** it from outside household consumption, or simply **circulate** within it?

| Transaction | Classification |
|---|---|
| A household buys groceries from a local shop | Neither: consumer spending circulating within the flow |
| A worker pays income tax | Leakage (T) |
| A family puts part of its income into a savings account | Leakage (S) |
| A bakery buys a new oven from a domestic manufacturer | Injection (I) |
| A household buys an imported television | Leakage (M) |
| A foreign tourist pays for a hotel room in the country | Injection (X): spending by non-residents on domestic output |
| The government pays nurses' salaries | Injection (G) |
| The government pays a pension to a retired person | Neither on its own: a transfer payment, not spending on output |
| A firm pays wages to its workers | Neither: a factor income flow within the circle |

The pension row is the most tested. A transfer payment moves income from taxpayers to recipients without buying any output. It only adds to spending if the recipient spends it, and then it appears as consumption.

## Equilibrium and disequilibrium

National income is in **equilibrium** when it has no tendency to change. That happens when total injections equal total leakages.

```
Two sectors:          S = I
Closed with government: S + T = I + G
Open economy:         S + T + M = I + G + X
```

Only the **totals** must match. The pairs do not need to balance individually. Saving can exceed investment while the government runs a budget deficit (G above T) and exports exceed imports, as long as the sums are equal.

**Disequilibrium** exists when injections and leakages differ, so national income is changing.

### How the economy adjusts

1. **Injections greater than leakages.** Spending on domestic output is greater than the income firms paid out. Firms find their stocks of unsold goods falling and orders rising. They raise output and employ more workers, so national income rises. As income rises, people save more, pay more tax and buy more imports, so leakages rise until they equal injections at a higher income.
2. **Leakages greater than injections.** Spending falls short of the income paid out. Unsold stocks build up, so firms cut output and employment. National income falls, leakages fall with it, and a new equilibrium is reached at a lower income.

### Worked example: three changes in one economy

Country Y (invented, billion cu) has saving 60, taxes 110 and imports 90, so leakages are 260. Investment is 70, government spending 120 and exports 80, so injections are 270.

```
Start:       injections 270 > leakages 260 -> national income rises
Change A:    taxes rise by 15 -> leakages 275 > injections 270 -> national income falls
Change B:    (from the start) exports fall by 10 -> injections 260 = leakages 260 -> equilibrium
```

The example shows why it is the **gap**, not the size, of injections and leakages that matters. The question asks only for the direction of change; the multiplier is not required.

## Tracing an event through the model

**A rise in foreign demand for the country's exports.** Exports, an injection, rise. Domestic firms receive more revenue than before, so they raise output and hire workers. Households receive more factor income and spend more, which raises firms' revenue again. As incomes rise, saving, taxes and imports rise. National income settles at a higher equilibrium when total leakages have risen to match the higher injections.

**A rise in household saving.** Saving, a leakage, rises. Less income is spent with firms, stocks build up, output and employment fall, and national income falls until lower income has reduced leakages back to the level of injections.

## Equilibrium is not the same as a good outcome

An equilibrium level of national income is simply a level with no tendency to change. It may leave many workers unemployed, or it may be associated with rising prices if spending exceeds what the economy can produce. Judging whether an equilibrium is desirable needs the AD/AS model and the policy objectives in Topic 5.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain why national income falls when leakages exceed injections in the circular flow. **[4]**

> When leakages exceed injections, more income is being **withdrawn** from the flow through saving, taxes and imports than is **added** by investment, government spending and exports *(1: condition explained)*. Spending on domestic output is therefore **less than the income firms paid out** *(1: spending shortfall)*. Firms cannot sell all their output, so **unsold stocks rise and they cut production and employment** *(1: firms' response)*. Households receive less income, so national income falls until leakages, which fall as income falls, again equal injections *(1: adjustment to new equilibrium)*.

## Common mistakes

- Calling transfer payments an injection.
- Treating household spending on domestic goods as an injection. It circulates within the flow.
- Saying equilibrium requires S = I, T = G and X = M separately.
- Calculating a multiplier effect when the syllabus says it is not required at AS.
- Describing equilibrium as full employment.
- Mixing up which direction money and real flows move.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** questions on the model: name each sector, the real flow and the money flow between them, and each leakage and injection.
- **Explain** a change: identify the injection or leakage affected, compare totals, then give the chain through firms' output and households' income to the new equilibrium.
- Paper 1 items may list transactions; classify each as an injection, leakage or neither before choosing.
- In Section C essays, the circular flow is a useful first model; follow it with AD/AS if the question asks about prices or employment.
