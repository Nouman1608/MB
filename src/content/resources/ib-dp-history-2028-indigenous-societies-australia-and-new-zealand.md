This is a **higher level (HL) only** page. Indigenous societies and national identity in Australia and New Zealand (circa 1770 to 2020) is one of the twelve regional studies in the Asia and Oceania option, examined on Paper 3, the 2-hour essay paper that standard level students do not sit. This guide follows the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. The guide lets you choose **either** Australia **or** New Zealand for the two post-1945 inquiry topics (social and cultural developments, and foreign policy). Both are summarised here, so use the one you study.

Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-indigenous-societies-australia-and-new-zealand-revision-notes/) and the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-indigenous-societies-australia-and-new-zealand-practice/).

## Impact of colonisation (to about 1900)

**Settlement.** Captain James Cook claimed the east coast of Australia for Britain in 1770. The First Fleet reached Sydney Cove in January 1788 with convicts, beginning a colony that received transported convicts until 1868. Later waves were free settlers drawn by land, wool and gold. In New Zealand, whalers, traders and missionaries came first. The New Zealand Company organised settlement from 1839 and the colony was founded in 1840.

**Land.** British law treated Australia as *terra nullius*, land belonging to no one, so Aboriginal ownership was ignored. "Squatters" occupied large sheep runs beyond the official limits, and later gained legal leases. The **Selection Acts** of the 1860s (for example the Robertson Land Acts in New South Wales) aimed to let small farmers "select" land, but squatters often used dummy buyers to keep their runs, so the reforms had mixed success.

**The gold rushes.** Gold found in New South Wales and Victoria in 1851 brought a huge migration. Australia's population more than doubled in a decade. The conflict at the Eureka Stockade at Ballarat (December 1854) arose over licence fees and rights. Chinese miners faced hostility. In New Zealand the Otago rush (1861) had a similar effect. The rushes brought wealth, new towns, ideas of democratic rights and anti-Chinese feeling.

**Cities and labour.** Melbourne and Sydney grew into major ports and cities. Workers won the eight-hour day in the building trades in the 1850s. Unions grew and, after the strikes of the early 1890s, labour parties formed; this was the root of the Australian Labor Party. New Zealand's Liberal government (1890s) passed arbitration and pension laws and gave women the vote in 1893, the first self-governing country to do so.

## Experiences of Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander and Māori peoples

**Demographic and social impact.** Introduced disease, loss of land and violence reduced Indigenous populations sharply. Estimates of the Aboriginal population in 1788 vary widely, from several hundred thousand upwards, and historians debate them. The Māori population fell to around 40,000 by the 1890s. Governments created reserves and missions and appointed protectors or boards that controlled lives.

**World views.** For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, land (Country) carried law, kinship and spiritual duty, and was not owned in the European sense. For Māori, land (*whenua*) tied people to ancestors (*whakapapa*), with rights held by groups and obligations to care for it (*kaitiakitanga*). Settlers usually saw land as private property to be farmed. This difference helps to explain disputes over sales and boundaries.

**Conflict.** The **Australian Frontier Wars**, a series of local conflicts across the continent (the guide dates them 1840 to 1934, though violence began in 1788), involved massacres such as Myall Creek (1838) and the Tasmanian conflict of the 1820s and 1830s. In New Zealand the **New Zealand Wars** (1845 to 1872) included the Northland war, the Waikato invasion (1863) and wars in Taranaki and the Bay of Plenty. The government confiscated large areas of land under the New Zealand Settlements Act (1863), and a Native Land Court (1865) turned shared land into individual title, which made sales easier.

**The Treaty of Waitangi (1840).** Signed by Captain William Hobson for the Crown and many Māori chiefs, it exists in English and Māori versions. The English text cedes sovereignty to the Crown; the Māori text uses *kāwanatanga* (governance) for the Crown while guaranteeing chiefly authority (*tino rangatiratanga*) over lands and treasures. Differences between the texts caused lasting disputes. Australia had no treaty with Aboriginal peoples.

## Reasons for the emergence of the two nations

**Constitutional developments.** The Australian colonies won self-government in the 1850s. New Zealand did so in 1852 and had a unified Parliament from 1876, with four Māori seats from 1867.

**The Federation movement.** Fear of other powers, a shared market, defence and the "White Australia" idea pushed the colonies together. Henry Parkes called for union in 1889. The National Australasian Conventions of 1891 and 1897 to 1898 drafted a constitution, and referendums approved it. The Commonwealth of Australia was proclaimed on 1 January 1901. The Immigration Restriction Act (1901) began the White Australia policy. The Constitution did not count Aboriginal people in the census and excluded them from federal law-making.

**National identity.** The *Bulletin* magazine, bush poetry, the Heidelberg School of painters and cricket against England shaped an image of the "bush" and mateship. In New Zealand, rugby and the 1905 "Originals" tour built pride. Both pictures mostly left out Indigenous people and women.

**Dominion status.** Australia was a self-governing dominion from 1901 and New Zealand became the Dominion of New Zealand in 1907. New Zealand did not join the Australian federation.

## Impact of the First World War

**ANZACs and Gallipoli.** Soldiers of the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps landed at Gallipoli on 25 April 1915. The campaign failed and ended in evacuation, but it became a founding story for both countries and gave ANZAC Day. About 60,000 Australians and about 18,000 New Zealanders died in the war overall, a heavy toll for small populations.

**Political and social effects.** Australia's two conscription referendums (1916 and 1917) were narrowly rejected, divided Labor and split the community. New Zealand introduced conscription in 1916. Loans, shipping and wool trade shaped the economy, and the cost of war was borne by returned soldiers' pensions and debt. Women filled new jobs but mostly lost them later.

**Indigenous experience.** Many Aboriginal men enlisted although official rules at first barred them, and Māori served in the Māori Pioneer Battalion. Returned Indigenous soldiers often did not share the rewards, such as soldier settlement land, given to others.

## Post-1945 society (Australia or New Zealand)

**Australia.** The slogan "populate or perish" led to an immigration programme under Arthur Calwell, bringing British and then European migrants, including displaced persons. The White Australia policy was dismantled in stages and finished in the 1970s, and multiculturalism became official policy. The Menzies government (1949 to 1966) saw steady growth. The Whitlam government (1972 to 1975) brought universal health insurance (later Medicare, 1984). Women won equal pay decisions in 1969 and 1972, and the ban on married women in the public service ended in 1966. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, key steps were the right to vote in federal elections (1962), the **1967 referendum** (passed by over 90 per cent) allowing the Commonwealth to make laws for them and count them in the census, the Wave Hill walk-off (1966), the Tent Embassy (1972), the Northern Territory land rights law (1976), the **Mabo decision (1992)** that rejected *terra nullius*, the Native Title Act (1993), the *Bringing Them Home* report on the Stolen Generations (1997) and the national apology (2008).

**New Zealand.** The welfare state grew after 1938. Immigration from the Pacific and later Asia changed the population, and the 1970s "dawn raids" on Pacific overstayers are a painful example. Women's pay equality came in 1972. For Māori, urban migration after 1945 broke old community ties. The Māori land march (1975), the Bastion Point occupation (1977 to 1978), the Waitangi Tribunal (1975, with wider powers from 1985), Māori as an official language (1987) and treaty settlements such as that with Ngāi Tahu (1998) mark a revival.

## Post-1945 foreign policy (Australia or New Zealand)

**Relations with the UK.** The fall of Singapore in 1942 led Prime Minister Curtin to "look to America". Britain's entry to the European Economic Community in 1973 forced both countries to find new markets.

**Alliances.** ANZUS (1951) bound Australia, New Zealand and the United States. Both joined SEATO (1954) and sent forces to the Korean War. Australia sent a large force to Vietnam, including conscripts from 1965, and withdrew in 1972. New Zealand sent a smaller one. In 1984 to 1987 David Lange's New Zealand barred nuclear-armed or nuclear-powered ships, which led the US to suspend its ANZUS obligations towards New Zealand.

**Regional relations.** Trade with Japan grew from the 1950s, and Australia recognised the People's Republic of China in 1972. Both countries took more part in Pacific affairs as islands gained independence, such as Papua New Guinea in 1975.

## Perspectives and historiography

- Historians of the "Great Australian Silence" (W. E. H. Stanner, 1968) and Henry Reynolds (*The Other Side of the Frontier*) argued that Indigenous resistance and frontier violence were left out of national history. Others, including Keith Windschuttle, disputed the scale of violence, in the "history wars".
- James Belich and Claudia Orange reassessed the New Zealand Wars and the Treaty from Māori and Pākehā viewpoints.
- The ANZAC legend is celebrated as national founding story and questioned for what it leaves out.

## A worked example

**Question: "To what extent was the First World War the main influence on the growth of national identity in Australia or New Zealand?"** Argue that Gallipoli supplied powerful symbols and shared sacrifice, but identity was already forming through Federation, sport, the bush legend and the dominion status of 1901 and 1907. Include the exclusion of Indigenous people from the story and a judgement that the war crystallised an identity already in the making.

## Common mistakes

- Treating Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander and Māori experience as the same.
- Describing the Treaty of Waitangi as a single clear text.
- Forgetting that the guide asks for either Australia or New Zealand after 1945.
- Giving the 1967 referendum as the moment Aboriginal people became citizens.
