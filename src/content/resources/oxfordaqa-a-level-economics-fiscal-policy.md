## What fiscal policy is

Fiscal policy means changing what the government spends and how it raises revenue, so that the economy moves towards chosen objectives. The budget balance is revenue minus spending: a negative figure is a budget deficit and a positive figure is a budget surplus.

Governments may run deficits or surpluses deliberately to influence aggregate demand. Expansionary policy raises AD through higher spending, lower taxes, or both, and pushes the budget towards deficit. Contractionary policy lowers AD and pushes the budget towards surplus.

The specification links spending and taxation to several objectives: an equitable distribution of income, protection of the environment, low unemployment and stable economic growth. A good answer names the objective first, then the tool.

## Macroeconomic and microeconomic functions

Macroeconomic functions work on the whole economy: managing the level of AD to limit unemployment and inflationary pressure, and supporting steady growth. Microeconomic functions work on particular markets and groups:

- Correcting market failure, for example taxing a good with a negative externality or subsidising one with a positive externality.
- Providing public goods and merit goods.
- Redistributing income through progressive taxes and benefit payments.

One instrument can serve both. A road-building programme adds to AD now (macro) and changes which resources serve transport rather than other uses (micro).

## Influencing aggregate demand

AD = C + I + G + (X - M). Government spending (G) is a direct component. Taxes work indirectly, because they alter household disposable income and firms' profits, and so consumption (C) and investment (I).

The multiplier makes the final change in national income larger than the first injection. Its value is 1/MPW, where the marginal propensity to withdraw is the sum MPS + MPT + MPM.

**Worked example (Country D, illustrative).** There is spare capacity. MPS = 0.10, MPT = 0.20, MPM = 0.20.

- MPW = 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.20 = 0.50, so the multiplier is 1 / 0.5 = 2.
- Government spending rises by 50. Income rises by 50 x 2 = 100.
- Instead, taxes are cut by 50, and households spend 70% of the extra income in the first round. The first-round injection is 50 x 0.7 = 35, so income rises by 35 x 2 = 70.

A rise in spending of the same size has the larger effect because none of it leaks into saving in the first round. That favours spending in a deep slump, although a tax cut can sometimes be arranged faster.

**Diagram in words.** On an AD/AS diagram, real output runs along the horizontal axis and the price level up the vertical. Expansionary policy shifts AD right, from AD1 to AD2. If the economy starts on the flat, spare-capacity stretch of short-run aggregate supply (SRAS), output rises with little price effect. Near full capacity, on the steep stretch, the price level rises more than output.

**Evaluation chain.** The result depends on four things.

1. Spare capacity: with a large negative output gap, extra AD raises output and employment. With a positive gap it mainly raises prices.
2. The multiplier: larger MPS, MPT or MPM shrinks the effect, and a country that imports much of what it buys leaks demand abroad.
3. Time lags: it takes time to see a problem, agree a policy and spend the money, and by then the economy may have moved on.
4. Confidence: if households expect higher taxes later, they may save a tax cut.

## Influencing aggregate supply

Fiscal policy can also raise productive capacity, shifting long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) right and short-run supply with it.

- Spending on education, training, health and infrastructure raises the quantity and quality of labour and capital and lowers firms' costs.
- Lower taxes on profits can raise investment. Lower income tax may raise the reward for working, so more people take jobs or work longer.
- Subsidies can cut costs of production in a chosen sector.

The effect is slow. Potential output rises and inflationary pressure is lower at any level of output. Evaluation points: the spending has an opportunity cost, projects can be badly chosen, and a tax cut might be spent on leisure or saved rather than turned into extra work.

## Allocation of resources and pattern of activity

Spending and taxes move resources between uses, and change which sectors grow.

**Worked example (Gadget fizz market, illustrative).** Demand is Qd = 150 - 10P and supply is Qs = 30 + 20P.

| Case | Price paid by buyers | Price received by sellers | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| No tax | 4 | 4 | 110 |
| Tax of 3 per unit on sellers | 6 | 3 | 90 |

The tax shifts supply up by 3, so supply becomes Qs = 30 + 20(P - 3). Setting 150 - 10P = 20P - 30 gives P = 6 and Q = 90. Buyers pay 2 more per unit and sellers keep 1 less (3 instead of 4). Revenue is 3 x 90 = 270, of which buyers bear 2 x 90 = 180 and sellers 1 x 90 = 90. Buyers bear more because their demand is the less responsive side of this market.

If the drink creates an external cost of 3 per unit, the tax moves the price closer to the full social cost, so output falls towards the efficient level and resources move to other uses. A subsidy does the reverse for a good with an external benefit. The revenue can fund spending elsewhere.

A higher tax on one product shifts demand to substitutes. Spending on, say, renewable energy shifts the pattern of activity towards that sector. Evaluation: the gain depends on the tax matching the real external cost, and when demand is inelastic the tax raises revenue but changes quantity only a little.

## Direct and indirect taxes

- Direct taxes are paid by the person or firm on whom they fall, out of income or wealth: income tax, corporation tax.
- Indirect taxes are levied on spending, so they are collected by sellers: a sales tax, an excise duty on fuel.

## Progressive, proportional and regressive taxes

A tax is progressive if the average rate (tax paid / income x 100) rises with income, proportional if it is constant, and regressive if it falls.

**Worked example (Country A, illustrative).** Nothing is paid on the first 12,000 of income. The rate is 25% on income from 12,000 to 45,000 and 45% above that.

| Income | Tax calculation | Tax | Average rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9,000 | below allowance | 0 | 0% |
| 30,000 | 25% x 18,000 | 4,500 | 15% |
| 80,000 | 25% x 33,000 + 45% x 35,000 | 24,000 | 30% |

The average rate rises with income, so this tax is progressive. The marginal rate is 25% at 30,000 and 45% at 80,000. Compare a flat 18% on all income: that would be proportional.

**An indirect tax can be regressive.** A 10% sales tax is a fixed share of spending, but lower-income households spend a larger share of income. Household L has income 22,000 and spends 90% (19,800). Household H has income 90,000 and spends 55% (49,500). If prices include the tax, the tax is one-eleventh of the spending total.

- L pays 19,800 / 11 = 1,800, which is 8.2% of income.
- H pays 49,500 / 11 = 4,500, which is 5.0% of income.

The tax takes a falling share of income, so it is regressive against income. A system that leans on indirect taxes can therefore be less equitable than one that leans on progressive income tax.

## Budget balance and national debt

The budget balance describes a single year, while the national debt is the accumulated total of past borrowing. Each deficit adds to the debt, and a surplus can reduce it.

**Worked example (Country B, illustrative).** GDP is 3,200, spending is 870, revenue is 822, and opening debt is 1,280.

- Deficit = 870 - 822 = 48, which is 48 / 3,200 = 1.5% of GDP.
- Closing debt = 1,280 + 48 = 1,328. The opening debt/GDP ratio is 1,280 / 3,200 = 40%.
- If the deficit stays at 48 for three years and GDP is unchanged, debt reaches 1,424 and the ratio is 44.5%.
- If nominal GDP grows 3.5% a year, debt still reaches 1,424 but GDP reaches 3,547.9, so the ratio is 40.1%.

A deficit raises the debt, but growth can hold the ratio nearly steady. A surplus of 25 in a later year would cut debt from 1,328 to 1,303. The cost of a high debt is interest payments, which use money that could pay for other spending, and possible worry among lenders.

## Cyclical and structural influences

The cyclical part of the balance moves automatically with the economic cycle: in a downturn tax revenue falls and unemployment benefits rise. The structural part is what would remain at potential output.

**Worked example (Country C, illustrative).** Potential GDP is 2,400 and actual GDP is 2,250. Revenue is 28% of GDP. At potential output spending would be 690, but at actual output it is 20 higher.

- Output gap = (2,250 - 2,400) / 2,400 = -6.25%.
- Revenue falls from 672 to 630. Spending is 710.
- Actual deficit = 710 - 630 = 80. Structural deficit = 690 - 672 = 18. Cyclical deficit = 62.

When recovery closes the gap, 62 of the deficit disappears. The other 18 remains until taxes or spending policy change.

## Common errors

- Describing a deficit as the debt. One is a yearly flow and the other a stock.
- Declaring a tax progressive just because the sum paid grows. Check the average rate.
- Using the multiplier without saying there is spare capacity.
- Calling every deficit bad. A cyclical deficit during a slump is normal.
- Giving only one side of an evaluation: always add a time lag, a cost, or a limit.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is fiscal policy?**
Fiscal policy means deliberate changes to government spending and taxation to move the economy towards chosen objectives. Expansionary policy raises aggregate demand and pushes the budget towards deficit; contractionary policy does the reverse.

**What are the microeconomic functions of fiscal policy?**
Fiscal policy can correct market failure, for example taxing goods with negative externalities and subsidising those with positive externalities, provide public and merit goods, and redistribute income through progressive taxes and benefits.

**Why can an indirect tax be regressive?**
A sales tax is a fixed share of spending, but lower-income households spend a larger share of their income. So the tax takes a larger percentage of a poorer household's income than a richer one's.

**Is a budget deficit always bad?**
No. A cyclical deficit during a slump is normal, because tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises automatically. The cost of a high debt is the interest payments, which use money that could fund other spending.

**Which section of the OxfordAQA 9640 specification covers fiscal policy?**
Fiscal policy is section 3.2.4.2 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Economics (9640) specification. It is International AS content, with monetary and supply-side policies in sections 3.2.4.1 and 3.2.4.3.
