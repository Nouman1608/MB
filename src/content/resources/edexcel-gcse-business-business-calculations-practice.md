## Questions

**1.** Define the term "gross profit". **[2]**

**2.** A garden centre has sales revenue of £68,500 and cost of sales of £41,100. Calculate its gross profit. **[2]**

**3.** A toy shop has gross profit of £52,000, other operating expenses of £38,750 and interest of £2,250. Calculate its net profit. **[2]**

**4.** A furniture maker has sales revenue of £240,000, cost of sales of £156,000, other operating expenses of £62,400 and interest of £3,600.

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit margin. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the net profit margin. **[3]**

**5.** A café buys a coffee machine for £30,000. It expects profits from the machine of £4,500, £7,000, £8,500 and £10,000 in the next four years. Calculate the average rate of return. **[4]**

**6.** The table shows part of the accounts of a gift shop.

| Year | Sales revenue (£) | Cost of sales (£) | Gross profit (£) | Gross profit margin (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 150,000 | 97,500 | 52,500 | 35 |
| 2 | 180,000 | 120,600 | ? | ? |
| 3 | 210,000 | ? | 73,500 | 35 |

**(a)** Complete the table by working out the three missing values. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify the year with the lowest gross profit margin. **[1]**

**7.** Explain one reason why a profit margin is more useful than a profit figure when comparing a small shop with a large shop. **[3]**

**8.** Rowan Lane Bakery gave these figures for two years.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | £120,000 | £135,000 |
| Cost of sales | £54,000 | £64,125 |
| Other operating expenses | £51,000 | £58,300 |
| Interest | £1,200 | £1,200 |

In Year 2 the bakery began a half-price offer on cakes every Saturday afternoon. Its butter supplier also raised its prices.

**(a)** Calculate the net profit margin for each year, to one decimal place. **[4]**
**(b)** Analyse how the two changes in Year 2 may have affected the bakery's gross profit margin. **[4]**

**9.** Linden Print Studio has £30,000 in savings and is choosing between two printing presses. Press X costs £40,000 and is expected to give total profit of £70,000 over five years, so £10,000 would need to be borrowed. Press Y costs £25,000 and is expected to give total profit of £30,000 over three years.

**(a)** Calculate the average rate of return for each press. **[4]**
**(b)** Justify which press the studio should buy. **[6]**

**10.** Merrow Gifts owns three shops.

| Shop | Sales revenue (£) | Net profit (£) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 80,000 | 6,400 |
| B | 125,000 | 11,250 |
| C | 52,000 | 5,200 |

**(a)** Calculate the net profit margin of each shop and identify the shop with the highest margin. **[4]**
**(b)** Outline one reason why the owner may still regard Shop B as more successful than Shop C. **[2]**

**11.** Fennel Court Deli has sales revenue of £75,000 and a gross profit margin of 32%. The owner wants a margin of 40% on the same sales revenue.

**(a)** Calculate the deli's present gross profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cost of sales needed to reach a gross profit margin of 40%. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one way the deli could lower its cost of sales. **[3]**

**12.** Copperfield Lane Kayaks hires out kayaks to visitors. Its sales revenue is £210,000, with a gross profit margin of 42% and a net profit margin of 6.0%. The owner could spend £48,000 on a second fleet, using savings that now earn 4% a year. The forecast profits from the fleet are £6,000, £9,000, £10,500 and £10,500 over four years. Demand depends on the weather and the number of visitors. Evaluate whether the owner should buy the second fleet. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Gross profit is the profit left [1] after cost of sales has been taken away from sales revenue [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both parts of the definition, sales revenue and cost of sales.

**2.** Gross profit = sales revenue − cost of sales = £68,500 − £41,100 [1] = **£27,400** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction, because workings should be given.

**3.** Costs to deduct = £38,750 + £2,250 = £41,000 [1]. Net profit = £52,000 − £41,000 = **£11,000** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The net profit formula takes off both other operating expenses and interest. Answers that miss interest give £13,250.

**4. (a)** Gross profit = £240,000 − £156,000 = £84,000 [1]. Gross profit margin = £84,000 ÷ £240,000 × 100 [1] = **35.0%** [1]
**(b)** Net profit = £84,000 − £62,400 − £3,600 = £18,000 [1]. Net profit margin = £18,000 ÷ £240,000 × 100 [1] = **7.5%** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Both margins divide by sales revenue. Dividing the net profit by the gross profit would give 21.4%, which is a different measure.

**5.** Total profit = 4,500 + 7,000 + 8,500 + 10,000 = £30,000 [1]. Average annual profit = £30,000 ÷ 4 = £7,500 [1]. Average rate of return = £7,500 ÷ £30,000 × 100 [1] = **25%** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the cost of the machine, and count the years given.

**6. (a)** Year 2 gross profit = £180,000 − £120,600 = **£59,400** [1]. Year 2 margin = £59,400 ÷ £180,000 × 100 = **33%** [1]. Year 3 cost of sales = £210,000 − £73,500 = **£136,500** [1]
**(b)** **Year 2** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Work each missing value from the formulae, then check that the table is consistent.

**7.** A margin is a percentage of sales revenue [1], so the size of the business no longer affects the figure [1]. A small shop with lower profit in pounds can then be compared fairly with a large shop [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An Explain answer states one fact and develops it twice.

**8. (a)** Year 1: gross profit = £120,000 − £54,000 = £66,000; net profit = £66,000 − £51,000 − £1,200 = £13,800 [1]; margin = £13,800 ÷ £120,000 × 100 = **11.5%** [1].
Year 2: gross profit = £135,000 − £64,125 = £70,875; net profit = £70,875 − £58,300 − £1,200 = £11,375 [1]; margin = £11,375 ÷ £135,000 × 100 = **8.4%** [1]
**(b)** The half-price Saturday offer lowers the average price per cake [1], so each £1 of sales revenue is spread over more cost of sales and the gross margin falls [1]. Dearer butter raises the cost of sales, which rose 18.75% against a 12.5% rise in sales revenue [1], so the gross margin fell from 55.0% to 52.5% [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An Analyse answer should use the bakery's figures and link each cause to the margin.

**9. (a)** Press X: £70,000 ÷ 5 = £14,000 [1]; £14,000 ÷ £40,000 × 100 = **35%** [1]. Press Y: £30,000 ÷ 3 = £10,000 [1]; £10,000 ÷ £25,000 × 100 = **40%** [1]
**(b)** Press Y has the higher average rate of return, 40% against 35% [1]. It costs less than the £30,000 savings, so no borrowing and no interest is needed [1]. Its forecast runs for three years, not five, so there is less time for the forecast to go wrong [1]. Press X gives the larger total profit (£70,000 against £30,000) [1] and the larger average annual profit (£14,000 against £10,000) [1]. Judgement: Press Y is the better choice because it gives the better return on each £1 spent without the cost of borrowing, although Press X would give more profit if the forecast is reliable [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A Justify answer should recommend one option and use figures for both options. Rate alone, or total profit alone, leaves the argument one-sided.

**10. (a)** Shop A: £6,400 ÷ £80,000 × 100 = 8% [1]. Shop B: £11,250 ÷ £125,000 × 100 = 9% [1]. Shop C: £5,200 ÷ £52,000 × 100 = 10% [1]. Highest margin: **Shop C** [1]
**(b)** Shop B earns the largest net profit in pounds, £11,250 [1], because its sales revenue is much larger than Shop C's, even though its margin is lower [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A margin and a profit figure measure different things. An Outline links two points, here the larger sales revenue and the larger profit.

**11. (a)** Gross profit = 32% × £75,000 [1] = **£24,000** [1]
**(b)** Gross profit needed = 40% × £75,000 = £30,000 [1]. Cost of sales = £75,000 − £30,000 [1] = **£45,000** [1]
**(c)** The deli could find a cheaper supplier for its stock [1]. This lowers the cost of each item it sells [1], so cost of sales falls from £51,000 towards the £45,000 target and gross profit rises [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Rearrange the formula, working backwards from the target margin.

**12.** Total profit from the fleet = £36,000, so average annual profit = £36,000 ÷ 4 = £9,000 [1]. Average rate of return = £9,000 ÷ £48,000 × 100 = 18.75% [1]. The savings earn only 4% a year, so the fleet offers a much higher return for the same money [1]. The business now earns a net profit of £12,600 (6.0% of £210,000), so an extra £9,000 a year on average would be a large boost [1]. Against this, the profits are forecasts and depend on weather and visitors [1], and the average hides that the first year is expected to be lowest at £6,000 [1]. Spending all the savings leaves less cash to deal with a poor season [1]. Judgement: the owner should buy, because the return is well above 4% and the profit gain is large compared with present profit [1]. The owner should do so cautiously, perhaps by checking how bookings held up in wet summers, because the forecast may not be met [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An Evaluate answer needs a supported conclusion. Quote the 18.75% against the 4% alternative, give at least one limitation, then say which factor outweighs the other.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by gross profit, not by sales revenue, when finding a net profit margin.
- Forgetting interest in a net profit calculation.
- Dividing total profit, not average annual profit, by the cost of the investment.
- Using the wrong number of years in the average.
- Writing a margin as a decimal or leaving off the percentage sign.
- Describing a fall from 40% to 37% as a "3% fall" instead of 3 percentage points.
- Answering Analyse, Justify or Evaluate with only a calculation and no comment on what it means for the business.
- Recommending an option without comparing it with the alternative.
