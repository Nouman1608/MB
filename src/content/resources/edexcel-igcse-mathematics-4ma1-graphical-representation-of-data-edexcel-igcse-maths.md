This study guide goes deeper into section 6.1, Graphical representation of data, of topic 6 (Statistics and probability) in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017). Section 6.1 has Foundation Tier content, which both tiers need, and Higher Tier content on histograms and cumulative frequency, marked **Higher tier only** below.

## What the specification asks

| Tier | Statement |
|---|---|
| Foundation 6.1 A | use different methods of presenting data (note: pictograms, bar charts and pie charts, and only two-way tables) |
| Foundation 6.1 B | use appropriate methods of tabulation to enable the construction of statistical diagrams |
| Foundation 6.1 C | interpret statistical diagrams |
| Higher 6.1 A | construct and interpret histograms (note: for continuous variables with unequal class intervals) |
| Higher 6.1 B | construct cumulative frequency diagrams from tabulated data |
| Higher 6.1 C | use cumulative frequency diagrams |

The Foundation note lists the diagrams exactly, and says "only two-way tables": other kinds of table display are not required.

## Pictograms (both tiers)

A pictogram uses a symbol to stand for a stated number of items. The key is essential: without it the chart means nothing.

**Worked example 1.** In a pictogram, one symbol represents 8 visitors.

- Monday shows 2¾ symbols: 2 × 8 + ¾ × 8 = 16 + 6 = **22 visitors**.
- Tuesday had 30 visitors: 30 ÷ 8 = 3.75, so draw **3¾ symbols**.

Choose a key that divides the data neatly; with 8, a quarter symbol is 2 visitors.

## Bar charts (both tiers)

Bars have equal widths and equal gaps, and the height shows frequency. A **dual** (side-by-side) bar chart puts two bars for each category, one for each group, with a key. Read a value by following the top of the bar across to the axis, after working out what each square on the axis is worth, exactly as for a measuring scale.

When you interpret a dual bar chart, compare the two groups category by category, and use numbers: "Year 10 chose football 6 more times than Year 11" is better than "more Year 10 chose football".

## Pie charts (both tiers)

```
angle for one item = 360° ÷ total
sector angle = frequency × angle for one item
frequency = sector angle ÷ angle for one item
```

**Worked example 2 (reading backwards).** In a pie chart, the sector for "bus" has an angle of 72° and represents 18 students. How many students are in the survey?

```
18 students ↔ 72°
1 student ↔ 4°
total = 360 ÷ 4 = 90 students
```

**Worked example 3 (comparing pie charts).** School A has 200 students and school B has 600. In school A's pie chart the cycling sector is 90°; in school B's it is 45°.

```
School A: 90/360 × 200 = 50 students cycle
School B: 45/360 × 600 = 75 students cycle
```

School B's sector is smaller but it has **more** cyclists. A pie chart shows **proportions**, not numbers, so two pie charts can only compare numbers if you know both totals. A larger proportion of school A cycles (25% against 12.5%).

## Two-way tables (both tiers)

Every row and every column must add to its total. Fill in whichever cell can be found from two known values, then repeat.

**Worked example 4.** 60 students travel to school by bus, car or on foot. 28 are girls. 15 walk, and 9 of these are girls. 12 boys come by car. 20 students come by bus.

| | Bus | Car | Walk | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boys | 14 | 12 | 6 | 32 |
| Girls | 6 | 13 | 9 | 28 |
| Total | 20 | 25 | 15 | 60 |

Order of working: boys = 60 − 28 = 32; boys walking = 15 − 9 = 6; boys by bus = 32 − 12 − 6 = 14; girls by bus = 20 − 14 = 6; girls by car = 28 − 9 − 6 = 13; car total = 12 + 13 = 25. Check the bottom row: 20 + 25 + 15 = 60. ✓

## Tabulation (both tiers)

Statement 6.1 B is about organising raw data so that a diagram can be drawn. For a list of values, use a tally and frequency table; for many different values, group them.

- Classes must not overlap. Write continuous classes as inequalities, such as 10 < t ≤ 20, so it is clear that 20 belongs in this class and not the next.
- Classes must cover every value with no gaps.
- Check the frequencies add to the number of data values.

Classes written as "0–10, 10–20, 20–30" are poor because a value of exactly 10 or 20 could go in two classes.

## Histograms (Higher tier only)

For continuous data with **unequal** class widths, the **area** of each bar represents the frequency, so the vertical axis is frequency density.

```
frequency density = frequency ÷ class width
frequency = frequency density × class width
```

**Worked example 5 (reading a histogram).** A histogram of heights h cm has these bars:

| Height h (cm) | 0 < h ≤ 20 | 20 < h ≤ 30 | 30 < h ≤ 35 | 35 < h ≤ 50 | 50 < h ≤ 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency density | 0.5 | 2.4 | 5.2 | 1.6 | 0.3 |
| Class width | 20 | 10 | 5 | 15 | 30 |
| Frequency | 10 | 24 | 26 | 24 | 9 |

Total = 10 + 24 + 26 + 24 + 9 = 93.

**Estimate the number with h > 40.** The class 35 < h ≤ 50 is only partly included: the part from 40 to 50 has width 10, so it contributes 10 × 1.6 = 16. Add the whole of the last class:

```
16 + 9 = 25 plants (an estimate, assuming values are spread evenly within the class)
```

The tallest bar (30 < h ≤ 35) is the most densely packed class, but it is not the class with the most items unless the widths are equal.

## Cumulative frequency (Higher tier only)

Add the frequencies as a running total, plot each total at the **upper class boundary**, start at (lowest boundary, 0), and join the points with a smooth curve or straight line segments.

**Worked example 6.** Journey distances d km for 100 people:

| Distance d (km) | 0 < d ≤ 10 | 10 < d ≤ 20 | 20 < d ≤ 30 | 30 < d ≤ 40 | 40 < d ≤ 50 | 50 < d ≤ 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 8 | 14 | 22 | 30 | 16 | 10 |
| Cumulative frequency | 8 | 22 | 44 | 74 | 90 | 100 |

Plot (0, 0), (10, 8), (20, 22), (30, 44), (40, 74), (50, 90) and (60, 100).

**Estimate how many travelled more than 35 km.** Read up from 35 to the graph and across. With straight segments, 35 is halfway from 30 to 40, so the cumulative frequency is about 44 + ½ × 30 = 59. The number travelling **more** than 35 km is about 100 − 59 = **41**. With a curve your reading may differ slightly; examiners allow a range for graph readings.

## Exam technique

- **"Complete the table"** or **"complete the histogram"**: use the information already drawn to find the scale first, for example from a bar whose frequency you know.
- **"Compare"**: make two comparisons with numbers, in context.
- **"Estimate"** for histograms and cumulative frequency: show the partial-class or graph-reading working.

## Common mistakes

- Missing the key on a pictogram, or reading a part symbol wrongly.
- Comparing the angles of two pie charts with different totals as if they were numbers.
- Plotting cumulative frequency at midpoints instead of upper boundaries.
- Using frequency instead of frequency density for unequal widths.
- Taking the tallest histogram bar as the class with the highest frequency.
