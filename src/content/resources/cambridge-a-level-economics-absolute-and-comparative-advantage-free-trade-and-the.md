## 6.1.1 Absolute and comparative advantage

**Absolute advantage** means being able to make a good with fewer resources than a rival country needs, or to make more of it from the same resources. It is a comparison of efficiency in terms of resources used.

A country has a **comparative advantage** in a good if it can produce it at a lower opportunity cost than another country. It is a comparison of what must be given up. The two ideas can point in different directions, and this is the key insight of the theory: a country with an absolute advantage in everything still has a comparative advantage in only some goods.

### Worked example 1: finding both advantages

Country Alpha and Country Beta each make tea and cloth using only labour. The table shows labour-hours needed per unit. Alpha has 240 labour-hours and Beta has 432.

| | Hours per unit of tea | Hours per unit of cloth |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 5 | 8 |
| Beta | 9 | 12 |

Step 1, absolute advantage. Alpha needs fewer hours for both goods, so it has an absolute advantage in both.

Step 2, maximum output if all labour makes one good. Alpha: 240 ÷ 5 = 48 tea or 240 ÷ 8 = 30 cloth. Beta: 432 ÷ 9 = 48 tea or 432 ÷ 12 = 36 cloth.

Step 3, opportunity costs. In Alpha, 1 tea costs 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625 cloth and 1 cloth costs 8 ÷ 5 = 1.6 tea. In Beta, 1 tea costs 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75 cloth and 1 cloth costs 12 ÷ 9 = 1.33 tea.

Step 4, comparative advantage. Alpha gives up less cloth per tea (0.625 against 0.75), so Alpha has the comparative advantage in tea. Beta gives up fewer tea per cloth (1.33 against 1.6), so Beta has the comparative advantage in cloth.

A check: with two goods and two countries, if one country has the lower opportunity cost of tea, the other must have the lower opportunity cost of cloth. If both countries had identical opportunity costs, there would be no comparative advantage and no gain from specialising.

## 6.1.2 Specialisation, free trade and the trading possibility curve

**Specialisation** means a country concentrates on the goods in which it has a comparative advantage. **Free trade** (trade liberalisation) means exchange without tariffs, quotas or other barriers. Together they let countries produce more in total and consume more than they could alone.

### Worked example 2: the gains from specialising

Before trade, each country spends half its hours on each good.

| | Tea | Cloth |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha (120 hours each) | 24 | 15 |
| Beta (216 hours each) | 24 | 18 |
| World total | 48 | 33 |

After specialising completely, Alpha makes 48 tea and Beta makes 36 cloth. World output is 48 tea and 36 cloth, which is 3 more cloth with no loss of tea.

The gain is shared through trade. The price of tea in cloth must lie between the two opportunity costs: above 0.625 (or Alpha would rather make cloth itself) and below 0.75 (or Beta would rather make tea itself). Take 2/3 cloth per tea, so 3 tea exchange for 2 cloth. Alpha sells 24 tea and receives 24 × 2/3 = 16 cloth.

| After trade | Tea | Cloth | Change from before |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 48 − 24 = 24 | 16 | +1 cloth |
| Beta | 24 | 36 − 16 = 20 | +2 cloth |

Both countries consume more cloth and no less tea (the 3 extra cloth is shared 1 and 2). Neither could do this by producing at home alone.

### The trading possibility curve

A **trading possibility curve** (TPC) shows the combinations of two goods a country can consume after specialising and trading at given terms. It lies outside the production possibility curve (PPC) when the terms of trade differ from the domestic opportunity cost.

For Alpha, the PPC is a straight line from 48 tea (no cloth) to 30 cloth (no tea). The TPC starts at the same 48 tea on the horizontal axis but ends at 48 × 2/3 = 32 cloth on the vertical axis, because all the tea could be swapped for cloth. It is a steeper-than-PPC line that lies outside it everywhere except the tea-only point. Alpha's after-trade point (24 tea, 16 cloth) is on the TPC. On the PPC, 24 tea would allow only 15 cloth, so the point lies outside the PPC: trade has pushed consumption beyond what Alpha could produce.

The better the price Alpha gets for its tea, the more cloth each unit buys and the further out the TPC swings. At 0.75 cloth per tea (Beta's opportunity cost) Alpha would take the whole gain.

### Wider benefits of free trade

- Greater variety and lower prices for consumers, because imports compete with domestic goods.
- Larger markets let firms gain economies of scale and lower their unit costs.
- Competition pushes firms to improve efficiency and innovate.
- Access to resources and inputs a country lacks.
- Higher export revenue and, through the multiplier, higher income and employment, although this is not guaranteed.

## 6.1.3 Exports, imports and the terms of trade

**Exports** are goods and services sold to other countries. **Imports** are goods and services bought from other countries. The **terms of trade** (ToT) show the rate at which a country's exports exchange for its imports.

### Measurement

Terms of trade index = (index of average export prices ÷ index of average import prices) × 100.

If export prices rise faster than import prices, the index rises: the terms of trade have **improved**, because each unit of exports buys more imports. If import prices rise faster, the index falls and the terms of trade have **worsened** (deteriorated).

### Worked example 3: measuring a change

Country A (illustrative), base year index 100 for both prices.

| Year | Export price index | Import price index | Terms of trade |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 114 | 95 | 114 ÷ 95 × 100 = 120.0 |
| 2 | 114 | 120 | 114 ÷ 120 × 100 = 95.0 |

The terms of trade fell from 120.0 to 95.0. The percentage change is (95.0 − 120.0) ÷ 120.0 × 100 = −20.8%. Export prices did not move, so the whole change is due to dearer imports. Say "worsened" and give the figures.

### Causes of a change

| Cause | Effect on terms of trade |
|---|---|
| Rise in world demand for a country's main export | Export prices rise: improve |
| Rise in the price of imported oil or other key inputs | Import prices rise: worsen |
| Appreciation of the exchange rate (prices set in home currency) | Import prices in home currency fall: improve |
| Depreciation of the exchange rate | Import prices in home currency rise: worsen |
| Higher productivity or lower costs in export industries | Lower export prices, so ToT may worsen even though competitiveness improves |
| Higher inflation at home than abroad | Export prices rise faster than import prices: improve at first, but competitiveness falls |
| Increase in the supply of a commodity from many producers | Export prices fall for commodity exporters: worsen |

### Impact of a change

An improvement means the country gets more imports for the same exports. Suppose it exports 400 units at 18 currency units (cu) while imports cost 15 cu each. The exports buy 400 × 18 ÷ 15 = 480 import units. Before the rise in export prices (when export prices were 15 cu) they bought only 400.

Do not stop there. The effect on export **revenue** depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports. Suppose export prices rise 20%:

- If demand is elastic, with PED of −1.8, volume falls 36%. Revenue index = 1.20 × 0.64 = 0.768, a 23.2% fall. The improved terms of trade then worsen the current account.
- If demand is inelastic, with PED of −0.5, volume falls 10%. Revenue index = 1.20 × 0.90 = 1.08, an 8% rise.

Other impacts to consider: cheaper imports lower costs for firms and reduce imported inflation, but a worsening of the terms of trade means a country must export more to buy the same imports, which reduces real income and living standards. A fall in the terms of trade is not always bad: if it comes from higher productivity, firms are more competitive and sell more.

## 6.1.4 Limitations of the theories

The theory rests on assumptions that rarely hold. State the limitation, then say what it does to the case for specialisation.

- **Transport costs and trade costs.** They reduce or remove the gain. In example 2 the total gain was 3 cloth. If shipping the goods costs the equivalent of 3 cloth, the net gain is zero; if it costs more, trade makes the countries worse off in total.
- **Increasing opportunity costs.** The model uses constant costs, but resources are not equally suited to every good. As Country H shifts resources into cloth, it gives up more tea for each extra unit:

| Step | Extra cloth | Tea given up | Tea per cloth |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 10 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 20 | 15 | 0.75 |
| 3 | 20 | 25 | 1.25 |

  The comparative advantage shrinks as output rises, so specialisation tends to stop short of complete.
- **Factors are not perfectly mobile.** Workers may take time to retrain, so unemployment can result from specialising.
- **Static analysis.** Comparative advantage can change over time with education, technology and investment, which is why some countries protect infant industries.
- **Overspecialisation and dependence.** A country relying on one export is exposed to price falls, shortages of imports and political disputes. Example 3 shows how fast the terms of trade can move.
- **Other assumptions.** Perfect competition, full employment, perfect information, identical goods and no trade barriers are assumed. Real trade involves exchange-rate changes, differences in quality, imperfect competition and external costs such as pollution from transport.
- **Distribution.** Gains are not shared equally. Some workers, industries and regions lose.

## Common errors

- Saying a country with an absolute advantage in both goods should not trade. It still gains from specialising where its opportunity cost is lower.
- Judging comparative advantage by which country makes more of a good. Compare opportunity costs.
- Reversing the terms of trade formula. Exports sit on the top.
- Calling a rise in the index "worse". A higher figure is an improvement.
- Ignoring the elasticity of demand when assessing the impact of a change in the terms of trade.
- Listing assumptions without saying how each limits the benefits.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between absolute and comparative advantage?**
Absolute advantage means producing a good with fewer resources than another country. Comparative advantage means producing it at a lower opportunity cost.

**Should a country with an absolute advantage in both goods still trade?**
Yes. It can still gain by specialising in the good where its comparative advantage lies and trading for the other.

**How is the terms of trade measured?**
The terms of trade index is the index of average export prices divided by the index of average import prices, multiplied by 100. A rise means the terms of trade have improved.

**What causes the terms of trade to change?**
Changes in world demand for exports, the prices of imported inputs such as oil, exchange rate movements, productivity in export industries, relative inflation, and the supply of commodities.

**What are the limitations of comparative advantage theory?**
Transport and trade costs, increasing opportunity costs, imperfect factor mobility, static analysis, risks of overspecialisation, other unrealistic assumptions, and unequal distribution of the gains.
