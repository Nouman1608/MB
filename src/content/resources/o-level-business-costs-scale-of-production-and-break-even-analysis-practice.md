## Questions

**1.** Define the term fixed costs. **[2]**

**2.** Vandrell Bakery has four costs: monthly oven rental, flour, packaging boxes and the manager's monthly salary. Identify which two are fixed costs and which two are variable costs. **[4]**

**3.** Ravenscombe Pottery made 12 000 bowls last month at a total cost of 84 000. This month it made 24 000 bowls at a total cost of 120 000.
**(a)** Calculate the average cost per bowl for each month. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the average cost fell when output doubled. **[3]**

**4.** Ledworth Ceramics sells 5 000 mugs a month at 6 each. The variable cost is 4.20 per mug. Fixed costs of 11 000 a month are charged to the mug line, but the business pays them whether or not mugs are made.
**(a)** Calculate the contribution from the mug line. **[2]**
**(b)** Justify whether Ledworth should stop making mugs. **[4]**

**5.** Salthaven Noodles is growing quickly.
**(a)** Identify the type of economy of scale in each case: (i) a supplier gives a lower price per kilo for very large flour orders; (ii) a bank offers the larger firm a lower interest rate; (iii) a high-speed packing line is installed that is only worth buying at high output. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how one advertising campaign could lower average cost as sales grow. **[2]**

**6.** Salthaven Noodles has grown from 60 to 900 employees across three sites. Managers say that instructions are often misunderstood and that staff turnover has risen. Explain **two** diseconomies of scale the firm may be suffering from. **[4]**

**7.** Vessmoor Pizza sells pizzas at 12 each. Variable cost is 5 per pizza and fixed costs are 28 000 a month. It expects to sell 5 000 pizzas next month.
**(a)** Calculate the break-even output. **[3]**
**(b)** Work out the margin of safety, first in pizzas and then as a percentage of the expected sales. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what the margin of safety tells the owner. **[2]**

**8.** Kellsworth Crafts sells one product at 20 each. These figures are for the break-even chart.

| Output | 0 | 200 | 400 | 600 | 800 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs | 6 000 | 6 000 | 6 000 | 6 000 | 6 000 |
| Total costs | 6 000 | 8 000 | 10 000 | 12 000 | 14 000 |
| Total revenue | 0 | 4 000 | 8 000 | 12 000 | 16 000 |

**(a)** Identify the break-even output. **[1]**
**(b)** State the variable cost per unit. **[1]**
**(c)** The firm now plans to make 750 units. Calculate the margin of safety. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the profit or loss at 400 units and at 800 units. **[2]**

**9.** Zelmoor Print sells at 25 per unit. Variable cost is 15 per unit and fixed costs are 9 000. The landlord raises the rent so that fixed costs become 10 500.
**(a)** Complete the revised total costs at outputs of 0, 600 and 1 200 units. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the new break-even output. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the lines on a break-even chart change, and what happens to the break-even point. **[2]**

**10.** Oswinne Cycles sells 4 000 bikes a year at 40 each. Variable cost is 24 per bike and fixed costs are 48 000. The owner is considering a price of 44, which she expects to cut sales to 3 600 bikes.
**(a)** Calculate the current break-even output. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the break-even output at the new price. **[2]**
**(c)** Justify whether she should raise the price. **[6]**

**11.** Plimmerton Foods uses a break-even chart for its tomato sauce. Explain **two** limitations of relying on this chart when planning production. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Costs that do not change [1] when the level of output changes [1].
*Examiner insight:* A good definition states how the cost behaves when output changes. Rent as an example alone does not define the term.

**2.** Fixed: oven rental [1] and manager's salary [1]. Variable: flour [1] and packaging boxes [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use the words in the stem, and say which group each item belongs to so that every item is clearly matched.

**3. (a)** Month 1: 84 000 / 12 000 = **7.00 per bowl** [1]. Month 2: 120 000 / 24 000 = **5.00 per bowl** [1].
**(b)** Total cost did not double (it rose from 84 000 to 120 000) [1]. Fixed costs such as rent stay the same, so they are spread over more bowls [1], which lowers the cost per bowl [1].
*Examiner insight:* Write out each division, because the working shows the method. In part (b), say average cost (cost per bowl) fell, not just "costs", because total cost went up.

**4. (a)** Revenue = 5 000 x 6 = 30 000 and variable costs = 5 000 x 4.20 = 21 000 [1]. Contribution = **9 000** [1].
**(b)** Continuing: contribution 9 000 - fixed costs 11 000 = a loss of 2 000 [1]. Stopping: no contribution but the 11 000 is still paid, a loss of 11 000 [1]. So **continue making mugs**, because the line contributes 9 000 towards fixed costs [1]. This could change if the fixed costs could be avoided, or if the resources could earn more elsewhere [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Justify" asks for a decision backed by figures. A reason such as "it makes a loss" is weak unless you compare the loss from continuing with the loss from stopping.

**5. (a)** (i) Purchasing [1]. (ii) Financial [1]. (iii) Technical [1].
**(b)** The cost of the campaign is a fixed amount [1], so as more units are sold it is shared out over more units, lowering the advertising cost per unit [1].
*Examiner insight:* Naming the type is enough for part (a). For part (b), link the idea to cost per unit rather than just saying advertising is cheaper.

**6.** Poor communication: with three sites and many layers, messages are changed or delayed, so instructions are misunderstood [1], causing errors and rework that raise average cost [1]. Lack of commitment from employees: in a large firm workers may feel remote from decisions, so turnover rises [1], and recruiting and training replacements raises average cost [1]. (Weak coordination between the three sites would also be credited.)
*Examiner insight:* Take each cause from the case and then finish by explaining the effect on average cost. A list of diseconomies with no link to the firm gives little to reward.

**7. (a)** Contribution per pizza = 12 - 5 = 7 [1]. Break-even = 28 000 / 7 [1] = **4 000 pizzas** [1].
**(b)** Margin of safety = 5 000 - 4 000 = **1 000 pizzas** [1]. As a percentage: 1 000 / 5 000 x 100 = **20%** [1].
**(c)** Sales can fall by 1 000 pizzas (20%) below the forecast [1] before the restaurant makes a loss, so the business has some protection against falling demand [1].
*Examiner insight:* Write the formula first. Part (c) asks for meaning, so a sentence that repeats the number without saying what it shows is unlikely to be enough.

**8. (a)** **600 units**, where total costs and total revenue are both 12 000 [1].
**(b)** (14 000 - 6 000) / 800 = **10 per unit**; also, total costs rise by 2 000 for each extra 200 units [1].
**(c)** 750 - 600 = **150 units** [1], so output could fall by 150 units before a loss is made [1].
**(d)** At 400 units revenue is 8 000 and total costs are 10 000, a **loss of 2 000** [1]. At 800 units revenue is 16 000 and total costs are 14 000, a **profit of 2 000** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Read from the table, then quote the figures you used. In part (d), state profit or loss, as a bare number leaves the reader guessing.

**9. (a)** Total costs = 10 500 + 15 x output. At 0: **10 500** [1]. At 600: 10 500 + 9 000 = **19 500** [1]. At 1 200: 10 500 + 18 000 = **28 500** [1].
**(b)** Contribution = 25 - 15 = 10 [1]. New break-even = 10 500 / 10 = **1 050 units** [1].
**(c)** The fixed cost line and total cost line both move up by 1 500 and stay parallel to the old lines [1]. The revenue line is unchanged, so break-even moves to the right, from 900 to 1 050 units [1].
*Examiner insight:* When amending a chart, say which line moves and which stays put. Saying break-even "increases" without the direction on the chart is less clear.

**10. (a)** Contribution = 40 - 24 = 16 [1]. Break-even = 48 000 / 16 = **3 000 bikes** [1].
**(b)** New contribution = 44 - 24 = 20 [1]. Break-even = 48 000 / 20 = **2 400 bikes** [1].
**(c)** Profit now = 16 x 4 000 - 48 000 = 16 000; at the new price = 20 x 3 600 - 48 000 = 24 000 [1]. Break-even falls from 3 000 to 2 400, and the margin of safety rises from 1 000 to 1 200 bikes [1]. She could even keep the old profit if she sold 3 200 bikes, because (48 000 + 16 000) / 20 = 3 200 [1], so the plan has room for error [1]. However, the fall to 3 600 is only a forecast, and a rival's reaction or the brand image could make demand fall by more [1]. On balance she **should raise the price**, because the profit rise is large, but she should test the forecast first [1].
*Examiner insight:* For a "justify" question, use the figures, give a point against, then make a clear decision. A bare "yes, profit goes up" gives little.

**11.** Any two, each explained. For example: the chart assumes everything made is sold [1], but if the sauce is unsold the firm still pays for the ingredients and storage, so the break-even point is less reliable [1]. It assumes the price is the same at every level of output [1], but the firm may offer discounts to big retailers, which lowers revenue per unit [1]. (Also acceptable: constant variable cost per unit; fixed costs not changing; one product only; depends on forecasts.)
*Examiner insight:* Each limitation needs a "so what" about the decision. Naming a limitation without showing how it distorts the chart gives only partial credit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by the selling price instead of contribution per unit.
- Saying "costs fall" instead of "average cost falls" when explaining economies of scale.
- Naming an economy or diseconomy without saying what happens to cost per unit.
- Judging a loss-making product on its loss alone, without checking contribution and which costs would remain.
- Giving margin of safety in the wrong units, or as a percentage of break-even rather than of expected sales.
- Drawing the fixed cost or total cost line from the origin.
- Forgetting that "justify" needs a decision supported by evidence from the case.
- Listing limitations of break-even without saying how they affect a decision.
