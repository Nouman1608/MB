This guide covers subtopic **12.3 Chromatography** of Topic 12, Experimental techniques and chemical analysis, in the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. Outcomes 1 and 2 are **Core**; outcomes 3 and 4 (colourless substances and Rf values) are **Supplement**, labelled *(Extended)*. Chromatography is also one of the separation techniques listed for the practical papers. For quick recall use the [chromatography revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-chromatography-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [chromatography practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-chromatography-practice/).

## What 12.3 asks you to do

- **Core 1:** describe how **paper chromatography** separates mixtures of **soluble coloured substances**, using a **suitable solvent**.
- **Core 2:** interpret simple chromatograms to identify **(a) unknown substances by comparison with known substances** and **(b) pure and impure substances**.
- **Supplement 3:** describe how paper chromatography separates **soluble colourless substances**, using a suitable solvent and a **locating agent**. Knowledge of specific locating agents is not required.
- **Supplement 4:** state and use the equation for **Rf**.

## How paper chromatography separates a mixture

A small spot of the mixture is placed on chromatography paper, and a solvent soaks up the paper past it. Each substance in the mixture dissolves in the solvent and is carried up the paper, but different substances travel **different distances** in the same time. A substance that is more soluble in the solvent, and less strongly held by the paper, travels further. Substances therefore separate into individual spots.

The word **soluble** in the syllabus is important: a substance that does not dissolve in the chosen solvent stays on the starting line. Choosing a **suitable solvent** means choosing one in which the substances dissolve, but to different extents. Water suits many inks and food colourings; other substances need a different solvent, such as ethanol.

## The method

1. Draw a **start line (baseline) in pencil** near the bottom of the paper. Pencil is used because graphite does not dissolve in the solvent; ink would run and add extra spots.
2. Put a **small, concentrated spot** of each sample on the line, spaced apart. Include known reference substances alongside the unknown.
3. Stand the paper in a beaker containing a **shallow layer of solvent**. The solvent level must be **below the baseline**, or the spots would dissolve into the solvent instead of moving up the paper.
4. Cover the beaker with a **lid**, so that the solvent does not evaporate from the paper as it rises.
5. Leave the solvent to rise until it is near the top, then remove the paper and **mark the solvent front** in pencil straight away, before it evaporates.
6. **Dry** the chromatogram.

The finished paper is the **chromatogram**.

## Interpreting chromatograms (Core)

### Pure or impure?

- A **pure** substance gives **one spot**.
- An **impure** substance (a mixture) gives **two or more spots**.

A sensible caution: two different substances could, by chance, travel the same distance in one solvent, so a single spot strongly suggests, rather than proves, purity. Running the chromatogram again in a different solvent would show any hidden second substance.

### Identifying an unknown

Run the unknown **on the same paper, with the same solvent**, beside known substances. If a spot from the unknown is at the **same height** as a known substance's spot, the unknown contains that substance.

### Worked example 1: reading a chromatogram

*A food colouring X is run beside three known dyes, A, B and C. X gives three spots: one level with A, one level with C, and one that has not moved from the baseline. B gives one spot that matches none of the spots from X. What can you conclude?*

- X contains **A** and **C**: those spots are at the same heights.
- X does **not** contain **B**.
- X also contains a **third substance** that is **insoluble** in this solvent, because it stayed on the baseline. A careless reading that counts only the spots above the line would miss it.
- X is **impure** (a mixture of at least three substances). A, B and C each give one spot, so each is likely to be pure.

## Colourless substances *(Extended)*

Colourless substances separate in exactly the same way, but their spots cannot be seen. After the chromatogram has dried, it is treated with a **locating agent**, which reacts with the substances to form **coloured spots** that can then be seen and measured. You do not need to name a specific locating agent.

The order of steps matters: run the chromatogram, mark the solvent front, **dry**, then apply the locating agent.

## Rf values *(Extended)*

The **Rf value** compares how far a substance travels with how far the solvent travels:

```
        distance travelled by substance
Rf  =  ---------------------------------
        distance travelled by solvent
```

Both distances are measured **from the baseline**: to the **centre of the spot**, and to the **solvent front**.

Key facts:

- Rf has **no units** (it is a ratio of two lengths).
- Rf is always **less than 1**, because a substance cannot travel further than the solvent.
- A substance has a characteristic Rf value **for a given solvent and conditions**, so Rf values can be compared with known values to identify a substance even without running the reference on the same paper.

### Worked example 2: calculating and using Rf *(Extended)*

*The solvent front is 8.0 cm above the baseline. A spot's centre is 3.2 cm above the baseline. Calculate Rf.*

Rf = 3.2 ÷ 8.0 = **0.40**

*A different substance has Rf = 0.65 in the same solvent. On a chromatogram where the solvent travels 6.0 cm, how far will its spot travel?*

Rearrange: distance travelled by substance = Rf × distance travelled by solvent = 0.65 × 6.0 = **3.9 cm**.

*A spot that stays on the baseline* has Rf = 0, because it travelled no distance: it is insoluble in that solvent.

## Common mistakes

- **Drawing the baseline in ink.**
- **Putting the solvent level above the baseline.**
- **Measuring to the top of the paper** instead of to the solvent front, or from the bottom of the paper instead of from the baseline.
- **Giving Rf a unit**, or an Rf greater than 1.
- **Saying the spots "dissolve" up the paper** without explaining that different substances travel different distances.
- **Concluding a substance is pure** from one spot when some material has stayed on the baseline.
- **Applying the locating agent before the paper is dry**, or forgetting it is needed only for colourless substances.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** (method): baseline in pencil, spot, solvent below line, lid, solvent front marked, dry (and locating agent if colourless).
- **Explain** (a step): pencil does not dissolve; solvent below line so spots do not dissolve into the solvent.
- **Interpret:** count spots for purity; compare heights for identity; note anything on the baseline.
- **Calculate** *(Extended)*: measure both distances from the baseline; give Rf to two significant figures, without units.
