## Sources

> **Source 1: Corrib Kitchenware Ltd**
>
> Corrib sells pans and utensils to department stores. To win a large new
> contract it offered bigger discounts, and it switched to a new supplier
> after its old one raised prices. Figures in $000:
>
> | | Year 1 | Year 2 |
> |---|---|---|
> | Revenue | 1,200 | 1,500 |
> | Cost of sales | 720 | 975 |
> | Other operating expenses | 330 | 360 |
> | Interest | 30 | 45 |
>
> **Source 2: Brennock Engineering**
>
> Brennock makes parts for farm machinery and has doubled its orders in a
> year. Its current assets are inventory $84,000, trade receivables
> $66,000 and cash $6,000. Its current liabilities are trade payables
> $78,000 and an overdraft $42,000. Customers take 90 days to pay, while
> suppliers want payment in 30 days. A factoring company has offered to
> pay 85% of $60,000 of invoices immediately, for a fee of 2.5% of their
> value. Brennock imports its steel, and the home currency has recently
> weakened. Interest rates are also expected to rise.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "cost of sales". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate Corrib's gross profit and operating profit for both years. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate Corrib's profit for the year margin for both years. **[4]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason for the fall in Corrib's gross profit margin between Year 1 and Year 2. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** way Corrib could improve its profitability. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse why Corrib's profit for the year did not rise even though revenue grew by 25%. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "liquidity". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate Brennock's current ratio and acid test ratio. **[4]**

**9.** Calculate the cash Brennock would receive immediately from the factoring offer, and the fee it would pay. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse how overtrading could lead to the failure of Brennock Engineering. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss whether factoring is the best way for Brennock to improve its liquidity. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether internal or external factors pose the greater threat to Brennock's survival. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The direct costs of producing or buying the goods that a business has sold in a period [1], such as materials or goods bought for resale [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Gross profit: Year 1 = 1,200 − 720 = **$480,000** [1]; Year 2 = 1,500 − 975 = **$525,000** [1]. Operating profit: Year 1 = 480 − 330 = **$150,000** [1]; Year 2 = 525 − 360 = **$165,000** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Profit for the year: Year 1 = 150 − 30 = $120,000 [1]; Year 2 = 165 − 45 = $120,000 [1]. Margin: Year 1 = 120 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = **10%** [1]; Year 2 = 120 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **8%** [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Corrib offered bigger discounts to win the new contract [1], so the price received per item fell [1] while the cost of each item did not fall with it [1]. Revenue therefore grew more slowly than cost of sales, which rose from 60% to 65% of revenue, cutting the gross margin from 40% to 35% [1]. *(Also creditable: the new supplier may be dearer or less efficient.)* **[4]**

**5.** Corrib could renegotiate with its supplier or find a cheaper one [1], reducing cost of sales per item [1]. Since cost of sales takes 65% of revenue, even a small saving would lift gross profit noticeably [1], improving the gross margin as long as quality does not fall and department stores keep buying [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Revenue rose by $300,000, but cost of sales rose by $255,000 [1], because the discounts and supplier change made each sale less profitable [1]. Other operating expenses rose by $30,000 [1], so operating profit grew by only $15,000 [1]. Interest rose by $15,000, perhaps from borrowing to fund the new contract [1], which absorbed the whole operating profit gain, leaving profit for the year unchanged at $120,000 and the margin down from 10% to 8% [1]. **[6]**

**7.** The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts [1] as they fall due, using cash or assets that can quickly be turned into cash [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Current assets = 84 + 66 + 6 = $156,000; current liabilities = 78 + 42 = $120,000 [1]. Current ratio = 156 ÷ 120 = **1.3** [1]. Acid test = (156 − 84) ÷ 120 [1] = **0.6** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Cash now = 85% × $60,000 [1] = **$51,000** [1]. Fee = 2.5% × $60,000 [1] = **$1,500** [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Brennock has doubled its orders, so it must buy much more steel and pay more staff before customers pay [1]. Suppliers want payment in 30 days but customers take 90 days [1], so cash goes out about 60 days before it comes in, and the gap grows with every extra order [1]. The acid test of 0.6 shows it cannot already cover its current liabilities from liquid assets [1]. If the overdraft limit is reached, it may be unable to pay suppliers, who could stop deliveries [1], halting production even though the business is winning orders and may be profitable, which is how overtrading leads to failure [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **For factoring:** it brings in $51,000 immediately [1], which tackles the 60-day gap between paying suppliers and being paid [1]; Brennock could reduce its $42,000 overdraft, saving interest as rates rise [1].
**Against:** it costs $1,500 on this batch of invoices alone, cutting profit [1], and if used continually the fees add up [1]. Alternatives exist: negotiating longer credit from suppliers, or asking customers to pay within 60 days, would improve liquidity without a fee [1], though customers may refuse [1].
**Judgement:** factoring is the quickest solution while orders grow so fast, but Brennock should combine it with action on credit terms so it is not permanently dependent on factoring; this depends on how reliable its customers are [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **Internal threats:** Brennock is overtrading, with orders doubling while its acid test is only 0.6 [1]; its credit terms are poorly matched, paying in 30 days but waiting 90 [1]; inventory of $84,000 is over half its current assets, tying up cash [1]; these are within its control and are already causing a liquidity problem [1].
**External threats:** the weaker currency makes imported steel dearer, raising costs [1]; rising interest rates make the $42,000 overdraft more expensive [1]; higher rates may also reduce farmers' spending on machinery, cutting future orders [1].
**Judgement:** internal factors pose the greater threat, because they are already causing a liquidity problem, whereas the external factors make an existing weakness worse [1]. If Brennock fixes its working capital, through factoring, better credit terms and lower inventory, it will be far better able to absorb the external pressures [1]; the judgement would change if the currency fall were severe enough to make its prices uncompetitive [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Deducting interest before calculating operating profit.
- Assuming higher revenue must mean higher profit.
- Leaving inventory in the acid test ratio.
- Stating the factoring fee as a percentage of the cash received rather than of the invoice value.
- Explaining overtrading without using the 30-day and 90-day credit terms.
- Listing internal and external threats without judging which matters more for this business.
