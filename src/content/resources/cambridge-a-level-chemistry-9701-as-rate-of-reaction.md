## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. explain and use the terms **rate of reaction**, **frequency of collisions**, **effective collisions** and **non-effective collisions**
2. explain qualitatively, in terms of frequency of effective collisions, the effect of **concentration** and **pressure** changes on the rate of a reaction
3. use **experimental data** to calculate the rate of a reaction

Rate equations, orders of reaction and rate constants belong to Topic 26 (A Level) and are not needed here.

## The four terms

- **Rate of reaction**: the change in the amount or concentration of a reactant or product **per unit time**. For a solution, the usual unit is mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
- **Frequency of collisions**: the number of collisions between reacting particles **per unit time**. "Frequency" always means "how often", so "more collisions" on its own is weaker than "more frequent collisions".
- **Effective collision**: a collision that **results in reaction**. The particles must collide with at least the **activation energy** (subtopic 8.2), and, for many reactions, in a suitable orientation.
- **Non-effective collision**: a collision that does **not** lead to reaction, because the particles have less than the activation energy (or collide in an unsuitable orientation). They simply bounce apart.

Rate depends on the **frequency of effective collisions**, not on the total number of collisions.

## Concentration and pressure

**Increasing the concentration** of a reactant in solution puts **more particles in the same volume**. Particles collide **more frequently**, so there are **more effective collisions per unit time**, and the rate increases.

**Increasing the pressure** of a gas (at constant temperature) packs the same particles into a smaller volume, which is the same as increasing the concentration of each gas. Again collisions are more frequent, so effective collisions are more frequent.

A subtle point that earns credit: changing concentration or pressure does **not** change the **fraction** of collisions that are effective, because the energy distribution of the particles depends only on temperature. Only the **number of collisions per unit time** changes. This is the key contrast with a temperature rise, which also increases the fraction of particles with energy at or above the activation energy.

### Annotated model answer

**Question.** Explain why increasing the pressure increases the rate of the reaction 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2SO₃(g). [3]

- There are more gas molecules per unit volume (higher concentration). *(one mark)*
- So the molecules collide more frequently. *(one mark)*
- The frequency of effective collisions increases, so the rate increases. *(one mark)*

An answer saying "molecules have more energy" scores nothing: pressure does not change the energy of the molecules at constant temperature.

## Calculating rates from experimental data

### Mean rate over an interval

    mean rate = change in quantity ÷ time taken

**Example.** The concentration of a reactant falls from 0.800 to 0.500 mol dm⁻³ in 120 s.

    rate = (0.800 − 0.500) / 120 = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

Rates are quoted as **positive** numbers, whether a reactant is used up or a product is formed.

### Rate from a graph: drawing a tangent

On a graph of concentration (or volume of gas) against time, the rate at any instant is the **gradient of the tangent** to the curve at that time.

1. Draw a straight line that touches the curve at the chosen time without crossing it.
2. Make the line long, then read two points far apart on it.
3. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x. Take the magnitude.

**Example.** A tangent drawn at t = 0 on a concentration–time graph passes through (0 s, 0.800 mol dm⁻³) and (200 s, 0 mol dm⁻³).

    initial rate = 0.800 / 200 = 4.00 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

The **initial rate** (tangent at t = 0) is the fastest point for most reactions, because the reactant concentration is highest. The gradient falls as reactants are used up, and becomes zero when the limiting reactant runs out.

### Following a reaction

| Method | Measured | Rate unit |
|---|---|---|
| Gas syringe or inverted burette | volume of gas against time | cm³ s⁻¹ |
| Balance (gas escapes) | mass loss against time | g s⁻¹ |
| Colorimeter | colour intensity, converted to concentration | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| Sampling and titration | concentration of a reactant at set times | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| "Clock" reaction | time t for a fixed change (e.g. a colour to appear) | relative rate = 1/t, in s⁻¹ |

### Worked example: converting units

Calcium carbonate chips react with excess hydrochloric acid in a flask on a balance. In the first 300 s, the mass falls by 0.88 g as carbon dioxide escapes.

    mean rate of mass loss = 0.88 / 300 = 2.9 × 10⁻³ g s⁻¹
    n(CO₂) = 0.88 / 44.0 = 0.0200 mol
    mean rate of CO₂ formation = 0.0200 / 300 = 6.7 × 10⁻⁵ mol s⁻¹

### Worked example: using stoichiometry

For 2N₂O₅(g) → 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g), N₂O₅ is used up at 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. NO₂ forms **twice as fast**, 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹, and O₂ **half as fast**, 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Always state which species a rate refers to.

### Worked example: a clock reaction

| [acid] / mol dm⁻³ | Time for cross to disappear / s | 1/t / s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 125 | 0.0080 |
| 0.40 | 63 | 0.016 |

*(Invented data.)* Doubling the concentration roughly halves the time, so the rate (proportional to 1/t) roughly doubles. 1/t can be used as a measure of rate because the same amount of reaction happens each time.

## Interpreting volume–time graphs

When magnesium reacts with excess acid and the hydrogen is collected:

- a **steeper** initial curve means a **faster** reaction;
- the curve **levels off** when the limiting reactant is used up;
- the **final volume** depends on the **amount of limiting reactant**, not on the rate. Using a more concentrated (but still excess) acid makes the curve steeper but gives the same final volume.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "more collisions" instead of "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per unit time".
- Claiming that higher concentration or pressure gives particles more energy.
- Saying a higher proportion of collisions are effective at higher concentration; the **number per unit time** increases, not the proportion.
- Drawing a chord between two points on the curve instead of a tangent.
- Reading tangent points too close together, which gives an inaccurate gradient.
- Giving a rate without units, or with a negative sign.

## Exam technique

- For "explain the effect of…" questions: particles per unit volume → frequency of collisions → frequency of **effective** collisions → rate. Each arrow is typically a marking point.
- In calculations, show the gradient working with the two points you used.
- Use the term "effective collisions" exactly as the syllabus does.
