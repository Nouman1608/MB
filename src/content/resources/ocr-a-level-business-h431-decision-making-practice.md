## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain the purpose of decision making in a business. **[2]**

**2.** Graydon Logistics is a delivery company. Classify each decision as strategic, tactical or operational: (i) buying a rival delivery company; (ii) a three-month discount for new customers in one region; (iii) allocating drivers to routes for tomorrow. **[3]**

**3.** Explain **one** way in which optimism bias could lead to a poor business decision. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the accuracy of forecasts matters when a business uses break-even analysis to decide whether to launch a product. **[3]**

**5.** A business scores an option 7 for cost (weight 0.5), 4 for quality (weight 0.3) and 9 for speed (weight 0.2). Calculate the weighted score. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Hartree Software is choosing between two offices. Its managers have agreed these weights and scores out of 10.

| Criterion | Weight | Office X | Office Y |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rent | 0.35 | 7 | 5 |
| Staff commuting | 0.25 | 5 | 8 |
| Access for clients | 0.25 | 8 | 6 |
| Space to expand | 0.15 | 4 | 8 |

**(a)** Calculate the weighted score for each office. **[4]**

**(b)** Evaluate the usefulness of this weighted scoring matrix to Hartree Software in making its decision. **[6]**

**7.** Sorrell Drinks makes energy drinks. Its chief executive wants to build a second factory costing £8 million, based on a forecast that sales will grow 15% a year for five years. In the last three years, sales grew 22%, then 3%, then fell 6%. New competitors enter the market each year. The chief executive first proposed the factory two years ago and has already spent £400,000 on plans for it. Evaluate the factors Sorrell Drinks should take into account before making this decision. **[9]**

**8.** Fallow Lane Farm had a poor harvest and its cash reserves are low. Its objective for the next year is survival. It can either spend £60,000 on a farm shop or rent its spare barn to a storage company for £8,000 a year. Explain how the farm's objective should influence this decision. **[4]**

**9.** Quarry Point Leisure runs an indoor climbing centre. Last year's revenue was £1.8 million. This year's results are shown with its targets.

| Measure | Target | This year |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | 10% | Revenue £1.89 million |
| Net profit margin | 10% | Net profit £226,800 |
| Customer satisfaction | 85% | 79% (88% last year) |
| Market share | 15% | 15% (14% last year) |

**(a)** Calculate the revenue growth and the net profit margin. **[4]**

**(b)** Evaluate the extent to which Quarry Point Leisure has been a success this year. **[9]**

**10.** Pellingham Press prints regional newspapers. Demand for printed newspapers is falling by about 8% a year. The directors have two options.

Option A: close one printing line and make 20 staff redundant. Redundancy costs are £150,000 and the saving is £400,000 a year.

Option B: spend £1.2 million converting the line to print food packaging. From year 2, forecast profit is £300,000 a year. Pellingham has no packaging customers yet.

Evaluate which course of action Pellingham Press should take. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** To choose between alternative courses of action [1] so that limited resources are used to move the business towards its aims and objectives [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (i) Strategic [1]. (ii) Tactical [1]. (iii) Operational [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Managers may overestimate sales or underestimate costs of a project they favour [1], so the business invests in something that loses money or runs short of cash [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Break-even uses forecast price, variable cost and fixed costs, and the result is compared with forecast sales [1]. If any forecast is wrong, the break-even output or the margin of safety will be wrong [1], so the business may launch a product that cannot cover its costs, or reject one that would have been profitable [1]. **[3]**

**5.** (0.5 × 7) + (0.3 × 4) + (0.2 × 9) = 3.5 + 1.2 + 1.8 [1] = **6.5** [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Office X: (0.35 × 7) + (0.25 × 5) + (0.25 × 8) + (0.15 × 4) = 2.45 + 1.25 + 2.0 + 0.6 [1] = **6.3** [1]. Office Y: (0.35 × 5) + (0.25 × 8) + (0.25 × 6) + (0.15 × 8) = 1.75 + 2.0 + 1.5 + 1.2 [1] = **6.45** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Useful: it combines a financial factor, rent, with non-financial ones such as staff commuting in a single comparison [1], and it makes the managers' priorities visible so they can be debated [1].
Limitations: the margin is only 0.15, so a small change to a weight or score would reverse the result [1]; for example, if rent were weighted more heavily, Office X might win [1]; the scores are judgements and may reflect managers' own commuting preferences [1].
Judgement: the matrix is useful for structuring the decision, but because the result is so close Hartree should test it with different weights and check the actual rent figures before choosing Office Y [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Accuracy of forecasts: the 15% growth forecast is far above the recent record of 22%, 3% and −6%, which averages about 6% a year [1], so the forecast looks unreliable [1].
Volatility: sales have swung widely and new competitors enter every year [1], so conditions may change long before an £8 million factory pays back, which makes a large, hard-to-reverse commitment risky [1].
Potential for bias: the chief executive has championed the factory for two years [1], and the £400,000 already spent may encourage sunk cost thinking, although that money cannot be recovered and should not influence the choice [1].
Level and nature of risk: £8 million is a large commitment, and the main risks, competitor entry and changing tastes, are unquantifiable [1].
Evaluation: the most important factor is the accuracy of the forecast, because the whole case depends on it [1]. Judgement: Sorrell should not commit until an independent forecast is made, and should consider a smaller or leased plant, or contracting out production, which would be easier to reverse [1]. **[9]**

**8.** With a survival objective, protecting cash matters more than growth [1], so options should be judged on how they affect cash flow rather than on long-term profit [1]. The farm shop needs £60,000 that the farm may not have, and its returns are uncertain [1], while renting the barn brings a certain £8,000 a year with no outlay, so it better fits the survival objective [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Revenue growth: (1.89 − 1.8) ÷ 1.8 × 100 [1] = **5%** [1]. Net profit margin: 226,800 ÷ 1,890,000 × 100 [1] = **12%** [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Successes: the net profit margin of 12% beats the 10% target [1], and market share rose to the 15% target [1], so the centre is profitable and winning customers from rivals [1].
Failures: revenue grew only 5%, half the target [1]; customer satisfaction fell from 88% to 79%, below the 85% target [1].
Analysis: the rise in market share while revenue grew slowly suggests the whole market is growing slowly or shrinking [1]; falling satisfaction is a warning that future revenue and market share may fall, even though profit is strong now [1].
Evaluation: from the owners' view, this year is a success because profit beat its target [1]; but the fall in satisfaction is the most important result, because it signals a problem the financial figures do not yet show, so the centre is only a partial success and should find out why satisfaction fell [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Option A: net saving in year 1 is 400,000 − 150,000 = £250,000, and £400,000 a year after that [1]; redundancy costs are recovered in under five months [1]. It is low risk and quick [1], but it does not solve the underlying problem: with demand falling 8% a year, further cuts are likely [1]. Twenty employees lose their jobs, which may lower morale among remaining staff [1].
Option B: £1.2 million with profit of £300,000 a year from year 2 gives a payback of five years from the start [1]. It moves Pellingham into a market that is not declining, so it addresses the long-term problem [1]. But it has no packaging customers, so the forecast is highly uncertain [1], and it is diversification into an unfamiliar market [1].
Evaluation: Option A protects profit now; Option B may protect the business's future [1]. The key factor is the accuracy of the packaging forecast [1]. Judgement: Pellingham should take Option A now to protect cash, while seeking firm packaging orders; if contracts can be secured, it should then convert the line, so the two options are combined rather than treated as alternatives [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing the level of a decision with its time horizon.
- Treating a weighted score as an objective answer.
- Ignoring the record of past forecasts when judging a new one.
- Letting money already spent influence a recommendation.
- Judging success on one measure, usually profit.
