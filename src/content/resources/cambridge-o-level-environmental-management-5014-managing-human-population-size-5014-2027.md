Sub-topic 7.3 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus (2027-2029) is the final part of the course. It has two outcomes: describe five strategies for managing human population size (7.3.1), and discuss their benefits and limitations (7.3.2). The five strategies are access to contraception, population policies (pro-natalist and anti-natalist), access to education, access to healthcare, and control of migration. Because 7.3.2 is a "discuss" outcome, the marks go to balanced, reasoned evaluation, so this guide spends as much time on limitations and judgements as on description.

For the whole of Topic 7, see the [human population study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-human-population-study-guide/).

## Why governments manage population size

A government may want to **slow growth** when its population is growing faster than it can provide jobs, housing, water, food and services (the impacts in sub-topic 7.2). It may want to **increase growth**, or at least the birth rate, when children per woman is well below replacement level and the population is ageing, leaving too few workers to support pensions and healthcare. Always identify which aim a strategy serves: most strategies in 7.3.1 are used to slow growth, but pro-natalist policies and some forms of migration control aim the other way.

## The five strategies (7.3.1)

### (a) Access to contraception

Making contraception available, affordable and acceptable, often through family-planning clinics, community health workers and information campaigns.

- *How it works:* couples can choose the number and timing of children, so unplanned births fall and children per woman decreases.
- *Benefits:* respects individual choice; relatively cheap per person; also improves maternal and child health by spacing births.
- *Limitations:* works only if people want smaller families; supply can be unreliable in remote rural areas; cultural or religious objections may limit use.

### (b) Population policies: pro-natalist and anti-natalist

A **population policy** is a deliberate government plan to change the size or growth rate of the population.

**Anti-natalist policies** aim to reduce the birth rate. They range from incentives (tax benefits or priority for housing for smaller families) and campaigns to legal limits on the number of children. The best-known example of a strict limit is China's one-child policy, which operated from about 1980 until 2015.

- *Benefits:* can lower the birth rate quickly; reduces future pressure on resources and services.
- *Limitations:* strict limits restrict personal freedom and raise human-rights concerns; they can produce an imbalance between the numbers of boys and girls where sons are preferred; they cause rapid ageing later, as small generations of workers support large elderly generations.

**Pro-natalist policies** aim to increase the birth rate. Measures include payments to parents for each child, paid parental leave, subsidised childcare, tax relief and help with housing for families.

- *Benefits:* can slow ageing and protect the future workforce; support families with the cost of children.
- *Limitations:* very expensive; the effect on births is often small or temporary, because career, housing and lifestyle factors also shape family size; results take a generation to affect the workforce.

### (c) Access to education

Especially schooling for girls and women.

- *How it works:* educated women tend to marry later, know more about family planning, and have more opportunities for paid work, so they tend to have fewer children. Education also lowers child mortality because parents know more about health and nutrition.
- *Benefits:* brings many benefits beyond population (literacy, income, health); widely accepted as fair.
- *Limitations:* slow, as effects appear over a generation; costs money for schools and teachers; does little if girls are kept out of school for cultural or economic reasons.

### (d) Access to healthcare

- *How it works:* this one can seem surprising. Better healthcare lowers death rates, which at first increases growth. But as child mortality falls, parents become confident that their children will survive and choose to have fewer, so birth rates then fall. Clinics also deliver contraception and maternal health care.
- *Benefits:* saves lives directly; supports a fall in birth rates over time.
- *Limitations:* the short-term effect is faster growth; expensive to provide in rural areas; relies on trained staff.

### (e) Control of migration

Governments control who may enter and leave, using visas, work permits, quotas and border controls. Some encourage immigration of workers to fill labour shortages; others limit it to reduce pressure on housing and services. Countries can also try to reduce emigration of skilled workers.

- *Benefits:* immigration of young workers can offset an ageing population quickly; limits can reduce pressure on services where growth is high.
- *Limitations:* illegal migration continues where controls are hard to enforce; immigration can cause tension if services do not expand; for sending countries, emigration of young, skilled workers is a loss ("brain drain").

## Worked example: what slower growth means

*The figures are invented for practice.* A country of 20 million is growing at 2.5% a year. A combination of contraception access and education reduces growth to 1.5% a year.

- First year's increase at 2.5% = 20 000 000 x 0.025 = **500 000** people.
- First year's increase at 1.5% = 20 000 000 x 0.015 = **300 000** people.

Multiplying by 1.025 each year for ten years gives about **25.6 million**; multiplying by 1.015 each year gives about **23.2 million**. The difference after ten years is roughly **2.4 million** fewer people who need housing, schooling, water and jobs. The example shows why a strategy that changes growth by one percentage point matters, and why slow-acting strategies such as education still have a large effect over time.

## Discussing benefits and limitations (7.3.2)

Use four tests to evaluate any strategy:

1. **Effectiveness**: does it change birth rate, death rate or migration as intended?
2. **Speed**: how long before it has an effect?
3. **Cost**: who pays, and can a low-income country afford it?
4. **Acceptability**: does it respect people's rights and choices?

| Strategy | Effectiveness | Speed | Cost | Acceptability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Access to contraception | High if families want fewer children | Medium | Low to medium | High |
| Anti-natalist policy (strict) | High | Fast | Medium | Low |
| Pro-natalist policy | Often small | Slow | High | High |
| Access to education | High over time | Slow | Medium to high | High |
| Access to healthcare | Raises growth first, lowers it later | Slow | High | High |
| Control of migration | Can be high | Fast | Medium | Varies |

### Annotated model answer

**Question:** Discuss the benefits and limitations of access to education and an anti-natalist policy for reducing population growth in an LIC. **[6]**

**Answer:** Educating girls tends to delay marriage and increase women's paid work, so they choose to have fewer children *(benefit, 1)*. It also brings wider benefits such as better child health *(benefit, 1)*. However, it takes many years to affect the birth rate and needs money for schools and teachers, which an LIC may lack *(limitation, 1)*. An anti-natalist policy such as incentives or limits on family size can lower the birth rate faster *(benefit, 1)*, but strict limits restrict personal freedom and can lead to a gender imbalance and rapid ageing *(limitation, 1)*. Overall, education is more acceptable and lasting, so it is the better long-term strategy, possibly supported by voluntary incentives and access to contraception *(judgement, 1)*.

## Common mistakes

- Saying healthcare "reduces population" without explaining the two stages.
- Describing pro-natalist policies when the question is about slowing growth.
- Giving only the benefits of a strict anti-natalist policy.
- Treating migration control as only about reducing immigration; it can also encourage immigration or limit emigration.
- Ending a discuss answer without a judgement.

## Exam technique

**Describe** a strategy by saying what is done and which population factor it changes. **Discuss** by giving benefits and limitations for each strategy named and a judgement suited to the country in the question: an LIC with rapid growth or an HIC with an ageing population. Use the four tests to generate points quickly.

## Check your understanding

1. What is an anti-natalist policy? *A government policy to reduce the birth rate.*
2. Give one limitation of a pro-natalist policy. *It is expensive, and the effect on births is often small.*
3. Why can better healthcare eventually lower the birth rate? *Parents are confident their children will survive, so they have fewer.*
4. Give one benefit of encouraging immigration in an ageing HIC. *It adds young workers quickly.*
5. A population of 8 million grows at 2%. What is the increase in one year? *160 000.*
