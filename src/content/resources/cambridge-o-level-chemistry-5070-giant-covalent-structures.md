Section 2.6 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has three outcomes. You must describe the giant covalent structures of graphite, diamond and silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂; relate the structure and bonding of graphite and diamond to their uses, "limited to" graphite as a lubricant and as an electrode, and diamond in cutting tools; and describe the similarity in properties between diamond and silicon(IV) oxide, related to their structures. Every outcome is required for every 5070 candidate.

The [Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding guide](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/) gives a short summary of diamond and graphite. This guide describes all three structures in the detail examiners expect, builds the structure → property → use chain for each use the syllabus names, and treats silicon(IV) oxide properly.

## What "giant covalent" means

A **giant covalent structure** (also called a giant molecular or macromolecular structure) is a huge three-dimensional network of atoms in which **every atom is joined to its neighbours by strong covalent bonds**. There are no separate small molecules. The network continues throughout the whole crystal, so a single diamond is, in effect, one enormous molecule.

Compare this with section 2.5. In a simple molecular substance, melting only overcomes weak forces **between** molecules. In a giant covalent structure there are no separate molecules, so melting means **breaking strong covalent bonds**, and very many of them. That is the key to almost every property in this section.

## Diamond

**Structure.** Diamond is a form of carbon. Each carbon atom forms **four covalent bonds** to four other carbon atoms, arranged **tetrahedrally**. This pattern repeats in three dimensions to make a rigid giant lattice.

**Properties explained.**

- **Very hard**: every atom is held in place by four strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D network; there are no weak points along which the structure can easily break.
- **Very high melting point**: a very large amount of energy is needed to break the many strong covalent bonds.
- **Does not conduct electricity**: all four outer electrons of each carbon are used in bonds, so there are **no delocalised electrons** (and no ions).
- **Insoluble in water**.

**Use: cutting tools.** Diamond is used on the tips of drills and the edges of saws for cutting rock, glass and concrete. The use follows from its hardness, which comes from the rigid network of strong covalent bonds.

## Graphite

**Structure.** Graphite is also pure carbon, but each carbon atom forms only **three covalent bonds**, to three other carbon atoms. The atoms form flat **layers of hexagonal rings**. The **fourth outer electron** of each carbon atom is **delocalised**: free to move along the layer. The layers are held to each other only by **weak forces**.

**Properties explained.**

- **Soft and slippery**: the forces between layers are weak, so the layers can **slide over each other**.
- **Conducts electricity**: the **delocalised electrons** can move along the layers and carry charge. This is unusual for a non-metal element.
- **Very high melting point**: melting requires breaking the strong covalent bonds within the layers, not just separating the layers.

**Use 1: lubricant.** Graphite powder reduces friction between moving metal parts, for example in locks and hinges, because its layers slide easily over each other. It is useful where oil would not work well, such as at high temperatures, because its high melting point means it stays solid.

**Use 2: electrode.** Graphite rods are used as electrodes in electrolysis because graphite **conducts electricity** (delocalised electrons) and has a **high melting point**, so it can be used with molten electrolytes.

**Worked example 1.** Explain why graphite is used as an electrode in electrolysis.

*Annotated answer.* Each carbon atom in graphite forms three covalent bonds, leaving **one outer electron delocalised** [1]. These electrons are **free to move** through the layers, so graphite conducts electricity [1]. Its giant covalent structure gives it a **high melting point**, so it remains solid in hot or molten electrolytes [1].

Note that the explanation for "electrode" is about conductivity; the explanation for "lubricant" is about layers sliding. Mixing them up is a common error.

## Diamond and graphite compared

| | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| Bonds per carbon atom | 4 | 3 |
| Arrangement | rigid 3D tetrahedral network | flat hexagonal layers |
| Delocalised electrons | none | one per carbon atom |
| Forces between layers | not applicable | weak |
| Hardness | very hard | soft, slippery |
| Electrical conductivity | does not conduct | conducts |
| Melting point | very high | very high |
| Use (syllabus) | cutting tools | lubricant, electrodes |

Both are pure carbon, so the differences in properties come **only from the differences in structure and bonding**. Examiners often ask you to state this explicitly.

## Silicon(IV) oxide

**Structure.** Silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂, occurs naturally as quartz, the main compound in sand. Each **silicon atom** forms **four covalent bonds** to four oxygen atoms, arranged tetrahedrally. Each **oxygen atom** forms **two covalent bonds**, to two silicon atoms. The network continues in three dimensions.

The formula SiO₂ gives the **ratio** of atoms in the giant structure (one silicon for every two oxygens), not a molecule. Each silicon is bonded to four oxygens, but each oxygen is shared between two silicons, so the ratio works out as 1 : 2.

Do not confuse SiO₂ with carbon dioxide, CO₂. Carbon dioxide is a **simple molecular** gas of separate O=C=O molecules. Silicon and carbon are in the same group, but their oxides have completely different structures.

## The similarity between diamond and silicon(IV) oxide

The syllabus asks you to describe the **similarity in properties** and relate it to structure.

| Property | Diamond | Silicon(IV) oxide |
|---|---|---|
| Hardness | very hard | very hard |
| Melting point | very high | very high |
| Electrical conductivity | does not conduct | does not conduct |
| Solubility in water | insoluble | insoluble |

**Why they are alike.** Both have a **giant covalent structure** in which every atom is held in a rigid **three-dimensional tetrahedral network** of **strong covalent bonds**. Breaking or scratching either solid, or melting it, means breaking many strong bonds. Neither has **delocalised electrons or ions**, so neither conducts electricity.

**Worked example 2.** Sandpaper is made by sticking grains of sand onto paper. Suggest why sand is suitable for this use, by comparison with diamond.

*Answer.* Sand is mainly silicon(IV) oxide, which, like diamond, has a giant covalent structure with each atom held by strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D network. This makes it **very hard**, so it can scratch and wear away softer materials such as wood.

## Common mistakes

- Saying graphite conducts because it has "free ions" or "free atoms": it is **delocalised electrons**.
- Saying graphite is slippery because its covalent bonds are weak. The **forces between layers** are weak; the bonds within layers are strong.
- Saying graphite has a low melting point because it is soft.
- Describing SiO₂ as molecules, or giving it the structure of CO₂.
- Explaining diamond's hardness with "strong bonds" alone, without the rigid 3D network.
- Answering "uses" questions with uses that are not linked to structure.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a structure: number of bonds per atom, arrangement (layers or 3D tetrahedral), and any delocalised electrons or weak forces.
- **Relate structure to use**: write a three-link chain, structure → property → use.
- **Describe the similarity**: name at least two shared properties and the shared structural reason.
