## Core recall

- An aim is a long-term goal; an objective is a specific, measurable target that moves the business towards it.
- The specification's main objectives: survival, profit maximisation, growth (domestic and international), market share, customer satisfaction, social and ethical objectives, shareholder value.
- Objectives give direction, guide decisions, motivate staff, and let results be measured.
- Objectives differ because of size, level of competition and type of business (such as not-for-profit).
- Objectives change as businesses evolve; larger, established businesses may aim to dominate the market, expand internationally, increase shareholder value or meet ethical and environmental goals.
- Triggers for change: growth, new competitors, a worse economic situation, a change of ownership, changing customer expectations.
- Objectives can conflict, so businesses must prioritise.
- Success can be measured in ways other than profit.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Aim | A long-term goal that sets the general direction of a business |
| Objective | A specific, measurable target that helps achieve an aim |
| Survival | Continuing to trade by covering costs and paying bills |
| Profit maximisation | Making the largest possible profit |
| Domestic growth | Getting bigger in the home country |
| International growth | Expanding sales or operations abroad |
| Market share | A business's sales as a percentage of total market sales |
| Customer satisfaction | The extent to which customers are happy with what they buy |
| Social and ethical objectives | Targets for treating people and the environment fairly |
| Shareholder value | The return to shareholders through dividends and the share price |
| Dominant business | The business with the largest share of its market |

## Measuring each objective

| Objective | Measure |
|---|---|
| Survival | Costs covered; bills paid |
| Profit maximisation | Profit over time |
| Growth | Revenue, outlets, customers; sales abroad for international growth |
| Market share | Sales ÷ total market sales × 100 |
| Customer satisfaction | Survey scores, repeat customers, complaints, reviews |
| Social and ethical | Waste, emissions, fair supplier prices, community support |
| Shareholder value | Dividends, change in share price |

## Typical objectives by stage

| Start-up | Growing | Large and established |
|---|---|---|
| Survival | Profit | Becoming the dominant business |
| Building a customer base | Domestic growth | International expansion |
| Covering costs | Market share | Increasing shareholder value |
| | Customer satisfaction | Ethical and environmental considerations |

## Conflicts to recognise

- Market share against profit: lower prices win customers but cut profit per sale.
- Growth against survival: expansion uses cash needed to pay bills.
- Ethics against profit: fairer or greener choices raise costs.
- Shareholder value against growth: dividends reduce retained profit.

## Calculation methods

```
Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100
Market share (%)  = business sales ÷ total market sales × 100
Survey score (%)  = satisfied responses ÷ total responses × 100
```

## Exam traps

- Judging success by profit alone.
- Using the new value as the base of a percentage change.
- Stating that objectives change without a trigger from the case.
- Naming a conflict without suggesting how to resolve it.
- Giving a start-up the objectives of a multinational.
- Calling "be successful" an objective; it is not measurable.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish an aim from an objective.
2. List the main objectives named in the specification.
3. Give the three reasons the specification gives for objectives differing between businesses.
4. Why is survival often the main objective of a start-up?
5. Revenue rose from £250,000 to £285,000. Calculate the percentage increase.
6. The target was growth of 15%. Was it met?
7. A firm sells £1.2 million in a market worth £16 million. Calculate its market share.
8. 432 of 480 customers surveyed said they were satisfied. Calculate the satisfaction score.
9. Give one example of objectives that conflict and explain why.
10. State two objectives a large, established business might add as it grows.
11. How might a charity measure its success?
12. Give one trigger that could make an established business return to survival as its main objective.

### Answers

1. An aim is a long-term goal giving general direction; an objective is a specific, measurable target that helps achieve the aim.
2. Survival, profit maximisation, growth (domestic and international), market share, customer satisfaction, social and ethical objectives, shareholder value.
3. Size of the business, level of competition faced, type of business (such as not-for-profit).
4. It has few customers, little cash and no reputation, so staying in business comes first.
5. (285,000 − 250,000) ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **14%**.
6. No, 14% is below the 15% target.
7. 1.2 ÷ 16 × 100 = **7.5%**.
8. 432 ÷ 480 × 100 = **90%**.
9. For example, market share and profit maximisation: cutting prices to win customers lowers the profit on each sale.
10. Any two: becoming the dominant business in the market; international expansion; increasing shareholder value; ethical and environmental objectives.
11. By what it achieves for its cause, such as people helped or meals served, while covering its costs.
12. Any one: an economic downturn; a strong new competitor; a large unexpected cost.
