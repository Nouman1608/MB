**This practice set covers topic 2.2, the psychological environment, in Specialist Option 2, Consumer Psychology, of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style questions on the key study, research methods and planning. All businesses and people in the scenarios are fictional.

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline **two** factors that help shoppers find their way in a shopping mall. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the difference between a *round* trip and a *wave* trip. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how the primacy and recency effects could influence what diners order from a menu. **[3]**

**4.** Outline the *overload* and *arousal* functions of personal space. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Describe research into defending a place in a queue. **[6]**

**5. (b)** Evaluate research into consumer behaviour and personal space, including a discussion of cultural differences. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6. (a)** Explain why Robson et al. (2011) used three different dining scenarios. **[2]**

**6. (b)** Robson et al. used rating scales. Explain **one** strength and **one** weakness of this. **[4]**

**7.** A supermarket uses CCTV to track shoppers' routes. Explain **one** ethical issue and how the supermarket's researchers could deal with it. **[3]**

**8.** Kofi's café wants to sell more of its most profitable dish, a vegetable curry.

**(a)** Suggest **two** changes to the menu, each based on a different psychological finding. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain how eye-tracking could be used to check whether one of your changes worked. **[2]**

**9.** A psychologist wants to replicate Robson et al. (2011) as a field experiment in a real restaurant.

**(a)** Describe how she could manipulate table spacing and measure diners' comfort. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain **one** problem with controlling variables in this field experiment. **[2]**

**10.** Dogu and Erkip (2000) used questionnaires to study wayfinding. Explain **one** weakness of this method for studying how shoppers find their way, and suggest an alternative. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** 1 mark each for any two: directional signs at decision points such as junctions and escalators; "you are here" maps aligned with the direction the viewer faces; distinctive landmarks such as an atrium or fountain; a simple, regular layout; familiarity with the mall from previous visits.

**2.** Up to 2 marks. A round trip moves around the outer edge or perimeter of the store (1), whereas a wave trip zig-zags up and down the aisles, covering the store row by row (1).

**3.** Up to 3 marks. Items at the start of a list receive more attention and are better remembered (primacy) (1), and items at the end are freshest in memory when the diner decides (recency) (1). So dishes placed first or last in a category are chosen more often, as Dayan and Bar-Hillel (2011) found, and restaurants can put the dishes they most want to sell in those positions (1).

**4.** 2 marks each. Overload: being close to others exposes us to too much stimulation, such as noise, conversation and eye contact (1), so we keep a distance to avoid being overwhelmed (1). Arousal: closeness increases physiological arousal (1), which is interpreted as pleasant with a partner but unpleasant with a stranger, so we keep strangers further away (1).

**5. (a)** Up to 6 marks. Milgram et al. (1986) studied real queues in New York, such as at ticket counters (1). Confederates intruded into the queue with a brief, neutral comment (1), with one or two intruders (1), and sometimes with passive confederates ("buffers") standing behind the intrusion point (1). Observers recorded the responses of people in the queue: mainly verbal objections, some non-verbal reactions, and rarely physical action (1). Two intruders produced more objection than one, people behind the intrusion point objected most, and buffers reduced objection, showing that a queue is a social system with shared rules (1).

**5. (b)** Level-based: 9–10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation with cultural differences developed and a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Cultural differences*: comfortable distances vary between cultures, so Hall's zones and Robson et al.'s American findings may not apply elsewhere; queuing norms also differ, so responses to intrusion may vary.
- *Validity*: Milgram et al. observed real queues (high ecological validity); Robson et al. used imagined scenarios, which may not predict real behaviour.
- *Ethics*: Milgram et al. deceived people and caused irritation without consent; Robson et al.'s online survey raised few ethical issues.
- *Individual and situational*: spacing and intrusion are situational, but individual differences (for example women reporting more discomfort in Robson et al.) also matter.
- *Application*: findings guide restaurant layout and queue management.
- *Conclusion*: consistent evidence that people protect personal space, but distances and responses depend on culture and context.

**6. (a)** Up to 2 marks. To see whether the effect of table spacing depends on the type of meal (1), since conversations on a romantic date or business meal may need more privacy than a meal with a friend, linking to the behaviour-constraint function (1).

**6. (b)** 2 marks each. Strength: rating scales give quantitative data (1) that can be compared statistically across the spacing and scenario conditions (1). Weakness: ratings of an imagined scene are subjective (1) and may not reflect how respondents would feel or behave in a real restaurant, reducing validity (1).

**7.** Up to 3 marks. Privacy or consent: shoppers are recorded without agreeing to take part in research (1). The researchers could put up clear signs at the entrance explaining that movement is being recorded for research (1), and store only anonymous route data, with no faces or identities, deleting video after coding (1).

**8.** **(a)** 2 marks for each change linked to a finding. Position: place the curry first or last in its menu category (1), because items in these positions are chosen more often (primacy and recency, Dayan and Bar-Hillel, 2011) (1). Highlighting: put the curry in a box or add a graphic (1), because features that draw the eye make an item more likely to be chosen, as Pavesic (2005) suggests (1). Credit a descriptive name ("slow-cooked garden vegetable curry"), linked to Lockyer (2006).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Record diners' eye movements as they read the old and the new menu (1), and compare how quickly and for how long they look at the curry; more and longer fixations on the new menu would suggest the change worked (1).

**9.** **(a)** Up to 4 marks. Manipulation: on alternate weeks, set the tables for two either close together (for example 15 cm apart) or further apart (for example 60 cm) (1), keeping the same tables, lighting and staff (1). Measure: give diners a short card at the end of the meal with rating scales for comfort, privacy and satisfaction from 1 to 7 (1), and record objective data such as how long they stay and whether they order dessert (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Diners cannot be randomly allocated to weeks, so the type of diners (for example more couples one week) or busier evenings may differ between conditions (1), and these uncontrolled variables could affect comfort rather than table spacing (1).

**10.** Up to 3 marks. Questionnaires rely on shoppers remembering and reporting their route and difficulties accurately (1), but people may forget wrong turns or say signs were helpful when they did not use them, so the data are subjective (1). Alternative: observe or track shoppers' actual routes from the entrance to a target shop, recording time taken and wrong turns (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**How many studies do I need for this topic?**
One for each point marked "including a study" in the syllabus, plus Robson et al. (2011) in full detail as the key study.

**Can I use Hall's zones in a menu or wayfinding question?**
Only if they are relevant; for example, crowding near a "you are here" map could affect how easily shoppers use it.
