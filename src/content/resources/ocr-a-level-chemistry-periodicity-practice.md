## Questions

**1.** This question is about how the periodic table is organised.

**(a)** State what is meant by the term periodicity. **[1]**
**(b)** Lithium and sodium react with water in a similar way. Explain this in terms of their electrons. **[1]**
**(c)** Mendeleev published his table in 1869, before many elements were known. Explain how his approach led to the Periodic Law being accepted by other scientists. **[2]**

**2.** The proton numbers of three elements are 55, 52 and 25.

**(a)** For each element, state the block of the periodic table it is in. **[3]**
**(b)** State the group and period of the element with proton number 52. **[1]**

**3.**

**(a)** Define the term first ionisation energy. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation, with state symbols, for the third ionisation energy of phosphorus. **[1]**

**4.** The first ionisation energies of three Group 16 elements are: oxygen 1314, sulfur 1000 and selenium 941 kJ mol⁻¹. Describe and explain the trend shown. **[3]**

**5.** Magnesium has a higher first ionisation energy than aluminium, and phosphorus has a higher first ionisation energy than sulfur.

**(a)** A student says: "Aluminium has one more proton than magnesium, so its first ionisation energy must be higher." Evaluate this statement. **[3]**
**(b)** Account for the fall in first ionisation energy from phosphorus to sulfur. **[2]**

**6.** Element Q is in Period 3. Its first six ionisation energies, in kJ mol⁻¹, are 1012, 1907, 2914, 4964, 6274 and 21267.

**(a)** Explain why the 2nd ionisation energy of Q is larger than the 1st. **[1]**
**(b)** Use the data to find the group of Q, showing your reasoning. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify Q. **[1]**

**7.** A graph of log₁₀(ionisation energy) against the number of electrons removed is plotted for all twelve electrons of magnesium. There is a large rise between the 2nd and 3rd electrons removed, and another between the 10th and 11th.

**(a)** State the number of electrons in each shell of a magnesium atom. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the large rise between the 2nd and 3rd electrons removed. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest how graphs like this supported the Bohr model of the atom. **[1]**

**8.** Rubidium melts at 39 °C and strontium at 777 °C.

**(a)** Describe the bonding and structure in strontium. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why strontium has the higher melting point. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why solid strontium conducts electricity. **[1]**

**9.** Diamond, graphite and graphene are all forms of carbon.

**(a)** Graphite is used to make electrodes; diamond could not be. Explain this difference in terms of bonding. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why graphene is very strong, and suggest one possible application of graphene. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why diamond does not dissolve in water. **[1]**

**10.** The melting points of the Period 3 elements, in °C, are: Na 98, Mg 650, Al 660, Si 1414, P 44, S 115, Cl −102, Ar −189.

Describe and explain the variation in melting point across Period 3 in terms of structure and bonding. **[8]**

**11.** D, E and F are elements in Period 3. Their successive ionisation energies, in kJ mol⁻¹, are given below.

| Element | Successive ionisation energies |
|---|---|
| D | 496, 4562, 6910 |
| E | 787, 1577, 3232, 4356, 16091 |
| F | 1251, 2298, 3822, 5159, 6542, 9362, 11018, 33604 |

**(a)** Deduce the group of each element. Show how you used the data. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify D, E and F. **[1]**
**(c)** Predict the order of melting points of D, E and F, from highest to lowest. Explain your order. **[3]**
**(d)** State which of D, E and F conducts electricity as a solid, and give the reason. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A repeating pattern (trend) in physical and chemical properties across each period [1]
**(b)** Both have one electron in an outer s sub-shell (2s¹ and 3s¹), the same outer arrangement [1]
**(c)** He left spaces in his table for elements not yet found and forecast what they would be like [1]; when elements such as germanium were found with the predicted properties, this supported the law [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), a description of how the table looks earns little; the credit is for the predictions and their later confirmation by experiment.

**2. (a)** 55: [Xe] 6s¹, **s-block** [1]; 52: [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s² 5p⁴, **p-block** [1]; 25: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s², **d-block** [1]
**(b)** **Group 16, Period 5** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write the configuration first; the block follows from the sub-shell the last electron enters, so a correct configuration makes the answer easy to justify.

**3. (a)** The energy required for 1 mol of atoms in the gas phase to lose 1 mol of electrons [1], producing 1 mol of 1+ ions, also gaseous [1]
**(b)** **P²⁺(g) → P³⁺(g) + e⁻** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Missing "gaseous" or the mole quantities loses credit in (a); in (b) the left-hand species must already be the 2+ ion, with (g) on both ions.

**4.** First ionisation energy **decreases** down the group [1]. Each element has an extra shell, so its outer electron sits further out [1] and more inner electrons shield it; these effects outweigh the extra protons, so less energy is required [1]
*Examiner insight:* Mention nuclear charge and explain why it is outweighed; an answer that only lists radius and shielding leaves the extra protons unaccounted for.

**5. (a)** The statement is wrong: magnesium's first ionisation energy is higher than aluminium's [1]. Aluminium's electron is removed from the 3p sub-shell, which is higher in energy than magnesium's 3s [1], so less energy is needed even though aluminium has an extra proton [1]
**(b)** Sulfur's electron is removed from a 3p orbital that holds a pair of electrons [1]; repulsion between the paired electrons makes it easier to remove [1]
*Examiner insight:* With "evaluate", say plainly whether the claim is right before giving reasons; also keep the two reasons separate, as sub-shell energy explains (a) and pair repulsion explains (b).

**6. (a)** The 2nd electron is removed from a Q⁺ ion, which attracts its electrons more strongly than the neutral atom does [1]
**(b)** The largest jump is between the 5th and 6th values (21267 ÷ 6274 = 3.39) [1]; so Q has five outer-shell electrons and is in **Group 15** [1]
**(c)** **Phosphorus** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Quote the two values either side of the jump; stating "Group 15" with no reference to the data does not show how you used it.

**7. (a)** **2, 8, 2** [1]
**(b)** The 3rd electron is taken from the second shell, nearer the nucleus [1]; fewer inner electrons shield it, so the attraction is far stronger [1]
**(c)** The jumps show electrons are arranged in separate shells (energy levels), as the Bohr model proposed [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), say which shell the electron comes from; "it is harder to remove" only restates the data.

**8. (a)** Giant metallic lattice of Sr²⁺ cations [1] held by strong electrostatic attraction to delocalised electrons [1]
**(b)** Strontium forms 2+ ions and rubidium 1+ ions; Sr²⁺ is also smaller [1]. Strontium gives two delocalised electrons per atom, rubidium one [1]. So the attraction between cations and delocalised electrons is stronger in strontium and needs more energy to overcome [1]
**(c)** Delocalised electrons can move through the lattice and carry charge [1]
*Examiner insight:* Use "cations" and "delocalised electrons" in every metallic bonding answer; "atoms" or "nuclei" in place of cations does not describe the bonding.

**9. (a)** In graphite each carbon is bonded to three others [1], so the fourth outer electron is delocalised and can move along the layers [1]. In diamond all four outer electrons are in covalent bonds, so there are no mobile charged particles [1]
**(b)** Every atom is held by strong covalent bonds across the whole sheet [1]; one use, such as flexible electronics, sensors or strong lightweight composites [1]
**(c)** The covalent bonds are too strong for water to break, so the lattice does not break up [1]
*Examiner insight:* For conductivity, name the mobile charged particle (delocalised electrons); "free ions" in graphite is wrong chemistry.

**10.** Sodium, magnesium and aluminium have giant metallic lattices [1]. Melting overcomes the attraction between cations and delocalised electrons [1]. From Na⁺ to Mg²⁺ to Al³⁺ the charge increases, the ions are smaller and more electrons are delocalised, so the bonding is stronger and the melting point rises [1]. Silicon has a giant covalent lattice [1]; melting requires many strong covalent bonds throughout the lattice to break, which gives the highest value in the period [1]. Phosphorus (P₄), sulfur (S₈) and chlorine (Cl₂) are simple molecular, and argon exists as single atoms [1]. Melting overcomes only weak London forces between molecules, not the covalent bonds, so melting points are low [1]. S₈ has 128 electrons, P₄ 60, Cl₂ 34 and Ar 18; more electrons give stronger London forces, so the order is S > P > Cl > Ar [1]
*Examiner insight:* In an extended response, link each structure to what is overcome and how strong it is; a list of structures with no explanation of the order of values gains only part of the credit.

**11. (a)** D: largest jump after the 1st value (4562 ÷ 496 = 9.20), so **Group 1** [1]. E: after the 4th (16091 ÷ 4356 = 3.69), so **Group 14** [1]. F: after the 7th (33604 ÷ 11018 = 3.05), so **Group 17** [1]
**(b)** D is **sodium**, E is **silicon** and F is **chlorine** [1]
**(c)** E > D > F. E (silicon) is giant covalent, so many strong covalent bonds must be broken [1]. D (sodium) is giant metallic, with attraction between Na⁺ and delocalised electrons that is strong but weaker than silicon's network of bonds [1]. F (chlorine) is simple molecular, Cl₂, with only weak London forces between molecules [1]
**(d)** **D**, because it has delocalised electrons that can move [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), give the order and also the structure of each element; a correct order with no reasons, or reasons with no order, earns limited credit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out "gaseous" or the mole amounts when defining first ionisation energy.
- Writing successive ionisation equations that start from the wrong ion.
- Swapping the two explanations for the small falls in first ionisation energy across a period.
- Ignoring nuclear charge when explaining the decrease down a group.
- Counting electrons after the largest jump in successive ionisation energies.
- Describing metallic bonding with "atoms" instead of cations and delocalised electrons.
- Saying covalent bonds break when a simple molecular element melts.
- Saying graphite conducts because of ions, or that diamond conducts.
- Ranking simple molecular elements without referring to the number of electrons and London forces.
