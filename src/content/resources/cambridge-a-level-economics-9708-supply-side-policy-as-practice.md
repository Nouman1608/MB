## Section A

**1.** Define supply-side policy. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is most likely to shift the long-run aggregate supply curve to the right?

A: a cut in interest rates

B: government grants that help firms adopt new production technology

C: a rise in the world price of oil

D: a rise in consumer confidence **[1]**

**3.** State the two objectives of supply-side policy named in the syllabus. **[2]**

**4.** A factory employs 250 workers for 38 hours a week and produces 19,000 units a week. A training scheme raises output per hour by 8%. Calculate output per hour before the scheme and weekly output after it. **[2]**

**5.** The wage at the factory in Question 4 is 24 per hour. Calculate unit labour cost before and after the training scheme, and the percentage change. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why supply-side policy is sometimes described as a route to non-inflationary growth. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A new motorway cuts a manufacturer's transport cost from 6.00 to 4.50 per unit. Its other costs are 30.00 per unit.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in the manufacturer's total cost per unit. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how improvements of this kind across the economy affect the SRAS and LRAS curves. **[3]**

**8.** Country P's full-capacity output is 1,200 billion and its actual real output is 1,140 billion. A supply-side programme raises full-capacity output by 4%.

**(a)** Calculate the new full-capacity output and the spare capacity if actual output does not change. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why actual output may not rise after the programme. **[2]**

**9.** A government introduces a large, successful training programme.

**(a)** Using an AD/AS diagram, analyse the effect on real output, the price level and employment. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why employment may rise by less than real output. **[2]**

**10.** A government spends 2 billion a year on building new railways. Explain the effect on the economy in the short run and in the long run. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate whether supply-side policy is a better way than expansionary monetary policy to increase economic growth. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Government action to increase the economy's productive capacity by improving the quantity, quality or efficiency of factors of production [1], shown as a rightward shift of the LRAS curve [1].

**2.** **B** [1]. New technology raises productivity and capacity. A and D shift AD; C shifts SRAS left.

**3.** Increasing productivity [1] and increasing productive capacity [1].

**4.** Hours = 250 × 38 = 9,500; output per hour = 19,000 ÷ 9,500 = **2.0 units** [1]. After: 2.0 × 1.08 = 2.16 units per hour, so weekly output = 2.16 × 9,500 = **20,520 units** [1].

**5.** Before: 24 ÷ 2.0 = **12.00 per unit**; after: 24 ÷ 2.16 = **11.11 per unit** [1]. Change = (11.11 − 12.00) ÷ 12.00 × 100 = **−7.4%** [1].

**6.** It raises the economy's capacity (LRAS shifts right), so output can grow without AD exceeding what the economy can produce [1]. Lower unit costs also shift SRAS right, which reduces pressure on the price level [1].

**7.** **(a)** Total cost falls from 36.00 to 34.50, a fall of 1.50 [1]. 1.50 ÷ 36.00 × 100 = **4.2%** [1].

**(b)** Lower transport costs reduce firms' costs of production, so they supply more at each price level and SRAS shifts right [1]. Goods move faster and more cheaply, so the same resources produce more output and firms can reach wider markets [1]. The economy's productive capacity rises, so LRAS shifts right [1].

**8.** **(a)** 1,200 × 1.04 = **1,248 billion** [1]. Spare capacity = 1,248 − 1,140 = **108 billion**, up from 60 billion [1].

**(b)** Actual output depends on aggregate demand as well as capacity [1]. If AD does not rise, firms have no reason to produce more, so the extra capacity stays unused and spare capacity simply grows [1].

**9.** **(a)** Workers produce more per hour, so unit costs fall and SRAS shifts right [1]; the economy's capacity rises, so LRAS shifts right [1]. With AD unchanged, the new equilibrium has higher real output [1] and a lower price level, while employment tends to rise as output rises [1]. (A diagram with labelled axes, LRAS1, LRAS2, AD and both equilibria supports the marks.)

**(b)** Part of the extra output comes from each worker producing more, not from more workers [1]. If AD grows more slowly than productivity, firms can meet demand with the same or fewer workers [1].

**10.** Short run: railway building is government spending, a component of AD, so AD shifts right [1], raising output and employment in construction and supplying industries, with some upward pressure on prices if the economy is near capacity [1]. Long run: once the railways operate, transport costs and journey times fall, raising productivity [1], so SRAS and LRAS shift right and the economy can produce more at lower cost [1].

**11.** For supply-side policy: it shifts LRAS right, so it raises potential output, which is the only basis for long-term growth [1]; it can raise output while lowering inflationary pressure [1]; expansionary monetary policy at full capacity raises the price level rather than output [1].

For monetary policy: it works faster, often within one or two years, while training and infrastructure take many years [1]; when there is large spare capacity, the problem is weak AD, which monetary policy addresses directly and supply-side policy does not [1]; supply-side measures are costly, with a high opportunity cost [1], and their results are uncertain [1].

Judgement: neither is better in all cases; supply-side policy is better for raising the long-run growth rate, while monetary policy is better for closing a short-run gap caused by weak demand, so the answer depends on how much spare capacity the economy has [1].
