These questions practise key question 6.1 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What are crime,
deviance and social control?* Set A follows the six-part (a)–(f) structure the syllabus describes for

## Set A — Crime, deviance and social control

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *deviance* **[2]**
- (ii) *informal social control* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** examples of how crime or deviance is relative to the situation. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** ways formal agencies of social control may prevent crime. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways the media may act as an agency of social control. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss the strengths and limitations of victim surveys as a way of measuring crime. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that informal social control is more effective than formal social control
in preventing crime and deviance. **[14]**

## Set B — Extra practice

**(g)** Distinguish between expressive and instrumental crime. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** reasons why white-collar and corporate crime may be under-recorded. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** limitations of self-report studies. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** ways religion may control behaviour. **[4]**

**(k)** Explain **two** reasons why cyber crime may be difficult to control. **[4]**

**(l)** Discuss the usefulness of official statistics in understanding crime. **[8]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Behaviour that breaks the norms of a society or group (1) and is likely to attract
disapproval; it may or may not be illegal (1).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Control through everyday relationships and unwritten norms (1), using social sanctions
such as praise, gossip or exclusion by agencies such as the family or peer group (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: killing in self-defence or war versus murder; nudity in a changing
room versus in the street; shouting at a sports match versus in a library; wearing pyjamas at home
versus at work.

**(c) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each (point plus development):
- **Deterrence**: visible police patrols and the threat of punishment discourage offending.
- **Surveillance**: CCTV and policing make it more likely offenders are caught.
- **Arrest and conviction**: courts punish offenders, signalling that the behaviour is unacceptable.
- **Incapacitation**: the penal system removes offenders' ability to reoffend while imprisoned.
- **Law-making**: government creates laws and crime-prevention policies such as better street
  lighting.
- **Armed forces**: used in emergencies to restore order or enforce curfews.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Setting norms**: news and entertainment show what is acceptable and what is punished.
- **Public shaming**: digital media can expose and condemn deviant acts quickly.
- **Moral panics**: exaggerated coverage leads to public pressure for tougher control.
- **Crime appeals**: the media help police identify suspects and locate evidence.
- **Role models**: celebrities and influencers model behaviour, positive or deviant.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, both sides with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic.
- *Strengths:* reveal crimes not reported to the police; national surveys such as the Crime Survey for
  England and Wales show trends independent of police recording; local surveys such as the Islington
  Crime Survey (1986) show crime in particular areas and among groups; anonymity encourages honesty.
- *Limitations:* miss crimes with no obvious victim, such as corporate or drug crime; memory errors
  about timing; some victims (for example of domestic abuse) may not disclose even in a survey; may
  not reach homeless people or those in institutions.
- *Evaluation:* more complete than official statistics for many crimes, but best used alongside
  other measures.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but less
balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For informal control:*
- Internalised norms mean people control themselves without police (family socialisation).
- Hirschi (1969): bonds of attachment, commitment, involvement and belief prevent offending.
- Informal sanctions such as shame or exclusion are immediate and personal.
- Religion and community norms shape behaviour where formal control is weak.
- Works before crime occurs; much cheaper than policing and prisons.

*For formal control:*
- Necessary for serious, organised and stranger crime that informal control cannot reach.
- Backed by the law and force; clear rules applied to everyone.
- Courts and prisons incapacitate dangerous offenders.
- Informal control weakens in anonymous cities and can encourage deviance (peers, gangs).

*Evaluation:* formal control misses much crime and can be applied unfairly; social media shaming can
be inaccurate; the two work together.

*Conclusion:* informal control produces most everyday conformity, while formal control deals with
what it fails to prevent. Credit a reasoned judgement.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Expressive crime is committed for emotional reasons or thrill, not material gain (1), for
example vandalism or fighting for excitement (1). Instrumental crime is committed to achieve a goal,
usually money (1), for example theft or fraud (1).

**(h) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each: victims may not know they have been harmed, such as customers
of a price-fixing company; it is complex and hard to detect; companies may deal with it internally to
protect reputation; regulators rather than police often handle it; powerful offenders can afford
lawyers to avoid prosecution.

**(i) [4]** Two limitations, 2 marks each: respondents may lie, exaggerate or forget; usually ask
about minor offences, missing serious crime; samples are often young people in schools, missing
other groups; results may not be representative.

**(j) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: teachings define what is sinful or moral; the faith community may
approve of conformity and disapprove of deviance; belief in punishment or reward after death; religious
leaders give guidance on behaviour; rituals reinforce shared values.

**(k) [4]** Two reasons, 2 marks each: offenders may be in another country, beyond national laws;
anonymity and encryption make offenders hard to identify; victims may not realise or report the
crime; technology changes faster than laws and policing.

**(l) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *Useful:* show trends over time; fairly accurate for serious crimes such as murder; cheap and
  available; used to plan policing.
- *Limitations:* the dark figure of unreported and unrecorded crime; police discretion and
  priorities; Cicourel (1968) shows stereotypes shape arrests, so statistics are socially
  constructed.
- *Evaluation:* useful as a starting point but should be compared with victim surveys and self-report
  studies.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can one crime belong to several types?** Yes. A gang robbery is gang, violent and instrumental
crime; say which feature you are using.

**Is school an informal agency?** Yes, at the level of society: it does not use the law, even though
it has its own rules and sanctions.

**Which measure of crime is best?** None is complete; each reveals different parts of the picture.
