The IB Diploma Programme History course for first assessment in 2028 names four **specified historical concepts**: **cause and consequence**, **continuity and change**, **perspectives** and **significance**. The guide says that all four must be taught, that they should not be taught in isolation, and that they should be returned to many times during the course. This guide explains each concept, its three conceptual understandings, how the concepts are examined, and how to use them to turn description into argument. It applies to both Standard Level (SL) and Higher Level (HL).

## Why concepts matter in this course

A concept is a powerful, pervasive and debatable idea that focuses historical inquiry. "Powerful" means it helps you think about many different events. "Pervasive" means it turns up in every part of the course. "Debatable" means historians can reasonably disagree about it. When you ask why a revolution happened, you are using cause and consequence. When you ask whether life really changed for ordinary people, you are using continuity and change.

The course is organised into four inquiry contexts: the focused study, the thematic study, the regional study (HL only) and the historical investigation. The concepts are not a stand-alone unit and are not an introduction to the course. They are threaded through all four. They are also not the only concepts you can use. The guide suggests others, such as conflict, revolution, nation, power and identity, and the historical investigation lets you explore a concept beyond the four specified ones.

## How the concepts are assessed

Paper 2, section A has two questions on the specified concepts, and you answer one. It is worth 6 marks. You are assessed on your **analysis** of a historical concept, illustrated with an understanding of a relevant example from your thematic study. The published markband rewards three things:

- accurate analysis of the concept itself, not just a description of it
- a relevant, specific example from your thematic study that supports the analysis
- a response that is clear, rather than one that lists facts around the concept

At the top of the band (5 to 6 marks) the concept is "clearly and accurately" analysed and "effectively supported by a relevant, specific example". At 1 to 2 marks the concept is described with minimal analysis and the example is only mentioned. The concepts are also used, where relevant, when essay questions are written in paper 2, section B. In the historical investigation, criterion A (6 marks) rewards a precise explanation of how a concept informs your inquiry question.

The command terms matter. Typical terms are "analyse" (break down to bring out the essential elements or structure), "explain" (give a detailed account including reasons or causes), "examine" (uncover assumptions and interrelationships) and "to what extent" (consider the merits or otherwise of an argument or concept).

## Cause and consequence

**Definition.** The interplay of multiple reasons for, and results of, historical events. Historians build complex explanations for why things happened.

**The three conceptual understandings.**

1. Events always have multiple, interrelated, short-term and long-term causes and consequences.
2. Causes and consequences come from the interplay of **historical actors** (people, individually or in groups, who take action) and the **conditions** within which they operate.
3. Events and their consequences were never **inevitable**. Causes and consequences can be probable, improbable, intentional or accidental.

**Using it.** The most useful habit is to separate actors from conditions. Take the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. The conditions included the alliance system, rivalry over colonies and naval power, nationalism in the Balkans and military plans that depended on speed. The actors included the assassin Gavrilo Princip, who shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in June 1914, and the leaders in Vienna, Berlin, St Petersburg and Paris who made choices in July. The conditions made a large war possible. The actors' decisions made it happen when it did. Saying the war was inevitable ignores the second set of factors; saying it was caused by one shot ignores the first.

Short-term and long-term causes are not the same as important and unimportant causes. A long-term cause (such as imperial rivalry) can be decisive, and a short-term trigger (such as an assassination) can be trivial without the conditions behind it. Intentional consequences are those the actors aimed for; accidental ones are not. The Treaty of Versailles aimed to prevent German aggression, but historians argue that its terms contributed to resentment that helped later politics. That is an unintended, long-term consequence, and the argument is debatable, which is exactly what makes it good material.

## Continuity and change

**Definition.** The processes of societies becoming different and maintaining similarity across time and place. Historians explore what has changed and what has stayed the same.

**The three conceptual understandings.**

1. Societies can undergo continuity and change **at the same time**. They are interwoven processes.
2. Continuity and change can be **rapid and transformative** over the short term, and can also follow **slower patterns and trends** over the long term.
3. People have experienced continuity and change as **positive, negative or a combination** of both.

**Using it.** Do not treat "change" as the interesting half and "continuity" as filler. The Meiji government after 1868 abolished the domains and the samurai's legal privileges within a few years, which was fast and transformative. Yet the emperor remained the centre of political legitimacy, and much of rural life and family structure continued. The accurate statement contains both. Always say who experienced the change. Factory work in Britain during industrialisation brought higher output and, for many, higher wages in the long run, but it also brought long hours, child labour and crowded housing. The same change was positive for some people, negative for others and mixed for many.

Think about the speed and the pattern. A revolution can look like a sudden break, but historians often show slow changes beforehand (economic strain, new ideas) and continuities afterwards (the same officials, the same inequalities in land ownership). Words such as "turning point", "gradual", "transformative" and "superficial" are claims about change, and you should be able to defend them with evidence.

## Perspectives

**Definition.** Diverse points of view from and about the past. Historians consider who has a view on past events.

**The three conceptual understandings.**

1. There are always **diverse** perspectives, held both by people who took part in or observed events and by people looking back from a later date.
2. Perspectives are not all equally **valid**. Historians decide this by examining the **claims** being made.
3. Perspectives can **corroborate and support** each other or be **contradictory**, leading to ambiguities that historians must try to resolve.

**Using it.** Perspectives are not the same as "bias". A perspective is a position from which someone viewed events: a soldier, a minister, a woman excluded from the vote, a colonised farmer, a later historian with access to archives. Take European contact with the Americas after 1492. A Spanish chronicler, an Indigenous eyewitness account written later in Nahuatl and a modern historian will emphasise different things. None of these is automatically right. You decide which claims are best supported, and you can show where perspectives agree (corroboration) and where they conflict.

In the focused study you meet perspectives through sources in paper 1, and the third question there asks you to examine the perspectives in all the sources. In the thematic study you meet them through participants and historians. In the investigation you must show how your chosen sources provide diverse perspectives. A good habit is to name the perspective precisely ("a Kikuyu fighter in the forest", not "the Africans") and then say what that position allowed the person to see and what it hid.

## Significance

**Definition.** The value judgements made about historical people and events. Historians determine which stories should be told.

**The three conceptual understandings.**

1. Significance is **constructed** through judgements about which people and events are included in, or excluded from, narratives.
2. Judgements of significance can reflect the **evidence** the historian has and also their **values** and those of their society.
3. People and events can be significant because of their **power, importance and impact**, and also because of what they **reveal** about the past, even if they lacked power or were marginalised.

**Using it.** Significance is not a fact waiting to be found. Compare two ways of judging the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955 to 1956. One looks at impact: it helped launch a national civil rights movement and brought Martin Luther King Jr to prominence. Another looks at what it reveals: the organisation by Black churches and by women in the Women's Political Council shows how a movement was built from below. Both are legitimate and they highlight different things. Judgements can also change over time. People once left out of textbooks, such as enslaved communities and women workers, are now studied because historians ask different questions and have new evidence.

Always give criteria. Useful ones are scale (how many people), depth (how much it changed lives), duration (how long it lasted), and what it reveals. Then compare two cases against the same criteria instead of declaring that one is "more significant" without a standard.

## Linking the concepts to examples

Because paper 2, section A asks you to illustrate a concept with an example from your thematic study, build a small bank of examples for each concept before the exam. For each example, write one line for each concept. For instance, the Salt March of 1930 in the popular movements study: a cause (the salt tax and a campaign needing a simple symbol), a consequence (international attention and mass arrests), a change (a shift from elite politics to mass participation), a continuity (British rule continued until 1947), a perspective (a British official's alarm versus a participant's account) and a significance (a symbol for later movements). One example can serve several concepts, and that is intended.

## How to write a strong section A answer

1. **Define the concept briefly in your own words**, using a conceptual understanding.
2. **Choose one specific example** and give accurate detail: names, dates, places.
3. **Analyse, do not narrate.** Tie each point back to the concept: "This shows that the cause was the interplay of actors and conditions because ..."
4. **Qualify.** Mention who experienced the change differently, or which perspective is better supported.
5. **Finish with a short judgement** that answers the question.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the concept and then telling the story of the example without linking the two.
- Using vague examples ("many revolutions").
- Treating cause as a list of factors with no ranking or interplay.
- Saying an event "changed everything" with no mention of what continued.
- Confusing consequence (what followed) with significance (why it matters and by what standard).
- Calling a perspective "biased" and stopping there, instead of explaining what it reveals.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are the concepts a separate topic?** No. The guide says they should not be taught in isolation and should be returned to throughout the course.

**Can I use a concept that is not one of the four?** In the historical investigation, yes. For paper 2, section A, the questions are on the specified concepts.

**Do I need an example from every region?** Section A needs one relevant example from your thematic study. Section B essays need examples from at least two regions.
