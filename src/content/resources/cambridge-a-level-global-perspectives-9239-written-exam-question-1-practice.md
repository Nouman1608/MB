These questions practise Question 1 of Component 1 in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, which the syllabus describes as "a structured question based on two source documents" in which candidates "answer short questions that require candidates to demonstrate AO1 skills". For the overview of the paper, see the [Component 1 Written Exam study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-global-perspectives-written-exam/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, with two original sources. They are **not** past-paper questions and do **not** copy the real paper's structure: the real Question 1 is worth 5 marks in total, while this set has twelve short questions so that each skill can be practised separately. Use them alongside the specimen and past papers your teacher provides.

Revise first with the [Question 1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-written-exam-question-1/) and the [Question 1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-written-exam-question-1-revision-notes/).

## Sources

**Source A** (statement from a fictional airline industry association)

> Banning short flights would achieve little. Domestic flights under 500 km produce a small share of our members' total emissions, because most fuel is burned on long-haul routes. Rail journeys between many regional cities still take over six hours, so a ban would cut regional towns off from business. Some campaigners claim travellers will simply switch to trains, but where no fast line exists they will drive instead. New fuel-efficient aircraft will halve emissions per passenger within twenty years. Governments should invest in cleaner aircraft, not ban flights.

**Source B** (blog post by a fictional university transport researcher)

> Short flights are often slower than the train. On the route I study, the train takes two and a half hours from city centre to city centre, while the flight takes three hours once check-in and airport transfers are counted. A survey of 1,200 passengers on that route found that most would choose the train if fares were equal. So the main obstacle is price, not time. Critics argue that limiting flights harms regional economies, yet a region connected by frequent trains keeps its links. Governments should therefore cap short flights on routes with a fast rail alternative and use the money raised to lower rail fares.

## Section A

**1.** Identify the main conclusion of Source A. **[1]**

**2.** Identify the main conclusion of Source B. **[1]**

**3.** Identify one prediction in Source A. **[1]**

**4.** Identify the counter-assertion in Source B. **[1]**

**5.** "Domestic flights under 500 km produce a small share of our members' total emissions, because most fuel is burned on long-haul routes." State whether this sentence from Source A is an argument or an explanation, and explain your answer. **[2]**

**6.** Identify one piece of evidence in Source B and state what type of evidence it is. **[2]**

**7.** Identify one unstated assumption that Source A needs for its conclusion to follow. **[2]**

## Section B

**8.** Identify one claim of fact and one opinion in Source A. For each, explain why it belongs in that category. **[4]**

**9.** Explain one similarity and one difference between the perspectives in Source A and Source B. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how the context of the writer of Source A might shape its perspective. **[3]**

**11.**

**(a)** Identify the intermediate conclusion in Source B. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how it links the evidence to the main conclusion. **[2]**

**12.** A student answers a comparison question with: "Both sources are about planes and trains." Explain why this would gain little credit, and rewrite it as a precise comparison. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Governments should invest in cleaner aircraft, not ban flights [1]. Accept "banning short flights would achieve little" only if linked to the recommendation; on its own it is an intermediate claim. **[1]**

**2.** Governments should cap short flights on routes with a fast rail alternative and use the money raised to lower rail fares [1]. **[1]**

**3.** "New fuel-efficient aircraft will halve emissions per passenger within twenty years" [1]. **[1]**

**4.** "Critics argue that limiting flights harms regional economies" [1]. Do not credit the writer's reply ("a region connected by frequent trains keeps its links"); that is the response to the counter-assertion. **[1]**

**5.** Explanation [1]. The writer is not trying to persuade the reader that the share is small; the clause after "because" accounts for why the share is small, by pointing to where most fuel is burned [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any one of:
- The survey of 1,200 passengers on one route [1]: survey or statistical evidence, primary research reported by the writer [1].
- The journey-time comparison of two and a half hours by train against three hours by air [1]: factual or measured evidence from the writer's own study [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Any one, stated as a full sentence:
- The predicted cleaner aircraft will actually be developed and widely used [1], because the recommendation relies on them replacing current aircraft [1].
- A reduction in emissions in twenty years is soon enough [1], because the source rejects a ban, which could act now, in favour of a slower route [1].
- Rail journeys on these routes will not become faster [1], because the six-hour figure is used as a lasting reason against a ban [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Claim of fact: "Rail journeys between many regional cities still take over six hours" (or the claim about the share of emissions) [1]; it could be checked against timetables or emissions data, even though the source does not prove it [1]. Opinion: "Banning short flights would achieve little" (or "Governments should invest in cleaner aircraft") [1]; it is a judgement about value or what ought to happen, which cannot simply be checked [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Similarity: both sources want governments to act on aviation emissions rather than leave them alone [1], and both accept that travellers' choices depend on the alternatives available to them [1]. Difference: Source A trusts technological change to cut emissions while keeping flights, whereas Source B wants to shift passengers to rail now [1]; A's perspective is built on regional access and industry interests, B's on journey times and price signals [1]. **[4]**

**10.** An industry association represents airlines, so it has an economic interest in flights continuing [1]. This may explain why it frames the issue around investment in aircraft, which keeps its members flying, rather than limits on flights [1]. It also explains the emphasis on regional business links, an argument that appeals to governments without mentioning airline revenue [1]. Credit any reasonable point that links the writer's position to the perspective. **[3]**

**11. (a)** "So the main obstacle is price, not time" [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** The journey times show the train is already faster, and the survey shows passengers would switch at equal fares [1]; from these the writer concludes that price is the obstacle, which then supports the recommendation to use money raised from capping flights to lower rail fares [1]. **[2]**

**12.** It identifies only the shared topic, not a specific point on which the sources agree or differ, so it shows no analysis of their arguments or perspectives [1]. A precise version names the point and each position [1], for example: "On whether regional towns would lose connections, Source A says a ban would cut them off because rail is slow, whereas Source B says frequent trains would keep their links" [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Identifying the topic or a reason when the question asks for the main conclusion.
- Quoting the writer's reply instead of the counter-assertion itself.
- Calling a sentence an argument because it contains "because", without asking what it is trying to do.
- Writing an assumption that the source actually states.
- Naming the type of evidence without saying which piece of evidence you mean.
- Comparing the subjects of the sources rather than their positions on a named point.
