Revise first with the [factors of production study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-the-factors-of-production-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [factors of production revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-the-factors-of-production-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/). All people, places and figures are invented.

## Section A

**1.** Which factor of production is rewarded with interest? **[1]**

A: Land

B: Labour

C: Capital

D: Enterprise

**2.** Which of the following is an example of land? **[1]**

A: A fishing boat

B: A shoal of fish in the sea

C: A fish-processing factory

D: The manager of a fishing company

**3.** In which situation is a car a factor of production? **[1]**

A: It is used by a family for weekend trips.

B: It is kept in a museum.

C: It is used by a driving school to give lessons.

D: It is given as a birthday present.

**4.** Which change would increase the quality, rather than the quantity, of labour? **[1]**

A: Immigration of working-age people

B: A rise in the retirement age

C: A national training programme

D: A rise in the birth rate

**5.** Define *enterprise* as a factor of production. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why money is not a factor of production. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Rosa runs a small bakery. She rents the shop, employs three bakers, has borrowed money to buy two ovens, and makes all the decisions about what to bake.

**(a)** Identify each factor of production in Rosa's bakery and state its reward. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why Rosa's reward is less certain than the rewards of the other factors. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **two** influences on the occupational mobility of labour. **[4]**

**9.** A town's car factory closes. The town has vacancies for nurses and software developers.

**(a)** Explain why the former car workers may stay unemployed despite the vacancies. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why the factory's specialised equipment may be hard to reuse. **[2]**

**10.** Country Q's labour force rose from 8.0 million to 8.6 million over five years.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in the labour force. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **two** possible causes of the increase. **[4]**

**11.** A farming region has 2,000 hectares producing 3 tonnes of rice per hectare. Over ten years, 150 hectares are lost to new housing, but irrigation raises the yield to 3.6 tonnes per hectare.

**(a)** Calculate total rice output before and after the changes. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain the change in output using the ideas of quantity and quality of land. **[3]**

**12.** Discuss whether an increase in the quantity of capital will always increase a country's output. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. Capital is rewarded with interest.

**2.** **B** [1]. Fish in the sea are a natural resource. The boat and factory are capital; the manager supplies labour or enterprise.

**3.** **C** [1]. When used to produce a service (driving lessons), the car is capital. In A, B and D it is not used in production.

**4.** **C** [1]. Training makes each worker more productive. A, B and D increase the number of workers.

**5.** The factor that organises land, labour and capital to produce goods and services [1] and bears the risk of the business, rewarded by profit [1].

**6.** Money cannot produce anything by itself [1]; it is used to buy factors such as machines and labour, which are the real resources used in production [1].

**7. (a)** The rented shop site: land, reward rent [1]. Bakers: labour, reward wages [1]. Ovens: capital, reward interest (on the money borrowed) [1]. Rosa: enterprise, reward profit [1].

**(b)** Rosa's profit is what is left after rent, wages and interest have been paid [1]. If sales fall, those costs must still be paid, so her profit may be small or negative because she bears the risk [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Skills and qualifications: many jobs need specific training [1], so a worker without them cannot move into that job until retrained [1]. Cost and time of retraining: retraining may take months or years and be expensive [1], so older workers, or those with families to support, may be unable or unwilling to retrain [1].

**9. (a)** The car workers' skills are specific to car production [1], while nursing and software development need different qualifications [1]. Retraining takes time and money, and some workers may be too old to benefit [1], so they are occupationally immobile and stay unemployed even though vacancies exist [1].

**(b)** Equipment designed for making cars is specific capital [1], so it cannot easily be converted to other uses and may be costly to move or sell [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** Increase = 8.6 − 8.0 = 0.6 million [1]. Percentage increase = 0.6 ÷ 8.0 × 100 = **7.5%** [1].

**(b)** Immigration of working-age people [1] adds directly to the number of people able to work [1]. More people choosing to work, for example more women joining the labour force or people retiring later [1], increases the share of the population in the labour force [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Before: 2,000 × 3 = **6,000 tonnes** [1]. Land after: 2,000 − 150 = 1,850 hectares [1]. After: 1,850 × 3.6 = **6,660 tonnes** [1].

**(b)** The quantity of farmland fell by 150 hectares, which on its own would reduce output [1]. The quality of land rose, as irrigation increased yield by 20% [1]. The quality gain outweighed the quantity loss, so output rose by 660 tonnes [1]. **[6]**

**12.** More capital, such as machines and infrastructure, usually lets each worker produce more [1], so output tends to rise [1]. However, the new capital needs workers with the right skills to operate it; without training, it may be under-used [1]. If capital rises only because more of the same old machines are bought, output may rise less than expected compared with investing in better technology [1]. Output also depends on whether there is enough demand for the extra goods [1]. Conclusion: an increase in capital usually raises the ability to produce, but output rises only if the capital is used, which depends on skills, quality of the capital and demand [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a factor without its reward when both are asked for.
- Calling money or savings capital.
- Explaining mobility without saying whether it is geographical or occupational.
- Treating more workers as better quality labour.
- Using the original land area after part of it has been lost.
