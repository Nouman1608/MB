## Section A

**1.** Define the term "resilience" in the context of natural hazards. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between mitigation and preparedness. **[2]**

**3.** State two reasons why people continue to live in hazardous environments. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what is meant by "building back better". **[3]**

**5.** Explain how sea walls can create a false sense of security. **[3]**

**6.** Outline two ways that the cost of losses from a hazard can be shared. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows information about the fictional volcanic island of Sorrel before and after an eruption that began in 2010.

| | 2009 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 12,400 | 5,580 |
| Land area open to residents (km²) | 102 | 41 |
| Farmland in use (km²) | 38 | 9 |
| Share of government budget from overseas aid (%) | 8 | 46 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in population between 2009 and 2020. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the population density of the area open to residents in 2009 and in 2020. **[2]**

**(c)** Using the data, suggest why the management of Sorrel may not be sustainable. **[4]**

**8.** The fictional coastal town of Hama has a 9 m sea wall. A planner proposes three options after a tsunami overtopped it: (A) raise the wall to 14 m; (B) move housing to a plateau 30 m above sea level and use the lowland for parks and fishing facilities; (C) keep the wall and build two evacuation towers.

**(a)** Suggest one advantage and one disadvantage of option B. **[2]**

**(b)** Evaluate which option is most sustainable. **[5]**

**9.** Explain why the poorest people are often the most vulnerable in a hazardous environment. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how the disaster management cycle can be used to evaluate management of a hazard. **[5]**

**11.** With reference to a case study, describe the problems of sustainable management of a hazardous environment. **[6]**

**12.** With reference to a hazardous environment you have studied, evaluate the success of attempts to manage it sustainably. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The ability of a community to absorb the effects of a hazard event [1] and recover quickly, returning to normal functioning [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Mitigation reduces the effects of a hazard before it happens, for example through building codes or zoning [1], whereas preparedness plans the response, for example warnings, drills and stockpiles [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Fertile soils or valuable resources such as fishing grounds provide a living [1]. Family ties, cultural attachment or the cost of moving keep people in place [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Rebuilding after a disaster in a way that reduces future risk [1], for example stronger buildings or relocating housing out of the danger zone [1], rather than replacing what existed before [1]. **[3]**

**5.** People believe the wall will protect them from any event [1], so more development takes place behind it [1], and in an event larger than the design height people may evacuate late or not at all [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Insurance spreads losses among many policyholders over time [1], so individuals can rebuild without bearing the full cost [1]. Government or international aid transfers money from unaffected areas to the affected one [1], paying for response and reconstruction [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** (12,400 - 5,580) / 12,400 x 100 [1] = 55% fall [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 2009: 12,400 / 102 = about 122 people per km² [1]. 2020: 5,580 / 41 = about 136 people per km² [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Farmland in use fell from 38 to 9 km², so food production and farm incomes are much lower [1]. Population fell by more than half, reducing the workforce and tax base [1]. Aid rose from 8% to 46% of the budget, showing dependence on outside funding [1]. Density in the open area rose, so pressure on the remaining land and services increased [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Advantage: housing is above the reach of a large tsunami, protecting life even in rare events [1]. Disadvantage: residents are further from the sea, their work and their community, and new infrastructure is costly [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Option A protects existing housing, but it is very costly and may still be overtopped by a larger event [1]. Option C is cheaper and saves lives if people can reach the towers in time [1], but property in the lowland remains exposed [1]. Option B removes housing from the danger zone, so it is resilient to rare large events [1]. Option B is likely to be most sustainable over the long term, provided residents are consulted and fishing and port activity can continue in the lowland [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Poorer people can only afford cheaper land, which is often in the most exposed places such as steep slopes, floodplains or coasts [1]. Their housing is weaker and collapses more easily [1]. They are less likely to have insurance or savings to recover [1]. They may lack transport, phones or education to receive and act on warnings [1]. Their livelihoods, such as farming or fishing, depend on the hazardous land itself [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The cycle divides management into mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery [1]. Each stage can be checked for what was done and how well it worked [1]. Investment in mitigation and preparedness can be compared with spending on response [1]. Recovery can be judged by whether it built back better or recreated the same risk [1]. A sustainable system shows improvement in each round of the cycle, with losses falling over successive events [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Credit a named hazardous environment with relevant detail, for example: the hazard and its recurrence are identified [1]; physical problems such as uncertainty of timing or size are explained [1]; human problems such as population pressure or poverty in exposed areas are explained [1]; economic problems such as cost or dependence on aid are given [1]; conflicts between groups or land uses are identified [1]; supporting dates or data are included [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content (Montserrat is used here; credit any valid case study).

*Introduction:* locate the environment and its hazard [1]; define sustainable management [1].

*Problems:* uncertainty about the eruption's course [1]; pyroclastic flows destroyed Plymouth and the airport [1]; best farmland lay in the south [1].

*Successes:* the Montserrat Volcano Observatory provides continuous monitoring [1]. Hazard zoning and an exclusion zone keep people away from the main danger [1]. Development moved to the safer north [1]. UK aid funded housing and infrastructure [1].

*Limitations:* the 1997 deaths show zoning failed when people re-entered [1]. The population fell sharply [1]. The economy depends on aid [1]. Less land is available for farming and building [1].

*Evaluation against the tests:* effective for protecting life [1]; less successful economically [1]; social costs fell on those who lost homes and land [1]; resilient to future eruptions because people are away from the danger [1].

*Judgement:* successful socially, partly economically [1]; sustainability depends on long-term external support [1]; a reasoned final view supported by evidence [1]. **[20]**
