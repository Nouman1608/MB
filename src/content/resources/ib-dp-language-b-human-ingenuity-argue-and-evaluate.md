# Human ingenuity: argue and evaluate

The model texts are in English and every example is only an illustration of a skill; do the language-producing tasks in the target language of your school course and ask your school teacher to check them. The brief's recommended topics are optional and its possible questions are only possible, so your teacher chooses what to teach.

[Language B course hub](/boards/ib/ib-dp/language-b/) · [printable checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/language-b/). Applies to SL and HL, first assessment 2020, examined until the new syllabus is first assessed in May 2029. This page is a writing and speaking companion to the reviewed [Human ingenuity study guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-human-ingenuity/).

## The theme in the brief

- Guiding principle: explore the ways in which human creativity and innovation affect our world.
- Optional recommended topics: entertainment; artistic expressions; communication and media; technology; scientific innovation.
- Possible questions: what can we learn about a culture through its artistic expression? How do the media change the way we relate to each other?

## Evaluate, do not only describe

Texts on this theme often ask you to judge an effect. A reliable frame:

1. **Claim:** say what the innovation or creation does.
2. **Evidence:** a precise example.
3. **Effect:** who benefits and who does not.
4. **Counterpoint:** a reasonable opposite view.
5. **Judgement:** your balanced conclusion.

## Worked example: a short evaluative paragraph (original, English)

"Streaming services have made films available to people who live far from a cinema (claim). A student in a small town can now watch a film the same week as a city audience (evidence). This widens access, although it can leave local cinemas with fewer visitors (effect and counterpoint). On balance, access matters most, but towns should support their cinemas (judgement)."

## Worked example: reading a text on artistic expression

A short original text: *A mural painted by pupils on a school wall shows the town's river, a bridge and a baker.* Question: what can we learn about the culture? Answer plan: what is shown (river, bridge, baker); what that suggests people value (local trade, shared places); a limit (a mural shows what pupils chose, not the whole town).

## Text types and conventions

Your school guide sets the text types. Evaluative writing suits opinion pieces, reviews and articles; check register against audience. Paper 1 tasks have a context, audience and purpose; match them.

## Common errors

- A list of benefits with no counterpoint.
- Technology vocabulary with no example.
- Opinion with no evidence.

## Check your understanding

1. Give two recommended topics. 2. What does the five-step frame start with? *A claim.* 3. Why is a counterpoint useful? *It shows balanced thinking and higher-order language.*
