**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 2, "Pre-colonial sub-Saharan African states (circa 800 to 1945)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). It is assessed in Paper 3, where you write two essays of 15 marks from different regional studies. The guide states that every examination question on this study will require you to refer to **one sub-Saharan state**. The suggested examples are the Ghana Empire (830 to 1240), the Swahili city-states (1000 to 1500), the Mali Empire (1235 to 1400), Ashanti (1701 to 1902), Kongo (1400 to 1914), the Ethiopian Empire (1850 to 1945) and the Zulu Kingdom (1816 to 1887).

You should know at least one state in real depth, and preferably two from different regions and periods, so that you can answer any of the six inquiry topics. This guide uses three working examples, **Mali** (West Africa, trans-Saharan trade), **Ashanti** (forest and coast, gold and Atlantic trade) and the **Zulu Kingdom** (southern Africa, military and political reorganisation), and adds short notes on the others. Do not treat these as the only choices; swap in your own class examples, using the same questions.

## 1. Emergence and consolidation of the state

The inquiry topic looks at political, military, social and economic factors.

**Mali.** Before Mali, the Ghana Empire had grown rich on gold and salt trade, with its capital at Koumbi Saleh. After Ghana fragmented, the Sosso king Sumanguru dominated the region. Sundiata Keita, a Mandinka leader, gathered allied clans and defeated Sumanguru at Kirina in about 1235. This **military** success was backed by **political** alliances among Mande clans, a shared **social** identity of hunters and farmers, and control of **economic** assets: the gold fields of Bambuk and Bure.

**Ashanti.** In the late seventeenth century, Akan clans around Kumasi were subject to Denkyira. Osei Tutu united them, with the priest Okomfo Anokye, in about 1701. The symbol of unity was the Golden Stool, said to have come down from the sky. The confederation defeated Denkyira and expanded using firearms bought through trade with coastal Europeans.

**Zulu.** In the early nineteenth century, pressure on land, drought and competition for cattle and trade routes led northern Nguni chiefdoms to merge. Dingiswayo's Mthethwa chiefdom was a leading power. Shaka, a Zulu chief who had served in it, took the Zulu throne in about 1816 and defeated rivals such as the Ndwandwe. He reorganised young men into age regiments (*amabutho*), trained them in close combat with the short stabbing spear and the "buffalo horns" formation, and incorporated defeated people into the kingdom.

**Arguments to weigh:** states emerged mainly through conquest (Zulu), or through alliance and shared ideology (Mali, Ashanti). Historians also debate how far the Zulu rise was the cause or the result of the wider upheaval called the *mfecane*, and how far European trade influenced it. Treat these as live arguments, not fixed answers.

## 2. Reasons for the growth of production and trade

The specified content is main commodities, trade routes, productive lands (including agriculture and minerals) and mobilisation of labour.

- **Mali:** gold from the south, salt from Saharan mines such as Taghaza, copper, cloth, kola nuts and slaves moved by camel caravan across the Sahara to North Africa. The Niger River and its floodplain supported farming, fishing and river trade. Taxes on caravans and a monopoly on gold dust funded the state. Towns such as Timbuktu, Gao and Djenné grew as markets and centres of Islamic learning.
- **Ashanti:** gold was the main export, with kola and, from the eighteenth century, enslaved people sold to Europeans at the coast (for example through Elmina). Imports included cloth, metal goods, guns and gunpowder. Roads out of Kumasi were maintained for trade, and the state taxed traders.
- **Zulu:** cattle were the key form of wealth. Land for grazing mattered more than long-distance trade, though contact with Delagoa Bay and traders in Natal brought beads, cloth and weapons. Labour was organised by the kingdom: regiments herded the king's cattle and farmed royal lands as well as fighting.
- **Swahili city-states (note):** Kilwa and Mombasa grew by linking inland ivory and gold with Indian Ocean merchants through the monsoon winds.

**Argument to weigh:** "Trade, not warfare, was the main driver of state growth." Mali and the Swahili states support it strongly; the Zulu example is the strongest challenge.

## 3. Maintenance of authority

The specified content is the role of leaders, centralisation of power, dynasties and succession, and diplomacy.

- **Mali:** the mansa ruled through provincial governors, army commanders and the court. Mansa Musa (c.1312 to 1337) made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 to 1325. His gifts of gold in Cairo are recorded by Arab writers, and his reign brought Islamic scholars and builders to Timbuktu. Rule stayed in the Keita family but succession disputes weakened it.
- **Ashanti:** the Asantehene sat on the Golden Stool, supported by a council of chiefs and a bureaucracy of officials. Power was balanced: the queen mother and clan heads had real roles under matrilineal inheritance. Osei Bonsu (1804 to 1824) used diplomacy and war to hold the confederation together.
- **Zulu:** Shaka governed through personal authority and the regiments. After his assassination in 1828, Dingane and later Mpande and Cetshwayo kept the system, but the king's power depended on the military and on distributing cattle.

## 4. Impact of the state

The specified content is religious, cultural and social impact, and the experiences of women.

- **Religion:** in Mali, Islam spread through the court and trading towns, while traditional beliefs continued in the countryside. In Ashanti, ancestral worship and the Golden Stool shaped political identity, though Christian missions appeared later. Under the Zulu kings, ritual specialists and ceremonies tied to kingship held importance.
- **Culture:** Timbuktu's mosques and libraries in Mali; goldwork, kente cloth and court ceremonies in Ashanti; praise poetry and military dress among the Zulu.
- **Society:** the states reorganised clans and created classes of officials, traders and soldiers. Slavery existed within many societies, and the Atlantic trade changed its scale.
- **Women:** Ashanti queen mothers held formal influence. Women worked in farming and markets across the region. Under Zulu rule, women were organised into female age groups and the king controlled marriage timing, which shows limits on women's freedom. Take care to separate evidence from later European accounts, which often distort women's roles.

## 5. Reasons for decline and collapse

Specified content: opposition, resistance and civil wars; foreign challenges; economic factors; and the trade in enslaved people.

- **Mali:** disputes over succession, revolts of tributary peoples, and the rise of Songhai under Sunni Ali, who took Timbuktu in 1468. Changing trade routes later shifted wealth toward the Atlantic coast.
- **Ashanti:** wars with Britain, including 1824, 1874 (when British forces entered Kumasi), 1896 (annexation) and 1900 (the War of the Golden Stool, led by Yaa Asantewaa); British protection of coastal peoples; and the decline of the slave trade, which weakened income. Ashanti became part of the Gold Coast colony in 1902.
- **Zulu:** civil conflict between Cetshwayo's supporters and rivals, then war with Britain in 1879. The Zulu won at Isandlwana in January but were beaten at Ulundi in July. Britain divided the kingdom and later annexed it in 1887.
- **Others:** Kongo lost power after defeat by Portugal at Mbwila in 1665 and civil wars. Ethiopia under Menelik II defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896, one of the few African states to resist conquest, and was briefly occupied by Italy from 1936 until 1941.

## 6. Impact of decline and collapse

Specified content: successor states, challenges for marginalised groups, changing traditional practices, and commercial and social networks.

After Mali, Songhai took over the Niger trade, then fell to Morocco in 1591. In the Ashanti area, smaller states broke away and then came under British rule. After the Zulu defeat, chiefdoms were split and later land was lost to settlers, while labour migration to mines began. Groups on the margins, such as people held in slavery or conquered peoples, could gain freedom but often lost protection. Chiefs sometimes kept authority under colonial rule, which changed their role. Trading networks shifted from caravan routes and local markets to railways and ports controlled by Europeans.

## Worked example: planning a Paper 3 essay

*Question:* "The strength of leadership was the main reason for the growth of a sub-Saharan African state." Evaluate this claim with reference to one state.

1. **Identify the argument.** Leadership is the main factor. To test it, name the other factors: trade and resources, military organisation, ideology.
2. **Choose a state.** For Ashanti: Osei Tutu and Okomfo Anokye's Golden Stool united clans; Osei Bonsu extended the state.
3. **Evaluate perspectives.** One view stresses founders' vision. A second view stresses gold and firearms from Atlantic trade: without them, leadership could not have defeated Denkyira. A third view stresses social structures such as clan and matrilineal institutions that lasted beyond any one ruler.
4. **Judge.** Leadership started the process but trade and institutions made it last, so "main" overstates it.

## Common mistakes

- Describing several states briefly instead of using one in depth; the question will name "one state".
- Using sources from colonial observers without noting their bias.
- Treating decline as only European conquest and ignoring internal causes.
- Mixing up the Ghana Empire (medieval West Africa) with the modern state of Ghana.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use a state not on the suggested list?** Yes. The guide says the examples are suggestions, so you may choose another sub-Saharan state, provided you can cover all six inquiry topics.

**Do I need to know dates exactly?** Know the main ones above, and say "about" when a date is approximate.

**Which concept fits this study best?** Cause and consequence, and continuity and change, appear most; significance appears when you judge a leader or state.
