## Section A

**1.** Give the meaning of the term "tourism multiplier effect". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between allocentric and psychocentric tourists. **[2]**

**3.** Tourists spend 20 million currency units in each of two fictional resorts. In Resort A, 25% of spending leaks out of the local economy at each round; in Resort B, 75% leaks out.

**(a)** Using total income = initial spending ÷ leakage share, calculate the total local income generated in each resort. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest reasons why leakage is much higher in Resort B. **[3]**

**4.** Outline Doxey's irritation index and explain how it relates to carrying capacity. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **two** supply-side reasons for the growth of international tourism. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows illustrative shares of international tourist arrivals by world region.

| Region | Share in 1990 (%) | Share in 2019 (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Europe | 60 | 51 |
| Americas | 21 | 15 |
| Asia and the Pacific | 13 | 25 |
| Africa | 3 | 5 |
| Middle East | 3 | 4 |

**(a)** Describe the changes in the regional shares of arrivals. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest reasons for the growth in the share of Asia and the Pacific. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how the economic impacts of tourism can differ between the local and the national scale. **[6]**

**8.** Read the description of a fictional lodge.

> Jaguar Ridge Lodge lies inside a rainforest reserve in a middle-income country. It has 20 cabins powered by solar panels, and guests are guided on forest walks by trained local residents. A share of each booking pays for park rangers. The lodge is owned by an overseas company, most food is flown in, and nearly all guests arrive on long-haul flights.

Evaluate how far Jaguar Ridge Lodge meets the principles of ecotourism. **[6]**

**9.** Explain why a destination's physical, ecological and perceptual carrying capacities may be reached at different numbers of visitors. **[4]**

**10.** "Butler's tourism area life cycle model is of little use in managing tourist destinations." Critically evaluate this view. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The process by which tourist spending generates further rounds of income in a destination [1], as businesses buy local supplies and workers spend their wages, so total income is greater than the original spending [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Allocentric tourists are adventurous and seek new, less-visited places, so they arrive early in a destination's development [1]; psychocentric tourists prefer familiar, safe, well-provided places, so they arrive at the mass-tourism stage [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Resort A: 20 ÷ 0.25 = 80 million [1]; Resort B: 20 ÷ 0.75 = 26.7 million [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Hotels in B may be foreign-owned, so profits leave the area [1]. Food, drink and fuel may be imported rather than bought from local producers [1]. All-inclusive resorts may keep tourists' spending inside the hotel, so little reaches local businesses [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Doxey suggested that residents' attitudes change as tourism grows [1], from euphoria, to apathy, to annoyance and finally antagonism [1]. Rising resident irritation shows that perceptual or social carrying capacity is being approached or exceeded [1], even if physical space or ecosystems could still absorb more visitors [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Cheaper air travel, through wide-bodied jets and low-cost airlines [1], cut the price of long-distance and short-break travel, so more people could afford to go abroad [1]. Online booking and price comparison websites [1] made it easy to plan independent trips and find low prices, widening the range of destinations [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit easier visa rules or government marketing and infrastructure, each with its effect on growth.

**6. (a)** Europe remained the largest region but its share fell from 60% to 51% [1]. Asia and the Pacific almost doubled its share, from 13% to 25% [1]. The Americas' share fell from 21% to 15%, while Africa and the Middle East rose slightly, to 5% and 4% [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Rising incomes in Asian MICs created large numbers of new tourists, many travelling within the region [1]. Low-cost airlines and new airports improved access [1]. Governments marketed destinations and eased visa rules [1]. Long-haul visitors from Europe and North America were attracted by lower costs and distinctive cultures and landscapes [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Nationally, tourism earns foreign exchange and tax revenue [1], which can fund infrastructure and reduce a trade deficit [1]. Locally, it creates jobs in hotels, transport and crafts [1], but these may be seasonal and low-paid [1]. Leakage through foreign-owned hotels may be high nationally, while the local area also loses spending to enclave resorts [1]. Tourism can raise local land and house prices, harming residents, even when national figures show overall gain [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Solar power reduces the lodge's environmental impact [1]. A share of each booking funds rangers, supporting conservation [1]. Local residents work as guides and share knowledge, giving local benefit and an educational role [1]. However, overseas ownership means profits leak abroad [1]. Food flown in creates emissions and limits benefit to local farmers [1]. On balance it meets some principles in its operation but falls short on local ownership and on the emissions of guests' long-haul flights [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Physical capacity depends on space and facilities such as beds and car parks [1], which may be large in a well-built resort. Ecological capacity depends on how fragile habitats are, so sensitive sites such as reefs or dunes may be damaged by relatively few visitors [1]. Perceptual capacity depends on how many visitors residents and tourists will accept [1], which varies with culture, expectations and how benefits are shared, so it may be reached before or after the other limits [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* outline Butler's model (1980) and its stages [1], and state what managing a destination involves [1].

*Evidence that it is useful:* it links growth to carrying capacity, warning managers when to act [1]. Stage indicators such as ageing facilities and resident tension help diagnose problems [1]. It shows that rejuvenation is possible, encouraging investment and reinvention [1]. It explains changes in visitor types, linked to Plog's allocentric and psychocentric travellers [1]. Managers in places such as Venice can see stagnation-stage pressures and respond with access fees or limits [1].

*Evidence that it is of little use:* stages are usually identified only in hindsight [1]. Stage lengths vary greatly, so it cannot predict timing [1]. Destinations may skip stages after large-scale planned investment [1]. A destination can serve several markets at different stages at once [1]. External shocks such as pandemics or conflict cause decline unrelated to the cycle [1]. Defining the area being studied is difficult [1].

*Critical analysis:* the model is descriptive, not predictive [1]. Its usefulness rises when combined with carrying-capacity indicators and resident attitudes [1]. Intervention by managers changes the path, which is the point of using the model [1].

*Use of examples:* a named destination that fits the model [1]; a named destination that does not [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned conclusion, such as disagreeing: limited as a forecast, but useful as a diagnostic framework for managers [1]. A clear final view [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* "critically evaluate" rewards answers that test the model against real destinations, not ones that only describe the stages.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Multiplier calculations that use the share retained instead of the leakage share as the divisor.
- Describing regional shares without figures.
- Treating any nature-based tourism as ecotourism.
- Ignoring scale when explaining economic impacts.
- Essays that describe Butler's stages with no evaluation.
