Section 2.3, *Research issues*, is the evaluative toolkit of Paper 1 in Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699). Section 2.1 tells you what the methods are; 2.3 gives you the concepts for judging them and explains why researchers choose and use methods as they do. Every answer that asks you to evaluate a research method draws on it.

This guide goes further than the 2.3 sections of the [research methods, design and issues guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-research-methods-design-and-issues/). Revise with the [2.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-research-issues-revision-notes/) and practise with the [2.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-research-issues-practice/).

## What the syllabus lists

- The theoretical, practical and ethical considerations influencing the choice of topic, choice of method(s) and conduct of research.
- How research findings may be biased by the actions and values of the sociologist and by choices made in funding, designing and conducting the research.
- Validity, reliability, objectivity, representativeness and ethics as important concepts in assessing the value of different research methods.

## Three kinds of consideration at three points

The first bullet is best learned as a grid: three kinds of consideration, each acting at three points in the research process.

| | Choice of topic | Choice of method | Conduct of research |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Theoretical** | Perspective shapes what seems important: Marxists study class and power, feminists study gender inequality | Positivists favour reliable, quantitative methods; interpretivists favour valid, qualitative ones | How far the researcher tries to stay detached or to share participants' experience |
| **Practical** | Funding, access, current public concern, what can be researched at all | Time, money, size of sample needed, researcher's skills | The researcher's age, sex, ethnicity or class affecting access and rapport; safety |
| **Ethical** | Some topics involve vulnerable people or illegal activity | Covert methods involve deception; some methods intrude on privacy | Consent, confidentiality, avoiding harm during fieldwork and in publication |

### Theoretical considerations

A researcher's view of what society is shapes what they study and how. A positivist who sees society as a set of measurable social facts will design a survey to test a hypothesis. An interpretivist who sees society as built from meanings will choose participant observation or unstructured interviews. Theoretical commitments are not always conscious: they show in the questions a researcher thinks are worth asking.

### Practical considerations

- **Time and money**: ethnography may take years; an online survey may take weeks.
- **Funding**: funders decide which projects go ahead and may set the timescale.
- **Access**: closed groups, elites and institutions such as prisons control entry; gatekeepers such as head teachers can block or shape access.
- **The researcher's characteristics**: a young female researcher may find it easier to interview young mothers and harder to join an all-male workplace.
- **The nature of the topic**: hidden or sensitive behaviour rules out some methods.

### Ethical considerations

Professional bodies such as the British Sociological Association publish ethical guidelines. The core principles are:

- **Informed consent**: participants agree, knowing what the research involves and that they can withdraw.
- **Confidentiality and anonymity**: identities and personal information are protected.
- **Avoiding harm**: physical, psychological, legal or social harm to participants, and to the researcher.
- **Deception**: avoided unless the research could not otherwise be done and the benefits are judged to outweigh the costs.
- **Vulnerable groups**: children, people with learning difficulties, prisoners and others who may find it hard to refuse need extra protection, often including consent from parents or guardians.
- **Legality**: researchers should not commit crimes and may face difficult choices if they learn of serious crime.

Covert research tests these principles hardest. James Patrick's covert study of a Glasgow gang (1973) used a pseudonym and delayed publication, partly to protect himself and the gang members, which shows that ethical and practical concerns often overlap.

## How research can be biased

Bias is any systematic distortion that pushes findings away from the truth. The syllabus names two sources.

### The actions and values of the sociologist

- **Values in topic choice**: researchers often study what they care about. Max Weber accepted this, provided the research itself is carried out objectively.
- **Interviewer effect**: the researcher's appearance, manner or reactions influence answers, often towards what seems acceptable.
- **Going native**: in long participant observation the researcher may come to share the group's views and report them uncritically.
- **Selective perception and interpretation**: in qualitative research the researcher decides what to record, which quotations to use and how to code material.
- **Taking sides**: Howard Becker's "Whose side are we on?" (1967) argued that research cannot avoid sympathy with one group, so honesty about it matters more than a claim to neutrality.

### Choices in funding, designing and conducting research

- **Funding**: a sponsor may favour certain questions, limit what is studied, or decline to publish unwelcome findings. Research funded by an industry body on its own product calls for particular scrutiny.
- **Design**: leading or loaded questions, narrow answer categories, a poor sampling frame or an unrepresentative sample all build bias in before any data is collected.
- **Conduct**: inconsistent procedures between interviewers, or low and uneven response rates, distort results.
- **Analysis and publication**: choosing which results to report, or presenting correlations as causes.

## The five concepts for judging research

| Concept | Question it asks | How to improve it | Usually stronger in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Validity** | Does the data give a true picture of what it claims to measure? | Rapport, natural settings, triangulation, careful operationalisation | Unstructured interviews, participant observation |
| **Reliability** | Would the same method give the same results if repeated? | Standardised questions and procedures, pilot studies, clear coding frames | Questionnaires, structured interviews, official statistics |
| **Objectivity** | Are the findings free from the researcher's values? | Standardisation, peer review, openness about values | Debated: positivists claim it for quantitative methods |
| **Representativeness** | Is the sample typical of the population? | Good sampling frame, random or stratified sampling, high response rate | Large-scale social surveys |
| **Ethics** | Is the research morally acceptable? | Informed consent, anonymity, avoiding harm | Overt methods with consent |

**Trade-offs.** The concepts often pull against each other. Standardising a method raises reliability but may lower validity, because fixed questions can miss what matters to respondents. Covert observation can raise validity, because people act naturally, but it breaks informed consent. Small qualitative samples give depth but weak representativeness. Strong answers name the trade-off rather than treating any method as simply "good" or "bad".

## Worked example: applying 2.3 to a scenario

*Scenario (practice): A researcher funded by a mobile phone company uses an online questionnaire, advertised on social media, to study how much time teenagers spend on their phones and whether it affects their schoolwork.*

- **Funding bias**: the sponsor has an interest in the findings, so readers should ask who designed the questions and whether all results will be published.
- **Representativeness**: advertising on social media recruits heavier users and those who choose to respond, so the sample is self-selecting.
- **Validity**: teenagers may under- or over-estimate screen time; self-reported effects on schoolwork may not match teachers' records.
- **Reliability**: a standardised questionnaire could be repeated, which is a strength.
- **Ethics**: under-age participants need parental consent; online anonymity must be protected.
- **Improvement**: triangulate with screen-time records and school data, and draw a stratified sample through schools.

Why it works: every point names a 2.3 concept, applies it to a detail of the scenario, and explains the effect on the findings.

## Exam technique

- Use the concept words by name: examiners look for validity, reliability, objectivity, representativeness and ethics.
- Apply each point to the context in the question, not to research in general.
- When asked about the choice of method, organise your answer by theoretical, practical and ethical factors.
- Show interaction: a practical factor (no sampling frame) can force a theoretical compromise (snowball sampling, weaker representativeness).

## Common mistakes

- Confusing validity with reliability.
- Treating ethics as only "informed consent" and forgetting harm, anonymity and vulnerable groups.
- Assuming quantitative research is automatically objective.
- Writing about bias in general without naming where it enters: values, funding, design or conduct.
- Listing theoretical, practical and ethical factors without linking them to topic, method or conduct.
