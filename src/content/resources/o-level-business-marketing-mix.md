The marketing mix is the combination of decisions a business makes about its product, price, place and promotion. The syllabus calls these the four Ps, but in answers it is safest to name the elements in full: product, price, place and promotion.

## 3.3.1 Product

### New products

Developing a new product gives a business a chance to open a new market, replace products that are declining and stand out from competitors. The limitations are cost and risk: research and development, testing and launch spending all happen before any sales, and many new products fail. A business may also take sales from its own existing products.

### Brand image

A brand is a name, symbol or design that identifies a product and sets it apart. Brand image is what customers think and feel about it. A strong, positive image can support sales because customers trust the quality before they buy, and it can allow a higher price. It also builds customer loyalty: people who like the brand return and recommend it. The risk is that a single scandal or faulty batch can damage the image quickly.

### Packaging

Packaging has several roles: it protects the product in transport and storage, keeps it fresh, gives information (ingredients, instructions), and helps promote the brand through colours and design. Packaging also adds cost, and wasteful packaging can harm the image of a business with environmentally aware customers.

### The product life cycle

The product life cycle shows how the sales of a product change over time. The main stages are development (before launch, no sales and spending), introduction, growth, maturity and decline. A diagram has time on the horizontal axis and sales on the vertical axis.

```
Sales
  |              ____
  |           /      \
  |          /        \
  |        /           \
  |      /               \
  |   __/                  \___
  +---------------------------------> Time
    Intro   Growth  Maturity  Decline
```

Worked example. Harbourlight Candles launches a lavender candle. Sales (thousands of units) over eight years are in the table.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 2 | 9 | 21 | 33 | 38 | 38.5 | 33 | 22 |

Interpret it in steps.
1. Year 1 sales are small: introduction.
2. Sales rise by 350% in year 2, then 133% and 57% in years 3 and 4: growth.
3. Growth slows to about 15% in year 5 and 1.3% in year 6, with sales flat near 38 thousand: maturity.
4. Sales fall 14.3% in year 7 and 33.3% in year 8: decline.


### Extension strategies

An extension strategy tries to lengthen the life of a product before it declines. Examples: new packaging or a design update, new variants (flavours, sizes), finding new markets or new uses, a price change, or fresh promotion. If Harbourlight launched a candle in a reusable glass jar in year 6, the aim would be to hold sales near maturity instead of letting them fall.

### Life cycle and marketing decisions

The stage affects the other parts of the mix.

| Stage | Promotion | Pricing |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Informative: make people aware of the product | Penetration or skimming, depending on aims |
| Growth | Persuasive: build the brand as rivals enter | Prices may be held or adjusted as competitors arrive |
| Maturity | Remind and defend market share; promotion often more competitive | Competitive pricing or promotional offers |
| Decline | Spending cut, or an extension strategy tried | Prices cut to clear stock, or the product withdrawn |

## 3.3.2 Price

### Pricing methods

Fill the table with a benefit and limitation for each method the syllabus lists.

| Method | How it works | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost plus | Add a mark-up (a percentage of unit cost) to the cost of making the product | Simple; covers costs and gives a known profit per item | Ignores competitors and what customers will pay |
| Competitive | Set prices close to rivals' prices | Avoids losing sales to cheaper rivals; avoids price wars | Little freedom; costs may not be covered if rivals are efficient |
| Penetration | Low price at launch to win market share quickly | Attracts customers to a new brand; builds sales fast | Low profit per unit at first; customers may dislike a later rise |
| Skimming | High price at launch for a new or unusual product, lowered later | High profit per unit from customers who pay for novelty; recovers development cost | Few buyers; high prices attract rivals |
| Promotional | Temporary price cuts, bundles or special offers | Boosts short-term sales and clears stock | Reduces profit per item; customers may wait for the next offer |

Worked example. Meadowsweet Preserves makes a jar of jam. Costs per jar: ingredients 1.10, jar and label 0.45, labour 0.60, overheads 0.35.
- Total cost = 1.10 + 0.45 + 0.60 + 0.35 = 2.50.
- Cost plus with a 40% mark-up: 2.50 × 1.40 = 3.50, so profit per jar is 1.00.
- A new competitor sells similar jam at 3.30 to 3.60, so 3.50 also fits a competitive approach.
- Penetration at 20% below normal price: 3.50 × 0.80 = 2.80. Profit per jar falls to 0.30, so the owner must expect volume to rise enough to make up for it.
- A promotional offer of two jars for 6.00 gives 3.00 a jar, a discount of 14.3% on 3.50.

Mark-up is based on cost, not on selling price. The profit of 1.00 is 40% of cost but only 28.6% of the selling price. Do not mix the two.

### Recommending and justifying a pricing method

Link the method to the situation. A new product with no close rival and customers who want novelty suggests skimming. A new entrant in a crowded market suggests penetration or competitive pricing. A product in decline suggests promotional pricing to clear stock. A product with a stable, easily measured cost and few rivals suggests cost plus. A justified answer says why, then gives one limitation.

### Price elasticity

Demand is price elastic when a change in price causes a larger proportionate change in the quantity demanded. It is price inelastic when the quantity changes by proportionately less. The syllabus says the PED formula and calculations are not assessed, so you need the idea, not the number.

Why it matters: for elastic demand, a price cut can raise sales enough to increase revenue, and a rise can lose many customers. For inelastic demand, a price rise can increase revenue because few customers leave, but a cut gives away revenue. Products with many substitutes tend to be elastic, while necessities and strongly branded goods tend to be inelastic.

## 3.3.3 Place: distribution channels

A distribution channel is the route a product takes from producer to consumer. The syllabus mentions wholesalers, retailers and direct to consumers.

| Channel | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Wholesaler then retailer | Reaches many shops; wholesaler holds stock and breaks bulk | Each step takes a margin, so producer earns less per item; less control of image |
| Direct to retailer | Fewer margins; closer relations with the shop | Producer deals with many small orders and delivery |
| Direct to consumer | Producer keeps the whole margin; direct customer contact and control of image | Producer pays for storage, delivery and handling; reaches fewer customers |

Worked example. Fernwick Footwear makes a boot at a cost of 30 a pair.
- Via a wholesaler: sells to the wholesaler at 38, who sells to a retailer at 48, who sells to the consumer at 70. Fernwick earns 8 a pair.
- Via a retailer only: Fernwick sells at 45. Profit is 15 a pair.
- Direct online at 65, with delivery and packing of 6 and a payment platform fee of 4% (2.60): profit = 65 − 30 − 6 − 2.60 = 26.40 a pair.

On 1,000 pairs, that is 8,000, 15,000 and 26,400. Direct selling earns most per pair, but it only works if the website can attract customers and fulfil orders. Recommend based on the situation: a start-up with no buyers may start with a retailer for reach and add a direct channel as it learns.

## 3.3.4 Promotion

### Aims

Promotion aims to inform customers, persuade them to buy, remind them of the product, build or protect brand image, increase sales and market share, and launch a new product.

### Forms

Advertising pays for messages in media such as newspapers, radio, television, posters and online. Sales promotion uses short-term incentives: discounts, buy-one-get-one-free, free samples, loyalty cards, competitions and coupons. Sales promotion tends to boost sales quickly, while advertising builds awareness and image over time. The syllabus names advertising and sales promotion as examples.

### Cost-effectiveness

A marketing budget is limited, so a business must ask how much extra sales each method brings per dollar spent. Quarrymoor Outdoor can spend on three options. Each extra sale earns the business 18 profit before promotion costs.

| Method | Cost | Extra sales | Cost per extra sale | Net gain after cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Local newspaper advert | 2,000 | 80 | 25.00 | −560 |
| Social media campaign | 1,200 | 96 | 12.50 | 528 |
| Discount voucher | 1,800 | 120 | 15.00 | 360 |

The newspaper advert costs more per sale than each sale earns, so it loses money. Social media is most cost-effective on these figures. Voucher gives the most extra sales but a lower net gain. Say what the business cares about: cost per sale, or total sales.

## 3.3.5 Technology and the marketing mix

E-commerce is buying and selling goods and services over the internet. Online shops, delivery apps and digital downloads are examples.

| | Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|---|
| Business | Reach customers anywhere; open all hours; lower costs (fewer shops); data on customers; easy to target promotion | Heavy competition from many sellers; website failures; security and data risks; delivery costs; customers cannot see the product |
| Consumer | Wider choice; easy price comparison; convenience; delivery to home | Fraud and security concerns; cannot inspect products; delivery delays; returns can be awkward; those without internet are left out |

Internet and social media promotion: businesses can post offers, share customer reviews and respond to questions. They can also target adverts by age or location, measure responses and spend less than on television. Limitations include negative comments spreading fast, the time needed to manage accounts and customers who ignore online advertising.

## Common errors

- Mixing up penetration (low price at launch) and skimming (high price at launch).
- Calling promotion only "advertising"; sales promotion is a separate form.
- Describing the life cycle stages without using the data given in a question.
- Writing PED calculations; the syllabus says they are not assessed.
- Recommending a method without giving a reason linked to the business described.
- Listing e-commerce benefits only for the business when the question asks about consumers.
