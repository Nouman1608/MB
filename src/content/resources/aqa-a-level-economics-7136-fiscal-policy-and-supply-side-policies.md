## What fiscal policy is

Fiscal policy "involves the manipulation of government spending, taxation and the budget balance". It has:

- **Macroeconomic functions:** influencing AD, output, employment and inflation, and AS through spending on
  infrastructure, education and incentives.
- **Microeconomic functions:** changing the pattern of economic activity, for example taxing a demerit good or
  subsidising training in a particular industry.

**Types of public expenditure.** Current spending (wages of public sector workers, medicines), capital spending
(roads, schools, hospitals) and transfer payments (pensions, benefits, which redistribute income but do not buy
output directly). **Reasons:** providing public and merit goods, redistributing income, building infrastructure
and managing demand.

**Why governments levy taxes.** To raise revenue for spending, to redistribute income, to correct market failures
such as negative externalities, and to manage AD.

## Fiscal policy and aggregate demand: a worked comparison

**Worked example (invented).** Country N's MPC is 0.75, so the multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = **4**. The
government considers two options, each costing 10 billion.

- **Option A: raise spending by 10 billion.** The whole 10 billion is an injection. National income rises by up to
  10 × 4 = **40 billion**.
- **Option B: cut income tax by 10 billion.** Households' disposable income rises by 10 billion, but with an MPC of
  0.75 they spend only 7.5 billion in the first round. National income rises by up to 7.5 × 4 = **30 billion**.

Pound for pound, extra spending raises AD more than an equal tax cut, because part of a tax cut is saved (or spent
on imports, or both) before it enters the flow. The tax cut may still be preferred if it improves incentives to
work and invest, which affects AS: a reminder that fiscal policy works on both sides of the economy.

## Taxes: direct, indirect, progressive, regressive

**Direct taxes** are levied on income and wealth and paid directly to the government by the person taxed (income
tax, corporation tax). **Indirect taxes** are levied on spending and collected through sellers (VAT, excise
duties on fuel and tobacco).

**Worked example (invented).** Country N's income tax: 0% on the first 10,000; 20% on income from 10,000 to
40,000; 40% on income above 40,000.

| Income | Tax | Average rate | Marginal rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30,000 | 20% × 20,000 = 4,000 | 13.3% | 20% |
| 60,000 | (20% × 30,000) + (40% × 20,000) = 14,000 | 23.3% | 40% |

The average rate rises with income, so the tax is **progressive**. A **proportional** tax takes the same
percentage from every income. A **regressive** tax takes a falling percentage as income rises.

Indirect taxes are often regressive **in relation to income**. If a household on 15,000 pays 300 in fuel duty
and one on 60,000 pays 450, the richer household pays more in total, but the tax takes 2.0% of the first income
and only 0.75% of the second.

**Principles of taxation.** The specification gives the example that taxes should be **equitable**. Adam Smith's
classic principles were equity (according to ability to pay), certainty (taxpayers know what they owe), convenience
(easy to pay) and economy (cheap to collect relative to revenue). Modern additions include efficiency (minimising
distortion of decisions) and flexibility (rates can be changed as conditions change). Use these as criteria when
asked about "the relative merits of different UK taxes", such as income tax, National Insurance contributions,
VAT, corporation tax, excise duties and council tax.

## Deficits, debt and the OBR, in brief

- **Budget balance:** revenue minus spending in a year. A deficit adds to the **national debt** (a stock).
- **Cyclical deficit:** caused by the economic cycle, through automatic stabilisers; it disappears as the economy
  recovers. **Structural deficit:** remains even when the economy is at potential.
- **Consequences** of deficits and surpluses depend on the state of the cycle, how the borrowing is used, interest
  rates and the size of the debt.
- **The Office for Budget Responsibility** provides independent forecasts and assesses the government's
  performance against its fiscal targets.

## Supply-side policies and supply-side improvements

The specification distinguishes **supply-side policies** (deliberate government measures) from **supply-side
improvements** (increases in productive capacity from any source). Many improvements "originate in the private
sector, independently of government", through innovation, investment and productivity growth.

| Free market supply-side policies | Interventionist supply-side policies |
|---|---|
| Tax cuts to raise incentives to work and invest | Government spending on education and training |
| Privatisation | Industrial policy |
| Deregulation | Subsidising research and development |
| Some labour market reforms (for example, reducing trade union power or reforming benefits) | Spending on infrastructure |

Supply-side policies aim to raise **potential output** and the **trend rate of growth**. They can:

- **Reduce unemployment**, especially the **natural rate**, by improving skills, mobility and incentives.
- **Reduce inflationary pressure**, because a rightward shift of LRAS allows growth without a positive output gap.
- **Improve the current account**, if lower costs and higher quality make exports more competitive.

They can also tackle **market failures such as short-termism**, where firms and financial markets underinvest in
long-term projects like research or training.

## The same policy on both sides

Many policies work through both demand and supply, at different speeds:

| Policy | Demand-side effect (short run) | Supply-side effect (long run) |
|---|---|---|
| More spending on rail infrastructure | AD rises through G and the multiplier | Lower transport costs raise productivity, shifting LRAS |
| Cut in corporation tax | Retained profits may raise investment, raising AD | Larger capital stock raises capacity |
| Training subsidies | Spending on training providers raises AD slightly | Skills reduce structural unemployment |
| Cut in benefits | Lower consumption reduces AD | Stronger incentive to work may raise labour supply |

## Evaluating policies in this sub-section

Use the same criteria for any fiscal or supply-side measure:

1. **Effectiveness:** how large is the effect? Consider the multiplier, elasticities of response (do people work
   more when taxes fall?) and spare capacity.
2. **Time lags:** fiscal changes need legislation and take time; supply-side gains from education may take years.
3. **Cost and opportunity cost:** what else could the money fund? What happens to the deficit and debt?
4. **Equity:** who gains and who loses? Tax cuts for high earners and benefit cuts raise questions of fairness.
5. **Side effects:** crowding out, higher imports, inflation near capacity, or regulatory changes reducing worker
   protection.
6. **Uncertainty:** forecasts of potential output and responses to incentives are uncertain.

## Common errors

- Treating every rise in government spending as a supply-side policy; it is supply-side only if it raises capacity.
- Calling an indirect tax progressive because richer households pay more in total.
- Confusing the marginal rate with the average rate.
- Assuming a tax cut and an equal spending rise have the same effect on AD.
- Saying supply-side policies always work quickly.
- Treating the national debt and the deficit as the same thing.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** average tax rate = total tax ÷ income × 100; marginal rate = tax on the last unit of income.
- **Explain:** state whether a measure works through AD, AS or both, then follow the chain.
- **Evaluate:** use the criteria above and reach a judgement for the economy described in the extract, for
  example whether there is a negative output gap.
