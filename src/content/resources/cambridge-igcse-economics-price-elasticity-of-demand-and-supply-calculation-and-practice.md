## Questions

**1.** Give a definition of price elasticity of demand. **[2]**

**2.** A school canteen (invented) raises the price of a pastry from 1.20 to 1.50. Weekly sales fall from 800 to 680.

**(a)** Calculate price elasticity of demand. **[3]**
**(b)** State whether demand is elastic or inelastic, with a reason. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the change in the shop's revenue from notebooks. **[2]**

**3.** The price elasticity of demand for a good is -1.5. Its price rises by 6% and sales before the rise were 3,000 a week.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the new weekly sales. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what happens to revenue. **[2]**

**4.** Using a price and quantity diagram:

**(a)** Describe the demand curve when PED is zero. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the demand curve when PED is infinite. **[1]**
**(c)** Two straight demand curves, D1 and D2, are drawn on the same scales. D1 is shallower. State which is more elastic over the range shown and why. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **two** reasons why demand for piped tap water is price inelastic. **[4]**

**6.** In Market M (invented) the demand schedule is:

| Price | 12 | 10 | 8 | 6 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |

**(a)** Calculate total revenue at each price. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate PED when price falls from 12 to 10. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate PED when price rises from 4 to 6. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain what the answers to (b) and (c) tell a seller about changing price. **[2]**

**7.** Government B (invented) taxes a good so that its price rises by 12%. PED is -0.25. Sales before the tax were 40,000 units.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate sales after the tax. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the tax will raise revenue for the government. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain whether the tax is likely to reduce consumption much. **[2]**

**8.** A workshop (invented) sees the price of its product rise from 40 to 46 and raises output from 200 to 230 units a month.

**(a)** Calculate price elasticity of supply. **[3]**
**(b)** State what type of elasticity this is. **[1]**
**(c)** Another firm has PES of 0.6. Price rises by 10% and it was producing 500 units. Calculate its new output. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **two** reasons why the supply of a manufactured good may be more elastic than the supply of a farm crop. **[4]**

**10.** Describe the supply curve with:

**(a)** PES of zero. **[1]**
**(b)** infinite PES. **[1]**
**(c)** PES of 1 at every point. **[1]**

**11.** Farmers in Country C (invented) grow a crop with PED of -0.4. A bumper harvest moves the price from 8 to 6 a kilo, and sales were 1,000 kilos at the old price.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate farmers' revenue before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why revenue changed in this direction. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether farmers are better off after a bumper harvest. **[4]**

**12.** In Town Z (invented) the supply of rented flats is inelastic in the short run (PES = 0.2). Rents rise by 30% and 2,000 flats were being offered.

**(a)** Calculate the new quantity of flats supplied. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** reason why supply is inelastic. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether the government should subsidise builders to deal with high rents. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** How far quantity demanded responds [1] when the price changes [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that give only the formula, or say "how much demand changes", tend to lose the link to price. Name both quantity demanded and price.

**2. (a)** % change in price = (1.50 - 1.20) / 1.20 = +25% [1]. % change in quantity = (680 - 800) / 800 = -15% [1]. PED = -15 / 25 = **-0.6** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** **Inelastic**, because the size (0.6) is less than 1 [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Before: 1.20 x 800 = 960; after: 1.50 x 680 = 1,020 [1]. Revenue **rises by 60** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Whether you quote PED with or without the minus sign, judge the type from its size. Showing both percentages makes the working easy to follow.

**3. (a)** -1.5 x 6 = **-9%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 3,000 x 0.91 = **2,730** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Demand is elastic [1], so the percentage fall in quantity is larger than the percentage rise in price and revenue falls [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The explanation gains credit by comparing the two percentages. "Elastic, so revenue falls" without the comparison is thinner.

**4. (a)** A **vertical** line [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** A **horizontal** line [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** **D1** [1], because a given fall in price produces a larger change in quantity (the curve is shallower) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Diagram descriptions should name the axes. The comparison in (c) depends on the scales being equal, which is worth stating.

**5.** Piped tap water is a necessity [1], so households keep using it when its price rises [1]. It has no close substitute for washing and cooking [1], so buyers cannot switch away and quantity changes little [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason needs the link to the good. A list of determinants with no application usually earns less.

**6. (a)** Prices 12, 10, 8: 240, 400, 480 [1]. Prices 6, 4: 480, 400 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** % change in price = -2/12 = -16.67% [1]. % change in quantity = +20/20 = +100% [1]. PED = 100 / -16.67 = **-6.0** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** % change in price = +2/4 = +50% [1]. % change in quantity = -20/100 = -20% [1]. PED = -20 / 50 = **-0.4** [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** At high prices demand is elastic (6.0), so a price cut raises revenue (240 to 400) [1]. At low prices it is inelastic (0.4), so a price rise raises revenue (400 to 480) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the starting point for each percentage. A move from 4 to 6 and a move from 6 to 4 give different figures, so state the direction you used.

**7. (a)** -0.25 x 12 = [1] = **-3%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 40,000 x 0.97 = **38,800** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Demand is inelastic [1], so sales fall by little and the tax is collected on almost the same quantity at a higher price [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** No [1], because a 12% price rise cuts quantity by only 3%, so the aim of reducing use is only slightly met [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Distinguish the two aims of an indirect tax. High revenue and low reduction in use come from the same inelastic demand.

**8. (a)** % change in price = 6/40 = +15% [1]. % change in quantity = 30/200 = +15% [1]. PES = 15 / 15 = **1.0** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** **Unitary** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** % change = 0.6 x 10 = 6% [1]. New output = 500 x 1.06 = **530** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* PES is positive, so a negative answer suggests a sign slip. Name the type of elasticity when the question asks.

**9.** A factory can hold stocks of finished goods [1], so it can release more when price rises [1]. A crop takes a whole growing season [1], so farmers cannot raise output within that time, whereas a manufacturer can use spare capacity or run extra shifts quickly [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Another valid pair is spare capacity and ease of switching factors. A comparison needs both goods to be mentioned.

**10. (a)** A **vertical** line [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** A **horizontal** line [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** A **straight line through the origin** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The straight line must pass through the origin itself. A line that merely starts near the origin does not have PES of 1 at every point.

**11. (a)** Price change = -2/8 = -25% [1]. Quantity change = -0.4 x -25 = **+10%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 8 x 1,000 = 8,000 [1]. After: 6 x 1,100 = 6,600 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Demand is inelastic [1]. The fall in price (25%) is larger than the rise in quantity (10%), so revenue falls by 1,400 [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Revenue falls by 1,400 [1], and extra output may also cost more to harvest, so profit could fall further [1]. On the other hand, some farmers may be able to store the crop or sell it abroad at a better price [1]. Farmers as a group are probably worse off, although it depends on costs and storage [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit from a judgement supported by the figures. A bare "it depends" with no reason does not do this.

**12. (a)** 0.2 x 30 = 6% [1]. New quantity = 2,000 x 1.06 = **2,120** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Building a flat takes a long time [1], so landlords cannot raise the number offered quickly when rents rise [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A subsidy lowers builders' costs and shifts supply to the right [1]. In the short run supply is inelastic [1], so output rises little and rents fall little [1]. Over a longer period, supply becomes more elastic and the subsidy has more effect [1]. The subsidy costs taxpayers and has an opportunity cost [1]. Judgement: it is useful as a long-term measure, but it will not cut rents quickly [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh the short run against the long run, and say what the subsidy costs. Description of the policy alone is rarely enough for an evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting price on top in the formula.
- Taking the percentage from the new value.
- Giving a figure for PED and not stating elastic, inelastic or unitary.
- Applying the revenue rule the wrong way round.
- Listing a determinant with no application to the good in the question.
- Drawing a demand or supply curve with no labelled axes.
- Treating the steepness of a straight line as the same thing as elasticity at every point.
- Writing a one-sided answer to an evaluation question and not reaching a judgement.
