**How to use this set.** Paper 4 asks for "a critical commentary" on a printed poem or prose extract.
Treat both poems below as genuinely unseen: they are original, written for this page. Section A
builds the planning skills; Section B asks for written commentary.

**Poem A: Night Ferry** *(original poem)*

> The engines start below us like a thought
> we cannot finish. Lights along the quay
> grow small and smaller, till the town is caught
> inside a pocket of the dark. The sea
>
> is all we have now: black, and folding, wide.
> My father holds the rail as if it might
> remember for him which way home is. Outside
> the window of his face, I watch the night.

**Poem B: The Lesson** *(original poem)*

> My grandmother taught me to bake bread
> without a recipe. "You'll know," she said,
> "by how it feels." I didn't. I still don't.
> I measure everything. She never would.
>
> This morning, kneading, I forgot the scales.
> My hands went on without me, pushing, turning,
> and for a moment the dough felt right,
> and for a moment she was in the room.

## Section A

**1.** Read Poem A. In **two** sentences, state the literal situation of the poem. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the turn in Poem B and explain how the poet signals it. **[3]**

**3.** In Poem A, explain the effect of the enjambment across the stanza break ("The sea / is all we
have now"). **[2]**

**4.** In Poem B, explain the effect of the short sentences in line 3: "I didn't. I still don't."
**[2]**

**5.** Give **two** interpretations of "Outside / the window of his face" in Poem A. **[2]**

**6.** A question reads: "How does the poet vividly convey a sense of loss in Poem A?" Write a
one-sentence answer that could open a commentary. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Read Poem A.
**(a)** Explore how the poet uses imagery of distance and darkness in lines 1–5. **[6]**
**(b)** Explore how the poet presents the father in lines 6–8. **[4]**

**8.** Read Poem B. Explore how the poet presents the relationship between the speaker and the
grandmother. **[8]**

**9.** Explain how the poet uses repetition in the final two lines of Poem B, and why it makes an
effective ending. **[4]**

**10.** Write a five-point plan for the question: "How does the poet movingly convey memory in Poem
B?" **[5]**

**11.** Poem A uses a regular rhyme scheme; Poem B does not rhyme. Explain how each choice suits its
poem. **[4]**

**12.** Rewrite this weak paragraph as a strong commentary paragraph on Poem A of about 80–100 words:
"The poet uses a simile in line 1. The engines are like a thought. There is also rhyme. The poem is
sad." **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The speaker and their father are leaving a town at night on a ferry [1]; the speaker watches
the father, who seems lost or confused, as the lights of the quay disappear [1].

**2.** The turn comes at "This morning" in the second stanza [1], which shifts from the past and the
speaker's failure to the present moment [1]. It is signalled by the change of time and by the
speaker's admission "I forgot the scales", which breaks a lifelong habit of measuring [1].

**3.** The sentence runs over the gap between stanzas, so the reader is suspended on "The sea" [1],
mirroring the way the sea now surrounds them, with nothing else to hold onto [1].

**4.** The short, blunt sentences sound like a confession [1]; the shift from "didn't" to "still
don't" shows the failure has lasted, adding regret [1].

**5.** Any two, [1] each: the speaker looks past the father's face to the night, unable to read his
thoughts; the father's face is like a window onto darkness, suggesting confusion or memory loss; the
speaker feels shut out of the father's mind; the father is looking out at the night and the speaker
sees it reflected in him. Maximum 2.

**6.** A clear answer [1] that names how the loss is conveyed [1], for example: "The poet vividly
conveys loss by matching the shrinking lights of home with the father's fading memory, so leaving a
place becomes an image of losing a person."

**7. (a)** Any three developed points, [2] each:
- The simile "like a thought / we cannot finish" connects the engines to an incomplete thought,
  hinting at the father's confusion before he appears [2].
- "small and smaller" shows distance growing step by step, so the reader watches home disappear [2].
- The town "caught / inside a pocket of the dark" suggests it is swallowed and hidden, perhaps lost
  for good [2].
- "black, and folding, wide": the list of adjectives makes the sea vast and shapeless, with
  "folding" suggesting things closing over [2].
- "The sea / is all we have now" presents isolation: nothing remains but the dark water [2].
Maximum 6.
**(b)** Any two developed points, [2] each:
- He "holds the rail as if it might / remember for him which way home is", suggesting he has lost
  his sense of direction and memory [2].
- The rail is personified as able to "remember", which makes his dependence on an object pitiful [2].
- "the window of his face" suggests the speaker looks at him but cannot reach his thoughts; the
  final line leaves the speaker watching "the night", as if the father has become part of the
  darkness [2].
Maximum 4.

**8.** Any four developed points, [2] each:
- The grandmother's instruction "You'll know… by how it feels" shows a teacher who trusts instinct
  [2].
- The speaker's failure, "I didn't. I still don't.", presents a gap between them in confidence and
  method [2].
- "I measure everything. She never would." The balanced contrast sets the speaker's caution against
  the grandmother's ease [2].
- The past tense ("taught", "said") and "She never would" suggest the grandmother has died [2].
- Forgetting the scales lets the speaker work as the grandmother did; the hands moving "without me"
  suggest the lesson was learned in the body, not the mind [2].
- "she was in the room" shows the relationship continuing through memory and shared action [2].
Maximum 8.

**9.** The repeated "and for a moment" links the dough feeling right with the grandmother's presence
[1], suggesting that doing the task her way brings the grandmother back [1]. The repetition also limits the
experience to "a moment", so the ending is both comforting and sad [1]; the long, flowing final lines
contrast with the clipped sentences of stanza 1, showing the speaker has relaxed [1].

**10.** One mark for each of five relevant, sequenced points [5], for example:
1. Introduction: memory is carried in actions, not in instructions.
2. The grandmother's words, quoted in the poem, preserve her voice.
3. The speaker's failure and measuring show memory as a lesson not yet understood.
4. The turn: forgetting the scales allows the hands to remember what the mind could not.
5. The final repeated lines: memory briefly becomes presence; conclusion on how the brevity of "a
   moment" makes this moving.

**11.** Any two developed points, [2] each:
- Poem A's regular rhyme gives a steady, rocking pattern like the motion of the ferry [2].
- In Poem A, the order of the rhymes contrasts with the confusion of the father, which makes his loss
  of direction more noticeable [2].
- Poem B's unrhymed lines sound like speech and suit a personal memory told plainly [2].
- In Poem B, the lack of rhyme matches the grandmother's method of working without a recipe [2].
Maximum 4.

**12.** Award up to [6]: a point answering a focus on loss or mood [1]; accurate, embedded quotation
[1]; analysis of the simile's effect [1]; analysis of a second method such as rhyme or line break
[1]; a link to the father or the poem's meaning [1]; a personal response that grows from analysis
[1]. For example: "The poem opens with engines that start 'like a thought / we cannot finish', and
the simile quietly introduces the poem's real subject: a mind that can no longer complete its
thoughts. The line break enacts this, leaving the thought unfinished at the end of the line before
'we cannot finish' arrives. The steady rhymes, such as 'thought' and 'caught', give the voyage an
orderly rhythm that makes the father's confusion all the sadder to me, because the world moves on
smoothly while he loses his way."

## Where marks are usually lost

- Summarising each line instead of analysing.
- Ignoring line breaks and stanza breaks.
- Inventing facts, such as naming an illness the poem does not mention.
- No clear answer to the question's key word.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I always choose the poem on Paper 4?**
Read both questions in the planning time and choose the text you can say most about; practise both
forms during the course.

**Do I need to use technical terms?**
Use them when they help you explain an effect precisely; the effect is what earns credit.
