## 1. Mission statements

A **mission statement** is a short, qualitative statement of why a business exists and what it values. It answers "what are we here for?" rather than "what number must we hit?".

**Purpose:** to communicate purpose and values to employees, customers and investors; to give a reference point for objectives and strategy; to shape culture and motivate staff.

**Value (evaluate both sides):**

- Useful when it is specific enough to guide decisions and managers act on it. "Affordable fresh food within walking distance" rules some strategies in and others out.
- Of little value when it is so vague it could belong to any firm, is written for public relations, or is contradicted by daily behaviour, which damages trust.

## 2. Objectives

Objectives turn the mission into measurable targets. Effective ones are **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. Section 3.3.1 names five kinds.

### Shareholder value and shareholder returns

**Shareholder value** is the value of the business to its owners. For a public company, a useful measure is **market capitalisation**:

`Market capitalisation = total number of shares in issue × current market price per share`

**Shareholder returns** are what owners receive. Two measures:

`Dividend per share = total dividends paid ÷ number of ordinary shares in issue`

`Dividend yield (%) = (dividend per share ÷ market price per share) × 100`

**Worked example: Kitewood Foods plc**

Kitewood has 48 million ordinary shares in issue. The share price is £3.25 and the company paid total dividends of £7.2 million this year.

```
Market capitalisation = 48,000,000 × £3.25 = £156,000,000 = £156 million
Dividend per share    = £7,200,000 ÷ 48,000,000 = £0.15 (15p)
Dividend yield        = (0.15 ÷ 3.25) × 100 = 4.615...% = 4.62% (2 d.p.)
```

Interpretation: if the share price falls to £3.00 with the same dividend, the yield rises to 5.00% but market capitalisation falls to £144 million. A higher yield can signal lost confidence, so compare it with similar companies and savings rates.

### Growth, social and environmental objectives

- **Growth**: revenue, market share, outlets, new markets. Brings economies of scale but needs finance.
- **Social**: fair pay, community projects, ethical sourcing, staff wellbeing.
- **Environmental**: lower emissions, less waste, renewable inputs.

### Impact of different objectives on functional areas

Trace one objective through all four functions.

| Objective | Marketing | Operations | Finance | Human resources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Growth into a new region | New research and promotion budget | Extra capacity or a new site | Raise funds; cash flow pressure | Recruit and train staff |
| Higher shareholder returns | Focus on profitable lines | Cut unit costs | Pay out more profit, retain less | Control labour costs |
| Environmental | Green positioning, eco labels | New processes, recycled inputs | Higher short-term costs | Train staff in new methods |
| Social | Ethical brand message | Ethical suppliers | Lower margins in the short run | Better pay and conditions |

Objectives can conflict. Higher dividends leave less retained profit to fund growth or environmental investment.

### Influences on the objectives a business sets

- **Mission and culture**
- **Ownership**: plc shareholders may push for returns; a family owner may value control
- **Size and age**: a new firm may aim for survival, an established one for growth or returns
- **Stakeholder power**: major investors, regulators, unions
- **Internal position**: finance, capacity, skills (shown by SWOT)
- **External environment**: competition, the economy, law, technology
- **Past performance**

## 3. SWOT analysis

**SWOT** lists **strengths** and **weaknesses** (internal) and **opportunities** and **threats** (external). Sections 3.3.3 and 3.3.4 analyse the external side; here the focus is using data to judge strengths and weaknesses.

**Value:** simple, quick and cheap; gives managers a shared picture; matches strengths to opportunities; a starting point for objectives and strategy.

**Limitations:** a snapshot that soon dates; subjective unless backed by data; factors are not weighted; a list is not a plan.

### Strengths and weaknesses from data

A figure is only a strength or a weakness **in comparison** with something: last year, a competitor, the industry average or the business's own target.

**Worked example 1: financial ratios, Halvorsen Ceramics Ltd (£000)**

Data: revenue 4,800; cost of sales 2,880; operating expenses 1,320; non-current liabilities 1,500; total equity 2,500; inventory 400; trade receivables 480; cash 20; current liabilities 600; trade payables 360.

```
Gross profit         = 4,800 − 2,880 = 1,920   → gross margin 1,920/4,800 × 100 = 40%
Operating profit     = 1,920 − 1,320 = 600     → operating margin 600/4,800 × 100 = 12.5%
Capital employed     = 1,500 + 2,500 = 4,000
ROCE                 = 600/4,000 × 100 = 15%
Gearing              = 1,500/4,000 × 100 = 37.5%
Current assets       = 400 + 480 + 20 = 900
Current ratio        = 900/600 = 1.5
Acid test ratio      = (900 − 400)/600 = 0.83
Receivables days     = 480/4,800 × 365 = 36.5 days
Payables days        = 360/2,880 × 365 = 45.6 days
Inventory turnover   = 2,880/400 = 7.2 times (inventory taken as the average)
```

The industry averages are ROCE 11%, gearing 45%, acid test 1.0. Strengths: ROCE above average, lower gearing, and customers pay about nine days before Halvorsen pays suppliers. Weakness: an acid test below 1 means it relies on selling inventory to meet short-term debts. Shareholder ratios (dividend per share and yield, above) complete the financial picture. Section 3.3.2, on analysing the existing internal position, develops ratio analysis further.

**Worked example 2: marketing, operations and HR data, Sunfield Juices**

*Marketing data.* The market grew from £50 million to £54 million: growth = 4/50 × 100 = **8%**. Sunfield's sales were £6.5 million last year and £6.48 million this year, so its market share fell from 13% to **12%**. Flat sales in a growing market are a weakness.

- A 5% price rise cut quantity demanded by 8%: PED = −8 ÷ 5 = **−1.6** (price elastic, so little pricing power).
- A rival cut its price by 10% and Sunfield's quantity fell 6%: cross-price elasticity = −6 ÷ −10 = **+0.6** (substitutes).
- Incomes rose 4% and quantity rose 2%: income elasticity = 2 ÷ 4 = **+0.5** (normal good, income inelastic).
- The correlation between monthly advertising spend and sales is +0.85: a strong positive link, though correlation does not prove that advertising caused the sales.

*Break-even.* Fixed costs £1.2 million; price £2.40; variable cost £1.60 per bottle.

```
Contribution per unit = 2.40 − 1.60 = £0.80
Break-even output     = 1,200,000 ÷ 0.80 = 1,500,000 bottles
Actual output         = 2,700,000 bottles (6,480,000 ÷ 2.40)
Margin of safety      = 2,700,000 − 1,500,000 = 1,200,000 bottles (44.4% of output)
```

A wide margin of safety is a strength.

*Operations data.* Total costs = 1.2m + (1.60 × 2.7m) = £5.52 million, so unit cost = 5.52m ÷ 2.7m = **£2.04**. With 45 employees, labour productivity = 2,700,000 ÷ 45 = **60,000 bottles per employee**. Capacity is 3.6 million bottles, so capacity utilisation = 2.7 ÷ 3.6 × 100 = **75%**. A customer survey shows 78% satisfied against a target of 85% (weakness), and emissions fell from 52 kg to 47 kg of CO₂ per 1,000 bottles, a 9.6% cut (strength).

*HR data.* Nine of an average 45 employees left: labour turnover = 9/45 × 100 = **20%**. Of 12 people recruited a year ago, 9 are still employed: retention = **75%**. Employee costs of £1.08 million are 16.67% of turnover, or £0.40 per bottle. An engagement survey scores 64%, and 30 of 45 staff are represented by a trade union (a representation rate of **66.7%**). High turnover alongside middling engagement is a weakness to probe.

## 4. Stakeholder needs when making decisions

### Stakeholder mapping

**Stakeholder mapping** places each group on a grid of **power** (ability to affect the business) against **interest** (how much it cares about a decision), to decide how much attention each needs:

- high power, high interest: manage closely and involve in decisions
- high power, low interest: keep satisfied
- low power, high interest: keep informed
- low power, low interest: monitor

It focuses limited management time, but positions change and placing a group is a judgement.

**Influences on power and interest:** size of the group and whether it is organised; how dependent the business is on it (a single key supplier or lender); legal rights; access to media; how directly the decision affects the group.

### Overlap, conflict and managing relationships

Objectives **overlap** when one action serves several groups: a profitable, growing firm can pay dividends, raise wages and buy more from suppliers. They **conflict** when gains for one group cost another: cutting labour costs raises short-term returns but hurts employees; a new site creates jobs but adds traffic for the community.

**Worked example: Juniper Rail depot**

Juniper Rail plans a larger maintenance depot on the edge of a town. The planning authority (high power, high interest) must be managed closely. Nearby residents (low power alone, high interest) need regular information, and their power rises if they organise a campaign. Shareholders (high power, moderate interest) want higher returns; employees gain jobs.

Managing the relationships: consult residents early, offer noise barriers and limited night working, brief the planning authority before applying, and explain the payback timing to shareholders.

**Actions stakeholders can take:** customers switch or boycott; employees strike or leave; suppliers change credit terms; lenders refuse finance; shareholders sell or vote against directors; government regulates or fines; communities object to planning applications or campaign. Each can raise costs, delay plans or harm reputation.

**Value of considering stakeholder needs:** fewer disputes, better reputation and loyalty, decisions that last. But consultation takes time and no decision satisfies everyone.

## Common errors

- Calling a mission statement an objective: a mission has no number or deadline.
- Dividing total dividends by market capitalisation and calling the answer dividend per share.
- Labelling a figure a strength without a comparison.
- Putting a rival's price cut under weaknesses: it is an external threat.
- Treating a correlation as proof of cause.
- Listing stakeholders without saying which has power over this decision and why.
