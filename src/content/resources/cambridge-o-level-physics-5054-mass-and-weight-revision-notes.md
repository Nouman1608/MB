## Statements to learn

- **Mass** is a measure of the quantity of matter in an object at rest relative to the observer.
- The mass of an object **resists change** from its state of rest or motion: this is **inertia**.
- Weights, and therefore masses, can be **compared** with a beam balance or equal-arm balance.
- **Gravitational field strength** is force per unit mass, g = W ÷ m; it is equivalent to the
  acceleration of free fall.
- A **gravitational field** is a region in which a mass experiences a force due to gravitational
  attraction.

## Equations and units

```
g = W / m        W = m g        m = W / g
W in newtons (N), m in kilograms (kg), g in N/kg  (1 N/kg = 1 m/s²)
g on Earth ≈ 9.8 N/kg
```

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Mass** | Quantity of matter, in kg; same everywhere |
| **Weight** | Gravitational force on a mass, in N; a vector acting downwards |
| **Inertia** | Resistance of a mass to a change in its motion |
| **Beam balance** | Compares an unknown mass with standard masses; level beam = equal weights = equal masses |
| **Electronic balance** | Reads mass directly; measures the force on the pan, calibrated for local g |
| **Tare** | Resetting a balance to zero, for example with an empty container on the pan |
| **Force meter** | Spring-based instrument reading force in newtons |
| **Gravitational field** | Region where a mass feels a gravitational force |

## Measuring: what to say in a "describe" answer

**Electronic balance:** level surface, no draughts → tare to zero → add object → read steady
display in g or kg. For a liquid: tare with the empty container, then pour in the liquid.

**Force meter:** check zero → hold vertical → hang object → wait for it to stop moving → read at
eye level in N.

**Beam balance:** object on one pan → standard masses on the other until level → masses equal.

## Why the instrument matters

- A beam balance gives the correct mass anywhere, because both pans are in the same field.
- An electronic balance calibrated on Earth would read too low on the Moon, because it responds to
  force.
- A force meter measures weight, which changes with g.

## Calculation patterns

| Given | Find | Example |
|---|---|---|
| m and g | W | 2.5 kg × 9.8 N/kg = **24.5 N** |
| W and g | m | 49 N ÷ 9.8 N/kg = **5.0 kg** |
| W and m | g | 128 N ÷ 80 kg = **1.6 N/kg** (Moon) |
| Weight–mass graph | g | gradient of the line through the origin |

## Inertia in everyday examples

- Passengers lurch forwards when a bus brakes.
- A full trolley is harder to start and to stop than an empty one.
- A heavy box on the Moon is easier to lift but no easier to push into motion sideways.

## Two-step calculation in exam layout

*A box weighs 147 N on Earth. Find its mass and its weight on the Moon (g = 1.6 N/kg).*

```
m = W / g = 147 / 9.8 = 15 kg
W(Moon) = m g = 15 × 1.6 = 24 N
```

Find the mass first: it is the quantity that stays the same.

## Exam traps

- Weight in kg is wrong: weight is in N.
- "No gravity in space, so no mass" is wrong: mass never changes.
- g = m ÷ W is the wrong way round.
- Inertia depends on mass, not weight.
- Forgetting the beam balance compares weights, which is why it compares masses.
- g is a vector: it points towards the centre of the planet.
- Giving a weight to too many significant figures: match the data, usually 2 or 3 s.f.

## Self-test

1. Define mass.
2. What is inertia?
3. Define gravitational field strength.
4. State what is meant by a gravitational field.
5. Find the weight of a 0.60 kg book on Earth.
6. A force meter reads 7.35 N. Find the mass of the object.
7. Show that 1 N/kg is the same as 1 m/s².
8. Why does a beam balance give the same result on the Moon as on Earth?
9. Why would an electronic balance calibrated on Earth read too low on the Moon?
10. An astronaut weighs 640 N on Earth. Find her mass.
11. A weight–mass graph has a gradient of 9.8. What does the gradient represent?
12. Why do standing passengers lurch forwards when a bus brakes?

### Answers

1. A measure of the quantity of matter in an object at rest relative to the observer.
2. The resistance of a mass to a change in its state of rest or motion.
3. Force per unit mass (g = W ÷ m).
4. A region in which a mass experiences a force due to gravitational attraction.
5. 0.60 × 9.8 = **5.9 N**.
6. 7.35 ÷ 9.8 = **0.75 kg**.
7. 1 N = 1 kg m/s², so 1 N/kg = 1 kg m/s² ÷ 1 kg = 1 m/s².
8. Both pans are in the same field, so equal weights still mean equal masses.
9. It measures the force on its pan, and the weight on the Moon is much smaller.
10. 640 ÷ 9.8 = **65 kg** (2 s.f.).
11. The gravitational field strength, g, in N/kg.
12. Their bodies tend to keep moving at the original speed because of inertia while the bus slows.
