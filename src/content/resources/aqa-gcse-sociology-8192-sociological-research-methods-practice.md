These questions cover the research design row of section 3.7 Sociological research methods in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification: aims, hypotheses, pilot studies, sampling and the analysis of data. Methods questions can appear in Paper 1 or Paper 2. All people, places and numbers are fictional.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** A researcher selects every 25th name from a list of gym members. Which sampling method is this?

A. Random
B. Systematic
C. Quota
D. Snowball

**[1]**

**2.** Which of the following is a hypothesis?

A. "To find out how teenagers use social media."
B. "How do teenagers use social media?"
C. "Girls spend more time on social media each day than boys."
D. "Social media is an interesting topic."

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "representative sample". **[2]**

**4.** A researcher has a sampling frame of 2,400 tenants and wants a systematic sample of 120. Calculate the sampling interval. Show your working. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why qualitative research often starts with an aim rather than a hypothesis. **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** problem a pilot study could reveal in a questionnaire about family conflict. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** A researcher wants a quota sample of 300 adults in Larkford (a fictional town) that matches the town's age profile.

| Age group | Share of Larkford adults |
|---|---|
| 18 to 34 | 30% |
| 35 to 59 | 45% |
| 60 and over | 25% |

**(a)** Calculate how many people aged 35 to 59 the quota should include. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain **one** reason why the quota sample might still be unrepresentative. **[2]**

**8.** A sociologist wants to study the experiences of young people who have run away from home. Explain why snowball sampling might be the most appropriate method, and **one** limitation of using it. **[4]**

**9.** Read the item.

> A researcher states: "My hypothesis is that working-class pupils are less ambitious than middle-class pupils." She plans a questionnaire for Year 11 pupils at Daleside Academy (a fictional school).

Explain **two** ways the researcher would need to operationalise her hypothesis before she could test it. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a researcher would analyse data from unstructured interviews **and** from closed-question questionnaires. **[4]**

**11.** A sociologist wants to find out whether pupils in top sets are more likely to say they plan to go to university than pupils in bottom sets. Describe and explain how you would design this research, justifying your choices. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far a large sample guarantees that research findings can be generalised. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B. Systematic [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "every nth name" is the signal for systematic sampling.

**2.** C. "Girls spend more time on social media each day than boys." [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A is an aim and B is a question; a hypothesis is a testable statement predicting a relationship.

**3.** A sample that shares the key characteristics of the population, such as age, sex, class and ethnicity [1], so that findings from it can be generalised to the whole population [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the second mark is for the purpose (generalisation), not for more examples of characteristics.

**4.** 2,400 ÷ 120 [1] = every 20th tenant [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* show the division; an answer of 20 with no working may lose a mark when working is asked for.

**5.** Qualitative researchers want to understand people's own meanings and experiences [1], so they avoid deciding the answer in advance [1]. An open aim lets unexpected themes emerge during interviews or observation, which a fixed hypothesis might cause the researcher to miss [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* link the choice to the type of data being collected.

**6.** A pilot might reveal that some questions are upsetting [1], for example asking directly about arguments between parents could distress some respondents [1]. The researcher could then reword or remove the question and add support information before the main study, reducing harm and improving the response rate [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* unclear wording or answer options that miss common responses are also valid problems.

**7. (a)** 300 × 0.45 = 135 people [1]
**(b)** The researcher chooses who fills each quota [1], so she may pick people who look approachable or who are in town at a certain time, meaning the sample may differ from the population in class, ethnicity or employment [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the quota matches only the characteristics it controls for; that is the key idea.

**8.** There is no sampling frame listing young people who have run away [1], and they may distrust researchers [1], so being introduced by someone they know can build trust and make access possible [1]. Limitation: participants tend to introduce people similar to themselves, so the sample is unlikely to be representative of all runaways [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* name the absence of a sampling frame explicitly; it is the main reason for snowball sampling.

**9.** First, "working-class" and "middle-class" must be defined in a measurable way [1], for example by asking for parents' occupations and grouping them into classes [1]. Second, "ambition" must be turned into something measurable [1], such as whether pupils plan to stay in education after 16 or the type of job they hope for at 25 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* operationalising means turning both sides of the hypothesis into indicators, not just one.

**10.** Unstructured interviews: transcribe the recordings and read them closely [1], then sort the material into themes and support each theme with quotations [1]. Closed-question questionnaires: count responses for each option [1], and turn them into percentages, tables or charts to compare groups and identify patterns [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the question asks for both methods; balance your answer between them.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Hypothesis: pupils in top sets are more likely than pupils in bottom sets to say they plan to go to university [1].
- Operationalise: set level from school records; plans measured by a closed question on intentions after school [1].
- Method: a questionnaire, because the aim is to compare how common a plan is across groups [1].
- Closed questions give quantitative, comparable data; one open question could explore reasons [1].
- Pilot with a few pupils to check clarity and that questions are not upsetting [1].
- Sampling frame: school rolls from several schools, with the head teacher's permission [1].
- Systematic or random sample within each set level so top and bottom sets are both represented [1].
- Using several schools improves representativeness compared with one school [1].
- Ethics: informed consent from pupils and parents; anonymous questionnaires to protect confidentiality [1].
- Harm: avoid labelling pupils as "bottom set" in wording or reports [1].
- Analysis: percentages planning university in each set, presented in a table [1].
- Limitation and justification: answers show stated plans not actual outcomes, so a longitudinal follow-up or interviews could add validity [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "justifying your choices" means every decision needs a "because"; a list of steps with no reasons stays in the lower levels.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Generalising means applying findings to the wider population [1].
- A large sample reduces the chance that results reflect a few unusual individuals [1].
- Large samples allow comparisons between subgroups, such as class or gender [1].
- But size does not ensure representativeness: Halsey, Heath and Ridge studied over 8,000 males but excluded females [1].
- A poor sampling frame or method can bias a large sample, for example a quota chosen in one shopping centre [1].
- Low response rates mean those who reply may differ from those who do not [1].
- A small, well-chosen sample can be more representative than a large biased one [1].
- Judgement: a large sample helps, but representativeness depends on the sampling frame and method, so size alone does not guarantee generalisation [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* use a named study as evidence; it turns a general claim into an evaluated point.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a hypothesis as a question or an aim.
- Choosing a sampling method without saying why it fits the group.
- Forgetting working in a calculation.
- Describing a research plan with no justification.
- Assuming large equals representative.
