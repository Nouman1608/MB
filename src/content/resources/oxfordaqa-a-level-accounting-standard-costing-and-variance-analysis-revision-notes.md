## 3.2.1 at a glance

The specification lists four content statements:

1. Purpose of a standard costing system, with its advantages and disadvantages (terms: standard
   cost, variance analysis).
2. Calculation and interpretation of variances: materials (price and usage), labour (efficiency and
   rate), sales (volume and price).
3. The interrelationship between variances.
4. Statements reconciling budget and actual cost, and budget and actual profit.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Standard cost | Cost of one unit worked out in advance from planned quantities and prices, assuming efficient working |
| Standard cost card | The build-up of that cost: kg × price per kg, hours × rate per hour |
| Variance analysis | Measuring the gap between standard and actual, splitting it into price and quantity parts, and explaining it |
| Favourable (F) | Leaves profit higher than the standard result |
| Adverse (A) | Leaves profit lower than the standard result |
| Flexing | Restating the standard for the actual level of output |
| Management by exception | Investigating only significant or recurring variances |

## Purpose, advantages and disadvantages

**Purpose:** planning and control. Targets are set in advance; actual results are measured against them;
managers act on the differences.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Each variance has an owner, so accountability is clear | Setting and maintaining standards costs time and money |
| Management by exception saves managers' time | Out-of-date standards give misleading variances |
| Standard costs support budgets and pricing | Suits repetitive output, not one-off work |
| Agreed targets can motivate | Unrealistic targets demotivate; managers may protect their own variance |
| Inventory can be valued at standard | Reports come after the event |
| Setting standards reveals waste | Ignores non-financial measures such as quality |

## Variance formulas

SP = standard price, AP = actual price, SQ = standard quantity for actual output, AQ = actual quantity,
SR = standard rate, AR = actual rate, SH = standard hours for actual output, AH = actual hours.

| Variance | Usually answerable | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Materials price | Purchasing manager | AQ × (SP − AP) |
| Materials usage | Production manager | SP × (SQ − AQ) |
| Total materials | | standard cost of actual output (SQ × SP) minus actual spend |
| Labour rate | Personnel or whoever sets pay | AH × (SR − AR) |
| Labour efficiency | Production supervisor | SR × (SH − AH) |
| Total labour | | standard labour cost of actual output (SH × SR) minus actual spend |
| Sales price | Sales manager | units actually sold × (actual selling price − standard selling price) |
| Sales volume | Sales manager | standard contribution per unit × (units actually sold − units in budget) |

Positive answer = F, negative = A, with the formulas written as above. Check direction with common sense
too: paying more, using more or selling fewer is adverse.

## Method in steps

1. Divide each actual cost by the actual kg or hours to get AP and AR.
2. Flex: multiply actual output by the standard kg per unit (SQ) and by the standard hours per unit (SH).
3. For price and rate, multiply the unit difference by **actual** kg or hours.
4. For usage and efficiency, value the quantity difference at the **standard** price or rate.
5. Check that the two materials variances sum to the total, and likewise the two labour variances.
6. Label every variance F or A.
7. Interpret: give a likely cause, the manager responsible, and whether it matters.

### Small worked reminder: Bramcote Mugs

Standard per mug: 5 kg of clay at 1.20, and 0.4 hours at 14.00. Output 640 mugs. Actual: 3,120 kg of clay
costing 3,900; 270 hours costing 3,672.

```
AP = 3,900 ÷ 3,120 = 1.25          AR = 3,672 ÷ 270 = 13.60
SQ = 640 × 5 = 3,200 kg            SH = 640 × 0.4 = 256 hours
Price      = (1.20 − 1.25) × 3,120 = 156 A
Usage      = (3,200 − 3,120) × 1.20 = 96 F      Total materials 60 A
Rate       = (14.00 − 13.60) × 270 = 108 F
Efficiency = (256 − 270) × 14.00 = 196 A        Total labour 88 A
```

Reading: dearer clay but less waste; cheaper labour but slower work. Both pairs point to a quality
trade-off.

## Interrelationship: must-know links

- **Arithmetic links.** The two parts sum to the total, so given any two figures you can find the third,
  then work back to actual kg, hours, price or rate.
- **Cheap material:** price F → usage A and efficiency A.
- **Premium material:** price A → usage F and efficiency F.
- **Junior or agency staff on lower pay:** rate F → efficiency A, often usage A.
- **Skilled staff on higher pay:** rate A → efficiency F, often usage F.
- **Price rise:** sales price F → sales volume A. **Price cut:** the reverse.
- **Poor quality output:** sales volume A through lost orders.

The point for evaluation: judge the **net** effect on profit of one decision, not each variance alone,
and be careful about blaming the manager whose variance shows the damage.

## Reconciliation layouts

**Budget to actual cost**

```
Budgeted cost (budgeted output × standard cost per unit)
± volume adjustment (difference in output × standard cost per unit)
= Standard cost of actual output
+ adverse cost variances
− favourable cost variances
= Actual cost
```

**Budget to actual profit**

```
Budgeted profit
+ favourable variances (sales, materials, labour)
− adverse variances
= Actual profit (check it against revenue − all actual costs)
```

Must-know distinction: in a **cost** reconciliation adverse variances are added; in a **profit**
reconciliation adverse variances are subtracted. Any difference between budgeted and actual fixed
overheads goes in as one separate line.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a standard cost.
2. Standard: 3 kg at 2.40 per kg. Output 400 units. Actual: 1,230 kg costing 2,829. Calculate the
   materials price and usage variances.
3. Standard: 1.5 hours at 12.00 per hour. Output 300 units. Actual: 440 hours costing 5,544. Calculate
   the labour rate and efficiency variances.
4. Budget 2,000 units; standard contribution 7 per unit. Actual sales 2,150 units at 0.50 below standard
   price. Calculate both sales variances.
5. Total materials variance 340 A; usage variance 520 A. Find the price variance.
6. Why is standard quantity based on actual output, not budgeted output?
7. Which manager is usually answerable for the labour rate variance?
8. In a profit reconciliation, what happens to an adverse variance?
9. A firm has materials price F and labour efficiency A. Suggest one cause linking them.
10. Give one reason a favourable variance may not be good news.
11. Why is the sales volume variance valued at standard contribution rather than selling price?
12. State one type of business for which standard costing is poorly suited, and why.

### Answers

1. A target cost per unit, fixed before the period from the quantities and prices a business expects
   when it works efficiently.
2. AP = 2,829 ÷ 1,230 = 2.30. Price = (2.40 − 2.30) × 1,230 = **123 F**. SQ = 1,200 kg.
   Usage = (1,200 − 1,230) × 2.40 = **72 A**.
3. AR = 5,544 ÷ 440 = 12.60. Rate = (12.00 − 12.60) × 440 = **264 A**. SH = 450.
   Efficiency = (450 − 440) × 12.00 = **120 F**.
4. Price = −0.50 × 2,150 = **1,075 A**. Volume = (2,150 − 2,000) × 7 = **1,050 F**.
5. Price = total − usage = −340 − (−520) = **180 F**.
6. So the comparison is like-for-like; using budgeted output would mix a volume difference into the
   usage or efficiency result.
7. The person or department that agrees pay rates, such as personnel.
8. It is subtracted from budgeted profit.
9. Cheaper, lower-grade material was bought; it is harder to work, so labour took longer.
10. It may have caused adverse variances elsewhere, or come from cutting quality, so profit overall
    may fall.
11. Each extra unit sold also brings standard variable cost; only the contribution changes profit.
12. A business doing one-off or bespoke work, because there is no repeated unit to set a standard for.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Flexing with budgeted output rather than actual output.
- Mixing actual and standard prices in the same variance.
- Leaving off F or A, or writing a negative number with no label.
- Giving a cause without linking it to the figures or to a responsible manager.
- Valuing sales volume at selling price.
- Adding adverse variances in a profit reconciliation.
- Starting a cost reconciliation from the original budget without the volume adjustment.
- Not checking that the reconciliation ends on the independently calculated actual profit.
- In evaluation, judging each variance alone and missing the overall effect on profit.
