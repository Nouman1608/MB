## The item in one diagram of words

**Trigger** (why) → **need still exists or new need appears** → **route** (how) → **opportunity for a new business, threat to an old one**

- Triggers in the specification: changes in technology; changes in what consumers want; products and services becoming obsolete.
- Routes in the specification: original ideas; adapting existing products/services/ideas.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dynamic | Constantly changing; business conditions do not stay the same |
| Change in technology | New equipment, software or methods that make new products possible or existing ones cheaper or easier to supply |
| Change in consumer wants | A shift in what customers want to buy, caused by fashion, health, environmental concern, lifestyle or income |
| Obsolete | Out of date; replaced by something that does the job better, so few people still want it |
| Original idea | A product or service not offered before |
| Adapting an idea | Changing an existing product, service or idea: what it is, who it is for, where or how it is supplied |
| Opportunity | A chance to meet a need that is not being met well |
| Threat | A change that could reduce a business's sales or force it to close |

## Condensed recall

### Changes in technology
- Can make a brand-new product possible.
- Can cut the cost or time of supplying an existing product, letting small firms compete.
- Can change how firms reach customers, for example by online ordering.
- Can make older products obsolete.

### Changes in what consumers want
- Driven by fashion, health, the environment, working patterns, income.
- Best evidence in a case: a trend over time, customer requests, falling sales of an older product.
- Growing wants create gaps; fading wants cut sales.

### Obsolescence
- Usually the result of a technology change or a change in wants.
- The underlying need often survives; only the way of meeting it changes.
- The owner of an obsolete product can decline with it or adapt.

### Original or adapted?

| | Original idea | Adapted idea |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | Unproven | Already shown by existing sales |
| Competition | None at first | Rivals already exist |
| Main advantage | Head start; can build a name first | Lower risk; customers understand it |
| Main drawback | Customers may not buy; may need heavy promotion | Hard to stand out; easy to copy |

### Four ways to adapt
1. Change the product (new feature, new material, new size).
2. Change who it is for (a new age group or type of customer).
3. Change where or how it is delivered (online, by post, at home).
4. Change how customers pay (renting, subscribing).

## Linking 1.1.1 to the rest of Theme 1

- **Risk and reward (1.1.2):** an original idea usually carries more risk and a possibly bigger reward.
- **Market research (1.2.2):** research tests whether a new idea meets a real need.
- **Customer needs (1.2.1):** a change in wants is a change in customer needs, such as convenience.

## Exam traps

- Naming "technology" as the reason when the case shows customers' tastes changed.
- Saying obsolete means broken or faulty. It means out of date.
- Calling every new product original. If the basic product already exists, it is adapted.
- Describing the change but never saying what it means for the named business.
- Writing that adapting has no risk.
- Giving three reasons when the question asks for one, which wastes time and gains nothing.

## Answer frames

These are suggested frames, not specification wording.

- Explain (no context): fact → because → so.
  "A product can become obsolete when newer technology replaces it. Because customers still have the same need, they look for a newer way to meet it, so an entrepreneur who supplies that newer way can win their sales."
- Outline (context): point about the case → linked consequence for the business.
- Analyse: trigger in the case → effect on customers → opportunity or threat for this business → effect on sales, costs or survival.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the three reasons in the specification why new business ideas come about.
2. Give the two ways in the specification that new business ideas come about.
3. Define obsolete.
4. A cinema starts streaming its own short films online. Is this original or adapted?
5. A firm invents a product that has never been sold before. Which route is this?
6. State one advantage of adapting an existing idea.
7. State one disadvantage of an original idea.
8. Why is obsolescence often linked to a change in technology?
9. A gym opens 24 hours a day because more members work night shifts. Which trigger is this?
10. Give two ways a business can adapt a service.
11. Why can a change that helps one business harm another?
12. Explain why the need met by an obsolete product may still exist.

### Answers

1. Changes in technology; changes in what consumers want; products and services becoming obsolete.
2. Original ideas; adapting existing products, services or ideas.
3. Out of date, usually replaced by something that does the job better, so few people still want it.
4. Adapted: showing films already exists; the change is where and how it is delivered.
5. An original idea.
6. Demand is already proven, so it is less risky (or customers already understand the product).
7. Customers may not buy it, so sales are uncertain (or it may need costly promotion to explain it).
8. A new technology often does the old product's job better or more cheaply, so customers switch and the old product goes out of date.
9. A change in what consumers want (working patterns have changed).
10. Any two: change the service itself; change who it is for; change where or how it is delivered; change how customers pay.
11. The change creates demand for a new product but reduces demand for the old one, so the business selling the old one loses sales.
12. The product went out of date, not the reason people bought it. For example, people still want to keep photos when a format is replaced, so they buy whatever now meets that need.
