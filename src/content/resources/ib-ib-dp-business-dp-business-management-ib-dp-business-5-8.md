This guide goes deeper into section 5.8, Research and development, of the IB *Business management guide* (first assessment 2024). The whole section is HL only. The guide lists four content points: the importance of research and development (R&D) for a business, developing goods and services that address customers' unmet needs, intellectual property protection (copyrights, patents and trademarks), and innovation, both incremental and disruptive. The first is set at AO3 and the others at AO2.

The [study guide to sections 5.8 and 5.9](/resources/ib-dp-business-management-research-and-development-hl-only-and-management-information-systems-hl-only/) defines each term and gives one example of each. This page adds what that guide leaves short: how an R&D pipeline narrows ideas down, what drives the amount a business spends, how to choose between forms of protection, and how to judge innovation strategy in an AO3 answer. Condense it with the [5.8 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-5-8-revision-notes/) and practise with the [5.8 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-business-dp-business-management-ib-dp-business-5-8-practice/).

## Research, development and innovation are different words

- **Research** discovers new knowledge, for example how a material behaves or what customers struggle with.
- **Development** turns that knowledge into a product, service or process that works and can be produced.
- **Innovation** is the successful commercial use of a new idea. An invention that never reaches customers is not an innovation in business terms.

Keeping the three apart helps in analysis answers. A business can spend heavily on research and still fail to innovate if development is slow or the product does not meet a need.

## How an R&D pipeline narrows ideas

Most firms move ideas through stages and drop weak ones at each step. The stage names vary between textbooks; a common sequence is:

1. Idea generation (staff, customers, research teams, observation).
2. Screening against objectives, budget and capability.
3. Concept testing with potential customers.
4. Development of a working prototype.
5. Test marketing in a limited area or group.
6. Full launch.

### Worked example: the cost of one successful product

Halvard Kitchenware is an invented business. All figures are invented and in dollars. Last year its pipeline ran as follows.

| Stage | Ideas entering | Cost per idea | Stage cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screening | 40 | 2,000 | 80,000 |
| Concept testing | 10 | 25,000 | 250,000 |
| Prototype | 4 | 150,000 | 600,000 |
| Test marketing | 2 | 300,000 | 600,000 |
| Launch | 1 | (marketing budget) | |

Total R&D spending = 80,000 + 250,000 + 600,000 + 600,000 = **1,530,000** for one launched product. Only 1 idea in 40, or 2.5%, reached the market.

What this shows: the successful product has to earn enough to pay for the 39 failures as well as itself. That is why R&D spending is certain but the return is uncertain, and why judging R&D on a single project can mislead. A firm that cut the prototype stage to save money might launch more failures, which cost far more after launch.

## What drives R&D spending

| Factor | Effect on R&D |
|---|---|
| Industry | Pharmaceuticals and technology rely on new products; a cleaning service needs far less R&D |
| Competition | Rivals launching new products push a firm to keep up |
| Finance | R&D is often funded from retained profit; a firm short of cash may cut it first |
| Shareholder outlook | Owners focused on short-term dividends may resist spending that pays back slowly |
| Product life cycle | Products in decline need replacements; a portfolio of ageing products raises the need |
| Government | Grants or tax incentives can encourage R&D in some countries |

A judgement on whether a firm spends "enough" must use this context. A falling R&D budget is a warning sign in a fast-changing market but may be sensible in a stable one.

## Unmet needs: from idea to product

The guide asks how businesses develop goods and services that address customers' unmet needs. Two routes are useful in analysis.

- **Market pull.** The business finds a need through research such as complaint data, surveys or observing customers, then develops a solution. Risk is lower because demand is evidenced.
- **Technology push.** R&D creates a capability first, then the business looks for a use. Rewards can be large if the product reveals a latent need, but the risk of no demand is higher.

Example: an invented care-home supplier, Brightmere, notices from staff interviews that residents struggle to open medicine packs. It develops an easy-open pack (market pull). Meanwhile its lab develops a pack that changes colour when opened, and the firm searches for buyers (technology push).

## Choosing a form of intellectual property protection

The guide names three forms. Rules vary by country, so describe them generally.

| Question to ask | Copyright | Patent | Trademark |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is protected? | Original creative work: text, code, images, music | A new invention: product or process | A sign showing the origin of goods: name, logo, slogan |
| Does it need an application? | Usually arises automatically in most countries | Yes, and the invention is published | Registration gives the strongest protection |
| How long? | A long but limited period | A limited period, commonly 20 years | Can usually be renewed |
| Main business benefit | Stops copying of content and software | Time-limited monopoly to recover R&D costs | Protects brand identity and customer loyalty |

A single product often needs all three. A new smart kettle may have a patented heating process, copyrighted app code and a trademarked name. In an answer, match the form to the specific asset in the case.

The limits matter for AO3. Protection costs money to obtain and enforce, enforcement across countries is difficult, and a patent publishes the invention, so rivals can learn from it and design around it. When protection ends, rivals can copy freely, so the business needs a plan for that date.

## Incremental and disruptive innovation in strategy

| | Incremental | Disruptive |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | Small improvements to existing products or processes | A new product or business model that changes how a market works |
| Risk | Low | High |
| Cost | Usually lower | Often high, with uncertain payback |
| Customers | Existing customers, who understand it | Often new or ignored customers at first |
| Threat | Can leave a firm exposed if a rival disrupts the market | Can make the firm's own existing products obsolete |

A well-known pattern is that disruptive products often start cheaper or simpler than existing ones, so established firms ignore them until they improve enough to take mainstream customers. Firms that only innovate incrementally can be overtaken this way. Firms that chase only disruptive ideas may run out of cash before one succeeds. Many businesses therefore balance both.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (AO3 style):* Halvard Kitchenware is considering doubling its R&D budget. Evaluate this decision.

> Doubling R&D would allow more ideas to reach the prototype stage. As only 2.5% of ideas currently reach launch, more spending could raise the number of new products, helping Halvard replace ageing lines in a competitive market.

*Annotation:* uses the case figure and links R&D to the product portfolio.

> However, R&D spending is certain while returns are not. Doubling a budget of 1.53 million would cut retained profit sharply, and shareholders may object if payback takes years.

*Annotation:* a counter-argument tied to finance and stakeholders.

> The decision depends on whether Halvard's market is changing fast and whether its new products are protected; without patents or a strong trademark, rivals could copy them and the extra spending would not be recovered.

*Annotation:* brings in IP as a condition.

> On balance, a phased increase linked to clear stage-gate targets is better than doubling at once, because it limits risk while still building the pipeline.

*Annotation:* a justified judgement.

## Exam technique

- **Explain (AO2)** a form of protection by linking it to a named asset in the case and saying what it stops.
- **Distinguish (AO2)** incremental from disruptive innovation by stating both and the point of difference.
- **Evaluate or discuss (AO3)** the importance of R&D with arguments on both sides, using case data, and reach a judgement that names the conditions.
- Do not claim a patent guarantees sales, or that disruptive innovation is always better.

## Common mistakes

- Using "invention" and "innovation" as if they mean the same.
- Giving a trademark for a production process or a patent for a logo.
- Judging R&D only on one product's success or failure.
- Forgetting that this section is HL only and comes up in HL papers.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is research and development in the SL course?**
No. Section 5.8 is HL only in the IB Business Management guide, first assessment 2024.

**Why does R&D spending carry high risk?**
Most ideas fail at some stage of development, so the cost of many failures must be recovered from a few successful products, and payback can take years.

**Which form of intellectual property protects a new manufacturing process?**
A patent, because it protects a new invention, which can be a product or a process.

**Should a business choose incremental or disruptive innovation?**
It depends on its finance, competitors and how fast its market changes. Many firms do both: incremental innovation protects current sales while disruptive projects prepare for change.
