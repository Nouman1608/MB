Sub-topic 7.1 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680, 2027-2029) gives you the vocabulary and skills for describing where people live and what a population is made of. It has four statements: describing density and distribution (7.1.1), interpreting population pyramids (7.1.2), describing the World Bank's income groups (7.1.3) and comparing the population structure of a low-income and a high-income country (7.1.4). Much of the assessment is data-based, so this guide concentrates on how to read maps, tables and pyramids accurately. For the whole topic, see the [Human population study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-human-population-study-guide/).

## Density and distribution (7.1.1)

The syllabus gives three ideas to use precisely.

- **Sparsely and densely populated places.** A sparsely populated place contains few people; a densely populated place contains many.
- **Population density** is the number of people living in an area, usually measured in **people per km²**. Density = population ÷ area.
- **Population distribution** is how people are spread over an area.

Density and distribution answer different questions. Density is a single number for an area; distribution is a pattern. Two countries can have the same density yet very different distributions: in one, people may be spread evenly across farmland; in the other, almost everyone may live in a narrow strip along a river or coast, with empty desert elsewhere.

### Describing a distribution

When a question shows a map, describe the pattern before you explain anything:
1. **Overall pattern**: even or uneven; clustered or dispersed.
2. **Where it is dense**: name regions, coasts, river valleys or cities, using compass directions and place names from the map.
3. **Where it is sparse**: name the empty areas.
4. **Data**: quote a density value or a percentage from the key.
5. **Anomaly**: any area that breaks the pattern.

At a world scale, people are very unevenly distributed. Dense areas tend to be lowland river valleys, coastal plains and areas with a reliable water supply, fertile soil, moderate climate and jobs. Sparse areas tend to be hot and cold deserts, high mountains and dense forest, where farming, building and transport are difficult.

## Interpreting population pyramids (7.1.2)

A population pyramid is a pair of horizontal bar charts placed back to back. Each bar is an age group, usually five years wide; males are normally on the left and females on the right. The bars show either numbers of people or percentages of the total population.

The syllabus asks you to use a pyramid to describe three things:
- **Young dependent population**: children, usually aged 0 to 14, who rely on others.
- **Working population**: usually aged 15 to 64, who earn income and pay taxes.
- **Elderly dependent population**: usually aged 65 and over.
- **Female and male population**: compare the two sides, especially in the oldest groups, where women often outnumber men because women tend to live longer.

### A reading routine

1. Read the axis: are the bars in numbers or percentages?
2. Look at the **base**: a wide base means many children and a high birth rate.
3. Look at the **sides**: steadily narrowing sides mean many people die at each age; straight sides mean most people survive to old age.
4. Look at the **top**: a wide top means many elderly people and a long life expectancy.
5. Compare **left and right**.
6. Look for **bulges and notches**: a bulge can show a past baby boom or in-migration of working-age adults; a notch can show a war, an epidemic or a period of low births.

## World Bank income groups (7.1.3)

The World Bank classifies the economies of countries by income per person (gross national income per capita). The syllabus uses three groups:

- **Low-income countries (LICs)**: the lowest income per person.
- **Middle-income countries (MICs)**: a broad middle group, which the World Bank divides into lower-middle and upper-middle income.
- **High-income countries (HICs)**: the highest income per person.

The income limits for each group are updated every year, so you do not need to learn the figures. Remember that this is a classification of national economies by average income, so it hides differences between rich and poor people within a country.

## Comparing an LIC and an HIC (7.1.4)

| Feature | Typical LIC pyramid | Typical HIC pyramid |
|---|---|---|
| Base | Wide: many children | Narrow: fewer children |
| Sides | Narrow quickly with age | Fairly straight up to old age |
| Top | Narrow: few elderly | Wide: many elderly |
| Young dependants | High percentage | Low percentage |
| Elderly dependants | Low percentage | High percentage |
| Shape | Triangular | Column or rectangle, sometimes narrower at the base than in the middle |
| Female and male | Similar on both sides | More women than men in the oldest groups |

"Compare" means you must give similarities and differences, and each point should mention both countries in the same sentence: "The LIC has a much wider base than the HIC, showing a higher proportion of children."

## Worked example 1: comparing densities

The data are invented.

| Region | Population | Area (km²) |
|---|---|---|
| Upland North | 2,400,000 | 60,000 |
| Coastal South | 900,000 | 1,500 |

1. Upland North: 2,400,000 ÷ 60,000 = 40 people per km².
2. Coastal South: 900,000 ÷ 1,500 = 600 people per km².
3. Coastal South is 600 ÷ 40 = 15 times as densely populated, even though it has fewer people.

This shows why density, not total population, is used to compare how crowded places are.

## Worked example 2: reading structure data

The data are invented percentages of each country's total population.

| Age group | Country X | Country Y |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 14 | 42% | 15% |
| 15 to 64 | 55% | 64% |
| 65 and over | 3% | 21% (females 12%, males 9%) |

1. Total dependants: X = 42 + 3 = 45%; Y = 15 + 21 = 36%.
2. Dependants per 100 working-age people: X = 45 ÷ 55 × 100 = 81.8; Y = 36 ÷ 64 × 100 = 56.3 (to one decimal place).
3. Country X is the LIC: its dependants are mostly young. Country Y is the HIC: its dependants are mostly elderly, and elderly women outnumber elderly men.

The two countries face different pressures: X needs schools and jobs for a growing number of young people; Y needs healthcare and pensions for an ageing population.

## Common mistakes

- Defining density without "per km²" or confusing it with distribution.
- Describing a pyramid using shapes only ("it is a triangle") without age groups and percentages.
- Reading percentages from only one side of a pyramid when the question asks about the whole population.
- Writing "poor countries" instead of the World Bank term, low-income countries.
- Comparing two pyramids in separate paragraphs instead of directly.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a distribution: pattern, named places, data, anomaly.
- **Interpret** a pyramid: quote figures for each named group and say what they show.
- **Compare**: use comparative words such as "higher", "wider" and "whereas" in each sentence.
- **Calculate**: give units (people per km²) and round as instructed.
