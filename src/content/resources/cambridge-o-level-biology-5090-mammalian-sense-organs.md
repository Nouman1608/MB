This guide covers subtopic **14.2 Mammalian sense organs** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe sense organs as groups of receptor cells that respond to specific stimuli; to identify the structures of the eye (cornea, iris, pupil, lens, ciliary muscles, suspensory ligaments, retina, fovea, optic nerve and blind spot); to state the function of each; to explain the pupil reflex in terms of light intensity and the antagonistic action of circular and radial muscles; and to explain accommodation to view near and distant objects.

The [Coordination and control topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-coordination-and-control/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on the sense organs, with clear step-by-step explanations of the two eye mechanisms that are often examined. Use the [14.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-mammalian-sense-organs-revision-notes/) for recall and the [14.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-mammalian-sense-organs-practice/) to test yourself.

## Sense organs and receptors

A **sense organ** is a group of **receptor cells** that responds to a specific type of stimulus. Each type of receptor detects one kind of change, and it sends an impulse along a sensory neurone to the central nervous system when it is stimulated. The syllabus lists five kinds of stimulus.

| Stimulus | Example of a sense organ |
|---|---|
| Light | Eye |
| Sound | Ear |
| Touch | Skin |
| Temperature | Skin |
| Chemicals | Nose (smell) and tongue (taste) |

The key idea is **specificity**. The light receptors in the eye do not respond to sound, and the sound receptors in the ear do not respond to light. The brain knows what the stimulus is from the path that the impulse took.

## The structure of the eye

The eye is the sense organ for light. Learn the parts as a path that light follows from the front to the back.

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Cornea | The clear window at the front; **refracts** (bends) light as it enters |
| Iris | A ring of muscle that **controls how much light enters** the pupil |
| Pupil | The hole in the centre of the iris through which light passes |
| Lens | Clear and flexible; **focuses light onto the retina** |
| Ciliary muscles | Ring of muscle that **controls the shape of the lens** |
| Suspensory ligaments | Fibres that hold the lens and **control its shape** by pulling on it |
| Retina | The inner layer, **containing light receptors**, some sensitive to light of different colours |
| Fovea | The part of the retina with the **greatest density of light receptors** |
| Optic nerve | **Carries impulses to the brain** |
| Blind spot | The place where the optic nerve leaves the eye; it has no receptors |

Several points about this table are worth stopping on.

- The cornea does most of the bending of light. The lens then makes fine adjustments so that the image is focused.
- The retina contains the receptors. The fovea is the most sensitive part and the place where the image is focused when you look directly at something.
- The blind spot has no light receptors because the nerve fibres leave the eye there. An image that falls on this spot cannot be detected.
- The optic nerve is a bundle of sensory neurones. It does not detect light itself.

## The pupil reflex

The iris contains two sets of muscles that work against each other, which is called **antagonistic action**.

- **Circular muscles** form rings around the pupil. When they contract, the pupil gets smaller.
- **Radial muscles** run outwards from the pupil like the spokes of a wheel. When they contract, the pupil gets larger.

When one set contracts, the other relaxes. The reflex protects the retina from damage in bright light and lets enough light in when it is dim.

### Bright light

1. Receptors in the retina detect the high light intensity.
2. Impulses pass along sensory neurones to the CNS.
3. The circular muscles **contract** and the radial muscles **relax**.
4. The pupil gets **smaller**, so less light enters the eye.

### Dim light

1. The receptors detect low light intensity.
2. The radial muscles **contract** and the circular muscles **relax**.
3. The pupil gets **larger**, so more light enters the eye.

This is a reflex because it is rapid and automatic. The receptors are in the retina and the effectors are the muscles of the iris.

### Worked example: pupil size

*In a bright room, the pupil of a person measured 2 mm across. After the lights were switched off, it measured 7 mm. Calculate the percentage increase in the diameter.*

1. Increase = 7 - 2 = 5 mm.
2. Percentage = 5 divided by 2 x 100 = 250 per cent.

Explain the change: in dim light the radial muscles contract and the circular muscles relax, so the pupil widens and more light can reach the retina.

## Accommodation

**Accommodation** is the change in the shape of the lens so that light from objects at different distances is focused on the retina. The lens is elastic. The ciliary muscles and suspensory ligaments work together to change its shape. Light from a near object is spreading out as it arrives, so it needs more bending to focus. Light from a distant object arrives almost parallel and needs less.

### Viewing a near object

1. The **ciliary muscles contract**.
2. The ring of muscle becomes smaller, so the **tension in the suspensory ligaments falls** (they slacken).
3. The lens is no longer pulled flat and becomes **thicker and more curved**.
4. The lens **refracts light more strongly**, so the image of the near object is focused on the retina.

### Viewing a distant object

1. The **ciliary muscles relax**.
2. The ring of muscle becomes wider, so the **suspensory ligaments are pulled tight** and the tension increases.
3. The lens is pulled **thinner and flatter**.
4. The lens **refracts light less**, so the image of the distant object is focused on the retina.

A good way to remember it: **near means muscles contract, ligaments loose, lens fat.** **Far means muscles relax, ligaments tight, lens thin.**

## Worked example: an accommodation answer

*Explain how the eye changes focus when a person looks up from a book at a bird flying in the distance.*

- The ciliary muscles relax. [The change begins here.]
- The suspensory ligaments become taut. [Tension increases.]
- The lens becomes thinner, with a flatter shape. [Shape changes.]
- Less refraction by the lens focuses the light from the distant object on the retina. [The result.]

A full answer gives all four links in the chain. A common weakness is to say that "the lens moves", which is not correct, or to leave out the ligaments.

## Following light and impulses through the eye

Putting the parts in order helps with the "describe" questions. Light from an object passes through the cornea, which does most of the refraction, and then through the pupil. The size of the pupil has been set by the iris. The light then passes through the lens, which adjusts the focus, and falls on the retina. There the image is upside down. The light receptors in the retina are stimulated, and each sends impulses along sensory neurones that join to form the optic nerve. The optic nerve carries the impulses to the brain, which interprets them as a picture.

The retina has different kinds of receptor. Some respond to dim light and cannot tell colours apart, and some respond to light of different colours (colour vision). The syllabus only asks you to know that some of the receptors are sensitive to light of different colours. It does not ask you to name the types.

### Worked example: matching structure to function

*A question gives a function and asks for the part of the eye. Three are shown with the reasoning.*

- "Contains the greatest density of light receptors": the fovea, because it is the region of the retina where detail is sharpest.
- "Holds the lens in place and changes its shape when the muscle around it contracts": the suspensory ligaments.
- "Has no light receptors": the blind spot, because the nerve fibres leave there.

Read each function carefully. Words such as "greatest density" or "carries" point to a single answer.

## Other sense organs

The syllabus names the stimuli for the other sense organs but does not require detailed structure for them. In each case, receptor cells are grouped in an organ and detect one type of stimulus. The ear contains sound receptors. The skin has touch and temperature receptors. The nose and tongue contain chemical receptors that respond to molecules in air and in food. In all of them, the receptor cells convert the stimulus into impulses in sensory neurones, and the impulses are sent to the brain.

## Comparing the two eye mechanisms

| Feature | Pupil reflex | Accommodation |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | Size of the pupil | Shape of the lens |
| Stimulus | Light intensity | Distance of the object |
| Muscles | Circular and radial muscles of the iris | Ciliary muscles |
| Purpose | Control the amount of light entering | Focus the image on the retina |

Students often mix up the iris muscles and the ciliary muscles. The iris controls the pupil, and the ciliary muscles control the lens.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that the iris focuses light. The lens focuses it.
- Saying that the pupil is a muscle. It is a hole.
- Saying that in bright light the radial muscles contract. It is the circular muscles.
- Saying that the lens moves backwards and forwards. It changes shape.
- Saying that ciliary muscles contract to look at distant objects. They relax.
- Saying that the cornea focuses light exactly. It refracts it; the lens adjusts the focus.
- Confusing the blind spot with the fovea.
- Writing "the retina carries impulses". The optic nerve does this.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name the part on a diagram.
- **State**: give the function.
- **Explain**: give the cause and effect chain, as in the pupil reflex and accommodation.
- **Describe**: give the sequence of events.
