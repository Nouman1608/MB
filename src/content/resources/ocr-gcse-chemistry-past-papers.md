This page is a guide to OCR GCSE (9-1) Chemistry A (Gateway Science, J248) past papers from June 2024 to June 2026. For each series you get the grade thresholds, copied from OCR's grade-boundary documents, and for June 2025 and June 2024 a short account of what the examiners saw, written by us from OCR's examiner reports. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report is on OCR's own site or its teacher platform.

## Where to get the official papers

- [OCR GCSE Chemistry A (Gateway Science) J248 qualification page](https://www.ocr.org.uk/qualifications/gcse/chemistry-a-gateway-science-j248-from-2016/)
- [OCR past paper finder](https://www.ocr.org.uk/qualifications/past-paper-finder/)
- [OCR grade boundaries page](https://www.ocr.org.uk/administration/grade-boundaries/)

OCR publishes question papers and mark schemes through its past paper finder, and grade boundaries on the page above. Examiner reports for current series are held on Teach Cambridge, OCR's teacher site, so ask your teacher or exams officer if you cannot open them.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which tells you what each mark needs. Then read the examiner report for that paper: it tells you why answers that looked right still lost marks. Our notes for each series below are a quick way into that report.

Check which specification a paper was set on. J248 is a linear qualification first assessed in 2018 and is still the current specification. There are two Foundation Tier papers (J248/01 and J248/02) and two Higher Tier papers (J248/03 and J248/04). Each is 90 marks, 1 hour 45 minutes and worth 50% of the qualification. Papers 1 and 3 test topics C1 to C3 and Papers 2 and 4 test topics C4 to C6, with practical skills (topic C7) assessed in both and earlier content assumed in the second paper. The June 2024 to June 2026 papers below all follow this structure; for any older paper, check the specification version before relying on a question.

## June 2025

The June 2025 reports describe well-prepared candidates with no sign of timing problems. Across all four papers, the examiners keep returning to the same habits: showing working in calculations, using precise technical terms, and answering the question set rather than writing what the candidate knows about the topic. The reports also say a number of GCSE examiner reports this year raised concern about declining practical skills.

**Where marks were lost – J248/01 (Foundation, topics C1 to C3)**

- Data questions: candidates often picked the right option but gave no explanation, or copied values from a table rather than interpreting them.
- Exothermic and endothermic changes were swapped, in both temperature description and energy profile diagrams. Describing a temperature-change practical was weak on the initial and maximum temperatures and on reducing heat loss through the apparatus.
- Litmus was treated as if it measures pH, and universal indicator use (matching a colour to a chart) was rarely described.
- Alloys: few candidates explained that different-sized particles disrupt the layers so they cannot slide.
- Ion formation, electronic structure and isotope notation caused errors, including swapped protons and neutrons and wrong ionic charges.
- Calculations lost marks through missing working, wrong significant figures and forgetting to convert a ratio into a percentage. Surface area to volume questions lost marks when only one face of a cube was counted.
- Nanoparticle size and uses were not well known, and "pure substance" answers were often too vague.

**Where marks were lost – J248/02 (Foundation, topics C4 to C7)**

- Reading the question: candidates wrote about causes of global warming when asked about consequences, and described what they knew rather than what was asked.
- Test results were poorly known, for example the colour of a copper precipitate, the test for an unsaturated compound, and flame and anion tests in the six-mark plan.
- Zinc as a protective coating: few discussed both oxygen and water, or why zinc still protects once scratched.
- Mass calculations went wrong where a number in front of a formula sat next to a subscript. A titration question exposed gaps in practical familiarity, including apparatus choice, indicator choice and reading a burette scale.
- Explaining concentration and rate: answers used "more" particles and collisions instead of more particles in a given volume and more frequent collisions. Catalyst explanations stayed general and did not mention activation energy.
- Halogen displacement: product formulae for the reaction were frequently wrong.

**Where marks were lost – J248/03 (Higher, topics C1 to C3)**

- Relative mass and charge of subatomic particles, and the typical size of an atom (including units), were not secure. Candidates also did not always double a radius to get a diameter before counting atoms.
- The six-mark structure and bonding question: candidates misidentified a malleable substance as giant covalent, treated a simple molecular substance as a gas or polymer, and brought intermolecular forces into ionic, metallic or giant covalent explanations.
- Lithium fluoride dot and cross diagrams showed sharing of electrons, a full outer shell on the metal ion, or missing charges.
- State symbols in a balanced equation were often wrong, and the explanation of which experiment had the higher acid concentration used no experimental data.
- Calculation slips: rounding too early, using the wrong formula mass, dividing the wrong way round with the Avogadro constant, and unclear half-equations at electrodes.
- Experiment description: a good answer named insulated apparatus and measured the highest temperature; weaker ones measured the start temperature after mixing.

**Where marks were lost – J248/04 (Higher, topics C4 to C6)**

- Multiple-choice weaknesses: equilibrium read from where graph lines cross rather than where they level off, the effect of pressure and temperature on position of equilibrium, reduction in a displacement reaction, and Group 1 against transition metal properties.
- Catalysts: misconceptions included catalysts providing activation energy, changing the position of equilibrium or altering temperature.
- Interpretation questions: candidates restated data rather than comparing values, and described disposal as recycling in life cycle assessment.
- Polymers: drawing the repeat unit left in a double bond, and giving water instead of hydrogen chloride as the small molecule in a nylon-type condensation polymer.
- Practical chemistry: wrong apparatus for measuring 25.0 cm3 accurately, universal indicator chosen for a titration, wrong answers to decimal places, and unit mismatches between cm3 and dm3.
- Evaluating two copper extraction methods needed a judgement with comparisons, not two descriptions. Moles of gas to volume was missed by candidates who did not recall the molar volume relationship.

## June 2024

The June 2024 reports again found candidates well prepared and attempting most of the paper, though a small but significant group left the harder questions blank on Paper 1. Common advice was to show working, check significant figures and decimal places, and use the mark allocation and answer lines to judge how much to write.

**Where marks were lost – J248/01 (Foundation, topics C1 to C3)**

- Bonding was the weakest area: covalent bonding as sharing a pair of electrons was rarely given, and many answers swapped electrons or talked of metallic bonds.
- Electrolysis was poorly understood, including inert electrodes, which products form where, and what the electrode material does.
- A formulation was treated as a compound rather than a mixture; empirical formula was confused with molecular formula; and polymer property comparisons were vague.
- Standard form and order of magnitude calculations lost marks, often through inverted division or missing working.
- Practical items: drawing gas collection apparatus, naming locating agents in chromatography, calculating Rf, and changing a distillation setup to be safer.
- The six-mark question on metals and non-metals: candidates reversed the sections or described only one group.

**Where marks were lost – J248/02 (Foundation, topics C4 to C7)**

- Basic mathematics: ratios, rounding to one significant figure and "how many times higher" caused errors, and working was sometimes missing.
- Explanations stayed too vague ("pollution", "harmful") where a specific effect or cause was needed.
- Balancing equations and recognising a balanced equation were weak; complete combustion was not always linked to oxygen as a reactant.
- Titration was unfamiliar to many candidates, including the apparatus name and the reasons for its use.
- Rate of reaction: surface area was reasoned from individual lumps rather than a whole sample, cause and effect between rate and temperature was reversed, and improvements to the investigation were limited to repeats.
- Group 1 trends: some answers confused trends with single properties, and boiling points were given when melting points were asked for. Halide test limitations (a positive result for the wrong reason) were rarely seen.
- Atom economy: calculations were fine but explaining why a reaction has 100% atom economy was hard.

**Where marks were lost – J248/03 (Higher, topics C1 to C3)**

- Energy changes: candidates referred to bonds or lost energy rather than using the reaction profile, and a few subtracted the wrong way in bond energy calculations.
- Practical choices: glass beakers were chosen over polystyrene cups for energy-change experiments; thermometer placement in distillation was wrong.
- Models: Rutherford against Thomson was described loosely; particle diagrams for solids and liquids and limitations of the particle model were weak, with bonds between particles often cited.
- Typical atomic radius was not well known, and units were not matched when counting ions across a sample.
- The six-mark question on melting points: candidates mixed up covalent bonds and intermolecular forces, giving diamond intermolecular forces or ranking simple molecules by number of covalent bonds.
- Ionic conductivity was credited to electrons rather than ions, magnesium chloride dot and cross diagrams showed sharing, and excess reagent in a reacting mass calculation was not added back.

**Where marks were lost – J248/04 (Higher, topics C4 to C6)**

- Group 1 explanations: attraction to the outer electron was called intermolecular or magnetic, and losing "an electron" was not tied to the outer shell.
- Chloride test: acid choice was missed, with hydrochloric acid not recognised as adding chloride ions.
- Corrosion: candidates knew the conditions for rusting but not why scratched zinc still protects, and some said zinc rusts first.
- Organic and crude oil topics: soot omitted as a product of incomplete combustion, hydrogen given instead, petrochemical feedstock rarely recalled, and boiling points explained by breaking bonds rather than overcoming intermolecular forces.
- Atmosphere history and acid rain graphs: sequencing the changes needed the cooling Earth and photosynthesis, and some candidates went beyond the years specified.
- Titration: concordant results were defined loosely, and the indicator colour change was often reversed. Drawing and using a tangent for a rate was a skill many found unfamiliar.
- The six-mark rate question: strong answers explained the faster rate with surface area and temperature in terms of collision frequency; others stopped at energy or speed.

## What examiners keep saying

Across the two series with reports, the same points come up in more than one paper.

- Show every step in calculations. Marks for a correct method are only available when the working can be read.
- Answer the question that is set. Underline the command word and the key words, and check the number of marks against the length of answer.
- Compare, do not restate. Data questions want values used in a comparison.
- Keep bonding ideas apart: covalent bonds are not broken when a simple molecular substance melts or boils, and ionic compounds conduct through ions, not electrons.
- Rate answers need particles in a given volume and collision frequency, plus activation energy for catalysts and temperature.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find OCR GCSE Chemistry A (J248) past papers?**
OCR publishes question papers and mark schemes through its past paper finder. Grade boundaries are on OCR's grade boundaries page, and examiner reports are on Teach Cambridge.

**Which J248 papers should I sit?**
Sit the two papers for your tier: Papers 1 and 2 for Foundation, or Papers 3 and 4 for Higher. Each is 90 marks and 1 hour 45 minutes.

**How are the grade thresholds used?**
The overall grade comes from adding the two paper marks of your tier, out of 180, and comparing the total with that series' boundaries. The paper rows are shown as a guide to how each paper performed.

**What mistakes do J248 examiners report most often?**
Missing working in calculations, vague answers that restate the question, confusing covalent bonds with intermolecular forces, and rate explanations that leave out collision frequency and activation energy.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
