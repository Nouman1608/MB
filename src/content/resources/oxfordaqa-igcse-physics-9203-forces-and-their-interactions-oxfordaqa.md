## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| a | Classify a force as contact or non-contact (field), using gravity, electrostatics, magnetism, friction, air resistance, tension and normal contact force |
| b | Explain that friction impedes motion and may cause heating; air resistance is a form of friction |
| c | Describe the pair of forces produced when two objects interact, drawn as vectors |
| d | Distinguish scalars from vectors and represent a vector by an arrow |
| e | Use W = m × g, with g supplied |
| f | Describe stretching, bending and compressing; elastic and inelastic distortion |
| g | Explain that a stretched spring stores elastic potential energy |
| h | Use F = k × e up to the limit of proportionality; carry out the force-extension required practical |

## Thinking in interactions, not single forces

The specification's starting point is that forces come from **interactions** between pairs of objects. Every force has a source (the object exerting it) and a receiver (the object it acts on). If you can name both, you can classify the force and find its partner.

A quick test for statement a: ask whether the two objects must touch. A rope must touch the crate it pulls, so tension is a contact force. A magnet can pull a paper clip across a gap, so magnetism is a non-contact (field) force. Gravity, electrostatic forces and magnetic forces are the three field forces named in the specification.

### Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram shows **one** object and only the forces acting **on** it, each drawn as an arrow from the object. Arrow length shows size and arrow direction shows the direction of the force. Two rules keep the diagram correct:

- Draw forces that act on the chosen object only. The force the object exerts on something else does not belong on its diagram.
- Label each arrow with a name and, where known, a value in newtons.

**Worked example 1: a hanging lamp.** A lamp of mass 2.5 kg hangs at rest from a ceiling by a cable. Take g = 9.8 N/kg.

- Forces on the lamp: weight W = 2.5 × 9.8 = 24.5 N downwards (non-contact, from the Earth) and tension upwards (contact, from the cable).
- The lamp is at rest, so the tension also equals 24.5 N.
- The partner of the weight is the lamp's gravitational pull on the **Earth**, 24.5 N upwards, acting on the Earth.
- The partner of the cable's pull on the lamp is the lamp's pull on the **cable**, 24.5 N downwards, acting on the cable.

The weight and the tension are equal and opposite, but they are not a pair in the sense of statement c, because they act on the same object and are different types of force. The two genuine pairs each involve two objects and one type of force.

## Friction and heating

Friction acts between two surfaces and **impedes** motion: it opposes the movement, or the attempted movement, of one surface over the other. Work done against friction transfers energy by heating, which is why a drill bit gets hot and why brakes warm up. Air resistance is friction between an object and the air, and it grows as speed increases. In an explanation, link three things in order: surfaces rub, friction opposes the motion, energy is transferred to the surroundings by heating.

Friction is not always unwanted. Tyres need friction with the road to grip, and walking needs friction between shoe and floor. A question may ask you to identify where friction is useful and where it wastes energy in the same machine.

## Scalars, vectors and arrows

A scalar has magnitude only; a vector has magnitude and direction. The specification names distance, speed and time as scalars, and displacement, velocity, acceleration, force and momentum as vectors. Mass and energy are also scalars, although the specification's list does not mention them.

Drawing to scale is the skill tested in statement d. Choose a scale that makes the arrow a sensible length, say what it is, and measure carefully.

**Worked example 2: comparing two arrows.** On a diagram drawn to a scale of 1 cm to 4 N, a push arrow is 6.5 cm long and a friction arrow pointing the other way is 2.0 cm long.

- Push = 6.5 × 4 = 26 N. Friction = 2.0 × 4 = 8.0 N.
- The arrows point in opposite directions, so the friction arrow is drawn with its head facing the opposite way. Its length alone tells you only its size.

## Weight and gravitational field strength

Weight is the force on an object due to gravity, and its size depends on the gravitational field strength at the place where the object is. Mass measures the amount of matter and stays the same.

**Worked example 3: a rover on two planets.** A rover has a mass of 900 kg. On Earth g = 9.8 N/kg; on Mars g = 3.7 N/kg.

```
Earth: W = m × g = 900 × 9.8 = 8820 N
Mars:  W = m × g = 900 × 3.7 = 3330 N
```

The mass is 900 kg in both places. The weight on Mars is smaller because the field there is weaker. Exam questions give g, so use the value supplied, even if it differs from one you have seen before.

## Changing shape: elastic and inelastic distortion (P)

Statement f says that **more than one force** is needed to change an object's shape. One force on its own simply accelerates the object; two or more forces acting on it in different places or directions can stretch, bend or compress it. A diving board is bent by the diver's weight pushing down at one end and the clamp holding the other end. A sponge squeezed between finger and thumb is compressed by two inward forces.

- **Elastic distortion:** the object returns to its original shape when the forces are removed. A rubber band stretched gently is an example.
- **Inelastic distortion:** the object does not return to its original shape. Modelling clay and a crushed drinks can are examples.

The same object can behave in both ways. A steel spring distorts elastically for small loads and inelastically if loaded far beyond its limit of proportionality.

## Springs, stored energy and Hooke's law

When a force stretches an elastic object such as a spring, work is done on it and the object stores **elastic potential energy** (statement g). The equation for that energy belongs to section 3.2.1, so a 3.1.1 question will usually ask you to state that energy is stored, not to calculate it.

For an object behaving elastically, extension is directly proportional to the applied force, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded:

```
F = k × e      F in N, k in N/m, e in m
```

**Worked example 4: from lengths to a load.** A spring is 15.0 cm long with no load. A force of 2.4 N makes it 21.0 cm long. A 0.30 kg mass is then hung from it instead. Take g = 9.8 N/kg and assume the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

```
e = 21.0 - 15.0 = 6.0 cm = 0.060 m
k = F / e = 2.4 / 0.060 = 40 N/m
F = m × g = 0.30 × 9.8 = 2.94 N
e = F / k = 2.94 / 40 = 0.0735 m = 7.35 cm
new length = 15.0 + 7.35 = 22.35 cm, so about 22.4 cm
```

Three steps catch candidates out: subtracting the original length, converting centimetres to metres, and turning a mass into a force with W = m × g before using F = k × e.

### Spotting the limit of proportionality in data

In the required practical, the limit shows up as the point where force divided by extension stops being constant. Calculate F ÷ e for each row. While the ratio stays the same (within experimental scatter), the spring obeys Hooke's law. Once the extension grows faster than the force, the ratio falls and the graph of force against extension curves away from the straight line.

## Common mistakes

- Putting forces that act on other objects onto a free-body diagram.
- Calling weight and the normal contact force on a resting object a force pair. They act on one object, so they are a balance, not a pair.
- Using the new length instead of the extension in F = k × e.
- Leaving a mass in kilograms in F = k × e without converting it to a weight.
- Writing that an object distorts elastically "because it is a spring". Elastic means it returns to its original shape when the forces are removed.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** or **name**: one precise fact or term, no explanation needed. "Tension" is enough for "name the force in the rope".
- **Describe**: say what happens or what you would do, step by step, without needing reasons. For the required practical, describe the measurements in order.
- **Explain**: give the reason, using a linking word such as "because" or "so". "The book does not accelerate because the forces on it are balanced" earns more than "the forces are balanced".
- **Calculate**: write the equation, substitute with units converted, and give the answer with its unit. Keep unrounded values until the final line.
- **Draw**: use a ruler, label each arrow, and state the scale for a scale drawing.
