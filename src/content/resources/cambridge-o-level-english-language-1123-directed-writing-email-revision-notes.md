These notes cover the **email**, one of the five forms the syllabus names for Paper 2 Section A: candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, email, report, letter or article". The general method is in the [directed writing revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-revision-notes/); the full explanation and a model email are in the [writing an email study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing-email/).

## Task facts

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Marks | 25: writing 15 (W1–W5), reading 10 (R3, R5) |
| Length | about 250–350 words |
| Form objectives | W4 register appropriate to context; W2 organisation for deliberate effect |
| Purposes | discursive, argumentative or persuasive |

## Email in three words

**Direct, organised, appropriate.**

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Recipient | the person the email is addressed to |
| Register | the level of formality and tone chosen for the recipient and purpose |
| Purpose line | the opening sentence or two that say why you are writing |
| Subject line | an optional, specific heading for the email |
| Paragraph job | the single purpose of each short paragraph |
| Recommendation | your reasoned choice between options |
| Next step | what you ask the reader to do, or what you offer to do |
| Sign-off | the closing words before your name |

## Email versus letter

| Feature | Letter | Email |
|---|---|---|
| Recipient | often an official or editor | often someone you know or work with |
| Register | usually formal | formal to friendly, set by recipient |
| Greeting | "Dear Sir or Madam", "Dear Mr..." | "Dear Ms...", "Hello Mr...", "Hi..." |
| Sign-off | "Yours faithfully / sincerely" | "Kind regards", "Best wishes", "Speak soon" |
| Paragraphs | fuller | shorter, one job each |
| Point | may build up | in the first two sentences |

## Register by recipient

| Recipient | Register | Sign-off |
|---|---|---|
| head teacher | formal, respectful | Kind regards / Yours sincerely |
| organising teacher | polite, clear | Kind regards |
| company | formal, precise | Kind regards |
| friend or cousin | warm, conversational, accurate | Speak soon / Love |

## Paragraph jobs (about 300 words)

1. Greeting + purpose + view or request.
2. Point or option one: source used and developed.
3. Point or option two: source evaluated.
4. Recommendation or answer to the other side.
5. Request or next step.
6. Sign-off and name.

## Opening lines that work

- "Thank you for asking for our views on... I would like to recommend..."
- "I am writing to ask whether the school could..."
- "Following your email about..., I wanted to share some concerns."
- (Friend) "Hi Leila, I've been thinking about your message about the job, and here's what I think."

## Weak to strong

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Hey sir!!" | "Dear Mr Tan," |
| "I am writing this email because I wanted to write about the trip." | "Thank you for asking for our views on the trip. I would recommend the museum." |
| "Theme park = fun but expensive" | "The theme park would be fun, but its cost would leave some students behind." |
| (to a friend) "Ur gonna regret it lol" | "I think you might regret it, and here's why." |

## Mini example (original)

> Hi Leila,
>
> I've read your message about the café job, and I can see why you're tempted: the money would help,
> and you'd learn a lot. But twelve hours a week is a lot in the term before your exams. Could you ask
> the manager about weekends only until June?
>
> Speak soon,
>
> Sam

Friendly register, contractions and a direct question, but accurate punctuation, a clear view and a practical suggestion developed from the situation.

## Exam traps

- Letter sign-offs in the wrong place.
- Text-speak, emojis or missing capital letters.
- Too casual for a head teacher; too stiff for a friend.
- A slow, empty opening.
- Bullet points instead of sentences.
- No next step at the end.
- Retelling the sources.

## Self-test

1. Which writing objective is most often lost in emails, and why?
2. What should the first two sentences of an email do?
3. Give a suitable greeting and sign-off for an email to a head teacher.
4. Give a suitable greeting and sign-off for an email to a friend.
5. How do email paragraphs differ from letter paragraphs?
6. Why should an email end with a request or next step?
7. Rewrite for an email to a teacher: "Can u tell me when the trip is?"
8. Why are bullet-point lists risky in a directed writing email?
9. Is a subject line required?
10. What three questions decide the register?
11. Give two linking phrases useful in short paragraphs.
12. Does a friendly email still need accurate punctuation?

**Answers**

1. W4, register, because emails can range from formal to friendly and candidates misjudge the recipient.
2. Greet the reader, give the reason for writing and signal your view or request.
3. For example: "Dear Mrs Okoye" and "Kind regards" (or "Yours sincerely").
4. For example: "Hi Leila" and "Speak soon" (or "Love, Sam").
5. They are shorter and each has a single job.
6. It tells the reader what to do or what happens next, so the email has a clear purpose.
7. "Could you please let me know the date of the trip?"
8. They leave little room for the development and evaluation that earn reading marks.
9. No; it is optional, but a specific one can help.
10. Who the recipient is, what the purpose is, and what the recipient already knows.
11. Any two: "However", "On the other hand", "For these reasons", "With that in mind".
12. Yes: W5, accuracy, is assessed in every directed writing answer.
