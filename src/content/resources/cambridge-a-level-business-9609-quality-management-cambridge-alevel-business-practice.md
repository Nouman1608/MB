All businesses are invented. Money is in dollars.

## Section A

**1.** Define *quality assurance*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the meaning of quality depends on the customers a business serves. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between internal failure costs and external failure costs, with an example of each. **[4]**

**4.** Identify **two** principles of Total Quality Management (TQM). **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** negative impact of quality control on a business. **[2]**

**6.** Delmont Foods produces 400,000 ready meals a year. Its reject rate is 2.5%, and each rejected meal costs $1.80.

**(a)** Calculate the annual cost of rejected meals. **[2]**

**(b)** The reject rate falls to 1.0% after quality assurance procedures costing $6,000 a year. Calculate the net annual saving. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Tamworth Glass makes windows. Six inspectors check every window at the end of the line. 4% of windows fail inspection and must be remade. Customers rarely complain, but costs are higher than rivals'.

**(a)** Analyse **two** impacts of Tamworth's current method of quality control on the business. **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate whether Tamworth should replace its current method with TQM. **[12]**

**8.** Severn Insurance's call centre compares its performance with a leading rival.

| Measure | Severn | Leader |
|---|---|---|
| Calls answered within 60 seconds | 72% | 90% |
| Claims processed without error | 94% | 99% |
| Customers renewing policies | 78% | 86% |

**(a)** Severn has 120,000 customers. Calculate how many more customers would renew each year if Severn matched the leader's renewal rate. **[2]**

**(b)** Analyse **two** reasons why benchmarking is important for quality management at Severn. **[6]**

**(c)** Explain **one** limitation of benchmarking for Severn. **[2]**

**9.** Corrigan Medical makes surgical instruments sold to hospitals. Hospitals require suppliers to hold a recognised quality certificate. Assess the importance of quality assurance to Corrigan. **[8]**

**10.** "TQM fails more often because of people than because of cost." Evaluate this view. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A system of setting standards and checking procedures at every stage of production [1], with the aim of preventing faults rather than finding them afterwards [1].

**2.** Quality means meeting customer expectations [1], and these depend on the price paid and the market segment [1], so a basic product can be high quality for budget customers while premium customers expect more features and service [1].

**3.** Internal failure costs arise from faults found before the product reaches the customer [1], for example scrap or rework [1]. External failure costs arise from faults found by the customer [1], for example refunds, guarantee repairs or lost future sales [1].

**4.** Any two: customer focus, including internal customers [1]; right first time or zero defects [1]; continuous improvement; employee involvement.

**5.** Inspection adds cost but no value, because it finds faults only after materials and labour have been used [1], so waste remains and the cause of faults is not removed, keeping unit costs high [1].

**6. (a)** Rejects = 400,000 × 0.025 = 10,000 [1]; cost = 10,000 × 1.80 = **$18,000** [1].

**(b)** New rejects = 400,000 × 0.01 = 4,000, costing $7,200 [1]. Saving on rejects = 18,000 − 7,200 = $10,800 [1]. Net saving = 10,800 − 6,000 = **$4,800** [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Impact 1: 100% end-of-line inspection catches faulty windows before they reach customers [1], which explains the low level of complaints [1] and protects Tamworth's reputation with builders [1]. Impact 2: the 4% failure rate means materials and labour are wasted on windows that must be remade [1], and six inspectors add wage costs without adding value [1], so unit costs are higher than rivals', reducing competitiveness or margins [1].

**(b)** For TQM: workers would check their own work at each stage, so faults are found early or not made at all [1], cutting the 4% remake rate and saving materials and labour [1]. Fewer inspectors would be needed, reducing wage costs [1]. Responsibility for quality may motivate workers [1]. Continuous improvement could make Tamworth's costs competitive with rivals over time [1]. Against: training costs are high and benefits take time [1]. Staff may resist extra responsibility, and inspectors may fear job losses [1]. Without strong commitment from managers, TQM may not change the culture [1]. The current system already protects customers well, so the risk of more faults reaching customers during the change must be managed [1]. Judgement: Tamworth should move towards TQM because its problem is cost, not customer satisfaction [1], but keep some final inspection during the transition [1], and success will depend on managers' commitment and on retraining inspectors as quality trainers [1]. **[18]**

**8. (a)** Current renewals = 120,000 × 0.78 = 93,600; at the leader's rate 120,000 × 0.86 = 103,200 [1]. Difference = **9,600** customers [1].

**(b)** Reason 1: benchmarking shows the size of the gap with evidence [1], for example 18 percentage points on calls answered within 60 seconds [1], which turns a vague aim into a specific target managers can monitor [1]. Reason 2: it shows how the leader achieves better results [1], so Severn can learn methods that reduce errors in claims processing [1], which matters because errors drive customers away, as the 8-point renewal gap suggests [1].

**(c)** The leader's methods may not suit Severn, for example if it uses expensive technology or serves different customers [1], so copying them could raise costs without the same improvement [1]. **[10]**

**9.** Quality assurance is essential because hospitals require certification, so without it Corrigan cannot sell to its main customers [1] [1]. Surgical instruments are safety-critical, so a fault could harm patients [1], leading to legal claims and the loss of hospital contracts [1]. QA traces faults to their cause through records [1], helping Corrigan respond quickly if a problem is found [1]. However, QA has costs, including procedures and staff time, which raise unit costs [1]. Judgement: for Corrigan the importance is very high, because certification is a condition of trading and failure costs in this market are severe [1]. **[8]**

**10.** For the view: TQM requires every employee to take responsibility for quality [1], so success depends on staff attitudes [1]. Staff may resist extra responsibility without extra pay [1], and inspectors may fear losing their jobs [1]. If senior managers do not show commitment, staff see TQM as a passing initiative [1]. Against the view: training is costly, and a small business may not afford it [1]; benefits take years, so cash-flow pressure may end the programme early [1]; changes to processes and equipment may also be needed [1]. However, costs are often recovered through lower failure costs, whereas a culture that rejects TQM cannot be fixed by money alone [1]. Judgement: the view is largely correct [1], because people determine whether the principles are applied, but cost can be decisive for small or struggling businesses [1], so failure usually reflects both, with people the more common cause [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a method without stating its impact on cost, staff or customers.
- Treating the remake rate as the only cost and forgetting inspector wages.
- Giving benchmarking data without turning it into a specific target or number.
- In evaluate answers, ignoring how long a quality change takes to pay back.
- Ending without a judgement that names the condition for success.
