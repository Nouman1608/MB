Quick-recall notes for the bystander-factors strand of **3.2.2 Social influence** in OxfordAQA
International GCSE Psychology (9218): "how social factors (presence of others and the cost of
helping) and dispositional factors (similarity to victim and expertise) affect bystander
intervention." For full explanations and a worked answer, use the
[factors affecting bystander intervention study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-psychology-9218-bystander-intervention-factors/);
for conformity and obedience, see the
[Conformity and Obedience revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-obedience-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Bystander** | Someone who witnesses an event without being directly involved |
| **Bystander intervention** | A bystander acting to help |
| **Bystander effect** | Help becomes less likely as the number of bystanders increases |
| **Diffusion of responsibility** | Responsibility to help is shared among bystanders, so each feels less obliged |
| **Pluralistic ignorance** | Everyone reads others' calm as a sign there is no emergency |
| **Audience inhibition** | Holding back for fear of looking foolish in front of others |
| **Cost of helping** | What helping might lose the bystander: safety, time, effort, embarrassment |
| **Cost of not helping** | Guilt, blame, knowing the victim suffers |
| **Arousal: cost-reward model** | Piliavin's idea that bystanders reduce unpleasant arousal by the action with the lowest net cost |
| **Similarity to victim** | Seeing the victim as like oneself or in one's own group |
| **Expertise** | Knowledge or training relevant to the emergency |

## The two kinds of factor

| Social (situation) | Dispositional (person) |
|---|---|
| Presence of others | Similarity to victim |
| Cost of helping | Expertise |

## Latané and Darley's decision model

Notice → interpret as emergency → accept responsibility → know how to help → decide to help.

| Factor | Main step affected | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Presence of others | Interpret (pluralistic ignorance); responsibility (diffusion) | Less help |
| Cost of helping | Decide | High cost → less (or indirect) help |
| Similarity to victim | Responsibility; decide | More help for "one of us" |
| Expertise | Interpret; responsibility; know how | More help, and less risk |

## Each factor in two lines

**Presence of others.** More witnesses → responsibility diffused and others' calm misread → less and
slower help. Strongest when the emergency is ambiguous.

**Cost of helping.** Bystanders weigh danger, effort, embarrassment and unpleasantness against guilt
and blame. High cost of helping → less direct help, perhaps indirect help such as fetching someone.

**Similarity to victim.** Shared identity raises empathy and the cost of not helping. The boundary of
"us" can widen, so a rival can become similar at a broader level of identity.

**Expertise.** Training lets the bystander recognise the emergency, feel responsible and know what to
do, lowering the cost of helping. Untrained people may step back if an expert is already helping.

## Evidence links

| Factor | Study |
|---|---|
| Presence of others | Latané and Darley's smoke study |
| Cost of helping | Piliavin's subway study (cane versus drunk victim) |
| Similarity to victim | Levine's football supporter study |
| Expertise | Explained through the decision model (knowing how to help) |

Details of the three studies: [Studies of Bystander Behaviour revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-psychology-9218-bystander-studies-revision-notes/).

## Evaluation points

- **Support:** each social factor and similarity is backed by a named study.
- **Exception:** the subway study found no diffusion of responsibility in larger groups when the
  emergency was obvious.
- **Application:** first-aid training (expertise) and asking one named person for help (removing
  diffusion) increase intervention.
- **Interaction:** real events combine factors; explanations using only one are incomplete.

## Applying the factors to a scenario

| Detail in the question | Factor to name | Likely effect |
|---|---|---|
| "a crowded platform", "dozens of people" | Presence of others | Less help |
| "covered in blood", "shouting aggressively" | High cost of helping | Less direct help |
| "wearing her school's blazer", "from his home town" | Similarity to victim | More help |
| "a trained nurse", "had just done a first-aid course" | Expertise | More help |

## Exam traps

- Diffusion of responsibility = **who** acts; pluralistic ignorance = **whether** it is an emergency.
- Similarity and expertise are **dispositional** in this specification, not social.
- The bystander effect is about situations, not selfish people.
- A high cost of helping often leads to **indirect** help rather than none.
- In scenario questions, quote the detail (number of people, the victim's appearance, the helper's
  training) and link a factor to it.

## Self-test

1. Define bystander intervention.
2. Name the two social factors in the 9218 specification.
3. Name the two dispositional factors.
4. Explain diffusion of responsibility.
5. Explain pluralistic ignorance.
6. Give the five steps of Latané and Darley's decision model.
7. Give two costs of helping and one cost of not helping.
8. What does Piliavin's model say bystanders try to reduce?
9. Why might someone help a stranger wearing their own team's shirt?
10. Give two ways expertise increases helping.
11. Why might expertise sometimes reduce helping by others?
12. Suggest one way a person needing help in a crowd could increase their chances of being helped.

**Answers:**
1. A bystander acting to help in an emergency.
2. Presence of others; cost of helping.
3. Similarity to victim; expertise.
4. With more bystanders, responsibility is shared, so each person feels less personally obliged to
   help.
5. Bystanders look to each other to judge the situation; seeing others calm, each wrongly concludes
   there is no emergency.
6. Notice the event; interpret it as an emergency; accept responsibility; know how to help; decide to
   help.
7. Costs of helping, any two: danger; time; effort; embarrassment; unpleasantness. Cost of not
   helping, any one: guilt; blame; knowing the victim suffers.
8. The unpleasant arousal caused by seeing someone in distress.
9. They see the victim as similar (part of their group), which increases empathy and the cost of not
   helping.
10. Any two: they recognise the emergency; they feel responsibility falls on them; they know what to
    do, which lowers the cost of helping.
11. Untrained bystanders may decide their help is not needed once an expert is helping.
12. Point to one specific person and ask them directly, removing diffusion of responsibility.
