These questions test the section 3.2 row on necessity defences: self-defence and prevention of crime, duress by threats and duress of circumstances. In Section B, set out each stage of the relevant test and apply it.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the two questions the jury must answer in self-defence. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the effect of a mistaken belief in self-defence, giving an authority. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the special rule for householder cases under s76(5A) Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008. **[2]**

**4.** State the two-stage test for duress from *R v Graham* (1982). **[2]**

**5.** Identify **three** characteristics that may be given to the person of reasonable firmness under *R v Bowen* (1996). **[3]**

**6.** Explain the difference between duress by threats and duress of circumstances. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Ash is approached in the street by a man who raises his fist. Ash, believing he is about to be punched, strikes first and breaks the man's nose. In fact the man was waving to a friend. Advise Ash on self-defence. **[6]**

**8.** Bea wakes at night to find a burglar in her kitchen. She hits him several times with a rolling pin, fracturing his skull. Advise Bea on self-defence. **[6]**

**9.** Cal is told by a drug dealer to whom he owes money that his legs will be broken unless he pays by Friday. To get the money, Cal steals from his employer. Advise Cal on duress. **[5]**

**10.** Dee joined a gang knowing it carried out armed robberies. The leader threatens to kill her unless she acts as lookout in a robbery. Advise Dee on duress. **[5]**

**11.** Eli, who has been drinking, sees his friend collapse with a severe asthma attack in a remote place with no phone signal. He drives her to hospital. Advise Eli on duress of circumstances if he is charged with drink driving. **[5]**

**12.** "The law on duress strikes the right balance between protecting defendants and protecting the public." Discuss. **[12]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Was the use of force necessary in the circumstances as the defendant honestly believed them to be [1]? Was the degree of force used reasonable in those circumstances [1]?

**2.** The defendant is judged on the facts as he honestly believed them to be, even if the mistake was unreasonable [1] (*R v Williams (Gladstone)*, 1984; *Beckford v R*, 1988; s76(4)), unless the mistake was caused by voluntary intoxication [1].

**3.** Where a person uses force against an intruder in a dwelling to defend himself or others [1], the force is regarded as unreasonable only if it was grossly disproportionate in the circumstances as he believed them to be [1].

**4.** Was the defendant compelled to act because he reasonably believed that a threat of death or serious injury had been made [1]? Would a sober person of reasonable firmness sharing his characteristics have responded in the same way [1]?

**5.** Any three of: age [1]; sex [1]; pregnancy, serious physical disability, or a recognised mental illness or psychiatric condition [1].

**6.** In duress by threats, a person threatens the defendant with death or serious injury unless he commits a particular offence [1]. In duress of circumstances, the threat arises from the situation, for example the need to escape a danger, rather than from a demand to commit a crime [1]. The same *Graham* test and the same exclusions for murder and attempted murder apply to both (*R v Pommell*, 1995) [1].

**7.** Ash's belief that he was about to be attacked is judged on the facts as he honestly believed them (*R v Williams (Gladstone)*, 1984; s76(3)–(4)) [1]. His mistake need not be reasonable, though reasonableness is evidence of whether the belief was genuine [1]. He did not have to wait to be struck: pre-emptive force can be necessary (*Beckford v R*, 1988) [1]. The jury must then decide whether one blow was reasonable in the circumstances as he believed them [1], allowing that he could not weigh to a nicety the exact force needed (s76(7); *Palmer v R*, 1971) [1]. A single blow against an expected punch is likely to be reasonable, so Ash is likely to be acquitted of the s47 or s20 charge [1].

**8.** Bea honestly believed force was needed against an intruder in her home, so force was necessary [1]. This is a householder case under s76(5A), as she was in a dwelling and he was an intruder [1], so her force is unreasonable only if it was grossly disproportionate [1]. Repeated blows causing a fractured skull may be disproportionate, but disproportionate force can still be reasonable in a householder case depending on the circumstances (*R (Collins) v Secretary of State for Justice*, 2016) [1]. The jury will consider whether the burglar was still a threat after the first blow and allow for her fear at night (s76(7)) [1]. If the force was grossly disproportionate, the defence fails completely and she may be guilty under s18 or s20 [1].

**9.** The threat of serious injury (broken legs) is sufficient (*R v Hasan*, 2005) [1]. But the threat is about repaying a debt, not about committing theft from his employer, so the offence was not nominated by the person making the threat (*R v Cole*, 1994) [1]. The threat is not immediate or almost immediate, since he has until Friday [1] and could go to the police, which is evasive action reasonably open to him [1]. Borrowing from a dealer may also amount to voluntary association with a person likely to threaten violence. Duress is very unlikely to succeed [1].

**10.** A threat to kill satisfies the requirement of death or serious injury [1], and it is directed at the commission of the robbery [1]. However, Dee voluntarily joined a gang she knew carried out armed robberies [1], so she foresaw or ought reasonably to have foreseen that she might be subjected to compulsion by threats of violence [1]. Under *R v Hasan* (2005) and *R v Sharp* (1987) the defence is not available [1].

**11.** Duress of circumstances applies where the threat of death or serious injury comes from the situation (*R v Willer*, 1986; *R v Conway*, 1988) [1]. A severe asthma attack in a remote place threatens death or serious injury to his friend, a person for whom he is responsible [1]. He must reasonably believe there is no alternative, such as calling an ambulance, and he has no phone signal [1]. A sober person of reasonable firmness would have driven her to hospital [1]. The defence lasts only while the danger continues, so it covers driving to the hospital but not driving on afterwards (*DPP v Bell*, 1992) [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Protecting defendants:** duress is a complete defence, recognising that a person faced with threats of death or serious injury may not have a real choice [1]. The *Graham* test allows relevant characteristics to be taken into account (*R v Bowen*, 1996) [1], and the threat may be against family or others for whom the defendant feels responsible [1]. **Protecting the public:** *R v Hasan* (2005) restricted the defence [1]: the belief must be reasonable [1], the threat immediate or almost immediate with no chance of evasive action [1], and the defence is lost through voluntary association with violent criminals [1], which prevents gang members from manufacturing a defence. **Murder:** duress is excluded for murder (*R v Howe*, 1987) [1] and attempted murder (*R v Gotts*, 1992) [1], to protect the sanctity of life [1]. This can be harsh: in *R v Wilson* (2007) a 13-year-old acting under his father's threats had no defence [1]. A reasoned conclusion, for example that the balance favours the public, perhaps too strongly for murder cases [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying a reasonable-belief test to self-defence.
- Missing the householder rule, or the *Collins* gloss on it.
- Forgetting immediacy and evasive action in duress.
- Not checking voluntary association.
