## Section A

**1.** State **three** properties that distinguish a liquid from a gas. **[3]**

**2.** Name the change of state in each case: **(a)** solid to liquid, **(b)** liquid to solid, **(c)** gas to liquid, **(d)** liquid to gas at the boiling point. **[4]**

**3.** State the value of absolute zero in degrees Celsius and describe the particles of a substance at this temperature. **[2]**

**4.** Explain, in terms of particles, why a gas can be compressed easily but a liquid cannot. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Describe the arrangement, the forces between particles and the motion of the particles in a solid, a liquid and a gas. **[6]**

**6.** Explain, in terms of particles, how a gas in a container exerts a pressure on the container walls. **[3]**

**7.** A partly inflated balloon is placed in a refrigerator. The pressure of the air inside stays approximately constant. Explain, in terms of particles, why the balloon becomes smaller. **[3]**

**8.** The outlet of a bicycle pump is blocked and the piston is pushed in slowly, so the temperature of the trapped air does not change. Explain, in terms of particles, why the pressure of the air increases. **[3]**

## Section C

**9.** A syringe contains 45 cm³ of air at a pressure of 100 kPa. The outlet is sealed and the plunger is pushed in until the pressure is 180 kPa. The temperature stays constant. Calculate the new volume of the air. **[3]**

**10.** An air bubble of volume 1.5 cm³ is released by a diver at a depth where the pressure is 250 kPa. It rises to the surface, where the pressure is 100 kPa.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of the bubble at the surface. **[3]**
**(b)** State the assumption made in your calculation. **[1]**

**11.** A student traps some air and measures its pressure and volume at constant temperature.

| p / kPa | 50 | 75 | 125 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V / cm³ | 60 | 40 | 24 | 20 |

**(a)** Show that the data obey p1V1 = p2V2. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the shape of the graph of p against V. **[2]**

**12.** A sealed container of gas is cooled steadily. Explain, in terms of particles, why its pressure falls, and why there is a lowest temperature to which it could in principle be cooled. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: a liquid has a **fixed volume** but a gas fills its container; a liquid is **almost incompressible** but a gas is easily compressed; a liquid has a **much higher density**; a liquid has a **surface** and fills the bottom of a container, while a gas spreads throughout it.

**2. (a)** Melting [1]. **(b)** Freezing (solidification) [1]. **(c)** Condensation [1]. **(d)** Boiling [1].

**3.** **−273 °C** [1]; the particles have the **least possible kinetic energy** [1].

**4.** Gas particles are **far apart** with large spaces between them, so they can be pushed closer together [1]; liquid particles are **already close together**, so there is very little space to remove [1].

**5.** 2 marks per state (arrangement and forces, motion):
- **Solid:** close together in a regular arrangement with strong forces [1]; particles vibrate about fixed positions [1].
- **Liquid:** close together, irregular arrangement, weaker forces [1]; particles move around each other [1].
- **Gas:** far apart, negligible forces between particles [1]; fast, random motion in straight lines between collisions [1].

**6.** Gas particles move randomly and **collide with the walls** [1]; each collision exerts a **force** on the wall [1]; the total force from many collisions, divided by the area, is the pressure: **force per unit area** [1].

**7.** Cooling makes the particles **move more slowly** (lower average kinetic energy) [1], so they hit the walls **less often and less hard** [1]; for the pressure to stay the same, the volume decreases until the collision rate per unit area of wall is restored [1].

**8.** The same number of particles is squashed into a **smaller volume** [1]; the particles' speed is **unchanged** because the temperature is constant [1]; they hit each unit area of wall **more often**, so the force per unit area, the pressure, increases [1].

**9.** p1V1 = p2V2, so V2 = p1V1 ÷ p2 [1] = 100 × 45 ÷ 180 [1] = **25 cm³** [1].

**10. (a)** V2 = p1V1 ÷ p2 [1] = 250 × 1.5 ÷ 100 [1] = **3.75 cm³** (3.8 cm³) [1].
**(b)** The **temperature** of the air in the bubble stays constant (and no air enters or leaves) [1].

**11. (a)** pV = 50 × 60 = 3000; 75 × 40 = 3000; 125 × 24 = 3000; 150 × 20 = 3000 [1]; pV is constant, so p1V1 = p2V2 for every pair of readings [1].
**(b)** A smooth **curve** with p decreasing as V increases [1], falling steeply at small volumes and levelling off at large volumes, never meeting the axes (inverse proportion) [1].

**12.** As the gas cools, the particles **move more slowly** [1], so they collide with the walls **less often and with less force**, and the pressure falls [1]. At **absolute zero (−273 °C)** the particles have the least kinetic energy they can have, so the gas cannot be cooled further [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that particles expand or shrink with temperature.
- Saying particles speed up when a gas is compressed at constant temperature.
- Leaving out "force per unit area" in pressure explanations.
- Mixing units between the two sides of p1V1 = p2V2.
- Describing solid particles as stationary.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these questions from past papers?**
No. They are original practice questions for subtopic 2.1 and do not copy the exam's structure, question count or mark tariffs.

**Can I use p1V1 = p2V2 with volumes in cm³?**
Yes. Any unit works as long as both volumes use the same unit and both pressures use the same unit.

**Do I need to calculate how pressure changes with temperature?**
No. In 5054 the pressure–temperature and volume–temperature relationships are explained qualitatively; only p1V1 = p2V2 is used in calculations.
