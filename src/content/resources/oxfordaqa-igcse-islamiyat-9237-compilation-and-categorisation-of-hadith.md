**This guide covers content area 3.2.2.4 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Islamiat (9237), The compilation and categorisation of Hadith.** The specification lists three things: "the different categories of Hadith in relation to their reliability including Sahih, Hasan Daeef and Maudu"; "the methods used to test the reliability of Hadith including the Isnad (chains of transmission) and the Matn (text)"; and "the role of the main compilers of the major Hadith collections, including the main features of Sahih Bukhari and Sahih Muslim." The prescribed passage is Sahih al-Bukhari 3461.

A Hadith that is wrongly attributed to the Prophet (pbuh) is a serious matter, because Muslims use it to decide what is lawful and what is not. This is why Muslim scholars built a careful science for sorting Hadith. This guide explains why the science was needed, how it works and who did the main work.

## Why Hadith had to be tested

- **A serious warning.** The prescribed Hadith reports the Prophet (pbuh) saying: "Convey (my teachings) to the people even if it were a single sentence ... and whoever tells a lie on me intentionally will surely take his place in the Fire." Muslims must pass on his words, and must be extremely careful not to invent them.
- **Fabrication.** After the early period of the caliphs, disputes arose between political and religious groups. Some people made up sayings to support their side, and some storytellers invented reports to win a crowd.
- **Human error.** Even honest narrators can forget words or mix up details.
- **Distance in time.** The Prophet (pbuh) died in 11 AH, and the great collections were made in the third century AH. The chain of narrators is the link across that gap.

## How Hadith were compiled

1. **In the Prophet's (pbuh) lifetime.** Many companions memorised what they heard, and some wrote it down. Abdullah ibn Amr ibn al-As (RA) kept a written collection, which he called al-Sadiqa.
2. **Early collections.** In the first and second centuries AH, scholars such as Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri were encouraged by the caliph Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz to write down Hadith. Imam Malik compiled the *Muwatta*, which combines Hadith with the practice of Madinah.
3. **Musnads.** Collections arranged by the name of the companion who narrated, such as the *Musnad* of Ahmad ibn Hanbal.
4. **The Sahih collections.** In the third century AH, scholars set out to collect only sound Hadith, and arranged them by subject. These are the best-known collections.
5. **The six books.** Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim, Sunan Abi Dawud, Jami' al-Tirmidhi, Sunan al-Nasa'i and Sunan Ibn Majah are known together as the six major collections.

## How reliability is tested

### The Isnad (chain of transmission)

The Isnad is the list of narrators, from the collector back to the Prophet (pbuh): "A told me that B said that C said that the Prophet (pbuh) said ...". Scholars examined each link by asking:

- **Did they meet?** Could this narrator have heard from the one before? Were they alive at the same time and in a place where they could meet?
- **Were they upright?** Were they honest, known for good conduct, and not accused of lying?
- **Did they remember well?** Were they accurate in memory or in their written notes?
- **Is the chain complete?** A missing link makes it a broken chain.

Scholars wrote biographical dictionaries of narrators (the science of *rijal*), recording their dates, teachers, students, character and reliability. Early scholar Abdullah ibn al-Mubarak is reported to have said that the Isnad is part of the religion, and that without it anyone could say whatever they wished.

### The Matn (text)

The Matn is the wording of the report. Even if the chain looks good, the text is checked for problems such as:

- **Conflict with the Qur'an.** A Hadith should not contradict clear Qur'anic teaching.
- **Conflict with stronger Hadith.** It should not oppose well-established reports.
- **Sense and history.** It should not be absurd, or contradict known facts.
- **Style and character.** Exaggerated promises and threats, or wording unlike the Prophet's (pbuh) manner, raise suspicion.
- **Hidden defects.** Scholars compared all versions of a report to spot mistakes and odd wording.

## The four categories

| Category | Meaning | Conditions | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sahih** (sound) | Authentic | Continuous chain; every narrator upright and with excellent memory; no hidden defect; no conflict with stronger reports | Can be used as a basis for belief and law |
| **Hasan** (good) | Acceptable | Meets the conditions of Sahih, except that one or more narrators have a slightly weaker memory | Can be used for law, ranking below Sahih |
| **Da'if** (weak) | Weak | Fails a condition: a broken chain, a narrator of poor memory or doubtful character, or a defect | Not used for rulings on belief or halal and haram; some scholars allow it for encouragement to good deeds, with conditions |
| **Maudu'** (fabricated) | Invented | A narrator known to lie, or a text that is false; the report is falsely attributed to the Prophet (pbuh) | Rejected and must not be reported without a warning |

Weak Hadith are not all the same: some are slightly weak and some are very weak. A fabricated report is not a Hadith in the proper sense.

**Worked example of grading.** Suppose a report has a chain of five narrators. Four are known for honesty and excellent memory, and each met the one before. The fifth is known to be honest but sometimes mistaken. The report is *Hasan*, because the chain is complete and the narrators are upright, but one has a slightly weaker memory. If the third narrator is not known to have met the fourth, the chain is broken and the report is *Da'if*. If a fourth narrator is known to have invented reports, the report is likely *Maudu'*.

## The main compilers

### Imam al-Bukhari

- **Who:** Muhammad ibn Ismail al-Bukhari (194-256 AH, 810-870 CE), born in Bukhara, who travelled widely to collect Hadith.
- **Work:** *al-Jami' al-Sahih*, known as Sahih al-Bukhari, which took about sixteen years.
- **Selection:** according to tradition, he chose the Hadith from a very large number of reports he had heard, and kept only those he judged sound. The traditional count is about 7,000 reports including repeats, and about 2,600 when repeats are removed.
- **Strict condition:** a narrator and the narrator he heard from must have been shown to have **met**, not just lived at the same time.
- **Arrangement:** by subject, in books and chapters, such as faith, knowledge, prayer, Zakah and Hajj. His chapter headings often contain his own legal conclusions.
- **Reputation:** most Sunni Muslims regard it as the most authentic book after the Qur'an.

### Imam Muslim

- **Who:** Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (about 206-261 AH), of Nishapur, a student of al-Bukhari.
- **Work:** *Sahih Muslim*.
- **Condition:** he required that the narrators lived at the same time, and accepted a chain where meeting was possible.
- **Arrangement:** all versions of one Hadith are placed together, so it is easy to compare wording and chains.
- **Introduction:** he wrote a long introduction explaining his method.
- **Reputation:** together with al-Bukhari's book it is called the *Sahihayn* ("the two Sahihs"). A Hadith agreed by both is held in the highest esteem.

| Feature | Sahih al-Bukhari | Sahih Muslim |
|---|---|---|
| Compiler | Muhammad ibn Ismail al-Bukhari | Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj |
| Place | Bukhara | Nishapur |
| Condition on narrators | They must have met | They must have been contemporaries |
| Arrangement | By subject, with legal chapter headings | By subject, with versions grouped together |
| Special feature | Chapter titles show his legal views | Detailed introduction on method |

Other compilers include Abu Dawud (legal Hadith), al-Tirmidhi (who graded his Hadith and noted opinions of the schools), al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah.

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

**Question:** Explain **two** methods used to test the reliability of a Hadith. Use a source of authority. **[6]**

> One method is to study the Isnad [1]: scholars checked whether each narrator was honest, had a good memory and had really met the one before, so a broken chain or an unreliable narrator made the Hadith weak [1]. Another method is to examine the Matn [1]: a Hadith that contradicts the Qur'an or established facts is doubted even if the chain looks sound [1]. The Prophet (pbuh) said that whoever lies about him intentionally will take his place in the Fire (Bukhari 3461), which is why scholars felt a duty to test every report [2].

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing Isnad and Matn.** The Isnad is the chain of narrators. The Matn is the text.
- **Saying a Da'if Hadith is the same as a Maudu'.** Weak and fabricated are different grades.
- **Mixing up Bukhari and Muslim.** Bukhari required that narrators had met; Muslim required that they could have met.
- **Claiming Hadith were written only centuries later.** Writing began in the Prophet's (pbuh) lifetime.
- **Listing categories without conditions.** The definition of each grade is what scores.
