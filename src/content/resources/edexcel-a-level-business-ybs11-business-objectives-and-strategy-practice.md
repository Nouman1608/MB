## Sources

> **Source 1: Harbourlight Ferries**
>
> Harbourlight runs ferries between two island groups. Its mission is "to be
> the best way to travel between the islands". The market is worth $320m a
> year and is expected to grow by 3% a year. Harbourlight has 10% of the
> market and has set an objective of 14% within three years. The board is
> considering either a new fast service on its existing routes or a
> new route to a third island group.
>
> **Source 2: Harbourlight's product range**
>
> | Product | Market growth | Harbourlight share | Largest rival's share |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Day trips | High | 30% | 15% |
> | Freight | High | 5% | 20% |
>
> Only two other firms operate in the market. Building a new ferry costs
> $40m and takes two years, and ferry operators buy fuel from one local
> supplier.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "corporate objective". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate Harbourlight's current annual sales. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate the sales Harbourlight needs in three years to reach its objective, and the percentage increase on today's sales. **[4]**

**4.** Explain **one** weakness of Harbourlight's mission statement. **[4]**

**5.** Identify which of Ansoff's strategies each of the two options represents, and explain which is riskier. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse why a mission statement alone is not enough to guide Harbourlight's decisions. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "relative market share". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the relative market share of each product and state which Boston Matrix category each falls into. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **one** barrier to entry in the ferry market. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse how the power of its fuel supplier could affect Harbourlight. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss whether Harbourlight should keep investing in freight. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether Harbourlight should choose the fast service on existing routes or the new route to a third island group. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A specific, measurable target [1] that a business sets for the whole organisation to achieve, usually within a time limit [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Sales = share x market size = 10% x $320m [1] = 0.10 x 320 [1] = **$32m** [2]. (Award the last 2 marks together for the correct answer with units.) **[4]**

**3.** Market in three years = 320 x 1.03 x 1.03 x 1.03 = $349.67m [1]. Sales needed = 14% x 349.67 = **$48.95m** [1]. Increase = (48.95 - 32) / 32 x 100 [1] = **53%** [1]. **[4]**

**4.** The statement "the best way to travel" is vague and not measurable [1], so managers cannot tell whether it has been achieved or use it to choose between options [1]. It does not say who the customers are or what "best" means (cheapest, fastest or most reliable) [1], so different departments could interpret it differently and pull in different directions [1]. **[4]**

**5.** A new fast service on existing routes is **product development**, as it is a new product in an existing market [1]. A new route to a third island group is **market development**, as it is an existing service in a new market [1]. The new route is probably riskier, because Harbourlight has no customers, reputation or local knowledge on that route [1] and it must invest in a new ferry and port arrangements before earning any revenue [1]. (Accept the fast service as riskier if it is justified by the cost of a new ferry.) **[4]**

**6.** A mission statement is general and does not set targets [1], so Harbourlight needs objectives such as 14% market share to turn it into measurable aims [1]. Managers also need a strategy, for instance Ansoff's choices, to decide how to reach the objective [1]. The statement does not say how to use limited finance and ferries, so choices between routes would still need analysis [1]. Without detailed objectives and tactics, staff may follow the mission differently, such as one team cutting prices and another raising service quality [1], which wastes resources and may cause the board to miss its 14% target [1]. **[6]**

**7.** A product's share of its market [1] divided by the market share of the largest rival [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Day trips = 30 / 15 = **2.0** [1]; high growth and high relative share make it a **star** [1]. Freight = 5 / 20 = **0.25** [1]; high growth and low relative share make it a **question mark** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** A new ferry costs $40m [1], so a new entrant needs very large capital and a long wait for a return [1]. It also takes two years to build [1], so potential entrants cannot respond quickly to profits earned by existing firms, which protects the current operators [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Harbourlight buys fuel from one local supplier, so the supplier has strong bargaining power [1]. It can raise prices, and Harbourlight cannot switch easily [1]. Fuel is a major cost for a ferry operator, so a price rise would increase operating costs [1]. If Harbourlight cannot pass the cost on, because only two rivals compete on price, its profit margin falls [1]. A lower margin reduces cash for the $40m ferry and may make the 14% market share objective harder to fund [1]. It could reduce the risk by agreeing a long-term fixed price or finding another source [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **For investing:** freight is in a high-growth market, which offers future sales [1]; investing could raise Harbourlight's share from 5% closer to the leader's 20% and turn a question mark into a star [1]; freight may use spare capacity on existing ferries [1].
**Against:** Harbourlight's relative share is only 0.25, so it has a long way to go to catch the leader [1]; investment is costly and risks wasting money if share does not improve [1]; the money could go to day trips, where it already leads [1].
**Judgement:** invest selectively in freight if Harbourlight can win a clear niche, such as particular routes or customers, and set a target for gaining share by a given date; otherwise concentrate cash on day trips, where its strong position is more likely to produce returns [1]. The decision depends on how much cash day trips generate [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **Fast service (product development):** it builds on existing customers and routes, so demand is better understood [1]; a faster service could be a source of differentiation against only two rivals [1]; it would support the objective of reaching 14% share in the existing market [1]. But the cost of a new vessel is high and it could be copied [1].
**New route (market development):** it opens a new market with no competition from rivals' existing customer bases [1]; it could spread risk if the existing routes decline [1]; but it carries more uncertainty, because Harbourlight lacks local knowledge and must build a reputation from nothing [1]. Both options need a $40m ferry, which takes two years [1].
**Judgement:** the fast service on existing routes is probably the better choice, because it is lower risk, fits the 14% objective in the existing $349.67m market and can be sold to customers Harbourlight already has [1]. The new route is worth considering later if the existing market stops growing [1]. The choice depends on whether the board can fund one ferry or two, on its attitude to risk and on how quickly rivals could copy a fast service [1]. The board should also check the fast service against its mission and its wider position in the market [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating sales from the market size without multiplying by the share.
- Forgetting that the market itself grows when working out the sales needed.
- Mixing up product development and market development.
- Treating relative market share as the firm's plain share.
- Discussing a strategy in general without using the $40m cost or the two-year delay.
