These notes condense **International trade** from Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification. The [international trade study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-international-trade-ocr-aslevel-economics/) has the full explanations and worked examples, and the [international trade practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-international-trade-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/) give exam-style work. Worked figures are invented; real trends are summarised broadly, so check exact data against current sources.

## What you must be able to do

| Specification wording | In one line |
|---|---|
| Explain international trade | Exchange of goods and services across borders, driven by differences in resources and the gains from specialisation |
| Explain patterns of international trade over time | Trade outgrowing output, the rise of emerging economies, supply chains, services, trade blocs |
| Evaluate advantages and disadvantages of trade to developed, emerging and developing countries | Gains and costs differ by type of economy and by group within each economy |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| International trade | Exchange of goods and services between countries |
| Exports | Goods and services sold abroad; an injection |
| Imports | Goods and services bought from abroad; a withdrawal |
| Trade openness | (Exports + imports) / GDP x 100 |
| Specialisation | Concentrating production on a narrow range of goods or services |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up |
| Economies of scale | Falling average costs as output rises |
| Global supply chain | Production split across several countries |
| Intermediate goods | Components and parts used to make other goods |
| Trade bloc | A group of countries with reduced trade barriers between members |
| Primary product | A raw material or crop, such as copper or coffee |
| Diversification | Widening the range of goods and services a country exports |
| Developed, emerging, developing | High income; fast-industrialising; low income |

## Why countries trade

1. Different natural resources, climate and factor endowments.
2. Specialisation raises total output.
3. Economies of scale from larger markets.
4. Wider choice and better quality for consumers.
5. Competition and the spread of technology.

### Worked reminder: openness

Exports 45, imports 55, GDP 125 (billions): openness = 100 / 125 = **80%**.

### Worked reminder: specialisation

Country F can make 40 cloth or 20 tools; Country G 10 cloth or 30 tools. Splitting resources equally and not trading: 20 + 5 = 25 cloth and 10 + 15 = 25 tools. Each specialising: **40 cloth and 30 tools**, more of both.

### Worked reminder: shares versus values

Exports to Asia: 10 of 100 in year 1 (10%), 30 of 200 in year 10 (15%). Exports to Europe: 60 of 100 (60%) then 100 of 200 (50%). Europe's **share** fell by 10 percentage points, but its **value** rose from 60 to 100.

## Patterns over time

| Trend | Key point |
|---|---|
| Trade growing faster than output | Lower transport and communication costs, tariff cuts; sharp falls in 2008 to 2009 and 2020 |
| Rise of emerging economies | China joined the WTO in 2001 and became the largest exporter of goods |
| Global supply chains | More trade in components and intermediate goods |
| Growth of services trade | Communications technology makes services easier to sell abroad |
| Trade blocs | Much trade within groupings such as the EU single market |
| Commodity dependence | Many developing economies still rely on a few primary exports |
| UK | Large share of exports in services; EU the largest partner as a bloc, the US the largest single country; left the EU in January 2020 |

## Advantages and disadvantages by economy type

| Economy | Main advantages | Main disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Developed | Cheaper imports, export markets for high-value services, competition and scale | Structural unemployment in import-competing industries, supply chain risks, current account deficits |
| Emerging | Export-led growth, inward investment and technology, job creation | Exposure to world downturns, pollution, regional inequality |
| Developing | Export earnings to buy capital goods, bigger markets, cheaper consumer goods | Volatile commodity earnings, difficulty building new industries, poor working conditions |

### Worked reminder: commodity dependence

Coffee is 50% of exports and its price falls 20%: earnings fall by **10%** if volumes are unchanged. With coffee at 5% of exports, earnings fall by only **1%**.

## Evaluation points

- Gains are spread widely (consumers), losses concentrated (particular workers and regions).
- Support for losers decides whether trade benefits most people.
- The type of goods exported affects stability of earnings.
- Adjustment costs come first; productivity gains take longer.
- Environmental and labour standards affect whether trade supports sustainable development.

## Exam traps

- A trade deficit does not mean a country loses from trade.
- A falling share is not a falling value.
- Use percentage points for changes in shares.
- Tailor advantages to the type of economy named.
- Services are a large part of UK exports: do not describe UK trade as only goods.
- Imports are not "bad": they raise choice and lower costs.

## Self-test

1. Define trade openness and give its formula.
2. Exports 200, imports 260, GDP 920. Calculate openness.
3. Give three reasons why countries trade.
4. State two long-run changes in the pattern of world trade.
5. When did China join the World Trade Organization?
6. What is a global supply chain?
7. Give one advantage of trade for an emerging economy.
8. Give one disadvantage of trade for a developing economy that exports one commodity.
9. Give one disadvantage of trade for a developed economy.
10. A country's share of exports going to one market falls from 45% to 38%. Describe the change.
11. Oil is 80% of exports and its price falls by 25%. Estimate the fall in export earnings.
12. Why might the gains from trade fail to benefit everyone in a country?

### Answers

1. The value of exports plus imports as a percentage of GDP: (X + M) / GDP x 100.
2. 460 / 920 = **50%**.
3. Any three: different resources; specialisation; economies of scale; choice and quality; competition and technology.
4. Any two: trade growing faster than output; rise of emerging economies; supply chains; growth of services trade; trade blocs.
5. **2001**.
6. Production split across countries, with components made and assembled in different places.
7. Any one: export-led growth; inward investment and technology; job creation; economies of scale.
8. Volatile prices make export earnings unstable.
9. Any one: structural unemployment in import-competing industries; supply chain disruption; current account deficits.
10. A fall of **7 percentage points**; its value may still have risen if total exports grew.
11. 0.8 x 25% = **20%**.
12. Workers in industries facing import competition may lose jobs, and gains to consumers are spread thinly, so without support some groups lose.
