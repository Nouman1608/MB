"A divided union: civil rights in the USA, 1945–74" is Depth Study 7 on Paper 1 of Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE History (4HI1). It covers the Red Scare and McCarthyism, the campaign against
segregation in the 1950s and 1960s, the rise of Black Power, student, anti-war and women's protest
movements, and the Watergate scandal that ended Nixon's presidency in 1974. You answer one
three-part question: extract impression, two effects and a 16-mark essay.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics:

1. **The Red Scare and McCarthyism** — reasons for the Red Scare, the Hiss and Rosenberg cases, the
   FBI, HUAC and the Hollywood Ten; McCarthy's methods, opposition, downfall and impact.
2. **Civil rights in the 1950s** — segregation and discrimination, the Supreme Court and Congress,
   Brown v Board of Education (1954), Emmett Till (1955), the Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56),
   Little Rock (1957), the Civil Rights Act 1957 and the revival of the Ku Klux Klan.
3. **The impact of civil rights protests, 1960–74** — Freedom Riders, the Anniston fire-bombing,
   sit-ins, voting rights, the Meredith case, Martin Luther King, Birmingham, the March on Washington,
   Freedom Summer, legislation, Selma, the Nation of Islam and Malcolm X, Black Power, the 1968
   Olympics, Stokely Carmichael, the Watts riots and the Black Panthers.
4. **Other protest movements** — students and the anti-Vietnam War movement, Berkeley, Students for a
   Democratic Society and hippies; Betty Friedan, Eleanor Roosevelt, NOW, women's liberation and
   abortion; Phyllis Schlafly and opposition to the women's movement.
5. **Nixon and Watergate** — the scandal, its impact on Nixon and US politics, new laws (War Powers
   Act 1973, Election Campaign Act 1974, Privacy Act 1974, Congressional Budget Control Act 1974),
   Gerald Ford and the pardon.

## Why did a Red Scare grow after 1945?

The Cold War — Soviet control of Eastern Europe, the Soviet atomic bomb (1949), the communist
victory in China (1949) and the Korean War (1950) — made Americans fear communism at home. The
**House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC)** investigated Hollywood in 1947; the **Hollywood Ten**
refused to answer and were jailed for contempt. **Alger Hiss**, a former State Department official,
was convicted of perjury in 1950 after being accused of spying, and **Julius and Ethel Rosenberg**
were executed in 1953 for passing atomic secrets. J. Edgar Hoover's **FBI** kept files on
suspected radicals.

Senator **Joseph McCarthy** claimed in February 1950 to have a list of communists in the State
Department. His methods were unsupported accusations, guilt by association and bullying witnesses.
Opposition grew from journalists such as Ed Murrow and from politicians. In the televised
**Army–McCarthy hearings** (1954) the army's lawyer Joseph Welch asked, "Have you no sense of
decency?", and the Senate **censured** McCarthy in December 1954. McCarthyism ruined careers, made
dissent dangerous and helped tie anti-communism to opposition to civil rights.

## How did civil rights develop in the 1950s?

In the South, **Jim Crow** laws segregated schools, transport and public places, and literacy tests
and intimidation kept most black people from voting. Change came first through the **Supreme Court**.
In **Brown v Board of Education of Topeka** (1954), backed by the NAACP, the Court ruled segregated
schools unconstitutional; but it set no deadline, and many Southern states resisted.

The murder of 14-year-old **Emmett Till** in Mississippi (1955), and the acquittal of his killers,
shocked the nation after his mother insisted on an open-casket funeral. In December 1955 **Rosa Parks**
was arrested for refusing to give up her seat in **Montgomery**, Alabama. The bus boycott that
followed lasted over a year, made **Martin Luther King** a national leader and ended when the Supreme
Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional (1956). At **Little Rock** (1957) Governor Faubus used
the National Guard to stop nine black students entering Central High School; Eisenhower sent federal
troops to protect them. The **Civil Rights Act 1957** created a Civil Rights Commission but did
little to increase black voting. The **Ku Klux Klan** revived in response, using violence and
intimidation.

## What did protest achieve in the 1960s?

**Sit-ins** began at Greensboro, North Carolina, in February 1960 and led to the founding of SNCC.
In 1961 **Freedom Riders** tested the desegregation of interstate buses; at **Anniston**, Alabama, a bus
was fire-bombed. **James Meredith**'s admission to the University of Mississippi (1962) needed federal
troops after riots.

King's **Birmingham Campaign** (1963) deliberately provoked a violent response from police chief
"Bull" Connor; television images of dogs and fire hoses turned against children shocked the nation.
At the **March on Washington** (August 1963), about 250,000 people heard King's "I have a dream" speech.
During **Mississippi Freedom Summer** (1964), volunteers registered black voters and three civil rights
workers were murdered. These protests helped secure the **Civil Rights Act 1964**, banning segregation
in public places and discrimination in employment. Violence against marchers at **Selma** in March
1965 led to the **Voting Rights Act 1965**, which ended literacy tests and sent federal registrars to
the South.

Not all activists accepted non-violence. **Malcolm X** of the **Nation of Islam** called for black pride
and self-defence; he was assassinated in 1965. Riots in the **Watts** district of Los Angeles (1965)
showed anger at poverty and police treatment in Northern and Western cities. **Stokely Carmichael**
popularised "Black Power" in 1966, and the **Black Panthers**, founded by **Huey Newton and Bobby
Seale** in 1966, organised armed patrols and community programmes. At the **1968 Mexico City Olympics**,
Tommie Smith and John Carlos raised gloved fists on the podium. King was assassinated in April 1968.

## Which other protest movements grew in the 1960s?

Students protested against the draft and the **Vietnam War**. The **Berkeley Free Speech Movement**
(1964) demanded the right to political activity on campus, and **Students for a Democratic Society**
(SDS) organised anti-war protest; **hippies** rejected mainstream values. Women's campaigns drew on
**Betty Friedan**'s *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) and the Presidential Commission on the Status of
Women chaired by **Eleanor Roosevelt** (1961). Friedan co-founded **NOW** (1966) to fight
discrimination. The more radical **women's liberation** movement challenged gender roles, and the
Supreme Court's ruling in **Roe v Wade** (1973) legalised abortion. **Phyllis Schlafly** led
conservative opposition, especially to the Equal Rights Amendment.

## How did Watergate change US politics?

In June 1972 burglars linked to Nixon's re-election committee were arrested at the Democratic
headquarters in the **Watergate** building. Nixon tried to cover up the White House's involvement.
Investigations by journalists and a Senate committee revealed a taping system in the Oval Office; in
1974 the Supreme Court ordered Nixon to hand over the tapes, which showed he had obstructed justice.
Facing impeachment, Nixon **resigned on 9 August 1974**. **Gerald Ford** became president and
pardoned him. Congress passed laws to restrain the presidency: the **War Powers Act (1973)**, the
**Election Campaign Act (1974)**, the **Privacy Act (1974)** and the **Congressional Budget Control
Act (1974)**.

## Frequently asked questions

**Was King the most important factor in civil rights progress?**
A classic essay. Weigh King
against the NAACP and the courts, grassroots activists (SNCC, CORE), federal government action, the
media and the pressure created by more radical groups.

**What was the difference between Black Power and King's approach?**
King sought integration
through non-violence; Black Power stressed black pride, self-reliance and self-defence.

**Why does Watergate belong in a civil rights unit?**
The depth study is about a "divided union":
Watergate deepened distrust of government and led to laws protecting citizens' rights.
