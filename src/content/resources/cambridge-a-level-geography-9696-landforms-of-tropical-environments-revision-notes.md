Condensed recall for **7.2 Landforms of tropical environments** in Topic 7, Tropical environments, Paper 3 Advanced Physical Geography Options. The syllabus names granite landforms (deep weathering profiles, tors, inselbergs and bornhardts) and limestone landforms (tropical karst: cone, tower and cockpit karst). For full explanations, read the [7.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-landforms-of-tropical-environments/); then try the [7.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-landforms-of-tropical-environments-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hydrolysis | Chemical reaction of minerals with water and hydrogen ions; turns feldspar into kaolinite clay |
| Oxidation | Reaction with oxygen, especially of iron in minerals such as biotite mica |
| Carbonation | Solution of calcium carbonate by water containing carbon dioxide |
| Saprolite | Chemically rotted rock that keeps the original structure |
| Grus | Sandy saprolite from granite, mostly quartz grains |
| Corestone | Rounded block of fresh rock surrounded by saprolite |
| Weathering front | Boundary between rotted and fresh rock; irregular because of joint spacing |
| Joint | Crack in rock along which water moves |
| Sheeting joint | Curved joint parallel to the surface, caused by pressure release |
| Exfoliation | Peeling of rock slabs along sheeting joints |
| Etching | Deep weathering followed by stripping of the weathered layer |
| Tor | Pile of rounded blocks of fresh rock above the surface; called a kopje in southern Africa |
| Inselberg | Isolated hill rising abruptly from a plain |
| Bornhardt | Large dome-shaped, bare-rock inselberg |
| Castle koppie (nubbin) | Smaller inselberg covered with boulders |
| Doline | Enclosed hollow formed by solution in limestone |
| Cockpit | Star-shaped enclosed hollow between residual hills |
| Fengcong | Cone karst: hills sharing a common base |
| Fenglin | Tower karst: isolated towers on a plain |
| Foot cave | Cave cut by solution at the base of a karst tower |
| Equifinality | Similar landforms produced by different processes |

## Why weathering is deep

- Reaction rates roughly double for each 10 °C rise: 27 °C against 10 °C gives about 3 times the rate.
- Permanent moisture, organic acids from rapid decay, and long periods without glaciation.
- Result: saprolite tens of metres deep in many tropical granite areas.

## Granite sequence: from profile to landform

1. Water enters along joints; hydrolysis rots feldspar to kaolinite; quartz survives.
2. Closely jointed rock rots deeply; widely jointed rock survives as corestones; massive rock survives as large unrotted masses.
3. The weathering front becomes very irregular.
4. Uplift, a fall in base level or climate change allows erosion to strip the saprolite.
5. Corestones emerge as **tors**; large massive compartments emerge as **bornhardts**; together they form **inselbergs** above a plain.
6. Pressure release forms sheeting joints, and exfoliation keeps domes rounded.

## Theories at a glance

| Landform | Main theory | Rival or extra theory | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tor | Linton (1955): deep weathering then stripping | Palmer and Nielson (1962): one-stage periglacial frost shattering, for Dartmoor | Equifinality; both may apply in different places |
| Bornhardt | Etching of massive granite | Scarp retreat (pediplanation), linked to Lester King | Long timescales; several cycles of weathering and stripping |
| Tower karst | Lowering to water table, then foot undercutting | Develops where uplift is slow; cone karst where uplift is faster | Not always a later stage of cone karst; may be partly relict |

## Karst at a glance

| Type | Form | Key process | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cockpit karst | Star-shaped enclosed hollows between rounded hills | Solution concentrated where joints meet; dolines merge | Cockpit Country, Jamaica |
| Cone karst | Cones with a common base; closed depressions | Solution lowers depressions but not to the water table | Southern China; Chocolate Hills, Bohol, Philippines |
| Tower karst | Isolated steep towers on an alluvial plain | Depressions reach water table; alluvium spreads; foot caves undercut | Guilin, China; Ha Long Bay, Vietnam (drowned); Viñales, Cuba |

**Carbon dioxide paradox:** warm water holds less dissolved carbon dioxide, but tropical soils produce so much from roots and microbes, and rainfall is so heavy, that solution is rapid.

## Exam traps

- Hydrolysis, not freeze-thaw, is the main tropical granite process.
- Joint spacing is the control on which rock survives. Always mention it.
- A bornhardt is a type of inselberg, not the other way round.
- Uluru is an inselberg, but it is arkose sandstone, not granite.
- Explain *formation*, not just appearance. Give stages in order.
- Do not claim tropical landforms all formed under today's climate. Many are partly relict.
- Draw and label a cross-section when it helps, and refer to it in the text.

## Self-test

1. Which mineral in granite is most attacked by hydrolysis, and what does it become?
2. Which mineral survives as sand grains?
3. What is a weathering front, and why is it irregular?
4. State the two stages of Linton's model of tor formation.
5. What alternative explanation was proposed for the tors of Dartmoor?
6. What causes sheeting joints in a bornhardt?
7. Name the rock type of Uluru.
8. Write a word equation for carbonation.
9. Why is limestone solution rapid in the humid tropics even though warm water holds less carbon dioxide?
10. Distinguish between cone karst and tower karst.
11. What are foot caves and how do they help form towers?
12. Give one reason why tower karst is not always a later stage of cone karst.

### Answers

1. Feldspar; it becomes kaolinite clay.
2. Quartz.
3. The boundary between rotted and fresh rock; it is deeper where joints are close together and higher where rock is massive.
4. Deep chemical weathering of jointed granite, then stripping of the saprolite to expose corestones.
5. A one-stage periglacial origin by frost shattering and solifluction (Palmer and Nielson, 1962).
6. Pressure release as overlying rock is removed.
7. Arkose, a feldspar-rich sandstone.
8. Calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide → calcium hydrogen carbonate in solution.
9. Very high carbon dioxide from soil respiration and very large volumes of rainfall.
10. Cone karst has hills joined at a common base with closed depressions; tower karst has isolated, steep towers rising from a flat plain.
11. Caves cut by acidic water at the base of hills; they undercut the sides, which collapse to leave steep walls.
12. Where uplift is faster, depressions never reach the water table, so cone karst persists; rock purity and joint pattern also affect the form.
