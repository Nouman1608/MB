## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Activity | Something that causes overhead cost, such as setting up machines or processing orders |
| Cost pool | The overhead cost collected for one activity |
| Cost driver | The factor that causes the cost of an activity and measures each product's use of it |
| Cost driver rate | Cost pool divided by total quantity of the driver |
| Absorption rate (traditional) | Total overheads divided by one basis, such as direct labour hours |

## Formulae

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Driver rate | Pool cost / total driver quantity |
| Overhead for a product from one pool | Driver rate × product's use of the driver |
| Overhead per unit | Total overhead for the product / units made |
| Total cost per unit | Direct costs per unit + overhead per unit |
| Selling price (mark-up) | Total cost × (1 + mark-up %) |
| Selling price (margin) | Total cost / (1 − margin %) |
| Traditional rate | Total overheads / total hours (or other basis) |

Mark-up is profit as a percentage of cost. Margin is profit as a percentage of selling price. Read the question to see which is asked for.

## Method in steps

1. Split overheads into cost pools, one per activity.
2. Choose the cost driver for each pool.
3. Calculate each driver rate.
4. Multiply each rate by each product's driver quantity, add the pools and divide by units.
5. Add direct costs, then add the mark-up or margin to reach the price.
6. Check: product overheads must add back to the total overhead.

## Choosing a cost driver

- Cause and effect: the driver really changes the pool cost.
- Measurable for each product.
- Data available at reasonable cost.
- Products use it in different proportions (otherwise the answer is the same as traditional costing).

Examples: set-ups cost, number of set-ups. Invoicing, number of invoices. Stores handling, number of requisitions. Machine power, machine hours.

## Must-know distinctions

| | Traditional absorption | ABC |
|---|---|---|
| Overhead pools | One, or one per department | One per activity |
| Basis | One volume measure, such as labour hours | Several cost drivers |
| Product costs | Can over-cost high-volume products and under-cost complex, low-volume ones | Follow each product's use of activities |
| Effort and data | Low | High |

Total overhead is the same under both. The method changes how it is shared out between products, so product profit changes. Total profit changes only when closing inventory differs, because overhead is carried forward in inventory valuation.

## Uses and limitations

| Uses | Limitations |
|---|---|
| More accurate product costs | Costly and time-consuming to set up and run |
| Better pricing for complex products | Choice of pools and drivers needs judgement |
| Exposes products that look profitable but are not | Some overheads have no clear driver |
| Targets cost control through the drivers | Savings appear only if resources are removed |
| Supports range and process decisions | Based on past data; little gain where overheads are small |

## Worked reminder: a service business

Verdane Linen Services washes linen for hotels (H) and restaurants (R). Overheads of 180,000 are currently shared on service labour hours: 8,000 for H and 4,000 for R, a total of 12,000. The rate is 15 per hour. Hotels take 120,000 and restaurants take 60,000. Hotels send 160,000 kg a year and restaurants 80,000 kg. That makes the overhead 0.75 per kg for each.

| Pool | Cost | Driver | H | R | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Collections | 36,000 | Visits | 600 | 1,200 | 20 |
| Washing | 96,000 | Kilograms | 160,000 | 80,000 | 0.40 |
| Stain treatment | 30,000 | Items treated | 2,000 | 8,000 | 3 |
| Invoicing | 18,000 | Invoices | 120 | 480 | 30 |

ABC overhead: H = 12,000 + 64,000 + 6,000 + 3,600 = 85,600, or 0.535 per kg. R = 24,000 + 32,000 + 24,000 + 14,400 = 94,400, or 1.18 per kg. The total is 180,000.

With direct costs of 0.55 per kg and a price of 1.60 per kg for both, the traditional margin is 0.30 per kg on each. ABC gives hotels 0.515 per kg profit and restaurants a loss of 0.13 per kg. The restaurants need many small collections and stain treatments. A sensible response is to raise restaurant prices, charge for stain treatment or reduce visits, but weigh this against the risk of losing those customers.

## Absorption method, cost and profit

- If everything made is sold, total profit is the same under every method. Only the profit reported for each product changes.
- If a product's ABC cost is higher than its traditional cost, traditional costing was under-costing it, and a price built on the old figure may be too low.
- If units are left in closing inventory, the method matters for total profit. Overhead sits in the inventory value, so the method giving the higher unit cost gives the higher closing inventory and the higher profit.
- Compare methods by working out both unit costs, then both profits, then saying what the manager should do differently.

Example: 500 units are unsold. Unit overhead is 7.00 under ABC and 5.50 under labour hours. Closing inventory carries 500 × 1.50 = 750 more overhead under ABC, so profit is 750 higher.

## Decisions and recommendations

For any recommendation question:

1. State the ABC cost and profit for each product.
2. Compare with the traditional figures and say why they differ.
3. Give financial reasons for and against each option.
4. Add non-financial factors, such as customer relationships, reputation, capacity and staff.
5. Make a clear recommendation and justify it. The syllabus says Paper 4 candidates should support decisions with a balance of reasons.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a cost driver.
2. A pool of 45,000 is driven by 150 inspections. What is the rate?
3. A product needs 12 inspections and 800 units are made. Find the inspection cost per unit.
4. Direct costs are 9.00 per unit. Using your answer to question 3 as the only overhead, find the total cost and the selling price with a 40% mark-up on cost.
5. What is the profit margin on that price?
6. Which is the better driver for an invoicing pool: direct labour hours or number of invoices?
7. Overheads of 90,000 and 12,000 labour hours: what is the traditional rate, and what overhead does a unit taking 2 hours absorb?
8. Give two limitations of ABC.
9. All 800 units were made, but 400 remain in closing inventory. ABC overhead is 6.00 per unit and traditional is 4.50. How much higher is profit under ABC?
10. Why can ABC raise the cost of a low-volume product?

### Answers

1. The factor that causes the cost of an activity to be incurred, and which measures how much of it each product uses.
2. 45,000 / 150 = **300 per inspection**.
3. 12 × 300 = 3,600; 3,600 / 800 = **4.50 per unit**.
4. Total cost = 9.00 + 4.50 = **13.50**. Price = 13.50 × 1.40 = **18.90**.
5. Profit = 18.90 − 13.50 = 5.40; 5.40 / 18.90 = **28.6%** of selling price.
6. **Number of invoices**, because invoices cause the work.
7. 90,000 / 12,000 = **7.50 per hour**; 2 × 7.50 = **15.00**.
8. For example: high cost and time to set up and maintain; subjective choice of pools and drivers; some costs with no clear driver; savings need resources to be removed.
9. 400 × (6.00 − 4.50) = **600 higher** under ABC, because more overhead is carried forward in inventory.
10. Low-volume products use set-ups, inspections and orders heavily relative to their size. Traditional labour hours spread these costs over high-volume lines, so ABC moves the cost back to the products that cause it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing a pool by units made rather than by the total driver quantity.
- Using the same driver for every pool, which gives the traditional answer.
- Stopping at overhead per unit and not adding direct costs.
- Confusing mark-up on cost with margin on selling price.
- Leaving out the check that product overheads sum to the total.
- Writing a recommendation with no reasons against it, or with no non-financial points.
- Saying ABC reduces costs. It changes the cost shares, and savings come only from acting on the drivers.
- Describing limitations in general terms, with no link to the business in the question.
