- Functions of management.

## Core recall

### Definition

- **Management:** getting things done through people and other resources to achieve the objectives of a business.
- Levels: senior (direction), middle (departmental plans), supervisors (daily work).

### The functions (Fayol's list, the most widely used)

| Function | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | Set objectives and decide how to reach them (forecasts, budgets, timetables) | Next quarter's sales target and staffing |
| Organising | Arrange resources, structure, tasks and responsibilities | Kitchen stations and rotas |
| Commanding (directing, leading) | Give instructions, motivate, guide | Briefing the team and setting priorities |
| Coordinating | Make departments and people work together | Linking kitchen, waiting staff and deliveries |
| Controlling | Monitor results against plan and correct | Budget variance review |

The functions form a cycle: control results feed into the next plan.

### Budget variances

- **Variance** = actual − planned.
- **Adverse:** costs higher than planned (or revenue lower). **Favourable:** the opposite.
- **Variance %** = variance ÷ planned × 100.
- Act on the largest variances first; find the cause (one-off or continuing).

### Constraints on management

| Internal | External |
|---|---|
| Finance and budget | Law and regulation |
| Skills and attitudes of staff | Competition |
| Structure and systems | The economy (interest rates, inflation, recession) |
| Culture | Technology change |
| Owners' objectives | Suppliers and customers |
| Time and information | Trade unions and pressure groups |
| Existing contracts | Ethical and social expectations |

- Evaluate: how serious for this business, can it be overcome, at what cost?
- Law is fixed; culture can change slowly.

### Judging success

| Evidence | Examples |
|---|---|
| Objectives achieved | Sales growth, profit, market share |
| Financial | Profit margin, return on capital, cash flow, variances |
| Efficiency | Productivity, waste, cost per unit |
| People | Turnover, absence, morale |
| Customers | Satisfaction, complaints, loyalty |
| Stakeholders | Suppliers, community, regulators |
| Long term | Investment, innovation, reputation |

- Separate what managers could control from what they could not.
- Check whether the targets were realistic and what rivals achieved.

## Worked reminder

Linford Foods (invented).

- Planned costs £480,000, actual £516,000: variance **£36,000 adverse** (7.5%). Overheads £50,000 planned, £68,000 actual: **£18,000 adverse** (36%).
- Sales £2,000,000. Target growth 8% gives £2,160,000; actual growth 5% gives £2,100,000: shortfall £60,000.
- Profit target 12.0% × £2,160,000 = £259,200; actual 11.4% × £2,100,000 = £239,400: shortfall **£19,800**.
- Turnover (12% against a maximum of 15%) and satisfaction (88% against 85%) met their targets. Two of four objectives met.

## Evaluating management success: a checklist

1. Compare results with objectives.
2. Use several measures, not profit alone.
3. Separate controllable from uncontrollable causes.
4. Were the targets realistic? What did rivals achieve?
5. Short term against long term.
6. Stakeholder views.
7. Judgement and what it depends on.

## Quick case practice

A department manager (invented) planned costs of £80,000 and actual costs were £88,000, though sales were 5% above plan.

- Variance = £88,000 − £80,000 = **£8,000 adverse**.
- Variance % = 8,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = **10%**.

Model response outline: this is the controlling function. A cost overrun of 10% alongside higher sales may be reasonable, because costs rise with volume, so the manager should compare costs with the expected level at the higher sales. If costs rose faster than sales, investigate the cause. Then plan any corrective action, organise it and direct the staff concerned. Judgement: the variance is a signal, not proof of poor management.

Each function appears: planning (the budget), controlling (the comparison), organising and directing (the response) and coordinating (keeping departments aligned while costs are cut).

A second check on constraints: the same manager has a fixed budget, a team with limited skills and a new safety rule. The rule is an external constraint that cannot be changed and must be built into the plan; the skills gap is internal and can be closed by training, which takes time and money. Rank them: the legal rule is the most certain, the skills gap the most open to action.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Management | Getting things done through people to achieve objectives |
| Budget | A financial plan for income and costs |
| Variance | The difference between actual and planned |
| Adverse variance | Worse than planned |
| Favourable variance | Better than planned |
| Constraint | A limit on what managers can do |
| Coordination | Making the parts of the business work together |

## Exam traps

- List each function with an example; do not just name them.
- Management is wider than leadership.
- A favourable variance is not always good news.
- Do not judge success by profit alone.
- Separate external causes from management decisions.
- Say which constraint is most serious in the case and whether it can be overcome.

## Self-test

1. Define management.
2. Name the five functions of management in this list.
3. Which function compares actual results with plans?
4. Budget £60,000, actual £66,000. Calculate the variance and say whether it is adverse or favourable.
5. Calculate the variance as a percentage.
6. Give two internal constraints on managers.
7. Give two external constraints.
8. A business sets a profit target of £90,000 and makes £81,000. Calculate the percentage shortfall.
9. Give two measures other than profit for judging management success.
10. Why might a favourable cost variance be a problem?

### Answers

1. Getting things done through people and other resources to achieve the objectives of a business.
2. Planning, organising, commanding (or directing), coordinating and controlling.
3. Controlling.
4. £66,000 − £60,000 = **£6,000 adverse** (costs higher than planned).
5. 6,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **10%**.
6. Any two: finance; staff skills; structure; culture; time and information.
7. Any two: law; competition; the economy; technology; suppliers and customers.
8. Shortfall £9,000; 9,000 ÷ 90,000 × 100 = **10%**.
9. Any two: productivity; customer satisfaction; labour turnover; market share; stakeholder views.
10. It may show under-investment, for example less spent on maintenance or marketing than needed.
