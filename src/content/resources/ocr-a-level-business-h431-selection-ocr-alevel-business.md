## What selection is

**Selection** is the process of choosing the most suitable candidate from the people who applied. It follows recruitment, which attracts the applicants. Selection compares each candidate against the job description and, above all, the person specification, using one or more methods, and ends with an offer to the chosen person.

A good selection process is **valid** (it measures what really predicts success in the job), **reliable** (different assessors would reach similar conclusions), **fair** (free of discrimination) and **affordable**.

## The methods

### Application form

A standard form completed by each applicant, covering personal details, qualifications, experience and sometimes short questions. **Purpose:** to collect the same information from everyone so that candidates can be compared and shortlisted fairly. **Strength:** consistent and easy to compare against the person specification. **Weakness:** time-consuming for the applicant and reviewer, and gives limited insight into personality.

### Curriculum vitae (CV)

The applicant's own summary of education, work history and skills. **Purpose:** to show experience and achievements in the applicant's chosen format. **Strength:** cheap, and shows how well the person presents themselves. **Weakness:** the format differs between applicants, so comparison is harder, and it may exaggerate.

### Letter of application

A personal letter, often with the CV, explaining why the applicant wants the job and why they are suitable. **Purpose:** to show motivation, written communication and how well the applicant understands the job. **Weakness:** may be written with help, and takes time to read.

### Testing

Tests used in selection include:

- **Aptitude tests** (skills such as numeracy or typing);
- **Psychometric tests** (personality or reasoning);
- **Practical tests** (for example cooking a dish or fixing a fault).

**Purpose:** to measure ability or traits objectively. **Strength:** consistent, can predict job performance for specific skills. **Weakness:** costs money; badly chosen tests may be unfair or irrelevant; candidates may be anxious.

### Interview

A structured conversation between the candidate and one or more interviewers. Formats include one-to-one, panel and group. **Purpose:** to assess communication, personality, motivation and fit, and to clarify answers on the application. **Strength:** two-way: the candidate can ask questions too; allows probing. **Weakness:** costly in managers' time; subjective, since interviewers may favour people like themselves or be swayed by first impressions; poor at predicting performance if unstructured.

### Work trial

The candidate does the job, or part of it, for a short period, such as a day, under observation. **Purpose:** to see real performance and how the candidate fits the team. **Strength:** the best evidence of actual ability. **Weakness:** takes time and supervision; may disrupt normal work; candidates may be unable to take unpaid time (and a trial should be paid or lawful).

### References

Statements from a previous employer or teacher about the candidate's character, reliability and performance. **Purpose:** to check facts and get an independent view. **Strength:** can confirm honesty. **Weakness:** referees may be reluctant to be critical; usually positive; slow to arrive.

## The selection process as a funnel

Typically a business moves from many applicants to one offer.

1. Screen applications against the essential criteria (forms, CVs, letters).
2. Shortlist the best.
3. Test or trial the shortlist if the job needs specific skills.
4. Interview.
5. Check references and eligibility to work.
6. Offer and negotiate.

Each stage costs time, so the business uses cheaper methods first and more expensive ones later for fewer people.

### Worked example 1: costs and the expected cost of a poor appointment

Cardew Engineering (invented) receives 120 applications for a technician post and runs this process.

| Stage | Number | Working | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screen applications | 120 | 120 × (10 ÷ 60) hour × £25 | £500 |
| Aptitude test for shortlist | 24 | 24 × £15 | £360 |
| Interview (two interviewers, 1.5 hours each) | 8 | 8 × 1.5 × 2 × £30 | £720 |
| Work trial | 3 | 3 × £140 | £420 |
| **Total** | | | **£2,000** |

Compare with a short process: screening plus interviews only (£500 + £720 = **£1,220**).

A poor appointment costs about £7,500 in lost output, wasted pay and re-recruitment. Cardew estimates that a short process gives a 25% chance of a poor appointment and the full process an 8% chance.

- Short process: expected cost of poor appointment = 25% × £7,500 = £1,875. Total expected cost = £1,220 + £1,875 = **£3,095**.
- Full process: expected cost = 8% × £7,500 = £600. Total = £2,000 + £600 = **£2,600**.

The full process costs £780 more to run (£2,000 − £1,220) but is expected to save £1,275 in poor appointments (£1,875 − £600). The net benefit is £495 (£3,095 − £2,600).

Evaluation:

- The thorough process is better on these estimates, but the 25% and 8% are guesses. If the full process cut the risk only to 18%, its expected cost would be £2,000 + £1,350 = £3,350, which would be worse than the short process.
- The cost of a poor appointment varies. For a senior or safety-critical post it is much higher, which strengthens the case for a thorough process.
- Non-financial effects: a work trial lets the candidate see the workplace, so both sides learn; a long process can put off good candidates who accept another offer.

**Judgement.** Cardew should use a work trial and test for a technical post where skills are vital, but could drop the test for routine posts. The right mix depends on the cost of error and how many candidates there are.

## Impact of selection

Good selection:

- puts the right person in the right job, raising productivity and quality;
- reduces turnover and the costs of retraining and re-recruiting;
- improves teamwork and morale;
- reduces absence and disciplinary problems;
- protects the business legally if the process is fair.

Poor selection leads to the opposite: wasted pay, errors, low morale in the team and early resignations.

Stakeholders: the successful candidate (satisfying job), unsuccessful candidates (fair treatment and feedback), colleagues (a good team member), customers (service quality), owners (cost and performance).

## Evaluating methods

When judging a method ask:

- **Is it valid?** Does it test what the job needs? A typing test for a typist, not for a chef.
- **Is it reliable and fair?** Would different assessors agree? Does it disadvantage a group?
- **Is it affordable?** Compare cost with the cost of a poor appointment.
- **Does it suit the post and the number of candidates?** Cheap methods for mass screening, costly ones for the final few.
- **What do candidates experience?** A good process presents the business well.
- **Is it legal?** No discrimination in questions or tests.

## Recommending methods

Link the recommendation to the post. For a manager: application, interview, psychometric test, references. For a craft or technical job: practical test or work trial plus interview. For a high-volume, low-skill job: application form and short group or one-to-one interview. For a role with sensitive duties: thorough references and checks. Give the cost and what it depends on.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing selection with recruitment.
- Treating the interview as the best predictor in all cases. Unstructured interviews are subjective.
- Listing methods without matching them to the job.
- Ignoring the cost of making a poor appointment.
- Forgetting references and checks, which come late in the process.
- Saying that tests are always objective. They can be unfair if they test the wrong thing.

## Exam technique

For "explain the content and purpose", give what the method contains and why a business uses it. For "evaluate usefulness", give a strength and a weakness for the post in the case. For "recommend", select two or three methods, explain how they work together and give the main risk.
