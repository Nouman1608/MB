These notes condense section 8.1, *Globalisation, poverty and inequalities*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 4 Section A. Syllabus strands: debates about globalisation's impact on life chances in developing countries in education, income and health; the role and success of transnational organisations; and explanations of global inequality — capitalism, colonialism, post-colonialism and patriarchy.

Full explanations: [globalisation, poverty and inequalities study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-poverty-inequalities/).

## Core argument

Globalisation has improved some life chances — vaccination, primary enrolment, poverty reduction in some fast-growing economies — but unevenly, and often on unequal terms. Whether transnational organisations succeed depends on the organisation, the indicator and who benefits. Explanations differ on whether the cause of inequality lies inside poor countries or in the global system.

## Measuring life chances

- Income per head hides distribution.
- **Human Development Index** (UNDP, since 1990): life expectancy, education, income; reflects Sen's capabilities approach.
- Ask whose life chances changed; inequality within countries can rise as averages grow.
- Terms such as "developing" carry assumptions of one path to development.

## Life chances: evidence grid

| Area | Optimistic | Critical |
|---|---|---|
| **Education** | Fee abolition raised enrolment: Malawi (1994), Uganda (1997), Kenya (2003); global goals | Quality fell as classes grew; structural adjustment linked to cuts and user fees; girls' access limited in some regions |
| **Income** | China's poverty reduction after late-1970s reforms; Grameen Bank microfinance (Yunus, Nobel Peace Prize 2006); remittances | Low pay in export zones; microfinance debt cycles; commodity dependence; Collier's traps |
| **Health** | Smallpox declared eradicated 1980; Gavi (2000); Global Fund (2002) | Patent rules raised medicine prices (Doha Declaration 2001 response); brain drain; pollution from relocated industry |

**Collier, *The Bottom Billion* (2007):** traps of conflict, natural resources, being landlocked with poor neighbours, and bad governance.

## Transnational organisations

| Body | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| IMF and World Bank | Infrastructure, stability lending | Structural adjustment cuts; Stiglitz (2002) criticised the IMF |
| HIPC initiative (1996) | Debt relief freed funds | Conditions; slow |
| Jubilee 2000 | Civil society pressure for debt cancellation | Did not change trade rules |
| Health partnerships | Mass vaccination and treatment | Donor-led priorities |
| NGOs | Local projects, relief | Small scale; may bypass governments |
| Aid debate | Sachs (2005): aid breaks poverty traps | Moyo (2009): aid fuels corruption and dependency |

**Judge success by:** which indicator; for whom; compared with what.

## Explanations of global inequality

| Explanation | Key names | Cause | Solution implied |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modernisation / neo-liberal | Rostow (1960); Collier (2007) | Internal barriers | Integration and investment |
| Capitalism: dependency / world-systems | Frank (1966); Wallerstein | Unequal global system | Change or delink from the system |
| Colonialism | Rodney, *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972) | Extraction and export-oriented economies | Repair historical damage |
| Post-colonialism | Nkrumah, *Neo-Colonialism* (1965) | Continuing economic and cultural control | End neo-colonial relations |
| Patriarchy | Boserup (1970); Pearce (1978) feminisation of poverty; Mies (1986) | Women's work ignored or exploited | Gender-focused development |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Life chances | Opportunities for education, income, health and long life |
| Human Development Index | UN measure combining life expectancy, education and income |
| Capabilities | Real freedoms people have to achieve well-being (Sen) |
| Structural adjustment | Policy conditions attached to international loans |
| User fees | Charges for public services such as schooling or clinics |
| Microfinance | Small loans to poor borrowers |
| Debt relief | Cancellation or reduction of countries' debts |
| Core / semi-periphery / periphery | Wallerstein's positions in the world system |
| Neo-colonialism | Continued external control after formal independence |
| Feminisation of poverty | Women's over-representation among the poor (Pearce) |
| Poverty trap | Conditions that keep poor countries poor (Collier) |
| Brain drain | Emigration of skilled workers |

## Worked essay plan

*"Global inequality is mainly the result of capitalism." Evaluate this view.*

1. Define global inequality and note measurement (Human Development Index, distribution).
2. For: world-systems theory (Wallerstein); dependency (Frank); Marxist critique of structural adjustment.
3. Alternatives: colonialism (Rodney); post-colonialism (Nkrumah); patriarchy (Boserup, Pearce, Mies).
4. Internal explanations: modernisation and Collier's traps.
5. Evidence: fast-growing economies after integration challenge dependency; commodity-dependent countries support it.
6. Judgement: capitalism sets the structure, but colonial history, patriarchy and governance shape outcomes.

## Exam traps

- Treating "developing countries" as one group.
- Using averages without distribution.
- Missing one of the three named areas: education, income, health.
- Calling all transnational organisations failures or successes.
- Listing explanations without comparing causes and solutions.
- Half-remembered statistics; named policies and dated events are safer.

## Self-test

1. What does the Human Development Index combine?
2. Name two countries that abolished primary school fees and the years.
3. Give one criticism of rapid fee abolition.
4. What was the Grameen Bank, and who founded it?
5. Name Collier's four traps.
6. Give one success and one problem in global health.
7. What did Stiglitz criticise in 2002?
8. What was the HIPC initiative?
9. What did Rodney argue in 1972?
10. Define neo-colonialism and name the writer associated with it.
11. What did Boserup show?
12. Define the feminisation of poverty.

### Answers

1. Life expectancy, education and income.
2. Any two: Malawi (1994), Uganda (1997), Kenya (2003).
3. Enrolment outpaced teachers and classrooms, so quality fell.
4. A Bangladeshi bank offering small loans to poor people, especially women; Muhammad Yunus.
5. Conflict, natural resources, being landlocked with poor neighbours, and bad governance.
6. Success: smallpox declared eradicated in 1980 or expanded vaccination through Gavi; problem: patent rules raising medicine prices or brain drain.
7. The IMF's policies and conditions.
8. A 1996 debt-relief initiative for highly indebted poor countries.
9. Colonial rule extracted resources and labour and shaped African economies around exports to Europe.
10. Continued external economic control after formal independence; Kwame Nkrumah.
11. Development projects ignored women's agricultural work and directed resources to men.
12. Women's over-representation among the poor (Pearce, 1978).
