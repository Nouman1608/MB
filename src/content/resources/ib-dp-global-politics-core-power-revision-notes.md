## Core definition

- The guide's starting point: power as an **ability to effect change**.
- Its second view: power as a feature of **relations among people** in a social organisation, not a separate force.
- **Capacity view:** power is held and can be counted (armies, GDP, votes).
- **Relational view:** power exists only in a relationship; it depends on how others respond.
- **Resources are not outcomes.** An actor rich in resources can still fail to get its way.

## The types of power the guide lists

| Type | One-line definition | Guide's example | Another real example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard | Coercion: threats, force, penalties | Military intervention; sanctions | Russia halting gas supplies to Poland and Bulgaria in April 2022 after they refused to pay in roubles |
| Soft | Persuasion and attraction | Culture, ideology, economic aid | Qatar hosting the 2022 FIFA World Cup (critics called it "sportswashing") |
| Smart | Hard and soft combined to fit the aim | Climate approaches; negotiations | Sanctions plus diplomacy before the 2015 Iran nuclear deal |
| Structural | From position in rules or institutions | UNSC membership; heads of state | IMF votes: major decisions need 85%, and the US holds over 15%, so it can block them |
| Relational | From ties between actors | Alliances; civil movements | AUKUS (2021); Black Lives Matter protests in many countries in 2020 |
| Economic | Control of money, trade, supply chains | -- | Export controls on advanced chips |
| Political | Authority to make binding decisions | -- | A legislature passing a law |
| Social | Networks, norms, ability to mobilise | -- | The campaign behind the 1997 Ottawa Treaty banning landmines |
| Cultural | Shaping values and identity | -- | Film, music and language reach |
| Power over | Control of others' actions | -- | A state forcing policy change by sanction |
| Power to | Capacity to act or achieve | -- | A state's ability to run elections |
| Power with | Collective power through cooperation | -- | Small island states jointly pushing for the 1.5°C goal |

The guide gives its own examples only for the first five rows; the others are Marlbridge examples.

## Method in steps

**Classifying power in a case**

```
1. Name the actor, the target and the action (with a year).
2. Ask: what resource is used? (military, money, votes, ideas, people)
3. Ask: does it work by force/penalty or by attraction? -> hard or soft
4. Ask: does it come from a position or from ties? -> structural or relational
5. Ask: over others, to act, or with others? -> over / to / with
6. Note any overlap and say which label fits best, and why.
```

**Evaluating a classification as a tool**

```
1. Strength: what does the label reveal about this case?
2. Limit: what does it hide or blur? (overlap, measurement, hidden power)
3. Perspective: would a realist, a Gramscian or a decolonial thinker read it differently?
4. Judgement: how useful is the label here, on balance?
```

## Worked reminders

- **Sanctions on Russia after 2022:** economic *and* hard power; exercised as power over; given force by coalitions of states (relational). Their effect is debated, because some states kept trading with Russia.
- **Russia's Security Council veto (February 2022):** structural power blocking a resolution. The General Assembly's 141-vote resolution days later shows relational pressure, but it was not binding.
- **Korean Wave:** cultural power producing soft power. The state helps but does not control it.
- **NATO enlargement:** Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joined after the 2022 invasion. Relational power: security comes from the tie created by Article 5, not from their own armies alone.
- **Fridays for Future:** social and relational power, built from numbers and networks. It works as power with its members and aims at power over governments' agendas.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Hard vs soft:** coercion versus attraction. The resource does not decide it; the way it works does. Aid can be either.
- **Soft vs smart:** smart power is a *strategy* that combines tools; soft power is one kind of tool.
- **Structural vs relational:** position in a system versus ties between actors. A permanent seat is structural; an alliance is relational.
- **Power over vs power with:** control of others versus collective capacity. One coalition can be both: power with its members, power over its target.
- **Power to vs power over:** capacity to act need not involve controlling anyone.
- **Lukes' second vs third dimension:** keeping an issue off the agenda versus shaping what people want so they never raise it.
- **Power vs legitimacy:** power is the ability to effect change; legitimacy is whether it is accepted. Keep them apart. See the [legitimacy guide](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-core-legitimacy/) and the [sovereignty guide](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-core-sovereignty/) for the neighbouring core concepts.

## Power in the exam papers

- **Paper 1** (1 hour 15 minutes; 25 marks; 30% SL, 20% HL) is source-based and draws on the core topics. Four sources, four compulsory structured questions. The paper always includes a non-textual source, such as a table or image.
- Early questions test understanding and application: pick out a type of power in a source, or explain a term. Later questions ask you to compare two sources, then evaluate a claim using the sources and your own knowledge.
- **Paper 2** (1 hour 45 minutes; 30 marks) has section B integrating questions that link the thematic studies with the core topics. Power is the natural bridge.
- Key command terms: *identify* and *outline* (AO1); *explain* and *distinguish* (AO2); *compare and contrast*, *evaluate* and *to what extent* (AO3).
- Actors matter: the [stakeholders and systems guide](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-core-stakeholders-and-systems/) covers who holds power in each case.
- Link power to the thematic studies: who can enforce rights in [rights and justice](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-rights-justice/), and how the balance of power shapes war and peace in [peace and conflict](/resources/ib-dp-global-politics-peace-conflict/).

## Quick self-test

1. Give the two views of power in the guide's initial guidance.
2. Which type of power does the guide pair with coercion, and which with persuasion?
3. State two examples the guide gives of structural power.
4. State two examples the guide gives of relational power.
5. Who coined the term "soft power", and in which book?
6. What is Lukes' third dimension of power?
7. Which thinker distinguished "power to" from "power over"?
8. Why can economic aid be classified as either soft or hard power?
9. Classify a coalition of small states pressing for a stricter climate target.
10. Give one limitation of soft power as an analytical tool.
11. According to Arendt, how does violence relate to power?
12. Which type of power does a permanent member use when it casts a veto?

### Answers

1. Power as an ability to effect change; power as an aspect of relations among people in a social organisation.
2. Hard power with coercion; soft power with persuasion.
3. UN Security Council membership; heads of state.
4. Alliances; collective civil movements.
5. Joseph Nye, in *Bound to Lead* (1990).
6. Shaping people's wants and beliefs so that conflicts of interest never surface.
7. Hanna Pitkin.
8. It depends on use and perception: aid given freely attracts (soft); aid with conditions or debt leverage can coerce (hard).
9. Power with among its members (relational); it may also seek power over larger emitters.
10. Any one: hard to measure; not fully controlled by governments; depends on how others see the actor; overlaps with economic power.
11. They are opposites: violence appears where power, the ability to act together, is being lost.
12. Structural power, from its position in the UN Security Council; the veto is also a form of political power.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a type of power with no real example, when the guide says to apply types to cases.
- Giving an example with no actor or date ("some countries used sanctions").
- Calling sanctions soft power because no weapons are used.
- Describing smart power as "hard plus soft" without naming the tools or the aim.
- Treating structural power as purely economic and missing institutional positions.
- Listing thinkers without applying their idea to the source or case in front of you.
- In Paper 1, ignoring the source and writing only from own knowledge.
- Never judging the classification itself, when the guide invites you to weigh its strengths and limits.
- Confusing power with legitimacy or sovereignty; these are separate core concepts.
