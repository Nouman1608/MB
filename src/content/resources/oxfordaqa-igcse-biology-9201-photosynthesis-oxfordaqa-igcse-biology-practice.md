These questions cover section **3.2.1 Photosynthesis** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Biology (9201)
specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered. All data are
invented for practice. A calculator is useful for questions 10, 11 and 12.

## Section A

**1.** State where chlorophyll is found, and describe its role in photosynthesis. **[2]**

**2.** The balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis is shown with two numbers missing, labelled x and y.

x CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + y O₂

Give the values of x and y. **[2]**

**3.**

**(a)** Name the by-product of photosynthesis. **[1]**
**(b)** State where the water used in photosynthesis comes from. **[1]**

**4.** Name **two** substances that a plant makes from glucose for storage. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a growing plant needs to convert some of its glucose into cellulose. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a plant does not photosynthesise at night, even though it still has carbon dioxide and
water. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A student measures the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed at different temperatures. Light intensity and
carbon dioxide concentration are kept high.

| Temperature (°C) | 5 | 15 | 25 | 35 | 45 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate (cm³ oxygen per hour) | 4 | 12 | 22 | 26 | 8 |

**(a)** Describe the effect of temperature on the rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the rate increases from 5 °C to 25 °C. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the rate is lower at 45 °C than at 35 °C. **[2]**
**(d)** Estimate the optimum temperature from these data. **[1]**

**8.** The table shows the rate of photosynthesis (arbitrary units) at different carbon dioxide concentrations, at
high and low light intensity. The temperature is 25 °C.

| CO₂ (%) | 0 | 0.02 | 0.04 | 0.06 | 0.08 | 0.10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate in high light | 0 | 10 | 20 | 28 | 32 | 32 |
| Rate in low light | 0 | 10 | 15 | 15 | 15 | 15 |

**(a)** Name the factor limiting the rate at 0.02% CO₂ in both light intensities. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the factor limiting the rate at 0.08% CO₂ in low light, and explain how the data show this. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest the factor limiting the rate at 0.10% CO₂ in high light. **[1]**

**9.** Sensors record the carbon dioxide concentration in a closed greenhouse.

| Time | 02:00 | 06:00 | 10:00 | 14:00 | 18:00 | 22:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ (ppm) | 560 | 640 | 470 | 260 | 300 | 450 |

**(a)** Explain the fall in carbon dioxide between 06:00 and 14:00. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the rise in carbon dioxide between 18:00 and 06:00. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest the time of day at which adding extra carbon dioxide would be most useful. **[1]**

**10.** A tomato grower can buy one of two heaters. Tomatoes sell for £1.80 per kg.

| Heater | Running cost per week | Extra tomatoes per week |
|---|---|---|
| A: paraffin heater | £50 | 40 kg |
| B: electric heater | £45 | 25 kg |

**(a)** Calculate the net gain or loss per week for each heater. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why heater A increases the yield more than heater B. **[2]**

**11.** A student uses a gas syringe to collect oxygen from pondweed with a lamp at 10 cm. She collects 4.2 cm³ in
6 minutes.

**(a)** Calculate the rate of oxygen production. Give the unit. **[2]**
**(b)** Name **one** variable she must control when she moves the lamp to 20 cm, and say how she could control it.
**[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why she waits five minutes after moving the lamp before starting to collect oxygen. **[1]**

**12.** A plant makes 120 g of glucose in one day. 30% is used in respiration. Half of the remaining glucose is
converted into starch.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of glucose used in respiration. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of glucose converted into starch. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why many seeds store oil rather than glucose. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the plant must also absorb nitrate ions to use some of its glucose for growth. **[1]**

**13.** Explain how light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature interact to control the rate of
photosynthesis, and how a greenhouse grower can use this to make the most profit. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** In chloroplasts, in some plant cells and in algae [1]; it absorbs light, which provides the energy to
convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose [1]

**2.** x = 6 [1]; y = 6 [1]

**3. (a)** Oxygen [1]
**(b)** From the soil (absorbed by the roots) [1]

**4.** Any two of the following, 1 mark each [2]: starch; fat; oil

**5.** Cellulose strengthens the cell wall [1]; a growing plant makes new cells, each needing a cell wall [1]

**6.** There is no light at night [1]; light provides the energy needed to convert carbon dioxide and water into
glucose, so the reaction cannot happen [1]

**7. (a)** The rate increases from 4 to 26 cm³ per hour as temperature rises to 35 °C [1]; it then falls sharply to
8 cm³ per hour at 45 °C [1]
**(b)** Low temperature was limiting the rate; as it rises, the enzyme-controlled reactions go faster [1]
**(c)** At 45 °C the enzymes involved in photosynthesis are damaged (denatured) [1]; so they cannot work, and the
rate falls [1]
**(d)** About 35 °C (accept any value between 30 °C and 40 °C) [1]

**8. (a)** Carbon dioxide concentration [1]
**(b)** Light intensity [1]; increasing CO₂ from 0.04% to 0.08% does not change the rate in low light (15), but
the rate is higher in high light (32) [1]
**(c)** Temperature (or any factor other than light and carbon dioxide) [1]

**9. (a)** Light increases in the morning, so photosynthesis increases [1]; the plants take in carbon dioxide
faster than respiration releases it, so the concentration falls [1]
**(b)** There is no (or little) light, so no photosynthesis [1]; respiration by the plants continues and releases
carbon dioxide, which builds up in the closed greenhouse [1]
**(c)** Around midday or early afternoon (about 14:00), when carbon dioxide is lowest and light is high [1]

**10. (a)** A: 40 × 1.80 = £72; 72 − 50 = **+£22** [1]; B: 25 × 1.80 = £45; 45 − 45 = **£0** (no gain) [1]
**(b)** Heater A raises the temperature and also releases carbon dioxide when the paraffin burns [1]; so two
limiting factors are increased, not just one [1]

**11. (a)** 4.2 ÷ 6 [1] = **0.70 cm³ per minute** [1]
**(b)** Any one variable with a method, 2 marks [2]: temperature, controlled with a water bath or a heat shield
between lamp and plant; carbon dioxide concentration, controlled by using the same concentration of sodium
hydrogencarbonate solution; the same piece of pondweed, kept in the tube throughout
**(c)** To let the plant adjust to the new light intensity, so the rate is steady before measuring [1]

**12. (a)** 0.30 × 120 = **36 g** [1]
**(b)** 120 − 36 = 84 g; 84 ÷ 2 = **42 g** [1]
**(c)** Oil is an energy-rich store and is insoluble, so it does not affect water movement in the seed [1]
**(d)** Proteins contain nitrogen, which glucose does not; nitrate ions supply the nitrogen [1]

**13.** Marking points, 1 mark each [6]:
- the rate may be limited by low temperature, shortage of carbon dioxide or shortage of light
- the factor in shortest supply limits the rate at any moment
- increasing a factor that is not limiting has no effect on the rate
- the limiting factor can change, for example light at dawn and carbon dioxide at midday
- a grower can increase temperature with heaters, carbon dioxide by burning fuel or releasing the gas, and light
  with lamps
- profit increases only if the value of the extra crop is greater than the cost of enhancing the limiting factor

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming all three factors when asked for the one limiting the rate in given conditions.
- Saying high temperature increases the rate without mentioning the fall after the optimum.
- Explaining a carbon dioxide change in a greenhouse without mentioning both photosynthesis and respiration.
- Forgetting the unit in a rate calculation.
- Recommending a greenhouse change without comparing income with cost.
- Leaving out nitrate ions when explaining protein synthesis.
