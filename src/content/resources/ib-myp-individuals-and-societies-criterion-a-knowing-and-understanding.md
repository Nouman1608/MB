This study guide teaches Criterion A: Knowing and understanding in IB MYP Individuals and Societies. It follows the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Individuals and societies guide, for use from September 2014 or January 2015. The subject covers history, geography and economics, so this page uses examples from all three. Criterion A rewards two things: using the right words in the right place, and showing that you understand the content by describing it, explaining it and giving examples.

## What the guide says about Criterion A

The objective is "Knowing and understanding". Students develop factual and conceptual knowledge about individuals and societies. At the end of Year 5 the two strands are:

1. **Use** a wide range of terminology in context.
2. **Demonstrate** knowledge and understanding of subject-specific content and concepts through developed descriptions, explanations and examples.

Criterion A has a maximum of 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8. The command term "demonstrate" means to make clear by reasoning or evidence, illustrating with examples or practical application.

## How the Year 5 bands change

| Band | Strand i: terminology | Strand ii: knowledge and understanding |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | uses limited relevant terminology | basic knowledge with minimal descriptions and/or examples |
| 3-4 | uses some terminology accurately and appropriately | adequate knowledge through satisfactory descriptions, explanations and examples |
| 5-6 | uses a range of terminology accurately and appropriately | substantial knowledge through accurate descriptions, explanations and examples |
| 7-8 | consistently uses a wide range of terminology effectively | excellent knowledge through thorough, accurate descriptions, explanations and examples |

In Year 3, the same pattern uses "a range of terminology" and "developed and accurate" descriptions, explanations and examples at the top band. Ask your teacher which year's descriptors apply to your task.

The words that move you up a band are **range** (how many relevant terms), **accuracy** (are they correct), **appropriateness** (are they in the right context) and **thorough** (how much detail the examples carry).

## Command terms and what they ask for

The guide gives a list of MYP command terms for the subject. These are the ones used most for Criterion A.

| Command term | Meaning in the guide | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| Identify | provide an answer from a number of possibilities; state briefly a distinguishing fact or feature | one short point |
| List | give a sequence of brief answers with no explanation | words or short phrases |
| Describe | give a detailed account or picture of a situation, event, pattern or process | what, where, when, how much |
| Explain | give a detailed account including reasons or causes | what and why |
| Demonstrate | make clear by reasoning or evidence, illustrating with examples or practical application | idea plus example |
| Use | apply knowledge or rules to put theory into practice | apply the idea to the case |
| Summarize | abstract a general theme or major point(s) | the main idea only |
| Exemplify (glossary) | represent with an example | a specific case |

## Strand i: using terminology in context

A subject term is a word that has a specific meaning in history, geography or economics. Using terminology in context means using it **correctly**, in a sentence where it fits, and showing by the sentence that you know what it means.

Examples of terms by discipline:

- **Geography**: urbanisation, migration, push and pull factors, natural increase, sustainable management, globalisation, scale, process.
- **History**: causality (cause and consequence), conflict, cooperation, ideology, innovation, perspective, significance, industrialisation.
- **Economics**: scarcity, demand, supply, opportunity cost, market, producer, consumer, inflation.

The guide lists related concepts such as power, processes, scale and sustainability for geography, and causality, conflict, cooperation, culture, governance, identity, ideology, innovation and revolution, interdependence, perspective and significance for history. Learning them well gives you a ready-made vocabulary.

**Using a term well.**

- Weak: "Lots of people moved to cities." (no term)
- Better: "Rural to urban migration increased the population of cities." (term used)
- Best: "Rural to urban migration, driven by push factors such as unemployment on farms and pull factors such as factory jobs, caused rapid urbanisation." (several terms, each used accurately and linked)

**A tip.** Do not drop terms into a sentence to impress. A term that is used wrongly lowers your accuracy. If you are unsure, use a simpler term correctly.

## Strand ii: descriptions, explanations and examples

A top response combines all three.

- **Description**: what is happening, with detail (names, places, dates, numbers).
- **Explanation**: why it happens, with a reason or cause.
- **Example**: a specific case that shows the idea.

A good pattern is **point, detail, reason, example** (PDRE).

### Worked example 1 (geography): urbanisation

*Explain what is meant by urbanisation and why it happens, using an example.*

- **Point**: urbanisation is the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in towns and cities.
- **Detail**: it happens when the urban population grows faster than the rural population, so the urban share rises.
- **Reason**: people migrate to cities because of pull factors such as jobs, schools and hospitals, and because of push factors in the countryside such as low incomes, shortage of land or poor services. Cities also grow by natural increase, because most migrants are young adults who have children.
- **Example**: in a rapidly urbanising country such as Brazil during the second half of the twentieth century, many people moved from rural areas in the north-east to the large cities in the south-east in search of work.

Notice the terms: urbanisation, migration, push and pull factors, natural increase. Each is used in the right place.

### Worked example 2 (calculation): natural increase

A country has a birth rate of 18 per 1000 people and a death rate of 6 per 1000 people each year. Calculate the natural increase.

1. Natural increase = birth rate − death rate.
2. 18 − 6 = 12 per 1000.
3. As a percentage: 12 ÷ 1000 × 100 = 1.2% per year.

A number on its own is not enough for the upper bands. Add what it means: "This is a moderate rate of growth, which means the population is increasing even before migration is counted."

### Worked example 3 (history): industrialisation and cities

*Describe and explain the effects of industrialisation on towns in nineteenth-century Britain.*

- **Description**: towns with factories, such as Manchester, grew very quickly. Workers lived in crowded housing close to the mills, and many houses were built close together with little sanitation.
- **Explanation**: factories needed large numbers of workers, and people moved from the countryside for jobs. Because cities grew faster than councils could build and regulate housing, overcrowding and disease spread (cause and consequence).
- **Example**: outbreaks of cholera in industrial towns led to public health laws that required cleaner water and drains.

Here the key terms are industrialisation, migration, urbanisation and cause and consequence. The example supports the explanation.

### Worked example 4 (economics): demand and price

*Explain why the price of ice cream tends to rise in a heatwave.*

- **Point**: in a heatwave, demand for ice cream increases.
- **Reason**: more consumers want to buy ice cream at each price. If the quantity supplied cannot increase quickly, because production takes time, there is more demand than supply at the old price, so producers can raise the price until the quantity demanded matches the quantity supplied.
- **Example**: a seller at a beach with a fixed amount in the freezer might raise the price after the first hour, when the queue is long.

You can see how scarcity, demand, supply and price work together in a few sentences.

## Making examples work

An example is only useful if it is **specific** (a named place, event, person or number) and **linked** to the point. "For example, in Tokyo..." is stronger than "for example, in some countries...". After the example, add a clause that says how it shows the idea.

Real cases from your course and from the sources in a task are the best examples. Build a bank of two examples for each major concept.

## How Criterion A is examined

In the MYP on-screen examination for individuals and societies, the tasks are Investigating, Communicating and Thinking critically. Criterion A is assessed in all three: 6 marks in Investigating, 6 in Communicating and 8 in Thinking critically, which makes 20 marks out of 80 for the whole examination. The guide says students are asked to demonstrate knowledge and understanding either from their course or from information presented in the source material. The sources can include texts, images, maps, graphs, data tables, charts and videos. Examinable topics are listed separately for geography, history, economics and integrated humanities.

## Common errors

- Using a term with the wrong meaning (for example mixing up migration and urbanisation).
- Using a term without showing you know what it means.
- Describing without explaining, so there is no reason or cause.
- General examples ("some countries") instead of specific ones.
- Facts that are correct but irrelevant to the question.
- A list of terms with no sentences that use them.
- Stating a number with no unit or meaning.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Criterion A in IB MYP Individuals and Societies?**
Criterion A is Knowing and understanding. It assesses your use of subject terminology in context and your knowledge and understanding of content and concepts through descriptions, explanations and examples.

**How is Criterion A different from Criterion D?**
Criterion A is about what you know and can explain. Criterion D (Thinking critically) is about discussing, synthesising and evaluating, including judging sources and perspectives. A strong critical answer also needs strong knowledge.

**How many marks is Criterion A worth in the on-screen examination?**
Criterion A is assessed in all three tasks, for 6 + 6 + 8 = 20 marks out of 80. The guide says the distribution may vary by no more than three marks.

**Do I need to learn a list of terms?**
Learn the key terms for each unit and use them in your own sentences. The examination topics are listed in the guide, and the terms come from them.
