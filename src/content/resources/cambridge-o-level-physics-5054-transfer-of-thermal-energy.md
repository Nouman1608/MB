This guide covers subtopic **2.3 Transfer of thermal energy** from Topic 2, Thermal physics, in
Cambridge O Level Physics 5054 (2026–2028 series): 2.3.1 Conduction, 2.3.2 Convection, 2.3.3
Radiation and 2.3.4 Consequences of thermal energy transfer. The
[thermal physics topic guide](/resources/o-level-physics-thermal-physics/) summarises the three
mechanisms. This page goes further into the experiments the syllabus asks you to describe, how
to make them fair, how to interpret results, and how to explain the four named applications.

## Conduction

**Mechanism 1: lattice vibrations (all solids).** Particles at the hot end vibrate with more
energy. Because they are bonded to their neighbours, they make the neighbouring particles vibrate
more, and energy is passed along the lattice from particle to particle. This is slow.

**Mechanism 2: free electrons (metals).** Metals also contain free (delocalised) electrons that
move through the whole structure. At the hot end they gain kinetic energy and travel quickly to
cooler parts, colliding with particles there and transferring energy. This is why metals are
much better conductors than non-metals.

Liquids are poor conductors and gases are very poor conductors, because their particles are
further apart. Trapped air is the basis of most insulating materials.

**Experiment: comparing conductors**

1. Take rods of different materials (for example copper, aluminium, steel and glass) of the
   **same length and diameter**.
2. Attach a small drawing pin to the far end of each with a blob of wax, or coat each rod with a
   thin layer of wax.
3. Heat the near ends **equally**, for example by placing them together in the same flame or in a
   beaker of boiling water.
4. Record the order in which the pins fall, or how far along each rod the wax melts in a fixed
   time.

The best conductor drops its pin first. Fair-test points: same dimensions, same heating, same
amount of wax, same starting temperature.

## Convection

Convection happens in **liquids and gases**, which can flow.

1. The fluid near the heat source is heated; its particles move faster and further apart.
2. The fluid **expands**, so its **density decreases**.
3. The less dense, warmer fluid **rises**; cooler, denser fluid **sinks** to take its place.
4. A continuous circulation, a **convection current**, forms.

Convection cannot happen in a solid because the particles cannot move from place to place.

**Experiment: convection in water.** Fill a beaker with cold water and drop a single crystal of
potassium manganate(VII) (or a few drops of food dye) at the bottom near one side. Heat gently
directly beneath the crystal. A coloured stream rises above the heat, moves across the top,
sinks on the far side and returns along the bottom, tracing the convection current.

**Experiment: convection in air.** Hold a smouldering splint or a strip of light paper above a
lit candle or a heater: the smoke or paper rises in the warm current of air above it.

## Infrared radiation

All objects emit **infrared radiation**, a part of the electromagnetic spectrum. Unlike conduction
and convection, it **does not need a medium**, so it can travel through a vacuum. This is how
energy from the Sun reaches the Earth.

**Surface effects**

| Surface | Emission | Absorption | Reflection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dull black | Best emitter | Best absorber | Poor reflector |
| Shiny white or silver | Poorest emitter | Poorest absorber | Good reflector |

A useful rule: a good absorber is also a good emitter.

**Rate of emission.** An object emits infrared radiation faster when its **surface temperature is
higher** and when its **surface area is larger**. This is why cooling fins and radiators have a
large area.

**Experiment: good and bad emitters.** Use a metal cube whose four vertical faces have different
surfaces: dull black, shiny black, dull white and shiny silver (a Leslie cube). Fill it with hot
water, so all faces are at the same temperature. Place an infrared detector (or a thermometer with
a blackened bulb) at the **same distance** from each face in turn and record the reading. The dull
black face gives the highest reading.

**Experiment: good and bad absorbers.** Place two identical metal plates, one painted dull black
and one shiny silver, at **equal distances** on either side of a radiant heater. Fix a drawing pin
to the back of each with wax. Switch on the heater: the wax on the dull black plate melts first
and its pin falls first, so it is the better absorber.

## Interpreting results (illustrative data)

Two identical cans, one dull black and one shiny silver, each hold 200 g of water at 80 °C. After
10 minutes the black can is at 62 °C and the silver can at 71 °C.

```
temperature drop: black = 80 − 62 = 18 °C;  silver = 80 − 71 = 9 °C
```

The black can lost about twice as much temperature: dull black surfaces are better emitters.
For a fair comparison, the cans need the same size, the same volume of water, the same starting
temperature and the same surroundings, and lids so that evaporation from the surface does not
dominate.

## Everyday applications

**Kitchen pans.** The base is a metal such as aluminium or copper, a good conductor, so energy
passes quickly from the hob through the base to the food by conduction (lattice vibrations and
free electrons). The handle is plastic or wood, a poor conductor, so it stays cool to touch.

**Heating a room by convection.** A heater is placed **low** in a room. Air next to it is warmed,
expands, becomes less dense and rises. Cooler, denser air from the rest of the room sinks and
flows towards the heater, so a convection current circulates warm air around the whole room.

**Infrared thermometer.** Every object emits infrared radiation, and a hotter surface emits it at
a greater rate. An infrared thermometer detects the radiation from a surface and converts the
rate of emission into a temperature reading, so it can measure temperature without touching the
object (for example a person's forehead or a hot engine part).

**Keeping a liquid hot or cold: the vacuum flask**

- A **vacuum** between double walls stops conduction and convection, which need a medium.
- **Silvered** inner surfaces reflect infrared radiation back towards the liquid.
- An insulating **stopper** reduces conduction and stops convection and evaporation at the top.
- Insulated supports hold the inner container with little contact.

**Reducing energy transfer in buildings:** loft insulation and cavity wall foam trap air, a poor
conductor, in small pockets so convection currents cannot form; double glazing has a gap between
panes that reduces conduction; shiny foil reflects infrared radiation.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "heat rises". It is the **warmer, less dense fluid** that rises.
- Saying convection happens in solids.
- Explaining conduction in metals without the **free electrons**.
- Saying a vacuum stops radiation. It stops conduction and convection only.
- Writing "black absorbs heat" without naming **infrared radiation**.
- Forgetting fair-test controls such as equal distance from the heater or detector.

## Exam technique for command words

- **Describe** (an experiment): apparatus, what is kept the same, what is measured, the expected
  result.
- **Explain** (an application): name the mechanism, then link it to the design feature.
- **State**: a short fact, such as "radiation does not require a medium".

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a vacuum a good insulator?** There are no particles, so neither conduction nor
convection can occur; only radiation crosses, which silvering reduces.

**Why are radiators often painted white if black emits better?** A radiator transfers much of its
energy to a room by convection, so its colour matters less than its position and area.

**Does an infrared thermometer send out radiation?** No. It detects infrared radiation emitted by
the object.
