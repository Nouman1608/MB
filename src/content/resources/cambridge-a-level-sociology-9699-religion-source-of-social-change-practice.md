This set covers section 11.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) Paper 4: Weber's
theory, alternative views on religion and capitalism, liberation theology, and religious movements in
political struggles. Section A tests concepts; Section B asks for developed explanations and essays.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *asceticism*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how the doctrine of predestination led to salvation anxiety. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what Weber meant by the *spirit of capitalism*. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what Weber meant by an *elective affinity* between Calvinism and capitalism. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** Marxist criticism of Weber's theory. **[3]**

**6.** Identify **two** features of liberation theology. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** reason why liberation theology declined. **[3]**

**8.** Explain what Khomeini meant by *velayat-e faqih*. **[2]**

## Section B

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which liberation theology challenges Marxist views of religion.
**[8]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which the Evangelical movement has influenced US politics. **[8]**

**11.** "Weber's theory provides a convincing account of the role of religion in the rise of
capitalism." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "Religious movements have been a powerful force for political change." Evaluate this view
using case studies. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** Self-discipline and self-denial [1], rejecting luxury and pleasure, so that, for Calvinists,
profits were reinvested rather than spent [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Calvinists believed God had already decided who would be saved [1]. Believers could not know
whether they were among the elect [1], and nothing they did could change it, creating intense
psychological strain [1]. **[3]**

**3.** A rational, disciplined way of life devoted to making profit [1] for reinvestment rather than
enjoyment, illustrated by Franklin's maxim "time is money" [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A fit between Calvinist ideas and the interests and outlook of capitalism [1] in which each
reinforced the other [1]; Weber did not claim Calvinism alone caused capitalism, but that together
with economic conditions it helped it emerge [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Identify [1], explain [1], develop [1]: Kautsky argued that capitalism predated Calvinism [1],
so economic change came first [1], and early capitalists were attracted to Calvinism because it
justified what they were already doing [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Any two: preferential option for the poor [1]; base ecclesial communities [1]; reading the
Bible in relation to poverty and oppression [1]; opposition to military dictatorships [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Identify [1], explain [1], develop [1]: Vatican opposition [1] — from the 1980s the Vatican
criticised the movement's Marxist elements [1] and more conservative bishops were appointed,
reducing support within the Church [1]. Also creditable: democratisation; growth of
Pentecostalism. **[3]**

**8.** Guardianship of the Islamic jurist [1]: the doctrine that a senior religious scholar should
hold political authority [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop with evidence [1], evaluate [1].
- **Religion opposing the ruling class** [1]: Marx saw religion as legitimating inequality, but
  liberation theology sided with the poor [1]; clergy opposed military regimes, and Archbishop
  Romero was assassinated in 1980 [1]; this shows religion can be revolutionary [1].
- **Relative autonomy of religion** [1]: liberation theology shows that religious ideas are not
  simply determined by the economy [1]; Maduro (1982) argued clergy can side with the oppressed [1];
  Löwy (1996) described a movement combining Christian and Marxist ideas [1].
- Also creditable: base ecclesial communities developing working-class consciousness, similar to
  Gramsci's organic intellectuals. **[8]**

**10.** Up to 4 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop with evidence [1], evaluate [1].
- **Electoral mobilisation** [1]: the Moral Majority (1979) and Christian Coalition (1989)
  organised conservative Protestants as voters [1], making issues such as abortion and school prayer
  central to campaigns [1]; Bruce (2003) argued this gained attention more than policy change [1].
- **Long-term legal change** [1]: decades of campaigning against *Roe v. Wade* (1973) [1], working
  through elections and judicial appointments, ended with the *Dobbs* decision (2022) [1]; this
  suggests greater influence than Bruce allowed [1].
- Also creditable: shaping debates on same-sex marriage and family values. **[8]**

**11.** Marked in levels. The top level explains Weber's argument accurately, uses alternative views
and evidence, and reaches a judgement. Indicative content:
- **Weber:** predestination, salvation anxiety, the calling, asceticism, spirit of capitalism,
  elective affinity; comparative studies of China and India; verstehen.
- **Support:** Weber combines structure and meaning; Martin (1990) on Pentecostalism in Latin
  America as a modern parallel; Marshall (1980) found a Calvinist ethic in Scotland.
- **Criticism:** Tawney (1926) — economic change first; Kautsky — capitalism predated Calvinism;
  capitalism in some Catholic regions; Marshall — lack of capital and skilled labour slowed Scottish
  development, so religion was not sufficient.
- **Evaluation:** Weber himself did not claim religion was the sole cause; critics sometimes attack a
  stronger claim than he made.
- **Judgement:** for example, convincing as an account of one contributing cause, not of capitalism's
  origins as a whole. **[35]**

**12.** Marked in levels. The top level uses at least one syllabus case study in depth and weighs
evidence of change against evidence of limited or conservative influence. Indicative content:
- **Iran:** opposition to the Shah's modernisation and White Revolution (1963); Khomeini's
  velayat-e faqih; protests in 1978; the Shah's departure and Khomeini's return in 1979; referendum
  creating the Islamic Republic — religion as a revolutionary force.
- **US Evangelicals:** Moral Majority (1979); Christian Coalition (1989); *Roe v. Wade* (1973) and
  *Dobbs* (2022); Bruce (2003) on limited success in a pluralist system.
- **Liberation theology:** Medellín (1968); Gutiérrez (1971); opposition to dictatorships; later
  decline.
- **Other examples:** US civil rights movement and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference
  (1957); the Catholic Church and Solidarity in Poland.
- **Against:** functionalist and Marxist views of religion as conservative (11.2); religious
  movements often succeed only when economic and political conditions favour them.
- **Judgement:** for example, religious movements can be powerful when they combine with wider
  discontent, but their influence varies by society. **[35]**
