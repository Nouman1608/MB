These notes condense section **3.3.6.3 Infrared spectroscopy** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Infrared spectroscopy study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-infrared-spectroscopy/), and exam-style questions are in the [Infrared spectroscopy practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-infrared-spectroscopy-practice/). The previous sub-topic is in the [Mass spectrometry revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-mass-spectrometry-revision-notes/).

## What you must be able to do

- State that bonds absorb **infrared radiation** at **characteristic wavenumbers**.
- Use **infrared spectra** and the **Chemistry Data Sheet or Booklet** to identify bonds and functional groups, and to identify **impurities**.
- Explain **fingerprinting**.
- Link the absorption of infrared radiation by **CO₂, methane and water vapour** to **global warming**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Infrared radiation | Electromagnetic radiation that makes bonds vibrate |
| Wavenumber | Number of waves per centimetre, in cm⁻¹ |
| Transmittance | The percentage of radiation that passes through the sample |
| Fingerprint region | The region below about 1500 cm⁻¹ that is unique to each compound |
| Impurity | An unwanted substance in a sample |
| Greenhouse gas | A gas whose bonds absorb infrared radiation from the Earth |

## How it works

- Bonds **stretch and bend** with natural frequencies.
- A bond absorbs when the infrared frequency **matches** its vibration.
- Each type of bond absorbs at its own **characteristic wavenumber**.
- The spectrum shows **troughs** where radiation is absorbed (low transmittance).
- Infrared makes bonds **vibrate more**; it does not break them.

Wavenumber (cm⁻¹) = 10 000 ÷ wavelength (µm). For 5.00 µm, wavenumber = 2000 cm⁻¹.

## Typical absorptions (approximate; use the Data Booklet)

| Bond | Wavenumber / cm⁻¹ | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| O–H alcohol | 3230 to 3550 | Broad |
| N–H | 3300 to 3500 | Medium |
| C–H | 2850 to 3100 | Almost always present |
| O–H carboxylic acid | 2500 to 3000 | Very broad |
| C≡N | 2220 to 2260 | Sharp |
| C=O | 1680 to 1750 | Strong, sharp |
| C=C | 1620 to 1680 | Weak to medium |
| C–O | 1000 to 1300 | Strong |

## Reading a spectrum

1. Broad O–H above 3000? Alcohol or (very broad, lower) acid.
2. Strong C=O near 1700? Carbonyl compound.
3. Both? Carboxylic acid.
4. C=O and C–O but no O–H? Ester.
5. Sharp peak near 2240? Nitrile.
6. N–H pair near 3300 to 3500? Amine or amide.

## Quick identification table

| Spectrum shows | Compound type |
|---|---|
| Broad ~3350, no C=O | Alcohol |
| C=O ~1715, no O–H | Aldehyde or ketone |
| Very broad 2500 to 3000 + C=O | Carboxylic acid |
| C=O ~1740 + C–O ~1240, no O–H | Ester |
| ~2240 | Nitrile |

C=O alone cannot separate aldehyde from ketone, or ester from acid. Use the other peaks and other tests.

## Fingerprinting

- The region below about 1500 cm⁻¹ is **different for every compound**.
- Compare the sample spectrum with a **database**.
- A match across the whole spectrum identifies the compound.

## Impurities

An impurity gives an **extra absorption** that the pure compound would not have.

| Pure compound | Extra absorption | Impurity suggested |
|---|---|---|
| Ketone | Broad ~3300 | Alcohol (starting material) or water |
| Alcohol | C=O ~1710 | Aldehyde or ketone from oxidation |
| Ester | Very broad 2500 to 3000 | Carboxylic acid |
| Aldehyde | Very broad 2500 to 3000 plus C=O | Carboxylic acid from over-oxidation |

## Global warming

1. The Earth emits **infrared radiation**.
2. **CO₂** (C=O), **methane** (C–H) and **water vapour** (O–H) have bonds that **absorb** it.
3. The bonds vibrate more; energy is passed on or re-emitted, including back towards the surface.
4. Less energy escapes to space, so the lower atmosphere warms: the **greenhouse effect**.
5. More of these gases means a stronger effect: **global warming**.

N₂ and O₂ do not absorb because their vibration produces no change in dipole.

## Combining data

| Data | Gives |
|---|---|
| Mass spectrometry | Molecular formula |
| Infrared spectroscopy | Functional groups |
| Test-tube reactions | Confirm groups |

Example: formula C₃H₆O₂ with broad 2500 to 3000 and C=O 1710 gives propanoic acid.

## Command words

| Command word | What to write |
|---|---|
| Identify | The bond, the wavenumber and the group |
| Explain | The link between absorption and bond vibration |
| Suggest | A structure consistent with the data |

For a combined-data question, use mass spectrometry for the formula and infrared for the groups.

## Exam traps

- Reading the spectrum upside down: absorptions are troughs.
- Alcohol O–H and acid O–H confused.
- A C=O peak taken to prove a ketone.
- Relying on the C–H region to identify a group.
- Fingerprint region used to find functional groups, not to match spectra.
- "Infrared breaks the bonds."
- Nitrogen or oxygen named as greenhouse gases.
- Quoting wavenumbers with no bond named.

## Self-test

1. What happens to a bond when it absorbs infrared radiation?
2. What is the unit of wavenumber?
3. Convert 4.00 µm to a wavenumber.
4. Which bond absorbs strongly at about 1700 cm⁻¹?
5. How does the O–H absorption in a carboxylic acid differ from that in an alcohol?
6. What does a compound with C=O at 1740 cm⁻¹, C–O at 1240 cm⁻¹ and no O–H contain?
7. What is the fingerprint region used for?
8. A purified ketone shows a broad absorption near 3300 cm⁻¹. What does this suggest?
9. Name the bonds in CO₂, methane and water vapour that absorb infrared radiation.
10. Explain why this leads to global warming.
11. Why do nitrogen and oxygen not absorb?
12. What technique would you add to infrared to find a molecular formula?

### Answers

1. It vibrates more strongly.
2. cm⁻¹.
3. 10 000 ÷ 4.00 = 2500 cm⁻¹.
4. C=O.
5. The acid O–H is much broader and lower, 2500 to 3000, overlapping C–H; the alcohol O–H is near 3200 to 3550.
6. An ester.
7. To match the whole spectrum against a database and confirm identity.
8. An O–H impurity, such as the alcohol it was made from, or water.
9. C=O, C–H and O–H respectively.
10. They absorb infrared radiation emitted by the Earth and pass energy to the atmosphere or re-emit it, so less escapes to space.
11. Their diatomic molecules have no dipole change on vibration.
12. Mass spectrometry.
