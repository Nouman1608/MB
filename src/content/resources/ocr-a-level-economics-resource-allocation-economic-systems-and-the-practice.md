## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by resource allocation. **[3]**

**2.** A rail operator in Country A (illustrative) raises the peak fare from £8.00 to £9.20 to encourage passengers to travel off-peak. Peak journeys are 20,000 a day. PED for peak travel is −0.4.

**(a)** Calculate the new number of peak journeys. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in daily peak revenue. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the incentive changes behaviour only a little. **[2]**

**3.** In Country G (illustrative) demand for flour is Qd = 900 − 30P and supply is Qs = 300 + 20P, with quantity in tonnes and price in £.

**(a)** Work out the equilibrium price and the equilibrium quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Planners fix the price at £9. Calculate the shortage. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how a market would remove the shortage. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how a market economy would respond to a rise in demand for electric bicycles. **[4]**

**5.** Country D (illustrative) has government spending of £306bn in a GDP of £600bn. Country E has £245bn in a GDP of £700bn.

**(a)** Calculate government spending as a percentage of GDP in each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the figures alone do not show how resources are allocated in each. **[2]**

**6.** Evaluate whether a cash bonus is the most effective way for a firm to raise the output of its workers. **[8]**

**7.** A consumer has £12. Books cost £4 each and snacks cost £2 each. The marginal utility (MU) of each unit, in utils, is:

| Unit | Books | Snacks |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 40 | 18 |
| 2nd | 32 | 14 |
| 3rd | 24 | 10 |

**(a)** Calculate MU per £ for the first two units of each good. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the combination that maximises utility. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the total utility. **[1]**
**(d)** Show that three books and no snacks give less utility. **[1]**

**8.** A firm in Country H (illustrative) faces the demand and cost figures below. Marginal cost is a constant £16 a unit.

| Q | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price (£) | 72 | 64 | 56 | 48 | 40 | 32 | 24 | 16 |
| Total cost (£) | 96 | 112 | 128 | 144 | 160 | 176 | 192 | 208 |

**(a)** Calculate marginal revenue for output of 4 to 8 units. **[2]**
**(b)** State the output, price and profit when profit is at its greatest. **[3]**
**(c)** Find the revenue-maximising output and the profit made. **[2]**
**(d)** Find the output that maximises sales volume without making a loss. **[2]**
**(e)** Suggest why a manager paid on sales may prefer this to the answer in (b). **[1]**

**9.** Evaluate whether a mixed economy allocates resources better than a purely market economy. **[10]**

**10.** Explain two possible objectives of a government and one conflict between them. **[6]**

**11.** Explain two problems a planned economy faces in allocating resources. **[4]**

**12.** Identify two possible objectives of a worker and two of a firm other than profit maximisation. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Resource allocation is the way scarce resources are shared out [1] between competing uses [1]. It covers what is made, which methods are used and who receives the output [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that mentions scarcity but not the choice between uses is only half an answer. Naming the questions it answers shows you understand the term.

**2. (a)** % change in price = (9.20 − 8.00) ÷ 8.00 × 100 = +15% [1]. % change in quantity = −0.4 × 15 = −6% [1]. New journeys = 20,000 × 0.94 = **18,800** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Before: £8.00 × 20,000 = £160,000 [1]. After: £9.20 × 18,800 = £172,960, a rise of **£12,960** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Demand is inelastic, so quantity falls proportionately less than price rises [1]. Peak travellers have few substitutes, such as fixed work hours, so few change their time [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the percentage change in quantity before applying it. A final answer on its own gives a marker nothing to follow. In (c), tie the explanation to the elasticity figure given.

**3. (a)** 900 − 30P = 300 + 20P gives 600 = 50P, so P = **£12** [1]. Q = 900 − 360 = **540 tonnes** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At £9, Qd = 900 − 270 = 630 and Qs = 300 + 180 = 480 [1]. Shortage = 630 − 480 = **150 tonnes** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Buyers compete, so the price rises [1]. The higher price and profit give firms an incentive to supply more, and some buyers drop out, until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at £12 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Substitute the price into both equations. Using only the demand equation gives the wrong gap. In (c), a chain of cause and effect, not just the word "equilibrium", shows how the market works.

**4.** Demand rises, so the price of electric bicycles rises [1]. Higher price and profit signal that the good is wanted [1]. Firms produce more and bid for land, labour and capital, drawing them from other uses [1]. Resources are reallocated until the shortage ends and price settles [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain with each link explained gains more than a list of the words "signal" and "incentive". Keep the example in view through the answer.

**5. (a)** Country D: 306 ÷ 600 × 100 = **51%** [1]. Country E: 245 ÷ 700 × 100 = **35%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Spending shows only how much the state spends, not who decides what is produced [1]. A country with a lower share could still regulate or direct much of its private sector, so the mix of ownership and control matters too [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division. In (b), a developed point about control of resources earns more than saying "it depends".

**6.** A bonus is a financial reward linked to output [1]. It raises the pay-off from extra effort, so workers produce more [1]. It works best where output is easy to measure and workers respond to money [1]. Other motives, such as job satisfaction, recognition or a sense of fairness, may matter as much [1]. Paying by output can push workers to cut quality or safety [1]. The bonus is a cost to the firm and must be less than the extra revenue [1]. The effect may fade over time as the bonus becomes expected [1]. Overall, a bonus is likely to be effective for measurable tasks, but combining it with non-financial rewards and quality checks is likely to work better [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* For "evaluate", set out reasons for and against, then reach a judgement that depends on the context. A one-sided list of what a bonus does leaves the question "most effective" unanswered.

**7. (a)** MU per £: books 10 and 8; snacks 9 and 7 [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Rank by MU per £: book 10, snack 9, book 8, snack 7 [1]. Spend: £4 + £2 + £4 + £2 = £12 [1]. Buy **2 books and 2 snacks** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** 40 + 32 + 18 + 14 = **104 utils** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** 40 + 32 + 24 = 96, which is below 104 [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide MU by price before comparing goods with different prices. Comparing raw MU figures picks the wrong good.

**8. (a)** TR = 256, 280, 288, 280, 256 for Q of 4 to 8 [1]. MR: 40 (4th), 24 (5th), 8 (6th), −8 (7th), −24 (8th) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Produce while MR ≥ MC. The 5th unit has MR 24, above 16. The 6th has MR 8, below 16 [1]. Output is **5**, and price is **£56** [1]. Profit = 280 − 128 = **£152** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Revenue is highest at Q = 6, where TR is £288 (MR is still positive and then turns negative) [1]. Profit = 288 − 144 = **£144** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Profit at Q = 9 is 216 − 192 = £24 [1]. At Q = 10, profit is 160 − 208 = −£48, so volume is maximised at **9 units** [1]. **[2]**
**(e)** Pay or status may rise with sales, so the manager gains from more sales even though profit falls [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the rule that matches the objective: MR against MC for profit, MR above zero for revenue. State the output, the price and the profit separately when all three are asked.

**9.** A mixed economy combines the price mechanism with government action [1]. Markets send price signals and reward innovation and cost cutting [1]. They fail to provide public goods and ignore external costs [1]. The government can provide, tax, subsidise or regulate to correct these [1]. It can redistribute income to reduce inequality [1]. But governments can fail, with poor information, lobbying or high costs [1]. Government failure can leave resources worse allocated than the market did [1]. Higher taxes can also reduce incentives to work and invest [1]. The result depends on the quality of institutions and on how large and well-targeted the state role is [1]. Overall, a mixed economy is likely to allocate better where the market fails badly and the government is competent, while a minimal role suits well-functioning markets [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the context, such as the type of good, in the evaluation. The weaker answer lists market failure and stops, without weighing it against government failure.

**10.** One objective is economic growth [1]. It raises output and incomes, which can lift living standards [1]. A second is stable prices [1]. Low inflation protects the value of savings and aids planning [1]. A conflict is that fast growth can push up demand and inflation [1], so the government may have to slow growth to meet its price target [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the objectives, then say why each matters. The conflict must link the two chosen objectives, not some other pair.

**11.** Planners lack full information about what people want and about costs [1], so they may set targets that produce shortages and surpluses [1]. Without profit and prices, workers and managers have weaker incentives to work hard, cut costs or innovate [1], so output may be wasteful and of low quality [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each problem should come with its consequence for allocation. A bare list of problems, with no result, earns less.

**12.** Worker: higher wages [1], job security or satisfaction [1]. Firm: sales revenue or volume maximisation, or growth [1], satisficing or social and ethical goals [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Pick objectives that differ from each other and from the one you were told to exclude. Repeating profit in other words gains nothing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining resource allocation without scarcity or the three questions.
- Quoting a quantity change without first working out the percentage change in price.
- Using only one of the two equations when finding a shortage.
- Comparing raw marginal utility figures when prices differ.
- Using the profit rule for a revenue or volume objective.
- Describing a planned economy as having no prices.
- Listing market and government failures with no weighing.
- Concluding without a judgement that depends on the context.
