## 3.2.3.1 Sources of finance

**Internal:** retained profits, sale of assets, sale and leaseback, working capital.
**External:** trade credit, share capital, overdraft, loans, business angels, private equity, crowd funding.

| Source | One key plus | One key minus |
|---|---|---|
| Retained profits | No interest or dilution | Needs past profit; less for dividends |
| Sale of assets | Cash from idle assets | Asset lost; may sell cheaply |
| Sale and leaseback | Large sum, keep using asset | Ongoing lease payments |
| Working capital | Frees cash tied up in inventory or receivables | Small sums; risk of stock-outs |
| Trade credit | Free short-term finance | Supplier relations; lost discounts |
| Share capital | Permanent, no interest | Dilutes ownership and control |
| Overdraft | Flexible, short-term | Expensive; repayable on demand |
| Loans | Planned repayments | Interest and security |
| Business angels | Money plus expertise | Give up equity |
| Private equity | Large sums | Loss of control; pressure for returns |
| Crowd funding | Many backers; tests demand | May fail to reach target; idea exposed |

**Method: choosing a source.**
1. How much? Small sums rule out share issues and private equity.
2. How long? Short-term need → short-term source (overdraft, trade credit). Long-life asset → loan or equity.
3. Can it be repaid? Uncertain cash flow favours equity over debt.
4. Control? Owners who want control avoid share capital, angels and private equity.
5. What is available? A start-up has no retained profit and no trading record.

## 3.2.3.2 Break-even analysis

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Variable costs | Variable cost per unit × number of units |
| Total costs | Fixed costs + variable costs |
| Revenue | Price per unit × quantity sold |
| Profit | Total revenue − total costs |
| Contribution per unit | Price − variable cost per unit |
| Total contribution | Contribution per unit × units sold |
| Break-even output | Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit |
| Margin of safety | Actual output − break-even output |

**Shortcut:** above break-even, profit = margin of safety × contribution per unit. Below it, loss = fixed costs − total contribution.

**Chart reading.**
- Fixed costs: horizontal line.
- Total costs: starts at fixed costs, slope = variable cost per unit.
- Revenue: starts at zero, slope = price.
- Break-even: where revenue meets total costs.
- Profit or loss: vertical gap between revenue and total costs.

**Chart changes.**

| Change | Line that moves | Break-even |
|---|---|---|
| Price up | Revenue line steeper | Falls |
| Fixed costs up | Fixed and total cost lines shift up | Rises |
| Variable cost per unit up | Total cost line steeper | Rises |
| Variable cost per unit down | Total cost line flatter | Falls |

**Limits:** assumes all output sold at one price and straight-line costs; only as good as the cost estimates.

**Worked reminder.** A tutoring firm charges £30 per session, pays the tutor £12 per session and has fixed costs of £2,700 a month. Contribution = 30 − 12 = £18; break-even = 2,700 ÷ 18 = 150 sessions. At 190 sessions the margin of safety is 40 sessions and profit is 40 × 18 = £720. Check the long way: revenue 190 × 30 = £5,700; total costs 2,700 + (190 × 12) = £4,980; profit £720.

## 3.2.3.3 Profit and cash

### Profit

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Gross profit | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Operating profit | Gross profit − operating expenses |
| Net profit | Operating profit − interest − taxes |
| Gross profit margin (%) | Gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| Operating profit margin (%) | Operating profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| Net profit margin (%) | Net profit ÷ revenue × 100 |

**Reading margins together.** A high gross margin with a low operating margin points to heavy overheads. A fall in net margin while operating margin holds points to higher interest or taxes. Compare margins over time or with rivals, not in isolation.

**Ways to improve profit/profitability:** raise price (watch elasticity), cut variable costs (watch quality), cut fixed costs (watch capacity and morale), raise volume (watch promotion cost and capacity).

### Budgeting

**Variance = actual − budget.** Then decide F or A by its effect on profit:

| | Actual above budget | Actual below budget |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Favourable | Adverse |
| Cost | Adverse | Favourable |

**Method: amending a budget statement.**
1. Calculate each line's variance.
2. Label each F or A by its effect on profit.
3. Recalculate profit for budget and actual.
4. Check: profit variance = favourable total − adverse total.

### Cash flow

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Net cash flow | Inflows − outflows |
| Closing balance | Opening balance + net cash flow |
| Opening balance | Previous closing balance |
| Receivables days | Trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365 |
| Payables days | Trade payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 |

**Method: completing a forecast.**
1. Total inflows and outflows for the month.
2. Net cash flow = inflows − outflows (bracket negatives).
3. Closing = opening + net.
4. Carry the closing balance across as next month's opening.

**Why cash matters and why forecasts help:** cash pays wages, suppliers and lenders on time; a forecast shows when a shortfall is coming, supports a bank application and lets managers plan payments. It is only as reliable as its sales estimates.

**Reading the days figures:** if receivables days are longer than payables days, the business pays out before it is paid and must finance the gap.

**Improving cash flow:** debt factoring, shortening payment time from customers, early payment incentives, credit checks, increasing payment time to suppliers, reducing outgoings, better invoice management. Each one either speeds up inflows or delays or cuts outflows -- say which, then give its cost.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Profit vs cash:** profit is earned when a sale is made; cash arrives when the customer pays. Credit sales, buying inventory and paying for assets all make the two differ.
- **Profit vs profitability:** profit is a sum of money; profitability is profit relative to revenue (a margin).
- **Contribution vs profit:** contribution is before fixed costs; profit is after.
- **Overdraft vs loan:** overdraft for short-term, flexible needs; loan for a known sum over years.
- **Business angel vs private equity:** an angel is an individual backing small or new firms; private equity firms invest larger sums, often taking control.
- **Receivables vs payables:** money owed *to* the business vs money owed *by* it.

## Quick self-test

1. A product sells for £45, variable cost per unit is £27 and fixed costs are £54,000. Calculate break-even output.
2. Actual sales are 3,800 units. Calculate the margin of safety.
3. Calculate profit at 3,800 units.
4. Calculate total contribution at 3,800 units.
5. Revenue is £250,000 and gross profit is £95,000. Calculate the gross profit margin.
6. Operating profit is £30,000 on the same revenue. Calculate the operating profit margin.
7. Budgeted material costs were £20,000; actual were £22,500. State the variance.
8. Opening balance £1,800, inflows £6,400, outflows £7,900. Calculate the closing balance.
9. Trade receivables are £18,000 and revenue is £219,000. Calculate receivables days.
10. State what happens to break-even output if variable cost per unit falls.
11. Explain why an overdraft is unsuitable for buying a delivery van.
12. Define sale and leaseback.

### Answers

1. Contribution = 45 − 27 = £18; break-even = 54,000 ÷ 18 = **3,000 units**.
2. 3,800 − 3,000 = **800 units**.
3. 800 × 18 = **£14,400** profit.
4. 3,800 × 18 = **£68,400** (and 68,400 − 54,000 = £14,400, matching answer 3).
5. 95,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **38%**.
6. 30,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **12%**.
7. 22,500 − 20,000 = **£2,500 adverse** (a cost above budget).
8. 1,800 + 6,400 − 7,900 = **£300**.
9. 18,000 ÷ 219,000 × 365 = **30 days**.
10. Contribution per unit rises, so break-even output **falls**.
11. The van is a long-term asset but an overdraft is short-term, costly and can be withdrawn; a loan matches repayments to the van's life.
12. Selling an asset, usually property, to raise cash and then leasing it back so the business keeps using it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "break-even = fixed costs ÷ price" -- the divisor is contribution per unit.
- Leaving break-even as a decimal or rounding it down; you cannot sell part of a unit, so round up.
- Giving margin of safety in £ when the question asks for units, or the reverse.
- Labelling a variance F or A by its sign alone rather than by its effect on profit.
- Subtracting only interest, or only taxes, when calculating net profit.
- Using cost of sales as the denominator in a profit margin; margins are always over revenue.
- Starting a month's cash flow with the wrong opening balance.
- Listing ways to improve cash flow without the drawback for this business.
- Recommending a source of finance without linking it to the amount, time period and control.
