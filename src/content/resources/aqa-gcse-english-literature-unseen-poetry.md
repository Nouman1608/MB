This guide covers **Section C: Unseen Poetry**, part of Paper 2 (Modern Texts and Poetry) in AQA GCSE
English Literature (8702). It is distinct from the site's existing guide to
[Modern Texts and Poetry](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-literature-paper-2/), which focuses on Section A
(the studied modern prose or drama text) and Section B (the studied poetry anthology cluster) -- this
guide focuses specifically on Section C, where poems have never been seen before the exam.

## Where this fits in 8702

Section C is structurally different from the rest of Paper 2: Sections A and B reward detailed
knowledge of specific, pre-studied texts, while Section C rewards a transferable analytical skill applied
under time pressure to material no candidate has seen in advance. It carries 32 marks (24 for analysing
the first poem, 8 for comparing it with a second), making it a substantial share of Paper 2.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA GCSE ENGLISH LITERATURE (8702) -- PAPER 2, SECTION C: UNSEEN POETRY**

- Part (i): analyse one unseen poem in detail (24 marks)
- Part (ii): compare how the poets use methods in the first poem and a second unseen poem (8 marks)
- Preparation requires experiencing a wide range of poetry to develop the ability to closely analyse
  unseen poems -- no specific poems or poets are set in advance, since by definition the material is
  unseen until the exam
- Assessed alongside Section B's studied poetry anthology cluster (one of three thematically linked
  clusters -- Love and Relationships, Power and Conflict, or Worlds and Lives -- each containing 15
  poems written between 1789 and the present day), though Section C poems are always new to the
  candidate

## How to approach it

Because no specific poem can be revised in advance, preparation for Section C means practising a
transferable method, not memorising content. Read as many unfamiliar poems as possible before the exam,
timing yourself to build the habit of producing a structured, well-supported analysis within a fixed
time -- roughly 45 minutes for both parts of Section C combined is a reasonable target within Paper 2's
overall 2 hour 15 minute length.

## Part (i): analysing the first poem alone

The 24-mark analysis task rewards close reading of language, form and structure, linked to meaning and
effect, exactly as with the studied anthology poems in Section B -- the only difference is that the poem
itself is new. The same technique that works for anthology poems (identify a feature, name its effect,
link it to the poem's overall meaning) transfers directly, provided it can be applied quickly to
unfamiliar material.

## Part (ii): comparing methods across two unseen poems

The 8-mark comparison task asks specifically about how the poets use methods -- not simply what each poem
is about. A strong comparison identifies a specific technique (imagery, structure, form, tone) present in
both poems, and analyses how each poet uses it differently or similarly, rather than describing the two
poems' content side by side without any technical comparison.

## Worked example: a fast first-read routine for an unseen poem

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - read the whole poem once for overall subject and tone:
Before annotating anything, know roughly what the poem is about
and how it feels.

Step 2 - reread and annotate structure and form:
Stanza pattern, rhyme scheme (if any), line length, and any
notable shifts partway through.

Step 3 - annotate 3-4 strong language choices:
Imagery, word choice, sound effects -- select the clearest
examples rather than trying to cover everything.

Step 4 - link every annotation to an effect on meaning or the
reader:
A feature noted without its effect explained earns little credit.

Step 5 - for the comparison task, select ONE shared technique
across both poems:
Structure your 8-mark answer around that one point of comparison,
analysed in both poems, rather than several superficial points.
```

Step 5 matters specifically for the comparison task: a focused single-technique comparison, explored in
real depth, consistently outperforms a scattered list of surface similarities and differences.

## Why unseen poetry rewards breadth of practice, not memorisation

Because Section C poems cannot be known in advance, the most effective preparation is broad and varied
practice rather than deep study of any single text. Reading widely across different poets, periods and
styles -- not just poems similar to the anthology cluster studied for Section B -- builds a flexible
analytical vocabulary that transfers to whatever poem appears on the day. Candidates who have only
practised on poems resembling their anthology cluster can struggle when an unseen poem uses an
unfamiliar form or subject matter; candidates who have read broadly are better placed to recognise
common poetic techniques -- enjambment, caesura, extended metaphor, shifts in tone -- wherever they
appear. Regular timed practice on genuinely unfamiliar poems, rather than poems already partly discussed
in class, most closely replicates exam conditions and builds genuine unseen-analysis confidence.

## Common mistakes

Spending too long on Part (i) and rushing Part (ii), given its smaller but still significant 8 marks.
Describing what an unseen poem is about without analysing how language, form or structure creates that
meaning. In the comparison task, listing similarities and differences in content rather than in the
poets' techniques. Trying to name every possible feature rather than selecting a smaller number and
analysing each in depth.

## Quick revision checklist

- Practise a fast, structured first-read routine (subject/tone, then structure, then language, each
  linked to effect) on unfamiliar poems.
- Time full Section C practice attempts to build pacing across both parts.
- For the comparison task, practise selecting one shared technique and analysing it across two poems in
  depth, rather than listing multiple surface points.
- Apply the same close-analysis technique used for the studied anthology poems (Section B) to unfamiliar
  material.
- Allocate time roughly in proportion to the marks available: more time for the 24-mark single-poem
  analysis than the 8-mark comparison.
