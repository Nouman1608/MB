This guide covers **3.2.2.3 Organisational design** from OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725), Version 1.1, in Unit 2 (International AS). The [Human resources study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-human-resources/) defines each term; this page shows how to read a structure in numbers, what really drives the choice of structure, and how to judge a restructuring plan. Then condense it with the [organisational design revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-organisational-design-9725-revision-notes/) and practise with the [organisational design practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-organisational-design-9725-practice/).

All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

**Influences on and impact of decisions regarding:**

- span of control;
- levels of hierarchy;
- tall versus flat structures;
- delegation;
- centralisation and decentralisation;
- employee representation rates.

**Advantages and disadvantages of restructuring, including delayering.**

Note the wording: "influences on" means what shapes the choice; "impact of" means what the choice then does to communication, motivation, costs and control. Exam questions often target one or the other.

## Reading a structure in numbers

- **Span of control**: the number of people who report directly to one manager.
- **Levels of hierarchy**: the number of layers from the top of the organisation to the bottom.
- **Chain of command**: the route by which instructions pass down and information passes up.

### Worked example: Thistle Print

| Level | Role | Number | Each reports to | Span of the level above |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Managing director | 1 | – | – |
| 2 | Heads of department | 3 | Managing director | 3 |
| 3 | Team managers | 9 | A head | 3 |
| 4 | Supervisors | 27 | A team manager | 3 |
| 5 | Print staff | 216 | A supervisor | 8 |

```
Total employees       = 1 + 3 + 9 + 27 + 216 = 256
Levels of hierarchy   = 5
Managers/supervisors  = 1 + 3 + 9 + 27 = 40
Print staff per manager or supervisor = 216 ÷ 40 = 5.4
```

Thistle is a **tall** structure: five levels and narrow spans of 3 for most managers. A message from the managing director passes through three layers before reaching the print floor.

## Tall versus flat

| | Tall (many levels, narrow spans) | Flat (few levels, wide spans) |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | Slow; messages distorted on the way | Faster and more direct |
| Supervision | Close | Lighter; more self-direction |
| Cost | Many managers to pay | Fewer managers |
| Promotion | Many small steps | Fewer opportunities |
| Motivation | Can feel controlled | More responsibility; can feel overloaded |

## Influences on the choice of structure

- **Size**: larger organisations need more managers, which tends to add levels.
- **Nature of the work**: routine, similar tasks allow wide spans; complex or varied tasks need narrower ones.
- **Skills and experience of staff**: capable, trained staff need less supervision, allowing wider spans and more delegation.
- **Skills of managers**: experienced managers can handle larger teams.
- **Management style and culture**: an authoritarian style suits tight control; a democratic style suits delegation.
- **Geography**: many branches may push towards decentralisation.
- **Cost**: each management layer adds salaries.
- **Technology**: shared systems and video calls let managers oversee wider teams.

## Delegation

Delegation passes **authority** for a task down the hierarchy. The manager remains **accountable** for the result.

- **Impact if done well**: managers have time for strategic work; staff gain skills and responsibility, which can raise motivation (Herzberg's motivators, see the [motivation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-motivation-9725/)); decisions are made closer to the customer.
- **Impact if done badly**: mistakes when staff lack training; stress if responsibility is passed down without support or authority.
- **Influences**: trust, staff skills, the risk attached to the decision, time available for training.

## Centralisation and decentralisation

**Centralised**: key decisions are made at head office or by senior managers. **Decentralised**: authority is passed to branches, regions or lower levels.

**Example.** A chain of 40 garden centres must decide who sets plant prices.

- Centralised pricing gives one consistent price list, strong cost control and bulk-buying power, but cannot react to a local rival's offer or a local heatwave.
- Decentralised pricing lets each manager match local competition and weather, which may raise sales, but brings inconsistent prices across stores and requires capable branch managers.

Many businesses mix the two: central decisions on brand, buying and finance; local decisions on staffing rotas and displays.

## Employee representation rates

The employee representation rate is the proportion of employees represented by a trade union or another employee body, such as an elected staff council.

```
Representation rate = employees represented ÷ total employees × 100
Example: 162 of Thistle's 216 print staff are union members → 162 ÷ 216 × 100 = 75%
```

**Impact.** A high rate gives employees a stronger collective voice. Managers have one body to consult and negotiate with, which can speed up agreement on changes and make communication easier. But it can also make restructuring slower, because changes must be negotiated. **Influences** include the industry's tradition, the law, the size of the workplace and management's attitude to employee involvement. See the [employer-employee relations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-employer-employee-relations-9725/) for how representation shapes involvement and conflict.

## Restructuring and delayering

**Restructuring** means changing the organisation's design: merging departments, changing spans, centralising or decentralising. **Delayering** removes one or more levels of hierarchy.

### Worked example: two delayering options at Thistle

**Option A: remove the team manager level.** Each head now manages 9 supervisors. With team managers paid $48,000 a year:

```
Saving = 9 × 48,000 = $432,000 a year
Levels: 5 → 4; heads' span: 3 → 9
```

**Option B: remove the supervisor level.** Each team manager now manages 24 print staff directly (216 ÷ 9). With supervisors paid $36,000:

```
Saving = 27 × 36,000 = $972,000 a year
Levels: 5 → 4; team managers' span: 3 → 24
```

Option B saves more but leaves a span of 24 people doing technical work, which may be too wide for close quality control unless staff are experienced and organised into self-managing teams. Option A keeps supervision on the print floor and is lower risk, though it saves less. Either option would also mean redundancy costs in the first year.

| Advantages of delayering | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Lower salary costs | Redundancy costs and loss of experience |
| Faster communication | Wider spans may overload managers |
| More responsibility for remaining staff | Fewer promotion steps |
| Decisions closer to customers | Fear and lower morale among those who stay |

## Common mistakes

- Saying a flat structure is always better.
- Confusing delegation (passing authority for tasks) with decentralisation (moving decision-making power across the organisation).
- Forgetting that the manager remains accountable after delegating.
- Calculating savings from delayering without considering redundancy costs or the extra workload.
- Discussing "influences" when the question asks about "impact", or the reverse.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: count levels carefully and show each span.
- **Explain**: link a structural feature to one effect on communication, cost, motivation or control, in context.
- **Analyse**: trace a chain, such as wider span → less supervision → more empowerment → faster service, but risk of errors.
- **Evaluate**: weigh savings against risks, and say what the outcome depends on (staff skills, nature of the work, how the change is managed).
