These questions test the section 3.2 row on capacity defences: insanity, automatism and intoxication. In Section B, identify the cause of the defendant's condition and whether the offence is specific or basic intent before choosing the defence.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the three elements of the M'Naghten Rules. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by "disease of the mind", using **one** case. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by automatism and its effect if successful. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the difference between the defences available to a diabetic with hypoglycaemia and one with hyperglycaemia. **[4]**

**5.** State the rule in *DPP v Majewski* (1977). **[2]**

**6.** Explain the rule in *R v Kingston* (1994). **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Sid, who has epilepsy, has a seizure on a bus and strikes the passenger next to him, causing bruising. Advise Sid on any defence. **[5]**

**8.** Tia is driving when she is stung by a wasp and loses control of the car, injuring a pedestrian. Advise Tia on automatism. **[5]**

**9.** Umar drinks heavily at a party and, very drunk, stabs Viv, causing serious injury. He says he was too drunk to know what he was doing. Advise Umar on intoxication. **[6]**

**10.** Wen's drink is spiked with a strong drug at a club. She hits a stranger, causing a cut that needs stitches. Advise Wen on intoxication. **[5]**

**11.** Xander takes a sedative from his friend's prescription to calm his nerves before a job interview. It makes him aggressive and he punches a receptionist, causing ABH. He did not know the drug could have that effect. Advise Xander. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the law on insanity. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A defect of reason [1], caused by a disease of the mind [1], so that the defendant did not know the nature and quality of the act or, if he did, did not know it was wrong [1].

**2.** A legal, not medical, concept meaning an internal condition that affects the mental faculties of reason, memory and understanding, whether its origin is mental or physical [1], as in *R v Kemp* (1956), where arteriosclerosis was a disease of the mind (credit *Sullivan*, *Hennessy* or *Burgess*) [1].

**3.** Automatism is an act done by the muscles without any control by the mind, caused by an external factor (*Bratty*, 1963) [1]. If successful, non-insane automatism is a complete defence, leading to an acquittal [1].

**4.** Hypoglycaemia, caused by taking insulin without enough food, is caused by an external factor (the insulin) [1], so the defence is non-insane automatism (*R v Quick*, 1973) [1]. Hyperglycaemia, caused by the diabetes itself when insulin is not taken, is an internal cause [1], so the defence is insanity (*R v Hennessy*, 1989) [1].

**5.** Voluntary intoxication may be relied on as evidence that the defendant lacked the intent for a specific intent offence [1], but it is no defence to a basic intent offence, since becoming intoxicated is itself reckless [1].

**6.** Involuntary intoxication is a defence only if the defendant did not form the mens rea of the offence [1]. If he formed the mens rea despite the intoxication, it is no defence: "a drugged intent is still an intent" [1].

**7.** Epilepsy is an internal condition and has been held to be a disease of the mind (*R v Sullivan*, 1984) [1]. During a seizure Sid has a defect of reason and does not know the nature and quality of his act [1], so the defence is insanity under the M'Naghten Rules, which he must prove on the balance of probabilities [1]. Automatism is not available because the cause is internal [1]. If successful, the verdict is not guilty by reason of insanity, and the court is likely to give an absolute discharge or supervision order for a minor injury [1].

**8.** Automatism requires a total loss of voluntary control [1] caused by an external factor [1]. A wasp sting is external, and *Hill v Baxter* (1958) gave being attacked by a swarm of bees as an example of automatism [1]. Tia must have lost control completely; if she retained some control, the defence fails (*A-G's Reference (No 2 of 1992)*) [1]. Her automatism was not self-induced, so if the loss of control was total she will be acquitted [1].

**9.** Umar's intoxication was voluntary [1]. Causing serious injury with a knife could be charged as s18, a specific intent offence [1]; if he was so drunk that he did not form the intention to cause GBH, he cannot be guilty under s18 (*DPP v Beard*, 1920) [1]. But voluntary intoxication is no defence to a basic intent offence (*DPP v Majewski*, 1977) [1], so he is likely to be convicted under s20 [1]. If he formed the intent despite the drink, he is guilty of s18 [1].

**10.** Wen's intoxication is involuntary because her drink was spiked without her knowledge [1]. A cut needing stitches is likely to be a wound, so s20 is possible, or s47 [1]. Involuntary intoxication is a defence to both specific and basic intent offences [1] if she did not form the mens rea [1]. If she did form the mens rea despite the drug, the defence fails (*R v Kingston*, 1994) [1].

**11.** Xander's intoxication is self-induced, but the drug was a sedative, not alcohol or an illegal drug [1]. Under *R v Hardie* (1984), taking a sedative is not treated as voluntary intoxication if the defendant did not know of the risk of aggressive or unpredictable behaviour [1]. s47 is a basic intent offence, so if the *Majewski* rule applied he would have no defence [1]. Because he was unaware of the risk, he was not reckless [1], so the jury should consider whether he had the mens rea of the battery [1]. If he did not, he should be acquitted; if he did, he is guilty (*Kingston*) [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Definition:** M'Naghten Rules (1843) require a defect of reason from disease of the mind, with lack of knowledge of the nature and quality of the act or that it was wrong [1]. **Criticisms:** the definition is legal, not medical, and has not kept pace with psychiatry [1]. It labels people with epilepsy (*R v Sullivan*, 1984) [1], diabetes (*R v Hennessy*, 1989) and sleepwalking (*R v Burgess*, 1991) as insane, which stigmatises them [1]. The internal and external distinction is arbitrary: hypoglycaemia gives automatism (*Quick*), hyperglycaemia insanity [1]. "Wrong" means legally wrong (*R v Windle*, 1952), so seriously ill defendants who know the act is illegal cannot use the defence [1]. The burden of proof on the defendant is inconsistent with *Woolmington* [1]. **Improvements:** the Criminal Procedure (Insanity and Unfitness to Plead) Act 1991 gave judges flexible disposals, including absolute discharge, so the consequences are less severe than before [1]. **Reform:** the Law Commission's 2013 discussion paper proposed a new defence of not criminally responsible by reason of a recognised medical condition [1], which would base the defence on medical evidence [1] and remove the stigma of the label "insane" [1]. A reasoned conclusion [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing a defence before identifying the cause.
- Reversing the diabetes cases.
- Allowing voluntary intoxication for basic intent offences.
- Forgetting the conviction for the basic intent alternative when intoxication defeats s18 or murder.
