This guide goes deeper into **A2.1 Origins of cells**, an additional higher level sub-topic of Theme A (Unity and diversity) in the IB DP *Biology guide*, first assessment 2025. Every statement, A2.1.1 to A2.1.9, is HL only. The sub-topic asks a question that cannot be answered by direct observation: how did the first cells arise from non-living chemistry? Exam questions therefore reward reasoning about evidence and its limits more than recall.

The [origins of cells and viruses study guide](/resources/ib-dp-biology-origins-of-cells-and-viruses-hl/) gives the core facts for each statement. This page organises them around the problems any explanation must solve, shows how to evaluate each piece of evidence, and works through the kinds of reasoning examiners ask for. Condense it with the [A2.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-biology-dp-biology-ib-dp-biology-1-a2-1-revision-notes/) and practise with the [A2.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-biology-dp-biology-ib-dp-biology-1-a2-1-practice/).

## A2.1.2 Why the cell is the starting point

A cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the functions of life by itself: metabolism, growth, reproduction, response to stimuli, homeostasis, nutrition and excretion. Anything smaller, such as an organelle or a virus, depends on a cell to do some of these. That is why the origin of *cells*, not just of organic molecules, is the problem to explain.

**Viruses as a test case.** A virus has genes and can evolve, but it has no metabolism of its own and reproduces only inside a host cell. Most biologists therefore class viruses as non-living. The difficulty shows that "living" has no sharp boundary, and any definition is a decision about which features matter most.

## A2.1.3 The four problems any explanation must solve

The guide identifies processes that must have arisen for the first cell to exist. Treat them as a checklist when evaluating any hypothesis.

| Requirement | Why it is needed | Hypothesis that addresses it |
|---|---|---|
| Catalysis | Reactions must run fast enough to build and maintain a cell | Ribozymes (catalytic RNA), mineral surfaces |
| Self-replication | Information must be copied so features can be inherited | RNA copying by base pairing |
| Self-assembly | Large structures must form from small units without a cell to build them | Fatty acids forming vesicles; polymers forming on surfaces |
| Compartmentalisation | Internal chemistry must be kept separate and concentrated | Vesicles enclosing RNA and other molecules |

The deeper difficulty is that in modern cells these depend on each other. DNA needs proteins (enzymes) to be copied, and proteins need DNA (via RNA) to be made. Any explanation must break this circle.

**Testability.** The events happened about four billion years ago, left no direct fossils, and cannot be repeated. Scientists therefore test whether each step is *possible* under plausible conditions, which is weaker than showing that it *did* happen.

## A2.1.1 and A2.1.4 Making carbon compounds without life

**Conditions.** Early Earth had little or no free oxygen and no ozone layer, so ultraviolet radiation reaching the surface was intense. Lightning, volcanic heat and ultraviolet light were possible energy sources. Without oxygen, organic molecules formed in this way would not be oxidised and broken down.

**Miller and Urey (1952–53).** They circulated water vapour, methane, ammonia and hydrogen past electric sparks and condensed the products. Amino acids accumulated.

### Worked example 1: evaluating Miller–Urey

*Question (4 marks): Evaluate the Miller–Urey experiment as evidence for the pre-biotic origin of carbon compounds.*

> **Strength.** It showed that amino acids, the monomers of proteins, can form from simple inorganic molecules with an energy source and no living organisms **[1]**. Later analyses of samples from such experiments identified more kinds of amino acid **[1]**.
>
> **Limitation.** The gas mixture may not match early Earth's atmosphere, which many scientists now think was less strongly reducing **[1]**. It produced monomers only: no polymers, no self-replicating molecules and no membranes, so it addresses only the first step **[1]**.

"Evaluate" requires both sides. A list of strengths alone reaches half the marks at best.

## A2.1.5 Vesicles: compartments that build themselves

Fatty acids have a hydrophilic carboxyl head and a hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail. In water, above a certain concentration, they coalesce spontaneously into **spherical bilayers**: vesicles with tails inside the bilayer and heads facing water inside and outside. No enzymes are needed, so this addresses the self-assembly and compartmentalisation requirements together.

What a vesicle offers a protocell:

- Molecules made inside stay together instead of diffusing away.
- Internal concentrations can differ from the surroundings.
- Each vesicle is a separate unit, so vesicles whose contents replicate faster can increase in number: natural selection can begin.

## A2.1.6 RNA first

RNA can do two jobs that in modern cells are split between DNA and proteins:

- it stores information in a base sequence that can be copied by complementary pairing;
- some RNA molecules, **ribozymes**, act as catalysts.

So one type of molecule could have provided both self-replication and catalysis, breaking the DNA–protein circle described above.

### Worked example 2: building the argument

*Question (3 marks): Explain why RNA is presumed to have been the first genetic material.*

> RNA can store genetic information and be replicated by complementary base pairing **[1]**. RNA can also catalyse reactions as ribozymes, so it could act as its own catalyst for replication **[1]**. In modern ribosomes, the peptide bond is formed by rRNA, which is consistent with RNA catalysis having come before protein enzymes **[1]**.

## A2.1.7 to A2.1.9 The last universal common ancestor

**Evidence that LUCA existed (A2.1.7).** All known organisms share the same genetic code and many of the same genes, for example for components of the ribosome. Features shared across all three domains are best explained by inheritance from one ancestral population. Other early life forms probably existed but left no descendants, perhaps outcompeted by LUCA's lineage.

**Estimating dates (A2.1.8).** No single method is reliable alone, so evidence is combined:

- **Radiometric dating** of the rocks that contain fossils or chemical traces of life.
- **Fossil evidence**, such as layered microbial mats preserved in ancient rocks.
- **Molecular clocks**: comparing sequences in living species and using an estimated rate of change to work back to the time of divergence.

### Worked example 3: a molecular clock

Two invented lineages differ at 36 positions in a conserved gene. From fossil-dated pairs, differences between lineages are estimated to accumulate at 3 per 100 million years.

1. Time since divergence = 36 ÷ 3 × 100 million years = **1200 million years**.
2. Limitations: rates may not be constant over time or between lineages; very old divergences suffer from repeated changes at the same position, which hide differences; the calibration depends on fossil dates that themselves carry uncertainty.

Extending such calculations back to LUCA multiplies these uncertainties, which is why published estimates vary widely.

**Hydrothermal vents (A2.1.9).** Two lines of evidence point to LUCA living near deep-sea vents: fossilised traces of life in ancient vent precipitates, and genomic analysis of gene families conserved across domains, which suggests an organism adapted to hot, oxygen-free conditions rich in hydrogen and minerals.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that Miller and Urey created life or cells.
- Describing vesicle formation as needing enzymes; it is spontaneous.
- Saying RNA came first because it is "simpler"; give the dual role of storage and catalysis.
- Calling LUCA the first living cell. It is the most recent common ancestor of all surviving life.
- Presenting a molecular clock date without its assumptions.

## Exam technique for IB command terms

- **Outline**: give the four requirements briefly.
- **Explain**: link a property (catalysis by RNA) to the problem it solves (the DNA–protein circle).
- **Evaluate**: strength and limitation, each tied to the evidence.
- **Discuss**: weigh competing ideas, for example vents versus surface pools, and reach a conclusion.
- **Suggest**: apply the requirements to a new hypothesis or experiment.
