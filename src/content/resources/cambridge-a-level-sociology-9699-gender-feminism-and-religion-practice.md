These questions cover sub-topic 12.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699). Paper 4
itself contains only 35-mark essays; Section A here checks the concepts and evidence those essays
rely on.

## Section A

**1.** Define *agency* in the context of women and religion. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between radical feminist and liberal feminist views of religion. **[4]**

**3.** Explain **two** organisational explanations for gender inequality in religion. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how purity rules can contribute to gender inequality in religion. **[3]**

**5.** Outline Karen Armstrong's historical explanation of male dominance in religion. **[3]**

**6.** Describe **two** examples of moves towards gender equality in religious organisations. **[4]**

**7.** Explain what Brusco's study suggests about the benefits of religion for women. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why Saba Mahmood criticised Western liberal readings of religious women's choices. **[3]**

## Section B

**9.** "Feminist explanations of gender inequality in religion are more convincing than the view
that religion benefits women." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**10.** "Religious organisations are moving rapidly towards gender equality." Evaluate this view.
**[35]**

**11.** "Women who practise religion are victims of patriarchy rather than active agents."
Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** "Feminist perspectives on religion rest on Western assumptions about freedom." Evaluate
this view. **[35]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The capacity of women to make their own choices and act on them [1], for example choosing
how to practise their faith or using religion for their own purposes [1].

**2.** Radical feminists see religion as a central institution of patriarchy that sacralises male
power [1], so reform cannot remove the oppression (Daly) [1]. Liberal feminists see inequality as
a feature that can be changed from within [1], for example by opening leadership roles to women
[1].

**3.** Any two, 2 marks each (identification plus explanation):
- Male-only leadership: men control teaching, ritual and interpretation.
- Male control of resources and decision-making bodies: women's interests are under-represented
  when rules are made.
- Women concentrated in supporting roles (teaching children, caring, organising) with little
  formal authority.

**4.** Ideas that women are ritually impure at certain times [1] can exclude them from sacred
spaces or rituals [1], which keeps leadership and sacred knowledge in men's hands and presents
inequality as religiously required [1].

**5.** Armstrong argues that many early religions worshipped female deities [1]. With the rise of
monotheism, the divine came to be imagined as male [1], so male dominance in religion is a
historical development rather than a permanent feature [1].

**6.** Any two, 2 marks each:
- The Church of England ordained women priests from 1994 and consecrated its first woman bishop
  in 2015.
- Reform Judaism ordains women rabbis; Sally Priesand was the first in the American Reform
  movement, in 1972.
- Women's mosques led by women religious leaders among Hui Muslims in China.

**7.** Women's conversion to evangelical Protestantism in Colombia [1] encouraged husbands to give
up drinking and gambling [1], so women and households gained resources and stability even though
men remained heads of the family [1].

**8.** Mahmood argued that liberal feminism assumes agency means resisting religious norms [1].
In her study of a women's mosque movement in Cairo, women cultivated piety deliberately [1]; she
argued this is a form of agency that should be understood on its own terms [1].

**9.** *Indicative content.*

**Feminist explanations are convincing:**
- Organisational evidence: male-only priesthood in the Roman Catholic Church and Orthodox
  Judaism.
- Textual and theological evidence: male interpretation of texts; male images of God (Daly).
- De Beauvoir's false compensation; Marxist feminist ideology arguments.
- Armstrong shows inequality was historically produced.

**Religion benefits women:**
- Watson (1994) on veiling; Brusco (1995) on Colombian evangelicals; Mahmood (2005) on piety.
- Woodhead's religious forms of feminism; women-only spaces and support networks.

**Evaluation:** the two views are not opposites: women can benefit within a patriarchal structure.
Distinguish between authority (where feminist explanations are strong) and everyday life
chances (where benefits are well documented). Credit comparison across faiths.

**10.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Ordination of women in the Church of England (priests 1994, bishops 2015) and in non-Orthodox
  Judaism.
- Women's leadership in women's mosques and in many NRMs and New Age groups.
- Liberal feminist campaigns have succeeded in several denominations.

**Against the view:**
- "Rapidly" is questionable: Regina Jonas was ordained in 1935, yet women rabbis remained rare
  for decades.
- The Roman Catholic Church and Orthodox Judaism do not ordain women.
- Formal access does not mean equal influence; senior posts remain mostly male.
- Change can provoke resistance and division within organisations.

**Evaluation:** change is real but uneven between and within faiths; judge pace, not just
direction.

**11.** *Indicative content.*

**Victims of patriarchy:**
- Radical feminists (Daly): religion sacralises male power.
- De Beauvoir: false compensation discourages challenge.
- Religious family law and dress rules can limit women's choices; socialisation shapes what women
  "choose".

**Active agents:**
- Watson, Brusco, Mahmood and Woodhead show women using religion for their own purposes.
- Women lead in women-only spaces and in some new movements.

**Evaluation:** a structure and agency debate; agency operates within constraints. Strong answers
avoid treating religious women as a single group and recognise that judgements about "freedom"
can reflect Western assumptions.

**12.** *Indicative content.*

**Supporting the view:**
- Mahmood (2005): liberal feminism equates agency with resisting religious norms, so it misreads
  women in the Cairo mosque movement who cultivated piety by choice.
- Watson (1994): veiling can express identity and protect women from objectification, contrary to
  the assumption that it always signals oppression.
- De Beauvoir and Daly wrote mainly from Western Christian contexts, and their claims may not
  transfer to other faiths.

**Against the view:**
- Feminists within faith traditions, such as El Saadawi, criticise patriarchal interpretation from
  inside non-Western societies.
- Evidence of male-only leadership, purity rules and unequal family law exists across many
  societies, so criticism of it is not simply Western.
- Brusco's study shows benefits to women, but within a structure that still placed men at the
  head of the family, which supports feminist analysis of power.

**Evaluation:** separate the claim that some feminist *theories* carry Western assumptions (well
supported) from the claim that gender inequality in religion is a Western invention (not
supported). Strong answers compare evidence from more than one faith and society.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need examples from more than one religion?** It is not required, but comparison across
faiths makes evaluation far stronger.

**Can I use secularisation material here?** Only where it answers the question, for example the
argument that women's higher participation slows secularisation.
