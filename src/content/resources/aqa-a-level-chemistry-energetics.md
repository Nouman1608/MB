## 3.1.4.1 Enthalpy change

### Exothermic and endothermic

- An **exothermic** reaction transfers heat energy to the surroundings. The surroundings get warmer
  and **ΔH is negative**. Combustion and neutralisation are exothermic.
- An **endothermic** reaction takes in heat energy from the surroundings. The surroundings cool and
  **ΔH is positive**. Thermal decomposition is endothermic.

**Enthalpy change (ΔH)** is the heat energy change measured under conditions of **constant pressure**.
Its units are kJ mol⁻¹ when quoted per mole of the reaction as written.

### Standard conditions

A standard enthalpy change, written with the symbol ⦵, refers to **standard conditions: 100 kPa and a
stated temperature**. The temperature is shown as a subscript, for example ΔH⦵₂₉₈ for 298 K. Each
substance must also be in its **standard state**: its normal physical state under those conditions
(for example H₂O(l), Br₂(l) and C(s) at 298 K).

### The two definitions you must learn

**Standard enthalpy of combustion, ΔcH⦵**: the enthalpy change when **1 mole** of a substance is
**completely burned in oxygen** under standard conditions, with all reactants and products in their
standard states.

**Standard enthalpy of formation, ΔfH⦵**: the enthalpy change when **1 mole** of a compound is formed
**from its elements** under standard conditions, with all reactants and products in their standard
states.

Two consequences follow from the definitions:

- ΔfH⦵ of an **element in its standard state is zero**, because forming it from itself is no change.
  So ΔfH⦵ of O₂(g) is 0, but ΔfH⦵ of Br₂(g) is not, because bromine's standard state is liquid.
- The equation must show exactly 1 mole of the substance named, so fractions are allowed elsewhere.

```
ΔcH⦵ of methanol:   CH₃OH(l) + 1½O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)
ΔfH⦵ of ethanol:    2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l)
```

## 3.1.4.2 Calorimetry

The heat change, q, is given by

```
q = mcΔT
```

- **m** is the mass of the substance that changes temperature (usually the water or solution, **not**
  the mass of the reactant), in g.
- **c** is its specific heat capacity, in J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. You are not expected to recall c; the question
  gives it (4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ is the value usually given for water and dilute solutions).
- **ΔT** is the temperature change, in K. A change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K.

With these units, q comes out in **joules**. Divide by 1000 for kJ.

To turn q into a **molar enthalpy change**:

```
ΔH = −q ÷ n        (q in kJ, n = moles of the reactant that limits the reaction)
```

The minus sign is there because a temperature **rise** means heat has left the reaction (exothermic, ΔH
negative). If the temperature falls, ΔH is positive.

### Worked example 1: neutralisation

50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 50.0 cm³ of 1.10 mol dm⁻³ NaOH in a polystyrene cup.
The temperature rises by 6.7 K. Take the solution's mass as 100.0 g and c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Calculate the enthalpy change per mole of water formed.

```
q = mcΔT = 100.0 × 4.18 × 6.7 = 2800.6 J = 2.8006 kJ
n(HCl) = 1.00 × 50.0/1000 = 0.0500 mol   (NaOH is in excess, so HCl limits)
n(H₂O) = 0.0500 mol
ΔH = −2.8006 ÷ 0.0500 = −56.0 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The temperature change was given to only 2 significant figures, so report **ΔH = −56 kJ mol⁻¹**.
The specification expects you to report results only to the limits of the least accurate
measurement.

### Worked example 2: combustion of an alcohol

A spirit burner of butan-1-ol (Mr = 74.0) loses 0.592 g as it heats 150 g of water. The water
temperature rises by 25.0 K. c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate ΔcH of butan-1-ol.

```
q = 150 × 4.18 × 25.0 = 15 675 J = 15.675 kJ
n(butan-1-ol) = 0.592 ÷ 74.0 = 0.00800 mol
ΔcH = −15.675 ÷ 0.00800 = −1959 kJ mol⁻¹ ≈ −1960 kJ mol⁻¹ (3 s.f.)
```

Here m is the mass of **water**, not of fuel. The value is less exothermic than a data-book value
because of heat loss to the surroundings, incomplete combustion and evaporation of alcohol.

### Required practical 2: measurement of an enthalpy change

The specification suggests reactions such as dissolving potassium chloride or sodium carbonate,
neutralising NaOH with HCl, the displacement reaction between CuSO₄ and Zn, and combustion of
alcohols. For a reaction in solution, a typical method is:

1. Measure a known volume of one solution into a polystyrene cup with a lid, inside a beaker for
   stability.
2. Record its temperature every 30 seconds for a few minutes to get a steady starting value.
3. At a recorded time, add the second reagent (or a weighed solid) and stir.
4. Keep recording the temperature every 30 seconds until it has clearly peaked and started to fall.
5. Plot temperature against time. Draw a best-fit line through the cooling points and **extrapolate
   it back to the time of mixing**. The difference between this extrapolated value and the starting
   temperature is ΔT.

Extrapolating corrects for heat lost while the mixture was heating up. The polystyrene and lid
reduce heat transfer.

## 3.1.4.3 Applications of Hess's law

**Hess's law**: the enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the
initial and final conditions are the same.

This lets you find ΔH for reactions you cannot measure directly. You build a cycle in which the
direct route and an indirect route start and finish at the same substances.

### Using enthalpies of formation

Both reactants and products can be formed from the same elements, so the cycle gives:

```
ΔH = ΣΔfH⦵(products) − ΣΔfH⦵(reactants)
```

**Worked example 3.** Calculate ΔH for Fe₂O₃(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(s) + 3CO₂(g).
Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH⦵ Fe₂O₃(s) = −824, CO(g) = −111, CO₂(g) = −394.

```
Products:  2(0) + 3(−394) = −1182
Reactants: (−824) + 3(−111) = −1157
ΔH = −1182 − (−1157) = −25 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Fe(s) is an element in its standard state, so its ΔfH⦵ is zero.

### Using enthalpies of combustion

Both reactants and products burn to the same combustion products, so the arrows point **down to**
the combustion products and the cycle gives:

```
ΔH = ΣΔcH⦵(reactants) − ΣΔcH⦵(products)
```

**Worked example 4.** Calculate ΔfH⦵ of propane: 3C(s) + 4H₂(g) → C₃H₈(g).
Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔcH⦵ C(s) = −394, H₂(g) = −286, C₃H₈(g) = −2220.

```
Reactants: 3(−394) + 4(−286) = −1182 − 1144 = −2326
Product:   −2220
ΔfH⦵ = −2326 − (−2220) = −106 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The subtraction is the opposite way round to the formation rule, so draw the cycle every time.

### Hess's law with calorimetry

The specification's suggested practicals include the hydration of CuSO₄ or MgSO₄ and the thermal
decomposition of NaHCO₃. You cannot measure these directly in a cup: it is hard to add exactly the right
amount of water to a solid and measure its temperature change, and a decomposition needs continuous
heating. Instead you measure two reactions that you
**can** do in a cup and combine them.

**Worked example 5.** For CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s):

```
ΔH₁: CuSO₄(s) + aq → CuSO₄(aq)                  measured: −67 kJ mol⁻¹
ΔH₂: CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) + aq → CuSO₄(aq) + 5H₂O(l)    measured: +12 kJ mol⁻¹
Route: anhydrous → solution directly = anhydrous → hydrated → solution
ΔH₁ = ΔH(hydration) + ΔH₂
ΔH(hydration) = ΔH₁ − ΔH₂ = −67 − (+12) = −79 kJ mol⁻¹
```

## 3.1.4.4 Bond enthalpies

**Mean bond enthalpy**: the enthalpy change when **1 mole** of a stated covalent bond is **broken in
gaseous molecules**, **averaged over a range of different compounds** that contain that bond.

Breaking bonds is always endothermic and making bonds is always exothermic, so:

```
ΔH = Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds made)
```

This gives an **approximate** ΔH and only applies to reactions in the **gaseous phase**.

**Worked example 6.** Use mean bond enthalpies to estimate ΔH for C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g).
Data (kJ mol⁻¹): C=C 612, C–C 348, C–H 412, H–H 436.

```
Broken: 1 C=C + 4 C–H + 1 H–H = 612 + 1648 + 436 = 2696
Made:   1 C–C + 6 C–H         = 348 + 2472       = 2820
ΔH = 2696 − 2820 = −124 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Cancelling the four C–H bonds present on both sides gives the same answer: 1048 − 1172 = −124.

### Why bond enthalpy answers differ from Hess's law answers

- **Mean values.** Each bond enthalpy is averaged over many compounds. The actual strength of a
  bond in one particular molecule differs from that average, because it depends on the rest of the
  molecule.
- **States.** Bond enthalpies apply to gases. If a substance in the real reaction is a liquid or
  solid (such as H₂O(l)), the bond enthalpy method ignores the enthalpy change of the change of state.

ΔfH⦵ and ΔcH⦵ data refer to the actual substances, so the Hess's law value is more accurate.

## Common errors

- Using the mass of the fuel or solid as m in q = mcΔT. Use the mass of the water or solution.
- Forgetting to convert J to kJ before dividing by moles, giving an answer 1000 times too big.
- Leaving out the sign of ΔH, or giving a positive value when the temperature rose.
- Dividing by moles of the reagent in excess rather than the limiting one.
- Writing a formation equation from compounds (such as CO₂ or H₂O) instead of elements, or forming
  more than 1 mole of the product.
- Giving ΔfH⦵ of an element a non-zero value, or giving O₃(g) or Br₂(g) a value of zero.
- Reversing the subtraction in combustion-based Hess's law calculations.
- Miscounting bonds: draw displayed formulas and multiply by each coefficient.
- Quoting more significant figures than the least precise measurement allows.
