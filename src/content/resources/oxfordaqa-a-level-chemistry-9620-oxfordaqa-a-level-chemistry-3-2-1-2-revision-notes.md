These notes condense **3.2.1.2 Physical properties of Period 3 elements** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification: the trends in atomic radius, first ionisation energy and melting point from Na to Ar, and the reasons for them in terms of structure and bonding. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1.

For model answers and worked comparisons, read the [Period 3 properties study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-2-1-2/). ## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Atomic radius | A measure of the size of an atom (compare like with like: Na to Cl) |
| First ionisation energy | Energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions |
| Shielding | Repulsion of outer electrons by inner electrons, reducing the attraction of the nucleus |
| Metallic bonding | Attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons |
| Macromolecular | Giant covalent lattice (silicon) |
| Van der Waals forces | Weak forces between molecules caused by temporary dipoles |

## The trends in one line each

- **Atomic radius**: decreases from Na to Cl.
- **First ionisation energy**: general increase from Na (lowest) to Ar (highest), with dips at Al and S.
- **Melting point**: rises Na < Mg < Al, peaks at Si, drops sharply to P, rises slightly to S, then falls through Cl to Ar (lowest).

## Explanation checklist: radius and ionisation energy

Include all three:

- nuclear charge increases (one more proton each step)
- shielding is similar (same number of inner electrons; outer electrons all in shell 3)
- so attraction of the nucleus for the outer electrons increases

Then conclude: electrons pulled closer (smaller radius) or held more tightly (higher ionisation energy).

## The two dips

| Dip | Electron removed | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Mg (738) → Al (578) | Al loses a 3p electron; Mg loses a 3s electron | 3p is higher in energy than 3s, and partly shielded by the 3s electrons |
| P (1012) → S (1000) | S loses a paired 3p electron | repulsion between the two electrons in the same orbital |

Equation format: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻. State symbols are needed.

## Melting point checklist

| Element(s) | Structure | What is overcome | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na, Mg, Al | giant metallic | metallic bonding | increases: higher ion charge, more delocalised electrons, smaller ions |
| Si | macromolecular | many strong covalent bonds | very strong (highest) |
| P₄, S₈, Cl₂ | simple molecular | van der Waals forces between molecules | weak; more electrons per molecule = stronger |
| Ar | monatomic | van der Waals forces between atoms | weakest (lowest) |

Electrons per particle: S₈ 128, P₄ 60, Cl₂ 34, Ar 18. That matches the order S > P > Cl > Ar.

## Pairwise comparisons to practise

| Comparison | Key reason |
|---|---|
| Na atom larger than Mg atom | Mg has one more proton, similar shielding, so outer electrons pulled closer |
| Si first ionisation energy above Al | both lose a 3p electron; Si has greater nuclear charge, similar shielding |
| Cl first ionisation energy above S | greater nuclear charge, similar shielding; S also loses a paired electron |
| Al melting point above Mg | Al³⁺ has higher charge, three delocalised electrons per atom |
| Si melting point above Al | covalent bonds throughout a giant lattice must be broken |
| P melting point above Cl | P₄ has more electrons than Cl₂, so stronger van der Waals forces |
| Cl melting point above Ar | Cl₂ has more electrons than an Ar atom |

## Exam traps

- "Covalent bonds break when sulfur melts." They do not; only van der Waals forces are overcome.
- "Silicon has strong intermolecular forces." It has no molecules; it has covalent bonds throughout.
- "Shielding decreases across the period." It stays about the same.
- "Atoms get smaller because there are fewer shells." All have three occupied shells.
- Missing state symbols in ionisation energy equations.
- Explaining the S dip with "a new sub-shell": that is the Al dip.
- Comparing P and S melting points by "bond strength" without saying which forces.

## Quick self-test

1. Describe the trend in atomic radius from Na to Cl.
2. Explain that trend.
3. Write the equation for the first ionisation energy of magnesium.
4. Explain why aluminium has a lower first ionisation energy than magnesium.
5. Explain why sulfur has a lower first ionisation energy than phosphorus.
6. Which Period 3 element has the highest first ionisation energy?
7. Describe the structure of silicon and explain its high melting point.
8. Explain why sulfur has a higher melting point than phosphorus.
9. What is overcome when chlorine melts?
10. Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium.
11. Explain why argon has the lowest melting point in Period 3.
12. Which Period 3 element has the lowest first ionisation energy, and why?

### Answers

1. It **decreases**.
2. Nuclear charge increases while shielding stays similar (outer electrons all in the third shell), so the outer electrons are attracted more strongly and pulled closer.
3. **Mg(g) → Mg⁺(g) + e⁻**
4. The electron removed from aluminium is in the 3p sub-shell, which is higher in energy than 3s (and partly shielded by 3s electrons), so it is easier to remove.
5. In sulfur, the electron removed is from a 3p orbital containing a pair of electrons; repulsion between the pair makes it easier to remove.
6. **Argon**.
7. Macromolecular (giant covalent), each Si bonded to four others; many strong covalent bonds must be broken to melt it.
8. S₈ molecules have more electrons than P₄ molecules, so the van der Waals forces between S₈ molecules are stronger.
9. The van der Waals forces between Cl₂ molecules (not the Cl–Cl bond).
10. Mg²⁺ has a higher charge and is smaller than Na⁺, and magnesium has two delocalised electrons per atom, so its metallic bonding is stronger.
11. It is monatomic with only 18 electrons per atom, so the van der Waals forces between atoms are the weakest.
12. **Sodium**: it has the smallest nuclear charge in the period with similar shielding, and its single 3s electron is the furthest from the nucleus, so it is held least strongly.
