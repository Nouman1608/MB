## A1.2.1 DNA in all living things

Every living organism uses DNA as its genetic material. Some viruses use RNA instead, but viruses are not considered living, so the statement holds. This is worth saying explicitly when a question asks about "all organisms".

## A1.2.2 and A1.2.3 Inside a nucleotide

A nucleotide has three parts: a **phosphate group**, a **pentose sugar** and a **nitrogenous base**. The carbons of the sugar are numbered 1′ to 5′ ("one prime" to "five prime"), and the numbering tells you where everything attaches:

- the **base** is attached to carbon 1′;
- the **phosphate** is attached to carbon 5′;
- carbon 3′ carries an –OH group that links to the phosphate of the next nucleotide.

In a **condensation reaction**, the phosphate of an incoming nucleotide bonds covalently to the 3′ carbon of the sugar at the end of the chain, releasing water. Repeating this builds a **sugar–phosphate backbone**: sugar, phosphate, sugar, phosphate, joined by covalent bonds all the way along. The bases project to the side and do not form part of the backbone.

**Why the backbone matters.** Covalent bonds are strong, so the order of bases is held stably, and the sequence of information does not change when the strands separate.

**Drawing convention.** The guide expects simple shapes: a circle for phosphate, a pentagon for the sugar and a rectangle for the base. Join the phosphate to one corner of the pentagon (carbon 5′) and the base to the opposite corner (carbon 1′).

### Worked example 1: counting bonds and water

A single-stranded RNA molecule has 120 nucleotides. A linear double-stranded DNA fragment has 40 base pairs.

1. Joining 120 nucleotides in one chain needs 119 condensation reactions, so 119 water molecules are released and there are 119 sugar–phosphate links.
2. The DNA fragment contains 2 × 40 = 80 nucleotides in two strands of 40.
3. Each strand needs 39 condensations, so the fragment has 2 × 39 = 78 sugar–phosphate links. The two strands are held to each other by hydrogen bonds between bases, not by covalent bonds.

## A1.2.4 and A1.2.5 Bases and RNA

DNA uses adenine, cytosine, guanine and thymine; RNA uses adenine, cytosine, guanine and uracil. The bases are the only part that varies between nucleotides, so the **sequence of bases** is the code. RNA is a polymer formed by condensation of nucleotides in the same way as DNA, and it is usually single-stranded.

## A1.2.6 to A1.2.8 The double helix and pairing in use

DNA has two **antiparallel** strands wound into a double helix and linked by **hydrogen bonds between complementary bases**: adenine with thymine, cytosine with guanine.

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Number of strands | Two | One |
| Pentose | Deoxyribose (no –OH on carbon 2′) | Ribose (–OH on carbon 2′) |
| Base unique to it | Thymine | Uracil |

**What complementary pairing allows (A1.2.8).**

- **Replication:** each strand acts as a template, and only the complementary nucleotide fits each position, so both copies carry the same sequence.
- **Expression:** in transcription, RNA nucleotides pair with one DNA strand (uracil with adenine), copying the base sequence into mRNA; in translation, tRNA anticodons pair with mRNA codons.

Hydrogen bonds are individually weak, so strands can be separated for copying and then re-form. That combination of a strong backbone and weak cross-links is the key structural point.

### Worked example 2: base composition from pairing

In a sample of double-stranded DNA, 23% of the bases are adenine. Find the other percentages.

1. A pairs with T, so T = 23%.
2. A + T = 46%, so G + C = 100 − 46 = 54%.
3. G pairs with C, so G = C = 54 ÷ 2 = 27%.

If a sample has A = 23% but T = 31%, the bases are not paired one-to-one, so the nucleic acid is single-stranded.

## A1.2.9 and A1.2.10 Information and common ancestry

Any base can follow any other, and molecules can be very long, so the number of possible sequences is effectively limitless. A sequence of n bases has 4ⁿ possible orders, and each extra base multiplies the number of possibilities by four.

The genetic code, the relationship between codons and amino acids, is the same in almost all organisms. A shared, arbitrary code is best explained by inheritance from a common ancestor, so it is evidence of **universal common ancestry**.

## A1.2.11 to A1.2.13 (HL only) Structure in more detail

**Directionality (A1.2.11).** Because each link joins carbon 3′ of one sugar to the phosphate on carbon 5′ of the next, a strand has a 5′ end (free phosphate) and a 3′ end (free –OH). The two strands in DNA run in opposite directions. Nucleotides are added only to the 3′ end, which is why replication and transcription proceed 5′ to 3′, and translation reads mRNA 5′ to 3′.

**Purine–pyrimidine pairing (A1.2.12).** Adenine and guanine are purines with two rings; cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines with one ring. Every base pair contains one of each, so every pair is the same width. The helix therefore has a uniform diameter whatever the sequence, which contributes to its stability.

**Nucleosome (A1.2.13).** In eukaryotes, DNA is wound around a core of eight histone proteins; together they form a nucleosome. Another histone binds the linker DNA between nucleosomes. The positively charged histones attract the negatively charged phosphate groups of the backbone. When viewing a nucleosome in molecular visualisation software, look for the DNA coiled around the histone core and the points of contact with the phosphates.

## A1.2.14 and A1.2.15 (HL only) Evidence

**Hershey and Chase (1952).** They asked whether bacteriophage DNA or protein enters a bacterium and directs the production of new viruses. Protein contains sulfur but no phosphorus; DNA contains phosphorus but no sulfur. One set of phages was labelled with ³⁵S and another with ³²P. After infection, a blender removed the phage coats and centrifugation separated the bacteria (pellet) from the coats (supernatant). Most ³²P was found with the bacteria and most ³⁵S in the supernatant, supporting DNA as the genetic material. The experiment depended on radioisotopes becoming available as research tools.

**Chargaff's data.** Across diverse species, the amount of adenine roughly equals thymine, and guanine roughly equals cytosine, so purines roughly equal pyrimidines. The ratio of (A + T) to (G + C) differs between species. These data falsified the tetranucleotide hypothesis, which proposed a repeating four-base unit and so could not store varied information.

### Worked example 3 (HL): interpreting base-composition data

Invented data, percentage of each base:

| Source | A | T | G | C | U |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Species 1 | 30.4 | 29.8 | 19.7 | 20.1 | 0 |
| Species 2 | 21.0 | 20.6 | 29.1 | 29.3 | 0 |
| Virus 3 | 24.6 | 0 | 24.1 | 18.5 | 32.8 |

1. Species 1 and 2: A ≈ T and G ≈ C, consistent with double-stranded DNA.
2. Purines: Species 1, A + G = 50.1%; Species 2, A + G = 50.1%. Purines ≈ pyrimidines.
3. (A + T) ÷ (G + C) is 60.2 ÷ 39.8 = 1.51 for Species 1 and 41.6 ÷ 58.4 = 0.71 for Species 2: base composition is species-specific, which a fixed repeating tetranucleotide could not produce.
4. Virus 3 contains uracil and no thymine, so it is RNA; A ≠ U and G ≠ C, so it is single-stranded.

**Nature of science.** Data like these cannot prove that A pairs with T in every organism (no number of observations proves a generalisation), but a reliable counterexample can falsify a hypothesis.

## Common mistakes

- Putting the bases in the backbone.
- Saying the two strands are joined by covalent bonds.
- Writing that RNA contains thymine or deoxyribose.
- Attaching the phosphate to carbon 3′ in a single-nucleotide diagram.
- Applying A = T to RNA or single-stranded DNA.
- Claiming Chargaff's data proved base pairing.

## Exam technique for IB command terms

- **Draw**: use the circle, pentagon and rectangle convention with the bonds in the right places.
- **Distinguish**: give the difference for both molecules, as in the DNA–RNA table.
- **Explain**: link structure to function, such as weak hydrogen bonds allowing separation.
- **Calculate**: show each pairing step.
- **Evaluate** (HL evidence): give what the data support and their limits.
