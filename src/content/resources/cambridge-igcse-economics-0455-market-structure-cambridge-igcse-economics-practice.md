## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define a monopoly. **[2]**

**2.** Which is most likely in a market with a high number of firms selling similar products?

A: high prices and high profits

B: low prices and wide choice

C: few substitutes for each firm's product

D: strong barriers to entry

**[1]**

**3.** Identify **two** barriers to entry. **[2]**

**4.** A firm sells 42,000 units in a market where total sales are 60,000. Calculate its market share. **[1]**

**5.** Which industry is the clearest example of a natural monopoly?

A: hairdressing

B: restaurants

C: the supply of water through pipes

D: clothing retail

**[1]**

**6.** State **one** reason why a monopoly may have little incentive to improve quality. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Six cafes in a town each sell 15,000 cups of coffee a year at a price of 3.00. The cost per cup is 2.00.

**(a)** Calculate the profit of each cafe. **[1]**

**(b)** Four new cafes open. The price falls to 2.40 and total sales in the town rise to 110,000 cups, shared equally. Calculate the new profit of each cafe. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the new cafes opened and why profit per cafe fell. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how a high number of firms in a market may affect the quality of products and the choice available to consumers. **[4]**

**9.** A single railway company runs all trains between two cities. It charges 40 per journey and its cost per journey is 22. It carries 500,000 passengers a year.

**(a)** Calculate its yearly profit. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **two** characteristics of monopoly shown by this company. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why a patent can give a firm monopoly power. **[3]**

**11.** Analyse why a monopoly may be able to produce at lower cost than a market with many small firms. **[5]**

**12.** Discuss whether consumers are better off when a market changes from a monopoly to one with many competing firms. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A market with only one seller [1]; of a product with no close substitutes, often protected by barriers to entry [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two: patents or licences [1]; high start-up costs [1]; economies of scale of the existing firm; control of raw materials; brand loyalty. **[2]**

**4.** 42,000 / 60,000 x 100 = **70%** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** C [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A single pipe network is cheaper than duplicate networks. Hairdressing, restaurants and clothing shops have many firms and low start-up costs.

**6.** Consumers have no alternative supplier to switch to, so the firm keeps its sales even if quality is poor [1]. **[1]**

**7. (a)** (3.00 - 2.00) x 15,000 = **15,000** [1].

**(b)** Sales per cafe = 110,000 / 10 = 11,000 [1]; profit = (2.40 - 2.00) x 11,000 = **4,400** [1].

**(c)** High profits of 15,000 per cafe attracted new firms into the market [1]. With more cafes competing, each had to cut its price to keep customers, so profit per cup fell from 1.00 to 0.40 [1]. Sales were shared among more firms, so each cafe sold fewer cups, reducing profit further [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Firms compete for customers, so each tries to offer better quality or service than its rivals [1]; a firm whose quality falls loses customers to others [1]. Many firms offer different varieties, styles and locations [1], so consumers can choose the product that best suits their wants [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** (40 - 22) x 500,000 = **9,000,000** [1].

**(b)** It is the only seller of rail journeys between the cities [1], so passengers wanting to travel by train cannot switch to a rival [1]. It is a price maker [1]: it can charge 40, well above its cost of 22, and earn high profits because no rival can undercut it [1]. (Accept barriers to entry, such as the high cost of building track, developed.) **[5]**

**10.** A patent gives the inventor the legal right to be the only producer of a new product for a period [1]. Other firms cannot legally copy it [1], so the patent holder faces no competition and can set a high price [1]. **[3]**

**11.** A monopoly produces the whole market output, so it operates on a much larger scale [1]. It can buy materials in bulk at lower prices [1], use large specialised machinery [1] and borrow more cheaply [1]. These economies of scale lower its cost per unit below that of many small firms, each producing only a small share [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Better off: competition pushes prices down because buyers can switch to cheaper rivals [1]; firms compete on quality and service, and choice widens [1]; profits are competed down, so less of consumers' spending goes to profit [1].
Worse off: small firms may lose the monopoly's economies of scale, so costs and prices could rise [1]; in a natural monopoly, duplicating networks raises cost per customer [1].
Judgement: consumers are usually better off where the industry offers few economies of scale, such as retail, but may be worse off in network industries, where a regulated monopoly can be cheaper [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing competition or monopoly diagrams that the syllabus does not require.
- Saying a monopoly can charge any price without losing sales.
- Giving the four effects of competition as a list without reasons.
- Forgetting to divide total sales among the new number of firms after entry.
- Writing that monopoly is always bad, with no mention of economies of scale or regulation.
