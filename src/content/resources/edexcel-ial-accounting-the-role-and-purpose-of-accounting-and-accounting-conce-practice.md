## Questions

**1.** State the **three** parts of the role of accounting. **[3]**

**2.** Fenmark Tiles has an owner, a bank lender and a tile supplier. Identify **two** of these users. For each, state one piece of accounting information they would want and why. **[4]**

**3.** Yelverton Joinery reports the following.

```
                     Month 1    Month 2
Revenue               36,000     42,000
Cost of sales         21,600     27,300
```

**(a)** Calculate gross profit as a percentage of revenue for each month. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **one** way the manager could use the result. **[2]**

**4.** Name the accounting concept applied in each case.

**(a)** Yelverton Joinery records fitted doors as revenue in December, when they are installed, though the customer pays in February. **[1]**
**(b)** The owner's family holiday is paid from the business bank account and recorded as drawings. **[1]**
**(c)** A damaged batch of timber is valued at its expected selling price less repair costs, not at cost. **[1]**
**(d)** The financial statements spread the cost of a van over its useful life because the business is expected to keep trading. **[1]**
**(e)** The method used to calculate the allowance for irrecoverable debts is the same as last year. **[1]**

**5.** Sunderby Kites has a year end of 31 December. On 1 September it paid 2,400 for twelve months of insurance. Electricity used in December was 290, but the bill arrives in January.

**(a)** Calculate the insurance expense for the year and the prepayment at the year end. **[3]**
**(b)** State the amount and the classification of the electricity at the year end. **[2]**

**6.** Sunderby Kites holds three inventory lines at the year end.

```
Line              Units   Cost   Selling price   Selling costs
Stunt kites          40     22              30               4
Delta kites          60     15              17               3
Spare line sets     100      6               5               0
```

Costs and prices are per unit.

**(a)** Value each line using the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV). **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate total inventory and the effect on profit compared with valuing at cost. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the concept applied. **[1]**

**7.** Hollowell Print bought land for 150,000. An agent now values it at 210,000.

**(a)** State the amount at which the land is recorded under historic cost. **[1]**
**(b)** State **one** advantage and **one** limitation of historic cost. **[2]**
**(c)** The company's most experienced press operator is a major reason for its success. Explain why this is not in the statements. **[3]**

**8.** Veldane Cycles has revenue of 640,000 and treats an item as material if it costs at least 0.5% of revenue.

**(a)** Calculate the threshold. **[1]**
**(b)** A workshop pump costing 380 is expected to last five years. State and justify its treatment. **[2]**
**(c)** A machine costs 9,600. State its treatment and the percentage of revenue. **[1]**

**9.** Veldane Cycles has a year end of 31 October. A customer orders a bike on 12 October for 4,800 and pays a deposit of 1,200 on 14 October. The bike is delivered on 6 November and the balance is paid on 20 December.

**(a)** State the revenue recognised in the year to 31 October. **[1]**
**(b)** State how the deposit is shown at 31 October. **[1]**
**(c)** State the revenue recognised in November. **[1]**
**(d)** State the trade receivables balance on 30 November. **[1]**
**(e)** Name the concept applied. **[1]**

**10.** Brackwen Candles owes a bank loan that has been called in. The owner expects to close in two months. Machinery has a carrying value of 18,000 and would sell for 6,500. Inventory costing 9,000 would sell for 5,000.

**(a)** Explain the going concern concept. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the reduction in asset values if the statements were prepared on the basis that the business will close. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why users need this information. **[1]**

**11.** (a) State the IAS term for each: stock, debtors, net book value and bad debts. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain **two** benefits of businesses using International Accounting Standards. **[2]**

**12.** Marnock Pottery has a year end of 31 December and a draft profit of 31,500. The following errors are found.

- Pots that cost 240 were taken by the owner for family use and charged as an expense.
- A customer deposit of 900 for pots to be delivered in January was included in revenue.
- Inventory at cost of 4,200 includes damaged pots that cost 600. They can be sold for 350 after repairs costing nothing further.
- Insurance of 1,800 for twelve months from 1 October was all charged to this year.

**(a)** For each error, name the concept breached and state the adjustment to profit. **[8]**
**(b)** Calculate the corrected profit. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Record [1], analyse [1], communicate [1] information to users. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that say only "keep records" tend to earn one point, so give the three verbs in the order the specification uses.

**2.** Owner [1]: wants profit for the year [1], to decide on drawings or reinvestment. Lender [1]: wants evidence of ability to repay [1], to judge whether interest and capital will be paid. Other pairs are acceptable, such as supplier and trade payables position, to decide whether to supply on credit. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A named user with a vague need ("how the business is doing") is weaker than a link between that user and a specific figure.

**3.** **(a)** Month 1: 36,000 - 21,600 = 14,400 [1]; 14,400 / 36,000 = **40.0%** [1]. Month 2: 42,000 - 27,300 = 14,700 [1]; 14,700 / 42,000 = **35.0%** [1].
**(b)** The margin fell by five points although gross profit rose [1]. The manager could investigate supplier prices or discounting, and decide whether to raise selling prices [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A full calculation line showing gross profit first helps a marker see your method even if the final percentage slips.

**4.** **(a)** Realisation [1]. **(b)** Business entity [1]. **(c)** Prudence [1]. **(d)** Going concern [1]. **(e)** Consistency [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A single concept name is enough for each part, but one chosen from the wrong clue of the scenario will not gain credit.

**5.** **(a)** Monthly cost = 2,400 / 12 = 200 [1]. Four months used (September to December): 4 x 200 = **800** expense [1]. Prepayment = 2,400 - 800 = **1,600** [1].
**(b)** An accrued expense of **290** [1], shown as an other payable (a current liability) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Counting months inclusively from the payment date is the usual slip, so write the months out before multiplying.

**6.** **(a)** Stunt kites: net realisable value (NRV) = 30 - 4 = 26, cost 22 is lower, so 40 x 22 = **880** [1]. Delta kites: NRV = 17 - 3 = 14, lower than 15, so 60 x 14 = **840** [1]. Spare line sets: NRV = 5, lower than 6, so 100 x 5 = **500** [1].
**(b)** Total = 880 + 840 + 500 = **2,220** [1]. Cost = 2,380, so profit is lower by **160** [1].
**(c)** Prudence [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparing cost with NRV line by line, not on the total, is what this question rewards; a total-only comparison hides the 40 stunt kites staying at cost.

**7.** **(a)** **150,000** [1].
**(b)** Advantage: objective and can be checked to an invoice [1]. Limitation: may understate the current value of assets in a period of rising prices [1].
**(c)** Money measurement [1]. The operator's skill cannot be given a reliable amount in money [1], so it is not recorded as an asset [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the concept alone does not explain why the item is absent, so add the reason in a second sentence.

**8.** **(a)** 0.5% x 640,000 = **3,200** [1].
**(b)** 380 is below 3,200 (0.06% of revenue) [1], so it is immaterial and is treated as an expense, though it lasts five years [1].
**(c)** 9,600 / 640,000 = 1.5%, which is above the threshold, so it is material and is recorded as a non-current asset [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the comparison with the threshold, not just the label "material", is what supports the conclusion.

**9.** **(a)** **0** [1]. **(b)** A current liability (other payables), as goods are not yet supplied [1]. **(c)** **4,800** [1]. **(d)** 4,800 - 1,200 = **3,600** [1]. **(e)** Realisation [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Candidates often treat the deposit as revenue because cash arrived; tie your answer to the delivery date.

**10.** **(a)** Trading is expected to carry on for the foreseeable future [1], so assets stay at carrying value and costs are spread over their lives [1].
**(b)** Machinery: 18,000 - 6,500 = 11,500 [1]. Inventory: 9,000 - 5,000 = 4,000 [1]. Total reduction **15,500** [1].
**(c)** Users such as the bank or suppliers would otherwise overestimate what is available to repay them [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Applying the figures to the scenario, instead of restating a definition, is usually what separates stronger answers here.

**11.** **(a)** Inventory [1]; trade receivables [1]; carrying value [1]; irrecoverable debts [1].
**(b)** Two benefits, each stated and explained. For example, comparability between businesses, because the same rules are applied [1]; trust in the statements, because they follow recognised rules and not the preparer's choice [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The specification says candidate responses should use IAS terms, so older terms in your own answers are a risk.

**12.** **(a)** Owner's use: business entity [1]; add back 240 to profit [1]. Deposit: realisation [1]; reduce profit by 900 [1]. Damaged pots: prudence [1]; NRV 350, write-down 600 - 350 = 250, so reduce profit by 250 [1]. Insurance: accruals [1]; 9 months unused = 1,800 / 12 x 9 = 1,350, so increase profit by 1,350 [1].
**(b)** 31,500 + 240 - 900 - 250 + 1,350 [1] = **31,940** [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing each adjustment with its sign, then summing, lets a marker follow your working even if one item is misread.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a concept without applying it to the scenario.
- Naming users without linking them to a figure or need.
- Counting the wrong number of months for a prepayment.
- Treating a deposit as revenue before delivery.
- Using prudence to justify understating profit.
- Writing market value when historic cost is asked.
- Using older terms such as stock, debtors and net book value.
- Stating a result as material or immaterial without a comparison.
- Adding an adjustment to profit when it should be deducted, and the reverse.
