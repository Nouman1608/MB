## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inflation | A sustained rise in the general price level |
| Disinflation | A falling but still positive inflation rate |
| Deflation | A sustained fall in the general price level (negative inflation) |
| Hyperinflation | Extremely rapid, accelerating inflation; one benchmark is over 50% a month |
| Price index | Cost of a weighted basket relative to a base period set at 100 |
| Weight | An item's share of household spending, used to scale its price change |
| Base year | The period in which the index equals 100 |
| CPI | Consumer Prices Index; the UK target measure, harmonised internationally |
| RPI | Retail Prices Index; includes mortgage interest and council tax |
| Nominal value | Value in the prices of the time |
| Real value | Value adjusted for inflation, showing purchasing power |
| Real interest rate | Roughly the nominal interest rate minus inflation |
| Demand-pull inflation | AD grows faster than productive capacity |
| Cost-push inflation | Rising costs shift SRAS to the left |
| Wage-price spiral | Wages and prices chase each other upwards |
| Fiscal drag | Frozen tax thresholds plus rising nominal incomes raise the tax burden |

## Methods to recall

**Inflation rate from an index:** (new index - old index) / old index x 100.

**Weighted index:** multiply each category's index by its weight, add, and divide by the total weight.

**Contribution of one category (percentage points):** weight / total weight x that category's percentage price change.

**Real value:** nominal value / (price index / 100).

**Exact real change:** (1 + nominal growth) / (1 + inflation) - 1. The shortcut "nominal minus inflation" is fine for small rates but say it is approximate.

**Rebasing:** to move the base to a new year, divide every value by the new base year's value and multiply by 100.

### Worked reminder: weighted index

Weights: rent 40, food 35, clothing 25. Year 2 indices: rent 108, food 103, clothing 96.
(40 x 108 + 35 x 103 + 25 x 96) / 100 = (4,320 + 3,605 + 2,400) / 100 = **103.25**, so inflation is 3.25%. Rent alone contributes 40 x 8 / 100 = 3.2 points.

### Worked reminder: rebasing

An index reads 125 in year 3 and 135 in year 5. Rebased to year 3 = 100, year 5 becomes 135 / 125 x 100 = **108**. Prices rose 8% between the two years.

### Worked reminder: real interest

Nominal rate 5%, inflation 2%: real rate about **+3%** (exactly 1.05 / 1.02 - 1 = 2.94%). Nominal rate 1%, inflation -2%: real rate about **+3%** as well. Deflation can make money expensive to borrow even when nominal rates are low.

## CPI and RPI: the differences that score marks

- RPI includes **mortgage interest payments and council tax**; CPI does not.
- RPI mostly uses an **arithmetic** mean of item prices; CPI mostly a **geometric** mean. The arithmetic mean gives a higher reading (the formula effect).
- CPI covers a **wider population**; RPI leaves out the highest-income households and some pensioner households.
- CPI is **internationally comparable**; RPI is UK-specific.
- A rise in interest rates can **raise RPI** through mortgage costs while it is trying to **lower** inflation.

## Causes and consequences in brief

| Cause | Diagram | Output effect |
|---|---|---|
| Demand-pull | AD shifts right | Output rises, unless at full capacity |
| Cost-push | SRAS shifts left | Output falls (stagflation risk) |
| Excess money growth | Shows as sustained AD growth | Mostly prices, not output, in the long run |

| Consequence | Who is affected | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Redistribution | Savers, lenders and fixed-income groups lose; borrowers gain | Whether inflation was anticipated |
| Uncertainty | Firms planning investment | How volatile inflation is |
| Competitiveness | Exporters and import-competing firms | Inflation relative to trading partners and the exchange rate |
| Menu and shoe-leather costs | Firms and households | The rate: small at 2%, huge in hyperinflation |
| Fiscal drag | Taxpayers | Whether thresholds rise with prices |

**Deflation:** "good" deflation follows productivity gains (AS shifts right, output rises). "Bad" deflation follows falling AD: delayed spending, a heavier real debt burden, higher real interest rates and job losses.

## Exam traps

- An index of 126.9 does not mean 26.9% inflation this year; it means prices are 26.9% above the base year.
- The difference between two index values is in **index points**, not per cent.
- Disinflation is still inflation. Prices fall only if the rate is negative.
- Personal inflation differs from CPI when a household's spending pattern differs from the weights.
- Higher prices of a single product are a change in relative prices, not inflation, unless the general price level rises.
- Do not assume inflation always harms growth: moderate demand-pull inflation often comes with rising output.
- Real wage falls happen when nominal pay growth is below inflation, even if pay is rising.

## Self-test

1. Inflation falls from 4.2% to 1.8%. Name this change.
2. A price index rises from 140 to 147. Calculate the inflation rate.
3. State two differences between CPI and RPI.
4. Explain why a rise in Bank Rate can increase RPI in the short run.
5. Nominal wages rise by 3% and inflation is 5%. What happens to real wages, approximately?
6. Weights: energy 10, other 90. Energy prices rise 20% and other prices 2%. Calculate the inflation rate.
7. Give one reason why the UK inflation target is above zero.
8. Distinguish demand-pull from cost-push inflation, naming the curve that shifts in each.
9. Explain why unanticipated inflation redistributes income from lenders to borrowers.
10. State one cause of "good" deflation and one of "bad" deflation.
11. An index is 160 in year 2 and 176 in year 6. Rebase so that year 2 = 100 and give the year 6 value.
12. Define hyperinflation.

### Answers

1. Disinflation: the rate falls but stays positive.
2. (147 - 140) / 140 x 100 = **5.0%**.
3. Any two: RPI includes mortgage interest and council tax; formula (arithmetic in RPI, geometric in CPI); population coverage; CPI is internationally comparable.
4. Mortgage interest payments are in the RPI basket, so higher interest rates raise measured housing costs.
5. They fall by about 2% (exactly 1.03 / 1.05 - 1 = -1.9%).
6. (10 x 120 + 90 x 102) / 100 = (1,200 + 9,180) / 100 = 103.8, so **3.8%**.
7. Any one: a margin above deflation; lets real wages adjust without nominal cuts; the index may overstate true inflation because of quality changes.
8. Demand-pull: AD shifts right faster than capacity grows. Cost-push: SRAS shifts left because costs rise.
9. Loans are fixed in money terms, so higher-than-expected inflation means the money repaid buys less than the lender expected.
10. Good: productivity gains or cheaper technology shift AS right. Bad: a fall in AD, for example after a collapse in confidence.
11. 176 / 160 x 100 = **110**.
12. Extremely rapid, usually accelerating inflation that stops money working well; one benchmark is prices rising by more than 50% a month.
