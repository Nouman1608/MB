These notes cover Forensic Psychology for AQA A-level Psychology (7182), from the AQA AS and A-level Psychology (7181, 7182) specification, Version 1.4 (1 September 2025), for teaching from September 2025. The content is section 4.3.9, topic 16 in Option 3. It is assessed in Paper 3: Issues and Options in Psychology, where Section D is "one topic from option 3, 15–17 above, multiple choice, short answer and extended writing, 24 marks". Paper 3 is a 2-hour written exam, 96 marks in total, 33.3% of A-level.

## What the specification lists (4.3.9)

1. Offender profiling: the typology approach, including organised and disorganised types; the data-driven approach, including investigative Psychology; geographical profiling.
2. Biological explanations of offending behaviour: genetics and neural explanations.
3. Psychological explanations of offending behaviour: Eysenck's theory of the criminal personality; cognitive explanations; level of moral reasoning and cognitive distortions, including hostile attribution bias and minimalisation; differential association theory.
4. Dealing with offending behaviour: the aims of custodial sentencing and the psychological effects of custodial sentencing. Behaviour modification in custody. Anger management and restorative justice programmes.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Offender profiling | Using crime-scene and case evidence to infer the likely characteristics of an unknown offender |
| Typology (top-down) approach | Matching a crime scene to a pre-existing offender type (organised or disorganised) |
| Organised offender | Plans the crime, controls the victim, leaves few clues; socially competent |
| Disorganised offender | Little planning, spontaneous scene, evidence left behind; often lives alone, near the scene |
| Data-driven (bottom-up) approach | Building a profile from statistical patterns in databases of past crimes |
| Investigative psychology | Canter's statistical approach, using ideas such as interpersonal coherence and forensic awareness |
| Interpersonal coherence | Behaviour towards the victim reflects behaviour in everyday life |
| Forensic awareness | Signs the offender knows police methods, such as hiding evidence |
| Geographical profiling | Using crime locations to predict the offender's home or work base |
| Marauder / commuter | Offends near home (inside the circle) / travels away from home to offend |
| Concordance | The extent to which both twins in a pair share a characteristic |
| Diathesis-stress | A genetic vulnerability leads to offending only with an environmental trigger |
| Prefrontal cortex | Brain area involved in impulse control and emotional regulation |
| EPQ | Eysenck Personality Questionnaire, measuring E, N and P |
| Pre-conventional morality | Kohlberg's lowest level: reasoning based on punishment and reward |
| Hostile attribution bias | Interpreting ambiguous behaviour of others as hostile |
| Minimalisation | Downplaying the seriousness or harm of one's own offence |
| Differential association | Offending is learned through attitudes and techniques from close groups |
| Institutionalisation | Losing the ability to cope with life outside prison |
| Prisonisation | Adopting the norms and values of prison culture |
| Token economy | Operant conditioning: tokens for target behaviours, exchanged for privileges |
| Restorative justice | Offender and victim meet with a mediator; the offender takes responsibility |

## Theories and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ressler, Burgess and Douglas (1988) | Interviews with 36 sexually motivated murderers | Basis of organised/disorganised typology | Small, self-report sample |
| Canter et al. (2004) | Analysis of 100 US serial murders | Organised features common; no distinct disorganised type | Challenges the two-type model |
| Copson (1995) | Survey of UK police | Profiles often judged useful, rarely led to identification | Questions practical value |
| Canter and Larkin (1993) | Circle theory | Marauder and commuter patterns | Location alone does not name the offender |
| Lundrigan and Canter (2001) | Body-disposal sites in serial murders | Home often near centre of sites | Supports geographical profiling |
| Christiansen (1977) | Over 3,500 Danish twin pairs | Higher concordance for MZ than DZ | MZ twins share more similar environments |
| Mednick et al. (1984) | 14,427 Danish adoptees | Conviction rate 13.5% (no parent) to 24.5% (both) | Selective placement may confound |
| Raine et al. (1997) | PET scans, 41 murderers, 41 controls | Lower prefrontal activity | Correlational; murderers pleading insanity are a special group |
| Raine et al. (2000) | MRI scans, community volunteers with antisocial personality disorder | About 11% less prefrontal grey matter | Cause or effect unclear |
| Eysenck | E, N, P criminal personality | Poor conditioning leads to poor socialisation | Farrington et al. (1982): P consistently high, E not |
| Bartol and Holanchock (1979) | Offenders in a New York prison | Less extraverted than controls | Suggests cultural bias in Eysenck |
| Palmer and Hollin (1998) | Moral reasoning measure | Offenders less mature | Reasoning does not always predict behaviour |
| Schönenberg and Jusyte (2014) | Ambiguous facial expressions | Violent offenders saw more anger | Supports hostile attribution bias |
| Osborn and West (1979) | Conviction records of fathers and sons | Sons of convicted fathers more often convicted than sons of non-criminal fathers | Shared genes could explain it |
| Hobbs and Holt (1976) | Token economy, young offenders | Improved behaviour | Gains may not last after release |
| Sherman and Strang (2007) | Review of restorative justice | Reduced reoffending for some; more consistent for violent than property crime | Needs offender and victim consent |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Top-down vs bottom-up.** Top-down starts from FBI types; bottom-up starts from data.
- **Organised vs disorganised.** Planning and control vs spontaneity and evidence left behind.
- **Marauder vs commuter.** Inside vs outside the circle drawn through the two furthest crimes.
- **Genetic vs neural.** Inherited risk vs brain structure and activity.
- **Hostile attribution bias vs minimalisation.** About others' intentions vs about one's own crime.
- **Eysenck vs differential association.** Personality with a biological basis vs learning from close groups.
- **Aims vs effects of custody.** What prison is for vs what it does to the person.
- **Token economy vs anger management.** Changes behaviour through reinforcement vs changes thinking and coping.

## Mini essay plan

*Discuss two psychological explanations of offending behaviour.* Adapt this plan to the exact question. The specification's approximate Paper 3 weightings are 9–12% of the A-level for AO1 and 15–17% for AO3, so evaluation should take up most of your answer.

1. **AO1, brief and accurate.** Outline Eysenck (E, N, P; biological basis; poor conditioning) and cognitive distortions (hostile attribution bias, minimalisation). Keep it accurate and brief.
2. **AO3 point 1.** Eysenck and Eysenck (1977) support; Farrington et al. (1982) shows only P is consistently high; E is not.
3. **AO3 point 2.** Bartol and Holanchock (1979): cultural bias.
4. **AO3 point 3.** Schönenberg and Jusyte (2014) support hostile attribution bias.
5. **AO3 point 4.** Practical value: CBT programmes target distortions.
6. **Issues and debates.** Eysenck is more biologically determinist; cognitive explanations allow change. Interactionist view: combine with biological explanations. See the [Issues and debates guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-issues-and-debates/).
7. **Conclusion.** A balanced judgement backed by the evidence above.

## Quick self-test

1. What are the four stages of the FBI top-down approach?
2. Name two features of an organised offender.
3. What does "forensic awareness" suggest about an offender?
4. In circle theory, what is a commuter?
5. What did Mednick et al. (1984) find when both biological and adoptive parents had convictions?
6. Which brain area showed lower activity in Raine et al. (1997)?
7. What are Eysenck's three dimensions?
8. At which Kohlberg level are offenders thought more likely to reason?
9. Give an example of minimalisation.
10. Name the four aims of custodial sentencing.
11. What are the three stages of anger management in Novaco's model?

### Answers

1. Data assimilation, crime scene classification, crime reconstruction, profile generation.
2. Any two: plans the crime; selects a victim; controls the scene; leaves few clues; socially competent.
3. They may have been through the justice system before.
4. An offender who travels away from home to commit crimes.
5. 24.5% of adoptees had a conviction.
6. The prefrontal cortex.
7. Extraversion, neuroticism, psychoticism.
8. Pre-conventional.
9. Calling a burglary "borrowing", or saying a victim "wasn't really hurt".
10. Deterrence, incapacitation, retribution, rehabilitation.
11. Cognitive preparation, skill acquisition, application practice.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the typology without naming organised and disorganised features.
- Calling investigative psychology "top-down".
- Stating that twin studies "prove" offending is genetic.
- Forgetting that brain-scan research is correlational.
- Writing about MAOA in depth here when it belongs mainly to [Aggression](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-aggression/); keep it brief.
- Defining cognitive distortions but not linking them to the offender in the scenario.
- Confusing institutionalisation with prisonisation.
- Evaluating restorative justice only as "good for victims", with no evidence or limitation.
- Writing long AO1 in an extended answer and leaving little room for AO3.
