This practice set covers the six global contexts applied to design problems for IB MYP Design, aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Design*, from 2014. It suits MYP years 4 and 5 (no SL/HL split). MYP has no prescribed content list; schools design their own units. Your teacher will share the task-specific clarifications for any assessed work.

Each question is labelled with the criterion it trains. Real MYP work is judged against criterion level descriptors (achievement levels 1–8), so the `[1]` points in the answers are a revision aid, not IB marks. All people, places and data are fictional.

## Questions

**1.** (Criterion A) Name the six MYP global contexts. **[3]**

**2.** (Criterion A) For each term, state whether the brief gives it as a key concept, a related concept or a global context: (i) systems, (ii) ergonomics, (iii) Fairness and development, (iv) innovation. **[2]**

**3.** (Criterion A) Distinguish between a design situation and a problem statement. **[2]**

**4.** (Criterion A) Identify the main weakness in each problem statement.

**(a)** "Design a phone stand for the art room." **[1]**
**(b)** "Old people find things difficult." **[1]**
**(c)** "Students need less waste, more seating and faster queues at lunch." **[1]**

**5.** (Criterion A) Design situation: *"Cyclists arriving at Ferris Lane Sports Centre, a fictional centre, leave bikes against railings because the racks are always full, and several bikes have been knocked over."* Write a problem statement for this situation that names the target audience, the need, the evidence you would collect and one constraint. **[3]**

**6.** (Criterion A) The same Ferris Lane situation is explored through two different contexts: Orientation in space and time, and Fairness and development. For each context, suggest one research question it makes necessary and the kind of evidence you would collect. **[4]**

**7.** (Criterion A) A student surveys 60 residents of Alder Court, a fictional block of retirement flats, asking which daily task is hardest.

| Hardest task | Residents |
|---|---|
| Opening jars and packaging | 27 |
| Reading small print on labels | 18 |
| Carrying shopping up stairs | 9 |
| Other | 6 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of residents who chose each of the two most common tasks. **[2]**
**(b)** State which problem the data best supports pursuing, and justify your choice with the data. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest a suitable global context for this project and explain how it would shape the research. **[2]**

**8.** (Criterion D) The student builds a jar-opening aid. A success criterion linked to Fairness and development is: *"At least 80% of 20 testers open a standard jar within 15 seconds."* 17 of the 20 testers do so. The five testers with the weakest grip take 12, 14, 19, 11 and 16 seconds.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of all testers who met the target, and state whether the success criterion is met. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean time for the five weakest-grip testers. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why reporting only the overall percentage and this mean would be a weak evaluation for this context. **[2]**

**9.** (Criterion A and D) A student writes "Global context: Globalization and sustainability" on the first page of a folder for a desk lamp. Every test measures brightness and stability. Explain the weakness and describe two changes that would make the context do real work. **[4]**

**10.** (Criterion A and D) *Extended.* Design situation: *"Wendmere Library, a fictional town library, says fewer young people visit. Its records show 40 visitors aged 11–16 per week last year and 22 per week now."*

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall in weekly visitors aged 11–16. **[1]**
**(b)** Choose one global context for a project on this situation and justify your choice using two of the four context checks. **[3]**
**(c)** Write a problem statement for your chosen context. **[2]**
**(d)** Write two research questions -- one primary, one secondary -- that your context makes necessary. **[2]**
**(e)** Write two context-linked success criteria you could test. **[2]**

**11.** (Criterion A and D) *Extended.* A student's folder for a digital noticeboard in a school entrance hall is summarised below.

> Situation given: new students get lost in the first week. Context chosen: Identities and relationships. Problem statement: "Make a screen that shows a map." Research: specifications of three screens; prices. Tests: screen brightness; loading time. Evaluation: "The screen works well and looks good."

Evaluate how well the global context has been used across the folder, and recommend specific improvements. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Identities and relationships; Orientation in space and time; Personal and cultural expression; Scientific and technical innovation; Globalization and sustainability; Fairness and development. Any two correct [1]; any four correct [1]; all six correct [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Near-misses such as "Science and innovation" do not match the brief; learn the exact names.

**2.** (i) key concept, (ii) related concept, (iii) global context, (iv) related concept. Two correct [1]; all four correct [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Innovation" on its own is a related concept example; only the full phrase "Scientific and technical innovation" is the global context, so answering "global context" for (iv) loses the point.

**3.** A design situation is the open scenario you are given, which may contain several possible problems [1]. A problem statement is written by you and names one specific, evidenced need for a named audience, without naming a solution [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs both sides; describing only one earns at most half.

**4. (a)** It names the solution (a phone stand) before any inquiry, and gives no audience need or evidence [1].
**(b)** It is vague: no specific audience, no specific difficulty and no evidence [1].
**(c)** It contains three separate problems, so no single target can be tested [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the main weakness; a list of every possible fault adds no credit.

**5.** Model: "Cyclists at Ferris Lane Sports Centre need a safe place to leave their bikes, because the racks are full at peak times (evidence: hourly counts of bikes in racks and on railings for a week), within the space by the entrance." Named audience [1]; specific need with no solution named [1]; evidence and a constraint [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Design a bigger bike rack" names a solution and loses the need point.

**6.** Orientation in space and time: "At what times do the racks fill, and how long do bikes stay?" [1] Evidence: hourly counts over a week and a map of where bikes are left [1].
Fairness and development: "Which cyclists cannot use the current racks -- for example those with cargo bikes, trailers or adapted cycles?" [1] Evidence: interviews with cyclists who use the railings, and rack spacing measured against their bikes [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The two research questions must be different because the contexts are different; the same question written twice earns credit only once.

**7. (a)** Opening jars and packaging: 27 ÷ 60 × 100 = **45%** [1]. Reading small print: 18 ÷ 60 × 100 = **30%** [1].
**(b)** Opening jars and packaging, because it is the most common choice at 45%, half as many again as the next task [1].
**(c)** Fairness and development: current packaging excludes people with weak grip [1], so research would focus on residents who cannot open jars unaided, measuring grip and timing attempts with existing products [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division for each percentage; a bare "45%" with no working gives no evidence the data were used.

**8. (a)** 17 ÷ 20 × 100 = **85%** [1]. 85% is at least 80%, so the criterion **is met** [1].
**(b)** (12 + 14 + 19 + 11 + 16) ÷ 5 = 72 ÷ 5 = **14.4 s** [1].
**(c)** The mean (14.4 s) is under 15 s, yet two of the five weakest-grip testers (19 s and 16 s) failed, so only 3 of 5 (60%) of the users the context is about succeeded [1]. Fairness and development is about who is left out, so the evaluation should report these individual results and suggest an improvement for them [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An average can hide the users the context targets; (c) needs the named failures (19 s and 16 s), not "some were slow".

**9.** The context is only a label: no research or test relates to resources or waste [1], so the folder cannot show the context shaped the design [1]. Change 1: add research on materials and energy use, for example comparing bulb types or the recyclability of the lamp body [1]. Change 2: add a context-linked test such as power drawn in watts, or the share of parts that can be separated for recycling [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Add more about sustainability" is too vague; each change must name specific research or a measurable test.

**10. (a)** (40 − 22) ÷ 40 × 100 = **45%** fall [1].
**(b)** Model: Identities and relationships [1]. It changes who is researched -- young people in groups, not the library alone [1] -- and what is tested -- whether visitors aged 11–16 feel the space is for them, measured by a short rating before and after [1].
**(c)** "Visitors aged 11–16 in Wendmere need a space in the library they see as their own, because weekly visits fell from 40 to 22 (45%), within the current floor area and budget." Audience and need [1]; evidence and constraint, no solution named [1].
**(d)** Primary: "Where do 11–16-year-olds in Wendmere spend free time instead, and why?" (survey or interview) [1]. Secondary: "How have other libraries redesigned areas for teenagers, and what changed?" (published case studies) [1].
**(e)** "At least 70% of 20 teenage testers rate the new area 4 or 5 out of 5 for feeling welcome" [1]; "Weekly visitors aged 11–16 rise above 30 in the month after the change" [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Success criteria need a number and a group; "teenagers like it" cannot be tested and earns no credit.

**11.** Strengths: a context was chosen at the start [1], and Identities and relationships fits a situation about new students feeling lost [1].
Weaknesses: the problem statement names the solution ("a screen") and gives no evidence [1]; the research is about hardware and prices, not about new students [1]; the tests measure the screen, not whether new students find their way or feel settled [1]; the evaluation is a claim with no data and no mention of the target audience [1].
Improvements: rewrite the statement around new students' need with evidence, for example how many said they got lost [1]; add a test such as the share of new students who reach three named rooms unaided within a set time, and report their feedback in the evaluation [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs strengths and weaknesses weighed; a list of faults alone cannot reach the top.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Misnaming a global context, or treating a related concept (innovation, sustainability) as one.
- Problem statements that name the product ("design a lamp") instead of the need.
- Statements with no evidence -- no count, measure or source.
- Research questions that would be identical under any context.
- Success criteria with no number or no named group of testers.
- Evaluations that quote an average and hide the users the context was chosen to serve.
