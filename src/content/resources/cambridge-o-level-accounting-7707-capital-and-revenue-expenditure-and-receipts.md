## The two kinds of expenditure

**Capital expenditure** is money spent on buying non-current assets, adding
to them or improving them, including every cost of getting an asset ready
for use. The benefit lasts for more than one accounting period.

**Revenue expenditure** is money spent on running the business day to day,
including keeping non-current assets in their existing condition. The
benefit is used up within the period.

Ask one question: **does this spending create or improve an asset that will
help the business for more than a year, or does it keep the business (or an
existing asset) running as it is?**

| Spending | Capital or revenue | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Buying a delivery van | Capital | New non-current asset |
| Number plates and painting the business name on the new van | Capital | Cost of getting it ready for use |
| Fuel and insurance for the van | Revenue | Running cost |
| Legal fees on buying premises | Capital | Part of the cost of acquiring the asset |
| Building an extension to the premises | Capital | Improves the asset |
| Wages of the business's own staff who built the extension | Capital | Cost of creating the asset |
| Repainting the existing shop | Revenue | Maintains the asset |
| Delivery and installation of a new machine | Capital | Getting it ready for use |
| Replacing a worn part on an old machine | Revenue | Restores its existing condition |
| Buying goods for resale | Revenue | Trading inventory, not a non-current asset |

The same item can be either, depending on purpose. A computer bought by a
computer shop to sell is revenue expenditure (purchases). The same computer
bought for the office is capital expenditure.

## The two kinds of receipt

**Capital receipts** are amounts received that do not arise from normal
trading: capital introduced by the owner, loans received, and the proceeds
of selling a non-current asset.

**Revenue receipts** arise from normal trading and other regular income:
sales, rent received, commission received, interest received and discount
received.

## How each is accounted for

| Item | Ledger treatment | Where it ends up |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure | Debit a non-current asset account | Statement of financial position, then charged to the statement of profit or loss gradually as depreciation |
| Revenue expenditure | Debit an expense account (or purchases) | Statement of profit or loss for the period |
| Capital receipt: capital introduced | Debit bank, credit capital | Statement of financial position (capital) |
| Capital receipt: loan received | Debit bank, credit loan | Statement of financial position (liability) |
| Capital receipt: sale of a non-current asset | Debit bank, credit disposal account | Only the profit or loss on disposal reaches the statement of profit or loss |
| Revenue receipt | Debit bank (or a customer), credit an income account (or revenue) | Statement of profit or loss for the period |

Journal entries follow the same pattern. A machine bought on credit from
Delta Engineering for $8,500 is recorded as:

```
                                              Debit $   Credit $
Machinery                                      8,500
  Delta Engineering                                        8,500
(Purchase of machine on credit)
```

The credit is to the supplier's account in the general ledger as an other
payable, not to trade payables, because the machine is not goods for
resale.

## Worked example: Nadia's bakery

Nadia depreciates equipment at 10% per year on the cost of equipment held at
the year end. Premises are not depreciated. Her draft profit for the year
ended 31 December 2027 was $41,300. These items were found:

1. Installation of a new oven, $300, was debited to repairs.
2. Repainting the shop, $1,600, was debited to the premises account.
3. A bank loan of $5,000 received was credited to revenue.
4. The $450 received for an old mixer was credited to revenue. Its net book
value was $450, and it was still in the equipment account. (Ignore any
depreciation on the mixer this year.)
5. Wages of $2,200 paid to Nadia's own staff for building a storeroom on the
premises were debited to wages.

**Step 1: classify the mistake.** Items 1 and 5 are capital expenditure
treated as revenue. Item 2 is revenue expenditure treated as capital. Items
3 and 4 are capital receipts treated as revenue receipts.

**Step 2: work out each effect.**

1. The $300 should be in equipment, with depreciation of 10% × $300 = $30.
Instead, $300 was charged as an expense. Profit is **understated by $300 −
$30 = $270**. Equipment at net book value is **understated by $270**.
2. $1,600 of expense is missing, so profit is **overstated by $1,600**.
Premises are **overstated by $1,600**.
3. A loan is not income. Profit is **overstated by $5,000**. Assets are
correct, because the bank balance includes the $5,000, but non-current
liabilities are **understated by $5,000**.
4. Proceeds equal net book value, so there is no profit or loss on disposal.
Profit is **overstated by $450**. The mixer should have been removed, so
equipment is **overstated by $450**.
5. Premises are not depreciated, so profit is **understated by $2,200** and
premises are **understated by $2,200**.

**Step 3: summarise.**

```
Item                         Effect on profit       Effect on non-current assets
                                   $                          $
1 Installation expensed       270 understated            270 understated
2 Repainting capitalised    1,600 overstated           1,600 overstated
3 Loan as revenue           5,000 overstated                no effect
4 Mixer proceeds as revenue   450 overstated             450 overstated
5 Own wages on storeroom    2,200 understated          2,200 understated
```

```
Statement of corrected profit for the year ended 31 December 2027
                                                     $
Draft profit for the year                       41,300
Add  Installation (net of depreciation)            270
Less Repainting                                 (1,600)
Less Loan                                       (5,000)
Less Mixer proceeds                               (450)
Add  Storeroom wages                             2,200
                                                ------
Corrected profit for the year                   36,720
                                                ======
```

Non-current assets overall: +$270 − $1,600 − $450 + $2,200 = **+$420**. The
draft statement of financial position understated non-current assets by
$420.

## The general pattern

| Mistake | Profit | Asset valuation |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure treated as revenue | Understated | Understated |
| Revenue expenditure treated as capital | Overstated | Overstated |
| Capital receipt treated as revenue | Overstated | Depends: a loan or capital leaves assets correct but misstates liabilities or capital; an asset sale leaves the asset overstated |
| Revenue receipt treated as capital | Understated | Usually no effect on assets |

Where depreciation is charged, the effect of a capital-expenditure error is
the amount **less** the depreciation that should (or should not) have been
charged on it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating repairs and maintenance as capital because they relate to a
non-current asset.
- Forgetting that delivery, installation and legal costs are part of an
asset's cost.
- Treating the full sale proceeds of a non-current asset as income instead
of comparing them with net book value.
- Calling a loan "income" because cash came in.
- Ignoring the depreciation effect when a capital item has been expensed.
- Stating only "profit is wrong" instead of the direction and the amount.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** the type of item first, then **calculate** the effect.
- Always give **direction** (overstated or understated) and **amount**.
- **Explain** the importance of the distinction: matching costs against the
revenue they help earn, and showing assets at a fair value, so that profit
and the statement of financial position are both reliable.
- When asked to **advise** on an item, reason from purpose: will it give
benefit beyond the current year?
