## The three states and their names for changes

The three states of matter are **solid**, **liquid** and **gas**. A substance changes from one state to another
when it is heated or cooled enough.

| Change | From | To | Happens at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melting | solid | liquid | the melting point |
| Freezing | liquid | solid | the melting point |
| Boiling | liquid | gas | the boiling point |
| Condensing | gas | liquid | the boiling point |

Melting and freezing happen at the **same temperature** for a pure substance, and so do boiling and
condensing. Pure water melts and freezes at 0 °C and boils and condenses at 100 °C at normal pressure.

## The simple particle model

In the simple model, every particle is drawn as a small solid sphere. The model explains the differences
between the states.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | Regular pattern, touching | Touching, no regular pattern | Far apart, no pattern |
| Movement | Vibrate about fixed positions | Move around each other | Move quickly in all directions |
| Forces between particles | Strong enough to hold the particles in place | Strong enough to keep the particles together but let them slide | Almost no effect between particles |
| Shape and volume | Fixed shape, fixed volume | Takes the shape of the container, fixed volume | Fills the container, no fixed shape or volume |

The word "particles" here means whatever the substance is made of: atoms, molecules or ions. When a
substance is heated, its particles gain energy. In a solid they vibrate more strongly. At the melting point
they have enough energy to break out of their fixed positions and the solid melts. In a liquid they move
faster as it is heated. At the boiling point they have enough energy to separate from each other and the
liquid boils.

### Why the temperature stays constant during a change of state

While a pure substance is melting or boiling, the energy supplied is used to overcome the forces between
the particles, not to make them move faster. So the temperature stays the same until the whole sample has
changed state. A heating curve therefore has flat sections at the melting point and the boiling point.

## What decides the melting point and boiling point

The amount of energy needed to change a substance from solid to liquid, and from liquid to gas, depends on
the **strength of the forces between the particles**. The nature of those particles, and the forces
between them, depends on the type of bonding and the structure of the substance.

**The stronger the forces between the particles, the higher the melting point and boiling point.**

| Type of substance | Particles | Forces to overcome | Melting and boiling points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple molecular (small molecules) | Molecules | Weak intermolecular forces | Low |
| Ionic compound | Ions in a giant lattice | Many strong ionic bonds | High |
| Metal | Atoms (positive ions) in a giant structure | Many strong metallic bonds | Usually high |
| Giant covalent | Atoms in a network | Many strong covalent bonds | Very high |

Some typical values show the pattern.

| Substance | Structure | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methane, CH₄ | Small molecules | -182 | -162 |
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | Small molecules | -101 | -34 |
| Water, H₂O | Small molecules | 0 | 100 |
| Sodium chloride, NaCl | Giant ionic lattice | 801 | 1413 |
| Magnesium, Mg | Giant metallic | 650 | 1090 |
| Iron, Fe | Giant metallic | 1538 | 2862 |
| Silicon dioxide, SiO₂ | Giant covalent | 1710 | 2230 |

You do not need to remember these numbers. You do need to be able to use numbers like them.

### A common trap: what is overcome when a molecular substance boils

When water boils, the covalent bonds inside each H₂O molecule stay intact. The molecules separate from each
other. It is the weak intermolecular forces that are overcome. This is why small molecules have low boiling
points even though covalent bonds are strong. The ideas are developed in the guide to
[properties of small molecules](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-small-molecules/).

## Predicting the state of a substance at a given temperature

Use three rules.

1. If the temperature is **below the melting point**, the substance is a **solid**.
2. If the temperature is **at or above the melting point and below the boiling point**, it is a **liquid**.
3. If the temperature is **above the boiling point**, it is a **gas**.

### Worked example 1: reading data

*The table gives the melting points and boiling points of four substances. State the state of each at 25 °C
and at 150 °C.*

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| P | -70 | 35 |
| Q | 98 | 883 |
| R | -219 | -183 |
| S | 114 | 184 |

```
At 25 degrees C:
P: 25 is above -70 and below 35      -> liquid
Q: 25 is below 98                    -> solid
R: 25 is above -183                  -> gas
S: 25 is below 114                   -> solid

At 150 degrees C:
P: 150 is above 35                   -> gas
Q: 150 is above 98 and below 883     -> liquid
R: 150 is above -183                 -> gas
S: 150 is above 114 and below 184    -> liquid
```

Write the comparison in your answer. "25 °C is below 98 °C, the melting point of Q, so Q is a solid" earns the
mark. "Q is a solid" on its own may not.

## Explaining differences in melting points

### Worked example 2: sodium chloride and methane

*Explain why sodium chloride melts at 801 °C but methane melts at -182 °C.*

```
Sodium chloride:  giant ionic lattice
                  strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions in all directions
                  a lot of energy is needed to overcome them  -> high melting point
Methane:          small molecules
                  only weak intermolecular forces between molecules
                  covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break
                  little energy is needed  -> low melting point
```

A full-mark answer names the **type of particle** in each substance, the **type of force** that has to be
overcome, and compares the **amount of energy** needed. Always finish by linking back to the melting point
asked about.

### Worked example 3: comparing two states of one substance

*Explain, using the particle model, why water at 90 °C has no fixed shape but ice has.*

In ice the particles are held in a regular pattern and can only vibrate about fixed positions. In liquid
water the particles have enough energy to move past each other, so the sample flows and takes the shape of its
container.

## Atoms do not have bulk properties

Bulk properties such as hardness, melting point and electrical conductivity belong to large collections of
particles, not to single atoms. A single carbon atom is not hard, and a single copper atom does not melt or
conduct. These properties depend on how very many particles are arranged and bonded to each other. So
"diamond is hard because its atoms are hard" is wrong. The correct idea is "diamond is hard because its atoms
are held in a giant structure by strong covalent bonds".

## Higher tier: limitations of the simple particle model

The simple model draws particles as small, solid, inelastic spheres. Higher tier students must be able to
explain where this picture falls short in relation to changes of state. The specification names three
limitations.

| Limitation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| The model has **no forces** | Real particles attract each other, and the strength of those forces decides the melting and boiling points. A diagram of touching spheres does not show how strong the forces are, so it cannot explain why one substance melts at a higher temperature than another. |
| All particles are shown as **spheres** | Atoms, molecules and ions have different shapes and sizes. Molecules such as water are not spheres, and a sphere cannot show that a molecule is made of bonded atoms. |
| The spheres are **solid** | Atoms are mostly empty space around a tiny nucleus, with electrons around it. A solid sphere does not show the structure of an atom or the way particles can change shape when they collide. |

A good answer says what the model does, then what it leaves out, then why that matters for changes of state.
For example: "The model shows particles as spheres with no forces between them, so it cannot explain why
sodium chloride has a much higher melting point than methane, which depends on the strength of the forces."

## Common mistakes

- Writing that bonds break when a small molecular substance melts or boils. The covalent bonds stay; the
  intermolecular forces are overcome.
- Saying that particles "get bigger" when heated. They move or vibrate more, and the average spacing grows
  in a gas, but the particles themselves do not expand.
- Using "atoms melt" for ionic or metallic substances. Say ions or atoms, as appropriate, and say the
  structure loses its fixed pattern.
- Describing a boiling point as the temperature at which a liquid "starts to evaporate".
- Forgetting that stronger forces mean a higher melting point, then explaining the reverse.
- Quoting the melting point as if it were the temperature of a solid at any time. A solid can be at any
  temperature below its melting point.

## Exam technique

- **Predict / state:** give the state and quote the comparison of temperatures.
- **Explain:** name the particles, name the forces, link the strength of the forces to the energy needed.
- **Compare:** use a comparative word such as "higher", "stronger" or "more energy" in every sentence.
- **(HT only) Limitations:** name the limitation and then say what it prevents the model from explaining.
