## What the specification asks

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| 3.8.2 a | In a closed system, equilibrium is reached when the forward and backward reactions occur at exactly the same rate |
| 3.8.2 b | The relative amounts of substances at equilibrium depend on the conditions |
| 3.8.2 c | Raising temperature increases the yield from the endothermic reaction and decreases the yield from the exothermic reaction; lowering it does the opposite |
| 3.8.2 d | In gaseous reactions, increased pressure favours the reaction producing fewer molecules; decreased pressure favours the reaction producing more molecules |
| 3.8.2 e | These factors, together with rates, decide optimum industrial conditions, including the Haber process |

The specification asks about **temperature** and **pressure**. It does not ask you to predict the effect of changing concentration.

## What equilibrium means (3.8.2 a and b)

Picture a reversible reaction A + B ⇌ C + D starting with only A and B in a sealed container.

1. At first only the forward reaction happens, quickly, because there is plenty of A and B.
2. As C and D build up, the backward reaction starts and speeds up; the forward reaction slows as A and B are used.
3. Eventually the two rates are **equal**. From then on, C and D are made as fast as they are used up, so the amounts of all four substances stay **constant**.

Three points examiners test:

- **Both reactions continue.** Equilibrium is dynamic: nothing has stopped.
- **Constant does not mean equal.** The mixture might be 90% products or 10% products; only the amounts stay steady.
- **A closed system is needed.** If a product escapes, the backward reaction cannot keep pace and equilibrium is never reached.

## The temperature rule (3.8.2 c)

Every reversible reaction is exothermic in one direction and endothermic in the other, by the same amount of energy.

**Raise the temperature → the endothermic direction is favoured.**
**Lower the temperature → the exothermic direction is favoured.**

### Worked example 1: nitrogen dioxide and dinitrogen tetroxide

2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g); the forward reaction is exothermic.
NO₂ is a brown gas; N₂O₄ is colourless.

*A sealed tube of the equilibrium mixture is placed in hot water. Predict and explain what is seen.*

1. Forward = exothermic, so backward = endothermic.
2. Raising the temperature increases the yield from the endothermic (backward) reaction.
3. More NO₂ forms, so the mixture turns **darker brown**.

In iced water the exothermic forward reaction is favoured, more colourless N₂O₄ forms, and the mixture becomes **paler**.

### Worked example 2: the same reaction written backwards

N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g); the forward reaction is now **endothermic**.

Raising the temperature favours the endothermic (forward) direction: more NO₂, darker brown. The prediction is identical, as it must be. What matters is which **substance** is made by the endothermic direction, not which way the equation is printed.

## The pressure rule (3.8.2 d)

Count gas molecules on each side using the numbers in the balanced equation.

**Increase pressure → the side with fewer gas molecules is favoured.**
**Decrease pressure → the side with more gas molecules is favoured.**
**Equal numbers → pressure does not change the yield.**

### Worked example 3: three reactions

| Reaction | Molecules left : right | Effect of increasing pressure |
|---|---|---|
| 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g) | 2 : 1 | More N₂O₄ |
| CO(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ CO₂(g) + H₂(g) | 2 : 2 | No change in yield |
| H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) | 2 : 2 | No change in yield |

Increasing the pressure of gases still increases the **rate** of each reaction (3.8.1 d), even when it does not change the yield.

## Combining both factors

### Worked example 4: making hydrogen

CO(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ CO₂(g) + H₂(g); forward reaction exothermic.

*State the conditions that would give the highest yield of hydrogen.*

- **Temperature:** forward is exothermic, so a **low** temperature increases the yield of hydrogen.
- **Pressure:** 2 molecules on each side, so pressure has **no effect** on the yield. A moderate pressure can be chosen for rate or cost reasons.

## Reading yield data (3.8.2 b to d)

The table shows **invented data**, for practice, for a reaction 2X₂(g) + Y₂(g) ⇌ 2X₂Y(g).

| Temperature / °C | % X₂Y at 1 atm | % X₂Y at 50 atm | % X₂Y at 100 atm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | 60 | 80 | 88 |
| 400 | 30 | 52 | 63 |
| 600 | 10 | 22 | 31 |

### Worked example 5: deducing from data

*Use the data to deduce whether the forward reaction is exothermic or endothermic, and explain the effect of pressure.* **[4]**

**Model answer.** At any pressure, the yield of X₂Y falls as the temperature rises (for example from 60% to 10% at 1 atm) [1]. Raising the temperature favours the endothermic direction, so the backward reaction is endothermic and the forward reaction is **exothermic** [1]. At any temperature, the yield rises with pressure (30% to 63% at 400 °C) [1], because there are 3 molecules of gas on the left and 2 on the right, and higher pressure favours the side with fewer molecules [1].

**Why it scores:** each claim quotes data, then gives the rule.

## Optimum conditions in industry (3.8.2 e)

The conditions with the best yield are rarely the ones used, because yield is not the only concern.

| Factor | Pulls towards | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| High yield of an exothermic product | Low temperature | Reaction too slow |
| Fast rate | High temperature | Lower yield |
| High yield when product side has fewer molecules | High pressure | Expensive equipment, more energy for compression, safety risk |
| Catalyst | Faster rate in both directions | Does not change the yield, only how quickly equilibrium is reached |

A **compromise** temperature gives an acceptable yield at an acceptable rate. For the Haber process, applying these ideas is covered in the [production of ammonia and sulfuric acid guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-9202-production-of-ammonia-and-sulfuric-acid/).

## Common mistakes

- Saying the reactions stop at equilibrium.
- Saying the amounts of reactants and products are equal at equilibrium.
- Applying the temperature rule to the forward reaction without checking whether it is exothermic or endothermic.
- Counting atoms instead of molecules when applying the pressure rule.
- Including solids or liquids in the molecule count; the pressure rule is about gases.
- Claiming a catalyst increases the yield.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Predict:** state the direction favoured and name what increases or decreases.
- **Explain:** link the prediction to the rule (endothermic direction favoured; fewer molecules favoured).
- **Use the data:** quote numbers from the table before you explain.
- **Suggest the conditions used:** balance yield against rate and cost.
