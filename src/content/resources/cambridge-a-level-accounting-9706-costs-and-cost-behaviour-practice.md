## Section A

**1.** State **two** examples of a direct expense for a manufacturing business. **[2]**

**2.** Thornbury Chairs makes wooden chairs. Classify each cost as direct or indirect **and** as fixed, variable or semi-variable: (i) timber; (ii) the factory supervisor's salary; (iii) factory electricity, which has a standing charge plus a charge per unit used; (iv) glue and sandpaper used across all production. **[4]**

**3.** Kenmore Logistics needs one forklift driver, paid 2,400 a month, for every 2,000 pallets handled or part of 2,000. Calculate the driver cost for a month in which 5,100 pallets are handled, and the driver cost per pallet to two decimal places. **[3]**

**4.** A business has fixed costs of 30,000 a month. Using output levels of 5,000 and 7,500 units, explain what happens to fixed cost per unit as output rises. **[3]**

**5.** A machinist is paid 18.00 an hour. In one week she is paid for 45 hours, 5 of them overtime at time-and-a-half. Two hours were idle while a machine was repaired. The overtime was caused by general pressure of work. Calculate the direct labour cost and the indirect labour cost for the week. **[4]**

**6.** Explain why perpetual AVCO and periodic AVCO usually give different closing inventory values. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Wexcombe Couriers records its vehicle running costs.

| Month | Kilometres | Cost ($) |
|---|---|---|
| July | 12,000 | 9,200 |
| August | 15,500 | 10,950 |
| September | 9,000 | 7,700 |
| October | 14,000 | 11,400 |

**(a)** Use the high-low method to calculate the variable cost per kilometre and the fixed cost per month. **[4]**
**(b)** Forecast the running cost for a month of 11,000 kilometres. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on the October figure and on **one** limitation of the high-low method. **[3]**

**8.** Larchfield Paints keeps records for one pigment in March.

| Date | Transaction | Units | Price ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 March | Opening inventory | 400 | 2.50 |
| 6 March | Purchase | 600 | 2.80 |
| 12 March | Issue | 700 | |
| 20 March | Purchase | 500 | 3.00 |
| 28 March | Issue | 450 | |

**(a)** Prepare the inventory record using perpetual AVCO, showing the closing inventory value. **[5]**
**(b)** Calculate the closing inventory value using FIFO. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the closing inventory value using periodic AVCO. **[2]**
**(d)** The 1,150 units issued were used in products sold for 5,175. Calculate gross profit under FIFO and under perpetual AVCO, and explain the difference. **[3]**

**9.** Hollins Bakery makes a celebration cake. For each cake: ingredients 7.20, a presentation box 0.80, 1.5 direct labour hours at 14.00, and a licence fee of 0.50 for a character design. Production overheads are absorbed at 6.00 per direct labour hour. Administration and selling overheads are 3.50 per cake. The bakery adds a mark-up of 25% on total cost.

**(a)** Calculate the prime cost of one cake. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the production cost of one cake. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the total cost and the selling price of one cake. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the value of 40 unsold cakes in inventory, giving a reason for the cost layer you use. **[2]**

**10.** Ravensworth Ceramics pays its glazers 16.00 an hour for a 40-hour week. It is considering a piece rate of 1.60 a unit with a guaranteed minimum of 560 a week. Last week glazer A finished 420 units and glazer B finished 330 units.

**(a)** Calculate each glazer's pay under both schemes. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate the proposal to change to the piece rate. **[6]**

**11.** Calderbrook Engineering uses 48,000 kg of steel a year at 12.50 a kg and holds an average of 6,000 kg. Holding costs are 15% a year of the value held. Under JIT the average would fall to 800 kg and a rented warehouse costing 5,000 a year could be given up. However, a 1.5% bulk discount would be lost and ordering costs would rise by 4,200 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the net annual financial effect of adopting JIT. **[5]**
**(b)** Advise Calderbrook whether to adopt JIT. **[6]**

**12.** Pellam Electronics makes 2,800 units a month. It needs one supervisor, paid 3,000 a month, for every 1,000 units or part of 1,000. Variable cost is 9.00 a unit. A customer offers to buy an extra 400 units a month at 11.00 each.

**(a)** Calculate the effect on monthly profit of making the extra 400 units. **[4]**
**(b)** Suggest **two** ways Pellam could make the extra output worthwhile. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: a royalty paid per unit made [1]; hire of special equipment for one job, or subcontract work on one job [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An expense is direct only if it can be traced to a cost unit; rent or insurance would not score.

**2.** (i) Direct, variable [1]. (ii) Indirect, fixed [1]. (iii) Indirect, semi-variable [1]. (iv) Indirect, variable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each item needs both classifications. Glue is a material, but too small to trace to each chair, so it is indirect.

**3.** 5,100 ÷ 2,000 = 2.55, so 3 drivers are needed [1]. Cost = 3 × 2,400 = **7,200** [1]. Per pallet = 7,200 ÷ 5,100 = **1.41** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Always round the number of steps up; part of a step still needs a whole driver.

**4.** At 5,000 units fixed cost per unit is 30,000 ÷ 5,000 = **6.00** [1]. At 7,500 units it is **4.00** [1]. Total fixed cost is unchanged, so it is spread over more units and the cost per unit falls [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say clearly that total fixed cost stays the same; it is only the cost per unit that falls.

**5.** Total pay = 45 × 18.00 + 5 × 9.00 = 855 [1]. Direct labour = productive hours 43 × 18.00 = **774** [1]. Idle time 2 × 18.00 = 36 [1]; indirect labour = 36 + overtime premium 45 = **81** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Only the premium is an overhead; the basic-rate part of the overtime hours is direct labour if the time was productive.

**6.** Periodic AVCO uses one average of all units available in the period [1]. Perpetual AVCO recalculates the average after each receipt, so issues made before a purchase are priced at a different average and the remaining value differs [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Use the highest and lowest activity: August and September [1]. Variable cost = (10,950 − 7,700) ÷ (15,500 − 9,000) = 3,250 ÷ 6,500 [1] = **0.50 per km** [1]. Fixed cost = 10,950 − (15,500 × 0.50) = **3,200** [1].
**(b)** 3,200 + (11,000 × 0.50) [1] = **8,700** [1].
**(c)** The line predicts 3,200 + 7,000 = 10,200 for October, but the actual cost is 11,400, 1,200 higher [1], so October contains an unusual cost such as a repair and should not be used for the estimate [1]. Limitation: the method uses only two observations, so an unusual month at either extreme would distort the fixed and variable estimates [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* October has the second-highest activity but the highest cost; choosing it in (a) loses the method marks.

**8. (a)**

| Date | Receipts | Issues | Balance units | Balance value | Average |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 March | | | 400 | 1,000.00 | 2.50 |
| 6 March | 600 at 2.80 = 1,680 | | 1,000 | 2,680.00 | 2.68 |
| 12 March | | 700 at 2.68 = 1,876 | 300 | 804.00 | 2.68 |
| 20 March | 500 at 3.00 = 1,500 | | 800 | 2,304.00 | 2.88 |
| 28 March | | 450 at 2.88 = 1,296 | 350 | 1,008.00 | 2.88 |

Average of 2.68 after 6 March [1]; issue of 1,876 [1]; average of 2.88 after 20 March [1]; issue of 1,296 [1]; closing inventory **1,008** [1].
**(b)** The 350 units left are from the latest purchase [1]: 350 × 3.00 = **1,050** [1].
**(c)** Total cost 1,000 + 1,680 + 1,500 = 4,180 for 1,500 units [1]; 350 × (4,180 ÷ 1,500) = **975.33** [1].
**(d)** FIFO: cost of issues 4,180 − 1,050 = 3,130, gross profit **2,045** [1]. Perpetual AVCO: 4,180 − 1,008 = 3,172, gross profit **2,003** [1]. Prices are rising, so FIFO charges the older, cheaper units to production and leaves the dearer units in inventory, giving 42 more profit [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* An issue never changes the average under AVCO; only a receipt does.

**9. (a)** 7.20 + 0.80 + 21.00 + 0.50 [1] = **29.50** [1].
**(b)** 29.50 + (1.5 × 6.00) = **38.50** [1].
**(c)** Total cost 38.50 + 3.50 = **42.00** [1]; selling price 42.00 × 1.25 = **52.50** [1].
**(d)** 40 × 38.50 = **1,540** [1], because inventory is valued at production cost; administration and selling costs relate to the period, not to unsold goods [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* The licence fee is a direct expense because it can be traced to each cake.

**10. (a)** Time rate: A 40 × 16.00 = **640** [1]; B **640** [1]. Piece rate: A 420 × 1.60 = **672** [1]; B 330 × 1.60 = 528, below the guarantee, so **560** [1].
**(b)** For: pay is linked to output, so labour behaves more like a variable cost [1]; faster workers such as A are rewarded, which may raise output [1]. Against: workers may rush and quality may fall, raising rejects [1]; the guarantee means slow workers such as B still cost 560, so the saving is smaller than it looks [1]; more inspection and record-keeping are needed [1]. Judgement: adopt only with quality checks, as total pay here falls from 1,280 to 1,232 [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from (a) in the evaluation and finish with a decision.

**11. (a)** Holding cost now 6,000 × 12.50 × 15% = 11,250 [1]. Under JIT 800 × 12.50 × 15% = 1,500, a saving of 9,750 [1]. Lost discount 48,000 × 12.50 × 1.5% = 9,000 [1]. Warehouse saved 5,000; extra ordering cost 4,200 [1]. Net effect 9,750 + 5,000 − 9,000 − 4,200 = **1,550 saving** [1].
**(b)** For: a small net saving of 1,550 a year [1]; less cash tied up and less risk of damaged or obsolete steel [1]. Against: the saving is small compared with the risk of production stopping if a delivery is late [1]; it depends on reliable suppliers and accurate production planning [1]; the warehouse may be hard to rent again if JIT fails [1]. Advice: adopt JIT only if suppliers have a proven record of reliable delivery; otherwise the small saving does not justify the risk [1]. **[11]**

**12. (a)** Extra contribution = 400 × (11.00 − 9.00) = 800 [1]. Output rises from 2,800 to 3,200 units, so supervisors needed rise from 3 to 4 [1]. Extra supervisor cost 3,000 [1]. Profit falls by 3,000 − 800 = **2,200** a month [1].
**(b)** Any two: negotiate a higher price for the order [1]; take a larger order that uses more of the new supervisor's capacity, up to 4,000 units in total [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A stepped cost is fixed only within a range; an order that crosses a threshold carries a whole extra step.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the highest and lowest costs, not activity levels, in the high-low method.
- Rounding steps down in stepped-cost calculations.
- Charging all overtime hours to overheads instead of only the premium.
- Recalculating the AVCO average after an issue.
- Valuing finished goods at total cost.
- Ending an evaluation without a recommendation.
