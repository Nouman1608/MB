## Structure of graphite

Graphite is another form of the element carbon. Each carbon atom has four outer electrons. In graphite:

- three outer electrons are used to form **three covalent bonds** with three other carbon atoms;
- the atoms join to form flat **layers of hexagonal rings** (six-carbon rings, like a honeycomb);
- the fourth electron of each carbon atom is **delocalised**: it is free to move along the layer;
- there are **no covalent bonds between the layers**.

```
Layer 1:  flat sheet of hexagonal rings; each C bonded to 3 other C atoms
            ~ no covalent bonds between layers (weak forces only) ~
Layer 2:  flat sheet of hexagonal rings; each C bonded to 3 other C atoms
            ~ no covalent bonds between layers (weak forces only) ~
Layer 3:  ...
```

The layers are held together by weak forces. This is a different arrangement from diamond, where every carbon
atom has four bonds in all directions.

## Properties explained

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Conducts electricity | One delocalised electron per carbon atom is free to move, so charge can flow along the layers |
| Soft and slippery | Layers can slide over each other because there are no covalent bonds between them |
| Very high melting point | The covalent bonds within each layer are strong and there are very many of them; they must be broken for it to melt |

### Why graphite conducts

Each carbon atom uses only three of its four outer electrons for bonding. The fourth electron is delocalised
and can move through the layer. When a voltage is applied, these electrons carry charge. This is the same type
of explanation as for a metal, which is why the specification says graphite "is similar to metals in that it has
delocalised electrons". Graphite is unusual among non-metals because it conducts electricity.

### Why graphite is soft

There are no covalent bonds between the layers. The forces between layers are much weaker than the covalent bonds
within them, so the layers can slide over each other easily. When you write with a pencil, layers of graphite
rub off onto the paper. This also makes graphite useful as a lubricant.

### Why graphite has a very high melting point

To melt graphite, covalent bonds within the layers must be broken. Each atom has three strong bonds, and the
number of bonds is huge. A very large amount of energy is needed. The weak attraction between the layers does
not lower the melting point, because melting needs the atoms to be separated from their neighbours in the layer,
which means breaking covalent bonds.

## Graphite compared with diamond

| | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| Covalent bonds per carbon atom | 4 | 3 |
| Arrangement | 3D network | Layers of hexagonal rings |
| Bonds between layers | Not applicable | None |
| Delocalised electrons | None | One per carbon atom |
| Hardness | Very hard | Soft |
| Conducts electricity | No | Yes |
| Melting point | Very high | Very high |

## Uses linked to the properties

| Use | Property used |
|---|---|
| Pencil "lead" | Soft; layers rub off onto paper |
| Lubricant for machinery | Layers slide easily |
| Electrodes in electrolysis | Conducts electricity and does not react easily |
| Brushes in electric motors | Conducts and is slippery |

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: describing the structure

*Describe the structure and bonding in graphite.*

```
1. Each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds with three other carbon atoms.
2. The atoms form layers of hexagonal rings.
3. There are no covalent bonds between the layers.
4. One electron from each carbon atom is delocalised.
```

### Worked example 2: why graphite is soft but diamond is hard

*Explain why graphite is soft but diamond is hard.*

```
Graphite: layers with no covalent bonds between them; layers slide over each other -> soft
Diamond:  every carbon atom has four strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D network;
          atoms cannot slide -> hard
```

### Worked example 3: why graphite conducts but diamond does not

*Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not.*

```
Graphite: each carbon uses three outer electrons in bonds; the fourth is delocalised
          and is free to move, so it carries charge.
Diamond:  all four outer electrons of each carbon are used in bonds; no free charge carriers.
```

### Worked example 4: counting atoms in a hexagonal ring pattern

*In a graphite layer, every carbon atom is shared between three hexagons. How many carbon atoms belong to each
hexagon?*

```
Each hexagon has 6 carbon atoms at its corners.
Each atom is shared by 3 hexagons.
Atoms per hexagon = 6 / 3 = 2
```

Each hexagon "owns" two carbon atoms. This is a good check when you are asked to deduce the number of atoms in a
section of a layer.

### Worked example 5: comparing graphite with a metal

*Compare how graphite and a metal conduct electricity.*

Both have delocalised electrons that are free to move and carry charge. In graphite the electrons come from the
carbon atoms, one per atom, and move along the layers. In a metal the delocalised electrons come from the outer
shells of the metal atoms and move through the whole structure. In graphite the structure is giant covalent, but
in a metal it is positive ions in a sea of electrons.

### Worked example 6: evaluating graphite as a lubricant

*Graphite powder is used to lubricate the lock of a door. Give two properties of graphite that make it suitable
and one reason why it might not be suitable in some situations.*

```
Suitable: its layers slide easily over one another (no covalent bonds between layers),
          so it reduces friction; it has a very high melting point, so it does not melt in hot machinery.
Possible problem: it conducts electricity, so it might cause a short circuit in electrical equipment;
          it is a powder that can make a mess or get into other parts.
```

Evaluation questions give you information and ask you to weigh advantages and disadvantages. Use the structure of
graphite to justify each point.

## Representing graphite in two and three dimensions

A 2D drawing of one layer shows hexagonal rings clearly, but it cannot show how the layers stack. A 3D model can
show the layers, but not the forces between them. When you look at a diagram, check three things: each carbon
atom inside a layer has three bonds; the rings are hexagons; and the layers are drawn separately with no bond
lines joining one layer to the next.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that graphite has "weak covalent bonds between layers". There are **no covalent bonds** between layers.
- Writing "graphite is soft because the covalent bonds are weak". The covalent bonds within a layer are strong.
- Forgetting that one electron per atom is delocalised.
- Confusing the number of bonds: diamond has four per atom, graphite three.
- Describing graphite as having molecules. It is a giant covalent structure.
- Stating that graphite conducts "because it is a metal". It is a non-metal.

## Exam technique

- **Describe the structure:** three bonds per carbon, layers of hexagonal rings, no covalent bonds between layers,
  one delocalised electron per atom.
- **Explain softness:** layers slide because there are no covalent bonds between them.
- **Explain conduction:** delocalised electrons free to move and carry charge.
- **Compare with diamond or a metal:** use paired statements and the key words above.
