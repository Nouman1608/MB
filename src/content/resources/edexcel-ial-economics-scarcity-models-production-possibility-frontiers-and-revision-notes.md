## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Social science | Studies human behaviour; cannot normally run controlled experiments on whole economies |
| Model | Simplified description of reality built on assumptions |
| Ceteris paribus | Other things held equal while one variable changes |
| Positive statement | Can be tested against evidence (true or false) |
| Normative statement | Value judgement about what ought to be |
| Scarcity | Unlimited wants, finite resources |
| Opportunity cost | The best alternative forgone |
| Free good | No opportunity cost in use; zero price clears the market |
| Economic good | Scarce, uses resources, has an opportunity cost |
| PPF | Curve of the largest output pairs of two goods, using every resource and current technology |
| Capital good | Used to produce other goods and services |
| Consumer good | Satisfies wants directly |
| Productivity | Output per worker (or per unit of input) |

## Formulas and calculations

| Idea | Method |
|---|---|
| Opportunity cost of good X in Y | Y given up divided by X gained |
| Marginal opportunity cost along a PPF | Change in the other good divided by change in this good |
| Percentage change | (new − old)/old × 100 |
| Productivity | Total output divided by number of workers |
| Unit labour cost | Wage bill divided by output |
| Real value of money after inflation of i for n years | Amount divided by (1 + i)ⁿ |
| Dividend yield | Dividend per share divided by share price |

**Small reminder (invented).** A farmer has 100 hours. A tonne of potatoes takes 10 hours and a tonne of onions takes 4 hours. The most potatoes is 100/10 = 10 tonnes and the most onions is 100/4 = 25 tonnes. The opportunity cost of one tonne of potatoes is 2.5 tonnes of onions, and of one tonne of onions is 0.4 tonnes of potatoes. The frontier is a straight line because the ratio never changes.

## Method: reading a PPF

1. Check the axes and the units.
2. Points on the frontier: all resources used efficiently.
3. Points inside: unemployed or misallocated resources, so output can rise at no opportunity cost.
4. Points outside: unobtainable with current resources and technology.
5. Move along the frontier: calculate the marginal opportunity cost as the amount given up divided by the amount gained.
6. Ask whether the question is about a movement (reallocation) or a shift (change in potential).

## PPF distinctions

| | Movement along | Shift outwards | Shift inwards |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Reallocation between two goods | Productive potential rises | Productive potential falls |
| Causes | Change in priorities or choices | More or better resources, new technology, investment, education, migration | Destruction of capital, war, natural disaster, resource depletion, emigration |
| Opportunity cost | Shown by the slope | Not applicable | Not applicable |

- Bowed out: marginal opportunity cost rises because resources are not equally suited to both goods.
- Straight line: constant opportunity cost.
- Moving from inside the frontier to the frontier uses idle resources. It is not growth.
- Capital goods raise productivity and potential output, but making more now means fewer consumer goods now.
- The PPF cannot say which point is best. That depends on values.

## Specialisation and the division of labour

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Skill and dexterity grow | Repetition, boredom, higher turnover |
| Less time lost switching tasks | Loss of broader skills |
| More use of machinery | Dependence on every stage |
| Higher output per worker, lower unit cost | Risk of unemployment if demand shifts |

Adam Smith (*The Wealth of Nations*, 1776) argued that dividing work into small tasks, using a pin factory as his illustration, raises output.

**Small reminder (invented).** A wage bill of 300 and output of 50 gives a unit labour cost of 6. If specialisation doubles output to 100, the cost per unit falls to 3, a 50% fall.

## Money and financial markets

| Function of money | Link to specialisation |
|---|---|
| Medium of exchange | Avoids the double coincidence of wants in barter |
| Measure of value | Prices make goods and wages comparable |
| Store of value | Earnings can be saved for later purchases |
| Deferred payment | Credit and wage contracts become possible |

Inflation erodes the store of value. 5,000 units held for two years at 3% inflation have the purchasing power of 5,000/1.03² = 4,712.98.

| Role of financial markets | Example |
|---|---|
| Facilitate saving | Deposits and bonds |
| Make funds available | Loans, mortgages, share issues |
| Facilitate exchange | Payment systems, credit |
| Forward markets | Fix a future price of a commodity or currency |
| Market for equities | Buy and sell company shares |

## Economic systems

| | Free market | Command | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Allocation | Price mechanism | Central planning | Both |
| Advantages | Efficiency incentives, choice, flexibility | Equity, public provision, strategic direction | Combines markets with correction of failures |
| Disadvantages | Inequality, missing public goods, externalities, monopoly | Information problems, weak incentives, shortages and surpluses | Government failure, trade-offs about size of the state |

Role of the state in a mixed economy: public and merit goods, regulation, tax and benefits, competition policy, macroeconomic management and sometimes ownership.

## Models and ceteris paribus

- Economics is a social science. Economies cannot be put in a laboratory, people react to being studied, and many experiments would be unethical or too costly.
- A model keeps the key relationships and drops detail. Typical assumptions: rational decisions, two goods, fixed technology, no other changes.
- Ceteris paribus lets you isolate one cause. Its weakness is that in real markets other things do change, so predictions can fail.
- Evaluation: ask whether the dropped detail matters for the question. A model is useful if its assumptions are close enough to the case being studied.

## Positive or normative

Look for "should", "fair", "too high" and "better". These signal value judgements. Statements with measurable claims are positive even if false. Value judgements shape which objectives a government chooses and which trade-offs it accepts.

## Chains of reasoning

**Investment in capital goods and growth.** Resources move from consumer to capital goods (movement along the PPF) → fewer consumer goods now → the capital stock rises → output per worker rises → the PPF shifts outwards → more of both goods are possible later. Evaluate: depends on the quality of investment, the size of the sacrifice, whether savings are available, and whether the extra output is shared fairly.

**Forward markets.** A business agrees today to sell 10 tonnes of a crop in three months at 200 a tonne (invented). If the spot price then is 170, it gains (200 − 170) × 10 = 300 compared with selling at spot. If the spot price is higher, it earns less than it could have. The benefit is certainty, which helps planning and investment.

**Choosing a system.** Identify the problem (for example, public goods are missing in a free market). Explain why the system fails (no one pays, free riders). Show how the state can respond. Then evaluate: the response may bring costs such as taxes, poor information or reduced incentives.

## Quick self-test

1. Define ceteris paribus.
2. Is "the minimum wage should be raised" positive or normative?
3. Using the farmer above, what is the opportunity cost of one tonne of onions?
4. A frontier passes through (capital, consumer) = (0, 60), (10, 54), (20, 40) and (30, 0). Find the marginal opportunity cost of moving from 10 to 20 capital goods, and from 20 to 30.
5. A point lies outside a PPF. What does this mean?
6. Give two causes of an outward shift of a PPF.
7. 250 workers produce 12,500 units. After new machines, they produce 16,250. Find the percentage rise in productivity.
8. Why is a toll-free road not a free good?
9. State the four functions of money.
10. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of a command economy.

### Answers

1. Other things being equal: all variables except the one being studied are held constant.
2. Normative ("should" is a value judgement).
3. 0.4 tonnes of potatoes.
4. From 10 to 20: (54 − 40)/10 = 1.4 consumer goods per capital good. From 20 to 30: (40 − 0)/10 = 4.
5. It is unobtainable with current resources and technology.
6. Any two of: investment in capital, better education, new technology, more labour (migration), discovery of resources.
7. Before: 12,500/250 = 50. After: 16,250/250 = 65. Rise: 15/50 = 30%.
8. It costs resources to build and maintain, so it has an opportunity cost, even though users pay no toll.
9. Medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value, method of deferred payment.
10. Advantage: resources can be directed to social goals and equality. Disadvantage: planners lack full information, so shortages and surpluses arise.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining opportunity cost as the cost in money, or as all the alternatives rather than the next best.
- Calling a statement positive because it contains a number, when it also contains "should".
- Saying a point inside the PPF shows growth.
- Describing shifts when the question asks about movements along the curve.
- Dividing the wrong way round when calculating marginal opportunity cost.
- Naming the functions of money without linking them to the question.
- Listing advantages of a system with no disadvantages, or vice versa.
- Describing a model as useless because it is simplified, rather than judging whether the assumption matters.
