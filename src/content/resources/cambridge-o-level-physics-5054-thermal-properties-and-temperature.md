## Thermal expansion

**Particle explanation.** When a substance is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy. In a
solid they vibrate with larger amplitude, pushing each other slightly further apart; in a liquid
and a gas they move faster and the average separation increases. The particles themselves do
not get bigger.

**Relative order of magnitude:** for the same temperature rise, **gases expand much more than
liquids, and liquids expand more than solids**. In a solid, strong forces hold the particles in
place; in a gas, the forces are negligible.

**Applications and consequences**

| Example | What happens | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid-in-glass thermometer | The liquid in the bulb expands more than the glass and rises up a narrow tube | A thin bore makes a small volume change produce a long movement up the scale |
| Bridges and railway tracks | Steel sections lengthen on hot days | Expansion gaps or sliding joints stop the structure buckling |
| Overhead power cables | Cables lengthen and sag more in summer | They are hung with some slack so they do not snap when they contract in winter |
| Tight metal lid on a glass jar | Hot water makes the metal lid expand more than the glass | The lid loosens and is easier to open |

**Explaining the thermometer for full marks:** the liquid expands by a definite amount for each
degree; because liquids expand more than the glass, the level rises; the narrow tube magnifies
the small volume change into a visible length; a thin-walled bulb lets energy reach the liquid
quickly.

## Kelvin and Celsius

```
T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273
```

- −40 °C = −40 + 273 = **233 K**
- 300 K = 300 − 273 = **27 °C**
- Absolute zero: 0 K = −273 °C

A **change** of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K, so temperature differences such as Δθ in the specific
heat capacity equation are the same on both scales.

## Internal energy, temperature and specific heat capacity

Heating an object increases its **internal energy**. A rise in temperature means an increase in
the **average kinetic energy of all of the particles**.

**Specific heat capacity** is the energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase:

```
c = ΔE / (m Δθ)        ΔE = m c Δθ        Δθ = ΔE / (m c)
```

Unit: J/(kg °C). A high value means a material needs a lot of energy to warm up and releases a
lot as it cools; water's value is about 4200 J/(kg °C).

**Worked example 1: a kettle.** 1.5 kg of water is heated from 20 °C to 100 °C. Find the energy
needed and the time a 2.0 kW kettle would take, assuming no energy is wasted.

```
ΔE = m c Δθ = 1.5 × 4200 × 80 = 504 000 J
t = E / P = 504 000 / 2000 = 252 s
```

In practice it takes longer, because some energy heats the kettle and the surroundings.

**Worked example 2: temperature rise.** A 0.40 kg copper block (c = 385 J/(kg °C)) receives
3080 J. Find its temperature rise.

```
Δθ = ΔE / (m c) = 3080 / (0.40 × 385) = 20 °C
```

**Comparing materials.** The same energy given to equal masses of water and of copper raises the
copper's temperature about eleven times more, because water's specific heat capacity is about
eleven times larger (4200 ÷ 385 ≈ 10.9).

## Measuring specific heat capacity

**Liquid:** heat a known mass of liquid in an insulated container with an electric immersion
heater of known power for a measured time; stir and record the temperature rise.

**Solid:** use a metal block with holes for a heater and a thermometer; wrap it in insulation;
heat for a measured time and record the rise.

In both: energy supplied ΔE = power × time; c = ΔE ÷ (mΔθ).

**Analysing results (illustrative data).** A 50 W heater warms 0.25 kg of water for 180 s and the
temperature rises by 8.2 °C.

```
ΔE = 50 × 180 = 9000 J
c = 9000 / (0.25 × 8.2) = 4390 J/(kg °C)
```

The result is above 4200 J/(kg °C) because some energy heats the container and escapes to the
surroundings, so the temperature rise is smaller than it would be with no losses. Improvements:
better insulation and a lid, stirring, and a drop of oil in the thermometer hole of a solid block
for good thermal contact.

## Melting, boiling and the heating curve

During **melting** and **boiling**, energy is transferred to the substance **without a change in
temperature**. During **solidification** and **condensation**, energy is transferred out, again
at constant temperature.

For water at standard atmospheric pressure: **melting point 0 °C, boiling point 100 °C**.

**Reading a heating curve** (temperature against time for ice heated steadily from −20 °C):

1. Sloping line: ice warms to 0 °C.
2. Flat section at 0 °C: melting; energy goes into changing state.
3. Sloping line: water warms from 0 °C to 100 °C.
4. Flat section at 100 °C: boiling.
5. Sloping line: steam warms above 100 °C.

**Latent heat** is the energy required to change the state of a substance. During a change of
state the energy does work against the **forces between particles**, separating them, rather than
increasing their kinetic energy, so the temperature stays constant.

## Evaporation versus boiling

| Evaporation | Boiling |
|---|---|
| Happens at any temperature | Happens only at the boiling point |
| Only from the surface | Throughout the liquid, with bubbles of vapour forming |
| Slow | Rapid |
| Cools the liquid left behind | Temperature stays at the boiling point while energy is supplied |

**How evaporation works:** particles in a liquid have a range of speeds. The **more energetic**
particles near the surface can overcome the attractive forces and escape.

**Why evaporation cools:** the particles that escape are the faster ones, so the **average kinetic
energy of the remaining particles falls**, and the temperature of the liquid falls.

**Factors that increase the rate of evaporation**

- **Higher temperature:** more particles have enough energy to escape.
- **Larger surface area:** more particles are at the surface.
- **Air movement over the surface:** escaped particles are carried away before they can return.

## Common mistakes

- Saying particles expand when heated.
- Adding 273 the wrong way: kelvin values are larger than Celsius values.
- Using the final temperature instead of the temperature **change** in ΔE = mcΔθ.
- Saying the temperature rises during melting or boiling.
- Writing that evaporation happens only at 100 °C.
- Explaining cooling by evaporation without saying the **faster** particles escape.

## Exam technique for command words

- **Explain** (expansion or cooling): give the particle mechanism, step by step.
- **Describe** (an experiment): apparatus, measurements, calculation and one precaution.
- **Calculate**: equation, substitution, answer with unit J, J/(kg °C) or °C.
- **State** (a value): for example, water boils at 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the measured specific heat capacity usually too high?** Energy is lost to the
surroundings and container, so the measured temperature rise is too small.

**Do I need to calculate latent heat?** The 5054 syllabus asks you to describe and explain latent
heat in terms of particles; it does not list a latent heat equation.

**Is Δθ the same in kelvin and Celsius?** Yes. A change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K.
