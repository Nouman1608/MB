Quick recall for section 3.6 **Firms and production** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: the demand for factors of production ("demand for the product, the price of different factors of production, their availability and their productivity"), labour-intensive and capital-intensive production, and "the difference between, and influences on, production and productivity". Full explanations are in the [firms and production study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-firms-and-production/); test yourself with the [firms and production practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-firms-and-production-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Factors of production | Land, labour, capital and enterprise |
| Derived demand | Demand for a factor that comes from demand for the product it helps make |
| Labour-intensive production | Uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital |
| Capital-intensive production | Uses a high proportion of capital relative to labour |
| Production | Total output in a period |
| Productivity | Output per unit of input in a period |
| Labour productivity | Output per worker or output per hour worked |
| Labour cost per unit | Wage cost divided by output (wage per hour ÷ output per hour) |
| Substitution | Replacing one factor with another when relative prices change |

## Demand for factors: four influences

| Influence | Effect on demand for a factor |
|---|---|
| Demand for the product rises | Rises (derived demand) |
| Price of the factor rises | Falls; firms may switch to other factors |
| Price of an alternative factor falls | Falls, as firms substitute the cheaper one |
| Factor is scarce | Firm uses less of it or finds alternatives |
| Productivity of the factor rises | Rises, because each unit adds more output and revenue |

## Price and productivity together

Labour cost per unit = wage per hour ÷ output per hour.

- $4 an hour, 5 units an hour: $0.80 per unit.
- $20 an hour, 40 units an hour: $0.50 per unit.

High wages can still mean low costs if productivity is high enough. If wages and productivity both rise by 10%, cost per unit is unchanged.

## Choosing a method

| Reason | Labour-intensive | Capital-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Factor prices | Cheap labour | Expensive labour |
| Market size | Small or variable | Large and steady |
| Product | Custom or personal | Standard, in bulk |
| Finance | Scarce | Available |
| Skills and technology | Lacking | Available |

A machine's cost is spread over its output, so it only gives low cost per unit when it is kept busy.

Example: a car-wash machine costs $55 an hour to run with its attendant. At 20 cars an hour that is $2.75 a car; at 4 cars an hour it is $13.75 a car, dearer than two hand-washers at $30 an hour ($7.50 a car). The best method depends on how many customers there are.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Labour-intensive | Low set-up cost; flexible; personal or custom products; jobs | High unit cost when wages are high; slower; less consistent; absence and industrial action |
| Capital-intensive | Low unit cost at high output; consistent quality; runs continuously | High set-up cost; breakdowns; inflexible; needs skilled maintenance; fewer jobs |

## Production versus productivity

- Production rises if more factors are used, even if each produces no more.
- Productivity rises only if the same factors produce more.
- Use output per hour when hours change; output per worker can mislead.

Example: 10 workers, 40 hours, 2,000 units, then 36 hours, 1,980 units. Output per worker falls 1% (200 to 198); output per hour rises 10% (5.0 to 5.5).

## Influences

- **Production**: quantity and quality of factors, technology, hours worked, demand, weather and other events.
- **Productivity**: education and training, capital per worker, technology, management and organisation, division of labour, motivation, health.

## Exam traps

- Treating more output as higher productivity.
- Comparing wages without productivity.
- Calling capital-intensive methods "always cheaper".
- Forgetting derived demand.
- Giving "hire more workers" as a way to raise productivity.
- Mixing up the method of production (labour- or capital-intensive) with the sector of production (primary, secondary, tertiary).

## Quick self-test

1. Why is the demand for labour a derived demand?
2. State the four influences on the demand for factors named in the syllabus.
3. 25 workers produce 1,500 units a week. Calculate output per worker.
4. A worker earns $10 an hour and produces 25 units an hour. Calculate labour cost per unit.
5. Give two reasons a firm might choose labour-intensive production.
6. Give one disadvantage of capital-intensive production.
7. A firm hires 10 more workers and output rises. Has productivity necessarily risen? Explain.
8. Output rises from 6,000 to 6,600 units while workers rise from 50 to 60. What happens to output per worker?
9. State two influences on labour productivity.
10. Why might a small bakery in a village not buy an automated production line?
11. What happens to a firm's demand for labour if machines become much cheaper?

### Answers

1. Firms want workers only to make goods and services, so demand for labour depends on demand for those products.
2. Demand for the product; the price of different factors; their availability; their productivity.
3. 1,500 ÷ 25 = 60 units.
4. 10 ÷ 25 = $0.40.
5. Any two: cheap labour; small or variable market; custom or personal product; lack of finance; lack of skills or technology.
6. High set-up cost; breakdowns halt output; inflexible; needs skilled maintenance; fewer jobs (any one).
7. No. Production rose, but output per worker could stay the same or fall.
8. It falls from 120 to 110, a fall of 8.3%.
9. Any two: training, capital per worker, technology, management, motivation, health.
10. Its output is small, so the machine's high cost would be spread over few loaves, making cost per loaf high.
11. It may fall, as the firm substitutes capital for labour, although if lower costs raise sales it may still need some workers.
