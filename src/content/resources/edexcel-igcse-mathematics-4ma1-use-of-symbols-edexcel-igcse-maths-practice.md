## Answers

**1. (a)** **Expression** (no equals sign) [1].

**(b)** **Equation** (true only for x = 3) [1].

**(c)** **Formula** (links force, mass and acceleration) [1].

**(d)** **Identity** (true for every value of x) [1].

**2. (a)** **1** [1].

**(b)** 1/3² = **1/9** [1].

**(c)** **4** [1] (the reciprocal of 1/4).

**3. (a)** **a⁸** [1].

**(b)** **b⁶** [1].

**(c)** **c¹⁰** [1].

**4. (a)** 2 × (−5)² = 2 × 25 = **50** [1].

**(b)** −(−5)² = −25 [1]; 3 × (−5) = −15, so −25 − 15 = **−40** [1].

**5. (a)** n + 5 = 12, so **n = 7** [1].

**(b)** 3n = 15, so **n = 5** [1].

**6.** For example a = 7, b = 2: a − b = 5 but b − a = −5, so they are not equal [1]. They are equal only when **a = b** (both are then 0) [1].

**7. (a)** 7 × 2 = 14 and x⁻² × x⁵ = x³ [1]; **14x³** [1].

**(b)** 24 ÷ 8 = 3; m² ÷ m⁻¹ = m³ and n⁵ ÷ n⁵ = n⁰ = 1 [1]; **3m³** [1].

**(c)** 2⁴ = 16 and (y⁻³)⁴ = y⁻¹² [1]; **16y⁻¹²** (or 16/y¹²) [1].

**8.** (5a²b)³ = 125a⁶b³ [1]; 125 ÷ 25 = 5 and a⁶b³ ÷ a⁴b² = a²b [1]; **5a²b** [1].

**9. (a)** x² + x³ = 4 + 8 = 12 [1]; x⁵ = 32, and 12 ≠ 32, so Jo is wrong [1].

**(b)** **x² × x³** [1].

**10. (a)** **2** [1].

**(b)** Fourth root of 16 is 2, and 2³ = 8 [1]; the negative power gives the reciprocal, **1/8** [1].

**(c)** Cube roots: 3/4 [1]; squared: **9/16** [1].

**11. (a)** 2^(2x) = 2⁵ [1]; 2x = 5, so **x = 5/2** [1].

**(b)** 10^(3x) = 10⁻² [1]; 3x = −2, so **x = −2/3** [1].

**12. (a)** **x^(−1/3)** [1].

**(b)** Fourth root of 81 is 3 [1]; (a¹²)^(1/4) = a³ and (b⁻⁸)^(1/4) = b⁻², so **3a³b⁻²** (or 3a³/b²) [1].

**(c)** 8ᵖ = 2^(3p) and 4√2 = 2² × 2^(1/2) = 2^(5/2) [1]; 3p = 5/2, so **p = 5/6** [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling 5(x + 2) = 5x + 10 an equation: it is true for every x, so it is an identity.
- Giving 3⁻² as −9 or −1/9. A negative power never makes the answer negative on its own.
- Writing −x² as +25 when x = −5. The square is worked out before the minus sign.
- In question 7(c), raising only y⁻³ to the fourth power and leaving the 2 unchanged.
- In question 8, cancelling 125 and 25 to 25 instead of 5, or subtracting the powers before cubing the bracket.
- In questions 11 and 12(c), trying to compare 4ˣ with 32, or 8ᵖ with 4√2, without first writing every number as a power of the same base.
- For fractional powers, finding 16³ first in question 10(b): taking the root first keeps the numbers small.

## Notes on the method

Questions 11 and 12(c) use one idea: once both sides are powers of the same base, the powers must be equal. Choose the smallest base that works (2 for 4, 8, 16, 32 and √2, since √2 = 2^(1/2); 3 for 9 and 27; 10 for 1000 and 0.01). Question 6 is the arithmetic rule behind statement 2.1 B: subtraction and division are not commutative, which is why 3 − x and x − 3 are different expressions and why the order of a fraction matters. Question 9 shows the quickest way to disprove a claimed rule: one value of x that makes the two sides different is enough. Question 4 is a reminder that substitution follows the same order of operations as arithmetic: put the value in brackets, square it, then apply any sign or coefficient outside the bracket.
