Section 12.3 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has four learning outcomes. You must be able to describe how paper chromatography separates mixtures of soluble substances "using a suitable solvent"; describe the use of **locating agents** for colourless substances (no specific locating agent is required); interpret simple chromatograms to identify unknown substances by comparison with known ones and to tell pure from impure substances; and state and use the equation for **Rf**. 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so all four outcomes apply to every candidate.

The broader [Practical Techniques guide](/resources/practical-techniques-titrations-chromatography-separation/) gives the outline. This guide explains each step of the method, shows how to read a chromatogram described in words or as a table, and works through Rf calculations in both directions.

## Why the substances separate

Chromatography paper is the material the substances travel over. As the solvent soaks up the paper, it carries the dissolved substances with it. Each substance spends a different proportion of its time dissolved in the moving solvent and a different proportion held by the paper. A substance that is very soluble in the solvent and weakly held by the paper travels far; one that is less soluble, or more strongly held, travels a shorter distance. So substances that start in the same spot finish at different heights.

This explains the phrase "using a suitable solvent". A suitable solvent must **dissolve** the substances being separated. A substance that does not dissolve in the solvent stays on the baseline and is not separated at all. If water does not work, another solvent, such as ethanol, may.

## The method, step by step

| Step | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw a baseline in **pencil** about 1–2 cm above the bottom of the paper | pencil (graphite) does not dissolve in the solvent; ink would run and add spots |
| 2 | Put small, concentrated spots of each sample on the baseline, spaced apart; let each dry and re-spot if needed | small spots give clear, separate results; spots too close can merge |
| 3 | Put solvent in a beaker to a depth **below the baseline** | if the baseline is under the solvent, the samples dissolve into the solvent in the beaker instead of travelling up the paper |
| 4 | Stand the paper in the beaker and cover it with a lid | reduces evaporation of the solvent from the paper |
| 5 | Remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top; mark the **solvent front** in pencil at once | the solvent front fades as the solvent evaporates; it is needed for Rf |
| 6 | Let the paper dry | any colourless spots can now be located |

## Colourless substances: locating agents

Many substances, such as sugars or amino acids, are colourless. Their spots are invisible after the run. A **locating agent** is applied to the dried chromatogram, usually by spraying; it reacts with the substances to produce coloured spots, which can then be seen and measured. The syllabus does not require the name of any locating agent: describe what it does and when it is used.

## Interpreting a chromatogram

**Identifying an unknown.** Run the unknown alongside known substances on the same paper with the same solvent. If a spot from the unknown is at the **same height** as a known substance, the unknown probably contains that substance. Only compare spots on the same chromatogram, or Rf values measured with the same solvent, because distances depend on the solvent used and on how far it travelled.

**Pure or impure.** A pure substance gives **one spot**. An impure substance, a mixture, gives **two or more spots**. Count all spots for that sample, including any that stayed on the baseline because they did not dissolve.

## Rf values

```
Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent
```

Both distances are measured **from the baseline**: to the centre of the spot and to the solvent front. Rf has no units and is always less than 1, because no substance can move further than the solvent carrying it. For a given solvent and paper, each substance has a characteristic Rf value.

## Worked example 1: identifying the parts of a mixture

*A food colouring, X, and four known dyes, A to D, are run on the same paper with water. The solvent front is 8.0 cm above the baseline. The results are:*

| Sample | Distance of spot(s) from baseline / cm |
|---|---|
| X | 2.0 and 5.6 |
| A | 2.0 |
| B | 3.2 |
| C | 5.6 |
| D | 7.2 |

*(a) Is X pure? (b) Which dyes does X contain? (c) Calculate the Rf value of dye B.*

**(a)** X gives two spots, so X is **not pure**; it is a mixture of at least two substances.

**(b)** X's spots are level with A (2.0 cm) and C (5.6 cm), so X contains **A and C**. It does not contain B or D.

**(c)** Rf of B = 3.2 ÷ 8.0 = **0.40**.

## Worked example 2: using Rf in reverse

*Dye Y has an Rf value of 0.45 in ethanol. In a run where the ethanol front travels 6.0 cm, how far above the baseline will the spot of Y be?*

Rearrange the equation: distance travelled by substance = Rf × distance travelled by solvent = 0.45 × 6.0 = **2.7 cm**.

## Worked example 3: annotated answer

*Describe how paper chromatography could show whether a colourless sample of an amino acid is pure. [4]*

**Model answer.** Spot the sample on a pencil baseline on chromatography paper [1]. Stand the paper in a suitable solvent with the solvent level below the baseline [1]. When the solvent has nearly reached the top, remove the paper, mark the solvent front and dry the paper; then spray it with a locating agent to make the spots visible [1]. If there is one spot, the sample is pure; two or more spots show it is impure [1].

*Why it scores:* the locating agent is the step that makes this a colourless-substance question. An answer that never makes the spots visible cannot reach a conclusion.

## Common mistakes

- **Drawing the baseline in ink.** The ink separates and adds extra spots.
- **Putting the baseline below the solvent level.**
- **Measuring to the top of the paper** instead of to the solvent front, or measuring from the bottom edge instead of from the baseline.
- **Writing Rf greater than 1**, or the equation upside down.
- **Comparing Rf values found with different solvents.**
- **Calling a single spot proof of purity in every case.** It is the expected result for a pure substance, but two substances with the same Rf in that solvent would also give one spot; the syllabus question usually asks only for the one-spot rule.
- **Naming a locating agent instead of describing its use.** The use is what is credited.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the method: baseline in pencil, spots, solvent below the baseline, lid, solvent front, drying, locating agent if colourless.
- **State** the Rf equation in words exactly as above.
- **Calculate**: show both distances, divide, and give the answer to two significant figures or two decimal places, matching the data.
- **Deduce** or **identify** from a chromatogram: refer to heights or Rf values ("same height as A") rather than colours, because different substances can share a colour.
- In the Alternative to Practical paper you may have to measure distances on a printed chromatogram with a ruler. Measure in millimetres or to 0.1 cm, from the baseline to the centre of each spot.
