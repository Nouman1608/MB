These questions cover section **3.1.3.6 Bond polarity** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Bond polarity study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-bond-polarity/) and the [Bond polarity revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-bond-polarity-revision-notes/). Electronegativity values (Pauling scale): H 2.1, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0, Cl 3.0, Br 2.8, S 2.5.

## Questions

**1.** Define electronegativity. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine. **[3]**

**3.** For each bond, state whether it is polar and mark any partial charges.

**(a)** H–Br **[1]**

**(b)** O–H **[1]**

**(c)** Cl–Cl **[1]**

**4.** Hydrogen fluoride and hydrogen chloride both contain a polar bond.

**(a)** Explain which bond is the more polar. **[2]**

**(b)** State and explain whether the molecule HCl has a permanent dipole. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why:

**(a)** the molecule CO₂ has polar bonds but no permanent dipole **[3]**

**(b)** the molecule H₂O has a permanent dipole **[2]**

**6.** Tetrachloromethane, CCl₄, and trichloromethane, CHCl₃, both have tetrahedral molecules.

**(a)** Explain why CCl₄ has no permanent dipole. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why CHCl₃ has a permanent dipole. **[2]**

**7.** Predict, with a reason, which of these molecules has a permanent dipole: BF₃, NH₃, CH₄, SF₆. **[3]**

**8.** Two isomers of 1,2-dichloroethene exist: cis (both Cl atoms on the same side of the C=C bond) and trans (Cl atoms on opposite sides).

**(a)** Which isomer has a permanent dipole? **[1]**

**(b)** Explain your answer. **[2]**

**9.** A thin stream of liquid is allowed to run from a burette past a charged plastic rod. Water is deflected towards the rod and hexane is not.

**(a)** Explain why the water is deflected. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why hexane, C₆H₁₄, is not deflected. **[2]**

**10.** Methane is non-polar but chloromethane, CH₃Cl, is polar. Explain the difference. **[3]**

**11.** Place the following bonds in order of increasing polarity, using the electronegativity values: C–H, C–Cl, C–N, C–O, C–F. **[2]**

**12.** A student states that "all molecules with polar bonds are polar molecules". Use two examples to show why the statement is wrong. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The power of an atom [1] to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Fluorine atoms are smaller than chlorine atoms, so the bonding pair is closer to the nucleus [1]; fluorine has fewer inner electron shells, so there is less shielding [1]; the nucleus therefore attracts the bonding pair more strongly [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** Polar: H δ+, Br δ− [1]

**(b)** Polar: O δ−, H δ+ [1]

**(c)** Not polar, because both atoms are the same [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** H–F is more polar [1] because fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine, so the electronegativity difference with hydrogen is larger [1]

**(b)** HCl has a permanent dipole, because it has one polar bond and nothing to cancel it [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** The C=O bonds are polar, with O δ− [1]; CO₂ is linear and symmetrical [1]; the two dipoles are equal and opposite, so they cancel [1]

**(b)** The O–H bonds are polar [1]; the molecule is bent, so the dipoles do not cancel and there is an overall dipole [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** The four C–Cl bonds are polar but identical [1]; they are arranged symmetrically (tetrahedral), so the dipoles cancel [1]

**(b)** In CHCl₃ the C–Cl bonds are polar but the C–H bond is essentially non-polar [1]; so the three C–Cl dipoles are not balanced and there is an overall dipole [1]. **[4]**

**7.** **NH₃** has a permanent dipole [1]. The N–H bonds are polar and the molecule is pyramidal, so the dipoles do not cancel [1]. BF₃ and SF₆ have polar bonds but are symmetrical (trigonal planar and octahedral), and CH₄ has essentially non-polar bonds, so none of them has a permanent dipole [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** The cis isomer [1]

**(b)** In the cis isomer both C–Cl dipoles point to the same side, so they do not cancel [1]; in the trans isomer the C–Cl dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Water molecules have a permanent dipole, with δ+ and δ− ends [1]; the molecules turn so that the end with the opposite charge faces the rod, and so are attracted towards it [1]

**(b)** Hexane has no permanent dipole, because the bonds are essentially non-polar [1]; there is no charge separation to be attracted to the rod [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The C–H bonds in methane are essentially non-polar, and the molecule is symmetrical [1]. In chloromethane the C–Cl bond is polar, with Cl δ− because chlorine is more electronegative [1]; the molecule is not symmetrical, so the dipole does not cancel [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Differences from carbon (2.5): C–H 0.4, C–N 0.5, C–Cl 0.5, C–O 1.0, C–F 1.5 [1]. Order: **C–H < C–N = C–Cl < C–O < C–F** [1]. **[2]**

**12.** Carbon dioxide [1] and tetrachloromethane (or BF₃ or SF₆) [1] each contain polar bonds but are symmetrical, so the dipoles cancel and the molecule is non-polar [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating that polar bonds always make a polar molecule.
- Forgetting to mention symmetry when explaining that dipoles cancel.
- Writing δ+ on the more electronegative atom.
- Leaving out "pair of electrons" or "covalent bond" in the definition of electronegativity.
- Saying that water is deflected because it is "charged".
