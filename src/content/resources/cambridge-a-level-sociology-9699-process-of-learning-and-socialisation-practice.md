## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *values*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** agencies of secondary socialisation. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between *ascribed status* and *achieved status*. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what sociologists mean by *ideology*, using an example. **[3]**

**5.** Explain **one** piece of evidence that supports the nurture side of the nature versus nurture debate. **[3]**

**6.** Explain what Erving Goffman meant by a *total institution*. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain **two** ways in which education acts as an agency of socialisation. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** criticisms of the functionalist view of socialisation. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the use of overt non-participant observation to study how parents socialise young children. **[8]**

**10.** *"Human behaviour is shaped far more by socialisation than by biology."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the quotation. **[10]**

**11.** "Religion is no longer an important agency of socialisation." Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers

### 1. Values [2]

General, shared beliefs about what is good, right or worth achieving [1], which guide behaviour and give rise to more specific norms, for example the value of respect for elders [1]. **[2]**

### 2. Agencies of secondary socialisation [2]

Any two, one mark each: education, peer group, media, religion, the workplace [1] [1]. The family alone is the main agency of primary socialisation, so it earns no credit here unless clearly linked to later life. **[2]**

### 3. Ascribed and achieved status [4]

- Ascribed status is a social position given at birth or by circumstances outside a person's control [1], for example sex category, caste or being born into a royal family [1].
- Achieved status is a position gained through a person's own effort, choices or merit [1], for example becoming a doctor or a champion athlete [1]. **[4]**

### 4. Ideology [3]

A set of ideas and beliefs [1] that presents the interests of a particular group as natural, fair or in everyone's interest, disguising inequality [1]. Example: Marxists argue the belief that anyone can succeed through hard work justifies inequality in capitalist societies; feminists argue that the idea women are naturally suited to caring justifies their unpaid domestic labour [1]. **[3]**

### 5. Evidence for nurture [3]

One piece of evidence, identified [1], explained [1] and linked to the debate [1]. For example: gender roles vary greatly between societies and have changed quickly within them [1]; this variation and speed of change could not occur if behaviour were fixed by genes [1]; so behaviour must be learned through socialisation [1]. Credit cases of children deprived of human contact who fail to acquire language, or studies showing boys and girls treated differently by parents. **[3]**

### 6. Total institution [3]

An institution where people live cut off from wider society, under one authority, for a long period [1], such as a prison, psychiatric hospital or military training camp [1]. Goffman (*Asylums*, 1961) argued that these institutions resocialise inmates by stripping away their previous identity (the "mortification of the self") and replacing it with a new one [1]. **[3]**

### 7. Education as an agency of socialisation [6]

Up to 3 marks per way: identify [1], explain [1], develop with a concept, example or theory [1].

- **Formal curriculum:** pupils are taught a society's language, history and shared knowledge, which builds a common culture; functionalists see this as transmitting shared values and a sense of belonging to the nation.
- **Hidden curriculum:** rules on punctuality, uniform, obedience and competition teach norms not on the timetable; Bowles and Gintis (1976) argued this prepares pupils to accept hierarchy at work.
- **Sanctions:** rewards (praise, grades, prizes) and punishments (detention, exclusion) teach and enforce acceptable behaviour, so education is also an agency of social control.
- **Gender socialisation:** subject choices and teacher expectations may channel boys and girls differently. **[6]**

### 8. Criticisms of functionalist views of socialisation [6]

Up to 3 marks per criticism.

- **Oversocialised view:** Dennis Wrong (1961) argued functionalism treats people as passively absorbing society's values, ignoring free will and choice; interactionists show that identities are negotiated.
- **Ignores conflict and power:** Marxists argue that socialisation passes on ruling-class ideology, not neutral shared values; feminists argue it reproduces patriarchy.
- **Assumes value consensus:** in diverse societies there may be several value systems, so it is unclear whose values are being transmitted.
- **Cannot explain deviance and resistance well:** subcultures and anti-school groups show that socialisation is not always successful. **[6]**

### 9. Overt non-participant observation of parents [8]

Credit up to 4 marks for strengths and up to 4 for limitations, applied to the context; at least one point should use a research concept such as validity, reliability or ethics.

**Strengths:**
- Records what parents actually *do* rather than what they say they do, which may raise validity [1].
- Overt observation allows informed consent from parents, which is ethical, especially important when young children are involved [1].
- A structured observation schedule (for example, counting instances of praise or gendered toy choice) gives data that can be compared between families, improving reliability [1].
- The observer is not involved in the family's activities, reducing the risk of influencing what happens [1].

**Limitations:**
- The Hawthorne effect: parents who know they are being watched may behave as they think they should, reducing validity [1].
- Access to private homes is difficult, so the sample may be small and unrepresentative, for example only confident middle-class families agreeing [1].
- Observation shows behaviour but not the meanings parents attach to it, so it may need to be combined with interviews [1].
- It is time-consuming and much socialisation happens at times observers cannot be present, such as bedtime [1]. **[8]**

### 10(a). Explaining the view [6]

Up to 6 marks for explaining the nurture position, with concepts and evidence applied.

- Sociologists argue that most behaviour is learned through socialisation, not fixed by biology [1].
- Behaviour varies widely between cultures, for example in gender roles and family forms [1], which would not happen if it were genetically fixed [1].
- Behaviour changes over short periods, faster than genes could change [1].
- Children deprived of normal human contact struggle to learn language and social skills, showing socialisation is necessary [1].
- Agencies such as the family and school actively shape behaviour through sanctions and role models [1]. **[6]**

### 10(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument: argument identified [1], explained [2], supported with evidence or theory [1], evaluated or applied [1].

- **Sociobiology:** E.O. Wilson (1975) argued that some social behaviour has an evolutionary basis, such as care for close kin. Hormone research is used to explain average differences in aggression. These suggest biology sets limits on what socialisation can achieve.
- **Interaction, not opposition:** humans have a biological capacity for language and learning; socialisation can only work on this capacity, so behaviour results from both, not "far more" from one.
- **Agency:** interactionists argue people are not simply shaped by socialisation but interpret and resist it; Wrong's (1961) critique of the oversocialised view supports this, so socialisation is not the only influence either.

Credit any two well-developed arguments. Answers that only restate the view, or give one-word counter-points, stay in the lower part of the range. **[10]**

### 11. Essay: religion as an agency of socialisation [26]

Marked by levels across AO1 (knowledge and understanding), AO2 (interpretation and application) and AO3 (analysis and evaluation).

**Indicative content for the view:**
- Secularisation in many societies: declining attendance and weaker influence of religious institutions on law, education and family life.
- Other agencies, especially media and peer groups, now supply many of the moral messages and role models religion once did.
- Postmodernists argue that people now pick and mix beliefs, so religion's socialising role is individual and weaker.

**Indicative content against the view:**
- In many societies religion remains central to daily life, law and family customs; global comparisons show wide variation.
- Durkheim: shared rituals strengthen the collective conscience and social solidarity.
- Religion shapes norms on diet, dress, marriage and festivals, often through family and community even where formal attendance falls.
- Religious identity can strengthen among migrant and minority communities as cultural defence.
- Religious schools combine education and religion as agencies.

**Evaluation:** distinguish formal religious practice from religious values; judge by society and social group; recognise that religion may work through other agencies rather than alone.

**Level guidance:** top-level answers apply accurate evidence from more than one society, weigh both sides and reach a supported conclusion; middle-level answers describe religion's role with limited evaluation; low-level answers rely on assertion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Definitions with no example or link to behaviour.
- Two "ways" that are really the same point.
- Evaluating a method in general terms without applying it to parents and children.
- Arguing against the quotation by repeating it in other words.
- Essays that describe religion without assessing how important its socialising role still is.
