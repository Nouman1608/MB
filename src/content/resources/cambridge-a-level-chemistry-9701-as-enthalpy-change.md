## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. understand that chemical reactions are accompanied by enthalpy changes, which can be exothermic (ΔH negative) or endothermic (ΔH positive)
2. construct and interpret a **reaction pathway diagram**, in terms of the enthalpy change of the reaction and of the activation energy
3. define and use **standard conditions** (this syllabus assumes 298 K and 101 kPa, shown by ⦵) and **enthalpy change** with particular reference to reaction (ΔH_r), formation (ΔH_f), combustion (ΔH_c) and neutralisation (ΔH_neut)
4. understand that energy transfers occur because of the **breaking and making of chemical bonds**
5. use **bond energies** (ΔH positive for bond breaking) to calculate ΔH_r
6. understand that some bond energies are **exact** and some are **averages**
7. calculate enthalpy changes from experimental results, using **q = mcΔT** and **ΔH = −mcΔT/n**

## Exothermic and endothermic: the sign convention

ΔH is the enthalpy change **of the chemical system**. In an exothermic reaction the system loses energy to the surroundings, so ΔH is **negative** and the surroundings warm up. In an endothermic reaction the system gains energy from the surroundings, so ΔH is **positive** and the surroundings cool down. Always write the sign, even for positive values: "+25.9 kJ mol⁻¹".

## Reaction pathway diagrams

A reaction pathway diagram plots **enthalpy** (vertical axis) against **progress of reaction** (horizontal axis).

- Draw a horizontal line for the **reactants** and another for the **products**, labelled with formulae.
- Join them with a curve that rises to a peak.
- Draw a vertical arrow from the reactant line to the peak and label it **E_A** (activation energy).
- Draw a vertical arrow from the reactant line to the product line and label it **ΔH**, pointing **down** for exothermic and **up** for endothermic.

For an **exothermic** reaction the products are **below** the reactants. For an **endothermic** reaction they are **above**, and E_A must be larger than ΔH, because the peak is above the products.

Interpreting a diagram: the activation energy of the **reverse** reaction is the height of the peak above the **products**, which equals E_A(forward) − ΔH(forward). For an exothermic reaction with E_A = +80 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −50 kJ mol⁻¹, the reverse activation energy is 80 − (−50) = **+130 kJ mol⁻¹**.

## The four standard enthalpy changes

| Term | Definition (under standard conditions) | Always exothermic? |
|---|---|---|
| ΔH_r⦵ | enthalpy change when the amounts of reactants shown in the equation react | no |
| ΔH_f⦵ | enthalpy change when **one mole of a compound** is formed from its **elements** in their **standard states** | no |
| ΔH_c⦵ | enthalpy change when **one mole of a substance** is **completely burned in oxygen** | yes |
| ΔH_neut⦵ | enthalpy change when an acid and an alkali react to form **one mole of water** | yes |

Points examiners check:

- **One mole** of the named substance in ΔH_f, ΔH_c; one mole of **water** in ΔH_neut.
- **Elements in their standard states**: carbon as graphite, oxygen as O₂(g). So ΔH_f of any element in its standard state is zero.
- **Complete** combustion: carbon goes to CO₂, hydrogen to H₂O.
- State symbols matter: ΔH_f⦵(H₂O(l)) and ΔH_f⦵(H₂O(g)) are different.

Equations that match the definitions:

    ΔH_f⦵ of ethanol:   2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l)
    ΔH_c⦵ of ethanol:   C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)
    ΔH_neut⦵:           ½H₂SO₄(aq) + NaOH(aq) → ½Na₂SO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

Fractions are allowed (and often required) to keep one mole of the stated substance.

## Bond breaking and bond making

Breaking a bond needs energy (endothermic); making a bond releases energy (exothermic). A reaction is exothermic overall if more energy is released forming the new bonds than is absorbed breaking the old ones.

    ΔH_r = Σ(bond energies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond energies of bonds formed)

### Worked example 1: combustion of methane

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g). Data booklet values: C–H 410, O=O 496, C=O (in CO₂) 805, O–H 460 kJ mol⁻¹.

    Broken: 4(C–H) + 2(O=O) = 4(410) + 2(496) = 1640 + 992 = 2632 kJ
    Formed: 2(C=O) + 4(O–H) = 2(805) + 4(460) = 1610 + 1840 = 3450 kJ
    ΔH_r = 2632 − 3450 = −818 kJ mol⁻¹

The standard enthalpy of combustion of methane is about −890 kJ mol⁻¹. Two reasons for the difference:

- the C–H and O–H values are **averages** over many compounds, not exact for methane and water, and
- bond energies refer to **gases**, but ΔH_c⦵ forms **liquid** water; condensing the water releases extra energy.

## Exact and average bond energies

A bond energy is **exact** when the bond exists in only one compound, such as H–H in H₂ or O=O in O₂ (the data booklet lists diatomic molecules as exact values). It is an **average** when the same type of bond occurs in many different compounds, such as C–H, because the strength varies slightly with the neighbouring atoms. Calculations using average values therefore give approximate answers.

## Enthalpy changes from experiments

    q = mcΔT        ΔH = −mcΔT / n

- m: mass of the substance being **heated** (for solutions, take 1.00 cm³ = 1.00 g)
- c: specific heat capacity; for water and dilute solutions 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ (data booklet)
- ΔT: temperature change in K (the same numerical value as in °C)
- n: moles of the **limiting** reactant, or moles of water for neutralisation

### Worked example 2: neutralisation

25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The temperature rises by 13.6 K.

    m = 50.0 g;   q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 13.6 = 2842 J
    n(H₂O) = 0.0250 × 2.00 = 0.0500 mol
    ΔH_neut = −2842 / 0.0500 = −56 850 J mol⁻¹ = −56.8 kJ mol⁻¹

Note that m is the mass of the **combined** solution, 50.0 g, not 25.0 g.

### Worked example 3: combustion with a spirit burner

0.600 g of propan-1-ol (Mr 60.0) is burned to heat 200 g of water, whose temperature rises by 20.5 K.

    q = 200 × 4.18 × 20.5 = 17 138 J
    n = 0.600 / 60.0 = 0.0100 mol
    ΔH_c = −17 138 / 0.0100 = −1 714 000 J mol⁻¹ = −1710 kJ mol⁻¹ (3 s.f.)

This is much less exothermic than the data-book value (about −2020 kJ mol⁻¹). Typical reasons: **heat lost** to the surroundings and to the container, **incomplete combustion** (soot forms), and **evaporation** of the alcohol from the wick before weighing.

## Common mistakes

- Using the mass of the fuel, not the mass of water, as m.
- Using one solution's volume instead of the total volume in a mixing experiment.
- Forgetting the negative sign in ΔH = −mcΔT/n for a temperature rise.
- Not converting J to kJ, or quoting ΔH without units.
- Leaving out "one mole", "standard conditions" or "standard states" from a definition.
- Reversing bonds broken and bonds formed in the subtraction.
- Drawing E_A from the products, or ΔH from the peak.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: include the amount (one mole of what), the process, and standard conditions.
- **Calculate**: set out q, n and ΔH as separate lines; give the sign and kJ mol⁻¹.
- **Explain** a discrepancy: name a specific cause (heat loss, incomplete combustion, average bond energies, gaseous versus liquid water) and say which way it moves the result.
