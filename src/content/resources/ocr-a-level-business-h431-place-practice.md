## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by 'place' in the marketing mix. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** reason why a producer might use a wholesaler. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between intensive and exclusive distribution. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Lowther Lamps makes a desk lamp at a cost of £8. The price to consumers is £20 in every channel.

- Direct (own website): delivery costs £4 per order, fixed website costs are £18,000 a year and forecast sales are 6,000 lamps.
- Via retailers: Lowther sells to retailers at £12 and forecast sales are 16,000 lamps.
- Via a wholesaler: Lowther sells to the wholesaler at £10 and forecast sales are 40,000 lamps.

**(a)** Calculate the contribution per lamp in the direct channel. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the direct channel's total contribution after fixed costs. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the total contribution from the retailer channel. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the retailers' margin as a percentage of the consumer price. **[2]**

**(e)** Evaluate which channel, or combination of channels, Lowther Lamps should use. **[9]**

**5.** Fenwick Fresh makes ready-to-cook meals with a five-day shelf life. It currently sells only through supermarkets and now plans to sell directly to customers online with delivery in refrigerated vans. Evaluate the logistics of this online distribution strategy for Fenwick Fresh. **[9]**

**6.** Verity Beauty makes a luxury skin cream that sells for £90 a jar. Its brand image is based on exclusivity and expert advice. Recommend and justify a pattern of distribution and a place mix for Verity Beauty. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The part of the marketing mix concerned with how the product reaches the customer [1], including where it is sold and how it is distributed [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A wholesaler buys in large quantities from the producer [1], breaks it into smaller loads and sells to many small retailers that the producer could not reach economically [1], so the producer saves on selling, storage and delivery costs and can sell larger volumes [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Intensive distribution uses as many outlets as possible [1]. Exclusive distribution uses one or very few outlets in an area [1]. Intensive suits convenience goods and gives wide availability, while exclusive suits luxury goods and protects the image [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 20 − 8 − 4 [1] = **£8** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 6,000 × 8 = £48,000 [1]; 48,000 − 18,000 = **£30,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** (12 − 8) × 16,000 [1] = **£64,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** (20 − 12) ÷ 20 × 100 [1] = **40%** [1]. **[2]**

**(e)** Direct: the highest contribution per lamp (£8 against £4 and £2) and the producer keeps the customer data and controls the brand [1]; but the total contribution is only £30,000 after fixed costs because the reach is small [1].
Retailer: £64,000 total, more than double the direct channel, because retailers provide outlets, display and traffic [1]; the contribution per lamp is lower and Lowther depends on retailers' decisions [1].
Wholesaler: £2 per lamp × 40,000 = £80,000 is the highest total, with the least selling effort [1]; but the margin per lamp is small and Lowther loses contact with consumers, so profit is sensitive to cost increases [1].
Judgement: use a combination: sell to wholesalers and retailers for volume, and run a direct website for brand building, higher margin products and customer data [1]. The risk is channel conflict if the website undercuts retailers, so prices should be consistent [1]. All the forecasts are uncertain, so Lowther should review actual sales regularly [1]. **[9]**

**5.** Benefits: online sales let Fenwick keep the full retail margin and gain data on its customers [1]; it reaches customers who do not shop in the supermarkets that stock the product [1]; subscriptions could give regular, predictable orders [1].
Logistics problems: a five-day shelf life leaves little time for delays, so it needs fast, reliable delivery [1]; refrigerated vans and cold packaging are expensive, and the cost per delivery is high compared with bulk delivery to supermarkets [1]; small orders to many addresses are harder to plan than pallet loads [1]; failed deliveries and returns waste perishable stock [1].
Judgement: online distribution can work if orders are large enough, perhaps through a minimum spend or subscription, so that delivery cost per meal is acceptable [1]; Fenwick should start in a limited area where delivery is quick and compare the extra margin with the extra costs before expanding [1]. **[9]**

**6.** Recommendation: selective or exclusive distribution, for example in a small number of department store beauty counters and specialist pharmacies, plus the brand's own website [2].
Justification: the brand relies on exclusivity and expert advice, so outlets with trained staff protect the image [1]; selling in mass-market supermarkets would place the cream next to cheaper brands and risk the premium image [1]; a high price of £90 means each outlet earns a good margin, so retailers will want to stock it [1]. The website offers a direct channel with consultations and gives data, and avoids the margin taken by retailers [1].
Compare: an exclusive pattern gives the strongest image but limits sales; a selective pattern gives more reach while keeping control [1].
Risks: a small number of outlets makes sales depend on them, and online sales could conflict with the stores if prices differ [1]. Judgement: selective distribution combined with a website is most suitable, with premium packaging and fast delivery, because it protects the image and still gives sufficient reach [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the retail price instead of the producer's selling price in the channel contribution.
- Treating place as shops only.
- Recommending a distribution pattern without linking it to the brand image.
- Forgetting delivery and returns costs for online sales.
