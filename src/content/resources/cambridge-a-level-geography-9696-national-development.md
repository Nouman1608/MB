Section 14.1 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026 has three parts: "the nature of the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors and their roles in economic development"; "the nature, causes (physical and human) and distribution of global inequalities in social and economic wellbeing"; and the ability to "critically evaluate some of the measures and indices of social and economic inequality". It opens Topic 14, Economic transition, one of the Paper 4 Advanced Human Geography options.

The [Topic 14 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-economic-transition/) summarises the three-sector model and gives a table of measures. This page goes further: how to read employment-structure data, how physical and human causes of inequality work as chains rather than lists, and how the main indices are actually built, so that your critical evaluation rests on how they work. For quick recall use the [14.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-national-development-revision-notes/), then try the [14.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-national-development-practice/).

## The four sectors and what each does for development

| Sector | Nature of the work | Role in development |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting or growing raw materials | Feeds the population and earns early export income; releases labour to other sectors when farm productivity rises |
| Secondary | Processing and manufacturing | Adds value to raw materials; creates large numbers of urban jobs; drives export-led growth in many newly industrialised economies |
| Tertiary | Providing services to people and firms | Supports the other sectors (transport, finance, retail); grows as incomes rise and demand shifts to services |
| Quaternary | Producing and handling knowledge and information | Research, design and ICT raise productivity across the whole economy; concentrated in high-income cores |

Two ideas sharpen answers about "roles":

- **Linkages.** A sector matters not only for its own jobs but for the demand it creates elsewhere. A car assembly plant buys steel, glass and components (backward linkages) and supports dealers and repair shops (forward linkages). A mine exporting unprocessed ore may create few linkages, so its role in development is smaller than its export value suggests.
- **Productivity versus employment.** In many low-income countries most people work in farming, yet farming produces a much smaller share of national output, because output per worker is low. Employment share and output share tell different stories, and the gap between them is itself a measure of development.

### Reading employment-structure data

Employment structure is often shown as a triangular graph, with primary, secondary and tertiary percentages on the three axes, or as a stacked bar for each country. The figures below are for three fictional countries.

| Country (fictional) | Primary (%) | Secondary (%) | Tertiary and quaternary (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arlenia | 60 | 15 | 25 |
| Bescova | 25 | 30 | 45 |
| Corvant | 3 | 20 | 77 |

**Annotated interpretation.**

- *Arlenia* has a pre-industrial structure: most workers farm, and the small secondary sector suggests limited processing of its raw materials.
- *Bescova* is industrialising: secondary employment is highest of the three and primary has fallen, consistent with workers moving from farms to factories and cities.
- *Corvant* is post-industrial: almost no primary employment and a large service and knowledge sector. Its secondary share is below Bescova's, showing deindustrialisation.
- *Caution:* the data show structure at one date, not change over time. They do not show the informal sector, which can be large in countries like Arlenia, or the productivity of each sector.

The pattern fits the sequence described by the Clark-Fisher model, but countries do not have to pass through every stage. Some move from farming to services with only a short period of industrial employment growth.

## Global inequalities: nature, causes and distribution

**Nature.** Wellbeing has economic dimensions (income, wealth, employment) and social dimensions (health, education, housing, gender equality, political freedom). Inequality exists at every scale: between world regions, between countries, and within them. A country can rank highly on income and much lower on a social measure, which is why multiple indicators are needed.

**Distribution.** The highest levels of wellbeing are in North America, Western Europe, Japan, South Korea and Australasia, with some Gulf states high on income. The lowest are concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia. The Brandt Report (1980) drew a single North-South line; today that line is a poor fit because many East Asian economies have moved into the high-income group and inequality within countries has grown.

**Causes as chains.** Examiners reward explaining how a cause works, not naming it. For each cause, show the mechanism and the outcome:

| Cause | Type | Mechanism | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Landlocked location | Physical | Exports must cross other countries to reach a port, adding cost and delay | Less competitive trade, lower investment |
| High disease burden, such as malaria | Physical (with human factors) | Illness reduces school attendance and working days | Lower productivity and income |
| Climatic hazards (drought, cyclones) | Physical | Repeated loss of crops, livestock and infrastructure | Savings destroyed, recovery spending replaces investment |
| Dependence on one primary export | Human (often colonial in origin) | Income rises and falls with world prices | Unstable revenue, debt problems |
| Conflict | Human | Destroys infrastructure, displaces people, deters investment | Development reversed for years |
| Weak governance or corruption | Human | Public money diverted, contracts unreliable | Poor services, less investment |
| Low investment in education and health | Human | Workforce lacks skills for higher-value sectors | Economy stuck in low-productivity activities |

Most real cases combine causes. A landlocked country exposed to drought and dependent on one export suffers from several chains at once. Physical causes are rarely decisive on their own: human responses such as irrigation, trade corridors or disease control can reduce their effect.

## Measures and indices: how they are built

Critical evaluation is easier once you know the construction.

### The Human Development Index (HDI)

The HDI combines three dimension indices, each scaled from 0 to 1: health (life expectancy at birth), education (mean and expected years of schooling) and income (GNI per capita at purchasing power parity, on a logarithmic scale). Since 2010 the HDI is the **geometric mean** of the three indices: multiply them and take the cube root.

**Worked example (fictional values).**

```
Country P: health 0.80, education 0.60, income 0.70
  arithmetic mean = (0.80 + 0.60 + 0.70) / 3 = 0.700
  HDI = cube root of (0.80 x 0.60 x 0.70) = cube root of 0.336 = 0.695

Country Q: health 0.90, education 0.40, income 0.80
  arithmetic mean = (0.90 + 0.40 + 0.80) / 3 = 0.700
  HDI = cube root of (0.90 x 0.40 x 0.80) = cube root of 0.288 = 0.660
```

Both countries have the same arithmetic mean, but Q's HDI is lower because its education index is very weak. The geometric mean penalises uneven development: a high income cannot fully make up for poor schooling. This is a strength to quote in evaluation.

**Limitations.** National averages hide regional and social differences; only three dimensions are included; it ignores environmental sustainability, security and political freedom; data for some countries are estimates and arrive late.

### The Gini coefficient and the Lorenz curve

A **Lorenz curve** plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of the population, from poorest to richest. A straight diagonal line means perfect equality. The **Gini coefficient** is the area between the diagonal and the curve divided by the whole area under the diagonal: 0 is perfect equality and 1 is maximum inequality.

**Worked example (fictional income shares by fifth of the population, poorest first).**

```
Country R: 5%, 10%, 15%, 25%, 45%
  cumulative: 5, 15, 30, 55, 100
Country S: 10%, 15%, 18%, 22%, 35%
  cumulative: 10, 25, 43, 65, 100
```

Using the trapezium rule on these points gives a Gini of 0.38 for R and 0.23 for S. R is more unequal: its richest fifth receives 45% of income, nine times the share of its poorest fifth.

**Limitations.** Very different Lorenz curves can cross and give similar Gini values; it measures distribution, not how poor anyone is; survey data on high incomes are often incomplete.

### Choosing between measures

| If the question is about... | Strongest measure | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Average living standards between countries | GNI per capita (PPP) | Comparable, widely available |
| Broad human development | HDI | Combines health, education and income |
| How fairly income is shared | Gini coefficient | Captures distribution |
| Deprivation within households | Multidimensional Poverty Index | Measures overlapping deprivations directly |
| Gender inequality | Gender Inequality Index | Includes reproductive health, empowerment and labour market participation |

## Common mistakes

- Equating employment share with economic importance.
- Listing causes of inequality without the mechanism that turns each into lower wellbeing.
- Calling a cause "physical" when its effect depends mainly on human responses; say how the two interact.
- Saying the HDI is an average of three numbers without noting it is a geometric mean.
- Reading a Gini coefficient as a poverty measure.
- Evaluating a measure only by its weaknesses: give a strength, a limitation and a judgement about when it is most useful.

## Command words

- **Describe** a distribution: name the high and low regions, exceptions and the overall pattern.
- **Explain** causes: show the chain from cause to outcome.
- **Assess** or **evaluate** a measure: judge how well it captures inequality for a stated purpose, then conclude.
