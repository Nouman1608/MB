This guide covers **8.2 Characteristics and formation of coastal landforms** in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists three groups: **erosional landforms** (cliffs and wave-cut platforms, caves, arches and stacks); **depositional landforms** (beaches in cross section and plan, swash and drift aligned beaches, simple and compound spits, tombolos, offshore bars, barrier beaches, coastal dunes, tidal sedimentation in estuaries, coastal saltmarshes and mangroves); and **the role of sea level change** in forming coastal landforms.

The content is assessed in Paper 3, Advanced Physical Geography Options, in the 10-mark structured question and the 20-mark essays on Coastal environments. ## Describing a landform: "characteristics" and "formation"

The subtopic title asks for two things, and examiners mark them separately.

- **Characteristics** are what the landform looks like: shape in plan and profile, size, gradient, sediment type and sorting, and position relative to tide levels.
- **Formation** is the sequence of processes from 8.1 (wave action, erosion, transport, deposition, sub-aerial processes) acting on a particular geology over time.

A strong answer moves from one to the other: "the platform slopes gently seaward at about 1° *because* erosion is concentrated in the intertidal zone and the cliff retreats across it".

## Erosional landforms: the control of geology

### Cliff profiles

Cliff shape depends on rock **strength**, **structure** (the dip of beds, joints and faults) and the balance between marine and sub-aerial processes.

- **Resistant, horizontally bedded or vertically jointed rock** (for example many limestones and granites) gives near-vertical cliffs, because blocks fall cleanly along joints.
- **Beds dipping steeply seaward** give a cliff face that follows the bedding planes. Blocks can slide down the bedding planes towards the sea, so the cliff is often less steep but prone to sliding.
- **Beds dipping landward** give steep, stable faces, because blocks are tilted back into the cliff.
- **Weak, unconsolidated material** such as glacial till or clay gives lower-angled cliffs shaped mainly by slumping and mudflows, often with a hummocky, terraced profile.

Where waves remove debris from the cliff foot faster than it arrives, the cliff stays steep. Where debris accumulates, the base is protected and sub-aerial processes lower the cliff angle. This is why the *same* rock can give different profiles on exposed and sheltered coasts.

### Wave-cut platforms

A wave-cut (shore) platform is a gently sloping rock surface, usually less than about 4°, extending from the cliff foot to below low-tide level. Its characteristics give clues to formation:

- it is widest and best developed in the intertidal zone, where waves attack for the longest time;
- its surface often shows rock pools, ridges along harder beds, and grooves along joints;
- it carries little sediment on exposed coasts, because waves sweep it away.

As the platform widens, waves break further out and lose energy crossing it, so cliff retreat slows. Weathering of the platform surface itself (wetting and drying, salt crystallisation, biological action by rock-boring organisms) also lowers it, so platforms are not cut by waves alone.

### Concordant and discordant coasts

The pattern of headlands and bays reflects how rock bands meet the coast. On a **discordant** coast, bands of hard and soft rock run at right angles to the shoreline, so soft rock is eroded into bays and hard rock is left as headlands. On a **concordant** coast, bands run parallel to the shoreline, so a resistant outer band protects softer rock behind it until it is breached. In Dorset, England, the coast around Swanage is discordant, while the Lulworth area is concordant: Lulworth Cove is a near-circular bay eroded in softer rock after the sea breached the outer limestone. Headlands on discordant coasts are where caves, arches and stacks develop. Old Harry Rocks, in chalk at the end of the Purbeck headland, is a well-known group of stacks.

## Depositional landforms: sediment supply and wave direction

### Beaches in profile

The beach profile is not fixed. It responds to wave conditions:

- **High energy (storm) conditions** move sediment offshore, lowering and flattening the upper beach and building offshore bars. The profile is called a *storm* or winter profile.
- **Low energy (swell) conditions** move sediment onshore, building berms and steepening the upper beach. This is a *swell* or summer profile.

Sediment size controls gradient. Coarse shingle lets swash percolate, so backwash is weak and material stays high on the beach, giving gradients that can exceed 10°. Fine sand stays saturated, backwash is strong, and gradients are often only 1-2°. Smaller features include **beach cusps** (regular crescent-shaped embayments along the upper beach) and **ridges and runnels** on the lower beach of sandy coasts with a large tidal range.

### Beaches in plan

A **swash-aligned** beach faces the dominant waves, which arrive almost parallel to the shore after refraction. Sediment moves up and down the beach rather than along it, and the beach is often a curved bay-head beach between headlands. A **drift-aligned** beach lies at an angle to the dominant waves, so longshore drift is strong and sediment is carried towards one end. Drift-aligned beaches feed spits and are sensitive to interruption by groynes or harbour walls.

### Spits, tombolos, bars and barrier beaches

All of these depend on a supply of sediment and a place where drift slows or meets sheltered water.

- **Simple spit:** a linear ridge of sand or shingle extending from the land where the coast changes direction. Sandy Hook in New Jersey, USA, is an example.
- **Compound spit:** a spit with several recurved ridges on its landward side. Blakeney Point in Norfolk, England, has a series of shingle recurves enclosing saltmarsh.
- **Spits that deflect rivers:** Orford Ness in Suffolk has pushed the mouth of the River Alde many kilometres southwards.
- **Tombolo:** a ridge linking an island to the mainland, formed where wave refraction around the island makes waves from both sides meet and drop sediment. St Ninian's Isle in Shetland is joined to the mainland by a sand tombolo.
- **Offshore bar:** a submerged or partly exposed ridge parallel to the coast, often where breaking storm waves deposit sediment eroded from the beach.
- **Barrier beach and barrier island:** a ridge above high-tide level separated from the mainland by a lagoon. The Frisian Islands off the Netherlands and Germany, and Padre Island in Texas, are barrier islands. Many barriers migrate landward by **overwash**: storm waves carry sediment over the crest and deposit it on the lagoon side, so the barrier "rolls over" as sea level rises.

### Coastal dunes

Dunes need a large beach that dries out at low tide, onshore winds strong enough to move sand, and obstacles to start deposition. A dune system shows **succession** inland: embryo dunes, foredunes (yellow dunes, dominated by marram grass, which grows upwards through fresh sand), then grey dunes with more humus and a greater variety of plants. Where wind removes sand down to the water table, damp **dune slacks** form. Studland in Dorset and Formby in Merseyside, England, have well-studied dune systems.

### Estuaries, saltmarshes and mangroves

Tidal sedimentation in estuaries depends on the **tidal cycle**. Flow is fastest in mid-tide and slowest at high and low slack water, when fine sediment settles. In brackish water, clay particles **flocculate** (clump together) and settle faster. Mudflats grow until pioneer plants such as glasswort (*Salicornia*) and cordgrass (*Spartina*) colonise them. Plants slow the water, trap more sediment and raise the surface, so the marsh is flooded less often and less salt-tolerant plants follow, giving a **low marsh** and then a **high marsh**, drained by branching creeks. Mangroves occupy the same intertidal zone on tropical coasts. Red mangroves (*Rhizophora*) have arching prop roots, and black mangroves (*Avicennia*) send up breathing roots called pneumatophores; both slow tidal currents and trap mud.

## The role of sea level change

Changes in relative sea level leave landforms that present-day processes did not make.

- **Eustatic change** (global ocean volume): after the last glacial maximum, sea level rose by over 100 m. This drowned river valleys to form **rias** and glacial troughs to form **fjords**, and pushed sediment onshore, building many barrier beaches and shingle ridges.
- **Isostatic change** (local land level): where ice sheets melted, the land rebounded. Former beaches and wave-cut platforms were lifted above present wave reach as **raised beaches**, often backed by a **relict cliff** with old caves. Western Scotland has many examples, including raised beaches around the Isle of Arran.
- **Drowned parallel ridges:** where sea level rises against a coast with ridges parallel to the shore, valleys flood to leave long islands and inlets parallel to the coast, as on the Dalmatian coast of Croatia.

## Worked example

*Explain why a compound spit differs from a simple spit. [5]*

> A spit forms where longshore drift continues to carry sediment past a change in coastline direction, so the ridge grows out into open water [1]. Waves refracting around the tip, or a secondary wave direction, curve the end into a recurve [1]. A simple spit has a single recurve or none, because conditions at the tip stayed the same [1]. A compound spit has several recurves along its landward side, each marking an earlier position of the tip [1], so it records repeated changes in dominant wave direction or storm events as the spit grew [1].

The answer scores because every characteristic (number of recurves) is tied to a process (refraction, changes in wave direction).

## Common mistakes

- Describing a landform's formation without its characteristics, or the reverse.
- Treating all cliffs as the same shape; rock structure and debris removal must be mentioned.
- Writing that a tombolo is "a spit that reaches an island" without explaining wave refraction around the island.
- Forgetting that beach profiles change seasonally and with storms.
- Using "sea level rose" without stating eustatic or isostatic and the landform it produced.

## Exam technique for 8.2

- **Describe** questions on a photograph or map want shape, size and materials, with figures if a scale is given.
- **Explain** questions want a process sequence. A labelled sketch can carry several marks if every label states a process.
- **Assess** or **how far** essays (for example on the role of sea level change) need a judgement that varies by landform and timescale, supported by named places.
