These questions cover section 3.2.10, The Young modulus, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), shared International AS and A-level content. Learn the content first with the [Young modulus study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-the-young-modulus-oxfordaqa/) and the [Young modulus revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-the-young-modulus-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/). Take g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. Assume every wire stays within its limit of proportionality unless a question says otherwise.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the Young modulus of a material and state its SI unit. **[2]**

**2.** A wire has diameter 0.56 mm.

**(a)** Calculate its cross-sectional area in m². **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the tensile stress in the wire when the tension is 35 N. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why two wires made of the same steel but with different diameters have different spring constants but the same Young modulus. **[2]**

**4.** Wire B is made of the same material as wire A. B is three times as long as A and has half its diameter. The same tension acts in each. Calculate the ratio extension of B : extension of A. **[2]**

**5.** On the straight part of a stress-strain graph, a stress of 175 MPa corresponds to a strain of 0.25%. Calculate the Young modulus. **[2]**

**6.** A copper wire of length 1.20 m and diameter 0.50 mm carries a tension of 20 N. The Young modulus of copper is 1.3 × 10¹¹ Pa. Show that the extension is about 0.9 mm. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** A lift car of mass 800 kg hangs from a steel cable of length 40 m and diameter 15 mm. The Young modulus of the steel is 2.1 × 10¹¹ Pa. Ignore the mass of the cable.

**(a)** Calculate the tensile stress in the cable when the car is at rest. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the extension of the cable when the car is at rest. **[2]**

**(c)** The car accelerates upwards at 1.5 m s⁻². Calculate the additional extension of the cable. **[2]**

**8.** A student loads a wire of original length 1.60 m and diameter 0.30 mm and records these results.

| Load / N | 0 | 5.0 | 10.0 | 15.0 | 20.0 | 25.0 | 30.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extension / mm | 0 | 0.55 | 1.10 | 1.65 | 2.20 | 2.85 | 3.70 |

**(a)** Use the data to show that extension is proportional to load up to 20 N but not beyond. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the Young modulus of the material. **[3]**

**(c)** State why the readings at 25 N and 30 N must not be used to find the Young modulus. **[1]**

**9.** In a Young modulus experiment the following values are recorded: original length 1.250 ± 0.005 m; diameter 0.45 ± 0.01 mm; tension 40.0 N (uncertainty negligible); extension 1.80 ± 0.05 mm.

**(a)** Calculate the Young modulus. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the length, the area and the extension, and hence in the Young modulus. **[3]**

**(c)** Give the Young modulus with its absolute uncertainty, and suggest one change that would most reduce the uncertainty. **[2]**

**10.** One arrangement for Required practical 2 hangs two identical wires from the same beam. One carries a fixed small load and a vernier scale; the other is the test wire, which carries the increasing load and the reading mark.

**(a)** Explain why the second, reference wire improves the measurement of extension. **[2]**

**(b)** Before readings start, a small load is left on the test wire. Explain why, and why this does not affect the value of the Young modulus found from the gradient of a graph. **[2]**

**(c)** State one safety precaution for this experiment. **[1]**

**11.** A uniform horizontal rod of weight 120 N hangs from two vertical wires, one at each end. Both wires have original length 1.50 m. Wire S is steel (E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa) with diameter 0.60 mm. Wire T is brass (E = 1.0 × 10¹¹ Pa).

**(a)** Explain why the tension in each wire is 60 N. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the extension of wire S. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the diameter wire T must have for the rod to stay horizontal. **[3]**

**12.** Two materials are tested. The stress-strain graph for material X is a straight line from the origin to a strain of 2.0 × 10⁻³ at a stress of 140 MPa, then curves. The graph for material Y is a straight line from the origin to a strain of 1.0 × 10⁻³ at a stress of 190 MPa, where Y fractures.

**(a)** Calculate the Young modulus of each material. **[2]**

**(b)** State which material is stiffer, giving a reason. **[1]**

**(c)** A rod of material X has length 0.60 m and diameter 8.0 mm. Calculate its extension under a tension of 4.0 kN. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain, within the limit of proportionality [1]; unit pascal, Pa (N m⁻²) [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "Stress over strain" without the condition is often accepted for the ratio mark, but stating the limit shows you know where E applies.

**2. (a)** A = π × (0.28 × 10⁻³)² = 2.46 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]

**(b)** Stress = 35 / 2.46 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.42 × 10⁸ Pa [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Using 0.56 mm as the radius gives an area four times too large.

**3.** The spring constant k = EA/L depends on the cross-sectional area, so the thicker wire has a larger k [1]. The Young modulus uses stress and strain, which remove the effect of area and length, so it depends only on the material, which is the same [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Quote k = EA/L; it makes the explanation precise.

**4.** ΔL ∝ L/d², so B : A = 3 / (1/2)² [1] = 12 : 1 [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Halving the diameter quarters the area: the factor is 4, not 2.

**5.** Strain = 0.25/100 = 2.5 × 10⁻³ [1]; E = 175 × 10⁶ / 2.5 × 10⁻³ = 7.0 × 10¹⁰ Pa [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Substituting 0.25 as the strain gives an answer 100 times too small.

**6.** A = π × (0.25 × 10⁻³)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; ΔL = FL/(EA) = 20 × 1.20 / (1.3 × 10¹¹ × 1.96 × 10⁻⁷) = 9.4 × 10⁻⁴ m, which is about 0.9 mm [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* In a "show that", give the unrounded result (0.94 mm) before comparing with the stated value.

**7. (a)** F = 800 × 9.81 = 7850 N and A = π × (7.5 × 10⁻³)² = 1.77 × 10⁻⁴ m² [1]; stress = 4.44 × 10⁷ Pa [1]

**(b)** Strain = 4.44 × 10⁷ / 2.1 × 10¹¹ = 2.11 × 10⁻⁴ [1]; ΔL = 2.11 × 10⁻⁴ × 40 = 8.5 × 10⁻³ m [1]

**(c)** T − mg = ma, so T = 800 × (9.81 + 1.5) = 9050 N [1]; new extension = 9.75 mm, so the additional extension is 1.3 × 10⁻³ m [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Extension is proportional to tension, so the extra extension can also be found as 8.46 mm × 1200/7848.

**8. (a)** Load/extension = 9.09 N mm⁻¹ for every reading from 5.0 N to 20 N, so extension is proportional to load [1]; at 25 N it is 8.77 N mm⁻¹ and at 30 N it is 8.11 N mm⁻¹, so the extension is now larger than proportional [1]

**(b)** A = π × (0.15 × 10⁻³)² = 7.07 × 10⁻⁸ m² [1]; using 20 N and 2.20 mm, E = FL/(AΔL) = 20 × 1.60 / (7.07 × 10⁻⁸ × 2.20 × 10⁻³) [1] = 2.1 × 10¹¹ Pa [1]

**(c)** They are beyond the limit of proportionality, so stress is no longer proportional to strain [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* For (a), a comparison of ratios (or of equal increments of 0.55 mm per 5 N) is required; "the numbers go up" scores nothing.

**9. (a)** A = π × (0.225 × 10⁻³)² = 1.59 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; E = 40.0 × 1.250 / (1.59 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.80 × 10⁻³) [1] = 1.75 × 10¹¹ Pa [1]

**(b)** Length 0.4%; diameter 2.2%, so area 4.4% [1]; extension 2.8% [1]; total 0.4 + 4.4 + 2.8 = 7.6% [1]

**(c)** E = (1.75 ± 0.13) × 10¹¹ Pa [1]; the area term is largest, so measure the diameter with more readings or use a more precise instrument; alternatively increase the extension (longer wire or larger load within the limit) to reduce the 2.8% term [1] **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* An improvement must target the largest percentage uncertainty to earn credit; a better metre rule changes almost nothing here.

**10. (a)** Any sagging of the beam or change in temperature affects both wires equally [1], so the vernier reading changes only because of the extension caused by the added load [1]

**(b)** The small load straightens kinks in the wire so that later readings are true extensions [1]; any constant offset this causes shifts all readings equally, so it changes the intercept of the graph, not its gradient [1]

**(c)** Wear eye protection in case the wire snaps (or place a box of soft material under the masses and keep feet clear) [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Link the reference wire to both effects it cancels; "to make it more accurate" alone earns nothing.

**11. (a)** The rod is uniform, so its weight acts at the centre, equidistant from the wires; taking moments about either end, each wire supports half the weight [1]

**(b)** A = π × (0.30 × 10⁻³)² = 2.83 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; ΔL = 60 × 1.50 / (2.0 × 10¹¹ × 2.83 × 10⁻⁷) = 1.6 × 10⁻³ m [1]

**(c)** Equal tension, length and extension need equal values of EA [1], so the brass area = 2 × 2.83 × 10⁻⁷ = 5.66 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]; d = √(4A/π) = 0.85 mm [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Doubling the area needs the diameter multiplied by √2, not by 2.

**12. (a)** X: 140 × 10⁶ / 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 7.0 × 10¹⁰ Pa [1]; Y: 190 × 10⁶ / 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.9 × 10¹¹ Pa [1]

**(b)** Y, because its Young modulus (the gradient of its graph) is larger, so it has a smaller strain for a given stress [1]

**(c)** A = π × (4.0 × 10⁻³)² = 5.03 × 10⁻⁵ m², so stress = 4000 / 5.03 × 10⁻⁵ = 7.96 × 10⁷ Pa, below 140 MPa so on the straight part [1]; strain = 7.96 × 10⁷ / 7.0 × 10¹⁰ = 1.14 × 10⁻³ [1]; ΔL = 1.14 × 10⁻³ × 0.60 = 6.8 × 10⁻⁴ m [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Checking that the stress is within the straight section shows the equation is valid before you use it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating the diameter as the radius, or not squaring it.
- Using a strain percentage as if it were a decimal.
- Using readings beyond the limit of proportionality.
- Suggesting an improvement that does not target the largest uncertainty.
- Forgetting g when a load is given as a mass.
- Writing that a thicker wire has a larger Young modulus.
