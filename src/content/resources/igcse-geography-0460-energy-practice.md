These questions cover Topic 3.5 Energy in the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026, Version 2 (published November 2022). The topic is examined in Paper 1 Geographical Themes; questions 3, 4, 5 and 7 practise the data and calculation skills tested in Paper 2 Geographical Skills, where the syllabus says graphs may show energy. All places and data in the questions are fictional unless a question asks for your own case study.

## Questions

**1.** Define the term "non-renewable energy". **[2]**

**2.** HEP and tidal power both use moving water. For each, state one physical condition needed for a good site and one disadvantage of using it. **[4]**

**3.** The table shows electricity generated by source in Corvania, a fictional country, in 2005 and 2025.

| Source | 2005 (TWh) | 2025 (TWh) |
|---|---|---|
| Coal | 48 | 22 |
| Natural gas | 20 | 30 |
| HEP | 12 | 13 |
| Wind | 2 | 18 |
| Solar | 1 | 12 |
| Nuclear | 8 | 5 |
| **Total** | **91** | **100** |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of Corvania's electricity that came from coal in 2005. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of electricity from renewable sources in 2025. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe the changes in Corvania's electricity generation between 2005 and 2025. Use figures. **[4]**
**(d)** Suggest two reasons why electricity from coal fell. **[2]**

**4.** A divided bar graph shows the energy used in three fictional countries, P, Q and R, as a percentage of each country's total.

- Country P: fuelwood and other biomass 72%, oil 18%, HEP 8%, other 2%. Energy use: 15 GJ per person.
- Country Q: biomass 20%, oil 35%, coal 30%, natural gas 10%, HEP 5%. Energy use: 55 GJ per person.
- Country R: oil 38%, natural gas 30%, nuclear 12%, coal 10%, other renewables 8%, biomass 2%. Energy use: 170 GJ per person.

**(a)** Identify the country most likely to be a low-income country. Give one piece of evidence from the data. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times more energy per person is used in R than in P. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why fuelwood is so important in countries like P. **[4]**

**5.** A line graph shows a fictional village solar mini-grid in Tamsa on a dry-season day. Solar output rises from 0 kW at 06:00 to a peak of 60 kW at 13:00, then falls to 0 kW by 18:30. Village demand is 20 kW at 13:00 and peaks at 75 kW at 20:00.

**(a)** State how much of the demand at 20:00 is not met by the solar panels. **[1]**
**(b)** Suggest two ways the village could meet its evening demand. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why solar mini-grids can suit rural villages in low-income countries. **[3]**

**6.** A company plans 40 wind turbines on a ridge above Glenmorrow, a fictional upland farming valley. Four views are recorded:

> *Farmer:* "The rent for turbine sites would help keep my farm going, and sheep can still graze around them."
> *Guesthouse owner:* "Walkers come here for the empty skyline. I worry they will go elsewhere."
> *Council officer:* "Construction would bring jobs, and the company would pay into a community fund."
> *Bird group volunteer:* "The ridge is on a flight route for birds of prey."

Suggest the advantages and disadvantages of the wind farm for Glenmorrow. **[6]**

**7.** A fictional nuclear power station has a capacity of 1,200 MW and runs at 90% of capacity on average. A fictional wind farm has a capacity of 300 MW and runs at 30% of capacity on average. Use 8,760 hours in a year.

**(a)** Calculate the nuclear station's output in one year in GWh. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many wind farms of this size would be needed to produce the same yearly output. Show your working. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why energy use per person is usually higher in high-income countries than in low-income countries. **[4]**

**9.** Explain two disadvantages of nuclear power. **[4]**

**10.** To what extent are biofuels a good replacement for oil in transport? Use an example. **[5]**

**11.** "Renewable energy is a better choice than nuclear power for a country that wants to use less fossil fuel." To what extent do you agree? Use examples. **[8]**

**12.** For a named country or area you have studied, describe and explain its energy supply. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Energy from a source that is a finite stock [1]; it cannot be replaced within a human lifetime once used, for example coal, oil, gas or uranium [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the second mark needs the idea that the stock is not replaced on a human timescale; "it runs out" alone is thin.

**2.** HEP condition: high, reliable rainfall or river flow, or a deep, narrow valley to dam [1]. HEP disadvantage: flooding land and displacing people, or trapping sediment [1]. Tidal condition: a large tidal range, often in an estuary [1]. Tidal disadvantage: very few suitable sites, or a barrage changes estuary habitats [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* a condition must be physical and specific; "lots of water" or "near the sea" earns nothing.

**3. (a)** 48 ÷ 91 × 100 [1] = 52.7% [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 13 + 18 + 12 = 43 TWh out of 100, so 43% [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Total generation rose from 91 to 100 TWh [1]. Coal more than halved, from 48 to 22 TWh [1]. Wind rose ninefold, from 2 to 18 TWh, and solar from 1 to 12 TWh [1]. Natural gas rose by 10 TWh, while HEP stayed almost the same and nuclear fell from 8 to 5 TWh [1]. **[4]**
**(d)** The government may have closed coal stations to cut carbon dioxide emissions or air pollution [1]; wind and solar became cheaper, and gas emits less carbon dioxide than coal, so they replaced it [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* in "describe" items, a mark often needs both the direction of change and a figure with units, so quote TWh values, not just "coal fell".

**4. (a)** Country P [1], because 72% of its energy comes from fuelwood and other biomass, or because it uses only 15 GJ per person [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 170 ÷ 15 = 11.3 times [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Many rural homes have no connection to an electricity grid [1]. Wood can be collected free, which matters when incomes are low [1]. Families cannot afford imported fuels such as kerosene or gas, or the stoves to use them [1]. Wood is used directly for cooking, which is a large share of household energy use in poorer countries [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "explain" needs a reason linked to poverty or lack of infrastructure; just saying "they use a lot of wood" repeats the data.

**5. (a)** 75 kW [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Store daytime surplus in batteries for use after dark [1]; add a different source such as a small HEP scheme, a wind turbine or a diesel generator [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Panels can be installed on single buildings or small grids, so no long power lines to a national grid are needed [1]. Many low-income countries are at low latitudes with strong sunshine for much of the year [1]. There is no fuel to buy or transport once panels are installed [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* use the graph's evidence that demand peaks after sunset; an answer that ignores the timing misses the point of the question.

**6.** Advantages: rent gives farmers extra income [1], and grazing can continue, so land is not lost [1]; construction jobs and a community fund bring money into the valley [1]. Disadvantages: the turbines spoil the open skyline [1], which may reduce walkers and harm guesthouse income [1]; turbines on a bird flight route may kill birds of prey [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* the top marks go to answers that use the speakers' points and develop them (rent -> income -> farm survives) rather than listing general wind-power facts.

**7. (a)** 1,200 × 8,760 × 0.9 = 9,460,800 MWh [1] = 9,460.8 GWh [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** One wind farm: 300 × 8,760 × 0.3 = 788,400 MWh [1]; 9,460,800 ÷ 788,400 = 12 wind farms [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* show each step with units; capacity (MW) is not the same as output (MWh or GWh), and mixing them loses the working mark.

**8.** People in high-income countries own more cars and travel further, so they use more oil [1]. Homes have more electrical appliances and are often heated or air-conditioned [1]. The economy has more industry and services, which use large amounts of electricity [1]. In low-income countries many people have no grid connection and use small amounts of fuelwood for cooking [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each reason must say why it raises energy use; "they are richer" is a starting point, not an explanation.

**9.** Radioactive waste stays dangerous for thousands of years [1], so it must be stored securely, which is costly and opposed by people near storage sites [1]. Accidents are rare but severe, as at Chernobyl in 1986 or Fukushima Daiichi in 2011 [1], where radiation forced people to leave their homes over a wide area [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* for "explain two", each disadvantage needs a development point; two bare statements score half marks.

**10.** Biofuels such as ethanol from sugarcane can replace petrol in cars [1]; Brazil has run a national programme since 1975, and flex-fuel cars there can use any blend of petrol and ethanol [1]. They are renewable and cut oil imports [1]. However, crops for fuel take farmland that could grow food [1], and expanding farmland can lead to forest clearance, so biofuels are best where there is spare land, as in Brazil, rather than in every country [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* the judgement mark needs a conditional conclusion, such as "where", "when" or "for whom" biofuels work.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these indicative points show what a strong answer includes.
For renewables: they have no fuel cost and produce no radioactive waste [1]. Iceland makes almost all its electricity from hydro and geothermal because of its high rainfall and plate-boundary heat [1]. Small solar systems can supply villages in low-income countries that nuclear power could never reach [1].
Against renewables: wind and solar are intermittent, so they need storage or back-up [1], and large HEP can displace people, as at the Three Gorges Dam [1].
For nuclear: it gives a large, steady supply; France built reactors after the 1973 oil crisis and made about 72% of its electricity from nuclear by 2018 [1].
Against nuclear: high cost, long-lived waste and accident risk, as at Fukushima in 2011 [1].
Judgement: renewables are the better choice where physical conditions suit them and for poorer countries, but nuclear can provide reliable base load for wealthy countries, so many countries combine both [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* the highest level needs a clear judgement that depends on the type of country, supported by at least two named examples.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these indicative points use Iceland.
Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a constructive plate boundary, so hot rock lies close to the surface [1]. In 2025, 29.4% of its electricity came from geothermal plants such as Hellisheiði (303 MW) [1]. High rainfall and glacier meltwater feed fast rivers in steep valleys [1], so 70.5% of electricity came from hydropower in 2025 [1]. The 690 MW Kárahnjúkar scheme was built to power an aluminium smelter [1]. About 90% of homes are heated with geothermal hot water [1]. Cheap renewable power has attracted energy-hungry industry, which used 72% of electricity in 2005 [1]. Oil is still imported for vehicles, ships and aircraft, so total energy supply is not fully renewable [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "describe and explain" needs both the energy mix with figures and the physical or economic reasons for it; place-specific detail decides the top level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing MW where the question asks for energy produced in MWh, GWh or TWh.
- Describing a table with no figures, or with figures but no units.
- Calling nuclear renewable, or saying renewables have no environmental impact.
- Giving general wind-power facts instead of using the speakers' views in a scenario question.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages with no final judgement in "to what extent" questions.
- Case-study answers with no place names, plant names or percentages.
- Forgetting that fuelwood and levels of development are named in the syllabus.
- Rounding too early in multi-step calculations.
