## Questions

**1.** State **two** of the external factors that the specification lists as affecting a business's costs and demand. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by *market conditions*. **[2]**

**3.** Kettleburn Garden Furniture has a variable-rate loan of £150,000. The interest rate rises from 4.8% to 6.4%.

**(a)** Calculate the annual interest on the loan before and after the rise. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in the annual interest bill. **[2]**

**4.** Moorcliffe Coffee Roasters has a unit cost of £12.00 and sells at £20.00. Packaging is 30% of unit cost and its price rises by 15%.

**(a)** Calculate the new unit cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in unit cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the selling price needed to keep the margin at 40% of the selling price. **[2]**

**5.** Hexwood Bakery sells 600 cakes a week at £9.00. Variable cost is £4.50 a cake and fixed costs are £1,800 a week. A national chain opens nearby.

**(a)** Calculate Hexwood's weekly profit before the chain opens. **[2]**
**(b)** The owner matches the chain by cutting the price to £8.20. Volume becomes 640 a week. Calculate the new weekly profit. **[2]**
**(c)** If the owner held the price at £9.00 instead, volume would fall to 520 a week. Recommend which response gives the better weekly profit, using your calculations. **[2]**

**6.** Strathbeck Cycles sells premium e-bikes and budget bikes. Local real incomes fall by 4%. Premium sales fall by 12% and budget sales rise by 6%.

**(a)** Calculate how many times larger the percentage fall in premium sales is than the percentage fall in incomes. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why budget sales rose when incomes fell. **[3]**

**7.** In Hollin Cross Care Homes' town, people aged over 80 were 5.1% of the population five years ago and are now 6.4%.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in the share of the population aged over 80. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse how this demographic change could affect Hollin Cross's demand and its costs. **[4]**

**8.** Thorsgill Packaging makes 250,000 gift boxes a year. Retailers increasingly ask for recyclable board, which adds £0.04 to each box.

**(a)** Calculate the extra annual cost of switching all production to recyclable board. **[1]**
**(b)** Analyse how this environmental pressure could affect Thorsgill's costs and demand. **[4]**

**9.** Wharfe Ices sells ice cream. Its unit sales last year were: January to March 18,000; April to June 61,000; July to September 74,000; October to December 27,000.

**(a)** Calculate total annual unit sales and the percentage sold between April and September. **[2]**
**(b)** Analyse **one** problem for costs and **one** problem for demand that this pattern could create. **[4]**

**10.** Greyfriars Cafe has 1,000 customers a week, each spending £7.50 on average, for 52 weeks a year. Variable costs are 38% of revenue. Operating profit before interest is £48,000 a year. Its £90,000 variable-rate loan is expected to move from 6% to 8%. A national chain is opening nearby and the owner expects to lose 8% of customers. Local real incomes are forecast to fall by 1.5%.

Evaluate whether competition is the most significant external threat to Greyfriars Cafe's profit. **[12]**

**11.** Fallowdene Coffee Roasters sells 80,000 bags a year at £5.50. Unit cost is £3.60, of which coffee beans are £1.80. Fixed costs are £120,000 a year. The owner is considering fair trade beans, which cost 15% more. She expects to raise the price to £5.70 and to sell 4% more bags.

**(a)** Calculate annual profit before the switch and after it. **[4]**
**(b)** Recommend whether Fallowdene should switch to fair trade beans. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two from: competition; market conditions; incomes; interest rates; demographic factors; environmental issues and fair trade. [1] [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A "state" question rewards only what is asked, so name two factors and move on.

**2.** The state of the market a business trades in, such as whether demand is growing, falling or seasonal [1], and the supply and price of the inputs it buys [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" usually needs a point plus a development.

**3. (a)** Before: £150,000 × 4.8% = £7,200 [1]. After: £150,000 × 6.4% = £9,600 [1].
**(b)** Increase = £9,600 − £7,200 = £2,400 [1]. Percentage increase = 2,400 ÷ 7,200 × 100 = **33.3%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A 1.6 percentage point rise in the rate is a 33.3% rise in the bill. Divide by the original and show the working.

**4. (a)** Packaging = 30% × £12.00 = £3.60 [1]. New packaging = £3.60 × 1.15 = £4.14, so new unit cost = £12.00 − £3.60 + £4.14 = **£12.54** [1].
**(b)** Rise = £0.54 ÷ £12.00 × 100 = **4.5%** [1].
**(c)** Price = unit cost ÷ (1 − 0.40) = £12.54 ÷ 0.60 [1] = **£20.90** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Isolate the part of cost that changes first. In (c), margin is a share of price, so divide by 0.60.

**5. (a)** Contribution per cake = £9.00 − £4.50 = £4.50 [1]. Profit = 600 × £4.50 − £1,800 = **£900** [1].
**(b)** Contribution = £8.20 − £4.50 = £3.70 [1]. Profit = 640 × £3.70 − £1,800 = £2,368 − £1,800 = **£568** [1].
**(c)** Holding the price: 520 × £4.50 − £1,800 = £540 [1]. Matching the cut gives £28 more, so recommend matching; the gap is small, so the choice is close and depends on whether lost customers would return [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State which option wins and by how much, then add one comment on risk.

**6. (a)** 12 ÷ 4 [1] = **3 times** [1].
**(b)** Budget bikes behave as an inferior good: demand rises when incomes fall [1]. Customers who can no longer afford premium e-bikes trade down [1], so the rise in budget sales partly offsets the premium fall [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain customers' behaviour, not just the numbers.

**7. (a)** 6.4 − 5.1 = 1.3 percentage points; 1.3 ÷ 5.1 × 100 = **25.5%** [2].
**(b)** Demand: a larger share of over-80s means more potential residents, so demand for places is likely to rise [1], raising occupancy and revenue [1]. Costs: more residents need more care staff [1], and recruiting them from a smaller pool of younger workers may mean higher pay or training costs [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the trend to this business's product and workforce.

**8. (a)** 250,000 × £0.04 = **£10,000** [1].
**(b)** Costs: recyclable board raises the cost of every box, so unless price rises, profit per box falls [1]. Demand: retailers who want recyclable boxes may move to a supplier that offers them, so switching could protect orders [1]; not switching risks losing them [1]. Net effect: switching pays if the protected orders are worth more than £10,000, or customers accept a small price rise [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Environmental pressure works through costs and demand. Show both, then weigh them.

**9. (a)** Total = 18,000 + 61,000 + 74,000 + 27,000 = **180,000** [1]. April to September = 135,000, and 135,000 ÷ 180,000 × 100 = **75%** [1].
**(b)** Costs: rent and equipment are paid all year, yet January to March sales are only 18,000 against 74,000 in July to September [1], so capacity sits idle in quiet months [1]. Demand: sales depend on the warm months, so a cold, wet summer would cut them sharply [1], and low winter demand strains cash flow [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quoting figures from the stem makes the point specific to this business.

**10.** Competition: 8% of 1,000 customers is 80 a week [1]. Annual revenue is 1,000 × £7.50 × 52 = £390,000, so an 8% loss is £31,200 [1]. Variable costs are 38%, so contribution is 62% and the lost contribution is £31,200 × 62% = £19,344 [1].
Interest: £90,000 × 6% = £5,400 rises to £90,000 × 8% = £7,200, an increase of £1,800 [1].
Combined: £48,000 − £19,344 − £1,800 = £26,856 [1]. Competition is 40.3% of current profit against 3.75% for interest, about 10.7 times larger [1].
Incomes: a 1.5% fall may cut eating out or push people to cheaper options [1], but no size is given, so it cannot be quantified [1].
Evaluation: the interest cost is known and small, and a fixed rate could limit it [1], whereas the 8% loss is only an expectation, and loyalty offers could limit it [1].
Judgement: competition is probably the most significant threat because it affects profit far more [1], but this depends on the 8% forecast and on whether customers return once the new chain is no longer novel [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculate each effect, then compare sizes before judging.

**11. (a)** Before: 80,000 × (£5.50 − £3.60) = 80,000 × £1.90 = £152,000 [1]; less £120,000 = **£32,000** [1].
After: beans cost £1.80 × 1.15 = £2.07, so unit cost = £3.60 − £1.80 + £2.07 = £3.87 [1]. Bags = 83,200. Profit = 83,200 × £1.83 − £120,000 = £152,256 − £120,000 = **£32,256** [1].
**(b)** Unit cost rises by £0.27 and price by £0.20, so margin per bag falls from £1.90 to £1.83 [1]. Profit rises by only £256 (about 0.8%), so the case rests on forecasts [1]. If demand rose by only 2% (81,600 bags), profit would be £29,328, below today's £32,000 [1]. If customers rejected the price rise, profit at £5.50 with 83,200 bags would be £15,616 [1]. Break-even output rises from about 63,158 to about 65,574 bags, so the margin of safety is thinner [1]. Non-financial gains: ethical reputation and appeal to fair trade customers [1].
Recommendation: switch only if a trial or customer research shows buyers will accept £5.70 and demand will rise by at least 4% [1], because the gain is small and the downside large; a phased switch on one blend limits risk [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Test the forecasts. Showing what happens if demand or price turns out worse supports a conditional judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a factor without saying whether costs, demand or both change.
- Applying a percentage rise on one input to the whole unit cost.
- Mixing up a percentage point change in a rate with a percentage change in the interest paid.
- Describing a trend without linking it to the business's product or workers.
- Treating fair trade or recyclable materials as only a cost.
- Listing effects without comparing their size, or giving a judgement with no condition.
