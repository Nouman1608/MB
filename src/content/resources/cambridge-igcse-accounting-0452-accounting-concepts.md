The syllabus lists ten: business entity, consistency, duality, going concern, historic cost, matching / accruals, materiality, money measurement, prudence and realisation. The existing [concepts, ethics and technology guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-accounting-concepts-ethics-technology-and-sustainabilit/) explains each concept with a cycle shop. This page concentrates on the exam skill: recognising which concept a situation involves, telling look-alike concepts apart, and using the concepts to correct figures. All businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars.

## How 7.1 is examined

Concept questions come in four forms:

1. **Identify** the concept shown by a situation, often as a multiple-choice question.
2. **Explain** a concept, usually with reference to a given case.
3. **Apply** concepts to adjust a draft profit or a statement of financial position.
4. **Justify** a treatment, by naming the concept that requires it.

In every form the marks go to the link between the concept and the figures. A definition alone rarely earns full marks.

## The ten concepts as decision rules

Think of each concept as a rule that answers a particular question.

| Concept | Question it answers | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Business entity | Whose transaction is this? | Record only the business's transactions; the owner's private spending is drawings |
| Duality | How many entries? | Every transaction has two equal and opposite effects |
| Money measurement | Can it be recorded? | Only items that can be reliably measured in money |
| Historic cost | At what value? | Assets at their original cost |
| Going concern | For how long? | Assume the business will continue for the foreseeable future |
| Realisation | When is revenue earned? | When goods or services are provided and the customer is committed to pay |
| Matching / accruals | Which period? | Revenue and the expenses of earning it belong to the period in which they arise, not when cash moves |
| Prudence | What if uncertain? | Do not overstate profit or assets; recognise expected losses promptly |
| Materiality | Is it worth separate treatment? | Items too small to affect users' decisions may be treated simply |
| Consistency | Can the method change? | Use the same methods from one period to the next |

## Telling look-alike concepts apart

**Matching against realisation.** Realisation decides *when revenue counts*. Matching decides *which period* revenue and expenses belong to. A deposit received for goods not yet delivered is a realisation point; an electricity bill for December paid in January is a matching point.

**Prudence against realisation.** Both stop profit being anticipated, but prudence goes further: it also requires expected losses to be recognised now. Inventory below cost is written down (prudence); a gain on goods not yet sold is ignored (realisation, and prudence).

**Historic cost against going concern.** Historic cost says what value is recorded. Going concern explains why that value, less depreciation, is still sensible: the asset will be used, not sold. If the business is about to close, going concern fails and expected sale values become relevant.

**Business entity against money measurement.** Both explain why something is left out of the books, for different reasons. The owner's family holiday is excluded because it is not the business's transaction (entity). A loyal customer base is excluded because it cannot be reliably valued (money measurement).

**Materiality against consistency.** Materiality allows a small item to be expensed; consistency requires the business to keep applying the same limit each year.

## Worked example: correcting a draft profit

Tolland Opticians, owned by Farah Qureshi, has a draft profit for the year ended 31 December of 46,800. The book-keeper lists six matters.

1. Farah's private car insurance of 960 was paid from the business bank account and debited to insurance.
2. On 1 November, 3,600 was received from a tenant for six months' rent of a spare room. All of it was credited to rent received.
3. Wages of 1,150 for the last week of December are unpaid and unrecorded.
4. Closing inventory includes frames that cost 2,400 and are expected to sell for only 1,650.
5. A customer ordered spectacles worth 3,000 on 28 December, to be made and delivered in January. The order was recorded as a credit sale.
6. A set of small tools costing 45 was recorded as a non-current asset. Tolland's policy, applied every year, is to expense items under 200.

| Item | Concept | Effect on profit | Effect on statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Business entity | + 960 | Drawings increase by 960, reducing capital |
| 2 | Matching / accruals | − 2,400 (only 3,600 × 2/6 = 1,200 earned) | Other payables (income received in advance) 2,400 |
| 3 | Matching / accruals | − 1,150 | Other payables (accrued wages) 1,150 |
| 4 | Prudence | − 750 | Inventory reduced by 750 |
| 5 | Realisation | − 3,000 | Trade receivables reduced by 3,000 |
| 6 | Materiality and consistency | − 45 | Non-current assets reduced by 45 |

Corrected profit = 46,800 + 960 − 2,400 − 1,150 − 750 − 3,000 − 45 = **40,415**.

Notes on the method:

- Lay out the draft figure, then one line per item with a plus or minus sign. Partial marks follow the method if the arithmetic slips.
- Item 2 is income received in advance: four of the six months fall in next year.
- Item 5 is not a sale yet, because the spectacles have not been provided. The customer has ordered, but revenue is recognised on delivery.
- Item 6 is tiny, but the question tests whether you know the policy is applied consistently.

## Duality in the accounting equation

Duality is easiest to show through the equation. If Tolland buys a testing machine for 5,200 on credit, assets rise by 5,200 and liabilities rise by 5,200. If Farah takes 300 of frames for her own use, inventory (an asset) falls by 300 and capital falls by 300 through drawings. Every correction in the table above also has two sides: each changes profit, and therefore capital, and changes an asset or liability by the same amount.

## Going concern, historic cost and money measurement in one case

Tolland's shop fittings cost 18,000 three years ago and are depreciated at 20% of cost, so their net book value is 18,000 − (3 × 3,600) = 7,200. A second-hand dealer would pay 2,500 for them.

- While Tolland is a going concern, the fittings stay at 7,200: they will be used for two more years, and historic cost less depreciation is recorded.
- If Farah announced the shop would close next month, the going concern assumption would fail and 2,500 would be the more relevant value, a reduction of 4,700.
- Farah's reputation in the town for careful eye tests is valuable but cannot be reliably measured in money, so it is not recorded however much it helps sales.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why Tolland should not include the 3,000 order in revenue for the year.* [3 marks]

> The realisation concept says revenue is recognised when goods or services have been provided and the customer is committed to pay **(concept)**. The spectacles will not be made and delivered until January **(applied to the case)**. So the 3,000 is next year's revenue; including it now would overstate this year's profit and trade receivables by 3,000 **(effect)**.

## Common mistakes

- Naming the concept without applying it to the case.
- Giving matching when the issue is whether revenue has been earned at all (realisation).
- Treating prudence as deliberately understating profit, instead of not overstating it.
- Recording a private payment as an expense rather than drawings.
- Forgetting the statement of financial position effect of an adjustment.
- Correcting income received in advance by the months already earned instead of the months still to come.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **Identify**: the name of the concept is enough.
- **Explain**: concept, how it applies here, and its effect on the figures.
- **Calculate**: start from the draft figure and show each adjustment with its sign.
- **Justify** a treatment: the concept that requires it, plus a reason from the case.
