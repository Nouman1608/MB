## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by added value. **[2]**

**2.** A salon charges £36 for a treatment. Products and other bought-in items cost £6. Calculate the added value and express it as a percentage of the price. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why added value is not the same as profit. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Dunmore Bikes sells a bicycle for £450. The bought-in parts cost £270. It sells 2,000 bicycles a year and has 9 employees. Wages are £200,000 a year and other overheads are £90,000.

**(a)** Calculate the added value per bicycle. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the added value as a percentage of the selling price. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the total added value and the added value per employee. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate Dunmore's profit, assuming no other costs. **[2]**

**(e)** A supplier raises the price of parts by 10%. Calculate the new added value per bicycle and the new total added value, assuming the price and sales stay the same. **[3]**

**5.** Dunmore considers upgrading the bicycle with higher-quality parts. The selling price would rise to £520 and bought-in costs to £310. Sales are expected to fall to 1,800 bicycles. Evaluate whether Dunmore should make the upgrade. **[9]**

**6.** A restaurant owner says: "Our added value is high, so our profit must be high." Evaluate the usefulness of added value to the restaurant's owner and other stakeholders. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The difference between the selling price of a product [1] and the cost of the bought-in materials and services used to make it [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Added value = 36 − 6 = **£30** [1]. 30 ÷ 36 × 100 [1] = **83.3%** [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Added value is calculated before wages, rent and other overheads are paid [1], so it is the pool from which these costs are met [1]. Profit is only what is left after they have been paid, so a business can have high added value but a low profit if its wages and overheads are high [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 450 − 270 = **£180** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 180 ÷ 450 × 100 [1] = **40%** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** 2,000 × 180 = **£360,000** [1]. 360,000 ÷ 9 = **£40,000** per employee [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Profit = 360,000 − 200,000 − 90,000 [1] = **£70,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(e)** New parts cost = 270 × 1.10 = £297 [1]. New added value per bicycle = 450 − 297 = **£153** [1]. New total = 2,000 × 153 = **£306,000** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** New added value per bicycle = 520 − 310 = £210 [1]. Total = 1,800 × 210 = £378,000 [1], which is £18,000 more than the present £360,000 [1]. Added value as a percentage of price is 210 ÷ 520 = 40.4%, almost the same as 40%.
For: total added value rises, so there is more to pay wages and profit [1]; the upgrade may improve the brand and allow a premium position [1].
Against: sales fall by 200 bicycles (10%), which may mean lost customers and lower use of workshop capacity [1]; if wages and overheads stay at £290,000, profit rises from £70,000 to £88,000 only if costs are unchanged [1]; the forecast of 1,800 sales is an estimate: if sales fell to 1,600, total added value would be £336,000, lower than at present [1].
Judgement: the upgrade is worthwhile only if demand is reliable, so Dunmore should test the upgraded model before committing, and should check the effect on profit rather than relying on added value alone [1]. **[9]**

**6.** Useful: it shows how much value the restaurant creates beyond the cost of ingredients [1]; it shows the amount available to pay wages, overheads and profit, which helps staff and lenders see the scope for pay and repayments [1]; the owner can compare dishes and see which add most value [1].
Limits: added value ignores wages, rent and other overheads, so a high figure may still mean a low profit, as the owner assumes [1]; it may reflect location or market power rather than efficiency [1].
Judgement: the owner is wrong to equate high added value with high profit; it is a useful starting point but needs cost and profit information as well [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Deducting wages or rent when calculating added value.
- Calling added value profit.
- Forgetting to multiply by the number sold for total added value.
- Judging an upgrade by the per-unit figure only, without the effect on sales.
