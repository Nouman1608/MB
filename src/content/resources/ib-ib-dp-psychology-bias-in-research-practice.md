> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on bias in research (IB DP Psychology, first
> assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions. Both study summaries are invented. Question 12
> uses the 6-mark tariff of question 3 of HL Paper 3; the others use practice tariffs.

Related: [Bias in research study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-bias-in-research/),
[revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-bias-in-research-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define "bias" in research. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the type of bias in each case: (a) a focus group member answers every question first and others agree with her; (b) a participant agrees with every statement on a questionnaire, including two that contradict each other. **[2]**

**3.** Outline one way to reduce social desirability bias in an interview. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a double-blind procedure reduces expectancy effects. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish personal reflexivity from epistemological reflexivity. **[2]**

**6.** Rewrite this leading question as a neutral one: "How much did the stressful inspection affect your sleep?" **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Read the summary (invented).

> A researcher tested whether listening to music improves performance on a puzzle task. Forty
> students were recruited in the school library. The researcher, who plays in the school orchestra,
> told each student, "We think music helps people concentrate." Each student completed one set of
> puzzles in silence and then a second set with music, while the researcher timed them.

Identify and explain two sources of bias in this study, and suggest how each could be reduced. **[6]**

**8.** Explain why random allocation to conditions helps to reduce bias in an experiment. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how researcher triangulation could reduce confirmation bias in a thematic analysis. **[3]**

**10.** A researcher interviewing patients about their experience of a hospital is employed by the hospital. Explain two ways this could bias the findings. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why the strategies for dealing with bias in a semi-structured interview study differ from those in a laboratory experiment. **[3]**

**12.** Read the summary (invented).

> A researcher wanted to explore how young adults experience moving to a new country for university.
> She ran three focus groups of six international students each at her own university, where she
> works as the international student adviser. Groups met in her office. She led the discussions,
> took notes by hand and identified themes afterwards. She reported two themes: homesickness and
> gratitude for university support.

Discuss how the researcher in the study could avoid bias. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A systematic influence on the results **[1]** that pushes them away from the truth, consistently in one direction **[1]**.

**2.** (a) Dominant respondent bias **[1]**. (b) Acquiescence bias **[1]**.

**3.** For example, guarantee anonymity and confidentiality at the start **[1]** so participants have less reason to present themselves favourably, and interview in a private setting where no one can overhear **[1]**.

**4.** The researcher collecting data does not know which condition a participant is in **[1]**, so cannot, even unintentionally, treat participants differently or score them in line with the hypothesis **[1]**.

**5.** Personal reflexivity is reflection on how the researcher's own background and beliefs may shape the research **[1]**; epistemological reflexivity is reflection on how the choice of method and design may have shaped the findings **[1]**.

**6.** For example: "How, if at all, did the inspection week affect your sleep?" **[1]**

**7.** Any two sources, three marks each:
- **Demand characteristics:** telling students "We think music helps" reveals the hypothesis **[1]**, so students may try harder in the music condition **[1]**. Use standardised instructions that do not state the expected result **[1]**.
- **Order effects acting as bias:** music always came second, so practice could make performance better in that condition **[1]**, systematically favouring the hypothesis **[1]**. Counterbalance the order of conditions **[1]**.
- Also creditable for three marks: **researcher bias**, because the researcher who timed the tasks expected music to help and may have timed inconsistently; reduce it with automatic timing or a second timer who does not know the hypothesis.

**8.** Random allocation means each participant has an equal chance of being in either condition **[1]**, so participant variables such as ability are spread evenly and do not systematically favour one condition **[1]**.

**9.** A second researcher codes the transcripts independently, without seeing the first researcher's themes **[1]**. The two sets of themes are compared and differences discussed **[1]**, so a theme that appears only because of one researcher's expectations is more likely to be detected **[1]**.

**10.** Participants may give socially desirable, positive answers to someone they see as representing the hospital **[2]**. The researcher may, consciously or not, ask fewer probing questions about failures or interpret answers favourably for the employer, a form of confirmation bias **[2]**.

**11.** In an experiment, bias can be designed out with controls such as random allocation, standardised procedures and blind conditions **[1]**. In a semi-structured interview, the researcher asks the questions and interprets the answers, so bias cannot be designed out **[1]**; strategies therefore aim to make it visible and checkable, such as reflexivity, recording and researcher triangulation **[1]**.

**12.** Markband guide (Paper 3 question 3 markbands): 5-6 for a detailed discussion of the research consideration with explicit reference to the relevant supporting evidence from the source; 3-4 for some understanding, with some inaccuracies and only implicit reference to the source; 1-2 for limited understanding with limited or missing reference to the source. Indicative content, credit developed points up to **[6]**:
- Social desirability: students meeting in the adviser's office may praise the support she provides; meet in a neutral room with a moderator who is not the adviser.
- Dominant respondent: in groups of six, confident students may steer discussion; use a trained moderator and add individual interviews.
- Confirmation bias: as adviser, she may expect gratitude and record it; keep a reflexive diary and use a second analyst.
- Biased reporting: handwritten notes may capture only some comments; audio-record and transcribe, and report all themes with quotations.
- Judgement: her role as adviser is the most serious source of bias, because it affects both what students say and how she interprets it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 7:** naming "demand characteristics" without the sentence that caused it.
- **Question 10:** describing ethical problems instead of bias.
- **Question 12:** listing six biases without connecting any to the office, the adviser role or the handwritten notes.
