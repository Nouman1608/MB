## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term "marketing objective". **[2]**

**2.** State **two** types of marketing objective. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why a business needs clear marketing objectives. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why a marketing objective should be time-bound. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**5.** Larkfield Garden Centre sells £1,800,000 of goods a year in a regional market worth £24,000,000. The market is forecast to grow by 5% next year. The owner sets a marketing objective of a 9% market share next year.

**(a)** Calculate Larkfield's current market share. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the sales revenue needed to reach a 9% share next year. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in sales this requires. **[2]**

**6.** Larkfield has 2,500 customers on its loyalty scheme. Last year 28% of them did not buy again this year.

**(a)** Calculate how many customers bought again. **[2]**

**(b)** Larkfield sets an objective of 80% retention. Calculate how many more customers it must retain than it did this year. **[2]**

**(c)** Analyse how a retention objective could help Larkfield achieve a corporate objective of higher profit. **[6]**

**7.** Larkfield sells a hanging basket at £12.00. The variable cost is £7.20 and it sells 25,000 baskets a year. A manager proposes a 10% price cut to meet a market share objective.

**(a)** Calculate the current margin on a basket as a percentage of the price. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of baskets that must be sold at the lower price to earn the same total contribution as now. **[4]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Larkfield should cut the price to achieve its market share objective. **[9]**

**8.** Verde Bars is a new business that makes organic snack bars. It will sell in independent shops and online. It has a small marketing budget and two founders who have not worked in marketing before. Recommend and justify marketing objectives for Verde Bars for its first two years. **[9]**

**9.** Hallam Outdoor Equipment has set a corporate objective of increasing profit by 20% over three years. Its marketing director says that clear marketing objectives are the most important factor in achieving this. Evaluate the importance of marketing objectives in the achievement of Hallam's business objectives. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A target [1] for the marketing function to achieve in a stated period of time [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: sales volume or value; market share; brand awareness; customer retention or loyalty; entering a new market or segment; launching a new product [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Clear objectives give everyone a common direction [1], so the marketing mix and spending can be planned to fit the same target [1], and results can be compared with the objective to show whether marketing is working [1]. **[3]**

**4.** A deadline allows progress to be checked at set points [1], so managers can act if sales are behind target [1], and the budget and resources can be planned for the period [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 1,800,000 ÷ 24,000,000 × 100 = **7.5%** [1 for method, 1 for answer]. **[2]**

**(b)** Market next year = 24,000,000 × 1.05 = £25,200,000 [1]. 9% × 25,200,000 [1] = **£2,268,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** (2,268,000 − 1,800,000) ÷ 1,800,000 × 100 [1] = **26%** [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 72% bought again [1]; 0.72 × 2,500 = **1,800 customers** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 80% × 2,500 = 2,000 [1]; 2,000 − 1,800 = **200 more customers** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Existing customers usually cost less to keep than new customers cost to win, because less advertising and discounting is needed [1]. Keeping 200 more customers adds repeat sales revenue each year [1], and because the marketing cost per sale is lower, more of each sale becomes profit [1]. Loyal customers may spend more per visit and recommend the garden centre, which brings in new customers at little cost [1]. A retention objective also gives staff a measurable target [1]. However, profit will rise only if the cost of the retention measures, such as discounts for members, is less than the extra contribution earned [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** (12.00 − 7.20) ÷ 12.00 × 100 [1] = **40%** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Current contribution per basket = £4.80, so total = 25,000 × 4.80 = £120,000 [1]. New price = £10.80 [1]; new contribution per basket = 10.80 − 7.20 = £3.60 [1]. Baskets needed = 120,000 ÷ 3.60 = 33,333.3, so **33,334 baskets** (an increase of one third) [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** For the cut: it makes baskets cheaper than rivals, which may attract new customers and raise market share [1]; if demand is price sensitive, volume might rise by more than a third [1]; a larger share gives Larkfield more buying power and brand presence [1].
Against: Larkfield needs a one-third rise in volume just to keep the same total contribution [1], so a smaller rise would lower profit while share rises [1]; rivals may copy the cut, leaving share unchanged and margin lower [1]; the cut may damage the image of the product [1].
Judgement: the cut is justified only if there is evidence that demand is price sensitive and that rivals will not respond [1]; otherwise a short, limited promotion or a focus on other parts of the mix would pursue share with less risk to profit [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Objectives (any suitable, with numbers and a time): for example brand awareness among the target customer group of 20% by the end of year 1 [1]; stock in a set number of independent shops, such as 150, by the end of year 1 [1]; online sales revenue of a stated amount by the end of year 2 [1].
Justification: the brand is new, so few customers know it, which makes awareness and distribution the priority [1]; the small budget makes broad objectives such as large market share unrealistic [1]; a limited number of shops can be served well by two inexperienced founders [1].
Unrealistic targets would demotivate two inexperienced founders [1]. Objectives should be reviewed after year 1 and tightened, for example moving towards repeat purchase and margin in year 2 [1]. A judgement that links the objectives to the aim of surviving the launch [1]. **[9]**

**9.** Importance: the corporate objective of 20% more profit is only a number until marketing turns it into a sales or margin target, for example a share or revenue target [1]; clear objectives allow the sales and marketing budget to be set and checked [1]; they show which customers and products matter most [1]; they allow quick correction if sales fall below plan [1].
Limits: profit also depends on costs, operations and finance, so marketing objectives alone cannot guarantee it [1]; if the objectives are based on poor forecasts, the business may spend heavily on a target that cannot be met [1]; sales volume targets might reduce margin and so work against the profit target [1]; external changes, such as a new rival or a fall in spending, can make the objectives unachievable [1].
Evaluation of importance: the marketing objectives are very important as the link between the business's aim and what customers will actually buy [1], but they are one of several factors [1]. Judgement: they are necessary rather than sufficient, and their value depends on being realistic, reviewed regularly and consistent with cost and operations plans [1]. Seasonal sales and strong rivals mean Hallam should review them often [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a profit target when asked for a marketing objective, or giving objectives with no number or date.
- Forgetting to grow the market before applying the target share.
- Praising a price cut for share without checking the extra volume needed to protect profit.
