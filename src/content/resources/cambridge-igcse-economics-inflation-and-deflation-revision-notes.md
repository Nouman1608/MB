## 4.8.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inflation | The general price level keeps rising over time |
| Deflation | The general price level keeps falling over time |
| Rate of inflation | How fast the price level changes, as a percentage, normally per year |
| Disinflation | Prices still rising, but more slowly (the rate falls but stays positive) |
| Price index | Prices compared with a base year set at 100 |
| Real value | A money value adjusted for price changes |
| Nominal value | A money value not adjusted for price changes |

## 4.8.2 Measuring with the CPI

The **Consumer Prices Index (CPI)** follows a basket of items households buy. Each group in the basket carries a **weight**, matching its share of typical spending.

| Skill | Formula |
|---|---|
| Single price index | Current price ÷ base-year price × 100 |
| Weighted CPI | Total of (weight × group index) ÷ total of weights |
| Rate of inflation | Change in CPI ÷ old CPI × 100 |
| Real wage index | Money wage ÷ CPI × 100 |
| Real interest rate (approx.) | Nominal interest rate − inflation rate |

### Method in steps: weighted CPI

1. Put each weight and group index in a table.
2. Multiply weight by index for each row.
3. Add up the products.
4. Divide that sum by the total weight.
5. Write the meaning: 107 means prices are 7% above the base year.

Reminder: weights 40 and 60, indices 104 and 109. (40 × 104 + 60 × 109) ÷ 100 = (4,160 + 6,540) ÷ 100 = **107.0**.

### Method in steps: rate of inflation

1. Subtract old CPI from new CPI.
2. Divide the result by the old CPI.
3. Multiply that by 100.
4. Positive means inflation. Negative means deflation.

Reminders: 150 to 153 gives 3 ÷ 150 × 100 = **2.0%**. 125 to 120 gives −5 ÷ 125 × 100 = **−4.0%** (deflation).

A single item: a bag of rice costs 40 in the base year and 43.20 now. Index = 43.20 ÷ 40 × 100 = **108.0**.

### Limits of the CPI

- It measures an average household, so individual experience differs.
- The basket and weights need regular updating.
- It may not capture quality improvements.

## 4.8.3 Causes

| | Demand-pull inflation | Cost-push inflation |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Total demand grows faster than output can | Costs of production rise |
| Typical causes | Cheaper borrowing, lower taxes, extra public spending, optimism, overseas orders | Higher wages, dearer raw materials or energy, indirect taxes, import prices |
| Output | Rises, if there is spare capacity | Tends to fall |
| Jobs | Joblessness tends to fall | Joblessness tends to rise |

Chain for demand-pull: spending rises, orders increase and warehouses empty. Firms close to full capacity cannot expand output, so they charge more and offer higher pay to attract staff; the general price level climbs.

Chain for cost-push: input costs rise, so average cost rises. Firms raise prices to protect profit, so the price level rises.

| | Demand-side deflation | Supply-side deflation |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Weak total demand | Falling costs or rising productive capacity |
| Typical causes | High interest rates, higher taxes, cuts in government spending, low confidence, falling exports | Cheaper raw materials or energy, new technology, higher productivity, cheaper imports |
| Output | Falls | Rises |
| Jobs | Unemployment tends to rise | Jobs tend to be maintained or grow |
| Overall effect | Usually harmful | Often welcome |

### Worked reminder: cost pass-through

A brick yard makes 5,000 bricks a month. Costs are clay 8,000, labour 12,000, fuel 4,000 and other 6,000, total 30,000, or 6.00 per brick. Clay rises 10%, labour 5% and fuel 25%.
New costs: 8,800 + 12,600 + 5,000 + 6,000 = **32,400**. Per brick = **6.48**. The rise is 2,400 ÷ 30,000 = **8.0%**.

### Worked reminder: demand outruns output

Spending grows 6% while output can grow only 2%. Price level change ≈ 1.06 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = **3.9%**.

## 4.8.4 Consequences

| Group | Inflation | Deflation |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Buying power falls, hardest on fixed incomes | Lower prices, but purchases may be delayed |
| Workers | Real pay falls if pay rises slower than prices | Pay cuts or job losses as firms' revenue falls |
| Savers | Lose if the interest rate is below inflation | Gain: money buys more |
| Lenders | Lose if the loan rate is below inflation | Gain: repayments worth more |
| Borrowers | Gain: debts shrink in real terms | Lose: debts harder to repay |
| Firms | Uncertainty, costs of changing prices, dearer inputs, weaker exports | Falling revenue, squeezed profit, delayed investment, closures |
| Economy | Weaker competitiveness, less saving and investment, redistribution | Weak spending, falling output, unemployment, a downward spiral |

### Worked reminders

- **Real interest rate:** a saving rate of 3.5% with inflation 2% gives a real rate of about **+1.5%**. With inflation 5% it gives about **−1.5%**.
- **Real pay:** prices rise 6% and pay 4%. Real pay changes by 1.04 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = about **−1.9%**.
- **Menu costs:** reprinting 25 menus at 2 each costs 50 each time prices change.
- **Deflation and debt:** a fixed debt takes up a larger share of income if wages fall.

Shoe leather costs: the effort households spend guarding the value of their money, for example hunting for lower prices. Menu costs: what firms pay each time they must change a price list.

## 4.8.5 Policies

| Aim | Policy | How it works | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control inflation | Higher interest rates | Dearer borrowing and better saving returns reduce spending | Takes time; cuts output too |
| Control inflation | Lower money supply | Less money available to spend | Hard to control precisely |
| Control inflation | Higher taxes, lower government spending | Reduces spending | Unpopular; may raise unemployment |
| Control inflation | Higher exchange rate | Imports cheaper | Exports become dearer |
| Control inflation | Supply-side policy (education, training, investment) | Raises productivity and capacity | Slow |
| Control deflation | Lower interest rates, higher money supply | Cheaper borrowing encourages spending | Limit to how far rates fall; low confidence |
| Control deflation | Lower taxes, higher government spending | Raises spending | Bigger budget deficit; tax cut may be saved |
| Control deflation | Lower exchange rate | Exports cheaper, imports dearer | Imported inputs cost more |

Evaluation prompts: What caused the price change? How long will the policy take? Does it conflict with another aim, such as low unemployment? Who gains and loses? What if the cause is outside the country's control?

## Must-know distinctions

- A rate that eases from 6% to 3% still means rising prices. Deflation needs a rate below zero.
- A high CPI does not mean high inflation. The CPI is a level. Inflation is its rate of change.
- Demand-pull starts with demand. Cost-push starts with costs.
- Supply-side deflation is often good. Demand-side deflation is usually harmful.
- Higher interest rates are a better answer to demand-pull than to cost-push.
- Nominal values ignore price changes. Real values allow for them.

## Quick self-test

1. Define deflation.
2. Weights are 50, 30 and 20, with indices 110, 100 and 120. Calculate the CPI.
3. The CPI rises from 125.0 to 130.0. Calculate the rate of inflation.
4. The CPI falls from 200 to 194. State the rate and what it means.
5. Give two causes of demand-pull inflation.
6. State one cause of supply-side deflation.
7. A saver earns 2% when inflation is 3.5%. State the approximate real rate.
8. Who gains from unexpected inflation, a borrower or a lender?
9. Explain why a higher interest rate may fail to control cost-push inflation.
10. Give one reason why consumers might delay buying during deflation.

### Answers

1. A sustained fall in the general level of prices.
2. (50 × 110 + 30 × 100 + 20 × 120) ÷ 100 = (5,500 + 3,000 + 2,400) ÷ 100 = **109.0**.
3. (130.0 − 125.0) ÷ 125.0 × 100 = **4.0%**.
4. (194 − 200) ÷ 200 × 100 = **−3.0%**: deflation of 3%.
5. Any two: lower interest rates, lower taxes, higher government spending, higher confidence, higher export demand.
6. Any one: cheaper raw materials, new technology, higher productivity, cheaper imports.
7. About **−1.5%** (2 − 3.5).
8. The borrower, because the debt falls in real terms.
9. Cost-push inflation comes from rising costs, not excess spending, so cutting demand lowers output and jobs but does not remove the cost pressure.
10. They expect prices to fall further and prefer to wait.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a slower rise in prices deflation.
- Giving the CPI as the rate of inflation, or the reverse.
- Dividing by the new CPI instead of the old one.
- Forgetting to divide by the total of the weights.
- Not stating whether the answer is inflation or deflation.
- Describing consequences for "people" instead of the named group in the question.
- Giving a policy without saying how it reaches prices.
- Evaluation that lists drawbacks without judging which matters most in the case given.
- Treating all deflation as harmful.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between demand-pull and cost-push inflation?**
Demand-pull inflation happens when total demand rises faster than the economy's ability to produce. Cost-push inflation happens when rising production costs, such as wages, raw materials, energy or indirect taxes, are passed on in higher prices.

**Who loses from inflation?**
Savers whose interest rate is below inflation and lenders at fixed rates lose in real terms, and workers lose if pay rises more slowly than prices. Borrowers with fixed repayments can gain.

**Is deflation always harmful?**
No. Supply-side deflation from falling costs or new technology usually comes with rising output and is often welcome. Demand-side deflation from weak total demand usually comes with falling output and rising unemployment.

**What are the limits of the CPI?**
The CPI reflects an average household, so groups such as pensioners or students may face different rates. The basket and weights must be updated as habits change, and it does not fully capture improvements in quality.
