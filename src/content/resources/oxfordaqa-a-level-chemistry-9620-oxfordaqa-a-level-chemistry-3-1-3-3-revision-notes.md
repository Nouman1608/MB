These notes condense **3.1.3.3 Metallic bonding** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1, drawn on by Unit 2 and able to reappear in Unit 5. The [metallic bonding study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-3/) has model answers and fuller explanations; the [metallic bonding practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-3-practice/) test them.

## The definition

**Metallic bonding is the attraction between delocalised electrons and positive ions arranged in a lattice.**

Learn it in that form; it matches the specification's wording.

## The model in five points

1. Each metal atom releases its outer-shell electrons.
2. These electrons become **delocalised**: free to move through the whole structure, not tied to one atom.
3. The rest of each atom is a **positive ion**.
4. The ions are arranged in a regular **lattice**.
5. The **electrostatic attraction** between the ions and the delocalised electrons holds the structure together, in all directions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Delocalised electrons | Electrons not associated with any single atom, free to move throughout the structure |
| Positive ion (cation) | What remains of a metal atom after it releases its outer electrons |
| Lattice | Regular repeating three-dimensional arrangement of particles |
| Sea of electrons | Informal description of the delocalised electrons spread through the lattice |
| Non-directional bonding | Attraction that acts equally in all directions, not along particular lines between atoms |
| Giant structure | Lattice that continues throughout the sample, with no separate molecules |

## Ions and electrons in common metals

| Metal | Ion | Delocalised electrons per atom |
|---|---|---|
| Lithium, sodium, potassium | Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ | 1 |
| Magnesium, calcium | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ | 2 |
| Aluminium | Al³⁺ | 3 |

The structure is neutral overall: the total charge of the ions equals the total charge of the delocalised electrons.

## What makes the bond stronger

| Factor | Effect | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Higher ion charge | Stronger | Greater positive charge attracts the electrons more strongly |
| More delocalised electrons per atom | Stronger | More negative charge in the sea to attract the ions |
| Smaller ions | Stronger | Positive charge closer to the delocalised electrons |

Charge and number of delocalised electrons usually go together: a 2+ ion goes with two delocalised electrons per atom.

## Evidence from melting points

Approximate values:

| Comparison | Values | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Across Period 3 | Na 98 °C, Mg 650 °C, Al 660 °C | Ion charge and delocalised electrons increase; ions get smaller |
| Down Group 1 | Li 181 °C, Na 98 °C, K 63 °C | Same charge; ions get larger, so attraction weakens |

Use "more energy is needed to overcome the stronger attraction" to finish any melting point explanation.

## Compare the three strong bonds

| Bond | Positive partner | Negative partner |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Cations | Anions |
| Covalent | Two nuclei | Shared pair between them |
| Metallic | Cations in a lattice | Delocalised electrons |

## Diagram checklist

- Several rows of circles representing positive ions.
- Each ion labelled with its charge (such as 2+ for Mg).
- Electrons (e⁻) shown between the ions, in the right ratio.
- A label for the delocalised electrons.
- No labels calling the circles "atoms".

## Link to properties (subtopic 3.1.3.4)

- Delocalised electrons can move, so metals conduct electricity.
- Bonding is non-directional, so layers of ions can slide, so metals are malleable.
- Strong attraction throughout a giant lattice gives high melting points for most metals.

## Exam traps

- The lattice contains positive **ions**, not atoms.
- The negative particles are **electrons**, not negative ions.
- Metallic bonding is not ionic or covalent, and there are no intermolecular forces in a metal.
- Magnesium ions are 2+, with two delocalised electrons per atom.
- The bond is the **attraction**, not the electrons themselves.
- Explain melting points with attraction and energy, not with the mass of the atoms.
- The electrons are delocalised within the metal, not lost from it.

## Self-test

1. Define metallic bonding.
2. What does "delocalised" mean?
3. What charge do the ions in a calcium lattice carry?
4. How many delocalised electrons are released per aluminium atom?
5. Why is a piece of magnesium electrically neutral overall?
6. Name two factors that make metallic bonding stronger.
7. Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium.
8. Explain why potassium has a lower melting point than sodium.
9. Why is metallic bonding described as non-directional?
10. State one way in which metallic bonding differs from ionic bonding.
11. A student labels the circles in a metallic lattice diagram as "Mg atoms". Correct the label.
12. Which feature of the metallic bonding model allows metals to conduct electricity?

### Answers

1. Attraction between delocalised electrons and positive ions arranged in a lattice.
2. Not associated with any single atom; free to move throughout the structure.
3. 2+ (Ca²⁺).
4. Three.
5. The total charge of the delocalised electrons equals the total charge of the Mg²⁺ ions.
6. Higher ion charge (more delocalised electrons per atom); smaller ions.
7. Mg²⁺ has a higher charge and is smaller than Na⁺, and each Mg releases two delocalised electrons, so the attraction is stronger and needs more energy to overcome.
8. Both form 1+ ions, but K⁺ is larger, so the attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons is weaker.
9. Each ion is attracted to the delocalised electrons in all directions, not along particular bonds.
10. In ionic bonding the negative particles are anions; in metallic bonding they are delocalised electrons.
11. "Mg²⁺ ions".
12. The delocalised electrons are free to move through the lattice.
