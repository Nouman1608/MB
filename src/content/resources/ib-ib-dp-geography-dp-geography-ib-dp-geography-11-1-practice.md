## Section A

**1.** Define global power. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between hard power and soft power. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by the Brussels effect. **[2]**

**4.** State two criteria that could be used to judge whether a state is a superpower. **[2]**

**5.** Identify one non-state actor that holds global power. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows invented scores (0 to 100) for two states.

| | Economic | Military | Cultural |
|---|---|---|---|
| State A | 80 | 40 | 90 |
| State B | 70 | 90 | 40 |

**(a)** Calculate each state's score using equal weights. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate each state's score if military power is weighted at 50% and the other two at 25% each. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what your answers show about measuring power. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how control of a global currency can give a state power over others. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why interdependence between two states may give one of them more power than the other. **[3]**

**9.** Suggest two reasons why emerging powers have created new international institutions. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** "Global power is now more widely shared than at any time since 1945." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

**11.** Examine the role of non-state actors in global power. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The ability of a state, organisation or group to influence the behaviour of others or shape outcomes at a global scale [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Hard power is influence through coercion or payment, such as military force or sanctions [1]; soft power is influence through attraction, such as culture, values, education and diplomacy [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Because the European Union is a very large market, companies that want to sell there adopt its rules [1] and often apply them worldwide, so EU regulations, such as its data protection rules applied from 2018, shape behaviour far beyond Europe [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Economic size and influence over trade and finance [1]; the ability to project military force globally [1]. (Also credit political influence in global institutions or worldwide cultural reach.) **[2]**

**5.** For example, a transnational corporation, an international organisation such as the IMF, or an NGO [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** A: (80 + 40 + 90) / 3 = **70** [1]. B: (70 + 90 + 40) / 3 = **66.7** [1].

**(b)** A: 0.25 x 80 + 0.5 x 40 + 0.25 x 90 = 20 + 20 + 22.5 = **62.5** [1]. B: 0.25 x 70 + 0.5 x 90 + 0.25 x 40 = 17.5 + 45 + 10 = **72.5** [1].

**(c)** The ranking reverses with the weighting, although the data are the same [1], so any measure of power reflects choices about which kinds of power matter most and should be evaluated, not accepted as objective [1]. **[6]**

**7.** If most trade, debt and reserves are held in one currency, other states and firms need access to that currency and the banking system that clears it [1]. The issuing state can borrow more cheaply because demand for its currency is high [1], and it can exclude others from the financial system through sanctions, as the USA can with the dollar [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Interdependence is rarely balanced: one state may depend on the other for an essential import, such as energy, while the other has alternative suppliers [1]. The less dependent state can threaten to cut supply or demand [1], as Russia did when it cut gas supplies through Ukraine in 2006 and 2009, so the relationship becomes a source of leverage [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Existing institutions such as the IMF and World Bank give emerging powers less voting weight than their economic size suggests [1], so they build alternatives where they have more say [1]. New institutions, such as the New Development Bank set up by the BRICS countries, also let them finance infrastructure on their own terms [1] and extend their influence over borrowing countries [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines global power and identifies its dimensions [1].
- Compares periods: a bipolar Cold War world, a period of US dominance after 1991, and the present [1].
- For: emerging economies, led by China and India, have a growing share of world output and trade [1].
- For: new groupings and institutions, such as the G20 and BRICS, give more states a voice [1].
- For: non-state actors such as technology corporations hold significant power [1].
- Against: military reach and financial power remain concentrated, notably in the USA [1].
- Against: rules of older institutions, including the UN Security Council veto, lock in post-1945 power [1].
- Against: power may be shifting between a few large states rather than being widely shared among many [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence on both sides [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that economic power is more widely shared but military and financial power remain concentrated [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines non-state actors: TNCs, international organisations, NGOs, and influential individuals [1].
- TNCs shape jobs, tax revenues and technology, and the revenues of the largest exceed the GDP of many countries [1].
- Technology firms control platforms through which information and data flow [1].
- International organisations such as the IMF set conditions on loans, influencing national policy [1].
- NGOs set agendas through campaigning and can influence international agreements [1].
- Individuals, such as business leaders and philanthropists, can direct large flows of funding [1].
- Limits: states still make laws, collect taxes and control borders, and can regulate non-state actors [1].
- Non-state power is often exercised through states, for example by lobbying, rather than replacing them [1].
- Uses accurate examples [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on how far non-state actors rival or depend on states [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* HL extension questions reward conceptual precision. Define power and name the dimension you are discussing in each paragraph.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating weighted scores without multiplying each score by its weight.
- Describing power as a fixed list of superpowers instead of applying criteria.
- Treating interdependence as always mutual and equal.
- Writing about states only, with no reference to non-state actors.
