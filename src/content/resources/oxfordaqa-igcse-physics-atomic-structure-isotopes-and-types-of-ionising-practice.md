The qualification is untiered. Take the radius of a nucleus to be about 10⁻¹⁵ m where a question does not give it.

## Questions

**1.** Give the approximate radius of an atom, then describe the simple nuclear model of the atom, saying where almost all of the mass is found. **[3]**

**2.** Copy and complete the table for the relative mass and relative charge of the three particles. **[3]**

| Particle | Relative mass | Relative charge |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | | |
| Neutron | | |
| Electron | | |

**3.** A neutral atom of lithium is written ⁷₃Li.

**(a)** Give the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons it contains. **[3]**
**(b)** Another lithium atom has 3 protons and 3 neutrons. Explain why the two atoms are isotopes of lithium. **[2]**

**4.** **(a)** An iron atom ⁵⁶₂₆Fe has lost three electrons. Calculate how many electrons it now has and state its overall charge. **[2]**
**(b)** A sulfur atom ³²₁₆S gains electrons and forms an ion with a charge of –2. State how many electrons the ion has and show your working. **[2]**

**5.** Alpha particles are fired at thin metal foil. Describe the three main observations, and for each one say what it shows about the structure of the atom. **[6]**

**6.** An atom has a radius of 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Its nucleus has a radius of 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.

**(a)** Find the factor by which the atom's radius exceeds the nucleus's radius. **[2]**
**(b)** In a scale model the nucleus is a sphere of radius 2.0 mm. Calculate the radius the atom would have in this model. **[2]**

**7.** A student holds a radioactive sample in a flame and says that its nuclei will now decay more quickly.

**(a)** Explain what is meant by saying that radioactive decay is random. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain whether the student is correct. **[2]**

**8.** **(a)** State two sources of background radiation. **[2]**
**(b)** A detector with no source nearby records 138 counts in 15 minutes. With a source placed close to it, the detector records 2520 counts in 4 minutes. Calculate the count rate caused by the source alone, in counts per minute, to 3 significant figures. **[3]**

**9.** Complete the missing mass number and atomic number in each nuclear equation.

**(a)** ²³⁸₉₂U → ᴬ_Z X + ⁴₂He **[2]**
**(b)** ²¹⁴₈₃Bi → ᴬ_Z X + ⁰₋₁e **[2]**
**(c)** ᴬ_Z X → ²⁰⁶₈₂Pb + ⁰₋₁e **[2]**

**10.** **(a)** Put alpha, beta and gamma in order of ionising power, starting with the greatest. **[1]**
**(b)** State which of the three has the longest range in air. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the most strongly ionising radiation has the shortest range. **[2]**
**(d)** State what gamma radiation is. **[1]**

**11.** A detector is placed in front of two sources, R and S. The background count rate is 20 counts per minute. Absorbers are placed between each source and the detector.

| Absorber | Count rate for R (per minute) | Count rate for S (per minute) |
|---|---|---|
| None | 640 | 905 |
| Paper | 26 | 903 |
| 3 mm aluminium | 22 | 902 |
| Thick lead | 21 | 330 |

**(a)** Calculate the corrected count rate for R with no absorber. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the radiation from R and give a reason. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify the radiation from S, and explain why it is neither alpha nor beta. **[3]**
**(d)** A student says that thick lead stopped the radiation from S. Use the data to comment. **[2]**

**12.** A small source, T, is placed at different distances from a detector in air. The background count rate has been subtracted from every reading.

| Distance (cm) | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count rate (per minute) | 1210 | 1195 | 3 | 0 |

**(a)** State the range of distances in which the radiation from T stops reaching the detector. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the type of radiation and give a reason. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain, in terms of ionisation, why this radiation has such a short range in air. **[3]**
**(d)** A sheet of paper is placed between T and the detector at a distance of 2 cm. Predict the corrected count rate and explain your prediction. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Radius about 10⁻¹⁰ m [1]. At the centre is a small, positively charged nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons around it [1]. The nucleus is far smaller than the atom but holds almost all of the mass [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover size, structure and mass location with a statement for each. "Mostly empty space" describes the atom, not the nucleus.

**2.** Proton: **1, +1** [1]. Neutron: **1, 0** [1]. Electron: **very small, –1** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the sign of the charge. "1" and "+1" are not the same thing for a proton, and the electron's mass should be described as very small, not as zero.

**3. (a)** Protons = atomic number = **3** [1]. Neutrons = 7 – 3 = **4** [1]. Electrons = protons in a neutral atom = **3** [1].
**(b)** Both have 3 protons, so they are the same element [1]. They have different numbers of neutrons (4 and 3) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An isotope answer should compare both numbers: same protons and different neutrons. Giving only one of them is incomplete.

**4. (a)** Electrons = 26 – 3 = **23** [1]. Charge = 26 – 23 = **+3** [1].
**(b)** The nucleus has 16 protons, and a charge of –2 means two more electrons than protons [1], so 16 + 2 = **18 electrons** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction of protons and electrons in the charge. A negative ion has more electrons than protons, which is a common slip in the other direction.

**5.** Most alpha particles carry straight on [1], so most of an atom is empty space [1]. Some are deflected through small angles [1], so they are repelled by a positive charge (the nucleus is positive) [1]. Very few bounce back through large angles [1], so a tiny nucleus holds the positive charge and most of the mass [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Match every observation to a conclusion of its own. Three observations followed by a single general statement tend to earn less than three linked pairs.

**6. (a)** Factor = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ [1] = **2.5 × 10⁴** (25 000) [1].
**(b)** Atom radius = 2.0 mm × 2.5 × 10⁴ [1] = 5.0 × 10⁴ mm = **50 m** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert units at the end, not halfway. 5.0 × 10⁴ mm is 50 m, and an answer given only in millimetres is easy to misread.

**7. (a)** There is no way to predict the moment at which any individual nucleus decays [1], and which nucleus in the sample goes next is equally unpredictable [1].
**(b)** The student is wrong [1]. Decay is a change in the nucleus, and physical processes such as heating do not affect it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a decision and a reason. "No, because it is random" is weaker than saying that heating does not alter the nucleus.

**8. (a)** Any two of these three: radioactive substances in the environment, for example radon from rocks; radiation from space; devices such as hospital X-ray machines [1] [1].
**(b)** Background rate = 138 / 15 = 9.2 per minute [1]. Rate with source = 2520 / 4 = 630 per minute [1]. Corrected rate = 630 – 9.2 = 620.8, so **621 counts per minute** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert both counts to rates before subtracting. Subtracting 138 from 2520 mixes counts taken over different times.

**9. (a)** A = 238 – 4 = **234** [1]; Z = 92 – 2 = **90** [1].
**(b)** A = **214** [1]; Z = 83 + 1 = **84** [1].
**(c)** A = **206** [1]; Z = 82 + 1 = **81** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) the unknown is on the left, so the parent's atomic number is one less than the product's. Check by adding the right-hand side: 206 + 0 = 206 and 82 + (–1) = 81.

**10. (a)** **Alpha, beta, gamma** [1].
**(b)** **Gamma** [1].
**(c)** A strongly ionising radiation transfers energy to atoms as it ionises them [1], so it loses its energy over a short distance and is soon stopped [1].
**(d)** Electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the ideas in part (c): ionisation uses up energy, so range is short. Repeating "alpha is easily stopped" does not explain why.

**11. (a)** 640 – 20 = **620 counts per minute** [1].
**(b)** **Alpha** [1], because paper reduces the count rate to close to background (26 against 20) [1].
**(c)** **Gamma** [1]. Not alpha, because paper makes almost no difference (905 to 903) [1]. Not beta, because 3 mm of aluminium makes almost no difference (902) [1].
**(d)** Thick lead reduced the corrected rate from 885 to 310 [1], so about a third still gets through, and lead did not stop it completely [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the figures from the table. Small differences of a few counts are within random variation, so do not call them absorption.

**12. (a)** Between **4 cm and 6 cm** [1].
**(b)** **Alpha** [1], because the count rate falls to almost nothing within a few centimetres of air (the range is short) [1].
**(c)** Alpha particles are strongly ionising, so they knock electrons off many air molecules along their path [1]. Each ionisation transfers energy from the alpha particle [1], so it quickly runs out of energy and stops after a few centimetres [1].
**(d)** About **0 counts per minute** (close to the background) [1], because paper stops alpha particles [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (a) give the interval, not a single value, because the readings do not show exactly where the count rate reaches zero. For part (c), name ionisation and energy loss in that order.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the atom as "mostly empty space" without saying that the nucleus is tiny and holds the mass.
- Listing alpha scattering observations without a matching conclusion.
- Using the atomic number instead of mass number minus atomic number for neutrons.
- Getting the charge of an ion the wrong way round: protons minus electrons.
- Not converting counts to rates before subtracting background.
- Adding 1 to the atomic number for alpha decay, or subtracting 4 from it.
- Writing that heating a sample changes its decay.
- Saying "lead stops gamma" when the data show a count rate that is reduced but not removed.
- Leaving out the unit (counts per minute, metres) in a final answer.
