These questions cover the "practical issues" row of section 3.7 in the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification: time, cost and access. Methods can be assessed in Paper 1 or Paper 2. All people, places and figures are fictional.

Revise first with the [practical issues study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-practical-issues/) and the [practical issues revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-practical-issues-revision-notes/). For questions on all of section 3.7, use the [research methods practice set](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods-practice/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of the following is a practical issue named in the specification?

A. Consent
B. Confidentiality
C. Access
D. Harm to participants

**[1]**

**2.** A person who controls a researcher's entry to a group or setting is called a:

A. Respondent
B. Gatekeeper
C. Sampling frame
D. Pilot

**[1]**

**3.** Explain the difference between a practical issue and an ethical issue in research. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** costs a researcher might face when carrying out face-to-face interviews across a large city. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** reason why participant observation takes a long time. **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** reason why it can be difficult to gain access to company directors for research. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** A researcher has a budget of £6,000. Interviewers cost £150 per completed interview, including travel. Transcription costs £50 per interview. The data are fictional.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum number of interviews she can afford. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** way this budget could affect the representativeness of her findings. **[2]**

**8.** Read the item.

> A researcher wants to observe how pupils behave in lessons at Brookfield School (a fictional school). The head teacher agrees, but only allows her to visit Year 9 top-set classes on Tuesday mornings.

Using the item, explain **two** ways the gatekeeper's decision could affect the research. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** practical issues a researcher might face when studying families in their own homes. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a researcher could overcome **two** access problems when studying people who sleep rough. **[4]**

**11.** A sociologist wants to study why some young people leave school at 16 without qualifications. Identify and explain the practical issues she would need to consider, and how her choice of method could deal with them. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far practical issues are the most important influence on a sociologist's choice of research method. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** C. Access [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* the other three are the ethical issues named in the specification.

**2.** B. Gatekeeper [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* in research methods, a gatekeeper controls access; do not confuse this with the media meaning of the word.

**3.** A practical issue concerns whether research can be carried out, such as time, cost and access [1], whereas an ethical issue concerns whether it is morally acceptable to do it in a particular way, such as consent, confidentiality and harm [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* give the named examples of each to secure the marks.

**4.** One cost correctly identified, such as paying interviewers [1], and a second, such as travel across the city, recording equipment or transcription [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "money" is not a cost; name what the money pays for.

**5.** The researcher must first gain acceptance and trust from the group [1], which can take weeks or months before people behave naturally in front of them [1]. They then need to spend long periods observing to see how behaviour varies over time, as Ball did over three years of fieldwork [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* writing up field notes is another valid reason.

**6.** Company directors are powerful and busy [1], so they can refuse requests easily and often have assistants or press officers who control access to them [1]. They may also want to protect their company's reputation, so they have little reason to agree to research that might reveal problems [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the key idea is that power gives the ability to refuse.

**7. (a)** Cost per interview = £150 + £50 = £200 [1]; £6,000 ÷ £200 = 30 interviews [1].
**(b)** Thirty interviews is a small sample [1], so the people interviewed may not reflect the variety of the population, for example in class, ethnicity or age, and findings may not be generalisable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* add the two costs before dividing; using only £150 gives 40, which is wrong.

**8.** First, she can only observe top-set classes [1], so she will not see behaviour in lower sets, where Ball found anti-school attitudes were more common, making her findings unrepresentative of the school [1]. Second, she can only visit on Tuesday mornings [1], so she may miss behaviour that differs later in the day or week, such as in afternoon lessons, which limits the validity of her picture [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each point must name the restriction in the item and explain its effect.

**9.** First, access: families may refuse to let a researcher into their home [1], because family life is private and members may feel judged or watched [1]. Second, time: observing family routines would mean repeated visits at different times of day, such as mealtimes and bedtime [1], which takes a long time and may not fit around the family's schedule [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the observer effect is a validity point, not a practical one, unless you link it to time or access.

**10.** First, there is no sampling frame of people who sleep rough [1], so the researcher could work through a homelessness charity or day centre whose staff act as gatekeepers and introduce potential participants [1]. Second, people may distrust researchers or officials [1], so the researcher could spend time volunteering at a centre to build trust and offer confidentiality before asking for interviews [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* snowball sampling is another strong solution for the first problem.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Access: young people who have left school are no longer on school rolls, so there is no easy sampling frame [1].
- They could be contacted through youth services, colleges or employment programmes, which act as gatekeepers [1].
- Gatekeepers may introduce only those who are engaged with services, so the sample may be unrepresentative [1].
- Snowball sampling could reach others through friends [1].
- Some young people may distrust researchers linked to schools or officials, so trust must be built [1].
- Time: unstructured interviews take time to arrange, conduct and transcribe [1].
- But they suit the topic, as reasons for leaving school are personal and varied [1].
- Cost: a small number of in-depth interviews is cheaper than a large survey with interviewers [1].
- A short questionnaire could reach more people quickly but give less depth [1].
- Secondary data, such as official figures on young people not in education or employment, are quick and free but do not explain reasons [1].
- Combining official figures with a small set of interviews balances cost, time and validity [1].
- Judgement: practical issues push the design towards a small, gatekeeper-assisted interview study supported by secondary data, accepting limits on representativeness [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* the question asks how the method deals with the issues; each issue should be paired with a design decision.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Time, cost and access limit which methods are possible [1].
- A small budget or short deadline often leads to questionnaires or secondary data [1].
- Access problems can make some methods impossible, such as covert observation of pupils by an adult [1].
- But the research aim matters: "how many" questions suit questionnaires, "why" questions suit interviews [1].
- The topic matters: sensitive topics need methods that build trust, such as unstructured interviews [1].
- Ethical issues can rule out methods even when they are practical, such as covert observation without consent [1].
- The researcher's preference for reliable or valid data shapes the choice [1].
- Judgement: practical issues set the limits, but the aim, topic and ethics decide which method is chosen within them, so they are important but not always the most important [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* "most important" means weighing practical issues against at least two other influences.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing practical and ethical issues.
- Writing "it costs a lot" without saying what costs money.
- Ignoring the specific restriction in an item.
- Naming a problem without explaining its effect on the research.
- Forgetting to add up all costs in a budget calculation.
