These questions cover section 3.10.1 managing change of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-managing-change/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-managing-change-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A new law forces a firm to change its packaging. What type of change is this? **[1]**

A: Internal change

B: External change

C: Incremental change only

D: Organic change

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a restraining force in Lewin's force field analysis? **[1]**

A: Rivals have already introduced the change

B: Customers are asking for the change

C: Staff fear losing their jobs

D: Potential cost savings

**3.** Define "delayering". **[2]**

**4.** Name **two** of Kotter and Schlesinger's reasons why people resist change. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why an organic structure suits a firm in a fast-changing market. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**6.** Wyndham Council is considering a four-day working week. The managers score the forces from 1 to 5:

Driving forces: staff demand for flexible work 4; advantage in recruiting 3; rival councils already offer it 2.
Restraining forces: cover for public services 4; managers' doubts 3; complexity of payroll 2.

Calculate the total for each side and comment on the likely ease of change. **[4]**

**7.** Explain **two** actions the council could take to reduce the restraining forces. **[4]**

**8.** Wyndham has 640 operational staff. With a span of control of 4, the number of managers needed (all levels) is 214. With a span of 8, it is 93. Calculate the saving if the average cost of a manager is £40,000, and explain **one** risk of widening the span. **[4]**

**9.** Wyndham plans to use more zero-hours contracts for its leisure centre staff. Analyse **two** effects on the council. **[6]**

**10.** Staff at Wyndham do not believe the four-day week will protect their pay. Identify the reason for resistance from Kotter and Schlesinger's list and explain which approach to overcoming it would be most suitable. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse why the move from a mechanistic to an organic structure might be difficult for a long-established public sector organisation. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that the most important factor in successful change is communicating well with staff. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. The trigger is outside the firm. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Fear of job losses holds back change; the other options are driving forces. **[1]**

**3.** Removing one or more levels [1] of management from the organisation, making it flatter [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two: parochial self-interest [1]; misunderstanding [1]; low tolerance for change [1]; different assessments of the situation [1]. **[2]**

**5.** An organic structure is flat, with decisions made close to customers [1], so the firm can respond quickly to change without waiting for approval through many layers [1]. Broad roles and open communication help staff adapt and share new ideas [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Driving total = 4 + 3 + 2 = **9** [1]; restraining total = 4 + 3 + 2 = **9** [1]. The forces are balanced [1], so change will be hard unless the restraining forces are reduced or the driving forces strengthened [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Action 1: pilot the four-day week in one department to show that public services can still be covered [1], which reduces the largest restraining force [1]. Action 2: provide briefing and training for managers, and involve them in designing the rota [1], which reduces their doubts and builds ownership [1]. (Credit also: updating the payroll system in advance.) **[4]**

**8.** Saving = 214 − 93 = 121 fewer managers [1]; 121 × £40,000 = **£4.84m** a year [1]. Risk: each manager supervises more staff, so there is less support and supervision [1], which could lower service quality and morale, and redundancy costs reduce the first-year saving [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Effect 1: lower costs and more flexibility [1]. Staff can be used only when demand is high, such as at weekends [1], so the council saves on wages in quiet periods and can match staffing to demand [1]. Effect 2: lower commitment and higher turnover [1]. Zero-hours staff have no certainty of income and may leave for more secure jobs [1], which raises recruitment and training costs and may lower service quality [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The reason is misunderstanding, or perhaps different assessments of the situation [1]. The most suitable approach is education and communication [1]: explain clearly, with figures, how pay will be protected [1], and answer questions in meetings so staff trust the information [1]. **[4]**

**11.** A long-established public body has a tall hierarchy with fixed roles and rules [1], so moving to flat, flexible teams means changing how people work [1]. Staff and managers may fear losing status and security (parochial self-interest) [1]. Accountability to the public and legal duties require clear lines of responsibility, which are harder in an organic structure [1]. A strong culture of following procedures resists change [1], and budgets and funding may not allow quick restructuring [1]. **[6]**

**12.** For: poor communication leads to misunderstanding, which is a main cause of resistance [1], so clear explanation reduces fear and builds trust [1]. Staff who understand the reasons are more likely to take part [1]. Communication is cheap compared with negotiation or coercion [1]. Against: communication alone does not remove real losses, such as job cuts, which need negotiation or support [1]. Some resistance comes from self-interest or different views, which information does not change [1]. Leadership, resources, timing and the skill of managers matter as well [1]. Participation gives people ownership that one-way communication does not. Factors: the reasons for resistance [1], the speed required [1], and the trust between staff and managers [1]. Judgement: communication is essential and usually comes first [1], but it is rarely enough; success depends on matching the approach to the cause of the resistance [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* High marks go to answers that name the model, apply it to the case and decide which approach fits, with a reason.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating all change as beneficial.
- Listing approaches without choosing one for the case.
- Confusing organic and mechanistic structures.
- Describing delayering only as cost cutting.
- Failing to link the type of change to its pace and cause.
