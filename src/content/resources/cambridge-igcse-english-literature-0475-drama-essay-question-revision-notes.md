**These notes cover the (b) question in Paper 2 Drama.** The syllabus offers on each play "either
(a) a passage-based question or (b) an essay question", and candidates "must answer one
passage-based question and one essay question". Set texts "may not" be taken into the exam room, so
the essay is written from memory. Full method: the
[drama essay question study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-drama-essay-question/).
Paper format: the [Paper 2 Drama revision notes](/resources/igcse-english-literature-paper-2-drama-revision-notes/).

## The task in one paragraph

An essay question names a focus in one set play (a character, a relationship, a theme, a situation)
and asks how the playwright presents it, or how far you agree with a view of it. You answer from
memory in about 45 minutes, for 25 marks, choosing evidence from across the whole play. Your other
answer on the paper must be a passage-based question on your other play.

## Decoding the title

| Part of the title | Example | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | "the relationship between Agnes and her son" | Every paragraph stays on this |
| Key word | "memorable", "sympathy", "powerful", "changing" | The test of relevance; return to it in each topic sentence |
| "Explore the ways in which…" | Methods | Analyse how the playwright creates the effect |
| "How does the playwright…?" | Methods | As above; focus on choices and their effects |
| "To what extent…?" / "How far…?" | Judgement | Weigh evidence for and against, then decide |
| "What do you think…?" | Direct personal response | State your view early and support it throughout |

## The five-minute plan

1. **One-sentence answer** to the question.
2. **Four or five moments** from across the play (beginning, middle, end).
3. **For each moment:** one or two short quotations, or a precise reference, and one dramatic method.
4. **Order:** chronological development, contrasting views, or grouped by method.
5. **Conclusion:** a judgement on the key word, not a summary.

## From title to plan: a quick example

Title (invented play): "How does the playwright make the friendship between Sam and Ellie so
important in the play?"

- **Focus:** the friendship. **Key word:** "important". **Instruction:** "How does the playwright",
  so methods.
- **One-sentence answer:** the friendship matters because it is the only relationship that survives
  the play's conflicts, and the playwright makes us feel its value by threatening it repeatedly.
- **Moments:** their first scene; the quarrel at the midpoint; the reconciliation; the final image.
- **Structure:** chronological development, each topic sentence explaining why the friendship matters
  at that point.

## Argument structures

| Structure | Best for | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Chronological development | "changing", "develops", "presents… through the play" | Turning into plot retelling |
| Contrasting views | "To what extent", "sympathy", "How far do you agree" | Sitting on the fence at the end |
| By method | "Explore the ways", "dramatic" | Losing the key word in a list of techniques |

## Methods you can use without a printed passage

- **Structure:** where revelations are placed; how acts and scenes end.
- **Dramatic irony:** what the audience learns before a character.
- **Contrast and parallel:** foils and mirrored scenes.
- **Recurring props or images** that change meaning.
- **Remembered stage directions:** silences, exits, lighting.
- **Speech patterns** that shift as a character changes.

## The essay paragraph

**Topic sentence** that answers the question → **moment** in the play → **short quotation or precise
reference** → **dramatic method** → **effect on the audience** → **link** to the key word and to the
argument's development.

## Evidence from memory

- Learn quotations of a few words that fit several questions.
- A precise reference ("when he leaves without taking his coat") is valid evidence.
- Never invent a quotation; inaccurate words weaken AO1.
- Spread evidence across the play to show knowledge of the whole text.

## Exam traps

- **Biography.** Retelling a character's story in order.
- **Lost key word.** Paragraphs that never mention sympathy, power or whatever the title asks.
- **One-sided "to what extent".** Without counter-evidence there is no judgement.
- **One act only.** Evidence from a single part of the play.
- **Novel-style writing.** No audience, no staging, no sense of performance.
- **Overrunning.** Taking time from the passage question on your other play.

## Self-test

1. Can you take the set play into the Paper 2 exam?
2. How many essay questions must you answer on Paper 2?
3. What three things should you underline in an essay title?
4. What should you write before you plan anything else?
5. How many moments should an essay plan usually include, and from where?
6. Which argument structure suits a "To what extent" question?
7. Name three dramatic methods you can discuss without a printed passage.
8. What is a precise reference, and when is it useful?
9. What should a topic sentence do?
10. What should a conclusion contain?
11. Why is a character biography a weak response to an essay question?
12. A title asks "What do you think of the way the playwright ends the play?" Where should your view
    first appear?

**Answers:** 1. No. 2. One, together with one passage-based question on your other play. 3. The
focus, the key word and the instruction. 4. A one-sentence answer to the question. 5. Four or five,
spread across the beginning, middle and end of the play. 6. Contrasting views, ending in a judgement.
7. Any three: structure and placing of revelations; dramatic irony; contrast or parallel between
characters; recurring props or images; remembered stage directions; changing speech patterns.
8. Specific reference to a moment without exact quotation; useful when you cannot recall the exact
words. 9. Answer the question and connect to the key word, rather than introduce a plot event.
10. A judgement on the key word that follows from the argument. 11. It retells what happens rather
than answering the question or analysing methods. 12. In the introduction, then supported in each
paragraph.
