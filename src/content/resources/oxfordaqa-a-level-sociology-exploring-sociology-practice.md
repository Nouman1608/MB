## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between formal and informal social control, giving one example of each. **[4]**

**2.** Identify **three** agencies of socialisation and explain, for each, one way it transmits norms and values. **[6]**

## Section B

**3.** Using the example of truancy from school, explain how the same behaviour can be regulated by both formal and informal social control simultaneously. **[6]**

**4.** Explain how the positivist and interpretivist traditions differ in their approach to choosing a research method, giving one example of a method favoured by each. **[8]**

**5.** For the research question "why do some working-class students underachieve at school?", explain what a positivist approach and an interpretivist approach would each contribute, and one limitation of each. **[10]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by triangulation, and evaluate whether it resolves the disagreement between positivist and interpretivist approaches. **[8]**

**7. Evaluate** the claim that a sociologist's choice of research method is determined more by theoretical position than by practical or ethical factors. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. Formal social control** relies on **written rules and designated institutions with the power to punish**, such as the law and the criminal justice system, or school disciplinary codes [1] [1]. **Informal social control** relies on **unwritten expectation and social reaction**, such as disapproval, gossip or exclusion from family and peers [1] [1].

**2.** Any three, 2 marks each: **Family** — transmits norms and values through **primary socialisation**, modelling behaviour and directly rewarding or discouraging conduct from early childhood [1] [1]. **Education** — transmits norms and values through both the **formal curriculum** and the **hidden curriculum** of school rules, routines and hierarchy [1] [1]. **Peer group** — transmits norms and values through **approval and disapproval among equals**, especially influential in adolescence [1] [1]. **Media** — transmits norms and values by **presenting and normalising particular behaviours, roles and lifestyles** to a mass audience [1] [1].

**3.** Truancy is regulated **formally** through fines or legal action taken against parents, and through the school's own disciplinary procedures [1] [1]. It is regulated **informally** through disapproval or pressure from family and peers, and through the social expectation that a child should be in school [1] [1]. A strong answer recognises that these two routes **operate simultaneously on the same behaviour**, rather than treating formal and informal control as applying to separate categories of conduct [2].

**4. Positivists** favour methods that produce **quantifiable, comparable data across large populations**, such as **structured questionnaires or official statistics**, on the grounds that sociology should model itself on the natural sciences and identify general patterns [1] [1] [1]. **Interpretivists** favour methods that capture **meaning and lived experience**, such as **unstructured interviews or participant observation**, on the grounds that human behaviour cannot be reduced to countable variables without losing what makes it meaningful to those involved [1] [1] [1]. The disagreement is therefore **theoretical, not just practical** — it concerns what counts as valid sociological knowledge in the first place [2].

**5.** A **positivist approach** might use large-scale official statistics on exam results by social class [1]; this is valued for being **representative and able to reveal broad patterns** across a large population [1]; its limitation is that it is **limited in explaining why the pattern exists** [1]. An **interpretivist approach** might use unstructured interviews or classroom observation with a small number of students [1]; this is valued for **revealing the lived experience** of labelling, teacher expectation or subcultural pressure [1]; its limitation is **limited generalisability** beyond the small group studied [1]. A strong answer notes that the two approaches are not simply competing but **answer different questions** — one reveals the pattern, the other reveals the mechanism — which is itself an argument for **triangulating** them [2] [2].

**6. Triangulation** is **combining quantitative and qualitative methods within a single piece of research**, so that the weaknesses of one method are offset by the strengths of the other [1] [1]. It does not fully resolve the theoretical disagreement, since a committed positivist or interpretivist may still regard the other tradition's data as invalid or unscientific in principle [2]; however, in practice, most modern sociological research draws on both approaches, using quantitative data to establish a pattern and qualitative data to explore the meaning behind it, which is a genuinely productive compromise even if it does not settle the underlying epistemological debate [2] [2].

**7.** **For theoretical determination:** a sociologist's theoretical position defines what counts as valid evidence in the first place — a committed positivist is unlikely to regard unstructured interview data as scientifically respectable regardless of practical convenience, and vice versa for an interpretivist confronted with a structured questionnaire [2] [2]. **Against — practical and ethical factors matter independently:** even a sociologist with a clear theoretical preference may be unable to use their preferred method due to **time and funding constraints**, the **size or accessibility of the sample**, or **ethical requirements** such as informed consent, protection from harm and the right to withdraw, which can rule out certain designs (for example, covert observation) regardless of theoretical preference [2] [2] [2]. **Judgement:** theoretical position is usually the **starting point** that narrows the field of acceptable methods, but practical and ethical constraints frequently determine the **final, specific choice** within that narrowed field, so the strongest answer treats the two as operating together rather than ranking one as simply more important than the other [2].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing agencies of socialisation and types of social control as two separate, disconnected lists rather than pairing a specific agency with a specific mechanism.
- Describing a research method's practical strengths and weaknesses without connecting the choice back to a theoretical justification.
- Treating triangulation as automatically resolving the positivist–interpretivist disagreement, rather than evaluating how far it actually does.
- Writing about Unit 2 in isolation from Unit 1, when strong answers draw on family-related content as worked examples of the methodological issues discussed here.
- On the final evaluate question, ranking theoretical, practical and ethical factors without reaching a reasoned judgement about how they interact.
