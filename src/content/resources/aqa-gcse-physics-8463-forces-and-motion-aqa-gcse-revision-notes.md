## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Displacement | Straight-line distance from start to finish, with direction (vector) |
| Velocity | Speed in a given direction (vector) |
| Acceleration | Rate of change of velocity, in m/s² |
| Deceleration | Slowing down; negative acceleration |
| Terminal velocity | Steady velocity reached when the resultant force on a falling object is zero |
| Inertia (HT) | Tendency of an object to stay at rest or in uniform motion |
| Inertial mass (HT) | How difficult it is to change an object's velocity; force ÷ acceleration |
| Thinking distance | Distance travelled during the driver's reaction time |
| Braking distance | Distance travelled under the braking force |
| Stopping distance | Thinking distance + braking distance |

## Equations

| Equation | Recall or given? |
|---|---|
| s = v t | Recall |
| a = Δv / t | Recall |
| F = m a | Recall |
| v² − u² = 2 a s | Equation sheet |

Units: s in m, v and u in m/s, t in s, a in m/s², F in N, m in kg.

## Typical values to recall

- Walking ~1.5 m/s; running ~3 m/s; cycling ~6 m/s.
- Speed of sound in air ~330 m/s.
- Free-fall acceleration near Earth ~9.8 m/s².
- Reaction time 0.2 s to 0.9 s.

## Graph rules

| Graph | Gradient | Area under line | Horizontal line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distance–time | Speed | (not used) | Stationary |
| Velocity–time | Acceleration | Distance travelled (HT) | Constant velocity |

- **Higher tier only:** speed at an instant on a curved distance–time graph = gradient of the tangent.
- **Higher tier only:** count squares for the area under a curved velocity–time line; multiply by the
  distance each square represents.

*Worked reminder.* A cyclist goes from 4.0 m/s to 10.0 m/s in 3.0 s.
a = 6.0 / 3.0 = **2.0 m/s²**; distance (HT, trapezium area) = ½ × (4.0 + 10.0) × 3.0 = **21 m**.

## Terminal velocity sequence

```
1  weight > drag            → resultant down → accelerates
2  speed rises → drag rises → resultant falls → acceleration falls
3  drag = weight            → resultant zero → terminal velocity
(parachute opens) drag > weight → decelerates → new, lower terminal velocity
```

On a velocity–time graph: a curve with falling gradient that levels off.

## Newton's laws in one line each

1. Zero resultant force → no change in velocity (stays still, or same speed and direction).
2. Resultant force → acceleration: F = m a; a ∝ F and a ∝ 1/m.
3. Two interacting objects exert equal and opposite forces on each other.

### Required practical 7 summary

- Trolley pulled by a hanging mass over a pulley; light gates (or timer and ruler) give the acceleration.
- Vary force at constant mass: move masses from the trolley to the hanger.
- Vary mass at constant force: add masses to the trolley.
- Results: a ∝ F (straight line through origin); a falls as mass rises.

## Stopping distances

| Factor | Affects | How |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Both | Thinking ∝ speed; braking ∝ speed² |
| Tiredness, drugs, alcohol, distractions | Thinking distance | Longer reaction time |
| Wet or icy road | Braking distance | Less friction, so smaller deceleration |
| Worn brakes or tyres | Braking distance | Less braking force or grip |

*Worked reminder.* Thinking distance at 15 m/s with 0.6 s reaction time = 15 × 0.6 = **9 m**. At 30 m/s,
thinking distance doubles to 18 m and braking distance becomes four times as long.

### Braking physics

- Friction between brakes and wheel does work → kinetic store falls → brake temperature rises.
- Faster vehicle → bigger braking force needed to stop in the same distance.
- Large decelerations → brakes overheat, loss of control.
- **Higher tier only:** estimate the braking force with v² − u² = 2as then F = ma.

### Reaction time from a ruler drop

```
v = √(2 × 9.8 × distance fallen)      then  t = v / 9.8
```

## Exam traps

- Leaving km, minutes or hours unconverted in s = v t.
- Forgetting that velocity can change at constant speed (direction change).
- Using driving force, not resultant force, in F = ma.
- "No forces act at terminal velocity" (the forces are balanced).
- Third-law pairs on the same object.
- Forgetting the square root after v² − u² = 2as, or using u where v is needed.
- Saying braking distance doubles when speed doubles.

## Quick self-test

1. A runner completes two laps of a 400 m track. State the distance travelled and the displacement.
2. A bus travels 2.4 km in 6.0 minutes. Calculate its average speed in m/s.
3. What does a horizontal line on a velocity–time graph show?
4. A car slows from 18 m/s to 6.0 m/s in 4.0 s. Calculate its deceleration.
5. A stone falls from rest through 5.0 m. Calculate its speed, using g = 9.8 m/s².
6. Explain why a skydiver reaches terminal velocity.
7. A resultant force of 1200 N acts on a 400 kg motorbike and rider. Calculate the acceleration.
8. (Higher tier only) A force of 90 N gives an object an acceleration of 0.60 m/s². State its inertial mass.
9. (Higher tier only) Why is a car moving round a bend at a constant speed accelerating?
10. Name two factors that increase thinking distance.
11. A car's speed doubles. By what factor does its braking distance increase, for the same braking force?
12. Explain why the brakes of a car get hot when it stops.

### Answers

1. Distance **800 m**; displacement **0**.
2. 2400 ÷ 360 = **6.7 m/s**.
3. Constant velocity (zero acceleration).
4. (18 − 6.0) ÷ 4.0 = **3.0 m/s²**.
5. v² = 2 × 9.8 × 5.0 = 98; v = **9.9 m/s**.
6. As speed increases, air resistance increases until it equals the weight; the resultant force is then zero,
   so the velocity stays constant.
7. 1200 ÷ 400 = **3.0 m/s²**.
8. 90 ÷ 0.60 = **150 kg**.
9. Its direction is changing, so its velocity is changing.
10. Any two: tiredness, drugs, alcohol, distractions.
11. **Four** times.
12. Work done by friction between the brakes and the wheel transfers energy from the kinetic store to the
    thermal store of the brakes.
