## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define government failure. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is an example of an unintended consequence of intervention?

A: A tax on tobacco raises revenue for the government

B: A maximum rent leads some landlords to stop letting flats

C: A subsidy lowers the price of bus travel

D: A health campaign increases awareness of a risk **[1]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by regulatory capture. **[2]**

**4.** A scheme removes a welfare loss of £8 million a year but costs £11 million a year to administer. Calculate the net effect on society's welfare. **[1]**

**5.** Explain, using an example, how conflicting objectives can lead to government failure. **[2]**

**6.** Distinguish between partial and complete government failure. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** In an invented market for single-use coffee cups, demand is Qd = 240 − 6P and supply is Qs = 4P − 40, with Q in million cups and P in pence. Each cup imposes a marginal external cost of 5p.

**(a)** Calculate the free-market price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the socially optimal quantity, reached with a 5p tax. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the welfare loss with no intervention. **[1]**

**(d)** The government overestimates the external cost and sets a tax of 15p. Calculate the new quantity and the welfare loss. **[3]**

**(e)** State whether the 15p tax is an example of government failure, and why. **[1]**

**8.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, how a maximum price on rented housing could lead to government failure. **[6]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why a subsidy paid to farmers for each tonne of a crop might cause government failure. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why time lags can cause government failure. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that the risk of government failure means governments should not intervene in markets. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Government failure occurs when intervention leads to a less efficient allocation of resources [1], so there is a net welfare loss compared with the free market or with what better intervention could achieve [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **B** [1]. The ceiling reduces the supply of flats, which the policy did not intend. **[1]**

**3.** Regulatory capture is when a regulator, set up to protect the public [1], comes to act in the interests of the firms it regulates, for example because it relies on them for information or staff [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 8 − 11 = **−£3 million**: society is worse off [1]. **[1]**

**5.** A fuel tax may cut emissions, meeting an environmental objective [1], but it raises costs for rural households and haulage firms, conflicting with aims on living costs and competitiveness, so the policy may be weakened or produce a less efficient outcome [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Partial government failure: intervention improves on the market outcome, but by less than it could or at excessive cost [1]. Complete government failure: the outcome is worse than if the market had been left alone [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 240 − 6P = 4P − 40, so 10P = 280, **P = 28p** and **Q = 72** million [1][1].

**(b)** With a 5p tax, Qs = 4P − 60, so 300 = 10P, P = 30p and **Q = 60** million [1][1].

**(c)** ½ × (72 − 60) × 5 = **30** (£300,000, since 12 million cups × 5p × ½ = 30 million pence) [1].

**(d)** Qs = 4P − 100, so 340 = 10P, P = 34p and **Q = 36** million [1]. At Q = 36, buyers value the last cup at 34p and the social cost is 19p + 5p = 24p, a gap of 10p [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × (60 − 36) × 10 = **120** (£1.2 million) [1].

**(e)** Yes: the loss of 120 is four times the loss of 30 with no intervention, so the tax makes allocation worse, which is complete government failure [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Diagram with demand and supply for rented housing and a maximum rent below equilibrium [1], showing quantity supplied below quantity demanded and the shortage marked [1]. The ceiling aims to make renting affordable [1], but landlords supply fewer homes at the lower rent, so fewer people are housed than in the free market [1]. Those who cannot find a home legally may pay side payments or black-market rents above the free-market level [1], and landlords have less incentive to maintain property, so quality falls [1]. **[6]**

**9.** The subsidy encourages more production than consumers want at the price, leading to surpluses that must be stored or destroyed [1], which wastes resources and adds to public spending [1]. The government may not know the correct subsidy, and once in place it is politically hard to remove [1], so farmers come to depend on it and have less incentive to cut costs or switch to crops consumers value more [1]. **[4]**

**10.** It takes time to recognise a problem, decide on a policy and for the policy to take effect [1]. By the time it works, conditions may have changed [1], so the policy may be too strong, too weak or no longer needed, leading to a misallocation of resources [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Government failure is real: poor information can lead to taxes set at the wrong level [1], and price controls create shortages and black markets [1].
- Administrative costs can exceed the welfare gain from intervention [1], and political pressures may lead to short-term choices [1].
- But without intervention, market failures such as pollution or underprovision of education remain [1], and these losses can be large [1].
- Partial government failure may still improve on the market outcome [1].
- The risk can be reduced by choosing market-based tools, using pilot schemes and reviewing policy [1].
- The case for intervention is strongest when the market failure is large and its size can be estimated [1].
- Judgement: the risk of government failure is a reason to intervene carefully and to choose and design tools well, not a reason never to intervene [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers compare the size of the market failure with the risk and size of government failure, rather than treating either as automatic.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining government failure as "a policy that fails" without reference to resource allocation.
- Naming causes without the mechanism.
- Reading the welfare loss with the wrong prices.
- Concluding that governments should never intervene.
- Forgetting administrative costs.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is government failure always worse than market failure?**
No. Partial government failure can still leave society better off than the free market. The judgement depends on the size of each loss.

**Which interventions are most likely to cause government failure?**
Price controls carry a high risk because they stop prices from rationing goods, creating shortages, surpluses and black markets. Any tool set without good information also carries a risk.
