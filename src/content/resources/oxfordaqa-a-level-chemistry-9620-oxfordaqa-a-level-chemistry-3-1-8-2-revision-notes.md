These notes condense **3.1.8.2 Gibbs free-energy change, ∆G, and entropy change, ∆S** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 3.

For worked examples and graph work, use the [3.1.8.2 study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-8-2/); for exam-style work, the [3.1.8.2 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-8-2-practice/). ## Core recall

- ∆H alone **cannot** explain feasible change: some endothermic changes happen.
- **Entropy, S**: a measure of disorder. ∆S accounts for the gap, in both physical and chemical changes.
- **∆S = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants)**, using coefficients; units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
- **∆G = ∆H − T∆S** (derivation not required); T in kelvin.
- A reaction is **feasible when ∆G is zero or negative**.
- Temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible: set ∆G = 0, so **T = ∆H / ∆S**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Entropy, S | A measure of the disorder of a system; J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ |
| Absolute entropy | The entropy of one mole of a substance in a given state; always positive, non-zero for elements |
| Entropy change, ∆S | ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants) |
| Gibbs free-energy change, ∆G | ∆H − T∆S, in kJ mol⁻¹ |
| Feasible | Able to occur (∆G ≤ 0); says nothing about rate |

## Predicting the sign of ∆S

- Solid → liquid → gas: **positive**.
- More moles of gas produced: **positive**.
- Fewer moles of gas produced: **negative**.
- Gas dissolving in a liquid: **negative**.
- Same number of gas moles: small; calculate to be sure.

## Units: the conversion that loses most marks

- ∆S is usually given in **J** K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; ∆H in **kJ** mol⁻¹.
- Divide ∆S by 1000 before using ∆G = ∆H − T∆S.
- Example: +285 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = +0.285 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
- Temperatures: K = °C + 273.

### Worked reminder

For 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s), with S values (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹) Mg 33, O₂ 205 and MgO 27:

- ∆S = 2(27) − [2(33) + 205] = 54 − 271 = **−217 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.
- The sign is negative, as predicted: a mole of gas is used up and only solids form.
- The oxygen term is included even though O₂ is an element; leaving it out gives −12, a common wrong answer.

## The four sign combinations

| ∆H | ∆S | Feasible? | Feasibility temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| − | + | At all temperatures | None |
| + | − | At no temperature | None |
| + | + | Above T = ∆H/∆S | Becomes feasible on heating |
| − | − | Below T = ∆H/∆S | Stops being feasible on heating |

**Always** say "above" or "below" with a feasibility temperature.

## Graphs of ∆G against T

- Straight line (∆H and ∆S treated as constant).
- **Gradient = −∆S**: line slopes down if ∆S is positive, up if negative.
- **Intercept at T = 0 = ∆H**.
- **Crossing ∆G = 0** gives the feasibility temperature.
- A **kink** (change of gradient) shows that ∆S has changed, usually because a substance changes state.

## Limitations of the calculations

- ∆H and ∆S change slightly with temperature, so T = ∆H/∆S is an estimate. For water, it gives about 370 K against a measured boiling point of 373 K.
- A negative ∆G does not mean the reaction is fast: a high activation energy can make a feasible reaction very slow.

## Exam traps

- J and kJ mixed in one equation.
- °C used instead of kelvin.
- Element entropies left out (they are not zero).
- Coefficients forgotten in ∆S.
- "Feasible when ∆G is positive": wrong way round.
- Feasibility temperature given without "above" or "below".
- Gradient read as +∆S instead of −∆S.
- "Feasible, so it happens quickly": wrong.

## Quick self-test

1. Why is ∆H alone not enough to predict feasibility?
2. Predict the sign of ∆S for CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g).
3. Predict the sign of ∆S for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g).
4. Calculate ∆S for 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l). S values (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹): H₂ 131; O₂ 205; H₂O(l) 70.
5. Convert −198 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ to kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
6. MgCO₃(s) → MgO(s) + CO₂(g): ∆H = +101 kJ mol⁻¹, ∆S = +175 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Calculate ∆G at 298 K.
7. For the reaction in question 6, calculate the temperature above which it is feasible.
8. N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: ∆H = −92 kJ mol⁻¹, ∆S = −199 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Is it feasible at 500 K?
9. For the reaction in question 8, below what temperature is it feasible?
10. On a graph of ∆G against T, the line slopes upwards. What is the sign of ∆S?
11. A reaction has ∆H positive and ∆S negative. When is it feasible?
12. A reaction has ∆G = −150 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K but no reaction is seen. Suggest why.

### Answers

1. Some endothermic changes are feasible; the entropy change must also be considered.
2. **Positive**: a gas forms from a solid.
3. **Negative**: 4 moles of gas → 2 moles of gas.
4. ∆S = 2(70) − [2(131) + 205] = 140 − 467 = **−327 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.
5. **−0.198 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.
6. ∆G = 101 − (298 × 0.175) = 101 − 52.2 = **+48.9 kJ mol⁻¹** (not feasible).
7. T = 101 / 0.175 = **577 K**.
8. ∆G = −92 − 500 × (−0.199) = −92 + 99.5 = **+7.5 kJ mol⁻¹**: **not feasible**.
9. T = −92 / −0.199 = **462 K**; feasible below 462 K.
10. **Negative** (gradient = −∆S is positive).
11. **Never**: ∆G is always positive.
12. The activation energy is high, so the rate is very slow even though the reaction is feasible.
