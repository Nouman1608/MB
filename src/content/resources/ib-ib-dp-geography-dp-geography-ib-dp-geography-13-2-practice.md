## Section A

**1.** Define a tipping point in an environmental system. **[1]**

**2.** State the annual probability of a 1-in-25-year storm. **[1]**

**3.** Outline how global trade can spread an invasive species. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between an environmental risk that is global by nature and one spread by global interactions. **[2]**

**5.** Outline one reason why return periods based on past records may become out of date. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A town lies on a floodplain with a 1-in-100-year flood risk (annual probability 0.01).

**(a)** Calculate the probability of at least one flood in 25 years, using 1 - (1 - p)^n. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the probability if climate change raises the annual probability to 0.02. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what your answers suggest for people buying homes in the town. **[1]**

**7.** Explain how an environmental event in one country can create risks for people in another through trade. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why the governance of acid rain in Europe was more successful than global efforts on some other environmental risks. **[3]**

**9.** Suggest two reasons why tipping points make environmental risk harder to manage. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine the extent to which environmental risks are caused by human activity rather than physical processes. **[10]**

**11.** Evaluate the effectiveness of international cooperation in managing transboundary environmental risks. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A threshold beyond which a system shifts to a new state that is difficult or impossible to reverse [1]. **[1]**

**2.** 1 / 25 = **0.04, or 4%** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Organisms are carried accidentally in ships' ballast water, cargo or packaging [1], and released in a new environment where they have no natural predators, as with zebra mussels reaching the Great Lakes [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A risk that is global by nature affects Earth systems shared by everyone, such as climate change or ocean acidification [1]; a risk spread by global interactions starts in one place and is carried elsewhere by flows such as trade or travel, such as disease or invasive species [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Climate change and changes to land use, such as urbanisation, can increase the frequency or size of floods [1], so an event once rated 1-in-100 may now occur more often than the historical record suggests [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 0.99^25 = 0.778 [1]. Probability = 1 - 0.778 = **0.222, about 22%** [1].

**(b)** 0.98^25 = 0.603 [1]. Probability = 1 - 0.603 = **0.397, about 40%** [1].

**(c)** Homeowners face a much higher chance of flooding during typical ownership than "once a century" suggests, and this chance nearly doubles if the annual probability rises, affecting insurance costs and property values [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Environmental events such as drought or heatwaves can reduce harvests in an exporting country [1]. The government may restrict exports to protect domestic supply, as Russia did with grain in 2010 [1], so world prices rise and import-dependent countries, especially poorer households in them, face higher food costs [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Acid rain involved a relatively small number of European states with shared scientific evidence linking emissions to damage [1]. The 1979 Convention on Long-range Transboundary Air Pollution and its protocols set specific limits that could be monitored [1], whereas issues such as climate change involve almost every country, many sources and strong economic interests, making agreement and enforcement harder [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Thresholds are uncertain, so it is hard to know how close a system is to tipping [1], which makes it difficult to set safe limits for policy [1]. Changes past a tipping point may be irreversible [1], so normal approaches based on recovery after damage may not work [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines environmental risk as arising from physical processes, human activity or both [1].
- Physical processes generate hazards such as earthquakes, storms and droughts [1].
- Human activity creates exposure and vulnerability, as the pressure and release model shows [1].
- Human activity drives some risks directly, such as pollution, biodiversity loss and climate change [1].
- Human activity increases physical hazards, for example urbanisation raising flood peaks [1].
- Global interactions spread risks, such as invasive species and disease [1].
- Some risks are mainly physical in origin, but their impact depends on human factors [1].
- Uses accurate located examples, such as Haiti and Chile in 2010 or acid rain in Scandinavia [1].
- Distinguishes the cause of the hazard from the cause of the harm [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on the extent of human causation [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines transboundary environmental risk and international cooperation [1].
- Success: the 1979 convention and its protocols reduced sulphur emissions and acid rain in Europe [1].
- Success: cooperation on the Rhine improved after the 1986 Schweizerhalle spill [1].
- Success: shared monitoring builds trust and evidence [1].
- Limits: the ASEAN haze agreement has struggled to prevent repeated haze episodes [1].
- Limits: climate agreements rely on voluntary national targets [1].
- Limits: the free-rider problem weakens incentives to act [1].
- Effectiveness depends on the number of states, clarity of evidence and enforceability [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence on both sides [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that cooperation works best for regional, well-defined problems [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" answers on cooperation should state what made successful cases work, then test whether those conditions exist for harder problems.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Multiplying the annual probability by the number of years instead of using 1 - (1 - p)^n.
- Treating a return period as a guaranteed interval.
- Describing pollution crossing borders without explaining the governance response.
- Confusing the cause of a hazard with the cause of the harm it does.
