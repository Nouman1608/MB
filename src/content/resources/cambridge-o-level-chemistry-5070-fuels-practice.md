## Section A

**1.** Name the **three** fossil fuels. **[2]**

**2.** Which compound is a hydrocarbon?

A: C₂H₅OH

B: CH₃COOH

C: C₃H₈

D: CH₃Cl

**[1]**

**3.** State the name of the main constituent of natural gas. **[1]**

**4.** Name the fraction from petroleum that is used:

**(a)** as fuel in cars **[1]**

**(b)** for making roads **[1]**

**(c)** as fuel in ships and home heating systems. **[1]**

**5.** State what is meant by the term *viscosity*. **[1]**

**6.** Petroleum is described as a mixture. State what this tells you about its composition. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Describe how petroleum is separated into fractions by fractional distillation. **[4]**

**8.** The table shows three fractions, J, K and L, collected from a fractionating column. The figures are invented for practice.

| Fraction | Boiling range / °C | Approximate number of carbon atoms per molecule |
|---|---|---|
| J | 180–260 | 11–15 |
| K | below 30 | 1–4 |
| L | over 400 | over 35 |

**(a)** State which fraction is collected at the top of the column. **[1]**

**(b)** State which fraction is the least volatile. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the boiling range of L is higher than that of J. **[2]**

**(d)** Suggest the name of fraction K and give its use. **[2]**

**9.** Gasoline and lubricating oil are both fractions of petroleum.

**(a)** Compare the viscosity of the two fractions. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the property you named in (a) makes lubricating oil suitable for its use. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why gasoline's high volatility makes it suitable as a fuel for cars. **[1]**

**10.** Naphtha is a fraction of petroleum.

**(a)** State the use of naphtha. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain what this use means. **[1]**

**11.** A student writes: "The fractions collected from the fractionating column are pure compounds, and the heavier ones sink to the bottom."

Identify and correct **two** errors in this statement. **[4]**

**12.** Kerosene is collected higher up the column than diesel oil.

**(a)** State the use of each fraction. **[2]**

**(b)** Compare their chain lengths and boiling points. **[2]**

**13.** Explain, in terms of the forces between molecules, why fractions collected near the top of the column have low boiling points and low viscosity. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Coal, natural gas and petroleum: all three [2]; any two [1]. **[2]**

**2.** C [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* A, B and D all contain carbon and hydrogen, but each also contains another element (oxygen or chlorine), so none is a hydrocarbon.

**3.** Methane [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** Gasoline / petrol [1]

**(b)** Bitumen [1]

**(c)** Fuel oil [1]

**[3]**

**5.** How thick a liquid is / how slowly it flows (resistance to flow) [1]. **[1]**

**6.** It contains several (many) different hydrocarbons that are not chemically combined [1]. **[1]**

**7.** One mark each, up to 4:

- petroleum is heated so that it vaporises [1]
- the vapour enters a fractionating column which is hot at the bottom and cool at the top [1]
- vapours rise and condense at different heights, where the temperature falls below their boiling points [1]
- fractions with lower boiling points are collected higher up the column; those with higher boiling points lower down [1]

**[4]**

**8. (a)** K [1]

**(b)** L [1]

**(c)** L's molecules have longer chains than J's [1], so the forces between molecules are stronger and more energy is needed to overcome them [1].

**(d)** Refinery gas [1]; gas for heating and cooking [1].

**[6]**

*Examiner insight:* in (b), "least volatile" means the fraction that evaporates least easily, which is the one with the highest boiling range. Students who confuse volatility with viscosity often still pick L here, but for the wrong reason; in a written explanation that confusion costs the mark.

**9. (a)** Lubricating oil is more viscous than gasoline [1]

**(b)** It is thick, so it stays between moving parts and does not drain away / reduces friction [1]

**(c)** It vaporises easily, so it mixes with air and burns readily in the engine [1]

**[3]**

**10. (a)** Chemical feedstock [1]

**(b)** It is used as a raw material to make other chemicals [1]

**[2]**

**11.** Error 1: the fractions are not pure compounds [1]; each fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points [1]. Error 2: fractions do not separate by sinking according to mass [1]; they condense at different heights according to their boiling points, as the temperature falls up the column [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* "identify and correct" needs both halves for each error. Saying "they are not pure" without stating what they are instead earns only the first mark.

**12. (a)** Kerosene: jet fuel [1]; diesel oil: fuel for diesel engines [1]

**(b)** Kerosene has shorter chains [1] and lower boiling points [1] than diesel oil.

**[4]**

**13.** Molecules near the top have short chains [1]; the forces between them are weak, so little energy is needed to separate them, giving low boiling points [1]; weak forces also mean the molecules slide past one another easily, giving low viscosity [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "only" out of the definition of a hydrocarbon.
- Describing fractions as pure substances.
- Explaining separation by mass instead of boiling point.
- Reversing the temperature gradient in the column.
- Mixing up the uses of kerosene, diesel oil and fuel oil.
