## 3.2.1(a)-(c): Exothermic, endothermic and activation energy

You cannot measure enthalpy, H, itself, only the change: ∆H = H(products) − H(reactants).

- **Exothermic**: energy passes to the surroundings, which warm up. Products have less enthalpy than reactants, so ∆H is negative. Example: combustion.
- **Endothermic**: energy is taken from the surroundings, which cool down. Products have more enthalpy, so ∆H is positive. Example: thermal decomposition of a carbonate.

**Activation energy, Ea**, is the minimum energy required for a reaction to take place. This is why an exothermic reaction may still need a flame to start it.

### Worked example 1: drawing enthalpy profiles

Sketch a profile for the burning of methane, an exothermic reaction.

1. Draw a vertical axis labelled enthalpy and a horizontal axis labelled progress of reaction.
2. Draw a horizontal line for CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) on the left. Draw a lower line for CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) on the right.
3. Join them with a curve that rises to a hump before falling to the product level.
4. Draw ∆H as a downward arrow from the reactant level to the product level (∆H negative).
5. Draw Ea as an upward arrow starting at the reactant line and ending at the peak.

For an endothermic reaction the product line sits higher and the ∆H arrow points up. Ea is always measured from the reactants.

## 3.2.1(d): Standard conditions and enthalpy definitions

**Standard conditions**: 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K. The symbol ⦵ shows a value measured under standard conditions, for example ∆fH⦵.

**Standard state** means the physical state under standard conditions: H₂O(l), O₂(g), C(s).

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ∆rH, enthalpy change of reaction | The enthalpy change for the amounts in a stated equation |
| ∆fH, enthalpy change of formation | The enthalpy change, under standard conditions, for making 1 mol of a compound from its constituent elements, each in its standard state |
| ∆cH, enthalpy change of combustion | The enthalpy change when 1 mol of a substance is burned completely in oxygen, under standard conditions, all species in standard states |
| ∆neutH, enthalpy change of neutralisation | The enthalpy change when an acid and a base react to form 1 mol of water, under standard conditions, all species in standard states |

Learn the wording for ∆fH, ∆cH and ∆neutH. Two consequences: ∆fH of an element in its standard state is zero, and each definition fixes 1 mol of something, so fractions are often needed.

### Worked example 2: writing definition equations

- ∆fH of propanone: 3C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → CH₃COCH₃(l)
- ∆cH of butan-2-ol: CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃(l) + 6O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l)
- ∆neutH for sulfuric acid with sodium hydroxide: ½H₂SO₄(aq) + NaOH(aq) → ½Na₂SO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

## 3.2.1(e): Finding ∆H from experimental results

In calorimetry, the reaction changes the temperature of a known mass of water or solution. Use:

q = mc∆T

- q: energy transferred, in J
- m: mass of the water or solution heated, in g (take 1.00 cm³ of a dilute solution as 1.00 g)
- c: specific heat capacity, 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water
- ∆T: temperature change in K (a 1 °C change equals a 1 K change)

Then ∆H = −q ÷ n, with q in kJ and n the amount of the substance in the definition. A temperature rise means energy was released, hence the minus sign.

### Worked example 3: enthalpy change of combustion

A spirit burner containing butan-2-ol (M = 74.0 g mol⁻¹) is used to heat 175 g of water. Its mass falls by 0.444 g while the water warms by 13.4 K.

1. q = 175 × 4.18 × 13.4 = 9802 J = 9.802 kJ
2. n(butan-2-ol) = 0.444 ÷ 74.0 = 0.00600 mol
3. ∆cH = −9.802 ÷ 0.00600 = **−1630 kJ mol⁻¹** (3 s.f.)

The answer is less exothermic than a data value because of heat loss and incomplete combustion.

### Worked example 4: enthalpy change of neutralisation

35.0 cm³ of 1.20 mol dm⁻³ KOH(aq) is mixed with 35.0 cm³ of 1.20 mol dm⁻³ HNO₃(aq) in a polystyrene cup. The temperature rises by 8.2 K.

1. m = 35.0 + 35.0 = 70.0 g of solution
2. q = 70.0 × 4.18 × 8.2 = 2399 J = 2.399 kJ
3. n(H₂O) = 0.0350 × 1.20 = 0.0420 mol
4. ∆neutH = −2.399 ÷ 0.0420 = **−57.1 kJ mol⁻¹**

Both solutions are warmed, so m uses the total volume.

## 3.2.1(f): Average bond enthalpies

Bond enthalpy is the energy needed to break 1 mol of a given bond in gaseous molecules. Breaking bonds is endothermic; making bonds is exothermic. A bond's strength depends on its surroundings: C–H in methane is not identical to C–H in ethanol. Tables therefore give **average** values over many compounds, and the actual value in a given molecule may differ.

A reaction is **exothermic** when making new bonds releases more energy than breaking old ones takes in, and **endothermic** when the reverse is true.

∆H = Σ(energy to break the reactant bonds) − Σ(energy released forming the product bonds)

### Worked example 5: ∆H from bond enthalpies

Calculate ∆H for 2NH₃(g) + 3Cl₂(g) → N₂(g) + 6HCl(g). Use these values (kJ mol⁻¹): N–H 391, Cl–Cl 243, N≡N 945, H–Cl 432.

1. Broken: 6 N–H + 3 Cl–Cl = 6(391) + 3(243) = 2346 + 729 = 3075 kJ
2. Made: 1 N≡N + 6 H–Cl = 945 + 6(432) = 945 + 2592 = 3537 kJ
3. ∆H = 3075 − 3537 = **−462 kJ mol⁻¹**

### Worked example 6: finding a bond enthalpy

For H₂(g) + Br₂(g) → 2HBr(g), ∆H = −103 kJ mol⁻¹. H–H is 436 and Br–Br is 193 kJ mol⁻¹. Find the H–Br bond enthalpy.

1. −103 = (436 + 193) − 2x
2. 2x = 629 + 103 = 732
3. x = **366 kJ mol⁻¹**

Bond enthalpy answers differ from ∆fH-based values because averages are not exact for your molecules, and because they apply only to gases, so changes of state for liquids or solids are left out.

## 3.2.1(g): Hess' law and enthalpy cycles

Hess' law: going from the same reactants to the same products, every route gives the same total ∆H. It follows from conservation of energy (HSW2).

Draw the reaction across the top and a third box below, linked to both sides. Go the long way round: add a ∆H when you follow its arrow, subtract it when you go against it.

### Worked example 7: ∆rH from ∆cH values

Find ∆rH for C₃H₆(g) + H₂(g) → C₃H₈(g). Use ∆cH (kJ mol⁻¹): C₃H₆(g) −2058, H₂(g) −286, C₃H₈(g) −2219.

Both sides burn to the same products, 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l), so both arrows point down to that box.

∆rH = Σ∆cH(reactants) − Σ∆cH(products) = (−2058 + −286) − (−2219) = **−125 kJ mol⁻¹**

### Worked example 8: ∆rH from ∆fH values

Find ∆rH for CS₂(l) + 3O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2SO₂(g). Use ∆fH (kJ mol⁻¹): CS₂(l) +89, CO₂(g) −394, SO₂(g) −297.

Here the arrows point up from the elements, C(s) + 2S(s) + 3O₂(g), to both sides.

∆rH = Σ∆fH(products) − Σ∆fH(reactants) = (−394 + 2(−297)) − (+89 + 0) = −988 − 89 = **−1077 kJ mol⁻¹**

O₂ counts as zero. The ∆cH and ∆fH formulas run in opposite orders; drawing the cycle stops you mixing them up.

### Worked example 9: an unfamiliar cycle

Find ∆H for Na₂CO₃(s) + 10H₂O(l) → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O(s). Dissolving each solid in water gives the same solution. Use these values: dissolving anhydrous Na₂CO₃, −25.0 kJ mol⁻¹; dissolving Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, +66.0 kJ mol⁻¹.

Route: anhydrous solid → solution (−25.0), then solution → hydrated solid (the reverse of dissolving it, −66.0).

∆H = −25.0 − 66.0 = **−91.0 kJ mol⁻¹**

## 3.2.1(h): Techniques for direct and indirect determination

**Direct methods (PAG3)** measure the temperature change of the reaction you want.

- Solutions (neutralisation, or dissolving a solid for an enthalpy change of solution, 5.2.1(c)): use a lidded polystyrene cup in a beaker. Measure the volumes, or the solid's mass and the water's volume. Record the start temperature, mix, stir and read the temperature at regular intervals.
- Combustion: weigh a capped spirit burner, use it to heat a known mass of water in a metal can, stir, then reweigh. Use a draught shield.

**Temperature-time graphs (M3.1, M3.2).** Cooling starts before the reaction finishes, so the highest reading is too low. Plot temperature (y) against time (x), fit a straight line to the falling readings and extend it backwards to the moment of mixing. For example, a solution at 21.4 °C is mixed at 3 minutes. Readings at 4, 5, 6 and 7 minutes are 30.8, 30.5, 30.2 and 29.9 °C, a steady fall of 0.3 K per minute. Extrapolating back gives 31.1 °C at 3 minutes, so ∆T = 9.7 K, not 9.4 K.

**Indirect methods** combine two measurable reactions in a Hess cycle, for example worked example 9, or a thermal decomposition found from two reactions with excess acid.

**Main errors**: heat loss, ignoring the container's heat capacity, incomplete combustion (soot) and fuel evaporating from the wick.

## Common errors

- Putting the fuel's mass into q = mc∆T in place of the water's.
- Forgetting that a temperature rise gives a negative ∆H.
- Dividing by moles of acid instead of moles of water for ∆neutH.
- Using non-standard states such as H₂O(g) in a definition equation.
- Missing a bond: draw out every structure and count each bond.
