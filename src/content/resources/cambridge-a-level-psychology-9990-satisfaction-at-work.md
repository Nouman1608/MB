## 4.5.1 Herzberg's two-factor theory

Herzberg asked engineers and accountants to describe times when they had felt exceptionally good or exceptionally bad about their jobs (the critical incident technique). The events behind good feelings were different from those behind bad feelings, so he proposed that satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of one scale.

- **Hygiene factors** relate to the context of the job: pay, company policy, supervision, working conditions, relationships with colleagues and job security. If poor, they cause dissatisfaction; if good, they prevent dissatisfaction but do not create satisfaction.
- **Motivators** relate to the content of the job: achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility and advancement. These produce satisfaction and motivation.

*Application*: a pay rise may stop complaints but will not make a dull job satisfying; adding responsibility might.

*Evaluation*: the method may be biased, because people tend to credit themselves for good events (achievement) and blame others for bad ones (supervision, policy), which would produce the two-factor pattern by itself. The original sample was narrow: professional men in one region. Pay can motivate some workers, which the theory underplays.

## 4.5.1 Hackman and Oldham's job characteristics theory

Hackman and Oldham proposed that five **core job characteristics** produce three **critical psychological states**, which lead to outcomes such as high internal motivation, satisfaction and quality of work.

| Core characteristic | Meaning | Psychological state |
|---|---|---|
| Skill variety | The job uses a range of skills | Experienced meaningfulness |
| Task identity | Completing a whole, identifiable piece of work | Experienced meaningfulness |
| Task significance | The job affects other people | Experienced meaningfulness |
| Autonomy | Freedom to plan and decide how to do the work | Experienced responsibility for outcomes |
| Feedback | Clear information about performance | Knowledge of results |

The theory also proposes a **motivating potential score (MPS)**: the average of skill variety, task identity and task significance, multiplied by autonomy and by feedback. Because autonomy and feedback multiply, a job with none of either has a motivating potential of zero, however varied it is.

*Evaluation*: the model is specific and testable, and it guides practical redesign; but characteristics are usually rated by job holders themselves, so satisfied workers may simply rate their jobs more positively. Individuals differ in how much they want growth from their work.

## 4.5.1 Techniques of job design

| Technique | What changes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Job enrichment (vertical loading) | More responsibility, control and decision-making | A cleaner plans their own rota and orders supplies |
| Job enlargement (horizontal loading) | More tasks at the same level | A packer also labels and checks boxes |
| Job rotation | Moving between jobs at intervals | A shop assistant moves between till, stockroom and customer service |

Enrichment links to Herzberg's motivators and to autonomy in Hackman and Oldham. Enlargement and rotation add variety but critics describe enlargement as "more of the same". Belias and Sklikas (2013) is the syllabus example discussing these techniques.

## 4.5.2 Measuring job satisfaction

**The Job Descriptive Index (JDI)** measures satisfaction with five facets of the job: the **work itself**, **pay**, **opportunities for promotion**, **supervision** and **co-workers**. For each facet, the worker reads a list of adjectives or short phrases (such as "routine" or "fascinating") and answers "yes", "no" or "?" (cannot decide). It is quick, standardised and gives a profile across facets rather than one overall number, which helps an organisation see what to fix. Because it is self-report, answers may be affected by social desirability or by fear that managers will see them, so anonymity improves validity.

**Walton's quality of working life (QWL)** identifies eight conditions:

1. Adequate and fair compensation.
2. Safe and healthy working conditions.
3. Opportunity to use and develop human capacities.
4. Opportunity for continued growth and security.
5. Social integration in the work organisation.
6. Constitutionalism: rights such as privacy, free speech and fair treatment.
7. Work and the total life space: balance between work and the rest of life.
8. Social relevance of work life: the organisation behaves responsibly in society.

A **QWL evaluation scale** asks employees to rate how far each condition is met. Compared with the JDI, QWL takes a broader view, including work–life balance and the organisation's social responsibility.

## 4.5.3 Workplace sabotage

**Sabotage** is deliberate behaviour intended to damage an organisation's property, products, production or reputation. Methods range from slowing work down to destroying equipment.

**Key study: Giacalone and Rosenfeld (1987).**

- *Aim*: to investigate how employees justify sabotage, by examining which reasons they see as justifying it and which methods they see as justifiable.
- *Sample*: unionised factory workers.
- *Method*: a questionnaire in which workers rated how far a range of reasons would justify sabotage, and how justifiable four general methods were: slowdowns, destructiveness, dishonesty and causing chaos.
- *Results*: workers who accepted a wider range of reasons were more ready to justify every form of sabotage except dishonesty, which received little justification from anyone.
- *Conclusion*: employees often explain sabotage as a reasoned response to their situation, so it can be seen as a sign of grievances that management should address.

**Evaluation.** The study links to the situational explanation of behaviour. It measures *attitudes* about sabotage through self-report, not actual sabotage, so it may not predict behaviour. The sample came from one unionised workplace, limiting generalisation. Asking about justification rather than personal sabotage may reduce social desirability bias.

## 4.5.3 Blau and Boal's model of absenteeism and commitment

Blau and Boal combined two attitudes: **job involvement** (how much a person identifies with their job) and **organisational commitment** (how much they identify with the organisation). This gives four categories:

| Category | Job involvement | Organisational commitment |
|---|---|---|
| Institutionalised stars | High | High |
| Lone wolves | High | Low |
| Corporate citizens | Low | High |
| Apathetic employees | Low | Low |

The model predicts that the category affects how much absence occurs and why. Institutionalised stars are expected to be absent least, mainly for unavoidable reasons such as illness. Apathetic employees, with no attachment to the job or the organisation, are expected to be absent most, often by choice. Distinguishing **involuntary** absence (such as genuine illness) from **voluntary** absence (choosing not to attend) is central to applying the model.

## Annotated answer: a 4-mark application

*A bank's call centre has high staff turnover. Staff say the work is repetitive and they never see the results of their efforts. Using Hackman and Oldham's theory, suggest two changes.* [4]

"Increase task identity by letting each employee handle a customer's problem from start to finish rather than passing it on (1); this increases experienced meaningfulness (1). Give regular feedback, for example weekly reports of customers helped and satisfaction ratings (1); this gives knowledge of results, which the theory says increases satisfaction and motivation (1)."

## Common mistakes

- Saying hygiene factors produce satisfaction. They only prevent dissatisfaction.
- Listing Hackman and Oldham's characteristics without the psychological states.
- Confusing enrichment (more responsibility) with enlargement (more tasks).
- Writing that Giacalone and Rosenfeld measured actual sabotage. They measured how justifiable workers found it.
