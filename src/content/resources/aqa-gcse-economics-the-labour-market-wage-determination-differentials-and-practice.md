## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by **(a)** gross pay **[1]** and **(b)** net pay **[1]**.

**2.** The table shows the market for bakery workers in Town F (illustrative). W is the hourly wage.

| W (£) | Workers wanted | Workers willing |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | 200 | 100 |
| 14 | 180 | 130 |
| 16 | 160 | 160 |
| 18 | 140 | 190 |
| 20 | 120 | 220 |

**(a)** Use the table to find the wage at which the market clears, and the number of workers hired then. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the shortage or surplus at £20, and say which it is. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what is likely to happen to the wage if it starts at £12. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why the demand for labour is a derived demand. **[3]**

**4.** In Town F, a rise in demand for bread raises the number of bakery workers wanted by 50 at every wage. Before, demand was Qd = 320 − 10W and supply is Qs = 15W − 80.
**(a)** Calculate the new equilibrium wage and number employed. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in the hourly wage bill (wage multiplied by workers employed). **[2]**

**5.** Explain two factors that could reduce the supply of electricians in a region. **[4]**

**6.** Instead, in Town F the supply of bakery workers falls by 50 at every wage, so Qs = 15W − 130, with demand Qd = 320 − 10W. Calculate the new wage and number employed. **[3]**

**7.** A hospital porter earns £12.50 an hour. A laboratory technician earns £17.50.
**(a)** Calculate how much more, as a percentage, the technician earns than the porter. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons for the difference between the two occupations. **[4]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why a worker with 15 years' experience may be paid more than a new starter in the same job. **[4]**

**9.** A driver's basic week is 36 hours at £10 an hour. Another 8 hours are worked as overtime, paid at time and a half. Income tax is 25% of gross pay above £80, and National Insurance is 5% of gross pay.
**(a)** Calculate gross pay. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate net pay. **[3]**

**10.** A sales assistant is paid £1,400 a month plus 3% commission on sales. In one month, sales are £18,500. Total deductions are 20% of gross pay.
**(a)** Calculate gross pay. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate net pay. **[2]**
**(c)** A fixed gross salary of £1,700 a month is offered instead. Calculate the monthly sales at which the commission scheme gives the same gross pay. **[2]**
**(d)** The assistant also receives £310 net from a weekend job and £12 interest. Calculate total monthly income. **[1]**

**11.** A worker's salary rises from £24,000 to £25,200 a year. Income tax is 20% of pay above a £9,000 allowance, National Insurance is 6% of gross pay and the pension contribution is 2.5% of gross pay. Net pay before the rise was £18,960.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage rise in gross pay. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate net pay after the rise. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage rise in net pay, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**

**12.** In Town E, bus drivers earn more than cleaners. Explain why, and evaluate whether the gap is likely to last. **[9]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Total pay before any deductions are taken off [1]. **(b)** Pay left after deductions such as tax, National Insurance and pension contributions; take-home pay [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Net pay needs the idea of deductions; calling it "pay after tax" alone leaves out the other items.

**2. (a)** Wanted equals willing at £16 [1], with **160** workers [1].
**(b)** At £20, 220 − 120 = 100 [1], a **surplus** because more are willing than wanted [1].
**(c)** At £12 only 100 are willing but 200 are wanted, a shortage of 100 [1], so employers compete for workers and the wage is likely to rise towards £16 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the gap as a shortage and then saying what employers do about it is what turns a reading into an explanation.

**3.** Demand for labour comes from firms wanting workers to produce goods or services [1]. The firm only wants them because customers want the product [1]. For example, if demand for bread rises, bakeries want more bakers, and if it falls they want fewer [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An example alone is a description; the link between product demand and labour demand carries the explanation.

**4. (a)** New demand: Qd = 370 − 10W [1]. Set 370 − 10W = 15W − 80, so 450 = 25W and W = **£18** [1]. Q = 370 − 180 = **190** workers [1].
**(b)** Before: 16 × 160 = 2,560 [1]. After: 18 × 190 = 3,420, so the wage bill rises by **£860** an hour [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Write down the equation you solve. If the final figure is wrong, visible working gives something to credit.

**5.** Any two factors, each with an explanation: a long or costly training period means fewer people qualify [1], so fewer are willing at each wage [1]; better pay in a rival job such as a neighbouring region [1] draws electricians away, so supply falls [1]. Others: worse conditions, retirements, fewer migrants. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A factor named without saying why it cuts the number willing tends to earn less than one with its reason.

**6.** Set 320 − 10W = 15W − 130, so 450 = 25W [1]. W = **£18** [1]. Q = 320 − 180 = **140** workers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare with question 4: the wage is the same but employment is 50 lower, because a fall in supply and a rise in demand move employment in opposite directions.

**7. (a)** Gap = 17.50 − 12.50 = £5 [1]. 5 ÷ 12.50 × 100 = **40%** [1].
**(b)** Technician needs qualifications, so fewer people can do it and supply is lower [1], giving a higher wage [1]. The technician's work may bring in more value, or carry more responsibility, so demand for the job is stronger [1], which also pushes the wage up [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by the technician's pay instead of the porter's gives 28.6%, a common slip; the comparison base is the pay you are measuring against.

**8.** Experience makes a worker more productive [1], so the firm gains more from them and will pay more to keep them [1]. Seniority or responsibility, such as supervising others [1], justifies a higher wage and may mean fewer people can do the role [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Paid more because experienced" repeats the question; the reason is what experience does for output or supply.

**9. (a)** Basic: 36 × 10 = 360 [1]. Overtime rate 10 × 1.5 = 15, so 8 × 15 = 120 [1]. Gross = 360 + 120 = **£480** [1].
**(b)** Tax: 25% × (480 − 80) = 25% × 400 = £100 [1]. National Insurance: 5% × 480 = £24 [1]. Net = 480 − 100 − 24 = **£356** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Applying the tax rate to the whole £480 gives £120 and a net of £336; the allowance wording matters.

**10. (a)** Commission: 0.03 × 18,500 = £555 [1]. Gross = 1,400 + 555 = **£1,955** [1].
**(b)** Deductions: 0.20 × 1,955 = £391 [1]. Net = **£1,564** [1].
**(c)** Commission needed: 1,700 − 1,400 = £300 [1]. Sales = 300 ÷ 0.03 = **£10,000** [1].
**(d)** 1,564 + 310 + 12 = **£1,886** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Total income is the sum of net amounts received; adding the gross figure instead overstates what the person has.

**11. (a)** (1,200 ÷ 24,000) × 100 = **5%** [1].
**(b)** Tax: 0.20 × (25,200 − 9,000) = £3,240 [1]. National Insurance 0.06 × 25,200 = £1,512 and pension 0.025 × 25,200 = £630 [1]. Net = 25,200 − 3,240 − 1,512 − 630 = **£19,818** [1].
**(c)** Rise = 19,818 − 18,960 = £858 [1]. 858 ÷ 18,960 × 100 = **4.5%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Net rises by less than 5% because the allowance stays fixed; saying why shows understanding beyond the arithmetic.

**12.** Drivers need a licence and training, so fewer people can supply the job than cleaning [1]. Supply curve for drivers lies to the left of the cleaners' [1]. Responsibility for passengers' safety and unsocial hours may need extra pay to attract workers [1]. Demand for drivers is derived from passengers' demand for travel, and each driver may bring in more revenue [1]. Low supply with strong demand gives a higher wage [1]. Evaluation: the gap may last because training takes time and cost, so cleaners cannot quickly move [1]. It may narrow if more people train as drivers, if cleaners become scarce, or if demand for buses falls [1]. Some workers choose jobs for hours or location, not pay [1]. Judgement: likely to last while the licence needed keeps driver supply low [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to gain more credit when each cause is tied to a curve, and the closing judgement names the condition it rests on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "supply and demand" with no statement of which curve moves, or which way.
- Calling a surplus a shortage, or the reverse.
- Using the wrong base for a percentage gap.
- Applying a tax rate to the whole of gross pay when an allowance is given.
- Leaving out the overtime multiplier.
- Adding gross pay, not net pay, to find total income.
- Giving a cause of a wage gap with no link to the wage.
- Stating a conclusion in an evaluation with no reason for it.
