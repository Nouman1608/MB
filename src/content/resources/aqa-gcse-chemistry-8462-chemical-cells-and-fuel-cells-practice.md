These questions cover section **4.5.2 Chemical cells and fuel cells (chemistry only)** of the AQA GCSE
Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016. The content is assessed on Paper 1 in the
separate chemistry qualification. Questions 8 and 9 test half equations, which the specification marks
(HT only), and are labelled **Higher tier only**.

Learn the content first in the
[chemical cells and fuel cells study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemical-cells-and-fuel-cells/)

## Questions

**1.** A simple cell is made from two metals in an electrolyte.

**(a)** State what must be true about the two metals. **[1]**
**(b)** Name **one** other thing needed to complete the cell. **[1]**
**(c)** State **one** factor that affects the voltage of the cell. **[1]**

**2.** A student makes cells using different metals paired with copper in the same electrolyte.

| Metal paired with copper | Voltage / V |
|---|---|
| Magnesium | 2.7 |
| Zinc | 1.1 |
| Iron | 0.8 |
| Silver | 0.5 |

**(a)** Put magnesium, zinc and iron in order of reactivity, most reactive first, using the data. **[1]**
**(b)** Predict the voltage when a copper electrode is paired with another copper electrode. Explain your answer. **[2]**
**(c)** The student suggests silver is more reactive than copper. Explain how the data fit this idea, and what other information would help to check it. **[2]**

**3.** A torch uses three 1.5 V cells connected in series.

**(a)** Calculate the total voltage. **[1]**
**(b)** State the name given to cells connected in this way. **[1]**

**4.** An alkaline battery in a clock stops working after a year. A rechargeable battery in a phone can be used for several years.

**(a)** Explain why the alkaline battery stops working. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how the phone battery can be used again. **[2]**
**(c)** Give **one** disadvantage of rechargeable batteries. **[1]**

**5.** A hydrogen fuel cell is used to power a bus.

**(a)** Name the two substances supplied to the fuel cell. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the overall equation for the reaction in the fuel cell. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the fuel cell does not need to be recharged. **[1]**

**6.** A company is deciding whether to use hydrogen fuel cells or rechargeable batteries in its delivery vehicles.

Evaluate the use of the two types of power source. **[6]**

**7.** Explain why a hydrogen fuel cell is described as producing electricity by oxidation of hydrogen, and name the waste product at the point of use. **[2]**

**8.** (Higher tier only) The negative electrode reaction in a hydrogen fuel cell with an acidic electrolyte is 2H₂ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻.

**(a)** Explain why this is an oxidation. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the half equation for the reaction at the positive electrode. **[2]**
**(c)** Show how the two half equations combine to give the overall equation. **[2]**

**9.** (Higher tier only) In a hydrogen fuel cell with an alkaline electrolyte, the reaction at the negative electrode is 2H₂ + 4OH⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻.

**(a)** Show that this equation is balanced for atoms and for charge. **[2]**
**(b)** The positive electrode reaction is O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. Add the two equations and write the overall equation. **[2]**

**10.** A student builds a simple cell using zinc and copper in salt solution, and measures 1.1 V. The student wants a cell that gives about 2.7 V.

**(a)** Suggest a change to the metals to increase the voltage. **[1]**
**(b)** Suggest another way to increase the total voltage. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The metals must be **different** [1]
**(b)** An **electrolyte** (or a wire / voltmeter connecting the electrodes) [1]
**(c)** Any one: type of electrode (metals); type of electrolyte; difference in reactivity of the metals [1]

**2. (a)** **Magnesium, zinc, iron** [1] (the voltage falls as the metal gets less reactive)
**(b)** **0 V** [1], because the electrodes are the **same metal** so there is no difference in reactivity [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A cell gives a voltage, which suggests a difference in reactivity between silver and copper [1]; but the data cannot show **which** is more reactive; need reaction data such as displacement reactions or the **reactivity series** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Silver is actually less reactive than copper. The voltage alone shows only that the metals differ.

**3. (a)** 3 × 1.5 = **4.5 V** [1]
**(b)** A **battery** [1]

**4. (a)** One of the reactants has been **used up**, so the reaction stops [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** An **external electrical current** is supplied [1], which **reverses** the chemical reactions, remaking the reactants [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any one: wear out after many cycles; need a charger / take time to charge; metals used need careful disposal [1]. **[1]**

**5. (a)** **Hydrogen** [1] and **oxygen (or air)** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O [formulae 1; balanced 1] **[2]**
**(c)** It is **supplied with fuel from outside**, so the reaction continues as long as fuel is provided [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Level of response, one mark per point up to six, including at least one point on each option and a conclusion:
- fuel cell is refuelled quickly [1]
- only water produced at the point of use [1]
- hydrogen is flammable / hard to store / few filling stations [1]
- hydrogen is often made using fossil fuels, so there may be carbon dioxide emissions [1]
- rechargeable batteries are charged from the grid and the technology is established [1]
- batteries take hours to charge / limited range / wear out and need disposal [1]
- a conclusion with a reason, such as fuel cells for long routes with quick turnaround, batteries for short routes with overnight charging [1]
(maximum **[6]**)

**7.** Hydrogen is **oxidised** electrochemically [1] (it reacts with oxygen / loses electrons) and the product is **water** [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Hydrogen **loses electrons** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O [species correct 1; balanced 1] **[2]**
**(c)** Add: 2H₂ + O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + 2H₂O [1]; the 4H⁺ and 4e⁻ appear on both sides and cancel, leaving **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O** [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** Atoms: left H = 4 + 4 = 8 and O = 4; right H = 8 and O = 4 [1]. Charge: left 4− (from 4OH⁻), right 4− (from 4e⁻) [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Sum: 2H₂ + 4OH⁻ + O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻ + 4OH⁻ [1]. Cancel 4OH⁻, 4e⁻ and 2H₂O: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O** [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** Use **magnesium** instead of zinc (with copper) [1]; a bigger reactivity difference gives a larger voltage. **[1]**
**(b)** Connect **several cells in series** (a battery) [1]; the voltages add up, for example two 1.1 V cells give 2.2 V [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the battery has run out of energy" instead of saying a reactant is used up.
- Describing a fuel cell as burning hydrogen.
- Evaluations that list only advantages, or give no conclusion.
- Balancing half equations for atoms but forgetting charge.
- Treating the voltage data as proof of order without comparing with the reactivity series.
