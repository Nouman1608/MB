## Organisation of the nervous system (5.1.5(g))

| Structural | Made of |
|---|---|
| Central nervous system (CNS) | Brain plus spinal cord |
| Peripheral nervous system (PNS) | Nerves outside the CNS (sensory in, motor out) |

| Functional (within PNS) | Somatic | Autonomic |
|---|---|---|
| Effector | Skeletal muscle | Cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, glands |
| Control | Voluntary | Involuntary |
| Pathway | One motor neurone | Two neurones, ganglion between |
| Divisions | None | Sympathetic (activity) and parasympathetic (rest) |

## The brain (5.1.5(h))

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Cerebrum | Thought, memory, language, sensory interpretation, voluntary movement |
| Cerebellum | Movement coordination, balance, posture |
| Medulla oblongata | Heart rate, breathing rate, blood pressure (autonomic) |
| Hypothalamus | Monitors blood composition and temperature; controls the pituitary |
| Pituitary gland | Releases hormones (anterior and posterior lobes differ) |

## Reflexes (5.1.5(i))

Reflex arc order: stimulus, receptor, sensory neurone, then a relay neurone in most reflexes (absent in the knee jerk), motor neurone, effector.

**Knee jerk method in steps**

1. A tap on the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps.
2. Stretch receptors fire and impulses enter the spinal cord along a sensory neurone.
3. A direct synapse with a motor neurone (monosynaptic).
4. The motor neurone stimulates the quadriceps, which contracts.
5. The leg extends.

**Survival value:** fast (few synapses, no conscious decision), automatic, innate, protective, and helps maintain posture and balance.

**Reflex versus voluntary:** a reflex is involuntary, fast and stereotyped; a voluntary response involves the cerebrum, is slower and can be varied.

## Nervous and hormonal coordination (5.1.5(j))

| | Nervous | Hormonal |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Impulse, transmitter | Hormone in blood |
| Speed | Fast | Slower |
| Duration | Short | Longer |
| Target | Specific | Widespread |

**Fight or flight:** threat, hypothalamus, sympathetic nerves, adrenal glands release adrenaline. Effects: higher heart rate and stroke volume, deeper breathing, wider pupils, glycogen to glucose, blood diverted to muscles.

**Cell signalling in outline**

1. Adrenaline (first messenger) binds to a surface receptor.
2. Adenylyl cyclase is activated.
3. Adenylyl cyclase makes cAMP, the second messenger, from ATP.
4. cAMP then triggers enzymes within the cell and the response follows, with amplification.

## Heart rate (5.1.5(k))

| Factor | Pathway | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Sympathetic nerve | Medulla, then accelerator nerve to SAN, noradrenaline | Faster |
| Parasympathetic (vagus) | Medulla, then vagus nerve to SAN, acetylcholine | Slower |
| Adrenaline | Blood to SAN | Faster |
| Raised blood carbon dioxide (lower pH) | Chemoreceptors to medulla | Faster |

The SAN is the pacemaker: the heart is myogenic.

**Rate from a pulse count:** beats counted ÷ time (s) × 60. A count of 25 beats in 20 s is 25 × 3 = 75 per minute. **Rate from an ECG:** 60 ÷ time between R peaks (s).

## Muscle (5.1.5(l))

| | Skeletal | Involuntary (smooth) | Cardiac |
|---|---|---|---|
| Striated | Yes | No | Yes |
| Nuclei | Many | One | Usually one |
| Shape | Long, unbranched | Spindle | Branched, intercalated discs |
| Fatigue | Yes | Slowly | Does not |

**Sarcomere regions:** A band = full myosin length (constant on contraction). I band = actin only (shortens). H zone = myosin only (shortens). Sarcomere = A band + I band.

**Skeletal muscle fibre features:** many nuclei, sarcolemma folded into T tubules, sarcoplasmic reticulum storing calcium ions, many mitochondria, and myofibrils made of actin (thin, with troponin and tropomyosin) and myosin (thick, with heads that act as ATPase).

**Reading a stained section or photomicrograph**

| You see | It is |
|---|---|
| Long parallel fibres, regular cross-striations, several nuclei at the edge | Skeletal muscle, longitudinal section |
| Rounded polygons packed together, nuclei at the edge | Skeletal muscle, transverse section |
| Branched fibres with dark lines across them, one central nucleus | Cardiac muscle (the dark lines are intercalated discs) |
| Short spindle-shaped cells, no striations, one central nucleus | Involuntary (smooth) muscle |

Under a light microscope, draw what you see, label the nuclei and striations, and show a scale bar or magnification.

**Sarcomere reminder:** with an A band of 1.5 µm and thin filaments of 0.9 µm, a sarcomere of 2.4 µm has an I band of 2.4 − 1.5 = 0.9 µm and an H zone of 2.4 − 1.8 = 0.6 µm. When the sarcomere shortens to 1.8 µm, the I band is 0.3 µm and the H zone is 0 µm.

**Neuromuscular junction in steps**

1. Action potential opens calcium channels in the neurone terminal.
2. Vesicles release acetylcholine.
3. It binds to receptors on the sarcolemma, causing depolarisation.
4. Acetylcholinesterase removes it.

**Sliding filament in steps**

1. Calcium ions flood out of the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
2. They attach to troponin, and tropomyosin shifts.
3. Myosin heads latch onto actin as cross-bridges.
4. The power stroke pulls actin inwards.
5. ATP binds, the head detaches, and ATP hydrolysis resets it.

**ATP supply:** respiration; creatine phosphate donates phosphate to ADP, giving ATP quickly but only for seconds.

**Worked reminders**

- Actual length = image length ÷ magnification. At ×5000, 12 mm on the image is 12 ÷ 5000 = 0.0024 mm = 2.4 µm.
- Magnification from a scale bar: a bar of 3 µm (0.003 mm) drawn 45 mm long gives 45 ÷ 0.003 = ×15 000.
- Percentage change = change ÷ original × 100: a pulse from 70 to 133 is (133 − 70) ÷ 70 × 100 = 90%.
- Creatine phosphate alone: 12 mmol at 3 mmol per second lasts 12 ÷ 3 = 4 s.

## Monitoring heart rate: practical reminders

- Measure pulse at rest, immediately after exercise and at set times during recovery; count for at least 15 s and scale up.
- Keep exercise type, duration and intensity the same, use the same person and repeat to find a mean.
- An ECG sensor or heart rate monitor gives a continuous trace and removes counting error.
- Use healthy, consenting volunteers who may stop at any time.
- The recovery time is the time taken for the rate to return to the resting value.

## Extended answer frame: sliding filament

Write it in this order: action potential, calcium ion release, troponin and tropomyosin, cross-bridge, power stroke, ATP and detachment, repeat. Then state what happens to the A band, I band and H zone. Mention the source of ATP (respiration and creatine phosphate) if the question asks about sustained or sudden activity.

## Short answer reminders

- Why is a reflex quicker than a voluntary action? Fewer synapses, no processing in the cerebrum, and a short pathway.
- Why does adrenaline need a receptor? Target cells respond only if they have the receptor for that hormone.
- Why can the heart beat without nerves? The SAN is myogenic; nerves and hormones only change the rate.
- Why do muscle fibres have many mitochondria? Contraction and calcium pumping use large amounts of ATP.
- Why is the muscle fibre multinucleate? It is a long cell formed by fused cells, so many nuclei control protein synthesis along its length.

## Must-know distinctions

- Somatic (skeletal muscle, voluntary) versus autonomic (smooth and cardiac muscle, glands).
- Sympathetic (noradrenaline, speeds heart) versus parasympathetic (acetylcholine, slows heart).
- First messenger (adrenaline, outside) versus second messenger (cAMP, inside).
- Cerebrum (thought) versus cerebellum (coordination, balance).
- Filaments slide; they do not shorten.

## Quick self-test

1. State the two parts of the central nervous system.
2. Which part of the autonomic nervous system speeds the heart?
3. Name the brain region that coordinates movement and balance.
4. Why is the knee jerk faster than a response that needs thought?
5. Name the second messenger that adrenaline causes to be made.
6. Which part of a sarcomere stays the same length during contraction?
7. What is the role of calcium ions in contraction?
8. What does creatine phosphate do?
9. A 20 s count gives 25 beats. What is the rate per minute?
10. Which structure lies between the neurone and the muscle fibre at a neuromuscular junction?
11. Which enzyme ends the stimulation of a muscle fibre by acetylcholine?
12. A heart rate rises from 64 to 144 beats per minute. What is the percentage increase?

### Answers

1. Brain and spinal cord.
2. The sympathetic division (accelerator nerve).
3. Cerebellum.
4. Few synapses, no conscious processing and a short pathway.
5. Cyclic AMP (cAMP).
6. The A band.
7. They bind to troponin, which moves tropomyosin and exposes the binding sites on actin.
8. It gives its phosphate to ADP to make ATP rapidly.
9. 25 × 3 = 75 beats per minute.
10. The synaptic cleft (with acetylcholine crossing it).
11. Acetylcholinesterase.
12. (144 − 64) ÷ 64 × 100 = 125%.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the brain" or "the nervous system" where a named region or division is needed.
- Saying the medulla "controls the heart" without saying it changes the rate through the SAN.
- Describing the knee jerk with a relay neurone, when it is monosynaptic.
- Saying adrenaline "enters the cell" or that cAMP is the first messenger.
- Writing that filaments or the A band shorten.
- Leaving out ATP for detachment of myosin and for pumping calcium ions back.
- Forgetting units, or not converting mm to µm before using the magnification.
- Giving percentage change relative to the new value instead of the original.
