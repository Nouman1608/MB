These questions cover sections 1.1 and 1.2 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, version 2.0, first assessment 2019): economic groups, factors of production, scarcity and opportunity cost. J205 has no tiers. Every figure is made up. Start with the [guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-economic-groups-factors-of-production-scarcity-and/) or the [revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-economic-groups-factors-of-production-scarcity-and-revision-notes/) first, and see the [course hub](/boards/ocr/gcse/economics/) and [checklist](/checklists/ocr/gcse/economics/).

## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by scarce resources. **[2]**

**2.** A student has £40 and ranks her options: concert ticket (£40), trainers (£35), computer game (£25). She buys the ticket. Identify her opportunity cost and explain why it is not the trainers and the game together. **[2]**

**3.** A small farm in Country A uses a field, a tractor, two farmhands and an owner who risked her savings to start it. Identify the factor of production each represents: **(a)** the field; **(b)** the tractor; **(c)** the farmhands; **(d)** the owner. **[4]**

**4.** Country B can produce bricks and timber using all its resources, as shown.

| Option | Bricks (millions) | Timber (thousand tonnes) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 90 |
| B | 20 | 80 |
| C | 40 | 64 |
| D | 60 | 40 |
| E | 80 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost, in thousand tonnes of timber, of increasing bricks from 40 to 60 million. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of each extra million bricks over that change. **[1]**

**(c)** Using the table, explain what happens to the opportunity cost of bricks as output rises. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how producers and the government are interdependent. **[4]**

**6.** A tea estate owner compares two harvesting methods. Pickers earn £70 a day.

| | Method 1: by hand | Method 2: cutting machine |
|---|---|---|
| Workers | 10 | 4 |
| Equipment cost per day | £40 | £440 |
| Output per day | 5 tonnes | 8 tonnes |

**(a)** Calculate the output per worker for each method. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the cost per tonne for each method. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one reason the owner might still choose Method 1. **[2]**

**7.** A government builds a new clinic for an ageing population. Explain how this decision involves the questions of what, how and for whom goods and services should be produced. **[6]**

**8.** A laptop costs £250 before a sales tax of 12%.

**(a)** Calculate the price a consumer pays. **[2]**

**(b)** 400 laptops are sold. Calculate the tax revenue. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain one way the government's spending of this revenue could benefit producers. **[2]**

**9.** Explain, using an example, how opportunity cost differs from the total cost of a choice. **[3]**

**10.** Country C's government is deciding whether to allow a larger fishing fleet. At present the catch is 40,000 tonnes a year. With the larger fleet it would be 55,000 tonnes a year for 5 years, then 25,000 tonnes a year for the next 5 years as fish stocks fall. Evaluate whether the government should allow the larger fleet, referring to economic, social and environmental sustainability. **[9]**

**11.** A council can turn a derelict site into a car park or a community garden. The car park costs £360,000, has 120 spaces, and earns £3 per space per day for 300 days a year. The garden costs £60,000 and earns nothing. Evaluate which the council should choose. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Resources (land, labour, capital, enterprise) are limited in supply [1], so they cannot produce everything people want, because wants are unlimited [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A good answer says limited relative to wants; "there is not much of them" alone leaves the contrast unstated.

**2.** Her opportunity cost is the trainers [1], the next best alternative. She could only spend the £40 once, so she would not have had the game as well; only the single best alternative given up counts [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the item is not enough for the second point; show you understand why the others are excluded.

**3.** **(a)** Land [1]. **(b)** Capital [1]. **(c)** Labour [1]. **(d)** Enterprise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Take care with the tractor: it is capital, not money, and it is man-made.

**4.** **(a)** Timber falls from 64 to 40 [1], so the opportunity cost is **24 thousand tonnes** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 24 ÷ 20 = **1.2 thousand tonnes of timber per million bricks** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Cost per tonne is 0.5 (A to B), 0.8, 1.2, then 2.0 [1]: it rises, because later resources are less suited to making bricks [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* When a question says use the table, quote its figures. Include units in each calculation.

**5.** Producers pay taxes to the government [1], which funds roads and training that producers use [1]. The government depends on producers for tax revenue and for jobs that raise incomes [1]. The government makes laws, such as safety rules, that producers must follow, while their output supports the economy [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show a two-way link; describing only what the government does for firms gives one direction only.

**6.** **(a)** Method 1: 5 ÷ 10 = **0.5 tonnes** [1]; Method 2: 8 ÷ 4 = **2.0 tonnes** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Method 1: (10 × £70 + £40) ÷ 5 = £740 ÷ 5 = **£148** [1]; Method 2: (4 × £70 + £440) ÷ 8 = £720 ÷ 8 = **£90** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Demand may be uncertain [1], so the machine's high daily cost is a risk if fewer tonnes are needed [1]. Other reasons, such as keeping local jobs, also work if developed. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the total cost before dividing; a bare answer gives the reader nothing to follow if one figure is slightly wrong.

**7.** What: the government chooses health care instead of other services [1], so it gives up another use of its limited funds [1]. How: it chooses methods, for example more nurses (labour) or more scanners (capital) [1], to fit its budget [1]. For whom: the clinic is aimed at older people [1], and who benefits depends on how it is funded and its location [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply each question to the clinic; definitions of what, how and for whom earn little without the context.

**8.** **(a)** Tax = 12% × £250 = £30 [1]; price = **£280** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 400 × £30 = **£12,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** It could fund training courses [1], which give producers more skilled workers [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish the chain of reasoning; "it benefits producers" with no link is only an assertion, so spell out how.

**9.** The opportunity cost is the benefit of the best alternative not chosen [1]. For example, a firm spends £50,000 on a van, so the total cost is £50,000 [1], but the opportunity cost is the best other use of that money, such as a machine [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An example without the contrast between the two ideas does not complete the explanation.

**10.** Larger fleet: 5 × 55,000 + 5 × 25,000 = 400,000 tonnes [1]. Present catch: 10 × 40,000 = 400,000 tonnes, so there is no gain in total [1]. Economic: income and jobs rise early, but the extra boats cost money and later income falls, so it is not economically sustainable [2]. Social: fishing communities face job insecurity once catches fall [1]. Environmental: stocks decline and future generations inherit fewer fish [2]. Judgement: it should not be allowed without limits, because the timing of the benefits is poor and the total catch is the same [1]; a quota would address this [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data, then weigh the effects against each other; a list of pros and cons with no conclusion cannot show evaluation.

**11.** Car park income = 120 × £3 × 300 = £108,000 a year [1]. Payback = £360,000 ÷ £108,000 = 3.3 years [1]. The garden costs £300,000 less but earns nothing [1]. Economic: the car park gives lasting income [1]. Social: the garden gives recreation and health benefits [1]. Environmental: it keeps green space; cars add pollution [1]. Opportunity cost: the option not chosen [1]. Judgement: the garden if the council values well-being over revenue, or the car park if it needs income; justify which [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach a decision and say what it depends on; both options can be defended if you justify the choice.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining capital as money.
- Naming several alternatives as the opportunity cost.
- Forgetting units or the working in a calculation.
- Describing a shortage of one product as scarcity.
- Writing about a group's role but not the link to another.
- Listing costs and benefits without a judgement.
- Mentioning sustainability without saying which type.
- Ignoring the context given in the question.
