The specification flags two misconceptions here: misreading rate graphs, and thinking catalysts run out or get used up.

## What "rate" means

The **rate of reaction** is how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. You find it by measuring an amount that changes and dividing by the time taken.

| What you measure | Rate calculation | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Volume of gas made | volume ÷ time | cm³/s |
| Mass lost from the flask | mass ÷ time | g/s |
| Time for a fixed change (cross disappears, ribbon dissolves) | 1 ÷ time | s⁻¹ (also written /s) |

Change ÷ time gives a **mean rate** over that interval.

## Measuring rate in practice (C5.2a, PAG C8)

Pick a method that tracks something that changes in the reaction you are given.

1. **Collect the gas.** Use a gas syringe, or an upturned measuring cylinder or burette full of water. Read the volume at regular times.
2. **Measure mass loss.** Stand the flask on a balance with a loose cotton wool plug in the neck. The plug lets gas out but keeps acid spray in.
3. **Time a colour change or a precipitate.** In the disappearing cross experiment, sodium thiosulfate solution reacts with hydrochloric acid and the mixture clouds as sulfur, a pale yellow solid, precipitates. You time it until a cross drawn beneath the flask can no longer be seen.
4. **Time a solid disappearing.** Time how long a measured length of magnesium ribbon takes to react completely.

### Worked example 1: choosing and using a method (C5.2a, CM5.2i)

A student wants to follow the reaction of magnesium ribbon with dilute hydrochloric acid.

*Why is mass loss a poor choice?* Hydrogen is very light: 0.10 g of magnesium makes only about 0.008 g of it, too little for a school balance to track well. A gas syringe is better.

The syringe collects 66 cm³ of hydrogen in the first 24 s.
Mean rate = 66 ÷ 24 = **2.75 cm³/s**.

Carbon dioxide from marble chips and acid is much heavier, so mass loss works there. A fall of 0.84 g in 120 s gives 0.84 ÷ 120 = **0.007 g/s**.

**Fair test.** Change one variable only; keep acid volume and concentration, solid mass and size, and temperature fixed. Repeat, discard anomalies and take a mean.

## Interpreting rate graphs (C5.2b, CM5.2ii, CM5.2iii)

Plot time along the horizontal axis and the amount of product (or flask mass) up the vertical axis.

- The **steeper** the line, the **faster** the reaction.
- The curve is steepest at the start and gets less steep as reactants are used up.
- The line goes **flat** when one reactant has run out: the reaction has stopped.
- The final height (total product) depends on the amount of limiting reactant, not on the rate.

The **rate at one moment** is the gradient of a **tangent**: a straight line that just touches the curve at that time. Pick two points far apart on it and calculate rise ÷ run.

### Worked example 2: reading a gas volume curve

Marble chips react with excess dilute hydrochloric acid, and a gas syringe collects the CO₂ given off.

| Time (s) | 0 | 25 | 50 | 75 | 100 | 125 | 150 | 175 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of CO₂ (cm³) | 0 | 30 | 50 | 63 | 72 | 77 | 80 | 80 |

(a) *When did the reaction stop?* The volume first stops changing at **150 s**.

(b) *Mean rate over the first 50 s:* 50 ÷ 50 = **1.0 cm³/s**.

(c) *Mean rate from 50 s to 100 s:* (72 − 50) ÷ 50 = 22 ÷ 50 = **0.44 cm³/s**. The rate falls as reactants are used up.

(d) *Rate at 50 s.* A tangent drawn at 50 s passes through (0 s, 18 cm³) and (100 s, 82 cm³).
Gradient = (82 − 18) ÷ (100 − 0) = 64 ÷ 100 = **0.64 cm³/s**.

### 1/t as a measure of rate

When every run ends at the same fixed point (cross gone, ribbon gone), rate is proportional to 1/t. Halving the time doubles the rate.

### Worked example 3: proportionality from the disappearing cross (CM5.2iv)

Different volumes of sodium thiosulfate solution are made up to 50 cm³ with water, so the volume of thiosulfate is a measure of its concentration. The same volume of acid is added each time.

| Thiosulfate (cm³) | 50 | 40 | 30 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time for cross to vanish (s) | 36 | 45 | 60 | 90 |
| 1/t (s⁻¹) | 0.0278 | 0.0222 | 0.0167 | 0.0111 |

Going from 20 cm³ to 40 cm³ doubles the concentration. The time halves from 90 s to 45 s, so 1/t doubles from 0.0111 to 0.0222 s⁻¹. 1/t ÷ volume is about 0.00056 every time, so the rate is **directly proportional** to concentration: a graph of 1/t against volume is a straight line through the origin.

## Factors that change the rate (C5.2c, C5.2d)

Particles must **collide** with at least the **activation energy** (the minimum energy needed) to react. More frequent successful collisions mean a faster reaction.

| Change | Effect on rate | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Higher **temperature** | Increases | Particles move faster, so collisions are more frequent **and** a bigger fraction have at least the activation energy. |
| Higher **concentration** (solutions) | Increases | Reacting particles are more crowded, so they collide more often |
| Higher **pressure** (gases) | Increases | Gas particles squeezed into a smaller volume, so more frequent collisions |
| Larger **surface area** (smaller pieces) | Increases | More solid particles exposed, so more frequent collisions |

Of the four, only temperature alters collision **energy** as well as **frequency**, so a temperature answer must mention activation energy.

The suggested practical reacts magnesium with acid warmed in a water bath to different temperatures, timing each run.

## Surface area to volume ratio (C5.2e, CM5.2iv)

Only surface particles of a solid can be hit. Cutting a lump up keeps the volume but exposes more surface, so the **surface area to volume ratio**, and the rate, go up.

### Worked example 4: one cube or many

A marble cube has sides of 6 mm.
Surface area = 6 faces × 6 mm × 6 mm = 216 mm²; volume = 6 × 6 × 6 = 216 mm³.
Ratio = 216 : 216 = **1 : 1**.

Cut it into 2 mm cubes. There are 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 small cubes.
Each has surface area 6 × 2 × 2 = 24 mm², so the total is 27 × 24 = 648 mm². The volume is still 216 mm³.
Ratio = 648 : 216 = **3 : 1**.

The ratio has tripled, so more particles are exposed and the initial rate is greater. A powder is fastest. The suggested practical compares marble chip sizes with hydrochloric acid at the same total mass.

## Catalysts (C5.2f, C5.2g)

A **catalyst** speeds up a reaction and is still there, unchanged, when the reaction ends. Its characteristics:

- It speeds up the reaction.
- It is chemically unchanged, and the same mass is left, at the end.
- It does not change the amount of product.
- Only a small amount is needed, because it is not used up.
- It is specific: a catalyst that works for one reaction may do nothing for another.

**Identifying a catalyst** needs two pieces of evidence: the reaction is faster with it, **and** the same mass is recovered at the end.

### Worked example 5: which black powder is a catalyst? (C5.2g)

Hydrogen peroxide slowly decomposes to water and oxygen; the specification suggests testing black powders, including manganese(IV) oxide, with it. A student stirs 0.40 g of a powder into peroxide solution, times how long the mixture takes to give 20 cm³ of oxygen, then filters, dries and reweighs the powder. Each powder is tested on fresh solution from one bottle.

| Powder | Time for 20 cm³ of oxygen (s) | Mass recovered (g) |
|---|---|---|
| None | over 600 | – |
| A | 18 | 0.40 |
| B | over 600 | 0.40 |
| C | 75 | 0.33 |

- **A is a catalyst**: 20 cm³ in 18 s instead of over 600 s, and all 0.40 g recovered.
- **B is not a catalyst for this reaction**: it is no faster than no powder at all.
- **C is not acting as a catalyst**: it speeds things up, but 0.07 g of it has been used up, so it is reacting.

Likewise, copper powder makes zinc react faster with dilute sulfuric acid and is recovered unchanged.

## How catalysts work (C5.2h)

A catalyst gives the reaction an **alternative pathway** whose activation energy is lower. Without any change in temperature, a larger share of collisions now clear the energy barrier.

On a **reaction profile** (energy on the y-axis, progress of reaction on the x-axis):

- the reactant and product levels stay exactly where they were, so the overall energy change is the same;
- the catalysed hump is lower than the uncatalysed hump;
- activation energy is the height of the hump measured upwards from the **reactants' energy**.

**Worked example 6.** Uncatalysed activation energy 75 kJ/mol, catalysed 58 kJ/mol: the catalyst lowers it by 75 − 58 = **17 kJ/mol**. The energy change is unaffected.

## Enzymes (C5.2i)

**Enzymes** are catalysts in biological systems: they speed up reactions in living cells without being used up. Catalase, for example, speeds up the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide in cells.

## Common errors

- Saying a flat graph means "the reaction is at a constant rate". Flat means it has **stopped**.
- Reading tangent points off the curve instead of the tangent line.
- Thinking a longer time means a faster reaction; rate is proportional to **1/t**.
- Writing "more collisions" instead of "more frequent collisions".
- Explaining temperature without activation energy.
- Claiming a catalyst energises the particles. What it actually does is lower the barrier.
- Saying a catalyst is used up or changes the amount of product.
- Calling a substance a catalyst because the rate went up, without checking that its mass is unchanged.
- Saying smaller pieces have less surface area. Total surface area goes **up**.
