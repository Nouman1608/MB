These questions cover content point **3.2.1.4 Demographic changes - migration and diasporas** of OxfordAQA International A-level Sociology (9690), Version 1.1, assessed in the Unit 3 People and development exam. All people, places and figures in the items are fictional.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by an internally displaced person. **[2]**

**2.** Identify two types of migrant, other than refugees, named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by chain migration. **[3]**

**4.** Outline two ways in which borders, passports and visas create inequalities in mobility. **[4]**

**5.** Outline two ways in which a diaspora may affect development in its country of origin. **[4]**

**6.** Study the table, which gives fictional data for the fictional country of Pellar.

| Indicator (fictional) | 2005 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Internal migrants moving to cities in the previous five years (thousands) | 900 | 1,800 |
| Citizens emigrating abroad in the previous five years (thousands) | 150 | 240 |
| Share of emigrants who are women (%) | 28 | 52 |

**(a)** Calculate how many times larger internal migration was than emigration in 2025. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the table, explain how the data support two claims made by sociologists of migration. **[4]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> Lena, a fictional software designer from a rich country, has spent two years working online from cafés in the fictional city of Port Amari. She entered on a special remote-work visa and plans to move on next year. Joseph, a fictional builder from a neighbouring poor country, also lives in Port Amari. His work visa expired a year ago, so he now works without papers. His employer has not paid him for six weeks, but Joseph is afraid to complain to the authorities.

**(a)** Identify two differences between Lena's and Joseph's situations. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item and Bauman's ideas, analyse how mobility is shaped by inequality. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why climate change does not always lead to more migration. **[4]**

**9.** Outline three explanations of migration other than push and pull. **[6]**

**10.** Analyse how transnational networks have changed the experience of living in a diaspora. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that states, rather than individual choices, decide who migrates. **[12]**

**12.** To what extent are diasporas a positive force for development in their countries of origin? Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. **[16]**

## Answers

Extended answers (Questions 10, 11 and 12) are shown as indicative creditworthy points. The real exam marks extended responses with levels of response, judging the answer as a whole.

### Section A

**1.** A person forced to leave their home, for example by conflict or disaster [1], who stays within their own country and so is not a refugee in law [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: economic migrants [1]; undocumented workers [1]; digital nomads. **[2]**

**3.** Migration that follows earlier migrants from the same family or community [1], who provide information, housing or job contacts [1], so that flows continue along the same routes over time [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Citizens of rich countries can enter many states without a visa, while citizens of poorer or conflict-affected states need visas almost everywhere [1], so the right to move depends on where you were born, not on need [1]. States rank migrants by skill and wealth, offering easier visas to professionals and investors [1], while low-wage workers face stricter controls or tied sponsorship systems such as kafala [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Remittances provide income for families at home [1], paying for schooling, health care or housing and reducing household poverty [1]. Diaspora members may invest in businesses or return with skills and contacts [1], transferring knowledge and capital back to the country of origin (brain gain) [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** 1,800 / 240 [1] = 7.5 times larger [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Internal migration (1,800,000) was far greater than emigration (240,000) [1], supporting the claim that most migration is internal rather than international [1]. The share of emigrants who are women rose from 28% to 52% [1], supporting Castles and Miller's idea of the feminisation of migration [1]. **[4]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Any two: Lena has legal status and Joseph does not [1]; Lena comes from a rich country and Joseph from a poor one [1]; Lena chooses to move on while Joseph's options are limited; Lena works online while Joseph does manual work. **[2]**
**(b)** Lena fits Bauman's "tourist": she moves by choice [1], and her passport and skills give her a special visa, showing that mobility follows global privilege [1]. Joseph fits the "vagabond": he moved out of need [1], and once his visa expired he became undocumented, showing how state rules turn the same worker from legal to illegal [1]. His fear of complaining about unpaid wages shows that lack of status increases exploitation [1], so borders create unequal power between workers and employers, not just unequal travel [1]. **[6]**

**8.** The poorest households may lack money or contacts to move when harvests fail [1], so they become trapped populations who stay in danger (Foresight report, 2011) [1]. Sudden disasters often cause short, temporary displacement [1], with people returning to rebuild rather than migrating permanently [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Household strategy: families place one member in another labour market to spread risk [1], so remittances rather than individual wages drive the decision [1]. Network approach: earlier migrants lower costs for later ones [1], producing chain migration along established routes [1]. Structural or world-systems views: capitalism uproots people in poorer regions and global cities demand cheap labour (Sassen) [1], so migration links unequal regions of the world economy [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Indicative content:
- Glick Schiller and colleagues: transmigrants build social fields across countries [1].
- Video calls and messaging let families share daily life across distance [1].
- Cheap money transfer makes remittances regular and fast [1].
- Home-town associations and online groups organise community projects [1].
- Diaspora members take part in homeland politics and campaigns [1].
- Hall: identity becomes hybrid, remade through memory and mixing [1].
- Counter: later generations may weaken ties and identify with the settlement society [1].
- Analysis: networks make diaspora a continuing relationship, not a one-way departure [1].
**[8]**

**11.** Indicative content:
- Torpey: states monopolise the legitimate means of movement [1].
- Visa and passport rules allow some citizens to move freely and block others [1].
- States recruit labour through schemes, such as Gulf sponsorship systems [1].
- States rank migrants by skill and wealth [1].
- The refugee definition is set by states through the 1951 Convention [1].
- Application: own-society example of a visa or recruitment rule [1].
- Counter: push-pull (Lee) stresses individual weighing of costs and benefits [1].
- Household strategies show families decide who goes [1].
- Networks shape routes even where states restrict entry [1].
- Undocumented migration shows people move despite states [1].
- Structural views place capitalism, not only states, behind migration [1].
- Judgement: states set the boundaries; families and networks decide within them [1].
**[12]**

**12.** Indicative content:
- Define a diaspora as a dispersed population that keeps links with a homeland [1].
- Remittances raise household incomes and pay for education and health [1].
- Own-society application: remittances supporting families or local building [1].
- Diaspora investment and returning skills create brain gain [1].
- Transnational networks spread information about jobs and markets [1].
- Diaspora associations fund schools, clinics or disaster relief [1].
- Hall and Gilroy: diasporas create cultural exchange and hybrid identities [1].
- Counter: brain drain removes doctors, nurses and engineers [1].
- Remittances can create dependency on income from abroad [1].
- Remittances may widen inequality between households with and without migrants [1].
- Diaspora funding can sustain conflict at home [1].
- Own-society application: debate about skilled workers leaving [1].
- Later generations may lose ties, so benefits decline over time [1].
- Remittances fund consumption rather than structural change, a dependency criticism [1].
- Analysis: effects depend on the type of diaspora and the home state's policies [1].
- Judgement: positive for household welfare, more mixed for national development [1].
**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate household-level gains from national-level development; it gives a natural structure for the judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling asylum seekers or undocumented workers "illegal" without analysis.
- Describing types of migrant without the feature that defines each.
- Leaving push-pull unevaluated.
- Treating climate change as always increasing migration.
- Using an item's details without linking them to a named idea.
