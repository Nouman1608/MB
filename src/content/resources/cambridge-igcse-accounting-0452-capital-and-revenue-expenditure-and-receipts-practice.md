These questions cover sub-topic **4.1 Capital and revenue expenditure and receipts**. Cambridge 0452 papers are not divided into lettered sections, so Sections A and B below simply group shorter and longer questions. The number in square brackets after the last part of each question is the total for the whole question. Businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars.

## Section A

**1.** Define revenue expenditure. **[1]**

**2.** Which is capital expenditure?

A: repairs to a roof

B: installation of a new machine

C: insurance of a delivery van

D: wages of a machine operator **[1]**

**3.** Which is a revenue receipt?

A: a loan from a bank

B: capital introduced by the owner

C: commission received

D: proceeds from the sale of a van **[1]**

**4.** State whether each item is capital expenditure or revenue expenditure: (a) carriage paid on a new machine; (b) carriage paid on goods bought for resale; (c) replacing a broken window; (d) purchase of land for a car park. **[4]**

**5.** Capital expenditure of 3,000 was treated as revenue expenditure. Ignoring depreciation, state the effect on the profit for the year and on non-current assets. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the proceeds from selling a non-current asset are not shown as revenue in the statement of profit or loss. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Hollybank Couriers bought a new van. The bills were: van 24,000; delivery to the depot 400; signwriting the business name before use 650; roof rack fitted before use 380; first tank of fuel 90; one year's insurance 1,100; service after six months 260.

**(a)** Calculate the total capital expenditure and the total revenue expenditure.

**(b)** Vans are depreciated at 20% of cost per year, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase. Calculate the depreciation for the first year.

**(c)** The signwriting and the roof rack were wrongly debited to motor expenses. Calculate the effect of this error on the profit for the year and on non-current assets. **[8]**

**8.** For each receipt of Hollybank Couriers, state whether it is a capital receipt or a revenue receipt, and name the account credited.

(i) Delivery fees from customers.

(ii) A loan of 12,000 from the bank.

(iii) 1,500 received from selling an old van.

(iv) Rent received for letting part of the yard.

(v) An extra 5,000 paid in by the owner.

(vi) Interest received on a savings account. **[6]**

**9.** Kingsway Dental Supplies depreciates equipment and fixtures at 10% of cost per year, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase. It does not depreciate premises. For each error, calculate the effect on the profit for the year and on non-current assets.

(i) New display cabinets costing 4,600 were debited to purchases.

(ii) Repairs to equipment of 1,300 were debited to the equipment account and depreciated.

(iii) An old cabinet with a net book value of 2,500 was sold for 2,500. The proceeds were credited to sales and the cabinet was not removed from the accounts. Ignore depreciation on the old cabinet.

(iv) Interest received of 180 was credited to the capital account. **[8]**

**10.** Kingsway Dental Supplies' draft profit for the year was 38,750. Using your answers to Question 9, prepare a statement showing the corrected profit for the year. **[5]**

**11.** Storm damage to the roof of Kingsway's premises was repaired at a cost of 6,000, restoring it to its previous condition. The owner wants to debit the cost to the premises account so that the profit looks higher when she applies for a bank loan.

**(a)** State the correct treatment of the 6,000 and explain why.

**(b)** State the effect on profit if the owner's treatment is used. Premises are not depreciated.

**(c)** Explain why the owner's treatment would be wrong, referring to an accounting concept and to the bank. **[6]**

**12.** **(a)** Explain **two** reasons why it is important to distinguish correctly between capital and revenue expenditure.

**(b)** Explain how an overstated profit could mislead the owner of a business. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Spending on the day-to-day running of the business, used up within the period [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**2.** **B** [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**3.** **C** [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**4.** (a) Capital [1]; (b) revenue [1]; (c) revenue [1]; (d) capital [1]. Total: 4 marks.

**5.** Profit for the year understated by 3,000 [1]; non-current assets understated by 3,000 [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**6.** The proceeds do not come from normal trading; they convert an existing non-current asset into cash [1]. Only the difference between the proceeds and the net book value, a profit or loss on disposal, is shown in the statement of profit or loss [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**7. (a)** Capital: 24,000 + 400 + 650 + 380 [1] = **25,430** [1]. Revenue: 90 + 1,100 + 260 [1] = **1,450** [1].

**(b)** 25,430 x 20% = **5,086** [1].

**(c)** Expensed 650 + 380 = 1,030; depreciation not charged 1,030 x 20% = 206 [1]. Profit for the year understated by 1,030 - 206 = **824** [1]. Non-current assets understated by **824** [1]. Total: 8 marks.

*Examiner insight:* Signwriting and the roof rack are made before the van is used, so they are part of its cost. Fuel, insurance and servicing are running costs.

**8.** One mark for each item with type and account correct [6]:

(i) Revenue; sales (or delivery fees).

(ii) Capital; loan.

(iii) Capital; disposal of van.

(iv) Revenue; rent received.

(v) Capital; capital.

(vi) Revenue; interest received.

Total: 6 marks.

**9.** Two marks for each error: profit effect [1] and asset effect [1].

(i) Depreciation 4,600 x 10% = 460. Profit understated by 4,600 - 460 = **4,140**; non-current assets understated by **4,140** [2].

(ii) Depreciation charged 1,300 x 10% = 130. Profit overstated by 1,300 - 130 = **1,170**; non-current assets overstated by **1,170** [2].

(iii) Profit overstated by **2,500**; non-current assets overstated by **2,500**, because the cabinet is still included [2].

(iv) Profit understated by **180**; no effect on non-current assets [2].

Total: 8 marks.

**10.**

| | $ |
|---|---|
| Draft profit for the year | 38,750 |
| Add display cabinets, net of depreciation | 4,140 |
| Less repairs, net of depreciation | (1,170) |
| Less sale proceeds wrongly treated as sales | (2,500) |
| Add interest received | 180 |
| **Corrected profit for the year** | **39,400** |

One mark for each adjustment [4]; corrected profit 39,400 [1]. Total: 5 marks.

**11. (a)** It should be debited to repairs (an expense) and charged in full to the statement of profit or loss [1], because it restores the roof to its previous condition and does not improve the premises [1].

**(b)** Profit would be overstated by 6,000 [1].

**(c)** The matching concept requires the cost of restoring the roof to be charged in the period it was incurred, so capitalising it misstates profit [1]. The bank would lend on the basis of a profit 6,000 higher than the business really earned [1], so the loan might be larger than the business can repay, and knowingly misleading the bank would breach the ethical principle of integrity [1]. Total: 6 marks.

**12. (a)** Two reasons, each identified [1] and explained [1], for example: so that profit is correctly measured, since wrong treatment over- or understates expenses [2]; so that non-current assets are shown at a fair value in the statement of financial position [2]; so that the matching concept is applied, spreading the cost of assets over the periods that benefit.

**(b)** The owner might believe the business earned more than it did [1] and take drawings or make spending decisions, such as expanding, that the business cannot afford [1]. Total: 6 marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating costs of getting a new asset ready for use as revenue.
- Ignoring the depreciation that should, or should not, have been charged when calculating an error's effect.
- Treating the whole of a sale proceeds figure as income.
- Stating "profit is affected" without the direction and the amount.
- Saying a loan credited to sales overstates assets rather than understating liabilities.
