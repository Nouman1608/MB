## Section A

**1.** Explain the meaning of the term diffraction. **[2]**

**2.** Water waves diffract through a gap in a barrier. State two quantities that are the same for the waves after the gap as before it. **[2]**

**3.** State two ways in which diffraction differs from refraction. **[2]**

**4.** Plane waves pass through a gap. The gap is reduced from about ten wavelengths wide to about one wavelength wide. State and explain how the diffracted waves change. **[2]**

**5.** A person speaking in a corridor can be heard, but not seen, by someone standing round a corner. Explain this observation. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** A straight dipper vibrating at 12 Hz produces plane waves in a ripple tank. Six adjacent bright lines (crests) on the screen span a distance of 7.5 cm. Assume the image on the screen is the same size as the waves.

**(a)** Determine the wavelength of the waves. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the speed of the waves. **[1]**

**(c)** Describe the wavefronts observed beyond a gap of width **(i)** 1.5 cm and **(ii)** 9.0 cm. **[3]**

**7.** In a ripple tank, waves travel at 0.15 m s⁻¹ and pass through a gap of width 2.0 cm. The frequency of the dipper is increased from 5.0 Hz to 20 Hz.

**(a)** Calculate the wavelength at each frequency. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe and explain how the diffraction pattern changes. **[2]**

**8.** Describe an experiment using microwaves to demonstrate diffraction, and to show how the amount of diffraction depends on the width of the gap. **[4]**

**9.** A beam of red light from a laser passes through a single vertical slit about 0.1 mm wide and falls on a distant screen.

**(a)** Describe what is seen on the screen, compared with what would be seen with no slit. **[1]**

**(b)** State the effect on the pattern of making the slit narrower. **[1]**

**(c)** The red laser is replaced by a green laser, which has a shorter wavelength. The slit is unchanged. State the effect on the pattern. **[1]**

**10.** Plane water waves of wavelength 1.0 cm meet a gap of width 5.0 cm. Describe the shape of the wavefronts beyond the gap and state their spacing. **[2]**

**11.** Long-wave radio broadcasts have wavelengths of around 1500 m, while VHF broadcasts have wavelengths of around 3 m. Explain why a house in a valley behind a range of hills can receive the long-wave broadcast more easily than the VHF broadcast. **[2]**

**12.** In a two-slit interference experiment with light, each slit is very narrow. Explain why the slits must be narrow for an interference pattern to be observed. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Diffraction is the **spreading** of a wave [1] as it passes **through a gap or round the edge of an obstacle** [1].

**2.** Any two [1] each: **wavelength**; **frequency**; **speed**.

**3.** Any two [1] each: diffraction occurs at a gap or edge **within one medium**, whereas refraction occurs at a **boundary between media**; in diffraction the **wavelength (and speed) is unchanged**, whereas in refraction they change; refraction is caused by a change in **speed**, diffraction is not.

**4.** The waves **spread out much more**, emerging as almost **semicircular** wavefronts instead of nearly straight ones [1], because diffraction is greatest when the gap width is **similar to the wavelength** [1].

**5.** Sound has a wavelength of the order of **1 m**, similar to the width of the corridor or doorway, so it **diffracts strongly** round the corner [1]. Light has a wavelength of about **5 × 10⁻⁷ m**, millions of times smaller than the opening [1], so its diffraction is **negligible** and it travels in straight lines, leaving the speaker out of sight [1].

**6. (a)** Six crests span **five** wavelengths [1]: λ = 7.5 ÷ 5 = **1.5 cm** [1].

**(b)** v = fλ = 12 × 0.015 = **0.18 m s⁻¹** [1].

**(c) (i)** Gap equal to λ: wavefronts **spread widely**, almost semicircular [1]. **(ii)** Gap = 6λ: wavefronts mostly **straight**, curving only at their ends, with **little spreading** into the regions behind the barriers [1]. In both cases the wavefront **spacing stays 1.5 cm** [1].

**7. (a)** At 5.0 Hz: λ = 0.15 ÷ 5.0 = **3.0 cm** [1]. At 20 Hz: λ = 0.15 ÷ 20 = **0.75 cm** [1].

**(b)** The diffraction (spreading) **decreases** [1], because the wavelength becomes much smaller than the 2.0 cm gap, whereas at 5.0 Hz it was comparable to the gap [1].

**8.** Any four [1] each:

- A microwave **transmitter** is aimed at a gap between two **metal plates** (which reflect microwaves).
- A **receiver** connected to a meter is placed behind the gap.
- The receiver is moved along an **arc** centred on the gap, and the readings are noted at different angles from the straight-through direction.
- A signal is detected well away from the straight-through line, showing that the microwaves **spread** through the gap.
- The gap is **narrowed** towards the microwave wavelength (a few cm) and the measurements repeated.
- With the narrower gap, the signal is detected over a **wider range of angles** (more spreading).

**9. (a)** A **wider** band of light (a central bright band, with fainter bands either side) instead of a small spot, showing the light has **spread out** [1].

**(b)** The pattern becomes **wider** (more spreading) [1].

**(c)** The pattern becomes **narrower** (less spreading), because the wavelength is shorter relative to the slit width [1].

**10.** Mostly **straight (plane) wavefronts**, curved only near their ends, with little spreading, since the gap is five wavelengths wide [1]. The spacing remains **1.0 cm** [1].

**11.** The hills are comparable in size with the **long-wave wavelength**, so those waves **diffract** significantly round them into the valley [1]. The VHF wavelength is **much smaller** than the hills, so it diffracts very little and the valley lies in its "shadow" [1].

**12.** Diffraction at each slit **spreads** the light, and a narrow slit gives more spreading [1], so the light from the two slits **overlaps** on the screen and can superpose to form the interference pattern [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining diffraction as "bending" with no mention of a gap or obstacle (Question 1).
- Saying the wavelength changes after diffraction (Questions 2 and 10).
- Counting crests instead of the gaps between them when finding λ (Question 6).
- Reversing the effect of gap width or wavelength on spreading (Questions 4, 7 and 9).
- Describing an experiment without stating what was varied and what was observed (Question 8).
- Comparing the two wavelengths in Question 5 without referring to the size of the gap.
