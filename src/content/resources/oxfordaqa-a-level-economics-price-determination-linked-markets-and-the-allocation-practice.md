## Questions

**1.** In Country B the weekly market for cassava is Qd = 520 − 40P and Qs = −80 + 20P (P in currency units per kg, Q in thousand kg).

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[3]**
**(b)** State the size and type of disequilibrium at a price of 12. **[2]**

**2.** A drought removes 30 thousand kg of supply at every price in the market in question 1, so Qs = −110 + 20P.

**(a)** Work out the equilibrium price and quantity after the drought. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in price and in quantity. **[2]**

**3.** The market for a metal in Country B has Qd = 200 − 10P and Qs = −40 + 20P.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Traders expect the price to rise and buy an extra 30 units at every price. Work out the price and quantity that result. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the price rise may be self-fulfilling. **[2]**

**4.** In a market, Qd = 300 − 10P. Demand then rises by 40 at every price.

**(a)** Calculate the new price if supply is Qs = 10P, and if supply is fixed at 150. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why price rises more when supply is fixed. **[2]**

**5.** The price of new cars rises in Country C. Explain the likely effect on **(a)** the market for car tyres **[2]** and **(b)** the market for bus travel. **[3]**

**6.** In Country C, timber is used for furniture, Qd = 180 − 10P, and for building, Qd = 120 − 5P. Supply is Qs = −60 + 25P.

**(a)** Define composite demand. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Building demand rises by 40 at every price. Calculate the new price and the quantity going to furniture. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain the effect on furniture makers. **[1]**

**7.** Country C has 80 thousand cattle, each giving one hide, so hide supply is fixed at 80 thousand. Hide demand is Qd = 260 − 2P.

**(a)** Calculate the price of hides. **[1]**
**(b)** Demand for beef rises and 10% more cattle are slaughtered. Calculate the new hide price. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this is joint supply and why hide supply does not respond to the hide price. **[2]**

**8.** A factory fits 3 sensors to each machine. Orders are 12,000 machines.

**(a)** Calculate the demand for sensors. **[1]**
**(b)** Orders fall by 25%. Calculate the new sensor demand and the percentage change. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this is derived demand. **[1]**

**9.** A cold snap destroys part of the winter vegetable crop in Country D and the price rises. Using this scenario, show what each of rationing, incentive and signalling does. **[6]**

**10.** Assess the claim that allocating resources through prices is impersonal. **[8]**

**11.** A public clinic in Country D sells priority places in its waiting queue. Evaluate the case for extending the price mechanism in this way. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Set Qd = Qs: 520 − 40P = −80 + 20P [1]. 600 = 60P, so P = **10** [1]. Q = 520 − 400 = **120 thousand kg** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** At 12, Qd = 520 − 480 = 40 and Qs = −80 + 240 = 160 [1]. **Excess supply of 120 thousand kg** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both substitutions at the disequilibrium price; a bare "surplus" without the figures or the word supply tends to leave the answer incomplete.

**2. (a)** 520 − 40P = −110 + 20P, so 630 = 60P and P = **10.5** [1]; Q = 520 − 420 = **100** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Price: 0.5 ÷ 10 = **+5%** [1]. Quantity: −20 ÷ 120 = **−16.7%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide each change by the starting value, and keep the sign: a fall in quantity is negative.

**3. (a)** 200 − 10P = −40 + 20P, so 240 = 30P [1], P = **8**, Q = **120** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Qd = 230 − 10P, so 270 = 30P and P = **9** [1], Q = **140** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The higher price confirms the traders' expectation [1], so more join in and buy, pushing price up further, which can build a bubble [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The explanation scores when it links the expectation to the extra demand and then to the higher price; repeating "speculation raises price" earns little.

**4. (a)** Responsive supply: 340 − 10P = 10P, so P = **17** [1]. Fixed supply: 340 − 10P = 150, so P = **19** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Producers cannot extend output when supply is fixed [1], so the whole excess demand must be removed by a higher price shrinking quantity demanded [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say which curve is steeper and what that stops producers doing; a bare "supply is inelastic" does not explain the mechanism.

**5. (a)** Higher car prices cause a contraction in quantity of cars demanded [1], so demand for tyres, in joint demand with cars, shifts left and tyre price and quantity fall [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Cars and bus travel are substitutes (competitive demand) [1]. Some buyers switch, so the demand curve for bus travel shifts right [1] and price and quantity rise, by an amount limited by how close the substitutes are [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the link and the direction of the shift in the second market; saying "buses do better" without a shifting curve usually loses the chain.

**6. (a)** Demand for one good that has several uses [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Total Qd = 300 − 15P. 300 − 15P = −60 + 25P, so 360 = 40P and P = **9** [1], Q = **165** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Qd = 340 − 15P, so 400 = 40P and P = **10** [1]. Furniture takes 180 − 100 = **80** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Furniture makers face a higher input price and get less timber (80 against 90), so their costs rise [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Add the two demands at each price before equating with supply, and use the new price to find each use's share.

**7. (a)** 260 − 2P = 80, so P = **90** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Hides = 80 × 1.1 = 88 [1]. 260 − 2P = 88, so P = **86** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Hides and beef come from the same animals, so a change in beef output changes hide supply [1]. Cattle are raised for beef, so the hide price does not alter the number slaughtered [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A good joint supply answer shows the direction: more of one product means more of the other, and the price of the by-product falls.

**8. (a)** 12,000 × 3 = **36,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Orders = 9,000, so sensors = **27,000** [1], a fall of **25%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Sensors are wanted only to make machines, so their demand depends on machine demand [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the fixed ratio, then link the two percentage falls; the percentage fall is the same only because the ratio is fixed.

**9.** Rationing: less supply is available, so the higher price allocates vegetables to those willing and able to pay [1] and reduces quantity demanded to match the smaller supply [1]. Incentive: higher prices raise profit for growers who still have crops [1], encouraging growers elsewhere and importers to supply more [1]. Signalling: the price tells buyers vegetables are scarcer, so they economise or switch [1], and tells producers where more resources are wanted [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply each function to vegetables rather than defining it; two sentences per function, linked to the scenario, tend to earn more than three bare definitions.

**10.** Meaning: prices apply the same rule to every buyer and seller [1]. For: no planner or official decides, so favouritism cannot decide who gets what [1]; millions of choices are coordinated through responses to price [1]. Against: what a person can buy depends on income, so the result reflects the distribution of income [1]; people without income send no signal [1]; firms with market power can set prices to suit themselves [1]. Judgement: impersonal does not mean neutral [1], and the view is stronger where competition is strong and incomes are fairly distributed [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that names the condition it depends on usually reads better than one that restates "it depends".

**11.** For: a price rationing priority places gives a quick way to allocate them [1]; the income funds care for others [1]; the price signals how much people value early treatment [1]. Against: access depends on income, not need [1]; those who pay jump ahead of sicker patients [1]; the nature of the service changes, as care becomes something bought [1]. Judgement: it is more defensible for minor, non-urgent treatment than for serious cases [1], provided funds go to the wider service [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation tends to gain from weighing efficiency against equity and the nature of the activity, with a clear final judgement and condition.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not showing the equation step, so one slip loses the whole calculation.
- Dividing by the final value instead of the starting value in percentage changes.
- Stating a price without saying which side has the excess.
- Describing speculation without the link from expectation to price.
- Stopping at "substitutes" or "complements" without a curve shifting.
- Mixing up joint demand and joint supply.
- Defining the price mechanism functions without applying them.
- Writing an evaluation with no judgement or no condition.
