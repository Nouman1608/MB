These questions cover **5.4 The management of international migration** from Paper 2 Core Human Geography. Questions 1 to 6 use fictional places and data; questions 7 to 10 ask you to use the migration stream you have studied as your case study. Revise first with the [5.4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-international-migration/) and the [5.4 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-management-of-international-migration-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State **two** features of a guest worker scheme. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term "family reunification" as it applies to international migration. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how a points-based system controls the character of an international migration stream. **[3]**

**4.** The table shows a fictional migration stream from Pelora to Avenland. Avenland opened its labour market to Pelorans in 2004 and introduced an annual quota in 2013.

| Year | Pelorans arriving in Avenland (thousands) | Arrivals who were male (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 12 | 82 |
| 2005 | 48 | 70 |
| 2010 | 30 | 58 |
| 2015 | 9 | 45 |
| 2020 | 7 | 40 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in arrivals between 2000 and 2005. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the changes in the scale and character of the stream between 2000 and 2020. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest reasons for the changes you have described. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** way in which the government of a source country may try to manage emigration. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why migration schemes intended to be temporary often lead to permanent settlement. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** For an international migration stream you have studied, describe its character and scale. **[6]**

**8.** For the same stream, explain its pattern, including where migrants come from and where they settle. **[5]**

**9.** For the same stream, explain its impacts on the source area. **[6]**

**10.** With reference to an international migration stream you have studied, evaluate the success of attempts to manage it. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Workers are recruited for a fixed period, often on a contract arranged before departure [1]. They are expected to return home afterwards, and may be tied to one employer or sector [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Migration of the spouses, children or other close relatives of migrants already living in a country [1], so that families separated by the original move live together in the destination [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Applicants score points for characteristics such as skills, qualifications, language ability, age or a job offer [1]. Only those above a threshold may enter [1], so the stream becomes dominated by younger, skilled and educated migrants, and low-skilled workers are excluded [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** (48 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 [1] = 300% increase [1].

**(b)** Arrivals quadrupled from 12 000 in 2000 to a peak of 48 000 in 2005 [1], then fell to 7 000 by 2020 [1]. The stream changed from mostly male (82%) to mostly female (40% male) [1].

**(c)** Opening the labour market in 2004 removed the legal barrier, so arrivals rose sharply [1]. The 2013 quota capped numbers, explaining the low figures in 2015 and 2020 [1]. Later arrivals may be mainly wives and children joining earlier male workers through family reunification [1]. Over time, jobs in sectors employing women, such as care work, may have grown, or growth at home in Pelora may have reduced the need for men to emigrate [1]. **[9]**

**5.** A government may sign labour agreements with destination countries [1], arranging recruitment, contracts and minimum conditions for its workers abroad [1]. This aims to reduce unemployment at home while protecting workers and securing remittances [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit restrictions on emigration of key workers, such as health staff, or schemes to attract back the diaspora, if developed in the same way.

**6.** Employers want to keep trained, experienced workers rather than recruit new ones [1]. Migrants who have settled build ties, savings and housing in the destination [1]. If entry rules tighten, migrants who leave may not be able to come back, so they stay [1]. Spouses and children join them, and children born and schooled in the destination see it as home [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Answers depend on the stream chosen. Credit: a named stream between two countries [1]; the age profile of migrants, for example mainly young adults [1]; their sex balance and how it changed [1]; their skills or type of work [1]; legal status or whether moves were temporary or permanent [1]; numbers for at least one date, with change over time [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* for Turkey to Germany, good detail includes the 1961 recruitment agreement, young workers for factory and mining jobs, the shift to family reunification after 1973 and a Turkish-origin population of about three million today.

**8.** Credit: named source regions within the origin country [1]; named destination regions or cities [1]; an explanation of why migrants went to those destinations, such as jobs in particular industries [1]; the role of chain migration in concentrating migrants from particular places [1]; a change in pattern over time [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Credit: remittances and how they are used [1]; effect on unemployment or pressure on land [1]; loss of young or skilled adults and its effect on the local economy [1]; demographic change, such as ageing or an unbalanced sex ratio [1]; social impacts, such as divided families or returning migrants' new ideas [1]; specific place or data detail from the case study [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* names the stream and identifies management at both ends [1]; defines success, for example meeting aims without serious unintended effects [1].

*Destination policies:* recruitment schemes and their aims [1]; restrictions such as quotas, recruitment stops or border controls [1]; integration and citizenship measures [1]; evidence of what each achieved [1].

*Source policies:* labour export agreements or protection of workers abroad [1]; attempts to use remittances or returning skills for development [1].

*Evaluation:* a policy that met its aim, with evidence [1]; a policy that had unintended effects, such as a recruitment stop leading to permanent settlement [1]; recognition that success differs between source and destination [1]; recognition that success differs between groups, such as employers, migrants and local workers [1]; change in success over time [1].

*Judgement:* a clear overall view on how successful management has been [1], supported by the evidence above [1]. **[15]**

*Examiner insight:* top answers judge each policy against its own aim, then reach an overall view; a narrative of events with no evaluation stays in the lower levels.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering case study questions with a general account and no named stream.
- Giving scale without numbers or dates.
- Confusing character (who moves) with causes (why they move).
- Writing only about the destination when the question asks about the source.
- Describing policies without judging whether they met their aims.
- Ignoring the context given in a stem, such as the dates of policy changes in Question 4.
