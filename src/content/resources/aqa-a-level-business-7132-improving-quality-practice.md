These questions cover section 3.4.4 Making operational decisions to improve performance: improving quality of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [improving quality study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-improving-quality/) and the [improving quality revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-improving-quality-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of quality assurance rather than quality control? **[1]**

A: Testing a sample from each batch before it is shipped

B: Training every worker in how to spot and prevent faults at their own stage

C: Rejecting faulty items at the end of the production line

D: Inspecting returned goods

**2.** A sample of 400 items contains 12 faulty ones. What is the defect rate? **[1]**

A: 1.2%

B: 3%

C: 12%

D: 33%

**3.** Define the term "quality". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** weakness of relying only on quality control. **[3]**

**5.** Brinley Bikes makes 5,000 bikes a month. 4% are defective. Of the defective bikes, 80% are found by inspection and reworked at a cost of £25 each; the rest reach customers and each costs the company £120 in repairs and compensation.

**(a)** Calculate the number of defective bikes. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the monthly cost of rework. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the monthly cost of defective bikes reaching customers. **[2]**

**6.** Brinley tests a sample of 400 frames from a batch of 8,000 and finds 12 are faulty.

**(a)** Calculate the defect rate in the sample. **[1]**

**(b)** Estimate how many faulty frames are in the whole batch. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain **one** limitation of using a sample to judge a whole batch. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** consequence of poor quality for a restaurant. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** benefits to a food manufacturer of using quality assurance rather than relying only on quality control. **[6]**

**9.** A budget hotel chain is considering spending £2 million on staff training, better beds and an improved booking system to raise the quality of its guests' experience. Assess whether this is a sound decision. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse the possible consequences for a car manufacturer of a major quality failure. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** difficulties a business might face when introducing total quality management. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether quality assurance is always better than quality control. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Quality assurance builds quality in and prevents faults; the other options find faults after they occur. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. 12 ÷ 400 × 100 = 3%. **[1]**

**3.** How well a product or service meets customers' expectations and needs [1], including factors such as reliability, performance, appearance, safety and consistency [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Quality control finds faults after they have been made [1], so the cost of materials and labour has already been spent on faulty items, leading to waste and rework [1]. Inspection also adds cost without adding value, and with sampling some faulty items may still reach customers [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 5,000 × 0.04 = **200** [1].

**(b)** 80% of 200 = 160 reworked [1]; 160 × £25 = **£4,000** [1].

**(c)** 200 − 160 = 40 reach customers [1]; 40 × £120 = **£4,800** [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** 12 ÷ 400 × 100 = **3%** [1].

**(b)** 8,000 × 0.03 = **240** faulty frames [1].

**(c)** The sample may not be representative, so the true rate may differ from 3% [1]. A larger sample would be more reliable but costs more; a random sample reduces bias [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Customers who receive poor food or service are unlikely to return and may post negative reviews [1], which damages the restaurant's reputation and reduces future bookings [1]. Accept: refunds and wasted food. **[2]**

**8.** Benefit 1: fewer defects and less waste [1]. QA prevents faults at each stage, for example by checking ingredients from suppliers and training staff in hygiene [1], so less food is thrown away and the cost per unit falls [1]. Benefit 2: protects reputation and avoids costly recalls [1]. A food fault that reaches shoppers may be dangerous and require a recall [1], so preventing it protects brand image, sales and avoids legal action [1]. Credit also: employee ownership and motivation. **[6]**

**9.** For: better quality of guest experience should increase satisfaction, positive reviews and repeat bookings [1]. It may allow higher room rates, or higher occupancy [1]. Improved booking systems reduce errors and staff time [1]. Against: £2 million is a large investment, and guests at budget hotels may be most interested in price, not extra quality [1]. Higher costs may force price rises that lose price-sensitive customers [1]. The benefits may take time to appear and are hard to measure [1]. Training takes time and staff turnover in hotels is high, so training could be lost [1]. Judgement: it is sound if research shows that customers will value the improvements and that occupancy or room rates rise enough to repay the investment within a reasonable time [1]; the chain could test the changes in a few hotels first [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Direct costs: recalls, repairs and compensation could be very high for thousands of cars [1], and legal action may follow if safety is involved [1]. Sales: customers lose trust and switch to rival makers [1], so sales may fall for years [1]. Brand and finance: share price may fall and the company may have to cut prices to rebuild demand [1]. Other: staff morale and relations with dealers or suppliers may suffer [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Difficulty 1: it needs a change of culture [1]. Staff and managers used to inspectors checking quality may resist taking responsibility, so the system works only partly [1]. Difficulty 2: cost and time [1]. Training, new procedures and supplier checks require spending before benefits are seen, and it may take years to reach "right first time" [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: prevention reduces waste, rework and scrap, which are costly [1]. It stops faults reaching customers, so external failure costs fall [1]. Staff take ownership, which improves motivation [1]. It builds a reputation for reliability [1]. Against: QA needs investment in training and systems before savings appear [1]. Small or low-volume businesses may find simple inspection cheaper [1]. Some items, such as safety-critical products, need final testing whatever the process [1]. QA depends on staff commitment; without it, the system fails [1]. A balance is normal: QA to prevent, QC to check the system works [1]. Factors: the cost of failure (high for safety-critical goods), the size of the business, and the level of training [1]. Judgement: QA is usually better in the long run because prevention is cheaper than failure [1], but it is not a replacement for all checking; most businesses need both, in proportions that suit their products and risks [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Use the words "prevent" and "detect" accurately and link your method to the product in the case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying quality control stops faults happening.
- Describing quality assurance as more inspection.
- Treating quality as the same as luxury.
- Giving benefits of quality with no cost or difficulty.
- Leaving out the cost of poor quality when evaluating spending.
- Calculating a defect rate without converting to a percentage.
