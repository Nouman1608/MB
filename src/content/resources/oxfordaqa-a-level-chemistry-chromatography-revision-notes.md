## What the specification asks for

| 3.3.16.1 outcome | In one line |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Chromatography separates and identifies the components in a mixture |
| Types | TLC, column chromatography (CC) and gas chromatography (GC) |
| How it works | Balance between a component's solubility in the moving phase and its retention by the stationary phase |
| Identification | Retention times and Rf values, compared with standards |
| GC-MS | Mass spectrometry analyses the components that GC has separated |
| Skill | Calculate Rf values from a chromatogram |
| Skill | Compare retention times and Rf values with standards |

Linked outcome from 3.3.13.2: amino acids can be separated by TLC, located with developing agents such as ninhydrin or with ultraviolet light, and identified by their Rf values (see the [amino acids and proteins notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-amino-acids-and-proteins-revision-notes/)).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stationary phase | The part that stays fixed: the solid on the plate or in the column, or the solid (which may be coated by a liquid) in a GC column |
| Moving phase | The solvent (TLC, CC) or gas (GC) that flows past the stationary phase |
| Chromatogram | The developed plate, or the detector trace, that records the separation |
| Baseline | Pencil line on a TLC plate where samples are spotted |
| Solvent front | The furthest point the solvent reached before the plate was removed |
| Rf value | How far the spot centre moved divided by how far the solvent moved, each measured from the baseline |
| Retention time | Time from injecting a sample into a GC column to a component reaching the detector |
| Standard | A pure sample of a known substance, run alongside the unknown |
| Fraction | A portion of liquid collected from the bottom of a column |

## One idea explains every method

Each component moves between the two phases. How fast it travels is set by the **balance between its solubility in the moving phase and its retention by the stationary phase**.

| Balance favours... | TLC | CC | GC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moving phase | Large Rf | Leaves the column early | Short retention time |
| Stationary phase | Small Rf | Leaves the column late | Long retention time |

Two substances with the same balance do not separate. Change the solvent, the solid or the conditions, and the balance changes.

On a polar stationary phase, retention rises with polarity: a molecule that forms hydrogen bonds is held more strongly than one with only weak dipoles, which is held more strongly than a non-polar one of similar size.

## The three methods side by side

| | TLC | CC | GC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moving phase | A solvent | A solvent | A gas |
| Stationary phase | Thin solid layer on a plate | Solid packed into a column | Packed solid, may be liquid-coated |
| Flow | **Up** the plate | **Down** the column | Through the column, under pressure, at high temperature |
| Main result | Spots and Rf values | Separate fractions of each component | Peaks and retention times |
| Typical use | Quick identification and checking purity | Obtaining each component on its own | Identifying volatile components; feeds GC-MS |

## Rf values

```
       distance travelled by the spot (baseline to spot centre)
Rf  =  --------------------------------------------------------
       distance travelled by the solvent (baseline to front)
```

- A pure number between 0 and 1.
- Measure both distances from the baseline, to the centre of the spot.
- Only compare with standards on the same plate, in the same solvent.

**Worked reminder.** Solvent front 7.5 cm, spot centre 3.3 cm: Rf = 3.3 / 7.5 = **0.44**.

### Method in steps: identifying by Rf

1. Spot the sample and the standards side by side on one baseline.
2. Run the plate, then mark the solvent front straight away.
3. Locate colourless spots (ultraviolet light, or ninhydrin for amino acids).
4. Calculate the Rf of every spot.
5. Match each sample spot to a standard with the same Rf.
6. A sample spot with no match is a substance not among the standards.
7. To strengthen a match, rerun in a different solvent.

### TLC technique in brief

| Step | Why |
|---|---|
| Pencil baseline | Graphite does not dissolve; ink would run with the solvent |
| Solvent level below the baseline | The spots must be carried up, not dissolved into the solvent |
| Small, concentrated spots | Large spots spread out and overlap |
| Lid on the container | Keeps the air full of solvent vapour so the plate does not dry as the solvent rises |
| Mark the front immediately | The solvent evaporates and the front can no longer be seen |

## Column chromatography

- Mixture added at the top; solvent run down through the packed solid.
- The component with the balance most in favour of the solvent leaves first.
- Collect the liquid in a series of fractions; check each fraction by TLC against standards.
- Combine fractions that contain only one component; leave out fractions that contain two.

## Gas chromatography and GC-MS

- Sample injected and vaporised; a gas carries it through the column under pressure at high temperature.
- A detector records a peak for each component as it emerges.
- Identify a peak by matching its retention time to a standard run on the same column, at the same temperature and gas flow.
- A peak with no matching standard is an unknown: send it to the mass spectrometer.
- **GC-MS:** the column's output passes straight into a mass spectrometer. Components arrive one at a time, so each gives its own spectrum. The molecular ion gives Mr, and a precise m/z value fixes the formula; the method is in the [organic analysis notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-organic-analysis-revision-notes/) (section 3.3.6.2).

### Method in steps: precise m/z to formula

1. Write down every candidate formula (all have the same integer Mr).
2. Total the precise masses of all the atoms in each candidate.
3. Pick the total that equals the measured m/z.
4. Show every sum, not just the one that matches.

## Must-know distinctions

| Don't confuse | Difference |
|---|---|
| TLC flow vs CC flow | Up the plate vs down the column |
| Moving phase in GC | A gas, never a liquid; a liquid can only be part of the stationary phase |
| High Rf vs long retention time | High Rf means weak retention; a long retention time means strong retention |
| Separating vs identifying | GC separates; mass spectrometry analyses each separated component |
| One spot vs pure | One spot in one solvent does not prove purity |
| Matching vs proving | A matching Rf or retention time is evidence, not proof |

## Quick self-test

1. A spot centre is 3.8 cm above the baseline and the solvent front is 5.0 cm above it. Calculate the Rf value.
2. In a solvent where the front rises 8.5 cm, a compound has Rf = 0.62. How far above the baseline is its spot?
3. The solvent front is 50 mm and a spot is 41 mm from the baseline. A student divides 50 by 41 and reports Rf = 1.22. Find the error and give the correct value.
4. State the moving phase in gas chromatography.
5. In which method does the solvent move down through the stationary phase?
6. A GC component has a long retention time. What does this tell you about its balance between the phases?
7. Why is ninhydrin sprayed on a plate used to separate amino acids?
8. A tripeptide is hydrolysed and the TLC plate shows only two spots. Give two possible reasons.
9. The molecular ion of a component leaving a GC column is at m/z = 96.0573. Is it C₆H₈O, C₅H₄O₂ or C₅H₈N₂? (C = 12.0000, H = 1.0078, N = 14.0031, O = 15.9949)
10. Why must standards for TLC be run on the same plate as the sample?
11. A column is checked fraction by fraction. One fraction shows both components on TLC. What should you do with it?

### Answers

1. Rf = 3.8 / 5.0 = **0.76**.
2. 0.62 × 8.5 = **5.27 cm**.
3. The ratio is upside down; an Rf value cannot exceed 1. Rf = 41 / 50 = **0.82**.
4. A **gas**.
5. **Column chromatography.**
6. It is retained strongly by the stationary phase compared with its solubility in the moving phase.
7. Amino acids are colourless; ninhydrin is a developing agent that makes their spots visible so Rf values can be measured.
8. One amino acid appears twice in the tripeptide, or two different amino acids have the same Rf in this solvent.
9. C₆H₈O = 6(12.0000) + 8(1.0078) + 15.9949 = 96.0573; C₅H₄O₂ = 96.0210; C₅H₈N₂ = 96.0686. It is **C₆H₈O**.
10. Rf values depend on the solvent, the stationary phase and the conditions, so only values from the same run can be compared.
11. Keep it separate from the pure fractions of each component (it could be run through the column again).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking measurements from the bottom of the plate or to the leading edge of a spot; both distances start at the baseline, and the spot distance ends at its centre.
- Writing the Rf ratio upside down, giving a value above 1.
- Adding units such as cm to an Rf value.
- Explaining separation with one phase only, or with "mass" or "size" alone, instead of the balance between both phases.
- Getting the flow direction wrong: TLC solvent rises, column solvent falls.
- Describing the GC moving phase as a liquid.
- Defining retention time without its starting point, the moment of injection.
- Comparing an Rf or retention time with a value from a different plate, solvent or column.
- Claiming one spot proves purity, or a single match proves identity.
- Choosing a formula from a precise m/z without showing the sums for all the candidates.
