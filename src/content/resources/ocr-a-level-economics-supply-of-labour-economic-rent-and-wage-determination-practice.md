## Questions

**1.** A skilled pastry chef earns £48,000 a year. Her next best job would pay £30,000.

**(a)** Define transfer earnings. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate her economic rent and express it as a percentage of her earnings. **[2]**

**2.** A warehouse raises its hourly wage from £12.00 to £13.50. The number of workers offering their labour rises from 800 to 836. Calculate the wage elasticity of supply and interpret it. **[3]**

**3.** Explain two non-monetary factors that affect the supply of labour to the night-shift security industry. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows one person's chosen weekly hours (invented).

| Wage (£/h) | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hours | 30 | 36 | 40 | 37 | 33 |

**(a)** Calculate the elasticity of hours with respect to the wage between £15 and £20. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate it between £20 and £25. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the change of sign using substitution and income effects. **[3]**

**5.** Supply of a rare skill is w = 4 + 0.01L, where w is the annual wage in £000 and L is the number of workers. The wage is £16,000 (w = 16).

**(a)** Calculate employment, total earnings, economic rent and transfer earnings. **[4]**
**(b)** Demand rises and the wage becomes w = 20. Calculate the new economic rent. **[2]**
**(c)** State one reason why rent would be smaller if supply were more elastic. **[1]**

**6.** In a labour market, demand (MRP) is w = 30 - 0.02L and supply is w = 6 + 0.01L, with w in £ per hour and L in workers.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium wage and employment. **[3]**
**(b)** A new licence requirement shifts supply to w = 9 + 0.01L. Calculate the new equilibrium and the percentage change in the wage. **[3]**

**7.** In the original market from question 6, a union negotiates a wage of £18.

**(a)** Calculate employment, the number willing to work, and unemployment. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the wage bill before and after. **[2]**
**(c)** Use the midpoint method to find the wage elasticity of demand over this range and interpret it. **[2]**
**(d)** State one reason why the union's gain might not last. **[1]**

**8.** Suppose the market in question 6 (original supply) has a single employer.

**(a)** Derive the marginal cost of labour and find the profit-maximising employment. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the wage paid, the MRP of the last worker and the gap between them. **[2]**
**(c)** A wage floor of £15 is introduced. Calculate the effect on employment. **[3]**

**9.** Evaluate the likely effect on wages and employment of a bilateral monopoly. **[8]**

**10.** A night-shift role pays £19.50 an hour. A comparable day role pays £15.00.

**(a)** Calculate the night premium as a percentage of the day wage. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons for wage differentials other than compensation for unpleasant work. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the usefulness of wage determination theories in explaining pay in different regional and occupational sectors. **[10]**

**12.** A 6% pay rise in an invented occupation raises labour supply by 1.2% in the first year and by 9% after eight years.

**(a)** Calculate the wage elasticity of supply in each period. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the two values differ. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** What a worker must be paid to stay in the current job, which equals what the next best job would pay [1].
**(b)** Rent = 48,000 - 30,000 = £18,000 [1]. 18,000 ÷ 48,000 = **37.5%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* name the next best earnings as the transfer earnings before subtracting; the subtraction alone does not show you know which figure is which.

**2.** Wage change = 1.50 ÷ 12.00 = 12.5% [1]. Supply change = 36 ÷ 800 = 4.5% [1]. EWS = 4.5 ÷ 12.5 = **0.36**, inelastic, because supply rises proportionately less than the wage [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* show both percentage changes so the working is visible, and interpret the value using the word inelastic with a reason.

**3.** Any two, each identified then explained. For example: unsocial hours reduce supply because workers lose family and sleep time, so a higher wage is needed to compensate **[1][1]**. Personal safety risk deters workers, so supply is lower at any wage **[1][1]**. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* a factor that only restates pay (such as overtime rates) is monetary; keep to non-pay features and link each to willingness to supply.

**4. (a)** Wage +33.3% [1]; hours 36 to 40 = +11.1%, elasticity = **+0.33** [1].
**(b)** Wage +25% [1]; hours 40 to 37 = -7.5%, elasticity = **-0.30** [1].
**(c)** A higher wage makes leisure dearer, so the substitution effect raises hours [1]. A higher wage also lets the person reach a target income with fewer hours, so the income effect lowers hours [1]. Above £20 the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, so the net effect is negative [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* name both effects and say which dominates; describing only one gives an incomplete explanation of a backward bend.

**5. (a)** 16 = 4 + 0.01L, so L = **1,200** [1]. Total earnings = 16 x 1,200 = **19,200** [1]. Rent = 0.5 x (16 - 4) x 1,200 = **7,200** [1]. Transfer earnings = 19,200 - 7,200 = **12,000** [1].
**(b)** L = (20 - 4) ÷ 0.01 = 1,600 [1]. Rent = 0.5 x 16 x 1,600 = **12,800** [1].
**(c)** A flatter supply curve leaves a smaller triangle between the wage line and the curve [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* state the area you are calculating (the triangle above supply); give units as £000 per year so totals are not misread.

**6. (a)** 30 - 0.02L = 6 + 0.01L [1] gives L = **800** [1] and w = **£14** [1].
**(b)** 30 - 0.02L = 9 + 0.01L gives L = **700** [1] and w = **£16** [1]. Wage change = 2 ÷ 14 = **+14.3%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* equate the two curves in writing, then check the answer in both equations; it is the quickest self-check available.

**7. (a)** Demand at 18: (30 - 18) ÷ 0.02 = **600** employed [1]. Supply at 18: (18 - 6) ÷ 0.01 = **1,200** willing [1]. Unemployment = **600** [1].
**(b)** Before: 14 x 800 = £11,200 [1]. After: 18 x 600 = **£10,800** [1].
**(c)** Employment change = -200 ÷ 700 = -28.6%; wage change = 4 ÷ 16 = 25%; elasticity = **-1.14** [1]. Elastic, so the wage bill falls [1].
**(d)** For example, firms substitute capital for labour over time, or the unemployed undercut [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* the midpoint base should be the average of the two values; using the starting value for a large change gives a different, less reliable figure.

**8. (a)** Total cost = (6 + 0.01L)L, so MCL = **6 + 0.02L** [1]. Set MCL = MRP: 6 + 0.02L = 30 - 0.02L [1], so L = **600** [1].
**(b)** Wage from supply = 6 + 6 = **£12** [1]. MRP = 30 - 12 = £18, so the gap is **£6** [1].
**(c)** At £15, demand is (30 - 15) ÷ 0.02 = 750 [1]. Workers willing at £15 = 900, which is more than 750, so 750 are hired [1]. Employment rises by **150**, from 600 to 750 [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* the wage is read off the supply curve, not MCL; marking the wrong curve is a common way to lose the whole sequence of reasoning.

**9.** Define it: one employer facing one union [1]. The employer would push for the monopsony outcome, with lower wage and employment [1]. The union would push for a higher wage [1]. Theory gives a range, not a single wage [1]. The outcome depends on bargaining power, such as strike threats against relocation threats [1]. A union can offset monopsony power and raise both wages and employment [1]. An over-aggressive union may push the wage above MRP and cut employment [1]. Judgement: the result depends on relative power and the elasticity of labour demand, so the answer is a likely range, not a certain outcome [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* the evaluation gains credit when it names what the result depends on; "it depends" alone does not.

**10. (a)** Premium = 4.50 ÷ 15.00 [1] = **30%** [1].
**(b)** Any two, each with explanation. Skills and human capital: training raises MRP, so firms can pay more **[1][1]**. Union bargaining strength: a union with high coverage can negotiate a premium **[1][1]**. (Also acceptable: barriers to entry, monopsony power, discrimination, region.) **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "other than compensation" excludes risk and unsocial hours; repeating the stem's explanation scores nothing.

**11.** Competitive MRP theory fits sectors with many employers and measurable output [1]. It is weaker in public services with national pay scales [1], and where team output cannot be split by worker [1]. Monopsony explains low pay where one employer dominates a region or specialist occupation [1]. It is weaker where several employers lie within commuting distance [1]. Union bargaining theory fits high-density sectors [1]. Regional differences also reflect mobility and living costs [1]. Occupational differences reflect skills, licensing and barriers to entry [1]. Real markets mix the models, so none works alone [1]. Judgement: the best model is the one whose assumptions match the case (employers, visibility of output, union coverage) [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* tie each theory to a named type of sector; general praise or criticism of "the model" earns little.

**12. (a)** Short run: 1.2 ÷ 6 = **0.2** [1]. Long run: 9 ÷ 6 = **1.5** [1].
**(b)** In the short run the workforce is largely fixed, with little time to train or move [1]. In the long run people can train, migrate and enter from education, so supply is more elastic [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* give the reason for the difference, not just the two numbers.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reporting a ratio without both percentage changes.
- Treating all earnings as economic rent.
- Reading the monopsony wage off the MCL curve.
- Stopping at "wages rise" without following a union wage through to unemployment.
- Naming the substitution and income effects but not saying which dominates.
- Using the starting value when the midpoint method is asked for.
- Writing "it depends" with no statement of what it depends on.
- Leaving out units, such as £000 per year.
