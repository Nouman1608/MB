## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Devine interviewed manual workers employed at which workplace?

A. A Midlands coal mine
B. The Vauxhall car plant in Luton
C. A south coast comprehensive school
D. A London hospital

**[1]**

**2.** Which term describes prejudice or discrimination on grounds of age?

A. Institutional racism
B. Sexism
C. Ageism
D. Social closure

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "life chances". **[2]**

**4.** Describe what Lockwood meant by "privatised instrumentalism". **[2]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** way in which disability may affect a person's life chances. **[3]**

**6.** Identify and explain **one** way in which religion or belief may affect a person's life chances. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

> **Ravensby (a fictional city): percentage of adults reporting "good" or "very good" health, by neighbourhood income group, 2025**

| Neighbourhood income group | Adults surveyed | Reporting good or very good health |
|---|---|---|
| Highest income third | 300 | 255 |
| Middle income third | 300 | 216 |
| Lowest income third | 300 | 165 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of adults in the lowest income third reporting good or very good health. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the difference in percentage points between the highest and lowest income thirds. Show your working. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** reason why neighbourhood income might affect health. **[2]**

**8.** Explain **two** findings from Devine's study of affluent workers. **[4]**

**9.** Read the item.

> Priya, 58, was made redundant from a factory where she had worked for 30 years. She has applied for 40 jobs. At one interview she was asked whether she would "keep up with younger staff". She also cares for her mother most afternoons.

Using the item, explain how **two** different factors could be affecting Priya's life chances. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **one** strength and **one** limitation of Devine's research as evidence about the working class. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that social class is the most important factor affecting life chances in the UK today. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that rising living standards have made working-class people middle class. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B. The Vauxhall car plant in Luton [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* the school option is Ball's Beachside study and the Midlands option recalls Willis; keep studies and settings paired.

**2.** C. Ageism [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "ageism" is a key term in the specification's stratification list; spell it correctly.

**3.** A person's chances of achieving positive outcomes valued in society [1], such as good health, a long life, education, a good job and decent housing [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the second mark needs examples of the outcomes; "your chances in life" repeats the term.

**4.** Social life centred on the home and immediate family rather than the community [1], with work seen only as a means to an end, a way of earning money, rather than a source of collective solidarity [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* both halves of the term carry a mark: "privatised" and "instrumentalism".

**5.** Disabled people may face physical barriers, such as buildings or transport without step-free access [1]. This can limit where they can work or study [1], reducing their chances of a well-paid job and so their income and housing options [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* employer attitudes are an equally good mechanism; the chain from barrier to outcome earns the marks.

**6.** People whose religious identity is visible, for example through clothing, may face discrimination in hiring [1]. This can reduce their chances of getting a job or promotion [1], which affects income and, through that, housing and other life chances, even though such discrimination is unlawful [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* avoid stereotyping any faith group; explain the mechanism in general terms.

**7. (a)** 165 ÷ 300 × 100 = 55% [1]
**(b)** Highest third: 255 ÷ 300 × 100 = 85% [1]. 85 − 55 = 30 percentage points [1].
**(c)** Lower income may mean poorer housing, such as damp or overcrowding, or a less healthy diet [1], which raises the risk of illness and so lowers reported health [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* in part (b) the answer is in percentage points, not a percentage; the unit matters.

**8.** First, Devine found no evidence to support privatised instrumentalism [1]: her sample's lifestyle was less communal than the traditional working class but not as home-centred as Lockwood predicted [1]. Second, although living standards and consumer aspirations had risen [1], many respondents still resented inherited wealth and felt a sense of injustice about extreme class inequality [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* other creditable findings are that they had lost faith in the Labour Party and that she rejected the "new working class" idea.

**9.** Age: the interview question suggests ageism [1], since Priya is being judged on assumptions about older workers, which may explain why she has not been hired after 40 applications [1]. Gender: Priya cares for her mother most afternoons, reflecting the expectation that women do unpaid caring [1], which limits the hours she can work and so her income and job options [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* class is also defensible (factory redundancy, deindustrialisation); any two factors from the specification list, each tied to the item, can score.

**10.** Strength: interviews gave detailed accounts of workers' and wives' views, and returning to Luton allowed a direct comparison with the 1960s research [1], so changes over time can be seen in the same place [1]. Limitation: the sample was male manual workers at one car plant in one town [1], so the findings may not represent women, service workers or other regions [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* method points score when they are tied to the conclusion the study is used to support.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Life chances are chances of good outcomes such as health, education, work and housing [1].
- Weber linked shared class situation to shared life chances [1].
- Class shapes income, which affects diet, housing and health [1].
- Class gaps in education, such as Halsey, Heath and Ridge's findings on university entry, support class as central [1].
- Marxists see class as the root inequality under capitalism [1].
- Devine found affluent workers still felt a sense of class injustice, suggesting class identity endures [1].
- Against: gender shapes life chances through unpaid caring, the pay gap and the glass ceiling [1].
- Race and ethnicity: racial discrimination and institutional racism affect jobs and policing across classes [1].
- Age, disability and sexuality also affect outcomes, protected under the Equality Act 2010 [1].
- Functionalists argue differences reflect talent and effort, and social mobility weakens class effects [1].
- Factors overlap: class often shapes how far gender or ethnicity limit someone [1].
- Judgement: class has the widest effect across health, education and work, but gender and ethnicity operate within every class, so class is the most pervasive factor but not the only important one [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* a strong answer compares factors directly rather than describing each one in turn.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Embourgeoisement is the idea that affluent workers become middle class [1].
- Rising wages allow manual workers to buy homes, cars and consumer goods once linked to the middle class [1].
- Lockwood predicted privatised instrumentalism among affluent workers [1].
- Devine found rising living standards and consumer aspirations in Luton [1].
- But she found no evidence for privatised instrumentalism [1].
- She rejected the "new working class" idea; workers had not accepted capitalism uncritically [1].
- Many still resented inherited wealth and extreme inequality, a traditional working-class value [1].
- Marxists argue workers still sell their labour, so their class position is unchanged by consumption [1].
- Weber: market situation, not lifestyle, defines class; manual jobs still offer less security [1].
- Subjective class: many affluent manual workers still describe themselves as working class [1].
- Evaluation: Devine's sample was one plant in one town in the late 1980s [1].
- Judgement: rising incomes changed lifestyles, but class identity and position remained, so the claim is largely rejected [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* this is Devine's home ground; describe her findings accurately and use them as the core of the argument.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using Devine as evidence for embourgeoisement.
- Giving percentage instead of percentage points for a difference.
- Naming a factor with no explanation of how it works.
- Ignoring the details of an item, such as Priya's caring role.
