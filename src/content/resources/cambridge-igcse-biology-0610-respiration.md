## 12.1.1 The uses of energy

Respiration releases energy from nutrient molecules inside cells. Outcome 1 asks what that energy is **used for**. The syllabus names seven uses. Learn the exact phrases, because examiners credit the syllabus wording.

| Use of energy | What the energy does | Example in a human | Example in a plant or microorganism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Muscle contraction | allows muscle cells to shorten and pull | leg muscles in walking; heart muscle | not applicable: plants have no muscle |
| Protein synthesis | joins amino acids into proteins | making antibodies and enzymes | making enzymes in a yeast cell |
| Cell division | copies cell contents and splits cells | replacing skin cells | cells dividing at a root tip |
| Active transport | moves substances against a concentration gradient | absorbing glucose in the small intestine | root hair cells taking up ions from soil |
| Growth | builds new cells and tissues | a child getting taller | a seedling increasing in size |
| Passage of nerve impulses | keeps impulses travelling along neurones | impulse from eye to brain | not applicable: plants have no neurones |
| Maintenance of a constant body temperature | releases heat to keep the body warm | keeping the body close to 37 °C | not applicable: plants do not keep a constant body temperature |

### Applying the list

Questions often describe an organism and ask which uses apply. Work through the list and cross out uses that need a structure the organism lacks.

**Worked example 1.** *State three uses of energy in a cactus plant.*

- Muscle contraction: no muscles, so cross out.
- Nerve impulses: no neurones, so cross out.
- Constant body temperature: plants do not maintain one, so cross out.
- That leaves: **protein synthesis, cell division, active transport, growth**. Any three score.

**Worked example 2.** *A sprinter's leg muscles release a lot of energy during a race. State the use of this energy and one other use in the same muscles after the race.*

- During the race: **muscle contraction**.
- After the race, for repair and strengthening: **protein synthesis** (making new muscle proteins) or **cell division**. Some energy released also helps **maintain body temperature**.

"Movement", "keeping warm", "making proteins" and "thinking" are weaker than the syllabus phrases. "Breathing" is not a use of energy on the list; breathing movements are muscle contraction.

## 12.1.2 Temperature and respiration in yeast

Yeast is a single-celled fungus. Given glucose and water, its cells respire and release **carbon dioxide**. The faster the carbon dioxide is released, the faster the yeast is respiring, so the volume of gas per minute is a measure of the **rate of respiration**.

### Method: measuring gas volume with a syringe

1. Put a fixed volume of glucose solution of a fixed concentration into a boiling tube.
2. Add a fixed mass of dried yeast, or a fixed volume of yeast suspension, and stir.
3. Stand the tube in a thermostatically controlled water bath at the first temperature, for example 20 °C. Leave it for 10 minutes so the mixture reaches that temperature.
4. Connect the tube by a delivery tube to a gas syringe.
5. Record the volume of gas in the syringe after a fixed time, for example 5 minutes.
6. Repeat at least twice more at this temperature, using fresh yeast and glucose each time, and calculate a mean.
7. Repeat at 30 °C, 40 °C, 50 °C and 60 °C.

Measuring gas volume is more accurate than counting bubbles, because bubbles vary in size.

### Variables and controls

| Type | In this investigation |
|---|---|
| Independent variable | temperature of the water bath |
| Dependent variable | volume of carbon dioxide released in a fixed time |
| Controlled variables | concentration and volume of glucose solution; mass of yeast; time allowed to warm up; time over which gas is collected; type of yeast |
| Control | a tube with glucose solution but no yeast, at each temperature: no gas should be produced, showing the gas comes from the yeast |

### An alternative detection method

**Hydrogencarbonate indicator** changes colour as carbon dioxide concentration rises: from **red** (atmospheric level) to **yellow** (high carbon dioxide). Place indicator in a tube above the yeast mixture, separated by gauze, and time how long it takes to turn yellow at each temperature. A **shorter** time means a **faster** rate.

### Worked example 3: analysing results as a rate

A student collected gas for 5 minutes at each temperature. (Example data written for this guide.)

| Temperature / °C | Mean volume of gas in 5 minutes / cm³ | Rate / cm³ per minute |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 3.0 | 0.6 |
| 30 | 7.5 | 1.5 |
| 40 | 12.0 | 2.4 |
| 50 | 6.0 | 1.2 |
| 60 | 0.5 | 0.1 |

**Calculating the rate:** rate = volume ÷ time. At 40 °C: 12.0 ÷ 5 = **2.4 cm³ per minute**.

**Describing the trend (with data):**

- As temperature increases from 20 °C to 40 °C, the rate of respiration **increases**, from 0.6 to 2.4 cm³ per minute.
- The highest rate in these results is at **40 °C**.
- Above 40 °C the rate **decreases**, to 0.1 cm³ per minute at 60 °C.

**Explaining the trend (Core level):** respiration is a series of reactions controlled by **enzymes**. Up to the optimum, higher temperatures speed up the enzyme-controlled reactions. Above the optimum, the enzymes are **denatured**, so the rate falls.

**What you can and cannot conclude:** the true optimum lies between 30 °C and 50 °C. To find it more precisely, repeat with temperatures closer together, such as every 2 °C between 34 °C and 46 °C.

### Drawing the graph

Put temperature on the x-axis and rate on the y-axis, with units on both. Plot points accurately and join them with straight lines point to point, or a smooth curve if the pattern is clear. Do not extend the line beyond the data.

## Common mistakes

- Giving "energy is used for movement" instead of **muscle contraction**.
- Listing breathing, digestion or "living" as uses of energy.
- Giving muscle contraction or nerve impulses as uses in a plant.
- Saying respiration "makes" or "produces" energy. It **releases** it.
- Calling temperature a controlled variable in the yeast investigation; it is the **independent** variable.
- Forgetting to let the mixture reach the bath temperature before collecting gas.
- Saying "the yeast is denatured" or "the yeast dies" as the full explanation. Say the **enzymes** are denatured.
- Stating the optimum is exactly the temperature with the highest reading when readings are 10 °C apart.

## Exam technique for 12.1 command words

- **State**: use the syllabus phrase for each use of energy, one per line.
- **Investigate**: in a planning question, include independent, dependent and controlled variables, repeats, and how the rate is measured.
- **Describe** results: trend, highest point, fall, and quoted values with units.
- **Explain**: link the trend to enzymes and denaturing.
- **Suggest** an improvement: smaller temperature intervals, more repeats, or a gas syringe instead of counting bubbles.
