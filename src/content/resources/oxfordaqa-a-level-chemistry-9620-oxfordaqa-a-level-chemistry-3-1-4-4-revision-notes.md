These notes condense **3.1.4.4 Bond enthalpies** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. For full worked examples, read the [bond enthalpies study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-4/); for exam-style questions, use the [bond enthalpies practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-4-4-practice/).

## Core ideas

- **Mean bond enthalpy**: enthalpy change to break **one mole** of a specified covalent bond in **gaseous** molecules, **averaged** over a range of compounds.
- Bond breaking is **endothermic**; bond making is **exothermic**.
- **ΔH ≈ Σ(broken) − Σ(formed)** for reactions in the **gaseous phase**.
- Answers are **approximate** and differ from Hess's law values.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bond enthalpy | Energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond in gaseous molecules |
| Mean bond enthalpy | That energy averaged over many different compounds containing the bond |
| Bonds broken | Bonds in the reactants that do not survive; energy taken in |
| Bonds formed | Bonds in the products that were not in the reactants; energy released |
| Displayed formula | A formula showing every atom and every bond, used for counting bonds |
| Gaseous phase | All reactants and products are gases, as bond enthalpies require |

## Method in five steps

1. Draw displayed formulae.
2. Count each bond broken (× balancing number).
3. Count each bond formed (× balancing number).
4. Add each list.
5. ΔH = broken − formed; give sign and kJ mol⁻¹.

Shortcut: bonds that appear unchanged on both sides cancel, so you may count only those that change.

## Reading the answer

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Broken > formed | ΔH positive, endothermic |
| Broken < formed | ΔH negative, exothermic |

## Reverse problems

Set up broken − formed = ΔH with the unknown bond as x, then solve.

Example: ΔH for H₂(g) + F₂(g) → 2HF(g) is −542 kJ mol⁻¹, H–H 436, F–F 158.

```
(436 + 158) − 2x = −542
594 + 542 = 2x
x = 568 kJ mol⁻¹ (H–F)
```

## Why estimates differ from Hess's law values

- **Mean values**: the actual bond enthalpies in these particular molecules differ from the averages used.
- **States**: bond enthalpies apply to gases; if the Hess's law value involves liquids (such as H₂O(l)), the estimate ignores the enthalpy of changes of state.
- The Hess's law value, from measured data for the actual compounds, is more accurate.
- Diatomic bonds (H–H, O=O, Cl–Cl) have exact values, as each comes from one compound only.

## Typical comparisons

| Reaction | Bond enthalpy estimate | Hess's law value |
|---|---|---|
| CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O, all gases | −814 kJ mol⁻¹ | about −802 kJ mol⁻¹ |
| C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ | −125 kJ mol⁻¹ | −137 kJ mol⁻¹ |
| ½N₂ + 1½H₂ → NH₃ | −46.5 kJ mol⁻¹ | −46 kJ mol⁻¹ |

## Exam traps

- Formed − broken (wrong sign).
- Missing bonds, or forgetting the balancing number in front of O₂ or H₂O.
- Using C–O (358) for C=O (805), or counting C=C as two C–C bonds.
- Not halving when the target is "per mole" but the equation makes two moles.
- Explaining the difference from Hess's law as experimental error or heat loss.
- Using bond enthalpies for a reaction with liquids without noting the extra discrepancy.
- Leaving "gaseous" or "averaged" out of the definition.

## Self-test

1. Define mean bond enthalpy.
2. Why are all bond enthalpy values positive?
3. Write the expression for ΔH in terms of bond enthalpies.
4. Why is the value for Cl–Cl exact rather than a mean?
5. Using C–H 413, Cl–Cl 243, C–Cl 346 and H–Cl 432, calculate ΔH for CH₄(g) + Cl₂(g) → CH₃Cl(g) + HCl(g).
6. Using H–H 436, O=O 498 and O–H 463, calculate ΔH for 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(g).
7. From your answer to 6, give ΔH per mole of H₂O(g).
8. The Hess's law value for question 6 is −242 kJ per mole of H₂O(g). Give one reason for the difference.
9. Why would the difference be bigger if the product were H₂O(l)?
10. How many C–H bonds are broken when one molecule of propane, C₃H₈, burns completely?
11. ΔH for H₂(g) + I₂(g) → 2HI(g) is −11 kJ mol⁻¹. H–H 436 and I–I 151. Find H–I.
12. Which value is more reliable for a specific reaction: a bond enthalpy estimate or a Hess's law value?

### Answers

1. The enthalpy change to break one mole of a specified covalent bond in gaseous molecules, averaged over a range of compounds.
2. Breaking a bond always needs energy (it is endothermic).
3. ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed).
4. It is found in only one compound (Cl₂), so there is nothing to average.
5. Broken: 413 + 243 = 656; formed: 346 + 432 = 778; ΔH = **−122 kJ mol⁻¹**.
6. Broken: 2(436) + 498 = 1370; formed: 4(463) = 1852; ΔH = **−482 kJ mol⁻¹**.
7. −482 ÷ 2 = **−241 kJ mol⁻¹**.
8. The O–H value is a mean taken over many compounds, not exact for water.
9. The estimate does not include the energy released when water vapour condenses.
10. Eight.
11. (436 + 151) − 2x = −11, so 2x = 598 and x = **299 kJ mol⁻¹**.
12. The Hess's law value, because it is based on measured data for the actual compounds.
