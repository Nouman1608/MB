## The paper in brief

- One written paper: 1 hour and 20 minutes, 50 marks, all questions compulsory.
- Short-open, open-response and calculation questions; at least 6 marks of Level 2 mathematics.
- Calculators may be used. Contexts can be familiar or new.
- Skills tested: solving practical problems, controlling variables, presenting and evaluating data, uncertainty, graphs, significant figures, accuracy and precision, and choosing and using apparatus safely.

## Key equations and ratios

| Situation | Relationship |
|---|---|
| Iodine and thiosulfate | I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2 |
| Manganate(VII) and iron(II) | MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5 |
| Iodate(V) releasing iodine | IO₃⁻ + 5I⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3I₂ + 3H₂O, so IO₃⁻ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 6 |
| Clock reactions | Initial rate ∝ 1/t |
| Arrhenius (given if needed) | ln k = −Ea/(RT) + constant; Ea = −gradient × R |
| Half-neutralisation | pH = pKa; Ka = 10^(−pH) |
| Weak acid of known concentration | Ka ≈ [H⁺]² ÷ [HA] |
| Cell emf | E⦵cell = E⦵(positive electrode) − E⦵(negative electrode) |
| Percentage uncertainty | (uncertainty ÷ value) × 100; a titre or difference carries twice the single-reading uncertainty |
| Percentage yield | (actual mass ÷ theoretical mass) × 100 |

## Method in steps

**Core Practical 9a, quench and titrate**
1. Start the clock when the iodine is added.
2. Pipette a sample into sodium hydrogencarbonate solution; note the time.
3. Titrate with thiosulfate; add starch when pale yellow.
4. Plot titre (or [I₂]) against time. A straight line means zero order in iodine.

**Core Practical 9b, clock reaction**
1. Fix every volume except one; make up the total with water.
2. Mix quickly, start timing, stop at the blue-black colour.
3. Compare 1/t values: ×2 concentration with ×2 rate is first order; ×4 rate is second order.

**Core Practical 10, activation energy**
1. Bring the solutions to temperature separately.
2. Mix, time, and record the mixture's temperature.
3. Plot ln(1/t) (y) against 1/T in K⁻¹ (x). Ea = −gradient × 8.31, then divide by 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹.

**Core Practical 11, Ka**
1. Calibrate the pH meter with buffers.
2. Find the end-point volume, V, for one portion of acid.
3. Add V/2 of alkali to an identical portion; read the pH.

**Core Practical 12, cells**
1. Clean metals with emery paper.
2. Use a potassium nitrate salt bridge (it forms no precipitate with metal ions).
3. Read the emf on a high-resistance voltmeter.

**Core Practicals 13a and 13b, redox titrations**
1. Rinse the burette with the titrant and the pipette with the sample solution.
2. Manganate(VII): acidify with dilute sulfuric acid; end point is the first permanent pale pink.
3. Thiosulfate: titrate to pale yellow, add starch, continue until the blue-black goes.
4. Repeat until titres agree within 0.10 cm³; average only those.

**Core Practicals 14 and 16, preparations**
1. Use the limiting reagent for the theoretical yield.
2. Collect crystals by filtration under reduced pressure; wash with a small volume of cold solvent.
3. Recrystallise: minimum hot solvent, filter hot, cool, filter, dry.
4. Check purity by melting temperature: sharp and close to the data value means pure.

## Worked reminder: standardising thiosulfate

A standard solution holds 0.642 g of KIO₃ (Mr 214.0) in 250.0 cm³. Each 25.0 cm³ aliquot is treated with excess KI and dilute acid; the iodine released needs a mean of 22.50 cm³ of thiosulfate.

- KIO₃ per aliquot = (0.642 ÷ 214.0) ÷ 10 = 3.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol
- I₂ released = 3 × 3.00 × 10⁻⁴ = 9.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol; thiosulfate used = 2 × 9.00 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.80 × 10⁻³ mol
- c(S₂O₃²⁻) = 1.80 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02250 = **0.0800 mol dm⁻³**

## Must-know distinctions

- **Equivalence point vs half-neutralisation:** pKa is the pH at half the end-point volume, not at the end point.
- **Quenching vs slowing:** removing the acid catalyst stops the iodination; dilution with cold water only slows it.
- **Accuracy vs precision:** a measured E⦵cell close to the calculated value is accurate; repeat readings close together are precise.
- **Random vs systematic error:** repeats reduce random error; air oxidation of iron(II) before titration is systematic and lowers every result.
- **Yield vs purity:** a high mass of wet or impure product is not a high yield of pure product.

## Quick self-test

1. A 10.0 cm³ quenched sample needs 12.50 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ thiosulfate. Find [I₂].
2. In a clock reaction, doubling [X] cuts the time from 64 s to 16 s. What is the order in X?
3. At half-neutralisation the pH is 5.12. Find Ka.
4. Use E⦵: Mg²⁺/Mg = −2.37 V and Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V. Find E⦵cell.
5. A titre of 18.00 cm³ of 0.0150 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄ reacts with how many moles of Fe²⁺?
6. The burette is read to ±0.05 cm³ at the start and at the end. Find the percentage uncertainty in a 22.50 cm³ titre.
7. The Arrhenius graph (ln k on y, 1/T on x) slopes at −6200 K. Find Ea (R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).
8. What is seen when NaOH(aq) is added to Fe³⁺(aq), then in excess?
9. A crude aspirin sample melts over 126 to 131 °C. What does this show?
10. Why is a potassium chloride salt bridge unsuitable with a silver half-cell?
11. Why is the sample in Core Practical 9a run into sodium hydrogencarbonate?
12. Why is ethanol added when preparing tetraamminecopper(II) sulfate?

### Answers

1. n(S₂O₃²⁻) = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ mol; n(I₂) = 1.25 × 10⁻⁴ mol; [I₂] = **0.0125 mol dm⁻³**.
2. Rate rises 4 times when [X] doubles: **second order**.
3. Ka = 10^(−5.12) = **7.59 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³**.
4. 0.34 − (−2.37) = **+2.71 V**.
5. 5 × 0.0150 × 0.01800 = **1.35 × 10⁻³ mol**.
6. 0.10 ÷ 22.50 × 100 = **0.44%**.
7. Ea = 6200 × 8.31 = 51 500 J mol⁻¹ = **51.5 kJ mol⁻¹**.
8. Red-brown precipitate; **no change** in excess.
9. It is **impure**: impurities lower the melting temperature and widen the range.
10. Chloride ions would **precipitate silver chloride**, changing [Ag⁺].
11. It **neutralises the acid catalyst**, stopping the reaction at a known time.
12. The complex is **less soluble in ethanol**, so it crystallises out.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "colour change" without giving both the starting and final colours.
- Recording the time a sample was drawn into the pipette rather than the time it was quenched.
- Plotting ln t instead of ln(1/t), then reporting a negative activation energy.
- Leaving Ea in J mol⁻¹ when the question asks for kJ mol⁻¹, or using T in °C.
- Taking pKa from the pH at the end point.
- Writing the cell diagram with the positive electrode on the left.
- Missing the 1 : 5 or 1 : 2 ratio in a redox titration, or the scale-up from portion to flask.
- Giving a general hazard ("chemicals are dangerous") instead of a named hazard with a matching precaution.
- Quoting a percentage yield to more significant figures than the masses justify.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long is the Edexcel IAL Chemistry Unit 6 exam?**
Unit 6: Practical Skills in Chemistry II in the Edexcel IAL Chemistry specification (YCH11) is one written paper of 1 hour and 20 minutes, out of 50 marks, with every question compulsory. It tests the practical work of Units 4 and 5, including Core Practicals 9 to 16, and the tests from Units 1 and 2.

**How do you find the end point in a thiosulfate-iodine titration?**
Run in thiosulfate until the brown iodine colour fades to pale yellow, then add starch, which turns blue-black. The end point is the first drop that removes the blue-black colour completely.

**What is the end point in a potassium manganate(VII) titration?**
Manganate(VII) acts as its own indicator, so the end point is the first permanent pale pink colour. The titration is acidified with dilute sulfuric acid.

**Why is a potassium nitrate salt bridge used in electrochemical cells?**
Potassium nitrate forms no precipitate with the metal ions in the half-cells. Potassium chloride would precipitate silver chloride in a silver half-cell and change the concentration of silver ions.

**How do you show an aspirin sample is pure?**
Measure its melting temperature. A pure sample melts sharply close to the data value, while impurities lower the melting temperature and widen the range.
