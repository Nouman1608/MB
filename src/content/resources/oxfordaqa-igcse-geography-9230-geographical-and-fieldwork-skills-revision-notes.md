These notes cover section 3.3, Geographical and Fieldwork skills, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3 (exams May/June 2020 onwards): 3.3.1 Geographical skills and 3.3.2 Fieldwork skills and the individual fieldwork enquiry. Paper 3 (1 hour 15 minutes, 60 marks, 28%) has three compulsory 20-mark sections: Section A geographical skills, Section B fieldwork in unfamiliar contexts and Section C your own enquiry. Geographical skills can also be tested on Papers 1 and 2.

Test yourself afterwards with the [practice questions for this unit](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills-practice/). The [OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/igcse/geography/) and the [9230 checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/igcse/geography/) cover the other units.

## Paper 3 at a glance

- **Section A** (20 marks): geographical skills.
- **Section B** (20 marks): the enquiry process applied to fieldwork you have not done.
- **Section C** (20 marks): your own individual enquiry. You must identify its title.
- **Assessment objectives**: 34 marks AO3, 22 marks AO4, 4 marks AO2, 0 marks AO1. Using the resource matters more than recall.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Four-figure grid reference | Names a whole grid square: easting then northing |
| Six-figure grid reference | Names a point to one-tenth of a square |
| Scale 1:50 000 | 1 cm = 500 m; 2 cm = 1 km |
| Scale 1:25 000 | 1 cm = 250 m; 4 cm = 1 km |
| Contour | Line joining points of equal height |
| Gradient | Vertical rise / horizontal distance, same units, written "1 in x" |
| Cross-section | Side view of relief along a line |
| Transect | Line along which changes are recorded |
| Isoline | Line joining points of equal value |
| Choropleth | Areas shaded by class of value |
| Desire line | Straight line from origin to destination |
| Flow line | Line along a route; width shows the amount |
| Modal class | The group with the highest frequency |
| Inter-quartile range | Upper quartile minus lower quartile |
| Percentile | Value below which a given percentage of data lies |
| Interpolation | Estimating within the range of the data |
| Extrapolation | Estimating beyond the range of the data |
| Anomaly | A result that does not fit the general pattern |
| Reliability | Repeating the method gives similar results |
| Accuracy | How close a reading is to the true value |
| Control group | A comparison where the tested factor is absent |
| Hypothesis | A testable statement predicting a result |

## Skills at a glance

| Skill | How to do it | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|
| Six-figure grid reference | Eastings: two digits plus tenths across; then northings: two digits plus tenths up | Only as precise as your estimate of tenths |
| Distance | Measure in cm, multiply by the scale; use string for curves | Map may not show changes since it was printed |
| Gradient | Rise from contours or spot heights / horizontal distance in metres | Average gradient hides steep and gentle sections |
| Mean | Total / number of values | Pulled by extreme values |
| Median | Middle value of ordered data; average of two middle values if n is even | Ignores the size of other values |
| Mode / modal class | Most frequent value or group | Can be unclear with small samples |
| Range | Highest minus lowest | Set by the two extremes only |
| Quartiles | Positions (n + 1) / 4 and 3(n + 1) / 4 in ordered data | Need enough data to be meaningful |
| Percentage change | (new - old) / old x 100 | Large percentages from small starting numbers can mislead |
| Pie chart angle | Percentage x 3.6 degrees | Hard to compare many small segments |
| Line of best fit | By eye, balanced points either side, close to the mean point | Shows a link, not a cause |
| Cumulative frequency | Running total; median at half the total | Grouped data loses exact values |

## Graph and map choice

- Change over time: **line chart**.
- Categories: **bar chart**. Continuous grouped data: **histogram** (equal class intervals, bars touching).
- Parts of a whole: **pie chart** or **divided bar**.
- Two variables: **scattergraph**.
- Age and sex: **population pyramid**.
- Spread of one data set: **dispersion graph**.
- Values for areas: **choropleth**. Continuous surface (such as temperature): **isoline**.
- Where people come from: **desire lines**. Amount along a route: **flow lines**.
- Quantity at points: **proportional symbols** or **dot map**.

## Describing patterns on maps and graphs

1. **Overall pattern first**: where values are high and low, or whether the trend rises, falls or stays level.
2. **Quote evidence**: at least two figures with units, or grid references on a map.
3. **Name the exception**: an anomaly, a cluster, or a change in the trend.
4. **Stop if the question says "describe"**. Save reasons for questions that ask you to explain or suggest; they use up time on a describe question.

On an atlas or thematic map, also say whether the pattern is clustered, linear (along a coast, river or road) or evenly spread, and whether it matches another map, such as relief or rainfall. On an isoline map, closely spaced lines mean the value changes quickly over a short distance.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Label vs annotation**: a label names; an annotation explains.
- **Primary vs secondary data**: collected by you vs by someone else.
- **Qualitative vs quantitative**: words and images vs numbers.
- **Accuracy vs reliability**: close to the truth vs repeatable.
- **Interpolation vs extrapolation**: safer within the data than beyond it.
- **Desire lines vs flow lines**: straight origin-to-destination vs along a route with width for amount.
- **Bar chart vs histogram**: gaps for categories vs touching bars for continuous data.
- **Section B vs Section C**: unfamiliar fieldwork vs your own titled enquiry.
- **Correlation vs cause**: two variables can change together without one causing the other.
- **Magnitude vs frequency**: bigger events usually happen less often.

## Sampling

| Method | How | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random | Random numbers pick sites or people | Avoids your bias | May cluster or miss areas |
| Systematic | Regular interval, such as every 20 m | Even coverage, easy | Can miss features between points |
| Stratified | Sample groups in proportion | Represents each group | Needs prior information |

## The six enquiry strands

1. **Suitable question**: a hypothesis or question linked to a theory, with safe, accessible locations and a risk assessment.
2. **Selecting, measuring and recording**: primary and secondary data, physical and human data, sampling, justified methods.
3. **Processing and presenting**: choose methods that suit the data; adapt them if needed.
4. **Describing, analysing, explaining**: use figures, link data sets, use statistics, spot anomalies.
5. **Conclusions**: answer the original aim with evidence.
6. **Evaluation**: problems with methods, limits of data, other useful data, reliability of conclusions.

Alternative data collection is allowed if it involves planning and selecting, not pre-processed data: for example, choosing internet data, raw teacher data, interviews in school or at home, or selecting from photographs and maps.

## Mini essay plan

**Question:** "Assess how far the conclusions of your individual fieldwork enquiry answered your original enquiry question."

1. **Introduction**: state your title and hypothesis, and the theory behind it.
2. **Where the conclusion was strong**: quote the clearest result with figures and a statistic (for example, a median or range at each site).
3. **Where it was weaker**: name an anomaly or a site where the pattern broke, and explain why.
4. **Method limits**: sample size, timing, one day only, equipment accuracy.
5. **Other data**: say what extra primary or secondary data would test the conclusion.
6. **Judgement**: "largely", "partly" or "only weakly", with the main reason.

Extended answers are normally marked by levels, so a clear judgement backed by your own data matters more than the number of points.

## Quick self-test

1. Convert 5.2 cm on a 1:50 000 map to a real distance.
2. A symbol lies in square 2865, four-tenths east and nine-tenths north. Give the six-figure grid reference.
3. Ground rises 80 m over a horizontal distance of 1.2 km. Give the gradient as "1 in x".
4. Find the median of 4, 7, 9, 12, 20, 21.
5. A value rises from 250 to 310. Calculate the percentage increase.
6. How many degrees represent 35% on a pie chart?
7. Convert 2.4 cm on a 1:25 000 map to metres.
8. Which map shows straight lines from where people live to where they went?
9. Students record vegetation every 20 m along a line. Name the sampling method.
10. What is the difference between interpolation and extrapolation?
11. What must you identify at the start of Section C answers?

### Answers

1. 5.2 x 500 m = 2,600 m = **2.6 km**.
2. **284659**.
3. 1.2 km = 1,200 m; 1,200 / 80 = 15, so **1 in 15**.
4. Middle values are 9 and 12, so the median is **10.5**.
5. (310 - 250) / 250 x 100 = **24%**.
6. 35 x 3.6 = **126 degrees**.
7. 2.4 x 250 = **600 m**.
8. A **desire line** map.
9. **Systematic** sampling.
10. Interpolation estimates **within** the data range; extrapolation estimates **beyond** it and is less reliable.
11. The **title** of your individual fieldwork enquiry.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing northings before eastings, or giving four figures when six are asked for.
- Forgetting to convert centimetres to metres before working out gradient.
- Dividing by the new value instead of the original in percentage change.
- Finding the median from unordered data.
- Joining points dot to dot on a scattergraph instead of drawing one line of best fit.
- Describing a graph without quoting figures and units.
- Naming a sampling method without explaining how it was carried out or why it suited the enquiry.
- Giving a risk without a matching way to reduce it.
- Concluding without linking back to the original question or hypothesis.
- In Section C, describing fieldwork in general rather than your own titled enquiry.
