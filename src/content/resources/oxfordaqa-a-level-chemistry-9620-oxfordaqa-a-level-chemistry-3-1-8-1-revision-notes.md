## Core recall

- Lattice enthalpy can be defined as **lattice dissociation** (endothermic) or **lattice formation** (exothermic): same size, opposite sign.
- A Born–Haber cycle uses: **enthalpy of formation, ionisation energy, enthalpy of atomisation, bond enthalpy, electron affinity**.
- Hess's law: ΔfH = (steps from elements to gaseous ions) + lattice enthalpy of formation.
- Any **one** unknown can be found if the rest are known.
- Born–Haber value **more exothermic** than the perfect ionic model value → **covalent character**.
- Solution cycle: **ΔsolH = lattice dissociation + Σ ΔhydH**.

## Key terms

| Term | Equation for one mole (example) | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Enthalpy of formation | Ca(s) + Cl₂(g) → CaCl₂(s) | Usually negative |
| Enthalpy of atomisation | ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g); Ca(s) → Ca(g) | Positive |
| Bond enthalpy | Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) | Positive |
| First ionisation energy | Ca(g) → Ca⁺(g) + e⁻ | Positive |
| Second ionisation energy | Ca⁺(g) → Ca²⁺(g) + e⁻ | Positive, larger |
| First electron affinity | Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g) | Negative for halogens and O |
| Second electron affinity | O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) | **Positive** (repulsion) |
| Lattice dissociation | CaCl₂(s) → Ca²⁺(g) + 2Cl⁻(g) | Positive |
| Lattice formation | Ca²⁺(g) + 2Cl⁻(g) → CaCl₂(s) | Negative |
| Enthalpy of hydration | Ca²⁺(g) → Ca²⁺(aq) | Negative |
| Enthalpy of solution | CaCl₂(s) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) | Either sign |

## Cycle equations by formula type

| Compound type | Steps from elements to gaseous ions |
|---|---|
| MX (e.g. KCl) | ΔatH(M) + IE₁(M) + ΔatH(X) + EA₁(X) |
| MX₂ (e.g. CaCl₂) | ΔatH(M) + IE₁ + IE₂ + 2ΔatH(X) + 2EA₁(X) |
| M₂O (e.g. Na₂O) | 2ΔatH(M) + 2IE₁(M) + ΔatH(O) + EA₁(O) + EA₂(O) |
| MO (e.g. MgO) | ΔatH(M) + IE₁ + IE₂ + ΔatH(O) + EA₁(O) + EA₂(O) |

Then: **lattice formation = ΔfH − (steps to gaseous ions)**.

**Atomisation and bond enthalpy**: for a gaseous diatomic element, ΔatH = ½ × bond enthalpy. Two Cl atoms need one full Cl–Cl bond broken.

## Drawing the cycle

- Elements in standard states at **zero**.
- Endothermic steps (atomisation, ionisation, second EA) are arrows **up**.
- Exothermic steps (first EA, lattice formation, usually ΔfH) are arrows **down**.
- Label every level with all species and state symbols, including electrons.

## Covalent character

- Perfect ionic model: spherical ions, evenly spread charge, purely electrostatic attraction.
- Values close → bonding nearly purely ionic.
- Born–Haber value more exothermic → extra attraction from **covalent character**.
- Caused by **polarisation** of the anion by the cation: greatest for a **small or highly charged cation** and a **large anion**.

## Hydration and solution

- Hydration is always exothermic: water's δ− oxygen attracts cations, δ+ hydrogen attracts anions.
- Smaller or more highly charged ions have **more exothermic** hydration enthalpies.
- Count each ion: for MgCl₂, add **two** ΔhydH(Cl⁻).
- ΔsolH negative → dissolving releases heat; positive → solution cools.

## Exam traps

- One multiplier missed for two identical ions (Na₂O, CaCl₂).
- Full bond enthalpy used for one mole of atoms.
- Second electron affinity given a negative sign.
- Lattice formation and dissociation confused.
- A 2+ ion with only the first ionisation energy.
- Hydration counted once for two anions.
- Units left off: all values in **kJ mol⁻¹**.
- Positive answer for an atomisation found as negative: a sign error somewhere.

## Quick self-test

1. Define lattice enthalpy of dissociation.
2. Why is the second electron affinity of oxygen endothermic?
3. The Cl–Cl bond enthalpy is +242 kJ mol⁻¹. What is ΔatH(Cl)?
4. Write the equation for the second ionisation energy of calcium.
5. CaO data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH −635; ΔatH(Ca) +178; IE₁ +590; IE₂ +1145; ΔatH(O) +249; EA₁ −141; EA₂ +798. Calculate the lattice enthalpy of formation.
6. LiF data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH −616; IE₁(Li) +520; ΔatH(F) +79; EA₁(F) −328; lattice formation −1046. Calculate ΔatH(Li).
7. What does a Born–Haber lattice enthalpy much more exothermic than the perfect ionic value show?
8. Which pair would show more covalent character: a small 2+ cation with a large anion, or a large 1+ cation with a small anion?
9. Define enthalpy of hydration.
10. KBr (kJ mol⁻¹): lattice dissociation +670; ΔhydH(K⁺) −322; ΔhydH(Br⁻) −335. Calculate ΔsolH.
11. CaCl₂ (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔsolH −83; lattice dissociation +2258; ΔhydH(Cl⁻) −364. Calculate ΔhydH(Ca²⁺).
12. Why is ΔhydH(Mg²⁺) more exothermic than ΔhydH(Na⁺)?

### Answers

1. The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid separates into its gaseous ions.
2. The electron is added to a negative ion, so energy is needed to overcome the repulsion.
3. **+121 kJ mol⁻¹** (half the bond enthalpy).
4. Ca⁺(g) → Ca²⁺(g) + e⁻
5. Steps = 178 + 590 + 1145 + 249 − 141 + 798 = +2819; lattice formation = −635 − 2819 = **−3454 kJ mol⁻¹**.
6. −616 = ΔatH + 520 + 79 − 328 − 1046, so ΔatH(Li) = **+159 kJ mol⁻¹**.
7. The compound has **covalent character** (the anion is polarised).
8. The **small 2+ cation with a large anion**.
9. The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water to form one mole of aqueous ions.
10. +670 − 322 − 335 = **+13 kJ mol⁻¹**.
11. −83 = 2258 + ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) + 2(−364), so ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) = **−1613 kJ mol⁻¹**.
12. Mg²⁺ is smaller and has a higher charge, so it attracts the δ− oxygen of water molecules more strongly.
