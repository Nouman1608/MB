Section 3.2 is International AS content. All countries, firms and figures are invented. ## Questions

Try each question without the notes first. Show every step of a calculation and write the unit. For the longer questions, build a chain of reasoning (cause, effect, effect on jobs or prices) and finish with a judgement. Questions 10 and 11 reward evaluation as well as explanation, so allow time for both.

**1.** Distinguish between deflation and disinflation. **[2]**

**2.** Country Epsilon has 2,376,000 people employed, 99,000 unemployed on the ILO measure and 925,000 economically inactive.

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the participation rate, as a percentage of the working-age population, to one decimal place. **[1]**

**3.** State whether each person is employed, ILO unemployed, economically inactive or underemployed. **(i)** A retiree not looking for work. **(ii)** A laid-off driver who is searching and could start next week. **(iii)** A shop assistant on 15 hours a week who wants 35. **(iv)** A full-time carer who is not seeking a job. **[4]**

**4.** In Country Epsilon the next year, employment is unchanged, unemployment falls from 99,000 to 81,000 and the other 18,000 become inactive.

**(a)** Calculate the new unemployment rate to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this fall is weak evidence of a stronger labour market. **[1]**

**5.** In Country Epsilon, 297,000 of the 2,376,000 employed want more hours.

**(a)** Calculate the underemployment rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why underemployment means a low unemployment rate may understate spare capacity. **[3]**

**6.** A consumer price index rises from 108.0 to 111.2. Nominal pay rises 3.4%.

**(a)** Find the rate of inflation, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in real pay to one decimal place. **[2]**

**7.** A furniture workshop's cost per table is 200: timber 50%, labour 30%, other 20%. Timber costs rise 8% and labour costs rise 3%. Other costs are unchanged. Calculate the percentage rise in cost per table and the new cost. **[4]**

**8.** The same rise in aggregate demand has these effects: Economy X, price level 100 to 102, real GDP 440 to 470; Economy Y, price level 100 to 109, real GDP 497 to 500.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the price level in each. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the difference using aggregate demand and aggregate supply. **[3]**

**9.** An imported good costs 60 foreign units. The exchange rate falls from 1 domestic unit = 3.0 foreign units to 1 domestic unit = 2.4 foreign units.

**(a)** Calculate the domestic price before and after, and the percentage change. **[3]**
**(b)** Imports are 15% of the consumer basket and pass-through is complete. Calculate the effect on the price level. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one other way the fall affects inflation. **[2]**

**10.** Country Zeta has 8% unemployment. Evaluate whether expansionary demand policy is the best way to reduce it. **[8]**

**11.** Country Omega exports one manufactured product. World demand for it falls and cheaper foreign rivals enter its market. Discuss the effects on employment and unemployment in Country Omega. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Deflation means the price level is falling [1]. Disinflation means inflation is slowing: prices still rise, but more slowly [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A contrast needs both definitions; answers that define only one term leave the distinction unstated.

**2. (a)** Labour force = 2,376,000 + 99,000 = 2,475,000 [1]. Rate = 99,000 ÷ 2,475,000 × 100 = **4.0%** [1].
**(b)** Working-age population = 3,400,000; 2,475,000 ÷ 3,400,000 × 100 = **72.8%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Using 2,376,000 or 3,400,000 as the denominator in part (a) is the usual slip; show the labour force line so the method is visible.

**3.** (i) Economically inactive [1]. (ii) ILO unemployed [1]. (iii) Employed and underemployed [1]. (iv) Economically inactive [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the three-part test: no job, searching, available. Underemployed people are in work, so do not label them unemployed.

**4. (a)** Labour force = 2,376,000 + 81,000 = 2,457,000 [1]. Rate = 81,000 ÷ 2,457,000 × 100 = **3.3%** [1].
**(b)** No extra jobs were created; the rate fell because people left the labour force [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers say what happened to employment, since that is the evidence a tighter market needs.

**5. (a)** 297,000 ÷ 2,376,000 × 100 = **12.5%** [1].
**(b)** These workers count as employed [1]. They have unused hours or skills, so labour capacity is idle [1]. Output could rise without new hires, which links to a negative output gap [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the point back to spare capacity; restating the definition adds little.

**6. (a)** (111.2 − 108.0) ÷ 108.0 [1] × 100 = **3.0%** [1].
**(b)** 1.034 ÷ 1.0296 − 1 [1] = **+0.4%**, so real pay rises slightly [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing is the exact method. Subtracting inflation from nominal growth gives a similar figure here, but the gap widens as the rates grow.

**7.** Timber: 0.50 × 8 = 4.0 [1]. Labour: 0.30 × 3 = 0.9 [1]. Total rise = **4.9%** [1]. New cost = 200 × 1.049 = **209.8** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Weights matter. Adding 8% and 3% without the shares gives a wrong total.

**8. (a)** X: **+2%** [1]. Y: **+9%** [1].
**(b)** In X there is spare capacity, so firms raise output instead of price (real GDP +6.8%) [1]. In Y real GDP is near potential, so factors are scarce and costs rise (real GDP +0.6%) [1]. SRAS is flatter far from capacity and steeper near it, so AD pushes up the price level more in Y [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data in the explanation, naming both price and output movements, rather than describing the diagram generally.

**9. (a)** Before: 60 ÷ 3.0 = 20 [1]. After: 60 ÷ 2.4 = 25 [1]. Change = **+25%** [1].
**(b)** 0.15 × 25 = **3.75 percentage points** [1].
**(c)** Exports become cheaper abroad [1], so net exports and AD rise, adding demand-pull pressure [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the direction of the exchange rate clear; a fall in the currency raises import prices in domestic currency.

**10.** Cyclical unemployment comes from weak AD [1]. Higher AD raises output, labour demand and jobs [1], if a negative output gap exists [1]. If the cause is structural, extra AD does not fix the skills mismatch [1]. Supply-side unemployment lowers normal capacity (LRAS further left) [1]. Stimulus near capacity raises prices [1]. Policy lags may mean it arrives late [1]. Retraining, mobility help and job information target the cause [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach a judgement that depends on the cause of the unemployment; a list of policies without it reads as description.

**11.** Falling world demand cuts export orders [1], so AD shifts left, causing cyclical unemployment [1]. Cheaper rivals take market share, so the firm closes, cutting jobs [1]. Workers' skills are specific, so unemployment becomes structural [1]. Falling exports may weaken the exchange rate, helping competitiveness [1]. Falling AD may bring disinflation [1]. Evaluation: openness and dependence on one product make it worse [1]; flexible, skilled workers recover faster [1]; the effect is short run if switching industries is quick [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the cyclical effect from the structural effect, and say which is likely to last longer.

## Where marks are usually lost

* Using the working-age population instead of the labour force for the rate.
* Treating people who stop searching as employed or unemployed.
* Calling disinflation falling prices.
* Subtracting inflation from nominal growth instead of dividing.
* Adding percentage cost rises without weights.
* Assigning one policy to every cause of unemployment.
* Quoting a global influence without tracing it through AD or SRAS.
* Stopping at the first judgement without giving a reason that depends on cause or time.
