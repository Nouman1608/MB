This guide covers subtopic **24.1, Electrolysis**, from Topic 24, Electrochemistry, of
Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content.
The broader
[Electrochemistry: Electrolysis and Electrode Potentials guide](/resources/a-electrochemistry-electrolysis-and-electrode-potentials/)
summarises the rules and works one copper-deposition calculation; this page sets out a method
for predicting products at each electrode, covers gas volumes, time and charge-on-ion
problems, and describes the Avogadro constant experiment step by step.

## What the syllabus asks for

The learning outcomes for 24.1 are:

1. predict the identities of substances liberated during electrolysis from the **state of the
   electrolyte** (molten or aqueous), **position in the redox series** (electrode potential)
   and **concentration**;
2. state and apply **F = Le**, linking the Faraday constant, the Avogadro constant and the
   charge on the electron;
3. calculate the quantity of charge passed, using **Q = It**, and the **mass and/or volume** of
   substance liberated;
4. **describe the determination of a value of the Avogadro constant** by an electrolytic
   method.

Values from the 9701 data list: F = 9.65 × 10⁴ C mol⁻¹; L = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹;
e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (magnitude); molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at room conditions.

## Electrodes and what happens at them

- **Cathode** (negative electrode): **reduction**; cations or water molecules gain electrons.
- **Anode** (positive electrode): **oxidation**; anions, water molecules or the anode metal
  itself lose electrons.

Electrons flow from the power supply into the cathode and out of the anode. Inside the
electrolyte, charge is carried by moving ions, not electrons.

## Predicting the products: a three-step method

### Step 1: molten or aqueous?

A **molten** binary compound contains only its own ions, so the metal forms at the cathode and
the non-metal at the anode. Molten lead(II) bromide gives lead at the cathode
(Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb) and bromine at the anode (2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻).

An **aqueous** solution also contains water, which can itself be reduced or oxidised:

- at the cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ (or 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ in acid);
- at the anode: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (or 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ in alkali).

### Step 2: use electrode potentials

At the **cathode**, the species with the **more positive** E⦵ for its reduction is reduced
first, because it is the better oxidising agent. Compare the metal ion with water or H⁺:

| Cation in solution | E⦵ / V | Cathode product |
|---|---|---|
| Ag⁺ / Ag | +0.80 | silver |
| Cu²⁺ / Cu | +0.34 | copper |
| H⁺ / H₂ | 0.00 | hydrogen (from acid) |
| Al³⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ | −1.66 to −2.92 | hydrogen (from water); the metal is not formed |

At the **anode**, the species whose half-equation has the **less positive** E⦵ is oxidised
first, because its reduced form is the better reducing agent. Compare the anion with water,
using O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ ⇌ 2H₂O, E⦵ = +1.23 V:

| Anion | E⦵ / V | Anode product (inert electrode) |
|---|---|---|
| I⁻ (I₂/I⁻) | +0.54 | iodine |
| Br⁻ (Br₂/Br⁻) | +1.07 | bromine |
| Cl⁻ (Cl₂/Cl⁻) | +1.36 | depends on concentration (Step 3) |
| SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻ | not oxidised | oxygen from water |

If the anode is a metal such as copper rather than an inert material (platinum or graphite),
the anode itself is oxidised: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻.

### Step 3: allow for concentration

E⦵ values apply at 1.00 mol dm⁻³. When two possible reactions have close E⦵ values,
concentration decides. The key case is chloride against water at the anode (+1.36 V against
+1.23 V):

- **concentrated** chloride solution (e.g. concentrated NaCl(aq) or concentrated HCl): chlorine
  is the main anode product;
- **dilute** chloride solution: oxygen is the main product.

A high concentration of Cl⁻ makes the Cl₂/Cl⁻ potential less positive, so chloride becomes
easier to oxidise. This links to the Nernst equation in 24.2.

### Putting the steps together

| Electrolyte (inert electrodes unless stated) | Cathode | Anode |
|---|---|---|
| molten NaCl | sodium | chlorine |
| CuSO₄(aq) | copper | oxygen |
| CuSO₄(aq), copper electrodes | copper | copper dissolves as Cu²⁺ |
| KI(aq) | hydrogen | iodine |
| Na₂SO₄(aq) | hydrogen | oxygen |
| concentrated NaCl(aq) | hydrogen | chlorine |
| very dilute NaCl(aq) | hydrogen | oxygen |
| AgNO₃(aq) | silver | oxygen |

## Charge, the faraday and F = Le

The charge passed is **Q = It** (coulombs = amperes × seconds). Always convert time to seconds.

One mole of electrons carries a charge equal to the Avogadro constant multiplied by the charge
on one electron:

**F = Le** = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C ≈ 9.64 × 10⁴ C mol⁻¹

which agrees with the data value of 9.65 × 10⁴ C mol⁻¹ to the precision of e given.

The general route for any calculation:

charge (Q = It) → moles of electrons (Q ÷ F) → moles of product (from the half-equation) →
mass (× M) or gas volume (× 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹)

## Worked example 1: gas volumes

Dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed with inert electrodes for 40.0 minutes at 0.750 A.
Calculate the volumes of gas collected at room conditions.

Q = 0.750 × (40.0 × 60) = 1800 C

moles of electrons = 1800 ÷ 96 500 = 0.018 65 mol

Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂, so moles H₂ = 0.018 65 ÷ 2 = 0.009 326 mol;
volume = 0.009 326 × 24.0 = 0.224 dm³ = **224 cm³**

Anode: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻, so moles O₂ = 0.018 65 ÷ 4 = 0.004 663 mol;
volume = **112 cm³**

The 2:1 ratio of hydrogen to oxygen follows directly from the electrons in each half-equation.

## Worked example 2: finding a time

How long must a current of 0.250 A flow to deposit 1.00 g of silver from AgNO₃(aq)?
(A<sub>r</sub>: Ag = 107.9)

moles Ag = 1.00 ÷ 107.9 = 9.268 × 10⁻³ mol; Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag, so moles of electrons are the same.

Q = 9.268 × 10⁻³ × 96 500 = 894 C

t = Q ÷ I = 894 ÷ 0.250 = **3580 s** (about 59.6 minutes)

## Determining the Avogadro constant by electrolysis

**Method** (copper electrodes in aqueous copper(II) sulfate):

1. Clean two copper electrodes with fine emery paper, wash, dry and weigh them.
2. Set up a series circuit: d.c. supply, variable resistor, ammeter and the two electrodes
   dipping into CuSO₄(aq).
3. Switch on, start a timer and adjust the resistor to keep the current constant.
4. After a measured time, switch off. Remove the electrodes, rinse gently with distilled water,
   then with propanone, dry and reweigh.
5. Use the mass lost by the anode (or gained by the cathode). The anode loss is often preferred
   because copper deposited on the cathode can flake off.

**Calculation.** A current of 0.200 A flows for 30.0 minutes and the mass of copper transferred
is 0.118 g. (A<sub>r</sub>: Cu = 63.5; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)

Q = 0.200 × 1800 = 360 C

number of electrons = 360 ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 2.25 × 10²¹

moles of Cu = 0.118 ÷ 63.5 = 1.858 × 10⁻³ mol; Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻, so moles of electrons =
3.717 × 10⁻³ mol

L = number of electrons ÷ moles of electrons = 2.25 × 10²¹ ÷ 3.717 × 10⁻³ =
**6.05 × 10²³ mol⁻¹**

Errors that make the value inaccurate include a current that drifts, copper flaking off before
weighing, and electrodes that are not completely dry.

## Common mistakes

- **Time in minutes.** Q = It needs seconds.
- **Ignoring the electron ratio.** Cu²⁺ needs 2 electrons per atom; oxygen needs 4 electrons per
  molecule of O₂.
- **Saying sodium forms at the cathode in aqueous solution.** Hydrogen forms instead.
- **Using the wrong comparison at the anode.** The less positive E⦵ is oxidised; halide ions
  other than Cl⁻ are oxidised even when dilute.
- **Forgetting active electrodes.** A copper anode dissolves rather than releasing oxygen.
- **Using 22.4 dm³ for room conditions.** Use 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ unless s.t.p. is stated.

## Exam technique

- **Predict**: name the product at each electrode and give the half-equation. State whether
  the electrolyte is molten or aqueous before reasoning.
- **Explain**: cite the relevant E⦵ values and say which species is more easily reduced or
  oxidised; for chloride, mention concentration.
- **Calculate**: show Q, moles of electrons, the mole ratio from the half-equation, then the
  mass or volume, with units and three significant figures.
- **Describe** the Avogadro experiment: apparatus, measurements (current, time, mass change),
  and how L is calculated from them.
