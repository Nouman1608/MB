## 4.6.2.1 Variation

**Variation** is the differences in the characteristics of individuals in a population. It may be due to:

- the **genes** they have inherited (**genetic causes**);
- the **conditions** in which they have developed (**environmental causes**);
- a **combination** of genes and the environment.

| Characteristic (human examples) | Main cause |
|---|---|
| Blood group | Genetic |
| A scar | Environmental |
| Height | Genes and environment (diet) |
| Body mass | Genes and environment (diet, exercise) |

The specification asks you to describe simply how the **genome and its interaction with the environment**
influence the phenotype. A person may inherit alleles that allow them to grow tall, but a poor diet during
childhood means they will not reach that height.

### Mutations: the source of all variants

There is usually **extensive genetic variation** within a population. **All variants arise from mutations**, and:

- **most** have **no effect** on the phenotype;
- **some influence** the phenotype;
- **very few determine** the phenotype.

**Mutations occur continuously.** Very rarely, a mutation leads to a **new phenotype**. If that phenotype suits an
**environmental change**, it can lead to a **relatively rapid change in the species**.

## 4.6.2.2 Evolution

**Evolution** is a **change in the inherited characteristics of a population over time** through **natural
selection**, which may result in the formation of a new species. The theory states that **all species have
evolved from simple life forms that first developed more than three billion years ago**.

### Natural selection as a chain

1. **Variation:** individuals in a population show genetic variation, caused by mutations.
2. **Competition and selection pressure:** some variants have phenotypes better suited to the environment.
3. **Survival:** individuals with the better-suited phenotype are more likely to survive.
4. **Reproduction:** they are more likely to breed and pass on their alleles.
5. **Over many generations:** the allele, and the phenotype, become more common in the population.

**Annotated model answer** (invented example)

*Question: A species of beetle lives on dark tree bark. Some beetles are pale and some are dark. Birds eat the
beetles. Explain how the population could change over many generations. [4]*

> There is variation in colour in the beetle population, caused by mutations [1]. Dark beetles are better
> camouflaged on dark bark, so birds eat fewer of them and they are more likely to survive [1]. The surviving dark
> beetles breed and pass on the alleles for dark colour to their offspring [1]. Over many generations the
> proportion of dark beetles in the population increases [1].

The answer never says the beetles "decided" or "adapted on purpose" to be dark. Individuals do not change; the
population changes because some survive and breed more than others.

### New species

If two populations of one species become so different in phenotype that they **can no longer interbreed to
produce fertile offspring**, they have formed **two new species**.

## 4.6.2.3 Selective breeding

**Selective breeding (artificial selection)** is the process by which **humans** breed plants and animals for
particular genetic characteristics. Humans have done this for thousands of years, since they first bred food
crops from wild plants and domesticated animals.

1. Choose **parents with the desired characteristic** from a mixed population.
2. **Breed them together.**
3. From the offspring, choose those with the desired characteristic and **breed them together**.
4. **Continue over many generations** until **all the offspring** show the desired characteristic.

Characteristics chosen for **usefulness or appearance**: **disease resistance** in food crops; animals that produce
**more meat or milk**; **domestic dogs with a gentle nature**; **large or unusual flowers**.

**Risk:** selective breeding can lead to **inbreeding**, where some breeds are particularly prone to **disease or
inherited defects**. The reason is reduced variation: closely related animals share many alleles, so harmful
recessive alleles are more likely to be inherited from both parents, and the population has less variation to
cope with a new disease.

| | Natural selection | Selective breeding |
|---|---|---|
| Who chooses | The environment | Humans |
| What is favoured | Characteristics that help survival | Characteristics humans want |
| Speed | Usually slow | Faster |

## 4.6.2.4 Genetic engineering

**Genetic engineering** modifies the **genome** of an organism by **introducing a gene from another organism** to
give a desired characteristic.

- **Plant crops** have been engineered to be **resistant to diseases** or to produce **bigger, better fruits**.
- **Bacterial cells** have been engineered to produce useful substances such as **human insulin** to treat diabetes.
- **GM crops** include ones resistant to **insect attack** or to **herbicides**, and generally show **increased
  yields**.
- Modern medical research is exploring genetic modification to **overcome some inherited disorders**.

**Concerns:** the effect of GM crops on **populations of wild flowers and insects**; some people feel the effects of
**eating GM crops on human health** have not been fully explored.

**Higher tier only: the main steps.**

1. **Enzymes** are used to **isolate** the required gene.
2. The gene is inserted into a **vector**, usually a **bacterial plasmid** or a **virus**.
3. The vector is used to **insert the gene into the required cells**.
4. Genes are transferred to the cells of animals, plants or microorganisms **at an early stage in their
   development**, so that they develop with the desired characteristics.

## 4.6.2.5 Cloning (Biology only)

| Method | How it works | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue culture | Small groups of cells from part of a plant are grown into identical new plants | Preserving rare plant species; commercially in nurseries |
| Cuttings | Pieces of a parent plant are grown into new plants | Older, simple method used by gardeners |
| Embryo transplants | Cells are split apart from a developing animal embryo **before they become specialised**, and the identical embryos are transplanted into **host mothers** | Producing several identical animals |
| Adult cell cloning | See steps below | Producing a clone of an adult animal |

**Adult cell cloning:**

1. The **nucleus is removed** from an **unfertilised egg cell**.
2. The **nucleus from an adult body cell**, such as a skin cell, is **inserted** into the egg cell.
3. An **electric shock** stimulates the egg cell to **divide** to form an embryo.
4. The embryo cells contain the **same genetic information as the adult skin cell**.
5. When the embryo has developed into a **ball of cells**, it is inserted into the **womb of an adult female** to
   continue its development.

Benefits and risks: cloning produces many animals or plants with known desirable characteristics, and can
preserve rare species; but it reduces variation, so a new disease could affect all the clones, and some people
have ethical objections.

## Common mistakes

- Saying organisms "evolve because they need to". Mutations are random; selection acts on existing variation.
- Writing that individuals evolve. Populations evolve.
- Mixing up natural selection (environment chooses) and selective breeding (humans choose).
- Saying the clone is identical to the egg donor. It is identical to the **body-cell donor**.
- Forgetting the electric shock, or the removal of the egg's own nucleus, in adult cell cloning.
- Describing genetic engineering without a **vector** (Higher tier only).

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Explain** natural selection: variation, survival, breeding, alleles passed on, many generations.
- **Describe** selective breeding or cloning: the steps in order.
- **Evaluate** GM crops or cloning: benefits, risks or objections, and a conclusion.
- **Compare** natural selection and selective breeding: paired points on who selects and what is selected.
