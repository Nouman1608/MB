## Questions

**1.** State **two** rules for writing a binomial name. **[2]**

**2.** Use the key to identify a leaf that is simple, with a smooth edge and a pointed tip.

```
1a  Leaf divided into leaflets ............ Species P
1b  Leaf simple (one blade) ............... go to 2
2a  Edge toothed .......................... Species Q
2b  Edge smooth ........................... go to 3
3a  Tip rounded ........................... Species R
3b  Tip pointed ........................... Species S
```

**[1]**

**3.** Suggest **two** limitations of using a dichotomous key in the field. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what is meant by a *range of tolerance* and explain how it can determine where a
species is found. **[3]**

**5.** Deer were counted on a reserve each year.

| Year | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deer | 120 | 190 | 300 | 450 | 600 | 680 | 710 | 705 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase from year 2 to year 3. **[1]**
**(b)** Estimate the carrying capacity of the reserve. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the rate of increase falls after year 4. **[3]**

**6.** Distinguish between density-dependent and density-independent factors, giving an example of
each. **[4]**

**7.** Two species of warbler feed in the same conifer trees. Explain how they can coexist. **[2]**

**8.** Compare r-strategists and K-strategists. **[4]**

**9.** Describe the shape of a Type III survivorship curve and state the type of species it usually
describes. **[2]**

**10.** Twenty-five 0.25 m² quadrats were placed randomly in a 2,000 m² meadow. Cowslips occurred in
18 quadrats; 75 plants were counted in total.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage frequency of cowslips. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the density of cowslips per m². **[2]**
**(c)** Estimate the cowslip population of the meadow. **[1]**

**11.** In a capture-mark-release-recapture study, 60 voles were marked and released. Later, 45 were
caught, of which 9 were marked.

**(a)** Calculate the population size using the Lincoln index. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how the estimate would be affected if some marks were lost before the second
sample. **[2]**

**12.** Design an investigation to test whether light intensity affects the distribution of a
woodland plant. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: two parts, genus then species [1]; genus starts with a capital letter, species with a
lower-case letter [1]; written in italics, or underlined when handwritten [1]. **[2]**

**2.** 1b → 2b → 3b: **Species S** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two: juveniles or damaged specimens may lack the features used [1]; features may vary
between individuals, sexes or seasons [1]; vague terms such as "large" lead to errors [1]; a key only
covers the species it was written for [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The span of values of an abiotic factor, such as temperature or pH, within which a species
can survive [1]. Populations thrive near the optimum and become stressed towards the limits [1], so
the species is found only where conditions fall within its range, and is absent where any factor lies
outside it [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** (450 − 300) / 300 × 100 = **50%** [1]
**(b)** About **705–710 deer** [1]
**(c)** As numbers approach carrying capacity, food per deer falls and competition increases [1];
density-dependent factors such as disease or parasites become more frequent [1]; birth rates fall and
death rates rise until they are roughly equal, so growth levels off (negative feedback) [1]. **[5]**

**6.** Density-dependent factors have a greater effect as population density increases [1], for
example disease or competition for food [1]. Density-independent factors affect a population
regardless of density [1], for example drought, frost or fire [1]. **[4]**

**7.** They partition the resource, for example by feeding in different parts of the tree or on
different insects [1], so their realised niches differ and competition is reduced enough for both to
persist [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Any four comparisons, each must cover both types: r produce many offspring, K produce few [1];
r give little parental care, K give extensive care [1]; r mature early, K mature late [1]; r are
short-lived, K are long-lived [1]; r colonise unstable habitats, K dominate stable ones [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Curve falls steeply at young ages, then flattens with few survivors to old age [1]; typical of
r-strategists such as many fish or plants [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** 18 / 25 × 100 = **72%** [1]
**(b)** Mean per quadrat = 75 / 25 = 3.0 [1]; density = 3.0 / 0.25 = **12 plants per m²** [1]
**(c)** 12 × 2000 = **24,000 cowslips** [1] **[4]**

**11. (a)** N = (60 × 45) / 9 = 2700 / 9 = **300 voles** [1]
**(b)** Fewer marked animals would be recaptured, so R would be too small [1], making the estimate
too large [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Up to 6 marks from:
- a belt or line transect running from open ground into dense shade [1];
- quadrats at regular intervals along the transect (systematic sampling) [1];
- light intensity measured with a light meter at each quadrat, at the same time of day and height
  [1];
- plant abundance recorded as density or percentage cover in each quadrat [1];
- several transects or repeats to improve reliability [1];
- other variables, such as soil moisture or pH, measured or kept similar [1];
- results analysed by plotting abundance against light intensity and testing for a correlation [1];
- minimal trampling and no removal of plants [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing binomial names without the capital genus.
- Quoting K as the last value in the table rather than the level the data fluctuate around.
- Dividing by the wrong quadrat area when finding density.
- Saying lost marks make the estimate "inaccurate" without saying it becomes too large.
