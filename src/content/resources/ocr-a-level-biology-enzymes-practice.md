## Questions

**1.** Catalase and amylase are both enzymes. State which catalyses an intracellular reaction and which an
extracellular reaction, and give the reaction each catalyses. **[2]**

**2.** Describe the induced-fit hypothesis of enzyme action. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why an enzyme is specific to one substrate. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction increases from 10 to 35 degrees Celsius and then falls
sharply above 45 degrees Celsius. **[4]**

**5.** Explain how a change of pH away from the optimum reduces the activity of an enzyme. **[3]**

**6.** The rate of a reaction catalysed by an enzyme was 3.6 units at 25 degrees Celsius and 6.3 units at 35 degrees
Celsius.

**(a)** Calculate the temperature coefficient (Q₁₀) for this temperature range. **[1]**
**(b)** At 55 degrees Celsius the rate was 1.2 units and at 45 degrees Celsius 4.8 units. Calculate Q₁₀ for this range
and explain the value. **[2]**

**7.** A student prepares a tenfold serial dilution of a 2.0% amylase solution to produce four concentrations,
including the starting solution. Each tube must contain 10 cm cubed.

**(a)** List the concentrations produced. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe how the student makes the second solution. **[2]**

**8.** In a catalase experiment, the volume of oxygen collected was recorded.

| Time (s) | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen (cm cubed) | 0 | 14 | 25 | 32 | 36 | 37 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of oxygen production in the first 20 s. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the rate decreases over time. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the initial rate is used to compare different hydrogen peroxide concentrations. **[1]**
**(d)** Give **two** variables the student should control, and how. **[2]**

**9.** Using starch and amylase, a student timed how long it took until a sample no longer turned iodine solution
blue-black. At pH 7 the time was 50 s. Calculate the relative rate (1 / time). **[1]**

**10.** Amylase from saliva works much more slowly if chloride ions are removed. Explain why. **[2]**

**11.** Explain what a coenzyme is and how organisms obtain many coenzymes. **[2]**

**12.** Compare the effects of a competitive and a non-competitive inhibitor on the rate of an enzyme-controlled
reaction as substrate concentration is increased. **[4]**

**13.** A poison binds to an enzyme by covalent bonds. Explain why its effect is described as non-reversible. **[2]**

**14.** In a metabolic pathway, substance D (the end product) inhibits enzyme 1, which converts A to B. Explain the
advantage of this to the cell. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Catalase is intracellular: it breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen [1]. Amylase is
extracellular: it hydrolyses starch into maltose [1].

**2.** The active site is not exactly complementary to the substrate at first [1]; as the substrate binds, the active
site changes shape to fit more closely [1]; this puts strain on bonds in the substrate, lowering the activation
energy [1].

**3.** The enzyme has a specific tertiary structure, so the active site has a specific shape [1] which is
complementary to only one substrate, so only that substrate can bind / form an enzyme-substrate complex [1].

**4.** 10 to 35 degrees Celsius: molecules gain kinetic energy [1], so there are more frequent successful collisions
and more enzyme-substrate complexes form [1]. Above 45 degrees Celsius: vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds in
the tertiary structure [1], so the active site changes shape, substrate can no longer bind and the enzyme is
denatured [1].

**5.** Hydrogen ions (or hydroxide ions) interact with charged R groups [1], disrupting ionic and hydrogen bonds [1],
so the shape / charge of the active site changes and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form [1].

**6. (a)** 6.3 / 3.6 = **1.75** [1].
**(b)** 1.2 / 4.8 = **0.25** [1]; the value is less than 1 because the enzyme is being denatured, so the rate falls as
temperature rises [1].

**7. (a)** 2.0%, 0.2%, 0.02%, 0.002% [1].
**(b)** Take 1 cm cubed of the 2.0% solution [1] and add 9 cm cubed of distilled water (or buffer), then mix [1].

**8. (a)** 14 / 20 = **0.7 cm cubed per second** [1].
**(b)** Hydrogen peroxide (substrate) is used up, so its concentration falls [1], so there are fewer collisions and
fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form per second [1].
**(c)** At the start the substrate concentration is known and has not yet been reduced / the reaction is not limited
by substrate being used up, so it is a fair comparison [1].
**(d)** Any two, with method [1 each]: temperature, using a thermostatically controlled water bath; pH, using a
buffer; volume and concentration of enzyme (same mass of yeast or potato); volume of hydrogen peroxide.

**9.** 1 / 50 = **0.02 per second** [1].

**10.** Chloride ions are a cofactor for amylase [1]; they help the active site take the correct shape / help
enzyme-substrate complexes form, so without them fewer complexes form [1].

**11.** A small organic (non-protein) molecule that helps an enzyme work by carrying atoms or groups between
reactions [1]; many are made from vitamins obtained in the diet [1].

**12.** Competitive: the inhibitor competes with substrate for the active site [1]; as substrate concentration rises,
the substrate outcompetes the inhibitor and the rate approaches the uninhibited maximum [1]. Non-competitive: the
inhibitor binds an allosteric site and changes the active site's shape [1]; increasing substrate has no effect on
this, so the maximum rate stays lower [1].

**13.** Covalent bonds are strong and do not break easily [1], so the inhibitor cannot leave and the enzyme is
permanently inactivated [1].

**14.** When D builds up, it inhibits enzyme 1, so less A is converted and less D is made [1]. When D is used up,
inhibition is removed and production resumes [1]. This regulates the amount of product / stops resources and energy
being wasted making product that is not needed [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating the temperature coefficient upside down (lower rate divided by higher rate).
- Describing the active site as "the same shape" as the substrate.
- Saying enzymes die at high temperature.
- Saying a non-competitive inhibitor binds the active site.
- Taking the rate from the flat part of a curve instead of the start.

## Frequently asked questions

**What value of the temperature coefficient should I expect?**
For many enzyme-controlled reactions below the optimum the value is about 2. Above the optimum it falls below 1.

**How do I find an initial rate from a curve?**
Draw a tangent to the curve at time zero and calculate its gradient (change in y / change in x).

**What does a boiled enzyme control show?**
That the reaction depends on active enzyme; if a reaction still happens, something else is causing it.
