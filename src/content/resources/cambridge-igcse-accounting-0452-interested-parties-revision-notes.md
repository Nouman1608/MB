> **Syllabus edition note.** These notes follow the Cambridge IGCSE Accounting 0452 syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029 (version 1). The 2026 syllabus (version 2), still examined in November 2026, lists fewer parties: owners, managers, trade payables, banks, investors, club members and others such as governments and tax authorities.

Related: [interested parties study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-interested-parties/) | [interested parties practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-interested-parties-practice/)

Condensed recall for **6.4 Interested parties**: "how accounting information may be used for decision-making" by each party the syllabus names. Figures below are invented; amounts in dollars.

## The answer formula

**Party + decision + figure + reason.** For example: "A supplier deciding whether to give credit would look at the acid test ratio, because it shows whether the business can pay its current liabilities without selling inventory."

## The ten parties

| Party | Question it asks | Figures it uses |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Is my return worth the risk? Expand, carry on or close? How much can I withdraw? | Profit for the year, profit margin, ROCE, capital |
| Managers | Which prices, costs and inventory levels should change? | Gross profit margin, each expense, inventory turnover, receivables days |
| Employees | Is my job safe? Can the business afford a pay rise? | Profit trend, cash, liquidity |
| Banks | Lend or allow an overdraft? Can it repay with interest? | Current and acid test ratios, profit before interest, existing loans, assets for security |
| Investors | Buy, keep or sell shares? | Profit growth, ROCE, dividends |
| Lenders | Lend long term? Will interest be paid and the loan repaid? | Liquidity, existing borrowing, profit to cover interest, security |
| Suppliers | Supply on credit? How much and for how long? | Liquidity, trade payables turnover against terms |
| Customers | Will it keep supplying, honour guarantees and supply spare parts? | Profit and liquidity as signs of survival |
| Governments / tax authorities | How much tax is due? Grants? Statistics? | Profit for the year, revenue |
| Club members | Are subscriptions well spent? Raise them? | Surplus or deficit, accumulated fund, receipts and payments |
| Other parties, e.g. public and environmental bodies | Effect on local jobs, the community and the environment | Survival, profit, spending on obligations where shown |

## Pairs that examiners separate

- **Owners and managers.** Managers have detailed internal records and make day-to-day decisions; owners of a larger business may see only the published statements.
- **Investors and lenders.** Investors share in profit, so they look at profitability and dividends. Lenders receive fixed interest, so they look at liquidity, borrowing and security.
- **Banks and suppliers.** Both give credit. Suppliers give short-term trade credit and watch payables days; banks lend larger sums, often for longer, and want security.

## Conflicts of interest

| Parties | Conflict |
|---|---|
| Owners and lenders | Drawings or dividends against keeping cash to repay loans |
| Owners and employees | Pay rises against profit |
| Owners and customers | Higher prices against value for customers |
| Owners and tax authorities | Lower reported profit means less tax |

## Quick calculation for a club

A chess club has 75 members paying $40 each, so subscriptions are $3,000. Expenditure is $3,450, a deficit of $450, which is $6 per member. Members might raise the subscription to $46, cut costs or hold an event.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interested party | A person or group that uses a business's accounting information to make decisions |
| Stakeholder | Another word often used for an interested party |
| Security | An asset a lender can claim if a loan is not repaid |
| Trade credit | Time allowed by a supplier before payment is due |
| Surplus / deficit | A club's excess of income over expenditure, or of expenditure over income |
| Accumulated fund | A club's equivalent of capital |
| Dividend | A share of a company's profit paid to shareholders |

## Exam traps

- **Vague purpose.** "To see how the business is doing" is not a decision.
- **Profit for clubs.** Clubs have a surplus or deficit, not a profit.
- **Investors are not lenders.** Give each its own decision.
- **Missed parties.** Customers and public or environmental bodies are on the current list.
- **Ratio without reason.** Say why the ratio helps that party.
- **Ignoring the case.** If the question gives ratios, quote them.
- **Limits of the statements.** Public bodies and employees may need information the statements do not contain, such as environmental impact or future plans.

## Self-test

1. List the ten interested parties in the 0452 syllabus.
2. Why would a supplier look at the acid test ratio?
3. Give one decision an owner makes using accounting information.
4. How do managers' information needs differ from those of owners of a large company?
5. Distinguish between how an investor and a lender use the statements.
6. What does a tax authority use profit for the year for?
7. Why do club members not look for a profit?
8. A club has 50 members paying $60 and a deficit of $400. By how much would the subscription need to rise to cover it?
9. Give one conflict between owners and lenders.
10. Why might an environmental body need more than the financial statements?
11. Which ratio would an employee seeking a pay rise most likely mention?
12. Why are customers interested in a business's accounting information?

**Answers:** 1. Owners; managers; employees; banks; investors and lenders; suppliers; customers; governments / tax authorities; club members; other interested parties, e.g. public and environmental bodies. 2. To decide whether to give credit, because it shows whether current liabilities can be paid without selling inventory. 3. Any one: whether to expand, continue or close; how much to withdraw; whether to invest more. 4. Managers need detailed internal information for daily decisions on prices, costs and inventory; owners of a large company may receive only summarised published statements. 5. An investor looks at profit, ROCE and dividends to judge return; a lender looks at liquidity, existing borrowing and security to judge whether interest and repayment will be made. 6. To work out the tax due. 7. A club exists to provide activities for members; it measures a surplus or deficit to see whether subscriptions cover costs. 8. $400 ÷ 50 = $8 per member, to $68. 9. Owners want high drawings or dividends; lenders want cash kept in the business. 10. The statements show only items measured in money, so environmental effects such as pollution are not shown. 11. Profit for the year or the profit margin, to show the business can afford it. 12. To judge whether it will survive to keep supplying, honour guarantees and provide spare parts or after-sales service.
