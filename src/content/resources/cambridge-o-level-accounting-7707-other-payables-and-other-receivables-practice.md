## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Explain the importance of matching costs and revenues when calculating profit for the year. **[2]**

**2.** State whether each item at the year end is an other payable or an other receivable.

**(a)** Rent owed by the business to its landlord **[1]**

**(b)** Commission earned by the business but not yet received **[1]**

**(c)** Insurance paid for next year **[1]**

**(d)** Rent received from a tenant for next year **[1]**

**3.** A business started trading on 1 July 2027 and its year ends on 31 December 2027. On 1 July 2027 it paid rates of $2,400 for the twelve months to 30 June 2028. Calculate the rates expense for the year and the amount prepaid. **[2]**

**4.** Prepare the year-end journal entries to record:

**(a)** telephone charges of $180 accrued **[2]**

**(b)** rent of $600 received in advance. **[2]**

**5.** A business forgot to record commission of $250 owed to it at the year end. State the effect on:

**(a)** profit for the year **[1]**

**(b)** current assets. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why income received in advance is shown as a liability. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** On 1 January 2028 Sipho owed $380 for electricity. During 2028 he paid $3,920 for electricity. On 31 December 2028 he owed $460.

**(a)** Prepare the electricity account for the year ended 31 December 2028, showing the transfer to the statement of profit or loss and the balance brought down on 1 January 2029. **[5]**

**(b)** Prepare the journal entry to record the accrual at 31 December 2028. **[2]**

**8.** Aisha lets part of her premises at $600 a month. On 1 January 2028 the tenant owed her the rent for December 2027. During 2028 she received $8,400 from the tenant.

**(a)** Prepare the rent receivable account for the year ended 31 December 2028. **[5]**

**(b)** State the amount and the classification of the balance at 31 December 2028 in Aisha's statement of financial position. **[2]**

**9.** Peter's draft profit for the year ended 31 December 2027 was $24,600. No adjustments had been made for the following:

1. Wages owing, $840.

2. Insurance paid in advance, $360.

3. Commission of $290 owed to Peter.

4. Rent received of $1,100 for January 2028, included in rent received.

**(a)** Calculate Peter's corrected profit for the year. **[5]**

**(b)** Calculate the totals of other receivables and other payables at 31 December 2027. **[2]**

**10.** Lindiwe's financial year ends on 30 June. On 1 July 2027 insurance of $900 was prepaid for July to September 2027. On 1 October 2027 she paid $4,200 for insurance for the twelve months to 30 September 2028.

**(a)** Calculate the insurance expense for the year ended 30 June 2028. **[3]**

**(b)** Prepare the insurance account for the year ended 30 June 2028. **[4]**

**11.** Using an example with figures, explain how recording expenses only when they are paid could mislead an owner comparing profits for two years. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Profit should show the revenue earned in the period less the costs of earning it [1], whatever the timing of cash, so that it measures that period's performance fairly and can be compared with other periods [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **(a)** Other payable [1]

**(b)** Other receivable [1]

**(c)** Other receivable [1]

**(d)** Other payable [1] **[4]**

**3.** Expense: $2,400 × 6/12 = **$1,200** [1]. Prepaid: $2,400 × 6/12 = **$1,200** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **(a)** Dr Telephone $180 [1], Cr Other payables $180 [1]

**(b)** Dr Rent receivable $600 [1], Cr Other payables $600 [1] **[4]**

**5.** **(a)** Profit is understated by $250 [1]

**(b)** Current assets are understated by $250 [1] **[2]**

**6.** The business has received money for a service it has not yet provided [1], so it owes the tenant or customer that service (or a refund) in the next period [1]. **[2]**

Opening balance $380 on the credit side [1]; bank $3,920 [1]; balance c/d $460 on the debit side [1]; transfer $4,000 [1]; balance b/d $460 on the credit side [1].

**(b)** Dr Electricity $460 [1], Cr Other payables $460 [1]. **[7]**

**8.** **(a)** Rent for 2028 is 12 × $600 = $7,200. The $8,400 received covers December 2027 ($600), all of 2028 ($7,200) and January 2029 ($600).

```

```

Opening debit $600 [1]; bank $8,400 [1]; transfer $7,200 [1]; balance c/d $600 on the debit side [1]; balance b/d $600 on the credit side [1].

**(b)** $600 [1], shown under current liabilities as other payables (income received in advance) [1]. **[7]**

**9.** **(a)**


−$840 [1]; +$360 [1]; +$290 [1]; −$1,100 [1]; $23,310 [1].

**(b)** Other receivables: $360 + $290 = $650 [1]. Other payables: $840 + $1,100 = $1,940 [1]. **[7]**

**11.** Example: a business pays its December electricity bill of $500 in January, so in Year 1 only eleven bills are charged and in Year 2 thirteen [1]. Year 1 profit is overstated and Year 2 profit understated by $500, although the business used the same electricity each year [1]. The owner may wrongly conclude that performance fell in Year 2 and make poor decisions, because the change is caused by the timing of payments, not by trading [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting the wrong number of months.
- Bringing accrued expenses down on the debit side.
- Leaving out the opening balance on the account.
- Treating income received in advance as income of this year.
- Showing the adjustment amount, not the year's total, as the transfer to the statement of profit or loss.
