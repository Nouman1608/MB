This guide covers section 3.6.1 Sources of finance of the AQA GCSE Business (8132) specification, for first teaching from September 2017, the first section of Topic 6, Finance. The [Finance study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-finance/) lists every source with one advantage and one disadvantage; this page shows how to match a source to a need, works through the cost of borrowing and of giving away shares, and builds the judgement examiners want for new and established businesses. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

| You must be able to | Covered below |
|---|---|
| Understand the sources named: family and friends, a new share issue, a loan or mortgage, overdrafts, retained profit, selling unwanted assets, trade credit, hire purchase and government grants | The sources |
| Analyse the advantages and disadvantages of each source in a given situation | Matching source to need |
| Evaluate the suitability of sources for new and established businesses | New or established |

**Internal** finance comes from within the business: retained profit and selling unwanted assets. **External** finance comes from outside: all the others.

## Why businesses need finance

- **To start up:** premises, equipment, first stock and the cash to survive until customers pay.
- **To cover a short-term gap:** wages and suppliers due before customers' money arrives, or a quiet season.
- **To grow:** a second outlet, a new machine, a larger vehicle, or entering a new market.

The reason sets the timescale, and the timescale is the first test of whether a source fits.

## The sources in more depth

### Internal

**Retained profit** is profit kept in the business instead of being paid to the owners. It costs no interest and gives away no control, but the owners give up that income, and a new business has none.

**Selling unwanted assets**, such as an old van or a spare piece of land, turns things the business no longer needs into cash. It is a one-off, and selling quickly may mean accepting a low price.

### External

**Family and friends** may lend or invest on easy terms, often with little or no interest. Amounts are usually small, and a disagreement over repayment can damage relationships.

**A new share issue** raises money by selling part-ownership in a company. Nothing has to be repaid and no interest is charged, but new shareholders own part of the business, share future profits and may want a say in decisions. Only companies can issue shares.

**A loan** is a fixed sum borrowed from a bank and repaid with interest in instalments over an agreed period. A **mortgage** is a long-term loan secured on property, so the lender can take the property if repayments are missed.

**An overdraft** lets a business spend more than it has in its bank account, up to an agreed limit. Interest is charged only on the amount overdrawn, but the rate is usually high and the bank can reduce or withdraw it.

**Trade credit** is buying from a supplier now and paying later, often within 30 or 60 days. It costs nothing if the business pays on time, but late payment harms the relationship and any discount for paying early is lost.

**Hire purchase** lets the business use an asset straight away, paying a deposit and then instalments. The total paid is more than the cash price, and the business does not own the asset until the final payment.

**Government grants** are sums given to businesses that meet set conditions, for example creating jobs in a particular area. They often do not need to be repaid, but applying is slow and competitive.

## Matching source to need

| Need | Short-term or long-term | Suitable sources |
|---|---|---|
| Wages due before a large customer pays | Short | Overdraft, trade credit with suppliers |
| Stock for a busy season | Short | Trade credit, overdraft |
| A delivery van | Medium | Hire purchase, loan, selling an old van |
| New factory building | Long | Mortgage, share issue, retained profit |
| Opening a first shop | Long | Owner's funds with family and friends, a loan, a grant |

A short-term source should not be used for a long-term purchase. An overdraft used to buy a building could be withdrawn while the debt still remains.

### Worked example: the cost of a loan

Trethow Tiles borrows £20,000 to buy a tile-cutting machine. The bank sets 36 monthly repayments of £640.

- Total repaid: 36 × £640 = £23,040
- Cost of borrowing (interest): £23,040 − £20,000 = **£3,040**

The machine is expected to save £900 a month in labour, so the repayment of £640 is covered by the saving. The loan fits a long-term asset and the business keeps full ownership.

### Worked example: the cost of a share issue

Polgrain Prints Ltd has 10,000 shares, all owned by its founder. To raise £50,000 for a second shop, it sells 2,500 new shares to an investor at £20 each.

- Money raised: 2,500 × £20 = £50,000
- Total shares now: 10,000 + 2,500 = 12,500
- Founder's share of ownership: 10,000 ÷ 12,500 × 100 = **80%**
- Investor's share: 2,500 ÷ 12,500 × 100 = **20%**

There is nothing to repay and no interest. The cost is permanent: the investor receives 20% of every future dividend and has a say in decisions. If the second shop doubles profit, the founder may wish she had borrowed instead.

### Worked example: overdraft or loan?

Penwell Garden Services needs £6,000 to pay wages during February and March, before spring work starts. An overdraft at a high rate would cost about £150 in interest for the two months. A one-year loan would cost £450 in interest, and the business would still be repaying long after the busy season had brought in cash. The overdraft is cheaper here because it is only used for the weeks it is needed, matching the short-term nature of the gap.

## New or established business?

| Factor | New business | Established business |
|---|---|---|
| Retained profit | None yet | Often available |
| Assets to sell | Few | Some, such as old equipment |
| Track record for lenders | None, so loans are harder to get or more expensive | Accounts show it can repay |
| Security for a loan or mortgage | Little | Property or equipment |
| Share issue | Possible for a new company, but investors see high risk | Easier if profits are proven |
| Likely sources | Owners' savings, family and friends, a grant, trade credit if suppliers trust it, hire purchase | Retained profit, loans, mortgages, share issues, overdrafts |

A new business is a risk to lenders because nobody knows whether it will survive. That is why family and friends, and grants aimed at start-ups, matter so much at the beginning.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Evaluate whether Maenlow Bakery, which has traded for eight years, should use retained profit or a bank loan to fund a £40,000 shop extension.* (The case says it has £55,000 of retained profit and a loan would cost £7,000 in interest.)

"Retained profit avoids the £7,000 of interest and keeps full control. *(advantage, using the figure)* The bakery has £55,000, so it can cover the cost. *(application)* However, using £40,000 leaves only £15,000, which might not cover an emergency such as an oven breaking down. *(drawback)* A loan keeps the cash reserve but adds monthly repayments. *(alternative)* I would use retained profit, because the bakery is established, has enough, and saves £7,000; it should keep the remaining £15,000 as a safety margin." *(judgement with reason)*

## Common mistakes

- Calling retained profit "money from the owner". It is profit kept in the business.
- Saying a share issue must be repaid, or that a sole trader can issue shares.
- Using an overdraft for a long-term purchase.
- Forgetting that hire purchase and loans cost more in total than the cash price.
- Choosing a loan for a new business with no mention of the difficulty of borrowing without a track record.
- Listing advantages of a source without linking them to the business in the case.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate:** total repaid, cost of interest or share of ownership; show working.
- **Explain:** one advantage or disadvantage of a source for this business.
- **Analyse:** cost, timescale, control and risk, applied to the case.
- **Evaluate:** compare two sources and decide, using the age of the business and the figures given.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are internal sources of finance?**
Retained profit and selling unwanted assets, because the money comes from within the business.

**Why is it harder for a new business to get a bank loan?**
It has no trading record to show it can repay and often little property to offer as security, so the lender sees it as a high risk.

**What is the main drawback of a new share issue?**
The original owners give up part of the ownership, so they share future profits and control with the new shareholders.
