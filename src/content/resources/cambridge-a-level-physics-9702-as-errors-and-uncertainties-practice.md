## Section A

**1.** A set of measurements is precise but not accurate. Which statement explains this?

A: The readings are widely scattered about the true value.

B: The readings are close together but all differ from the true value in the same direction.

C: The readings are close together and close to the true value.

D: The readings are widely scattered and their mean is far from the true value. **[1]**

**2.** A quantity Q is calculated from Q = x²/y. The percentage uncertainty in x is 2% and in y is 3%. What is the percentage uncertainty in Q?

A: 1%

B: 5%

C: 7%

D: 12% **[1]**

**3.** State **one** difference between a systematic error and a random error. **[1]**

**4.** The end of a metre rule is worn, so its zero mark is 2 mm from the end of the rule. A student lines up the end of the rule, not the zero mark, with one end of each object measured. Explain the effect on the measured lengths and state the type of error. **[2]**

**5.** A micrometer screw gauge reads −0.02 mm when its jaws are closed. With a wire between the jaws it reads 0.47 mm. Calculate the diameter of the wire. **[1]**

**6.** The diameter of a wire is measured at five points as 0.42, 0.40, 0.43, 0.41 and 0.42 mm. Calculate the mean diameter and its absolute uncertainty. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A trolley travels a distance s = (1.50 ± 0.01) m in a time t = (0.62 ± 0.02) s.

**(a)** Calculate its average speed. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the speed. **[2]**

**(c)** Express the speed with its absolute uncertainty, to an appropriate number of significant figures. **[1]**

**(d)** Suggest which measurement contributes more to the uncertainty, and one way to reduce it. **[2]**

**8.** A steel ball has diameter d = (2.00 ± 0.02) cm and mass m = (32.6 ± 0.1) g. The volume of a sphere is πd³/6.

**(a)** Calculate the density of the steel in g cm⁻³. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the density. **[2]**

**(c)** Give the density with its absolute uncertainty in kg m⁻³. **[2]**

**9.** A force F = (250 ± 5) N acts on a square plate of side l = (0.050 ± 0.001) m. The pressure is p = F/l².

**(a)** Calculate p. **[1]**

**(b)** Determine the absolute uncertainty in p. **[3]**

**10.** The energy supplied to a heater is E = VIt, where V = (6.0 ± 0.1) V, I = (1.20 ± 0.02) A and t = (120 ± 1) s.

**(a)** Calculate E. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the absolute uncertainty in E. **[3]**

**(c)** The heater warms a block from (19.5 ± 0.5) °C to (23.0 ± 0.5) °C. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the temperature rise and comment on which measurement most limits the experiment. **[3]**

**11.** A student measures g with a pendulum and obtains (9.42 ± 0.19) m s⁻².

**(a)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the result. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage difference between the result and 9.81 m s⁻². **[1]**

**(c)** State whether the result agrees with 9.81 m s⁻² within its uncertainty, and suggest one systematic error that could explain the difference. **[2]**

**12.** Explain why measuring the thickness of a single sheet of paper with a metre rule gives a large percentage uncertainty, and describe how the measurement could be improved. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** 2 × 2% + 3% = 7%, so **C** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** For example: a systematic error shifts all readings in the same direction by the same amount, while a random error scatters them above and below the true value; or repeating and averaging reduces random error but not systematic error [1]. **[1]**

**4.** The first 2 mm of each object lies before the zero mark, so every reading is 2 mm **less** than the true length [1]. This is a systematic error (a zero error), which affects accuracy but not precision [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 0.47 − (−0.02) = **0.49 mm** [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Mean = 2.08 ÷ 5 = **0.416 mm ≈ 0.42 mm** [1]. Half-range = (0.43 − 0.40) ÷ 2 = 0.015 mm, so d = **(0.42 ± 0.02) mm** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** v = 1.50 ÷ 0.62 = **2.42 m s⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** %s = 0.01 ÷ 1.50 × 100 = 0.67%; %t = 0.02 ÷ 0.62 × 100 = 3.2% [1]; %v = 0.67 + 3.2 = **3.9%** [1].

**(c)** Δv = 0.039 × 2.42 = 0.09 m s⁻¹, so v = **(2.42 ± 0.09) m s⁻¹** [1].

**(d)** The time (3.2% compared with 0.67%) [1]. Use light gates and an electronic timer instead of a hand-operated stopwatch, or increase the distance so the time is longer [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** V = π × 2.00³ ÷ 6 = 4.19 cm³ [1]; ρ = 32.6 ÷ 4.19 = **7.78 g cm⁻³** [1].

**(b)** %m = 0.31%; %d = 1.0%, tripled to 3.0% [1]; %ρ = 0.31 + 3.0 = **3.3%** [1].

**(c)** Δρ = 0.033 × 7.78 = 0.26 g cm⁻³ [1]; ρ = **(7800 ± 300) kg m⁻³** [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** p = 250 ÷ 0.050² = **1.0 × 10⁵ Pa** [1].

**(b)** %F = 5 ÷ 250 × 100 = 2.0%; %l = 2.0%, doubled to 4.0% [1]; %p = 6.0% [1]; Δp = 0.060 × 1.0 × 10⁵ = **6 × 10³ Pa** [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** E = 6.0 × 1.20 × 120 = **864 J** [1].

**(b)** %V = 1.7%, %I = 1.7%, %t = 0.83% [1]; total = 4.2% [1]; ΔE = 0.042 × 864 = **36 J**, so E = (860 ± 40) J [1].

**(c)** Rise = 3.5 °C with uncertainty 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0 °C [1]; percentage uncertainty = 1.0 ÷ 3.5 × 100 = **29%** [1]. This is far larger than the 4.2% in E, so the temperature rise most limits the experiment; a larger rise, or a thermometer with a finer scale, would help [1]. **[7]**

**11. (a)** 0.19 ÷ 9.42 × 100 = **2.0%** [1].

**(b)** (9.81 − 9.42) ÷ 9.81 × 100 = **4.0%** [1].

**(c)** No: the 4.0% difference is larger than the 2.0% uncertainty [1]. For example, the length was measured to the top of the bob instead of its centre, making every value of l too small; or the timer ran fast [1]. **[4]**

**12.** The thickness (about 0.1 mm) is smaller than the smallest scale division of 1 mm [1], so the absolute uncertainty is about as large as the value and the percentage uncertainty is close to 100% or more [1]. Measure the thickness of a stack of many sheets (for example 200) and divide by the number of sheets, or use a micrometer screw gauge [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding percentage uncertainties when two quantities are subtracted.
- Forgetting to multiply by the power for squared or cubed quantities.
- Quoting the uncertainty to several significant figures, or the value to more decimal places than the uncertainty.
- Saying averaging corrects a systematic error.
- Comparing a result with an accepted value without comparing the percentage difference with the percentage uncertainty.
