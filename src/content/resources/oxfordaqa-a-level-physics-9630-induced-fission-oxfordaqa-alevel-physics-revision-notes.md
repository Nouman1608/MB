## Condensed recall

- **Induced fission:** a nucleus such as uranium-235 absorbs a neutron, becomes unstable and splits into two fragments plus 2 or 3 neutrons, releasing energy (about 200 MeV in total).
- Fragments are usually unequal in size; they carry most of the energy as kinetic energy.
- **Thermal neutrons** (about 0.03 eV) are much more likely to cause fission of uranium-235 than fast fission neutrons (1 to 2 MeV).
- **Chain reaction:** neutrons from one fission cause further fissions.
- Critical: on average exactly one neutron per fission causes another fission (steady). Supercritical: more than one (growing). Subcritical: fewer than one (dying).
- Neutron fates: further fission; escape through the surface; capture by uranium-238; absorption by moderator, coolant, structure or control rods.
- **Critical mass:** the minimum mass of fissile material that sustains a chain reaction. Below it too many neutrons escape through the surface.
- Critical mass is reduced by a spherical shape, higher enrichment, higher density and the presence of a moderator.
- Equations: conserve nucleon number and proton number; include the incoming neutron.
- Control: lowering the rods absorbs more neutrons and reduces the fission rate; raising them increases it. Steady power needs the rods set so that exactly one neutron per fission goes on to cause another.

## Reactor components

| Component | Function | Desirable properties | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moderator | Slows neutrons to thermal speeds by elastic collisions | Light nuclei; absorbs few neutrons | Water, heavy water, graphite |
| Control rods | Absorb neutrons to control the fission rate | Strong neutron absorber; keeps its strength when hot | Boron, cadmium |
| Coolant | Removes energy from the core | High specific heat capacity; absorbs few neutrons; easy to pump | Pressurised water, carbon dioxide |

## The mechanical model

For a head-on elastic collision of mass m (speed u) with stationary mass M:

- incoming body after: v = u(m − M)/(m + M)
- target after: V = 2mu/(m + M)
- fraction of kinetic energy kept: ((M − m)/(M + m))²

| Target | M/m | Fraction of KE kept |
|---|---|---|
| Equal mass (hydrogen-1) | 1 | 0 |
| Deuterium | 2 | 1/9 |
| Carbon-12 | 12 | (11/13)² = 0.72 |

Light targets remove the most energy per collision. A heavy target sends the neutron back with almost its original speed.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Induced fission | Fission caused by absorption of a neutron |
| Thermal neutron | A neutron with kinetic energy comparable with the thermal energy of its surroundings |
| Chain reaction | Self-sustaining sequence of fissions, each triggered by neutrons from earlier ones |
| Critical mass | Minimum mass of fissile material for a self-sustaining chain reaction |
| Moderator | Material that slows neutrons by elastic collisions |
| Control rod | Neutron-absorbing rod that is raised or lowered to control the reaction rate |
| Coolant | Fluid that transfers energy from the core |
| Enrichment | Increasing the proportion of uranium-235 in uranium fuel |

## Exam traps

- "The moderator absorbs neutrons": no, it slows them.
- "The moderator slows the reaction down": no, it makes fission more likely.
- Critical mass defined as "the mass needed for an explosion".
- Heavy nuclei suggested for a moderator because they are dense.
- The incoming neutron left out when balancing an equation.
- A rebounding neutron given a positive velocity after hitting a heavier nucleus.
- Coolant described only as "keeping the reactor cool" without saying the energy is carried away for use.

## Quick self-test

1. Define induced fission.
2. What is a thermal neutron?
3. ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹³⁷₅₅Cs + ᴬ₃₇Rb + 2 ¹₀n. Find A.
4. Define critical mass.
5. Give three factors that reduce the critical mass.
6. State the function of the moderator.
7. State the function of the control rods and give one suitable material.
8. State the function of the coolant and give one example.
9. What fraction of its kinetic energy does a neutron keep after a head-on elastic collision with a stationary deuterium nucleus?
10. Hydrogen-1 takes the most energy per collision. Why might heavy water be chosen instead?
11. How many fissions per second, at 200 MeV each, give a power of 1.0 GW?
12. On average each fission in a reactor leads to 0.98 further fissions. What happens to the reaction?

### Answers

1. Fission of a nucleus caused by the absorption of a neutron.
2. A neutron that has been slowed until its kinetic energy is comparable with the thermal energy of the surrounding particles (a few hundredths of an eV).
3. 236 = 137 + A + 2, so A = 97; check protons: 92 = 55 + 37.
4. The minimum mass of fissile material that can sustain a chain reaction.
5. Any three of: spherical shape; higher enrichment in uranium-235; higher density; presence of a moderator.
6. To slow fast neutrons to thermal speeds by elastic collisions, so they are more likely to cause fission.
7. To absorb neutrons and so control the rate of fission; boron or cadmium.
8. To carry energy away from the core; pressurised water or carbon dioxide.
9. ((2 − 1)/(2 + 1))² = 1/9.
10. Hydrogen-1 absorbs some neutrons; deuterium absorbs far fewer, so fewer neutrons are wasted and less enriched fuel is needed.
11. 1.0 × 10⁹ / (200 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³) = 3.1 × 10¹⁹ per second.
12. It is subcritical: the rate of fission falls with each generation and the reaction dies away.
