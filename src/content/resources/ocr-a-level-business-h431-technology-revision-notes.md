Condensed notes on the Technology content (4.12 on this site) in the area of study Productive efficiency, OCR A Level Business H431, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027. Full explanations and two costed examples are in the [technology study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-technology/). Test yourself with the [technology practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-technology-practice/). All figures are invented.

## What the specification lists

- Explain how technology can be applied to the operations of a business.
- Evaluate the impact and importance of technology to the operations of a business.
- Evaluate the impact and importance of technology to the stakeholders of a business.

The specification names no particular technologies, so any that fit the case may be used.

## Core recall

### Where technology is applied

| Stage | Technology | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Design | CAD; 3D printing for prototypes | Faster, cheaper development; easy changes |
| Production | CAM; automation; robots | Higher output, consistent quality, less waste |
| Stock and supply | Barcodes, radio-frequency tags, stock software | Real-time records, automatic reorders, supports JIT |
| Quality | Sensors, cameras, machine data | Every item checked; faults predicted |
| Selling and service | E-commerce, online booking, customer records, chat | Sales and service outside normal hours |
| Management information | Sales and machine data | Better forecasting and capacity planning |

### Benefits

- Higher productivity (output per worker).
- Lower unit cost once the equipment is paid for.
- Consistent quality.
- Speed.
- Some flexibility from computer control.
- Better information.

### Costs and risks

- Capital cost to buy and install.
- Running cost: maintenance, energy, licences.
- Training.
- Job losses and redundancy cost.
- System failure, software faults, cyber-attack.
- Inflexibility of specialised equipment.
- Obsolescence.

### Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Gain | Loss or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Lower costs; competitive edge | Large investment; risk of failure |
| Employees | Safer, less repetitive work; higher skills | Redundancy; retraining; stress |
| Customers | Quality, speed, online ordering | Less personal service; data security |
| Suppliers | Electronic ordering; bigger orders | Pressure to meet system standards |
| Community | Skilled jobs; less waste | Fewer local jobs; energy use |

### Useful calculations

- **Productivity** = output ÷ number of workers (or hours).
- **Payback** = cost of investment ÷ annual net cash benefit.
- **Annual net cash benefit** = savings − running costs.
- **Unit cost** = total cost ÷ output.
- Include one-off costs such as redundancy in payback.

## Worked reminder

Barrow Metalworks (invented): 120,000 parts, 10 workers at £33,600 including on-costs, robot £240,000 replacing 4 workers.

- Productivity: 12,000 → 20,000 parts per worker, **+66.7%**.
- Labour saving 4 × £33,600 = £134,400; scrap saving (6,000 − 2,400) × £4 = £14,400; maintenance £30,000.
- Annual net cash benefit = 134,400 + 14,400 − 30,000 = **£118,800**.
- Payback = 240,000 ÷ 118,800 = **2.0 years**; with £36,000 redundancy costs, 276,000 ÷ 118,800 = **2.3 years**.
- 3D printer: £36,000 ÷ £15,000 saving = **2.4 years**.

## Judging a technology decision: five questions

1. Is demand secure for long enough to repay the investment?
2. Can the business afford the cost, or should it lease or phase it in?
3. What happens if the system fails?
4. Are staff trained, consulted and, if possible, redeployed?
5. Is the technology a necessity (rivals have it) or an option?

## Linking technology to other operations content

- **Productivity and unit cost:** technology raises output per worker, but capital cost and depreciation add to unit cost, so compare the two.
- **Capacity:** automated lines raise maximum output, but fixed costs rise, so low utilisation hurts more.
- **Quality:** sensors and machine control cut defects, which cuts wastage and the cost of rework.
- **Stock control and lean production:** barcodes and software make just in time and kanban possible.
- **Methods of production:** technology suits flow and batch production most, because tasks repeat; job production gains mainly through CAD and 3D printing.
- **Location and logistics:** online selling and tracking change where stock is held and how orders are delivered.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| CAD | Computer-aided design |
| CAM | Computer-aided manufacture: computers control machines |
| Automation | Machines doing tasks with little human input |
| E-commerce | Selling goods or services over the internet |
| Productivity | Output per unit of input, usually per worker |
| Payback | Time to recover the cost of an investment |
| Obsolescence | Becoming out of date |
| Cyber-attack | Attempt to damage or steal from computer systems |

## Exam traps

- Link each technology to a stage of operations, not just a name.
- Do not assume technology always cuts cost; include capital and running costs.
- Balance job losses with benefits to staff such as safer work and new skills.
- Mention system failure and data security.
- In services, think of booking systems, self-service and records rather than robots.
- Show payback working and say what it assumes.

## Self-test

1. Give two uses of technology in the design stage.
2. What does CAM mean?
3. Output rises from 80,000 units with 20 workers to 99,000 units with 18 workers. Calculate the productivity change as a percentage.
4. A machine costs £90,000 and saves £30,000 a year net. Calculate the payback period.
5. If redundancy costs of £12,000 are added, what is the new payback period?
6. Give two risks of relying on technology.
7. Why might a small business prefer to lease equipment?
8. State one benefit of e-commerce to a customer.
9. State one negative effect of automation on employees.
10. Why can technology give only a temporary advantage?

### Answers

1. Any two: CAD drawings and testing; 3D printing prototypes; sharing digital designs with suppliers.
2. Computer-aided manufacture: a computer controls machines directly from the design file.
3. Before: 80,000 ÷ 20 = 4,000. After: 99,000 ÷ 18 = 5,500. Change = 1,500 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = **+37.5%**.
4. £90,000 ÷ £30,000 = **3 years**.
5. (£90,000 + £12,000) ÷ £30,000 = **3.4 years**.
6. Any two: breakdown or software failure; cyber-attack; high running costs; obsolescence.
7. Leasing avoids a large capital outlay and spreads the cost, and the equipment may be replaced when it becomes out of date.
8. Any one: can order at any time; wider choice; order tracking.
9. Any one: redundancy; need to retrain; stress about job security.
10. Rivals can buy the same equipment, and the technology becomes out of date.
