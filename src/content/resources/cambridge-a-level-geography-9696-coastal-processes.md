## Wave generation

Waves are generated by wind blowing over the sea surface. Friction and pressure differences transfer energy from the air to the water. Wave size depends on three factors:

- **Wind speed:** stronger winds transfer more energy.
- **Wind duration:** the longer the wind blows, the larger the waves grow, up to a limit.
- **Fetch:** the distance of open water over which the wind blows. Coasts facing long fetches, such as those exposed to the open Atlantic, receive the largest waves.

Waves that have left their generating area become **swell**: long, regular waves that travel thousands of kilometres with little energy loss. Waves still driven by the wind that formed them are **sea**: shorter, steeper and less regular.

## Wave characteristics

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wave height (H) | Vertical distance from trough to crest |
| Wavelength (L) | Horizontal distance between successive crests |
| Wave period (T) | Time for successive crests to pass a fixed point |
| Wave steepness | Height ÷ wavelength |
| Wave speed (C) | Wavelength ÷ period |

In open water, water particles move in near-circular **orbits**; the wave form moves forward but the water itself moves very little. Orbits shrink with depth and become negligible at the **wave base**, roughly half a wavelength below the surface.

**Deep-water relationships.** In deep water, wavelength and speed depend on the period:

- L ≈ 1.56 × T² (metres, with T in seconds)
- C ≈ 1.56 × T (metres per second)

**Wave energy.** The energy of a wave per square metre of sea surface is proportional to the **square of its height** (E = ⅛ρgH², where ρ is the density of sea water and g the acceleration due to gravity). So a 3 m wave carries 9 times the energy of a 1 m wave.

### Worked example

*A swell wave in deep water has a period of 10 s and a height of 2 m. A storm wave has a period of 6 s and a height of 4 m.*

- Swell wavelength ≈ 1.56 × 10² = **156 m**; speed ≈ 1.56 × 10 = **15.6 m/s**.
- Storm wavelength ≈ 1.56 × 6² = **56.2 m**; speed ≈ 1.56 × 6 = **9.4 m/s**.
- Steepness: swell 2 ÷ 156 = **0.013**; storm 4 ÷ 56.2 = **0.071**.
- Energy ratio, storm to swell = (4 ÷ 2)² = **4**.

The storm wave is more than five times steeper and carries four times the energy per square metre. Steep, high-energy waves like this are the ones that remove sediment from beaches.

## Waves approaching the shore

When the water depth becomes less than about half the wavelength, the orbits touch the sea bed. Friction slows the wave, so:

- wavelength shortens and the waves bunch together;
- wave height increases as energy is concentrated into a smaller volume (shoaling);
- the orbits become elliptical, and the base of the wave slows more than the crest.

The wave **breaks** when it becomes too steep to support itself, usually when the water depth is roughly 1.3 times the wave height.

**Breaker types:**

| Type | Beach gradient | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Spilling | Gentle | Crest tumbles gradually down the front of the wave; energy released over a wide surf zone |
| Plunging | Moderate | Crest curls over and crashes down; energy released violently in a small area |
| Surging | Steep | Wave slides up the beach without fully breaking; strong backwash |

### Refraction

Where waves approach an irregular coastline, the parts of each wave in shallow water slow down first. Wave crests bend to become more parallel to the depth contours. Lines drawn at right angles to the crests (orthogonals) **converge** on headlands and **diverge** in bays. Energy is therefore concentrated on headlands, which are eroded, and spread out in bays, where deposition occurs. Over time, refraction tends to straighten an indented coastline.

### High-energy and low-energy waves

| | High-energy (destructive) waves | Low-energy (constructive) waves |
|---|---|---|
| Form | High, steep, short wavelength | Low, flat, long wavelength |
| Swash and backwash | Backwash stronger than swash | Swash stronger than backwash |
| Effect | Remove sediment; lower and flatten the beach; build offshore bars | Push sediment up the beach; build berms; steepen the beach |

Waves and beach gradient form a **feedback**: constructive waves steepen a beach, which makes waves plunge and backwash strengthen, so the beach is then flattened. Beach profiles therefore shift between calm and stormy spells.

## Marine erosion

The five processes:

- **Hydraulic action:** the force of water striking rock, and air trapped and compressed in joints, widening cracks.
- **Cavitation:** bubbles in the water collapse, sending out small shock waves that weaken rock.
- **Corrasion (abrasion):** sediment thrown at, or dragged across, the rock surface by waves. Often the most effective process where waves carry sand and shingle.
- **Solution:** chemical dissolving of rock, especially limestone and chalk, by sea water.
- **Attrition:** particles collide and wear each other into smaller, rounder fragments; it changes sediment, not the cliff.

**Factors controlling the rate of erosion:**

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Wave energy | Higher waves, longer fetch and storms increase erosion |
| Rock type | Weak clays and glacial till erode far faster than granite or basalt |
| Rock structure | Joints, faults and bedding give lines of weakness; seaward-dipping beds are prone to sliding |
| Beach width | A wide beach absorbs wave energy and protects the cliff foot |
| Human activity | Defences can protect one area while starving another of sediment |

## Sub-aerial processes

Sub-aerial processes act on the cliff face above the reach of the waves.

**Weathering:**

- **Salt crystallisation:** growing salt crystals from evaporating spray force rock grains apart.
- **Wetting and drying (slaking):** clays swell when wet, then shrink and crack.
- **Biological weathering:** roots, and molluscs that bore into rock near the shore.
- **Freeze-thaw** in cold climates, and **solution** of limestone by rainwater.

**Mass movement:**

- **Rockfall** from steep cliffs of jointed rock.
- **Sliding** along bedding planes that dip towards the sea.
- **Rotational slumping** in clays and till after heavy rain saturates the cliff, giving curved slip surfaces and a stepped cliff profile.
- **Mudflows** of saturated fine material.

**Linking sub-aerial and marine processes.** Marine erosion undercuts the cliff and removes debris from its foot. Sub-aerial processes deliver material down the cliff face. Where waves remove debris quickly, the cliff stays steep and retreats fast. Where debris accumulates, it protects the foot until waves remove it. Cliff profiles therefore reflect the **balance** between the two sets of processes: steep, vertical cliffs where marine processes dominate, and lower-angled, slumped cliffs where sub-aerial processes dominate.

## Transport and deposition

Waves transport sediment by **traction** (rolling), **saltation** (bouncing), **suspension** and **solution**. Sediment moves both **onshore-offshore**, changing the beach profile, and **alongshore**, by longshore drift.

### Longshore drift

Where waves approach at an angle, swash carries sediment up the beach at the angle of wave approach. Backwash returns it straight down the slope under gravity. The result is a zig-zag movement along the beach in the direction of the prevailing waves. 
### Sediment sources and characteristics

**Sources:** rivers (globally the largest source), cliff erosion, the offshore sea bed, biological material such as shells and coral fragments, wind-blown sand, and human inputs such as beach nourishment.

**Characteristics:**

- **Size:** from clay and silt through sand to shingle and boulders. Larger particles need more energy to move.
- **Shape and roundness:** rounding increases with distance and time of transport through attrition.
- **Sorting:** how similar particles are in size. Waves sort sediment well; slumped cliff debris is poorly sorted.

Deposition occurs where energy falls: in sheltered bays, where the coast changes direction and behind obstacles.

### Sediment cells and budgets

A **sediment cell** is a length of coast, usually bounded by major headlands or estuaries, within which sediment circulates between **sources**, **transfers** and **sinks** with little exchange across its boundaries. The coast of England and Wales has been divided into 11 major cells for shoreline planning, each split into sub-cells.

A **sediment budget** compares inputs and outputs:

**Worked example (fictional sub-cell, thousands of m³ per year).**

| Inputs | | Outputs | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cliff erosion | 180 | Loss offshore | 90 |
| River supply | 70 | Deposition on a spit at the cell end | 120 |
| Onshore from sea bed | 50 | Dredging for navigation | 40 |
| **Total** | **300** | **Total** | **250** |

Net budget = 300 − 250 = **+50** thousand m³ per year. The sub-cell is gaining sediment overall, so beaches in it may widen. If a sea wall halted cliff erosion, inputs would fall to 120 thousand m³, giving a budget of 120 − 250 = **−130**: beaches would be starved and erosion would increase elsewhere.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that energy doubles when wave height doubles; it rises four times.
- Treating attrition as erosion of the cliff; it acts on sediment.
- Describing sub-aerial processes without linking them to marine removal at the cliff foot.
- Explaining longshore drift without stating the role of the angle of wave approach.
