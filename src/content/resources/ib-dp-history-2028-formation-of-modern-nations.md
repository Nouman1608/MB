**HL only.** This is the fifth study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 asks you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. Every question on this study will require you to refer to **one country** from the region. This guide uses the **United States** as its main case, with **Argentina** and **Canada** as comparisons. The question is how states in the Americas became industrial, diverse and more democratic between 1860 and 1929, and who gained and lost.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: economic transformation; the impact of migration; the impact of ideological trends; political and social transformations; the role of leaders; and challenges. They link to **continuity and change**, **cause and consequence** and **significance**. See the [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/) for help in using them.

## Key chronology (United States, with comparisons)

- 1862: Homestead Act. 1867: Canadian Confederation.
- 1869: first transcontinental railroad completed.
- 1870s to 1880s: Argentine campaigns against Indigenous peoples; 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act.
- 1887: Dawes Act. 1890: Sherman Antitrust Act; Wounded Knee.
- 1892: Ellis Island opens; Populist Party forms. 1896: *Plessy v. Ferguson*.
- 1901 to 1909: Theodore Roosevelt. 1913 to 1921: Woodrow Wilson.
- 1913: 16th and 17th Amendments. 1919: Red Scare. 1920: 19th Amendment.
- 1924: Immigration Act with national-origin quotas.

## Economic transformation

**Railroad construction.** US railway mileage grew from about 30,000 miles in 1860 to roughly 190,000 by 1900. The railroads linked farms to cities and the Atlantic and Pacific coasts, created national markets, and spurred demand for steel, coal and capital. Government land grants helped, and Chinese and Irish workers did much of the construction. In Argentina, mainly British-owned railways fanned out from Buenos Aires to carry grain and beef to the port. In Canada, the Canadian Pacific Railway (completed 1885) bound the new country together.

**Industrial growth and urbanisation.** After the Civil War, the US became the world's largest industrial economy by the early 20th century. Steel (Andrew Carnegie), oil (John D. Rockefeller) and later automobiles (Henry Ford's assembly line) led growth. Cities such as Chicago and New York expanded fast, and by the 1920 census more than half of Americans lived in urban areas. Cities brought factories and opportunity, and also slums, disease and pollution.

**Trade.** The US exported farm goods and manufactures, and from the 1890s sought markets in Latin America and Asia. Argentina became a major exporter of beef and wheat, mainly to Britain. Brazil exported coffee. Trade between American states was growing but was small compared with trade with Europe.

**Neocolonialism and dependency.** Many Latin American economies depended on exporting a few raw materials and on foreign capital, often British, then American. Critics later called this **dependency**: a pattern of development shaped by outside investors, in which local elites gained but the economy was exposed to price falls. Others argue that the export boom brought real growth, and ask what the alternative was. Both positions are useful for evaluation.

## Impacts of migration

**Arrival of immigrants.** About 20 million or more immigrants came to the US between the 1880s and 1920, mostly from southern and eastern Europe in the later period. Argentina and Brazil also received millions of Europeans, especially Italians, Spaniards and Portuguese, and Canada drew European and Asian settlers. Asian migrants, such as Chinese and Japanese workers, met barriers: the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) in the US and head taxes in Canada.

**Land.** The Homestead Act opened western land to settlers. The Dawes Act (1887) broke up Indigenous communal land into individual allotments, which reduced the land Native peoples held. In Argentina, the "Conquest of the Desert" (1879 to 1880) under General Roca seized Indigenous land in Patagonia and the pampas for settlement and cattle.

**Religion.** Immigration added to Catholic, Jewish and Orthodox communities, and produced both new institutions and anti-Catholic and antisemitic prejudice. Protestant reform movements sought to "Americanise" newcomers.

**Indigenous peoples.** Wars, the loss of the buffalo, reservations and boarding schools reduced Native American independence. The massacre at Wounded Knee (1890) is often taken as an end point. In Canada, residential schools were set up to assimilate Indigenous children.

## Impact of ideological trends

- **Nationalism, liberalism and progressivism.** Liberal ideas of free trade, property rights and limited government shaped the economy. **Progressivism** responded to the problems of industrial society with reforms to regulate business, clean up politics and improve conditions. Theodore Roosevelt, Robert La Follette and Woodrow Wilson are associated with it.
- **Social Darwinism.** Writers such as Herbert Spencer and William Graham Sumner applied "survival of the fittest" to society, and used it to defend inequality and, with racial ideas, empire and immigration restriction. Carnegie's "Gospel of Wealth" (1889) argued that the rich had a duty to give.
- **Expansionist ideas.** Manifest destiny and the belief in a civilising mission led to western settlement and overseas expansion in the 1890s.
- **Indigenismo and nativism.** *Indigenismo*, notably in Peru and Mexico, valued Indigenous culture and called for social reform, though it was often led by non-Indigenous intellectuals. **Nativism** in the US favoured native-born Protestants against immigrants, and led to the 1924 quota law and the revival of the Ku Klux Klan after 1915.

## Political and social transformations

**Constitutional reforms.** The US adopted the 16th Amendment (income tax), the 17th (direct election of senators) and the 18th (Prohibition) and the 19th (women's suffrage). The Pendleton Act (1883) began civil service reform. Argentina's 1912 Sáenz Peña law introduced secret, compulsory male voting.

**Party systems.** The US had a stable two-party system, though the Populist Party (1892) and later the Progressive movement challenged it. In Argentina, the Radical Civic Union broke the hold of the old conservative elite, and Hipólito Yrigoyen became president in 1916.

**Democracy and women's suffrage.** Women's suffrage in the US came with the 19th Amendment (1920), after decades of campaigning led by organisations such as the National American Woman Suffrage Association. Canada's women gained the federal vote in stages around 1917 to 1918. Many Black Americans in the South were barred from voting by poll taxes, literacy tests and violence. Women in Argentina did not receive the vote until 1947.

**The arts.** Realist and naturalist writing (Mark Twain, Theodore Dreiser), skyscraper architecture in Chicago, and in the 1920s jazz and the Harlem Renaissance expressed urban and racial change. In Latin America, writers and painters explored national and Indigenous themes.

## Role of leaders

Leaders offered ideas, direction and mobilisation. Theodore Roosevelt used the presidency to regulate trusts, protect land and promote a "square deal". Wilson passed tariff, banking (the Federal Reserve, 1913) and antitrust reforms. Outside government, **W. E. B. Du Bois** and **Booker T. Washington** offered competing strategies for Black progress. In Argentina, Yrigoyen mobilised middle-class and immigrant voters. In Canada, John A. Macdonald's National Policy of tariffs, railways and settlement shaped the nation. Judge leaders by what they changed, and for whom.

## Challenges in the transformation of nations

**Economic challenges and inequality.** Wealth was concentrated: a few industrialists became very rich, while many workers earned low wages and endured long hours. Farmers faced falling prices and debt, which fuelled Populism.

**Marginalised groups.** African Americans faced segregation (*Plessy v. Ferguson*, 1896), disenfranchisement and lynching, and many left the South in the Great Migration. Indigenous peoples lost land. Asian immigrants faced exclusion. Women lacked equal rights.

**Labour movements.** The Haymarket affair (1886), the Homestead strike (1892), the Pullman strike (1894) and the steel strike of 1919 show conflict between capital and labour. The American Federation of Labor under Samuel Gompers won modest gains. The Industrial Workers of the World was more radical. The state often took the side of employers. In Argentina, the "Tragic Week" of 1919 saw violent repression of strikers. In Canada, the Winnipeg General Strike (1919) was ended by force.

**Internal conflicts.** Racial violence (such as the Tulsa massacre of 1921), the Red Scare of 1919 to 1920 and conflicts between farmers and railroads show tensions under the surface.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Was industrialisation the main force shaping the modern nation, or was it politics and reform?
- Did Progressivism democratise society, or did it protect middle-class and white interests?
- Was dependency a real constraint on Latin American development, or an excuse for local failures?
- Frederick Jackson Turner argued in 1893 that the frontier shaped American democracy. Later historians criticised this for ignoring Indigenous peoples and the role of the state.

## Common mistakes

- Writing about several countries with thin evidence. Choose one for depth.
- Describing industrial growth without linking to the nation's formation.
- Ignoring marginalised groups or women.
- Treating "progressive" as simply good.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use Argentina or Canada instead of the US?**
Yes, if you know the country in depth.

**How do I use the concepts?**
Use change and continuity to judge how far life changed for different groups.
