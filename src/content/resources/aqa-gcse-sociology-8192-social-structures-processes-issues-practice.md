## Questions

**1.** Define the term "theoretical perspective". **[2]**

**2.** Define "triangulation" and give one reason a sociologist might use it. **[3]**

**3.** Identify the sociological perspective that each of the following wrote from, as the specification presents them: **(i)** Zaretsky; **(ii)** Heidensohn; **(iii)** Davis and Moore; **(iv)** Becker. **[4]**

**4.** Explain one similarity and one difference between Merton's and Albert Cohen's explanations of crime. **[4]**

**5.** Study the table. The data are fictional.

| Calderby (a fictional town): 4,000 households surveyed | Households classed as poor |
|---|---|
| Measure A: income below the level that qualifies for state benefits | 260 |
| Measure B: income below a set share of the average for similar households | 480 |
| Measure C: lacking several items on a deprivation index (diet, heating, clothing, social activities) | 840 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of households classed as poor under Measure C, and how many times larger this is than the percentage under Measure A. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the three measures give such different figures for the same town. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain which measure Townsend would prefer, and give one criticism of that measure. **[4]**

**6.** Read the extract. It was written for this practice set and does not quote a real sociologist.

> "After a day on the production line, the worker goes home to be fed and cared for, ready to return tomorrow. Wages are spent on cars, furniture and holidays. Home seems a private world, separate from work, but it keeps the factories and shops running."

**(a)** Identify the perspective the extract most closely reflects, and quote one phrase that shows it. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how Delphy and Leonard might respond to the extract. **[4]**

**7.** A researcher wants to find out why young women in Fendlow (a fictional city) are convicted of fewer crimes than young men. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using in-depth unstructured interviews for this research, and one way mixed methods could improve the study. **[6]**

**8.** Using Halsey's findings, explain how a sociologist could challenge the everyday view that "school gives everyone the same chance, so the cleverest succeed". **[4]**

**9.** Explain how the idea of labelling connects the study of education and the study of crime. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate the usefulness of qualitative methods for studying social issues in education and crime. **[12]**

**11.** Discuss how far feminist interpretations of family life, crime and power remain convincing today. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** A general approach or set of ideas used to explain how society works [1], such as functionalism, Marxism, feminism or interactionism [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs a named example; "a point of view" alone is too vague to earn both marks.

**2.** Using more than one method or source of data [1] to check whether the findings agree [1]. Reason: if two methods give the same result, the researcher can be more confident the findings are valid [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep triangulation separate from mixed methods; the reason must refer to checking or confidence in findings.

**3. (i)** Marxist [1]; **(ii)** feminist [1]; **(iii)** functionalist [1]; **(iv)** interactionist [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Learn the perspective printed with each named sociologist; questions on extracts often start from this link.

**4.** Similarity: both are functionalist accounts in which working-class people share society's success goals [1] but lack legitimate opportunities to reach them, for example through educational failure [1]. Difference: Merton describes individual responses to the strain, such as innovation [1], while Cohen describes a group response: a delinquent subculture in which boys gain status and prestige from their peers, a reaction to status frustration [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each half needs a point and its development; a similarity that just says "both are about crime" earns nothing.

**5. (a)** 840 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = 21% [1]. Measure A is 260 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = 6.5%, and 21 ÷ 6.5 ≈ 3.2 times larger [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Poverty is a social construct, so the figure depends on how it is defined [1]. Measure A depends on benefit rules set by government, which Townsend called arbitrary [1]. Measure B compares income with similar households, so it counts more people but ignores what life is actually like [1]. Measure C counts lack of everyday items and activities, which picks up households the income measures miss, so it gives the highest figure (840) [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** Townsend would prefer Measure C [1], because relative deprivation means lacking the resources for the diet, activities and living conditions widely available in society [1]. Criticism: the index may confuse choice with poverty [1]; a household may lack an item, such as fresh meat, because it chooses to, not because it cannot afford it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) show both percentages before the comparison; in (b) refer to the table's measures by letter so the examiner can see you used the data.

**6. (a)** Marxist [1]. Phrase: "it keeps the factories and shops running" (also accept "Wages are spent on cars, furniture and holidays") [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Delphy and Leonard are feminists who would agree the family is a site of exploitation [1]. However, they would ask who does the feeding and caring the extract describes [1]. They argue men, rather than capitalists, are the main beneficiaries, because the family is an economic system in which men exploit women's work [1]. Even when wives have paid jobs, they still do household tasks that are not equally shared [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the extract's own words ("fed and cared for") to show what the feminist response adds; a general account of feminism scores less.

**7.** Advantage: unstructured interviews let the young women explain their lives in their own words [1], giving high validity and detail, as Carlen found in her interviews about class and gender deals [1]. Disadvantage: they take a long time, so only a small sample is usually possible [1], which means the findings may not be representative of all young people in Fendlow [1]. Mixed methods: the researcher could add a questionnaire or use official conviction statistics for the city [1], to show how widespread the patterns from the interviews are [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each point to young women and crime in Fendlow; a list of general advantages of interviews stays in the lower marks.

**8.** The everyday view assumes a meritocracy in which ability alone decides success [1]. Halsey, Heath and Ridge studied over 8,000 males and grouped them by father's occupation [1]. A boy from the service class had four times the chance of a working-class boy of being at school at 16, and about eleven times the chance of attending university [1]. Differences this large suggest class background, not just cleverness, shapes success, which challenges the everyday view [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use Halsey's actual ratios; "Halsey found working-class pupils did worse" is too thin for full marks.

**9.** In education, Ball's work on teacher expectations shows that how teachers see pupils can shape their progress [1]; pupils may live up or down to the label, a self-fulfilling prophecy [1]. In crime, Becker argues an act becomes deviant only when others label it, and the label can become a master status [1]. In both cases the reaction of people with power (teachers, police, courts) shapes the outcome, not just the person's behaviour [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The fourth mark is for the connection itself; two separate descriptions with no link between them cap the answer.

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points. Qualitative methods produce data in words and meanings, such as participant observation and unstructured interviews [1]. They aim for validity by seeing the world as participants see it [1]. Willis used observation, group discussions and informal interviews to understand school from the pupils' point of view [1], revealing a counter school culture that a questionnaire might miss [1]. Ball spent three years as a participant observer, showing how banding and mixed ability shaped pupils' experience [1]. Carlen's unstructured interviews with 39 women showed how the class deal and gender deal break down [1]. Limitation: small samples, such as one school or 39 women, may not be representative [1]. Limitation: findings are hard to repeat, so reliability is low [1]. Limitation: the researcher's presence or interpretation may bring bias [1]. Quantitative studies such as Halsey's sample of over 8,000 males show how common a pattern is, which qualitative work cannot [1]. Mixed methods can combine depth with scale [1]. A possible judgement: qualitative methods are most useful for explaining *why* a process happens, but need quantitative support to show *how widespread* it is [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs both education and crime examples; an answer that uses only Willis covers half the question.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point; these are indicative creditworthy points. Feminists argue society is patriarchal: men dominate institutions and benefit from women's subordination [1]. Families: Delphy and Leonard argue men exploit women's labour within the family [1]. Oakley examined the power of the idea of the "conventional family" [1]. Crime: Heidensohn argues women commit less crime because they are controlled more closely at home, in public and at work [1]. Carlen argues working-class women turn to crime when the class deal and gender deal fail [1]. Power: Walby identifies six patriarchal structures, including paid work and the state [1], and argues private patriarchy has given way to public patriarchy [1]. Still convincing: inequalities in paid work and household tasks remain, as Walby argued [1]. Less convincing: Heidensohn is criticised for generalising to all women [1]. Oakley's paper predates civil partnerships and same-sex marriage, and family life has become more diverse [1]. Functionalists such as Parsons see family roles as meeting society's needs rather than as exploitation [1]. A possible judgement: feminist accounts still explain gender inequality well, but work best when they recognise differences between women by class, ethnicity and family type [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* "Today" asks you to judge change since the texts were written; using their dates and context earns evaluation credit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a perspective in general instead of its key features in the topic the question names.
- Giving only differences when a question asks for a similarity and a difference.
- In data questions, not showing working or not quoting figures from the table.
- Treating the extract as background, instead of quoting its words and judging them.
- Naming a method without linking its strengths and weaknesses to the research scenario.
- Confusing triangulation with mixed methods.
- Answering "challenge the everyday view" with personal opinion instead of named evidence.
- Writing two separate topic descriptions when the question asks for a connection.
- Ending an extended answer without a judgement that follows from the evidence.
