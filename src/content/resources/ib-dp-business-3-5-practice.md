These questions cover section 3.5 (Profitability and liquidity ratio analysis) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024, for SL and HL. Learn the method from the [3.5 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-3-5/) and the [3.5 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-business-3-5-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars. Give percentages to one decimal place and ratios to two decimal places unless told otherwise.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *liquidity*. **[2]**

**2.** State the formula for the acid test (quick) ratio. **[1]**

**3.** Distinguish between gross profit margin and profit margin. **[2]**

**4.** Brayford Bakery has sales revenue of 600,000, cost of sales of 390,000 and expenses of 150,000. Non-current liabilities are 100,000 and equity is 250,000.

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit margin. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the profit margin. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the ROCE. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a business with a current ratio of 1.8 might still struggle to pay its suppliers on time. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one strategy that would improve a business's profit margin but not its gross profit margin. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

Questions 7 to 11 use the data below for Larkhill Toys, a maker of wooden toys.

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 500,000 | 560,000 |
| Cost of sales | 300,000 | 364,000 |
| Expenses | 120,000 | 126,000 |
| Capital employed | 400,000 | 400,000 |
| Current assets | 150,000 | 140,000 |
| of which stock | 60,000 | 90,000 |
| Current liabilities | 100,000 | 125,000 |

In Year 2 Larkhill switched to a more expensive sustainable timber supplier and kept its prices unchanged for most products. It also built up stock ahead of an expected contract with a national retailer, which has not yet been signed.

**7.** Calculate for both years:

**(a)** the gross profit margin. **[2]**

**(b)** the profit margin. **[2]**

**(c)** the ROCE. **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the current ratio and acid test ratio for both years. **[4]**

**9.** Analyse the changes in Larkhill's profitability between Year 1 and Year 2. **[6]**

**10.** Larkhill plans to sell 20,000 of stock at cost and use all the cash to reduce current liabilities. Calculate the new current ratio and acid test ratio, and comment on the result. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate two strategies Larkhill could use to improve its gross profit margin. **[10]**

**12.** Explain why ROCE might fall even when profit before interest and tax rises. **[2]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts when they fall due [1], using current assets such as cash, debtors and stock [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (Current assets − stock) ÷ current liabilities [1] **[1]**

**3.** Gross profit margin compares gross profit (sales revenue minus cost of sales) with sales revenue [1], whereas profit margin uses profit before interest and tax, so it also reflects expenses such as rent and salaries [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** Gross profit = 600,000 − 390,000 = 210,000 [1]; 210,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **35.0%** [1]

**(b)** Profit before interest and tax = 210,000 − 150,000 = 60,000 [1]; 60,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **10.0%** [1]

**(c)** Capital employed = 100,000 + 250,000 = 350,000 [1]; 60,000 ÷ 350,000 × 100 = **17.1%** [1] **[6]**

**5.** Much of its current assets may be stock that sells slowly [1], so it may have little cash or debtors available when supplier payments fall due, which the acid test would reveal [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Cutting expenses such as rent, energy or administration [1] raises profit before interest and tax for the same sales, but gross profit margin is unchanged because cost of sales and prices are unaffected [1]. **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** Year 1: 200,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **40.0%** [1]; Year 2: 196,000 ÷ 560,000 × 100 = **35.0%** [1]

**(b)** Year 1: 80,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **16.0%** [1]; Year 2: 70,000 ÷ 560,000 × 100 = **12.5%** [1]

**(c)** Year 1: 80,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = **20.0%** [1]; Year 2: 70,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = **17.5%** [1] **[6]**

**8.** Year 1 current ratio = 150,000 ÷ 100,000 = **1.50** [1]; acid test = 90,000 ÷ 100,000 = **0.90** [1]. Year 2 current ratio = 140,000 ÷ 125,000 = **1.12** [1]; acid test = 50,000 ÷ 125,000 = **0.40** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Gross profit margin fell by 5 percentage points, from 40.0% to 35.0% [1], because cost of sales rose from 60% to 65% of revenue after the switch to more expensive timber [1], while prices were not raised to cover it [1]. Profit margin fell by 3.5 points, less than gross margin, because expenses fell from 24.0% to 22.5% of revenue [1]. ROCE fell from 20.0% to 17.5% because profit before interest and tax fell by 10,000 on unchanged capital employed [1]. Overall, sales grew by 12% but profit fell, so Larkhill is earning less on each dollar of sales and of capital, mainly because of the timber cost [1]. **[6]**

**10.** New current assets = 140,000 − 20,000 = 120,000; new current liabilities = 125,000 − 20,000 = 105,000 [1]. Current ratio = 120,000 ÷ 105,000 = **1.14**; stock = 70,000, so acid test = 50,000 ÷ 105,000 = **0.48** [1]. Both ratios improve slightly [1], but the acid test is still well below Year 1's 0.90, so Larkhill remains dependent on selling stock, and selling stock built for the retailer contract could leave it short if the contract is signed [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Strategy 1, raise prices on products made with sustainable timber: explanation, a higher price per unit with the same cost of sales raises gross profit per sale [1]; benefit, customers who value sustainability may accept a higher price, especially if it is promoted [1]; limitation, toy buyers may switch to cheaper rivals, so volume and total profit could fall [1]; limitation, the national retailer may resist higher prices [1].
Strategy 2, reduce cost of sales: explanation, negotiate bulk discounts with the timber supplier or reduce waste in cutting [1]; benefit, protects price competitiveness while restoring margin [1]; limitation, returning to cheaper timber would undermine the sustainability decision and possibly the brand [1]; limitation, discounts may depend on buying more stock, which would worsen the already weak acid test [1].
Judgement: a modest price rise, explained by the sustainable materials, is likely to be the better first step because it keeps the timber choice and does not add stock [1]; it depends on how price-sensitive Larkhill's customers are, which market research could test [1]. **[10]**

**12.** ROCE compares profit with capital employed [1]; if capital employed rises proportionally more than profit, for example after a large new loan or share issue that has not yet earned a full return, ROCE falls [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

* Using profit after interest in ROCE or profit margin.
* Forgetting to subtract stock in the acid test.
* Describing changes as "percent" when they are percentage points.
* Recommending a strategy that worsens another weak ratio without noticing.
* Ignoring case details, such as the unsigned contract, when judging a strategy.
