## Why organisms need energy

The syllabus uses three examples. For each, be able to say what the energy is used for.

- **Active transport**: carrier proteins change shape to move ions or molecules against their concentration gradient; ATP is hydrolysed to drive the change. Examples: mineral ion uptake by root hair cells, and the proton pumps in companion cells.
- **Movement**: muscle contraction, the beating of cilia and flagella, and the movement of chromosomes by spindle fibres in cell division.
- **Anabolic reactions**: building large molecules from small ones. In **DNA replication**, energy is needed to join nucleotides into new strands. In **protein synthesis**, energy is needed to attach amino acids to tRNA and to join them by peptide bonds.

Other uses you can mention if asked to suggest include maintaining body temperature in mammals and birds, and transmitting nerve impulses.

## ATP: the universal energy currency

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide: the base **adenine**, the pentose sugar **ribose**, and **three phosphate groups**.

```
ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi    (energy released, about 30.5 kJ per mole)
ADP + Pi → ATP + H₂O    (energy needed, supplied by respiration)
```

### Why ATP rather than glucose?

Respiring one glucose molecule releases a large amount of energy through dozens of reactions; much of it would be wasted as heat if released at once, and the process is too slow for sudden demands. ATP solves both problems. Its features:

| Feature | Why it suits ATP to its role |
|---|---|
| Hydrolysis is one step, catalysed by ATPase | Energy is released **immediately** where it is needed |
| Releases a **small, manageable** quantity | Matches the needs of individual reactions; little waste |
| **Small and water-soluble** | Moves easily through the cell to where it is used |
| **Easily regenerated** from ADP and Pi | A small pool is recycled very rapidly |
| Used in **all** cells of all organisms | Hence "universal" currency |

ATP is not a long-term energy store; it is made and used continuously. Long-term stores are starch, glycogen and lipids.

## Two ways of making ATP

| | Substrate-linked phosphorylation | Chemiosmosis |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | A phosphate group is transferred directly from a substrate molecule to ADP | A proton gradient across a membrane drives ATP synthase |
| Where (respiration) | Glycolysis (cytoplasm) and Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix) | Inner mitochondrial membrane |
| Where (photosynthesis) | — | Thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts |
| Membrane needed? | No | Yes |
| Share of ATP in aerobic respiration | Small | Most |

## Energy values of respiratory substrates

Typical values per gram: carbohydrate about 16 kJ, protein about 17 kJ, lipid about 39 kJ.

The explanation is **hydrogen**:

- Most ATP is made by oxidative phosphorylation, which is driven by hydrogen atoms carried to the inner mitochondrial membrane by reduced NAD and reduced FAD.
- Fatty acids are long hydrocarbon chains with many hydrogen atoms per carbon and very few oxygen atoms. Glucose already has one oxygen per carbon (it is partly oxidised).
- So a gram of lipid supplies more hydrogen atoms, more reduced coenzymes, and drives more ATP synthesis. It also needs more oxygen and produces more water when oxidised.
- Proteins give slightly more than carbohydrates per gram. They are not a normal fuel, being used in respiration mainly during starvation or when eaten in excess; the amino groups are first removed.

## Respiratory quotient

**Definition (learn exactly)**: RQ is the ratio of the number of molecules of carbon dioxide produced to the number of molecules of oxygen taken in, as a result of respiration.

```
RQ = CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ taken in
```

Equal numbers of gas molecules occupy equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure, so volumes can be used in experiments.

### Worked example 1: RQ from equations

**Palmitic acid** (a fatty acid):

```
C₁₆H₃₂O₂ + 23O₂ → 16CO₂ + 16H₂O
check O: 2 + 46 = 48 left; 32 + 16 = 48 right
RQ = 16 ÷ 23 = 0.70
```

**Tripalmitin** (a triglyceride):

```
C₅₁H₉₈O₆ + 72.5O₂ → 51CO₂ + 49H₂O
check O: 6 + 145 = 151 left; 102 + 49 = 151 right
RQ = 51 ÷ 72.5 = 0.70
```

**Glucose**: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O, RQ = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0.

Lipids need proportionally more oxygen because of their extra hydrogen, so their RQ is lower.

### Interpreting RQ values

| RQ | Likely meaning |
|---|---|
| About 0.7 | Lipid being respired |
| About 0.9 | Protein being respired |
| 1.0 | Carbohydrate being respired |
| Between 0.7 and 1.0 | A mixture of substrates |
| Greater than 1.0 | Some anaerobic respiration (CO₂ made without O₂ use), or carbohydrate being converted to lipid |

Germinating seeds that store lipid (such as sunflower) give RQs near 0.7; seeds storing starch (such as wheat) give values near 1.0.

## Measuring RQ with a respirometer

### The method

1. Place the organisms (e.g. blowfly larvae on gauze) in a tube with a **CO₂ absorbent** such as potassium hydroxide solution below them. Set up an identical **control** tube with glass beads of the same volume instead of organisms.
2. Connect a capillary tube containing coloured liquid; leave the apparatus in a **water bath** to reach a constant temperature, with the screw clip or syringe open.
3. Close the system and record the distance the liquid moves in a set time. With CO₂ absorbed, the liquid moves towards the organisms because **oxygen is used**.
4. Reset the liquid with the syringe, replace the absorbent with the **same volume of water**, and repeat. Now the movement shows **oxygen used minus carbon dioxide produced**.
5. Calculate volumes using πr² × distance, where r is the radius of the capillary bore.

### Worked example 2: blowfly larvae

Capillary bore radius 0.40 mm; 15 minutes each run.

- With potassium hydroxide: liquid moves 42 mm towards the larvae.
- With water: liquid moves 6 mm towards the larvae.

```
O₂ taken in = π × 0.40² × 42 = 21.1 mm³
net decrease (water) = π × 0.40² × 6 = 3.0 mm³ = O₂ in − CO₂ out
CO₂ given out = 21.1 − 3.0 = 18.1 mm³
RQ = 18.1 ÷ 21.1 = 0.86
rate of O₂ uptake = 21.1 ÷ 15 = 1.41 mm³ min⁻¹
```

Since πr² cancels, (42 − 6) ÷ 42 = 0.86 gives the RQ directly. A value of 0.86 suggests a mixture of substrates, possibly with protein, which is plausible for larvae feeding on meat. If in the water run the liquid had moved **away** from the larvae, CO₂ output would exceed O₂ uptake and RQ would be greater than 1.

### Evaluating the method

- **Control tube** compensates for changes in temperature and atmospheric pressure that move the liquid without respiration.
- **Equilibration** before readings, with the system open, prevents expansion or contraction of air being mistaken for gas exchange.
- **Same organisms, same mass, same temperature** in both runs; otherwise the two readings cannot be combined.
- **Repeats** and a mean improve reliability. Handle potassium hydroxide with care: it is corrosive and must not touch the organisms.

## Common mistakes

- Defining RQ as O₂ ÷ CO₂, or leaving out "as a result of respiration".
- Using the water-run reading as the oxygen uptake.
- Saying lipids release more energy "because they are bigger molecules"; the reason is more hydrogen per gram.
- Saying ATP "stores" large amounts of energy.
- Confusing chemiosmosis with substrate-linked phosphorylation or placing chemiosmosis in the cytoplasm.
- Forgetting the control tube or equilibration in a plan.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Outline** (need for energy): name the process and what the energy does.
- **Describe** (ATP features): feature plus why it helps.
- **Explain** (energy values): link hydrogen content to reduced NAD and ATP yield.
- **Calculate** (RQ): show balanced equation or both volumes; give 2 significant figures.
- **Describe** (investigation): apparatus, variables, control, readings, calculation.
