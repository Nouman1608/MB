## Questions

**1.** Hollin Ridge Growers Cooperative grows apples, presses them into juice, sells the juice in its farm shop, and funds a small team that researches new apple varieties using genetic data. Identify the sector of each of the four activities. **[4]**

**2.** (a) Define the term entrepreneur. **[2]**

**(b)** Outline two challenges of starting up a business. **[4]**

**3.** Eskarra is an invented country with 4,000,000 workers. Employment by sector is: primary 480,000, secondary 1,120,000, tertiary 2,240,000 and quaternary 160,000.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of workers employed in the tertiary sector. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of workers employed in the primary and secondary sectors combined. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one way a new business in Eskarra might be affected by the size of the tertiary sector. **[3]**

**4.** Distinguish between the private sector and the public sector. **[3]**

**5.** Ines Valdry and Tomas Mistlethwaite run a partnership, a bakehouse. Ines invested £90,000 and Tomas £60,000. Their agreement shares profit in proportion to the capital each invested. This year's profit is £45,000.

**(a)** Calculate the profit each partner receives. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one disadvantage of operating as a partnership rather than as a privately held company. **[3]**
**(c)** State two features of a partnership. **[2]**

**6.** Outline two features of a sole trader. **[4]**

**7.** Garnwick Foods is a privately held company with 2,000,000 shares. The founding family holds 1,500,000 of them. The directors propose to become publicly held by selling 1,000,000 new shares at £4 each.

**(a)** Calculate the money raised, and the percentage of the company the family would hold afterwards. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Garnwick Foods should become a publicly held company. **[7]**

**8.** Hollin Ridge Growers Cooperative made a surplus of £36,000. Its rules say 25% is reinvested in the business and the rest is shared equally among its 240 members.

**(a)** Calculate the payment to each member. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one way a cooperative differs from a privately held company. **[3]**

**9.** Quorvale Reading Trust is an NGO that supplies books to village schools, funded by donations and grants.

**(a)** Distinguish between an NGO and a for-profit social enterprise. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one challenge the trust may face because of the way it is funded. **[3]**

**10.** Dovrenne Metro Link is owned by a regional government. It sells tickets and aims to cover its costs while keeping fares affordable. Explain one way a public sector company differs from a private sector company. **[3]**

**11.** Noor plans to open Cavendale Kite Studio, which would sell and repair kites. Start-up costs are £24,000. She has £9,000 of savings. A friend, Elias, offers to join her and invest £8,000.

**(a)** Calculate the extra finance Noor needs if she starts alone, and if she starts with Elias. **[2]**
**(b)** Recommend whether Noor should open as a sole trader or in a partnership with Elias. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Growing apples is primary [1]. Pressing them into juice is secondary [1]. The farm shop is tertiary [1]. The genetic-data research team is quaternary [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Classify each activity by what it does, one answer per activity; naming only the firm's overall sector does not show you can separate the four.

**2. (a)** An entrepreneur is a person who identifies a business opportunity [1] and organizes resources and bears the risk of loss to exploit it [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** First challenge: raising start-up finance [1], because a new business has no trading record, so lenders may see it as risky [1]. Second challenge: early cash flow [1], since the business pays for premises and stock before customers pay it [1]. Competition, regulation and long hours would also fit. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" asks for a brief account: a named challenge plus one line of detail.

**3. (a)** 2,240,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 [1] = **56%** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** (480,000 + 1,120,000) = 1,600,000 [1]; 1,600,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 = **40%** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** A large tertiary sector, 56% of workers, means many people work in services [1], so a new service firm may find skilled staff easily but face many competitors [1]. A manufacturer might find fewer available workers with factory skills, because only 28% work in the secondary sector [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division by the total; in part (c), tie the effect to a figure in the stem.

**4.** The private sector is made up of businesses owned by private individuals or groups [1]. The public sector is made up of organizations owned and controlled by the government [1]. A distinction needs both sides: private firms are usually run for profit and answer to owners, while public organizations usually aim to provide a service and answer to government [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" asks you to make the difference clear; describing only one side, or using a sentence that fits both sectors, leaves the difference unstated.

**5. (a)** Total capital = £90,000 + £60,000 = £150,000 [1]. Ines: 90,000 ÷ 150,000 = 60%, so £27,000 [1]. Tomas: 40%, so **£18,000** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** In general terms, partners have unlimited liability, so each could be held responsible for business debts beyond what they invested [1], whereas a privately held company's owners are in general limited to their investment [1]. If the bakehouse owed £60,000 against assets of £42,000, the £18,000 shortfall could fall on the partners' personal wealth [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Two features, for example: owned by two or more people [1]; decisions and risk are shared, often under an agreement [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A disadvantage is stronger when set against the alternative the question names, with a number from the stem.

**6.** Feature one: one owner, who keeps all the profit [1] and makes decisions alone [1]. Feature two: unlimited liability in general terms, so the owner's own possessions could be used to pay debts if it fails [1], and finance is limited to what one person can raise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give each feature a short reason or consequence; restating "one owner" in new words adds nothing.

**7. (a)** Money raised = 1,000,000 × £4 = **£4,000,000** [1]. New total = 3,000,000 shares [1]. Family share = 1,500,000 ÷ 3,000,000 = **50%**, down from 75% [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** In favour: £4,000,000 of new finance, a large sum compared with a private company's usual sources [1], could fund growth without the interest cost of a loan [1]. Against: the family's stake falls from 75% to 50%, so it has less control, and any further share issue could remove majority control [1]. A publicly held company must also disclose more information, which is costly and shows competitors its position [1]. Managers may face pressure for short-term returns [1]. Judgement: if the money funds a large, profitable project, going public is justified because the family still keeps 50% [1]; if loans or retained profit can fund growth, staying private protects control, so the decision turns on the funding need [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" asks you to weigh strengths and limitations, so a final judgement that depends on a stated condition from the case usually reads as more convincing than a list of pros and cons.

**8. (a)** Reinvested = 25% × £36,000 = £9,000 [1]. Shared = £36,000 − £9,000 = £27,000 [1]. Per member = £27,000 ÷ 240 = **£112.50** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** A cooperative is owned and run by its members [1], who typically have one vote each regardless of the amount they contribute [1]. A privately held company is owned by shareholders whose voting power usually follows the number of shares held, so control can sit with the largest holder [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A difference is clearest when it contrasts the same feature in both organizations, here how votes are allocated, rather than describing each separately.

**9. (a)** An NGO is independent of government and does not aim to make profit for owners, so it is non-profit [1]. A for-profit social enterprise, such as a cooperative or a private sector company with a social aim, trades to make a surplus or profit as well as pursuing its purpose [1]. In an NGO any surplus is kept for its mission, while a for-profit form may share profit with owners or members [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** The trust depends on donations and grants [1], which may not be renewed, so income is uncertain [1]. This makes it hard to plan long-term, for example committing to supply books to schools for several years [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the cause (funding source) to a consequence in the trust's actual activity.

**10.** A public sector company is owned by the government [1], so its main aim is to serve the public interest, such as affordable transport, even when this limits profit [1]. A private sector company is owned by private shareholders and usually puts the return to owners first, so it could raise fares or drop unprofitable routes [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the stem: referring to affordable fares shows you applied the idea.

**11. (a)** Alone: £24,000 − £9,000 = **£15,000** [1]. With Elias: £15,000 − £8,000 = **£7,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Partnership, advantages: the extra finance cuts the amount to find from £15,000 to £7,000 [1], which makes a loan more likely to be approved [1], and Noor gains a second person to share the work and skills [1]. Sole trader, advantages: Noor keeps all profit and all decisions [1], and avoids disagreement with a partner [1]. Partnership, risks: in general terms both would have unlimited liability, so Noor could be answerable for Elias's decisions [1]. Judgement: a partnership seems better, because over half the shortfall is covered and the finance risk is the key start-up challenge [1], provided they agree profit shares and duties in writing and Noor trusts Elias; if she doubts that, she should open alone and seek a loan or grant for the £15,000 [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A recommendation gains from a stated reason tied to the numbers and a condition that would change it; a choice with no weighing of the alternative reads as description.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a sector label but not tying it to a named activity from the stem.
- Mixing up the private/public sectors with the four economic sectors.
- Calculating a percentage without showing the division by the total.
- Listing entity features with no consequence for the owner.
- Describing only one side in a "distinguish" question.
- Treating limited liability as freedom from all loss.
- Calling every social enterprise non-profit, when the guide lists for-profit forms too.
- Writing "evaluate" or "recommend" answers with no judgement.
