## Section A

**1.** Name the **four** stages of the project life cycle in order. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term stakeholder and give **one** example. **[2]**

**3.** State what is meant by project scope. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by scope creep and why it is a problem. **[2]**

**5.** Give **two** reasons for holding a project review. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **two** things that are monitored during a project. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Hartwell Council plans an online booking system for its leisure centres. The phases and durations are: requirements 4 weeks, build 6 weeks, testing 5 weeks, launch and training 3 weeks. The phases run one after another.

**(a)** Calculate the total duration of the project in weeks. **[1]**

**(b)** The council wants the system live in 15 weeks. Suggest **two** ways in which this could be achieved. **[4]**

**8.** The testing phase needs 4 testers working for 12 days each. A tester costs £200 per day.

**(a)** Calculate the effort in person-days and the cost. **[2]**

**(b)** Only 3 testers are available. Calculate how many days the testing will take, and state the effect on the cost. **[3]**

**9.** The online booking system has a budget of £90,000. At month 4, the plan said that 40% of the work would be complete. In fact, 30% is complete and £31,500 has been spent.

**(a)** Calculate the planned value of the work and the earned value of the work done. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the schedule variance and the cost variance, and state what each shows. **[4]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** actions the project manager could take. **[2]**

**10.** Write **two** SMART success criteria for the booking system. **[4]**

**11.** Describe the activities carried out in the project initiation stage. **[6]**

**12.** Explain why a project manager must report regularly to stakeholders, and describe what a good report contains. **[5]**

**13.** Evaluate the importance of the project close stage for the council. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Initiation [1], planning, execution and monitoring, close [1 for all three in the correct order]. **[2]**

**2.** Anyone affected by or interested in the project [1]. Example: the sponsor, the end users, the project team, or a supplier [1]. **[2]**

**3.** What is included in the project [1] and what is not, so the boundaries of the work are clear [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Extra features or tasks are added without agreeing changes to time and budget [1]. The project becomes late and over budget, and quality may suffer [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two from: to check whether the objectives were met [1]; to learn lessons for future projects [1]; to identify good practice [1]; to check that stakeholders are satisfied [1]; to provide a record of what happened [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two from: progress against time [1]; cost [1]; quality [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 4 + 6 + 5 + 3 = 18 weeks [1].

**(b)** Any two, each with a reason: overlap tasks, such as starting training during the last week of testing, so that the total is shorter [1][1]; add more staff to the build or test phases, to finish them sooner (though this raises the cost) [1][1]; reduce the scope, such as dropping some features, so there is less to build and test [1][1]; simplify the requirements phase [1][1].

**8. (a)** 4 × 12 = 48 person-days [1]; 48 × 200 = £9,600 [1].

**(b)** 48 ÷ 3 = 16 days [1]; the total person-days are the same so the cost stays at £9,600 [1], but the phase takes 4 days longer [1].

**9. (a)** Planned value = 0.40 × 90,000 = £36,000 [1]; earned value = 0.30 × 90,000 = £27,000 [1].

**(b)** Schedule variance = 27,000 − 36,000 = −£9,000 [1], so the project is behind schedule [1]. Cost variance = 27,000 − 31,500 = −£4,500 [1], so it is over budget [1].

**(c)** Any two from: add staff to the late tasks [1]; reduce the scope [1]; reorder or overlap tasks [1]; renegotiate the deadline or budget with the sponsor [1]; find and fix the cause of delays [1].

**10.** Any two criteria, each [1] for a measurable target and [1] for a time or a number: for example, residents can complete a booking in under 3 minutes [2]; the system is live in all five leisure centres by the end of week 18 [2]; at least 90% of bookings are made online within three months of launch [2]; the project is completed within the £90,000 budget [2]. **[4]**

**11.** Any six from: identifying the objectives [1]; deciding the resources needed [1]; setting the success criteria [1]; identifying the stakeholders and their needs [1]; defining the scope [1]; developing a high-level schedule [1]; deciding whether the project is feasible and affordable [1]; producing a project brief for approval [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Stakeholders need to know progress, costs, risks and problems [1], so that they can make decisions and give approval [1]. Early reports of problems allow action while it is still possible [1]. A good report is short and clear [1], and gives progress against time, cost and quality, and risks and decisions needed, in a form suited to the reader [1]. **[5]**

**13.** The council confirms that the deliverables meet the success criteria and signs them off [1]; the system is handed over with documentation and training, so staff can use it [1]; support arrangements are agreed [1]; suppliers are paid and resources released [1]. The review compares results with the plan [1], and the lessons learned help future projects so mistakes are not repeated [1]. Without close, faults and unmet needs may be left, and the lessons lost [1]. Judgement: it is important because it ensures benefits and learning, though it must be carried out properly and not skipped when the pressure is over [1]. Maximum 6 marks. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Listing stage names with no activities.
- Success criteria that cannot be measured.
- Giving the cost of a phase without the effort calculation in person-days.
- Saying variances are "bad" without stating whether the project is late or over budget.
- Ignoring the project review in the close stage.
