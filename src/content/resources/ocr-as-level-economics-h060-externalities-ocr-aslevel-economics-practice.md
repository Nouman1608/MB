The mark totals are our own and every market and figure is invented. Explanations are in the [externalities study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-externalities-ocr-aslevel-economics/) and the [externalities revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-externalities-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define a negative externality. **[2]**

**2.** Smoke from a steelworks damages the health of nearby residents. This is an example of:

A: a positive consumption externality

B: a negative consumption externality

C: a positive production externality

D: a negative production externality **[1]**

**3.** The marginal social benefit of a unit is £45 and the marginal private benefit is £38. Calculate the marginal external benefit. **[1]**

**4.** A homeowner keeps a colourful front garden that passers-by enjoy. Classify this externality and explain your answer. **[2]**

**5.** State which curve differs from its private equivalent when there is a negative consumption externality, and whether it lies above or below. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why the price at the social optimum is higher than the market price when there is a negative production externality. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** In an invented market for an industrial chemical, MPB = 150 − 5Q and MPC = 30 + 3Q, with Q in thousand tonnes and prices in pounds. Each tonne imposes an external cost of £16 on people living downstream.

**(a)** Calculate the market output and price. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the socially optimal output and the price buyers would pay at that output. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the welfare loss. **[2]**

**8.** Home insulation reduces energy use and so lowers emissions that harm others. In an invented market, MPB = 80 − 4Q, MPC = MSC = 20 + 2Q, and the marginal external benefit is £12 per unit (Q in thousand homes, prices in £ hundreds).

**(a)** Calculate the market output and the socially optimal output. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the welfare loss. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the market underprovides home insulation. **[2]**

**9.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, why the free market leads to overconsumption of car journeys in a congested city centre. **[6]**

**10.** Explain why a firm that trains its workers in general skills may provide less training than is socially optimal. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the extent to which external costs from road transport cause significant market failure. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A negative externality is a cost imposed on a third party [1] that is not reflected in the market price, so the decision maker ignores it [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **D** [1]. The cost arises from producing steel and falls on residents. **[1]**

**3.** MEB = 45 − 38 = **£7** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** A positive consumption externality [1]: the homeowner's use of the garden gives a benefit to passers-by, who are third parties and pay nothing for it [1]. **[2]**

**5.** MSB lies **below** MPB [1]. **[1]**

**6.** At the optimum, output is lower because the external cost is counted [1]. With a downward-sloping demand curve, buyers are willing to pay a higher price for the smaller quantity, which now reflects the full social cost [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 150 − 5Q = 30 + 3Q, so 8Q = 120 and Q = **15** thousand tonnes [1]; P = 150 − 75 = **£75** [1].

**(b)** MSC = 46 + 3Q; 150 − 5Q = 46 + 3Q gives Q = **13** thousand tonnes [1] and price = 150 − 65 = **£85** [1].

**(c)** At Q = 15, MSC = 91 and MSB = 75, a gap of £16 [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × 2 × 16 = **£16 thousand** [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Market: 80 − 4Q = 20 + 2Q, so Q = **10** thousand homes [1]. Optimum: MSB = 92 − 4Q, so 92 − 4Q = 20 + 2Q and Q = **12** thousand [1].

**(b)** ½ × 2 × 12 = **12**: half of 2 thousand homes × £1,200, which is **£1.2 million** [1].

**(c)** Households weigh only their own benefit, such as lower energy bills, against the cost [1]. They ignore the benefit to others from lower emissions, so MSB is above MPB and the market stops at 10 thousand homes, below the optimum of 12 thousand [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* State the units of the welfare loss carefully when values are given in hundreds of pounds.

**9.** Diagram with MPB above MSB, both sloping down, and one cost curve MPC = MSC sloping up [1]; Q market where MPB = MPC is greater than Q optimum where MSB = MSC [1]; welfare loss triangle shaded between the two outputs [1]. Each driver weighs their private benefit against private costs such as fuel and parking [1]. They ignore the cost they impose on other road users and residents through congestion, delay and fumes, so MSB is below MPB [1]. The market therefore has more journeys than the social optimum, and the extra journeys cost society more than they are worth [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Congestion from car use is a consumption externality; moving the cost curve instead of the benefit curve loses the diagram marks.

**10.** Workers with general skills can move to other employers [1], so other firms gain the benefit of the training without paying for it, which is an external benefit of production [1]. The training firm counts only its own benefit [1], so it provides training up to where its private benefit equals its private cost, below the level where social benefit equals social cost [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Road transport creates external costs of congestion, air pollution, noise and accidents [1], borne by third parties such as residents and other road users [1].
- Drivers and hauliers ignore these costs, so MSC exceeds MPC or MSB falls below MPB, and the market produces too many journeys [1], causing a welfare loss [1].
- The significance depends on place and time: external costs are high in crowded cities at peak times [1] but low on empty rural roads [1].
- Some costs are already reflected in prices through fuel taxes, which narrows the gap between private and social cost [1].
- External costs are hard to value, so the size of the welfare loss is uncertain [1].
- Cleaner vehicles may reduce pollution costs over time, though congestion remains [1].
- Judgement: external costs cause significant market failure in congested urban areas, but much less elsewhere, so the extent depends on location and timing [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Good evaluation explains where and when the externality is large, rather than asserting that it is always large.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Moving the wrong curve for a consumption externality.
- Forgetting to name the third party.
- Using the market price instead of the gap between MSC and MSB as the welfare-loss height.
- Missing units on calculated answers.
- Writing a one-sided answer to an evaluation question.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I tell a production externality from a consumption externality?**
Ask what causes the effect. If the act of making the good causes it, as with factory emissions, it is a production externality. If using the good causes it, as with noise from a party, it is a consumption externality.

**Is a positive externality a market failure?**
Yes. The market ignores the external benefit, so it produces or consumes too little, and the units not produced represent a welfare loss.
