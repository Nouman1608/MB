## Syllabus coverage

The syllabus lists thirteen statements for 3.4. In summary, you need to:

- describe how vibrating sources produce sound, and the longitudinal nature
  of sound with compressions and rarefactions
- state the human audible range as about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz
- explain why sound cannot travel in a vacuum and describe an experiment to
  show it
- link amplitude to loudness and frequency to pitch, and describe how
  different sources give different qualities (timbres) on an oscilloscope
- describe an echo as reflected sound, and experiments to show reflection
- describe a distance-and-time method for the speed of sound in air, and
  know its value is about 330–350 m/s
- know that sound generally travels faster in solids than liquids, and
  faster in liquids than gases
- define ultrasound as sound above 20 kHz and describe its uses in
  cleaning, prenatal and other medical scanning, and sonar, including depth
  and distance calculations

## Production and the longitudinal wave

A loudspeaker cone, a tuning-fork prong or a guitar string vibrates. As it
moves forward it pushes the neighbouring air particles closer together,
making a **compression** (higher pressure). As it moves back it leaves a
region where the particles are spread out, a **rarefaction** (lower
pressure). The particles themselves only vibrate backwards and forwards
about fixed positions, **parallel** to the direction the wave travels, so
sound is a **longitudinal** wave.

The wavelength of a sound wave is the distance from the centre of one
compression to the centre of the next. The wave equation from 3.1 still
applies.

**Worked example 1.** Taking the speed of sound in air as 340 m/s, find
the wavelengths at the limits of human hearing.

```
20 Hz:      λ = v / f = 340 / 20     = 17 m
20 000 Hz:  λ = v / f = 340 / 20 000 = 0.017 m  (1.7 cm)
```

Audible sound spans wavelengths from centimetres to many metres, which is
why sound diffracts round doorways much more than light does.

## Why sound needs a medium

Sound is passed on by particles colliding with their neighbours, so where
there are no particles, as in a vacuum, there is nothing to carry the
vibration.

**The bell-jar experiment, in describe-level detail.** Hang an electric
bell inside a glass bell jar, supported on soft foam or elastic so that
vibrations cannot travel out through the stand. Connect the jar to a vacuum
pump and switch the bell on. As the pump removes the air, the sound heard
becomes steadily fainter until it is almost silent, while the hammer can
still be seen striking the gong. Let the air back in and the sound
returns. The bell can be seen because light travels through a vacuum;
it cannot be heard because sound does not.

## Loudness, pitch and timbre on an oscilloscope

A microphone turns the sound into an electrical signal that an
oscilloscope displays as a wave trace. With the oscilloscope settings
unchanged:

| Change to the sound | Change to the trace |
|---|---|
| Louder | Taller trace (larger amplitude), same spacing |
| Higher pitch | More waves across the screen (higher frequency), same height |
| Different instrument, same note and loudness | Same height and same number of waves, but a different shape |

The last row is **timbre** (quality). A tuning fork gives a smooth, regular
wave; a violin or a voice playing the same note gives a more complex
repeating shape, because each source vibrates in its own way. Pitch and
loudness can match while the shape still differs.

## Echoes and the reflection of sound

An **echo** is sound reflected from a hard surface. Hard, smooth surfaces
reflect sound well; soft furnishings absorb it.

**Experiment to show reflection.** Place two cardboard tubes pointing at
a hard board, with a screen between them so that sound cannot pass
directly. A ticking clock or a small speaker sits at the end of one tube;
the observer listens at the other. Move the second tube until the sound is
loudest. The angle between each tube and the normal to the board is then
the same, showing that sound obeys the law of reflection.

## Measuring the speed of sound in air

All the methods measure a **distance** and a **time**, then use
speed = distance ÷ time.

**Method 1: two observers.** Two people stand a measured distance apart,
for example 500 m across an open field. One makes a sound that can also be
seen, such as banging two blocks together above the head or firing a
starting pistol. The other starts a stopwatch on seeing the action and
stops it on hearing the sound. Light arrives almost instantly, so the time
measured is the time for the sound to travel.

**Worked example 2.** The time recorded over 500 m is 1.5 s.

```
v = d / t = 500 / 1.5 = 333 m/s
```

Repeat several times and average, and swap the observers' positions so that
any effect of wind is reduced. A long distance makes reaction time a
smaller fraction of the measured time.

**Method 2: echo, clapping in rhythm.** Stand a measured distance from a
large wall, say 80 m. Clap, then keep clapping so that each clap coincides
with the echo of the previous one. A second person times a large number of
intervals, for example 20, with a stopwatch.

**Worked example 3.** Twenty intervals take 9.6 s.

```
time for one interval = 9.6 / 20 = 0.48 s
in that time the sound goes to the wall and back = 2 × 80 = 160 m
v = 160 / 0.48 = 333 m/s
```

Timing many intervals divides the reaction-time error by twenty. The most
common mistake is to forget the factor of 2 for the return journey.

## Speed in different media

In general, sound travels **faster in solids than in liquids**, and
**faster in liquids than in gases**, because the particles are closer
together and pass on the vibration more quickly. Roughly: air about
330–350 m/s, water about 1500 m/s, and many metals several thousand metres
per second. A person with an ear to a rail hears an approaching train
through the rail before hearing it through the air.

## Ultrasound

**Ultrasound** is sound with a frequency **higher than 20 kHz**, above the
range humans can hear. It is still a longitudinal mechanical wave.

- **Cleaning.** Delicate objects such as jewellery or small machine parts
  are placed in a liquid bath through which ultrasound passes. The
  vibrations shake dirt loose without dismantling or scratching the object.
- **Prenatal and other medical scanning.** A probe sends pulses into the
  body. At each boundary between tissues of different kinds, part of the
  pulse reflects. The time each echo takes to return gives the depth of
  that boundary, and a computer builds an image. It is used for scanning
  an unborn baby because it is not ionising radiation.
- **Sonar.** A ship sends ultrasound pulses down through the water and
  times the echoes from the sea bed, a shoal of fish or a wreck.

For every echo calculation:

```
distance to reflector = (speed × time for echo to return) / 2
```

**Worked example 4.** A ship's sonar receives an echo from a shoal of fish
after 0.040 s and from the sea bed after 0.30 s. The speed of sound in sea
water is 1500 m/s.

```
fish:     d = 1500 × 0.040 / 2 = 30 m
sea bed:  d = 1500 × 0.30 / 2  = 225 m
```

**Worked example 5.** In a scan, an echo from a boundary in the body
returns 8.0 × 10⁻⁵ s after the pulse is sent. Take the speed of sound in
soft tissue as 1500 m/s.

```
d = 1500 × 8.0 × 10⁻⁵ / 2 = 0.060 m  (6.0 cm below the skin)
```

## Common mistakes

- Saying sound particles travel from the source to the ear. They vibrate
  about fixed positions; the energy travels.
- Linking loudness to frequency or pitch to amplitude.
- Leaving out the factor of 2 in echo, rhythm-clapping and sonar
  calculations.
- Writing the audible range as 20–20 000 kHz. It is hertz; 20 kHz is the
  upper limit.
- Describing timbre as "a different frequency". Two instruments on the same
  note have the same frequency but a different waveform shape.
- In the bell-jar experiment, failing to say the bell is still seen
  working, which is the evidence that it is the sound, not the bell, that
  has stopped.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe an experiment**: give the measured distance, how the time is
  taken, the calculation, and at least one way of improving reliability.
- **Explain why sound cannot travel in a vacuum**: refer to the need for
  particles to pass on the vibrations.
- **Describe the differences in traces**: compare height, spacing and shape
  separately, using the words amplitude, frequency and waveform.
- **Calculate**: show the halving step explicitly so a slip still earns
  method marks.
