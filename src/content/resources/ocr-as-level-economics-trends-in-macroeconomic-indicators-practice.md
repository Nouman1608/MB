## Questions

**1.** Real GDP in Country K is indexed at 100 in year 1, 104.0 in year 2 and 100.9 in year 3.

**(a)** Calculate the growth rate in year 2 and in year 3. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how far output in year 3 is above or below the year 1 level. **[1]**

**2.** Unemployment in Country N is 4.0% in year 1 and 6.5% in year 2. Calculate the change in percentage points and the percentage change in the unemployment rate. **[3]**

**3.** A news report says: "Unemployment has fallen for three years, so the labour market is back to normal." Using the ideas of level and rate, explain why this conclusion may be wrong. **[4]**

**4.** Real GDP in Country S rises from an index of 100 to 128.0 over ten years, with the growth rates varying from year to year.

**(a)** Calculate the average annual growth rate using the compound method. **[2]**
**(b)** The sum of the ten growth rates divided by 10 is 2.8%. Explain why this is higher than your answer to (a). **[2]**

**5.** Two economies are described below, with invented data.

| | Country L | Country M |
|---|---|---|
| Real GDP, year 1 (bn cu) | 900 | 400 |
| Real GDP, year 2 (bn cu) | 927 | 436 |
| Population, year 1 (millions) | 18.0 | 40.0 |
| Population, year 2 (millions) | 18.18 | 40.8 |

**(a)** Calculate the growth in real GDP per head for Country L and for Country M. **[4]**
**(b)** Country M's real GDP per head was 10,000 cu in year 1 and about 10,686 cu in year 2. Country L's was 50,000 cu in year 1 and about 50,990 cu in year 2. Assess whether Country M is catching up with Country L. **[2]**

**6.** Explain two reasons why unemployment may rise by less than output falls in a recession. **[6]**

**7.** Explain two key trends in UK macroeconomic performance over the last 20 years. **[6]**

**8.** The table gives average figures for three economies over a ten-year period (invented data).

| | Country U (developed) | Country V (emerging) | Country W (developing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average annual real GDP growth (%) | 1.6 | 7.2 | 3.4 |
| Average inflation (%) | 2.4 | 3.8 | 9.5 |
| Unemployment rate (%) | 5.2 | 4.1 | 11.5 |
| GDP per head (cu) | 41,000 | 7,800 | 1,900 |
| Current account balance (% of GDP) | -2.8 | +1.2 | -5.5 |

**(a)** Calculate Country V's GDP per head as a percentage of Country U's. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the economy with the highest inflation and explain one possible reason for it. **[3]**
**(c)** Country V's unemployment rate is lower than Country U's. Explain one reason why this may not show that V's labour market is stronger. **[2]**

**9.** Using the data in question 8, evaluate the performance of Country U compared with Countries V and W. **[8]**

**10.** Inflation in Country X is 3.0% in year 1, 1.5% in year 2, 0.5% in year 3 and -0.4% in year 4. The price level at the start of year 1 is 100.

**(a)** Calculate the price level at the end of year 3. **[1]**
**(b)** State the year in which the general price level fell. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why years 2 and 3 are examples of disinflation but not deflation. **[2]**

**11.** Nominal wages in Country Y rise by 3.5% in a year when inflation is 4.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the change in real wages. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one consequence for the economy of falling real wages. **[2]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that growth in real GDP is the best single indicator of how well an economy is performing. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Year 2: (104.0 - 100) / 100 = 4.0% [1]. Year 3: (100.9 - 104.0) / 104.0 = -2.98%, about **-3.0%** [1].
**(b)** (100.9 - 100) / 100 = **0.9% above** the year 1 level [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A fall in the second year can be missed if you only compare with year 1; calculate each year's growth from the year before it.

**2.** Change in percentage points = 6.5 - 4.0 = **2.5 percentage points** [1]. Percentage change = (6.5 - 4.0) / 4.0 [1] = **62.5%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving "2.5%" for the first part treats a rate change as a level change; use the correct unit.

**3.** The rate of unemployment may have fallen, but the level may still be above its previous low point [1]. A fall from 8% to 6% is still above a pre-recession 4% [1]. "Back to normal" needs other evidence, such as participation, under-employment, the quality of jobs or wage growth [1]. The fall may reflect people leaving the labour force [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Contrasting a falling rate with an unrecovered level, with a figure, gains more than a general statement.

**4. (a)** (128.0 / 100) to the power of 1/10 = 1.0250 [1], so average growth is about **2.5%** a year [1].
**(b)** Simple averaging adds the rates and ignores that each year's growth builds on a larger base [1]. Because of compounding, a smaller average rate each year gives the same total rise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the power calculation. A bare answer is harder to credit if it is slightly out.

**5. (a)** Country L: real GDP per head year 1 = 900 / 18.0 = 50 (thousand) and year 2 = 927 / 18.18 = about 50.99 [1]; growth = **1.98%** [1]. Country M: 400 / 40.0 = 10 and 436 / 40.8 = about 10.69 [1]; growth = **6.86%** [1].
**(b)** Yes in relative terms: M's real GDP per head rises from 20.0% of L's to about 21.0% [1]. But the absolute gap widens, from 40,000 cu to about 40,304 cu, so M has not yet caught up in cash terms [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate a relative gain from a closing absolute gap; the two can differ.

**6.** Reason 1: firms often keep skilled workers (labour hoarding) because rehiring is costly and they expect recovery [1]. This holds down job losses while output falls [1], so unemployment rises less than output has fallen [1]. Reason 2: firms may cut hours or pay instead of jobs, or government support schemes may keep workers on payrolls [1]. This spreads the fall in output across hours rather than headcount [1], so measured unemployment rises by less [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Develop each reason as far as the gap between the output fall and the rise in unemployment.

**7.** Two trends, each scored for the trend, the evidence or timing, and the cause.
- Persistent current account deficit [1]: the UK has run a deficit for most of the period [1], reflecting imports plus outgoing income exceeding exports plus incoming income [1].
- Weaker productivity growth after 2008 to 2009 [1]: output per worker rose more slowly than before the financial crisis [1], limiting the growth of potential output, with low investment a likely cause [1].
- Other acceptable trends include the inflation surge from 2021 and large budget deficits after 2008 to 2009 and 2020. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the trend, anchor it in the period and give the cause; remembered figures should be broadly right, dated and few.

**8. (a)** 7,800 / 41,000 x 100 = **19.0%** [1].
**(b)** Country W has the highest inflation (9.5%) [1]. A possible reason: heavy dependence on imported goods and a weak currency, which raise import prices (cost-push) [1], or rapid growth in the money supply or demand without matching supply [1].
**(c)** Unemployment data may not capture informal or part-time work [1], so a low rate may hide under-employment or low participation; definitions also differ across countries [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use figures from the table; naming W and quoting 9.5% avoids a general answer.

**9.** Award up to 8 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Growth: U's 1.6% is the lowest of the three, worse than V (7.2%) and W (3.4%) [1]. But U is far richer per head (41,000 cu against 7,800 and 1,900), so slower growth from a high base is expected [1].
- Inflation: U's 2.4% is the lowest, better than V (3.8%) and W (9.5%), suggesting price stability [1].
- Unemployment: U's 5.2% is higher than V's 4.1% but far lower than W's 11.5% [1].
- Current account: U runs a deficit (-2.8% of GDP) while V has a surplus (+1.2%) [1]; a deficit is not necessarily a problem if financed by investment [1].
- Judgement: U is strong on stability and income per head but weak on growth and the external balance, and ten-year averages hide year-to-year swings [1]. A clear overall conclusion with reasons earns the final mark [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation credit comes from weighing indicators against each other and caveating the data, not from adding more description.

**10. (a)** 100 x 1.03 x 1.015 x 1.005 = about **105.1** [1].
**(b)** **Year 4**, when inflation is negative [1].
**(c)** In years 2 and 3 the rate of inflation falls (from 3.0% to 1.5% to 0.5%) [1], but it is still positive, so prices are still rising, just more slowly [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The key is the sign of the inflation rate: falling inflation is not falling prices.

**11. (a)** Real wage change = (1.035 / 1.045) - 1 [1] = -0.0096 [1], so real wages fall by about **0.96%** [1].
**(b)** Lower real wages reduce households' purchasing power [1], so consumption and aggregate demand may fall, slowing growth [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The approximation 3.5 - 4.5 = -1.0% is close; label it approximate, as the exact method gives -0.96%.

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Real GDP growth shows how fast output rises after removing inflation [1] and is easy to compare across time and countries [1].
- Sustained growth expands capacity and can raise living standards [1].
- Limits: it ignores population (use GDP per head) [1], the distribution of income [1], the environment and resource use [1], and unpaid and informal activity [1].
- Other indicators matter: unemployment shows spare capacity and hardship, inflation the cost of living, the current account and public finances sustainability [1].
- Judgement: growth is a necessary first indicator but not sufficient; read it with GDP per head, unemployment and inflation, and weight each by circumstances, such as recession or boom [2]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned conclusion about weighing indicators gains more than a longer list of the limits of GDP.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "2.5%" where percentage points are needed.
- Averaging growth rates by simple addition.
- Treating per-head growth as total growth.
- Saying positive growth means recovery.
- Describing a table without quoting its figures.
- Treating falling inflation as falling prices.
- Comparing countries on different years or units.
- Listing indicators with no judgement.
