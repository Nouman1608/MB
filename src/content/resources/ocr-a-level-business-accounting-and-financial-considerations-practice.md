## Questions

**1.** Define working capital and explain what is meant by the working capital cycle. **[4]**

**2.** Tansy Row Foods (a) records a £4,000 delivery as sales in the month it is sent, though the customer pays next month; (b) provides against a £1,500 invoice it expects not to be paid; (c) uses the same depreciation method as last year. Name the accounting convention in each case and explain it. **[6]**

**3.** Sorrel Bindery has fixed costs of £27,000 a year, sells books at £15 and has variable costs of £9 per book. It sells 6,000 books.
**(a)** Calculate break-even output. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the margin of safety in books and as a percentage of sales. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the output needed for a target profit of £12,000. **[2]**
**(d)** A bulk order of 800 books at £11 each arrives. There is spare capacity. Calculate the extra profit and state one non-financial factor to consider. **[3]**
**(e)** Recommend and justify one way to lower break-even. **[3]**

**4.** Roan Hill Farm Shop: budget and actual for a month (£): sales 90,000 and 97,000; wages 18,000 and 21,000; stock purchases 45,000 and 47,500; other costs 12,000 and 11,000.
**(a)** Work out the variance for each item and label it favourable or adverse. **[4]**
**(b)** Find the variance in profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse why the wages variance may not show poor control. **[2]**

**5.** Tansy Row Foods plans a £54,000 chilling unit. Cash inflows are £20,000, £25,000, £25,000 and £10,000 in years 1 to 4. Discount factors at 10% are 0.909, 0.826, 0.751 and 0.683.
**(a)** Calculate payback. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the average rate of return (ARR). **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate net present value (NPV). **[3]**
**(d)** Recommend whether to invest, justifying your answer with quantitative and qualitative points. **[4]**

**6.** Tansy Row Foods forecasts opening cash of £8,000. Receipts are £30,000, £26,000 and £32,000 and payments £28,000, £36,000 and £27,000 over three months.
**(a)** Calculate the closing cash each month. **[3]**
**(b)** Month 3 receipts fall 10%. Calculate the new month 3 closing cash. **[2]**
**(c)** Distinguish cash-flow from profit. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate one strategy for the month 2 position. **[2]**

**7.** Vessel & Vine buys a machine for £36,000 with a residual value of £6,000 and a life of five years.
**(a)** Calculate the yearly charge under straight line depreciation. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the charge for years 1 and 2 using reducing balance at 25%, and the net book value after year 2. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why a provision for depreciation is needed. **[2]**

**8.** Vessel & Vine: revenue £600,000; cost of sales £330,000; operating expenses £195,000; interest £10,000; tax £13,000. Calculate gross profit, operating profit and profit for the year, then the gross and net profit margins. **[6]**

**9.** Vessel & Vine also has current assets £100,000 (inventory £40,000), current liabilities £80,000, non-current liabilities £150,000 and equity £250,000. Use the figures from question 8. It has 260,000 shares priced at £2.00 and pays 8p per share.
**(a)** Calculate the current ratio and acid test. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate gearing and interest cover. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate ROCE and return on equity. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate earnings per share, dividend yield and the price/earnings ratio. **[3]**
**(e)** Evaluate the financial position of the business. **[4]**

**10.** Roan Hill Farm Shop Ltd needs £60,000 for a cold store. It has operating profit of £22,000, non-current liabilities of £20,000 and equity of £80,000. Options: a 9% bank loan, or new shares sold to a family investor. Using relevant calculations, evaluate the options and recommend one. **[8]**

**11.** Tansy Row Foods plans to fund expansion mainly by borrowing, and interest rates are expected to rise. Evaluate the effect of this external influence on its financial strategy. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities [1], the money available for day-to-day running [1]. The cycle is the time from paying for inventory, through production and sale and the credit period, to receiving cash [1]; a longer cycle ties up more cash and raises the need for short-term finance [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A formula alone describes the first idea; the cycle needs the stages in order.

**2.** (a) Realisation [1]: revenue is recorded when the sale is made, not when cash arrives [1]. (b) Prudence [1]: avoids overstating profit and assets [1]. (c) Consistency [1]: makes results comparable year to year [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the convention and then tie it to the facts given, as a bare label usually earns little.

**3.** (a) Contribution 15 - 9 = £6 [1]; 27,000 / 6 = **4,500 books** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 6,000 - 4,500 = **1,500 books** [1]; 1,500 / 6,000 = **25%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** (27,000 + 12,000) / 6 [1] = **6,500 books** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Contribution 11 - 9 = £2 [1]; 2 x 800 = **£1,600** [1]; regular customers may expect lower prices [1]. **[3]**
**(e)** Cut fixed costs by £3,000 [1]; break-even 24,000 / 6 = 4,000 books [1]; it leaves price and quality unchanged, but the saving may be hard to achieve [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the contribution step, since it supports every later figure; for "recommend", give a reason, not only a method.

**4.** (a) Sales £7,000 favourable [1]; wages £3,000 adverse [1]; stock £2,500 adverse [1]; other costs £1,000 favourable [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Budget profit 90,000 - 75,000 = £15,000; actual 97,000 - 79,500 = £17,500 [1]; **£2,500 favourable** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Higher sales may have needed more staff hours [1], so wages should be judged against volume before blaming managers [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Label each variance by its effect on profit, not by its arithmetic sign.

**5.** (a) Cumulative 20,000, 45,000, 70,000 [1]; 2 + 9,000/25,000 = **2.36 years** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Total 80,000 - 54,000 = 26,000; 26,000 / 4 = 6,500 [1]; 6,500 / 54,000 = **12.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Present values 18,180 + 20,650 + 18,775 + 6,830 = 64,435 [2]; NPV = **£10,435** [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** Invest [1]: NPV is positive [1], and payback is within the 4 years [1]; weigh qualitative risk, such as food safety gain against disruption and uncertain demand [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach a decision and support it with both the figures and a qualitative point.

**6.** (a) Month 1 £10,000 [1]; month 2 £0 [1]; month 3 £5,000 [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Receipts 28,800 [1]; closing 5,000 - 3,200 = **£1,800** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Cash-flow is money actually received and paid [1]; profit is revenue less costs, including credit sales and non-cash items [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** An overdraft covers the gap quickly [1] but costs interest and may be withdrawn [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each month's closing cash is the next month's opening cash, so show that link.

**7.** (a) (36,000 - 6,000) / 5 [1] = **£6,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Year 1: 9,000 [1]; year 2: 25% of 27,000 = 6,750 [1]; NBV **£20,250** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Assets lose value through use and age [1]; matching cost to the years used avoids overstating profit and assets [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Reducing balance applies the rate to the net book value, not the original cost.

**8.** Gross profit £270,000 [1]; operating profit 270,000 - 195,000 = £75,000 [1]; profit before tax £65,000 [1] and profit for the year **£52,000** [1]; gross margin **45%** [1]; net margin **8.7%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State the formula used for net margin, as practice varies on which profit is the numerator.

**9.** (a) 100/80 = **1.25**; (100 - 40)/80 = **0.75** [2]. **[2]**
**(b)** Capital employed 400,000; gearing 150/400 = **37.5%** [1]; cover 75,000/10,000 = **7.5 times** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** ROCE 75/400 = **18.75%** [1]; return on equity 52/250 = **20.8%** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** EPS 52,000/260,000 = **20p** [1]; yield 8/200 = **4%** [1]; P/E 2.00/0.20 = **10** [1]. **[3]**
**(e)** Acid test below 1 suggests a liquidity risk [1]; interest cover and gearing look comfortable [1]; returns are good, though without comparison data this is limited [1]; judgement: sound profitability, but watch short-term cash [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Group the ratios (liquidity, solvency, profitability) and finish with a judgement.

**10.** Loan interest 9% x 60,000 = £5,400 [1]; interest cover 22,000/5,400 = about 4.1 times [1]; gearing rises from 20% to 50% [1]. Loan: keeps control and interest is affordable, but raises risk [1]. Shares: no repayment or interest [1], but the family investor gains a say and a dividend claim [1]. Judgement [1]: the loan is reasonable because cover is above 4, if profits are stable, otherwise shares [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation scores for a reasoned choice that uses the case figures, not for listing pros and cons.

**11.** Higher rates raise interest costs and cut profit and cover [1]; they lower the net present value of projects and may make investment unattractive [1]. Fixed-rate borrowing or retained profit reduces the exposure [1]. However, rates may not rise, and expansion could raise sales [1]. Recommend a mix of loan and retained profit [1], because it spreads risk [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Connect the external influence to a specific finance decision.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a bare number without the formula or working.
- Giving gearing against equity rather than capital employed.
- Mislabelling favourable and adverse variances.
- Not carrying closing cash forward to the next month.
- Treating depreciation as a cash cost.
- Listing points when asked to evaluate, with no judgement.
- Ignoring qualitative factors in investment decisions.
- Using the wrong base for turnover ratios.
