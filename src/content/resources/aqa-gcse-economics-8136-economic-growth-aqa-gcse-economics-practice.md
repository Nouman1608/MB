These questions cover **section 3.2.2.2 Economic growth** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017. There are no tiers. All countries and figures are invented. For explanations, see the [economic growth study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-economic-growth-aqa-gcse-economics/) and the [economic growth revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-economic-growth-aqa-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is the best measure of whether an economy's output has increased? **[1]**

A: Nominal GDP

B: Real GDP

C: The consumer price index

D: The size of the population

**2.** State what is meant by GDP per capita. **[1]**

**3.** Country G's real GDP rose from 420 billion to 433.44 billion. Calculate the rate of economic growth. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** benefit of economic growth for the government. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how spending on training workers can lead to economic growth. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why growth caused only by rising consumer spending may not last. **[3]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** The table shows data for two countries.

| | Year 1 GDP | Year 1 population | Year 2 GDP | Year 2 population |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Country H | 900 billion | 30 million | 945 billion | 31.5 million |
| Country J | 200 billion | 10 million | 208 billion | 10 million |

**(a)** Calculate GDP per capita for each country in each year. **[4]**

**(b)** Country H's GDP grew faster than Country J's. Explain why this does not mean living standards rose faster in Country H. **[2]**

**8.** Country K's nominal GDP rose from 1,500 billion to 1,620 billion. Inflation over the year was 6%.

**(a)** Calculate the nominal growth rate. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate real GDP in the second year and the real growth rate, each to one decimal place. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why the government should judge its growth objective using the real figure. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which rapid economic growth may harm groups of people. **[4]**

**10.** Country L has high unemployment and many factories working below full capacity. Explain why a cut in interest rates could raise economic growth quickly. **[3]**

**11.** Distinguish between growth from using spare capacity and growth from increasing productive capacity. **[4]**

**12.** "Government spending on infrastructure, such as roads and broadband, is the best way to achieve economic growth." Evaluate this view. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. **[1]**

**2.** GDP divided by the population, giving the average output per person [1]. **[1]**

**3.** (433.44 − 420) ÷ 420 × 100 [1] = **3.2%** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Higher incomes and spending mean more income tax and VAT is collected without raising tax rates [1], which can fund public services or reduce borrowing [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Training raises workers' skills [1], so each worker can produce more in an hour (higher productivity) [1], which raises the amount the economy can produce, so real GDP increases [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Spending can only raise output while there is spare capacity [1]. Once firms are working at full capacity, more spending pushes up prices instead of output [1], so the growth turns into inflation, and spending may fall back if it was funded by borrowing [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** H year 1: 900 billion ÷ 30 million = **30,000** [1]; H year 2: 945 billion ÷ 31.5 million = **30,000** [1]; J year 1: 200 billion ÷ 10 million = **20,000** [1]; J year 2: 208 billion ÷ 10 million = **20,800** [1].

**(b)** H's population grew by the same proportion as its GDP, so output per person did not change [1], whereas J's GDP per capita rose by 4%, so average living standards rose faster in J [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** (1,620 − 1,500) ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **8%** [1].

**(b)** Real GDP = 1,620 ÷ 1.06 [1] = **1,528.3 billion** [1]; real growth = 28.3 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **1.9%** [1].

**(c)** Most of the nominal rise came from higher prices, not more output [1]; the real figure shows the change in the quantity of goods and services, which is what the growth objective is about [1]. **[6]**

**9.** More production can increase pollution and congestion [1], harming the health of people living near factories and busy roads [1]. Demand may grow faster than capacity, causing inflation [1], so people on fixed incomes, such as some pensioners, can buy less [1]. Also accept: growth concentrated in some regions or skilled jobs, so other groups gain little and inequality widens. **[4]**

**10.** Lower interest rates make borrowing cheaper and saving less rewarding [1], so households spend more and firms invest more [1]. Because there is spare capacity and unemployed workers, firms can raise output quickly without pushing up prices much [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Growth from spare capacity happens when idle workers and machinery are brought back into use [1], usually because total spending rises; it is limited by the economy's existing capacity [1]. Growth from increased capacity happens when the economy can produce more [1], through more or better-skilled workers, more capital or new technology, and can last without causing inflation [1]. **[4]**

**12.** *For the view:*
- Building infrastructure creates jobs and raises spending straight away [1].
- Better roads and broadband cut firms' costs and raise productivity [1], so the economy's capacity grows in the long run [1].

*Against the view:*
- Projects take years to plan and build, so benefits are slow [1].
- They are expensive and may need more government borrowing [1].
- Other policies may be more effective, such as education and training, which raise the quality of labour [1], or lower interest rates when there is spare capacity [1].

*Judgement:* Infrastructure is a strong supply-side policy because it raises both demand now and capacity later [1], but it is not always the best: in a downturn with spare capacity a faster policy may be needed, and it works best alongside spending on skills [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving the answer to a growth calculation without a percentage sign.
- Dividing billions by millions incorrectly; write both in full or cancel carefully.
- Subtracting inflation from the GDP level instead of the growth rate.
- Treating faster GDP growth as faster growth in living standards.
- Giving a 9-mark answer with no judgement.
