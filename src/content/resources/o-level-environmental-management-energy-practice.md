## Section A

**1.** State whether each of the following is renewable or non-renewable: coal, wind, nuclear power,
hydro-electric power. **[4]**

**2.** Explain why nuclear power is classified as non-renewable even though it does not burn fossil
fuel. **[2]**

## Section B

**3.** A country is deciding between building a new coal power station or a wind farm. Explain **two**
factors it should consider when choosing between them. **[6]**

**4.** Explain **two** factors that affect a country's demand for energy. **[4]**

**5.** An oil tanker collides with rocks near a coastline, spilling crude oil that begins spreading
toward a coral reef. Describe **one** cause-side factor that contributed to this type of incident and
**one** response-side action that could reduce its impact. **[4]**

**6.** Explain **two** strategies a country could use to reduce its energy demand through conservation.
**[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Coal: non-renewable [1]. Wind: renewable [1]. Nuclear power: non-renewable [1]. Hydro-electric
power: renewable [1].

**2.** Nuclear power relies on uranium, which is a finite resource formed over geological timescales far
slower than it is consumed [1] — the classification depends on the finite nature of the fuel source, not
on whether combustion occurs [1].

**3.** **Environmental impact** — coal produces greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution during
combustion, while wind produces no emissions during generation but has a visual/land-use impact [1] [1]
[1]. **Reliability and cost** — coal offers established, reliable infrastructure and high energy
density, while wind depends on weather/geography and requires high upfront capital cost, so a country
without consistently strong winds gains little from a wind farm [1] [1] [1].

**4.** **Wealth** — wealthier households and countries consume more energy per person, e.g. through more
appliances, vehicles and heating/cooling [1] [1]. **Climate** — colder regions have higher heating
demand while hotter regions have higher cooling demand, so a country's climate directly shapes seasonal
energy demand [1] [1].

**5.** Cause-side: shipping accidents (tanker collisions) are a recognised cause of oil pollution
alongside off-shore extraction and pipeline failure [1] [1]. Response-side: booms could be deployed to
contain the spill before it spreads further toward the reef, or skimmers used to physically remove oil
from the water surface [1] [1].

**6.** **Reducing consumption** — improving insulation and using energy-efficient devices lowers the
amount of energy needed for the same activity [1] [1]. **Education for energy conservation** — raising
public awareness of energy-saving behaviour (e.g. turning off unused appliances) reduces demand without
requiring new infrastructure [1] [1].

## Exam technique for this topic

Build one table classifying all nine named energy resources as renewable or non-renewable before
attempting any question in this topic, since nearly every question here assumes that classification is
already secure. When comparing energy resources (as in Q3), always structure the answer around matched
pairs of factors — an environmental point balanced against an economic or reliability point — rather
than listing one-sided advantages, since Paper 2 typically asks candidates to weigh a specific energy
choice for a named country or region rather than to describe resources in isolation. For oil pollution
questions, keep causes (extraction, pipelines, shipping) and management strategies (MARPOL,
double-hulled tankers, clean-up methods) in separate mental columns, since mixing a cause into a
management answer (or vice versa) is a common way marks are dropped even when the underlying knowledge
is correct.

## Practising the "weigh two options" question type

Question 3's style — choosing between two named energy options for a described scenario — recurs often
in this topic and rewards a specific structure: identify the relevant factor, state how each option
performs on that factor, and only then state which option that factor favours in the given scenario.
Repeating this three-step structure across several factors (environmental impact, cost/reliability,
land-use/geography, social acceptability) for a range of practice scenarios — a coastal country
considering tidal power, a landlocked country considering solar, an economy heavily dependent on
imported fuel considering domestic coal — builds the flexible comparative reasoning this topic actually
tests, rather than a fixed list of pros and cons that doesn't adapt to the specific scenario given.

## Linking this topic to Topic 1

Topic 1 (Rocks and Minerals) and Topic 2 (Energy and the Environment) share a common underlying theme —
the extraction and use of a natural resource, weighed against its environmental cost — so revision that
explicitly connects the two topics tends to be more resilient than revising each in isolation. For
example, the same rationing/sustainability logic that applies to extracting a mineral resource (using it
at a rate that allows future availability) applies equally to non-renewable energy resources, and the
same environmental-economic-social weighing structure used to evaluate a mining project in Topic 1 can
be reused, with different specific content, to evaluate an energy resource choice in Topic 2. Practising
this kind of cross-topic transfer is valuable because Paper 2's synoptic-style questions sometimes draw
on more than one topic within a single structured question.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Classifying nuclear power as renewable because it doesn't burn fossil fuel.
- Giving a generic "pollution is bad" answer instead of naming a specific impact (e.g. on coral reefs, marine mammals, birds).
- Confusing MARPOL (an international convention) with a physical clean-up method like a boom or skimmer.
- Listing energy demand factors without explaining the mechanism by which each one increases or decreases demand.
