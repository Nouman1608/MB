This guide covers **9.3 Hazards resulting from atmospheric disturbances** in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists: the **global distribution of areas most at risk** from large-scale tropical disturbances (cyclones, hurricanes, typhoons) and small-scale disturbances (tornadoes); the **processes causing their formation and development**; **hazards from large-scale disturbances** (storm surges, coastal flooding, intense rainfall leading to severe river floods and mass movement, and high winds); **hazards from small-scale disturbances** (intense precipitation as rain and hail, high winds, and pressure imbalances); **primary and secondary impacts**; and **prediction, preparedness and monitoring** and **perception of risk**. ## Distribution of areas most at risk

**Tropical cyclones** form over warm oceans between about 5° and 20° of latitude, then move westward with the trade winds before often curving poleward. Areas at greatest risk are therefore the **eastern and poleward-facing coasts** within and just beyond the tropics: the Caribbean and the Gulf and Atlantic coasts of the USA; the Pacific coast of Mexico; the Philippines, southern China, Taiwan, Japan and Vietnam; the Bay of Bengal coasts of Bangladesh, India and Myanmar; Madagascar and Mozambique; and northern Australia. The South Atlantic and the south-east Pacific rarely have tropical cyclones, because cool sea temperatures and strong wind shear suppress formation.

**Tornadoes** are recorded on every continent except Antarctica, but the **United States** has far more than anywhere else, more than 1,000 in an average year, mainly in the Great Plains and the south-east. There, warm, moist air from the Gulf of Mexico meets dry air from the south-west and cold air from Canada over flat land. Bangladesh and eastern India also have frequent, deadly tornadoes in the pre-monsoon season.

## Formation and development of tropical cyclones

**Structure:**

- **Eye:** a calm, often clear centre of sinking air, typically tens of kilometres across, with the lowest pressure.
- **Eyewall:** a ring of towering cumulonimbus clouds around the eye with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
- **Spiral rainbands:** bands of thunderstorms curving in towards the centre.
- **Outflow:** air spreading out at high level, often visible as cirrus.

**Development as a heat engine.** Warm water evaporates; moist air converges at the surface and rises. Condensation releases **latent heat**, warming the core so that air rises faster and surface pressure falls further. More air is drawn in, deflected by the Coriolis effect into an anticlockwise spiral in the northern hemisphere (clockwise in the southern). As long as the storm stays over warm water with little wind shear, this positive feedback strengthens it.

**Life cycle** (with standard wind thresholds):

| Stage | Sustained wind |
|---|---|
| Tropical disturbance | Cluster of thunderstorms, no closed circulation |
| Tropical depression | Below 63 km per hour |
| Tropical storm (named) | 63-118 km per hour |
| Hurricane, typhoon or cyclone | 119 km per hour and above |

The **Saffir-Simpson scale** rates hurricanes from Category 1 (119-153 km per hour) to Category 5 (252 km per hour or more). Storms **decay** when they move over land (losing their moisture supply and gaining friction), over cooler water, or into strong wind shear.

## Formation of tornadoes

A tornado is a violently rotating column of air extending from a thunderstorm to the ground. Most strong tornadoes come from **supercells**, long-lived thunderstorms with a rotating updraught (**mesocyclone**). Formation needs:

1. **instability:** warm, moist air near the surface beneath cooler, drier air;
2. **wind shear:** winds that change speed and direction with height, creating horizontal rolling;
3. **lift:** a front, dry line or heating to start air rising.

The updraught tilts horizontal rotation into the vertical. A downdraught on the rear of the storm can help stretch and tighten the rotation down to the ground. Tornadoes are rated on the Enhanced Fujita scale (EF0-EF5) from the damage they cause.

## Hazards from large-scale disturbances

- **Storm surge:** low pressure raises the sea surface (about 1 cm for each 1 hPa fall), but most of a surge comes from strong onshore winds piling water against the coast. Surges are highest in shallow, funnel-shaped bays and when they coincide with high tide. **Cyclone Nargis**, which struck the Irrawaddy delta of Myanmar on 2 May 2008, killed about 138,000 people, mostly through surge flooding across very low, densely populated land.
- **Coastal flooding:** surge plus high waves and rain overwhelm defences. **Hurricane Katrina** (29 August 2005) caused levee and floodwall failures in New Orleans, flooding much of the city and contributing to about 1,800 deaths.
- **Intense rainfall:** slow-moving storms release huge totals. **Hurricane Harvey** (2017) stalled over south-east Texas and dropped over 1,500 mm of rain in places, causing severe river and urban flooding around Houston. On steep land the same rain triggers landslides and debris flows.
- **High winds:** destroy roofs, power lines, crops and weak buildings, and turn debris into missiles.

## Hazards from small-scale disturbances

- **Intense precipitation:** supercells produce torrential rain causing flash floods, and large **hail** that damages crops, vehicles and roofs.
- **High winds:** the strongest tornadoes have winds over 300 km per hour, destroying well-built houses along a narrow path. **The Moore, Oklahoma tornado** of 20 May 2013 (EF5) killed 24 people, including children in a school.
- **Pressure imbalances:** pressure falls sharply inside the vortex. The difference contributes to structural damage, but wind and flying debris do most of the harm.

## Primary and secondary impacts

Primary impacts include deaths from drowning and collapsing buildings, damaged homes, crops and infrastructure. Secondary impacts include contaminated water and disease, salinisation of farmland after surges, food shortages, lost income, displacement, and disruption to fuel and power supplies. Impacts depend on the storm's intensity, speed and track, the shape of the coast, the time of day, and the **vulnerability** of the population.

## Prediction, preparedness and monitoring

- **Monitoring:** satellites track cloud patterns; aircraft fly into hurricanes to measure pressure and winds; ocean buoys record sea temperatures; Doppler radar detects rotation in thunderstorms.
- **Prediction:** computer models forecast cyclone tracks days ahead within a cone of uncertainty; intensity is harder to forecast than track. Tornadoes are forecast as a **watch** (conditions favour tornadoes over a region for some hours) and a **warning** (rotation detected or a tornado seen, usually only minutes ahead).
- **Preparedness:** evacuation plans, cyclone shelters, building codes with roof tie-downs, safe rooms and storm cellars, raised homes, and public education.

**Odisha, India** shows how preparedness changes outcomes. The 1999 super cyclone killed about 10,000 people. When **Cyclone Phailin**, of similar strength, struck in October 2013, around a million people were evacuated beforehand and the death toll was under 50.

## Perception of risk

People may underestimate risk because storms forecast to hit often miss ("warning fatigue"), because they have survived weaker storms, because they fear looting or loss of livestock if they leave, because shelters are crowded or far away, or because they lack transport. Women, older people and people with disabilities often face extra barriers to evacuation.

## Worked example

*Explain why storm surges are especially dangerous on some coasts. [5]*

> Low pressure in the storm raises the sea surface beneath it [1], and strong onshore winds pile water against the coast [1]. Shallow, gently sloping sea beds let the water build up rather than spread out [1], and funnel-shaped bays concentrate it into a smaller area [1]. If landfall coincides with high tide on low-lying, densely populated land, as in the Irrawaddy delta in 2008, flooding reaches far inland [1].

## Common mistakes

- Stating that cyclones form at the equator.
- Explaining storm surge by low pressure alone, ignoring wind.
- Confusing a tornado watch with a warning.
- Describing impacts without linking them to vulnerability.
- Mixing up the scales: Saffir-Simpson uses wind speed; Enhanced Fujita uses damage.

## Exam technique

**Describe the distribution** with named ocean basins and coasts, and note exceptions. **Explain** formation as a sequence driven by latent heat and the Coriolis effect. **Assess** preparedness with paired examples, such as Odisha in 1999 and 2013.
