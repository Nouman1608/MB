Quick-recall notes for "Obedience as investigated by Milgram" and "Milgram's Agency theory of social
factors affecting obedience: agency, authority, culture and proximity", in **3.2.2 Social influence**
of OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology (9218). For full explanations and a worked answer, use the
[Milgram's Agency theory study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-psychology-9218-agency-theory/);
for conformity, see the
[Conformity and Obedience revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-obedience-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Obedience** | Following a direct order from a perceived authority figure |
| **Autonomous state** | Acting on one's own judgement and feeling responsible |
| **Agentic state** | Seeing oneself as an agent of another person, who holds responsibility |
| **Agentic shift** | Moving from autonomous to agentic when accepting someone's authority |
| **Moral strain** | Anxiety from doing something one believes is wrong |
| **Binding factors** | Thoughts that let a person keep obeying despite moral strain |
| **Legitimate authority** | Someone whose right to give orders is accepted because of their social position |
| **Proximity** | Physical or psychological closeness (of victim or authority) |
| **Culture** | Shared values and practices that shape how strongly obedience is learned |
| **Prods** | The four standard verbal instructions used to pressure participants to continue |

## Milgram's study (1963)

- **Participants:** 40 American men aged 20-50, recruited by newspaper advertisement.
- **Setting:** Yale University; experimenter in a grey lab coat.
- **Set-up:** real participant = teacher; confederate (Mr Wallace) = learner in next room.
- **Task:** shock for each wrong answer, 15 V to 450 V in 15 V steps. **No real shocks.**
- **Script:** learner bangs on wall at 300 V, then silent.
- **Prods:** four, from "Please continue" to "You have no other choice, you must go on."
- **Results:** 65% to 450 V; 100% to at least 300 V; extreme signs of stress.

## Agency theory in four steps

1. Person accepts someone as a **legitimate authority**.
2. **Agentic shift**: responsibility passed to the authority.
3. **Moral strain** if the order feels wrong.
4. **Binding factors** let the person continue.

## The four elements and their evidence

| Element | Effect on obedience | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Agency** | Agentic state → obedience; autonomous → defiance | Participants continued once told the experimenter was responsible |
| **Authority** | More legitimate → more obedience | Run-down office: 47.5%; ordinary person giving orders: 20%; Hofling: 21 of 22 nurses obeyed |
| **Culture** | Strong socialisation to obey → more obedience | Replications gave different rates, higher in Mantell's German study, lower in Kilham and Mann's Australian study |
| **Proximity** | Closer victim → less obedience; closer authority → more | Same room 40%; touch 30%; telephone orders 20.5% |

## Variation figures to learn

| Variation | % to 450 V |
|---|---|
| Baseline (Yale, learner in next room) | 65 |
| Run-down office building | 47.5 |
| Learner in the same room | 40 |
| Touch proximity | 30 |
| Orders by telephone | 20.5 |
| Ordinary person gives orders | 20 |

## Evaluation summary

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Explains why obedience changed across variations | Cannot explain why about a third refused in the same situation |
| Real-world support from Hofling's nurses | Agentic state is hard to measure separately from obedience (circular) |
| Participants' words fit passing on responsibility | Participants' distress suggests they still felt responsible |
| | Based on deceptive, distressing research |
| | Baseline sample: American men only |

## Applying the elements to a scenario

| Detail in the question | Element | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| A uniform, title or important workplace | Authority (legitimacy) | More obedience |
| The person giving orders leaves or phones in | Proximity of authority | Less obedience |
| The person harmed is face to face with the one obeying | Proximity of victim | Less obedience |
| "She told herself it was his decision" | Agency (agentic state) | Continued obedience |
| A society that strongly values respect for elders or officials | Culture | More obedience |

## Exam traps

- **No real shocks** were given.
- Agency theory is a **social** explanation; Adorno's is **dispositional**.
- Proximity covers **both** the victim and the authority.
- Do not swap the variation figures.
- In culture answers, note that replications differed in procedure, so cultural differences are
  suggestive, not proof.
- In scenario questions, name the state (agentic or autonomous) and the element at work.

## Self-test

1. What percentage of Milgram's participants went to 450 volts?
2. What did all participants do?
3. Distinguish the autonomous state from the agentic state.
4. What is moral strain?
5. Give an example of a binding factor.
6. What happened to obedience when the study moved to a run-down office?
7. Give the obedience rate when the learner was in the same room.
8. What happened when orders came by telephone?
9. What did Hofling's study show?
10. How might culture affect obedience?
11. Give one strength of Agency theory.
12. Why can Agency theory be described as circular?

**Answers:**
1. 65%.
2. Went to at least 300 volts.
3. Autonomous: acting on one's own judgement and feeling responsible. Agentic: acting as an agent of an
   authority, who is seen as responsible.
4. Anxiety caused by doing something one believes is wrong.
5. Any one: telling oneself the learner agreed to take part; focusing on reading the words correctly
   rather than on the learner's distress.
6. It fell to 47.5%.
7. 40%.
8. Obedience fell to 20.5%, and some participants pretended to give shocks.
9. 21 of 22 nurses were prepared to give an overdose of an unfamiliar drug on a telephone order from an
   unknown doctor, showing obedience to perceived legitimate authority in real life.
10. Cultures that strongly value respect for authority socialise people to obey, so they may enter the
    agentic state more readily.
11. Any one: it explains the changes in obedience across Milgram's variations; it is supported by
    Hofling's real-world study.
12. The agentic state is inferred from obedience, and then used to explain that obedience.
