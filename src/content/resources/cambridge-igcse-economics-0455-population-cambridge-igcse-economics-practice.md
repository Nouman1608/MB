Revise first with the [population study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-population-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [population revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-population-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A country has a birth rate of 14 per 1,000, a death rate of 16 per 1,000 and net migration of +5 per 1,000. What is its population growth rate? **[1]**

A: −0.2%

B: +0.3%

C: +0.5%

D: +3.5%

**2.** Which is most likely to reduce the birth rate in a low-income country? **[1]**

A: A fall in the number of doctors

B: More girls staying in secondary education

C: A rise in infant mortality

D: More families working on small farms

**3.** Define optimum population. **[2]**

**4.** Country F has a population of 8 million. In one year there are 120,000 births and 64,000 deaths. Calculate the birth rate and the death rate per 1,000. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a country with excellent healthcare may have a higher death rate than a country with poor healthcare. **[2]**

**6.** State two pull factors that may attract immigrants to a country. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Country G (invented) has a population of 30 million, a birth rate of 24 per 1,000, a death rate of 8 per 1,000 and net migration of −6 per 1,000.

**(a)** Calculate the natural increase rate as a percentage. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in population over one year. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain one effect of the emigration on Country G's economy. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows Country H's population pyramid as percentages of the total (invented).

| Age | Male % | Female % |
|---|---|---|
| 65+ | 2 | 3 |
| 40–64 | 9 | 9 |
| 15–39 | 17 | 18 |
| 0–14 | 21 | 21 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the population aged under 15. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe what the pyramid suggests about Country H's birth rate and life expectancy. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain two economic problems this structure may cause for the government. **[4]**

**9.** In Country J, the working-age population will fall from 40 million to 36 million while the over-65 population rises from 8 million to 12 million. Each pensioner receives 5,000 a year from taxes on people of working age.

**(a)** Calculate the number of workers per pensioner now and in the future. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the pension cost per working-age person now and in the future. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain one policy the government could use to reduce this burden. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how new technology can change whether a country is over-populated. **[3]**

**11.** Explain two possible effects of a gender imbalance in which most migrant workers who leave a country are men. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether an increase in population is beneficial for a low-income country. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. 14 − 16 + 5 = 3 per 1,000 = 0.3%. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Educated women tend to marry later, take paid work and have fewer children. A and C would raise the death rate or encourage larger families; D raises the value of children's labour. **[1]**

**3.** The size of population at which output per head is highest [1], given the country's resources, capital and technology [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Birth rate = 120,000 ÷ 8,000,000 × 1,000 = **15 per 1,000** [1]; death rate = 64,000 ÷ 8,000,000 × 1,000 = **8 per 1,000** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** It may have a large share of elderly people [1], who are more likely to die each year, so its death rate per 1,000 is high even though healthcare is good [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Any two: higher wages [1]; more jobs [1]; safety or political stability; better public services; family already living there. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 24 − 8 = 16 per 1,000 = **1.6%** [1].

**(b)** Growth rate = 16 − 6 = 10 per 1,000 = 1% [1]; 30,000,000 × 0.01 = **300,000** [1].

**(c)** If many emigrants are young skilled workers, the labour force loses skills [1], which can reduce productivity and tax revenue; however, money sent home by emigrants may raise household incomes [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** 21 + 21 = **42%** [1].

**(b)** A wide base shows a high birth rate [1]; only 5% are over 65, suggesting low life expectancy (or a high death rate) [1].

**(c)** Problem 1: many children need schools and healthcare [1], so government spending must rise or quality falls [1]. Problem 2: in 15 years many young people will need jobs [1]; if jobs are not created, youth unemployment will rise and income per head may fall [1]. **[7]**

**9. (a)** Now: 40 ÷ 8 = **5** [1]; future: 36 ÷ 12 = **3** [1].

**(b)** Now: 8 million × 5,000 = 40 billion; 40 billion ÷ 40 million = **1,000** [1]. Future: 12 million × 5,000 = 60 billion; 60 billion ÷ 36 million = **1,667** (to the nearest whole number) [1].

**(c)** Raise the retirement age [1], so people work and pay tax for longer and draw a pension for fewer years, lowering the number of pensioners per worker [1]. Other creditable policies: encourage immigration of workers; encourage private pension saving. **[6]**

**10.** Over-population means output per head would rise if the population were smaller [1]. New technology raises output per worker [1], which moves the optimum population upwards, so a population that was above the optimum may now be at or below it [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Effect 1: industries that rely mainly on male workers, such as construction, may face labour shortages [1], raising costs and slowing output [1]. Effect 2: fewer couples may form, so the birth rate may fall [1], reducing the future labour force [1]. Other creditable effects: more women in the labour force; remittances raising household incomes. **[4]**

**12.** Benefits: a larger labour force can raise total output [1]; a bigger home market lets firms gain economies of scale [1]; if the country is under-populated, output per head rises [1]. Costs: more spending is needed on schools, healthcare and housing, straining a limited budget [1]; if jobs do not grow as fast, unemployment rises [1]; output per head falls if population grows faster than output [1]; pressure on land and water may damage the environment [1]. Judgement: an increase is beneficial only if capital, jobs and public services grow at least as fast, which is often difficult in a low-income country, so rapid growth is more likely to be harmful [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Strong answers refer to the optimum population and to whether output grows faster than population.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding a net migration rate with the wrong sign.
- Dividing by the wrong base when converting numbers into rates per 1,000.
- Describing a pyramid without linking its shape to birth or death rates.
- Treating optimum population as a maximum population.
- Giving one side only in a "Discuss" question.
