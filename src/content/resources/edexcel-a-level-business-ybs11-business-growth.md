This guide covers section **3.3.2 Business growth** in Unit 3 (Business decisions and strategy) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. It explains why businesses grow, how they do it, and what can go wrong as they get larger. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Business decisions and strategy study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-business-decisions-and-strategy/). Condensed notes are in the [Business growth revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-business-growth-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Business growth practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-business-growth-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Growth:** the objectives of growth (economies of scale, internal and external; more market power over customers and suppliers; more market share and brand recognition; higher profitability); the distinction between inorganic and organic growth.
2. **Organic growth:** methods, advantages and disadvantages.
3. **Inorganic growth:** mergers and takeovers (reasons, the distinction between them, horizontal and vertical integration, conglomerates, financial risks and rewards); advantages and disadvantages.
4. **Problems arising from growth:** diseconomies of scale, internal communication, overtrading.

## Why businesses grow

**Economies of scale** are falling average costs as output rises. **Internal** economies come from within the business:

- **Purchasing:** bulk buying discounts.
- **Technical:** larger, more efficient machinery and specialised equipment.
- **Financial:** large firms borrow at lower interest rates.
- **Managerial:** specialist managers become affordable.
- **Marketing:** advertising costs spread over more sales.

**External** economies come from the growth of the industry or area, such as a skilled labour pool, specialist suppliers and good transport links near a business cluster.

**Worked example 1.** Calder Foods makes 10,000 units a month at a total cost of $120,000, 25,000 units at $250,000 and 60,000 units at $780,000.

- 10,000 units: 120,000 / 10,000 = **$12.00** per unit.
- 25,000 units: 250,000 / 25,000 = **$10.00** per unit (economies of scale).
- 60,000 units: 780,000 / 60,000 = **$13.00** per unit (diseconomies of scale).

Average cost falls by $2 from 10,000 to 25,000 units, then rises by $3 by 60,000 units. There is a most efficient output somewhere between, where average cost is lowest.

**Other objectives of growth:**

- **Market power:** a bigger buyer can demand lower prices from suppliers, and a bigger seller faces less competition and may set prices.
- **Market share and brand recognition:** a well-known brand attracts customers and makes new products easier to launch.
- **Profitability:** higher revenue and lower unit costs can mean higher profit, although profit does not always rise automatically.

## Organic and inorganic growth

**Organic (internal) growth** comes from the business's own resources: selling more, launching new products, opening new branches, or entering new markets. **Inorganic (external) growth** comes from joining with another business, by merger or takeover.

| | Organic | Inorganic |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Slower | Faster |
| Cost and risk | Lower, funded from profit; the business keeps control | Higher; a large purchase price, debt and integration risk |
| Control | Culture and systems stay unchanged | Cultures must be combined |
| Examples | New product, new shop, export sales | Buying a rival, merging with a supplier |

**Methods of organic growth:** developing new products, finding new customers (including export), expanding the sales force, opening new outlets, and franchising. **Advantages:** lower risk, steady learning, culture is kept, and no need for large external finance. **Disadvantages:** slow, so rivals may overtake; limited by the market size and the firm's own resources; some opportunities are missed.

## Mergers and takeovers

A **merger** is when two businesses agree to combine, usually forming a new organisation. A **takeover** (or acquisition) is when one business buys a controlling share in another. A takeover can be friendly (the target's board agrees) or hostile (the target's board opposes it).

**Reasons:** to gain market share quickly, to remove a competitor, to obtain new products or technology, to enter new markets, to gain economies of scale or to reduce risk by spreading activities.

**Types of integration:**

| Type | What it means | Example | Main benefit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | Joining firms at the same stage of production | Two supermarket chains | Larger market share; economies of scale; less competition |
| Vertical (backward) | Joining with a supplier | A bakery buys a flour mill | Secure supplies; control quality; capture supplier profit |
| Vertical (forward) | Joining with a distributor or retailer | A brewery buys pubs | Secure outlets; direct contact with customers |
| Conglomerate | Joining firms in unrelated industries | A food firm buys a hotel group | Spreads risk across markets |

**Worked example 2: the financial risks and rewards of a takeover.** Calder Foods plans to buy Dunmore Bakery, which has 5 million shares at a market price of $3.50. Calder offers $4.20 a share.

- Premium = 4.20 / 3.50 - 1 = **20%**.
- Market value before the offer = 5m x 3.50 = $17.5m. Cost of the offer = 5m x 4.20 = **$21m**.
- Calder pays 40% from cash ($8.4m) and borrows the rest ($12.6m). At 7% interest the loan costs 12.6m x 0.07 = **$882,000 a year**.

For the takeover to pay off, Dunmore must earn Calder more than the $882,000 interest plus the return Calder could have made on the cash. **Rewards** are extra profit, synergies (savings from combining) and a stronger market position. **Risks** are overpaying, high debt, falling share price if the deal disappoints, loss of key staff and failure to achieve synergies.

**Advantages of inorganic growth:** fast, immediate market share, access to new skills or markets, quick removal of a competitor. **Disadvantages:** expensive, culture clash, job losses and low morale, possible regulatory intervention, and no guarantee of gains. Many mergers fail to create the expected value because of poor integration.

## Problems arising from growth

**Diseconomies of scale** are rising average costs as a business gets too large. Causes include poor communication in a large hierarchy, slow decisions, lower motivation as employees feel remote from the business, duplicated work, and co-ordination problems between sites. Calder's cost rise from $10.00 to $13.00 a unit in worked example 1 is an example. Solutions include delegation, smaller business units, better communication systems and a clear culture.

**Internal communication** becomes more difficult with more staff, layers and sites. Messages can be delayed or distorted, staff may feel uninformed, and managers may lose contact with customers. Fixes include flatter structures, regular briefings, intranets, and clear reporting lines. A merger adds a particular problem: two different systems and cultures.

**Overtrading** means expanding sales faster than the business can finance. Costs rise before cash arrives, so the business runs short of working capital and may fail even though it is profitable on paper.

**Worked example 3.** Calder's sister business, a small catering firm, has sales of $500,000 and customers take 60 days to pay. Sales then grow by 60% to $800,000.

- Trade receivables before = 500,000 x 60/365 = **$82,192**.
- Trade receivables after = 800,000 x 60/365 = **$131,507**.
- Extra cash tied up = **$49,315**.

The firm must find $49,315 more finance just to wait for customers to pay, before it has paid for the extra ingredients and staff. If it cannot, it will fall behind with suppliers. Solutions include a bank loan, an overdraft, factoring, shortening credit terms, or slowing growth.

## Choosing between growth methods

When a question asks you to choose a method of growth, compare the options on the same criteria and use the figures in the source.

| Criterion | Organic growth | Merger or takeover |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Slower | Faster |
| Cost | Lower, often funded from profit | Higher, often needs debt (for example the $882,000 interest in worked example 2) |
| Risk | Lower | Higher: overpaying, culture clash, integration failure |
| Control | The business keeps its culture and systems | Two cultures and systems must be combined |
| Effect on cash | Gradual demand for working capital | Large, immediate demand for finance |

A business with strong cash reserves, a competitor available to buy and an urgent need to gain market share is more likely to choose a takeover. A business with limited finance, a loyal customer base and time to grow is more likely to choose organic growth. Whichever you recommend, name the factor on which your judgement depends.

## Common mistakes

- Treating growth as always good: it can lower unit cost but it can also raise it.
- Saying a takeover is the same as a merger: in a takeover one firm buys control of another.
- Mixing up backward and forward vertical integration.
- Calling any growth in sales "organic" without checking that it comes from the firm's own resources.
- Describing diseconomies of scale without a cause.
- Forgetting that overtrading is a cash problem, not a profit problem.

## Exam technique

For **explain**, give one reason or problem and follow it with a result for the business. For **analyse**, run through the chain, such as "takeover, high debt, high interest, lower profit, less cash for investment". For **discuss** and **assess** questions on a choice between organic and inorganic growth, weigh speed, cost, risk, control and the firm's resources, and judge using the source: a firm with high debt and a cautious owner should lean towards organic growth.
