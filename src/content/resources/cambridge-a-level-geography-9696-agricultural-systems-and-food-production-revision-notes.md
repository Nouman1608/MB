These notes condense section 11.1, Agricultural systems and food production, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026 (Paper 4, Topic 11 option). For full explanations and worked examples, use the [11.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-agricultural-systems-and-food-production/); then test yourself with the [11.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-agricultural-systems-and-food-production-practice/).

## The four syllabus statements

1. Factors (physical, social, economic, political) affecting farm land use and practices, especially irrigation, land tenure, demand and distance from markets, and technology.
2. The agricultural system (inputs, throughputs, subsystems, output) for one arable and one pastoral system.
3. Intensive and extensive production; agricultural productivity.
4. Issues in intensification and in the extension of cultivation.

## Factors in one view

| Group | Factor | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Temperature, growing season | Which crops ripen; length of grass growth; number of harvests |
| Physical | Effective rainfall, reliability | Whether rain-fed cropping is possible or irrigation is needed |
| Physical | Relief, aspect | Whether machinery can work; erosion risk; terracing |
| Physical | Soil depth, drainage, acidity | Crop choice; ploughing or grazing |
| Social | Land tenure, inheritance | Willingness to invest; plot size; access to credit |
| Social | Diet, culture, knowledge | Crops grown for the household; acceptance of new methods |
| Economic | Demand, prices, market distance | Subsistence or commercial; perishable goods near buyers |
| Economic | Capital and credit | Ability to buy machinery, seed, pumps |
| Political | Subsidies, price guarantees, land reform | Which crops pay; who holds land |

Physical factors set **limits**; human factors decide which possibility is chosen. Limits can be stretched at a cost (irrigation, greenhouses, lime, terraces).

## Named factors: the one-line explanation

- **Irrigation:** removes the water limit, so more harvests a year and water-demanding crops; flood methods waste water, drip methods save it but cost more.
- **Land tenure:** secure owners invest for the long term; sharecroppers keep only part of extra output; fragmented holdings are hard to mechanise; no title means no collateral for loans.
- **Demand:** subsistence follows the household diet; commercial farming follows prices; rising incomes shift demand towards meat, dairy, fruit and vegetables.
- **Distance from markets:** bulky and perishable goods are grown near buyers unless refrigeration and fast transport cut the friction of distance.
- **Technology:** raises output and cuts unit costs but needs capital, skills and spare parts, so it can widen inequality between farmers.

## Systems checklist

Every system answer needs:

- **inputs** (physical and human);
- **throughputs** (the processes that turn inputs into outputs);
- at least one **subsystem** link (crop residues feed livestock; manure returns to fields);
- **outputs** (products, waste, income);
- **feedback** (income from sales buys next season's inputs; a bad year depletes them).

Arable examples to know: commercial grain (Topic 11 guide) and smallholder rain-fed maize and beans. Pastoral examples: commercial dairying (Topic 11 guide) and nomadic pastoralism in East African drylands.

## Productivity measures

| Measure | Formula | Who tends to score highest |
|---|---|---|
| Land productivity (yield) | Output ÷ area (t per ha) | Intensive, irrigated or labour-rich farms |
| Labour productivity | Output ÷ workers (t per worker) | Mechanised commercial farms |
| Energy ratio | Food energy out ÷ energy in | Low-input traditional systems |

Intensity = inputs per hectare. It is not the same as farm size.

## Issues in one view

| Intensification (same land, more output) | Extension (more land) |
|---|---|
| Soil compaction and loss of organic matter | Deforestation and carbon release |
| Monoculture, pests, pesticide resistance | Habitat and biodiversity loss |
| Groundwater depletion; salinisation | Erosion on marginal semi-arid land |
| Eutrophication from fertiliser and manure | Loss of grazing; herder–farmer conflict |
| Farmer debt for inputs | Displacement of users with weak tenure |

Link: failed intensification on degraded land can push farmers on to new land.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Land use | What the land is used for (crop, livestock, orchard) |
| Farming practices | The methods used (hand weeding, rotational grazing, terracing) |
| Effective rainfall | Rainfall remaining after evaporation, available to plants |
| Growing season | Period warm enough for plant growth (about 6°C for grass) |
| Subsistence farming | Production mainly for the farm household |
| Commercial farming | Production mainly for sale |
| Cash tenancy | Rent fixed in money; tenant keeps extra output |
| Communal tenure | Shared group rights, often over grazing land |
| Throughput | Process within the system, such as sowing or milking |
| Feedback | An output that returns to change the inputs |
| Energy ratio | Energy in food produced divided by energy put in |
| Cold chain | Refrigerated storage and transport from farm to consumer |

## Exam traps

- "Intensive" and "extensive" refer to inputs per hectare, not farm size.
- Always give units for productivity, and compare like with like.
- A system without feedback or a subsystem is only a list.
- Physical factors rarely "cause" a land use; they make it possible or impossible.
- Distance from markets matters less where cold chains and air freight exist; say why.
- The syllabus wants issues of intensification, not only its gains.
- Name your systems: one arable and one pastoral.

## Self-test

1. Name the four groups of factors listed in 11.1.
2. Why does temperature fall with height matter for farming?
3. State one advantage and one drawback of drip irrigation.
4. Why might a sharecropper apply less fertiliser than an owner-occupier?
5. How does insecure tenure limit access to credit?
6. Give one way rising incomes change demand for farm products.
7. Name the five elements a full system answer should contain.
8. A farm has 3 ha, 6 workers and produces 12 t. Calculate yield and output per worker.
9. Which measure often favours low-input traditional systems?
10. Give two issues of intensification.
11. Give two issues of extending cultivation into semi-arid land.
12. How can intensification and extension be linked?

### Answers

1. Physical, social, economic, political.
2. Higher land has a shorter growing season, limiting crops and harvests.
3. Advantage: little water is wasted. Drawback: high cost to install, or pipes clog.
4. They give a share of the harvest as rent, so they keep only part of any extra yield.
5. Land without title cannot be offered as security for a loan.
6. More demand for meat, dairy, fruit or vegetables (and for animal feed).
7. Inputs, throughputs, subsystems, outputs, feedback.
8. Yield 12 ÷ 3 = 4 t per ha; output per worker 12 ÷ 6 = 2 t per worker.
9. The energy ratio.
10. Any two: soil compaction, pesticide resistance, groundwater depletion, salinisation, eutrophication, debt.
11. Any two: soil erosion, land degradation in dry years, loss of grazing, herder–farmer conflict.
12. Failed intensification on degraded land can push farmers to clear new land.
