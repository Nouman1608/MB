The guide gives one list of related concepts for the whole course, with no split between standard and extended mathematics.

## Key facts to learn

- The key concepts the guide gives to mathematics are form, logic and relationships.
- Related concepts belong to the subject. They let you look at a key concept more closely and can arise from a unit's topic or from the way mathematics is done.
- There are twelve in Table 2. Teachers may add others, including from other subject groups.
- A statement of inquiry combines a key concept, related concepts and a global context.
- The four branches are numerical and abstract reasoning, thinking with models, spatial reasoning and reasoning with data.

## Glossary meanings in one line each

| Concept | Remember it as |
|---|---|
| Approximation | Near the true value, but not exact |
| Change | A difference in size, quantity or behaviour |
| Equivalence | Same in value, so one can replace the other (statements, quantities, expressions) |
| Generalization | A broad claim built from particular cases |
| Models | Real-life events shown through expressions, equations or graphs |
| Patterns | Numbers or objects arranged by a set order or rule |
| Quantity | How much there is: a count or a measure |
| Representation | The form in which something is shown |
| Simplification | Rewriting in an easier form |
| Space | The geometric dimensions within which an object is described |
| Systems | Sets of parts that connect to and depend on one another |
| Validity | Sound, logical mathematics giving a true and accurate conclusion or a sensible interpretation of results |

## Branch links at a glance

| Branch | Related concepts the guide lists for it |
|---|---|
| Numerical and abstract reasoning | approximation, equivalence, generalization, quantity, simplification, systems, validity |
| Thinking with models | change, equivalence, patterns, quantity, representation, simplification, systems |
| Spatial reasoning | change, equivalence, models, patterns, quantity, representation, space, systems |
| Reasoning with data | all twelve except space: validity, systems, simplification, representation, quantity, patterns, models, generalization, equivalence, change, approximation |

Memory hooks:
- Equivalence, quantity and systems appear in all four branches.
- Space appears in spatial reasoning only.
- Reasoning with data has the longest list: eleven of the twelve, everything except space.
- Approximation, generalization and validity sit in numerical reasoning and reasoning with data.
- Models is not named under thinking with models, but a teacher may still use it there.

## Methods in steps

**Approximating and checking**
1. Round each number to one that is easy to use.
2. Calculate with the rounded numbers.
3. Work out the exact value if needed, then find the error: estimate minus exact.
4. Say whether the estimate is too high or too low, and decide whether the context needs rounding up or down.

**Describing a change**
1. Change = final value − starting value.
2. Rate = change ÷ time (or ÷ the other quantity).
3. Percentage change = change ÷ starting value × 100.

**Showing equivalence**
1. Expand and collect terms on both sides, or convert to the same form.
2. If the forms match, they are equivalent.
3. To show they are not equivalent, one counterexample is enough.
4. A matching test value supports equivalence but does not prove it.

**Generalizing from a pattern**
1. Tabulate terms and find differences.
2. Propose a rule, such as an + b.
3. Test it on every given term and on a new one.
4. Explain why it works, if you can.

**Using a model**
1. Define the variables and units.
2. Write the rule (expression, equation or graph).
3. Calculate, then say what the model leaves out.
4. Judge whether the answer is valid in the situation.

## Worked reminders

**Approximation.** 6.1 × 29.8 ≈ 6 × 30 = 180. Exact: 181.78. The estimate is 1.78 too low.

**Change.** A price rises from 40 to 46: change 6, percentage change 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%.

**Equivalence.** 4(x − 1) + 2x = 4x − 4 + 2x = 6x − 4. At x = 3 both sides give 14.

**Generalization.** Terms 4, 9, 14, 19 rise by 5, so the rule is 5n − 1. The 10th term is 49.

**Quantity.** 3.5 m³ is 3.5 × 1000 = 3500 litres.

**Simplification.** (10p²q) / (15pq²) = 2p / (3q), after dividing by 5, p and q.

**Space.** At scale 1:30 a real length of 4.5 m (450 cm) is 450 ÷ 30 = 15 cm on the model. Lengths scale by k, areas by k², volumes by k³.

**Systems.** 1011 in base 2 is 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 11.

**Models and patterns.** The sequence 3, 6, 9, 12 gives the model T = 3n. For n = 7, T = 21.

**Representation.** The rule y = 3 − x gives the table x = 0, 1, 2, 3 and y = 3, 2, 1, 0. The graph is a straight line sloping down with gradient −1.

**Validity.** x² + 2x − 24 = 0 gives x = 4 or x = −6 for a length. Reject −6 because a length cannot be negative.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Equivalence and equality of numbers | Equivalence covers statements and expressions too, such as 2(x + 3) and 2x + 6 |
| Approximation and error | The approximation is the value used. The error tells you how far from exact it is |
| Pattern and generalization | A pattern is the ordered set. A generalization is the rule you state about it |
| Model and representation | A model depicts a real-life event. Representation is the form it is shown in, such as a table or graph |
| Simplification and approximation | Simplifying an expression keeps its value exactly. Approximating changes the value slightly |
| Quantity and number | A quantity is an amount, usually with units |
| Validity and correctness | Valid means the reasoning is sound and the result makes sense in context |

## Command terms that go with the concepts

The guide's command terms tell you what a question wants. In your own words:
- Estimate: obtain an approximate value.
- Show: give the steps of a calculation or derivation.
- Describe: give a detailed account of a pattern or process.
- Explain: give reasons or causes.
- Justify: give valid reasons or evidence for a conclusion.
- Comment: give a judgment on a given result.

A question on validity often says "justify" or "comment", so state your reason and refer to the context.

## Linking concepts to a unit

To plan or describe a unit, pick a key concept, add related concepts, then add a global context. Example: key concept relationships; related concepts patterns and generalization; global context scientific and technical innovation; possible study statistics with scatter graphs. A statement could be: "Describing patterns in measurements allows us to make general rules that can be tested."

Link to the criteria as well. Criterion B (investigating patterns) leans on patterns and generalization. Criterion D (applying mathematics in real-life contexts) leans on models and validity. See [criteria in practice](/resources/ib-myp-mathematics-criteria-in-practice-revision-notes/) for more.

## Quick self-test

1. Which branch is space linked with in the guide?
2. Name the three related concepts the guide lists for all four branches.
3. Estimate 6.1 × 29.8 by rounding to whole numbers, then find the error against the exact value.
4. A price rises from 40 to 46. Find the percentage change.
5. Are 4(x − 1) + 2x and 6x − 4 equivalent? Show how you know.
6. Find the rule for 4, 9, 14, 19 and give the 10th term.
7. Convert 3.5 m³ to litres.
8. Simplify (10p²q) / (15pq²).
9. Write 1011 (base 2) in base 10.
10. A cost model is C = 5 + 2n. Find C when n = 8, and name one thing the model might ignore.

### Answers

1. Spatial reasoning.
2. Equivalence, quantity and systems.
3. 6 × 30 = 180. Exact 181.78. Error = 1.78, so the estimate is too low.
4. 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%.
5. Yes. 4(x − 1) + 2x = 4x − 4 + 2x = 6x − 4.
6. Rise by 5 each time, so 5n − 1. The 10th term is 5 × 10 − 1 = 49.
7. 3.5 × 1000 = 3500 litres.
8. 2p / (3q).
9. 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 11.
10. C = 5 + 2 × 8 = 21. It may ignore discounts for bulk buying, extra charges or limits on n (any sensible point).

## Where credit is usually lost

- Giving an everyday meaning of a concept when the question asks for its meaning in mathematics.
- Quoting a generalization that has been checked against only the given terms.
- Treating a matching test value as a proof of equivalence.
- Leaving out units, or forgetting to convert them, when working with a quantity.
- Scaling a volume by k instead of k³, or an area by k instead of k².
- Rounding to the nearest value when the context needs rounding up or down.
- Accepting a negative length or an impossible count because it solves the equation.
- Describing a model as perfect, without saying what it ignores.
- Claiming a link between two variables is causal because they are correlated.
