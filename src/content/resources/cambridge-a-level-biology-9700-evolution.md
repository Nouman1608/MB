This guide teaches section 17.3 Evolution from Topic 17, Selection and evolution, in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It covers all three learning outcomes in the section. This is A Level content, examined on Paper 4 (A Level Structured Questions), and Paper 5 can set data-handling tasks in the same context.

Section 17.3 builds directly on 17.1 (variation) and 17.2 (natural selection, genetic drift and the founder effect), which are taught in the [Selection and evolution study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-selection-and-evolution/). Here the focus is narrower and deeper: what "evolution" means at the level of a gene pool, how to read DNA sequence data as evidence of relationships, and how genetic isolation leads to new species. When you have worked through it, use the [Evolution revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-evolution-revision-notes/) for recall and the [Evolution practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-evolution-practice/) to test yourself.

## What section 17.3 covers

| Outcome | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 17.3.1 | Outline the theory of evolution as a process leading to new species forming from pre-existing species over time, as a result of changes to gene pools from generation to generation |
| 17.3.2 | Discuss how DNA sequence data can show evolutionary relationships between species |
| 17.3.3 | Explain how speciation may occur through genetic isolation by geographical separation (allopatric) or by ecological and behavioural separation (sympatric) |

## Evolution as a change in gene pools (17.3.1)

A **gene pool** is all the alleles of all the genes in a population at a given time. Because a population, not an individual, has a gene pool, it is populations that evolve. An individual's alleles are fixed at fertilisation; what changes over generations is the **allele frequency** in the population.

The syllabus definition has four parts, and an "outline" answer should touch each one:

1. **Pre-existing species**: every species descends from an earlier one; species are not created afresh.
2. **Changes to gene pools**: allele frequencies shift because of natural selection, genetic drift and the founder effect (17.2), with mutation supplying new alleles.
3. **Generation to generation**: the change is only visible across generations, because offspring receive a sample of the parents' alleles.
4. **Over time, leading to new species**: when the gene pools of two populations have changed enough that their members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, a new species has formed.

A useful way to see the link between 17.2 and 17.3: selection and drift explain *how* a single gene pool changes; speciation explains how *one* gene pool becomes *two* separate ones that change independently.

## DNA sequence data as evidence (17.3.2)

All living organisms use DNA and the same genetic code. When one species splits into two, both daughter species inherit the same base sequence for each gene. From then on, mutations build up independently in each lineage. The longer ago two species shared a common ancestor, the more differences their sequences for the same gene will show.

### What is compared

- The **same gene** (or the same non-coding region) in each species, lined up base by base so that each position matches.
- Genes found in all the species being studied, such as genes for respiratory enzymes or ribosomal RNA, so that very distant groups can be compared.
- The result is expressed as a number or a percentage of positions that differ.

### Building a tree from a difference table

**Worked example.** The same 100-base section of a gene is compared in four species. The table gives the number of positions that differ.

| | W | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| W | – | 4 | 10 | 18 |
| X | | – | 11 | 17 |
| Y | | | – | 19 |
| Z | | | | – |

1. Find the smallest number: W and X differ at only 4 positions, so they share the **most recent common ancestor**. Join them first.
2. Y differs from W by 10 and from X by 11; both values are much smaller than any comparison with Z, so Y joins the W–X branch next.
3. Z differs from all three by 17 to 19 positions, so it branched off first and is the **most distantly related**.
4. The tree reads: Z splits off earliest; then Y; then W and X separate most recently.

If the rate of base substitution in this gene is roughly constant (a **molecular clock**), the number of differences is roughly proportional to the time since two lineages diverged. Suppose, for illustration, that one difference accumulates per 1.5 million years in this section. W and X would then have diverged about 4 × 1.5 = 6 million years ago, and Z from the others about 26 to 29 million years ago.

### Why "discuss" needs limitations

The command word "discuss" asks for a balanced answer: what the data show *and* how far you can trust them.

- **Strengths**: sequence differences are objective and countable; they can compare organisms that look nothing alike (where morphology gives no clue); they can be checked against fossil evidence.
- **Limitations**: one short section may not represent the whole genome; different genes, and different lineages, can mutate at different rates, so the molecular clock is only approximate; the same base can mutate more than once, hiding earlier changes; small samples (one individual per species) ignore variation within a species.
- **Amino acid sequences** of a protein show fewer differences than the gene's DNA, because the genetic code is degenerate: some base substitutions do not change the amino acid. DNA data therefore give finer resolution between closely related species.

## Speciation (17.3.3)

Speciation is the formation of a new species. The syllabus links it to **genetic isolation**: a barrier that stops **gene flow** (the transfer of alleles between populations through interbreeding). Once gene flow stops, the gene pools of the separated populations change independently.

### Allopatric speciation: geographical separation

1. A **geographical barrier** divides one population into two, for example a new river channel, a rising sea level isolating an island, or a mountain range. "Allopatric" means "different homelands".
2. There is **no gene flow** between the two populations.
3. **Different mutations** arise in each population.
4. The two areas have **different environmental conditions**, so there are **different selection pressures**. Different alleles give an advantage in each area, so different allele frequencies are selected for.
5. In small populations, **genetic drift** also changes allele frequencies by chance.
6. Over many generations the gene pools become so different that, even if the barrier disappears, individuals from the two populations **cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring**. They are now separate species.

The finches of the Galápagos Islands are the standard illustration: populations on different islands, separated by sea, adapted to different food sources and diverged into separate species.

### Sympatric speciation: ecological and behavioural separation

"Sympatric" means "same homeland". The populations share one area but still stop interbreeding.

- **Ecological separation**: members of the population come to use different parts of the habitat. They may feed on different host plants, live in different microhabitats, or breed at different times of year (for example, plants that flower in different months cannot cross-pollinate). The North American apple maggot fly is often cited: some flies now mate and lay eggs on apple trees rather than on their ancestral hawthorn host, and the two groups interbreed less and less.
- **Behavioural separation**: courtship displays, songs, calls or mate preferences diverge, so individuals only mate with partners showing the "right" signal. Different mutations and selection then act on each group.

In both cases, the barrier to gene flow is not physical distance. Once gene flow is reduced, the same processes as in allopatric speciation (mutation, selection, drift) drive the gene pools apart.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain how a population of beetles living in a forest could become two species after the forest is split by a wide area of farmland.* **[5]**

> The farmland is a geographical barrier, so this is allopatric speciation **[1: names the barrier]**. Beetles in the two forest fragments cannot meet, so there is no gene flow between the populations **[1]**. Different mutations occur in each population **[1]**. The fragments differ, for example in tree species or temperature, so different alleles are selected for in each; drift may also change allele frequencies in a small fragment **[1]**. Over many generations the gene pools become so different that beetles from the two fragments could no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so they are separate species **[1]**.

Why it scores: each step is a separate idea, the answer names *alleles* and *gene pools* rather than "the beetles adapt", and it ends with the test of a species.

## Common mistakes

- Saying an **individual** evolves or "adapts to survive". Individuals do not change their alleles; allele frequencies in a population change.
- Writing that the barrier **causes** mutations. Mutations are random; the barrier only stops alleles being shared.
- Forgetting to say the new species **cannot produce fertile offspring** together. "Cannot breed" alone is too weak, because some hybrids are born but are sterile.
- Calling a difference in behaviour or breeding time "allopatric". If the populations occupy the same area, it is sympatric.
- Treating sequence data as proof. Similar sequences *suggest* a more recent common ancestor.
- Comparing **different genes** in different species. Only the same gene (or region) can be compared.

## Exam technique for 9700 command words

- **Outline** (17.3.1): give the main points briefly; for evolution, include gene pool, allele frequencies, generations and new species.
- **Discuss** (17.3.2): give what the data show and at least one limitation; one-sided answers lose marks.
- **Explain** (17.3.3): give reasons in sequence (barrier → no gene flow → mutation → different selection → reproductive isolation).
- **Suggest**: apply the ideas to an unfamiliar organism; use the information in the stem, such as different flowering times or songs, to decide which type of speciation fits.
