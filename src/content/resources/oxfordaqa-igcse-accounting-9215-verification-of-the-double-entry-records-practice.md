These questions cover "Verification of the double entry records", the first content point of section 3.2 in the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification, version 1.2. They test what each of the four verification techniques compares and which errors it can reveal. All amounts are in dollars. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured ones.

Revise first with the [verification of the double entry records study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-verification-of-the-double-entry-records/) and the [verification revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-verification-of-the-double-entry-records-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State the four verification techniques listed in the specification. **[2]**

**2.** Which one of these records is produced outside the business?

A: the trial balance

B: the trade receivables ledger control account

C: the bank statement

D: the general journal

**[1]**

**3.** In this specification, control accounts are memorandum records. Explain what this means. **[1]**

**4.** State the two records that are compared when a trade payables ledger control account is used to verify the payables ledger. **[2]**

**5.** Which one of these errors would be revealed by a trial balance?

A: a credit sale of 500 entered in neither account

B: a payment of 300 for rent debited to the rates account

C: the wages account overcast by 100

D: a credit purchase of 420 entered as 240 in both the purchases account and the supplier's account

**[1]**

**6.** Explain why a bank reconciliation can reveal a payment that was entered in the cash book and the supplier's account at the same wrong amount, when the trial balance cannot. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** For each error, state which verification technique would reveal it, and give a reason. If none of the four techniques would reveal it, say so.

**(a)** A direct debit of 85 for electricity appears on the bank statement but was not entered in the cash book. **[2]**

**(b)** A credit sale of 1,360 was correctly entered in the sales journal but posted to the customer's account as 1,630. **[2]**

**(c)** A cheque of 410 received from customer A Patel was credited to the account of customer A Patil. **[2]**

**8.** At 31 August the trade receivables ledger control account of Fenwick Tiles has a balance of 31,460. The list of balances in the receivables ledger totals 31,820.

**(a)** Calculate the difference. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest two different errors in the receivables ledger that could cause exactly this difference. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why each of your errors would also cause the trial balance to disagree. **[1]**

**9.** At 30 September the cash book of Orla's Bakery shows a debit balance of 5,320. The bank statement shows a balance of 4,980. On comparing them:

- a direct debit of 160 is on the bank statement but not in the cash book;
- a credit transfer of 240 from a customer is on the bank statement but not in the cash book;
- a cheque for 960 paid to a supplier was entered in the cash book as 690; the bank statement shows the correct amount;
- cheques totalling 1,100 have not yet been presented;
- takings paid in on 30 September have not yet appeared on the bank statement.

**(a)** Calculate the updated cash book balance. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the amount of the takings not yet on the bank statement. **[2]**

**(c)** Identify two items above that the trial balance could not have revealed, and explain why. **[2]**

**10.** The owner of a shop says: "Our trial balance agrees and our bank reconciliation agrees, so our records must be free from errors." Evaluate this statement. **[4]**

**11.** Explain three ways in which a trade receivables ledger control account helps the owner of a business. **[3]**

**12.** A business checks its records only with a trial balance. Give two errors that would go undetected but that a bank reconciliation statement would reveal. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Trial balance and trade receivables ledger control accounts [1]; trade payables ledger control accounts and bank reconciliation statements [1]

**2.** **C** [1]

**3.** They are kept **outside the double entry system**, only to check the accuracy of the ledger accounts [1]

**4.** A total built from the **books of prime entry** (purchases journal, purchases returns journal, cash book, general journal) [1]; compared with the **total of the individual supplier balances** in the payables ledger [1]

**5.** **C** [1]

*Examiner insight:* A is omission, B is commission and D is original entry; all three leave the totals equal.

**6.** The cash book and the supplier's account both use the same wrong figure, so debits still equal credits and the trial balance agrees [1]; the bank statement is an independent record showing the **actual amount paid**, so it disagrees with the cash book [1]

**7. (a)** **Bank reconciliation** [1]; the bank statement shows the direct debit but the cash book does not, so the two records differ [1]

**(b)** **Trial balance** (debits 270 too high), and the **receivables control account** locates it [1]; the control account uses the correct journal total of 1,360 but the customer's account shows 1,630, so the list of balances is 270 higher than the control account [1]

**(c)** **None** of the four [1]; this is an error of commission, so debits equal credits, the control account total is unchanged, and the cash book agrees with the bank [1] **[6]**

**8. (a)** 31,820 − 31,460 = **360** (the list is higher) [1]

**(b)** Any two [1] [1], for example: a sales invoice posted to a customer's account at 360 more than its correct amount (such as 4,630 instead of 4,270); sales returns of 180 debited instead of credited to a customer's account; a credit note for 360 entered in the sales returns journal but never posted to the customer's account

**(c)** In each case one side of the double entry in the ledger was wrong (the customer account) while the other side was correct, so total debits exceed total credits by 360 [1] **[4]**

**9. (a)** 5,320 − 160 + 240 [1]; − 270 (960 − 690) [1]; updated balance = **5,130** [1]

**(b)** 4,980 − 1,100 + takings = 5,130 [1]; takings = **1,250** [1]

**(c)** The **direct debit of 160** (or credit transfer of 240) was not recorded anywhere in the ledger, so there was nothing to make the totals disagree [1]; the **cheque entered as 690** was recorded at the same wrong amount in the cash book and the supplier's account, so debits still equalled credits [1] **[7]**

**10.** Agreement does show that debits equal credits and that the cash book matches the bank statement after timing differences [1]; but the trial balance cannot reveal errors such as omission, commission, principle or compensating errors [1]; and the bank reconciliation checks only the bank columns of the cash book, not the other accounts [1]; so the records are more likely to be accurate, but the owner cannot conclude they are free from error [1]

**11.** Any three [1] [1] [1]: it **locates** errors to the receivables ledger instead of the whole ledger; it gives a **quick total of trade receivables** for the financial statements without adding every customer balance; it acts as a **check on the work** of the person keeping the customer accounts

**12.** Any two [1] [1]: bank charges, interest, direct debits or standing orders on the bank statement but missing from the cash book; credit transfers received but not recorded; a bank payment or receipt entered at the same wrong amount in the cash book and the other account

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a technique without the reason (which two records disagree).
- Claiming a control account reveals a posting to the wrong customer.
- Treating agreement of a trial balance as proof of accuracy.
- In bank questions, putting a cash book item on the bank statement side or the other way round.
- Giving the difference without saying which record is higher.
