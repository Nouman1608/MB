These questions cover section 3.12.1, Radius of the nucleus, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), which is International A-level only content. Learn the content first with the [nuclear radius study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-radius-of-the-nucleus/) and the [nuclear radius revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-radius-of-the-nucleus-revision-notes/). Use e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻², h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J, 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and R₀ = 1.2 fm unless a question says otherwise.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State the order of magnitude of the radius of a nucleus and of an atom, in metres. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why high-energy electrons, rather than neutrons, are used to measure nuclear radii by diffraction. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the ratio radius of a uranium-238 nucleus : radius of a carbon-12 nucleus. **[2]**

**4.** Use R = R₀A^(1/3) to show that the volume of a nucleus is proportional to its nucleon number. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the distance of closest approach of an alpha particle gives only an upper limit for the radius of the target nucleus. **[2]**

**6.** Calculate the radius and the volume of a nucleus with nucleon number 125. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** An alpha particle of kinetic energy 6.0 MeV travels directly towards a silver nucleus. Silver has proton number 47 and nucleon number 107.

**(a)** Calculate the distance of closest approach. **[3]**

**(b)** State two assumptions made in your calculation. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the radius of the silver nucleus from R = R₀A^(1/3) and comment on the answer to (a). **[1]**

**8.** A beam of electrons of energy 300 MeV is directed at a thin target containing oxygen-16 nuclei. The first diffraction minimum is detected at 57°. For this minimum, sin θ ≈ 1.22λ/2R.

**(a)** Calculate the wavelength of the electrons. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the radius of an oxygen-16 nucleus. **[2]**

**(c)** Use your answer to (b) to find a value of R₀. **[1]**

**(d)** Describe the graph of electron intensity against diffraction angle. **[2]**

**(e)** State how the angle of the first minimum would change for a target of lead nuclei with the same beam. **[1]**

**9.** The following radii were obtained for five nuclei. They are rounded values for illustration.

| A | 9 | 27 | 64 | 125 | 216 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R / fm | 2.6 | 3.8 | 5.0 | 6.2 | 7.5 |

**(a)** Show by calculation that the data are consistent with R = R₀A^(1/3). **[2]**

**(b)** State the value of R₀ given by these data. **[1]**

**(c)** Predict the radius of a nucleus with A = 343. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain how the relationship shows that all nuclei have the same density. **[2]**

**10.** Nuclear matter contains closely packed nucleons.

**(a)** Calculate the number of nucleons per cubic metre in nuclear matter. **[2]**

**(b)** Estimate the mean distance between the centres of neighbouring nucleons. **[1]**

**(c)** The density of nuclear matter is 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³. Calculate the mass of 1.0 cm³ of nuclear matter. **[1]**

**(d)** Explain why the density of an ordinary solid is so much smaller than this. **[2]**

**11.** A gold atom has radius 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Gold has nucleon number 197.

**(a)** Calculate the radius of a gold nucleus. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the fraction of the atom's volume occupied by its nucleus. **[2]**

**(c)** State how this result explains the behaviour of most alpha particles fired at a thin gold foil. **[1]**

**12.** Alpha particles of 4.0 MeV and 8.0 MeV are each directed head-on at gold nuclei (Z = 79, A = 197). Both follow the Coulomb prediction for scattering.

**(a)** Calculate the distance of closest approach for each energy. **[2]**

**(b)** State what can be concluded about the radius of the gold nucleus. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the radius of a gold nucleus and of an alpha particle (A = 4) from R = R₀A^(1/3). **[1]**

**(d)** Estimate the kinetic energy, in MeV, an alpha particle needs for the surfaces of the two nuclei just to touch. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Nucleus: about 10⁻¹⁵ m (1 fm to 7 fm) [1]; atom: about 10⁻¹⁰ m [1] **[2]**

**2.** Electrons are leptons and are not affected by the strong interaction, so they respond only to the charge of the nucleus [1]; at high energy their de Broglie wavelength is comparable with the nuclear diameter, so they diffract measurably [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Neutrons interact through the strong force near the nucleus, which makes their scattering harder to interpret.

**3.** Ratio = (238/12)^(1/3) [1] = 2.7 [1] **[2]**

**4.** V = (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)π(R₀A^(1/3))³ [1] = (4/3)πR₀³A, and (4/3)πR₀³ is a constant, so V ∝ A [1] **[2]**

**5.** The alpha particle is repelled and stops before reaching the nucleus [1], so the nuclear radius must be smaller than the distance of closest approach [1] **[2]**

**6.** R = 1.2 × 125^(1/3) = 1.2 × 5 = 6.0 fm [1]; V = (4/3)π(6.0 × 10⁻¹⁵)³ = 9.0 × 10⁻⁴³ m³ [1] **[2]**

**7. (a)** E_k = 6.0 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 9.6 × 10⁻¹³ J [1]; d = (8.99 × 10⁹ × 2 × 47 × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)²)/9.6 × 10⁻¹³ [1] = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁴ m [1]

**(b)** Any two: the approach is head-on; the nucleus does not recoil; only the electric force acts; both behave as point charges [1] [1]

**(c)** R = 1.2 × 107^(1/3) = 5.7 fm, which is smaller than 23 fm, as an upper limit should be [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* The charge of the alpha particle is 2e; using e halves the answer.

**8. (a)** λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(300 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³) = 4.1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m [1]

**(b)** 2R = 1.22λ/sin 57° = 1.22 × 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 0.839 [1] = 6.03 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, so R = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m [1]

**(c)** R₀ = R/A^(1/3) = 3.01/16^(1/3) = 3.01/2.52 = 1.2 fm [1]

**(d)** A high central maximum at 0°, falling to a first minimum [1]; further maxima that become progressively weaker, with minima that do not fall to zero [1]

**(e)** Lead nuclei are larger, so the first minimum is at a smaller angle [1] **[7]**

**9. (a)** A^(1/3) = 2.08, 3, 4, 5, 6 [1]; R/A^(1/3) = 1.25, 1.27, 1.25, 1.24, 1.25, which is constant to within the rounding of the data [1]

**(b)** R₀ = 1.25 fm [1]

**(c)** R = 1.25 × 343^(1/3) = 1.25 × 7 = 8.75 fm, about 8.8 fm [1]

**(d)** R³ ∝ A, so volume ∝ A [1]; mass is also proportional to A, so mass/volume is the same for every nucleus [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Testing a proportionality needs a calculated constant ratio; a sketch or "R increases with A" earns nothing.

**10. (a)** n = 3/(4πR₀³) [1] = 3/(4π × (1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵)³) = 1.4 × 10⁴⁴ m⁻³ [1]

**(b)** Spacing ≈ (1/n)^(1/3) = (7.2 × 10⁻⁴⁵)^(1/3) = 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, about 2 fm [1]

**(c)** Mass = 2.3 × 10¹⁷ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 2.3 × 10¹¹ kg [1]

**(d)** In ordinary matter almost all the mass is in the nuclei [1], but each nucleus fills only a tiny fraction of its atom's volume, the rest being occupied by electrons of negligible mass [1] **[6]**

**11. (a)** R = 1.2 × 197^(1/3) = 7.0 fm [1]

**(b)** Fraction = (R_nucleus/R_atom)³ [1] = (6.98 × 10⁻¹⁵/1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰)³ = 1.2 × 10⁻¹³ [1]

**(c)** The atom is almost entirely empty space, so most alpha particles pass through with little or no deflection [1] **[4]**

**12. (a)** 4.0 MeV: d = 5.7 × 10⁻¹⁴ m [1]; 8.0 MeV: d = 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁴ m [1]

**(b)** The radius of the gold nucleus is less than 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁴ m (28 fm) [1]

**(c)** Gold: 7.0 fm; alpha particle: 1.2 × 4^(1/3) = 1.9 fm [1]

**(d)** Centre separation at contact = 7.0 + 1.9 = 8.9 fm [1]; E_k = (8.99 × 10⁹ × 2 × 79 × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)²)/8.9 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 4.1 × 10⁻¹² J ≈ 26 MeV [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* An estimate should state its assumption: here, that both nuclei are spheres with radii given by R₀A^(1/3).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using e instead of 2e for the alpha particle charge.
- Substituting MeV directly into the Coulomb equation.
- Treating the closest approach as the nuclear radius.
- Forgetting to cube when finding a volume, or cube-rooting when comparing radii.
- Placing the diffraction minimum for a larger nucleus at a larger angle.
