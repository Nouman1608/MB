These questions cover section 3.3 Waves of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408, version 1.3, AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards): 3.3.1 Progressive and stationary waves and 3.3.2 Refraction, diffraction and interference, including Required practicals 1 and 2. Waves is AS and A-level content, assessed at A-level in Paper 1 and assumed in Paper 2; it is not one of the Paper 3 Section B options (sections 3.9 to 3.13).

Learn the content first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-waves/). Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and the refractive index of air as 1.00.

## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by a transverse wave and by a longitudinal wave. Give one example of each. **[4]**

**2.** A sound wave travelling at 330 m s⁻¹ has a period of 2.5 ms.

**(a)** Calculate its frequency. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its wavelength. **[2]**

**3.** A wave of frequency 50 Hz travels along a rope at 12 m s⁻¹. Points P and Q on the rope are 0.18 m apart along the direction of travel. Calculate the phase difference between P and Q in radians. **[3]**

**4.**

**(a)** Explain why light can be polarised but sound cannot. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the rods of a receiving aerial should be aligned in a particular direction. **[2]**

**5.**

**(a)** Explain, using the principle of superposition, how a stationary wave forms on a string fixed at both ends. **[3]**
**(b)** State two differences between a stationary wave and a progressive wave. **[2]**

**6.** In Required practical 1, a string of length 0.65 m and mass per unit length 1.2 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹ vibrates in its first harmonic at 196 Hz.

**(a)** Calculate the tension in the string. **[3]**
**(b)** State the frequency of the third harmonic and the number of nodes it has, including the ends. **[2]**
**(c)** The string is replaced by one with four times the mass per unit length. Length and tension are unchanged. State the new first-harmonic frequency. **[1]**
**(d)** Describe how to measure the mass per unit length of the string accurately. **[2]**

**7.** A microwave transmitter faces a metal plate. A probe moved along the line between them detects a series of minima.

**(a)** Explain why minima form. **[2]**
**(b)** The probe moves 8.4 cm from the 1st minimum to the 7th minimum. Calculate the frequency of the microwaves. **[3]**

**8.** A laser lights a double slit and fringes form on a screen.

**(a)** State what is meant by coherent sources. **[1]**
**(b)** 11 bright fringes span 15.0 mm on a screen 2.40 m from slits 0.90 mm apart. Calculate the wavelength. **[3]**
**(c)** Describe the pattern seen if the laser is replaced by a white-light source and a single slit. **[2]**
**(d)** State one safety precaution when using the laser. **[1]**

**9.**

**(a)** Derive the equation d sin θ = nλ for a diffraction grating at normal incidence. **[3]**
**(b)** A grating with 400 lines per mm is lit normally with light of wavelength 546 nm. Calculate the angle of the second-order maximum. **[2]**
**(c)** Determine the highest order that can be seen. **[2]**

**10.** Monochromatic light passes through a single narrow slit onto a screen.

**(a)** State the effect on the width of the central maximum of making the slit narrower. **[1]**
**(b)** State the effect of replacing red light with blue light. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe the pattern seen with white light. **[2]**

**11.** A step-index optical fibre has a core of refractive index 1.50 and cladding of refractive index 1.40.

**(a)** Calculate the speed of light in the core. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the critical angle at the core–cladding boundary. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the largest angle of incidence at the end of the fibre (in air) for which light is totally internally reflected at the core–cladding boundary. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain how material and modal dispersion cause pulse broadening, and state how each can be reduced. **[4]**

**12.** Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator emit sound of frequency 680 Hz. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the wavelength. **[1]**
**(b)** Point X is 4.25 m from one loudspeaker and 5.00 m from the other. Determine whether X is a point of loud or quiet sound. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest why the sound at X is not completely silent. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why two loudspeakers on separate signal generators do not give a steady pattern. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Transverse: oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy propagation [1]. Example: any EM wave or a wave on a string [1]. Longitudinal: oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy propagation [1]. Example: sound [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Direction of the wave" is weaker than "direction of energy propagation"; an example only scores if it matches the correct definition.

**2. (a)** f = 1/T = 1/(2.5 × 10⁻³) = **400 Hz** [1]
**(b)** λ = c/f = 330/400 [1] = **0.83 m** (0.825 m) [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An error in (a) carried into (b) is normally allowed as error carried forward, so show the substitution.

**3.** λ = c/f = 12/50 = 0.24 m [1]. x/λ = 0.18/0.24 = 0.75 of a cycle [1]. Phase difference = 0.75 × 2π = **3π/2 rad (4.71 rad)** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer of 270° does not earn the final mark when radians are asked for.

**4. (a)** Light is transverse, so its oscillations can be restricted to one plane [1]. Sound is longitudinal; its oscillations are along the direction of travel, so there is no other plane to filter out [1].
**(b)** The transmitted radio waves are plane polarised [1]. The aerial picks up the strongest signal when its rods are aligned with the plane of polarisation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each 2-mark part needs two separate points; "it gets a better signal" alone scores nothing.

**5. (a)** A wave travels along the string and reflects at the fixed end [1]. The incident and reflected waves have the same frequency and travel in opposite directions, and superpose [1]. Where they always cancel, nodes form; where they always reinforce, antinodes form [1].
**(b)** Any two: a stationary wave stores energy rather than transferring it [1]; its amplitude varies from zero at nodes to maximum at antinodes, while a progressive wave's amplitude is the same everywhere; points between adjacent nodes are in phase, while phase varies continuously along a progressive wave [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Same frequency" and "opposite directions" are both needed; missing either loses the mark.

**6. (a)** T = μ(2lf)² [1] = 1.2 × 10⁻³ × (2 × 0.65 × 196)² [1] = **78 N** (77.9 N) [1]
**(b)** f₃ = 3 × 196 = **588 Hz** [1]; **4 nodes** [1]
**(c)** f ∝ 1/√μ, so f = 196/2 = **98 Hz** [1]
**(d)** Measure a long length of the string (several metres) with a metre rule [1], weigh it on a balance and divide mass by length [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d) the reason for using a long length (smaller percentage uncertainty) is what makes the method "accurate".

**7. (a)** Microwaves reflected from the plate travel back and superpose with the incident waves of the same frequency [1]. At minima the two waves are always in antiphase and cancel, forming nodes [1].
**(b)** 1st to 7th minimum = 6 × λ/2 = 3λ = 8.4 cm, so λ = 2.8 cm [1]. f = c/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 0.028 [1] = **1.1 × 10¹⁰ Hz** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Using 7 spacings instead of 6 loses the first mark; the later marks can still be earned by error carried forward.

**8. (a)** Same frequency and a constant phase difference [1].
**(b)** 10 fringe spacings, so w = 1.50 mm [1]. λ = ws/D = (1.50 × 10⁻³ × 0.90 × 10⁻³)/2.40 [1] = **5.6 × 10⁻⁷ m** (563 nm) [1]
**(c)** A white central fringe [1]; the other fringes are coloured spectra, blue nearest the centre, fading after a few fringes [1].
**(d)** Never look into the beam or its reflections (or: remove reflective surfaces) [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing 15.0 mm by 11 instead of 10 is the usual error; units must be converted to metres before substituting.

**9. (a)** Light from adjacent slits travelling at angle θ has path difference d sin θ [1]. For a maximum the path difference must be a whole number of wavelengths, nλ [1]. So d sin θ = nλ, where n is the order [1].
**(b)** d = 1/(400 × 10³) = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m [1]. sin θ = 2 × 546 × 10⁻⁹ / 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.437, θ = **25.9°** [1]
**(c)** d/λ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ / 546 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.58 [1]. Highest order = **4** [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A diagram with the path difference marked can earn the first mark of (a); rounding 4.58 up to 5 loses the final mark.

**10. (a)** The central maximum becomes **wider** [1].
**(b)** The central maximum becomes **narrower** (shorter wavelength) [1].
**(c)** A white central maximum [1]; side maxima are spectra, with blue nearest the centre [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The answer must give a direction (wider or narrower), not just "changes".

**11. (a)** c_s = c/n = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 1.50 = **2.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** sin θc = n₂/n₁ = 1.40/1.50 = 0.933 [1]; θc = **69.0°** [1]
**(c)** Angle of refraction at the end face = 90° − 69.0° = 21.0° [1]. 1.00 × sin i = 1.50 × sin 21.0° = 0.539 [1]; i = **32.6°** (allow 32.5°) [1]
**(d)** Material dispersion: different wavelengths travel at different speeds in the core, so parts of a pulse arrive at different times [1]; reduce with a monochromatic source [1]. Modal dispersion: rays take different paths of different lengths, so the pulse spreads out and may overlap the next pulse [1]; reduce with a very narrow (single-mode) core [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c) the 90° − θc step is the method mark; using θc directly in Snell's law at the end face scores zero for the method.

**12. (a)** λ = v/f = 340/680 = **0.50 m** [1]
**(b)** Path difference = 5.00 − 4.25 = 0.75 m [1]. 0.75/0.50 = 1.5 wavelengths [1]. An odd number of half wavelengths, so destructive interference: **quiet** [1]
**(c)** The waves arrive with different amplitudes because X is at different distances from the speakers (or because of reflections) [1].
**(d)** The sources are not coherent: their phase difference is not constant [1], so the positions of loud and quiet points keep changing [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Quiet" with no path-difference working scores nothing; the conclusion mark depends on the 1.5λ step.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Counting fringes or nodes instead of the spacings between them.
- Leaving mm, nm or MHz unconverted before substituting.
- Giving a phase difference in degrees when radians are asked for, or with no unit.
- Rounding the highest grating order up.
- Missing "constant phase difference" from a definition of coherence.
- Forgetting the 90° − θc step when a ray enters the end of a fibre.
- Swapping material and modal dispersion, or giving one remedy for both.
- Describing stationary-wave formation without "same frequency" and "opposite directions".
