These notes condense subtopic **11.2, The chemical properties of the halogen elements and the hydrogen halides** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, AS Level). Full explanations are in the [11.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-the-chemical-properties-of-the-halogen-elements-and-the-hydrogen-halides/). The [Group 17 revision notes](/resources/as-chem-group17-revision-notes/) summarise the whole topic.

## The three outcomes

- Relative reactivity of the halogens as **oxidising agents**.
- Reactions with **hydrogen**, and why their reactivity differs.
- **Thermal stability** of the hydrogen halides, explained by **bond strength**.

## Oxidising power

```
X2 + 2e-  ->  2X-          halogen is reduced; it is the oxidising agent
Cl2  >  Br2  >  I2         oxidising power decreases down the group
```

Why: larger atom → incoming electron further from the nucleus → more shielding → weaker attraction → electron gained less readily.

## Displacement (ionic equations)

```
Cl2 + 2Br-  ->  2Cl- + Br2
Cl2 + 2I-   ->  2Cl- + I2
Br2 + 2I-   ->  2Br- + I2
I2  + Br-   ->  no reaction
Br2 + Cl-   ->  no reaction
```

Shake with cyclohexane to identify the halogen present:

| Halogen in organic layer | Colour |
|---|---|
| Br₂ | Orange to red-brown |
| I₂ | Purple |

"No reaction" still shows the colour of the halogen that was **added**.

## Reactions with hydrogen

| Equation | Conditions |
|---|---|
| H₂ + F₂ → 2HF | Explosive, even cold and dark |
| H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl | Explosive in sunlight/UV |
| H₂ + Br₂ → 2HBr | Heat with Pt catalyst |
| H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI | Heat with Pt catalyst; slow, reversible, incomplete |

**Bond energy calculation** (kJ mol⁻¹: H–H 436; Cl–Cl 242, Br–Br 193, I–I 151; H–Cl 431, H–Br 366, H–I 299):

```
HCl:  (436 + 242) - 2(431) = -184 kJ mol^-1
HBr:  (436 + 193) - 2(366) = -103 kJ mol^-1
HI:   (436 + 151) - 2(299) =  -11 kJ mol^-1
```

Reactivity falls because oxidising power falls **and** the H–X bond formed gets weaker, so much less energy is released.

## Thermal stability of HX

| | HF | HCl | HBr | HI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H–X bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 562 | 431 | 366 | 299 |
| On heating | Very stable | Stable | Slight decomposition | Decomposes readily; purple I₂ vapour with a hot wire |

Chain: larger X → longer H–X bond → bonding pair held less strongly → **weaker bond** → less energy to break → **less thermally stable**.

Decomposition: 2HX → H₂ + X₂.

## Linking to the halide ions

A strong oxidising agent forms a weak reducing agent, and the reverse. Cl₂ is the strongest oxidising agent of the three, so Cl⁻ is the weakest reducing agent; I₂ is the weakest oxidising agent, so I⁻ is the strongest reducing agent. Every displacement reaction shows both at once: in Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂, chlorine is the oxidising agent and iodide is the reducing agent.

## Results-grid shortcut

For a 3 × 3 grid of halogens against halides, count the reactions in each row: chlorine reacts twice, bromine once, iodine never. The halogen with the most reactions is the strongest oxidising agent.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Oxidising agent | A species that gains electrons and is reduced, oxidising something else |
| Displacement reaction | A more reactive halogen oxidises the halide ion of a less reactive one |
| Hydrogen halide | HX, a compound of hydrogen with a halogen |
| Thermal stability | Resistance of a compound to decomposition on heating |
| Bond energy | Energy needed to break one mole of a bond in gaseous molecules |
| Reversible reaction | A reaction that can go in both directions, reaching equilibrium |

## Exam traps

- Halogens written as single atoms in displacement equations.
- "No reaction" confused with "no colour".
- Forgetting the ⇌ for hydrogen and iodine.
- Explaining thermal stability with polarity or electronegativity instead of bond energy.
- Claiming reactivity with hydrogen falls because the X–X bond gets stronger.
- Mixing up halogens (oxidising agents) and halide ions (reducing agents).
- Bond energy calculations with only one H–X bond counted: two form.

## Self-test

1. Write the half-equation for chlorine acting as an oxidising agent.
2. Why is chlorine a stronger oxidising agent than iodine?
3. Write the ionic equation for bromine water reacting with potassium iodide.
4. What colour is seen in the cyclohexane layer after question 3?
5. Explain why iodine solution does not react with potassium bromide.
6. State the conditions needed for hydrogen to react with bromine.
7. Why does the reaction of hydrogen with iodine not go to completion?
8. Calculate ΔH for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl from the bond energies above.
9. What is observed when a hot wire is plunged into hydrogen iodide?
10. Explain why HI is less thermally stable than HCl.
11. Why is "HCl is more polar" not an acceptable explanation for its greater thermal stability?
12. In Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂, which species is oxidised?

**Answers:**

1. Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻
2. Chlorine atoms are smaller, so an incoming electron is closer to the nucleus and less shielded, and is attracted more strongly.
3. Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂
4. Purple (iodine).
5. Iodine is a weaker oxidising agent than bromine, so it cannot oxidise bromide ions.
6. Heating with a platinum catalyst.
7. It is reversible: ΔH is close to zero and the reaction reaches an equilibrium mixture of H₂, I₂ and HI.
8. (436 + 242) − 2(431) = **−184 kJ mol⁻¹**.
9. Purple vapour (iodine) forms as HI decomposes.
10. The H–I bond is longer and weaker (299 compared with 431 kJ mol⁻¹), so less energy is needed to break it.
11. The syllabus links thermal stability to bond strength. Decomposition requires the H–X bond to be broken, so the bond energy is the relevant property.
12. Bromide ions (Br⁻), oxidised from −1 to 0.
