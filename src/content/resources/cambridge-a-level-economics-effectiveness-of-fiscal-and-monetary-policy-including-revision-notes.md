## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Changes to government spending, taxation and the budget balance to influence the economy |
| Monetary policy | Central bank action on interest rates, money supply and related tools |
| Multiplier | The ratio of a change in output to the initial change in an injection |
| Crowding out | Public borrowing pushes up interest rates and reduces private investment |
| Laffer curve | The relationship between the tax rate and tax revenue, rising then falling |
| Quantitative easing | The central bank creates money and buys bonds to lower yields and raise spending |
| Real interest rate | The nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate |
| Government failure | Intervention that leaves the economy worse off than expected or worse than without it |

## Formulas and methods

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Multiplier | k = 1 / (s + t + m), with s, t and m as marginal propensities to save, be taxed and import |
| Output change | k × change in the injection |
| Net injection with crowding out | Government spending change minus the fall in private investment |
| Tax revenue | Tax rate × tax base |
| Real interest rate | Nominal rate − inflation rate (approximate) |
| Bond yield | Annual payment / price paid |
| Debt ratio | National debt / nominal GDP × 100 |
| Import change | Income elasticity of imports × percentage growth in income |

### Method in steps: a multiplier question

1. Add the three marginal propensities to get the withdrawal rate.
2. Take the reciprocal to get k.
3. Multiply by the injection.
4. Use t × change in output for extra tax revenue.
5. Subtract that revenue from the spending rise to find the change in the deficit.

### Worked reminder 1

With s = 0.05, t = 0.20 and m = 0.15, withdrawals are 0.40 and k = 2.5. A spending rise of 16 raises output by 40. Tax revenue rises by 0.20 × 40 = 8, so the deficit rises by 16 − 8 = 8.

## The Laffer curve

- Axes: tax rate (horizontal), tax revenue (vertical).
- Shape: revenue is zero at 0% and at 100%, with a maximum between.
- Left of the peak, a higher rate raises revenue. Right of the peak, a higher rate lowers it.
- Arithmetic effect: more revenue per unit of income. Behavioural effect: a smaller base through less work, avoidance, evasion and emigration.

### Worked reminder 2

Base = 300 − 2t (zu bn), revenue = t × base / 100.

| Rate (%) | Base | Revenue |
|---|---|---|
| 50 | 200 | 100 |
| 60 | 180 | 108 |
| 75 | 150 | 112.5 |
| 80 | 140 | 112 |
| 90 | 120 | 108 |

The peak is at 75%. A cut from 90% to 80% raises revenue by 4, because the rate is beyond the peak. A cut from 60% to 50% loses 8.

### Laffer evaluation points

- The peak is not observable, and it varies by tax and country.
- Revenue may not be the aim. Growth, incentives and equity can matter more.
- Behaviour takes time to change, so revenue may fall first.
- A cut beyond the peak is not the same as a cut that raises growth.

## Fiscal policy: effectiveness by objective

| Objective | Works through | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Spending and tax cuts raise AD | Short-run effect unless capacity grows |
| Unemployment | Higher AD cuts cyclical unemployment | No help for structural unemployment |
| Inflation | Cuts in AD reduce demand-pull inflation | Not cost-push, and politically hard |
| Balance of payments | Lower demand cuts imports | Expansion pulls in imports |
| Redistribution | Progressive tax and transfers | Possible effect on incentives |
| Sustainability | Taxes, subsidies, green spending | Tax base may fall as behaviour changes |

Fiscal strengths: it can be targeted at groups or regions, automatic stabilisers work without delay, and spending on infrastructure can lift capacity.

Fiscal weaknesses: long lags, deficit and debt costs, crowding out, and political pressure.

## Monetary policy: effectiveness by objective

| Objective | Works through | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | Higher rates cut consumption and investment and raise the exchange rate | Weak against cost-push |
| Growth | Lower rates raise borrowing and spending | Weak when confidence is low |
| Unemployment | Higher AD cuts cyclical unemployment | Not structural |
| Balance of payments | Higher rates raise the exchange rate | Effects on the balance depend on elasticities |
| Redistribution | Not a tool for this | Savers and borrowers affected unevenly |

Monetary strengths: quick to change, operated by a central bank at a distance from elections, and flexible in size.

Monetary weaknesses: lags before full effect, a blunt tool for the whole economy, little power when rates are near zero, and a limited effect on spending if banks do not lend.

Quantitative easing works by lowering bond yields. A bond paying 4 for a price of 80 yields 4/80 = 5%. At a price of 100 it yields 4%.

### Worked reminder 3

A government spends 16 more, but borrowing pushes up rates and private investment falls by 4. The net injection is 16 − 4 = 12. With k = 2.5 the output rise is 30, not 40. Crowding out cut the effect by a quarter.

### Evaluation checklist for any policy

1. Is there spare capacity? If so, expansion is less likely to raise prices.
2. What is the cause of the problem: demand, costs or structure?
3. How long is the lag, and could conditions change before it works?
4. Which other objective gets worse?
5. How uncertain is the size of the effect?
6. What does the final judgement depend on?

## Problems and conflicts

| Conflict | Reason |
|---|---|
| Inflation against unemployment | Lower AD cuts inflation but also output |
| Growth against balance of payments | Higher income draws in imports |
| Growth against sustainability | More output can mean more resource use |
| Redistribution against incentives | High taxes may reduce effort |
| Deficit reduction against recovery | Cuts lower AD in a slump |
| Tight money with loose fiscal policy | High rates, crowding out, a stronger currency |
| Time lags | Policy arrives after conditions change |

## Government failure

- Information: poor forecasts and uncertain output gaps.
- Time lags that make policy pro-cyclical.
- Political motives such as pre-election giveaways.
- Conflicting objectives.
- Unintended consequences such as evasion at high rates.
- Crowding out of private investment.
- A central bank pressed to finance government borrowing.

## Must-know distinctions

- Cyclical unemployment responds to AD policy. Structural unemployment does not.
- Demand-pull inflation responds to tighter demand. Cost-push inflation needs a supply response.
- Nominal rate against real rate.
- Arithmetic effect against behavioural effect on the Laffer curve.
- A discretionary change against an automatic stabiliser.

## Quick self-test

1. Define the Laffer curve.
2. With s = 0.08, t = 0.22 and m = 0.30, what is the multiplier, to 2 decimal places?
3. A spending rise of 16 with k = 2.5 raises output by how much?
4. Using the table above, how does revenue change if the rate falls from 90% to 80%?
5. What is the real interest rate if the nominal rate is 3% and inflation is 5%?
6. A bond with a payment of 4 costs 80. What is its yield?
7. National debt is 300 and nominal GDP is 400. What is the debt ratio?
8. Why is monetary policy weak against cost-push inflation?
9. Name two policy conflicts.
10. Give two examples of government failure.

### Answers

1. It shows tax revenue against the tax rate. Revenue rises, reaches a maximum, then falls as higher rates shrink the tax base.
2. The withdrawals are 0.08 + 0.22 + 0.30 = 0.60, so k = 1 / 0.60 = 1.67.
3. 2.5 × 16 = 40.
4. Revenue rises from 108 to 112, a rise of 4.
5. 3 − 5 = −2%.
6. 4 / 80 = 5%.
7. 300 / 400 = 75%.
8. Higher rates cut demand, but cost-push inflation comes from rising costs. Cutting demand lowers output and employment without removing the cost pressure.
9. Any two from: inflation against unemployment, growth against the balance of payments, growth against sustainability, redistribution against incentives.
10. Any two from: poor information, time lags, political motives, crowding out, unintended consequences.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a Laffer curve peak as a fact instead of an assumption.
- Forgetting that taxes and imports reduce the multiplier.
- Applying monetary policy to cost-push inflation without saying it is weak.
- Mixing up nominal and real interest rates.
- Describing a diagram with unlabelled axes or without naming the equilibrium.
- Listing evaluation points without a conclusion that says what it depends on.
- Writing about all policies when the question names one.
- Ignoring that a conflict has two sides, so only one objective is explained.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does the Laffer curve show?**
The Laffer curve plots tax revenue against the tax rate. Revenue is zero at 0% and at 100%, with a maximum in between, so left of the peak a higher rate raises revenue and right of the peak it lowers revenue.

**How do you calculate the multiplier with taxes and imports?**
Add the marginal propensities to save, to tax and to import to get the withdrawal rate, then take the reciprocal. For example, withdrawals of 0.40 give a multiplier of 2.5.

**How does quantitative easing work?**
Quantitative easing works by buying bonds, which raises their prices and lowers their yields. Lower yields reduce borrowing costs across the economy.

**What are examples of government failure in macroeconomic policy?**
Examples include poor forecasts and uncertain output gaps, time lags that make policy pro-cyclical, political motives such as pre-election giveaways, conflicting objectives, unintended consequences and crowding out of private investment.

**What are the strengths of monetary policy compared with fiscal policy?**
Monetary policy is quick to change and operated by a central bank at a distance from elections. Fiscal policy can be targeted at groups or regions, and automatic stabilisers work without delay.
