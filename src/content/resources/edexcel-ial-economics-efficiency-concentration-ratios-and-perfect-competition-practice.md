## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by allocative efficiency and by productive efficiency. **[4]**

**2.** Sales in the tea market of Country E (invented), in coins m: firm J 260, K 190, L 150, M 90, N 60. Ten smaller firms sell 250 between them.

**(a)** Calculate CR3 and CR4. **[3]**
**(b)** K and M merge. Calculate the new CR3. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one possible effect of the merger on consumers. **[2]**

**3.** A firm could make 500 units at a lowest possible unit cost of 12.00 coins. Its actual unit cost is 14.40.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage by which actual cost exceeds the lowest possible. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total cost wasted. **[1]**
**(c)** Identify one cause of this X-inefficiency. **[1]**

**4.** State three assumptions of perfect competition and explain why a firm in this market is a price taker. **[5]**

**5.** A perfectly competitive firm sells at 12 coins a unit. Its total cost is 40 at zero output, then 100, 150, 215, 300, 410 and 545 at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 units.

**(a)** Calculate marginal cost for each 10-unit step. **[2]**
**(b)** State and justify the profit-maximising output. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the profit and state whether it is supernormal. **[2]**

**6.** A firm has total fixed cost of 90 coins. At its chosen output of 30 units, total variable cost is 240.

**(a)** Calculate average variable cost. **[1]**
**(b)** The price is 7.50. Explain whether the firm should continue in the short run, using figures. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain what changes if the price is 9. **[1]**

**7.** Market demand is Q = 3,000 - 50P. At a price of 14, 20 firms each produce 115 units. The lowest ATC of each firm is 10, at 100 units.

**(a)** Calculate the number of firms in long-run equilibrium. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how the industry moves to this position. **[3]**

**8.** A firm's lowest ATC is 9.00 at 80 units. In the short run the price is 12 and the firm produces 110 units, where ATC is 10.20.

**(a)** Calculate its supernormal profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain whether it is productively and allocatively efficient now. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain the position in the long run. **[3]**

**9.** Explain dynamic efficiency and discuss whether perfect competition is likely to be dynamically efficient. **[8]**

**10.** Market R has four leading firms with shares of 44%, 18%, 12% and 6%. Market S has four leading firms with shares of 21%, 20%, 20% and 19%.

**(a)** Show that CR4 is the same in both. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate the usefulness of concentration ratios in judging how competitive a market is. **[8]**

**11.** Assess whether perfect competition achieves an efficient allocation of resources. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Allocative efficiency means resources are allocated to the goods consumers value most [1], which occurs where P = MC [1]. Productive efficiency means producing at the lowest possible average cost [1], at the bottom of the ATC curve where MC = ATC [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns more when paired with its condition, so write P = MC and the lowest point of ATC rather than only describing them in words.

**2. (a)** CR3 = (260 + 190 + 150) / 1,000 = 600 / 1,000 [1], = **60%** [1]. CR4 = (600 + 90) / 1,000 = **69%** [1].
**(b)** Merged firm sells 190 + 90 = 280, so the top three are 280, 260 and 150 [1]. CR3 = 690 / 1,000 = **69%** [1].
**(c)** Higher concentration may give firms more market power [1], so prices could rise or choice could fall, or collusion becomes easier [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show which firms you added. A bare percentage is hard to credit if one slip puts the wrong firms in the top three, especially after a merger changes the ranking.

**3. (a)** Excess = 14.40 - 12.00 = 2.40 [1]; 2.40 / 12.00 = **20%** [1].
**(b)** 2.40 x 500 = **1,200 coins** [1].
**(c)** Weak cost control or management, or lack of competitive pressure [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the lowest possible cost, not the actual cost. Dividing by 14.40 gives 16.7% and answers a different question.

**4.** Any three of: many buyers and sellers, homogeneous products, perfect information, freedom of entry and exit, profit maximisation [1] [1] [1]. Each firm supplies a negligible share, so its output cannot move the market price [1]. If it charged more, buyers with perfect information would buy an identical product elsewhere [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming assumptions is not enough for the explanation. Link at least one of them (many small sellers, identical product, full information) to why the firm cannot raise price.

**5. (a)** MC per unit: 6.0, 5.0, 6.5, 8.5, 11.0, 13.5 [1] [1].
**(b)** MR = price = 12. MC is 11 up to 50 units and 13.5 beyond, so output of **50** adds more to revenue than cost, and a further step does not [1] [1].
**(c)** TR = 600; profit = 600 - 410 = **190** [1]. ATC = 8.2, below price, so the profit is supernormal [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Justify the output by comparing MC with MR. Choosing the output with the lowest ATC, 30 units here, is a common wrong turn.

**6. (a)** 240 / 30 = **8** [1].
**(b)** Revenue = 7.50 x 30 = 225, below TVC of 240 [1]. Producing loses 90 + 240 - 225 = 105 [1], more than the 90 lost by shutting [1], so the firm should shut down.
**(c)** At 9, revenue is 270, above TVC. The loss is 60, below 90, so it continues [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare price with AVC, and use numbers. Saying only that the firm makes a loss does not show whether closing would be better.

**7. (a)** Demand at P = 10: 3,000 - 500 = 2,500 [1]. Firms = 2,500 / 100 [1] = **25** [1].
**(b)** At 14, price exceeds ATC, so there is supernormal profit [1]. New firms enter, supply shifts right and price falls [1]. Entry stops at 10, where P = lowest ATC and profit is normal [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the long-run price, not 14, to find market demand. Give the chain in order: profit, entry, supply, price.

**8. (a)** (12 - 10.20) x 110 = 1.80 x 110 = **198** [1] [1].
**(b)** Not productively efficient: output of 110 is above 80 and ATC of 10.20 exceeds 9.00 [1] [1]. Allocatively efficient, since the firm sets MC = P = 12 [1].
**(c)** Entry drives the price down to 9 [1]. Output is 80 at lowest ATC, so productively efficient [1]. P = MC, so allocatively efficient [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Treat short run and long run separately. Productive efficiency fails in the short run here, so a blanket claim that perfect competition is efficient would be marked as too general.

**9.** Dynamic efficiency is improvement in productive and allocative efficiency over time through investment and innovation [1]. Against: long-run supernormal profit is zero, limiting funds for research [1]. Perfect information means rivals copy an innovation immediately [1], weakening the incentive to invest [1]. For: competition forces cost cutting, since firms that fail to do so make losses and exit [1]. Short-run supernormal profit can fund investment [1]. Small firms lack scale for research, and identical products give no reason to develop new ones [1]. Judgement: dynamic efficiency is less likely than static efficiency, though cost pressure keeps firms adopting known technology [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Define the term, then argue both ways and end with a reasoned conclusion. A list of assumptions without a link to investment does not answer the question.

**10. (a)** R: 44 + 18 + 12 + 6 = **80%** [1]. S: 21 + 20 + 20 + 19 = **80%** [1].
**(b)** Ratios give a simple way to compare markets and a high figure suggests market power [1]. Yet R and S differ: in R one firm holds 44%, more than twice any rival, so it may lead on price, while S has four near-equals who may compete or collude [1] [1]. The ratio ignores how shares are spread [1]. Results depend on market definition [1], and ignore imports and the threat of entry [1]. Basis (sales, output, employment) changes the figure [1]. Judgement: useful as a first indicator, but behaviour needs other evidence [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data in the question. Pointing out that R and S share a CR4 yet differ in structure is stronger than a general list of limitations.

**11.** Allocative: P = MC in short and long run, so output matches what consumers value, with the outcome based on no externalities [1] [1]. Productive: only in the long run, at lowest ATC [1]. In the short run output can be above or below the lowest-ATC level [1]. Illustration: lowest ATC at 80 units, short-run output 110 [1]. Competition removes X-inefficiency, since high-cost firms make losses and exit [1]. Dynamic: little profit to invest and innovations are copied [1] [1]. Externalities mean P = MC can still be socially inefficient [1]. The assumptions are unrealistic, so the model is a benchmark [1]. Small firms may not reach minimum efficient scale [1]. Judgement: strong on static efficiency in the long run, weaker on dynamic efficiency and real-world fit [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate static from dynamic efficiency and short run from long run, then reach a judgement. Repeating the assumptions without linking them to efficiency earns little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the sum of the top n firms instead of the whole market.
- Not re-ranking firms after a merger before recalculating the ratio.
- Choosing the lowest-ATC output as profit-maximising instead of MC = MR.
- Using ATC rather than AVC for the shutdown decision.
- Claiming that perfect competition is productively efficient in the short run.
- Calling long-run profit zero, rather than normal profit with no supernormal profit.
- Describing efficiency definitions without the condition (P = MC, lowest ATC).
- Evaluating with a list of weaknesses and no judgement.
