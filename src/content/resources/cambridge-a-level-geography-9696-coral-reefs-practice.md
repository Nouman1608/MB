## Section B

**7.** The fictional Teraku Reef is a reef flat that has grown upward at a mean rate of 2.5 mm per year. A projection suggests local sea level will rise at a mean rate of 6.0 mm per year between 2025 and 2100.

**(a)** Calculate how far the reef surface will fall below its present depth relative to sea level by 2100 if both rates stay constant. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest two consequences for the low coral sand islands on the reef rim. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why a reef affected by pollution is less likely to keep up with sea-level rise. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows a survey of four fictional reef zones on the Malaro fringing reef.

| Zone | Mean depth at low tide (m) | Number of coral species recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Fore-reef slope (upper) | 8 | 46 |
| Reef crest | 0.5 | 9 |
| Reef flat | 1 | 14 |
| Fore-reef slope (deep) | 35 | 6 |

**(a)** Describe the pattern of coral diversity shown. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain the differences in coral diversity between the zones. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how overfishing and nutrient pollution can combine to damage a coral reef. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how marine heatwaves cause coral bleaching, and why repeated events are especially damaging. **[5]**

**11.** Describe the global distribution of coral reefs and explain one exception to the general pattern. **[6]**

**12.** "Possible management strategies for coral reefs are more effective at a local scale than at a global scale." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A ring or horseshoe-shaped reef [1] surrounding a lagoon with no central island [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Sediment blocks light needed by zooxanthellae for photosynthesis [1], and settling sediment smothers polyps, which use energy to clear it [1]. **[2]**

**3.** It absorbs and breaks incoming wave energy, protecting the reef flat and lagoon [1]. It produces coral and algal debris that supplies sand to the reef flat, lagoon and islands [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Zooxanthellae are algae living in the polyp's tissues [1]. They photosynthesise and pass sugars to the polyp, supplying most of its energy [1]. The polyp provides shelter, carbon dioxide and nutrients from its waste [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Fresh water lowers salinity below what corals tolerate [1]. The river's suspended sediment cuts light and smothers polyps [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Subsidence theory: a fringing reef round a volcanic island grows upward as the island sinks [1], becoming a barrier reef and then an atoll once the island is submerged [1]. Glacial control theory: waves cut platforms when sea level fell during glacial periods [1], and corals grew upward on the platform rims as sea level rose, so lagoons have similar depths [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Net rate = 6.0 - 2.5 = 3.5 mm per year; 2100 - 2025 = 75 years [1]. 3.5 x 75 = 262.5 mm, about 0.26 m deeper [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Larger waves can cross the reef flat, so the islands suffer more erosion and overwash [1]. Salt water may flood the islands and contaminate their freshwater lens [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Pollution, such as sediment and nutrients, reduces light and favours algae [1]. Coral growth and calcium carbonate production fall [1], so the rate of upward growth drops further below the rate of sea-level rise and the reef may "give up" [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Diversity is highest on the upper fore-reef slope (46 species) [1]. It is much lower on the reef crest (9) and reef flat (14) [1]. It is lowest on the deep fore-reef slope at 35 m (6) [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** The upper fore-reef slope has plentiful light, moderate wave action and clear, oxygenated water [1]. The reef crest takes the full force of breaking waves and may be exposed at low tide, so only robust species survive [1]. The reef flat is shallow, so water overheats and salinity rises at low tide, stressing corals [1]. At 35 m, light is too weak for most zooxanthellae to photosynthesise effectively [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Overfishing removes grazing fish such as parrotfish [1]. Algae are no longer kept in check [1]. Nutrient pollution from fertiliser or sewage speeds algal growth [1]. Algae overgrow and shade coral, and occupy space that coral larvae need to settle [1]. The reef can shift to an algae-dominated state that recovers slowly even if one pressure is removed [1]. **[5]**

**10.** When water stays above the usual summer maximum for several weeks [1], the zooxanthellae's photosynthesis is damaged and they produce harmful by-products [1], so the coral expels them and turns white [1]. Without the algae the coral is starved and may die if stress continues [1]. Repeated events leave too little time for recovery between them, so cover falls with each event [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Most reefs lie between about 30°N and 30°S [1]. Diversity is greatest in the Indo-Pacific, especially the Coral Triangle [1]. There are extensive but less diverse reefs in the Caribbean [1]. Reefs are rare on tropical western coasts of continents and near large river mouths [1]. Bermuda has reefs at about 32°N [1] because the warm Gulf Stream raises sea temperature above that expected for its latitude [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* local strategies include protected areas, fishing rules, mooring buoys and runoff control [1]; global strategies mean cutting greenhouse gas emissions [1].

*Local scale effective:* no-take zones restore grazing fish, so algae are controlled [1]. Community sanctuaries such as Apo Island, Philippines, gain local support because catches and tourism income rise [1]. Mooring buoys and diver education remove anchor and trampling damage cheaply [1]. Catchment management reduces sediment and nutrients at source [1]. Results can be seen within years [1].

*Local scale limited:* protected reefs still bleach in marine heatwaves [1]. Enforcement is costly and weak in many places [1]. Starfish control and coral gardening treat symptoms over small areas [1].

*Global scale:* only emissions cuts reduce bleaching and acidification [1]. Benefits are slow because warming continues for decades [1]. It needs international agreement and is outside the control of reef nations [1].

*Interaction:* local action raises resilience, helping reefs recover between heatwaves [1]. Without global action, events may come too often for recovery [1]. So the two scales complement each other rather than compete [1].

*Judgement:* local strategies are more effective at reducing local threats [1], but global action is the only answer to the largest threat [1]. Effectiveness depends on how severe local pressures are at a given reef [1]. A reasoned final view on "how far" with named examples [1]. **[20]**
