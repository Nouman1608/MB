## Distinguishing properties

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Fixed | Takes the shape of the container | Fills the whole container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Not fixed: fills the container |
| Can it flow? | No | Yes | Yes |
| Can it be compressed? | Almost not at all | Only very slightly | Easily |
| Density | High | Usually high, a little lower than the solid | Very low |

Water is an unusual exception: ice is less dense than liquid water, which is why it floats. Most substances are densest as solids.

## Particle structure

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separation | Very close, touching | Close, mostly touching | Far apart |
| Arrangement | Regular pattern (lattice) | Random | Random |
| Motion | Vibrate about fixed positions | Move around, sliding past one another | Move quickly in straight lines, in all directions, until they collide |

Each property comes from the structure. A gas can be compressed because there is a lot of empty space between its particles. A liquid flows because its particles can move past one another. A solid keeps its shape because its particles cannot leave their positions.

When you draw or interpret particle diagrams, keep the particles the same size in all three states. Particles do not grow or shrink when a substance changes state; only their spacing and motion change.

## Changes of state

| Change | From → to | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | Solid → liquid | Taken in |
| Boiling | Liquid → gas, throughout the liquid, at the boiling point | Taken in |
| Evaporating | Liquid → gas, at the surface only, below the boiling point | Taken in |
| Freezing | Liquid → solid | Given out |
| Condensing | Gas → liquid | Given out |

A pure substance melts and freezes at the same temperature, its melting point. Boiling happens at a fixed temperature, the boiling point, for a given pressure. Evaporation can happen at any temperature at which the substance is a liquid, which is why a puddle dries on a cool day.

## Worked example 1: deducing the state from data

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| P | −95 | 69 |
| Q | 44 | 280 |
| R | −210 | −196 |
| S | −39 | 357 |

What is the state of each at 25 °C?

The rule: below the melting point the substance is solid; between the melting and boiling points it is liquid; above the boiling point it is gas.

- P: 25 °C is between −95 °C and 69 °C, so **liquid**.
- Q: 25 °C is below 44 °C, so **solid**.
- R: 25 °C is above −196 °C, so **gas**.
- S: 25 °C is between −39 °C and 357 °C, so **liquid**.

A common follow-up asks which substance is liquid over the widest range: S, because 357 − (−39) = 396 °C. Take care with negative numbers: subtracting a negative melting point adds it.

## Heating and cooling curves (Extended only)

A heating curve shows temperature against time while a substance is heated at a steady rate.

### Worked example 2: interpreting a heating curve

A solid, X, is heated from −30 °C. The temperature rises to 16 °C, stays at 16 °C for 4 minutes, rises to 118 °C, stays at 118 °C for 11 minutes, then rises again.

1. **Sections.** Rising from −30 °C: solid. At 16 °C: melting, solid and liquid together. Rising from 16 °C to 118 °C: liquid. At 118 °C: boiling, liquid and gas together. Final rise: gas.
2. **Melting point and boiling point.** 16 °C and 118 °C, read from the flat sections.
3. **Why the temperature is constant at 16 °C.** The energy supplied is used to overcome the forces of attraction between the particles, so it does not increase their kinetic energy, and the temperature does not rise until all of X has melted.
4. **Why the boiling plateau is longer.** In boiling, the particles must be separated completely, which needs much more energy than loosening them in melting. At a steady heating rate, more energy takes more time.

A cooling curve shows the same features in reverse. On cooling, the temperature stays constant while the substance condenses and again while it freezes, because energy is given out as forces of attraction form between particles. That energy balances the energy lost to the surroundings.

## Gas volume, temperature and pressure

**Describe (Core).** For a fixed mass of gas:

- at constant pressure, raising the temperature increases the volume
- at constant temperature, raising the pressure decreases the volume.

**Explain (Extended only).**

- Heating gives the particles more kinetic energy, so they move faster and hit the walls of the container more often and with more force. If the container can expand, such as a balloon, the gas pushes outwards until the pressure inside again matches the pressure outside, so the volume increases.
- Increasing the pressure on a gas pushes the particles closer together into a smaller space. Because gas particles are far apart, there is room to do this, so the volume decreases.

Words like "more often" (frequency of collisions) and "with more force" (energy of collisions) are what earn the explanation marks.

## Common mistakes

- Saying particles in a solid "do not move". They vibrate about fixed positions.
- Drawing liquid particles widely spaced. They are almost as close as in a solid.
- Saying particles expand on heating. The spaces between them change, not the particles.
- Confusing evaporation and boiling: evaporation happens at the surface, below the boiling point.
- On a heating curve, labelling a flat section with only one state; during a change of state two states are present.
- Writing "the gas gets bigger" without referring to particle speed and collisions in an explain answer.

## Exam technique

- **State**: a short answer, such as "fixed volume".
- **Describe**: say what happens or what the arrangement is, without reasons.
- **Explain**: give the reason using particles, energy and forces of attraction.
- **Deduce**: use the data given; show the comparison you made with the melting and boiling points.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a substance evaporate below its boiling point?**
Yes. Particles at the surface with enough energy can escape at any temperature while the substance is a liquid. Boiling only happens at the boiling point.

**Why can gases be compressed but liquids hardly at all?**
Gas particles are far apart with large spaces between them, so they can be pushed closer. Liquid particles are already close together.
