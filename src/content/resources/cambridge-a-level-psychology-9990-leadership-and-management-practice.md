**This practice set covers topic 4.2, Leadership and management, in Specialist Option 4 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All organisations and people are fictional.

Related: [Leadership and management study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-leadership-and-management/) · [Leadership and management revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-leadership-and-management-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline the great person theory of leadership. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between the Ohio dimensions of initiating structure and consideration. **[4]**

**3.** Outline Scouller's three levels of leadership. **[3]**

**4.** Outline **three** of Heifetz's principles for meeting adaptive challenges. **[3]**

**5.** Theories of leadership have changed over time.

**(a)** Describe universalist theories of leadership. **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate theories of leadership, including a discussion of nature versus nurture. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** A hospital trust must merge two departments that have very different ways of working. Staff are anxious and resistant.

**(a)** Explain why this is an adaptive challenge rather than a technical problem. **[2]**

**(b)** Using Heifetz's principles, suggest **two** things the leader should do. **[4]**

**7.** Cuadrado et al. (2008) used a 2 × 2 design.

**(a)** Identify the two independent variables. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the researchers used a written story rather than real managers. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** weakness of using psychology students as participants. **[2]**

**8.** Marcus questions every decision his manager makes but does very little work on team projects.

**(a)** Identify Marcus's followership style using Kelley (1988). **[1]**

**(b)** Explain your answer using Kelley's two dimensions. **[2]**

**9.** A company wants to use the Leadership Practices Inventory to assess its managers.

**(a)** Explain **one** advantage of using both the self and observer versions. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why observer ratings might be biased. **[2]**

**10.** Using the Michigan studies, suggest which type of leader would be more likely to have a productive team, and explain why. **[3]**

**11.** Distinguish between charismatic and transformational leadership. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 2 marks. Leaders are born with special traits such as intelligence, confidence and determination (1) that make them effective leaders in any situation; leadership is not learned (1).

**2.** Up to 4 marks. Initiating structure is the extent to which a leader organises and defines tasks, roles and deadlines (1), e.g. setting clear schedules and standards (1). Consideration is the extent to which a leader shows trust, respect and concern for staff (1), e.g. listening to their problems (1). Credit noting the dimensions are independent.

**3.** One mark each. Public: behaviour towards a group, such as setting a shared vision (1). Private: one-to-one leadership, such as coaching individuals (1). Personal: the inner level of self-awareness, technical know-how and attitudes, which underpins the others (1).

**4.** One mark each for any three, briefly outlined: get on the balcony, stepping back to see the whole picture (1); identify the adaptive challenge (1); regulate distress, keeping pressure at a productive level (1); maintain disciplined attention on hard issues (1); give the work back to the people (1); protect voices of leadership from below (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Universalist theories assume the same qualities make a good leader in all situations (1). Great person theory: leaders are born with special traits such as confidence and intelligence (1), identified by studying famous leaders (1). Charismatic leaders inspire through personal appeal and a compelling vision, and followers become emotionally committed (1). Transformational leaders motivate followers to put group goals above self-interest and perform beyond expectations (1), e.g. through inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation and individualised consideration (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named debate, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Nature versus nurture*: great person theory takes the nature side, implying leaders cannot be trained; behavioural theories (Ohio, Michigan) take the nurture side, implying leadership behaviours can be learned; transformational skills can be developed through training, suggesting an interaction.
- *Situational criticism*: universalist theories cannot explain why a leader succeeds in one setting but fails in another; Heifetz and Muczyk and Reimann stress the situation.
- *Gender bias*: early great person research focused on men; Cuadrado et al. suggest relationship-oriented styles are valued.
- *Method*: behavioural theories rely on questionnaires, open to bias.
- *Application*: behavioural and transformational theories support leadership training.
- *Conclusion*: modern theories combine traits, behaviour and situation.

**6.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. There is no expert solution that can simply be applied (1); staff must change their ways of working and attitudes to resolve the problem (1).

**(b)** Two marks for each principle applied. Regulate distress: acknowledge staff anxiety and introduce changes in stages so pressure motivates without overwhelming (1+1). Give the work back to the people: let staff from both departments design the new procedures together (1+1). Credit other principles if applied.

**7.**

**(a)** One mark each: sex of the leader (man or woman) (1); leadership style (stereotypically masculine or feminine) (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. A story allows everything except the leader's sex and style to be kept the same (1), so differences in ratings can be attributed to the independent variables (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. Students may have little experience of being managed in a workplace (1), so their ratings of a fictional leader may not reflect how employees judge real managers (1).

**8.**

**(a)** Alienated (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Marcus is high on independent critical thinking, questioning decisions (1), but low on active engagement, doing little work (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Comparing the two shows whether the manager's view of their behaviour matches others' views (1), revealing blind spots for development (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Staff may rate their manager favourably for fear of consequences, or harshly because of a personal conflict (1), so ratings may reflect the relationship rather than leadership practices (1).

**10.** Up to 3 marks. An employee-centred leader (1), because the Michigan studies found employee-centred leaders were associated with more productive groups than job-centred leaders (1); concern for staff may increase their commitment and effort (1).

**11.** Up to 2 marks. Charismatic leaders inspire mainly through their personal appeal, so followers are committed to the leader (1); transformational leaders also develop followers and commit them to the group's goals, which can continue beyond the leader (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 2, giving definitions without examples.
- In question 3, mixing up private and personal leadership.
- In question 7(a), giving the dependent variable instead of an independent one.
