These notes condense **3.1.3 Estimation of physical quantities** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1, but estimate questions can be set on any topic. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [estimation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-estimation-of-physical-quantities-oxfordaqa/); for exam-style questions, use the [estimation practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-estimation-of-physical-quantities-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## The specification in one box

- Estimate approximate values of physical quantities to the nearest order of magnitude.
- Use those estimates with physics to produce further derived estimates, also to the nearest order of magnitude.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Order of magnitude | The power of ten closest to a value |
| Estimate | An approximate value from stated, reasonable assumptions |
| Derived estimate | An estimate calculated from other estimates using a physics equation |
| Assumption | A value you choose and state, such as "car mass ≈ 1000 kg" |
| Sense-check | Comparing an answer with a known value to test whether it is plausible |

## The rounding rule

- The boundary between 10ⁿ and 10ⁿ⁺¹ is at **3.16 × 10ⁿ** (√10 × 10ⁿ).
- Below 3.16: round down. Above 3.16: round up.
- 3 × 10⁴ is order 10⁴; 4 × 10⁴ is order 10⁵.

## Reference values worth knowing

| Thing | Value | Thing | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adult mass | 70 kg | Car mass | 1000 kg |
| Adult height | 1.7 m | Motorway speed | 30 m s⁻¹ |
| Walking speed | 1.5 m s⁻¹ | One storey | 3 m |
| Sprint speed | 10 m s⁻¹ | Football mass | 0.4 kg |
| Water density | 1000 kg m⁻³ | Air density | 1 kg m⁻³ |
| Atom diameter | 10⁻¹⁰ m | Nucleus diameter | 10⁻¹⁵ m |
| Atmospheric pressure | 10⁵ Pa | g | 10 N kg⁻¹ |

Any value in a sensible range is credited; an absurd value is not.

## Six-step method

1. Write the physics equation.
2. State each assumed value with its unit.
3. Round inputs to 1 s.f.
4. Convert to SI units and calculate.
5. Give the answer as a power of ten (or to 1 s.f., if that is what is asked).
6. Sense-check against a known value.

## Common derived estimates

| Estimate | Physics | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| Kinetic energy of a motorway car | Ek = ½mv² | 4.5 × 10⁵ J, order 10⁶ J |
| Pressure under a standing person | p = F/A | 1.4 × 10⁴ Pa, order 10⁴ Pa |
| Force of a kick on a football | F = Δ(mv)/Δt | 10³ N |
| Gravitational potential energy gained climbing one storey | ΔEp = mgΔh | 70 × 10 × 3 = 2100 J, order 10³ J |
| Momentum of a sprinter | p = mv | 70 × 10 = 700 kg m s⁻¹, order 10³ |
| Mass of air in a room 5 m × 4 m × 3 m | m = ρV | 60 kg, order 10² kg |

## Choosing the physics for a derived estimate

| Asked to estimate | Equation to start from | Values to assume |
|---|---|---|
| Energy of something moving | Ek = ½mv² | mass, speed |
| Energy to lift something | ΔEp = mgΔh | mass, height |
| Average force in an impact | F = Δ(mv)/Δt | mass, speed change, contact time |
| Power of a person or machine | P = W/t | energy transferred, time taken |
| Mass of a substance | m = ρV | density, dimensions |

## Exam traps

- No assumptions stated: few or no method marks.
- Rounding 4.5 × 10⁵ to 10⁵ instead of 10⁶.
- Too many significant figures in an estimate.
- Not converting cm or cm² to m or m² before calculating.
- Using mass where the equation needs weight.
- Choosing an unrealistic value and not noticing the result is absurd.
- Writing "about 1000" with no route to it.
- Stating assumptions without units.
- Giving an answer to the order of magnitude when the question asked for one significant figure, or the reverse.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the order of magnitude of 2.9 × 10⁻⁴.
2. Give the order of magnitude of 6.0 × 10⁷.
3. State the value of the boundary used when rounding to an order of magnitude.
4. Estimate the weight of an adult.
5. Estimate the volume of an adult, assuming the body has about the density of water.
6. Estimate the kinetic energy of a sprinter at top speed.
7. Estimate the gravitational potential energy gained by an adult climbing to the top of a 10-storey building.
8. Estimate the number of atoms in a line across a 1 mm gap.
9. Estimate the momentum of a car on a motorway.
10. A student calculates the mass of air in a classroom as 0.12 kg. Is this sensible? Explain.
11. Why may you use g = 10 N kg⁻¹ in an estimate?
12. Which input in a kick-force estimate is the least certain, and why does it matter less than you might expect?

### Answers

1. 2.9 < 3.16, so **10⁻⁴**.
2. 6.0 > 3.16, so **10⁸**.
3. **3.16** (√10).
4. 70 × 10 = 700 N, order **10³ N**.
5. V = m/ρ = 70 ÷ 1000 = 0.07 m³, order **10⁻¹ m³**.
6. ½ × 70 × 10² = 3500 J, order **10⁴ J** (3.5 > 3.16).
7. Δh ≈ 10 × 3 = 30 m; 70 × 10 × 30 = 2.1 × 10⁴ J, order **10⁴ J**.
8. 10⁻³ ÷ 10⁻¹⁰ = **10⁷** atoms.
9. 1000 × 30 = 3 × 10⁴ kg m s⁻¹, order **10⁴ kg m s⁻¹** (3 < 3.16).
10. **No.** A room of about 100 m³ at 1 kg m⁻³ holds about 100 kg of air; a unit or prefix error is likely.
11. Other inputs are far less certain than the 2% difference between 9.81 and 10, so the order of magnitude is unaffected.
12. The **contact time**. Doubling it only halves the force, which usually leaves the order of magnitude unchanged.
