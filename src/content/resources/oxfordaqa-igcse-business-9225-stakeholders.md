This guide covers section **3.1.4 Stakeholders** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 1, Business in the real world. The specification asks you to understand what a stakeholder is and who the main stakeholders are, namely **owners, employees, customers, the local community, the government and suppliers**; to understand their main objectives, including **maximising pay for workers, minimising environmental impact on the local community and high dividend payments for owners**; to understand the impact of business activity on stakeholders; and to analyse the influence stakeholders have on a business and the **conflict between stakeholders' interests**. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business in the Real World study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-business-business-in-the-real-world/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-stakeholders-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-stakeholders-practice/).

## What is a stakeholder?

A **stakeholder** is any person or group with an interest in what a business does, or who is affected by its decisions. Some are inside the business, such as owners and employees. Others are outside, such as customers, suppliers, the local community and the government. A **shareholder** is a stakeholder who owns shares in a company, but a stakeholder does not need to own anything.

## The main stakeholders and what they want

| Stakeholder | Main objectives | How they can be affected |
|---|---|---|
| Owners (shareholders) | High profit and **high dividend payments**; growth in the value of their investment | Profit falls if costs rise; they may lose their investment if the business fails |
| Employees | **Maximising pay**, job security, safe working conditions, training and promotion | Redundancy, pay freezes, new working hours or better working conditions |
| Customers | Good quality, fair prices, reliability and good service | Price rises, product recalls, better or worse service |
| Local community | **Minimising environmental impact**, jobs, little noise, traffic or pollution, support for local life | Noise, congestion, pollution; new jobs and spending in the area |
| Government | Tax revenue, legal compliance, jobs and economic growth | Receives taxes; may have to support people who lose jobs |
| Suppliers | Regular orders, prompt payment and fair prices | Lose income if the business reduces orders or pays late |

Other stakeholders can include lenders such as banks, and pressure groups. The specification lists the six above, so know these well.

## The impact of business activity on stakeholders

A business decision rarely affects only one group.

**Worked example 1: a decision with several effects.** Orchard Foods decides to build a bigger factory. The effects on stakeholders may be:

- **Owners:** higher output could raise future profit, but the building costs reduce profit now.
- **Employees:** more jobs and possibly promotion, but more pressure to work faster.
- **Customers:** more of the product available and possibly lower prices.
- **Local community:** extra jobs and spending in local shops, but more noise and lorries.
- **Government:** more tax from higher profit and wages.
- **Suppliers:** larger orders, which raises their sales, but they may have to deliver in bigger volumes.

When answering a question, choose the stakeholders that matter most in the case and say which way each is affected, positive or negative.

## The influence stakeholders have on business

Stakeholders can influence a business in different ways. The amount of influence depends on how much power they have and how much the business needs them.

| Stakeholder | How they can influence the business |
|---|---|
| Owners | Vote on major decisions, choose the managers and decide how much profit is kept in the business |
| Employees | Work harder or less hard; leave; take strike action; use trade unions to negotiate pay |
| Customers | Stop buying; leave bad reviews; recommend the business to others |
| Local community | Protest, complain to the government and refuse to buy; influence the council to refuse permission to expand |
| Government | Pass laws, set taxes and issue licences, or give grants |
| Suppliers | Refuse to supply, raise prices or deliver late |

**Influence depends on power.** A single customer of a large supermarket has little influence, but if thousands stop buying, the supermarket will notice. A supplier who is the only source of an important part has a lot of influence over the buyer.

## Conflicts between stakeholders

The interests of stakeholders often clash, because the business has limited money and resources.

| Conflict | Stakeholders | Why they conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Wages against dividends | Employees and owners | Higher pay reduces the profit available for dividends |
| Prices against profit | Customers and owners | Lower prices please customers but reduce profit |
| Growth against community life | Owners and the local community | Expansion brings jobs but also noise, traffic and pollution |
| Cost savings against supplier payments | Owners and suppliers | Paying suppliers late or demanding lower prices helps cash flow but hurts suppliers |
| Environmental measures against costs | Community and owners | Pollution filters and cleaner processes cost money |

**Worked example 2: wages against dividends.** A company has a profit of $200,000 and a wage bill of $1,200,000 for 50 staff (an average of $24,000 each). The staff ask for a 4% pay rise.

```
Cost of pay rise = 4% x $1,200,000 = $48,000
Profit left      = $200,000 - $48,000 = $152,000
```

If the owners were planning to pay all $200,000 as dividends, a pay rise of $48,000 cuts the dividend budget to $152,000, a fall of 24% (48,000 / 200,000). The employees gain and the owners lose. The business might compromise: a smaller pay rise, a bonus linked to profit, or a pay rise phased over two years.

### Resolving conflict

A business cannot please everyone fully. It can:

- **Consult** stakeholders, explaining its reasons.
- **Compromise**, giving each group part of what it wants.
- **Prioritise** the stakeholders with the most power or the greatest effect on the long-term future of the business.
- **Look for decisions that benefit more than one group**, for example training that raises staff skills and improves product quality for customers.

Long-term success often depends on keeping the key stakeholders reasonably happy. A business that ignores the community may face protests and delays, and one that ignores staff may lose its best workers.

**Worked example 3: the cost of ignoring a stakeholder.** A bakery uses 30 delivery trips a day through a quiet street. It plans 45 trips, a rise of (45 - 30) / 30 = 50%. Residents complain to the council, which limits deliveries to 35 a day. The bakery must pay for extra drivers on a new route or lose sales. If the bakery had talked to residents first, it could have agreed times that cause little disturbance.

## Worked exam-style answer

**Question (6 marks):** Explain how a new factory might affect two stakeholders.

**Answer:** The local community may benefit from new jobs and from workers spending their wages in local shops. However, residents may suffer from extra noise and lorries, for example if daily lorry trips rise from 30 to 45 (a 50% increase), and they could complain to the council. The owners are another stakeholder. They may benefit from higher output and profit in the future and receive larger dividends, but the cost of building the factory may reduce profit now, so their dividends could be lower for a time. The business has to balance these interests, perhaps by agreeing delivery times with residents.

## Applying the ideas: three quick cases

1. **A supermarket cuts prices.** Customers gain; owners may lose profit per item but may gain from more sales; suppliers may be pushed to accept lower prices.
2. **A factory installs a pollution filter costing $45,000.** The community gains; owners bear the cost; the government may be pleased by compliance.
3. **A firm cuts 20 jobs to save costs.** Owners may gain; employees lose; the government may pay benefits; the local community loses spending power.

For each case, name the stakeholders, say whether they gain or lose, and give one reason.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "stakeholders" and "shareholders" as the same. Shareholders are one type of stakeholder.
- Giving a list of stakeholders without saying what they want or how they are affected.
- Writing that every stakeholder wants the same thing. They have different objectives, and conflicts arise.
- Saying that only owners matter. Employees, customers and the community can change what a business does.
- Describing a conflict without naming both groups and the reason for the clash.

## Exam technique for this section

**Identify** and **state** questions need a named stakeholder and objective. **Explain** questions need a stakeholder, an objective or effect, and a reason. **Analyse** questions often ask about conflict. Name two stakeholders, show what each wants, explain why one gain means the other loses and use figures from the case if given. In **evaluate** questions, weigh stakeholders by the power they have and the length of time each effect lasts, then reach a clear judgement on which group the business should put first and why.
