## What the specification asks for

You need to know mass number (A) and atomic (proton) number (Z), and be able to:

- determine the number of fundamental particles in atoms and ions using mass number, atomic number and charge
- explain the existence of isotopes
- describe the principles of a simple TOF mass spectrometer, limited to ionisation, acceleration to give all ions constant kinetic energy, ion drift, ion detection and data analysis
- interpret simple mass spectra of elements
- calculate relative atomic mass from isotopic abundance, limited to mononuclear ions.

You also need to know that mass spectrometry gives accurate relative isotopic masses and relative abundances, can identify elements, and can determine relative molecular mass.

## Counting particles

Write a nuclide as the mass number at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left, for example ⁵⁹₂₇Co.

- Protons = Z
- Neutrons = A − Z
- Electrons = Z − (charge), so a 3+ ion has three fewer electrons than protons and a 2− ion has two more.

The same rules work for ions made of several atoms: add up each atom's protons and neutrons, then adjust electrons for the overall charge.

### Worked example 1: a polyatomic ion

**Question.** Find the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in the ammonium ion ¹⁴N¹H₄⁺.

**Method.**

1. Protons: N has 7, each H has 1, so 7 + 4 × 1 = **11**.
2. Neutrons: ¹⁴N has 14 − 7 = 7; ¹H has 0, so **7**.
3. Electrons: 11 − 1 = **10**.

The ammonium ion has the same number of electrons as a neon atom. Species with the same number of electrons are called isoelectronic, and spotting them is a useful check.

## Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

Isotopes exist because the identity of an element is fixed only by its number of protons. A nucleus with a given number of protons can be stable, or long-lived, with more than one number of neutrons, so most elements occur naturally as a mixture of isotopes.

| Property | Isotopes of one element | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical reactions | the same | Same number of electrons in the same arrangement; reactions involve electrons |
| Mass of each atom | different | Different numbers of neutrons |
| Physical properties that depend on mass, such as density | slightly different | Heavier atoms in the same volume |

**Relative isotopic mass** is the mass of an atom of one isotope relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. It is very close to the mass number but not exactly equal to it, except for carbon-12 itself. In calculations at this level the mass number is used.

## The time of flight mass spectrometer

Learn the five stages in order and what happens to the particles at each one.

### 1. Ionisation

The sample is turned into gaseous positive ions. A common method fires high-energy electrons at gaseous atoms, knocking out one electron from each:

X(g) + e⁻ → X⁺(g) + 2e⁻

The ions must be positive so that the electric field can accelerate them and the detector can register them. Most ions carry a 1+ charge, so their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) equals their relative mass.

### 2. Acceleration

An electric field accelerates the positive ions so that **all of them have the same kinetic energy**. Because kinetic energy = ½mv², ions of smaller mass must be moving faster to have the same kinetic energy.

### 3. Ion drift

The ions enter a region with no electric field and travel through it at constant speed. Lighter ions cover the distance in less time; heavier ions take longer. The flight time increases with the square root of the mass, so ions of different m/z separate in time.

### 4. Ion detection

Each positive ion hits the detector and gains an electron from it. This flow of electrons creates a current. The bigger the current at a given arrival time, the more ions of that m/z there are.

### 5. Data analysis

A computer converts flight times into m/z values and currents into relative abundances, producing the mass spectrum: a plot of abundance against m/z.

### Worked example 2: an annotated explanation

**Question.** Explain why ²⁰Ne⁺ ions reach the detector before ²²Ne⁺ ions. (3 marks)

**Model answer.** "Both ions are given the same kinetic energy in the acceleration stage ✔. Because KE = ½mv², the lighter ²⁰Ne⁺ ion has a greater speed ✔. In the drift region it therefore covers the same distance in a shorter time ✔."

**Why it scores.** It names the stage, states the constant quantity correctly (kinetic energy, not speed) and links speed to time over a fixed distance.

## Reading mass spectra of elements

Each peak in the spectrum of an element is one isotope. Its m/z gives the relative isotopic mass and its height (or percentage) gives the relative abundance.

**Relative atomic mass** is the weighted mean of the isotopic masses:

Ar = Σ(m/z × abundance) ÷ Σ(abundance)

Always divide by the total abundance. When abundances are percentages the total is 100, but peak heights in arbitrary units can add up to anything.

### Worked example 3: peak heights, not percentages

**Question.** The mass spectrum of an element has peaks at m/z 85 (height 18.2) and m/z 87 (height 7.0). Calculate Ar to one decimal place and identify the element.

**Method.**

1. Total abundance = 18.2 + 7.0 = 25.2
2. Ar = (85 × 18.2 + 87 × 7.0) ÷ 25.2 = (1547 + 609) ÷ 25.2 = 2156 ÷ 25.2 = **85.6**
3. The nearest Ar in the Periodic Table is rubidium (85.5), so the element is **rubidium**.

**Sense check.** The answer lies between 85 and 87 and closer to 85, the larger peak.

### Identifying elements and finding relative molecular mass

- **Identify an element** by calculating Ar from its spectrum and matching the value to the Periodic Table.
- **Find relative molecular mass** from the spectrum of a molecular compound: the molecular ion, M⁺, formed when a molecule loses one electron, gives the peak with the largest m/z (ignoring tiny peaks caused by heavier isotopes), and its m/z equals Mr. Calculations of Ar from abundance data, however, are limited to mononuclear ions: ions containing just one atom.

## Common mistakes

- Adding the charge to the number of electrons for a positive ion instead of subtracting.
- Defining isotopes as "atoms with different masses" without stating same protons, different neutrons.
- Saying isotopes have different chemical properties.
- Writing "all ions are accelerated to the same speed". The constant quantity is kinetic energy.
- Saying the detector "counts" ions without mentioning that each ion gains an electron and produces a current.
- Dividing by 100 when the peak heights do not add up to 100.
- Giving Ar to more decimal places than the data justify, or rounding to a whole number when the question asks for one decimal place.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Define**: give the exact meaning, for example isotopes "have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons".
- **Deduce**: use the data in the question; show protons, neutrons and electrons separately.
- **Describe**: for the TOF, say what happens at each named stage, in order.
- **Explain**: link cause to effect; for flight times, kinetic energy leads to speed, which leads to time.
- **Calculate**: show the expression with the data substituted before the answer, and give the precision asked for.
- **Identify**: name the element, then justify it by comparing your Ar with the Periodic Table value.
