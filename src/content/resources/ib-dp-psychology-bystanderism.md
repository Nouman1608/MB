This guide covers **"bystanderism"**, a content point in the "social responsibility" topic of the psychology of human relationships option in IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2019). The [human relationships study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-of-human-relationships-study-guide/) describes Darley and Latané's intercom study and Piliavin's subway study. This page goes further: each stage of the decision model with its own evidence, the three social processes that inhibit helping, the social identity explanation, and research suggesting that the bystander effect is weaker in real, dangerous emergencies than the classic studies imply. The course described here has its last examination session in November 2026.

## Key ideas

- **Bystanderism** refers to the behaviour of people who witness an emergency or someone in need: whether, how quickly and why they help.
- The **bystander effect** is the finding that a person is less likely to help, or slower to help, when other bystanders are present.

The bystander effect is a finding to be explained, not an explanation in itself. In an essay, always say which process accounts for it.

## The decision model, stage by stage

Latané and Darley proposed that a bystander must pass through five decisions before helping. A "no" at any stage means no help. Each stage can be linked to research:

| Stage | Question for the bystander | How other people interfere | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Notice | Have I noticed the event? | people in groups attend to each other or are distracted | busy, crowded settings reduce noticing |
| 2. Interpret | Is this an emergency? | others' calm behaviour suggests it is not | Latané and Darley (1968), smoke-filled room |
| 3. Responsibility | Is it my job to act? | responsibility is shared among everyone present | Darley and Latané (1968), seizure over intercom |
| 4. Decide how | Do I know what to do? | someone else may seem more competent | people with relevant training, such as first aid, are more likely to act |
| 5. Act | Will I actually help? | fear of embarrassment in front of others | audience inhibition |

Using the table, you can turn a descriptive answer into an analytical one: the model explains *where* in the process helping fails.

## Three inhibiting processes

**1. Diffusion of responsibility (stage 3).** When several people witness an emergency, each feels less personal responsibility. In the intercom study, participants who believed they were the only person hearing a fellow student's seizure were far more likely to seek help, and faster, than those who believed several others could hear.

**2. Pluralistic ignorance (stage 2).** In an ambiguous situation, each person looks at others for information. If everyone appears calm, each concludes there is no emergency, although each may privately be worried. In **Latané and Darley's (1968) smoke-filled room study**, male students filled in a questionnaire while smoke poured into the room through a vent. Most participants who were alone left to report the smoke. When the participant sat with two confederates who had been told to ignore the smoke, very few reported it, and groups of three genuine participants also reported it less often than people alone. Afterwards, non-reporting participants gave explanations that made the smoke harmless, such as steam or air-conditioning vapour.

**3. Audience inhibition, or evaluation apprehension (stage 5).** People fear looking foolish by overreacting, or being judged if they help badly.

**The effect of relationships between bystanders.** In **Latané and Rodin's (1969)** study, participants heard a female researcher fall and cry out in pain in the next room. Participants alone helped most often; those with a passive confederate helped least. Pairs of friends helped more often than pairs of strangers, perhaps because friends feel less embarrassed in front of each other and can talk about what is happening. This suggests the effect depends on who the other bystanders are, not just on how many there are.

## The arousal: cost-reward model

Piliavin and colleagues developed the **arousal: cost-reward model** to explain helping in field settings. Seeing someone in distress causes unpleasant arousal, which the bystander is motivated to reduce. The bystander weighs:

- the **costs of helping**: effort, danger, embarrassment, time;
- the **costs of not helping**: guilt, blame and the continued distress of watching.

Helping is most likely when the costs of helping are low and the costs of not helping are high. This explains why the victim's appearance mattered in the subway study: helping a person who appears drunk may seem more costly.

## Social identity and helping: Levine et al. (2005)

**Levine, Prosser, Evans and Reicher (2005)** applied social identity theory. In the first study, Manchester United supporters completed questionnaires that made their club identity salient, then walked to another building. On the way, a jogger (a confederate) fell and appeared hurt. He wore a Manchester United shirt, a Liverpool shirt or an unbranded shirt. Participants helped the jogger in a Manchester United shirt more often than in the other shirts.

In the second study, the questionnaires emphasised participants' identity as **football fans** in general. This time the jogger was helped more often in either club's shirt than in an unbranded shirt.

**What it shows.** Helping depends on whether the victim is seen as a member of the bystander's in-group, and the boundary of that in-group can shift. It supports a social identity explanation of helping and suggests that bystanders' group memberships, not just their number, matter.

**Evaluation.** A field setting gives realism, and the manipulation of identity is clever. However, the samples were small and all male, football supporters are a particular group, and the emergency was not severe.

## Is the bystander effect weaker than it seems?

Two pieces of evidence challenge the classic picture:

- **Fischer et al. (2011)** carried out a meta-analysis of bystander research. The bystander effect was reduced in dangerous emergencies, for example when a perpetrator was present. The researchers suggested that dangerous situations are recognised as emergencies more quickly and produce higher arousal, and that other bystanders can be a source of physical support.
- **Philpot et al. (2020)** analysed CCTV recordings of real public conflicts in Lancaster in the UK, Amsterdam in the Netherlands and Cape Town in South Africa. In nine out of ten incidents, at least one bystander intervened, and the chance that someone intervened rose as the number of bystanders rose. The pattern was similar in all three cities.

These findings do not contradict Darley and Latané: an *individual* may be less likely to help in a crowd, while the chance that *someone* helps can still rise with the number of people present. That distinction is a strong point for a top-band essay.

## Worked example: planning a 22-mark essay

**Question:** "Discuss factors influencing bystanderism."

1. **Focus:** define bystanderism and the bystander effect; state that several factors explain whether bystanders help.
2. **Factor 1, situational ambiguity and pluralistic ignorance:** Latané and Darley (1968), smoke-filled room; evaluation of laboratory realism and deception.
3. **Factor 2, relationships and identity:** Latané and Rodin (1969) on friends and strangers; Levine et al. (2005) on shared identity; evaluation of all-male and specific samples.
4. **Factor 3, danger and costs:** the arousal: cost-reward model; Fischer et al. (2011).
5. **Counter-evidence:** Philpot et al. (2020), with the individual versus group distinction.
6. **Conclusion:** the number of bystanders matters less than how the situation is interpreted, how bystanders relate to one another and to the victim, and how dangerous the emergency is.

## Common mistakes

- Using "the bystander effect" as if it explains itself.
- Describing the intercom study again without linking it to a stage of the model.
- Ignoring the ethical issues: deception, distress and lack of consent in field studies.
- Treating the bystander effect as universal, despite meta-analytic and CCTV evidence.
- Confusing diffusion of responsibility (who should act) with pluralistic ignorance (is this an emergency).

## Exam technique

**Discuss** needs several factors, each supported and evaluated, with a conclusion. **Evaluate** asks for a judgement of the evidence for one explanation. **Contrast** two explanations, such as the decision model and the social identity account, by referring to both throughout. Give each study a purpose in your argument and end each paragraph with what it shows about why bystanders do or do not help.
