## Section A

**1.** State the relative order of the expansion of solids, liquids and gases for the same temperature rise, and explain, in terms of particles, why gases expand the most. **[3]**

**2.** Convert **(a)** 25 °C into kelvin and **(b)** 77 K into degrees Celsius. **[2]**

**3.** Define specific heat capacity. **[2]**

**4.** Describe what happens to the particles of an object when its temperature increases. **[2]**

**5.** State the melting point and the boiling point of water at standard atmospheric pressure. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain how a liquid-in-glass thermometer works and why it has a very narrow tube. **[3]**

**7.** Steel railway tracks are laid with small gaps between sections. Explain why. **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the energy needed to raise the temperature of 0.80 kg of water from 15 °C to 40 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg °C). **[2]**

**9.** 0.50 kg of oil with a specific heat capacity of 2000 J/(kg °C) is at 20 °C. It absorbs 15 000 J of energy. Calculate its final temperature. **[3]**

**10.** A 40 W heater is placed in a 0.90 kg aluminium block for 8.0 minutes. The temperature of the block rises by 21 °C.

**(a)** Calculate the energy supplied by the heater. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium from these results. **[2]**
**(c)** The accepted value is about 900 J/(kg °C). Explain why the experimental value is higher, and suggest **one** improvement. **[2]**

## Section C

**11.** Ice at −10 °C is heated steadily until it becomes steam at 110 °C. Describe the graph of temperature against time and explain the flat sections in terms of energy and particles. **[4]**

**12.** State **three** differences between boiling and evaporation. **[3]**

**13.** A wet towel dries faster on a warm, windy day when it is spread out on a line. Explain this in terms of particles, referring to all three factors. **[4]**

**14.** Explain why sweating helps to cool the body. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Gases expand most, then liquids, then solids least [1]. Heated particles gain kinetic energy and move further apart [1]; in a gas the forces between particles are negligible, so nothing restricts their separation, whereas strong forces hold solid particles in place [1].

**2. (a)** 25 + 273 = **298 K** [1]. **(b)** 77 − 273 = **−196 °C** [1].

**3.** The energy required per **unit mass** [1] per **unit temperature increase** [1].

**4.** The particles gain kinetic energy: their **average kinetic energy increases** [1], so they move or vibrate faster, and the object's **internal energy increases** [1].

**5.** Melting point **0 °C** [1]; boiling point **100 °C** [1].

**6.** When warmed, the liquid in the bulb **expands** [1]; it expands **more than the glass**, so it is forced up the tube [1]. The **narrow tube** means a small increase in volume produces a large rise in length, so small temperature changes can be read [1].

**7.** On hot days the steel **expands** and the sections become longer [1]; the gaps leave room for this, so the tracks do not **buckle** [1].

**8.** ΔE = mcΔθ = 0.80 × 4200 × 25 [1] = **84 000 J** [1].

**9.** Δθ = ΔE ÷ (mc) = 15 000 ÷ (0.50 × 2000) [1] = 15 °C [1]; final temperature = 20 + 15 = **35 °C** [1].

**10. (a)** ΔE = P × t = 40 × 480 [1] = **19 200 J** [1].
**(b)** c = ΔE ÷ (mΔθ) = 19 200 ÷ (0.90 × 21) [1] = **1020 J/(kg °C)** (to 3 s.f.) [1].
**(c)** Some energy is **lost to the surroundings**, so the measured temperature rise is smaller than it would be with no losses, giving a larger value of c [1]. Improvement: **insulate the block**, or put oil in the thermometer hole for better thermal contact [1].

**11.** The graph rises from −10 °C to **0 °C**, is **flat** at 0 °C, rises to **100 °C**, is **flat** at 100 °C, then rises again [1]. On the flat sections the substance is **changing state** (melting, then boiling) [1]; energy is still being supplied [1], but it does work against the **forces between particles**, separating them, rather than increasing their kinetic energy, so the temperature stays constant [1].

**12.** Any three, 1 mark each: evaporation happens **at any temperature**, boiling only **at the boiling point**; evaporation occurs **only at the surface**, boiling **throughout the liquid** with bubbles; evaporation is **slow**, boiling is **rapid**; evaporation **cools** the remaining liquid, while a boiling liquid stays at the boiling point.

**13.** Evaporation is the escape of **more energetic particles** from the water's surface [1]. Warmth: more particles have enough energy to escape [1]. Spreading out: a **larger surface area**, so more particles are at the surface [1]. Wind: escaped particles are **carried away** before they can re-enter the water [1].

**14.** Sweat **evaporates** from the skin [1]. The **most energetic** water particles escape [1], so the average kinetic energy of the remaining liquid falls; it cools, and draws energy from the skin [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a final temperature instead of the temperature change in ΔE = mcΔθ.
- Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds before using E = Pt.
- Writing that particles themselves expand.
- Saying temperature rises while a substance melts or boils.
- Explaining evaporative cooling without the idea that the faster particles leave.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these questions from past papers?**
No. They are original practice questions for subtopic 2.2 and do not copy the exam's structure, question count or mark tariffs.

**Do I need to remember the specific heat capacity of water?**
Questions normally give it. Knowing that it is about 4200 J/(kg °C) helps you check whether an answer is sensible.

**Will I be asked to calculate latent heat?**
The 5054 syllabus asks you to describe and explain latent heat in terms of particles, not to calculate it.
