## Section A (practise using your own studied modern text)

**1.** Starting with a key extract of your choice, explore how the writer presents power (or another central theme) in your studied modern prose or drama text as a whole. **[30]**

**1b.** (Alternative Section A question) Starting with a key extract of your choice, explore how the writer presents a central relationship in your studied modern prose or drama text as a whole. **[30]**

## Section B (practise using your own studied poetry cluster)

**2.** Choose a poem from your studied anthology cluster that deals centrally with power or conflict, and compare how it and **one other poem** from the same cluster present this theme through their use of structure and language. **[26]**

**3.** (Alternative Section B question) Choose a poem from your studied anthology cluster that deals centrally with loss or separation, and compare how it and **one other poem** from the same cluster present this theme, focusing on structure as well as content. **[26]**

## Section C (unseen poetry)

Read the two original poems below, then answer both questions.

**The House After**

The kettle's cold. The chairs still face
the window like they're waiting still
for someone's key to turn the lock,
a voice to fill the empty hall.

Dust settles soft on unwashed cups,
the calendar stopped mid-July.
A coat hangs where it always hung —
too heavy now for me to move.

I leave the porch light on at night,
though no one's coming home to it.
The house forgets him slower than
the street outside forgets his name.

**Tideline**

The sea takes back what it once gave —
a shell, a shoe, a child's red pail —
and leaves them scattered on the sand
like sentences it can't quite finish.

Each wave arrives with such conviction,
certain this time it will stay,
then loses nerve against the shore
and drags its confidence away.

I used to think the tide was leaving.
Now I know it always comes again —
not the same wave, never quite,
but close enough to call it home.

**4.** In *The House After*, how does the poet present the speaker's experience of loss? **[24]**

**5.** Compare how the poets present ideas about permanence and change in *The House After* and *Tideline*. **[8]**

## Answers (worked approach — marks for Q1–3 depend on your studied text and cluster)

**1.** A top-band answer opens with **close analysis of the given extract** — how the writer's language, form and structure present power at that specific moment [10] — then extends outward to the **whole text**, tracing further key moments where power is exercised, resisted or lost, using precise, memorised textual evidence [14]. Throughout, the strongest responses weave in **social and historical context** relevant specifically to why the writer presents power this way (for a drama text, this may include staging or performance choices), rather than adding context as a disconnected final paragraph [6].

**1b.** As with Q1, a strong answer begins with the **extract itself**, examining what it reveals about the relationship at that specific point [10], then tracks how the relationship **develops across the whole text**, using precise evidence from at least two further moments [14], with relevant context integrated throughout to explain *why* the relationship changes as it does [6].

**2.** A strong response identifies the chosen poem's **method** for presenting power or conflict — form, structure, and specific language choices — before turning to the second poem [10], then builds a genuine **comparison** rather than two separate descriptions: using connectives ("similarly," "in contrast," "whereas") from the opening paragraph onward, and comparing structure and form as well as theme [12]. The strongest answers select the second poem specifically because it offers a clear point of connection or contrast worth exploring, decided in advance rather than under exam pressure [4].

**3.** As with Q2, a strong response opens by identifying how the first poem's **structure and language** present loss or separation [10], then compares this against the second poem using active comparison throughout rather than saved for a conclusion [12], with a brief closing judgement on which poem's method the candidate finds more effective and why [4].

**4.** A strong answer notices that the poem presents loss **indirectly**, through domestic detail rather than direct statement of grief — the cold kettle, the unwashed cups, the stopped calendar all suggest time frozen at the point of loss [1] [8]. The **porch light left on** is a key symbolic detail, showing the speaker's inability to accept the loss is final, and could be compared with the coat "too heavy now for me to move," which suggests both physical and emotional inability to let go [8]. The final line — "the house forgets him slower than / the street outside forgets his name" — personifies the house as sharing the speaker's grief, and a top-band answer will comment on how this shifts the poem's focus from the speaker alone to a shared, lingering absence, and on the effect of the enjambment across these closing lines, which delays and softens the poem's final admission [7].

**5.** *The House After* presents permanence through domestic objects that resist change — the frozen calendar, the immovable coat — suggesting the speaker experiences change as something imposed and resisted [1] [3]. *Tideline* presents the opposite dynamic: change is presented as constant and inevitable ("the tide always comes again"), and the poem's final stanza reframes what first seemed like loss (things being "taken back") as a repeating, almost comforting pattern [1] [3]. A strong comparative answer notes that both poems use a concrete, physical image (a house; a shoreline) to explore an abstract idea, but reach different conclusions — one poem's speaker resists change, the other's comes to accept it — which could be linked to each poem's structure: the settled, three-line stanzas of *The House After* versus the shifting, more uncertain rhythm of *Tideline*'s final stanza [1].

## Building an extract-proof revision approach for Section A

Because you cannot predict which extract will appear in the exam, revision should focus on applying
your knowledge to *any* extract from your studied text, not on memorising a response to one specific
passage. Practise this by choosing several short, unfamiliar extracts from your own modern text and
timing yourself writing a brief plan (5 minutes) identifying: what the extract reveals in isolation,
which whole-text moments it connects to, and which pieces of context are genuinely relevant. This
mirrors the actual exam skill far more closely than rehearsing a single polished essay on a
pre-chosen passage.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Extract analysis in Section A that doesn't connect outward to the whole text.
- Section B comparisons that address theme only, ignoring the structure and form AQA credits equally.
- Choosing a second poem for Section B under exam pressure rather than deciding on a pairing in advance.
- Section C answers that describe what an unseen poem is about without analysing how language and structure create that effect.
- Running short on time for Section C because it feels the least prepared-for, despite carrying the same weight as Sections A and B.
- Adding social or historical context as a bolt-on final paragraph instead of integrating it throughout the answer.

## Approaching unseen poetry under exam pressure

The two questions above follow the real Section C structure: a longer single-poem analysis question,
then a shorter comparison question worth roughly a third of the marks. On the single-poem question,
resist the urge to summarise what happens in the poem — instead, work through what the poet's
specific choices of language, form and structure *do*, using brief, precise quotation rather than
paraphrase. On the comparison question, decide quickly what the two poems share and where they
differ before writing a single word, since a clear comparative plan in the first two minutes produces
a far more focused answer than discovering the comparison as you write. Practising this two-question
structure regularly, on poems you have genuinely never seen before, is the only preparation that
transfers directly to the exam.
