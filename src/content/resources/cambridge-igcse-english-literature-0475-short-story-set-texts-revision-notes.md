**These notes cover the short-story option in Section B (Prose) of Paper 1.** The 2026 syllabus
lists a selection of stories "From Stories of Ourselves Volume 2, the following 10 stories", with
"passages from these stories in the paper … printed as in this text". They condense the
[short stories study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-short-story-set-texts/)
and add to the general [Section B: Prose revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-section-b-prose-revision-notes/),
which cover narrative terms shared with novels. The stories set can change each year, so confirm the
list for your series.

## The task in brief

- The selection of stories counts as **one** prose set text; you answer **one** question on it.
- There is a **choice of two questions** on the text; read which story each question names.
- Passages are printed on the paper; the book is **not** allowed in the exam room.
- The answer is worth **25 marks** and tests AO1 to AO4 equally.

## What makes short fiction different

| Feature | What it means | What to do in an answer |
|---|---|---|
| Compression | One situation, few characters, short time span | Treat single details as significant; explain why the writer chose them |
| Turning point | The moment the situation or a character's understanding changes | Locate the passage before or after it and say so |
| Epiphany | A sudden realisation by a character | Show how language marks the moment (pace, imagery, short sentences) |
| Withholding | Information implied, not stated | Explain what the reader infers and from which clues |
| Open ending | The story stops without resolving the situation | Discuss what is left uncertain and why |
| Twist or reversal | The ending overturns expectations | Show how earlier details prepared for it |
| Circular structure | The ending returns to an opening image | Explain how its meaning has changed |
| Framing narrative | A story told within another story or by an older narrator | Comment on the distance between the teller and the events |
| Title as symbol | The title names an object or phrase with wider meaning | Track the object through the story |

## The story card

Make one card per story, and test yourself on each separately:

- **Voice:** who narrates; first or third person; reliable or not.
- **Situation:** one sentence on who wants what.
- **Shape:** opening, turning point, ending.
- **Setting:** where and when, and the atmosphere it creates.
- **Central idea:** "The writer shows that…".
- **Quotations:** five short ones: opening, turning point, ending, and two showing style.

Ten cards are easier to keep apart than one long set of notes covering all ten stories.

## Placing a printed passage

Because you know the whole story, place a printed passage precisely:

1. What has just happened, in one sentence.
2. Whether the passage comes **before, at or after** the turning point.
3. What the reader knows here that a character does not, or the reverse.
4. How the passage prepares for the ending.

Keep this to two or three sentences in the introduction; the rest of the answer analyses the passage
itself.

## Analysing implication

Most short-story questions reward reading between the lines. Use this pattern:

- **Clue:** quote the detail ("as if someone might need it").
- **Inference:** what it implies about feeling or situation.
- **Method:** how the writer creates that implication (an object, a gesture, silence in dialogue).
- **Effect:** what the reader feels because it is left unsaid.

## Exam traps

- **Wrong story.** A quotation from the right writer but the wrong story still costs AO1 credit.
- **Comparing stories when not asked.** Stay on the story or stories the question names.
- **Full retelling.** A short story is easy to summarise from start to finish; select instead.
- **Ignoring the title.** It is often the central symbol.
- **Missing the turning point.** Without it, the answer cannot explain what the story is about.
- **No view on the ending.** In a short story the ending usually decides the meaning.
- **Treating the narrator as the writer.** Write "the narrator" or "the speaker" unless the story is
  clearly autobiographical.

## Quick technique for the wider question

When no passage is printed:

1. Open with a two-sentence answer that names the story and your main view.
2. Follow the story's shape: opening situation, build-up, turning point, ending.
3. Use two or three short quotations from memory per section.
4. Finish by explaining how the ending changes your reading of the key word.

## Self-test

1. How many set texts does the short-story selection count as in Section B?
2. Can you take the anthology into the Paper 1 exam?
3. What is a turning point in a short story?
4. Define "epiphany".
5. What does "withholding" mean in short fiction, and how should you write about it?
6. Name three kinds of ending a short story may have.
7. Why is a story's title often worth discussing?
8. What six things belong on a story card?
9. In an introduction to a passage question, what should you say about where the passage falls?
10. A candidate quotes accurately but from a different story in the selection. Which assessment
    objective suffers most?
11. Why should you refer to a story's ending even when the printed passage comes earlier?
12. In the original line "He set two plates on the table, then put one back in the cupboard", what
    does the writer imply, and how?

**Answers:** 1. One set text. 2. No; relevant passages are printed on the paper. 3. The moment at
which the situation or a character's understanding changes, which the story builds towards.
4. A sudden realisation in which a character sees their situation differently. 5. Leaving
information unstated so the reader must infer it; quote the clue, state the inference, name the
method and explain the effect. 6. Any three: resolved; open; twist or reversal; circular, returning
to an opening image. 7. It often names the central object or idea, and its meaning may change by the
end. 8. Voice; situation; shape (opening, turning point, ending); setting; central idea; five short
quotations. 9. Whether it comes before, at or after the turning point, and what the reader knows at
that moment. 10. AO1, knowledge of the content of the text. 11. Because the ending shapes the meaning
of the whole story, so it explains why the earlier moment matters. 12. That someone he used to eat with
is gone, and that he forgot for a moment; the writer implies it through a small action and its
reversal, leaving his grief unspoken.
