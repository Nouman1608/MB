This guide covers one route through Unit 4 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level English Language (9670) specification, Version 5.2: **3.4.1 Written unit**, also called Unit 4a. The other route, a non-exam language investigation, is covered in the [non-exam assessment guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-9670-non-exam-assessment-nea/). Unit 4 is assessed at International A-level only, and a student takes one route or the other.

The written unit asks you to do, in an exam room, the thinking that a small piece of research involves. You are given data, you decide what to find out, you analyse, and you report. Condense this guide with the [written unit revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-9670-written-unit-revision-notes/) and try the [practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-9670-written-unit-practice/).

## What the specification says about the written unit

| Feature | What the specification states |
|---|---|
| Format | Written exam of 2 hours 30 minutes; 50 marks; 30% of the International A-level |
| Structure | There are no sections. You answer one question, from a choice of two |
| Data | Each question gives a different set of data, drawn from spoken, written and multimodal sources. It may include lists of language items such as book titles, shop names or terms of address, as well as longer texts such as advertising, news articles or speech extracts |
| Link | The material is linked by a common topic or theme |
| Limit | No task involves more than three texts or data sets |
| Task | Devise a research question to suit the data, using some or all of it |
| Analysis | Plan and carry out an analysis using at least two of the texts or data sets |
| Output | Write up the results as a report, following a suggested structure |

The specification describes the unit as building on the data analysis skills that are integral to the International AS qualification.

The report structure suggested in the specification has four parts:

1. **Aim(s):** a statement about the aim or aims of the investigation.
2. **Method:** an explanation of which data has been selected and why, and how the data is to be analysed.
3. **Analysis:** a presentation of findings, categorised as appropriate to the aims and explored in detail.
4. **Conclusion:** conclusions drawn from the analysis and connected with the aims.

## What is being assessed

The unit is described as synoptic. It tests all the skills that the specification aims to develop:

- skills in critical thinking;
- planning and organisational skills;
- analytical skills;
- knowledge of linguistic methods, concepts and issues;
- data-handling skills;
- skills of academic argument and report-writing.

The three assessment objectives for the course are AO1 (understanding of methods, terminology, concepts and issues in English language), AO2 (analysing how meanings are shaped in a range of texts and data) and AO3 (the ability to use English in different ways). For Unit 4 the specification gives weightings of about 12%, 12% and 6% of the whole qualification. Since the unit is worth 30% overall, those figures work out to about 40%, 40% and 20% of the marks for the unit. The marking criteria published in the specification for the non-exam route use a split of 20, 20 and 10 marks out of 50. The mark scheme for the exam route is published separately by OxfordAQA, so check it for the exact wording.

In practice this means:

- **AO1** rewards a clear aim, appropriate use of methods and frameworks, and accurate terminology.
- **AO2** rewards close analysis of the actual data, showing how language choices create meanings and how context affects use.
- **AO3** rewards a well-organised academic report, with a suitable register and accurate writing.

## From data to research question

The first marks are earned by the question you set yourself. A good research question is:

- **specific**, so that it can be answered in one report;
- **suited to the data**, so that the data can answer it;
- **answerable with at least two** of the supplied texts or data sets.

The specification's own prompts for investigations, listed in the description of the non-exam route, are useful here because they apply equally well to supplied data. They are questions about genre (what are the features of this type of language?), function (what is the language used to do?), attitudes (how do people feel about this language?) and users (who uses it?).

### Worked example 1: choosing a question

Imagine a question gives three data sets on the theme of food and drink businesses.

- **Data set 1:** a list of 12 names of independent cafés in one town (for example *Crumb and Co*, *Bean There*, *The Daily Grind*, *Kettle and Cup*).
- **Data set 2:** a short transcript of a customer ordering at a café counter.
- **Data set 3:** a 70-word extract of a menu introduction from a café chain.

Possible questions, and how they fit:

1. *How do café names and a chain's menu introduction present the businesses as friendly and local?* Uses sets 1 and 3. Focus: lexis, semantics, pragmatics.
2. *How do customers and staff manage politeness when ordering in the café?* Uses set 2 only. This breaks the rule of at least two data sets, so it is not enough on its own.
3. *What language strategies do small and large cafés use to build a relationship with customers, in names, spoken service and written text?* Uses all three. It is broad, but the report must stay selective.

Question 1 is a good choice: it is focused, uses two sets, and the data suit it. Question 3 is possible if you focus on a few categories. Question 2 needs a second set to be added.

## Planning in the exam

A plan helps because the report needs a clear line of argument. A plan in five steps:

1. **Read all the data once** without writing.
2. **Notice patterns**: repeated structures, words, tones, features of speech or layout.
3. **Choose a question** that those patterns can answer.
4. **Choose your frameworks**, the language levels: phonetics and phonology, lexis and semantics, grammar, pragmatics, discourse and graphology.
5. **Decide the analysis categories**, such as "naming strategies" or "address and politeness", and select examples for each.

Marlbridge suggests spending about 25 to 30 minutes on reading, choosing and planning in a 2 hour 30 minute paper. This is a suggestion, not a requirement in the specification.

## Writing the report

### Aim(s)

State the question and what you are trying to find out. Keep it to a few sentences. Mention which data sets you will use.

> *Model aim:* The aim of this investigation is to find out how independent café names (data set 1) and the menu introduction of a café chain (data set 3) use language to present the businesses as friendly and local, and to compare the strategies used.

### Method

The specification asks for an explanation of **which data has been selected and why**, and **how it is to be analysed**.

> *Model method:* Data sets 1 and 3 were chosen because both present a business to the public, so they share a purpose of attracting customers. Data set 2 was not used because it shows spoken service, which is a different kind of language use. Each name was analysed for lexis and semantics, such as wordplay and informal words, and for graphology where relevant. The menu introduction was analysed for lexis, grammar (such as the pronoun "we") and discourse (the way a relationship is built through direct address). The results were then compared.

### Analysis

This is the largest part. Organise it by findings, not by data set. Each paragraph should have the pattern: **claim, example, linguistic term, effect, link to the aim**.

> *Model paragraph:* Several café names use wordplay on common phrases, such as *Bean There* (a pun on "been there") and *The Daily Grind*. Puns depend on shared knowledge of idiomatic English, so they treat the customer as an insider who will recognise the joke. This pragmatic effect creates a friendly, informal tone, which fits the aim of presenting the business as approachable. By contrast, the chain's introduction, "we've been getting to know your neighbourhood for over ten years", uses the inclusive pronouns "we" and "your" to simulate a personal relationship. The two strategies differ: the independents build familiarity through wordplay and local reference, while the chain builds it through address and a narrative of time spent in the area.

Notice what the paragraph does not do. It does not simply list features. Each feature is tied to an effect and to the aim, and a comparison is made.

### Conclusion

Return to the aim. State what you found, make clear whether the data supports the aim, and acknowledge a limit. A short conclusion with a modest claim is better than a large claim from small data.

> *Model conclusion:* The data suggest that both kinds of business present themselves as friendly and local, but by different means: wordplay and informal naming in the independents, and personal address in the chain. This is a small sample of twelve names and one text, so it cannot show that these strategies are typical of all cafés.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a question that needs data you do not have.
- Using only one data set, or giving a second set only a sentence.
- Describing the content of the texts instead of analysing language.
- Listing features without effects ("there are puns and the word 'we'").
- Writing a long method that describes what you will do in general terms without saying why the data were chosen.
- Naming a theory or study in the conclusion that the analysis has not used.
- Making claims about all speakers or all businesses from a small sample.
- Running out of time before writing the conclusion.

## Exam technique

- **Name the question in your first sentence.**
- **Reference the data precisely**, for example "data set 1, the name *Bean There*".
- **Use the vocabulary of the language levels** and define unusual terms.
- **Keep to the four headings**. The report format is part of AO3.
- **Leave two minutes to check the report**: aim answered, two data sets used, a conclusion present.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to use all the data?**
No. The task says "some or all". You must use at least two of the texts or data sets.

**Can I use language theories from the other units?**
Yes. The unit rewards knowledge of linguistic methods, concepts and issues, so relevant concepts from the rest of the course can strengthen the analysis. Use them only when they help to answer the question.

**How is this different from the non-exam route?**
In the exam you work on supplied data and devise a question to fit it. In the non-exam route you devise a question, collect your own data and write a longer report. The skills and assessment objectives are the same.
