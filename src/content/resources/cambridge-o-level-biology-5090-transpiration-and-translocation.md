## Transpiration

**Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the leaves of a plant.** Almost all of it takes place through the stomata.

### How it happens

1. Water reaches the leaf in the xylem and enters the mesophyll cells.
2. Water **evaporates** from the wet surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the **air spaces** inside the leaf.
3. The air spaces become saturated with water vapour. The air outside is usually drier, so water vapour **diffuses** down the concentration gradient out of the leaf **through the open stomata**.

Transpiration seems wasteful, but it has benefits: it keeps a stream of water and mineral ions moving up the plant, it cools the leaf as water evaporates, and it supplies water for the cells. The stomata must be open for carbon dioxide to enter, so some water loss cannot be avoided.

### Factors that affect the rate

Transpiration rate depends on how steep the water vapour concentration gradient is between the air spaces and the air outside, and on how fast the water vapour is removed.

| Factor | Change | Effect on rate | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind speed | Increase | Increases | Wind carries away water vapour near the leaf, so the gradient stays steep |
| Temperature | Increase | Increases | Water evaporates faster; vapour molecules have more kinetic energy and diffuse faster |
| Humidity | Increase | Decreases | The air outside holds more water vapour, so the concentration gradient is less steep |
| Light intensity | Increase | Increases | Stomata open wider in the light, giving more routes for diffusion; light also warms the leaf |

Learn the explanation for each. "Increases" with no reason earns only the effect mark.

### Wilting

A plant **wilts** when it loses water by transpiration faster than its roots can take water in. The cells lose water and their **turgor pressure falls**. The cells become flaccid and the stems and leaves lose their support and droop. Closing the stomata, which happens in wilted plants, cuts water loss and helps the plant to recover. Watering the plant restores turgor.

## Investigating transpiration: the potometer

A **potometer** measures the rate of water uptake of a cut shoot, which is used as an estimate of the rate of transpiration. The shoot is sealed into a tube connected to a capillary tube containing an air bubble. As the shoot takes up water, the bubble moves along the scale.

**Method**

1. Cut the shoot under water, and fit it into the apparatus under water so that no air enters the xylem.
2. Seal all joints with grease or petroleum jelly.
3. Dry the leaves, and introduce an air bubble into the capillary tube.
4. Record the position of the bubble, wait a set time (for example 5 minutes), and record the distance moved.
5. Repeat to find a mean. Then change **one** factor, such as setting up a fan for wind speed, a lamp for light, or a warm room for temperature. Keep the others constant.

**Calculation**: rate = distance moved by the bubble divided by time.

### Worked example

*In still air, the bubble moved 36 mm in 6 minutes. With a fan blowing on the leaves, it moved 84 mm in 6 minutes. The capillary tube has a radius of 0.5 mm.*

1. Rate in still air = 36 divided by 6 = **6 mm per minute**.
2. Rate with the fan = 84 divided by 6 = **14 mm per minute**.
3. Percentage increase = (14 - 6) divided by 6 x 100 = **133 per cent**.
4. Volume of water taken up per minute in still air = cross-section area x distance. Area = pi x radius squared = 3.14 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.785 mm squared. Volume = 0.785 x 6 = **4.7 mm cubed per minute**.
5. **Explanation**: the fan removes water vapour from around the leaf, which keeps the concentration gradient steep, so transpiration is faster.

The potometer measures water **uptake**. It is a good estimate of transpiration because most of the water taken up is lost. Weaknesses are that some water is used in photosynthesis, and that leaks of air would give false readings.

## How water moves up the xylem

Water travels many metres to the top of a tree. The mechanism is called **transpiration pull**.

1. Water evaporates from the mesophyll cells and leaves through the stomata.
2. This removes water from the xylem at the top, which **pulls** more water up from below.
3. The water molecules are held together by **forces of attraction** (cohesion), so the water behaves as a continuous **column** in the xylem. When the top molecules are pulled, the whole column is pulled up.
4. New water enters the xylem in the root from the root cortex cells.

The movement requires **no energy from the plant**. The energy comes from the Sun, which drives evaporation. The xylem vessels are dead, so they cannot pump the water.

## Structure and function of xylem

**Functions of xylem**: transport of **water and mineral ions**, and **support**.

| Feature | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Thick walls with lignin | Strong, so the vessels do not collapse under the pull of transpiration, and the stem is supported |
| No cell contents (dead, hollow cells) | Nothing blocks the flow of water |
| Cells joined end to end, with no cross walls | Form a long continuous tube, so water moves freely |

(Details of lignification are not required.)

## Translocation

**Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids in the phloem from the parts of the plant that produce or release them (sources) to the parts that use or store them (sinks).**

| Term | Examples |
|---|---|
| Source | Leaves (make sucrose by photosynthesis); a storage organ in spring that releases stored sugar |
| Sink | Growing roots and shoots, flowers, fruits, and storage organs such as tubers and seeds |

Sucrose is soluble, so it can be carried in solution in the phloem. Unlike xylem transport, translocation is in **both directions** in the plant (up and down), depending on where the sinks are. It needs living cells and uses energy.

A storage organ can be a **source** at one time of year and a **sink** at another. A potato tuber is a sink in summer when it fills with starch made from sucrose, and a source in spring when it releases sugar to feed the new shoots.

## Positions of tissues in roots and stems

In a **transverse section** of a non-woody dicotyledonous plant:

| Organ | Xylem | Phloem | Cortex |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root | In the **centre**, often in a star or cross shape | Between the arms of the xylem | A wide region outside the vascular tissue |
| Stem | In **vascular bundles** arranged in a **ring** | Outside the xylem in each bundle | Between the ring of bundles and the epidermis |

Remember that in a stem, xylem is on the **inside** and phloem on the **outside** of each bundle. In a root, the xylem is in the **centre** of the root.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that transpiration is the loss of water from the roots, or of liquid water.
- Writing that water evaporates **from the stomata**. It evaporates from the mesophyll cell surfaces and diffuses out through the stomata.
- Saying humidity increases the rate.
- Giving the effect of a factor without an explanation.
- Writing that transpiration pull needs energy from the plant.
- Saying that the phloem carries water.
- Reversing source and sink.

## Command words

- **Describe**: state what transpiration is and the route taken by the water.
- **Explain**: give the effect and the reason, as in the factor table.
- **Investigate**: plan or interpret a potometer experiment, including controls.
- **Relate**: link xylem structure to function.
- **Identify**: name a tissue on a diagram of a transverse section.
