## The main ideas

In a **purchase** (acquisition), one company (the buyer, here Bolton Ltd) takes over the business of another (the vendor, here Cray Ltd). The buyer pays the **purchase consideration**, either in cash or in new shares, or a mixture. In a **merger**, two companies combine, usually by exchanging shares, and often the shareholders of both continue as owners, sometimes through a new company. The double entry is similar in both, so the same accounts are used.

Before the transfer, assets and liabilities are **revalued to fair value**, which is what they are worth to the buyer. Differences between book value and fair value go through the revaluation account. The difference between the price paid and the fair value of the net assets is **goodwill**.

## The four accounts

| Account | Kept in the books of | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Revaluation account** | Vendor | Records gains and losses when assets and liabilities are restated to fair value |
| **Realisation account** | Vendor | Closes the assets and liabilities that are transferred, records the consideration and finds the profit or loss on the sale |
| **Sundry shareholders' account** | Vendor | Settles the shareholders' claim: records what they hold and what they receive |
| **Acquisition account** | Buyer | Records the assets and liabilities taken over, the consideration paid, and the goodwill |

## Worked example: Bolton Ltd buys Cray Ltd

**Cray Ltd's statement of financial position (book values)**

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Property | 120,000 |
| Equipment | 60,000 |
| Inventory | 40,000 |
| Trade receivables | 30,000 |
| Bank | 10,000 |
| Trade payables | (35,000) |
| Loan | (25,000) |
| **Net assets** | **200,000** |
| Share capital | 100,000 |
| Retained earnings | 100,000 |

**Agreed fair values:** property 150,000; equipment 60,000; inventory 36,000; receivables 28,000; bank 10,000. Liabilities are taken at book value.

**Step 1: net assets at fair value (2.1.13).** Assets 150,000 + 60,000 + 36,000 + 28,000 + 10,000 = 284,000. Liabilities 35,000 + 25,000 = 60,000. Net assets at fair value = **224,000**. The net revaluation gain is 224,000 − 200,000 = 24,000.

**Step 2: purchase consideration.** Bolton issues 100,000 new $1 ordinary shares valued at $2.20 each (220,000) and pays 30,000 in cash. Consideration = **250,000**.

**Step 3: goodwill (2.1.15).** Goodwill = consideration − fair value of net assets acquired = 250,000 − 224,000 = **26,000**.

| Dr | $ | Cr | $ |
|---|---:|---|---:|

The shareholders' original interest of 200,000 plus the gain on revaluation 24,000 plus the profit on realisation 26,000 equals the 250,000 they receive. Cray Ltd has no assets left apart from the shares and cash it receives, and these are passed to its shareholders.

### In the books of Bolton Ltd (the buyer)

**Acquisition account**

| Dr | $ | Cr | $ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Property | 150,000 | Trade payables | 35,000 |
| Equipment | 60,000 | Loan | 25,000 |
| Inventory | 36,000 | Cray Ltd (purchase consideration) | 250,000 |
| Trade receivables | 28,000 | | |
| Bank | 10,000 | | |
| Goodwill (balancing figure) | 26,000 | | |
| | **310,000** | | **310,000** |

**Payment journal.** Dr Cray Ltd 250,000; Cr ordinary share capital 100,000; Cr share premium 120,000 (100,000 × 1.20); Cr bank 30,000.

If the whole 250,000 were paid in cash, there would be no share issue and the credit would be bank 250,000. If it were paid wholly in shares, the credit would be share capital and share premium. In every case, the consideration is the **fair value** of what is given up.

## Merger of limited companies

In a merger, two companies, say Aston Ltd and Birch Ltd, combine. A new company, Cairn Ltd, may issue shares to the shareholders of both, or Aston may issue shares to Birch's holders. The shareholders of both companies become shareholders in the combined firm. The accounting is the same: assets are revalued to fair value, consideration is measured in shares (and any cash), and goodwill is calculated for each company bought. For example, if Cairn Ltd issues 150,000 $1 shares valued at $2 each (300,000) for Aston's net assets of 280,000 at fair value, goodwill is 20,000. If it issues 100,000 shares valued at $1.90 (190,000) for Birch's fair net assets of 190,000, there is no goodwill.

## 2.1.15 The calculation and treatment of goodwill

**Goodwill** = purchase consideration − fair value of identifiable net assets acquired.

- It represents the value of things such as reputation, customer relations, skilled staff and expected future profit, which the buyer pays for but which are not separate assets.
- **Purchased goodwill** is recognised as an intangible non-current asset on the buyer's statement of financial position, as above (26,000).
- **Internally generated goodwill** is not recognised, because it cannot be measured reliably.
- Under International Financial Reporting Standards purchased goodwill is **not amortised**. It is tested at least once a year for **impairment**. If the recoverable amount falls below the carrying amount, the loss is charged to profit. Example: if Bolton decides goodwill is worth only 16,000, the impairment is 10,000: Dr impairment loss (statement of comprehensive income) 10,000, Cr goodwill 10,000.
- **Negative goodwill** (a bargain purchase) occurs when the consideration is below the fair value of net assets. If Bolton had paid only 200,000, the difference of 24,000 would be a gain recognised in profit, after checking that the fair values are right.

## Common mistakes

- Using book values rather than fair values to calculate goodwill.
- Adding the vendor's reserves to the buyer's equity.
- Forgetting the share premium when shares are issued above nominal value.
- Putting goodwill on the wrong side of the acquisition account (it is a debit, like an asset).
- Forgetting to transfer the revaluation gain and the realisation profit to sundry shareholders.
- Including the vendor's share capital in the combined statement of financial position.
- Depreciating or amortising goodwill every year when the standard says to test for impairment.
- Valuing shares issued at nominal value, not at their agreed fair value.

## Exam technique

- Work in order: fair values, net assets, consideration, goodwill, then the accounts and the statement.
- Label each account with whose books it is in.
- Show the balancing figures: goodwill and profit on realisation.
- Prove the combined statement: net assets must equal equity.
- **Explain** goodwill with a link: "Goodwill arises because the buyer pays more than the fair value of the net assets, for reputation and future profit."
