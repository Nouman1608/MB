## 4.10.3.1 Corrosion and its prevention

**Corrosion** is the **destruction of materials by chemical reactions with substances in the environment**.
**Rusting** is an example of corrosion. Rusting is the corrosion of **iron (and steel)**, which forms hydrated
iron(III) oxide.

**Both air (oxygen) and water are necessary for iron to rust.**

### Experiment to show the conditions for rusting

Place iron nails in three test tubes:

| Tube | Contents | Conditions | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nail in water open to air | **Air and water** | **Rusts** |
| 2 | Nail in boiled (air-free) water with a layer of oil on top | Water but **no air** | No rust |
| 3 | Nail with anhydrous calcium chloride, sealed | Air but **no water** (calcium chloride absorbs moisture) | No rust |

Leave for several days and compare. Only tube 1 has both air and water, and only it rusts. The boiled water has
had the dissolved oxygen driven out, and the oil layer stops air dissolving back in. The experiment is a
**fair test** if the nails and temperature are the same. Salt in the water in tube 1 speeds up rusting.

### Preventing corrosion

| Method | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Barrier coating** | Keeps out air and water | **Greasing** (moving parts), **painting** (car bodies, bridges), **electroplating** (a thin layer of another metal) |
| **Oxide layer** | A tough oxide layer stops further reaction | **Aluminium** has an oxide coating that protects the metal below |
| **Sacrificial protection** | A **more reactive metal** corrodes instead of the iron | **Zinc** used to **galvanise** iron; blocks of magnesium on ship hulls and pipelines |

### Barrier and sacrificial protection compared

A **barrier** only works while it is **unbroken**. If a painted surface is scratched, the iron underneath can rust.
In **sacrificial protection**, the more reactive metal loses electrons in preference to the iron, so the iron is
protected **even if the coating is scratched**, until the sacrificial metal is used up.

### Explaining sacrificial protection

*Explain why zinc protects a galvanised iron bucket even when scratched.*

1. Zinc is **more reactive** than iron.
2. Zinc **loses electrons** (is oxidised) more readily than iron.
3. So the zinc corrodes **instead of the iron** and the iron does not rust.

Tin plating is different: tin is **less** reactive than iron, so once the tin coating is scratched, the iron
rusts **faster**. That is a common trap.

## 4.10.3.2 Alloys as useful materials

An **alloy** is a **mixture of a metal with other elements** (usually other metals). Most metals in everyday use
are alloys, because pure metals are often too soft. The different-sized atoms in an alloy **distort the regular
layers**, making it harder for layers to slide.

| Alloy | Made from | Properties and uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Bronze** | **Copper and tin** | Hard, resists corrosion; statues, medals, bearings |
| **Brass** | **Copper and zinc** | Hard, harder than copper; door handles, taps, musical instruments |
| **Gold alloys** | Gold with silver, copper and zinc | Jewellery (pure gold is too soft) |
| **High carbon steel** | Iron with a larger percentage of carbon | **Strong but brittle**; cutting tools |
| **Low carbon steel** | Iron with a small percentage of carbon | **Softer** and easily shaped; car bodies, wire |
| **Stainless steel** | Iron with **chromium and nickel** | **Hard and resistant to corrosion**; cutlery, sinks |
| **Aluminium alloys** | Aluminium with other metals | **Low density** and strong; aircraft |

### Carats

Gold used in jewellery is usually an alloy. The proportion of gold is measured in **carats**. **24 carat is
100%** (pure gold). The percentage of gold is carats ÷ 24 × 100.

### Worked example 1: carats

*What percentage of gold is in 18 carat gold, and what mass of gold is in a 50 g ring?*

```
percentage = 18 / 24 x 100 = 75 %
mass of gold = 0.75 x 50 g = 37.5 g
```

*A ring is 37.5% gold. How many carats is it?* 37.5 ÷ 100 × 24 = **9 carat**.

### Interpreting alloy data

You may be given the composition and uses of an alloy you have not met. Use what you know:

- **Low density** needed → aluminium alloy.
- **Hard but must not corrode** → alloy with chromium or nickel.
- **Strong but may snap** → high carbon.
- **Easily shaped** → low carbon.

## 4.10.3.3 Ceramics, polymers and composites

### Glass

| Glass | Made from | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| **Soda-lime glass** | Heating **sand, sodium carbonate and limestone** | The glass in windows and bottles |
| **Borosilicate glass** | **Sand and boron trioxide** | **Melts at a higher temperature** than soda-lime glass; used for laboratory glassware and oven dishes |

### Clay ceramics

**Clay ceramics** (pottery, bricks) are made by **shaping wet clay and then heating it in a furnace**. They are
hard, brittle and heat-resistant.

### Polymers

The properties of a polymer depend on **what monomers it is made from** and **the conditions under which it is
made**.

- **Low density (LD)** and **high density (HD) poly(ethene)** are both produced from **ethene** but under
  different conditions. LD poly(ethene) has branched chains that cannot pack closely, so it is flexible and low
  in density (carrier bags). HD poly(ethene) has straight chains that pack closely, so it is stronger and more
  rigid (bottles, pipes).
- **Thermosoftening polymers** **melt when heated**. They have **weak intermolecular forces** between separate
  chains, which break when heated. They can be reshaped.
- **Thermosetting polymers** **do not melt when heated**. Their chains are joined by **strong covalent
  cross-links** (a three-dimensional network). They are rigid and heat-resistant, and burn or char rather than
  melt.

### Composites

Most **composites** are made of two materials: a **matrix (or binder)** surrounding and binding together
**fibres or fragments** of the other material, the **reinforcement**.

| Composite | Matrix | Reinforcement |
|---|---|---|
| Fibreglass | Polymer resin | Glass fibres |
| Carbon fibre | Polymer resin | Carbon fibres |
| Concrete | Cement | Sand and gravel (and steel bars) |
| Plywood | Glue | Layers of wood |

The composite has properties that neither material has alone, such as being strong and light.

### Worked example 2: selecting a material

*A manufacturer needs a material for a bike frame that is strong, light and does not rust. Select a material and
justify the choice.*

Carbon fibre composite: it is **strong** because of the carbon fibres and **light** because of the polymer matrix,
and it does not rust. Steel is strong but heavy and rusts; aluminium alloy is light and corrosion-resistant but
less stiff. The best choice depends on cost: carbon fibre is expensive.

### Comparing properties quantitatively

| Material | Density / g/cm³ | Melting point / °C | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glass | 2.5 | about 1000 | Hard, brittle |
| Steel | 7.8 | about 1400 | Strong |
| Polymer | 1.0 | about 120 | Flexible, weak |

When comparing, quote the numbers: steel is about 3 times denser than glass (7.8 ÷ 2.5 = 3.1).

## Exam technique

- For rusting, always say **air (oxygen) and water**.
- For sacrificial protection, name the **more reactive metal** and say that it **corrodes instead**.
- Link each material's **properties** to its **use**.
- For polymers, link **structure** (cross-links or weak forces) to **behaviour on heating**.
- Quote the **numbers** when comparing data.

## Common mistakes

- Saying iron rusts without water or without oxygen.
- Saying galvanising works only as a barrier. It is sacrificial too.
- Saying an alloy is a compound.
- Mixing up thermosoftening and thermosetting.
- Saying carat gives the number of grams of gold.
- Writing that composites are "mixtures" without matrix and reinforcement.
