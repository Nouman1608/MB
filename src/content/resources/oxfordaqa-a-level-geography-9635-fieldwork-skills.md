This guide covers **3.5.2 Fieldwork skills** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2. Every student must "engage in a personal fieldwork investigation", and Unit 5 assesses it in two ways: **unfamiliar fieldwork questions**, which ask you to apply fieldwork knowledge to new scenarios, and **familiar fieldwork questions**, which ask about your own investigation. Unit 5 carries 60 marks, of which **15 assess your own investigation**. The specification names five aspects of enquiry on which questions are set: 3.5.2.1 planning and preparation, 3.5.2.2 collection of data, 3.5.2.3 presentation, 3.5.2.4 analysis, and 3.5.2.5 drawing conclusions and evaluating the enquiry.

The [geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/) outlines each stage briefly. This guide goes into the decisions behind each stage, with worked sampling and risk-assessment examples and annotated answers for both question types. Condensed notes are in the [fieldwork skills revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-fieldwork-skills-revision-notes/), and you can practise with the [fieldwork skills practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-fieldwork-skills-practice/). Places and data below are invented.

## What counts as a personal investigation

The specification says the investigation should ideally be practical, but where that is not possible, alternative methods may be used. It lists three: enquiries **within the school grounds**, **interviewing people** in school or at home, and working with **raw, unprocessed data** provided by the teacher, so that you help plan the investigation, select samples from the larger data set and consider its relevance and reliability. What is not acceptable is being handed pre-processed data ready for analysis: "there must be some element of planning and selecting". Keep records of every decision you made -- they are the evidence for familiar fieldwork answers.

## 3.5.2.1 Planning and preparation

**Aims, hypotheses and research questions.** An aim states what you want to find out. A hypothesis is a testable prediction based on theory; a research question is an open question used when a prediction is not appropriate, for example with qualitative data. The specification asks you to **establish and justify** the hypothesis or question, so link it to a concept you have studied.

> Aim: to investigate how pedestrian footfall changes with distance from the centre of Marton.
> Hypothesis: footfall decreases with distance from the main square.
> Justification: bid-rent theory predicts that the most accessible central locations attract the highest density of shops and therefore of shoppers.

**Location.** Choose a site that is safe, accessible, small enough to cover in the time available and appropriate to the question. A study of longshore drift needs a beach with a clear drift direction; a study of urban heat needs a gradient from centre to edge.

**Secondary research first.** The specification requires background research "before embarking on any data collection": maps, census data, previous studies, flood records. Secondary sources help you choose sites, predict results and later explain them.

**Risk assessment.** Identify each hazard, judge its likelihood and severity, and set a control measure.

| Hazard | Likelihood | Severity | Control measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traffic when counting pedestrians | Medium | High | Stand away from the kerb; work only at marked crossings |
| Fast-flowing water in a river | Low to medium | High | Do not enter above knee depth; check river levels beforehand |
| Hot weather | Medium | Medium | Carry water; work in shade; limit time outdoors |
| Lone working | Low | High | Work in groups; agree a meeting time and carry a phone |

## 3.5.2.2 Collection of data

**Primary and secondary data.** Primary data are collected by you, for example river velocity or questionnaire responses. Secondary data were collected by others, such as census statistics or gauging station records. Justify each source in terms of the hypothesis.

**Sampling.** The specification names **random, systematic and structured/stratified** sampling. Each can be applied at points, along lines (transects) or within areas (quadrats).

*Worked stratified sample:* a questionnaire on perceptions of a new market will be given to 80 people. Census data for the area show adults aged 18 to 34 make up 28%, 35 to 64 make up 47%, and 65 and over 25%.

> 18-34: 80 x 0.28 = 22.4, so 22 people
> 35-64: 80 x 0.47 = 37.6, so 38 people
> 65+: 80 x 0.25 = 20 people
> Check: 22 + 38 + 20 = 80

This makes the sample representative of age groups, which matters if views on the market are likely to differ by age.

**Pilot study.** A short trial run checks that equipment works, questions are understood and timings are realistic.

**Accuracy, precision, reliability and validity.** These words are often confused:

- **Accuracy:** how close a measurement is to the true value.
- **Precision:** how fine the measurement scale is (to the nearest millimetre rather than centimetre).
- **Reliability:** whether repeating the method would give similar results.
- **Validity:** whether the method actually measures what the hypothesis is about.

A footfall count taken once on a rainy Monday may be accurate for that moment but neither reliable nor valid as a measure of typical footfall.

## 3.5.2.3 Presentation of data

The specification asks for maps, graphs, photographs, field sketches, tables and interview quotations "as appropriate to the enquiry" **with justification for their selection**. Match technique to data: located proportional circles for footfall at named sites, a scatter graph for footfall against distance, a cross-section for river channel shape, annotated photographs for land use. Integrate presentation with location: putting graphs on a base map often shows spatial pattern more clearly than separate charts.

## 3.5.2.4 Analysis of data

Analysis means identifying patterns, trends, relationships and anomalies, using **statistical techniques where appropriate -- or other techniques where it is not**. Spearman's rank suits paired continuous data; chi-square suits counts in categories; measures of central tendency and dispersion suit comparisons of sites. Qualitative data, such as interviews, can be **coded** into themes, counted, and illustrated with short quotations. Always return to the hypothesis: what does each pattern show?

## 3.5.2.5 Conclusions, ethics and evaluation

**Conclusions** must refer back to the aims and **accept or reject the hypothesis**, with evidence. Partial acceptance is legitimate if the evidence is mixed.

**Ethics.** The specification asks you to consider "fieldwork ethics and the possible impacts of collecting, studying and representing geographical data". This includes informed consent, anonymity, not trampling vegetation or disturbing wildlife, and how publishing results -- for instance labelling a neighbourhood as deprived -- might affect the people who live there.

**Understanding of place and theory.** Show how the fieldwork changed your understanding of the place and of the geographical ideas behind the study.

**Evaluation of every stage.** Evaluate planning, sampling, methods, presentation, analysis and conclusions, then suggest specific improvements and say **how the enquiry could be used or taken forward**, for example by repeating it in another season or extending it to another town.

## Worked example: a familiar fieldwork answer

*Question: With reference to your own fieldwork, evaluate the sampling strategy you used.* **[9]**

> I used systematic sampling, counting pedestrians for five minutes every 100 m along a transect from Marton's main square **(states the method precisely)**. This gave even coverage and allowed me to plot footfall against distance for my Spearman's rank test **(strength linked to the hypothesis)**. However, it could miss small clusters of shops lying between sampling points, and one site happened to be next to a bus stop, which inflated its count **(limitations with evidence)**. Counts were taken on one afternoon, so the sample in time was very small **(reliability)**. A stratified sample by land-use zone, repeated on a weekday and a weekend, would have been more representative **(specific improvement)**. Overall, the strategy was adequate for showing the overall trend but limited for explaining local anomalies **(judgement)**.

## Worked example: an unfamiliar fieldwork answer

*Scenario: students want to find out whether flood defences in a riverside town affect residents' perception of flood risk. Suggest how they could collect primary data.* **[4]**

> Use a questionnaire with a rating scale for perceived risk, so answers can be compared statistically **(method linked to purpose)**. Survey residents both behind the defences and in an undefended area, so the two groups can be compared **(sampling linked to hypothesis)**. Use stratified sampling by age, because experience of past floods may vary with age **(justification)**. Pilot the questionnaire to check that the questions are clear and not leading **(reliability)**.

In unfamiliar questions, every suggestion must fit the scenario given; generic methods that could apply anywhere earn little.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a hypothesis that cannot be tested with the data collected.
- Naming random, systematic or stratified sampling with no reason linked to the enquiry.
- A risk assessment that identifies dangers but never says how each will be reduced.
- Confusing reliability with accuracy.
- Concluding without accepting or rejecting the hypothesis.
- Evaluating only the data collection stage instead of every stage.
