This guide goes deeper into one sub-topic of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: **18.2 Adaptive features**, the second part of topic 18, Variation and selection. It has three learning outcomes. Outcomes 1 and 2 are Core. Outcome 3, on hydrophytes and xerophytes, is Supplement content, so it is **Extended only** and appears on Papers 2 and 4.

The [variation and selection study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-variation-and-selection/) gives the whole topic, including a worked example on a snow hare. This page concentrates on the skill outcome 2 tests, reading unfamiliar information and turning it into marks, and on full explanations for plants adapted to water and to drought. When you have finished, use the [adaptive features revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-adaptive-features-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/) and the [adaptive features practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-adaptive-features-cambridge-igcse-biology-practice/).

## What sub-topic 18.2 asks you to do

| Outcome | Syllabus wording (summarised) | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 18.2.1 | Describe an adaptive feature as an inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment | Core |
| 18.2.2 | Interpret images or other information about a species to describe its adaptive features | Core |
| 18.2.3 | Explain the adaptive features of hydrophytes and xerophytes to their environments | Extended only |

Outcome 2 says **interpret** and **describe**: the organism will usually be one you have never studied, so the marks come from method, not memory. Outcome 3 says **explain**, so every feature needs a reason linked to the environment.

## 18.2.1 The definition, word by word

> An adaptive feature is an **inherited** feature that helps an organism to **survive and reproduce** in its **environment**.

- **Inherited** means it is coded for by genes and passed from parents to offspring. A sun tan, a broken antler or muscles built up by training are acquired during life, so they are not adaptive features.
- **Survive and reproduce** are both in the definition. Some features help mainly with reproduction: brightly coloured, scented petals attract insects that carry pollen between flowers.
- **Environment** matters because a feature is only adaptive in a particular setting. White fur is camouflage on snow but makes an animal easy to see on dark soil.

## 18.2.2 Interpreting information about a species

Questions give a photograph, a labelled drawing, a description or a table of data. Use a three-step chain for each feature:

> **Feature → what it does → why that helps survival or reproduction here.**

Before writing, underline the conditions in the question: hot or cold, dry or wet, predators, type of food. Every answer should connect to one of them.

### Worked example 1: a desert mammal

*Information:* Camels live in hot, dry, sandy deserts. They have broad, flat feet; long eyelashes and nostrils that can close; a hump containing fat; and thick fur on their backs.

| Feature | What it does | How it helps in a hot, sandy desert |
|---|---|---|
| Broad, flat feet | spread the body mass over a larger area | the camel does not sink into soft sand, so it can walk long distances to find food and water |
| Long eyelashes, closable nostrils | keep sand out of the eyes and airways | prevents damage during sandstorms |
| Fat stored in a hump | fat is concentrated in one place, not spread under the skin all over the body | the rest of the body loses heat more easily; the fat is an energy store when food is scarce |
| Thick fur on the back | insulates against heat from direct sunlight | less heat is absorbed by the body during the day |

**Watch the trap:** the hump stores fat, not water. Questions sometimes test whether you read the information or rely on a myth.

### Worked example 2: interpreting data

A table compares three plant species (illustrative data).

| Species | Habitat | Stomata per mm² (upper surface) | Stomata per mm² (lower surface) | Cuticle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | pond surface | 420 | 0 | very thin |
| Q | woodland | 20 | 240 | thin |
| R | desert | 0 | 35 | very thick |

- **P** has stomata only on the upper surface: its leaves float, the lower surface touches water, and gas exchange happens with the air above. A thin cuticle does not matter because water loss is no risk.
- **R** has the fewest stomata and a very thick waxy cuticle: both reduce water loss by transpiration in a dry habitat.
- **Q** is in between, with most stomata on the shaded lower surface, which loses less water than the sunlit upper surface.

Use numbers from the table in your answer: "R has only 35 stomata per mm², fewer than Q's 260 in total".

## 18.2.3 Hydrophytes (Extended only)

A **hydrophyte** is a plant adapted to live in water, either floating on it or submerged. Its problems are not water loss but getting enough **light** and **gases** (oxygen and carbon dioxide), and staying near the surface.

| Feature | Explanation linked to the environment |
|---|---|
| Large air spaces in leaves and stems | make the leaves buoyant so they float near the surface, where light intensity is highest; store gases and allow oxygen to diffuse to submerged roots |
| Stomata on the upper surface of floating leaves | the lower surface is in contact with water, so gas exchange can only happen with the air |
| Thin or absent waxy cuticle | water loss is not a problem when surrounded by water |
| Little xylem tissue | water is absorbed over the plant's surface, so little transport of water is needed |
| Little support tissue | the water supports the plant, so stiff tissue is not needed |
| Large, flat floating leaves | a large surface absorbs as much light as possible for photosynthesis |
| Thin or finely divided submerged leaves | a large surface area for absorbing carbon dioxide and mineral ions from the water |

## 18.2.3 Xerophytes (Extended only)

A **xerophyte** is a plant adapted to live where water is scarce, such as deserts, sand dunes or windy coasts. Its problem is **losing water faster than it can absorb it**.

Most xerophyte features reduce transpiration. Revise the factors from the [transport in plants study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-transport-in-plants/): water vapour diffuses out of stomata faster when the air outside is dry, hot or windy. Xerophyte features slow this diffusion or store water.

| Feature | Explanation linked to the environment |
|---|---|
| Thick waxy cuticle | waterproof layer reduces evaporation from the leaf surface |
| Sunken stomata, in pits or grooves | humid air is trapped near the stoma, so the water vapour concentration gradient is smaller and less water diffuses out |
| Hairs on the leaf surface | trap a layer of moist air and reduce air movement over the stomata |
| Leaves that roll up with stomata inside | trap humid air inside the roll and reduce the surface exposed to wind |
| Small leaves or leaves reduced to spines | smaller surface area from which water is lost |
| Fewer stomata | fewer pores through which water vapour can diffuse |
| Swollen, fleshy stems or leaves | store water for use in dry periods |
| Deep roots | reach water far below the surface |
| Shallow, widespread roots | absorb water quickly over a large area after rain |

### Worked example 3: annotated model answer

**Question:** A plant grows on dry, windy cliffs. Its small leaves are thick and fleshy, covered with dense hairs, and have a thick waxy cuticle. Explain how these features help it survive. [4]

**Answer:** The small leaves have a small surface area, so less water is lost by transpiration (1). The fleshy leaves store water that the plant can use when none is available from the soil (1). The hairs trap a layer of moist air next to the leaf, which reduces the water vapour gradient, so less water vapour diffuses out of the stomata, even in wind (1). The thick waxy cuticle reduces evaporation from the leaf surface (1).

**Why it scores:** each feature is joined to a consequence for water. "The hairs protect the leaf" would score nothing, because it does not say from what or why it helps.

## Comparing the two

| | Hydrophyte | Xerophyte |
|---|---|---|
| Main challenge | getting light and gases | reducing water loss |
| Cuticle | thin or absent | thick and waxy |
| Stomata | many, on upper surface | few, often sunken |
| Leaves | large and flat (floating) | small, rolled or reduced to spines |
| Water storage | not needed | often in fleshy stems or leaves |

## Common mistakes

- Calling an acquired feature, such as a scar or trained muscles, an adaptive feature.
- Forgetting "and reproduce" in the definition.
- Listing features without saying what each one does.
- Writing that a xerophyte "needs less water". It still needs water; it loses less of it.
- Saying sunken stomata "stop water loss". They reduce it, by trapping humid air.
- Putting the stomata of a floating leaf on the lower surface.

## Exam technique for 18.2 command words

- **Describe** (outcome 1): give the full definition, including "inherited".
- **Describe** from information (outcome 2): name the feature, then what it does. Use the conditions in the stem.
- **Explain** (outcome 3): feature + "so" + effect on water loss, light or gas exchange.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are hydrophytes and xerophytes Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
They are Supplement content, so they are Extended only. The Core outcomes are the definition of an adaptive feature and describing adaptive features from information.

**How do sunken stomata reduce water loss?**
They trap humid air close to the pore, which reduces the water vapour concentration gradient between the inside of the leaf and the air, so less water vapour diffuses out.
