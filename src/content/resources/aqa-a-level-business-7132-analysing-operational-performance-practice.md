These questions cover section 3.4.2 Analysing operational performance of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [operational performance study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-operational-performance/) and the [operational performance revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-operational-performance-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A factory produced 36,000 units in a month. Its maximum capacity is 45,000 units. What is its capacity utilisation? **[1]**

A: 20%

B: 80%

C: 125%

D: 9,000%

**2.** A team of 30 workers produces 2,400 units in a week. What is the labour productivity? **[1]**

A: 8 units per worker

B: 80 units per worker

C: 72,000 units per worker

D: 2,430 units per worker

**3.** Define the term "unit cost". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why a business might choose to operate below 100% capacity utilisation. **[3]**

**5.** Kestrel Candles produced 20,000 candles last month with 16 employees. Its maximum capacity is 25,000 candles a month. Fixed costs are £15,000 a month and variable costs are £1.50 per candle.

**(a)** Calculate the labour productivity (candles per employee). **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the unit cost. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the capacity utilisation. **[1]**

**6.** This month Kestrel produces 24,000 candles with the same 16 employees.

**(a)** Calculate the new unit cost, to the nearest penny. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the unit cost has fallen. **[2]**

**7.** Labour productivity at Kestrel was 1,250 candles per employee last month. Six months later it is 1,100. Calculate the percentage change. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** possible causes of a fall in labour productivity at a manufacturing business. **[6]**

**9.** Kestrel Candles is now operating at 96% capacity utilisation and demand is still rising. Assess whether it should invest in extra capacity. **[9]**

**10.** A retailer asks Kestrel to make an extra 5,000 candles at £2.60 each, in a month when output would otherwise be 20,000. Analyse how Kestrel's cost data could help it decide whether to accept the order. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** limitations of using operations data to judge a business's performance. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether labour productivity is the best measure of the operational performance of a business. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. 36,000 ÷ 45,000 × 100 = 80%. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. 2,400 ÷ 30 = 80. **[1]**

**3.** The average cost of producing one unit of output [1], found by dividing total costs by the number of units produced [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Spare capacity allows the business to cope with unexpected extra orders or seasonal peaks [1], so it avoids turning customers away or paying for overtime [1]. It also leaves time for maintenance and reduces the risk of machine breakdown, which protects quality and delivery dates [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 20,000 ÷ 16 = **1,250 candles per employee** [1].

**(b)** Variable costs = 20,000 × £1.50 = £30,000 [1]. Total cost = £15,000 + £30,000 = £45,000 [1]. Unit cost = £45,000 ÷ 20,000 = **£2.25** [1].

**(c)** 20,000 ÷ 25,000 × 100 = **80%** [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** Total cost = £15,000 + 24,000 × £1.50 = £51,000 [1]. Unit cost = £51,000 ÷ 24,000 = **£2.13** (£2.125) [1].

**(b)** Fixed costs of £15,000 do not change [1], so they are spread over more candles, and each candle carries a smaller share of fixed costs, even though variable cost per candle is unchanged [1]. **[4]**

**7.** (1,100 − 1,250) ÷ 1,250 [1] × 100 = **−12%**, a fall of 12% [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Cause 1: lower motivation or morale [1]. If staff feel undervalued or are worried about job security, they may work less effectively and be absent more often [1], so fewer units are produced per worker [1]. Cause 2: out-of-date equipment or poor training [1]. Machines that break down or workers who do not know new methods slow production [1], which reduces output per employee, especially if the business has recently taken on new staff [1]. Credit also: poor management and scheduling; shortages of materials. **[6]**

**9.** For: at 96% there is little spare capacity, so rising demand means turned-away orders or late deliveries [1]. Extra capacity would let Kestrel meet more orders and increase revenue [1]. Higher output could lower unit cost because fixed costs are spread more widely [1]. Against: investment is costly and may raise fixed costs [1]. If the rise in demand is temporary or seasonal, the new capacity could be under-used [1]. Alternatives exist, such as overtime, extra shifts or outsourcing [1]. The business must have the finance and space to expand [1]. Judgement: investing is sensible if forecasts show that demand is likely to stay high for several years [1]; otherwise, short-term measures such as overtime or subcontracting may be lower risk, and the decision should be reviewed against sales data [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Fixed costs of £15,000 are already covered by the normal 20,000 candles [1]. The extra order brings only extra variable costs of 5,000 × £1.50 = £7,500 [1], while the revenue is 5,000 × £2.60 = £13,000, so it adds £5,500 to profit [1]. The average unit cost at 25,000 candles is (£15,000 + £37,500) ÷ 25,000 = £2.10, which is below the price of £2.60 [1]. The order would take Kestrel to its full capacity of 25,000, leaving no flexibility for other customers [1]. It should check that regular customers will not be let down and that the lower price will not become a benchmark for other buyers [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Limitation 1: data is based on averages [1], which can hide differences between shifts or products, so problems in one area may go unnoticed [1]. Limitation 2: figures are about the past [1] and measure quantity, not quality; a business could raise productivity while errors and complaints increase [1]. Accept: data may be for an unrepresentative period; allocation of fixed costs is a judgement. **[4]**

**12.** For: labour productivity is simple to calculate and easy to compare over time and between firms [1]. It links directly to costs, because higher output per worker lowers labour cost per unit [1]. It shows the effect of training, motivation and methods [1]. It helps with staffing plans [1]. Against: it ignores quality, so productivity could rise while errors grow [1]. It does not show capital efficiency; a firm with robots may have high labour productivity but a large investment [1]. It ignores other objectives such as flexibility, speed of response and environmental impact [1]. It does not show whether the products are what customers want [1]. Other measures, such as unit cost, capacity utilisation, defect rate and delivery time, give a fuller view [1]. Factors: the type of business, since labour-intensive services depend more on staff output than a highly automated plant [1]. Judgement: labour productivity is a useful measure but not the best on its own; it should be combined with unit cost and a quality measure, chosen to fit the business's operational objectives [1]. Customer satisfaction should also be considered [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Always state the unit, and say what each figure means for the business rather than leaving it as a number.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving fixed costs out of total cost.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100 for capacity utilisation.
- Giving a percentage change without a sign or direction.
- Treating 100% utilisation as always desirable.
- Not interpreting the calculation.
- Judging performance from productivity alone, with no comment on quality.
