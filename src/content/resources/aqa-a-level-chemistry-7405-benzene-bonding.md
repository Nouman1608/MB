## The structure of benzene

Benzene has the molecular formula C₆H₆. The six carbon atoms form a regular hexagonal ring.

- The ring is **planar**: all 12 atoms lie in one plane.
- Each carbon is bonded to two other carbons and one hydrogen by **sigma bonds**, giving bond angles of **120°**.
- Each carbon has one electron left in a **p orbital** pointing at right angles to the ring.
- The six p orbitals overlap sideways, forming a ring of electron density **above and below the plane**. The six electrons are **delocalised**: they are shared around the whole ring rather than belonging to any one pair of carbons.
- All six carbon–carbon bonds are **identical** in length, intermediate between a single and a double bond.

The skeleton can be drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside, representing the delocalised electrons. The Kekulé structure, with alternating single and double bonds, is a simple model but it does not match the evidence.

## Evidence that benzene is not cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene

### 1. Bond lengths

In a hypothetical cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene with alternating bonds, the ring would not be a regular hexagon, because C=C is shorter than C–C.

| Bond | Approximate length / nm |
|---|---|
| C–C (single) | 0.154 |
| C=C (double) | 0.134 |
| C–C in benzene | 0.140 (all six equal) |

X-ray diffraction shows six equal bonds in benzene, with lengths between those of a single and a double bond. This is what delocalisation predicts.

### 2. Enthalpy of hydrogenation

This is the thermochemical evidence the specification names. Hydrogenation is addition of hydrogen across C=C bonds using a nickel catalyst.

| Compound | Enthalpy of hydrogenation / kJ mol⁻¹ | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclohexene (one C=C) | −120 | Cyclohexane |
| Cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene (three C=C, theoretical) | −360 (predicted: 3 × −120) | Cyclohexane |
| Benzene (measured) | −208 | Cyclohexane |

The measured value for benzene is **less exothermic by 152 kJ mol⁻¹** than the value predicted for cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene. All three compounds give the same product, cyclohexane, so the difference is due to the energy of the starting material. Benzene starts at a **lower energy** than the hypothetical triene would, so less energy is released on hydrogenation. The 152 kJ mol⁻¹ is the extra stability, often called the **delocalisation energy** or resonance energy.

```
Energy (higher at the top)

  cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene (hypothetical)      ─────────
        │                                         │
        │  152 kJ mol⁻¹ lower                     │ ΔH = −360 (predicted)
  benzene (real)                    ─────────     │
        │                                │        │
        │ ΔH = −208 (measured)           │        │
  cyclohexane (product)  ───────────────────────────────
```

### 3. Reactivity

Benzene does not decolourise bromine water at room temperature, unlike cyclohexene. A localised C=C double bond would be expected to react. The lack of reaction is consistent with the electrons being delocalised and so less available to attack.

## Why delocalisation stabilises benzene

Delocalised electrons are spread over six atoms instead of being held between two. Spreading the electrons over a larger region lowers the energy of the system compared with three localised double bonds. The stability is the reason that benzene is energetically reluctant to lose its delocalised system.

## Substitution rather than addition

Typical alkene reactions are additions, which break the pi bond. If benzene underwent addition, the delocalised ring would be destroyed, and the molecule would lose the 152 kJ mol⁻¹ of extra stability. This costs a large amount of energy, so addition is unfavourable.

In **substitution**, one hydrogen atom is replaced by another atom or group while the delocalised ring is restored. The product keeps the extra stability. Benzene therefore reacts by substitution.

The electron density in the ring is still enough to attract strong **electrophiles**, but it is lower than the density at a localised C=C bond, because the six electrons are spread over six carbon atoms. This is why benzene needs a more powerful electrophile (made using a catalyst) than an alkene. The details are in the Electrophilic substitution sub-topic.

## Benzene compared with cyclohexene and cyclohexane

| Feature | Cyclohexane | Cyclohexene | Benzene |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formula | C₆H₁₂ | C₆H₁₀ | C₆H₆ |
| Carbon–carbon bonding | Single only | One localised C=C | Delocalised ring |
| Shape of ring | Puckered, not planar | Not planar | Planar hexagon |
| Bond angle at carbon | About 109.5° | 120° at C=C | 120° |
| Reaction with bromine water | None | Decolourises (addition) | None |
| Typical reaction | Radical substitution | Electrophilic addition | Electrophilic substitution |

Reading across the table shows that the type of bonding decides the type of reaction. Use it to answer "compare" questions: for each feature, say what is different, then give the reason.

## Worked example 1: the delocalisation energy

**Question.** The enthalpy of hydrogenation of cyclohexene is −120 kJ mol⁻¹ and of benzene is −208 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the delocalisation energy of benzene.

```
Predicted for cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene = 3 × (−120) = −360 kJ mol⁻¹
Measured for benzene                 = −208 kJ mol⁻¹
Difference = −208 − (−360) = +152 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Benzene is more stable than the hypothetical triene by **152 kJ mol⁻¹**.

## Worked example 2: why the answer is not 120 × 3

**Question.** The enthalpy of hydrogenation of cyclohexa-1,3-diene is −232 kJ mol⁻¹. Compare it with the value predicted by adding the cyclohexene value twice.

```
Predicted: 2 × (−120) = −240 kJ mol⁻¹
Measured:  −232 kJ mol⁻¹
Difference: 8 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The diene is only slightly more stable than predicted (because the two double bonds are conjugated, which gives a small stabilisation). For benzene the difference is 152 kJ mol⁻¹, which is much larger. This shows that the stabilisation in benzene is a special effect of the complete ring of delocalised electrons.

## Worked example 3: comparing reactions with bromine

**Question.** Explain why cyclohexene decolourises bromine water readily but benzene does not.

Cyclohexene has a localised C=C bond with high electron density, which polarises a bromine molecule and reacts by electrophilic addition. In benzene the six pi electrons are delocalised over the whole ring, so the electron density between any two carbon atoms is lower. Benzene cannot polarise bromine strongly enough, and addition would also destroy the stable delocalised system. No reaction occurs at room temperature.

## Worked example 4: counting bonds in benzene

**Question.** State the number of sigma bonds and the number of delocalised electrons in benzene.

- C–H sigma bonds: 6.
- C–C sigma bonds in the ring: 6.
- Total sigma bonds: **12**.
- Each carbon contributes one p electron, so there are **6 delocalised electrons**.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the structure with alternating double and single bonds and treating it as correct.
- Saying "the bonds resonate" or that the molecule flips between structures. Benzene has one structure with delocalised electrons.
- Saying bond lengths are "the average" without stating that all six are equal.
- Explaining the extra stability as "benzene has a stronger bond", rather than lower energy through delocalisation.
- Subtracting the wrong way round, giving −152 instead of a stability of 152 kJ mol⁻¹ (or confusing the sign).
- Forgetting to state that the predicted value is for cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene, based on three times the value for cyclohexene.
- Claiming addition to benzene is impossible. It can happen under harsh conditions, such as hydrogenation with a catalyst, but it is less favourable.
- Stating that the reason benzene resists addition is "the double bonds are too strong".

## Exam technique

- **Describe the bonding**: planar, 120° angles, p orbitals overlap sideways, delocalised electrons above and below.
- **Use thermochemical evidence**: state predicted value, measured value, the difference, and what it shows.
- **Explain substitution**: addition would destroy the delocalised system and lose the extra stability.
- **Contrast with alkenes**: lower electron density in the ring, so benzene resists bromine water.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the hydrogenation of benzene less exothermic than predicted?**
Benzene starts at a lower energy because of delocalisation. Both benzene and the hypothetical triene end up as the same compound, cyclohexane, so less energy is released from benzene.

**Does the circle in the hexagon represent a ring of electrons?**
It represents the delocalised pi electrons shared by all six carbon atoms.

**What is the difference between benzene and cyclohexene?**
Cyclohexene has one localised C=C and reacts by addition. Benzene has a delocalised ring and reacts by substitution.
