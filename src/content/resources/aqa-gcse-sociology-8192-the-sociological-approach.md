This guide covers section 3.1, The sociological approach, of the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, Version 1.2 (June 2026), for GCSE exams 2019 onwards. Section 3.1 has no sub-sections and is not tied to one paper. Both Paper 1 (The sociology of families and education) and Paper 2 (The sociology of crime and deviance and social stratification) assess "relevant areas of social theory and methodology", and the specification expects you to draw on the whole course in both. Each paper is a 1 hour 45 minute written exam worth 100 marks and 50% of the GCSE.

## Key terms and concepts

The specification names nine concepts you must be able to explain and use. Appendix A says you may be asked to identify definitions of key terms "as used by sociologists".

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Society | A large group of people who share a territory and a set of social institutions, such as families, schools and the law |
| Socialisation | The lifelong process of learning the norms and values of a society. Primary socialisation happens in the family; secondary socialisation happens through agencies such as schools, peer groups, religion and the mass media |
| Norms | Unwritten rules or expected patterns of behaviour in a given situation, such as queuing |
| Values | Widely shared beliefs about what is important or desirable, such as honesty or achievement. Norms usually put values into practice |
| Roles | The behaviour expected of a person in a particular position, such as parent, pupil or police officer |
| Labelling | Attaching a name or category to a person, which shapes how others treat them and how they see themselves |
| Discrimination | Treating a person or group less favourably because of a characteristic such as ethnicity, gender, age or disability |
| Power | The ability to get others to do what you want, even against their will |
| Authority | Power that people accept as legitimate, so they obey willingly |

Two related ideas help you use these terms. Social control means the formal and informal ways a society makes people follow its norms, through sanctions (rewards and punishments). Status can be ascribed (fixed at birth, such as sex or a royal title) or achieved (earned, such as a qualification).

**Worked example (applying concepts):** *Knowledge:* Norms are learned through socialisation and backed by sanctions. *Application:* A new pupil learns not to call out in class because classmates stare and the teacher gives a warning, an informal and a formal sanction. *Analysis:* This shows secondary socialisation working through two agencies at once. *Evaluation:* However, an interactionist would add that the pupil also interprets the rule and may choose to break it, so socialisation is not a one-way process.

## Debates within sociology: consensus versus conflict

The main debate the specification names is **conflict versus consensus**.

- **Consensus approaches** (functionalism) see society as based on shared values, called a value consensus. Each institution performs a function that helps society run smoothly, in the way organs keep a body alive. Order is normal; deviance and conflict are problems to be managed.
- **Conflict approaches** (Marxism and feminism) see society as made up of groups with opposing interests. Powerful groups, such as the ruling class for Marxists or men for feminists, shape institutions to benefit themselves. Apparent agreement may simply reflect the ideas of the powerful.

A second debate runs through the course: **structure versus agency**. The specification's aims refer to "relationships and tension between social structures and individual agency". Structural theories (functionalism and Marxism) stress how institutions shape people. Interactionism stresses agency, meaning people's ability to interpret situations and make choices.

**Evaluation:** Consensus theories explain stability well but struggle with inequality and rapid change. Conflict theories explain inequality but can overstate division; many people do share values across class and gender lines. Neither label fits interactionism neatly, because it studies small-scale interaction rather than society as a whole.

## The four perspectives

### Functionalism

Functionalists ask what each institution does for society. Durkheim saw education as transmitting norms and values and turning individuals into "a united whole". Parsons argued the family has two basic functions, primary socialisation and the stabilisation of adult personalities, and that schools run on meritocratic principles. Merton, Albert Cohen, Davis and Moore, and Willmott and Young are the other writers the specification describes as functionalist.

**Strengths:** explains how societies hold together; links institutions to each other. **Limitations:** the specification notes that Parsons has been criticised for an idealised, middle-class picture of family life; functionalism tends to ignore power and conflict.

### Marxism

Marxists see capitalist society as divided between the bourgeoisie, who own the means of production, and the proletariat, who sell their labour. Institutions help keep this system going. Bowles and Gintis argue schooling corresponds to the workplace and produces an obedient workforce. Zaretsky sees the family as a prop to the capitalist economy.

**Strengths:** explains persistent class inequality, such as the class gaps in Halsey's education data. **Limitations:** focuses on class and can neglect gender and ethnicity; Willis, though a Marxist, showed that pupils do not passively accept school values.

### Feminism

Feminists see society as patriarchal, meaning male-dominated. Oakley questions the idea of the "conventional family". Delphy and Leonard argue men benefit from the unpaid work of women in the family. Walby identifies six patriarchal structures. Heidensohn and Carlen use control theory to explain women's crime and conformity.

**Strengths:** made gender, housework and the experiences of women visible in sociology. **Limitations:** the specification notes Heidensohn has been criticised for generalising about all women; feminists also disagree among themselves about whether capitalism or men are the main cause of women's position.

### Interactionism

Interactionists study small-scale, everyday interaction and the meanings people give to it. Becker argues that an act becomes deviant only when others define it as deviant. A label can become a **master status** and lead to a **self-fulfilling prophecy**. In education, labelling ideas are often applied to how teachers treat pupils in different sets or bands, a question Ball's study of Beachside Comprehensive explored by comparing a banded cohort with a mixed-ability cohort.

**Strengths:** shows how people actively create social life; explains why the same act is treated differently depending on who does it. **Limitations:** says less about where labels come from, so it can overlook the wider structures of class and gender power.

## Durkheim, Marx and Weber in context

The specification asks for a "sense of time and place": where each writer lived, what was changing around them, how that shaped their view of the world, and what they gave to sociology.

### Émile Durkheim (1858-1917)

**Time and place:** France during the Third Republic, a period of industrialisation in which older ties of religion and community could no longer be taken for granted. **View of the world:** his central question was what holds a modern society together. He saw shared norms and values, passed on through institutions such as education, as the answer. Without clear rules people face anomie, a state of normlessness. **Contribution:** Durkheim argued sociology should study "social facts", ways of acting that exist outside the individual and constrain them, in a scientific way (*The Rules of Sociological Method*, 1895). In 1895 he set up the first European department of sociology. His study *Suicide* (1897) used statistics to argue that even a very personal act varies with social integration. Merton later developed the idea of anomie to explain crime.

**Evaluation:** Durkheim showed that society shapes individuals, a founding idea of the subject. Critics say he overstated consensus, and interactionists argue the official statistics used in *Suicide* are themselves social constructions.

### Karl Marx (1818-1883)

**Time and place:** born in Trier in Prussia, Marx lived in London from 1849. The specification describes him as "writing in 19th-century Britain", the first industrial capitalist society, with factory labour and sharp divisions between owners and workers. **View of the world:** class struggle drives social change. Ownership of the means of production gives the ruling class economic and political power, and ruling class ideology makes capitalism seem natural. Workers who accept this show false class consciousness. He expected classes to polarise and a proletarian revolution to follow. **Contribution:** founded the conflict tradition and put economic inequality at the centre of sociology. *The Communist Manifesto*, written with Engels, appeared in 1848 and the first volume of *Capital* in 1867.

**Evaluation:** the predicted revolution did not happen in Britain, and the middle class grew rather than shrank. The specification notes that New Right theorists stress capitalism's benefits and opportunities for social mobility, while some sociologists believe Marx still gives the best explanation of capitalist society.

### Max Weber (1864-1920)

**Time and place:** Germany, which was industrialising fast and building large state and business bureaucracies. The specification notes he wrote in the early 20th century and died in 1920. **View of the world:** stratification has more than one dimension. Class depends on market situation, which shapes life chances; status (prestige) and party (groups seeking power) can also unite or divide people. He identified three types of authority, which the specification calls three sources of power: traditional, charismatic and rational-legal. Unlike Marx, he saw no polarisation and expected the middle class to expand. **Contribution:** Weber stressed understanding the meanings behind social action. In *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904-05) he argued that religious ideas helped capitalism develop, so ideas, not only economics, can cause change. His focus on meaning influenced later interactionist approaches.

**Evaluation:** his model fits modern societies with large professional and service classes. Marxists reply that ownership is still the main division, and the specification notes New Right critics accuse both traditions of bias.

**Worked example (classical sociologists):** *Knowledge:* Marx saw class as ownership of the means of production; Weber saw it as market situation. *Application:* In a modern call centre, managers and operators both own nothing, yet managers earn more and have more security. *Analysis:* Weber's idea of market situation explains this difference better than Marx's two-class model. *Evaluation:* Yet a Marxist would note that both groups depend on the owners for their jobs, so the basic division Marx identified still exists.

## How sociological ideas change over time

Ideas reflect the period in which they were written. Appendix B gives several examples you can use:

- Parsons (1959) wrote about the American family and was later criticised for an idealised middle-class picture.
- The Rapoports' (1982) types of family diversity predate gay and lesbian households becoming more openly accepted; Oakley's paper predates civil partnerships and same-sex marriage.
- Willis (1977) found anti-school boys moving into manual work that was relatively easily obtained in the 1970s.
- Halsey and colleagues (1980) studied males only, which may have affected the findings.
- Devine (1992) revisited Luton to test claims made by Goldthorpe and Lockwood in the 1960s and did not find the "privatised instrumentalism" they predicted.
- Walby (1990) argued that patriarchy has changed from private to public forms.

The general pattern: functionalism was influential in the mid-20th century; conflict and feminist approaches grew from the 1970s; New Right ideas, such as Murray's (1984), challenged both. Each new approach changes how we see the social world.

## The interrelationship between the core areas

The core areas of families, education, crime and deviance and social stratification are linked, and answers that make these links show understanding. Social class affects family life, educational achievement (Halsey), explanations of crime (Cohen on status frustration), the risk of being labelled (Becker) and life chances. Gender links Oakley's housework research to Heidensohn on social control and Walby on paid work. Socialisation starts in the family and continues in school; when it breaks down, functionalists link this to crime.

## Using research methods in context

The specification asks you to apply methods to each topic, and suggests teachers may set small-scale projects so you see the practical difficulties researchers face. Theory often guides method: Durkheim's scientific approach favoured statistics, while interactionists prefer qualitative methods such as participant observation, the method Ball used at Beachside. ## Common errors

- Calling interactionism a consensus or conflict theory without explanation. It focuses on interaction, not society-wide agreement or conflict.
- Treating "feminism" as one view. Writers such as Delphy and Leonard blame men rather than capitalists; others link women's position to capitalism.
- Giving Durkheim, Marx and Weber no context. The specification asks for time and place, so say when and where they wrote.
- Mixing up power and authority. Authority is power accepted as legitimate.
- Defining norms and values as the same thing. Values are beliefs; norms are the rules of behaviour that follow from them.
- Evaluating with "this theory is old". Explain what has changed, for example the growth of the middle class against Marx's prediction.
