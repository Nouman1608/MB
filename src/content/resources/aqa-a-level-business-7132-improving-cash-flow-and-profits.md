This guide goes deeper into section **3.5.4 Making financial decisions: improving cash flow and profits** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification's content is:

- **methods of improving cash flow**, which you should be able to assess
- **methods of improving profits and profitability**, which you should be able to assess
- **difficulties in improving cash flow and profit**.

## Two different problems

A business has a **cash flow problem** when money going out arrives before money coming in, so it cannot pay bills when they fall due. It has a **profit problem** when revenue does not cover costs over a period. A business can have either without the other: a fast-growing firm may be profitable but short of cash because it pays for stock and wages before customers pay; a firm with plenty of cash from a loan may still be making losses. The methods that fix one problem can make the other worse, so each has to be judged against the case.

**Profit** is the amount (revenue minus costs). **Profitability** is profit relative to something else, such as revenue (margins) or the money invested (return on investment). A firm can raise profit by growing larger while profitability falls, if the extra profit is small compared with the extra resources.

## Methods of improving cash flow

Cash flow improves by bringing cash in **sooner or in greater amounts**, or by sending it out **later or in smaller amounts**.

### Increasing or speeding up inflows

| Method | How it works | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce credit given to customers** (shorter terms, credit checks, deposits) | Cash comes in earlier | Customers may prefer a rival offering longer credit; sales may fall |
| **Early payment discounts** | Customers pay sooner in return for a small discount | Cash arrives earlier but revenue is reduced; costly if too many take it |
| **Chase late payers** (credit control) | Reminders, charging interest, using debt collectors | Cheap and direct, but can upset customers |
| **Debt factoring** | Sell invoices for immediate cash | Fast, but the fee reduces profit |
| **Sale of surplus assets** or **sale and leaseback** | Cash from selling assets, then renting them back | One-off boost; may lose assets that could earn profit; leaseback adds regular payments |
| **Increase sales for cash**, with deposits on orders | More immediate money | Needs marketing; discounting may reduce profit |
| **Raise finance** (loan, overdraft, share issue) | Brings in new cash | Not an improvement in trading; adds cost or dilutes ownership |

### Reducing or delaying outflows

| Method | How it works | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Delay payments to suppliers** (longer trade credit) | Cash stays in the business for longer | Free finance, but may damage relationships or lose prompt-payment discounts |
| **Lease rather than buy** equipment | Spread the cost over time | Smaller outflow now, but total cost over time is higher |
| **Reduce inventory** (for example JIT) | Less cash tied up in stock | Risk of stock-outs and lost sales |
| **Cut costs and delay non-essential spending** | Lower payments | Cuts may harm quality or growth |
| **Negotiate longer repayment terms on loans**, or pay by instalments | Smaller payments now | Interest may increase total cost |

### Worked examples

**Early payment discount.** A business has customers owing £120,000 on 60-day terms. It offers a 2% discount for payment within 10 days. Suppose customers owing £48,000 accept the offer.

- Cash received early = £48,000 × 0.98 = **£47,040**.
- Cost of the discount = £48,000 × 0.02 = **£960**.

The business gets cash about 50 days earlier at a cost of £960. Expressed as an annual rate, the cost is about 2 ÷ 98 × 365 ÷ 50 = 14.9% a year. That is cheaper than a high-interest overdraft but more expensive than a standard loan, so it is worthwhile only if the alternative finance is dearer, or if the cash is urgently needed.

**Delaying supplier payments.** A business buys £90,000 of materials a month. Extending payment terms from 30 to 45 days keeps roughly £90,000 ÷ 30 × 15 = **£45,000** more cash in the business, once. It is a one-off gain, and the benefit disappears if the supplier cuts credit or if prompt-payment discounts are lost.

**Lease or buy.** Buying a machine costs £60,000 in cash now. Leasing costs £1,500 a month, which is £18,000 in the first year. Leasing saves £42,000 of cash in year one, but if the machine is leased for five years, the total outflow is 5 × £18,000 = £90,000, which is £30,000 more than buying.

## Methods of improving profits and profitability

Profit rises if **revenue rises faster than costs**, or **costs fall faster than revenue**.

### Increasing revenue

- **Raise prices** (if demand is inelastic, or the brand allows it).
- **Increase sales volume** through marketing, new markets or new products.
- **Change the sales mix** towards products with higher margins.
- **Improve the marketing mix** (promotion, distribution).
- **Add value** with branding, design or service.

### Reducing costs

- **Negotiate with suppliers** or change supplier to cut variable costs.
- **Improve efficiency and productivity**, cut waste, reduce defects (see operations).
- **Reduce overheads**: premises, energy, administration.
- **Use technology** and automation to cut unit costs.
- **Reduce staff costs** through better scheduling, redundancy or relocation.
- **Outsource** activities to cheaper providers.

### Worked example: price against costs

Brindle Bags sells 20,000 bags a year at £10. Variable cost per bag is £6 and fixed costs are £50,000.

- Profit now = 20,000 × (£10 − £6) − £50,000 = 80,000 − 50,000 = **£30,000**. Revenue is £200,000, so profit margin = 30 ÷ 200 = **15%**.

**Option A: raise price to £10.50.** Suppose volume falls 8% to 18,400.

- Contribution per bag = £4.50; total contribution = 18,400 × £4.50 = £82,800.
- Profit = £82,800 − £50,000 = **£32,800** (up £2,800). Revenue falls to £193,200, but margin rises to 32,800 ÷ 193,200 = **17.0%**.
- If volume fell by 15% to 17,000: profit = 17,000 × £4.50 − £50,000 = **£26,500**, lower than now. So the result depends on how customers react (price elasticity).

**Option B: cut variable cost to £5.50 by changing supplier** (volume and price unchanged).

- Contribution = £4.50; profit = 20,000 × £4.50 − £50,000 = **£40,000**, an increase of £10,000. The margin becomes 40 ÷ 200 = **20%**. But a cheaper supplier may mean lower quality or less reliable delivery, which could reduce sales.

Notice the point that a price rise can increase profit while reducing revenue. That is why profit and revenue targets can conflict.

## Cash flow and profit: methods that affect both

| Method | Effect on cash | Effect on profit |
|---|---|---|
| Early payment discount | Improves | Reduces (discount cost) |
| Debt factoring | Improves | Reduces (fee) |
| Delaying supplier payments | Improves | No immediate effect (possibly lost discounts) |
| Leasing instead of buying | Improves now | Lower in the long run if lease payments exceed depreciation and interest |
| Price rise | Improves if volume holds | Improves if volume falls less than the contribution gain |
| Cost cutting | Improves | Improves (if quality holds) |
| Reducing inventory | Improves (releases cash) | Small effect, possibly lower storage costs |
| Selling an asset | Improves once | May lower future profit if the asset earned income |

## Difficulties in improving cash flow and profit

- **Customers' reactions**: price rises, shorter credit and tougher chasing may lose customers.
- **Suppliers' reactions**: they may withdraw credit or raise prices.
- **Competition and market conditions**: rivals may undercut; a recession reduces demand; interest rates affect borrowing costs.
- **Trade-offs**: cutting costs can reduce quality and long-term competitiveness; delaying payments may harm relationships; cutting marketing may reduce future sales.
- **Time lags**: some improvements (new products, training) take time to produce results while cash is needed now.
- **Uncertainty**: forecasts may be wrong, and a measure that works in one year may not work in another.
- **Costs of the measure itself**: factoring fees, discounts, redundancy payments, rebranding.
- **Ethical issues**: squeezing small suppliers by paying late, or cutting staff costs by poor working conditions, risk reputation damage.
- **One-off gains**: selling assets or extending supplier credit helps once but does not fix a lasting problem.
- **Legal limits**: for instance, rules on late payments and employment.

## Assessing which method to use

Ask:

1. **Which problem is it**: short-term cash gap, or lasting profit shortage?
2. **How fast** is the improvement needed?
3. **What does it cost**, directly and in lost sales or goodwill?
4. **Is it sustainable**, or a one-off?
5. **What is the risk** to quality, reputation and relationships?

A sensible plan usually combines methods, such as tighter credit control and a review of overheads for cash flow, and efficiency improvements with a modest price rise for profit. For a lasting profit problem, quick cash measures treat the symptom, not the cause.

## Technique for exam questions

- Say whether the issue is cash flow or profit, and use the right measures.
- Use numbers from the case to show the size of the effect.
- Give a benefit and a drawback for each method, then judge which is best for this business in this situation, and why.

## Common mistakes

- Treating cash flow and profit as the same problem.
- Recommending a loan as a way of improving trading cash flow.
- Forgetting that a price rise may reduce volume.
- Treating a one-off asset sale as a lasting solution.
- Ignoring the reaction of customers and suppliers.
- Giving a list of methods with no judgement.
