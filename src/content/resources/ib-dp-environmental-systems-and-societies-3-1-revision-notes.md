These notes condense subtopic 3.1 Biodiversity and evolution of IB DP Environmental Systems and
Societies (first assessment 2026). The subtopic title is provisional, because the IB's subject brief
does not list Topic 3's subtopics. The [3.1 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-3-1/)

## Condensed recall

**Levels of biodiversity**
- Genetic (alleles within a species), species (number and relative abundance), habitat (range of
  ecosystems). More habitats → more species → more genetic variety overall.
- Low genetic diversity → a single disease or climate shift can wipe out a population.

**Variation**
- Mutation: new alleles, mostly neutral or harmful, occasionally beneficial.
- Sexual reproduction: meiosis and fertilisation create new combinations.
- Only heritable variation can be selected.

**Natural selection examples**
- Peppered moth (*Biston betularia*): dark form spread in sooty 19th-century industrial areas,
  declined after air pollution fell; selective predation by birds.
- Antibiotic and insecticide resistance: the chemical selects resistant individuals; it does not
  create resistance.

**Isolation**
- Geographical (barrier) → allopatric speciation.
- Temporal (different breeding times) and behavioural (different courtship) → can cause sympatric
  speciation.
- Speciation is complete when populations can no longer interbreed to give fertile offspring.

**Patterns of evolution**
- Adaptive radiation: one ancestor → many species filling many niches (Darwin's finches; mammals
  after the end-Cretaceous extinction).
- Gradualism versus punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge and Gould, 1972).

**Plate tectonics**
- Separation: Gondwana break-up isolated Australia → marsupial diversification.
- Joining: Isthmus of Panama (roughly 3 million years ago) → land mammals crossed between the
  Americas; marine populations split.
- Mountain building: new habitats and barriers.

**Mass extinctions (approximate dates)**
- End-Ordovician ~444 Ma: glaciation, sea-level fall.
- Late Devonian ~372 Ma: ocean oxygen loss, climate change.
- End-Permian ~252 Ma: Siberian Traps volcanism; largest known.
- End-Triassic ~201 Ma: volcanism as Pangaea split.
- End-Cretaceous ~66 Ma: asteroid impact (plus volcanism).
- Present: caused by one species, over centuries; recovery after past events took millions of
  years.

## Method in steps: explaining speciation

```
1. A population shows heritable variation.
2. Name the isolating mechanism (geographical, temporal, behavioural).
3. State that gene flow stops.
4. Different selection pressures act on each population.
5. Favourable alleles increase in frequency over many generations.
6. Populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
```

Mini example (invented): two populations of a frog in one pond begin calling at different times of
night. Females respond only to calls at their own time, so the populations stop interbreeding
(temporal and behavioural isolation). Over many generations, differences accumulate until they are
separate species, without any physical barrier: sympatric speciation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Genetic diversity | Range of genes and alleles within a species or population |
| Mutation | Random change in DNA that can create a new allele |
| Natural selection | Better-suited individuals survive and reproduce more, passing on their alleles |
| Selection pressure | An environmental factor that affects survival and reproduction |
| Allele frequency | Proportion of a gene's copies in a population that are one allele |
| Industrial melanism | Increase of dark forms in polluted environments |
| Allopatric speciation | Speciation after geographical separation |
| Sympatric speciation | Speciation without geographical separation |
| Adaptive radiation | Rapid diversification of one ancestor into many species |
| Punctuated equilibrium | Long stability broken by rapid bursts of change |
| Gondwana | Southern supercontinent that broke apart |
| Mass extinction | Loss of a large share of species in a geologically short time |
| Background extinction rate | Normal rate of extinction between mass extinctions |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Variation vs adaptation:** differences between individuals vs an inherited feature that improves
  survival in a particular environment.
- **Geographical vs reproductive isolation:** a physical barrier vs differences in timing, behaviour
  or anatomy within one area.
- **Speciation vs adaptive radiation:** formation of any new species vs rapid diversification of one
  ancestor into many species filling different niches.
- **Background extinction vs mass extinction:** the normal low rate of loss vs a large share of
  species lost in a geologically short time.

## Exam traps

- **Selection acts on existing variation.** Never write "the moths turned dark to hide".
- **Percentages, not counts.** Compare samples of different size as percentages.
- **Gene flow is its own point.** Isolation must stop it.
- **Sympatric is possible.** Not all speciation needs a barrier.
- **Mass extinction answers need detail.** Give an approximate time and a named cause.
- **Present versus past.** Cause and rate are the key differences.

## Self-test

1. Give two sources of heritable variation.
2. Why is a population with low genetic diversity vulnerable?
3. What selection pressure explains industrial melanism?
4. Does an antibiotic cause resistance? Explain.
5. Distinguish allopatric from sympatric speciation.
6. Give an example of temporal isolation.
7. What is adaptive radiation? Give an example.
8. What does punctuated equilibrium describe?
9. How did the Isthmus of Panama affect land and marine species differently?
10. Which mass extinction was the largest, and what is its leading explanation?
11. In a sample, 84 of 350 beetles are green. Calculate the percentage.
12. Give two differences between present biodiversity loss and past mass extinctions.

### Answers

1. Mutation; sexual reproduction (meiosis and fertilisation).
2. It may lack alleles that would allow some individuals to survive a new disease or environmental
   change.
3. Selective predation by birds on moths that contrast with their background.
4. No: it kills susceptible bacteria, so resistant individuals that already exist survive and
   reproduce.
5. Allopatric follows geographical separation; sympatric happens in the same area through other
   isolating mechanisms.
6. Two populations breeding in different months or at different times of day.
7. Rapid diversification of one ancestor into many species filling different niches, such as
   Darwin's finches.
8. Long periods of little change interrupted by short bursts of rapid evolution.
9. Land mammals could cross between North and South America, while Atlantic and Pacific marine
   populations were separated.
10. The end-Permian, about 252 million years ago, linked to massive volcanic eruptions.
11. 84 ÷ 350 × 100 = **24%**.
12. It is caused by one species; it is happening over centuries rather than many thousands of years.
