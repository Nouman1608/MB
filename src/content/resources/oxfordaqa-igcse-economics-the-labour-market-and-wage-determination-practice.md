## Questions

**1.** Which one of these would shift the supply of labour in an occupation to the left? A) A rise in pay in similar occupations B) More training places C) A rise in demand for the product D) An increase in the working-age population **[1]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by the labour market. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows the market for fruit pickers in illustrative Region B. Wages are in cu per hour.

| Wage | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand (workers) | 90 | 78 | 66 | 54 | 42 | 30 |
| Supply (workers) | 18 | 30 | 42 | 54 | 66 | 78 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium wage and the number of pickers employed. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total wage bill per hour at equilibrium. **[1]**

**4.** Demand in the market in question 3 rises by 24 pickers at every wage. Demand is now 174 - 12W, where W is the wage. Supply is still 12W - 42.

**(a)** Find the new equilibrium wage and employment. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out how much the wage and employment have risen, as percentages of their question 3 values. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one possible cause of the rise in demand. **[2]**

**5.** The government sets a legal minimum wage of 10 cu in the market in question 3.

**(a)** Calculate the surplus of labour at this wage. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one group that gains and one group that loses. **[4]**

**6.** Explain two reasons why a hospital surgeon earns more than a shop assistant. **[4]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why two bus drivers working for different companies might earn different pay. **[4]**

**8.** Occupation X pays 18 cu per hour and occupation Y pays 9 cu per hour.

**(a)** Calculate by what percentage X pays more than Y. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate weekly pay for each for a 38-hour week. **[2]**
**(c)** X's wage then rises by 5% and Y's by 10%. Calculate both new wages and say what happens to the gap in cu and to the percentage gap. **[4]**

**9.** A training college opens, adding 24 workers to labour supply at every wage in the market in question 3. Supply becomes 12W - 18. Describe a diagram showing the effect and state the new wage and employment. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how occupational and geographical immobility help explain why wage differentials persist. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss whether wages are determined only by demand and supply. **[8]**

**12.** In illustrative Country C the market for care assistants has demand of 400 - 10W and supply of 10W - 100. The employer group pays 20 cu per hour.

**(a)** Calculate the shortage of care assistants at 20 cu. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether raising the wage is the best way to remove the shortage. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A [1] because workers move to better-paid similar jobs, so fewer offer to work at each wage here. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Option B and D would shift supply right and C shifts demand, so check which curve each option moves before choosing.

**2.** A market in which firms (employers) demand labour [1] and households supply their work, which sets the wage and employment [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A good answer names both sides and what the market sets; a bare "where people work" is incomplete.

**3. (a)** Demand equals supply at 8 cu [1] with 54 pickers employed [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 8 × 54 = **432 cu per hour** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the multiplication and give the unit; a figure without the wage and employment it comes from is hard to credit.

**4. (a)** 174 - 12W = 12W - 42, so 24W = 216 [1]; **W = 9 cu and 66 pickers** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Wage: (9 - 8) / 8 × 100 = 12.5% rise [1]. Employment: (66 - 54) / 54 × 100 = **22.2%** rise [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Demand for pickers is derived from demand for fruit [1], so higher consumer demand for fruit makes firms want more pickers (or higher productivity makes each picker more valuable) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the cause to labour specifically; "more people buy" needs the step that firms therefore hire more.

**5. (a)** At 10 cu demand is 150 - 120 = 30 [1] and supply is 120 - 42 = 78, so the **surplus is 48 workers** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Gain: pickers who keep their jobs earn 10 cu instead of 8 cu [1], an increase of 2 cu per hour, because the wage is above equilibrium [1]. Lose: employment falls from 54 to 30 [1], so 24 workers lose jobs, and farmers face higher costs [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name each group and give a consequence supported by the figures; saying "workers gain" alone does not say how.

**6.** Reason 1: surgery needs many years of costly training [1], so few people can supply the labour and the wage is higher [1]. Reason 2: a surgeon's work has a high value, with productivity and demand to match [1], so employers pay more to hire one [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each reason to supply or demand. Two causes named but not developed gain little.

**7.** Reason 1: company size or profitability differs [1], so a firm with higher profits can afford to pay more [1]. Reason 2: experience or length of service [1], since longer-serving drivers are more productive and are paid more [1]. (Other valid reasons: location, shifts, bonuses.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Within-occupation answers should refer to the worker or employer, not to the occupation as a whole.

**8. (a)** (18 - 9) / 9 × 100 [1] = **100%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** X: 18 × 38 = **684 cu** [1]. Y: 9 × 38 = **342 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** X: 18 × 1.05 = **18.90 cu**; Y: 9 × 1.10 = **9.90 cu** [1] [1]. The gap in cu is 18.90 - 9.90 = 9.00, so unchanged [1]. The percentage gap falls to 18.90 / 9.90 = 1.909, so X pays 90.9% more [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Distinguish the gap in cu from the percentage gap; a claim that the differential "narrowed" needs to say which measure.

**9.** With the wage up the side and workers along the bottom, supply shifts right from S to a new supply curve [1] while demand is unchanged [1]. The wage falls from 8 to 7 cu [1] and employment rises from 54 to 66 [1], found from 150 - 12W = 12W - 18. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Describing a diagram means naming axes, the curve that shifts, its direction and the new equilibrium.

**10.** Occupational immobility: workers lack the skills for jobs that pay more [1], so supply in high-paid jobs stays small and the wage stays high [1]. Geographical immobility: workers cannot or will not move to areas with higher wages [1], so shortages and surpluses persist and local pay gaps remain [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Define each type through its effect on the labour supply and then say what that does to pay.

**11.** Demand and supply are central: product demand and productivity set labour demand, while training and alternatives set supply [1], and where they meet fixes wage and employment [1]. Other factors alter this: trade unions can bargain wages above equilibrium [1], which cuts jobs; a minimum wage can do the same [1]. A large employer with few rivals can hold wages down [1] because workers have few alternatives. Differences in experience and performance pay also change pay for individuals [1]. Judgement: demand and supply set the overall level in most cases [1], particularly over the long term, but union power or regulation matter more in particular jobs, so wages are not determined only by demand and supply [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach a clear conclusion that weighs the factors, rather than simply listing influences.

**12. (a)** At 20 cu demand is 400 - 200 = 200 [1] and supply is 200 - 100 = 100, so the **shortage is 100** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Support: setting demand equal to supply gives 20W = 500, so W = 25 cu [1], where demand and supply are both 150 [1]. A higher wage pulls in more workers [1] and removes the shortage with 50 extra staff [1]. Against: the extra pay raises costs for employers and may be passed on to fees [1]; training takes time, so supply may respond slowly [1]; other options include training or recruiting from abroad [1]. Judgement: raising the wage is probably the quickest way to fill vacancies, but the best solution depends on cost and time [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures given and then weigh alternatives; an evaluation that has no comparison cannot support a judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a cause without linking it to the demand or supply curve.
- Calling a change in the wage a shift in the curve.
- Mixing up shortage and surplus.
- Leaving out units or the base figure in percentage changes.
- Using "the occupation" instead of a worker or firm for within-occupation differences.
- Listing factors in a discuss or evaluate question without a conclusion.
- Describing a diagram without naming axes and the new equilibrium.
