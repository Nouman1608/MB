## Questions

**1.** A firm sells 250 units for a total revenue of 4,000. **(a)** Calculate average revenue. **[1]** **(b)** Explain why the average revenue curve is the firm's demand curve. **[2]**

**2.** A firm faces the demand curve P = 60 − 5Q, where Q is units sold per week. **(a)** Calculate total revenue when 5 units and 6 units are sold. **[2]** **(b)** Calculate the marginal revenue of the 6th unit. **[1]** **(c)** The 7th unit would sell at a price of 25 on all units. Explain why total revenue falls when it is sold. **[2]**

**3.** A farm is a price taker and sells crates of produce at 18 each. It currently sells 400 crates. **(a)** Calculate total revenue, average revenue and the marginal revenue of the 401st crate. **[3]** **(b)** Explain why marginal revenue equals average revenue for this farm. **[1]**

**4.** A firm's demand curve is P = 90 − 3Q. **(a)** Calculate total revenue at 14, 15 and 16 units. **[3]** **(b)** Calculate the marginal revenue of the 16th unit. **[1]**

**5.** Firm H sells 3 units at 45 each. To sell a 4th unit it must cut its price to 40 on all four units. **(a)** Calculate the marginal revenue of the 4th unit. **[2]** **(b)** Explain why this is less than 40. **[2]**

**6.** In a year, Firm F has total revenue of 84,000 and total costs of 71,500, which include normal profit. It sells 5,000 units. **(a)** Calculate its profit. **[1]** **(b)** Calculate profit per unit. **[1]** **(c)** State whether it earns abnormal profit and justify your answer. **[1]** **(d)** Explain what is meant by normal profit. **[2]**

**7.** A retailer has revenue of 118,000 and pays 93,000 for rent, stock and wages. The owner could earn 28,000 in another job. **(a)** Calculate accounting profit. **[1]** **(b)** Calculate the profit left after counting the owner's opportunity cost, and state whether it is above or below normal profit. **[2]** **(c)** Explain what is likely to happen in the long run and how this shows a role for profit in a market economy. **[2]**

**8.** Researchers at a university in Country B find a plant-based coating that keeps fruit fresh for longer. Two years later, a food firm sells the first coated fruit in shops. **(a)** Identify the invention and the innovation. **[2]** **(b)** Explain why the economy gains mainly from innovation. **[2]**

**9.** A plant has 12 workers who make 960 units a week. After adding a new machine they make 1,320 units a week. Each worker costs 600 a week and the machine costs 1,800 a week to lease. **(a)** Find output per worker before and after the machine, and the percentage change. **[3]** **(b)** Calculate cost per unit before (labour only) and after (labour plus lease), and the percentage fall. **[3]** **(c)** The firm sells only 960 units a week after installing the machine. Calculate the cost per unit and explain what it shows. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse how technological change may affect the structure of a market. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that creative destruction does more good than harm. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) 4,000 ÷ 250 = 16 [1] (b) TR = P × Q, so AR = (P × Q) ÷ Q = P [1]. The demand curve shows the price at which each quantity is bought, so AR plotted against quantity is the same line [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A bare statement that "AR equals demand" earns little; show the step AR = TR ÷ Q = P and then link price to quantity demanded.

**2.** (a) At 5 units: 35 × 5 = 175 [1]. At 6 units: 30 × 6 = 180 [1]. (b) MR = 180 − 175 = 5 [1]. (c) The 7th unit adds 25 to revenue, but the price on the 6 earlier units falls by 5 each, a loss of 30 [1]. The net change is −5, so TR falls from 180 to 175 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Work out the price for each quantity first. Using the price of the previous row for the TR calculation is a common slip.

**3.** (a) TR = 18 × 400 = 7,200 [1]. AR = 7,200 ÷ 400 = 18 [1]. MR of the 401st crate = 18 × 401 − 7,200 = 7,218 − 7,200 = 18 [1]. (b) The farm can sell each extra crate at the same market price, so no earlier crate loses revenue [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A price taker's MR equals its price. Writing this for a firm with a downward-sloping demand curve is a basic error that costs the explanation.

**4.** (a) At 14: 48 × 14 = 672 [1]. At 15: 45 × 15 = 675 [1]. At 16: 42 × 16 = 672 [1]. (b) MR of the 16th unit = 672 − 675 = −3 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative MR must keep its minus sign, which shows that TR has begun to fall.

**5.** (a) TR with 3 units = 135 and with 4 units = 160 [1]. MR = 160 − 135 = 25 [1]. (b) The 4th unit sells for 40 [1], but the price cut costs 5 on each of the 3 earlier units, a loss of 15, so MR = 40 − 15 = 25 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The explanation needs the two effects, the gain from the extra unit and the loss on earlier units, with the figures.

**6.** (a) 84,000 − 71,500 = 12,500 [1]. (b) 12,500 ÷ 5,000 = 2.50 [1]. (c) Yes, because costs already include normal profit, so the remaining 12,500 is abnormal profit [1]. (d) Normal profit is the least return that stops the entrepreneur switching to another activity [1], equal to what they could earn in their next best use, so it is counted as a cost [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Connect your judgement on abnormal profit to the fact that costs include normal profit. Stating "yes" with no reason gains nothing.

**7.** (a) 118,000 − 93,000 = 25,000 [1]. (b) 25,000 − 28,000 = −3,000 [1], which is below normal profit [1]. (c) The owner earns more in the other job, so in the long run is likely to leave [1]. Profit works as a signal and discipline: resources move to activities with a higher reward [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep accounting profit and profit after opportunity cost separate. Treating the 28,000 as a loss rather than a cost misses the point.

**8.** (a) The discovery of the coating is the invention [1]. The first sale of coated fruit is the innovation [1]. (b) An invention that stays in the laboratory adds no output, jobs or choice [1]. Commercial use puts the new product or process into production and lets consumers and other firms benefit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Do not define the terms alone. Identify each in the context given.

**9.** (a) Before: 960 ÷ 12 = 80 per worker [1]. After: 1,320 ÷ 12 = 110 [1]. Rise: (110 − 80) ÷ 80 = 37.5% [1]. (b) Before: 12 × 600 = 7,200, and 7,200 ÷ 960 = 7.50 [1]. After: 7,200 + 1,800 = 9,000, and 9,000 ÷ 1,320 = 6.82 [1]. Fall: (7.50 − 6.82) ÷ 7.50 = 9.1% [1]. (c) 9,000 ÷ 960 = 9.38, above 7.50 [1]. The new machine only cuts unit cost if enough output is sold to spread its fixed cost [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Include the lease in the "after" cost. Comparing labour cost only with total cost gives a misleading result.

**10.** Lower barriers: technology such as online selling can cut start-up costs [1], so more firms can enter and the market becomes more competitive [1]. Higher barriers: costly research and development, patents and network effects favour firms that innovate first [1], so a few firms may dominate and others struggle to enter [1]. Link: early abnormal profit can fund further research, strengthening the leader [1]. Alternatively, a new technology may replace incumbents altogether, changing which firms lead the market [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show a chain from the technology to the barrier to the number of firms. Stating only that "markets change" is not analysis.

**11.** Creative destruction is the replacement of old products, methods and firms by innovations [1]. Benefits: higher productivity and lower costs can reduce prices and raise living standards [1], and new products give consumers more choice [1]. Costs: workers in declining firms lose jobs, and skills and regional industries may be lost [1], and the process can be slow to reverse for those affected [1]. Link to profit: innovators earn temporary abnormal profit, which rewards and signals, until imitators enter [1]. Evaluation: the balance depends on how quickly workers can retrain or move [1], on how strong competition is and how long patents last [1], and on whether gains and losses fall on the same people. Judgement: the gains probably outweigh losses over time, provided displaced workers are supported and monopoly profits do not last [1]. A reasoned conclusion with a condition [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Aim for a conclusion that weighs benefits against costs and says what it depends on. A list of pros and cons without a judgement stops short of evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using MR = P for a firm that faces a downward-sloping demand curve.
- Calculating TR with the wrong price for the quantity.
- Dropping the minus sign on negative marginal revenue.
- Treating the output with the highest TR as the one with the highest profit.
- Mixing up accounting profit with profit after the owner's opportunity cost.
- Defining invention and innovation without applying them to the context.
- Comparing before and after costs without including the new fixed cost.
- Writing evaluation as a list of points with no overall judgement.
