## Supply-side policies: the big idea

Demand-side policies try to change total spending. Supply-side policies try to raise the economy's capacity to produce, so that more output can be made at lower cost. In words: the long-run supply curve of the whole economy shifts to the right. Potential output rises, so the economy can grow without prices being pushed up.

Suppose potential output in Country A rises from 800 to 840 units.

`(840 - 800) / 800 × 100 = 5%`

Capacity is 5% higher. Whether actual output rises too depends on demand, which is one reason these policies work slowly.

## Policy 1: investment in education and training

**How it works:** schooling, apprenticeships and retraining raise the skills of workers. A skilled worker produces more per hour, so labour productivity rises and unit costs fall. Workers can also move more easily into growing industries.

**Worked example.** A government pays 1,200 to train each of 300 workers.

- Cost: `300 × 1,200 = 360,000`.
- Output per worker per week rises from 50 to 56 units: `(56 - 50) / 50 × 100 = 12%`.
- Extra output a year (50 working weeks): `6 × 300 × 50 = 90,000` units.
- If each extra unit adds 8 in value: `90,000 × 8 = 720,000` a year.
- Payback: `360,000 / 720,000 = 0.5` years, or 6 months.

Now suppose only 180 of the 300 end up using the new skills. Extra units become `6 × 180 × 50 = 54,000`, worth `54,000 × 8 = 432,000`. Payback lengthens to `360,000 / 432,000 ≈ 0.83` years, or 10 months.

**Advantages:** higher productivity and growth; lower structural unemployment because workers match vacancies better; higher earnings and a possible fall in inequality; lower costs help export competitiveness.

**Disadvantages:** costly, and the money has an opportunity cost (a hospital or road not built). Results take years, because children educated today join the workforce much later. Training may not match what employers want. Trained workers can emigrate or leave for rivals.

## Policy 2: lower direct taxes

Direct taxes are charged on what a person or firm earns, such as income tax. A lower rate lets workers keep more from every extra hour. The aim is to improve incentives to work, to take a job rather than live on benefits, and to work longer.

**Worked example.** A worker earns 2,800 a month before tax. The tax rate falls from 35% to 30%.

- Take-home before: `2,800 × 0.65 = 1,820`.
- Take-home after: `2,800 × 0.70 = 1,960`.
- Gain: `140`, which is `140 / 1,820 × 100 ≈ 7.7%` more.
- An overtime hour paying 40 now leaves `40 × 0.70 = 28` instead of `40 × 0.65 = 26`.

The incentive per hour is only 2 extra. That is why the effect on hours worked is uncertain.

**Advantages:** may increase labour supply; may attract skilled workers from abroad; higher spending power helps demand.

**Disadvantages:** the government loses revenue and may have to cut spending or borrow; the gain tends to go mostly to high earners, so inequality may widen; some workers use higher pay to work fewer hours, because they can reach their target income sooner.

## Policy 3: lower taxes on business profits

**How it works:** firms keep more of each unit of profit, so investment in machinery, premises and research becomes more attractive. Lower rates may also encourage new firms and attract foreign firms.

**Worked example.** A workshop makes 72,000 profit.

- At a 30% rate it keeps `72,000 × 0.70 = 50,400`.
- At 24% it keeps `72,000 × 0.76 = 54,720`, which is 4,320 more.

Now consider the government, with 1,000 identical workshops.

- Revenue at 30%: `1,000 × 72,000 × 0.30 = 21,600,000`.
- Revenue at 24%, same profits: `1,000 × 72,000 × 0.24 = 17,280,000`, a fall of 4,320,000.
- To collect 21,600,000 at 24%, total profit must reach `21,600,000 / 0.24 = 90,000,000`, which is 90,000 per workshop, a rise of 25%.

So a cut pays for itself only if profits rise a lot.

**Advantages:** more investment, higher capacity and jobs; helps start-ups; can attract investment from overseas.

**Disadvantages:** lost revenue; firms might pay out higher profits to owners instead of investing; benefits go to owners, who tend to be wealthier; the cut is of no use to a loss-making firm.

## Policy 4: privatisation and deregulation

**Privatisation** transfers a state-owned firm to private owners. The aim is to raise efficiency, because owners seek profit and face competition.

**Deregulation** removes rules that limit entry or restrict how firms operate, such as licence limits. More firms can enter, competition rises and prices tend to fall.

**Worked example.** A state bus company carries 5,000,000 trips a year at a cost of 12 per trip but charges a fare of 10.

- Loss per trip: 2. Government subsidy: `5,000,000 × 2 = 10,000,000`.
- After privatisation, suppose cost falls to 10.5 and the fare becomes 11.
- Profit per trip: `11 - 10.5 = 0.5`, so `5,000,000 × 0.5 = 2,500,000` profit.
- Fare rise: `(11 - 10) / 10 × 100 = 10%`.

The government saves the subsidy, but passengers pay 10% more.

**Advantages:** efficiency and innovation; the government saves subsidies and may raise money from the sale; more choice.

**Disadvantages:** a private monopoly may raise prices; unprofitable but valuable services may be cut; job losses; less regulation can mean lower safety or environmental standards.

## Helping achieve government objectives

| Objective | Link |
|---|---|
| Economic growth | Higher capacity and productivity |
| Employment | Skills and incentives; but privatisation can cut jobs in the short run |
| Price stability | Lower unit costs ease cost-push pressure |
| Balance of payments | Lower costs make exports cheaper and imports less attractive |
| Reducing inequality | Training can help; tax cuts may hinder |

Link each effect with a chain: policy, change in costs or incentives, effect on output, effect on the objective.

## Evaluating supply-side policies

- **Time lag:** most take years. This suits long-run aims, not a fast fix for a recession.
- **Cost and opportunity cost:** funding training needs taxes or borrowing.
- **Uncertainty:** people may not respond to incentives as expected.
- **Fairness:** lower direct taxes and profit taxes often help the better-off most.
- **Conflict:** privatisation may raise efficiency but cost jobs, so one objective can harm another.

## Government action on externalities (3.2.3.4)

An externality is a side effect, good or bad, that reaches someone who is neither the buyer nor the seller. The market ignores it, so a good with a negative externality is over-produced, and one with a positive externality is under-produced. Social cost is the private cost with the external cost added. Social benefit is the private benefit with the external benefit added. The government's tools aim to close the gap.

### Negative externalities: tax, limits and fines

**Indirect tax.** A tax per unit raises the supplier's cost, shifts supply left, raises price and cuts quantity.

Take charcoal burners, whose smoke harms nearby residents. Demand is `Qd = 110 - 2P`, supply is `Qs = 2P - 10`, in tonnes a week.

- Equilibrium: `110 - 2P = 2P - 10`, so `P = 30` and `Q = 50`.
- The external cost is 10 per tonne. A tax of 10 changes supply to `Qs = 2(P - 10) - 10 = 2P - 30`.
- New equilibrium: `110 - 2P = 2P - 30`, so `P = 35` and `Q = 40`.
- Buyers pay 5 more. Sellers receive `35 - 10 = 25`, which is 5 less. The burden is shared equally here.
- Tax revenue: `10 × 40 = 400`.
- External cost falls from `10 × 50 = 500` to `10 × 40 = 400`.

Price elasticity of demand over this change: `(-20%) / (+16.7%) ≈ -1.2`. Where demand is inelastic, a tax raises revenue but cuts consumption by little.

**Regulation and legislation.** Limits, bans and standards. Setting a cap of 40 tonnes gives the same quantity. The price is 35, but sellers keep it: `40 × (35 - 25) = 400` more than under the tax. The cap gives firms the revenue the tax gave the government.

**Fines** enforce rules, but they work only if checking is regular and the fine exceeds the gain from breaking the rule.

### Positive externalities: subsidies, provision and information

**Subsidy.** A payment per unit lowers costs, shifts supply right and raises output.

Rooftop solar panels cut pollution for everyone. Demand is `Qd = 100 - 2P`, supply is `Qs = 10 + P`, in installations a month.

- Equilibrium: `P = 30` and `Q = 40`.
- External benefit is 6 per installation. A subsidy of 6 gives `Qs = 10 + (P + 6) = 16 + P`.
- New equilibrium: `100 - 2P = 16 + P`, so `P = 28` and `Q = 44`.
- Buyers pay 28. Sellers receive `28 + 6 = 34`.
- Cost to the government: `6 × 44 = 264`.

**Direct provision** means the state supplies a service such as schooling itself, so that more of it is available. **Information campaigns** raise awareness of benefits or harms and so work by changing demand. Campaigns are cheap but their effect is uncertain.

### Evaluating externality policies

- The government must estimate the external cost or benefit. If the estimate is wrong, the tax or subsidy is too high or too low.
- Taxes can be regressive, hurting low-income households.
- Subsidies cost taxpayers and need funding.
- Bans and limits need enforcement and can push activity into illegal markets.
- Taxes may drive production abroad if neighbouring countries have none.

## Common errors

- Describing supply-side policy as changing demand. The main effect is on capacity and costs.
- Writing "reduces unemployment" with no chain.
- Treating a tax cut as always raising revenue.
- Showing a subsidy as shifting demand rather than supply.
- Using "externality" for any harm. It must fall on a third party.
