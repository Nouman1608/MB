## Section A: short questions

**1.** Read this prompt: *"Write an article for a travel website in which you advise first-time
visitors on how to explore your city on a small budget."* Identify the purpose, the audience and the
form. **[3]**

**2.** The sentence below is from a formal letter to a local council. Rewrite it in an appropriate
register.
*"Honestly, the park is a total disaster and you lot need to sort it out ASAP."* **[2]**

**3.** Write a headline and a one-sentence standfirst for a school magazine article arguing that
homework should be replaced by reading time. **[2]**

**4.** Give **three** features that distinguish the text of a leaflet from a newspaper article. **[3]**

**5.** The following sentence contains a comma splice. Rewrite it correctly in **two** different ways.
*"The library closes at five, many students cannot get there in time."* **[2]**

**6.** Write an opening sentence for a speech to Year 7 students that uses direct address and a
rhetorical question. **[2]**

**7.** Write a counter-argument and rebuttal, in two sentences, for an article arguing that every
student should learn to cook at school. **[2]**

**8.** A student's review of a restaurant reads: *"The food was nice and the waiter was nice. It was
nice overall."* Explain **two** weaknesses and rewrite the sentence to improve it. **[4]**

## Section B: full writing tasks

Each task is marked out of 45: AO4 27 marks, AO5 18 marks. Spend about 45 minutes, including five
minutes to plan and three to proofread.

**9.** Write a **speech** for a school assembly in which you argue that students should have a say in
how the school spends its budget. **[45]**

**10.** Write a **letter** to the editor of a local newspaper responding to a proposal to close a
community swimming pool. **[45]**

**11.** Write the **text of a leaflet** for teenagers giving advice on managing screen time during
exam season. **[45]**

**12.** Write a **review** for a website of a place, event or performance you have visited or seen
recently. Your review should help readers decide whether to go. **[45]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Purpose: to advise (and inform) (1). Audience: first-time visitors to the city who have a
limited budget (1). Form: an article for a website (1).

**2.** One mark for removing informal language (no "total disaster", "you lot", "ASAP") (1); one mark
for a courteous, formal sentence that keeps the request (1). Example: *"I am concerned about the
current condition of the park and would be grateful if the council could address the problem as soon
as possible."*

**3.** One mark for a headline that is concise and signals the argument (1); one mark for a standfirst
that summarises the article's view (1). Example: *"Close the Books, Open a Book"* / *"Why twenty minutes
of reading a night would teach us more than another worksheet."*

**4.** Any three (1 each): subheadings breaking the text into sections; short paragraphs or bullet
points for quick reading; direct address to the reader throughout; a call to action or contact details;
a more practical, advisory focus than an article's developed argument. Maximum 3.

**5.** One mark each for two correct versions, such as: a full stop ("...at five. Many students...");
a semicolon ("...at five; many students..."); a conjunction ("...at five, so many students..."); or
subordination ("Because the library closes at five, many students..."). Maximum 2.

**6.** One mark for direct address (for example "you" or "everyone") (1); one mark for an appropriate
rhetorical question suited to younger students (1). Example: *"Have you ever opened your locker and
wondered where your whole week went?"*

**7.** One mark for a fair counter-argument (1); one mark for a rebuttal that answers it (1). Example:
*"Some argue that the timetable is already too full for cooking lessons. Yet a single term of practical
cooking could teach budgeting, nutrition and teamwork more memorably than any textbook."*

**8.** Two weaknesses, 1 mark each: repetitive, imprecise vocabulary ("nice" three times) (1); no
specific evidence or detail to support the judgement (1). Rewrite, up to 2 marks: precise vocabulary (1)
and a specific supporting detail (1). Example: *"The lamb was tender and fragrant with cumin, and our
waiter remembered every request without writing anything down."* Total 4.

### Section B: how to mark questions 9 to 12

The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not Pearson's published mark scheme.

| AO4 (27) | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 22–27 | Sharply focused on purpose and audience; sophisticated, sustained control of form, tone and register; well-judged structure with a deliberate ending |
| 16–21 | Effective, secure adaptation to purpose and audience; clear structure; register consistent |
| 11–15 | Clear purpose and generally appropriate form and register; ideas developed in places |
| 6–10 | Some awareness of purpose and audience; form features present but uneven |
| 1–5 | Limited communication; little sense of form or reader |

| AO5 (18) | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 15–18 | Extensive, precise vocabulary; sentence structures and punctuation used deliberately for effect; very few errors |
| 11–14 | Wide vocabulary; varied, controlled sentences; paragraphing cohesive; occasional errors |
| 7–10 | Varied vocabulary and punctuation; meaning clear; some errors |
| 4–6 | Some range of sentences and paragraphs; errors noticeable |
| 1–3 | Basic vocabulary and control; frequent errors |

**9. Indicative content (speech).** Greeting to the assembly; direct address and inclusive pronouns
("we", "our school"); a clear thesis early; reasons such as students knowing their own needs, learning
responsibility and democratic participation; a specific example (a student vote on library spending);
a counter-argument (students lack experience) answered with a proposal for a student committee advised
by staff; a memorable close and thanks.

**10. Indicative content (letter).** Formal greeting ("Dear Editor") and sign-off ("Yours faithfully");
reason for writing in the first paragraph; developed points on health, safety (learning to swim),
community and young people's activities; acknowledgement of the council's costs with suggested
alternatives (reduced hours, sponsorship, volunteer support); a clear closing request; a measured,
persuasive register throughout.

**11. Indicative content (leaflet text).** Title and subheadings ("Why it matters", "Five simple
habits", "Where to get help"); short sections; second-person advice; practical tips such as phone-free
revision blocks, notifications off, screens out of the bedroom at night; an encouraging, non-lecturing
tone suited to teenagers; a call to action. No drawings or layout boxes required.

**12. Indicative content (review).** A title naming the subject; brief context (where, when, who it
suits); evaluative judgements supported by specific detail; balance (one weakness as well as
strengths); a clear verdict or recommendation; a personal but informed voice appropriate for website
readers.

**What separates stronger answers.** They choose a register in the first sentence and keep it, develop
each point with an example, use two or three persuasive methods with purpose, and end with a planned
conclusion. Weaker answers list points, drift into an essay voice regardless of form, or stop without
an ending.
