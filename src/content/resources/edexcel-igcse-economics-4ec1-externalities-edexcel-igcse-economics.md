This guide covers section 1.1.6, Externalities, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. It is examined in Paper 1, Microeconomics and Business Economics, with a single tier of entry. All firms, places and figures below are invented.

The combined [mixed economy and externalities study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-the-mixed-economy-public-goods-privatisation-and/) introduces the ideas with a chemical plant, a vaccination clinic and a congested road. This page uses new cases and focuses on classifying costs and benefits correctly and using the formulae in every direction. Recall the content with the [externalities revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-externalities-edexcel-igcse-economics-revision-notes/) and practise with the [externalities practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-externalities-edexcel-igcse-economics-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Point | Wording |
|---|---|
| a | Definition of external costs |
| b | Examples of external costs, including pollution, congestion and environmental damage |
| c | Definition of external benefits |
| d | Examples of external benefits, including education, healthcare and vaccinations |
| e | Definition and formula for social costs = private costs + external costs, and social benefits = private benefits + external benefits |

Notice the headings in the specification: **external costs of production** and **external benefits of consumption**. Those are the two cases you must be able to handle.

## Who are the "third parties"?

Every externality depends on one idea: a **third party** is anyone affected by a transaction who is neither the buyer nor the seller. Before deciding whether a cost is private or external, ask *who pays it?*

- If the producer or consumer making the decision pays it, it is **private**.
- If someone else bears it, without being paid compensation, it is **external**.

## External costs of production (points a and b)

**External costs** are costs of an activity that fall on third parties and are not paid for by the producer.

| Example from the specification | Invented case | Third parties affected |
|---|---|---|
| Pollution | A cement works releases dust and fumes | Residents with breathing problems, farmers with damaged crops, the health service treating them |
| Congestion | A new warehouse sends 300 lorries a day through a town centre | Other road users who face delays, bus passengers, shops that customers avoid |
| Environmental damage | A quarry removes a hillside and woodland | Walkers who lose a landscape, wildlife that loses habitat, future generations |

A strong answer names the activity, the harm and the third party. "Pollution is an external cost" earns less than "Fumes from the cement works damage the health of local residents, who are not paid for this harm."

### Why external costs lead to market failure

The producer decides how much to produce by comparing price with its **private** costs. External costs are ignored, so the true cost to society is higher than the cost the firm sees. As a result, more is produced than is best for society, and resources are allocated inefficiently. Government policy to deal with this, such as taxes and regulation, is covered in [government intervention](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-government-intervention-edexcel-igcse-economics/).

## External benefits of consumption (points c and d)

**External benefits** are benefits of an activity that are received by third parties, who do not pay for them.

| Example from the specification | Who consumes it | Third parties who benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Education | A student completing a training course | Employers gain a more productive worker; the economy gains higher output and tax revenue |
| Healthcare | A patient treated for an illness | Employers lose fewer working days; family members need to provide less care |
| Vaccinations | A child vaccinated against a disease | Other people, including those who cannot be vaccinated, are less likely to catch the disease |

### Why external benefits lead to market failure

Consumers decide how much to buy by comparing price with their **private** benefit. External benefits are ignored, so the good is under-consumed: fewer people take it up than would be best for society.

## The formulae (point e)

> **Social costs = private costs + external costs**

> **Social benefits = private benefits + external benefits**

"Social" means the total for the whole of society. You must be able to use the formulae in any direction:

- external cost = social cost − private cost
- private benefit = social benefit − external benefit

### Worked example 1: an airport's extra flights

*Invented figures.* An airport plans an extra 20 flights a day. Its own costs (fuel handling, staff, maintenance) are 18 000 a day. Noise and air pollution impose costs on local residents valued at 7 500 a day, and extra road traffic to the airport causes congestion valued at 2 500 a day.

```
External costs = 7 500 + 2 500 = 10 000 a day
Social costs   = 18 000 + 10 000 = 28 000 a day
External share of social cost = 10 000 ÷ 28 000 × 100 = 35.7%
```

The airport sees costs of 18 000, but society bears 28 000. If the flights bring in revenue of 24 000 a day, the airport makes a private profit of 6 000, yet society loses 28 000 − 24 000 = 4 000 a day once all costs are counted (treating the revenue as the value of the flights to passengers).

### Worked example 2: a college course

*Invented figures.* A two-year engineering course has a social benefit of 54 000 per student. The private benefit to the student, mainly higher future earnings, is 39 000.

```
External benefit = 54 000 − 39 000 = 15 000 per student
```

If the course costs the student 42 000 in fees and lost earnings, a student comparing only private benefit (39 000) with cost (42 000) would not enrol. But the social benefit (54 000) is greater than the cost, so society would gain if the student enrolled. This is why the course is under-consumed without support.

### Worked example 3: classifying items

*Invented case.* A paper mill produces 1 000 tonnes a month. Classify each item.

| Item | Private or external? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Wood bought by the mill, 90 000 | Private | Paid by the producer |
| Wages, 60 000 | Private | Paid by the producer |
| Cost to a fishing business of fish killed by waste water, 12 000 | External | Borne by a third party |
| Cost to a town of cleaning a polluted river, 8 000 | External | Borne by a third party |

```
Private costs  = 90 000 + 60 000 = 150 000
External costs = 12 000 + 8 000  =  20 000
Social costs   = 170 000, or 170 per tonne
```

## Exam technique

- **Define / What is meant by:** two parts, for example "a cost of production" and "borne by a third party who is not compensated".
- **State examples:** use the specification's examples, but link each to a third party.
- **Calculate:** write the formula first, then substitute. Label each figure as private, external or social.
- **Explain:** a statement plus two developments, for example: vaccination protects the person vaccinated (statement), it also reduces the spread of disease (development 1), so people who are not vaccinated are less likely to fall ill (development 2).

## Common mistakes

- Treating any cost as external. It must fall on a third party.
- Adding external costs to private benefits, or mixing the two formulae.
- Saying social cost is "the cost to society instead of the firm". It is the firm's private cost **plus** the external cost.
- Giving pollution as an external *benefit* of consumption, or education as an external cost.
- Naming an example with no third party.
- Claiming the 4EC1 specification requires an externality diagram; section 1.1.6 asks for definitions, examples and the formulae.
