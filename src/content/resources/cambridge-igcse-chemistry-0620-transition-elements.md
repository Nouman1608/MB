This guide covers subtopic **8.4 Transition elements** in Topic 8, The Periodic Table, of Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, 2026–2028 series. The [Periodic Table groups and trends study guide](/resources/periodic-table-groups-and-trends/) lists the four Core properties and the iron(II)/iron(III) example. This page supplies the evidence for each property (real data, named coloured compounds and named catalysts from elsewhere in the syllabus), contrasts transition elements with Group I so you can answer "compare" questions, and shows how variable oxidation numbers appear in formulae, names and tests. Then use the [transition elements revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-transition-elements-revision-notes/) and the [transition elements practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-transition-elements-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core** (all candidates). Describe the transition elements as metals that:

- (a) have high densities
- (b) have high melting points
- (c) form coloured compounds
- (d) often act as catalysts as elements and in compounds

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only):

- Describe transition elements as having ions with variable oxidation numbers, including iron(II) and iron(III).

## Where they are

The transition elements form the central block of the Periodic Table, between Group II and Group III. Iron, copper, nickel, chromium, manganese, cobalt and vanadium are among them. They do not have a Roman-numeral group number, and unlike the metals of Groups I and II, many of them form ions with more than one charge (see the Extended section below).

## Properties 1 and 2: high density and high melting point

The fastest way to show these properties is to compare with Group I metals. All values below are real data.

| Metal | Type | Melting point / °C | Density / g/cm³ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iron | Transition | 1538 | 7.87 |
| Copper | Transition | 1085 | 8.96 |
| Nickel | Transition | 1455 | 8.91 |
| Sodium | Group I | 98 | 0.97 |
| Potassium | Group I | 63 | 0.86 |

Iron melts at a temperature more than fifteen times higher (in °C) than sodium does, and is about eight times as dense. Transition elements are also hard and strong, which is why iron (as steel) and copper are used for structures, pipes and wiring, while Group I metals are soft enough to cut with a knife and too reactive to use as materials.

## Property 3: coloured compounds

Many transition element compounds are coloured, both as solids and in solution. You already meet several in other parts of the syllabus:

| Compound or ion | Colour | Where it appears in 0620 |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O | Blue | Reversible reactions; test for water |
| Copper(II) ions in solution | Blue | Salt preparation; electrolysis |
| Copper(II) carbonate | Green | Salt preparation |
| Hydrated cobalt(II) chloride | Pink | Test for water |
| Iron(II) hydroxide precipitate | Green | Test for Fe²⁺ |
| Iron(III) hydroxide precipitate | Red-brown | Test for Fe³⁺ |
| Potassium manganate(VII) solution | Purple | Redox colour test |

Compare Group I compounds such as sodium chloride and potassium nitrate, which are white solids that dissolve to give colourless solutions. A coloured compound is a strong hint that a transition element is present.

## Property 4: catalysts, as elements and in compounds

The syllabus wording has two parts: transition elements act as catalysts **as elements** and **in compounds**. Have an example of each ready.

| Catalyst | Element or compound? | Process |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | Element | Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ |
| Nickel | Element | Addition of hydrogen to alkenes |
| Vanadium(V) oxide, V₂O₅ | Compound | Contact process: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ |

A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end (6.2). Using a transition element catalyst lets an industrial process run at an acceptable rate without needing such a high temperature, which saves energy.

## Variable oxidation numbers (Supplement)

A Group I metal always forms a 1+ ion. A transition element can form ions with **different** oxidation numbers. The syllabus example is iron:

| | Iron(II) | Iron(III) |
|---|---|---|
| Ion | Fe²⁺ | Fe³⁺ |
| Chloride | FeCl₂ | FeCl₃ |
| Oxide | FeO | Fe₂O₃ |
| Sulfate | FeSO₄ | Fe₂(SO₄)₃ |
| Hydroxide | Fe(OH)₂ (green) | Fe(OH)₃ (red-brown) |

The Roman numeral in the name tells you which ion is present, so it must always be included: "iron chloride" is ambiguous.

**Which ion forms depends on the reaction.**

```
Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) → FeCl2(aq) + H2(g)       iron(II) formed
2Fe(s) + 3Cl2(g) → 2FeCl3(s)               iron(III) formed
```

**Changing between them is redox (6.4).** Iron(II) can be oxidised to iron(III): the oxidation number increases from +2 to +3 as each ion loses one electron.

```
Fe2+ → Fe3+ + e-
```

For example, iron(II) ions decolourise acidified potassium manganate(VII), showing that Fe²⁺ is a reducing agent.

**Telling them apart.** Adding aqueous sodium hydroxide gives a green precipitate with Fe²⁺ and a red-brown precipitate with Fe³⁺ (12.5).

Copper is another transition element with more than one oxidation number: copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O, and copper(II) oxide, CuO, are both known compounds.

### Worked example

*Iron forms two chlorides, A and B. A contains Fe²⁺ ions. B contains Fe³⁺ ions. (a) Give the formula and name of each. (b) Which property of transition elements does this illustrate? (c) Describe a test to show which chloride is which.*

- (a) A: FeCl₂, iron(II) chloride. B: FeCl₃, iron(III) chloride.
- (b) Transition elements form ions with variable oxidation numbers.
- (c) Add aqueous sodium hydroxide to a solution of each: A gives a green precipitate; B gives a red-brown precipitate.

## Common mistakes

- **Listing "conducts electricity" or "shiny"** as a transition element property. All metals have these; the syllabus properties are high density, high melting point, coloured compounds and catalytic activity.
- **Saying transition elements are coloured.** It is their **compounds** that are coloured; iron metal is grey.
- **Giving a catalyst without saying what it catalyses.**
- **Forgetting the compound example.** Vanadium(V) oxide is the clearest.
- **Writing iron(III) oxide as FeO** or iron(II) chloride as FeCl₃.
- **Leaving out the Roman numeral** in names.
- **Choosing a white compound as a colour example.** Zinc oxide, for example, is white. Use copper, iron or cobalt compounds.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** the transition elements: four properties, each a separate point.
- **Compare** with Group I: give the property for both (high/low melting point; high/low density; coloured/white compounds).
- **Give an example**: name the element *and* the reaction.
- **Explain why iron is described as a transition element** (Extended): it forms Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, so variable oxidation numbers, as well as any Core property.
