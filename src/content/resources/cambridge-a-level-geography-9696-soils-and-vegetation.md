## Biomass productivity

**Net primary productivity (NPP)** is the rate at which plants store energy as new biomass after their own respiration. In hot deserts NPP is very low, a small fraction of that of tropical rainforest, because:

- **water** is the limiting factor: plants must close their stomata to save water, which also stops carbon dioxide entering for photosynthesis;
- **vegetation cover is sparse**, so much sunlight falls on bare ground;
- **growth is pulsed**: most growth happens in short periods after rain, followed by long dormancy;
- **high temperatures** increase respiration, reducing the net gain.

Semi-arid grasslands and scrub have higher NPP than true deserts because of a reliable wet season.

### Biodiversity

Biodiversity is lower than in humid biomes, but deserts often have many **endemic** species adapted to local conditions, and diversity is concentrated where water collects: wadi floors, oases and the margins of playas. A single storm can trigger a brief, dramatic bloom of ephemeral plants.

### Limited nutrient cycling

In a desert nutrient cycle, the stores are small and the flows are slow:

- **Biomass store:** small, because plants are sparse.
- **Litter store:** very small. Little litter falls, and wind blows much of it away.
- **Soil store:** relatively large compared with the other two, because nutrients from weathering remain in the soil and are not leached by rain.
- **Flows:** decomposition is slow because decomposers need moisture. Nutrients are released mainly in short bursts after rain.

So the system is nutrient-limited by **water** rather than by nutrient supply: nutrients exist in the soil but cannot be taken up or cycled without moisture.

### Fragility

Desert ecosystems are **fragile** because recovery is slow. Slow growth means damaged plants take decades to regrow. **Biological soil crusts**, thin living layers of cyanobacteria, lichens and mosses, bind the surface, fix nitrogen and reduce erosion, but they are crushed by trampling and vehicles and recover only slowly. Once cover is lost, wind and flash floods strip the thin topsoil.

## Plant adaptations

### Adaptations to drought

Plants adapt in three broad ways.

- **Drought escapers (ephemerals):** complete their life cycle in weeks after rain and survive as seeds that can stay dormant for years.
- **Drought avoiders:** reach or store water. **Phreatophytes** such as mesquite and many acacias have very deep tap roots reaching groundwater. **Succulents** such as cacti store water in swollen stems; pleated stems can expand when water is plentiful. Shallow, wide-spreading roots catch water from light rain before it evaporates.
- **Drought tolerators (xerophytes):** survive water loss through small or spiny leaves, thick waxy cuticles, sunken stomata surrounded by hairs that trap humid air, rolled leaves, and shedding leaves in dry spells.

**CAM photosynthesis** is a key physiological adaptation of many succulents. Stomata open at night, when it is cooler and more humid, to take in carbon dioxide, which is stored as an acid. During the day, stomata stay shut while the stored carbon is used for photosynthesis. Water loss is greatly reduced.

### Adaptations to extreme temperatures

- **Reflecting heat:** pale, waxy or hairy surfaces reflect radiation.
- **Avoiding the sun:** leaves orientated vertically or edge-on to the midday sun; small leaves lose heat quickly to the air.
- **Shading:** spines and hairs cast shade on the stem.
- **Timing:** opening stomata at night and growing in the cooler season.
- **Tolerating cold nights:** some plants withstand frost on winter nights in deserts away from the equator.

### Physical and physiological drought

- **Physical drought:** there is not enough water in the soil.
- **Physiological drought:** water is present, but plants cannot take it up. In salty soils, the soil water has a higher solute concentration than the root cells, so osmosis draws water *out* of the roots. Halophytes such as saltbush tolerate this by accumulating salts in their own tissues or excreting them through leaf glands.

## Soil processes: salinisation

In humid climates, rain moves downward through the soil, leaching soluble minerals. In hot arid areas, **evaporation exceeds precipitation**, so the net movement of water is **upward**.

1. Water moves upward through fine pores by **capillary action** towards the dry surface.
2. Dissolved salts (sodium, calcium, magnesium chlorides and sulphates) travel with the water.
3. At or near the surface the water evaporates, leaving the salts behind.
4. Over time, a **salt crust** forms at the surface. Lower down, calcium carbonate can accumulate as a hard layer called **calcrete** (caliche), and gypsum as **gypcrete**.

**Irrigation accelerates salinisation** when more water is applied than crops use and there is no drainage. The water table rises, bringing it within reach of capillary rise. Waterlogging and salinisation often occur together. Examples include parts of the **Indus basin** in Pakistan, where canal irrigation raised water tables, and land near the **Aral Sea** in Central Asia, where irrigation along the Amu Darya and Syr Darya left salty soils.

**Consequences:** physiological drought, lower crop yields, damaged soil structure (sodium disperses clay particles), and eventually abandoned land.

**Remedies:** field drains and lowering the water table; leaching with extra water where drainage exists; drip irrigation to apply only what crops need; salt-tolerant crops; lining canals to reduce seepage.

## Desertification

Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas resulting from climatic variations and human activities. It is a **process** that degrades soils and vegetation, not the spreading of sand dunes.

### Natural factors

- Long droughts and high rainfall variability reduce plant cover.
- Climate change may shift rainfall belts.
- Strong winds and intense storms erode exposed soil.

### Human factors

- **Overgrazing:** too many animals for the land, especially around boreholes, remove cover and compact soil.
- **Overcultivation:** shorter fallow periods exhaust nutrients; cultivation spreads onto marginal land.
- **Deforestation:** cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood and charcoal.
- **Poor irrigation:** salinisation and waterlogging.
- **Underlying pressures:** population growth, poverty, insecure land tenure and the settlement of formerly mobile herders.

### Feedback loops

Desertification is driven by **positive feedbacks**:

1. Loss of vegetation exposes soil, which forms a crust under raindrop impact.
2. Crusted soil has lower infiltration, so more rain runs off and less is stored.
3. Less soil moisture supports fewer plants, and erosion removes fertile topsoil.
4. Bare ground has a higher albedo and supplies less moisture to the air, which some research suggests may reduce local rainfall.

Breaking the loop at any point, for example by restoring cover or improving infiltration, can reverse degradation.

## Worked example

*Explain why salinisation is more likely in irrigated land in hot arid areas than in humid areas. [5]*

> In hot arid areas evaporation exceeds precipitation, so water moves upward through the soil [1]. Irrigation without drainage raises the water table [1], bringing salty groundwater within reach of capillary rise [1]. At the surface water evaporates and leaves salts behind as a crust [1]. In humid areas, rain moves downward and leaches salts out of the soil, so they do not accumulate [1].

## Common mistakes

- Saying deserts have "no nutrients"; the soil store can be relatively large.
- Defining physiological drought as no water.
- Describing salinisation without capillary rise and evaporation.
- Treating desertification as advancing sand dunes.
- Listing human causes without the underlying pressures or feedbacks.

## Exam technique

**Explain** adaptations by linking each feature to the problem it solves. **Assess** the causes of desertification by weighing natural and human factors for a named area, and recognising that they interact.
