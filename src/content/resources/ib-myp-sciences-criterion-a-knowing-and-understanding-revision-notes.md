## What the guide states

- Criterion A assesses scientific knowledge (facts, ideas, concepts, processes, laws, principles, models and theories) and how you apply it to solve problems and express scientifically supported judgments.
- Maximum 8, in bands 1-2, 3-4, 5-6 and 7-8.
- Tests and exams must be assessed using this objective.
- To reach the highest level you must make scientifically supported judgments about the validity or quality of information, such as a scientific claim in a media article or the conclusions from experiments done by others.

## The three Year 5 strands

| Strand | Top band (7-8) wording | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| i | explain scientific knowledge | give the idea, the cause and the link |
| ii | apply knowledge to solve problems in familiar and unfamiliar situations | use the right idea on a known or new case |
| iii | analyse and evaluate information to make scientifically supported judgments | break down the information, weigh it up and give a verdict |

## How the bands climb

- **1-2**: state knowledge; suggest solutions to familiar problems; interpret information to make judgments.
- **3-4**: outline knowledge; solve familiar problems; interpret information to make scientifically supported judgments.
- **5-6**: describe knowledge; solve familiar problems and suggest solutions in unfamiliar situations; analyse information.
- **7-8**: explain knowledge; solve problems in familiar and unfamiliar situations; analyse and evaluate information.

## Key terms

- **Unfamiliar situation**: a problem where the context or application is modified so that it is new to you.
- **Data**: a measurement of a parameter. It can be quantitative (volume, temperature, pH) or qualitative (colour, shape, texture).
- **Hypothesis**: a tentative explanation for an observation that needs experimental confirmation.
- **Validity**: whether the evidence or method really supports the claim made.
- **Scientific language**: correct terms, units, symbols and formulas.

## Command terms

| Term | Meaning in the guide | Answer shape |
|---|---|---|
| State | give a specific name, value or other brief answer | one fact |
| Outline | give a brief account or summary | two or three points |
| Describe | give a detailed account of a situation, event, pattern or process | what happens |
| Explain | detailed account including reasons and causes | what and why |
| Apply | use an idea, equation, principle, theory or law in a given situation | choose and use |
| Suggest | propose a solution, hypothesis or other possible answer | reasoned idea |
| Analyse | break down to bring out essential elements | parts and links |
| Interpret | recognise trends and draw conclusions from information | say what it shows |
| Evaluate | weigh up strengths and limitations | both sides, verdict |
| Justify | give valid reasons or evidence | the proof |

## Method 1: claim, idea, link

For an explain question:

1. **Claim**: what happens.
2. **Idea**: the scientific concept that applies.
3. **Link**: the cause that joins them, usually at particle, cell or energy level.

Example: a sealed syringe is pushed in and the pressure rises. Claim: pressure increases. Idea: gas pressure comes from collisions of particles with the walls. Link: the same particles are in a smaller volume, so they hit the walls more often.

## Method 2: calculation

1. Write the relationship (for example density = mass ÷ volume).
2. Substitute numbers with units.
3. Calculate.
4. State the answer with a unit, normally to three significant figures unless told otherwise.

Worked check: a block of 54.0 g with volume 24.0 cm³ has density 54.0 ÷ 24.0 = 2.25 g/cm³. A car at 12 m/s accelerating at 1.5 m/s² for 8.0 s reaches 12 + 1.5 × 8.0 = 24 m/s.

Useful relationships from the guide's physics formula sheet include density = mass ÷ volume, force = mass × acceleration, v = u + at, pressure = force ÷ area, work = force × distance, power = work done ÷ time, current = charge ÷ time, voltage = current × resistance and wave speed = frequency × wavelength. Check the formula sheet your school gives you, because the sheet depends on the subject.

## Method 3: unfamiliar situations

Ask three questions.

1. What branch of science is this (particles, forces, energy, cells, ecosystems)?
2. Which variable has changed?
3. Which idea you know connects them?

Then write the idea first and the new context second.

## Method 4: judging a claim

1. Identify the claim.
2. Name the scientific idea that should support it.
3. Test it against the idea and any data.
4. Weigh up support and doubt.
5. Give a judgment and say what extra evidence would settle it.

A strong judgment sentence has a verdict, a reason and a limit. For example: "The claim is not supported, because plants respire at night and take in oxygen, but the effect of a few pot plants is too small to measure."

## Must-know distinctions

- **State vs explain**: a fact on its own is state. A fact with a cause is explain.
- **Describe vs explain**: describe says what happens. Explain says why.
- **Interpret vs analyse vs evaluate**: interpret says what the data show. Analyse breaks it into parts and links them. Evaluate judges strengths and limits.
- **Familiar vs unfamiliar**: the science is the same. Only the context is new.
- **Opinion vs scientifically supported judgment**: only the second uses a scientific idea or data.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering "explain" with a bare fact.
- Naming a law and not applying it to the case.
- No working shown, or no unit on a final answer.
- Rounding too early in a multi-step calculation.
- Missing subscripts in chemical formulas.
- Treating a media claim as true because it sounds technical.
- Giving a judgment with no reason.

## Quick self-test

1. State the unit used in this guide's examples for the density of a solid in laboratory work.
2. A gas is compressed in a closed syringe. Explain why its pressure rises.
3. Calculate the density of a sample of mass 135 g and volume 90 cm³.
4. What does "unfamiliar situation" mean?
5. Give the difference between "outline" and "describe".
6. A leaflet says "a cold drink warms you up because cold things give you heat". Judge the claim in one or two sentences.

### Answers

1. g/cm³ (grams per cubic centimetre). Mass is often in g and volume in cm³ in laboratory work.
2. The particles are in a smaller volume, so they hit the walls of the container more often. More collisions per second mean a larger force on each unit of area, so pressure rises.
3. Density = 135 ÷ 90 = 1.50 g/cm³.
4. A problem or situation in which the context or application has been changed so that it is new to the student.
5. Outline is a brief account or summary. Describe is a detailed account of a situation, event, pattern or process.
6. The claim is not supported. Energy is transferred from warmer objects to cooler ones, so a cold drink takes energy from the body and does not give it. Any warming effect is not explained by the claim.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the top-band difference in Criterion A?**
At 7-8 you explain knowledge, solve problems in both familiar and unfamiliar situations, and analyse and evaluate information. Lower bands stop at stating, outlining or describing, or at familiar situations.

**Do I need to memorise formulas?**
Check with your teacher. The on-screen examination provides a formula sheet and says symbols, units and equations will be provided where appropriate, but you must know how to use them.

**Do I need units in every answer?**
Numerical values usually relate to physical quantities, so you are expected to include appropriate units, normally SI units where possible.
