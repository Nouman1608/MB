## 3.2.2.1 HR objectives and planning

**The three HR objectives named in the specification**
- Number and skill level of employees
- Employee engagement
- Employee retention

**HR planning tools:** recruitment, training, redeployment, retention, redundancies. Purpose: match the workforce to the business's future needs. Value: avoids skill gaps and overstaffing, controls labour costs. Limit: only as good as the sales forecast behind it.

**Recruitment stages (in order)**

| Stage | What it produces | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Job analysis | A study of what the role involves | Everything after it depends on it |
| Job description | Tasks, duties, responsibilities | Tells applicants what the job is |
| Person specification | Skills, qualifications, qualities needed | Used to shortlist fairly |

**Internal v external recruitment**

| | Internal | External |
|---|---|---|
| For | Cheaper, quicker, known candidate, motivates through promotion | Bigger pool, new ideas and skills |
| Against | No new ideas, creates another vacancy, may upset unsuccessful colleagues | Costlier, slower, riskier, longer induction |

**Selection methods -- one point each way**
- CV: cheap / hard to compare.
- Application form: standard information / slow to complete.
- Interview: tests communication / open to bias.
- References: outside view / often vague.
- Testing: objective on specific skills / tests only part of the job.
- Assessment centre: rounded view from several tasks / expensive.

**Contracts:** full time, part time, temporary, permanent, zero hours. Trade-off in every case: **flexibility and lower cost for the business** v **security and commitment for the employee**.

**Training types:** induction (new starters), on the job (while working, cheap, relevant), off the job (away from work, wider skills, costlier). Benefit: productivity, quality, engagement. Risk: trained staff leave.

## 3.2.2.2 HR data -- formulas

From the specification's Appendix: Formulae and Key data:

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Labour turnover (%) | leavers in a year ÷ average number of total employees × 100 |
| Employee retention rate (%) | employees at end of period ÷ average employees at start of period × 100 (the appendix wording; in practice, count only the staff who were there at the start and are still there at the end, so new joiners do not push it above 100%) |
| Labour productivity | output in period ÷ average employees in period |
| Employee costs as % of turnover | total employee costs ÷ total revenue × 100 |
| Labour costs per unit | total labour costs ÷ units of output |
| Sales per employee | total sales revenue ÷ average employees |
| Profit per employee | total profit ÷ average employees |

Plus, named in the content: **% of employees still at the business one year after recruitment** = recruits still there after one year ÷ number recruited × 100.

**Method in steps**
1. Find the average number of employees: (start + end) ÷ 2, unless it is given.
2. Put the right figure on top (leavers, output, costs, revenue or profit).
3. Multiply by 100 only for the percentage measures.
4. Give units: %, units per employee, £ per unit, £ per employee.
5. Interpret: compare with last year, competitors or a target, and say why.

**Worked reminder -- Marigold Couriers** (average 250 employees; 30 leavers; 448,000 parcels delivered; revenue £16m; employee costs £5.6m; profit £2m)

```
Labour turnover        = 30 ÷ 250 × 100 = 12%
Labour productivity    = 448,000 ÷ 250 = 1,792 parcels per employee
Employee costs % rev.  = 5.6 ÷ 16 × 100 = 35%
Labour cost per unit   = 5,600,000 ÷ 448,000 = £12.50 per parcel
Sales per employee     = 16,000,000 ÷ 250 = £64,000
Profit per employee    = 2,000,000 ÷ 250 = £8,000
```

**Context matters:** the specification says measures such as labour productivity vary in different contexts. Labour-intensive services score lower on output per employee than automated production, so compare like with like.

## 3.2.2.3 Organisational design

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Span of control | Number of people a manager directly supervises |
| Levels of hierarchy | Number of management layers |
| Tall structure | Many levels, narrow spans |
| Flat structure | Few levels, wide spans |
| Delegation | Passing authority down; accountability stays with the manager |
| Centralisation | Decisions made at the top |
| Decentralisation | Decisions passed to branches or lower levels |
| Employee representation rate | Share of the workforce represented by a union or other employee body |
| Delayering | Removing a layer of management |

**Influences:** size, staff skills, nature of the work, culture, cost of managers, need for consistency v local responsiveness.

**Delayering:** for -- lower salary costs, faster communication, more responsibility lower down. Against -- fewer promotion steps, overloaded managers, lost experience, insecurity among remaining staff.

## 3.2.2.4 Motivation

**Theories at a glance**

| Theorist | Core idea | Business use | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taylor | Money motivates; one best way | Piece rate, close supervision, simple tasks | Ignores social and esteem needs |
| Maslow | Hierarchy: basic, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation | Meet lower needs first, then higher | People do not all move up in the same order |
| Herzberg | Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction; motivators motivate | Fix hygiene, then enrich jobs | Pay treated as hygiene, which may not fit every worker |

**Financial methods:** wages, piece rate, commission, salaries, performance-related pay, fringe benefits. Ask: does the method link reward to the output that matters, and what does it cost?

**Non-financial methods:** job enlargement, job enrichment, job rotation, empowerment, teamwork.

**Choosing a method:** nature of the work, ability to measure output, cost, management style, workforce expectations and culture. Assess effectiveness with HR data (turnover, productivity, absence) before and after.

## 3.2.2.5 Employer-employee relations

- **Employee involvement:** better decisions and commitment. Methods: consultation, trade unions, employee forums. Extent depends on management style, size, urgency, legislation and staff skills.
- **Conflict causes:** pay, conditions, job losses, changes to working practices, poor communication.
- **Employee actions:** withdraw cooperation, work to rule, leave, strike, overtime ban.
- **Resolution:** participation, bargaining, arbitration.
- **Managing HR:** technology (recruitment, nature of work, skills, remote working); people management and competitiveness; links with operations, marketing and finance; ethics (unsafe conditions, low pay).

## Must-know distinctions

- **Job description v person specification:** the job v the person.
- **Labour turnover v retention:** who left v who stayed.
- **Job enlargement v job enrichment:** more tasks at the same level v more responsibility.
- **Delegation v empowerment:** passing a task with authority v giving staff the power to decide how to work.
- **Consultation v bargaining:** asking for views v negotiating an agreement.
- **Bargaining v arbitration:** the two sides negotiate v an independent third party settles.
- **Work to rule v overtime ban:** obeying every rule to slow work v refusing extra hours.

## Quick self-test

1. A café has an average of 48 staff and 6 leave in a year. Calculate labour turnover.
2. A firm recruited 60 people; 48 were still employed one year later. What percentage were retained?
3. Labour costs are £84,000 and output is 12,000 units. Calculate labour cost per unit.
4. Employee costs are £1.5m and revenue is £5m. Calculate employee costs as a percentage of turnover.
5. A kitchen of 35 staff serves 9,450 meals in a month. Calculate labour productivity.
6. Name the stage of recruitment that lists the qualities of the ideal candidate.
7. Under Herzberg, is a pay rise a hygiene factor or a motivator?
8. Which non-financial method gives an employee more responsibility and more challenging work?
9. Give one disadvantage of a zero-hours contract for the business.
10. What is the difference between bargaining and arbitration?

### Answers

1. 6 ÷ 48 × 100 = **12.5%**.
2. 48 ÷ 60 × 100 = **80%**.
3. £84,000 ÷ 12,000 = **£7 per unit**.
4. 1.5 ÷ 5 × 100 = **30%**.
5. 9,450 ÷ 35 = **270 meals per employee per month**.
6. **Person specification**.
7. **Hygiene factor**: it removes dissatisfaction but does not create lasting motivation.
8. **Job enrichment**.
9. For example, staff with no guaranteed hours may feel less committed and leave, raising recruitment costs.
10. In **bargaining** the two sides negotiate an agreement themselves; in **arbitration** an independent third party hears both sides and recommends or decides the outcome.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the end-of-year headcount when the formula needs the average number of employees.
- Leaving out units, or writing a percentage for labour productivity.
- Calculating a figure but not saying what it means for the business in the case.
- Listing Maslow's levels without linking a level to a specific method the business could use.
- Saying Herzberg treats pay as a motivator.
- Describing delayering only as cost-cutting, ignoring the effect on promotion and workload.
- Recommending a motivation method without considering cost or whether output can be measured.
- Treating a strike as the only form of industrial action.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three HR objectives in OxfordAQA 9725?**
The specification names three HR objectives: the number and skill level of employees, employee engagement and employee retention.

**How do you calculate labour turnover?**
Labour turnover is the number of employees leaving in a year divided by the average number of employees, multiplied by 100. Find the average as the start figure plus the end figure divided by 2, unless it is given.

**What are the main motivation theories in OxfordAQA A-level Business?**
The notes cover Taylor, who saw money as the motivator and favoured one best way of working; Maslow, whose hierarchy runs from basic needs to self-actualisation; and Herzberg, who separated hygiene factors from motivators.

**What are the advantages and disadvantages of delayering?**
Delayering lowers salary costs, speeds up communication and gives more responsibility to staff lower down. Against that, it means fewer promotion steps, overloaded managers, lost experience and insecurity among remaining staff.
