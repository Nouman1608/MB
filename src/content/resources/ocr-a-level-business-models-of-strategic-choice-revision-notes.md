## The seven points to be able to do

| Point | One-line summary |
|---|---|
| Explain Five Forces (nature, purpose) | Industry framework of five competitive pressures; judges how attractive and profitable an industry is |
| Evaluate position or proposed position with Five Forces | Rank the forces using case evidence, judge the industry, state the response |
| Evaluate usefulness of Five Forces | Structured, but a snapshot, industry-level and judgement-based |
| Explain generic strategies (nature, purpose) | Broad routes to competitive advantage: cost leadership, differentiation, focus |
| Evaluate position or proposed position with generic strategies | Match evidence to a strategy, test fit and risk |
| Evaluate usefulness of generic strategies | Clear and simple, but real firms blend strategies |
| Explain competitive advantage | A feature that lets a business outperform rivals |

## Competitive advantage

- **Definition (standard sense):** something a business does better than rivals that earns it more customers, higher margins or both.
- **Sources:** lower costs, or something customers value that rivals lack.
- **Lasts when:** customers notice it and rivals cannot copy it quickly (a brand, patent, location, skills).
- **Does not last when:** it is a price cut that rivals can match.

## Five Forces at a glance

| Force | Stronger when | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | Many similar rivals, slow growth, little differentiation | Differentiate, avoid price wars, find a niche |
| New entrants | Low start-up cost, no loyalty, easy distribution | Build barriers: scale, brand, patents, loyalty schemes |
| Substitutes | Close alternatives, low switching cost | Improve value, stress benefits, add features |
| Buyer power | Few large buyers, easy switching, standard product | Widen the customer base, lock in with contracts, differentiate |
| Supplier power | Few suppliers, essential input, high switching cost | Use several suppliers, build relationships, hold stock, integrate |

Key idea: **a strong force reduces profit** by pushing prices down or costs up.

### Method: evaluate a position with Five Forces

1. Pick out case evidence for each force. Say "not enough information" where the case is silent.
2. Rank the forces. Which one limits profit most?
3. Judge the industry: attractive, moderate or unattractive.
4. Recommend a response, and say what could change it (for example, a new technology that creates substitutes).

For a **proposed position**, compare the forces in the new market with those in the current one.

### Usefulness of Five Forces

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Structured, covers the main pressures | Snapshot; markets change |
| Explains why industries differ in profit | Industry-level, not firm-level |
| Guides entry and exit decisions | Strength of each force is a judgement |
| Shows where to build barriers | Does not say what to do |

Verdict pattern: most useful as a first filter and when combined with SWOT or forecasts.

## Generic strategies at a glance

| | Whole market | Narrow segment |
|---|---|---|
| **Lower cost** | Cost leadership | Cost focus |
| **Differentiation** | Differentiation | Differentiation focus |

(Standard form of the model.)

| Strategy | What it needs | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Cost leadership | Scale, efficient processes, tight overhead control | Price war; a rival with lower cost; falling quality |
| Differentiation | Features customers value; spending on quality, design or brand | Imitation; customers stop valuing the difference |
| Focus | Deep knowledge of one segment | Segment shrinks; a larger rival enters it |

### Method: evaluate a position with the generic strategies

1. Match evidence (relative price, unit costs, target market, advertising claims) to a strategy.
2. Check the business has the capability that strategy needs.
3. Look for conflict between strategies.
4. Weigh the risks.
5. Link to the market (use Five Forces) and recommend.

### Usefulness of the generic strategies

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Simple, forces a clear choice | Real firms often combine strategies |
| Links to competitive advantage | Four boxes oversimplify |
| Helps align functions | Does not say how to build capability |
| Good for comparing rivals | Does not show whether the market is profitable |

## Worked reminder: price versus margin

A cost leader and a differentiator can earn very different margins and still make similar total profit.

```
gross profit per unit = selling price - unit cost
gross margin (%)      = gross profit per unit / selling price x 100
total gross profit    = gross profit per unit x units sold
```

Example: a firm sells 5,000 units at £40 with unit cost £34, giving £6 per unit, a 15% margin and £30,000 in total. A rival sells 1,000 units at £90 with unit cost £60, giving £30 per unit, a 33.3% margin and £30,000 in total. The same total comes from very different strategies.

## Linking the models and writing the answer

- Five Forces asks how hard it is to earn profit in the industry. Generic strategies ask how this business will win customers. Use one to test the other.
- Example link: strong buyer power makes a pure price strategy risky for a small supplier, so differentiating through service or reliability may be safer.
- For "evaluate" questions, use this order: point from the case, effect on the business or its stakeholders, counter-point, judgement that depends on a condition.
- For "explain" questions, define the model, then say what it is used for.
- Link back to the wider [strategy notes](/resources/a-business-strategy-revision-notes/) when a question mixes Ansoff, SWOT or decision trees with these models.

## Evaluation prompts to keep to hand

- Which force or strategy matters most here, and why?
- Is the evidence current, or could the market change quickly?
- Does the business have the capability to carry out the strategy?
- Which stakeholders gain or lose: owners, employees, customers, suppliers?
- What would change your conclusion?

## Must-know distinctions

- **Five Forces describes an industry; generic strategies describe a business's choice.**
- **Low price is not the same as cost leadership.** Cost leadership is about low cost.
- **Focus is about scope** (a narrow segment), not about size of firm.
- **A strong force is a problem to manage, not a reason to give up.**

## Quick self-test

1. Name the five forces in Porter's model.
2. What is the purpose of Five Forces?
3. State two conditions that make buyer power strong.
4. State two conditions that make the threat of new entrants weak.
5. Name Porter's generic strategies.
6. What is the difference between differentiation and differentiation focus?
7. Give one risk of a cost leadership strategy.
8. A firm sells 8,000 units at £25 with unit cost £21. Calculate its total gross profit and gross margin.
9. Give one reason why Five Forces may be of limited use to a business.
10. Explain what is meant by competitive advantage.

### Answers

1. Rivalry among existing competitors, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, bargaining power of suppliers.
2. To assess how attractive and profitable an industry is, and so guide decisions about entry and strategy.
3. Any two: few large buyers; easy switching; standard product; buyers can make the product themselves.
4. Any two: high start-up cost; economies of scale for existing firms; strong brand loyalty; difficult access to distribution; patents or other legal barriers.
5. Cost leadership, differentiation and focus (cost focus or differentiation focus).
6. Differentiation aims at the whole market; differentiation focus targets one narrow segment.
7. Any one: a price war; a rival with lower costs; quality falling as costs are cut; loss of sales if a single cost cut is copied.
8. Gross profit per unit is £4, so total gross profit is £32,000. Gross margin is 4/25 = 16%.
9. Any one: it is a snapshot while markets change; it is about the industry, not the business; judging each force is subjective; it does not recommend action.
10. A feature (such as lower costs or a valued difference) that lets a business do better than rivals, giving more customers, higher margins or both.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about one business's strengths when the question asks about the industry.
- Naming the forces without linking each to evidence in the case.
- Treating all five forces equally instead of ranking them.
- Saying a force is "high" without explaining the effect on profit.
- Mistaking low prices for cost leadership.
- Describing a strategy without checking whether the business has the capability to deliver it.
- Evaluating the usefulness of a model with generic points that would fit any model.
- Giving a one-sided answer to "evaluate" or ending without a judgement.
- Forgetting to say what the business should do, or what could change the conclusion.
- Using the wrong term, such as "Boston matrix", for a Porter model.
