## Why the world's population grew so quickly

The world reached about 1 billion people in the early 1800s. Growth then accelerated, and the United Nations marked the world's population passing 8 billion in November 2022. The curve is J-shaped because each generation added more people than the last.

The main reason is that **death rates fell long before birth rates did**. Vaccination, antibiotics, safer water, better sanitation and more reliable food supplies (including higher crop yields from the Green Revolution from the 1960s) let far more babies survive and adults live longer. In many lower-income countries birth rates stayed high for decades because cultural expectations, the need for children's labour and high infant mortality all changed slowly. The gap between the two rates is natural increase.

Global growth is now slowing, because birth rates have fallen in most regions. Rapid growth today is concentrated in the poorest countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa.

## The components of population change

A country's population changes in only three ways:

**Population change = (births − deaths) + (immigrants − emigrants)**

The first bracket is **natural change**; the second is **net migration**. The natural increase rate as a percentage is (birth rate − death rate) ÷ 10, because crude rates are per 1,000 people.

**Worked calculation (fictional country, invented data).** Country K has 20 million people, a birth rate of 34 per 1,000 and a death rate of 9 per 1,000.

- Natural increase rate = (34 − 9) ÷ 10 = **2.5% per year**
- Natural increase = 2.5% of 20 million = **500,000 people**
- If 80,000 people emigrate and 30,000 immigrate, net migration = **−50,000**
- Total change = 500,000 − 50,000 = **+450,000** in the year

A population can fall in two ways: deaths exceed births (natural decrease), or emigration outweighs natural increase. Several eastern European countries have shrunk partly through emigration of young adults.

## Why rates of natural change differ

**Birth rates** are shaped by:

- **Social factors**: women's education and employment; age at marriage; religious and cultural attitudes to family size and contraception.
- **Economic factors**: in farming households children can work and support parents in old age; in cities with high housing and education costs, children are expensive.
- **Health**: where infant mortality is high, families may have more children to ensure some survive.
- **Government policies**: incentives or restrictions (see below).

**Death rates** are shaped by healthcare, diet, clean water and sanitation, conflict and disease, and by the **age structure**. A high-income country with many elderly people can have a higher crude death rate than a lower-income country with a very young population, even though its healthcare is better. This is a common trap in data questions.

**HIV/AIDS** is named in the syllabus. In southern Africa in the 1990s and early 2000s it killed large numbers of young adults, and life expectancy in countries such as Botswana fell sharply. Death rates rose, fewer women survived through their child-bearing years, and many children were orphaned. From the early 2000s, wider access to antiretroviral treatment allowed life expectancy to recover.

## Over-population and under-population

Both terms compare **people with resources**, not with area.

| | Over-population | Under-population |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Too many people for the resources and technology available to support a decent standard of living | Too few people to use the resources fully; more people could raise living standards |
| Causes | Rapid natural increase; limited farmland or water; little capital or technology | Low birth rates; harsh climate limiting settlement; large resource base |
| Consequences | Unemployment, food and water shortages, overcrowded housing and services, environmental damage, out-migration | Labour shortages, unused resources, an ageing workforce, governments encouraging immigration |

A country can move between the two as technology, trade and resources change, so always describe the situation at a stated time.

## Building the four case studies

The syllabus requires four countries. Your revision notes give Bangladesh (over-populated) and Australia (under-populated); here are alternative well-documented choices to compare or substitute.

**Under-populated: Canada.** Canada is the second-largest country in the world by area, rich in forests, minerals, oil, fresh water and farmland, but its population passed 40 million only in 2023. Most people live in a narrow band in the south, near the border with the USA, because the north is very cold. The government uses a points-based immigration system to attract skilled workers to fill labour shortages.

**High rate of natural growth: Nigeria.** Nigeria is Africa's most populous country, with more than 200 million people, and women have on average around five children. Causes include early marriage in many areas, limited access to contraception (especially in rural northern states), the value of children's labour and the cultural importance of large families. Consequences include pressure on schools and jobs for the very large number of young people, and rapid growth of cities such as Lagos.

**Low growth or decline: South Korea.** South Korea's fertility rate is the lowest in the world: it fell below one child per woman in 2018 and was about 0.72 in 2023. In 2020, deaths exceeded births for the first time. Causes include very high housing and private education costs, long working hours and the difficulty for women of combining a career with children. Consequences include a rapidly ageing population, a shrinking workforce and school closures in rural areas. The government has responded with cash payments for births and longer paid parental leave, so far with little effect.

For each case study learn: the country's location, two or three facts you are sure of, two causes, two consequences and one response.

## Describing and evaluating a population policy

Singapore shows how a policy can reverse. In the 1960s and 1970s the government promoted small families with the slogan "Stop at Two", backed by incentives and disincentives. Fertility fell so far that the government changed direction: from 1987 it promoted "Have Three or More (If You Can Afford It)", and in 2001 it introduced a Baby Bonus of cash payments. Fertility has stayed very low and fell below one child per woman in 2023.

To evaluate any policy, ask:

- **Aim**: did the birth rate (or population) change in the intended direction?
- **Cause**: was the policy responsible, or were education, urbanisation and rising costs already changing behaviour?
- **Side effects**: ageing, gender imbalance, labour shortages?
- **Acceptability and cost**: did people accept it, and what did it cost?

Singapore suggests that pro-natalist incentives are much less effective than anti-natalist ones, because the economic and social pressures that keep families small are hard for cash payments to overcome.

## Model answer: explain contrasting rates

*Question: Explain why some countries have much higher rates of natural population growth than others.*

> Countries with high natural growth usually have high birth rates and falling death rates (knowledge). In Nigeria many families farm, so children help with work and support parents in old age, while limited access to contraception keeps fertility high (application). Because vaccination and clean water have reduced deaths, the gap between births and deaths is wide, so the population grows quickly (analysis). In contrast, in South Korea the high cost of housing and education means couples have few children, so births barely exceed, or now fall below, deaths (analysis). Growth rates therefore depend more on how fast birth rates fall than on death rates alone (evaluation).

## Common errors

- Defining over-population as "a large population" or "a high density". It is people relative to resources.
- Forgetting migration when explaining why a country's population is falling.
- Assuming a high crude death rate means poor healthcare; check the age structure.
- Describing a policy without any evaluation of whether it worked.
- Using the same country for "high natural growth" and "over-populated" without showing how each label applies.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** (a graph of world population): give the shape, quote figures with dates, and note any change in the rate of growth.
- **Explain**: give a reason and link it to the outcome with "so" or "because".
- **Evaluate** a policy: give a judgement backed by at least one success and one limitation.
- **Calculate**: show the formula, then the substituted numbers, then the answer with units (per cent or people).
