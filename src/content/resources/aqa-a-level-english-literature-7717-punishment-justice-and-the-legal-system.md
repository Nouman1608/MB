**This guide covers one of the longest elements in AQA's list for Option 2A Elements of Crime
Writing:** "punishment, justice, retribution, injustice, accusation, the legal system, criminal
trials and courtroom dramas, imprisonment, death". It links closely to two others: "how far there is
a moral purpose and restoration of order" and "the sense that there will be a resolution and the
criminal will be punished". The [Elements of Crime Writing study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-english-literature-7712-7717-option-2a-elements-of-crime-writing-7717/)
mentions two prison scenes; this page maps the whole element and shows how to argue about justice
in each set text.

## Three kinds of justice

Most crime texts set different kinds of justice against each other. Naming them makes an argument
precise.

| Kind | Who delivers it | Typical question |
|---|---|---|
| Legal justice | Courts, magistrates, police, prisons | Is the law fair, competent, or corrupt? |
| Retributive or private justice | The victim, a relative, a detective acting alone | Is revenge justice, or a second crime? |
| Moral or divine justice | God, fate, conscience, the narrative itself | Does the text punish the guilty when the law fails? |

AQA's list moves from "punishment" and "justice" to "injustice" and "accusation". The pairing matters:
many of these texts are more interested in the failures of justice than in its success.

## The legal system on the page

**Oliver Twist.** Dickens attacks the legal system as well as criminals. When Oliver is wrongly
accused of picking Mr Brownlow's pocket, he is taken before the magistrate Mr Fang, who bullies the
witnesses and nearly convicts the fainting boy until the bookseller arrives to testify. The scene is
satire: the court is noisier and crueller than the thieves' den. Later, the Artful Dodger's trial
turns into a performance in which he mocks the court and demands his rights as an Englishman, so the
law becomes theatre. Fagin's trial and his last night in the condemned cell are written in a
different key, focusing on his terror and the crowd's appetite for his death. Dickens asks the
reader to want justice and to be disturbed by it.

**Atonement.** Robbie is convicted on the word of a thirteen-year-old girl, and McEwan suggests that
class made the accusation easy to believe: Robbie is the son of the family's cleaner, educated by
the family's money. The real assailant of Lola, Paul Marshall, goes unpunished and later marries
her. The novel is built on an injustice that the legal system confirms rather than corrects, and
punishment falls on the innocent.

**The Ballad of Reading Gaol.** Wilde wrote the poem after his own imprisonment in Reading Gaol, and
published it under his cell designation, C.3.3. It describes the last weeks of a soldier condemned for
killing the woman he loved, seen by the other prisoners. Punishment is the subject: the routine, the
silence, the shame, and the burial in quicklime. The poem's refrain that each man kills the thing he
loves widens guilt to everyone, so the reader is invited to question who has the right to judge.

## Retribution and private justice

**Hamlet.** Revenge tragedy is one of the "types" AQA names. Because the murderer is the king, the
law cannot help Hamlet: the source of justice is the criminal. The Ghost demands revenge, and the
play explores whether private retribution can be just, whether it can be carried out without
damnation, and what it costs. Laertes, who seeks revenge for his father without hesitation, acts as
a foil: his speed exposes Hamlet's scruples, and his manipulation by Claudius shows how easily
revenge becomes someone else's weapon.

**"The Laboratory".** Browning's speaker plans to poison her rival. There is no court in the poem;
retribution is private, jealous and gleeful. The absence of any legal frame is itself significant:
the reader becomes the only judge.

**The Murder of Roger Ackroyd.** Poirot identifies the murderer but does not hand him to the police at
once. He offers Dr Sheppard the chance to take his own life, partly to spare his sister the
disgrace of a trial. Justice is delivered by the detective outside the courts, and order is restored
privately. You can argue that this ending confirms the genre's promise of punishment, or that it
quietly bypasses the legal system the genre usually trusts.

## Moral and divine justice

**The Rime of the Ancient Mariner.** Punishment comes from supernatural powers: the becalmed sea, the
spectre-ship on which Death and Life-in-Death dice for the crew, the deaths of the two hundred men,
and the Mariner's survival into suffering. Justice here is not proportionate by human standards,
which is one reason the poem disturbs readers.

**Brighton Rock.** The police barely matter. Ida Arnold pursues Pinkie because she believes in "right
and wrong", while Pinkie and Rose think in terms of "good and evil", the Catholic framework of sin and
damnation. Greene sets an everyday, secular idea of justice against a religious one, and the novel
leaves open which is more adequate.

**"Peter Grimes".** After his apprentices die, the town calls him to account and forbids him to take
another boy, but no court convicts him. The community's judgement is shunning and isolation, and the
punishment that follows is psychological: Grimes is haunted on the water until his death.

## Worked example: a set-text paragraph

**Question shape (original):** "In *Oliver Twist*, the law is as dangerous as the criminals." Discuss.

> Dickens places Oliver's first experience of the law directly after his first experience of crime,
> so the reader compares the two. In Mr Fang's court, the magistrate's insults, his refusal to hear
> the witness and his readiness to sentence a sick child make the institution meant to protect the
> innocent into a threat. The satire works through reversal: the boy who has committed no crime is
> treated as guilty, while the real pickpockets escape. Yet Dickens does not simply equate law and
> crime. The bookseller's testimony saves Oliver, and the novel ends with the law executing Fagin and
> breaking up his gang, so legal justice is eventually done. The more troubling point is that it is
> done through spectacle: the crowd at Fagin's trial and the gallows outside Newgate suggest that the
> law satisfies an appetite for punishment. The law is less dangerous than the criminals to Oliver's
> life, but it shares their indifference to his suffering.

**Why it works.** It analyses placement and satire (AO2), separates kinds of justice, links to
Victorian criminal justice (AO3) and reaches a qualified judgement (AO5).

## Justice in the unseen passage

In Section A, an extract may show an arrest, a trial, a sentence, a prison or an execution. Ask:

- Who holds power in the scene, and how does language show it (legal register, titles, commands)?
- Is the accused presented as guilty, innocent, or uncertain?
- Whose perspective controls the reader's sympathy?
- Does the extract suggest that order is being restored, or that justice is failing?
- Is punishment presented as proportionate, cruel or spectacular?

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming crime texts trust the law.** Many expose its failures.
- **Treating revenge as justice** without debating it.
- **Writing generally about "justice"** without naming which kind.
- **Using "context" as a list of dates.** Link Victorian prisons or the death penalty to specific
  scenes.
- **Ignoring injustice and accusation**, which AQA names alongside punishment.

## Exam technique

When a question uses "justice", "punishment" or "order", define the term in your opening and use the
three kinds of justice to organise paragraphs. In Section C, compare a text in which justice is legal
with one in which it is private or divine.
