These questions cover sub-topic 7.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680, 2027-2029): the factors that change human populations and the impacts of population growth. All places and data are invented.

---

## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by *life expectancy*. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between emigration and immigration. **[2]**

**3.** Define *urbanisation*. **[1]**

**4.** State what is meant by a *megacity*. **[1]**

**5.** Give **two** pull factors that attract people to cities. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain why the number of children per woman is often higher in rural areas of LICs than in cities of HICs. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how improvements in clean water and healthcare can lead to rapid population growth. **[3]**

**8.** In one year the invented city of Daruna, population 3,000,000, recorded 84,000 births, 21,000 deaths, 40,000 immigrants and 7,000 emigrants.
(a) Calculate the natural change and the net migration. **[2]**
(b) Calculate the total population change as a percentage of the starting population. **[2]**

**9.** Source: *Over 20 years, the invented country of Ostra's population grew from 10 million to 16 million. The share living in urban areas rose from 30% to 45%. Its capital now has 11 million people, and about a third of them live in informal settlements on hillsides around the city.*
(a) Calculate the urban population at the start and at the end of the period. **[2]**
(b) Using the source, describe **two** impacts of this growth on the capital. **[2]**
(c) Explain why informal settlements on hillsides may be at risk during heavy rain. **[2]**

**10.** Describe how population growth in a city can lead to **three** different types of pollution. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how rapid population growth can change employment opportunities. **[3]**

**12.** Explain why rural-urban migration can change the population structure of both the villages people leave and the cities they move to. **[4]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The average number of years a person can expect to live **[1]**.

**2.** Emigration is people leaving a country to live elsewhere **[1]**; immigration is people arriving in a country to live **[1]**.

**3.** An increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas **[1]**.

**4.** A city with a population of more than 10 million **[1]**.

**5.** Any two: jobs or higher wages **[1]**; better schools **[1]**; better healthcare **[1]**; access to services such as electricity and clean water; family or friends already living there; perception of a better quality of life. **[max 2]**

**6.** Any three: in rural LICs children help with farm work or support parents in old age **[1]**; infant mortality is higher, so parents have more children to ensure some survive **[1]**; access to contraception and family planning is more limited **[1]**; girls may leave education early and marry young **[1]**; in HIC cities children are expensive to raise and women often stay in education and work longer, delaying families **[1]**. **[max 3]**

**7.** Clean water and healthcare, including vaccination, reduce deaths, especially of infants and children **[1]**, so death rates fall and life expectancy rises **[1]**; birth rates often stay high for some time because attitudes to family size change slowly, so the gap between births and deaths widens and the population grows rapidly **[1]**.

**8.** (a) Natural change = 84,000 − 21,000 = +63,000 **[1]**; net migration = 40,000 − 7,000 = +33,000 **[1]**.
(b) Total change = 63,000 + 33,000 = 96,000 **[1]**; 96,000 ÷ 3,000,000 × 100 = 3.2% **[1]**.

**9.** (a) Start: 30% of 10 million = 3 million **[1]**; end: 45% of 16 million = 7.2 million **[1]**.
(b) Any two from the source: the capital has become a megacity of 11 million **[1]**; about a third of its people, roughly 3.7 million, live in informal settlements **[1]**, which are likely to lack water supply, sanitation and electricity; housing has spread onto hillsides, reducing available land. **[max 2]**
(c) Homes are built without planning on steep, unstable slopes, often after vegetation is cleared **[1]**; heavy rain saturates the soil, causing landslides that can destroy homes, especially as there are rarely proper drains **[1]**.

**10.** Any three types with a cause, 1 mark each: air pollution from more vehicle exhaust and burning of waste **[1]**; water pollution as untreated sewage enters rivers because sewers cannot keep pace **[1]**; land pollution from rubbish dumped where collection services are overwhelmed **[1]**; noise pollution from traffic and construction **[1]**; light pollution from street and building lights; visual pollution from billboards, sprawl or waste tips. **[max 3]**

**11.** New jobs are created in construction, trade and services to meet the needs of more people **[1]**; however, if the working-age population grows faster than the number of formal jobs, unemployment rises **[1]**, and many people turn to informal work, such as street selling, which is low-paid and insecure **[1]**.

**12.** Migrants are mostly young working-age adults **[1]**, so villages are left with a higher proportion of children and elderly people and fewer workers **[1]**. Cities gain more young adults, widening the working-age bars of the pyramid **[1]**, and because these migrants then start families, the number of young children in cities also increases **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping emigration and immigration, or push and pull factors.
- Forgetting migration when calculating total population change.
- Using percentages from a source without converting them into numbers when asked to calculate.
- Writing "pollution" without naming a type, or giving the same type twice.
- Describing urbanisation as cities "getting bigger" rather than an increasing urban proportion.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these population size questions from Cambridge past papers?**
No. They are original questions written for Marlbridge and do not copy past papers or their mark tariffs. Use them alongside the past papers for Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680).

**How does the syllabus measure birth and death rates in 7.2?**
It links birth rates to children per woman and death rates to life expectancy, so use those measures when explaining change.

**How many impacts of growth are named?**
Twelve, from informal urban settlements to changes in employment opportunities, with six named types of pollution.
