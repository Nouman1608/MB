These questions practise 3.2.2 Methods of language analysis from AQA A-level English Language (7702). All data and
texts below are **original**, written for this page; the concordance lines are invented examples, not corpus
results.

**Data G**: five invented concordance lines for "literally".

> 1 ...I was **literally** dying of laughter when she walked in...  
> 2 ...the instructions should be read **literally**, word for word...  
> 3 ...we **literally** waited forever and then gave up...  
> 4 ...the place name translates **literally** as "little hill"...  
> 5 ...my phone is **literally** always about to die...

**Text H**: a letter to a local newspaper.

> *"Sir, I was appalled to hear a newsreader say 'less people' on Tuesday evening. If those who are paid to speak
> properly cannot master the basic rules, what hope is there for our children? Standards are slipping in every
> classroom and every studio. It is high time broadcasters remembered that English is not theirs to mangle."*

**Text I**: dialogue from a novel.

> *"Wot you lookin' at?" sed the boy, wiping 'is nose on 'is sleeve. Margaret, who had never been spoken to in such a
> manner, turned away without a word.*

## Section A

**1.** Describe the grammar of each utterance, comparing it with Standard English: (a) "I never took nothing" (b) "We
was late" (c) "She's ten foot tall". **[6]**

**2.** Explain TH-fronting and the glottal stop, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**3.** Explain the difference between eye dialect and phonetic respelling. **[3]**

**4.** Identify **three** features of older English in *"Knowest thou not that he is come?"* **[3]**

**5.** As a hypothetical exercise, a word occurs 120 times in a 4-million-word corpus. Calculate its frequency per
million words, and give **one** reason why normalising frequencies matters. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the difference between a real-time and an apparent-time study, and explain why age grading is a
problem for apparent-time evidence. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Analyse what Data G suggests about the meanings of "literally", and explain **one** limitation of drawing
conclusions from these lines. **[5]**

**8.** Analyse how the lexis and grammar of Text H present the writer's ideas about language. **[6]**

**9.** Analyse the pragmatics and discourse structure of Text H. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how you would use a historical dictionary and a corpus to research whether "wicked" has developed a
positive meaning, including **one** caution for each source. **[6]**

**11.** Analyse how Text I represents the boy's speech, and what attitudes the representation may encourage.
**[5]**

**12.** Write one paragraph for a Section A essay on language diversity that uses a precisely described dialect
feature as evidence for an explanation of variation. Then explain what makes it effective. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Multiple negation: two negatives, "never" and "nothing" [1], where Standard English uses one ("I never
took anything" / "I didn't take anything") [1]. (b) Levelled past tense of BE: singular "was" with a plural subject
[1], where Standard English has "were" [1]. (c) Zero plural after a numeral: "foot" without "-s" [1], where Standard
English has "feet" [1].

**2.** TH-fronting is pronouncing the dental fricative /θ/ as /f/ (or /ð/ as /v/) [1], for example "fink" for
"think" or "bruvver" for "brother" [1]. The glottal stop replaces /t/ with a closure of the vocal folds [1], for
example in "butter" or "water" [1].

**3.** Phonetic respelling alters spelling to represent an actual non-standard pronunciation, such as "'ouse" for
h-dropping [1]. Eye dialect alters spelling without changing pronunciation, such as "wuz" for "was" [1], and so
signals the writer's view of the speaker (often as uneducated) rather than recording how they sound [1].

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each [1] [1] [1]: the second-person singular pronoun "thou"; the "-est" verb ending on
"knowest"; question formed by inverting the main verb with no do-support (modern "Don't you know"); BE as the
perfect auxiliary with a verb of motion, "is come" (modern "has come").

**5.** 120 ÷ 4 = **30 per million words** [1] [1]. Normalising lets you compare corpora or periods of different
sizes fairly, since raw counts rise with corpus size [1].

**6.** A real-time study compares a community's usage at two different dates [1]; an apparent-time study compares
age groups at one date, assuming older speakers preserve earlier usage [1]. Age grading means speakers change
usage as they move through life stages [1], so a difference between young and old may be a life-stage pattern
rather than evidence of change in the community [1].

**7.** Lines 2 and 4 use the original sense, "in a literal manner, word for word" [1]. Lines 1, 3 and 5 use it as an
intensifier with figurative or hyperbolic statements ("dying of laughter", "waited forever") [1], showing semantic
change towards an emphatic meaning, sometimes described as weakening [1]. The intensifier sense collocates with
informal, first-person anecdote [1]. Limitation (one): five invented lines cannot show frequency or date; a real
corpus sample with dates and text types would be needed [1].

**8.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: evaluative, emotive lexis "appalled" signals a strong prescriptive stance [1]; the
verb "mangle" uses a metaphor of physical damage, representing English as an object that can be broken [1]; "speak
properly" and "basic rules" presuppose a single correct form [1]; the conditional interrogative "If those... what
hope is there" frames the error as a sign of wider failure [1]; the generalising noun phrases "every classroom and
every studio" use parallelism to exaggerate the scale [1]; "English is not theirs" uses possession to present the
language as collectively owned and in need of guarding [1].

**9.** Up to 5 points, 1 mark each: the formal address "Sir" follows the conventions of a letter to the editor and
adds authority [1]; the opening anecdote of a single "error" is used as evidence for a general claim, a typical
structure of complaint discourse [1]; the movement from anecdote, to children, to broadcasters escalates the stakes
[1]; the rhetorical question presupposes that children's language is at risk [1]; "It is high time" signals a
call to action, closing with an implied demand [1].

**10.** Historical dictionary: look up "wicked" for dated quotations showing a new sense such as "excellent" [1], and
note when that sense is first recorded and labelled (for example as slang) [1]; caution: first recorded use is not
first use, and spoken slang may be recorded late [1]. Corpus: search for "wicked" and read concordance lines to
classify each use as negative or positive, then compare normalised frequencies across text types or periods [1]
[1]; caution: the corpus may under-represent the informal speech where the positive sense is most likely [1].

**11.** Up to 5 points, 1 mark each: "lookin'" and "'is" are phonetic respellings representing g-dropping and
h-dropping [1]; "Wot" and "sed" are eye dialect, since the standard pronunciation is unchanged [1]; eye dialect
signals the narrator's judgement of the boy as uneducated rather than recording his speech [1]; the contrast with
the standard, formal narration around Margaret ("who had never been spoken to in such a manner") constructs a
class hierarchy [1]; the reader is positioned to share Margaret's disapproval, linking non-standard speech with
rudeness [1].

**12.** The paragraph names a dialect feature with an accurate grammatical label [1], illustrates it with an example
[1] and links it to a named explanation of variation [1]. For example: "Speakers in close-knit communities often
keep levelled forms such as 'we was', in which singular 'was' is used with a plural subject. Milroy's Belfast
research suggests that dense, multiplex networks reinforce such vernacular norms, so the form signals belonging
rather than ignorance." Explanation of effectiveness: the label is precise rather than "bad grammar" [1]; the
feature is compared with Standard English or placed within a variety [1]; it is used as evidence for an argument
rather than listed for its own sake [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling dialect forms "slang" or "bad grammar".
- Treating all respellings as eye dialect, or not noticing eye dialect at all.
- Drawing conclusions about change from a handful of examples.
- Summarising Text H's opinion without naming the features that carry it.
