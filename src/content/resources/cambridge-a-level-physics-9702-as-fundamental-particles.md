This guide covers subtopic **11.2 Fundamental particles** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (AS Level). The broader [Particle physics study guide](/resources/as-physics-particle-physics/) lists the quark charges and the compositions of the proton and neutron; this page goes further: using quark charges to test whether a proposed particle can exist, classifying particles correctly, checking charge conservation through β decay at the quark level, and avoiding the classification errors that cost marks. For quick recall use the [Fundamental particles revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-fundamental-particles-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Fundamental particles practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-fundamental-particles-practice/).

## What 11.2 asks you to do

Candidates should be able to:

- understand that a **quark** is a fundamental particle and that there are six flavours (types) of quark: **up, down, strange, charm, top and bottom**
- recall and use the **charge** of each flavour of quark and understand that its respective **antiquark has the opposite charge** (no knowledge of any other properties of quarks is required)
- recall that protons and neutrons are **not** fundamental particles and describe them in terms of their quark composition
- understand that a **hadron** may be either a **baryon** (three quarks) or a **meson** (one quark and one antiquark)
- describe the changes to quark composition that take place during **β⁻ and β⁺ decay**
- recall that **electrons and neutrinos** are fundamental particles called **leptons**

The bracketed note matters: charge is the only quark property you need. Questions are built around adding charges.

## Fundamental or not?

A **fundamental particle** has no internal structure: it is not made of smaller particles. At this level, two families of matter particles are fundamental:

- **quarks** (six flavours), and their antiquarks
- **leptons**, including the electron and the neutrino, and their antiparticles (such as the positron and the antineutrino)

Protons and neutrons are **not** fundamental. Each is built from three quarks.

## Quark charges

Charges are in units of the elementary charge e.

| Quark | Symbol | Charge | Antiquark | Charge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| up | u | +⅔ | anti-up, ū | −⅔ |
| down | d | −⅓ | anti-down, d̄ | +⅓ |
| strange | s | −⅓ | anti-strange, s̄ | +⅓ |
| charm | c | +⅔ | anti-charm, c̄ | −⅔ |
| top | t | +⅔ | anti-top, t̄ | −⅔ |
| bottom | b | −⅓ | anti-bottom, b̄ | +⅓ |

A quick way to remember: **u, c, t are +⅔; d, s, b are −⅓**. Each antiquark has the opposite sign and the same size.

## Hadrons: baryons and mesons

A **hadron** is a particle made of quarks. There are two kinds at this level:

- **Baryons**: three quarks. The proton and neutron are baryons.
- **Mesons**: one quark and one antiquark.

(The antiparticle of a baryon, an **antibaryon**, is made of three antiquarks. The antiproton, ūūd̄, has charge −⅔ − ⅔ + ⅓ = −1, opposite to the proton.)

**The proton and neutron:**

```
proton  = uud:  +⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1
neutron = udd:  +⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ =  0
```

**Other hadrons you may be given.** Exam questions often give a composition and ask for the type and charge. You do not need to memorise these, but you must handle them:

| Composition | Type | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| u d̄ | Meson | +⅔ + ⅓ = +1 |
| d ū | Meson | −⅓ − ⅔ = −1 |
| u s̄ | Meson | +⅔ + ⅓ = +1 |
| u u u | Baryon | +2 |
| s s s | Baryon | −1 |
| u d s | Baryon | 0 |

## Using charge to test a proposed particle

Every hadron that has been observed has a whole-number charge. Combining that rule with the quark charges answers "could this exist?" questions.

**Mesons** (quark + antiquark): the quark is +⅔ or −⅓, the antiquark is −⅔ or +⅓. The possible totals are:

```
+⅔ − ⅔ = 0     +⅔ + ⅓ = +1     −⅓ − ⅔ = −1     −⅓ + ⅓ = 0
```

So a meson can only have charge **−1, 0 or +1**. A "meson of charge +2" is impossible.

**Baryons** (three quarks): the totals range from **ddd = −1** to **uuu = +2**. A baryon of charge +3 or −2 is impossible.

**Two quarks** with no antiquark give +4/3, +1/3 or −2/3: never a whole number, so such a combination is not a hadron.

## Worked example 1: identifying an unknown hadron

A particle has the composition u u s. State whether it is a baryon or a meson, and find its charge. Then give the composition and charge of its antiparticle.

```
three quarks → baryon
charge = +⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1
antiparticle = ū ū s̄
charge = −⅔ − ⅔ + ⅓ = −1
```

*Annotation.* The antiparticle replaces **every** quark by its antiquark. Its charge is always the negative of the original particle's charge, which is a useful check.

## β decay at the quark level

Topic 11.1 described β decay in terms of nucleons; 11.2 asks for the quark-level change.

**β⁻ decay**: a neutron becomes a proton. One **down** quark changes to an **up** quark, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino.

```
neutron → proton + β⁻ + ν̄
udd     → uud
d → u + e⁻ + ν̄
charge: −⅓ = +⅔ + (−1) + 0  ✓
```

**β⁺ decay**: a proton (inside a nucleus) becomes a neutron. One **up** quark changes to a **down** quark, emitting a positron and an electron neutrino.

```
proton → neutron + β⁺ + ν
uud    → udd
u → d + e⁺ + ν
charge: +⅔ = −⅓ + 1 + 0  ✓
```

In both cases the other two quarks are unchanged, and charge is conserved at the quark level as well as the nuclear level.

## Worked example 2: classifying a list

Classify each particle as a fundamental particle (quark or lepton) or a hadron (baryon or meson): electron, neutron, up quark, neutrino, positron, a particle with composition u d̄.

| Particle | Classification |
|---|---|
| Electron | Fundamental: lepton |
| Neutron | Hadron: baryon (udd) |
| Up quark | Fundamental: quark |
| Neutrino | Fundamental: lepton |
| Positron | Fundamental: antiparticle of a lepton (antilepton) |
| u d̄ | Hadron: meson |

*Annotation.* A common slip is to call the positron a hadron or a baryon because it is positively charged like the proton. Charge does not decide the family; composition does.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling the proton or neutron fundamental.**
- **Giving the strange quark a charge of +⅔** (it matches down and bottom, −⅓).
- **Building a meson from two quarks** rather than a quark and an antiquark.
- **Writing u → d for β⁻ decay.** In β⁻ decay, d → u; in β⁺, u → d.
- **Classifying electrons or neutrinos as hadrons.** They are leptons and contain no quarks.
- **Assigning properties beyond charge to quarks** (the syllabus requires only charge).

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** or **recall**: give the six flavours and charges as a table if asked for several.
- **Describe** (a proton or neutron): give the quark composition and show the charge sum.
- **Describe** (changes in β decay): name the quark that changes and what it becomes, and the leptons emitted.
- **Show that** (charge is conserved): write the charge of each particle on each side and add.
- **Explain** (why a proposed particle cannot exist): list the possible quark charge combinations and show none gives the stated charge.
