**What these questions cover.** Section 3 of the 2059 Paper 1 content, Key Questions 11 to 16: the establishment of the new state, the search for stability after Jinnah, the separation of East Pakistan, the governments of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, Zia-ul-Haq, Benazir Bhutto and Nawaz Sharif, and Pakistan's place in world affairs. Section B of the paper uses a 4-mark knowledge part, a 7-mark explanation and a 14-mark evaluation; the questions are grouped by those types.

## Section A: knowledge (4 marks each)

**1.** Describe the refugee crisis that Pakistan faced in 1947. **[4]**

**2.** Describe Ayub Khan's system of Basic Democracies. **[4]**

**3.** Describe the Tashkent Declaration of 1966. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the measures Zia-ul-Haq introduced as part of his Islamisation programme. **[4]**

**5.** Describe Pakistan's relations with Bangladesh in the years after 1971. **[4]**

## Section B: explanation (7 marks each)

**6.** Explain why Pakistan had so many changes of government between 1951 and 1958. **[7]**

**7.** Explain why Ayub Khan lost power in 1969. **[7]**

**8.** Explain why the results of the 1970 elections led to a crisis between East and West Pakistan. **[7]**

**9.** Explain why Zia-ul-Haq faced problems during his time in power (1977–88). **[7]**

**10.** Explain why Nawaz Sharif's government was overthrown in 1999. **[7]**

## Section C: evaluation (14 marks each)

**11.** "The Kashmir dispute was the most serious problem facing Pakistan in 1947–48." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**12.** "Ayub Khan's 'Decade of Progress' (1958–69) brought Pakistan more benefits than problems." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

**13.** "Since 1947 Pakistan's relations with Afghanistan have been more difficult than its relations with Iran." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[14]**

## Answers

Knowledge answers earn 1 mark per relevant point or supporting detail, to a maximum of 4. The 7- and 14-mark answers are marked by levels of response (tables after the answers). The content listed is indicative; credit any other accurate, relevant point.

**1.** Millions of Muslims crossed into Pakistan from India at Partition, while Hindus and Sikhs left for India [1]. Many were attacked on the way, and trains arrived carrying the dead and wounded [1]. Refugees needed food, shelter and medical care, and camps such as the Walton camp in Lahore were overcrowded, with disease a serious danger [1]. Jinnah set up the Quaid-e-Azam Relief Fund, and housing and jobs had to be found in cities such as Karachi and Lahore [1]. **[4]**

**2.** Ayub Khan introduced the system in 1959 [1]. Voters elected 80,000 Basic Democrats, divided equally between East and West Pakistan [1]. They sat on local union councils, which formed the lowest tier of a system of councils rising through the tehsil and district levels to the division [1]. The Basic Democrats also acted as an electoral college: they confirmed Ayub as President in 1960 and elected the President and the assemblies under the 1962 Constitution [1]. **[4]**

**3.** It ended the war of 1965 between Pakistan and India [1]. Ayub Khan and the Indian Prime Minister, Lal Bahadur Shastri, met at Tashkent in January 1966, with the Soviet Union acting as mediator [1]. Both sides agreed to withdraw their forces to the positions held before the war and to restore normal diplomatic relations [1]. It did not settle the Kashmir question, so it was unpopular in Pakistan, and Zulfikar Ali Bhutto later resigned as Foreign Minister [1]. **[4]**

**4.** The Hudood Ordinances of 1979 introduced Islamic punishments for crimes such as theft and adultery [1]. The Zakat and Ushr Ordinance of 1980 made the government responsible for collecting zakat from bank accounts and ushr from agricultural produce [1]. A Federal Shariat Court was set up to decide whether laws agreed with Islam [1]. Islamic studies and Arabic were given more importance in education, and Islamic banking was introduced alongside the existing system [1]. **[4]**

**5.** At first Pakistan refused to recognise Bangladesh [1], but it did so in February 1974, at the time of the Islamic Summit held in Lahore [1]. Problems remained over the return of people stranded after 1971, especially Urdu-speaking Biharis in Bangladesh, and over the division of assets [1]. Relations improved in later years with trade agreements and both countries joining SAARC in 1985 [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The deaths of Jinnah in 1948 and the assassination of Liaquat Ali Khan in 1951 removed the leaders with national authority.
- Later politicians had weaker personal followings and depended on unstable coalitions.
- Governors-General and Presidents used their powers to dismiss governments: Ghulam Muhammad dismissed Khawaja Nazimuddin in 1953 and dissolved the Constituent Assembly in 1954.
- Rivalry between the provinces, and between East and West Pakistan, made agreement difficult; the One Unit scheme of 1955 caused further resentment.
- Iskander Mirza changed prime ministers frequently; Pakistan had seven prime ministers between 1947 and 1958.
- Economic problems, food shortages and corruption made governments unpopular, until Mirza declared martial law in October 1958 and Ayub Khan took over. **[7]**

**7.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The Tashkent Declaration was seen as giving away gains made in the 1965 war; Bhutto left the government and formed the Pakistan People's Party (1967) to oppose Ayub.
- Economic growth had benefited a small number of wealthy families, and inflation hit ordinary people, so there was resentment of inequality.
- East Pakistan felt neglected, and the Agartala Conspiracy Case against Sheikh Mujib was unpopular there.
- The Basic Democracies system was criticised as undemocratic, and the press was restricted.
- Students, workers and opposition parties held mass protests in 1968–69, and the celebrations of the "Decade of Development" in 1968 increased anger.
- Ayub's health was poor, and in March 1969 he handed power to the army commander, Yahya Khan. **[7]**

**8.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The Awami League won 160 of the 162 seats in East Pakistan, giving it an overall majority in the National Assembly.
- The Pakistan People's Party won 81 seats, almost all in West Pakistan, so each wing had voted for a different party.
- The Awami League's programme was based on the Six Points, which West Pakistani leaders believed would weaken the central government too much.
- Bhutto refused to accept Sheikh Mujib as Prime Minister on those terms and said the PPP would not attend the National Assembly.
- Yahya Khan postponed the first session of the Assembly in March 1971, which led to strikes and civil disobedience in East Pakistan.
- Negotiations failed, and the army began military action in East Pakistan in March 1971, leading to civil war. **[7]**

**9.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The execution of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto in 1979 caused anger at home and criticism abroad.
- The Movement for the Restoration of Democracy (MRD) campaigned against martial law, with major protests in Sindh in 1983.
- The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979 brought millions of refugees into Pakistan, together with weapons and drugs.
- Some groups opposed the Islamisation programme or argued about how it was applied, and sectarian tension increased.
- Benazir Bhutto returned to Pakistan in 1986 and drew large crowds, increasing pressure for elections.
- Zia came into conflict with his own Prime Minister, Muhammad Khan Junejo, whom he dismissed in 1988. **[7]**

**10.** Mark by the Explanation (7) table. Indicative reasons:
- The Kargil conflict of 1999 ended with Pakistan withdrawing under pressure from the USA, which damaged relations between the government and the army.
- In October 1999 Sharif tried to dismiss the army chief, General Musharraf, while he was abroad, and his aircraft was not allowed to land; the army took control.
- Sharif had concentrated power in his own hands, including through constitutional amendments in 1997, and had clashed with the Chief Justice and the President.
- The economy was weak, with debt problems made worse by the sanctions that followed the 1998 nuclear tests.
- Opponents accused the government of corruption, so there was little public resistance to the takeover. **[7]**

**11.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Agree:* the Maharaja of Jammu and Kashmir, a Hindu ruling a Muslim-majority state, acceded to India in October 1947; fighting broke out between Pakistan and India; the matter was taken to the United Nations, which called for a ceasefire and a plebiscite, but the plebiscite never took place; Kashmir became the main cause of later wars with India.
- *Disagree, other problems:* the refugee crisis brought millions of people who needed food, shelter and jobs; the division of assets left Pakistan short of money and military supplies; the Radcliffe Award gave India areas such as Gurdaspur and left canal headworks in Indian territory, leading to the canal water dispute in 1948; the new government had to be set up in Karachi with few trained officials or offices; other princely states such as Junagadh and Hyderabad were absorbed by India; the national language question divided the two wings.
- *Judgement:* for example, the refugee crisis and lack of resources were the most urgent threats to survival in 1947–48, while Kashmir became the longest-lasting problem. **[14]**

**12.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Benefits:* agricultural output grew with new high-yielding seeds, fertilisers and tube wells (the Green Revolution); land reforms in 1959 set ceilings on landholdings; industry grew rapidly; the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) led to the building of the Mangla and Tarbela dams; a new capital was built at Islamabad; the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance of 1961 gave women more protection in marriage and divorce; the 1962 Constitution gave a period of stability.
- *Problems:* wealth was concentrated among a small number of families and regional inequality grew, especially between East and West Pakistan; Basic Democracy limited genuine democracy and political parties were restricted; the press was controlled; the 1965 war and the Tashkent Declaration were unpopular; protests in 1968–69 ended Ayub's rule.
- *Judgement:* for example, the decade brought real economic growth, but the political problems it created contributed to the crisis of 1969–71. **[14]**

**13.** Mark by the Extended judgement (14) table. Indicative content:
- *Afghanistan, difficult:* Afghanistan voted against Pakistan's admission to the United Nations in 1947; it refused to accept the Durand Line and supported the idea of Pakhtoonistan; Daud Khan pressed this claim, and diplomatic relations were broken off in 1961 until Iranian mediation restored them in 1963; Bhutto's government faced renewed Pakhtoonistan tension after Daud returned to power in 1973; the Soviet invasion of 1979 brought refugees and violence to Pakistan.
- *Afghanistan, better periods:* relations improved at times, for example after the Soviet withdrawal, when Pakistan supported Afghan groups and later recognised the Taliban government in 1997.
- *Iran, good:* Iran was the first country to recognise Pakistan in 1947; the two countries co-operated with Turkey in the Regional Cooperation for Development (RCD) from 1964; Iran supported Pakistan in the wars of 1965 and 1971; trade and religious links were strong.
- *Iran, difficult:* after the Islamic Revolution of 1979 relations became less close; there were sectarian concerns and disagreements over Afghanistan, where Iran opposed the Taliban.
- *Judgement:* for example, relations with Afghanistan were difficult from the start because of the border, while relations with Iran were generally friendly despite strains after 1979. **[14]**

## Levels of response used in these answers

The bands below are Marlbridge's own summary of how Paper 1 explanation and evaluation answers are marked.

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 13–14 | Explains both sides fully and reaches a supported judgement |
| 4 | 10–12 | Explains both sides |
| 3 | 7–9 | Explains one side only |
| 2 | 3–6 | Identifies or describes relevant factors |
| 1 | 1–2 | General comments without specific knowledge |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing relevant |

## Where marks are usually lost

- In Question 3, confusing the Tashkent Declaration (1966) with the Simla Agreement (1972).
- In Question 6, listing prime ministers without explaining why governments kept changing.
- In Question 8, giving the election results without explaining why they led to deadlock.
- In Question 11, describing the Kashmir dispute only, so the answer stays in Level 3.
- In Question 13, writing about only one of the two countries named.
