**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 11, The USSR and post-Soviet Russia (1945 to 2020), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option examined on HL Paper 3, where you write two essays from different regional studies. The guide has six inquiry topics: post-war USSR (1945 to 1953); Khrushchev (1953 to 1964); reaction and re-centralization (1964 to 1982); Gorbachev's reform and transformation (1985 to 1991); the transition to post-Soviet Russia (1991 to 1999); and Putin's Russia (2000 to 2020).

## Post-war USSR (1945 to 1953)

*Post-war reconstruction.* The USSR emerged as a superpower but at a terrible cost: about 27 million dead, devastated cities and farmland. The Fourth Five-Year Plan (1946 to 1950) restored heavy industry and the military sector, helped by reparations from occupied Germany and eastern Europe and by forced labour. Agriculture and consumer goods were neglected, and a famine struck in 1946 to 1947.

*Domestic political developments.* Stalin tightened control. The cultural campaign linked to **Andrei Zhdanov** (from 1946) forced writers and artists to follow the party line and attacked western influence. The "Leningrad Affair" (1949) purged officials, and antisemitic campaigns against "cosmopolitans" and the "Doctors' Plot" (1952 to 1953) showed the regime's paranoia. The Gulag remained large. Soviet soldiers who had been prisoners of war were treated with suspicion, and many were sent to filtration camps or to forced labour.

*Economic developments.* The state continued central planning, with heavy industry first, and rebuilt much by 1950. Collective farms were poorly rewarded.

*Prisoners of war.* The experiences of Soviet prisoners returning from German captivity contrasted with the labour of Axis prisoners in the USSR, who worked on rebuilding; many died of hunger and disease, and the last were released in the mid-1950s.

## Khrushchev (1953 to 1964)

*Secret Speech and de-Stalinization.* After Stalin died in March 1953, Beria was arrested and shot, and **Nikita Khrushchev** emerged as First Secretary. In February 1956, at the 20th Party Congress, he gave the **Secret Speech**, denouncing Stalin's crimes and the cult of personality. Many Gulag prisoners were freed and rehabilitated, censorship eased in a "Thaw", and Solzhenitsyn's *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich* was published in 1962. But reform was limited: the party kept its monopoly, and Soviet tanks crushed the Hungarian rising in 1956. The speech also shook Communist parties elsewhere.

*Economic and social policies.* Khrushchev focused on agriculture and housing. The Virgin Lands campaign (from 1954) ploughed huge areas of Kazakhstan and Siberia; early harvests were good, but poor farming methods and weather led to failures. The mass building of apartment blocks improved living conditions for millions. Regional economic councils were created in 1957, which upset ministries. The corn campaign and other schemes were unevenly successful.

*Technology and the Space Race.* The launch of **Sputnik** (October 1957), the flight of Yuri Gagarin (April 1961) and Valentina Tereshkova (1963) won prestige and showed Soviet science, but the space programme absorbed resources.

*Removal from power.* In October 1964 the Presidium, led by **Brezhnev** and others, removed him. His erratic style, failed agriculture (including grain imports in 1963), the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962, the split with China and constant reorganisations had alienated the elite.

## Reaction and re-centralization (1964 to 1982)

*Kosygin's economic policies.* Prime minister **Alexei Kosygin** introduced reforms in 1965 to give factories more autonomy and to use profit as a measure of success. They were limited by opposition from planners and officials, and were abandoned by the early 1970s.

*Brezhnev's policies.* **Leonid Brezhnev** valued stability, "trust in cadres" and a quiet life for the elite. The 1977 constitution confirmed the party's leading role. In foreign policy he oversaw détente with the West but also the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968 (the Brezhnev Doctrine) and Afghanistan in 1979. The economy slowed, with falling growth, falling productivity, reliance on oil exports and poor agriculture; later writers called this the "era of stagnation".

*The nomenklatura.* The ruling class of officials, appointed by the party, grew into a privileged and ageing elite with special shops, housing and holidays. Corruption was widespread.

*Dissidents.* Opponents included the physicist **Andrei Sakharov**, the writer **Alexander Solzhenitsyn**, who was expelled in 1974, and activists of the Helsinki Group (1976). They were watched by the KGB, tried, sent to psychiatric hospitals or exiled, as Sakharov was to Gorky in 1980. They were few in number, but they publicised human rights.

## Gorbachev: reform and transformation (1985 to 1991)

*Economic policies and perestroika.* **Mikhail Gorbachev** became General Secretary in March 1985 and first tried "acceleration" of production, then **perestroika** (restructuring). The 1987 law on state enterprises and the 1988 law on cooperatives allowed some private activity. But half-reforms caused shortages, rising budget deficits and disorganisation. Falling oil prices made things worse.

*Glasnost and democratization.* **Glasnost** (openness) relaxed censorship after Chernobyl (April 1986) exposed the cost of secrecy. Dissidents were freed, and the press and television discussed past crimes and present problems. In 1989 there were semi-competitive elections to the Congress of People's Deputies, and in 1990 the party's monopoly on power was abandoned and a presidency created.

*Growth of opposition.* Hardliners feared the loss of party power. Reformers such as **Boris Yeltsin** demanded faster change; Yeltsin became chairman of the Russian parliament in 1990 and Russia's elected president in June 1991. National movements grew in the Baltic states (the Baltic Way human chain of 1989), Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan and Ukraine. Miners' strikes added pressure. Withdrawal from Eastern Europe in 1989 removed outside support for communist regimes.

*August coup and aftermath.* In August 1991 a group of hardliners tried to remove Gorbachev, shortly before a new union treaty was due to be signed. The coup collapsed in three days, helped by Yeltsin's resistance and public support. The Communist Party was suspended, and the republics sped to independence. In December 1991 Russia, Ukraine and Belarus declared the USSR over, and Gorbachev resigned on 25 December.

## Transition to post-Soviet Russia (1991 to 1999)

*Failure to establish democracy.* Under Yeltsin, Russia held elections, but power was concentrated in the presidency. In autumn 1993 Yeltsin and the parliament clashed; he dissolved it and troops shelled the parliament building. A new constitution in December 1993 created a powerful president. The **Communist Party** re-emerged as the largest party in the 1995 Duma elections, and its leader Zyuganov nearly challenged Yeltsin in the 1996 presidential election, which Yeltsin won with the help of wealthy backers and a favourable press.

*Economic policies and the oligarchs.* "Shock therapy" in January 1992 freed prices; inflation soared and savings were wiped out. Voucher privatization and the "loans-for-shares" deals of 1995 transferred major assets cheaply to a few businessmen, the **oligarchs**. Output fell sharply, many lived in poverty, life expectancy dropped, and in August 1998 Russia defaulted on its debt.

*Role of Yeltsin.* He was a symbol of defiance in 1991, but his health, heavy drinking and reliance on a narrow circle weakened his authority. He resigned on 31 December 1999 and named **Vladimir Putin** acting president.

*War in Chechnya.* The first Chechen War (1994 to 1996) ended in a costly Russian withdrawal; the second began in 1999 and was fought with great force. The conflicts damaged Russia's image and fuelled terrorism.

## Putin's Russia (2000 to 2020)

*Political reforms and the authoritarian state.* Putin was elected in 2000 on promises of order. He strengthened the "vertical of power": federal districts, appointment of governors after 2004 (following the Beslan school attack), and dominance of the party United Russia. Dmitry Medvedev was president from 2008 to 2012 while Putin served as prime minister; Putin returned as president in 2012, and constitutional changes in 2020 let him seek further terms.

*Economic and social policies.* High oil and gas prices produced rapid growth in the 2000s, rising incomes and a larger middle class. Debts were paid off and a flat income tax simplified taxation. The state took more control of the energy sector, notably through the break-up of the oil firm Yukos after its owner Khodorkovsky was arrested in 2003. After 2014, sanctions and low oil prices slowed growth, and the pension reform of 2018 caused protest. The government emphasised the Orthodox Church and traditional values.

*Propaganda, repression and resistance.* State control of television, pressure on independent media, "foreign agent" laws (from 2012), and the killing of critics such as journalist Anna Politkovskaya (2006) and politician Boris Nemtsov (2015) limited opposition. Protests occurred in 2011 to 2012 and later, led by figures such as Alexei Navalny. Elections were held, but not on equal terms. Historians debate how far the system rests on genuine popularity and how far on control.

*Conflicts with neighbouring states.* Russia fought a short war with Georgia in 2008, annexed Crimea in March 2014 and supported separatists in eastern Ukraine, leading to sanctions.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I compare leaders?**
Use criteria such as economic results, political control and foreign policy, then judge each.

**Are recent events included?**
The study runs to 2020, so know the period to then.
