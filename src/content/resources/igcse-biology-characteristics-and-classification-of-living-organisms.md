This guide covers **Topic 1 Characteristics and classification of
living organisms**, for Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610, 2026–2028 series.
Cambridge IGCSE Biology is tiered — Core content is examined at grades
C–G, and Extended candidates (Core + Supplement) are assessed for
grades A*–G — and this guide marks which outcomes belong to each tier.

## Where this fits in 0610

Characteristics and classification of living organisms is the first of
twenty-one topics in 0610. It sets out what counts as "living" in the
first place, and how the diversity of life is organised into named
groups — both ideas that the rest of the syllabus takes for granted,
from the plant/animal cell comparisons in Topic 2 through to the
five-kingdom groupings that reappear whenever a later topic asks
candidates to classify an unfamiliar organism.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE BIOLOGY 0610 — TOPIC 1 CHARACTERISTICS AND
CLASSIFICATION OF LIVING ORGANISMS**

- 1.1 Characteristics of living organisms (Core) — describing the seven
characteristics shared by all living organisms: movement, respiration,
sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion and nutrition (often
remembered by the mnemonic MRS GREN)
- 1.2 Concept and uses of classification systems (Core) — that organisms
are classified into groups by shared features, that a species is a group
of organisms able to reproduce to produce fertile offspring, the
binomial system of naming species (genus + species), and constructing
and using dichotomous keys based on identifiable features; (Supplement)
that classification systems aim to reflect evolutionary relationships,
and that the similarity of DNA base sequences between organisms is used
as a means of classification — the more recently two groups shared a
common ancestor, the more similar their DNA
- 1.3 Features of organisms (Core) — the main features used to place
organisms into the animal and plant kingdoms, and into groups within the
animal kingdom (vertebrates: mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish;
arthropods: myriapods, insects, arachnids, crustaceans), and classifying
organisms using those features; (Supplement) the main features used to
place all organisms into one of five kingdoms — animal, plant, fungus,
prokaryote and protoctist — the features used to place plants into ferns
and flowering plants (dicotyledons and monocotyledons), and the features
of viruses (a protein coat and genetic material)

## How to approach it

Start with the MRS GREN characteristics, since exam questions often
present an unfamiliar organism or process and ask candidates to identify
which characteristic of life it demonstrates — precise definitions
matter more here than they first appear to, particularly distinguishing
respiration (a chemical process releasing energy) from breathing or gas
exchange, which students frequently conflate. For classification,
practise constructing and reading dichotomous keys directly, since this
is a practical skill examined by giving candidates a set of organisms
and asking them to build or follow a key, not just define one. Extended
candidates should be comfortable stating the five kingdoms and the
named groups within the animal and plant kingdoms precisely — these
recur as short-answer identification questions across past papers, and
partial recall (getting the kingdom right but the wrong subgroup) is a
common source of dropped marks.
