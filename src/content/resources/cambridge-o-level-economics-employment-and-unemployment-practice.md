## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Define unemployment. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why full employment does not mean that nobody is unemployed. **[2]**

**2.** Country P (illustrative) has 2,350,000 people in employment and 150,000 people unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows labour market data for Country Q (illustrative).

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Employed | 2,350,000 | 2,410,000 |
| Unemployed | 150,000 | 190,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate in each year, to 1 decimal place. **[4]**
**(b)** Employment rose, yet the rate rose. Explain how this is possible. **[2]**

**4.** In Country R (illustrative) the labour force is 2,800,000 and the claimant count is 126,000. A labour force survey questions 1,200 people in the labour force and finds 72 unemployed.
**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate shown by the survey. **[1]**
**(b)** Use it to estimate the number unemployed in Country R. **[2]**
**(c)** State the difference between the survey estimate and the claimant count. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain one reason for the difference. **[2]**

**5.** The unemployment rate in Country S (illustrative) is 6.5% and its labour force is 3,200,000. Calculate the number employed. **[3]**

**6.** Country T (illustrative) has a labour force of 2,000,000. Unemployment is made up of 25,000 frictional, 82,000 structural and 53,000 cyclical.
**(a)** Work out the unemployment rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the rate if the cyclical unemployment were removed, to 1 decimal place. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why unemployment would still remain. **[2]**

**7.** Tertiary workers were 30% of 4,000,000 employed people in Country U (illustrative) in an early year and 62% of 4,000,000 in a later year.
**(a)** Calculate the increase in the number of tertiary workers. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why the tertiary share rises as an economy develops. **[4]**

**8.** Country V (illustrative) has 1,700,000 women in a labour force of 4,000,000. Years later it has 2,300,000 women in a labour force of 4,600,000.
**(a)** Calculate women's share of the labour force in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons for the change. **[4]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain why the public sector's share of employment falls as a country moves towards a market economy. **[3]**
**(b)** The formal economy in Country W (illustrative) rises from 35% to 55% of 5,000,000 workers. Calculate the increase in formal workers. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one cause of the rise. **[2]**

**10.** Unemployment in Country X (illustrative) rises by 50,000. Each unemployed person pays 1,200 less tax and receives 2,000 in benefit a year, and would have produced 11,000 of output (invented currency units).
**(a)** Calculate the annual fiscal cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the output lost. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one consequence for individuals and one for firms. **[4]**

**11.** Country Y (illustrative) mainly has structural unemployment. It considers two schemes. Training costs 24,000,000 for 8,000 trainees, and 60% find jobs. A hiring subsidy costs 9,000,000 and creates 1,500 jobs.
**(a)** Calculate the cost per job of the training scheme. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the cost per job of the subsidy. **[1]**
**(c)** Evaluate which scheme is better for Country Y. **[6]**

**12.** Country Z (illustrative) has high unemployment caused by falling demand in a recession. Discuss whether cutting interest rates is an effective way to reduce unemployment. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Unemployment is where people are without a job [1] but are willing and able to work and looking for it [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Some people are always between jobs or changing industry [1], so frictional unemployment remains even when there is a job for everyone who wants one at the going wage [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that says only "without a job" gains little, because students and retired people are also without jobs. Add the willing-and-looking condition.

**2.** Labour force = 2,350,000 + 150,000 = 2,500,000 [1]. Rate = 150,000 ÷ 2,500,000 × 100 [1] = **6.0%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The denominator is the labour force, not employment. Dividing by 2,350,000 gives 6.4%, which is the usual wrong answer.

**3. (a)** Year 1: labour force 2,500,000 [1], rate = 150,000 ÷ 2,500,000 × 100 = **6.0%** [1]. Year 2: labour force 2,600,000 [1], rate = 190,000 ÷ 2,600,000 × 100 = **7.3%** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** The labour force grew by 100,000 [1] while employment grew by only 60,000, so more people entered the labour force than found jobs and unemployment rose by 40,000 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the figures from the table in (b). The point is a comparison of two changes, so name both.

**4. (a)** 72 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = **6.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 0.06 × 2,800,000 [1] = **168,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 168,000 − 126,000 = **42,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Some unemployed people want work but do not claim benefit, for example because they are not entitled to it [1], so the claimant count misses them while the survey asks all sampled people about job-seeking [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason must say who is missed or counted differently. "The survey is more accurate" is a claim without a reason.

**5.** Unemployed = 0.065 × 3,200,000 [1] = 208,000 [1]. Employed = 3,200,000 − 208,000 = **2,992,000** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* This is the formula run backwards. Stopping at 208,000 answers a different question, since the number unemployed is not the number employed.

**6. (a)** Unemployed = 25,000 + 82,000 + 53,000 = 160,000 [1]. Rate = 160,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 = **8.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Remaining unemployed = 25,000 + 82,000 = 107,000 [1]. Rate = 107,000 ÷ 2,000,000 [1] × 100 = **5.4%** (5.35 rounded) [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Frictional unemployment remains because people take time to find jobs [1], and structural unemployment remains because skills or locations do not match vacancies [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtract only the type asked about. Removing the wrong group is a common slip, so write out the remaining figure.

**7. (a)** Earlier: 0.30 × 4,000,000 = 1,200,000 [1]. Later: 0.62 × 4,000,000 = 2,480,000 [1]. Increase = **1,280,000** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Rising incomes lead households to spend more on services such as health and tourism [1], so more workers are needed in the tertiary sector [1]. Machinery raises output per worker in farming, so fewer primary workers are needed [1], and they move into other sectors [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each reason should carry a "so" step that links the cause to the shift in jobs. A list of sector names with no cause earns little.

**8. (a)** Earlier: 1,700,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 = **42.5%** [1]. Later: 2,300,000 ÷ 4,600,000 × 100 = **50.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Social attitudes change, so paid work for women is more accepted [1] and more women seek jobs [1]. More girls complete education, so more women qualify for jobs [1] and laws against discrimination make hiring easier [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason such as "more equality" is too vague. Say what changed and how it brought more women into the labour force.

**9. (a)** The government sells or closes state-owned firms [1], so private firms supply more of the goods and services [1] and they employ a bigger share of workers [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 0.35 × 5,000,000 = 1,750,000 and 0.55 × 5,000,000 = 2,750,000 [1]. Increase = **1,000,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Larger registered firms replace small unrecorded traders [1], offering contracts and recorded jobs [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the chain runs from policy to who supplies to who employs. Skipping the middle step leaves the answer as a statement rather than an explanation.

**10. (a)** Cost per person = 1,200 + 2,000 = 3,200 [1]. Total = 50,000 × 3,200 = **160,000,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 50,000 × 11,000 = **550,000,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Individual: lower income lowers living standards [1], and skills and confidence fade the longer the spell lasts [1]. Firm: unemployed households spend less [1], so the firm's sales and profit fall [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the two groups separate. Credit goes to each consequence that is developed, so give one cause-and-effect chain for each group.

**11. (a)** Jobs found = 0.60 × 8,000 = 4,800 [1]. Cost per job = 24,000,000 ÷ 4,800 [1] = **5,000** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 9,000,000 ÷ 1,500 = **6,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Training costs less per job (5,000 against 6,000) [1] and creates 4,800 jobs against 1,500, a larger effect [1]. Training tackles the cause of structural unemployment, the skills mismatch [1], and the jobs are likely to last [1]. However, results come slowly and only 60% find work [1]. A subsidy is faster and may help people who cannot retrain, so the best choice is training, possibly with a subsidy for hard-to-place workers, which is a judgement [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish with a verdict that rests on the comparison you have just made. Repeating the cost figures without a verdict leaves the answer as description only.

**12.** Lower interest rates make borrowing cheaper [1], so households and firms spend and invest more [1], total demand rises [1] and firms hire to meet it, which suits cyclical unemployment [1]. However, in a recession confidence may be low, so people and firms may not borrow [1]. Effects take time, and a judgement is needed: it is likely to help if confidence returns, but it works best alongside other policies [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the chain in order, from rate to borrowing to demand to jobs. A strong answer states why the policy might fail in this particular case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the number unemployed by the number employed.
- Forgetting that the labour force is employed plus unemployed.
- Leaving out the percentage sign or the rounding the question asks for.
- Defining unemployment as "no job" without "willing, able and looking".
- Explaining a type of unemployment without its cause.
- Giving two reasons that are really the same point.
- Recommending a policy that does not match the type of unemployment.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement.
