## Section A

**1.** State **three** sources of water pollution named in the syllabus. **[3]**

**2.** Define *biomagnification*. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between leaching and runoff. **[2]**

**4.** Name the type of organism that causes cholera and state how it reaches drinking water. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** pollutants that enter rivers from agricultural practices. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A river flows past a town that discharges untreated sewage at point X. A student measures dissolved oxygen at intervals downstream.

| Distance downstream of X (km) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dissolved oxygen (mg per litre) | 9.0 | 4.5 | 2.0 | 5.5 | 8.5 |

(a) Describe the pattern shown by the data, using figures.
(b) Explain why dissolved oxygen falls between 0 km and 2 km. **[6]**

**7.** A lake was sampled for a pesticide.

| Sample | Concentration (ppm) |
|---|---|
| Lake water | 0.00002 |
| Zooplankton | 0.01 |
| Small fish | 0.2 |
| Pike (predatory fish) | 1.6 |
| Osprey (fish-eating bird) | 8.0 |

(a) Calculate how many times more concentrated the pesticide is in the osprey than in the lake water.
(b) Explain the increase in concentration along the food chain. **[5]**

**8.** Explain how fertiliser spread on fields can lead to the death of fish in a nearby lake. **[6]**

**9.** Describe how acid rain affects organisms living in rivers and lakes. **[3]**

**10.** A city has frequent cholera outbreaks. Describe **two** strategies for improving water quality that would reduce them, and explain how each helps. **[4]**

**11.** A factory releases wastewater containing mercury into an estuary where people catch fish to eat.
(a) Explain why the mercury is a risk to the people even though the concentration in the water is very low.
(b) Describe how pollution control and legislation could reduce the problem. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of building a sewage treatment works for a growing town. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: domestic waste [1]; sewage [1]; industrial processes [1]. Also accept plastic waste or agricultural practices in place of any of these.

**2.** The increase in concentration of a toxic substance [1] at each trophic level as it is passed up a food chain [1].

**3.** Leaching is the washing of dissolved substances down through the soil [1]; runoff is water flowing over the land surface into rivers, carrying pollutants [1].

**4.** A bacterium [1]; through water contaminated by faeces from infected people, for example untreated sewage entering a water source [1].

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: nitrate fertiliser [1]; pesticides [1]. Also accept phosphate fertiliser, manure or slurry.

**6. (a)** Oxygen falls from 9.0 mg per litre at 0 km [1] to a minimum of 2.0 mg per litre at 2 km [1], then rises again to 8.5 mg per litre at 8 km [1].
**(b)** Sewage contains organic matter that bacteria feed on [1]; the bacterial population increases [1]; their aerobic respiration uses up dissolved oxygen faster than it is replaced [1].

**7. (a)** 8.0 / 0.00002 [1] = 400,000 times [1].
**(b)** The pesticide is not broken down or excreted, so it is stored in each organism's body (bioaccumulation) [1]; each consumer eats many organisms from the level below [1], so the concentration increases at each trophic level and is highest in the osprey at the top (biomagnification) [1].

**8.** Nitrate and phosphate ions from fertiliser leach through soil or run off into the lake [1]; the nutrients cause rapid growth of algae, an algal bloom [1]; the bloom blocks light so plants below cannot photosynthesise and die [1]; the algae also die [1]; bacteria decompose the dead material and their numbers increase [1]; bacterial respiration uses up dissolved oxygen so fish suffocate [1].

**9.** The water becomes more acidic / pH falls [1]; fish, fish eggs and invertebrates that cannot tolerate low pH die, so populations fall [1]; food webs are disrupted because prey or predators are lost (also accept: aluminium released from soils damages fish gills) [1].

**10.** Two strategies, 2 marks each. Improved sanitation, such as toilets connected to sewers or well-sited latrines [1], stops faeces carrying cholera bacteria from reaching drinking water [1]. Treatment of sewage before discharge [1] removes or kills bacteria, so rivers used for drinking water are not contaminated [1]. Also accept pollution control and legislation, for example laws banning discharge of untreated sewage, with fines.

**11. (a)** Mercury is not broken down, so it builds up in each fish over its lifetime (bioaccumulation) [1]; larger predatory fish eat many smaller contaminated fish, so concentration rises up the food chain (biomagnification) [1]; people eat the large fish at the top of the chain and receive a high dose [1].
**(b)** Set a legal limit on the amount of mercury the factory may discharge, with a permit [1]; require the factory to treat its wastewater on site to remove mercury before release [1]; monitor discharges regularly and fine the factory if limits are broken [1].

**12.** Benefits: removes or kills disease-causing bacteria, reducing cholera [1]; removes organic matter so bacteria in the river do not use up oxygen, protecting fish [1]; nutrient removal reduces eutrophication downstream [1]. Limitations: high cost to build and run, which a poorer town may not afford [1]; treatment works can overflow in heavy rain, releasing untreated sewage [1]. Judgement: worthwhile for a growing town because pollution and disease risk rise with population, but it needs funding for maintenance and expansion [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a data pattern with no figures, or with figures but no units.
- Saying the algae use up the oxygen, instead of the bacteria that decompose them.
- Mixing up bioaccumulation and biomagnification in part (b) of a food-chain question.
- Giving only benefits in a "discuss" question, or ending without a judgement.
- Dividing the wrong way in a concentration-factor calculation: always organism divided by water.
