## What recruitment is

**Recruitment** is the process of attracting suitable candidates to apply for a vacancy. It ends when there is a pool of applicants. **Selection** is the next stage, which chooses between them. The two are connected but distinct, and examination answers should not mix them up.

## Reasons for recruitment

- **Replacing staff** who have left, retired or been promoted (to keep numbers constant).
- **Growth.** The business is expanding or opening new sites.
- **New skills.** The business needs skills it does not have, for example in digital marketing.
- **Changes in demand.** Seasonal peaks or new contracts.
- **Changes in technology or law.** New roles to meet them.
- **Improving diversity** or the age balance of the workforce.
- **Restructuring.** New or changed posts after reorganisation.

## The documents

### Job description

A **job description** sets out the main features of the post: the job title, the department and who the post-holder reports to, the main duties and responsibilities, the place and hours of work, and sometimes the pay band. Its purpose is to tell applicants what the job involves, to help the business to design the job and to give a basis for appraisal and contracts.

### Person specification

A **person specification** describes the type of person needed: qualifications, experience, skills, knowledge, personal qualities and any special requirements. It usually separates **essential** from **desirable** criteria. Its purpose is to focus recruitment on the right candidates and to provide a fair checklist for shortlisting.

### Job advertisement

A **job advertisement** is the public notice of the vacancy. It includes the job title and main duties, the key requirements, location and pay, how to apply and the closing date, and something about the business. Its purpose is to attract suitable candidates, so it must be clear, accurate, free of discrimination and placed where the target candidates will see it.

How they link: the **job description** says what the job is; the **person specification** says who can do it; the **advertisement** uses both to attract applicants. Vague documents produce poor applicants and wasted time.

## Internal and external recruitment

**Internal recruitment** fills the vacancy from the existing workforce, by promotion or transfer. **External recruitment** fills it from outside the business.

| | Internal | External |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Lower: little or no advertising | Higher: advertising, agency fees |
| Speed | Often quicker | Slower |
| Knowledge | Candidate knows the business | Needs induction |
| Risk | Known strengths and weaknesses | Unknown until employed |
| Motivation | Promotion opportunities motivate staff | Can demotivate staff passed over |
| New ideas | Few | New skills and ideas |
| Chain effect | Creates another vacancy to fill | None |
| Pool | Limited | Wide |

## Methods of external recruitment

- **The business's own website and social media.** Low cost, reaches interested people, but needs a strong employer profile.
- **Job boards and professional networks.** Wide reach, easy to search, may attract many unsuitable applicants.
- **Newspapers and trade journals.** Targeted at a specific profession or area, costlier, and shorter reach for younger candidates.
- **Job centres.** Low cost and often locally targeted, but candidates may be less specialised.
- **Recruitment agencies and headhunters.** Save management time and find specialists, but charge a fee, often a percentage of first-year pay.
- **Schools, colleges and universities.** Suit trainee and graduate roles.
- **Word of mouth and referrals from staff.** Cheap and quick, but narrow and may limit diversity.

## Worked example 1: costing three ways to fill one vacancy

Trevelyan Hotels (invented) must fill a senior receptionist post paying £26,000. Managers' time is valued at £30 an hour. A new external recruit is expected to work at half productivity for the first four weeks, and a week's output from a receptionist is worth £500.

**Option 1: internal promotion.** No advertising. Manager time 6 hours = 6 × £30 = £180. Training for the promoted person £800. The vacancy created is filled by a junior external hire at £1,100 of cost. Total = £180 + £800 + £1,100 = **£2,080**.

**Option 2: external recruitment using the hotel's own advertising.** Advertisement £1,200. Manager time 18 hours = 18 × £30 = £540. Induction £1,000. Slow start: 50% of £500 for 4 weeks = 0.5 × 4 × £500 = £1,000. Total = £1,200 + £540 + £1,000 + £1,000 = **£3,740**.

**Option 3: external recruitment through an agency.** Fee 18% of £26,000 = £4,680. Manager time 6 hours = £180. Induction £1,000. Slow start £1,000. Total = £4,680 + £180 + £1,000 + £1,000 = **£6,860**.

Internal recruitment is £1,660 cheaper than the hotel's own external advert (£3,740 − £2,080) and £4,780 cheaper than the agency (£6,860 − £2,080).

Evaluation:

- Costs favour internal recruitment, but it depends on an internal candidate being good enough. Promoting a weak candidate to save £1,660 could cost more in poor service.
- External recruitment brings new ideas and a wider choice, which may matter if the hotel is changing its approach.
- An agency is the most expensive, but saves 12 hours of manager time and may be worth it for a hard-to-fill specialist role or an urgent vacancy.
- Other stakeholders: internal staff are motivated by a visible promotion route; guests may notice a change in service; the vacancy chain means a second recruitment.

**Judgement.** Trevelyan should first offer the post internally, because it is cheaper, quicker and good for staff morale, and advertise externally only if no suitable candidate applies.

## Impact and importance

Good recruitment:

- gives a strong pool from which to select, which raises the chance of a good match;
- reduces later costs of turnover, retraining and poor performance;
- supports the business's strategy by bringing in needed skills;
- builds the reputation of the business as an employer;
- helps legal compliance, because fair advertising avoids discrimination.

Poor recruitment: wasted time and money, vacancies that stay open, staff shortages that overload existing employees, hiring the wrong people and high turnover.

Stakeholders: applicants (a fair process, clear information), existing staff (workload, promotion prospects), customers (service quality), owners (cost), and the community (local jobs).

## Evaluating documents and methods

- Are the documents **clear and accurate**? A vague job description attracts unsuitable applicants.
- Do they use **essential and desirable** criteria to focus the pool?
- Are they **fair** and free of discrimination?
- Does the **method reach the right candidates** at an acceptable cost?
- Is it quick enough for the urgency of the vacancy?
- Is the business **paying for what it needs**? A national newspaper advertisement for a local part-time job is wasteful.

## Recommending methods

A recommendation should match the method to the post, the labour market and the budget. For a specialist, senior or scarce skill: use an agency or specialist journal. For routine local posts: use the business's website, a job centre and word of mouth. For posts that offer promotion: use internal advertisement first. Say what the recommendation costs and what it depends on, such as how many suitable applicants are likely to apply.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing recruitment with selection.
- Describing the job description and person specification as the same thing.
- Saying that internal recruitment is always best. It limits the pool and creates a vacancy elsewhere.
- Recommending a costly method without comparing the cost with the need.
- Ignoring the chain effect of internal promotion.

## Exam technique

For "explain the content and purpose", give both what is in the document and why the business uses it. For "recommend", name a method, give a reason linked to the case, and state a risk or condition. Use figures from the case to compare costs.
