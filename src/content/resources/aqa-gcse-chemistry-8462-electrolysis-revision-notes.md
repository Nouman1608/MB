These notes condense **4.4.3 Electrolysis** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification (for teaching from
September 2016). Paper 1 content. Writing half equations (4.4.3.5) is Higher tier only. For explanations use
the [electrolysis study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-electrolysis/); to test yourself use the
[electrolysis practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-electrolysis-practice/).

## Core facts

- An **electrolyte** is a molten or dissolved ionic compound. Its ions are free to move, so it conducts.
- **Cations** (positive) go to the **cathode** (negative electrode).
- **Anions** (negative) go to the **anode** (positive electrode).
- Ions are **discharged** and form **elements**. This is **electrolysis**.
- **Inert electrodes** (graphite, platinum) do not react.
- Memory aid: **PANIC**, Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.

## Molten binary compounds

| Electrode | Product |
|---|---|
| Cathode | The metal |
| Anode | The non-metal |

| Compound | Cathode | Anode |
|---|---|---|
| PbBr₂ | lead | bromine |
| NaCl | sodium | chlorine |
| ZnCl₂ | zinc | chlorine |
| Al₂O₃ | aluminium | oxygen |

## Extraction of aluminium

- Electrolysis is used for metals **too reactive** to be reduced by carbon, or that react with carbon.
- It uses a lot of **energy** (to melt the compound and to supply the current).
- Electrolyte: **molten aluminium oxide dissolved in cryolite**.
- Electrodes: **carbon** (graphite).
- Why a mixture: aluminium oxide has a very high melting point; cryolite gives a **lower melting
  point**, **saving energy** and cost.
- Cathode: aluminium forms (sinks and is tapped off).
- Anode: oxygen forms and **reacts with the carbon** to make **carbon dioxide**, so the **anodes burn away and
  must be replaced**.

## Aqueous solutions

Water supplies H⁺ and OH⁻ as well as the dissolved ions.

| Electrode | Rule |
|---|---|
| Cathode | **Hydrogen** if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen; otherwise the **metal** |
| Anode | **Oxygen**, unless **halide ions** (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) are present, then the **halogen** |

| Solution | Cathode | Anode |
|---|---|---|
| NaCl(aq) | hydrogen | chlorine |
| CuSO₄(aq) | copper | oxygen |
| H₂SO₄(aq) | hydrogen | oxygen |
| KI(aq) | hydrogen | iodine |
| CuCl₂(aq) | copper | chlorine |
| Na₂SO₄(aq) | hydrogen | oxygen |

Gas tests: hydrogen, lit splint and squeaky pop. Oxygen, glowing splint relights. Chlorine, damp blue litmus
paper turns red then bleaches.

## Required practical 3

- Use **inert electrodes** in several solutions.
- Make a **hypothesis** about the products from the reactivity of the metal and the presence of halides.
- Test each product and compare with the hypothesis.
- Hazards: chlorine is toxic, so use low concentrations and a fume cupboard or ventilated room.

## Higher tier only: half equations

- **Cathode: reduction**, ions **gain** electrons. **Anode: oxidation**, ions **lose** electrons.
- Balance **atoms and charge**. Electrons on the left at the cathode, on the right at the anode.

| Half equation | Where |
|---|---|
| 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | cathode |
| Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu | cathode |
| Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb | cathode |
| Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al | cathode |
| 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ | anode |
| 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ | anode |
| 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ | anode (molten) |
| 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ | anode (aqueous) |

Combining half equations: make the number of electrons equal. For aluminium oxide: 4Al³⁺ + 12e⁻ → 4Al and
6O²⁻ → 3O₂ + 12e⁻, giving 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electrolysis | Decomposing an ionic compound using electricity |
| Electrolyte | Molten or dissolved ionic compound that conducts |
| Cathode | Negative electrode, where reduction happens |
| Anode | Positive electrode, where oxidation happens |
| Discharged | Turned into an uncharged element at an electrode |
| Cryolite | Substance that lowers the melting point of aluminium oxide |

## Exam traps

- **Wrong product at the cathode in solution.** Reactive metals (sodium, potassium) give hydrogen.
- **Forgetting halides.** Chloride, bromide and iodide give halogens, not oxygen, at the anode.
- **Saying the aluminium anode "wears away".** Say it reacts with oxygen to make carbon dioxide.
- **Solid ionic compounds.** They do not conduct, because the ions cannot move.
- **Electrons on the wrong side.** Cathode: left. Anode: right.
- **Atoms instead of molecules.** Chlorine, bromine, hydrogen and oxygen are written Cl₂, Br₂, H₂, O₂.

## Quick self-test

1. Why can molten sodium chloride conduct but solid sodium chloride cannot?
2. Which electrode do positive ions move to?
3. Name the products of molten lead bromide electrolysis.
4. Why is cryolite added in aluminium extraction?
5. Why are the carbon anodes replaced regularly?
6. Name the products at each electrode for sodium chloride solution.
7. Name the product at the cathode when copper sulfate solution is electrolysed.
8. What forms at the anode if the solution contains no halide ions?
9. (Higher tier only) Write the half equation for the formation of chlorine.
10. (Higher tier only) Write the half equation for aluminium formation and say if it is oxidation or reduction.
11. (Higher tier only) Combine 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ and Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb into one equation.
12. (Higher tier only) What does the equation 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ describe?

### Answers

1. The ions are free to move in the molten state but are fixed in a lattice in the solid.
2. The cathode (negative electrode).
3. Lead at the cathode, bromine at the anode.
4. It dissolves the aluminium oxide and lowers the melting point of the electrolyte, saving energy.
5. The oxygen formed at the anode reacts with the carbon to make carbon dioxide, so the anodes burn away.
6. Hydrogen at the cathode, chlorine at the anode (sodium hydroxide stays in solution).
7. Copper.
8. Oxygen.
9. 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.
10. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al; reduction (gain of electrons).
11. 2Br⁻ + Pb²⁺ → Br₂ + Pb (electrons cancel).
12. Hydroxide ions lose electrons (oxidation) at the anode, producing oxygen and water.
