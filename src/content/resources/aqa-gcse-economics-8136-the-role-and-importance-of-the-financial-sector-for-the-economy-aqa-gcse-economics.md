The financial sector is the part of the economy that looks after people's money, moves it from savers to borrowers and keeps payments working. Section 3.2.5.2 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification has three content points: **the financial sector**, **the role of the Bank of England** and **the role of other institutions in the financial sector**. You must understand the main agents (the Bank of England, commercial banks and building societies), the Bank of England's role in **influencing interest rates** and **ensuring the stability of the financial system**, and the role of high street banks in **helping to fund investment** and **providing a service for savers and borrowers**. The introduction to 3.2.5 adds that you should see why this matters to consumers, producers and the government when they save, borrow and spend.

## Financial intermediation: the core idea

Savers have money they do not need now; borrowers need money now and will repay later. Most savers do not know which borrowers to trust, and most borrowers want larger sums for longer than any one saver could offer. Financial institutions act as **intermediaries** between them:

- they **collect** many small deposits and **lend** them in larger amounts to households and firms;
- they **assess** whether borrowers are likely to repay, which a saver could not easily do;
- they **spread risk**, because one unpaid loan is a small loss across thousands of savers' deposits;
- they **pay interest** to savers and **charge interest** to borrowers, and the gap covers their costs and profit.

Without this, savings would sit idle and many worthwhile investments would never happen.

## The main agents

| Institution | Owned by | Main role |
|---|---|---|
| Bank of England | The state (it is the UK's central bank) | Sets Bank Rate; keeps the financial system stable; issues banknotes in England and Wales; acts as the government's bank |
| Commercial (high street) banks | Shareholders | Current and savings accounts, loans, overdrafts, mortgages, payments |
| Building societies | Their members (savers and borrowers): they are **mutuals** | Mainly savings accounts and mortgages |
| Other institutions, such as insurance companies and pension funds | Usually shareholders or members | Insurers pool risks so households and firms can protect themselves against loss; pension funds invest savings for retirement |

Because a building society has no shareholders to pay, it can aim to offer members better interest rates instead of distributing profit.

## The Bank of England

### Influencing interest rates

The Bank of England's **Monetary Policy Committee** sets **Bank Rate**, the interest rate it pays to commercial banks on money they hold with it. Commercial banks' own rates for savers and borrowers tend to follow it. The government sets the inflation target (2% on the CPI measure), and the Bank of England has set interest rates independently since 1997.

The chain to learn for a rise in Bank Rate:

1. Bank Rate rises.
2. Commercial banks raise rates on loans, mortgages and savings.
3. Borrowing becomes dearer and saving more rewarding.
4. Households spend less on credit, homeowners with variable-rate mortgages have less to spend, and firms invest less.
5. Total demand in the economy grows more slowly, so inflation falls.

A cut works the other way round, encouraging spending, investment and growth, with a risk of higher inflation.

### Ensuring the stability of the financial system

A bank lends out most of its deposits, so it cannot repay every depositor at once. If depositors lose confidence and all try to withdraw (a **bank run**), even a sound bank can fail, and fear can spread to other banks. The Bank of England reduces this risk by:

- **regulating and supervising** banks, building societies and insurers through its Prudential Regulation Authority, for example by requiring banks to hold enough capital to absorb losses;
- **watching for risks** across the whole system through its Financial Policy Committee, such as too much lending on housing;
- acting as **lender of last resort**, lending to banks that are short of cash in a crisis so that a problem at one bank does not become a collapse of many.

**Why stability matters:** in the financial crisis of 2007–08, banks around the world made heavy losses and cut lending; in the UK the government had to support several large banks, and the economy went into recession. When banks stop lending, firms cannot invest and households cannot borrow, so output and employment fall.

## High street banks and building societies

### A service for savers

- Keep money safe in accounts.
- Pay interest on savings, which rewards people for delaying spending.
- Offer a range of accounts, from instant access to fixed-term savings.

### A service for borrowers

- **Mortgages** let households buy a home over many years.
- **Personal loans and credit cards** let households buy cars, furniture or holidays before saving the full price.
- **Overdrafts** let households and firms cover short gaps between payments and receipts.

### Funding investment

Firms borrow from banks to buy machinery, build premises, develop products or expand. Investment raises the economy's productive capacity, creates jobs and supports long-run economic growth.

### Worked example: is a loan worth taking? (illustrative)

A bakery can borrow £200,000 at 6% a year to buy an oven line that will add £30,000 a year to its profit before interest.

- Annual interest = 0.06 × 200,000 = **£12,000**.
- Extra profit after interest = 30,000 − 12,000 = **£18,000** a year, so the investment is worthwhile.
- If the interest rate rises to 9%, interest = **£18,000**, leaving **£12,000**. The investment is still worthwhile but less attractive; a firm expecting a smaller gain might cancel it.

This shows how the Bank of England's interest rate decisions reach firms' investment through high street banks.

### Worked example: a saver's return (illustrative)

Leila deposits £5,000 in a savings account paying 3% a year. After one year she earns 0.03 × 5,000 = **£150** interest. If inflation that year is 4%, her savings have lost purchasing power despite the interest, which is why savers care about both interest rates and inflation.

## Importance to consumers, producers and government

| Agent | Saving | Borrowing | Spending |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Earn interest and build up wealth | Buy houses and cars now through mortgages and loans | Pay quickly and safely by card and transfer |
| Producers | Hold spare funds and earn interest | Finance investment and cover cash-flow gaps | Pay suppliers and workers through the banking system |
| Government | Holds its accounts at the Bank of England | Borrows by selling bonds (gilts) to financial institutions such as pension funds | Pays benefits and wages through banks |

The financial sector is also important to the UK directly: financial services employ many people and are a major export, helping the UK's trade in services.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the government sets interest rates; the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee does.
- Describing a building society as a bank with a different name; it is owned by its members.
- Writing "the Bank of England lends to the public". It lends to banks, not to households.
- Explaining a change in Bank Rate without the chain from bank rates to spending.
- Forgetting the role in investment: banks lend savers' deposits to firms that buy capital.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **State / Identify:** for example, two agents in the financial sector.
- **Explain:** give the role and the effect. "Banks lend to firms (role), so firms can buy machinery (effect), which increases output and jobs (wider effect)."
- **Calculate:** interest = rate × sum borrowed or saved; show the decimal.
- **Analyse:** use the context, such as a firm's loan, to trace effects step by step.
- **Evaluate:** for example, weigh the benefits of bank lending for growth against the risks when banks lend too much, and reach a judgement on what it depends on, such as how well banks are regulated.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between Bank Rate and the rate my bank charges?** Bank Rate is set by the Bank of England; commercial banks set their own rates, which usually move with it but are higher for borrowers.

**What does lender of last resort mean?** The Bank of England will lend to banks in difficulty when no one else will, to stop a crisis spreading.

**Why do building societies matter?** They give savers and mortgage borrowers a choice of provider that is run for its members rather than shareholders.
