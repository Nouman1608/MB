## What 2.2.5 contains

The specification lists three points:

- how each element of the marketing mix can influence other elements
- using the marketing mix to build competitive advantage
- how an integrated marketing mix can influence competitive advantage.

Theme 1 introduced the marketing mix in item 1.4.3. In Theme 2 the emphasis moves from knowing the four Ps to using them together to make decisions in a competitive market. That makes 2.2.5 a natural focus for the longer Analyse, Justify and Evaluate questions.

## How each element influences the others

A change to one element rarely stays on its own. The table shows the main links; read each row as "a decision about this element affects..."

| Decision about | Effect on product | Effect on price | Effect on promotion | Effect on place |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Product** (better quality, new features) | — | Higher costs and higher value usually mean a higher price | The new features become the message | May need upmarket shops or a specialist website |
| **Price** (a low price) | Costs must be kept down, limiting features | — | Promotion stresses value; offers become common | Suits supermarkets, discount stores, marketplaces |
| **Promotion** (heavy advertising) | Product must live up to the claims | Costs must be covered by price or volume | — | Product must be widely available when demand rises |
| **Place** (move from shops to online only) | Packaging must survive delivery | Lower shop costs may allow a lower price, but delivery costs rise | Must shift to online promotion to bring visitors | — |

When answering, follow the chain at least two steps. "The product is improved, so unit costs rise, so the price must rise, so promotion must explain why it is worth more" is analysis. "Product affects price" is a statement.

### Worked example 1: Ember Pizza Co upgrades its product

Ember Pizza Co sells pizzas from a van at £8, with a variable cost of £3.20, selling 250 a week. The owner switches to slow-fermented dough and local ingredients, raising variable cost to £4.40. This product decision sets off changes elsewhere:

- **Price:** raised to £11 to reflect quality and cover cost. Sales are forecast to fall to 220 a week.
- **Promotion:** £60 a week on social media posts about the ingredients and the farms that supply them.
- **Place:** the van moves from a retail park to a weekly farmers' market and two village events, where customers value local food.

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution per pizza | £8 − £3.20 = £4.80 | £11 − £4.40 = £6.60 |
| Weekly contribution | £4.80 × 250 = £1,200 | £6.60 × 220 = £1,452 |
| After extra promotion | £1,200 | £1,452 − £60 = **£1,392** |

The change earns £192 a week more, but only because price, promotion and place were all adjusted to match the new product. If Ember had raised quality and price while staying at the retail park, where customers were looking for a cheap lunch, sales might have fallen far more.

## Competitive advantage

Competitive advantage is something that makes customers choose a business over its rivals, and that rivals find hard to copy. It is a standard term; the specification uses it without a definition. A business usually builds it in one of two ways, both shaped by the marketing mix:

- **Lower cost:** the business can charge less than rivals (or match their price with higher profit) because its costs are lower. Product, place and promotion are kept simple to protect the low price.
- **Differentiation:** customers see the product as different or better, so they choose it even at a higher price. Product, promotion and place all reinforce what makes it special.

Each element can contribute:

| Element | Example of building advantage |
|---|---|
| Product | A unique design, better quality or a feature rivals lack |
| Price | Consistently lower prices made possible by lower costs |
| Promotion | A strong brand that customers trust and recognise |
| Place | Being available where and when rivals are not, such as fast delivery or late opening |

An advantage based on one element alone is often easy to copy. A rival can cut its price or copy a feature. An advantage built from several elements working together is much harder to imitate, which leads to the third point.

## The integrated marketing mix

An integrated marketing mix is one in which all four elements are consistent with each other and aimed at the same target market. Each element supports the message of the others.

**Integrated example: Pulse Gym.** A budget gym offers basic equipment and no swimming pool (product), charges £18 a month with no contract (price), advertises mainly through targeted social media aimed at students and young workers (promotion), and opens 24 hours in a cheap unit on a retail park (place). Every element supports one idea: affordable, flexible fitness. Pulse's low costs let it keep the price low, and its customers do not expect luxury.

**Inconsistent example: Larkhill Running Shoes.** A maker promotes a premium running shoe with a £140 price and adverts featuring elite athletes, but sells it through a discount store with frequent "two for one" offers. Customers who want a premium shoe distrust the discount setting, and bargain hunters find the price too high even with the offer. The elements pull in different directions, so the mix gives no clear reason to buy and weakens the brand.

### Why integration influences competitive advantage

1. **Clarity:** customers quickly understand what the business offers and who it is for.
2. **Credibility:** a premium price is believed when the product, promotion and place all look premium; a low price is believed when everything else looks simple and efficient.
3. **Difficulty of copying:** a rival can copy one element quickly but finds it hard to copy a whole, consistent system.
4. **Efficiency:** money is not wasted on elements that work against each other, such as expensive advertising for a product sold mainly on price.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the four Ps as separate lists instead of showing how one changes another.
- Saying a business has a competitive advantage simply because it has a marketing mix.
- Recommending a premium element (for example, luxury packaging) for a business competing on low price.
- Describing an integrated mix as one where every element is "good" or "expensive". Integration means consistency with the target market and positioning.
- Forgetting the cost side: every change to the mix affects costs and therefore profit.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** contribution before and after a mix change, including any extra promotion or place costs.
- **Outline:** two linked points showing one element's effect on another in the case.
- **Explain:** a general statement with two expansions, for example why an integrated mix is hard to copy.
- **Analyse:** trace a change through at least two other elements to sales, costs and competitive advantage.
- **Justify:** choose between two mix changes, using figures and the target market, and show which keeps the mix integrated.
- **Evaluate:** judge which element gives a business its competitive advantage, or whether a mix is integrated, and conclude on what the advantage depends.
