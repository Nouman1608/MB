Section 3.3 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification lists "Accounting for capital and revenue expenditure and income", and its additional information asks for "the distinction between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure and capital income and revenue income". This guide goes deeper into that one statement than the combined [capital, revenue, depreciation and disposal guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-capital-and-revenue-items-depreciation-and-disposal-of/), which introduces the four categories. Here the focus is on borderline items, the double entry, the knock-on effect through depreciation, and correcting mistakes with journal entries. All figures are invented and in dollars.

Condensed recall is in the [capital and revenue revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-capital-and-revenue-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-revision-notes/), and exam-style questions are in the [capital and revenue practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-capital-and-revenue-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-practice/).

## Four tests for any payment

Examiners choose items that sit near the boundary. Work through these tests in order and you will rarely go wrong.

1. **Does it buy or create a non-current asset?** If the business ends up owning something it will use for more than one accounting period, the cost is capital expenditure.
2. **Is it a cost of getting that asset ready for use?** Delivery, installation, testing before use, and legal or architect's fees on property are all part of the asset's cost.
3. **Does it improve the asset or only keep it as it was?** An improvement increases capacity, speed, efficiency or useful life and is capital. A repair or replacement of a worn part with an identical part restores the asset and is revenue.
4. **Is the benefit used up within the period?** Running costs such as fuel, insurance, wages of operators, annual licences and routine servicing are revenue expenditure.

The word "new" in a question is not a test. A new set of tyres on an old van is still a replacement of a worn part.

## Worked example 1: borderline items

Okonjo Print Studio paid the following during the year.

| Payment | Amount | Classification | Test that decides it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital printing press | 26,000 | Capital | Buys a non-current asset |
| Carriage to deliver the press | 450 | Capital | Cost of getting it ready for use |
| Own staff's wages for building a storage mezzanine | 1,800 | Capital | Creates a non-current asset, even though the cost is wages |
| Faster paper feeder fitted to the old press | 2,200 | Capital | Improves output, so it is an improvement |
| Replacing worn rollers with identical rollers | 380 | Revenue | Restores, does not improve |
| Training staff to use the new press | 500 | Revenue | No asset the business records is created |
| Insurance on the press for the year | 720 | Revenue | Benefit used up in the period |
| Annual software licence | 600 | Revenue | Benefit used up in the period |

Two items catch candidates out. The mezzanine wages look like an expense because the account name is "wages", but the work produced an asset, so the cost is capitalised. The training is linked to the new press, but training is not part of the cost of the press.

## Capital and revenue income

Revenue income comes from normal trading and from the regular use of the business's resources:

- sales of goods or services
- rent received, commission received, interest received
- discount received.

Capital income is money that does not arise from normal trading:

- proceeds from selling a non-current asset, which are credited to the disposal account
- capital introduced by the owner, which is credited to the capital account.

A loan received is not income of either kind. It is a liability, so it is credited to a loan account and appears in the statement of financial position.

## The double entry

Classification decides which account is debited.

| Item | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure, paid by bank | Non-current asset account | Bank |
| Capital expenditure on credit | Non-current asset account | Supplier (entered through the general journal) |
| Revenue expenditure, paid by bank | Expense account | Bank |
| Revenue income received | Bank | Income account |
| Owner pays in extra capital | Bank | Capital |

Capital expenditure appears in the statement of financial position, and only its depreciation reaches the income statement. Revenue expenditure goes to the income statement in full in the year.

## Why the classification affects profit twice

If a capital item is charged as an expense, profit for the year is understated by the full amount. There is a second effect: the asset account is too low, so the depreciation charge, if it is based on cost, is also too low. The net effect on profit is the amount wrongly expensed less the extra depreciation that should have been charged.

## Worked example 2: correcting a draft profit

Okonjo's draft profit was 38,500. The press account showed 26,380, because the 380 roller replacement had been added to it. The 450 carriage was in general expenses, and the 2,200 feeder had been charged to repairs. The press is depreciated at 10% of cost a year, with a full year charged, and the draft depreciation was 10% of 26,380 = 2,638.

Correct cost of the press: 26,000 + 450 + 2,200 = 28,650. Correct depreciation: 10% of 28,650 = 2,865.

| Step | Effect | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| Draft profit | | 38,500 |
| Carriage was capital, remove from expenses | add 450 | 38,950 |
| Feeder was capital, remove from repairs | add 2,200 | 41,150 |
| Rollers were revenue, charge as repairs | deduct 380 | 40,770 |
| Extra depreciation (2,865 − 2,638) | deduct 227 | 40,543 |

Check through the statement of financial position. Draft net book value was 26,380 − 2,638 = 23,742. Corrected net book value is 28,650 − 2,865 = 25,785. The increase of 2,043 equals the increase in profit, 40,543 − 38,500.

The journal entries that make the correction (narratives shortened):

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Press | 450 | |
| General expenses | | 450 |
| Press | 2,200 | |
| Repairs | | 2,200 |
| Repairs | 380 | |
| Press | | 380 |
| Depreciation | 227 | |
| Provision for depreciation: press | | 227 |

## Worked example 3: income in the wrong place

In the same year Okonjo sold an old guillotine for 1,200 and the owner paid in 5,000 of extra capital. Both receipts were credited to sales.

- The guillotine proceeds overstate revenue and gross profit by 1,200. They belong in the disposal account, where they are compared with the net book value to find any profit or loss on disposal. Only that profit or loss reaches the income statement.
- The capital introduced overstates revenue and profit by 5,000.

The second error has an interesting effect on closing capital. With opening capital of 30,000, a draft profit of 45,000 (including the 5,000) and drawings of 12,000, closing capital was shown as 30,000 + 45,000 − 12,000 = 63,000. Correctly it is 30,000 + 5,000 + 40,000 − 12,000 = 63,000. The total is the same, but profit is overstated by 5,000, so any ratio based on profit or revenue is misleading.

## Concepts behind the distinction

- **Accruals.** Expenses are matched with the revenue of the period that benefits. A capital item benefits several periods, so its cost is spread through depreciation.
- **Historic cost.** The asset is recorded at what it cost to acquire and bring into use, which is why delivery and installation are added.
- **Materiality.** A low-cost item such as a stapler may last for years, but it is treated as revenue expenditure because recording and depreciating it would not change any decision.
- **Consistency.** Once a business sets a level below which items are expensed, it applies it every year so profits can be compared.

## Common mistakes

- Classifying by the account name (wages, carriage) instead of by what the spending produced.
- Treating any spending on an existing asset as revenue, even when it improves the asset.
- Forgetting the depreciation effect when asked for corrected profit.
- Crediting the proceeds of a non-current asset sale to sales.
- Calling a loan "capital income" and adding it to profit.
- Stating the effect on profit but not on the statement of financial position.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **identify**: one word or phrase per item, such as "capital expenditure". No reason is needed unless asked.
- **Explain**: give the classification, then the reason linked to the item, for example "capital, because the feeder increases the press's output".
- **Calculate**: set out a profit adjustment table like the one in worked example 2, so each step can earn credit even if the final figure is wrong.
- **Prepare a journal entry**: name both accounts, put debit before credit, and add a brief narrative.
- **Assess** or **evaluate**: give the effect on profit and on assets, then judge how far the error would mislead a user such as the owner or a lender.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are an employee's wages ever capital expenditure?**
Yes, when the employee's work creates or improves a non-current asset, such as building an extension. The wages are then part of the cost of that asset.

**Is the proceeds from selling a non-current asset revenue income?**
No. It is capital income. It is credited to the disposal account, and only the profit or loss on disposal appears in the income statement.

**Why does capitalising an expense change the depreciation charge?**
Depreciation is calculated on the asset's cost, so adding or removing an item from the asset account changes the charge, and that changes profit a second time.
