## Exam structure

**Open book**, 2 hours, 50 marks (50% of IAS grade). **Section A**: one pre-1900 text (choice of *Doctor Faustus*, *Othello*, *The Rover*, *She Stoops to Conquer*, *Twelfth Night*), one essay from a choice of two, **25 marks**. **Section B**: one post-1900 text (choice of *A Raisin in the Sun*, *A Streetcar Named Desire*, *Death of a Salesman*, *Top Girls*, *Waiting for Godot*), one essay from a choice of two, **25 marks**. Both sections assessed against the same four objectives: **AO1** (informed personal response), **AO2** (language/form/structure), **AO3** (context), **AO5** (interpretations).

## Since it's open book...

The value of memorising long quotations is lower than knowing your text well enough to **locate and deploy the right evidence quickly**. Bringing an *annotated* copy is **not permitted** — only a clean, unannotated text.

## Using AO5 — the most commonly under-used objective

A response that only argues one fixed reading answers AO1/AO2 but **leaves AO5 untouched**. Acknowledge that critics/directors have staged or read the play **differently** — then argue your own position within that range.

## Worked example: AO5 on Death of a Salesman

```
Weak (AO5-absent): "Willy Loman is a tragic figure destroyed by the
                    American Dream." -- a single fixed reading

Stronger (AO5-aware): some productions emphasise Willy as a
                    sympathetic victim of economic forces beyond his
                    control; others foreground his self-deception and
                    complicity in his own downfall; post-war
                    audiences may have responded to the play's
                    critique of capitalism differently from audiences
                    today. Naming this range, then arguing a position
                    within it, is what AO5 rewards.
```

The same claim-range-position approach applies to the pre-1900 text — for *Othello*, readings of Iago's motivation have shifted from morality-play villain to psychologically complex character across critical schools.

## AO3 (context) — different for each half

**Pre-1900**: engage with the conventions of the play's original genre (revenge tragedy for *The Rover*, morality-play structure for *Doctor Faustus*). **Post-1900**: situate the play within its immediate social/political moment (the McCarthy era for *A Streetcar Named Desire* and *Death of a Salesman*; 1980s Britain for *Top Girls*).

## Why both sections carry identical structure

Because both sections are assessed identically -- one essay from a choice of two, against the same four objectives -- the skills developed for one section transfer directly to the other. The main variable between them is the historical distance of the text and what that means for the kind of contextual knowledge AO3 rewards, not a different essay-writing technique altogether. Revising the essay structure (a clear argument, textual evidence deployed efficiently, contextual points integrated rather than bolted on, and at least one alternative interpretation acknowledged for AO5) as one transferable skill, applied to whichever text a question addresses, is more efficient than treating Section A and Section B as needing separate preparation strategies.

## Building a contextual note bank

Rather than trying to recall context from memory under exam pressure, build a short, structured note bank for each set text in advance: the play's original performance context (when and where it was first staged, and for what audience), its genre conventions where relevant, and at least two documented critical or directorial readings that differ from each other. Having this prepared in advance means AO3 and AO5 points can be deployed quickly and precisely in an open-book exam, rather than reconstructed from a general impression of the text under time pressure.

## Key terms

**AO5** — assessing awareness of different interpretations of a text, by critics, directors or across time. **AO3** — understanding of the context in which a text was written and received. **Dramatic form** — staging, stage directions, entrances/exits, silence — central to how meaning is made in drama, distinct from prose.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the pre-1900 and post-1900 sections as needing entirely different essay approaches, when both use the same four objectives and format.
- Under-using AO5 by presenting only one reading of the play.
- Discussing a play only as prose, ignoring staging and performance choices central to drama.
- Bringing an annotated text into the open-book exam.

## Quick self-test

1. How many marks is each section worth, and what is the total exam length?
2. Name the four assessment objectives used in both sections.
3. What is the key difference in what AO3 typically means for the pre-1900 text vs the post-1900 text?
4. Why does under-using AO5 lose marks even in a well-argued essay?
5. Why is analysing staging and stage directions important for a drama text specifically?
6. Name the two pieces of contextual information worth preparing in advance for each set text.

**Answers:** 1. 25 marks per section, 50 total, over a 2-hour exam. 2. AO1, AO2, AO3, AO5. 3. Pre-1900: usually engaging with original genre conventions (e.g. revenge tragedy, morality play). Post-1900: usually situating the play in its immediate social/political moment. 4. Because AO5 specifically rewards awareness of differing interpretations — an essay that argues only one fixed reading, however well-argued, leaves this objective largely unaddressed. 5. Because drama is written to be performed, not read as prose — meaning is made through staging and performance choices, so analysing them strengthens AO2 (form and structure) responses. 6. Its original performance context (when/where first staged, for what audience) and at least two documented critical or directorial readings that differ from each other.
