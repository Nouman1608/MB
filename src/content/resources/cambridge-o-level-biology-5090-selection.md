This guide covers subtopic **17.4 Selection** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe natural selection (variation within populations, production of many offspring, struggle for survival including competition, reproduction by the better-adapted individuals, and the passing on of their alleles); to describe how the inherited features of a population can evolve over time; to describe the development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, including MRSA, as an example; and to describe artificial selection (selective breeding) and its role in producing economically important plants and animals.

The [Inheritance topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-inheritance/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on selection, with step-by-step worked examples. Use the [17.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-selection-practice/) to test yourself.

## Natural selection

**Natural selection** is the process by which individuals that are better adapted to their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce, passing on their alleles to the next generation. It needs five ideas, which you should be able to put in order.

1. **Variation within populations.** The individuals of a species are not identical. Some of the differences are inherited (they depend on alleles).
2. **Production of many offspring.** Most species produce more offspring than can survive.
3. **Struggle for survival.** There is not enough food, water, space or mates for all. The individuals **compete** for resources, and are also affected by predators and disease.
4. **Reproduction by the better-adapted individuals.** Those with features that suit the environment are more likely to survive and breed.
5. **Passing on of alleles.** The survivors pass on the alleles that gave them an advantage. The next generation has a higher proportion of those alleles.

An **adaptation** is an inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment. "Survival of the fittest" means survival of those best suited to the environment, not necessarily the strongest.

### Evolution

**Evolution** is the change in the inherited features of a population over time as a result of natural selection. When the process is repeated over many generations, the proportion of individuals with the advantageous feature keeps rising. If the environment changes, different features may become advantageous, and the population changes in a different direction. Over a very long time this can produce new species.

## Worked example 1: a beetle population

*A population of beetles lives on a woodland floor. Most are brown, a few are green. Birds hunt the beetles by sight. The woodland floor is covered in leaf litter, which is brown. Explain what happens to the proportions of brown and green beetles over many generations.*

1. There is variation in the colour of the beetles (brown and green), which is inherited.
2. The beetles produce many offspring, but many are eaten.
3. Brown beetles are harder to see on brown leaf litter, so birds eat more green beetles.
4. More brown beetles survive and breed.
5. They pass on the allele for brown colour to their offspring.
6. In each generation, the proportion of brown beetles increases.

Numbers can show the effect. Suppose the first generation has 1 000 beetles, of which 900 are brown and 100 are green. Birds eat 10 per cent of the brown (90) and 60 per cent of the green (60). The survivors are 810 brown and 40 green, a total of 850. The percentage of brown beetles is 810 divided by 850 x 100 = 95.3 per cent, up from 90 per cent. The change is small in one generation but builds up over many.

## Antibiotic resistance and MRSA

Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria, or stop them growing. Bacteria show variation because **mutations** happen by chance in their DNA. A mutation may make a bacterium **resistant** to an antibiotic.

### How resistance develops (natural selection in bacteria)

1. In a population of bacteria, there is variation. A **mutation** gives one bacterium resistance to an antibiotic.
2. The population is treated with the antibiotic. The **non-resistant bacteria die**.
3. The **resistant bacterium survives** and reproduces. Bacteria can reproduce very rapidly, for example every 20 minutes.
4. The resistant bacteria pass on the resistance allele to their offspring.
5. In time, most of the population is resistant, and the antibiotic no longer works.

**MRSA** (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) is a strain of bacteria that has become resistant to several antibiotics. It is an example of natural selection. It is a particular problem in hospitals, where antibiotics are often used and patients are vulnerable.

### Worked example 2: how fast does resistance spread?

*A population of 1 000 000 bacteria contains 1 resistant bacterium. All the others are killed by an antibiotic. The survivors divide every 30 minutes. How long until there are more than 1 000 resistant bacteria?*

1. Doubling gives 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1 024. That is 10 doublings.
2. Time = 10 x 30 minutes = 300 minutes, or 5 hours.

After the antibiotic, the whole population descends from the resistant survivor, so everything is resistant. This shows how quickly resistant strains can build up.

### Reducing resistance

Doctors try to avoid antibiotic resistance by prescribing antibiotics only when they are needed, and by encouraging patients to complete the full course so that all the bacteria are killed. Resistant bacteria can then not survive to reproduce.

## Artificial selection (selective breeding)

**Artificial selection** is selection by **humans** of animals or plants with desirable features, to breed from. The steps are:

1. **Select** parents with the desirable features (for example high milk yield).
2. **Cross** (breed) them to produce the next generation.
3. **Select** the offspring that show the desirable features.
4. **Repeat** over many generations.

The difference from natural selection is that humans, not the environment, decide which individuals reproduce. Artificial selection also needs variation, because without it there would be nothing to choose between.

### Role in food production

| Organism | Desirable feature |
|---|---|
| Dairy cattle | High milk yield |
| Beef cattle | High meat yield |
| Hens | Large number of eggs |
| Wheat | Large grain yield, short stems that do not blow over, disease resistance |
| Rice and maize | High yield and resistance to pests |
| Fruit trees | Large, sweet fruit |

### Worked example 3: progress from selective breeding

*The mean milk yield of a herd of cows was 15 dm3 per day. After five generations of selective breeding it was 24 dm3 per day.*

1. Increase = 24 - 15 = 9 dm3 per day.
2. Percentage increase = 9 divided by 15 x 100 = 60 per cent.
3. The mean increase per generation = 9 divided by 5 = 1.8 dm3 per day.

This is possible because the farmer chose the cows with the highest yield and bred from them each generation.

### Disadvantages

Selective breeding reduces variation, because the population becomes more similar. A population with little variation may be at greater risk from a new disease or a change in the environment. It also takes many generations.

## Worked example 4: a model answer for MRSA

*Explain how MRSA has become common in some hospitals.*

- Bacteria show variation. A mutation by chance can make a bacterium resistant to an antibiotic.
- Antibiotics are used often in hospitals, so non-resistant bacteria are killed.
- The resistant bacteria are not killed, so they survive and have less competition.
- They reproduce quickly and pass the resistance allele to their offspring.
- Over time the proportion of resistant bacteria rises until the strain is common.
- Patients in hospital are often weak or have open wounds, so the resistant bacteria spread easily.

Every bullet point is a separate mark point. Notice that the explanation is the same pattern as natural selection in any other organism: variation, selection pressure, survival, reproduction and passing on of alleles.

## Selection pressures

A **selection pressure** is a factor in the environment that affects which individuals survive and reproduce. Examples are predators, disease, shortage of food, climate and, for bacteria, antibiotics. Natural selection always involves a selection pressure. If the pressure changes, the features that are advantageous may change as well. In artificial selection, the selection pressure is the choice made by the farmer or breeder.

| Situation | Selection pressure | Feature favoured |
|---|---|---|
| Insects hunted by birds on tree bark | Predation | Camouflage |
| Plants in a dry habitat | Shortage of water | Features that reduce water loss |
| Hospital bacteria | Antibiotics | Resistance |
| Dairy farm | Choice of the farmer | High milk yield |

## Linking selection to other subtopics

Natural selection depends on variation (17.1), which arises from mutation and from sexual reproduction (17.3). The features that are inherited depend on genes made of DNA (17.2). Asexual reproduction does not produce variation in the offspring, so a population of clones adapts only through rare mutations. This is why the variation produced by sexual reproduction is described as an advantage when the environment changes.

## Comparing the two types of selection

| Feature | Natural selection | Artificial selection |
|---|---|---|
| Who selects | The environment | Humans |
| What is selected | Features that help survival | Features useful to humans |
| Speed | Usually slow | Faster, but still takes generations |
| Result | Adapted populations; evolution | Crops and livestock with useful features |
| Needs variation | Yes | Yes |

## Common mistakes

- Saying that individuals "adapt" during their lifetime and pass this on. Natural selection acts on inherited variation.
- Saying that bacteria "become used to" antibiotics. Resistant bacteria survive and the others die.
- Saying that the antibiotic causes the mutation. The mutation occurs by chance; the antibiotic selects.
- Forgetting to include "passing on of alleles" in a natural selection answer.
- Writing that the strongest animals survive. The best adapted survive.
- Saying that selective breeding involves one generation.
- Missing out that repeated selection over many generations is needed.

## Command words

- **Describe**: give the sequence of ideas in natural selection.
- **Explain**: apply the steps to a situation, such as antibiotic resistance.
- **Discuss**: give the advantages and disadvantages of selective breeding.
