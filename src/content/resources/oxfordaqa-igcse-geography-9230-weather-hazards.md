This guide covers section 3.1.1.3, Weather hazards, in Section A (The challenge of natural hazards) of Unit 1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. The key ideas are: "Global atmospheric circulation helps to determine patterns of weather and climate"; "Tropical storms (hurricanes, cyclones, typhoons) develop as a result of particular physical conditions"; and "Tropical storms have significant effects on people and the environment." For the whole unit, start with the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/). This page goes deeper on the circulation model and tropical storms, and gives a worked example.

- The general atmospheric circulation model: pressure belts and surface winds.
- The global distribution of tropical storms and their relationship to atmospheric circulation.
- Causes of tropical storms and the sequence of their formation and development.
- The structure and features of a tropical storm; how climate change might affect their distribution, frequency and intensity.
- Primary and secondary effects; immediate and long-term responses.
- A **named example** of a tropical storm.
- Monitoring, prediction, protection and planning.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Tropical storm | A large, rotating storm with very strong winds and heavy rain that forms over warm tropical oceans |
| Hurricane, typhoon, cyclone | Regional names for the same type of storm |
| Atmospheric circulation | The large-scale movement of air around the Earth |
| Pressure belt | A band of high or low pressure at a particular latitude |
| Trade winds | Winds blowing towards the equator in the tropics |
| Westerlies | Winds blowing from the west in the mid-latitudes |
| Eye | The calm, clear centre of a tropical storm |
| Storm surge | A rise in sea level caused by low pressure and strong winds pushing water onto the coast |
| Coriolis effect | The deflection of moving air caused by the Earth's rotation |

## The general atmospheric circulation model

The sun heats the Earth most strongly at the equator, so there is a continuous movement of air to spread heat around the planet. The model has three cells in each hemisphere.

- **Hadley cell (equator to about 30°).** Air heated at the equator rises, creating a belt of **low pressure** with heavy rain. High in the atmosphere it spreads towards the poles, cools and sinks at about 30°, creating **high pressure** with dry conditions. At the surface, air flows back towards the equator as the **trade winds**.
- **Ferrel cell (about 30° to 60°).** Air flows at the surface from the subtropical high towards a belt of **low pressure at about 60°**, as the **westerlies**. In the northern hemisphere these are south-westerlies and in the southern hemisphere north-westerlies. The meeting of warm and cold air at 60° produces unsettled weather.
- **Polar cell (about 60° to the pole).** Cold air sinks at the poles, creating **high pressure**, and flows out as the **polar easterlies**.

Because of the Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect), winds are deflected to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere. That is why the trade winds blow from the north-east in the north and from the south-east in the south.

This pattern explains world climates. Rising air at the equator brings rainforests. Sinking air at about 30° brings hot deserts. It also helps to explain where tropical storms form and the paths they take.

## Global distribution of tropical storms

Tropical storms form over oceans between roughly **5° and 30° north and south** of the equator. They are not found on the equator itself, nor beyond about 30° except when they are drifting poleward as they weaken.

| Region | Name | Main area |
|---|---|---|
| Atlantic and eastern Pacific | Hurricane | Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico, east coast of the USA, Mexico |
| North-west Pacific | Typhoon | Philippines, China, Japan, Taiwan |
| Indian Ocean, South Pacific, Australia | Cyclone | Bay of Bengal, Bangladesh, India, Madagascar, northern Australia |

Tropical storms almost never form in the South Atlantic or the south-east Pacific, as the water is too cool and winds in the upper atmosphere disrupt development.

## Relationship with atmospheric circulation

The trade winds blow from east to west on either side of the equator, so tropical storms are carried **westwards** across the oceans. As the storm moves, the Earth's rotation turns it **poleward**. When it reaches the westerlies at higher latitudes, it is steered back **eastwards** and weakens over cooler water or land. This explains the curved tracks seen on maps, and why storms strike the **western** sides of oceans, such as the eastern coasts of the Americas and Asia.

The Coriolis effect also gives the storm its spin: anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern. It is too weak within about 5° of the equator to start rotation, which is why storms do not form there.

## Causes and sequence of formation

Several conditions must be met:

1. **Sea surface temperature of at least about 27°C**, to a depth of around 50 m, which provides heat and moisture.
2. **A position more than about 5° from the equator**, so there is enough spin.
3. **Light winds in the upper atmosphere (low wind shear)**, so the storm is not torn apart.
4. **A pre-existing area of low pressure and thunderstorms** to start things off.

The sequence is as follows.

1. Warm moist air **rises** from the ocean surface.
2. As the air rises, it **cools and condenses** to form huge cumulonimbus clouds, releasing **latent heat**. This heat warms the air and makes it rise faster.
3. Surface pressure **drops**, and air rushes in from the surrounding area.
4. The inrushing air is deflected by the Coriolis effect and begins to **spiral** around the centre.
5. As the storm grows, winds reach at least about 120 km/h, and a calm **eye** forms where air sinks in the centre.
6. The storm travels westwards with the trade winds, powered by warm water.
7. It **weakens** when it moves over land (cut off from its moisture and heat; friction slows the wind) or over cooler water.

## Structure and features

- **Size:** typically a few hundred kilometres wide.
- **Eye:** about 30 to 60 km wide, where air sinks. It is calm, with clear skies and low pressure.
- **Eyewall:** the ring of tall thunderclouds around the eye, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
- **Rainbands:** spiral bands of cloud and showers outside the eyewall.
- **Winds:** can exceed 250 km/h in the strongest storms.
- **Rainfall:** very heavy, often hundreds of millimetres in a day.
- **Storm surge:** a dome of raised sea level, often the most dangerous feature on low coasts.
- **Movement:** typically 10 to 40 km/h.

On a cross-section, label the eye, eyewall, rainbands, rising air in the wall, sinking air in the eye and the direction of spin.

## Climate change and tropical storms

Warmer oceans provide more energy and moisture, so the likely effects are:

- **Intensity:** the strongest storms are likely to become more intense, with higher wind speeds and heavier rain.
- **Distribution:** warm seas may spread further from the equator, so storms could affect places beyond their current range.
- **Frequency:** there is less agreement. The total number may stay the same or fall slightly, while the proportion of very strong storms rises.
- **Sea level rise:** higher sea levels make storm surges reach further inland.

Always write "might" or "is likely to". The science has uncertainty, and the specification itself uses the word "might".

## Effects

**Primary effects:** strong winds that damage buildings, power lines and crops; heavy rain; storm surge flooding the coast; deaths and injuries.

**Secondary effects:** river flooding and landslides from the rain; contaminated water and disease; loss of food and farm income; homelessness; disrupted transport, power and communications; loss of coastal habitats such as mangroves and coral; long-term economic loss and migration.

**Immediate responses:** evacuation before landfall, shelters, search and rescue, emergency food, water and medical care, clearing roads. **Long-term responses:** rebuilding homes and infrastructure, restoring livelihoods, rebuilding or building defences, improving warning and education.

## Named example: Typhoon Haiyan, Philippines, 2013

This is an example. You may have studied another storm, such as Hurricane Katrina in the USA in 2005; the structure of your answer will be the same. The figures are approximate.

- **The storm:** Typhoon Haiyan made landfall in the central Philippines in early November 2013, among the strongest tropical storms ever recorded at landfall, with sustained winds of around 300 km/h. The Philippines lies in the north-west Pacific typhoon belt on the western side of the ocean.
- **Primary effects:** a storm surge of several metres swept through coastal areas around Tacloban, destroying buildings, and winds flattened homes. More than six thousand people died.
- **Secondary effects:** millions of people were displaced, over a million homes were damaged or destroyed, crops and coconut plantations were lost, and many people lost their incomes. Debris blocked roads, delaying relief.
- **Immediate responses:** hundreds of thousands of people were evacuated before the storm. International aid, including military ships and aircraft, brought food, water and medical supplies, though damaged airports and roads slowed distribution.
- **Long-term responses:** rebuilding of homes with stronger designs, replanting of mangroves, improved evacuation planning and new coastal safety zones, though recovery was slow in poorer communities.

The Philippines is a lower-middle-income country. High population density on the coast, many weak homes and limited resources increased the harm.

## Managing tropical storms

- **Monitoring:** satellites, aircraft and weather buoys track storms and measure pressure and wind speed.
- **Prediction:** forecast models predict tracks several days ahead with good accuracy. Intensity is harder to predict.
- **Protection:** storm shelters; strong building design with reinforced roofs and shutters; sea walls, drainage and mangrove planting to absorb surge.
- **Planning:** evacuation routes, emergency stocks, public education, warning systems, land-use zoning that limits building on the most exposed coast.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain why tropical storms form between 5° and 30° north and south of the equator. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "Tropical storms need sea temperatures of at least 27°C, and these are found in the tropics. They need the Coriolis effect to make the air spiral, but this is too weak within about 5° of the equator, so storms do not form there. Beyond 30° the sea is usually too cool to supply enough heat and moisture, so storms weaken. This leaves a belt between about 5° and 30° where the water is warm enough and there is enough spin."

## Common mistakes

- **Saying storms form on land.** They form over warm oceans and weaken over land.
- **Confusing hurricane, typhoon and cyclone.** They are the same type of storm with different names by region.
- **Saying storms form on the equator.** The Coriolis effect is too weak there.
- **Describing the eye as the windiest part.** It is calm; the eyewall has the strongest winds.
- **Stating climate change will definitely increase frequency.** The evidence suggests intensity is more certain than frequency.
- **Giving an example without specific details.** Name the storm, the place and the date, and give at least two facts.
