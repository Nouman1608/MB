## Answers

**How the parts are marked.** One mark for each correct answer in the 1- and 2-mark questions. In the 4-mark questions, one mark for each point identified and one for explaining it. The 6-mark questions give four AO1 marks on that pattern plus up to two AO2 marks: **one** for a general, accurate use of a source of authority, **two** for a specific use applied to the question. The 12-mark question is marked by levels:

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 10–12 | Thorough examination of a range of points; logical reasoning with detailed evidence; excellent, direct use of the passage |
| 3 | 7–9 | Clear examination of more than one point; reasoning with some detail; good use of the passage |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some accurate examination; reasoning not always supported; general use of the passage. **Maximum Level 2 if only one point is made** |
| 1 | 1–3 | Limited examination with little reasoning. **Maximum Level 1 if the passage is not used** |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit |

**1.** The agreement (consensus) of qualified scholars on a religious ruling [1].

**2.** Reasoning by analogy: applying the ruling of a case in the text to a new case with the same cause [1].

**3.** Any two, [1] each: there is no clear text on the new case; the original case has a ruling in the Qur'an or Sunnah; the two cases share the same cause; the result does not contradict a text; it is done by a qualified scholar.

**4. (a)** Qiyas (analogy) [1].

**(b)** One reason identified [1] and explained [2].

- **Same cause** [1]: wine is forbidden because it clouds the mind and causes harm [1], and the modern drug has the same effect, so the same ruling applies [1].
- **No clear text** [1]: the Qur'an does not mention the drug [1], so the scholar compares it with the nearest case that it does mention [1].

**5.** For each of two examples: example identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Choosing Abu Bakr (RA)** [1]: the companions agreed to appoint him as the first caliph after the Prophet's (pbuh) death [1].
- **Collecting the Qur'an** [1]: the companions agreed to gather it into one book and later to standardise the copies [1].
- **Taraweeh behind one imam** [1]: Umar (RA) gathered people for the night prayer in Ramadhan and the companions accepted it [1].
- **The grandmother's share** [1]: they agreed she receives one-sixth when there is no mother [1].

**6.** For each of two reasons: reason identified [1] and explained [1].

- **Certainty** [1]: when qualified scholars agree, the ruling is firm and settles the question [1].
- **Unity** [1]: it holds the community together on important matters [1].
- **Protection from error** [1]: many scholars together are less likely to make a mistake than one, as a Hadith reports that the community will not agree on error [1].
- **Continuity** [1]: it carries the understanding of the early community forward [1].

**7.** AO1 **[4]**: for each of two reasons, reason identified [1] and explained [1]. AO2 **[2]**: one source of authority used generally [1] or specifically and applied [2].

Reasons (AO1):

- **New situations** [1]: it lets scholars give rulings on matters not named in the text [1].
- **Keeps reasoning disciplined** [1]: a ruling must be tied to a text and a shared cause, not personal opinion [1].
- **Keeps Shariah relevant** [1]: law can respond to changing times without new revelation [1].
- **Based on the Prophet's (pbuh) approval** [1]: he approved of striving to form an opinion [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- The Hadith of Mu'adh ibn Jabal (RA): the Qur'an, then the Sunnah, then his own striving, which the Prophet (pbuh) approved [2].
- Qur'an 4:59: "refer it to Allah and the Messenger" requires reasoning from the sources [2].

**8.** AO1 **[4]**: as in question 7. AO2 **[2]**: as in question 7.

Limits (AO1):

- **Cannot contradict the Qur'an or Sunnah** [1]: both are tested against the first two sources [1].
- **Only qualified scholars** [1]: ordinary opinion does not count as Ijma or Qiyas [1].
- **Only where no clear text exists** [1]: where a text decides, it overrides analogy [1].
- **A real shared cause** [1]: Qiyas fails if the reason is not truly the same in both cases [1].

Applications (AO2), for example:

- Qur'an 4:115: opposing the Messenger after guidance is clear is a serious wrong, so no ruling may go against the Sunnah [2].
- Qur'an 4:59: disagreements are referred to Allah and the Messenger [2].

**9.** Marked by the levels above. Possible content:

*Points of significance*

- **For the statement:** Ijma is more certain than the reasoning of one scholar; many scholars agreeing is strong protection from error; it settles questions for the whole community; many scholars use 4:115 to show it is binding; Qiyas can lead to disagreement between schools.
- **Against the statement:** Ijma is rare outside the early period; most new questions need Qiyas; Qiyas gives practical answers every day; Ijma itself often rests on reasoning from the text; Qiyas has a clear method of original case, new case and cause.
- **Balanced view:** each has a role; Ijma gives certainty on settled matters, Qiyas gives answers to new ones, and both depend on the Qur'an and Sunnah.

*Use of the passage*

- "Follows other than the way of the believers" is the phrase that many scholars use to support Ijma.
- "Opposes the Messenger after guidance has become clear" sets a limit on both Ijma and Qiyas.
- "Evil it is as a destination" shows how serious it is to leave the community's path.
- Credit candidates who note that the verse is a warning and that the case for Ijma comes from scholars' reading of it.

Level 4 answers use the passage directly, argue both sides with examples, and reach a reasoned judgement.
