These questions cover **section 3.2.2.6 Distribution of income** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: income and wealth inequality, its causes and consequences, and policies to redistribute income and wealth. There are no tiers. All countries, households and figures are invented. For explanations, see the [distribution of income study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-distribution-of-income/) and the [distribution of income revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-distribution-of-income-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of wealth? **[1]**

A: A monthly salary

B: Shares in a company

C: A weekly benefit payment

D: An annual dividend payment

**2.** A tax takes £1,000 from an income of £10,000, £4,000 from an income of £40,000 and £8,000 from an income of £80,000. Which **one** describes this tax? **[1]**

A: Progressive

B: Proportional

C: Regressive

D: Indirect

**3.** State what is meant by final income. **[1]**

**4.** An invented household has an original income of £18,000. It receives £2,400 in cash benefits, pays £2,900 in direct taxes and £3,100 in indirect taxes, and receives benefits in kind worth £7,200.

**(a)** Calculate its gross income. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate its disposable income. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate its final income. **[1]**

**5.** Country R charges no income tax on the first £10,000 of income and 25% on income above £10,000.

**(a)** Calculate the tax paid, as a percentage of income, by a person earning £16,000 and by a person earning £64,000. Give answers to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(b)** State whether the tax is progressive, proportional or regressive. **[1]**

**6.** The table shows the share of total income received by each quintile in two invented countries.

| Quintile | Country S | Country T |
|---|---|---|
| Poorest fifth | 8% | 5% |
| Second | 13% | 10% |
| Third | 17% | 15% |
| Fourth | 22% | 24% |
| Richest fifth | 40% | 46% |

Using calculations, identify which country has the more unequal distribution of income. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **one** reason why wealth is more unequally distributed than income. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain **two** causes of income inequality. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of means-tested benefits compared with universal benefits. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a rise in house prices may widen wealth inequality. **[3]**

**11.** Explain **two** consequences of high income inequality for an economy. **[4]**

**12.** "The government should reduce inequality by spending more on education and training, rather than by raising benefits." Evaluate this view. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Shares are an asset owned at a point in time; the others are flows of income. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Each household pays 10% of its income. **[1]**

**3.** Income after all taxes (direct and indirect) and cash benefits, plus the value of benefits in kind such as state education and healthcare [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** 18,000 + 2,400 = **£20,400** [1].

**(b)** 20,400 − 2,900 = **£17,500** [1].

**(c)** 17,500 − 3,100 + 7,200 = **£21,600** [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** £16,000: 0.25 × 6,000 = £1,500, which is **9.4%** [1]. £64,000: 0.25 × 54,000 = £13,500, which is **21.1%** [1].

**(b)** **Progressive**, because the percentage paid rises with income [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Country S: 40 ÷ 8 = 5.0 times; Country T: 46 ÷ 5 = 9.2 times [1]. Country T is more unequal, because its richest fifth receives a much larger multiple of the poorest fifth's share [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Wealth can be inherited and builds up over a lifetime [1], so differences grow across generations, while income is received and spent each period [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Any two developed: differences in skills and qualifications [1] mean workers with scarce skills earn higher wages [1]; unemployment or inactivity [1] leaves some households relying on benefits, which are lower than most wages [1]; ownership of wealth [1] gives some people rent, dividends and interest on top of their earnings [1]; discrimination [1] can lower the pay of some groups for the same work [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Advantage: means-tested benefits are targeted at the households who need them most [1], so each pound spent does more to reduce poverty and inequality [1]. Disadvantage: benefits are withdrawn as earnings rise [1], which reduces the reward from working more hours, and some eligible people do not claim [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Households who already own property see the value of their wealth rise [1]. People who rent cannot afford to buy as prices rise, so they miss out on these gains [1]. Owners can also borrow against or sell their homes and pass wealth to their children, so the gap grows [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Any two developed: low-income households spend less [1], which limits demand for firms' products and growth [1]; children from poorer homes get fewer opportunities [1], so their skills and future productivity are lower [1]; the government spends more on benefits [1], which must be funded by taxes or borrowing [1]. **[4]**

**12.** *For education and training:*

- It tackles a cause of low income: low skills [1].
- Higher skills raise productivity and wages, so inequality falls without paying benefits for ever [1].
- A more skilled workforce also supports economic growth [1].

*For benefits:*

- Benefits raise the incomes of poor households immediately, while education takes years to work [1].
- Some people cannot work, such as those with long-term illness or caring duties, so training does not help them [1] [1].

*Judgement:* education and training are better for reducing inequality in the long run because they tackle the cause [1], but benefits are still needed for people who cannot work and to reduce poverty while training takes effect, so the best approach combines both [1] [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Separate the short run from the long run; it is the clearest way to weigh the two policies.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a tax progressive because richer people pay more in pounds.
- Subtracting indirect taxes when calculating disposable income.
- Comparing quintile shares by subtraction only, without a clear comparison of the gap.
- Confusing income with wealth in definitions.
- Answering "Evaluate" with points on one side only.
