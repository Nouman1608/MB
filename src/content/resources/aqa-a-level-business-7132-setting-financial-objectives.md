This guide goes deeper into section **3.5.1 Setting financial objectives** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification's content is:

- **the value of setting financial objectives**, including the concept of a return on investment, revenue, costs and profit objectives, and cash flow objectives
- **the distinction between cash flow and profit**
- **the distinction between gross profit, operating profit and profit for the year**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## What financial objectives are

A **financial objective** is a measurable target for the financial performance of the business, set to support the corporate objectives. Finance is the function that records, plans and controls the money flowing into and out of a business, and its objectives connect the aims of the owners to figures that can be monitored.

## The value of setting financial objectives

- **Direction**: managers know what the finance function and the rest of the business must achieve.
- **Control**: actual results can be compared with targets, so problems are noticed early.
- **Motivation and accountability**: targets can be linked to bonuses and appraisals.
- **Coordination**: a profit target affects marketing spend, operations costs and HR budgets.
- **Reassurance to stakeholders**: lenders, investors and shareholders want to see clear financial aims.
- **Decision making**: a minimum return on investment gives a test for choosing projects.

**Limits:** targets may be unrealistic or out of date; a narrow focus on one figure (such as profit) can harm long-term health, for example by cutting training or maintenance; and managers may manipulate figures to meet targets.

## Types of financial objective

### Return on investment

**Return on investment (ROI)** measures the profit gained from an investment compared with the amount invested.

> ROI (%) = profit (or average annual profit) from the investment ÷ amount invested × 100

A business sets a **target ROI** (a required rate of return) and judges projects, products or the whole business against it. The target should be higher than what could be earned with little risk, such as interest on savings, because investments carry risk.

*Example.* Ledbury Printing buys a new press for £200,000 and expects it to add £30,000 a year to profit. ROI = 30,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **15%**. If the target is 12%, the project passes; if the bank pays 4% on deposits, 15% is attractive compensation for risk. Limits: ROI ignores the timing of profits and cannot say how reliable the forecast is.

### Revenue objectives

**Revenue** (sales revenue or turnover) is income from sales: price × quantity. A revenue objective might be "raise revenue by 8% next year". Revenue growth shows market success, but it does not guarantee profit, because higher sales may need heavy discounts or costs. It links closely to marketing objectives (see [setting marketing objectives](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-marketing-objectives/)).

### Cost objectives

A **cost objective** aims to reduce or control costs, for example "cut costs of sales by 3%" or "reduce overheads per unit". Lower costs raise profit without needing higher sales. The risk is cutting costs in ways that harm quality, staff morale or the future of the business. These objectives overlap with operational objectives such as unit cost.

### Profit objectives

A **profit objective** may be a total (for example "£300,000 profit for the year") or a **margin** (profit as a percentage of revenue, for example "operating profit margin of 10%"). Profit is the reward to owners and a source of funds for reinvestment (**retained profit**). Targets may differ by stage: a new business may aim just to break even, while a mature one aims for a rising margin or to sustain dividends. Not all businesses prioritise profit: a social enterprise may aim for a surplus to reinvest in its purpose.

### Cash flow objectives

**Cash flow** is the movement of money into and out of the business. A cash flow objective may be to "keep the bank balance above £50,000 at all times" or "cut the time customers take to pay from 45 to 30 days". Cash is the lifeblood of a business: even a profitable firm fails if it cannot pay its bills on time. Cash flow objectives matter most for new, fast-growing and seasonal businesses.

## Cash flow and profit: the distinction

| | Cash flow | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Money actually received and paid in a period | Revenue earned minus costs incurred in the period |
| Timing | Recorded when cash moves | Recorded when sales are made and costs are incurred, even if the money moves later |
| Credit sales | Counted only when the customer pays | Counted when the sale is made |
| Buying equipment | A large cash outflow immediately | Spread over the asset's life through depreciation |
| Loans | Loan received is a cash inflow | Not revenue, so no effect on profit (interest is a cost) |
| Importance | Ability to pay bills and survive | Long-run reward and success |

*Worked example.* In one month Rowle Foods records:

- sales revenue £120,000, of which £40,000 is on credit and unpaid at the month end
- costs £90,000, of which £20,000 is owed to suppliers and unpaid
- a new oven bought for cash, £25,000 (not charged in full as a cost this month).

**Profit** (simplified) = £120,000 − £90,000 = **£30,000**.

**Cash in** = £120,000 − £40,000 = £80,000. **Cash out** = (£90,000 − £20,000) + £25,000 = £70,000 + £25,000 = £95,000. **Net cash flow** = £80,000 − £95,000 = **−£15,000**.

Rowle is profitable but cash negative. If it started with only £10,000 in the bank it would be overdrawn by £5,000, despite the profit. This is why profit and cash targets must both be set.

## Gross profit, operating profit and profit for the year

The income statement shows profit at three stages.

| Line | How it is found |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Sales income |
| Cost of sales | Direct costs of the goods sold (materials, direct labour) |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Operating expenses (overheads) | Rent, wages of non-production staff, marketing, depreciation, etc. |
| **Operating profit** (profit from operations) | Gross profit − operating expenses |
| Finance costs (and income) | Interest paid on loans (less interest received) |
| Profit before tax | Operating profit − net finance costs |
| Tax | Corporation tax |
| **Profit for the year** | Profit before tax − tax |

*Worked example.* Marlow Interiors reports revenue of £2,400,000, cost of sales £1,440,000, operating expenses £720,000, interest paid £40,000. Assume tax is 20% of profit before tax.

- Gross profit = £2,400,000 − £1,440,000 = **£960,000**; gross margin = 960 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **40%**.
- Operating profit = £960,000 − £720,000 = **£240,000**; operating margin = **10%**.
- Profit before tax = £240,000 − £40,000 = **£200,000**.
- Tax = 20% × £200,000 = **£40,000**.
- Profit for the year = £200,000 − £40,000 = **£160,000**; net margin = 160 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **6.7%** (to one decimal place).

Each measure tells a different story:

- **Gross profit** shows how well the business makes or buys its product relative to the price: it is affected by pricing, materials costs and production efficiency.
- **Operating profit** shows how well the whole operation is run after overheads, before financing and tax.
- **Profit for the year** is what is left for shareholders after interest and tax, available for dividends or retention.

A business can have a healthy gross margin but a poor operating margin if overheads are too high, or good operating profit but a poor result after paying heavy interest.

## Setting good financial objectives

A good objective is specific, measurable and time-bound, and it considers:

- **corporate objectives**: survival, growth or profit
- **stage of life**: start-up, growth, maturity
- **market conditions and competition**: price pressure and demand
- **the business's stakeholders**: shareholders want returns; lenders want security
- **ethical and environmental influences**, which may add costs (fair pay, sustainable materials) but support reputation
- **technology**, such as crowd funding as a source of money and online payment systems that speed up cash receipts
- **links with other functions**: marketing's sales forecast, operations' cost targets and HR's staffing plans all feed the financial targets.

Objectives can conflict: a revenue growth target may require discounts that cut margins, and a profit target may mean cutting investment that would raise future returns.

## Technique for exam questions

- Write the formula, show working and state the unit.
- Use "profit" with its type: gross, operating or for the year.
- Explain why cash and profit differ, referring to credit, equipment purchases or depreciation.
- Evaluate by considering what the business most needs: a start-up usually needs cash; a mature company may prioritise ROI and dividends.

## Common mistakes

- Treating cash flow and profit as the same thing.
- Confusing gross profit with profit for the year.
- Forgetting that profit margins are percentages of revenue.
- Setting an objective with no number or time limit.
- Saying a profitable business cannot fail.
- Forgetting that a loan raises cash but not profit.
