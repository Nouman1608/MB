## Why businesses budget

A **budget** is a plan for a future period, expressed in money and quantities. **Budgetary control**
compares actual results with the budget, finds the differences (variances), and acts on them.

Budgeting serves several purposes:

- **Planning.** Managers think ahead about sales, costs and cash before committing resources.
- **Co-ordination.** The sales, production, purchasing and personnel plans must fit together.
- **Communication.** Each manager learns what is expected of their department.
- **Control.** Actual results can be checked against a target, so problems show up early.
- **Motivation and responsibility.** Each budget has a **budget holder** who answers for it.
- **Performance evaluation.** Variances show which managers met their targets.

Budgets are prepared in an order. The **principal budget factor** (often sales demand) limits
activity, so its budget comes first. The sales budget drives the production budget, which drives
the materials purchases and labour budgets. All of these feed the cash budget, then the budgeted
income statement and budgeted statement of financial position. Together these form the **master
budget**.

## Sales, production, purchases and labour budgets

### Worked example 1: Thornby Benches

Thornby Benches makes garden benches. Sales forecast: April 400, May 500, June 600, July 550 benches,
at £120 each. Closing inventory of finished benches is 20% of the next month's sales. Each bench
uses 2 kg of resin at £5 per kg. Closing resin inventory is 25% of the next month's usage, and
July production is planned at 560 benches. Each bench takes 1.5 labour hours at £14 per hour.

**Sales budget** (units × price): April £48 000, May £60 000, June £72 000.

**Production budget** (units). Production = sales + closing inventory − opening inventory.

| | April | May | June |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 400 | 500 | 600 |
| Add closing inventory (20% of next month) | 100 | 120 | 110 |
| Less opening inventory | (80) | (100) | (120) |
| **Production** | **420** | **520** | **590** |

April's opening inventory is 20% of April's sales (80), because March closed on the same policy.
Each month's closing inventory becomes the next month's opening inventory. Likewise April's opening
resin is 25% × 840 = 210 kg.

**Materials purchases budget.** Usage is production × 2 kg: April 840, May 1 040, June 1 180, July
1 120 kg.

| | April | May | June |
|---|---|---|---|
| Usage (kg) | 840 | 1 040 | 1 180 |
| Add closing inventory (25% of next month's usage) | 260 | 295 | 280 |
| Less opening inventory | (210) | (260) | (295) |
| **Purchases (kg)** | **890** | **1 075** | **1 165** |
| **Purchases at £5 per kg** | **£4 450** | **£5 375** | **£5 825** |

**Labour budget.** Hours = production × 1.5: April 630, May 780, June 885. Cost at £14: April
£8 820, May £10 920, June £12 390, a quarter total of £32 130.

The labour budget shows whether the workforce can deliver the plan. If normal hours available are
fewer than hours needed, the budget must include overtime (at a premium rate), new recruits or
reduced output.

## The cash budget

A cash budget shows expected receipts and payments month by month, and the bank balance at the end
of each month. It records **cash flows only**. Depreciation, irrecoverable debts written off and
provisions never appear, because no cash moves. Capital receipts and payments (loans received, new
non-current assets, capital introduced, drawings) do appear.

### Worked example 2: Tate Tiles (sole trader)

Rhian Tate's budgeted sales: May £30 000, June £32 000, July £36 000, August £40 000, September
£38 000. 25% of sales are for cash. Of credit sales, 60% are received the month after the sale and
40% two months after. Purchases are 60% of each month's sales and are paid the following month.
Wages: £5 200 in July and August, £5 600 in September. Rent of £4 500 for the quarter is paid in
July. General expenses are £1 800 a month. A delivery van costing £15 000 is paid for in August.
Drawings are £2 500 a month. The bank balance on 1 July is £3 200.

**Step 1: receipts schedule** (July shown; August and September follow the same pattern).

```
July: cash sales           25% × 36 000            =  9 000
      June credit sales    75% × 32 000 × 60%      = 14 400
      May credit sales     75% × 30 000 × 40%      =  9 000
                                                     32 400
```

**Step 2: the cash budget.**

| £ | July | August | September |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Receipts** | 32 400 | 35 800 | 38 300 |
| Payments to suppliers | 19 200 | 21 600 | 24 000 |
| Wages | 5 200 | 5 200 | 5 600 |
| Rent | 4 500 | – | – |
| General expenses | 1 800 | 1 800 | 1 800 |
| Delivery van | – | 15 000 | – |
| Drawings | 2 500 | 2 500 | 2 500 |
| **Total payments** | 33 200 | 46 100 | 33 900 |
| Opening balance | 3 200 | 2 400 | (7 900) |
| Net cash flow | (800) | (10 300) | 4 400 |
| **Closing balance** | **2 400** | **(7 900)** | **(3 500)** |

Brackets show an overdraft. The budget warns Rhian, two months ahead, that the van purchase will
push the account £7 900 overdrawn. She can arrange an overdraft facility or a loan, delay the van,
or reduce drawings. That early warning is the main benefit of a cash budget.

## Budgeted financial statements

The budgeted income statement uses the **accruals** basis, so it differs from the cash budget. On
1 July Tate Tiles had equipment (cost £60 000, accumulated depreciation £18 000), inventory £8 000,
trade receivables £33 000, trade payables £19 200, bank £3 200 and capital £67 000. Equipment is
depreciated at 10% a year on cost; the van at 20% a year on cost, from August. Inventory stays at
£8 000 because each month's purchases equal that month's cost of sales.

## Budgetary control and variances

A **variance** is the difference between a budgeted and an actual figure.

- **Favourable (F):** actual profit higher than budget, i.e. revenue above budget or a cost below it.
- **Adverse (A):** actual profit lower than budget, i.e. revenue below budget or a cost above it.

Comparing actual results with the original (**fixed**) budget misleads when output differs, because
variable costs rise with volume. A **flexed budget** recalculates revenue and variable costs at the
actual output, with fixed costs unchanged. Variances against the flexed budget show efficiency and
pricing; the gap between the flexed and fixed budget profit shows the effect of volume.

### Worked example 3: Ridley Lamps

Budget: 2 000 lamps at £60, materials £18 and labour £12 per lamp, fixed overheads £30 000. Actual
output and sales were 2 200 lamps.

| £ | Fixed budget | Flexed budget (2 200) | Actual | Variance (flexed v actual) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 120 000 | 132 000 | 128 700 | 3 300 A |
| Materials | 36 000 | 39 600 | 41 250 | 1 650 A |
| Labour | 24 000 | 26 400 | 25 960 | 440 F |
| Fixed overheads | 30 000 | 30 000 | 31 100 | 1 100 A |
| **Profit** | **30 000** | **36 000** | **30 390** | **5 610 A** |

Reconciliation: budgeted profit £30 000 + volume variance £6 000 F (36 000 − 30 000) − flexed
variances £5 610 A = actual profit £30 390.

Interpretation: on the fixed budget, profit is only £390 F and materials look £5 250 A, which hides
the real story. Selling 200 more lamps added £6 000 of profit, but that gain was almost wiped out.
The selling price averaged £58.50, not £60, perhaps a discount used to win the extra sales.
Materials cost £18.75 a lamp instead of £18, through a supplier price rise or more wastage. Labour
was efficient. Managers should investigate the large adverse items first and ask whether they are
controllable. Section 3.11 splits these variances further into price and usage, rate and efficiency.

## Benefits and limitations of budgeting

**Generic benefits:** planning, co-ordination, a basis for control and appraisal, clear
responsibility, and motivation when targets are agreed and achievable.

**Generic limitations:** budgets rely on forecasts that may be wrong; preparation takes time and
money; rigid budgets can stop managers reacting to change; imposed or unrealistic targets
demotivate; managers may build in **budgetary slack** (padding costs) or spend up to the limit to
protect next year's allocation; departments may chase their own budget at the expense of the whole
business.

**Specific budgets:**

- **Cash budget:** shows shortages early so finance can be arranged; but timing assumptions (when
  customers pay) are uncertain.
- **Sales budget:** sets the activity level for every other budget; any error flows through them all.
- **Production budget:** plans capacity and inventory levels; less useful where demand is volatile.
- **Purchases budget:** avoids stock-outs and excess inventory; prices may change.
- **Labour budget:** shows overtime or recruitment needs early; ignores staff absence and productivity changes unless built in.
- **Budgeted income statement and statement of financial position:** show whether the plans give an acceptable profit and financial position; only as reliable as every budget beneath them.

### Incremental and zero-based budgeting

**Incremental budgeting** takes last period's budget or actual figures and adjusts them, for example
by adding inflation. It is quick, cheap and stable. But it carries forward past inefficiency,
assumes existing activities are still worth doing, and encourages "spend it or lose it".

**Zero-based budgeting (ZBB)** starts every budget from zero: each activity and every pound must be
justified as if it were new. It removes waste and links spending to current objectives. But it is
slow and costly, needs skilled managers, and may favour activities with easily measured benefits.
Many businesses use ZBB periodically, or only for discretionary costs such as marketing and
training, and incremental budgeting otherwise.

## Common errors

- Putting depreciation or irrecoverable debts in a cash budget.
- Forgetting that this month's closing inventory is next month's opening inventory.
- Applying a credit percentage to total sales when only part of sales is on credit.
- Labelling a cost below budget as adverse; a lower cost is favourable.
- Comparing actual with the fixed budget when output differs, without flexing.
- Treating the cash budget closing balance as profit.
