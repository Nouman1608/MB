## Isomers: the definitions

**Isomers** are compounds with the **same molecular formula** but different arrangements of atoms.

- **Structural isomers** have the same molecular formula but **different structural formulas** (the atoms are joined in a different order).
- **Stereoisomers** have the same structural formula but a **different arrangement of the atoms in space**.

## Structural isomers

There are three types the specification names.

### Chain isomers

The carbon skeleton is arranged differently (straight chain or branched). They have similar chemical properties but different physical properties: a branched isomer has a lower boiling point than the straight-chain isomer, because branching reduces the surface contact between molecules and so weakens the London forces.

C₅H₁₂ has three chain isomers: pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane.

### Position isomers

The carbon skeleton and the type of functional group are the same, but the **functional group is attached at a different position**.

- Propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH, and propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃.
- 1-chlorobutane and 2-chlorobutane.
- But-1-ene and but-2-ene.

### Functional group isomers

The atoms are arranged to form **different functional groups**.

- Propanal, CH₃CH₂CHO (aldehyde) and propanone, CH₃COCH₃ (ketone): both C₃H₆O.
- Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH (alcohol) and methoxymethane, CH₃OCH₃ (ether): both C₂H₆O.
- Propanoic acid, CH₃CH₂COOH, and methyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₃: both C₃H₆O₂.

### A systematic method for finding all isomers

1. Find the formula and decide what the degree of unsaturation allows: an alkane CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ is saturated; CₙH₂ₙ may be an alkene or a ring.
2. Start with the longest straight chain.
3. Shorten the chain by one carbon and place the removed carbon as a branch at each different position.
4. Shorten again and repeat; do not count a structure twice if it is the same molecule turned over.
5. Name each one to check that you have not duplicated.

### Worked example 1: the isomers of C₆H₁₄

There are five:

1. hexane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃
2. 2-methylpentane, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂CH₃
3. 3-methylpentane, CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃
4. 2,2-dimethylbutane, (CH₃)₃CCH₂CH₃
5. 2,3-dimethylbutane, (CH₃)₂CHCH(CH₃)₂

Methyl at carbon 4 of pentane is the same as methyl at carbon 2, so it is not a new isomer.

### Worked example 2: the alcohols with molecular formula C₄H₁₀O

1. butan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH
2. butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃
3. 2-methylpropan-1-ol, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH
4. 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH₃)₃COH

The first two are position isomers of each other, as are the last two. The pairs butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol are chain isomers.

### Worked example 3: the isomers of C₄H₈ that are alkenes

- but-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃
- but-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃ (which exists as two stereoisomers, E and Z)
- 2-methylpropene, (CH₃)₂C=CH₂

Cyclobutane and methylcyclopropane have the same formula but are cycloalkanes, so they are also structural isomers of the alkenes (the carbon atoms are joined in a ring).

## E–Z isomerism

### Why it happens

Atoms joined by a single bond can rotate freely about it. The carbon–carbon double bond is different: it consists of a σ bond and a π bond, and the π bond prevents rotation. The two carbon atoms and the four groups attached to them lie in one plane. Because of this **restricted rotation**, two different arrangements of the groups are possible and they cannot interconvert at room temperature. They are stereoisomers.

E–Z isomerism occurs when:

1. there is a C=C double bond; and
2. **each** carbon of the double bond has **two different** groups attached.

### Examples and non-examples

| Compound | E–Z isomers? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| But-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | Yes | Each carbon has CH₃ and H |
| But-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ | No | One carbon has two H atoms |
| 2-methylpropene, (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ | No | Both groups on each carbon are the same |
| 1,2-dichloroethene | Yes | Each carbon has Cl and H |
| Pent-2-ene | Yes | Each carbon has two different groups |

## The CIP priority rules

To name an isomer **E** or **Z**, assign priorities to the two groups on each carbon of the double bond.

### The rules

1. Look at the two atoms directly attached to one carbon of the double bond. The atom with the **higher atomic number** has the higher priority.
2. If the atoms are the same, compare the atoms they are attached to, one step further out, at the first point of difference.
3. Repeat for the other carbon of the double bond.
4. Decide whether the two higher-priority groups are on the **same side** or **opposite sides** of the double bond.

- Same side: **Z** (from the German *zusammen*, together).
- Opposite sides: **E** (from *entgegen*, opposite).

### Worked example 4: but-2-ene

On each carbon, the groups are CH₃ and H. The carbon of CH₃ (atomic number 6) outranks H (1), so CH₃ is higher priority on both carbons.

```
 H₃C       CH₃            H₃C       H
    \     /                  \     /
     C = C                    C = C
    /     \                  /     \
   H       H                H       CH₃
 (Z)-but-2-ene            (E)-but-2-ene
```

If the two CH₃ groups are on the same side, it is Z. If on opposite sides, E.

### Worked example 5: a halogenoalkene

For BrClC=CHCH₃ (1-bromo-1-chloropropene):

- On the first carbon: Br (35) and Cl (17). Bromine has the higher atomic number, so Br is higher priority.
- On the second carbon: CH₃ (carbon, 6) and H (1). CH₃ is higher priority.
- If Br and CH₃ are on the same side, the isomer is Z. If they are on opposite sides, it is E.

### Worked example 6: going further out

Take CH₃CH=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ (3-methylpent-2-ene).

- On C2: CH₃ and H, so CH₃ is higher.
- On C3: CH₃ and CH₂CH₃. Both start with carbon. Compare the atoms attached to that carbon: CH₃ has (H, H, H) and CH₂CH₃ has (C, H, H). The ethyl group has a carbon where the methyl has a hydrogen, so ethyl is higher priority.
- If the CH₃ on C2 and the ethyl on C3 are on the same side, the isomer is Z.

### Cis and trans

Older names **cis** (same side) and **trans** (opposite sides) are used when each carbon has an H and one other group, as in but-2-ene. The E–Z system also works when there are three or four different groups, where cis–trans does not. You may be asked to say that cis–trans is a special case of E–Z.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that stereoisomers have different structural formulas. They have the same structural formula and differ in the arrangement of atoms in space.
- Missing that each carbon of the C=C must carry two different groups.
- Counting an isomer twice because it is drawn with the chain reversed.
- Assigning priority by the size of the group instead of by atomic number at the first point of difference.
- Deciding E or Z by the position of the substituents regardless of their priority.
- Calling a ring compound a chain isomer. The carbon atoms are joined in a ring, not a chain.
- Forgetting that E and Z isomers of an alkene have the same structural formula, so they are not structural isomers.
- Explaining restricted rotation by "the double bond is strong". The reason is that the π bond would have to break for the groups to rotate.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define structural isomer / stereoisomer**: same molecular formula, with a difference in either the order of bonding or the arrangement in space.
- **Draw isomers**: give structural formulas with names; check carbon counts.
- **Explain why X shows E–Z isomerism**: restricted rotation about the C=C, and two different groups on each carbon.
- **Deduce E or Z**: state the priority on each carbon, using atomic number, then the arrangement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is hex-1-ene an E–Z isomer?**
No. The first carbon of the double bond has two hydrogen atoms.

**Why do branched alkanes have lower boiling points?**
The molecules are more compact with less contact between them, so there are weaker London forces.

**Is cis always Z?**
Not always. When the higher-priority groups are not the same type of group on each carbon, cis and Z can differ, which is why E–Z is the more reliable system.
