These questions cover section **3.3.13.4 DNA** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3. Learn the ideas first in the [DNA study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-dna/) and the [DNA revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-dna-revision-notes/). The structures of the phosphate ion, 2-deoxyribose and the four bases are in the Chemistry Data Booklet.

## Questions

**1.** Name the three components that make up a nucleotide in DNA. **[3]**

**2.** Name the four bases found in DNA. **[2]**

**3.** Describe how nucleotides are joined together in a single strand of DNA. **[3]**

**4.** State what is meant by the sugar–phosphate backbone of DNA, and state where the bases are attached. **[2]**

**5.** One strand of DNA has the base sequence T–T–A–G–C–A. Write the sequence of the complementary strand. **[2]**

**6.** State the number of hydrogen bonds formed between:

**(a)** adenine and thymine **[1]**

**(b)** guanine and cytosine **[1]**

**7.** Explain how hydrogen bonding between base pairs leads to the two complementary strands of DNA. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why the strands of DNA can be separated without breaking the backbone of each strand. **[2]**

**9.** Calculate the total number of hydrogen bonds between the two strands in the section of double helix in which one strand has the sequence G–A–T–C–C–A. **[3]**

**10.** In a sample of double-stranded DNA, 18 % of the bases are thymine. Calculate the percentage of each of the other three bases. **[3]**

**11.** A double helix has 800 base pairs, of which 35 % are G–C pairs. Calculate the total number of hydrogen bonds between the strands. **[3]**

**12.** A scientist heats two samples of DNA of the same length. Sample P has 70 % G–C pairs and sample Q has 30 % G–C pairs. Predict which needs the higher temperature to separate the strands, and explain why. **[3]**

**13.** Compare the bonding in the backbone of a DNA strand with the bonding between the two strands, and explain why each type is suited to its job. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** A phosphate (ion/group) [1]; 2-deoxyribose [1]; a base [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Adenine and cytosine [1]; guanine and thymine [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The phosphate group of one nucleotide [1]; bonds covalently to the 2-deoxyribose of the next nucleotide [1]; forming a chain of alternating sugar and phosphate groups (by condensation, with loss of water) [1]. **[3]**

**4.** The backbone is the alternating sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate chain joined by covalent bonds [1]; the bases are attached to the sugars (2-deoxyribose) [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A–A–T–C–G–T [2] (1 mark if only one or two bases are wrong). **[2]**

**6. (a)** 2 [1]

**(b)** 3 [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Each base has a specific arrangement of groups that can form hydrogen bonds [1]; hydrogen bonds form between N–H and the lone pair on N or O of another base [1]; adenine pairs only with thymine (two hydrogen bonds) and guanine only with cytosine (three hydrogen bonds) [1]; so the sequence of bases on one strand determines the sequence on the other, giving two complementary strands [1]. **[4]**

**8.** The hydrogen bonds between the strands are weak, so little energy is needed to separate the strands [1]; the strong covalent bonds in the sugar–phosphate backbone are not broken [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Complementary strand pairs: G–C, A–T, T–A, C–G, C–G, A–T [1]; A–T pairs: 3 × 2 = 6 hydrogen bonds, G–C pairs: 3 × 3 = 9 hydrogen bonds [1]; total = **15 hydrogen bonds** [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Adenine = 18 % (A pairs with T) [1]; the remaining 100 − 36 = 64 % is shared equally by cytosine and guanine [1]; cytosine = 32 % and guanine = 32 % [1]. **[3]**

**11.** G–C pairs = 0.35 × 800 = 280, giving 280 × 3 = 840 hydrogen bonds [1]; A–T pairs = 800 − 280 = 520, giving 520 × 2 = 1040 hydrogen bonds [1]; total = **1880 hydrogen bonds** [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Sample P needs the higher temperature [1]; G–C pairs have three hydrogen bonds and A–T pairs only two, so P has more hydrogen bonds per base pair [1]; more energy is needed to break them and separate the strands [1]. **[3]**

**13.** The backbone has covalent bonds between phosphate and sugar groups, which are strong, so each strand is stable [1]; the bonds between strands are hydrogen bonds between base pairs, which are weak individually [1]; the large number of hydrogen bonds holds the helix together overall [1]; but they can be broken so that the strands separate, while the backbone stays intact [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Whenever a question asks about the "advantage" of hydrogen bonds, say both parts: strong enough in total to hold the strands and weak enough individually to allow separation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Pairing A with C or G with T.
- Stating that the bases are attached to the phosphate.
- Writing 2 hydrogen bonds for G–C or 3 for A–T.
- Describing the backbone as hydrogen bonded.
- Forgetting that the percentages of A and T are equal, and C and G are equal.
- Omitting the reason the strands can separate.
