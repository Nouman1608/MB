## The analysis formula

Every analytical point has three parts. Miss the third and you lose the mark.

```
1. IDENTIFY the feature (with correct terminology)
2. QUOTE it briefly
3. EXPLAIN the EFFECT on the reader, linked to purpose
```

```
Weak    "The writer uses adjectives to describe the city."
Strong  "The adjectives 'cramped', 'clamorous' and 'unrelenting'
         accumulate to make the city feel oppressive, positioning the
         reader to share the narrator's exhaustion."
```

## What to analyse

| Level | Look for |
|---|---|
| Language | Imagery, semantic fields, connotation, verbs, adjectives |
| Sentences | Short for impact, long for accumulation, minor for emphasis |
| Structure | Opening, shifts in time/focus, delayed information, closing |
| Tone | Admiring, critical, wry, nostalgic — and whether it **shifts** |
| Voice | First person immediacy vs detached third person |

**Structure is analysable too** — where a writer places information is a choice with an effect.

## Comparison

Organise by **point of comparison**, not text by text. Use explicit connectives: *whereas*, *similarly*, *by contrast*, *both*.

## Composition

| Type | Key move |
|---|---|
| Descriptive | One **moment** rendered fully through the senses — not a whole day thinly |
| Narrative | Decide the **ending** before you start; few characters |
| Viewpoint | Clear line of argument, 2–3 developed points, genuine counter-argument |

Vary sentence length deliberately: a short sentence after several long ones lands with force.

Match **register** to the stated audience and form, and hold it throughout.

## Technical accuracy — where marks go

Sentence demarcation (avoid comma splices) · consistent tense · subject–verb agreement · apostrophes · paragraphing that signals a shift in time, place or topic.

**Range** = varied structures and precise vocabulary. Ambition is rewarded only when controlled.

## Exam traps

- Feature-spotting with no explanation of effect.
- Summarising content instead of analysing craft.
- Comparing texts sequentially rather than by point.
- Describing an entire day instead of one moment.
- Starting a narrative with no ending in mind.
- Rhetorical questions used as filler.

## Self-test

1. State the three parts of an analytical point.
2. Why analyse structure as well as language?
3. How should a comparison be organised?
4. What is the commonest weakness in descriptive writing?
5. Name three technical accuracy features that carry marks.

**Answers:** 1. Identify the feature, quote it briefly, explain the effect on the reader in relation to purpose. 2. Where a writer places information — a delayed reveal, a short closing sentence, a shift in time — is a deliberate choice producing an effect, and structural comment is separately credited. 3. By point of comparison, integrating both texts under each point, with explicit comparative connectives. 4. Covering too much ground thinly instead of rendering a single moment with precise sensory detail. 5. Any three: sentence demarcation, consistent tense, subject–verb agreement, apostrophes, purposeful paragraphing.
