OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690), examined in Unit 2 Section A. The
specification lists "Culture: norms and values; roles; status; stereotypes; subcultures, including

personal troubles and public issues". The unit overview is in the
[Exploring Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-exploring-sociology/).

These are the building-block concepts of the whole subject. Examiners expect them to be used
precisely, so this guide concentrates on the distinctions between them and how to apply them.

## Culture

**Culture** is the learned, shared way of life of a group: its language, beliefs, customs,
knowledge, skills and the objects it produces. Sociologists sometimes separate **material culture**
(tools, buildings, clothing, technology) from **non-material culture** (values, norms, symbols,
language). Three features matter in answers:

1. **Culture is learned, not inherited.** Children raised in different societies acquire different
   languages, diets and manners, which shows behaviour is socially, not biologically, shaped.
2. **Culture is shared**, which makes social life predictable, although sharing is never total.
3. **Culture varies** between societies and over time. **Ethnocentrism** is judging other cultures
   by the standards of your own; **cultural relativism** is understanding a practice in its own
   context.

**Perspectives on culture.** Functionalists, following Émile Durkheim, stress shared values (a
**collective conscience**) that bind society together. Marxists argue the dominant culture reflects
ruling-class ideas, so it serves the powerful. Interactionists see culture as continually
negotiated in everyday interaction.

## Norms and values

**Values** are general beliefs about what is good, right and worth striving for, such as respect for
elders, individual achievement or religious devotion. **Norms** are specific rules of behaviour that
put values into practice: a value of respect for elders may produce norms such as standing when an
older person enters a room.

William Graham Sumner (*Folkways*, 1906) distinguished **folkways**, everyday conventions whose
breach brings mild disapproval, from **mores**, norms with strong moral weight whose breach brings
outrage. Some mores become **laws**, backed by formal sanctions. Norms vary: what is polite dress or
greeting in one society may be offensive in another, and norms change, as shown by shifting
expectations about women's employment or smoking in public.

## Roles and status

A **status** is a social position; a **role** is the set of behaviours expected of someone in that
position. Ralph Linton (*The Study of Man*, 1936) distinguished:

- **Ascribed status**: fixed at birth or beyond the individual's control, such as sex, caste,
  royal birth or age.
- **Achieved status**: earned through effort or merit, such as a qualification or job title.

Industrialisation is often said to shift societies from ascribed towards achieved status, although
class, gender and ethnicity still shape what people can achieve. Everyone holds many statuses at
once, which can produce **role conflict** (incompatible demands from two roles, such as employee and
carer) and **role strain** (pressure within one role). A **master status** is one that dominates how
others see a person, a concept that links to stigma in 3.1.2.4.

## Stereotypes

A **stereotype** is an oversimplified, generalised image of a group, applied to all its members.
The journalist Walter Lippmann popularised the term in *Public Opinion* (1922), describing
stereotypes as "pictures in our heads". Stereotypes:

- simplify a complex social world, which is why they are persistent;
- are often negative and can justify **prejudice** (attitudes) and **discrimination** (actions);
- can become **self-fulfilling prophecies** if people internalise them or institutions act on them;
- are reproduced by agencies of socialisation, especially the media.

Stereotypes link this section to identity (3.1.2.3) and to othering (3.1.2.4).

## Subcultures, including ethnic groups in multicultural societies

A **subculture** is a group whose norms and values differ in some ways from the mainstream while
sharing others. Examples include youth subcultures, occupational subcultures and religious
communities. The specification highlights **ethnic groups in multicultural societies**: societies
where several ethnic groups live together, each keeping distinctive languages, religions,
festivals, foods or family practices.

Examples include Singapore, whose state has long organised policy around Chinese, Malay, Indian and
"Other" categories, and Canada, which adopted an official multiculturalism policy in 1971. Ethnic
subcultures are maintained through family socialisation, religious institutions, community
organisations, media in community languages and, sometimes, separate schools.

Debates to use in evaluation:

- **Integration vs distinctiveness**: functionalists worry that strong subcultures could weaken
  shared values; multiculturalists argue diversity and shared civic values can coexist.
- **Change**: second and third generations often blend cultures, producing hybrid identities
  (3.1.2.3).
- **Power**: conflict theorists note that a dominant group defines what counts as "mainstream", so
  minority subcultures may be marginalised.

## The sociological imagination (Mills)

C. Wright Mills, in *The Sociological Imagination* (1959), argued that people experience their
lives as private matters, but that these lives are shaped by history and social structure. The
sociological imagination is the ability to see the link between **biography** and **history**,
between **personal troubles** and **public issues**.

- **Personal troubles** occur within an individual's character and immediate relationships, and
  are solved, if at all, within that milieu.
- **Public issues** concern the institutions of a society as a whole and can only be explained and
  addressed at that level.

Mills's best-known example is unemployment: if one person in a city is unemployed, that is a
personal trouble, and we look at their skills or character; if a large share of a nation's
workforce is unemployed, that is a public issue rooted in the economy. He applied the same logic
to divorce, war and urban life.

**Applying Mills today.** A graduate unable to find work in their field may blame themselves, but
widespread graduate underemployment reflects the structure of labour markets. A family struggling
with care for an older relative faces a trouble that, across a society, reflects ageing
populations and social policy.

**Evaluation.** Mills was a radical critic of American power elites, and his idea pushes sociology
towards public relevance, which links to Burawoy's public sociology in 3.1.3.5. Critics note that
the idea is a way of thinking rather than a testable theory, and interactionists would add that
individuals also shape structures through their choices.

## Worked example: annotated 8-mark answer

*Explain two ways in which norms differ from values.*

> **Generality.** Values are broad beliefs about what is desirable, such as respect for elders,
> whereas norms are specific rules for behaviour, such as greeting older relatives first
> (**define and contrast**). Norms put values into practice, so one value can generate many norms,
> which can differ between societies that share the value (**develop**).
>
> **Sanctions.** Norms are directly enforced by sanctions: breaking a folkway brings mild
> disapproval, breaking a more brings strong condemnation (Sumner) (**evidence**). Values are not
> broken in the same way; people may hold a value but fail to live up to it, which shows the gap
> between belief and behaviour (**analysis**).

## Common mistakes

- Using norms and values interchangeably.
- Treating role and status as the same: status is the position, role the expected behaviour.
- Describing Mills without naming both **personal troubles** and **public issues**.
- Writing about subcultures without the specification's focus on **ethnic groups in multicultural
  societies**.
- Assuming culture is entirely shared and fixed.

## Exam technique

Short definition questions are easy marks if you give a precise definition and an example from a
named society. In 8-mark **explain** questions, develop each point with a consequence. In 20-mark
**evaluate** questions, compare functionalist, Marxist and interactionist readings and finish with
a judgement.
