## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by computer-aided design (CAD). **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** way in which technology can improve stock control in a business. **[2]**

**3.** Kiln Ceramics made 90,000 plates with 45 workers. After installing computer-controlled machines it made 114,000 plates with 38 workers. Calculate the percentage increase in output per worker. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **one** risk to a business of depending on technology. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is an example of e-commerce?

A: A robot welding car panels.

B: Customers ordering and paying for goods on a website.

C: A computer drawing a product design.

D: Barcode scanners recording deliveries.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Kiln Ceramics is considering buying a 3D printer to make prototype moulds. The printer costs £36,000. The prototypes are currently made by an outside firm for £15,000 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period for the printer. **[2]**

**(b)** The printer would need £2,400 a year of materials and maintenance. Calculate the new payback period, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Kiln Ceramics should buy the printer. **[8]**

**7.** Kiln Ceramics is a family business that has always sold through gift shops. It is thinking of selling directly to customers through its own website. Costs are £28,000 to set up the site and £9,000 a year to run it.

**(a)** Explain **two** ways in which an online shop could change the operations of Kiln Ceramics. **[4]**

**(b)** Evaluate the impact of this decision on the stakeholders of Kiln Ceramics. **[9]**

**8.** A bank has replaced half of its branch counters with self-service machines and a mobile app. Analyse the effects of this change on the bank's employees and customers. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** CAD uses a computer to produce and test designs on screen [1], so changes can be made and tested without building physical models [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Barcode scanners and stock software record each item sold or delivered [1], so the business has an accurate stock level in real time and can reorder automatically when stock reaches the reorder level [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Before: 90,000 ÷ 45 = 2,000 plates per worker [1]. After: 114,000 ÷ 38 = 3,000 plates per worker [1]. Increase = (3,000 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **50%** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** If the system fails, because of a breakdown, software fault or cyber-attack, operations may stop completely [1], and the cost of lost output and repairs may be higher than the saving the technology brought [1]. Accept: staff skills are lost and the business becomes dependent on a few specialists. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** £36,000 ÷ £15,000 [1] = **2.4 years** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Net annual saving = £15,000 − £2,400 = £12,600 [1]. Payback = £36,000 ÷ £12,600 [1] = **2.9 years** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** The payback of 2.9 years is acceptable if the printer lasts for more than five years, which is common for such machines [1], and the saving is certain as long as Kiln needs the same number of prototypes [1].
Non-financial benefits: designers can test a mould in a day rather than waiting for an outside firm [1], so new designs reach the market sooner [1], and designs are kept in-house [1].
Risks and limits: the printer is useful only if enough prototypes are needed to keep it busy [1], staff need training [1], and the machine may be out of date in a few years [1].
Judgement: Kiln should buy it if it expects to make at least as many prototypes as now, because the payback is under three years and the speed benefit is large [1]; if prototype numbers are uncertain, it should keep using the outside firm for another year or lease the printer [1]. Marks are for any eight of these points. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Order handling: orders arrive one at a time from many customers instead of in bulk from shops, so Kiln needs to pick, pack and post single items [1], which requires different packaging and a way to deal with returns [1]. Stock: an online shop needs real-time stock records so that customers are not sold items that are out of stock [1], and this may need new stock software [1]. Accept: payment processing, customer service by email, data protection. **[4]**

**(b)** Owners: direct sales earn the full retail price instead of the wholesale price, which may increase profit [1]. They pay £28,000 to start and £9,000 a year, and sales are uncertain [1].
Employees: new skills are needed in website management and packing, which may give new job roles [1]; the workload for existing staff may increase before extra staff are recruited [1].
Gift shops (customers of the existing business): they may lose sales, and may complain or reduce orders if Kiln competes with them [1].
Consumers: wider choice and convenience, but they cannot see the product, and delivery may damage fragile plates [1] [1].
Evaluation: the effect depends on how many customers the website attracts and whether Kiln can keep the gift shops' loyalty [1]. Judgement: the website is worth building if Kiln can limit the conflict with shops, for example by selling designs that shops do not stock, because the extra margin could cover the £9,000 running cost [1]. **[9]**

**8.** Employees: fewer counter staff are needed, so jobs may be lost or staff redeployed to advice roles [1]; remaining staff need training in the new systems and may deal with more complex enquiries [1]; the work may be less routine [1].
Customers: they can bank at any time and avoid queues [1]; some, such as older customers or those without internet access, may find the new methods hard to use and lose personal service [1]; they must trust the security of the system [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a technology without saying how it changes an operation.
- Assuming that technology automatically cuts costs, with no capital or running cost.
- Writing only about job losses.
- Not calculating payback correctly (using the cost as the saving, or forgetting running costs).
- Ignoring risks such as system failure and data security.
