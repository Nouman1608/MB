## Section A: short questions

**1.** Correct the errors in this original sentence: *"Its obvious that the boys loose there sense
of order and this effects the hole island."* **[4]**

**2.** Rewrite as one sentence with the quotation embedded: *"The mother is controlling. 'Sit down
and be quiet.' This shows her control."* **[2]**

**3.** Fix this comma splice in two different ways: *"The speaker remembers the war, she cannot
forget the noise."* **[2]**

**4.** Rewrite in the present tense: *"Shakespeare showed that the king was weak when he gave away his
power."* **[2]**

**5.** Shorten this quotation using an ellipsis so that only the key words remain, then comment
briefly on them. Original line: *"I will go, and I will not look back, though every window calls my
name."* **[3]**

**6.** Replace the informal or vague words in this original sentence: *"The writer kind of shows
that the dad is really bad and stuff."* **[3]**

**7.** Show how to set out these two titles in a handwritten essay: a play called *The Lantern House*
and a poem called *Harbour*. Both titles are invented. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**8.** Write three thesis statements for this original question, labelled weak, better and strongest:
*"How does the writer present the effects of guilt?"* Then explain what makes your strongest
version effective. **[5]**

**9.** Rewrite this original paragraph so that it has a clear point, an embedded quotation, analysis,
evaluation and accurate Standard English:

> *Original paragraph:* In the play the writer shows us that Mara is lonely. "I talk to the walls
> now, they're the only ones who listen." This is a metaphor, it shows she is lonely. Their is lots of
> loneliness in the play and it makes the audience feel sad for her alot.

**[8]**

**10.** Here are two original topic sentences from an essay arguing that a writer presents ambition
as destructive: *"Another thing is the way the character speaks."* and *"Ambition is also shown in
the ending."* Rewrite each so that it advances the argument. **[4]**

**11.** Using your own studied text, write a paragraph of about 120 words answering the question
*"How does the writer present a character's attitude to responsibility?"* Your paragraph must embed
two short quotations, use at least two different sentence structures and include one sentence of
evaluation. **[6]**

**12.** Proofread your answer to Question 11. List any errors you find and correct them, then explain
one change you made to improve clarity. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** "It's obvious that the boys lose their sense of order and this affects the whole island."
One mark each, up to 4: "It's" with an apostrophe [1]; "lose" not "loose" [1]; "their" not "there"
[1]; "affects" not "effects" and "whole" not "hole" [1].

**2.** Example: *"The mother's command to 'sit down and be quiet' reveals her need to control the
family."* [1] The quotation is part of the sentence's grammar and is followed by analysis [1].

**3.** Any two: *"The speaker remembers the war. She cannot forget the noise."* [1] *"The speaker
remembers the war; she cannot forget the noise."* [1] Also acceptable: *"The speaker remembers the
war because she cannot forget the noise."*

**4.** *"Shakespeare shows that the king is weak when he gives away his power."* [1] All three verbs
are in the present tense [1].

**5.** Example: *"I will go... though every window calls my name"* [1]. Comment: the windows are
personified as calling to the speaker, suggesting the home's pull is strong [1], so the speaker's
determination to leave is presented as a painful choice [1].

**6.** Example: *"The writer presents the father as cruel and controlling."* One mark each: "kind
of" removed [1]; "dad" replaced with "father" [1]; "really bad and stuff" replaced with precise
adjectives [1].

**7.** The play title underlined, with capitals: The Lantern House, underlined [1]; the poem title in
quotation marks: "Harbour" [1].

**8.** Award marks as follows:

- A weak thesis that names the topic without an argument, such as "The writer presents guilt in many
  ways" [1].
- A better thesis with a view, such as "The writer presents guilt as a punishment" [1].
- A strongest thesis that is arguable, specific and covers the whole text, such as "The writer
  presents guilt as a slow punishment that isolates characters more completely than any outside
  justice could" [1].
- Explanation that the strongest version can be disagreed with and defended [1].
- Explanation that it promises development across the text [1].

**9.** Example: *"The writer presents Mara's loneliness as so complete that she has replaced people
with objects. Her admission that she talks 'to the walls' because they are 'the only ones who
listen' is darkly comic, but the humour exposes how little attention anyone pays her. This is more
affecting than a direct statement of misery would be, because Mara herself seems to accept her
isolation as normal."*

Award one mark each:

- A clear opening point linked to the focus [1].
- The quotation embedded in the sentence's grammar [1].
- Analysis of the method rather than a label such as "metaphor" alone [1].
- Effect on the audience explained precisely [1].
- A sentence of evaluation [1].
- The comma splice ("This is a metaphor, it shows...") corrected [1].
- "Their" and "alot" corrected or removed [1].
- Formal register and varied sentence structures [1].

**10.** Award two marks for each rewrite: one for linking back to the argument and one for moving it
forward. Examples: *"The character's speech begins to fracture as ambition takes hold, showing its
damage from within."* [2] *"By the ending, ambition has destroyed not only the character but
everyone who trusted them."* [2]

**11.** Award marks as follows:

- A topic sentence that answers the question [1].
- Two short quotations, both embedded [1].
- Analysis of at least one method [1].
- A sentence of evaluation [1].
- At least two different sentence structures, such as a complex sentence and a short sentence [1].
- Accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar throughout [1].

**12.** Award marks as follows:

- Errors identified accurately, or a justified statement that none were found after checking [1].
- Corrections made correctly [1].
- One change explained in terms of clarity, such as splitting a long sentence or replacing a vague
  word [1].

## Using these questions well

Do Section A as a quick accuracy drill. Then use Questions 8 to 12 to practise the habits that AO4 and
the higher levels reward: a sustained argument, embedded evidence and checked writing. Keep a list of
the errors you make most often and check for them first when you proofread in the exam.
