These notes condense subtopic **12.3 Immunity** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series: antigens and antibodies, active immunity from infection or vaccination, how vaccination works and controls disease, passive immunity, the importance of breast-feeding, and the effect of HIV on the immune system. ## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning in exam wording |
|---|---|
| Antigen | A molecule on a pathogen, with a specific shape, that the body recognises as foreign |
| Antibody | A protein made by lymphocytes that binds to a specific antigen |
| Active immunity | Defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body |
| Passive immunity | Short-term defence against a pathogen by antibodies acquired from another individual |
| Memory cells | Lymphocytes that remain after an immune response and give long-term immunity |
| Vaccination | Giving weakened pathogens or their antigens to stimulate immunity |

## Antibodies

- Each pathogen has its **own antigens with specific shapes**.
- Specific antibodies have **complementary shapes** which fit specific antigens.
- Antibodies bind to antigens and lead to **direct destruction** of the pathogen, or **marking** it for phagocytes.
- Lymphocytes make antibodies; phagocytes engulf pathogens.

## Active immunity

- Gained **after an infection** or by **vaccination**.
- The person's own lymphocytes make antibodies.
- Memory cells are made, which give **long-term immunity**.
- A second exposure gives a faster and stronger response.

## Vaccination: three steps

1. **Weakened pathogens or their antigens** are given.
2. The antigens **stimulate an immune response** by lymphocytes, which produce antibodies.
3. **Memory cells** are produced, giving long-term immunity.

If the real pathogen enters later, the memory cells make antibodies quickly, so the person does not become ill.

### Controlling the spread of disease

If most of a population is vaccinated, the pathogen finds few susceptible hosts, so it spreads poorly and unvaccinated people are also protected (herd immunity).

Example: 9200 of 10 000 vaccinated leaves 800 unvaccinated, which is 8 per cent.

## Passive immunity

- Antibodies come from **another individual**.
- Examples: **across the placenta** and **in breast milk**.
- Protection is **immediate** but **short term**.
- **No memory cells** are produced.

### Breast-feeding

Gives the baby antibodies against pathogens the mother has met, protecting the baby while its own immune system develops.

## Active and passive compared

| Feature | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Antibodies made by | The person | Another individual |
| Gained by | Infection or vaccination | Placenta or breast milk |
| Memory cells | Yes | No |
| Length | Long term | Short term |

## HIV and the immune system

- HIV infects **lymphocytes**.
- The number of lymphocytes **decreases**.
- The ability to **produce antibodies** is reduced.
- The immune system is weakened, so the person gets more infections.

## Antibody response graph

| | First exposure | Second exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Time to peak | Slow (days) | Faster |
| Peak level | Low | Much higher |
| Reason | Few lymphocytes fit the antigen | Memory cells respond quickly |

## Short model answers

**Explain why a vaccine gives long-term immunity.** The antigens stimulate lymphocytes to make antibodies and memory cells. If the pathogen enters later, the memory cells quickly produce antibodies.

**Explain why passive immunity is short term.** The antibodies come from another person and are broken down, and no memory cells are produced.

**Explain why a baby receives antibodies from its mother.** Antibodies cross the placenta before birth and are in breast milk after birth, protecting the baby while its own immune system develops.

**Explain why HIV weakens the immune system.** It destroys lymphocytes, so fewer are available and fewer antibodies are made.

## Exam traps

- Vaccines contain **weakened** pathogens or **antigens**, not active dangerous ones.
- Vaccination gives **active** immunity.
- Passive immunity has **no memory cells**.
- Lymphocytes make **antibodies**.
- Antibodies are **specific**.
- Do not mix up vaccines and antibiotics.
- HIV reduces the number of **lymphocytes**.

## Self-test

1. Define an antigen.
2. What is an antibody made of?
3. Which cells produce antibodies?
4. State two ways in which antibodies help to destroy pathogens.
5. How is active immunity gained?
6. What are memory cells?
7. Outline the three steps of vaccination.
8. Why does vaccination of most of a population protect those not vaccinated?
9. Give two ways in which passive immunity is gained by a baby.
10. Why does passive immunity not last long?
11. Why is breast-feeding important for a baby?
12. How does HIV weaken the immune system?

### Answers

1. A molecule with a specific shape on a pathogen that the body recognises as foreign.
2. Protein.
3. Lymphocytes.
4. Direct destruction of the pathogen; marking it for phagocytes to engulf.
5. After an infection, or by vaccination.
6. Lymphocytes that remain after an immune response and give long-term immunity.
7. Weakened pathogens or antigens are given; the antigens stimulate lymphocytes to make antibodies; memory cells are produced.
8. The pathogen finds few susceptible people, so it spreads poorly.
9. Across the placenta; in breast milk.
10. The antibodies are broken down and no memory cells are made.
11. It gives the baby antibodies (passive immunity) while its own immune system develops.
12. It destroys lymphocytes, so fewer antibodies are made.
