This guide covers sub-topic **8(a) Units** of the Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4 specification. Its single
statement, **8.1**, reads: use the following units: kilogram (kg), metre
(m), metre/second (m/s), metre/second² (m/s²), newton (N), second (s),
newton/kilogram (N/kg). It is core content. Astrophysics questions use
very large distances and very long times, so the main skill is converting
given values into these units before substituting. The ideas behind the
calculations are explained in the
[Motion in the universe study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-4ph1-motion-in-the-universe/)
and the topic overview,
[Astrophysics](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-astrophysics/).

## What each unit measures in Topic 8

| Unit | Symbol | Quantity | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| kilogram | kg | Mass | Mass of an astronaut, probe or planet |
| metre | m | Distance; orbital radius | v = 2πr / T; distances to galaxies |
| metre/second | m/s | Speed, velocity | Orbital speed; galaxy velocity; speed of light |
| metre/second² | m/s² | Acceleration | Acceleration of a falling object on another planet |
| newton | N | Force, including weight | Weight on Earth, the Moon or another planet |
| second | s | Time; orbital period | T in v = 2πr / T |
| newton/kilogram | N/kg | Gravitational field strength g | Comparing g on planets and the Moon |

## Mass, weight and gravitational field strength

Mass and weight are different quantities with different units, and
astrophysics questions test the difference directly.

- **Mass** (kg) is the amount of matter in an object. It is the same everywhere.
- **Weight** (N) is the gravitational force on the object. It depends on where the object is.
- **Gravitational field strength** g (N/kg) is the force on each kilogram of mass.

```
weight = mass × gravitational field strength
W = m × g      (N = kg × N/kg)
```

Statement 8.3 says that g varies, and is different on other planets and
the Moon from its value on Earth. On the Moon, g is about 1.6 N/kg; on
Earth, close to 10 N/kg. A question will always give you the value it
wants you to use.

The units N/kg and m/s² are **equivalent**: one newton is the force that
gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². So a planet's g, in N/kg, is also
the acceleration, in m/s², of an object falling freely near its surface,
ignoring air resistance.

## Orbital speed: metres and seconds

The orbital speed equation from statement 8.6 is:

```
orbital speed = (2 × π × orbital radius) / time period
v = 2πr / T
```

For v to come out in **m/s**, r must be in **metres** and T in
**seconds**. Questions often give r in kilometres and T in hours, days
or years, so convert first:

```
1 km   = 1000 m           (× 10³)
1 h    = 3600 s
1 day  = 24 × 3600 = 86 400 s
1 year ≈ 365 × 86 400 ≈ 3.15 × 10⁷ s
```

The orbital radius is measured from the **centre** of the body being
orbited. If a question gives a satellite's height above the surface, add
the planet's radius to get r.

## Speeds in astrophysics

The red-shift equation (statement 8.16P) compares two speeds:

```
change in wavelength / reference wavelength = velocity of a galaxy / speed of light
Δλ / λ₀ = v / c
```

Both sides are ratios with **no unit**. The two wavelengths only need to
be in the **same** unit as each other (often nanometres), and v comes out
in the unit used for c. With c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, v is in m/s.

## Standard form

Astrophysical numbers are huge, so use standard form throughout. For
example, Earth's orbital radius is about 150 000 000 000 m, written
1.5 × 10¹¹ m. Enter standard form on a calculator with the ×10ˣ key, not
by typing "× 10 ^" with extra brackets you might forget.

## Checking an answer with its units

Units can be treated like algebra to check that an equation has been
used correctly:

- **v = 2πr / T:** 2π has no unit, so the unit of v is m ÷ s = m/s. If your working gives km/h or m/day, a conversion has been missed.
- **W = m × g:** kg × N/kg = N. The kilograms cancel, leaving newtons.
- **T = 2πr / v:** m ÷ (m/s) = s. The metres cancel, leaving seconds.
- **Δλ / λ₀ = v / c:** nm ÷ nm and (m/s) ÷ (m/s) both cancel, leaving a pure number.

If the units of your answer do not match the quantity asked for, look
for the step where a unit was not converted.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (weight on the Moon).** An astronaut has a mass of 75 kg.
Take g = 10 N/kg on Earth and 1.6 N/kg on the Moon.

```
weight on Earth = 75 × 10  = 750 N
weight on Moon  = 75 × 1.6 = 120 N
mass on Moon    = 75 kg (unchanged)
```

**Example 2 (km and hours).** A navigation satellite orbits at a radius
of 26 600 km with a period of 12 h. Calculate its orbital speed.

```
r = 26 600 km = 2.66 × 10⁷ m
T = 12 × 3600 = 43 200 s
v = 2π × 2.66 × 10⁷ / 43 200 = 3.9 × 10³ m/s (2 s.f.)
```

**Example 3 (days).** Earth orbits the Sun at r = 1.5 × 10¹¹ m in 365
days.

```
T = 365 × 86 400 = 3.15 × 10⁷ s
v = 2π × 1.5 × 10¹¹ / 3.15 × 10⁷ = 3.0 × 10⁴ m/s
```

**Example 4 (rearranging).** The Moon's orbital radius is 3.8 × 10⁵ km
and its orbital speed is 1.0 km/s. Find the period in seconds and in
days.

```
T = 2πr / v
r = 3.8 × 10⁸ m, v = 1000 m/s
T = 2π × 3.8 × 10⁸ / 1000 = 2.4 × 10⁶ s
in days: 2.4 × 10⁶ / 86 400 ≈ 28 days
```

## Common mistakes

- Giving weight in kilograms, or mass in newtons.
- Saying an astronaut's mass changes on the Moon.
- Substituting kilometres and hours straight into v = 2πr / T; the answer is then in km/h, not m/s, and usually marked wrong.
- Using the height above the surface as the orbital radius.
- Typing 1.5 × 10¹¹ incorrectly into the calculator: check by estimating the order of magnitude.
- Writing N/kg for an acceleration in a question that asks for m/s²: they are equivalent, but use the unit the question asks for.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write each conversion as a separate line so a correct method earns credit even if the final number slips.
- **State the unit:** when asked "give the unit", N/kg for g, N for weight, m/s for orbital speed.
- **Estimate:** round to one significant figure first, such as 2π ≈ 6, to check an answer's order of magnitude.
- **Show that:** give your final value to at least one more significant figure than the value shown in the question.
