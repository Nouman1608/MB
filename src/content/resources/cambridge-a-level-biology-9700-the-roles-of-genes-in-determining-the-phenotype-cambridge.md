This guide covers section **16.2 The roles of genes in determining the phenotype** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is A Level content, examined in Paper 4, and the chi-squared test also appears in Paper 5. The [Inheritance study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-inheritance/) introduces every cross type with one example each; this page adds new worked examples, a reliable method for setting out genetic diagrams, practice in deciding which type of cross you are looking at, and model answers for the gene-to-phenotype outcomes. For recall use the [16.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-the-roles-of-genes-in-determining-the-phenotype-cambridge-revision-notes/); to test yourself use the [16.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-the-roles-of-genes-in-determining-the-phenotype-cambridge-practice/).

## A genetic diagram that earns every mark

Examiners award marks for each line, so set out every cross in the same six lines:

1. **Key**: define each symbol, for example "H = allele for normal clotting, h = allele for haemophilia".
2. **Parental phenotypes**.
3. **Parental genotypes**.
4. **Gametes**: circle each type; a homozygote gives one type.
5. **Offspring genotypes**, from a Punnett square.
6. **Offspring phenotypes with ratio** (or probability or percentage, if asked).

For sex-linked genes, write alleles as superscripts on X (Xᴴ, Xʰ) and show Y with no allele. For codominant alleles, use a capital letter with superscripts (for example Cᴿ and Cᵂ), because neither allele is recessive.

## Recognising the type of cross

| Clue in the data | Likely explanation |
|---|---|
| Three phenotypes in a 1 : 2 : 1 ratio from two heterozygotes | Codominance |
| More than two alleles at one locus (for example blood groups) | Multiple alleles |
| Ratios differ between males and females | Sex linkage |
| 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 from AaBb × aabb | Unlinked genes, test cross |
| Two large parental classes and two small recombinant classes | Autosomal linkage with crossing over |
| 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 pattern collapsed into three or two phenotype classes | Epistasis |

## Worked example 1: sex linkage with codominance

In cats, a gene on the X chromosome has two codominant alleles: Xᴼ (orange fur) and Xᴮ (black fur). A female XᴼXᴮ has patches of both colours (tortoiseshell). Cross a tortoiseshell female with a black male.

```
Parental phenotypes:  tortoiseshell female   ×   black male
Parental genotypes:   XᴼXᴮ                       XᴮY
Gametes:              Xᴼ, Xᴮ                     Xᴮ, Y

              Xᴮ          Y
   Xᴼ        XᴼXᴮ        XᴼY
   Xᴮ        XᴮXᴮ        XᴮY

Offspring: tortoiseshell female, black female, orange male, black male
Ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1
```

Notice that **males cannot be tortoiseshell**, because they have only one X chromosome and so only one allele for this gene.

## Test crosses

A **test cross** crosses an organism showing the dominant phenotype with a **homozygous recessive** individual. The recessive parent gives only recessive alleles, so the offspring phenotypes reveal the alleles in the gametes of the tested parent.

- All offspring dominant → tested parent was **homozygous dominant**.
- Half dominant, half recessive → tested parent was **heterozygous**.
- For a dihybrid test cross (AaBb × aabb), unlinked genes give **1 : 1 : 1 : 1**.

## Worked example 2: is this linkage? Use chi-squared

A dihybrid test cross AaBb × aabb gave 160 offspring: AaBb 68, Aabb 12, aaBb 14, aabb 66. Test whether the results fit the 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ratio expected for unlinked genes.

```
expected (E) = 160 ÷ 4 = 40 in each class
(O − E)² / E = 28²/40 + 28²/40 + 26²/40 + 26²/40
             = 19.6 + 19.6 + 16.9 + 16.9 = 73.0
degrees of freedom = 4 − 1 = 3
critical value at p = 0.05 = 7.82
```

73.0 is far greater than 7.82, so the difference is **significant** (p < 0.05): the results do not fit 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. The large classes, AaBb and aabb, are the **parental combinations**, so A and B are **linked** on the same chromosome (AB on one homologue, ab on the other). The small classes (12 + 14 = 26, or 16.25 %) are **recombinants** from crossing over between the two loci in prophase I.

### Writing the chi-squared conclusion

A full conclusion has four parts: compare the calculated value with the critical value, state whether the difference is significant, state the probability (p greater or less than 0.05), and say what this means for the hypothesis. In a 3 : 1 monohybrid check with 290 and 110 offspring: E = 300 and 100; χ² = 100/300 + 100/100 = 1.33; with 1 degree of freedom the critical value is 3.84; 1.33 < 3.84, so the difference is not significant and the results fit 3 : 1.

## Worked example 3: epistasis from first principles

You do not need to remember epistasis ratios, but you must be able to work them out. In a flower, a white precursor is converted to a white intermediate by enzyme A (dominant allele A), and the intermediate to purple pigment by enzyme B (dominant allele B). The genes are on different chromosomes.

- Purple needs **both** enzymes: genotype A_B_.
- aa plants cannot make the intermediate, so they are white whatever their B alleles.
- A_bb plants make the intermediate but not the pigment: white.

From AaBb × AaBb, the 16 boxes of the Punnett square give 9 A_B_ (purple), 3 A_bb (white), 3 aaB_ (white) and 1 aabb (white): **9 purple : 7 white**. Gene A is epistatic to gene B because aa hides the effect of the B alleles.

## From gene to phenotype (outcome 16.2.6)

Every one of the four named examples follows the same chain: **gene → protein → function → phenotype**. Write all four links.

| Gene | Protein and normal role | Change in the allele | Phenotype |
|---|---|---|---|
| TYR | Tyrosinase, an enzyme in the pathway that makes melanin from tyrosine | Recessive allele gives non-functional tyrosinase | Albinism: little or no melanin in skin, hair and eyes |
| HBB | β-globin, a polypeptide of haemoglobin | Base substitution: one amino acid changed (glutamic acid to valine) | Sickle cell anaemia in HbˢHbˢ |
| F8 | Factor VIII, a blood-clotting protein | Recessive allele on the X chromosome gives non-functional factor VIII | Haemophilia: blood clots slowly |
| HTT | Huntingtin | Dominant allele with too many repeats of the triplet CAG | Huntington's disease: progressive damage to brain neurones |

**Annotated model answer (HBB).** *Explain how a change in the HBB gene leads to sickle cell anaemia.*

1. A **base substitution** in the HBB gene changes one triplet, so one codon in the mRNA changes. *(Gene-level change.)*
2. In the β-globin polypeptide, **glutamic acid is replaced by valine**. *(Protein-level change, named.)*
3. Valine is non-polar, so haemoglobin S molecules **stick together and form long fibres** when oxygen concentration is low. *(Effect on protein behaviour.)*
4. Red blood cells become **sickle-shaped**, carry less oxygen and can **block capillaries**. *(Phenotype.)*

## Gibberellin and the Le/le alleles (outcome 16.2.7)

Gibberellin stimulates **stem elongation**. The dominant allele **Le** codes for a **functional enzyme** in the gibberellin synthesis pathway, which produces active gibberellin. The recessive allele **le** codes for a **non-functional** enzyme. So:

- **LeLe** and **Lele**: active gibberellin made → long internodes → **tall** plants. Le is dominant because one functional allele makes enough enzyme.
- **lele**: little active gibberellin → short internodes → **dwarf** plants.

A cross Lele × Lele gives 3 tall : 1 dwarf, an ordinary monohybrid ratio, but now you can explain the **mechanism** behind the dominance.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving out the key, or using letters that look alike in handwriting (S and s, C and c).
- Writing gametes as pairs of alleles from a monohybrid parent; each gamete has **one** allele per gene.
- Giving sex-linked genotypes without the X and Y.
- Forgetting that a dihybrid gamete carries one allele of **each** gene (AB, Ab, aB, ab).
- Calculating degrees of freedom as the number of offspring minus one. It is the **number of classes minus one**.
- Stating "the results are correct" after a chi-squared test. Say whether the difference is **significant**.
- In gene-to-phenotype answers, jumping from "mutation" to "disease" without the protein.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** terms (16.2.1): one precise sentence each; for "locus", say "the position of a gene on a chromosome".
- **Construct** a genetic diagram: follow the six lines, and label each.
- **Interpret** a cross: name the type of inheritance and quote the ratio or classes that show it.
- **Use** chi-squared: show the table of O, E and (O − E)²/E, the degrees of freedom and the critical value.
