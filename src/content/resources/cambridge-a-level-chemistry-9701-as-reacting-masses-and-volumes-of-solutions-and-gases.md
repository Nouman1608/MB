## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to perform calculations using the mole concept involving:

- (a) reacting masses, from formulas and equations, including percentage yield;
- (b) volumes of gases, for example in the burning of hydrocarbons;
- (c) volumes and concentrations of solutions;
- (d) limiting reagent and excess reagent;
- (e) deducing stoichiometric relationships from such calculations.

The syllabus adds that answers should reflect the number of significant figures given or asked for, without losing figures unnecessarily or using more than the data justify.

## The one route through every calculation

1. Convert what you know into **moles**.
2. Use the **mole ratio** from the balanced equation.
3. Convert moles of the required substance into what is asked: mass, gas volume or concentration.

| Convert from | Into moles | Back from moles |
|---|---|---|
| Mass (g) | n = m ÷ M | m = n × M |
| Gas volume at room conditions (dm³) | n = V ÷ 24.0 | V = n × 24.0 |
| Solution (mol dm⁻³, dm³) | n = c × V | c = n ÷ V |

Remember 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. Divide a volume in cm³ by 1000 before using it with mol dm⁻³ or 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

## (a) Reacting masses and percentage yield

**Worked example.** 25.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). The mass of calcium oxide obtained is 12.6 g. Calculate the percentage yield (CaCO₃ 100.1, CaO 56.1).

1. n(CaCO₃) = 25.0 ÷ 100.1 = 0.2498 mol.
2. Ratio 1 : 1, so n(CaO) expected = 0.2498 mol.
3. Theoretical mass = 0.2498 × 56.1 = 14.01 g.
4. Percentage yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100 = 12.6 ÷ 14.01 × 100 = **89.9 %**.

Yields fall below 100 % because of incomplete reaction, side reactions or loss of product during separation and purification.

## (b) Volumes of gases

At room conditions, one mole of any gas occupies 24.0 dm³. Two consequences follow:

- **Mass to volume:** 2.40 g of magnesium with excess acid, Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. n(Mg) = 2.40 ÷ 24.3 = 0.0988 mol = n(H₂); V = 0.0988 × 24.0 = **2.37 dm³**.
- **Volume to volume:** for gases at the same temperature and pressure, the volume ratio equals the mole ratio in the equation, so no conversion to moles is needed.

### Burning hydrocarbons

C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l)

20 cm³ of propane needs 5 × 20 = 100 cm³ of oxygen and forms 3 × 20 = 60 cm³ of CO₂. At room temperature the water is liquid, so its volume is negligible.

### Worked example: deducing a hydrocarbon formula from gas volumes

10 cm³ of a gaseous hydrocarbon CₓHᵧ is burned in 100 cm³ of oxygen (an excess). After cooling to room temperature the gas volume is 75 cm³. Shaking with sodium hydroxide solution, which absorbs CO₂, reduces it to 35 cm³.

1. Volume of CO₂ = 75 − 35 = 40 cm³. 10 cm³ of hydrocarbon gives 40 cm³ CO₂, so **x = 4**.
2. The 35 cm³ left is unused oxygen, so oxygen used = 100 − 35 = 65 cm³, which is 6.5 volumes per volume of hydrocarbon.
3. CₓHᵧ + (x + y/4)O₂ → xCO₂ + (y/2)H₂O. So x + y/4 = 6.5; with x = 4, y/4 = 2.5 and **y = 10**.
4. The hydrocarbon is **C₄H₁₀**. Check: before 110 cm³; after 40 + 35 = 75 cm³, as observed.

## (c) Volumes and concentrations of solutions

Concentration in mol dm⁻³ = amount (mol) ÷ volume (dm³). Mass concentration in g dm⁻³ = concentration in mol dm⁻³ × molar mass.

**Worked example: titration.** 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is neutralised by 21.40 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid. Find the concentration of the sodium hydroxide.

2NaOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

1. n(H₂SO₄) = 0.100 × 21.40 ÷ 1000 = 2.140 × 10⁻³ mol.
2. Ratio NaOH : H₂SO₄ = 2 : 1, so n(NaOH) = 4.280 × 10⁻³ mol.
3. c(NaOH) = 4.280 × 10⁻³ ÷ (25.0 ÷ 1000) = **0.171 mol dm⁻³**.

The acid concentration is given to three significant figures, so three (or four, 0.1712) is justified. The mass concentration is 0.171 × 40.0 = 6.85 g dm⁻³.

## (d) Limiting and excess reagent

The **limiting reagent** is completely used up and decides how much product forms. The other reactant is **in excess**: some is left over.

**Worked example.** 3.00 g of aluminium is heated with 10.0 g of chlorine: 2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃ (Al 27.0, Cl₂ 71.0, AlCl₃ 133.5).

1. n(Al) = 3.00 ÷ 27.0 = 0.111 mol; n(Cl₂) = 10.0 ÷ 71.0 = 0.141 mol.
2. To react with all the Al needs 0.111 × 3/2 = 0.167 mol Cl₂. Only 0.141 mol is present, so **chlorine is limiting**.
3. n(AlCl₃) = 0.141 × 2/3 = 0.0939 mol; mass = 0.0939 × 133.5 = **12.5 g**.
4. Al used = 0.0939 mol, so Al left = 0.111 − 0.0939 = 0.0172 mol = **0.465 g** in excess.

Never decide the limiting reagent by comparing masses. Compare moles, scaled by the equation.

## (e) Deducing stoichiometry from data

When the equation is unknown, measure amounts of reactants and products, convert to moles, and find the simplest whole-number ratio.

**Worked example.** 0.0500 mol of a metal X reacts completely with excess hydrochloric acid to give 1.80 dm³ of hydrogen at room conditions.

1. n(H₂) = 1.80 ÷ 24.0 = 0.0750 mol.
2. Ratio X : H₂ = 0.0500 : 0.0750 = 2 : 3.
3. 2 mol X release 6 mol H atoms, so each X atom loses 3 electrons and forms X³⁺.
4. Equation: **2X(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2XCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g)**.

## Significant figures

- Give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data, or one more.
- Titre volumes are often given to four figures (21.40 cm³); a concentration given to three figures limits you to three or four.
- Keep full values on your calculator; round only at the end.
- Never quote eight figures from a calculator display.

## Common mistakes

- Using cm³ directly with mol dm⁻³ or 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.
- Forgetting the mole ratio, especially 2 : 1 in titrations with sulfuric acid.
- Choosing the limiting reagent by smaller mass.
- Including liquid water in gas volumes at room temperature.
- Calculating yield the wrong way up (theoretical ÷ actual).
- Rounding intermediate steps, which changes the last figure of the answer.

## Exam technique

- Write the balanced equation first, even if one is given in words.
- Label every amount with its substance: n(NaOH), n(H₂).
- Show each conversion on its own line, with units.
- State the limiting reagent in words before calculating the product.
