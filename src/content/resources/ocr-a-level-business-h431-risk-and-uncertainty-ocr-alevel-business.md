## Risk, reward and spread

**Risk** is the chance that a decision turns out worse than hoped. **Reward** is the gain if it succeeds. Higher potential reward usually comes with higher risk, because the activities that could earn most are the least familiar or the most exposed to competition.

Expected value tells you the average outcome, but two options with the same average can carry very different risk. The **spread** of outcomes, from best to worst, is often more important to the decision.

### Worked example 1: same average, different risk

Ashcombe Print has £100,000 to invest and two options. Figures are net gains over three years.

| Option | Outcomes and probabilities | Expected value | Worst case |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: new digital press for existing customers | £70,000 (0.5), £50,000 (0.5) | £60,000 | +£50,000 |
| B: packaging printing for a new market | £200,000 (0.4), −£10,000 (0.4), −£20,000 (0.2) | £72,000 | −£20,000 |

Working for B: (0.4 × 200,000) + (0.4 × −10,000) + (0.2 × −20,000) = 80,000 − 4,000 − 4,000 = **£72,000**.

B has the higher expected value, by £12,000, but there is a 60% chance it makes a loss. A cannot make a loss on these figures.

**Evaluating the relationship.** Whether B's extra reward justifies its extra risk depends on:

- **Ability to survive the worst case.** If Ashcombe has large reserves, a £20,000 loss is bearable. If it is short of cash, it is not.
- **Objectives.** A business aiming for growth may accept B; one aiming for stable profit will prefer A.
- **Owners' attitude to risk.** Owners with other income may accept more risk than a family whose living depends on the business.
- **Reliability of the probabilities.** A new market makes B's estimates less reliable than A's.

A judgement: A is the better choice for a business that cannot afford a loss, because its worst case is still a gain of £50,000; B suits a business with reserves and a growth objective, provided the 0.4 chance of success is based on research rather than hope.

## Quantifiable or not

A **quantifiable risk** can be measured from data: the business can estimate how likely it is and how much it would cost, so it can be insured, provided for or priced in. An **unquantifiable risk** has no reliable basis for a number, so it can only be managed by flexibility, reserves and planning for several scenarios.

The line is not fixed. A risk that is unquantifiable for a new business may become quantifiable once it has a few years of records. When evaluating, ask where the numbers came from and how much the business would lose if they were wrong.

## The risk matrix and responses

A risk matrix, a common tool that the specification does not name, scores each risk for **likelihood** and **impact**, often from 1 (low) to 5 (high), and multiplies them. The highest scores get attention first.

**Example: Marrick Kitchens**, a kitchen-fitting business with 25 staff.

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Score | Quantifiable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main supplier of worktops fails | 2 | 5 | 10 | Partly |
| Customer fails to pay a large bill | 3 | 4 | 12 | Yes, from past bad debts |
| Fitter injured on site | 2 | 4 | 8 | Yes, insurable |
| A national rival opens nearby | 3 | 3 | 9 | No |
| Interest rate rise reduces home improvements | 4 | 3 | 12 | No |

There are four broad responses, and each should be evaluated for cost and effectiveness:

- **Reduce:** take deposits from customers to cut the bad-debt risk; train fitters to cut injuries.
- **Transfer:** insure against injury claims; insurance does not cover the loss of trade from a rival.
- **Avoid:** stop the activity, for example refuse very large jobs from new customers without a credit check.
- **Accept:** where the cost of action exceeds the likely loss, budget for the risk.

For Marrick, the two highest scores are bad debts and falling demand from interest rates. Bad debts can be reduced cheaply with deposits and staged payments; falling demand cannot be prevented, so the response is to keep a cash reserve and flexible staffing. The matrix is only as good as the scores, which are judgements, and it treats a rare disaster and a frequent small loss with the same score as equals.

## Consequences of poor risk management

| Stakeholder | Possible consequence |
|---|---|
| Owners | Lost profit, lost investment, possible closure |
| Employees | Short-time working, redundancy, stress |
| Lenders | Late or missed repayments |
| Suppliers | Unpaid invoices, loss of a customer |
| Customers | Unfinished work, no after-sales service |
| Community | Lost jobs and local spending |

The severity depends on the size of the loss relative to the business's resources and on how quickly it happens.

## Worked example 2: the entrepreneur's risks

Sana leaves a job paying £38,000 a year to open Sana's Bakehouse as a **sole trader**. She invests £25,000 of savings and borrows £15,000. In her first year the business makes a profit of £22,000 before she draws any income.

- **Income risk.** Her £22,000 profit is £16,000 below the salary she gave up. If the business had made a loss, she would have earned nothing.
- **Capital risk.** Her £25,000 of savings is at stake.
- **Liability risk.** As a sole trader she has unlimited liability, so if the business could not repay the £15,000 loan, her personal assets could be used to pay it.
- **Personal risks.** Long hours, stress and the loss of employment benefits such as a pension and sick pay.

**Evaluation.** The risks are high in year 1, but they are set against rewards: independence, all profits if the business grows, and an asset she could later sell. The risk would be lower if she traded as a private limited company, although lenders often ask small company owners for personal guarantees. The judgement depends on her savings beyond the £25,000, her experience in baking and the evidence of demand.

## Uncertainty

**Uncertainty** exists when a business cannot know what will happen or cannot give the possible outcomes reliable probabilities. **Internal causes** arise within the business, such as whether new staff perform, whether a new machine is reliable or how a reorganisation will work. **External causes** arise outside, such as exchange rates, interest rates, competitors, regulation, technology and consumer tastes.

### Worked example 3: an external cause and its effects

Calloway Wines imports 40,000 bottles a year at a price of €6.00 each and sells them at £9.50. Fixed costs are £60,000. Profit depends on the exchange rate, which Calloway cannot control.

| Exchange rate | Cost per bottle | Contribution per bottle | Annual profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| £1 = €1.10 | £5.45 | £4.05 | £101,818 |
| £1 = €1.20 | £5.00 | £4.50 | £120,000 |
| £1 = €1.30 | £4.62 | £4.88 | £135,385 |

Profits in the table are calculated from unrounded costs. A fall in the pound from €1.20 to €1.10 cuts profit by £18,182, about 15%.

How this uncertainty affects the five named areas:

- **Aims and objectives:** a single profit target is unrealistic; Calloway might set a range, such as £100,000 to £135,000.
- **Planning:** budgets are prepared for each scenario, and spending plans are linked to the lower one.
- **Decision making:** Calloway may agree a fixed exchange rate with its bank in advance, or buy more from UK suppliers to reduce exposure.
- **Business analysis:** a PESTLE or SWOT analysis will flag exchange rates as a threat, but the size of the threat changes month by month.
- **Forecasting:** three scenarios replace a single forecast.

The impact on stakeholders follows: owners face variable returns, staff may face a recruitment freeze in a bad year, and customers may see price rises if the pound weakens.

## Common mistakes

- Judging options on expected value alone, without the worst case.
- Treating a risk matrix score as an objective measurement.
- Saying an entrepreneur "risks everything" without stating which risks, and whether liability is limited.
- Listing the five effects of uncertainty without linking each to the case.

## Exam technique

"Distinguish" needs both terms and the difference between them. "Evaluate" needs both sides, a judgement and what it depends on. Use figures to show the size of a risk: a loss as a share of reserves or a fall in profit as a percentage.
