## Definitions to learn

- **Amine**: a compound based on ammonia in which one or more H atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups.
- **Primary / secondary / tertiary amine**: one / two / three carbons bonded to N (RNH₂, R₂NH, R₃N).
- **Quaternary ammonium ion**: four carbons bonded to N, R₄N⁺. Its salts are R₄N⁺X⁻.
- **Aliphatic amine**: N bonded to an alkyl group. **Aromatic amine**: N bonded directly to a benzene ring
  (phenylamine, C₆H₅NH₂).
- **Base** (Brønsted–Lowry): proton acceptor. **Weak base**: only partly reacts with water.
- **Nucleophile**: electron pair donor.
- **N-substituted amide**: an amide with an alkyl group on N, e.g. CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃, N-ethylethanamide.
- **Cationic surfactant**: a surfactant whose ionic head group is positive, e.g. a quaternary ammonium salt
  with one long hydrocarbon chain.

Classify amines by counting carbons **on N**. (CH₃)₃CNH₂ is a primary amine even though the carbon next to
N is tertiary.

## 3.3.11.1 Preparation summary

| Product | Starting material | Reagent and conditions | Equation (example) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary aliphatic amine | Halogenoalkane | Excess NH₃ in ethanol, heat in a sealed container | C₃H₇Cl + 2NH₃ → C₃H₇NH₂ + NH₄Cl | Mixture of products by further substitution |
| Primary aliphatic amine | Nitrile | Reduction, e.g. LiAlH₄ in dry ether, or H₂ with Ni catalyst | C₂H₅CN + 4[H] → C₂H₅CH₂NH₂ | Primary amine only; no mixture |
| Aromatic amine | Nitro compound | Reduction, e.g. Sn and conc. HCl under reflux, then NaOH | C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O | Used in the manufacture of dyes |

The specification names the reaction types (reaction with ammonia; reduction) but not specific reducing
agents. The reagents in the table are common choices.

**[H] counting.** Nitrile: C≡N → CH₂–NH₂ adds four H, so 4[H] (or 2H₂). Nitro: –NO₂ → –NH₂ removes two O as
2H₂O and adds two H to N, so 6[H].

**Chain length.** Halogenoalkane + NH₃ keeps the carbon count. Halogenoalkane + KCN, then reduction, adds
one carbon: 1-bromopropane → butanenitrile → butylamine.

## 3.3.11.2 Base properties

**Reactions as a base:**

```
RNH2 + H2O ⇌ RNH3+ + OH−        (weak base, solution alkaline)
RNH2 + HCl → RNH3+Cl−            (salt, e.g. propylammonium chloride)
RNH3+ + OH− → RNH2 + H2O         (NaOH releases the amine from its salt)
```

**Base strength order (learn it):**

| Strongest | Middle | Weakest |
|---|---|---|
| Primary aliphatic amine (e.g. CH₃NH₂) | Ammonia, NH₃ | Primary aromatic amine (C₆H₅NH₂) |

**Method in steps: explaining a base-strength order**

1. Say that the base accepts H⁺ using the **lone pair on N**.
2. For an aliphatic amine: alkyl group is **electron-releasing** (positive inductive effect), so electron
   density on N **increases**.
3. For an aromatic amine: the N lone pair is **delocalised into the benzene ring**, so electron density on N
   **decreases**.
4. Conclude: more available lone pair → accepts H⁺ more readily → stronger base.

Ammonia sits in the middle because it has neither effect.

## 3.3.11.3 Nucleophilic properties

### Substitution ladder with halogenoalkanes

```
NH3   →  RNH2  →  R2NH  →  R3N  →  R4N+X−
        primary  secondary tertiary quaternary salt
```

Each step: amine + RX → next amine (+ HX, taken up by more base as a salt).

- Excess **NH₃** → mainly primary amine.
- Excess **halogenoalkane** → mainly quaternary ammonium salt.
- The ladder stops at R₄N⁺: no lone pair and no H on N.

**Method in steps: substitution mechanism (amine + halogenoalkane)**

1. Curly arrow: lone pair on N → δ+ carbon of C–X.
2. Curly arrow: C–X bond → X. X⁻ leaves.
3. Intermediate: N⁺ with an extra alkyl group and at least one H.
4. Second amine (or NH₃) removes H⁺ from N⁺: arrow from its lone pair to H, arrow from N–H bond to N⁺.

**Cationic surfactants.** A quaternary ammonium salt with one long chain, e.g. CH₃(CH₂)₁₃N⁺(CH₃)₃ Cl⁻. The
N⁺ head is attracted to water and to negatively charged surfaces; the long chain is not attracted to water.

### Addition–elimination with acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides

| Nucleophile | Acyl chloride, RCOCl | Acid anhydride, (RCO)₂O |
|---|---|---|
| NH₃ | RCONH₂ (primary amide) + NH₄Cl | RCONH₂ + RCOOH |
| R′NH₂ | RCONHR′ (N-substituted amide) + R′NH₃⁺Cl⁻ | RCONHR′ + RCOOH |
| Speed | Fast at room temperature | Slower |

Example: CH₃CH₂CH₂COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CONH₂ (butanamide) + NH₄Cl.

**Method in steps: addition–elimination mechanism (acyl chloride)**

1. **Addition.** Arrow from lone pair on N → δ+ carbonyl carbon. Arrow from C=O π bond → O, giving O⁻.
2. Tetrahedral intermediate: C bonded to O⁻, Cl, R and N⁺.
3. **Elimination.** Arrow from O⁻ lone pair → reforms C=O. Arrow from C–Cl bond → Cl, Cl⁻ leaves.
4. Cl⁻ or a second NH₃/amine removes H⁺ from N⁺.

You must be able to outline this mechanism for **acyl chlorides**. For acid anhydrides the specification
asks only for the reactions.

## Small worked reminders

**Naming an N-substituted amide.** Ethanoyl chloride + propylamine. The acyl part (CH₃CO–) names the amide,
ethanamide; the amine's alkyl group goes on N. Product: CH₃CONHCH₂CH₂CH₃, **N-propylethanamide**.

**Choosing a route.** To make CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ cleanly from a three-carbon halogenoalkane: 1-bromopropane +
KCN (ethanol/water, reflux) → butanenitrile; then reduce → butylamine. Using NH₃ directly would give
propylamine, with one carbon too few, and a mixture.

**Salt formula.** Phenylamine + HCl → C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻, phenylammonium chloride. The positive charge is on N.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Amine vs amide.** Amine: N bonded to C and H only. Amide: N bonded to a C=O carbon. Amides are made
  from amines (or NH₃) plus acyl chlorides or anhydrides.
- **Primary amine vs primary amide.** CH₃CH₂NH₂ vs CH₃CONH₂.
- **Substitution vs addition–elimination.** Halogenoalkane: one-step replacement of X. Acyl chloride:
  adds across C=O, then eliminates Cl⁻.
- **Ammonium salt vs quaternary ammonium salt.** RNH₃⁺Cl⁻ gives back the amine with NaOH; R₄N⁺Cl⁻ cannot,
  because N has no H to lose.
- **By-product.** Acyl chloride gives HCl (as a salt with excess base); acid anhydride gives a carboxylic
  acid.

## Quick self-test

1. Classify (CH₃CH₂)₂NCH₃.
2. Give the product of reducing butanenitrile.
3. How many [H] are needed to reduce one nitrobenzene molecule to phenylamine?
4. Write the equation for methylamine with water.
5. Put in order of increasing base strength: phenylamine, propylamine, ammonia.
6. Why is phenylamine a weaker base than ammonia?
7. What conditions favour a quaternary ammonium salt?
8. Calculate the Mr of tetramethylammonium chloride (Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, Cl = 35.5).
9. Name the organic product of butanoyl chloride with excess methylamine.
10. Give the by-product when ethanoic anhydride reacts with ammonia (1 : 1).
11. Why can a quaternary ammonium ion not act as a nucleophile?

### Answers

1. **Tertiary** amine: three carbons on N.
2. **Butylamine**, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ (the nitrile carbon becomes the CH₂ on N, so still four carbons).
3. **6[H]**, giving 2H₂O.
4. **CH₃NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻**
5. **Phenylamine < ammonia < propylamine.**
6. The N lone pair is **delocalised into the benzene ring**, so it is less available to accept H⁺.
7. **Excess halogenoalkane.**
8. (CH₃)₄N⁺Cl⁻ = 4(12.0 + 3.0) + 14.0 + 35.5 = **109.5**.
9. **N-methylbutanamide**, CH₃CH₂CH₂CONHCH₃.
10. **Ethanoic acid**, CH₃COOH (as ammonium ethanoate if ammonia is in excess).
11. Its N has **no lone pair**: all four N electron pairs are used in bonds.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling (CH₃)₃CNH₂ a tertiary amine because the carbon is tertiary.
- Unbalanced [H] in reductions: 2[H] for a nitrile or 4[H] for a nitro compound.
- Forgetting that nitrile reduction keeps the C≡N carbon in the chain.
- Base-strength answers that never mention the lone pair or proton acceptance.
- Saying the alkyl group "donates a lone pair" instead of "releases electron density".
- Missing the deprotonation step at the end of either mechanism.
- Arrows starting from the N atom or the minus sign instead of from the lone pair.
- In addition–elimination, leaving out the arrow from C=O to O in the first step.
- Writing the amide product with an extra H on N (RCONH₂R′).
- Giving HCl as a free by-product when excess amine or ammonia is used.
