This guide covers one route through Unit 4 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level English Literature (9675) specification, Version 5.3: **4a Examination**. The other route, a non-exam assessment, is covered in the [Unit 4b guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-4b-non-exam-assessment/). Unit 4 is assessed at International A-level only, and a student takes one route or the other.

In 4a you meet texts you have not studied. You are not given a blank page, however: each question guides you to a particular aspect of representation. Condense this guide with the [Unit 4a revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-4a-examination-revision-notes/) and try the [practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-4a-examination-practice/).

## What the specification says about 4a

| Feature | What the specification states |
|---|---|
| Format | Written exam of 2 hours 30 minutes; 50 marks; 30% of the International A-level |
| Questions | Two separate questions on unseen texts: one on prose and one on poetry. Both are compulsory |
| Sections | Section A: Prose (25 marks), which can include literary non-fiction. Section B: Poetry (25 marks) |
| Texts | More than one unseen text may be offered in each question |
| Focus | In each question students are directed to the representation of an aspect of one of four areas. The two questions refer to different topic areas |
| Support | The question is framed to guide students towards specific elements of the unseen texts, rather than a completely unaided exercise in practical criticism |

The four broad areas named in the specification are:

- representations of childhood;
- representations of war;
- representations of women and men;
- representations of race, class and culture.

The specification stresses that these areas are **not topics prescribed for detailed study**. Students are **not expected to draw on extensive contextual knowledge** of the topic, nor to refer to wider reading. They are areas that teachers and students explore through the analysis of their own selected texts, so as to practise the skills of literary analysis and interpretation.

The specification's own example of a poetry question asks for an analysis of a poem in which you explore the significance of the poet's representation of social class and culture. Notice the shape of the task: one text, one aspect of one area, and the word "significance".

## What is representation?

The specification uses **representation** to emphasise the process of showing a view of the world, rather than the actual world itself. This opens texts to varieties of interpretation. For the exam, it means:

- you do not report what a poem or passage is about. You analyse how it **shows** childhood, war, gender or class, through the writer's choices;
- you consider that other readers might see the representation differently, which is the basis for interpretation;
- you weigh up the significance of what you find. The specification explains that significance involves the way the text is constructed and written, relevant contexts, and potential meanings and interpretations.

## The assessment objectives

The three assessment objectives, with the specification's wording, are:

- **AO1:** demonstrate understanding of the ways in which readers find meanings in texts, showing an understanding of genre, the significance of contexts, and the ways in which different interpretations can be found.
- **AO2:** analyse ways in which meanings are shaped in literary texts with close attention to authorial methods.
- **AO3:** express informed, personal and argued responses to literary texts, using appropriate concepts and terminology, and coherent, accurate writing.

All three are weighted equally in all questions, so about a third of the marks are for each. For 4a, that means close analysis of methods (AO2) is only one third of the task. You also need to show how genre and context shape meaning, and that other readings are possible (AO1), and write a clear, argued response (AO3).

## A method for the unseen text

The following steps are Marlbridge suggestions, not requirements.

1. **Read the question first.** Underline the area (for example childhood), the aspect (for example how a child is shown in relation to adults) and the word that tells you what to do (explore, analyse).
2. **Read the text twice.** The first reading is for sense. The second is for methods.
3. **Find a shape.** Where does the text begin, turn and end? Many representations develop or change.
4. **Choose three or four lines of argument** that answer the question. Do not work through the text line by line without a purpose.
5. **For each argument,** pick a short quotation, name a method, explain the effect on the representation and say how a reader might interpret it.
6. **Write a short introduction** that answers the question in one or two sentences.

### Methods to look for

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Voice and viewpoint | First or third person, who is speaking, whose view is missing |
| Language | Word choice, imagery, tone, repetition, contrast |
| Structure and form | Opening and ending, sentence length, stanza and line length, rhyme, enjambment |
| Setting and symbol | Objects, places and times that carry meaning |

## Worked example 1: an unseen poem

This poem is original, written for this guide.

> **Hand-me-downs**
>
> The coat was Ben's before it was mine,
> sleeves turned back twice, the cuffs a stitched-up line.
> It smelled of someone else's winter air,
> of chalk and rain and corridors elsewhere.
> I grew to fit it slowly, as you do
> into a promise others made for you.
> Mother pressed the collar flat and said,
> "It's good wool. It will do." And so it did.
> I wore the winter out, and in the spring
> I found his initials stitched inside the lining.

**Task:** Write an analysis of this poem in which you explore the significance of the representation of childhood.

**Reading the task.** The area is childhood. The aspect is the significance of the representation, so the answer must weigh meanings, not only list techniques.

**Possible lines of argument:**

1. **Childhood as inheriting.** The child receives a coat that was already used by "Ben". The title and the first line show that the child's experience is second-hand. The phrase "someone else's winter air" extends this to the whole atmosphere of childhood: the child lives in conditions made by others.
2. **Growth as adaptation.** "I grew to fit it slowly, as you do / into a promise others made for you" is a simile that treats growing up as living up to adult expectations. The pronoun "you" generalises the experience. The verb "grew" works literally, as the child's body grows into the coat, and figuratively.
3. **The adult voice.** The mother's words, "It's good wool. It will do", are plain and practical. The rhythm is flat, and the repeated "do" in "And so it did" closes the idea. A reader might see love in the practical care, or a limit on the child's choices. Both readings are available.
4. **Structure and ending.** The poem is in rhyming couplets, which gives a tidy, contained sound that mirrors the neatness of the coat. The rhyme breaks in the last couplet ("spring" and "lining" do not rhyme fully), so the discovery of the initials arrives with a small disturbance. The final image can be read as the child recognising the other child's identity, or as a quiet sense of being a stand-in.

**A model paragraph:**

> The poem represents childhood as a time of inheriting rather than choosing. The opening, "The coat was Ben's before it was mine", places the speaker second in line, and the stitched cuffs, "a stitched-up line", show that the garment has been altered to fit. The simile "as you do / into a promise others made for you" generalises this to the whole of growing up, so the child's body and the child's future are both shaped by the choices of adults. This could be read as a gentle picture of care, since the coat is "good wool", or as a more critical picture of a childhood defined by what others have decided. The poem allows both, and the discovery of the initials in the last line, which breaks the rhyme, leaves the reader with a sense that the child is both comforted and slightly displaced.

The paragraph does five things: it makes a claim, uses short quotations, names methods (simile, address), explains the effect on the representation, and offers an interpretation that allows alternatives.

## Worked example 2: an unseen prose passage

This extract is original, written for this guide.

> The telegram boy cycled past our door twice that week, and each time Mrs Harlow at number nine pulled her curtain closed before he reached the gate. We children kept count from the wall. Nobody told us to. When he turned up our road, the street went quiet in a way I have never heard since: shutters, cups set down, a dog silenced by the hush rather than a word. On the Thursday he stopped at ours. My mother wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, finger by finger, as if the cloth might be asked to give evidence. Then she opened the door.

**Task:** Explore the significance of the representation of war in this passage.

**Observations.**

- War is never named. It is represented through its effects on a street: the telegram boy, the curtain, the silence. The absence of battle scenes is itself a choice, and makes war an event that arrives at home.
- The child narrator ("We children kept count") shows that children absorb the pattern of loss without being told. The sentence "Nobody told us to" implies that they understood without explanation.
- The sensory detail of sound (the hush, the silent dog) turns the street into a place that holds its breath.
- The simile "as if the cloth might be asked to give evidence" gives the mother's delay a legal or fearful tone. She postpones the news by cleaning her hands.
- The final short sentence "Then she opened the door" ends before the news is delivered. The reader has to imagine the result, which makes the representation more powerful and more open to interpretation.

**Connecting the two texts.** In a question with a poem and a prose extract on different areas, you answer each separately. If a question offers more than one text on the same area, you may compare, but only if the question asks for it.

## Time planning

The paper is 2 hours 30 minutes for two compulsory 25-mark questions. A possible plan, which is a suggestion only: 10 minutes to read both questions and texts and choose arguments, about 65 minutes for each answer, and 10 minutes to check. That is 150 minutes.

## Common mistakes

- Summarising what happens in the text.
- Naming techniques without saying what they do to the representation.
- Bringing in prepared contextual information that the text does not support. The specification says you are not expected to draw on extensive contextual knowledge.
- Writing the same kind of answer for both questions, and ignoring the different topic area.
- Treating the first reading of a text as the only reading and not allowing for alternatives.
- Working through a poem line by line and running out of time.
- Spending far longer on one question than the other when both are worth 25 marks.

## Exam technique

- **Use the words of the question** in your opening sentence.
- **Quote briefly** and analyse single words as well as phrases.
- **Use "might", "could" and "suggests"** to show that you are weighing interpretations.
- **End each paragraph** by returning to the representation in the question.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know about the historical period of an unseen text?**
No. The specification says you are not expected to draw on extensive contextual knowledge of the topic. Context from the text itself, such as the date of publication where it is given, can be used.

**Can the prose be non-fiction?**
Yes. The specification says the prose can include literary non-fiction.

**Will there be more than one text in a question?**
There may be. More than one unseen text may be offered in each question, so read the question carefully to see whether it asks you to consider them separately or together.
