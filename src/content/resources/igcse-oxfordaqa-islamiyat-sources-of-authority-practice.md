## Section A

**1.** Name the four sources of authority in Islam in order, from primary to secondary. **[4]**

**2.** State the three distinct stages in the Qur'an's history as a source of authority. **[3]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain why the Hadith and Sunnah are needed as a source of authority alongside the Qur'an, giving **two** points. **[6]**

**4.** Explain how the reliability of a Hadith is tested.

**(a)** Name and explain what Isnad checks. **[3]**
**(b)** Name and explain what Matn checks. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why a Hadith needs to pass both tests, not just one. **[2]**

**5.** Name the four categories of Hadith reliability and explain what each means. **[8]**

**6.** Explain the meaning and role of Ijma as a source of Shariah, giving an example of the kind of situation in which it would be used. **[5]**

**7.** Explain the meaning and role of Qiyas as a source of Shariah, giving an example of the kind of situation in which it would be used. **[5]**

**8.** A student writes: "The Qur'an was revealed, and that is the whole story of how it became a source of authority." Explain why this statement is incomplete, referring to all three stages in the Qur'an's history. **[6]**

**9.** State one specific fact about the significance of Sahih Bukhari and one specific fact about the significance of Sahih Muslim as collections of Hadith. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **Qur'an** (the primary, directly revealed source) [1], then **Hadith and Sunnah** (recording the Prophet's [pbuh] words and practice, explaining and applying the Qur'an) [1], then **Ijma** (consensus) and **Qiyas** (analogy) together as secondary sources, used only where the Qur'an and Hadith give no explicit ruling [1] [1].

**2.** **Revelation** — how the Qur'an was received [1]. **Preservation** — how it was memorised and recorded [1]. **Compilation** — how it was assembled into a single text [1].

**3.** Any two, 3 marks each: the Qur'an provides **general commands and principles but does not always explain how to put them into practice**, so the Hadith and Sunnah supply the practical detail — for example, the Qur'an instructs Muslims to pray and give charity, but the Hadith records the exact method, timing and conditions the Prophet (pbuh) demonstrated [1] [1] [1]. Without the Hadith, **many Qur'anic commands would remain open to interpretation**, so it functions as the practical explanation of the Qur'an's guidance, which is why it is described as the second source of Shariah, subordinate to but essential alongside the Qur'an [1] [1] [1].

**4. (a)** **Isnad** checks the **chain of narrators** transmitting the Hadith back to the Prophet (pbuh) [1] — it examines whether each narrator in the chain was trustworthy and had a plausible opportunity to hear from the previous narrator [1] [1].
**(b)** **Matn** checks the **actual text or content** of the Hadith itself [1] — it examines whether the content is consistent with the Qur'an, with established Islamic teaching, and with reason [1] [1].
**(c)** A Hadith can have a **strong chain of narrators but still raise questions about its content**, or a plausible-sounding text but an unreliable chain, so both tests are needed together — passing only one does not establish reliability [2].

**5.** **Sahih** (sound) — a Hadith with a complete, reliable chain of trustworthy narrators and content free from contradiction [2]. **Hasan** (good) — reliable but with a chain slightly weaker than Sahih, for example a narrator with a less perfect memory [2]. **Daeef** (weak) — a chain with a significant weakness, such as a missing link or an unreliable narrator, making the Hadith doubtful [2]. **Maudu** (fabricated) — a Hadith invented and falsely attributed to the Prophet (pbuh), rejected as a source of authority [2].

**6.** **Ijma** means the **consensus of qualified scholars** on a ruling [1], used specifically in situations where the **Qur'an and Hadith are silent or ambiguous** and a decision is still needed [2]. Example: scholars reaching agreement on a point of Islamic law not explicitly addressed in the primary texts, such as an administrative or organisational matter facing the early Muslim community [2].

**7.** **Qiyas** means reasoning by **analogy** — applying the ruling of a case already addressed in the Qur'an or Hadith to a new, similar case [1], used where a new situation shares the same underlying reason (illah) as an existing ruling but is not itself named in the primary texts [2]. Example: extending a Qur'anic ruling on one intoxicating substance to a newly available substance that shares the same reason for prohibition [2].

**8.** The statement is incomplete because **revelation is only the first of three stages** [1]. **Preservation** matters because the Qur'an had to be **memorised by companions and recorded in writing** during the Prophet's (pbuh) lifetime and shortly after, to protect it from loss or distortion [1] [1]. **Compilation** matters because these memorised and written portions had to be **gathered and arranged into a single, authoritative text**, a process completed under the early caliphs [1] [1]. Treating revelation as "the whole story" ignores the human effort involved in preserving and compiling the text into the Qur'an Muslims use today [1].

**9.** **Sahih Bukhari** — compiled by Imam Bukhari, who is reported to have applied an exceptionally strict standard of authentication, examining hundreds of thousands of narrations to select only those he judged most reliable [2]. **Sahih Muslim** — compiled by Imam Muslim, regarded alongside Sahih Bukhari as one of the two most authoritative Hadith collections, distinguished by its systematic arrangement of narrations on each topic [2].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Ranking Ijma and Qiyas as equal in authority to the Qur'an and Hadith, rather than as secondary sources used only where the primary sources are silent.
- Confusing Isnad (the chain of narrators) with Matn (the text itself), or explaining only one when a question asks for both.
- Naming the four Hadith categories without being able to explain what distinguishes one from another.
- Describing the Qur'an's history as a single event rather than three distinct stages — revelation, preservation and compilation.
- Naming Sahih Bukhari or Sahih Muslim with no further detail about what makes each collection significant.
