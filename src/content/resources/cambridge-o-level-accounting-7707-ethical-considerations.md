This guide goes deeper into section **7.2 Ethical considerations** of the Cambridge O Level Accounting 7707 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Candidates should understand "the need for an ethical framework in accounting", "the fundamental principles of: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour", and "the significance to stakeholders and society of applying an ethical framework". This section is new for the 2027 to 2029 syllabus.

The shared [accounting concepts, ethics, technology and sustainability guide](/resources/igcse-accounting-accounting-concepts-ethics-technology-and-sustainabilit/) introduces the principles. This page focuses on applying them: recognising which principle a situation tests, measuring the effect of an unethical request on the figures, and explaining who would be harmed. Recall the points with the [ethical considerations revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-ethical-considerations-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [ethical considerations practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-ethical-considerations-practice/). All people, businesses and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## Why accounting needs an ethical framework

Accounting information is used by people who cannot check it themselves: owners, lenders, suppliers, investors and tax authorities. They rely on accountants and book-keepers to record transactions honestly and prepare statements fairly. Three features of the work create risk.

- **Pressure.** Owners and managers may want figures that look better, or worse, than the truth, for example to obtain a loan or pay less tax.
- **Judgement.** Many figures, such as depreciation and allowances, depend on estimates that could be bent.
- **Access to private information.** Accountants see salaries, customer details and plans that others must not see.

An ethical framework sets out in advance the standards an accountant must meet, so the right response does not depend on who is asking or how much pressure is applied.

## The five fundamental principles

| Principle | What it requires | Tested by situations such as |
|---|---|---|
| Integrity | Being honest and straightforward in all work | Recording a sale that never happened; hiding an expense |
| Objectivity | Not allowing bias, conflicts of interest or pressure from others to override judgement | Approving invoices from a relative's business; changing figures because the owner insists |
| Professional competence and due care | Having the knowledge and skill needed, keeping it up to date and working carefully | Preparing a statement without knowing the rules; not checking work |
| Confidentiality | Not disclosing or using information gained through work, unless there is a legal or professional duty to do so | Telling friends a client's profit; using inside knowledge for personal gain |
| Professional behaviour | Obeying laws and regulations and avoiding conduct that discredits the profession | Helping a client evade tax; making false claims about a rival accountant |

**Choosing the principle.** Ask what went wrong at the centre of the situation. A dishonest figure points to integrity. A decision swayed by a relationship or by pressure points to objectivity. A mistake through lack of skill or care points to competence and due care. Information passed on points to confidentiality. Breaking the law or damaging trust in accountants points to professional behaviour. A single situation can breach more than one principle; give a separate reason for each.

## Five quick cases

| Situation | Principle most clearly at risk | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A clerk at a tax adviser's office tells a neighbour how much a local shop owner earns | Confidentiality | Information gained at work is passed on without any duty to disclose it |
| A book-keeper records cash sales of $2,000 as $1,200 at the owner's request | Integrity | The records are knowingly false |
| An accountant reviews a loan application from a company her husband manages | Objectivity | A personal relationship could bias her judgement |
| A trainee prepares payroll using last year's tax rates without checking for changes | Professional competence and due care | The work was not done with up-to-date knowledge or care |
| An accountant advertises that his firm "guarantees" lower tax bills, which is untrue | Professional behaviour | Misleading claims damage trust in the profession |

## Worked example: inventory and a bank condition

Kamran is the book-keeper at Hilltop Furniture. The bank's loan agreement requires a current ratio of at least 1.5 : 1. At the year end:

- current assets total $54,000, including damaged inventory recorded at its cost of $6,000
- the damaged items can be sold for only $1,500
- current liabilities total $36,000.

The owner tells Kamran to leave the damaged inventory at cost "so the bank stays happy".

**Measure the effect.**

| | As the owner wants | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| Damaged inventory | 6,000 | 1,500 |
| Current assets | 54,000 | 49,500 |
| Current ratio | 54,000 / 36,000 = 1.50 : 1 | 49,500 / 36,000 = 1.38 : 1 |
| Profit for the year | Overstated by $4,500 | Correct |

Valuing inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value is required by prudence. Leaving it at cost overstates profit and current assets by $4,500 and hides a breach of the bank's condition.

**Identify the principles.** Agreeing would breach **integrity**, because Kamran would knowingly present misleading figures. It would breach **objectivity**, because his judgement would be overridden by pressure from his employer. It would probably breach **professional behaviour**, because misleading a lender could break the law and damage trust in accountants.

**The ethical response.** Kamran should explain to the owner why the inventory must be written down, record it at $1,500 and prepare correct figures. If pressure continues, he should seek advice, for example from a professional body, rather than produce figures he knows are false.

## Significance to stakeholders and society

Applying the framework protects everyone who relies on the figures. Using the Hilltop example:

| Stakeholder | Effect of honest figures | Effect if the framework is ignored |
|---|---|---|
| Bank | Can act early, perhaps by agreeing new terms | Continues lending on a false picture and may lose money |
| Owner | Faces the problem now while it can be solved | Problem grows; possible legal action later |
| Employees | Jobs protected by early action | Jobs lost if the business suddenly fails |
| Suppliers | Can set credit limits on true information | Give credit that may never be paid |
| Tax authority | Tax charged on the true profit | Tax based on false figures |
| Society | Trust in accounts supports lending, investment and trade | Loss of trust raises the cost of finance for everyone |

The last row matters most in "society" questions: when accounting information can be trusted, lenders and investors are willing to provide money, businesses grow and jobs are created. When it cannot, everyone pays through tighter lending and lost confidence.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a principle without explaining how the situation breaches it.
- Using one explanation for two principles.
- Confusing objectivity (bias or pressure) with integrity (honesty).
- Treating confidentiality as absolute: the law can require disclosure.
- Answering a "society" question only about the business owner.

## Exam technique

Ethics questions may be based on a short case. **Identify** questions need the principle's name. **Explain** questions need the principle, what it requires and how the case breaches or upholds it. **Suggest** or **advise** questions need a realistic action, such as refusing, explaining the correct treatment or seeking advice. Where figures are given, calculate the effect of the unethical treatment, as in the Hilltop example, because it shows exactly who would be misled.
