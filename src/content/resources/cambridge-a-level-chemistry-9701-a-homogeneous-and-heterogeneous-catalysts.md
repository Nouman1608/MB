This guide covers subtopic **26.2, Homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts**, from Topic 26,
Reaction kinetics, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It
is A Level content. The broader
[Reaction Kinetics: Rate Equations and Catalysis guide](/resources/a-reaction-kinetics-rate-equations-and-catalysis/)
lists the four named examples; this page explains each one step by step, shows how to classify
an unfamiliar catalyst, how to use electrode potentials to test whether an ion could act as a
homogeneous catalyst, and how energy profiles and rate equations give evidence for each type.

## What the syllabus asks for

The three learning outcomes for 26.2 are:

1. explain that catalysts can be **homogeneous** or **heterogeneous**;
2. describe the mode of action of a **heterogeneous** catalyst, to include **adsorption of
   reactants, bond weakening and desorption of products**, for example:
   - iron in the Haber process;
   - palladium, platinum and rhodium in the catalytic removal of oxides of nitrogen from the
     exhaust gases of car engines;
3. describe the mode of action of a **homogeneous** catalyst by **being used in one step and
   reformed in a later step**, for example:
   - atmospheric oxides of nitrogen in the oxidation of atmospheric sulfur dioxide;
   - Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺ in the I⁻/S₂O₈²⁻ reaction.

## Classifying a catalyst

- A **homogeneous** catalyst is in the **same phase** as the reactants, for example a gas
  catalysing a gas reaction, or a dissolved ion catalysing a reaction in aqueous solution.
- A **heterogeneous** catalyst is in a **different phase** from the reactants, usually a solid
  catalysing reactions of gases or of substances in solution.

Use state symbols to decide. Some examples:

| Reaction | Catalyst | Type |
|---|---|---|
| N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) | Fe(s) | heterogeneous |
| 2CO(g) + 2NO(g) → 2CO₂(g) + N₂(g) | Pt, Pd, Rh (s) | heterogeneous |
| 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2SO₃(g) in air | NO₂(g) | homogeneous |
| S₂O₈²⁻(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2SO₄²⁻(aq) + I₂(aq) | Fe²⁺(aq) or Fe³⁺(aq) | homogeneous |
| iodination of propanone in aqueous acid | H⁺(aq) | homogeneous |

Both kinds work by providing an **alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy**.
They are not consumed overall.

## Heterogeneous catalysis: three stages

1. **Adsorption.** Reactant molecules form weak bonds to **active sites** on the catalyst
   surface.
2. **Bond weakening.** Electron density in bonds within the adsorbed molecules shifts towards
   the bonds with the surface, so the bonds inside the reactant molecules are weakened. The
   reactants are also held close together on the surface, so they react more easily.
3. **Desorption.** The bonds between the product molecules and the surface break, and the
   products leave, freeing the active sites for more reactant.

Because only the surface takes part, heterogeneous catalysts are used in forms with a large
surface area: finely divided iron for ammonia manufacture, and a thin coating of the metals on a
honeycomb ceramic support in a catalytic converter.

### Iron in the Haber process

- N₂ and H₂ adsorb on the iron surface.
- The very strong N≡N triple bond and the H–H bond are weakened; adsorbed nitrogen and hydrogen
  react on the surface, building up N–H bonds.
- Ammonia desorbs, and the active sites are free again.

The key point to stress is the N≡N bond: its strength is why the uncatalysed reaction has such
a high activation energy.

### Platinum, palladium and rhodium in catalytic converters

In a car engine, nitrogen and oxygen from the air combine at high temperature to form oxides of
nitrogen. In the converter:

- NO (and NO₂) and CO molecules adsorb onto the metal surface;
- the N–O bonds are weakened;
- nitrogen atoms pair to form N₂, while oxygen is transferred to CO to form CO₂;
- N₂ and CO₂ desorb from the surface.

Overall equations: 2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂ and 4CO + 2NO₂ → 4CO₂ + N₂.

## Homogeneous catalysis: used, then reformed

A homogeneous catalyst reacts with one reactant in the first step, forming an intermediate,
and is **reformed** when the intermediate reacts in a later step. It therefore appears in the
mechanism but cancels from the overall equation.

### Oxides of nitrogen and atmospheric sulfur dioxide

Step 1: SO₂(g) + NO₂(g) → SO₃(g) + NO(g) (NO₂ used)

Step 2: NO(g) + ½O₂(g) → NO₂(g) (NO₂ reformed)

Overall: SO₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → SO₃(g)

NO₂ is the catalyst; NO is the intermediate. The sulfur trioxide formed dissolves in rain water,
contributing to acid rain, so this is a case where catalysis has a harmful effect.

### Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺ in the iodide–peroxodisulfate reaction

S₂O₈²⁻(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2SO₄²⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)

**Why the uncatalysed reaction is slow:** both reactants are negative ions, so they repel each
other and collisions with enough energy and the right orientation are rare. The activation
energy is high.

**Catalysed route with Fe²⁺:**

Step 1: S₂O₈²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺

Step 2: 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂

Each step is between oppositely charged ions, which attract, so both steps are faster. If Fe³⁺
is added instead, step 2 occurs first and step 1 regenerates the Fe³⁺; either oxidation state
works because iron can switch between them.

## Worked example: could another ion catalyse this reaction?

For an ion pair Mⁿ⁺/M⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾⁺ to work in the same way, **both** steps must be feasible. That
requires its E⦵ to lie **between** E⦵(I₂/I⁻) = +0.54 V and E⦵(S₂O₈²⁻/SO₄²⁻) = +2.01 V.

*Question:* Which of these could catalyse the reaction: Co³⁺/Co²⁺ (+1.82 V) or V³⁺/V²⁺
(−0.26 V)?

*Answer:*

- **Co³⁺/Co²⁺:** S₂O₈²⁻ oxidises Co²⁺ (E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = 2.01 − 1.82 = +0.19 V) and Co³⁺
  oxidises I⁻ (E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = 1.82 − 0.54 = +1.28 V). Both steps are feasible, so it
  **could** act as a catalyst on energetic grounds.
- **V³⁺/V²⁺:** S₂O₈²⁻ oxidises V²⁺ (feasible), but V³⁺ cannot oxidise I⁻
  (E⦵<sub>cell</sub> = −0.26 − 0.54 = −0.80 V). The catalyst would not be regenerated, so it
  **cannot** act as a catalyst.

Feasibility is necessary but not sufficient: the catalysed steps must also be fast.

## Evidence for how a catalyst acts

- **Energy profile.** A heterogeneous or homogeneous catalysed route has a lower maximum than
  the uncatalysed route. For a two-step homogeneous route, the profile has two humps, each lower
  than the single uncatalysed hump, with the intermediate in the trough between them. The
  overall enthalpy change is the same.
- **Rate equation.** A homogeneous catalyst that takes part in the rate-determining step
  appears in the rate equation, even though it is absent from the overall equation. H⁺ in the
  iodination of propanone is an example.

## Common mistakes

- Defining homogeneous as "same state as the products"; it is the **reactants**.
- Giving only "adsorption" for heterogeneous catalysis; all three stages are expected.
- Saying adsorbed molecules "break apart" in every case; the syllabus wording is **bond
  weakening**.
- Writing the Fe catalysis steps without balancing for electrons (two Fe²⁺ per S₂O₈²⁻).
- Forgetting to say why the uncatalysed I⁻/S₂O₈²⁻ reaction is slow (repulsion of negative ions).
- Saying a catalyst changes ΔH or the position of equilibrium.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** the difference between the types: refer to phase relative to the reactants.
- **Describe** heterogeneous action: adsorption onto active sites, weakening of named bonds,
  desorption of named products.
- **Describe** homogeneous action: give both steps as equations, then say the catalyst is used in
  step 1 and reformed in step 2.
- **Deduce** whether an ion can catalyse: compare E⦵ values for both steps.
- **Sketch** energy profiles with axes labelled, both routes shown and Eₐ marked.
