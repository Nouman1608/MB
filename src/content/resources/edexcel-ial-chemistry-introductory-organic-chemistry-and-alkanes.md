## Hazard, risk and working safely (4.1–4.3)

A **hazard** is anything with the potential to cause harm, such as a flammable, toxic or corrosive
substance. A **risk** is the chance that the hazard actually causes harm, given how, and how much of,
the substance is used. Hazard is a property of the substance; risk depends on what you do with it.

Many organic compounds are volatile and flammable, and some are toxic. Their vapours spread and can
ignite away from the liquid. So you carry out a **risk assessment** before any practical: identify each
hazard, judge the risk and decide how to control it. The specification names three ways to reduce risk:

1. **Work on a smaller scale**: less vapour, less heat, a smaller fire if one starts.
2. **Take precautions specific to the hazard**: no naked flames for flammable liquids, a fume cupboard for
   toxic vapour, gloves and goggles for corrosives.
3. **Use an alternative method with less hazardous substances**, such as a dilute solution of a reagent.

### Worked example: planning a halogenation

A student plans to shake cyclohexane with pure liquid bromine in bright light. Cyclohexane is highly
flammable; bromine is toxic and corrosive. Use a few cm³ in a test tube (smaller scale); work in a fume
cupboard away from flames, with gloves and goggles (hazard-specific); use a dilute solution of bromine
in an organic solvent instead of pure bromine (less hazardous alternative).

## Homologous series and functional groups (4.4)

In a **homologous series**, every member shares one functional group and one general formula, and
each member differs from the next by CH₂. Members show a gradual trend in physical properties
and have similar chemical properties.

The **functional group** is the part of the molecule responsible for its characteristic reactions: C=C
in alkenes, –OH in alcohols.

## Naming and drawing compounds (4.5)

The stem shows how many carbons the longest chain has: meth- C₁, eth- C₂, prop- C₃, but- C₄,
pent- C₅, hex- C₆, hept- C₇, oct- C₈, non- C₉, dec- C₁₀. The ending shows the series: -ane for alkanes.

**Rules for branched alkanes**

1. Find the longest continuous carbon chain. It does not have to be drawn in a straight line.
2. Number the chain from the end that gives the branches the lowest numbers.
3. Name each branch (methyl, ethyl) with its position; use di-, tri- for repeats.
4. List different branches alphabetically, ignoring di- and tri-. Commas go between numbers, hyphens
   between numbers and words.

**Formula types.** A **structural** formula shows the arrangement unambiguously, e.g. CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃.
A **displayed** formula draws every atom and bond. A **skeletal** formula is a zigzag in which each corner
and line end is a carbon and hydrogens on carbon are not drawn: pentane is a zigzag of four lines,
cyclohexane a hexagon.

### Worked example: a hidden longest chain

Name CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH(CH₂CH₃)CH₃.

- The row as written has five carbons, but through the ethyl branch the chain is six carbons long, so
  the parent is **hexane**.
- Two methyl branches remain. Numbering from the nearer end puts them on carbons 2 and 4, not 3 and 5.
- Name: **2,4-dimethylhexane**, C₈H₁₈.

## Classifying reactions (4.6)

| Type | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | Two molecules join to make one product | C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ |
| Substitution | One atom or group is replaced by another | C₂H₆ + Br₂ → C₂H₅Br + HBr |
| Oxidation | Gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen | Combustion of an alkane |
| Reduction | Gain of hydrogen or loss of oxygen | Hydrogen added to an alkene |
| Polymerisation | Many small monomers join into a long chain | n C₂H₄ → poly(ethene) |

One reaction can fit two labels. Adding hydrogen to an alkene is an addition and also a reduction.

## Bond breaking, free radicals and electrophiles (4.7–4.8)

- **Homolytic fission**: each atom takes one electron of the pair. The products are **free radicals**.
  Cl–Cl → 2Cl•
- **Heterolytic fission**: one atom takes both electrons. The products are **ions**.
  H–Br → H⁺ + Br⁻

A **free radical** is a species with an unpaired electron, shown by a single dot. An **electrophile**
is a species that accepts a pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond, such as H⁺ attacking an
alkene in Topic 5.

## Alkanes and cycloalkanes (4.9)

Alkanes and cycloalkanes are **hydrocarbons** (compounds of carbon and hydrogen only) that are
**saturated** (single bonds only).

- Alkanes: **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**
- Cycloalkanes: **CₙH₂ₙ** (closing the ring removes two hydrogens)

### Worked example: identifying from relative molecular mass

An alkane has Mᵣ = 114. Each CH₂ unit adds 14 and the two extra hydrogens add 2, so 14n + 2 = 114,
giving n = 8: **C₈H₁₈**. A cycloalkane with Mᵣ = 84 gives 14n = 84, so n = 6: **C₆H₁₂**.

## Structural isomerism (4.10–4.11)

**Structural isomers** have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae. For cycloalkanes, vary
ring size as well as branch positions.

### Worked example: the isomers of C₆H₁₄

| Structural formula | Name |
|---|---|
| CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ | hexane |
| CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃ | 2-methylpentane |
| CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ | 3-methylpentane |
| CH₃C(CH₃)₂CH₂CH₃ | 2,2-dimethylbutane |
| CH₃CH(CH₃)CH(CH₃)CH₃ | 2,3-dimethylbutane |

That is five isomers. An ethyl on carbon 2 of butane is not new: the longest chain becomes five carbons
(3-methylpentane). For cycloalkanes, C₄H₈ gives cyclobutane and methylcyclopropane.

## Fuels from crude oil (4.12)

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons, mostly alkanes. Alkanes are used as fuels.

- **Fractional distillation** separates crude oil into fractions by boiling temperature in a column
  that is cooler towards the top. It is a physical separation.
- **Cracking** breaks long-chain alkanes into shorter alkanes (for petrol) and alkenes (for polymers).
  Thermal cracking uses high temperature; catalytic cracking uses a zeolite catalyst.
- **Reforming** turns straight-chain alkanes into branched and cyclic hydrocarbons, which burn more
  smoothly in petrol engines.

### Worked example: writing the equations

Heptadecane, C₁₇H₃₆, cracks to nonane and butene only.

- Carbon left after nonane: 17 − 9 = 8, so two butene molecules.
- Check hydrogen: 20 + 2 × 8 = 36.
- **C₁₇H₃₆ → C₉H₂₀ + 2C₄H₈**

Reforming heptane into methylcyclohexane: C₇H₁₆ → C₇H₁₄ + H₂.

### Further suggested practical: cracking liquid paraffin

Liquid paraffin on mineral wool is heated with aluminium oxide as catalyst, and the gas is collected
over water. It decolourises bromine water, showing an alkene. Lift the delivery tube out of the water
before you stop heating, so cold water is not sucked back into the hot tube.

## Combustion and pollutants (4.13–4.14, 4.17)

**Complete combustion** gives carbon dioxide and water. For hexane:

2C₆H₁₄ + 19O₂ → 12CO₂ + 14H₂O

In a limited supply of oxygen, **incomplete combustion** gives carbon monoxide or carbon particulates:

2C₆H₁₄ + 13O₂ → 12CO + 14H₂O

The pollutants emitted when alkane fuels burn are carbon monoxide, oxides of nitrogen, oxides of
sulfur, carbon particulates and unburned hydrocarbons.

- **Carbon monoxide is toxic** because it binds to haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen does, so the
  blood carries less oxygen to the cells.
- **Oxides of nitrogen** form because the very high temperature inside an engine makes atmospheric
  nitrogen combine with oxygen (N₂ + O₂ → 2NO). NO is oxidised to NO₂, which forms nitric acid in rain:
  4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃.
- **Oxides of sulfur** come from sulfur impurities in the fuel (S + O₂ → SO₂), and SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃.

These acidic oxides cause acid rain.

## Alternative fuels and carbon neutrality (4.15–4.16)

Crude oil is finite, so its fuels are not **sustainable**, and burning them releases CO₂, a greenhouse
gas linked to climate change. A fuel is **carbon-neutral** if there is no net release of CO₂ over its
whole life cycle: production, transport and use.

- **Petrol** is not carbon-neutral: its carbon was locked underground for millions of years.
- **Bioethanol** is made by fermenting sugars from plants. In theory the CO₂ absorbed by photosynthesis
  (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂) equals the CO₂ released by fermentation
  (C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂) and by combustion (2C₂H₅OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O): six in, six out. In
  practice, farm machinery, fertiliser, distillation and transport release extra CO₂.
- **Hydrogen** burns to water only (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O), but its footprint depends on how it is made.
  Hydrogen from methane and steam (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂) releases carbon oxides; hydrogen from
  electrolysis is close to carbon-neutral only if the electricity is renewable or nuclear.

### Worked example: CO₂ from two fuels

Calculate the mass of CO₂ released when 1.00 kg of each fuel burns completely
(C = 12.0, H = 1.0, O = 16.0).

- Hexane: Mᵣ = 86.0, so 1000 ÷ 86.0 = 11.63 mol. Each mole gives 6 mol CO₂: 69.77 mol × 44.0 g mol⁻¹
  = **3.07 kg CO₂**.
- Ethanol: Mᵣ = 46.0, so 1000 ÷ 46.0 = 21.74 mol. Each mole gives 2 mol CO₂: 43.48 mol × 44.0 g mol⁻¹
  = **1.91 kg CO₂**.

All the hexane CO₂ is fossil carbon; the ethanol's carbon came from the air within one growing season.

## Halogenation: free radical substitution (4.17–4.18)

In ultraviolet light, a halogen atom replaces a hydrogen atom of the alkane. For ethane and bromine:

C₂H₆ + Br₂ → C₂H₅Br + HBr

**Initiation.** UV light breaks Br–Br homolytically: two curly half-arrows run from the middle of the
bond, one to each bromine atom.

Br₂ → 2Br•

**Propagation.** Each step uses one radical and makes another.

Br• + C₂H₆ → C₂H₅• + HBr

C₂H₅• + Br₂ → C₂H₅Br + Br•

**Termination.** Two radicals join.

Br• + Br• → Br₂

C₂H₅• + Br• → C₂H₅Br

C₂H₅• + C₂H₅• → C₄H₁₀

**Limited use in synthesis.** Bromoethane can itself be attacked by Br•, giving C₂H₄Br₂ and then more
highly substituted products, and termination adds butane. The mixture must be separated, so the yield
of any one product is low.

## Common errors

- Defining risk as "how dangerous the substance is". That is the hazard.
- Taking the row on the page as the longest chain without checking the branches.
- Using two-headed curly arrows in the initiation step instead of half-arrows.
- Making H• in propagation. The first step makes HBr and an alkyl radical.
- Saying CO "stops you breathing" instead of explaining its binding to haemoglobin.
- Calling bioethanol fully carbon-neutral, or hydrogen carbon-neutral whatever its source.
