These questions cover sub-topic 6.4 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680, 2027-2029): the definition of drought, its causes and impacts, and strategies for managing it before, during and after. Places and figures in the sources are invented.

## Section A

**1.** Define *drought*. **[1]**

**2.** State **two** ways in which climate change can cause drought. **[2]**

**3.** Name **two** methods of monitoring a developing drought. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by *desalination*. **[1]**

**5.** Give **two** examples of emergency water supplies. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain why a region with high average rainfall can still experience a drought. **[2]**

**7.** The table shows rainfall in the invented district of Kerowa.

| Year | Rainfall (mm) |
|---|---|
| Normal (long-term average) | 750 |
| Year 1 | 690 |
| Year 2 | 420 |
| Year 3 | 375 |

(a) Calculate Year 3 rainfall as a percentage of normal. Show your working. **[2]**
(b) Using the data, explain why the impacts of the drought were likely to be greater in Year 3 than in Year 2. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how a drought can lead to famine. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how a drought can cause a decrease in air quality. **[3]**

**10.** A village of 400 people relies on wells during the dry season. Explain why pumping more water from the aquifer during a drought may cause problems in later years. **[3]**

**11.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of rainwater harvesting and water conservation as strategies for a rural community at risk of drought. **[4]**

**12.** A wealthy coastal city and a low-income inland farming region both face more frequent droughts. Discuss which strategies for increasing water supply would suit each. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A period of dry weather that is longer or more severe than normal **[1]**.

**2.** Increase in global temperatures raises evaporation, so water stores dry faster **[1]**; increase in extreme weather events makes long dry spells more frequent or intense **[1]**.

**3.** Any two: measuring rainfall and comparing it with normal **[1]**; river or reservoir levels **[1]**; soil moisture **[1]**; satellite images of vegetation condition **[1]**; ground water or well levels. **[max 2]**

**4.** Removing salt from sea water to produce fresh water **[1]**.

**5.** Any two: water delivered by tankers **[1]**; bottled water **[1]**; temporary standpipes **[1]**. **[max 2]**

**6.** Drought is measured against what is normal for that place **[1]**, so if rainfall falls well below its usual high amount for a long period, plants, people and water supplies adapted to plenty of water suffer shortage **[1]**.

**7.** (a) 375 ÷ 750 × 100 **[1]** = 50% **[1]**.
(b) Year 3 rainfall was lower than Year 2 (375 mm compared with 420 mm) **[1]**, and it followed two dry years, so soil moisture, rivers and ground water had not been refilled and the effects built up **[1]**.

**8.** Lack of rain causes crops to fail and livestock to die **[1]**; food stores run out and food prices rise, so families cannot grow or buy enough food **[1]**; if food cannot be brought in, for example because of poverty, poor transport or conflict, widespread starvation (famine) follows **[1]**.

**9.** Dry soil without vegetation cover is easily picked up by wind **[1]**, creating dust in the air; dry vegetation catches fire easily, so wildfires release smoke **[1]**; dust and smoke particles cause breathing problems **[1]**.

**10.** If water is pumped faster than rain can recharge the aquifer, the water table falls **[1]**; shallow wells then dry up and deeper, more expensive wells are needed **[1]**; the aquifer may take many years to refill, so supplies are lower in future dry seasons (accept: near coasts, sea water seeps in and makes the water salty) **[1]**.

**11.** Rainwater harvesting: cheap tanks collect roof water for use in the dry season **[1]**, but collect little or nothing during the drought itself and hold limited amounts **[1]**. Water conservation, such as drip irrigation and fixing leaks: low-cost and makes existing water last longer **[1]**, but depends on everyone changing habits, and drip systems still need money and water to start **[1]**. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 6 for strategies matched to each place with reasons and limitations. Coastal city: desalination suits it because it is beside the sea and can afford the high energy and building costs **[1]**, though brine waste can harm marine life **[1]**; large dams and reservoirs, or piping water from them, are affordable for a wealthy city **[1]**. Inland farming region: rainwater harvesting is cheap and needs simple equipment **[1]**; aquifers and wells can supply water protected from evaporation **[1]**, but must not be over-pumped **[1]**; desalination is unsuitable because the region is far from the sea and cannot afford it **[1]**. **[max 6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining drought as "no rain" instead of "longer or more severe than normal".
- In data questions, ignoring the build-up over several dry years.
- Jumping from drought to famine without the food-shortage step.
- Recommending desalination for an inland, low-income region.
- Giving benefits without limitations in "discuss" answers.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are these Drought questions from Cambridge past papers?**
No. They are original questions written for Marlbridge and do not copy past papers or their mark tariffs. Use them alongside the past papers for Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680).

**What are the causes of drought in the 0680 syllabus?**
Two: lack of rain, and climate change through higher global temperatures and more extreme weather events.

**Which strategies count as increasing water supply?**
Dams and reservoirs, use of aquifers and wells, desalination and rainwater harvesting.
