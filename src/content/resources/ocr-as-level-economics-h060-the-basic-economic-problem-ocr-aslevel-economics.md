## Scarcity: why choice cannot be avoided

**Scarcity** is the situation in which wants are greater than the resources available to satisfy them. Wants are unlimited: as one is met, another appears. Resources (the time, materials, skills and equipment used to produce goods) are limited at any moment. So every individual, firm and government has to choose.

Scarcity is not the same as shortage or rarity. A shortage is a temporary market situation in which quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price. Scarcity is permanent and universal: a wealthy country with full shops still faces it, because the resources used to make one thing cannot also make another.

Because of scarcity, every economy must answer three questions:

- **What** should be produced, and how much of each good?
- **How** should it be produced: with more labour or more machinery, in large plants or small workshops?
- **For whom** should it be produced: who gets the output?

Different economic systems answer these in different ways, which is the subject of the next topic in this Area of Study, the market economy.

## Needs and wants

A **need** is something required for survival or to avoid serious harm: basic food, clean water, shelter, essential health care. A **want** is anything desired beyond that. The boundary is not fixed. Access to a mobile phone was a want for most people a generation ago; for a delivery driver who receives every job through an app, it is now close to a need for earning a living. Economics does not depend on drawing the line precisely. What matters is that wants as a whole have no upper limit.

## Economic goods and free goods

An **economic good** uses scarce resources to produce, so producing it has an opportunity cost. A **free good** is available without using up scarce resources, so more of it can be had without giving anything else up. Air in an open field and seawater in mid-ocean are standard examples.

The classification depends on context, not on the price label:

| Item (invented contexts) | Classification | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A library book borrowed at no charge | Economic good | Buildings, staff and books use resources; taxpayers pay |
| Fresh water from a glacier stream in a remote valley | Free good | No resources are used to supply it, and using more deprives no one |
| The same water piped to a desert city | Economic good | Pipes, pumping and treatment use resources |
| Sand in a desert | Free good, until it is dug and transported | Moving it uses labour, fuel and lorries |

The common error is to call anything without a price a free good. "Free at the point of use" describes how a good is paid for, not whether it uses resources.

## Positive and normative statements

A **positive statement** can be tested against evidence. It may be true or false, but evidence could settle it. A **normative statement** contains a value judgement about what ought to be, so evidence alone cannot settle it.

Consider a debate about a fuel subsidy in Country N:

- "The subsidy cost the government 2% of national income last year." Positive: check the accounts.
- "Removing the subsidy would raise bus fares." Positive: a prediction, testable once it happens.
- "The subsidy is a waste of public money." Normative: "waste" is a judgement.
- "Low-income households deserve help with transport costs." Normative.

Economists use positive analysis to predict what a policy will do; choosing between policies then needs normative judgements about which outcome is better. A strong exam answer separates the two.

## Economic agents and factors of production

**Economic agents** are the decision-makers. **Households** supply factors of production, mainly labour, and spend their incomes. **Firms** buy or hire factors and combine them to produce goods and services. **Government** taxes, spends, provides some goods and services, and sets the rules within which others act.

| Factor | Examples | Reward |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Farmland, fishing grounds, minerals | Rent |
| Labour | Nurses' skill, a builder's effort | Wages |
| Capital | Tractors, ovens, delivery vans | Interest |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors and taking risk | Profit |

Capital means man-made goods used to produce other goods. Money is not capital in this sense: it is used to buy capital.

## Sustainability and future scarcity

**Sustainability** means meeting the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Using resources faster than they are replaced makes future scarcity worse.

**Worked example (invented).** An underground water store beneath a farming region holds 900 million cubic metres. Rainfall adds 30 million a year; farms extract 45 million. The store falls by 15 million a year and would be exhausted in 900 / 15 = 60 years. Cutting extraction to 30 million would keep it stable, but farm output would fall today. The choice is between more output now and water for farmers in the future: the economic problem stretched across time.

Ways to manage such a trade-off include charging for extraction, setting quotas, or investing in water-saving technology. Each has costs, and evaluating them means asking who bears the cost now and who gains later.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (data response style): Explain why water supplied to homes in a city is an economic good even though rain is free. [4]*

> Rain falls without anyone using resources, so in that form it is a free good. To reach homes, however, water must be collected, treated and piped, which uses labour, chemicals, pumps and pipes. These resources have alternative uses, so supplying water has an opportunity cost. That makes piped water an economic good, whether or not households pay a price for it.

*Why it works:* it defines both terms through the use of resources, applies them to the specific case, and avoids the trap of judging by price.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing scarcity with shortage.
- Calling a good "free" because it has no price at the point of use.
- Treating a positive statement as normative because it is false.
- Treating money as a factor of production.
- Listing the three economic questions without linking them to scarcity.
- Describing sustainability without saying how it changes future scarcity.

## Exam technique

- **Section A (multiple choice):** these often test exact definitions. Read every option: "a statement that can be proven true" is not the same as "a statement that can be tested".
- **Section B (data response):** apply the definition to the context in the data, as in the model answer.
- **Section C (extended writing):** scarcity questions ask you to evaluate, so end with a judgement on how serious scarcity is in the case discussed and what it depends on, such as technology or the rate of resource use.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does economic growth end scarcity?**
No. Growth increases the goods and services available, but wants tend to grow too, and resources used for one purpose still cannot be used for another.

**Is a statement that includes numbers always positive?**
No. "Unemployment of 6% is unacceptable" contains a figure but expresses a judgement, so it is normative.
