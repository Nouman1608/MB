Condensed recall for **5.1 Migration as a component of population change**. For full explanations, read the [Core Human Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-human-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Migration | Movement of people to live elsewhere, for a year or more |
| Immigration / emigration | Movement into / out of an area |
| Net migration | Immigrants minus emigrants |
| Net migration rate | Net migration per 1000 of the population per year |
| Push factor | Reason to leave a place |
| Pull factor | Attraction of a destination |
| Chain migration | Later migrants follow earlier ones from the same origin, using family or community links |
| Barrier (obstacle) | Anything restricting movement: cost, borders, laws, distance, language |
| Distance decay | Fall in interaction (here, numbers of migrants) as distance increases |
| Age-selective migration | Migration in which some age groups move far more than others |
| Circulation | Short-term or repeated movement that is not migration, such as commuting or seasonal visits |

Population change = natural increase + net migration. Movements shorter than one year are excluded from the syllabus definition.

## Worked example: the population change equation

A region starts the year with 500 000 people. There are 9000 births, 4000 deaths, 6000 immigrants and 8000 emigrants.

- Natural increase = 9000 - 4000 = **+5000**
- Net migration = 6000 - 8000 = **-2000** (net out-migration)
- Population change = 5000 + (-2000) = **+3000**, so the population ends at 503 000.
- Net migration rate = -2000 / 500 000 x 1000 = **-4 per 1000**.

This shows a region can grow while losing people by migration, if natural increase is larger. In ageing HICs the reverse is common: natural decrease is offset by net in-migration.

## Push and pull factors

| | Push | Pull |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Unemployment, low wages, poverty | Jobs, higher wages |
| Social | Poor services, discrimination | Better education, health care, family |
| Political | Conflict, persecution | Safety, stability |
| Environmental | Hazard, drought, degradation | Safer or better environment |

## Patterns

- **Distance:** most movers go short distances; numbers fall as distance rises (distance decay).
- **Age:** young adults are the most mobile; families and older people move less, although retirement migration exists.
- Selectivity by education, skills and gender varies by stream.

**Why young adults move most:** they are leaving education, starting work, forming partnerships and have fewer ties such as owned homes and school-age children. They also gain the most from moving, since they have a long working life ahead to recover the cost. A typical migration-by-age graph peaks in the early twenties, with a smaller rise at retirement age in some HICs.

**Why distance matters:** longer moves cost more, need more information and cross more barriers. Better transport and communications have weakened distance decay, but not removed it.

## Models and ideas

- **Ravenstein's laws (1885):** among his generalisations, most migrants move short distances; migration often proceeds in steps; each stream produces a counter-stream; and urban dwellers are less migratory than rural ones.
- **Lee's model:** origin factors, destination factors, **intervening obstacles** and personal factors.
- **Gravity model:** movement rises with the size of places and falls with distance.
- **Stouffer's intervening opportunities (1940):** the number of moves to a place depends on the opportunities there and is reduced by opportunities nearer the origin.
- **Zelinsky's mobility transition (1971):** types of mobility change as a country develops, linking migration to the stages of demographic transition.
- Perception matters: people decide on what they believe, not full information.

**Evaluating the models:** they assume people act rationally on economic information, but many moves are driven by family, conflict or chance. They describe patterns well but explain forced migration poorly. Lee's model is useful because it recognises that personal factors and perception filter the same push and pull factors differently for each person.

## Processes: chain migration

Early migrants send back information, money for fares and offers of housing or jobs. Each wave lowers the cost and risk for the next, so streams grow and become concentrated in particular neighbourhoods of destination cities. This explains why migrants from one village or district often cluster in one area of a foreign city.

## Constraints

Cost of moving, visas and border controls, lack of information, family ties, and lack of skills or qualifications.

Constraints are **unequal**: the poorest often cannot afford to move far, so long-distance migration tends to come from middle-income households. Policy barriers such as visas and quotas are national; physical barriers include mountains, seas and deserts; social barriers include language and discrimination.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Explain why migration is often age-selective."*

> Young adults have fewer ties and the most to gain from moving (**reason**). They are seeking work, education or partners (**push and pull factors linked to life stage**). This produces a peak in migration in the early twenties (**pattern**), with a smaller retirement peak in some HICs (**variation**).

## Exam traps

- Push and pull factors work together; explain both for a stream.
- Do not call every move "migration": short-term movement is excluded.
- Barriers can be social or political as well as distance.
- Net migration can be negative; show the sign in calculations.
- Name the model you are applying and say where it fits poorly.

## Self-test

1. Define net migration.
2. What is chain migration?
3. Give one economic push and one pull factor.
4. Which age group is most likely to migrate?
5. Name an intervening obstacle in Lee's model.
6. Write the population change equation.
7. A town has 2000 births, 1500 deaths, 800 immigrants and 1100 emigrants. What is its population change?
8. What is distance decay?
9. State two of Ravenstein's laws.
10. Why do the very poorest often not migrate long distances?
11. Give one weakness of push-pull models.

**Answers:** 1. Immigrants minus emigrants. 2. Later migrants follow earlier ones through family or community links. 3. Push: unemployment. Pull: higher wages. 4. Young adults. 5. Distance, cost, border controls, language. 6. Natural increase plus net migration. 7. Natural increase +500, net migration -300, so +200. 8. The fall in the number of migrants as distance increases. 9. Most migrants move short distances; migration proceeds in steps; each stream has a counter-stream. 10. They cannot afford the cost or meet visa requirements. 11. They assume rational, economic decisions and explain forced migration poorly.
