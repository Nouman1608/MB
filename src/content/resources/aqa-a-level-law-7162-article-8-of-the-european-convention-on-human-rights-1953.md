This guide covers **Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights 1953** in section 3.5 Human
Rights of AQA A-level Law (7162). The specification states the content as "Article 8.1: right to respect
for private and family life, his home and for his correspondence". The permitted restrictions in Article
8.2 are a separate specification row, covered in the [Restrictions study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-restrictions/).
For the option as a whole, see the [Human Rights study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-human-rights-study-guide/).

## Why Article 8 needs careful handling

Article 8 is a **qualified** right, and the European Court has read its four interests broadly. That
makes it the Article most likely to be engaged in a Paper 3 scenario, but it also means students often
assert that "privacy" is breached without showing which interest is affected. A strong answer does three
things in order: identifies the interest under 8.1, shows that there has been an **interference** (or a
failure in a **positive obligation**), and then moves to 8.2.

Note the wording: the right is to **respect for** private and family life. This gives states room to
decide how to protect the interests, and it is the textual basis for positive obligations.

## The four interests in Article 8.1

### Private life

Private life has no exhaustive definition. The case law shows it includes:

- **Physical and psychological integrity.** In **X and Y v Netherlands (1985)** Dutch law left no way to
  bring a criminal prosecution for a sexual assault on a 16-year-old girl with a mental disability,
  because she could not make the complaint herself and her father could not make it for her. The Court found a breach: effective protection required
  criminal law provisions.
- **Sexual life.** **Dudgeon v United Kingdom (1981)**: criminalising consensual private sexual acts
  between adult men in Northern Ireland interfered with private life and could not be justified.
- **Personal identity.** **Goodwin v United Kingdom (2002)**: the failure to give legal recognition to a
  transsexual woman's acquired gender breached Article 8. Parliament responded with the Gender
  Recognition Act 2004.
- **Personal data.** **S and Marper v United Kingdom (2008)**: the blanket and indefinite retention of
  the DNA profiles, samples and fingerprints of people who had not been convicted breached Article 8. The
  rules were changed by the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012.
- **Image and public moments.** **Peck v United Kingdom (2003)**: a council released CCTV footage of a
  man in a street shortly after a suicide attempt, and it was broadcast without his face being properly
  masked. Being filmed in a public place did not remove his private life. **Von Hannover v Germany
  (2004)**: photographs of Princess Caroline of Monaco going about daily life engaged Article 8, as they
  contributed nothing to a debate of general interest.
- **Medical information.** **Campbell v MGN Ltd (2004)**: details and photographs of a model's
  attendance at Narcotics Anonymous were private information.
- **Decisions about how to end one's life.** In **Pretty v United Kingdom (2002)** the Court accepted that
  Article 8 was engaged by the ban on assisted suicide, although the interference was justified. In
  **R (Purdy) v DPP (2009)** the House of Lords held that Article 8 required the Director of Public
  Prosecutions to publish a policy on when assisting a suicide would be prosecuted.

### Family life

Family life is a question of fact: the Court looks for real and close personal ties rather than formal
status. In **Marckx v Belgium (1979)** family life existed between an unmarried mother and her child, and
Belgian rules that treated children born outside marriage less favourably breached Article 8 with
Article 14. Family life arguments are common in deportation cases, where removing a person would
separate them from a partner or children.

### Home

"Home" means the place where a person lives, whether owned or rented. Interference includes searches
and also serious environmental nuisance. In **López Ostra v Spain (1994)** fumes from a waste treatment
plant next to the applicant's home breached Article 8. By contrast, in **Hatton v United Kingdom (2003)**
the Grand Chamber held that the night-flight scheme at Heathrow did not breach Article 8, because the
state had struck a fair balance and enjoyed a wide margin of appreciation on economic and environmental
policy.

### Correspondence

Correspondence covers letters, telephone calls, emails and other communications.

- **Golder v United Kingdom (1975)**: stopping a prisoner from writing to a solicitor breached Article 8
  (and Article 6).
- **Malone v United Kingdom (1984)**: police telephone tapping had no clear legal basis, so it was not
  "in accordance with the law". This led to the Interception of Communications Act 1985.
- **Halford v United Kingdom (1997)**: interception of a senior police officer's calls from her office
  telephone breached Article 8. She had a reasonable expectation of privacy, and the 1985 Act did not
  regulate internal networks.

## Positive obligations and the press

Article 8 binds the state, but its protection reaches disputes between private parties in two ways.
First, the state has **positive obligations** to secure respect for private life, as X and Y v
Netherlands and Von Hannover show. Second, under section 6 of the Human Rights Act 1998 a **court is
itself a public authority**, so it must give effect to Article 8 when deciding a case between a
celebrity and a newspaper.

English law had no general tort of invasion of privacy: **Wainwright v Home Office (2003)**. Instead the
courts developed breach of confidence into **misuse of private information** (Campbell). The test asks
whether the claimant had a **reasonable expectation of privacy** and, if so, whether that is outweighed
by the publisher's Article 10 rights. In **Google Inc v Vidal-Hall (2015)** the Court of Appeal confirmed
that misuse of private information is a tort in its own right.

## Worked example

*Facts: Tom, a school teacher, attends a private support group for gambling addiction. A member secretly
photographs him and sells the photographs to a local news website, which plans to publish them with a
story about "teachers with secrets". Tom also learns that the local council installed a camera that films
straight into his back garden.*

```
Interest:        Private life (health and personal information, image) for the
                 photographs; private life and home for the camera.
Interference?    Publication would interfere with Tom's private life. The
                 website is not a public authority, but the court is (HRA s6),
                 so Tom sues for misuse of private information.
Expectation?     A support group for addiction is like Campbell's treatment:
                 a strong reasonable expectation of privacy.
Next stage:      Balance against Article 10 and apply 8.2 to the council
                 camera (lawful basis, legitimate aim, necessity).
```

The 8.2 and Article 10 stages are developed in the Restrictions and Article 10 guides. The point here is
that the first stage is fully argued with authority before moving on.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "privacy" without naming which of the four interests is engaged.
- Assuming that anything done in public is outside private life; Peck and Von Hannover show otherwise.
- Claiming English law has a general privacy tort. Wainwright says it does not; the protection comes
  from misuse of private information.
- Forgetting positive obligations, which explain why the state can be responsible for what private
  parties do.
- Misstating Hatton: the Grand Chamber found **no** breach of Article 8.

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: define each interest and support it with a case.
- **Suggest** and **Advise**: identify the interest, show the interference, consider whether the
  defendant is a public authority or whether the court must act, then apply 8.2 and conclude.
- **Consider**: argue both sides on whether the facts engage Article 8 at all, for example whether a
  photograph in a street can be private.
- **Discuss**: evaluate how well English law protects privacy without a statutory privacy tort, using
  Wainwright, Campbell and Vidal-Hall.
