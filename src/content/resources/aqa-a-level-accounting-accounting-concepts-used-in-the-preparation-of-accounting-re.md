This guide teaches section **3.5 Accounting concepts used in the preparation of accounting records** of the
AQA A-level Accounting (7127) specification, Version 1.0 (22 December 2016), for A-level exams from June 2019
onwards. The qualification is linear and untiered, so everything here applies to every candidate. Section 3.5
is one of the sections listed for **Paper 1** (sections 1–8 and 14–18), and the concepts are then used in
almost every set of financial statements you prepare.

## The ten general accounting concepts

### Money measurement

Only items that can be measured reliably in money are recorded. A skilled workforce or loyal customers have
value, but no reliable money figure exists for them, so they are left out of the financial statements.

### Duality

Every transaction has two equal and opposite effects. This is the basis of double entry: each debit has a
matching credit, and the accounting equation (assets = capital + liabilities) always balances. See the
[double entry model guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-accounting-double-entry-model/) for the recording technique.

### Cost

Assets are recorded at their original cost, not at current market value. Cost is objective, because it is
backed by an invoice, but assets may be shown well below what they are now worth.

### Going concern

The accounts assume the business will continue trading for the foreseeable future. So non-current
assets are shown at cost less depreciation, not at forced-sale prices. If a
business is about to close, this assumption no longer holds and assets should be shown at what they are
expected to realise.

### Accruals

Revenue and expenses are matched to the period in which they are earned or incurred, not the period in which
cash is received or paid. This is the reason for accruals, prepayments, depreciation and closing inventory
adjustments.

### Consistency

Once a business chooses an accounting method (for example a depreciation method or an inventory valuation
basis), it uses the same method from one period to the next. This makes results comparable over time. A method
may change only if the new one gives a fairer view, never just to improve profit.

### Prudence

Where judgement is needed, do not overstate assets or profit, and do not understate liabilities or losses.
Losses are recognised as soon as they are foreseen; gains are recognised only when realised. Prudence explains
the provision for doubtful debts and why inventory is written down to net realisable value. Prudence is not a
licence to understate profit on purpose.

### Materiality

An item is material if leaving it out or misstating it would affect a user's decisions. Immaterial items can
be treated in the simplest way. Materiality is relative: £400 may be material to a small market stall but not
to a business with revenue of £20 million.

### Realisation

Revenue is recognised when it is earned, which for goods is normally when they pass to the customer and the
customer accepts them. It is not recognised when an order is received, and it does not wait for the cash. This
concept settles the treatment of goods sent on sale or return.

### Business entity

The business is treated as separate from its owner. Only business transactions are recorded. Money or goods
the owner takes out are **drawings**, not expenses, and money the owner puts in is **capital**.

## Concepts in the situations the specification names

### 1. Preparation of financial statements

Every year-end adjustment is a concept at work. For each item ask: which concept requires this treatment, and
does it raise or lower profit?

**Worked example.** Fernlea Garden Supplies has a draft profit for the year of £52 000. Three items have not
been treated correctly:

1. The owner paid £1 800 for a family holiday from the business bank account. It was debited to sundry
   expenses.
2. Electricity of £1 200 for the final quarter has not been paid or recorded.
3. Closing inventory includes goods at their cost of £2 400. Their net realisable value is £1 500.

```
                                              £
Draft profit for the year                  52 000
Add:  holiday charged as an expense         1 800   (business entity: this is drawings)
Less: electricity accrued                  (1 200)   (accruals)
Less: inventory written down 2 400 - 1 500   (900)   (prudence)
Corrected profit for the year              51 700
```

The £1 800 is then shown as an increase in drawings, the £1 200 as an accrual (other payables) and inventory
falls by £900 in the statement of financial position.

### 2. Asset valuation

**Cost** says start from the price paid. **Going concern** says do not replace that with a break-up value,
because the business will keep using its assets. Together they explain why premises bought for £180 000 years ago may still be shown at cost even
though they would now sell for more. **Prudence** adds one exception: where an asset is worth less than its
carrying amount, as with damaged inventory or a debt that will not be paid, the lower figure is used.

### 3. Depreciation of non-current assets

Depreciation applies the **accruals** concept: the cost of an asset is spread over the periods that benefit
from it. **Consistency** means the method chosen is kept from year to year. **Prudence** supports realistic
estimates of useful life and residual value.

**Worked example.** A machine costs £24 000 plus £1 000 installation. It is expected to last 5 years and be
sold for £5 000 at the end.

```
Cost to capitalise = 24 000 + 1 000 = 25 000
Straight-line charge = (25 000 - 5 000) / 5 = £4 000 per year
```

If the owner had been using 20% reducing balance, the first-year charge would be 25 000 × 20% = £5 000.
Switching to straight line purely to report £1 000 more profit would breach **consistency**. The calculation
methods themselves belong to section 3.6.

### 4. Inventories: cost or net realisable value

Inventory is valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**.

```
NRV = expected selling price - costs still to be incurred to complete and sell
```

This applies **prudence** (do not show inventory at more than it will raise) and **cost** (do not show it above
cost, even if it will sell at a profit, because that profit is not yet realised). The comparison is made
**item by item**, not on the total.

**Worked example.** Hartwell Lighting holds three lines at the year end.

| Item | Units | Cost per unit £ | Selling price £ | Costs to sell £ | NRV per unit £ | Value used £ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Desk lamps | 120 | 18.00 | 30.00 | 2.00 | 28.00 | 120 × 18.00 = 2 160 |
| Floor lamps (damaged) | 40 | 45.00 | 40.00 | 6.00 | 34.00 | 40 × 34.00 = 1 360 |
| Lampshades | 200 | 6.00 | 5.00 | 0.50 | 4.50 | 200 × 4.50 = 900 |
| **Total** | | | | | | **4 420** |

Total cost is £5 160, so the write-down is £740. If you compared totals instead (total NRV £5 620 against total
cost £5 160) you would keep £5 160, hiding the £740 of losses inside the profit on the desk lamps. That is why
the item-by-item rule matters.

### 5. Recording the purchase of non-current assets

The cost of a non-current asset includes every cost needed to get it ready for use: purchase price, delivery,
installation and items that make it fit for its purpose. Running costs are **revenue expenditure** and go to the
income statement. This applies **cost** and **accruals**: the capital cost is spread through depreciation, the
running costs are matched to the year they relate to.

**Worked example.** A delivery business buys a van.

| Item | £ | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Van price | 18 000 | Capital |
| Delivery to the business | 250 | Capital |
| Signwriting with the business name | 600 | Capital |
| First year's insurance | 900 | Revenue |
| Fuel for the first week | 80 | Revenue |

Capital expenditure (debit Vehicles) = £18 850. Revenue expenditure (income statement) = £980.

**Materiality** also applies: a £30 hole punch lasts years but is usually expensed, as depreciating it is not worth the effort.

### 6. Recording transactions in ledger accounts

**Duality** means every entry has a debit and a credit of equal value. **Business entity** decides whose
transaction it is. **Money measurement** decides whether it can be recorded at all.

**Worked example.** Three events at a sole trader's shop:

```
(a) Owner pays a £650 business electricity bill from a private bank account
    Dr Electricity 650   Cr Capital 650        (business entity: owner has put money in)
(b) Owner takes goods costing £120 for family use
    Dr Drawings 120      Cr Purchases 120      (business entity: not a business expense)
(c) A rival offers to hire the shop's best salesperson
    No entry                                    (money measurement: no reliable money value)
```

### 7. Goods sold on a sale or return basis

Goods sent on sale or return remain the seller's property until the customer accepts them (or keeps them past
the agreed time limit). Under **realisation**, no sale has happened yet. At the year end, any such goods not
yet accepted are removed from revenue and trade receivables and included in closing inventory at **cost**
(or NRV if lower, under prudence).

**Worked example.** A business sent goods with a selling price of £6 000 on sale or return and recorded them as
credit sales. The customer has not accepted them by the year end. Mark-up is 50% on cost. Draft profit is
£38 500.

```
Cost of the goods = 6 000 / 1.5 = £4 000
Revenue                    reduce by £6 000
Trade receivables          reduce by £6 000
Closing inventory          increase by £4 000
Profit change = -6 000 + 4 000 = -£2 000
Corrected profit = 38 500 - 2 000 = £36 500
```

The £2 000 fall is the unrealised profit that had been taken too early.

## When concepts pull in different directions

- **Prudence and accruals.** Accruals would spread a cost or income over periods. Prudence says recognise a
  foreseeable loss now. Where a loss is likely, prudence wins.
- **Cost and prudence.** Cost says show inventory at cost; prudence says show it at NRV if lower. The
  lower-of rule combines them.
- **Consistency and a fairer view.** Consistency resists change, but a business may change method if the new
  method gives a fairer picture. The reason should be explained to users.

## Common errors

- Naming a concept without saying what it does to profit or to a statement of financial position figure.
- Valuing inventory on the total of cost and the total of NRV, rather than item by item.
- Removing sale or return goods from revenue but not adding them back to closing inventory, or adding them back
  at selling price instead of cost.
- Capitalising running costs such as insurance or fuel, or expensing delivery and installation.
- Treating the owner's private spending as a business expense instead of drawings.
- Describing prudence as deliberately understating profit.
