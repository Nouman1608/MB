Learning and cognition is the fourth of the four contexts in the IB Psychology course for first assessment 2027, and it applies to both SL and HL students. In a context, you use the content (the biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches and research methodology) and the six concepts to understand a real-world area. The guide's sub-headings, "Thinking and learning" and "Cognitive processes", are for convenience and are not used to form examination questions. The class practical for this context is an **experiment**. At HL the culture, motivation and technology extensions have learning and cognition strands, which are covered on their own pages.

Related: [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-learning-and-cognition-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-learning-and-cognition-practice/).

## What the guide says the context is about

Humans are born with innate behaviours that cover only basic survival. Humans also have highly developed cognitive abilities, including **metacognition** (thinking about our own thinking). So we need to understand what cognition is, how it can be described and explained, how it develops and how reliable it is in different situations.

Learning ranges from simple to sophisticated.

- **Conditioning** is simpler learning that can work on overt behaviour without needing cognitive processes.
- Sophisticated learning uses **mental representations** (cognitive schemas).
- **Social learning** happens when people learn by observing others.
- **Thinking and decision-making** are probably the most complex processes. The **dual processing model** and **cognitive biases** are used to investigate them.

Other processes are **attention, perception, memory and language**. To investigate one, scientists build a model, study how biological, cultural and environmental factors affect it and then, if the model is valid, design improvements.

## Thinking and learning

The guide lists five areas of study with learning objectives.

### Cognitive biases

You need the role of one or more cognitive biases in decision-making. The content section names **anchoring bias** and **confirmation bias**.

- **Anchoring:** decisions are pulled towards an initial reference value, even an irrelevant one. In Tversky and Kahneman (1974), a wheel of fortune was rigged to stop at 10 or 65. Participants then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. The median estimate was about 25 for the group that saw 10 and about 45 for the group that saw 65. The wheel was irrelevant to the question, so the anchor must have shifted the estimate.
- **Confirmation bias:** seeking or favouring information that supports an existing belief. Wason's 2-4-6 task (1960) is the classic demonstration: participants tested only triples that fitted their own rule instead of trying to disprove it.

Both are explained by the dual processing model: fast, automatic judgements take shortcuts (heuristics) that sometimes produce systematic errors.

### Conditioning

You need examples of classical and operant conditioning as ways of learning, and how operant conditioning can change behaviour.

- **Classical conditioning:** learning by association. A neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus that triggers a conditioned response. Pavlov's dogs learned to salivate to a bell. Watson and Rayner (1920) conditioned a fear response in a baby ("Little Albert"), a study now cited mainly for its ethical problems.
- **Operant conditioning:** behaviour is shaped by its consequences. Positive and negative reinforcement increase behaviour; punishment decreases it. Skinner's work with animals is the classic base.
- **Application:** a token economy, praise for desired behaviour in a classroom, or points in a habit-tracking app use reinforcement to change behaviour. Evaluate by asking whether the change lasts when rewards stop and whether the rewards reduce intrinsic motivation.

### Dual processing model

The model proposes two modes of thinking: a fast, automatic, intuitive mode and a slow, effortful, analytic mode (Kahneman calls them System 1 and System 2). It is valuable because it explains why people are usually efficient but sometimes biased, and it predicts when each mode will dominate (under time pressure or tiredness the fast mode takes over). A limitation is that the "two systems" are descriptive labels. It is hard to measure which system produced a specific decision.

### Schema theory

A schema is an organised mental framework built from experience that guides perception, memory and behaviour. Bartlett (1932) asked British participants to recall a Native American folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts", repeatedly. Recall became shorter and details changed to fit the participants' own cultural expectations. Loftus and Palmer (1974) is also explained by schema and reconstruction. Schema help us process information efficiently but can distort memory. Evaluation points are that the concept is flexible but vague and hard to measure directly.

### Social learning theory

Bandura proposed that people learn by observing models, through attention, retention, reproduction and motivation. In the Bobo doll studies (1961), children who saw an adult act aggressively towards the doll copied the aggression more than children who saw a non-aggressive model. Application: using role models in anti-bullying or health campaigns. Evaluation: strong experimental control but low ecological validity, and the ethics of exposing children to aggression.

## Cognitive processes

The guide asks you to study **one or more** of attention, memory, perception or language, and apply six areas of study.

| Area | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Biological factors | Explain the role of one or more biological factors in one or more cognitive processes |
| Cognitive models | Explain the value of models and **compare two models** of one process |
| Cultural factors | Explain the role of one or more cultural factors in one process |
| Environmental influences | Explain the possible influence of one or more environmental factors on one process |
| Potential for improving | Describe one or more strategies to improve one or more processes |

Because the guide lets you choose, a good approach is to build all five areas around **memory**, then check you can swap in another process.

**Biological factors.** Maguire et al. (2000) used MRI to compare London taxi drivers with controls and found a larger posterior hippocampus in the taxi drivers, with volume related to years of experience. This links spatial memory to a brain structure and to neuroplasticity, but the design is correlational, so it cannot show which came first.

**Cognitive models.** The multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968) proposes sensory memory, short-term memory and long-term memory linked by attention and rehearsal. The working memory model (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) replaces the single short-term store with a central executive and slave systems (the phonological loop and visuospatial sketchpad; the episodic buffer was added in 2000). A comparison should state where they differ (one short-term store versus several components), where they agree (limited capacity, transfer to long-term memory), and which explains more (the working memory model explains dual-task findings; the multi-store model explains primacy and recency effects well but oversimplifies rehearsal).

**Cultural factors.** Masuda and Nisbett (2001) showed Japanese and American students animated underwater scenes. Japanese participants tended to describe more of the background and relationships, while Americans described the focal fish. Japanese participants were also more affected when the focal fish was shown against a new background in a recognition task. The authors explained this as holistic versus analytic thinking. It links to the sociocultural approach but it is a lab study using students, so generalisation to whole cultures is limited.

**Environmental influences.** Loftus and Palmer (1974): 45 students watched seven films of traffic accidents and estimated the speed. The verb "smashed" gave a higher mean estimate (about 41 mph) than "hit" (about 34 mph). In a second experiment, a week later, 32 per cent of the "smashed" group said they had seen broken glass, against 14 per cent of the "hit" group. The wording of the question is an environmental influence on memory. Cognitive explanation: reconstructive memory. Limitation: the film was not as emotional as a real crash.

**Potential for improving.** Strategies include mnemonics, elaborative rehearsal, spaced practice (spreading learning over time), retrieval practice (testing yourself) and, for eyewitnesses, the cognitive interview. Evaluate each: what evidence supports it and does it transfer outside the laboratory?

## The class practical: an experiment

Table 5 of the guide requires an **experiment (true or quasi-)** in this context, with a minimum of five participants for a student-led practical. The guide suggests these as examples only: Loftus and Palmer (1974), Masuda and Nisbett (2001), Tversky and Kahneman (1974) and the music and memory (Mozart effect) work of Rauscher et al. You may choose a different experiment as long as it relates to learning and cognition. Remember that Paper 2 Section A asks about the research method, not the topic.

For the Mozart effect, the well-known first report was in 1993 (Rauscher, Shaw and Ky) and found a short-lived improvement in spatial reasoning after listening to Mozart; later attempts to replicate it were mixed. This is a good example of replication and publication bias.

## Linking the concepts

Choose three to four concepts for this context. Good choices are:

- **Causality:** experiments with controls give stronger causal claims about memory than correlational brain studies.
- **Bias:** cognitive biases (anchoring, confirmation) in participants and in researchers; sampling bias when most participants are students.
- **Measurement:** operationalising "memory" (recall versus recognition) changes results.
- **Perspective:** biological, cognitive and cultural accounts of the same process.
- **Responsibility:** ethics of misleading information in memory studies and of memory interventions.
- **Change:** strategies to improve cognition and whether they last.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a study without saying which learning objective it supports.
- Mixing two cognitive models without stating which is which in a comparison.
- Saying conditioning "explains all learning". The guide separates conditioning from more sophisticated learning.
- Forgetting evaluation: ecological validity, sampling, replication.
- Ignoring the experiment practical when answering Paper 2.
