These questions cover sub-topic 4.9, Barriers to economic growth and/or economic development, from the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. Countries and figures are invented, and the [1] points in the answers are a revision aid, not IB marks. Revise first with the [4.9 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-4-9/) or the [4.9 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-4-9-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define *capital flight*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by *appropriate technology*. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why being landlocked can be a barrier to economic growth. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** parts of a weak institutional framework. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why rising inequality may be a barrier to economic development even when GDP is growing. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how corruption can reduce inward investment. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Draw a poverty cycle diagram and explain how it can keep a country poor. **[4]**

**8.** The fictional country of Delvin earns 9.0 billion delvs a year from exports, of which raw cotton makes up 80%. Its annual debt service is 2.7 billion delvs.

**(a)** Calculate Delvin's debt service ratio. **[1]**

**(b)** The world price of raw cotton falls by 25%, with export volumes unchanged. Calculate the new value of exports and the new debt service ratio. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain how the combination of primary dependence and indebtedness can limit Delvin's development. **[3]**

**9.** In Delvin, recorded GDP is 20 billion delvs and informal output is estimated at a further 40% of recorded GDP. The average tax rate is 15%.

**(a)** Calculate the extra revenue if informal output were taxed at the average rate. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason, other than tax, why a large informal economy limits growth. **[2]**

**10.** Delvin has 4 million working-age women. Their labour force participation rate is 35%. Calculate the increase in the labour force if it rose to 50%, and explain one other way that reducing gender inequality could promote development. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how insecure property rights and a weak banking system can together trap a household in poverty. **[4]**

**12.** Using the information about Delvin, evaluate which barrier is the most significant obstacle to its growth and development. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Large outflows of savings and investment funds from a country [1], often because of political risk, fear of inflation or a weak currency [1].

**2.** Technology suited to a country's skills, costs and conditions [1], for example simple equipment that can be maintained locally rather than machinery needing imported parts and specialist engineers [1].

**3.** Exports and imports must cross neighbouring countries, adding transport costs and delays [1], which makes exports less competitive and imported inputs dearer, and gives neighbours bargaining power over trade routes [1].

**4.** Any two: an unreliable legal system [1]; ineffective taxation structures [1]; a weak banking system; insecure property rights.

**5.** If the gains from growth go mainly to the richest households, most people see little improvement in income, health or education [1], and the poor may be unable to afford schooling or start businesses, so opportunities stay limited [1].

**6.** Bribes and unpredictable demands raise costs and risks for foreign firms [1], so investors choose other countries or keep their funds out, reducing FDI [1].

**7.** Diagram with four labelled links in a loop: low income, low saving and tax revenue, low investment in physical and human capital, low productivity [1]. Low income leaves little to save or tax [1], so little is invested in machinery, schools and clinics [1]. Productivity stays low, so incomes stay low and the cycle repeats unless something breaks a link [1].

**8. (a)** 2.7 ÷ 9.0 × 100 = **30%** [1].

**(b)** Cotton exports = 0.8 × 9.0 = 7.2 billion; after the fall 7.2 × 0.75 = 5.4 billion [1]. New exports = 9.0 - 7.2 + 5.4 = **7.2 billion delvs** [1]. New debt service ratio = 2.7 ÷ 7.2 × 100 = **37.5%** [1].

**(c)** A single price fall cuts export earnings sharply because cotton dominates exports [1]. Debt service then absorbs a larger share of earnings, leaving less foreign currency for imports of capital goods and medicines [1]. The government may cut spending on health and education to keep paying, which slows improvements in human capital and living standards [1].

**9. (a)** Informal output = 0.4 × 20 = 8 billion delvs [1]; extra revenue = 0.15 × 8 = **1.2 billion delvs** [1].

**(b)** Informal firms cannot use formal credit or legal contracts, so they stay small [1] and miss economies of scale and investment in better technology, limiting productivity growth [1].

**10.** Workers rise from 0.35 × 4 = 1.4 million to 0.50 × 4 = 2.0 million, an increase of **0.6 million** [1] [1]. Other benefit: educated women tend to invest more in their children's health and schooling, raising future human capital (or any valid development benefit) [1].

**11.** Without registered title to land or a home, a household cannot offer collateral [1]. A weak banking system will not lend without collateral, or may not reach poor and rural areas at all [1]. The household cannot borrow to invest in productive assets such as tools or irrigation [1], so its income stays low, saving stays low, and it remains in the poverty trap [1].

**12.** Primary dependence: cotton is 80% of exports, so a 25% price fall cuts exports by a fifth and raises the debt service ratio to 37.5% [1]. Indebtedness: debt service already takes 30% of export earnings, limiting imports of capital goods and public spending [1]. These two are urgent, because one price shock damages both export earnings and the public finances [1]. Informal economy: untaxed output worth 8 billion means 1.2 billion of possible revenue is lost, nearly half of a year's debt service [1]. Gender inequality: participation of 35% wastes the skills of millions of women, affecting long-run growth and development [1]. Weak institutions may be the root cause of informality and the reliance on borrowing, and can be reformed, although slowly [1]. Some barriers, such as debt, can be eased quickly with outside help, while gender inequality and institutions take a generation [1]. Judgement: the dependence on cotton combined with debt is the most urgent barrier, but a narrow tax base rooted in weak institutions is arguably the most significant, because it explains both the informality and the need to borrow [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating the debt service ratio with GDP instead of exports.
- Listing barriers in an evaluation answer without using the data given.
- Treating informality only as a tax problem.
- Reaching no judgement about which barrier matters most.
