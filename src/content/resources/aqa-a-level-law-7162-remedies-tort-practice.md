These questions test the section 3.3 row on remedies: compensatory damages for physical injury, property damage and economic loss, mitigation of loss and injunctions. All sums of money are invented for practice.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the aim of compensatory damages in tort. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the difference between pecuniary and non-pecuniary loss, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by special damages. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the difference between a prohibitory and a mandatory injunction. **[2]**

**5.** State the effect of a claimant's failure to mitigate their loss. **[2]**

**6.** Identify **two** reasons why a court may refuse an injunction even though a nuisance has been proved. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how the court assesses damages where property is damaged, using **one** case. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the principle of mitigation of loss, using **two** cases. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how the courts decide whether to award damages instead of an injunction, referring to *Shelfer* (1895) and *Coventry v Lawrence* (2014). **[5]**

**10.** Ivo, a self-employed plumber, is injured by a negligent driver. He cannot work for six months, losing £18,000 of earnings, and pays £1,200 for physiotherapy. He can no longer play rugby. Doctors say there is a small chance he will later develop arthritis in his knee. Explain the damages Ivo may claim. **[6]**

**11.** Wynn's car is damaged by a negligent driver. Repairs would cost £5,000; the car's market value was £3,000. Wynn has it repaired anyway and, while it is off the road for two weeks, takes taxis costing £900 instead of hiring a car for £350. Advise Wynn on the damages she can recover. **[5]**

**12.** Pell Ltd runs a quarry next to a village. Blasting causes vibration and dust that a court finds to be a private nuisance to nearby residents. The quarry employs 120 local people and has planning permission. Advise the residents on whether they are likely to obtain an injunction. **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** To put the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in if the tort had not been committed (*Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co*, 1880) [1].

**2.** Pecuniary loss is financial loss that can be expressed in money [1], such as lost earnings or medical costs [1]. Non-pecuniary loss is non-financial loss [1], such as pain and suffering or loss of amenity [1].

**3.** Special damages compensate losses that can be calculated precisely up to the date of trial [1], such as earnings already lost or receipts for treatment, and must be specifically pleaded and proved [1].

**4.** A prohibitory injunction orders the defendant to stop doing something, such as making noise [1]. A mandatory injunction orders the defendant to do something, such as remove an obstruction [1].

**5.** The claimant cannot recover for the part of the loss that reasonable steps would have avoided [1], although they may recover the reasonable cost of the steps they should have taken [1].

**6.** Any two from: damages would be an adequate remedy [1]; the claimant delayed or acted unfairly [1]; an injunction would be oppressive to the defendant; the public interest in the activity is strong (*Miller v Jackson*, 1977).

**7.** Damages aim to restore the claimant to the position before the tort [1]. Where property can be repaired at reasonable cost, the measure is the cost of repair [1]; where it is destroyed or repair is uneconomic, the measure is its market value or diminution in value [1]. Consequential losses such as hire charges are also recoverable, and in *Lagden v O'Connor* (2003) an impecunious claimant recovered the higher cost of credit hire [1].

**8.** A claimant must take reasonable steps to limit their loss [1] and cannot recover for loss those steps would have avoided [1]. In *Darbishire v Warran* (1963) the claimant repaired a car at a cost above its market value and could recover only the market value [1]. In *Geest plc v Lansiquot* (2002) the Privy Council held that the defendant bears the burden of proving a failure to mitigate [1]. The standard is not demanding, since the defendant created the situation, so the claimant need only act reasonably [1].

**9.** An injunction is discretionary, and the court has power to award damages instead under s50 Senior Courts Act 1981 [1]. In *Shelfer* (1895) A.L. Smith LJ said damages should replace an injunction only where the injury is small, capable of being estimated in money [1], adequately compensated by a small payment, and an injunction would be oppressive [1]. In *Coventry v Lawrence* (2014) the Supreme Court held that this working rule must not be applied mechanically [1]; the court has a wide discretion and may consider public benefit and planning permission [1].

**10.** **Special damages:** £18,000 lost earnings to trial [1] and £1,200 physiotherapy costs, as calculable pecuniary losses [1]. **General damages:** pain and suffering from the injury [1] and loss of amenity for no longer being able to play rugby [1]. Any future loss of earnings or care would be estimated by the court [1]. Because of the chance of arthritis, Ivo may ask for provisional damages under s32A Senior Courts Act 1981, so he can return to court if it develops [1].

**11.** Repair cost (£5,000) exceeds market value (£3,000), so a reasonable person would have replaced the car rather than repair it [1]; following *Darbishire v Warran* (1963) she can recover only £3,000 [1]. Loss of use is a consequential loss [1], but taxis at £900 were more than the £350 cost of hiring a car, so she failed to mitigate [1]. She can recover £350 for loss of use, giving £3,000 + £350 = £3,350 [1].

**12.** An injunction is equitable and discretionary, not automatic once a nuisance is proved [1]. The residents would seek a prohibitory injunction [1]. Against them, the quarry's economic value and local employment are a public interest that may be weighed (*Miller v Jackson*; *Coventry v Lawrence*) [1], and planning permission may be relevant to the remedy [1]. However, vibration and dust affect many homes and are not a "small" injury, so the *Shelfer* approach points towards an injunction [1]. The most likely result is a limited injunction restricting blasting times or dust levels, as in *Kennaway v Thompson* (1981), with damages for past harm [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using contract's expectation measure.
- Saying a claimant who fails to mitigate gets nothing.
- Treating an injunction as automatic.
- Forgetting loss of amenity as a head of general damages.
