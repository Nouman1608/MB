## Questions

**1.** Define the term decision making. **[2]**

**2.** Stoneyfold Bakery is considering three decisions. Identify whether each is strategic, tactical or operational: (i) opening a production site in a new country; (ii) planning next month's discount offer; (iii) setting tomorrow's staff rota. **[3]**

**3.** Distinguish between quantitative and qualitative decision-making tools, giving one example of each. **[4]**

**4.** Larchmoor Print Works has an objective of raising its market share from 8% to 12% within three years. Explain how this objective should influence its decisions. **[4]**

**5.** Larchmoor must choose between Option A, a large press, and Option B, a second-hand press. Option A costs £70,000, with a 50% chance of a £180,000 payoff and a 50% chance of £60,000. Option B costs £30,000, with an 80% chance of £95,000 and a 20% chance of £40,000.

**(a)** Calculate the net gain of each option. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate which option Larchmoor should choose. **[4]**

**6.** Ferrowmoor Fitness can open a new studio at a cost of £100,000. It estimates a 25% chance of a £240,000 payoff, a 45% chance of £120,000 and a 30% chance of £20,000.

**(a)** Calculate the expected value and the net gain. **[3]**
**(b)** New research suggests the probabilities are 15%, 40% and 45%. Recalculate the net gain and interpret the change. **[3]**

**7.** A business uses a sales forecast to decide whether to launch a product. Explain how (i) inaccurate forecasts and (ii) bias among the managers could each lead to a poor decision. **[4]**

**8.** Stoneyfold Bakery's sales revenue rose from £450,000 to £480,000. Its net profit fell from £36,000 to £28,800. Its customer satisfaction score fell from 91% to 82%. Evaluate the usefulness of financial and non-financial measures in judging how well Stoneyfold performed. **[6]**

**9.** Explain the purpose of a decision tree and why the information it shows matters to a lender. **[4]**

**10.** Corriemoor Garden Centre has savings of £130,000 and two options. Option X, extending the café, costs £60,000, with a 60% chance of a £150,000 payoff and a 40% chance of £50,000. Option Y, opening a second site, costs £110,000, with a 35% chance of £320,000 and a 65% chance of £40,000. The forecast for Y rests on one customer survey. The owner values keeping close control of the business.

**(a)** Calculate the net gain of each option. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate which option Corriemoor should choose. **[6]**

**11.** Marshwick Tiles set objectives of 15% sales growth, a 10% net profit margin and staff turnover below 10%. It achieved sales growth of 18%, a net profit margin of 6% and staff turnover of 22%. Customer satisfaction rose. Evaluate the extent to which Marshwick can be considered a success. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Choosing between alternative courses of action [1] in order to achieve the aims and objectives of the business [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both the choice between options and the purpose; "making choices" alone is thin.

**2.** (i) Strategic: whole-business direction, costly and hard to reverse [1]. (ii) Tactical: delivers strategy over a short period in one function [1]. (iii) Operational: day-to-day running [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* One-word identifications are sufficient here, but a brief reason protects you if you choose the wrong label for a borderline case.

**3.** Quantitative tools use numerical data to compare options [1], for example expected value from a decision tree [1]. Qualitative tools use judgement and non-numerical evidence [1], for example staff opinion on a relocation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Make each example match its definition. A numeric technique offered as a qualitative example undermines the distinction.

**4.** The objective sets the criteria for judging options [1]. Larchmoor should favour options that add customers and capacity, such as a larger press [1], even if short-term profit is lower [1]. Later results can then be compared with the 12% target to judge the decision [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers keep returning to the stated objective and figures rather than describing objectives in general.

**5. (a)** Option A: EV = 0.5 x 180,000 + 0.5 x 60,000 = £120,000 [1]; net gain = 120,000 - 70,000 = £50,000 [1]. Option B: EV = 0.8 x 95,000 + 0.2 x 40,000 = £84,000 [1]; net gain = 84,000 - 30,000 = £54,000 [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** B has the higher net gain, but only by £4,000, so the numbers alone barely separate the options [1]. A could lose £10,000 (60,000 - 70,000) but could gain £110,000, whereas B always gains, between £10,000 and £65,000 [1]. Larchmoor's market share objective favours A's capacity, while limited resources favour B's lower risk [1]. Recommend B if cash is tight, or A if Larchmoor can absorb the loss and growth is the priority, stating that condition [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* When two net gains are close, credit usually comes from using risk, objectives and qualitative factors to break the tie rather than repeating the calculation.

**6. (a)** EV = 0.25 x 240,000 + 0.45 x 120,000 + 0.30 x 20,000 = 60,000 + 54,000 + 6,000 [1] = £120,000 [1]. Net gain = 120,000 - 100,000 = £20,000 [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** New EV = 0.15 x 240,000 + 0.40 x 120,000 + 0.45 x 20,000 = 36,000 + 48,000 + 9,000 = £93,000 [1]. Net gain = 93,000 - 100,000 = -£7,000 [1]. A small change in probabilities turns a profitable studio into a loss-making one, so the decision is sensitive to these estimates and Ferrowmoor should gather better evidence before committing [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Interpreting what the changed figure means for the decision is usually where the final mark sits, not in the arithmetic.

**7.** (i) If the forecast is inaccurate, the payoffs and probabilities are wrong, so the tree or budget gives a precise-looking but misleading answer [1], and the business may launch a loss-making product [1]. (ii) Bias, for example managers favouring their own project, may lead them to inflate forecasts or ignore warning evidence [1], so a weaker option is chosen over a better one [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs its own consequence for the decision. Defining bias without saying how it distorts the choice tends to earn less.

**8.** Financial: net margin fell from 8.0% to 6.0% (36,000 / 450,000 and 28,800 / 480,000) [1], so the business earns less profit per pound of sales despite revenue growing 6.7% [1]. Non-financial: satisfaction fell nine points, which the accounts do not show [1] and may signal lost future sales [1]. Financial measures are objective but backward-looking; non-financial measures can warn earlier but are harder to benchmark [1]. Judgement: both point to weaker performance, and the satisfaction fall suggests the profit decline may continue unless costs or service are addressed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculating the margin and then using it in the argument is usually rewarded more than quoting the raw profit figures.

**9.** A decision tree sets out options, outcomes and probabilities so choices under risk can be compared by expected value [1], making the assumptions behind a decision visible [1]. A lender wants to know whether the business can repay, so the tree shows the likely return [1] and the size of the downside in the worst case [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the stakeholder to something specific on the tree, such as the worst-case outcome, instead of saying the tree "helps everyone".

**10. (a)** X: EV = 0.6 x 150,000 + 0.4 x 50,000 = £110,000 [1]; net gain = £50,000 [1]. Y: EV = 0.35 x 320,000 + 0.65 x 40,000 = £138,000 [1]; net gain = £28,000 [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** X has the higher net gain (£50,000 against £28,000) [1]. Y has a higher EV and a larger upside, a £210,000 gain, but a 65% chance of a £70,000 loss, while X's worst case is a £10,000 loss [1]. That Y loss would use more than half of the £130,000 savings [1]. Y's forecast rests on one survey, so its probabilities are less reliable [1]. The owner's wish for control also favours the simpler extension [1]. Recommend X, provided the café extension does not itself depend on optimistic demand assumptions, and revisit Y if better evidence emerges [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A balanced answer weighs both options and uses the case facts (savings, one survey, owner's preference) to reach a conditional judgement.

**11.** Growth objective met: 18% against 15% [1]. Profit objective missed: 6% against 10% [1]. Staff turnover objective missed: 22% against below 10% [1]. Customer satisfaction rising is a positive non-financial result [1]. Owners may value growth, but staff and shareholders may judge the business on turnover and profit respectively [1]. Overall it is only partly successful: growth and customers are strong, but profitability and staff retention fall short, and the profit shortfall matters most if survival or returns are the priority [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that compare each result with its own objective, then reach a judgement of degree, tend to do better than a general verdict of success or failure.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stopping at expected value without subtracting the cost of the option.
- Probabilities that do not total 1.0 (100%) at a chance node.
- Defining strategic, tactical and operational by time alone, ignoring level and scope.
- Giving a qualitative example that is really a number, or the reverse.
- Listing limits of decision trees without applying them to the case business.
- Recommending the highest EV without discussing risk, savings, objectives or forecast reliability.
- Judging success from profit alone, ignoring non-financial measures and different stakeholders.
- Conclusions that offer "it depends" without saying on what.
