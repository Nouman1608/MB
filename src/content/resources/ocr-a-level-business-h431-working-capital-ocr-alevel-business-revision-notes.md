## What the specification lists

- Explain what is meant by working capital.
- Explain what is meant by the working capital cycle.

The wording is short, so questions are likely to combine a calculation with a short explanation or evaluation.

## Core recall

### Working capital

- **Working capital = current assets − current liabilities.**
- Current assets: inventory, receivables, cash (usable within a year).
- Current liabilities: payables, overdraft, short-term loans, tax due (payable within a year).
- It is money available to run the business day to day.
- It is not the same as cash: most may be tied up in inventory and receivables.
- Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities.

### The working capital cycle

The time between paying for stock or materials and receiving cash from customers.

1. Cash paid for materials (or credit taken).
2. Materials held as inventory.
3. Production turns materials into goods.
4. Finished goods held.
5. Goods sold, usually on credit (a receivable).
6. Customer pays; cash returns.

### Length of the cycle

| Item | Calculation |
|---|---|
| Inventory days | Inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365 |
| Receivable days | Receivables ÷ revenue × 365 |
| Payable days | Payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 |
| Cycle length | Inventory days + receivable days − payable days |

### Shortening the cycle

| Action | Risk |
|---|---|
| Hold less inventory | Stock-outs, lost sales |
| Collect from customers faster | Annoyed customers, discounts needed |
| Pay suppliers later | Damaged relationships, lost discounts, supply risk |

### Growth and working capital

- More sales mean more inventory and receivables, so more cash is tied up.
- A growing, profitable business can run out of cash if working capital is not funded (over-trading).

### Too much or too little

- Too little: cannot pay bills on time; reliance on overdraft.
- Too much: idle money earns nothing.
- The right level depends on the industry (a supermarket needs less than a made-to-order manufacturer).

### Evaluation points

Liquidity against profit, source of finance, the type of business, effect on suppliers, customers and lenders.

## Quick worked reminder

Fenn Furniture (invented): current assets £270,000 (inventory £120,000, receivables £135,000, cash £15,000), current liabilities £100,000 (payables £60,000, overdraft £40,000). Working capital £170,000; current ratio 2.7. With revenue £1,095,000 and cost of sales £730,000: inventory 60 days, receivables 45 days, payables 30 days; cycle 75 days. Cutting to 45, 36 and 40 days frees £77,000 and shortens the cycle to 41 days.

## Method checklists

### Working capital

1. List the current assets: inventory, receivables, cash.
2. List the current liabilities: payables, overdraft, short-term loans, tax due.
3. Add each list and subtract.
4. Give the current ratio if asked: current assets divided by current liabilities.
5. Comment: is the figure positive, and how much of it is cash?

### Cycle length in days

1. Inventory days from inventory and cost of sales.
2. Receivable days from receivables and revenue.
3. Payable days from payables and cost of sales.
4. Add the first two, subtract the third.
5. To find cash released by a change in days, multiply the new days by the daily figure (revenue or cost of sales divided by 365) and subtract from the old balance.

## Short written answers

- **Explain working capital:** define it, give the formula, name two current assets and two current liabilities, and say why it matters (paying day-to-day bills).
- **Explain the cycle:** name the stages in order, link them to the business in the case, and say what makes the cycle longer or shorter.
- **Evaluate a change:** state the cash effect with a figure, give one risk, and say whether the benefit outweighs it for this type of business.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Working capital | Current assets minus current liabilities |
| Current asset | An asset expected to become cash within a year |
| Current liability | A debt due within a year |
| Inventory | Stock held for use or sale |
| Receivables | Money owed by customers |
| Payables | Money owed to suppliers |
| Working capital cycle | Time from paying for stock to receiving customers' cash |
| Over-trading | Growing faster than working capital allows |
| Liquidity | Ability to pay short-term debts |

## Exam traps

- Working capital is not the same as cash.
- Use current items only; leave out non-current assets and long-term loans.
- Payables days are subtracted when finding the cycle length.
- A longer cycle needs more finance.
- Paying suppliers late has costs; mention them.

## Self-test

1. Write the formula for working capital.
2. Give two examples of current assets.
3. Give two examples of current liabilities.
4. Current assets £75,000; current liabilities £43,000. Calculate working capital.
5. Calculate the current ratio.
6. Explain what the working capital cycle is.
7. Inventory £18,000; cost of sales £365,000. Calculate inventory days.
8. Receivable days 30, inventory days 18, payable days 25. Calculate the cycle length.
9. Give one way of shortening the cycle and one risk.
10. Why can fast growth cause a cash problem?
11. Why is working capital not the same as cash?
12. Give one problem of having too much working capital.

### Answers

1. Working capital = current assets − current liabilities.
2. Any two: inventory, receivables, cash.
3. Any two: payables, overdraft, short-term loans, tax due.
4. £75,000 − £43,000 = **£32,000**.
5. £75,000 ÷ £43,000 = **1.74** (to two decimal places).
6. The time between paying for materials or stock and receiving cash from customers for the goods sold.
7. 18,000 ÷ 365,000 × 365 = **18 days**.
8. 18 + 30 − 25 = **23 days**.
9. Collect from customers faster; the risk is that customers are annoyed or want a discount. (Other answers possible.)
10. More sales need more inventory and more credit to customers, so cash is tied up before customers pay.
11. Much of it may be tied up in inventory and receivables that cannot be spent at once.
12. Money in excess stock or idle cash earns no return and could be used elsewhere.
