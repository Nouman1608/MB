This guide goes deeper into content item 1.2.3, Market segmentation, in Topic 1.2 Spotting a business opportunity of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022, part of Theme 1 (Paper 1, 1BS0/01). The broader [Spotting a business opportunity study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-spotting-a-business-opportunity-and-customer-needs/) defines the five bases and shows a price-quality map; this page shows how segmentation changes a business's decisions, how to compare segments by their value, and how to choose axes and judge what a market map can and cannot show. All businesses and figures are invented.

Companion pages: the [1.2.3 revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-market-segmentation-revision-notes/) and the [1.2.3 practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-market-segmentation-practice/).

## What 1.2.3 asks you to know

The heading is "How businesses use market segmentation to target customers".

| Strand | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| Identifying segments | location, demographics, lifestyle, income, age |
| Market mapping | market mapping to identify a gap in the market and the competition |

## Part 1: why segment at all?

A market contains customers with very different needs. A business that tries to please everyone usually pleases nobody very well, and spends money promoting to people who will never buy. Segmenting lets a small business:

- **target** the group whose needs it can meet best;
- **design** the product, price and promotion for that group;
- **spend less** on promotion that reaches the wrong people;
- **spot** groups whose needs are poorly met by existing businesses.

## Part 2: the five bases and what each changes

| Basis | What it groups by | Decisions it shapes (invented examples) |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Where customers live, work or travel | A bakery delivers only within three miles; a ski-hire shop opens near mountains |
| Demographics | Population characteristics such as gender, family size, occupation or ethnicity | A cleaning firm offers packages for large families; a uniform shop targets nurses |
| Lifestyle | How people live, their interests and values | A shop stocks camping gear for outdoor enthusiasts; a café offers vegan food |
| Income | How much customers earn or can spend | A hotel runs a budget brand and a luxury brand at different prices |
| Age | Which age group | A dance school runs toddler classes and adult beginner classes at different times |

Age is listed separately from demographics in the specification, even though it is a demographic feature. Use the specification's five headings in your answers.

Bases are often **combined** to describe a target market precisely: "working parents aged 30 to 45, living within five miles, on middle incomes" uses age, demographics, location and income together. The more precise the target, the easier it is to design for it, but the smaller the segment becomes.

## Part 3: comparing segments by value

The biggest segment is not always the best target. A business should compare how many people in each segment are likely to buy and how much each will spend.

### Worked example: Copperleaf Fitness

Copperleaf plans a small gym in a town of 32,000 adults. Its research estimates:

| Segment | People | Share interested | Expected monthly spend each |
|---|---|---|---|
| Students aged 18 to 24 | 4,800 | 15% | £20 |
| Young professionals aged 25 to 39 | 7,200 | 10% | £45 |
| Retired people aged 65 and over | 6,400 | 6% | £30 |

Interested people = people × share interested; monthly value = interested people × spend.

| Segment | Interested people | Monthly value |
|---|---|---|
| Students | 4,800 × 15% = 720 | 720 × £20 = £14,400 |
| Young professionals | 7,200 × 10% = 720 | 720 × £45 = £32,400 |
| Retired | 6,400 × 6% = 384 | 384 × £30 = £11,520 |

Students and young professionals have the same number of interested people, but young professionals are worth more than twice as much a month (£32,400 against £14,400). Targeting them would mean early-morning and evening opening, premium equipment and promotion through workplaces. The retired segment is smallest by value, but daytime classes for it would use the gym when professionals are at work, so it could be a useful second target.

The figures are estimates from research, so their reliability should be checked (1.2.2).

## Part 4: market mapping in more depth

A **market map** is a diagram with two axes, each showing a feature customers care about. Existing businesses or products are placed on it according to where they sit on each feature. The specification says a map is used to identify **a gap in the market** and **the competition**.

### Choosing the axes
Price and quality are common, but the best axes are the features that matter most to the customers in that market. Other pairs include:
- basic or luxury, and traditional or modern (for a restaurant);
- low or high convenience, and narrow or wide choice (for a food shop);
- for children or for adults, and active or relaxing (for leisure activities).

### Worked example: pizza takeaways in Dunbridge

A new owner rates six local pizza takeaways from 1 to 10 on price (1 = cheap, 10 = expensive) and on how healthy the menu is (1 = not healthy, 10 = very healthy).

| Takeaway | Price score | Healthy score |
|---|---|---|
| Slice Express | 2 | 2 |
| Big Dough | 3 | 1 |
| Napoli Nights | 4 | 3 |
| Crust Club | 3 | 2 |
| Verde Kitchen | 9 | 8 |
| Olive and Ember | 8 | 6 |

Reading the map in words:
- **Competition:** four takeaways crowd the cheap, less healthy area (price 2 to 4, healthy 1 to 3). A new business there faces strong competition, probably on price.
- **Two higher-priced, healthier rivals** sit at price 8 to 9.
- **Gap:** nobody offers a healthy menu at a low or mid price (price around 4 to 6, healthy 7 or more).

The owner must still test whether customers want that position. Healthy ingredients may cost more, which could make a mid-price hard to hold, and the gap might exist simply because demand is low.

### What a market map cannot do
- Positions are **judgements**, often by the owner, not measured facts.
- A gap shows **missing supply, not proven demand**.
- It shows only **two features**; customers may care about others, such as location.
- It is a **snapshot**; rivals can move or new ones can arrive.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the largest segment as automatically the best target.
- Using "demographics" to mean only age; the specification lists age separately.
- Saying a gap on a map proves customers will buy.
- Choosing axes that customers do not care about.
- Describing segmentation without saying how the business would change its product, price or promotion.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate:** segment sizes and values; show the multiplication steps.
- **Identify:** a gap or a crowded area from a map or table.
- **Explain:** one benefit of segmentation, developed to sales or costs.
- **Analyse:** link a segment's features to the business's decisions.
- **Justify / Evaluate:** choose a target segment using the case figures, and note the limits of the research or the map.
