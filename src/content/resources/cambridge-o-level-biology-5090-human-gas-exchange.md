## Gas exchange surfaces

Gas exchange is the movement of oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out of it. It happens by **diffusion**. Any good exchange surface has the same features.

| Feature | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Large surface area | More molecules can cross at the same time |
| Thin surface (one cell thick) | Short diffusion distance |
| Good blood supply | Blood carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, keeping the concentration gradient steep |
| Good air supply (ventilation) | Fresh air brings oxygen and removes carbon dioxide, keeping the gradient steep |

In humans, the exchange surface is the lining of the **alveoli**.

## The gas exchange system

Air passes along this route when we breathe in:

**nose or mouth → larynx → trachea → bronchi (one to each lung) → bronchioles → alveoli**

- The **larynx** (voice box) is at the top of the trachea.
- The **trachea** (windpipe) is held open by rings of cartilage, which stop it collapsing when pressure falls.
- The two **bronchi** (singular bronchus) branch from the trachea, one into each **lung**.
- The bronchi divide into smaller **bronchioles**.
- Each bronchiole ends in clusters of **alveoli**, which are tiny air sacs.

Each alveolus is surrounded by a network of **capillaries**.

## The alveoli

The alveoli are the site of gas exchange. Millions of alveoli give the lungs a huge surface area.

**Characteristics of the alveolar surface**

- **Large surface area**: very many tiny alveoli.
- **Thin walls**: the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is very short.
- **Moist lining**: gases dissolve in the thin film of water before diffusing.
- **Good blood supply**: a dense network of capillaries.
- **Good ventilation**: breathing keeps bringing fresh air, so the oxygen concentration in the alveoli stays higher than in the blood.

**Role in gas exchange**: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli, across the walls, into the blood in the capillaries. Carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveolar air and is breathed out.

## Composition of air

| Gas | Inspired (atmospheric) air | Expired air |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | about 78% | about 78% |
| Oxygen | about 21% | about 16% |
| Carbon dioxide | about 0.04% | about 4% |
| Water vapour | variable, usually lower | higher (saturated) |

The figures for expired air are approximate and vary from person to person. The oxygen percentage falls because oxygen is absorbed into the blood and used in respiration. The carbon dioxide percentage rises because carbon dioxide is released by respiration and passes from the blood into the alveoli. Expired air contains more water vapour because the moist alveolar surface evaporates and respiration also produces water. It is also warmer.

### Worked example: comparing the gases

*Compare the carbon dioxide in inspired and expired air.* Expired air contains 4.0%, inspired air 0.04%. 4.0 divided by 0.04 = **100**, so expired air has about **100 times** as much carbon dioxide.

*By how much does the oxygen percentage change?* 21 - 16 = **5 percentage points**. As a percentage of the original, 5 divided by 21 x 100 = **23.8 per cent less** oxygen.

### Investigating inspired and expired air

- **Limewater** goes cloudy (milky) when carbon dioxide is bubbled through it. Breathe in through one tube and out through another, with limewater in each flask. The limewater in the expired air flask goes cloudy faster, which shows that expired air contains more carbon dioxide.
- **Hydrogencarbonate indicator** changes from red-orange to yellow in a higher concentration of carbon dioxide, so it can be used in the same way.
- **Cobalt chloride paper** turns from blue to pink with water vapour; breathing on it shows that expired air contains water.
- **A thermometer** shows that expired air is warmer than inspired air.

## Breathing: ribs, intercostal muscles and diaphragm

Air moves in and out because the **volume** of the **thorax** (chest) changes. When the volume changes, the **pressure** in the lungs changes, and air moves from high pressure to low pressure.

The ribs form a cage around the thorax. Between the ribs are the **external intercostal muscles** and the **internal intercostal muscles**. Under the lungs is the **diaphragm**, a sheet of muscle.

| | Inhaling (breathing in) | Exhaling (breathing out) |
|---|---|---|
| External intercostal muscles | Contract | Relax |
| Internal intercostal muscles | Relax | Contract (in forced breathing out) |
| Rib cage | Moves up and out | Moves down and in |
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens | Relaxes and returns to a dome shape |
| Volume of thorax | Increases | Decreases |
| Pressure in the thorax | Falls below atmospheric pressure | Rises above atmospheric pressure |
| Air movement | Into the lungs | Out of the lungs |

In quiet breathing, breathing out is mostly passive. The external intercostals and the diaphragm relax and the elastic lungs recoil. In vigorous exercise, the internal intercostals contract and pull the ribs down hard, forcing air out.

A strong answer on breathing links four ideas in order: **muscle action, change in volume, change in pressure, movement of air.**

## The effect of exercise on breathing

During exercise, muscle cells respire faster, so they use more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide. The extra carbon dioxide in the blood is detected by the brain, which increases the **rate** (breaths per minute) and **depth** (volume of each breath) of breathing. This brings more oxygen and removes the extra carbon dioxide.

**Investigation**: count the number of breaths in one minute at rest, then after two minutes of exercise, then at intervals during recovery. Rate and depth both rise and then fall back to resting levels.

### Worked example: breathing data

*At rest, a person takes 14 breaths per minute with a volume of 0.5 dm cubed per breath. After exercise, the person takes 32 breaths per minute, with 1.2 dm cubed per breath. Compare the volume of air breathed per minute.*

1. At rest = 14 x 0.5 = **7.0 dm cubed per minute**.
2. After exercise = 32 x 1.2 = **38.4 dm cubed per minute**.
3. Ratio = 38.4 divided by 7.0 = **5.5 times** as much.
4. **Explanation**: exercise increases the muscle cells' demand for oxygen and the production of carbon dioxide, so the breathing rate and depth increase.

## Protecting the gas exchange system

The air we breathe contains dust, bacteria and viruses. The lining of the trachea and bronchi has two cell types that protect the lungs.

- **Goblet cells** make **mucus**. The mucus is sticky and **traps dust and pathogens**.
- **Ciliated cells** have tiny hair-like projections called **cilia**. The cilia **beat in waves** to move the mucus upwards, away from the lungs, to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out.

Smoking damages the cilia and can lead to a build-up of mucus, with more infection (see subtopic 12.1).

## Common mistakes

- Saying that the lungs "pump" air. The muscles change the volume of the thorax.
- Writing that the diaphragm moves up when we breathe in. It flattens and moves down.
- Describing the air in the alveoli as "full of oxygen" or expired air as "carbon dioxide only". Expired air still contains about 16% oxygen.
- Forgetting the pressure step in explanations of breathing.
- Writing that goblet cells have cilia, or that cilia make mucus.
- Saying that the alveoli are "one cell" rather than "one cell thick".
- Saying that bronchioles are held open by cartilage rings (the trachea is).

## Command words

- **Identify**: name the structure on a diagram.
- **Describe**: give the features of an exchange surface or the stages of breathing.
- **Explain**: give the reason, linking volume and pressure.
- **Investigate**: describe or interpret the limewater, indicator or exercise experiment.
- **State**: give the percentage of a gas.
