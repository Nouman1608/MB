## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must do |
|---|---|
| Explain why countries import and export goods and services and the benefits of this for consumers and producers | Give reasons for trade in both directions and explain who gains and how |
| Explain free trade agreements including the European Union | Define a free trade agreement, describe the EU, and weigh the effects |

## Imports, exports, goods and services

- **Exports**: goods and services produced in one country and sold to buyers in another. They bring money **into** the country.
- **Imports**: goods and services bought from other countries. Money flows **out**.

Trade is not only in goods such as cars and food. **Services** are traded too: a foreign tourist's hotel stay in Edinburgh is a UK export of a service; a UK family's holiday in Spain is a UK import. The UK is a major exporter of services such as finance, insurance, higher education and business consulting, and imports many manufactured goods, fuel and food.

## Why trade happens: specialisation

Countries differ in their natural resources, climate, skills and technology, so each is better at producing some goods than others. If each **specialises** in what it does best and trades for the rest, total output rises.

### Worked example 1: gains from specialisation

Two invented countries each have 100 workers.

| | Grain per worker (tonnes) | Cloth per worker (bolts) |
|---|---|---|
| Country A | 10 | 2 |
| Country B | 4 | 6 |

**Without trade**, each puts 50 workers on each good:
- A: 500 tonnes of grain and 100 bolts of cloth.
- B: 200 tonnes of grain and 300 bolts of cloth.
- Total: **700 tonnes of grain and 400 bolts of cloth**.

**With specialisation**, A makes only grain and B only cloth:
- A: 1,000 tonnes of grain; B: 600 bolts of cloth.
- Total: **1,000 tonnes of grain and 600 bolts of cloth**.

The same 200 workers now produce 300 tonnes more grain and 200 bolts more cloth. By trading some grain for cloth, both countries can consume more than they could alone. This is the core reason trade raises living standards.

## Why countries import and export

| Countries import because | Countries export because |
|---|---|
| They cannot produce some goods at all (climate, missing resources) | They can sell surplus output beyond what home buyers want |
| Foreign producers are cheaper | A larger market allows economies of scale |
| Foreign goods are better quality or offer variety | Export earnings pay for imports |
| Firms need foreign components and materials | Firms can spread risk across several markets |

## Benefits for consumers and producers

**Consumers** gain lower prices, wider choice, access to goods that cannot be grown or made at home, and better quality because domestic firms face competition.

**Producers** gain bigger markets, lower unit costs through economies of scale, access to cheaper or better inputs, and pressure to innovate.

### Worked example 2: a producer imports components

A furniture maker uses 10,000 hinges a year. Domestic hinges cost £12 each; imported ones cost £9.

- Saving = 10,000 × (12 − 9) = **£30,000** a year.

Lower costs let the firm cut prices, raise profit or invest. But if the domestic hinge maker loses the order, its workers may lose their jobs, a cost of trade.

## Free trade agreements

**Free trade** means trade without barriers such as **tariffs** (taxes on imports), **quotas** (limits on the quantity imported) or burdensome rules. Countries can remove barriers to different degrees.

| Level | What members agree | Trade with non-members |
|---|---|---|
| Free trade agreement | Remove or reduce tariffs and quotas on each other's goods | Each member sets its own tariffs |
| Customs union | Free trade between members | All members charge a **common external tariff** |
| Single market | A customs union plus shared rules, and free movement of goods, services, capital and people | Common external tariff |

### Worked example 3: an agreement removes a tariff

An imported bicycle costs £400. A 15% tariff makes its price £460. A domestic bicycle sells for £450.

- With the tariff, the domestic bicycle is £10 cheaper, so many buyers choose it.
- A free trade agreement removes the tariff. The imported bicycle now costs £400, **£50 less** than the domestic one and £60 less than before.
- Consumers gain; the domestic producer must cut costs, improve quality or lose sales.

## The European Union

The **European Union (EU)** is a group of 27 European countries. It goes further than a free trade agreement: it is a **customs union** with a common external tariff and a **single market** with shared product rules and free movement of goods, services, capital and people between members.

The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020, and the transition period ended on 31 December 2020. Since then UK-EU trade has been governed by the **Trade and Cooperation Agreement**, which allows goods that meet its rules to be traded without tariffs or quotas. Because the UK is outside the customs union and single market, exporters now face customs declarations and checks that did not apply before, which add time and cost.

## Evaluating free trade agreements

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Lower prices and more choice for consumers | Domestic firms that cannot compete may close |
| Larger markets and economies of scale for producers | Job losses in those industries, often concentrated in certain regions |
| More competition, so firms become more efficient | Less freedom to protect new or strategic industries |
| Foreign firms invest to serve the wider market | Shared rules limit what each member can decide alone |

**How to judge:** ask who gains and who loses, whether workers who lose jobs can move into growing industries, and whether the gains to many consumers outweigh concentrated losses to some producers.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain one benefit of international trade for consumers.*

"Trade gives consumers access to cheaper goods made by more efficient producers abroad. **[benefit stated]** For example, if imported clothing is cheaper than clothing made at home, households pay less and have more income left to spend on other goods, so their living standards rise." **[developed with an example and a consequence]**

*Evaluate the effects on producers of joining a free trade agreement.*

"Exporters gain tariff-free access to a larger market, so they can sell more and benefit from economies of scale, lowering unit costs. **[benefit]** Firms that import components also face lower costs. **[second benefit]** However, domestic firms competing with imports lose the protection of tariffs and may lose sales or close. **[cost]** Overall, efficient exporters gain most and less competitive firms lose most, so the effect depends on how competitive a country's industries are." **[judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting that services are traded.
- Saying a tariff is a limit on quantity; that is a quota.
- Calling the EU "just a free trade agreement".
- Listing benefits without saying whether consumers or producers gain.
- Ignoring the costs of trade to some workers and firms.

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: say who benefits and how, then develop with an example from the case study.
- **Calculate**: show prices with and without a tariff, or the cost saving on inputs.
- **Evaluate**: weigh gains to consumers and exporters against losses to firms facing new competition, and decide which matters more in the context.
