## Section A

**1.** State the marks for Question 1(a) and Question 1(b) on Paper 1, and explain what the difference in marks suggests about how to divide your time. **[3]**

**2.** Rewrite this weak analytical sentence so that it selects evidence precisely, names a feature and explains an effect: *"The writer says 'the streets were empty and nobody was around at all', which shows it was quiet."* **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between writing **in the same style** as a text and writing **in a different style** while reusing its content. **[4]**

**4.** Give **four** linking verbs more precise than "shows", and use **one** of them in a sentence about the phrase "a modest little victory". **[5]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by commenting on the **overall style** of a text, and why a style label must be supported by specific instances. **[3]**

## Section B

Read the passage below (original, written for this page) and answer Questions 6 to 9.

> *Nobody tells you that the hardest part of running a marathon is the morning. Not the twentieth mile, not the hill
> at the halfway point: the morning. You stand in a pen with four thousand strangers, all of you pretending to stretch,
> all of you lying about how little you slept. Then the horn sounds, and suddenly the lie becomes the truth: you are
> ready, because you have to be.*

**6.** Identify the form and likely audience of the passage, and state its overall style in a phrase. Support your style judgement with **two** brief, embedded quotations. **[5]**

**7.** Analyse how **three** linguistic elements come together in the passage to create a single effect. **[6]**

**8.** Write **two or three** sentences continuing the passage **in the same style**. Then list **three** stylistic features you deliberately reproduced. **[6]**

**9.** Using the content of the passage, write the opening **60-80 words** of a leaflet for first-time marathon runners, giving advice on race morning. Then write **one** comparison paragraph comparing your leaflet opening with the original passage. **[10]**

**10.** Read this student paragraph from a Question 2 answer:

> *The writer uses lots of techniques. There is a metaphor, "a sea of umbrellas". There is also alliteration in
> "grey and grim". The writer uses short sentences too. These make the reader feel sad.*

Identify **three** weaknesses in the paragraph and rewrite it as a single integrated analytical paragraph. **[7]**

**11.** Explain how integrating individual analytical points into a broader overall response differs from listing features, and describe **two** practical ways to achieve integration in a Question 2 answer. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Question 1(a) carries **10 marks** and Question 1(b) carries **15 marks** [1]. Because 1(b) is worth more, it deserves **at least as much time as the writing task, including planning** [1]; candidates who spend most of the section on the short directed piece leave too little time for the more heavily weighted comparison [1].

**2.** Example: *The phrase "nobody was around at all" uses an absolute pronoun and the intensifier "at all" to stress total emptiness, making the street feel abandoned rather than simply quiet.* Credit **selection of a short phrase rather than the whole clause** [1], **a named feature** such as the pronoun "nobody" or intensifier "at all" [1], and **a precise effect beyond "quiet"**, such as abandonment or unease [1].

**3.** **Same style**: you produce **new writing that reproduces the source's stylistic features** [1], such as its sentence types, register, person and characteristic devices, without reusing its words [1]. **Different style**: you **keep the source's content** but present it in **a new form, register and structure** [1] suited to a different audience and purpose, so the information is transformed rather than paraphrased [1].

**4.** Any four from: suggests, implies, foregrounds, undercuts, positions, intensifies, softens, distances, frames, juxtaposes 1 mark each, maximum [4]. Example sentence: *The double diminutive in "a modest little victory" softens the achievement, implying the speaker is wary of seeming boastful.* [1]

**5.** Overall style is **a summary judgement of the manner of the whole text**, such as "wry and conversational" [1]. It must be **supported by specific instances** [1] because a label without quoted evidence is only assertion, and the syllabus skill is recognising style **and exemplifying it** through particular choices [1].

**6.** Form: **a personal or opinion piece, such as a magazine column or blog post about running** [1]. Audience: **runners or general readers interested in running, including those who have never raced** [1]. Style: **conversational, confiding and wryly humorous**, or similar [1]. Supporting quotations, embedded, one mark each: e.g. the confiding opening "Nobody tells you" positions the writer as an insider sharing a secret [1]; the comic exaggeration "all of you lying about how little you slept" adds gentle humour [1].

**7.** Any three elements shown working together for one effect, such as **building suspense and then release**. **Second-person "you"** places the reader in the starting pen [1] [1]; **repetition and correction** ("Not the twentieth mile, not the hill... the morning") delays the key word, building anticipation [1] [1]; the **present tense** ("You stand", "the horn sounds") makes the moment immediate [1]; the **final antithesis "the lie becomes the truth"** resolves the tension [1]. Credit must be for the combined effect, not three separate observations.

**8.** Example continuation: *By mile three, nobody is lying any more. You can hear it: the slap of shoes, the ragged breathing, the strangers who are suddenly your closest friends.* Credit the continuation for **matching the conversational second-person address** [1], **mixing longer and short sentences** [1] and **sustaining the wry tone** [1]. Credit the feature list for naming accurately **three features reproduced**, e.g. second person, present tense, a pattern of repetition or a colon used to deliver a punchline [1] [1] [1].

**9.** Leaflet opening (example): *Race morning: your checklist. Arrive at least an hour early. Eat a breakfast you have already tested in training. Expect to feel nervous; almost everyone does. Use the warm-up area, not the start pen, for stretching. When the horn sounds, start slower than you want to.* Credit **leaflet conventions** such as a heading, imperatives and short, scannable sentences [1] [1], **content reused and transformed**, such as nerves and the start, without copying phrases [1] [1], and **an informative, supportive register** for beginners [1]. Comparison paragraph: credit **comparison by point** of purpose, structure or sentence type [1]; **quotation from both texts** [1]; **explanation of differences by audience and purpose**, e.g. the source's "you are ready, because you have to be" entertains while the leaflet's imperative "start slower" instructs [1] [1]; **at least one similarity**, e.g. both use second person, but for different ends [1].

**10.** Weaknesses, any three: **feature-spotting without effect** for each technique [1]; **"lots of techniques" is vague** and adds nothing [1]; **"short sentences" is not exemplified** with a quotation [1]; the effect **"feel sad" is imprecise and unexplained**; the points are **not connected to an overall reading**. Credit the rewrite for **embedding short quotations** [1], **explaining precise effects** [1], **linking the features to one effect** [1] and **connecting to the text's purpose or overall style** [1]. Example: *The metaphor "a sea of umbrellas" turns the crowd into one grey, featureless mass, and the harsh alliteration of "grey and grim" reinforces this bleakness; set beside the writer's short declaratives, these choices create a mood of flat resignation that frames the whole description of the city.*

**11.** Listing treats each feature as **a separate observation**, while integration makes each point **advance one overall argument** about the text [1] [1]. Practical methods, any two: **open with a thesis about overall style and purpose**, then refer back to it in each paragraph [1]; **organise paragraphs by effect rather than by feature**, combining several features under one effect [1]; alternatively, finish each paragraph with a sentence that links its point to the writer's purpose.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting whole clauses where two or three words carry the effect.
- Labelling the overall style without quoting instances that prove it.
- Imitating a style by borrowing phrases instead of features.
- Writing a "different style" response that is a light paraphrase of the source.
- Comparison paragraphs that describe one text and then the other, with no linking language.
