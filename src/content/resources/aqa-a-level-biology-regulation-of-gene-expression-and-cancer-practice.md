## Questions

**1.** Define epigenetics, and explain why a change in the methylation of DNA is not a gene mutation. **[3]**

**2.** Explain how the steroid hormone oestrogen causes the transcription of a target gene in a cell that has the right receptor. **[5]**

**3.** Explain how decreased acetylation of histones inhibits the transcription of a gene. **[4]**

**4.** Four cell lines were tested for methylation of the promoter of gene K and for the amount of mRNA from gene K.

| Cell line | Promoter sites methylated (%) | mRNA (arbitrary units) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 5 | 210 |
| B | 12 | 190 |
| C | 81 | 14 |
| D | 88 | 9 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean mRNA level for lines with low methylation (A and B) and for lines with high methylation (C and D). **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in mean mRNA from low to high methylation. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe the relationship shown by the table. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the table does not prove that methylation lowers the mRNA level, and suggest one investigation that would test the idea. **[2]**

**5.** A researcher wants to reduce the translation of an mRNA that contains the sequence GAUCGGAUUCAG.
**(a)** Write the sequence of the siRNA strand that would pair with this region. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the siRNA stops the protein being made. **[3]**

**6.** A tumour grows from one cell.
**(a)** State three differences between a benign and a malignant tumour. **[3]**
**(b)** The cells of a tumour divide once every 24 hours and none die. Calculate the number of cells formed from one cell after 10 days. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** Explain how a mutation in a proto-oncogene can lead to the formation of a tumour. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how a tumour could form because of a change to a tumour suppressor gene that does not involve a change in the base sequence. **[3]**

**8.** In a follow-up study, 600 women with a high lifetime exposure to oestrogen and 700 women with a low exposure were observed. Breast cancer developed in 54 of the high-exposure women and 21 of the low-exposure women.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage of each group that developed breast cancer. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times greater the risk is in the high-exposure group. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how raised oestrogen concentrations could increase the chance of some breast cancers. **[3]**
**(d)** Give two reasons the data are not enough to show that oestrogen causes the difference. **[2]**

**9.** A variant allele of a tumour suppressor gene was found in 90 people. Cancer developed in 18 of them. In 2400 people without the variant, cancer developed in 120.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage in each group who developed cancer. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the relative risk for people with the variant. **[1]**
**(c)** A newspaper states that "everyone with this variant will get cancer". Evaluate the statement using the data. **[4]**

**10.** In a study of twins, 80 identical pairs and 80 non-identical pairs were each selected because one twin had a certain cancer. The other twin also had it in 30 identical pairs and 12 non-identical pairs.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage of pairs in which both twins had the cancer for each type of twin. **[2]**
**(b)** What do the figures suggest about genetic and environmental influences? **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest two reasons why not all the identical pairs had the cancer in both twins. **[2]**

**11.** A cancer cell line over-expresses an oncogene, and its promoter is hypomethylated. Each of two flasks was seeded with 1.2 × 10⁵ cells. One flask received siRNA matching the oncogene mRNA. After 48 hours the control flask held 4.8 × 10⁵ cells and the siRNA flask 1.9 × 10⁵ cells.
**(a)** Calculate how many times the cell number increased in each flask, and the percentage by which the final siRNA count is lower than the control. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how the siRNA reduced cell numbers. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain how hypomethylation could lead to over-expression of the oncogene. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest two ways the investigation could be improved. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Epigenetics is a heritable change in gene function [1] without a change in the base sequence of DNA [1]. Methylation adds groups to DNA but no base is substituted or deleted, so it is not a mutation [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* use the phrase "base sequence" for the second part; saying only that it is "not permanent" does not distinguish it from a mutation.

**2.** Oestrogen is lipid-soluble and diffuses through the cell-surface membrane [1]. It binds to a complementary receptor in the cytoplasm [1]. The receptor changes shape and the complex acts as a transcription factor [1]. The complex enters the nucleus through a nuclear pore and binds to a specific base sequence near the gene [1]. This lets RNA polymerase bind, so transcription is stimulated [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* give the sequence of events in order and say what moves into the nucleus; "oestrogen switches on the gene" gives the result without the mechanism.

**3.** Histones are positively charged and attract the negatively charged phosphate groups of DNA [1]. Acetylation reduces this attraction, so decreased acetylation means the histones hold DNA more strongly [1]. DNA is more tightly wound [1]. Transcription factors and RNA polymerase cannot reach the gene, so transcription is reduced [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* link the change in charge to the strength of binding; reversing the direction (acetylation increasing the hold) loses the explanation.

**4. (a)** Low: (210 + 190) / 2 = **200** [1]; high: (14 + 9) / 2 = **11.5** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (11.5 − 200) / 200 × 100 = −94.25, so a fall of **94%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Higher methylation is associated with lower mRNA (a negative correlation) [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Correlation does not show cause, and only four lines were studied [1]. Remove the methyl groups from lines C and D, for example with a drug, and see whether the mRNA level rises [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* calculate percentage change from the low-methylation value and quote the means; an answer to (d) needs a stated test, not only "do more repeats".

**5. (a)** Pair A with U and G with C [1]: **CUAGCCUAAGUC** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The siRNA strand binds to the mRNA by complementary base pairing [1]. The mRNA is then hydrolysed (broken down) [1], so it cannot be translated and the polypeptide is not made [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* a partner strand of RNA contains U, not T; and the stage affected is translation, not transcription.

**6. (a)** Any three from: benign grows slowly, malignant faster [1]; benign has a distinct border, malignant invades surrounding tissue [1]; benign does not spread, malignant forms secondary tumours by metastasis [1]. A further valid difference is that malignant cells are often less specialised, with abnormal nuclei. **[3]**
**(b)** 10 days is 10 divisions [1]; 2¹⁰ = **1024** cells [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* state both sides of each difference; for (b) count the divisions first and then use the power of two.

**7. (a)** The mutation changes the proto-oncogene into an oncogene [1]. Its product is made in excess or is permanently active [1], so the cell divides without the normal signals and uncontrolled division forms a tumour [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Hypermethylation of the promoter of the tumour suppressor gene [1] stops it being transcribed, so its protein is not made [1]. Division is no longer slowed and DNA damage is not repaired, so cells divide uncontrollably [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* name what each altered gene normally does, and say what is lost or gained; "the gene stops working" alone is not enough.

**8. (a)** High: 54 / 600 × 100 = **9.0%** [1]; low: 21 / 700 × 100 = **3.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 9.0 / 3.0 = **3.0 times** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Oestrogen binds to receptors in breast cells and stimulates transcription of genes for cell division [1]. More cell divisions mean more DNA replication [1], so more chances of a mutation in an oncogene or tumour suppressor gene [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** It is an association only [1]; other factors such as age, family history or diet might differ between the groups [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* divide by the lower-exposure risk for a ratio; for (d) name a variable that could differ rather than writing "other factors".

**9. (a)** Variant: 18 / 90 × 100 = **20%** [1]; no variant: 120 / 2400 × 100 = **5.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 20 / 5.0 = **4.0** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 72 of the 90 carriers (80%) did not develop cancer, so the statement is false [1]. The variant raises risk four-fold, so it is linked to cancer [1], but other genes, environment and epigenetic changes must also be involved [1]. Only 90 carriers were studied, so 20% is a less precise estimate than 5.0% from 2400 people [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* quote the numbers that contradict the claim, then comment on sample size; a judgement without data does not evaluate.

**10. (a)** Identical: 30 / 80 × 100 = **37.5%** [1]; non-identical: 12 / 80 × 100 = **15%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The rate is higher in identical twins, who share all their alleles (non-identical share about half), suggesting a genetic influence [1]. Because it is well below 100%, environment also affects risk [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Different environments or lifestyles [1]; different epigenetic changes or chance mutations in body cells [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* compare the two figures in the same sentence and then explain what the gap from 100% means.

**11. (a)** Control 4.8 / 1.2 = **4.0 times** [1]; siRNA 1.9 / 1.2 = **1.6 times** [1]; (4.8 − 1.9) / 4.8 × 100 = **60%** lower [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** siRNA pairs with the oncogene mRNA by complementary bases [1], and the mRNA is broken down [1], so less of the protein that stimulates division is made, so cells divide less [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Fewer methyl groups on the promoter mean transcription factors and RNA polymerase can bind more easily [1], so more mRNA of the oncogene is made [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Add a control with siRNA that does not match the gene [1]; use replicate flasks or more time points [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* in (d) say what the improvement would show; a control with a non-matching siRNA tests whether the effect is specific to this gene.


## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the hormone, rather than the hormone-receptor complex, enters the nucleus and binds to DNA.
- Writing that methylation or acetylation changes the base sequence.
- Getting the direction of acetylation wrong: less acetylation means more tightly bound DNA.
- Placing RNA interference at transcription, or writing T in an RNA strand.
- Dividing the change by the final figure when the starting figure should be the divisor.
- Stating "oncogenes cause cancer" without saying what the normal gene does.
- Treating a correlation as proof, or giving no evaluation of sample size.
- Describing a tumour as cancerous when it is benign.
