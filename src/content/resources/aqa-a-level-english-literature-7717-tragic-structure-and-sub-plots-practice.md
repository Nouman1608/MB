## Section A: structural concepts

**1.** AQA describes tragic structure as moving "from order to disorder". Explain why a strong answer
must define the opening order before discussing disorder. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a sub-plot that mirrors the main plot and one that intensifies it, giving
an example of each from *King Lear*. **[4]**

**3.** Explain how the see-saw structure of *Richard II* shapes the play's view of kingship. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why the placement of the Boston hotel scene late in Act 2 of *Death of a Salesman* is a
structural choice rather than simply part of the story. **[3]**

**5.** Identify one way in which the ending of a tragedy you have studied restores order and one way
in which it leaves loss unresolved. **[4]**

## Section B: an original scene and essay skills

**Extract (original drama).** A merchant, Aldo, has just been told that his ship has been lost. His
daughter Bianca has secretly married his clerk. Earlier in the play, Aldo's servant was dismissed for
a small theft.

> **ALDO:** Lost? The whole cargo? Then I am a beggar
> who owns a house. *(He laughs.)* Well. Well, the house is good.
> **BIANCA:** Father, sit. There is something I must tell you.
> **ALDO:** Not now. Fetch Matteo, and the ledgers. We'll count
> what's left before the town begins to count it for us.
> **BIANCA:** Matteo is the thing I have to tell you.
> *(A pause. Off-stage, a bell rings for evening prayer.)*
> **ALDO:** The bell. My father said it rang for debts.
> Go on, then. Ruin me in order. Ship, then daughter.

**6.** Analyse how the extract uses structure within the scene to build tension. **[6]**

**7.** The extract comes from the middle of the play. Explain how it could function as a turning point
in a tragic structure. **[4]**

**8.** The dismissal of the servant is a minor strand earlier in the play. Explain two ways a writer
could develop it into a sub-plot that mirrors or contrasts with Aldo's fall. **[4]**

**9.** A student writes: "Structurally, *Othello* moves from Venice to Cyprus." Explain how to turn
this observation into an analytical point. **[3]**

**10.** Write a thesis sentence and three paragraph topics for the question: "Tragic endings restore
order, but at too great a cost." Discuss with reference to one text you have studied. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how the structure of one poem from the Keats selection or the anthology contributes to
its tragic effect. **[4]**

**12.** For a Section C answer linking two texts, explain how comparing their structures could meet
AO4, and give an example of a structural connection between two texts from the Option 1A list.
**[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Disorder can only be measured against what came before [1], so the answer must say what kind
of order existed (political, familial, moral) and whether it was ever stable, which allows a
judgement on what is lost or whether the opening was already flawed [1].

**2.** A mirroring sub-plot repeats the main plot's pattern [1]: Gloucester, like Lear, trusts the
deceitful child (Edmund) and banishes the loyal one (Edgar) [1]. An intensifying sub-plot makes the
main plot's metaphor literal or more extreme [1]: Lear's blindness of judgement becomes Gloucester's
physical blinding [1].

**3.** As Richard falls, Bolingbroke rises [1], so power appears to pass from one man to another rather
than disappear [1]; this suggests that kingship depends on political strength as well as divine
right, which raises the question of whether the crown can be held by the man who takes it [1].

**4.** The audience sees many earlier scenes of Biff's hostility without knowing the cause [1];
withholding the discovery until late creates a delayed recognition for the audience [1] and places
the truth close to the catastrophe, so it explains the conflict at the moment Willy is losing control
[1].

**5.** Restoration, any valid [2]: for example, at the end of *Othello* Lodovico takes command and
justice is arranged for Iago [1], so the state's authority returns [1]. Unresolved loss, any valid
[2]: for example, the marriage and Othello himself are destroyed [1], and the bodies on the bed leave
the audience with grief that public order cannot answer [1]. Accept equivalent points on any studied
text.

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- The scene opens with the catastrophe already reported ("Lost?"), so tension comes from what follows.
- Aldo's laugh and "the house is good" show denial, a pause before the full blow.
- Bianca's attempt to speak is deferred by "Not now", delaying a second revelation.
- His order to fetch Matteo creates dramatic irony, since Matteo is the secret.
- The shared verse lines show the characters talking across each other.
- The stage-directed pause slows the pace at the point of revelation.
- The bell intrudes from off-stage, linking the private moment to the wider world and to debt.
- "Ruin me in order. Ship, then daughter" names the scene's own structure, turning two blows into a
  sequence.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: the first loss (the ship) moves Aldo from prosperity towards ruin; the second
revelation (the marriage) threatens family order as well as wealth; his reaction suggests a choice is
coming, so the play's direction can change here; the bell suggests a sense of judgement or fate;
"Ruin me in order" shows his awareness that disaster is now gathering pace, a point of no return.

**8.** Two developments, [2] each, with [1] for the idea and [1] for its structural effect: the servant
could return as an opponent who exploits Aldo's ruin, so a minor injustice grows into a cause of the
catastrophe; the servant could suffer a parallel fall from a smaller theft, mirroring Aldo's own
losses on a smaller scale and questioning his judgement; the servant could show mercy that Aldo
refused him, a contrast that measures Aldo's pride.

**9.** Explain the effect of the move [1]: Cyprus removes Venetian law and society, leaving a military
outpost where Iago controls information [1]; link it to the structural pattern of order to disorder
or the move from public to private space, ending in the bedchamber [1].

**10.** A thesis that takes a position on "too great a cost" [1]; three topic sentences that each
address a stage of the ending [2 for three relevant, 1 for two]; one topic that weighs the
counter-view, such as consolation or recognition [1].

**11.** Identification of a structural feature in a named poem [1]; precise reference to where it
occurs [1]; analysis of its effect [1]; link to a tragic aspect such as fate, loss or absence [1]. For
example, the two movements of "The Convergence of the Twain" bring ship and iceberg together in the
form itself, so the collision seems fated.

**12.** AO4 rewards connections, and structure provides a shared method to compare [1]; the answer
should discuss both texts within each point [1]. Example [1]: *Death of a Salesman* and *The Great
Gatsby* both reveal the past late, so recognition comes near the end; or *Tess* and *Richard II* both
trace a fall in stages.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling the order of events instead of naming a structural choice and its effect.
- Treating the sub-plot as separate from the main plot.
- Ignoring the ending, or claiming that order is restored without asking for whom.
- In linking answers, describing each text's structure separately rather than comparing them.

For full practice, write a 25-mark Section B essay on your Shakespeare play in about 50 minutes,
using only structural evidence for the first half of your plan.
