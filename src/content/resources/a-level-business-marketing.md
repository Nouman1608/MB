Topic 3 **Marketing** is AS Level content in Cambridge International
AS & A Level Business (9609), following [Topic 2 Human Resource
Management](/resources/a-level-business-human-resource-management/).
Cambridge frames the topic around one central question: how does a
business find out what potential customers need, and then provide for
those needs? A Level candidates return to marketing again at A Level
in Topic 9 (Strategic marketing), which builds on this AS Level
content rather than repeating it.

## 3.1 The nature of marketing

This sub-topic sets out the conceptual foundations before the
practical tools that follow.

- **The role of marketing** and its relationship with other business
  functions: marketing objectives, and how they link to a business's
  wider corporate objectives.
- **Demand and supply**: the factors influencing demand for and supply
  of a business's products, and how demand, supply and price interact.
- **Markets**: how markets differ (consumer versus industrial; local,
  national and international), the distinction between **product
  orientation** (starting from what the business can make) and
  **customer/market orientation** (starting from what customers want),
  and how market share and market growth are measured and interpreted.
- **Consumer and industrial marketing**: classifying products, and how
  marketing differs between **B2C** (business to consumer) and **B2B**
  (business to business) contexts.
- **Mass marketing versus niche marketing**: the features, advantages
  and disadvantages of targeting the whole market versus a specific
  segment.
- **Market segmentation**: the three named methods -- geographic,
  demographic and psychographic -- and their advantages and
  disadvantages.
- **Customer relationship marketing (CRM)**: its aims, and its costs
  and benefits to a business.

## 3.2 Market research

This sub-topic covers how a business actually gathers the information
3.1's concepts depend on.

- **Purposes of market research**: identifying the main features of a
  market (size, growth, competitors) and identifying customer and
  consumer characteristics, profiles, wants and needs.
- **Primary versus secondary research**: the distinction between the
  two, the main features of each, and the usefulness of the data each
  produces.
- **Sampling**: why sampling is needed, and its limitations.
- **Market research data**: the reliability of collected data,
  analysing both quantitative and qualitative data, and interpreting
  information presented in tables, charts and graphs.

## 3.3 The marketing mix

This is the largest and most heavily applied sub-topic in Topic 3,
built around the **4Ps**: Product, Price, Promotion and Place.

**Product** covers the difference between goods and services, the
tangible and intangible attributes of a product, the importance of
product development, and **product differentiation** and **unique
selling point (USP)**. This flows into **product portfolio analysis**:
the product life cycle and decisions about extension strategies, the
**Boston Matrix** and its uses, and the impact of portfolio analysis
on marketing decisions -- a business rarely relies on a single product,
so decisions about one product in the portfolio need to account for
where the others sit.

**Price** covers the objectives and usefulness of a named set of
pricing methods: competitive, penetration, skimming, price
discrimination, dynamic, cost-based and psychological pricing. Exam
questions frequently ask you to recommend and justify a specific
method for a given scenario, so know not just what each method means
but the market conditions that make it the sensible choice.

**Promotion** covers the objectives and usefulness of different
promotion methods: advertising promotion, sales promotion, direct
promotion, developments in digital promotion, and the specific roles
of **packaging** and **branding** within promotion.

**Place** (channels of distribution) covers the objectives and
usefulness of different distribution channels, including both digital
and physical distribution.

## How the three sub-topics build on each other

3.1 gives you the concepts and vocabulary (orientation, segmentation,
demand and supply); 3.2 gives you the method for finding out which of
those concepts actually apply to a specific market; 3.3 gives you the
tools for acting on what 3.2 discovers. A well-structured answer to a
marketing-mix question should, where the scenario allows it, connect
back to segmentation or market research findings rather than
recommending a marketing mix element in isolation -- this is exactly
what the syllabus means by developing "the skills to be able to
recommend a suitable marketing mix for a business in a specific
context."

## How to approach it

Because 3.3 is the most application-heavy sub-topic and covers the
most named methods, build yourself a reference table of the seven
pricing methods and the market condition each suits best (for example,
penetration pricing for a new entrant trying to build market share
quickly; price skimming for a genuinely novel product with few early
competitors), since "recommend and justify" questions are the most
common format on this content. The Boston Matrix and product life
cycle are frequently tested together -- practise using one to explain
decisions made about the other, since a product entering the "decline"
stage of its life cycle is the same signal a Boston Matrix might
present as a "dog," and connecting the two frameworks demonstrates a
stronger command of the material than treating them as separate tools.
For 3.2, keep primary and secondary research anchored to concrete
examples (a survey a business commissions itself, versus government
trade statistics it already has access to) rather than abstract
definitions, since scenario questions test whether you can identify
which type of research a business used, not just define the terms.
